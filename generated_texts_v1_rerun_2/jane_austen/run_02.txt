The quiet of Mrs. Gable’s drawing room, usually disturbed only by the distant clatter of a passing cart or the gentle chime of the mantel clock, was on this particular morning animated by the arrival of Mr. Alistair Finch. He was a young man whose easy conversation and elegant manners seldom failed to enliven any company, and on this occasion, his attention was directed with singular charm towards Miss Eleanor Vance. Miss Eliza Pemberton, observing from her seat by the window, where the autumn light fell rather becomingly upon her work, noted how Mr. Finch leaned over the small portfolio of watercolour sketches, his expression one of profound, almost theatrical, admiration.

“These studies, Miss Vance,” he began, tracing a finger delicately near a depiction of a cluster of wild roses, “are truly beyond the common attainment. One sees in this particular rendering not merely the bloom, but the very essence of fleeting beauty, the wistful promise of spring fading into summer’s languor. Do you imagine,” he continued, turning his gaze, at once earnest and yet strangely knowing, towards Miss Vance, “that the subject itself, so delicate and so precisely observed, drew forth this unusual depth from your pencil, or that it is rather the pencil, guided by such exquisite feeling, which elevates the commonplace to the poetic?” He paused, inviting her reply. Miss Vance, whose natural modesty was as evident as her talent, blushed a shade deeper than the rose he had just praised.

“Indeed, Mr. Finch, I hardly know,” she murmured, her voice almost lost amidst the rustle of the leaves outside. “I simply endeavoured to capture the colours as they were. My hand, I fear, is often less obedient to my intention than one might wish.”

“Ah, but there is the genius of it!” cried Mr. Finch, with a slight, knowing glance towards Eliza, though his words were entirely for Miss Vance. “The very struggle, the modest disclaimers of the artist, only serve to highlight the spontaneous triumph. It is not the conscious striving, but the innate grace, which impresses us. Sometimes one aims for simple accuracy, and sometimes one achieves a deeper truth without even intending it. I confess, Miss Vance, I sometimes wish my own efforts, however painstaking, might yield such accidental felicity. What nonsense one talks, Miss Pemberton, when admiring such a gift, if one talks at all; your real artists, I suppose, let their work speak; but we gentlemen dilettantes, if we get hold of a brush, or a word. Miss Vance mentioned her hand being disobedient. There, it is a triumph regardless. I have the pleasure, madam,” he said, addressing Mrs. Gable, who had just entered with a tray of fresh ginger biscuits, “of presenting to you the work of an accomplished hand, quite healed of its supposed imperfections.”

He was warmly thanked by Mrs. Gable, who, with an air of bustling satisfaction, declared how proud she was of her dear Eleanor’s accomplishments, and what a joy it was to see such talent cultivated. To escape a little from this innocent, yet rather tiresome, panegyric, Mr. Finch turned to Eliza.

“Miss Pemberton, you have been remarkably silent. Does not this collection of nature’s portraiture strike you with a similar admiration? Or are you perhaps more critical than I, more apt to discern the technical deficiency than the emotional resonance?”

Eliza smiled, a smile that conveyed a quiet amusement mixed with a hint of challenge. “I confess, Mr. Finch, my eye is perhaps less attuned to the poetic flourish, and more to the honest representation. Miss Vance’s skill is undeniable; the colours are true, the forms delicate. But as for the deeper truths you speak of, one must be careful not to attribute too much to a simple flower, however charmingly rendered.”

He laughed, a light, agreeable sound. “Spoken with all the delightful candour I have come to expect. You are quite right, of course, to guard against oversentimentality. Yet, I maintain, there are certain works which, by their very simplicity, invite us to discover layers of meaning the artist herself may not have consciously intended. This, for instance,” he pointed to a study of a single violet, “carries with it, does it not, the quietude of a secluded garden, a hint of something shy and retiring, yet possessed of an intrinsic loveliness? One might almost imagine the very hand that drew it.”

Miss Vance, who had been quietly rearranging the sketches, looked up at him for a moment, coloured deeply, and quickly turned her attention to a different drawing. Mr. Finch, with an unaffected air, took another sketch from the portfolio and, turning to Eliza, said, “Here is something quite different. A view of the old mill. Do you know it? The perspective is admirable. And here, a series of architectural studies, perhaps for the new parsonage? That, from such a quarter, one might expect. These were all done, I understand, for the Reverend Mr. Dalton. Very thoughtful of him to commission them, was it not? He knew Miss Vance’s family could benefit. I honour that part of the attention particularly; it shows it to have been so thoroughly from the heart. Nothing hastily done; nothing incomplete. True benevolence only could have prompted it.”

Eliza wished he would be less pointed, yet could not help being amused; and when on glancing her eye towards Eleanor Vance she caught the remains of a smile, when she saw that with all the deep blush of consciousness, there had been a smile of secret delight, she had less scruple in the amusement, and much less compunction with respect to her. This amiable, diligent, seemingly unassuming Miss Vance was apparently cherishing very reprehensible feelings. He brought all the remaining drawings to her, and they looked them over together. Eliza took the opportunity of whispering, “You speak too plain. She must understand you.”

“I hope she does. I would have her understand me. I am not in the least ashamed of my meaning.”

“But really, I am half ashamed, and wish I had never entertained the idea.”

“I am very glad you did, and that you communicated it to me. I have now a key to all her modest demurs and blushes. Leave shame to her. If she feels it, it is a sign she might be at fault.”

“She is not entirely without it, I think.”

“I do not see much sign of it. She is now arranging the sketches of the mill, his favourite, at this moment.”

Shortly afterwards, Mrs. Gable, passing near the window, descried Mr. Harding on horseback not far off. “Mr. Harding I declare! I must speak to him if possible, just to thank him. I will not open the window here; it would give you all cold; but I can go into the conservatory you know. I dare say he will come in when he knows who is here. Quite delightful to have you all meet so! Our little room so honoured!” She was in the adjoining chamber while she still spoke, and opening the casement there, immediately called Mr. Harding’s attention, and every syllable of their conversation was as distinctly heard by the others, as if it had passed within the same apartment.

“How d’ye do? How d’ye do? Very well, I thank you. So obliged to you for the loan of your gardener last week. My hydrangeas are quite revived. Pray come in; do come in. You will find some friends here.” So began Mrs. Gable; and Mr. Harding seemed determined to be heard in his turn, for most resolutely and commandingly did he say, “How is your niece, Mrs. Gable? I want to inquire after you all, but particularly your niece. How is Miss Vance? I hope she caught no cold from sketching outdoors last Tuesday. How is she today? Tell me how Miss Vance is.” And Mrs. Gable was obliged to give a direct answer before he would hear her in any thing else. The listeners were amused; and Eliza gave Mr. Finch a look of particular meaning. But Mr. Finch still smiled in steady composure.

“So obliged to you! So very much obliged to you for the gardener,” resumed Mrs. Gable. He cut her short with, “I am going to Netherfield. Can I do any thing for you?”

“Oh! dear, Netherfield, are you? Mrs. Thorne was saying the other day she wanted something from Netherfield.”

“Mrs. Thorne has servants to send. Can I do any thing for you?”

“No, I thank you. But do come in. Who do you think is here? Miss Pemberton and Mr. Finch; so kind as to call to see Eleanor’s new sketches. Do put up your horse at the stables, and come in.”

“Well,” said he, in a deliberating manner, “for five minutes, perhaps.”

“And here is Miss Vance too! Quite delightful; so many friends!”

“No, not now, I thank you. I could not stay two minutes. I must get on to Netherfield as fast as I can.”

“Oh! do come in. They will be so very happy to see you.”

“No, no; your room is full enough. I will call another day, and see the sketches.”

“Well, I am so sorry! Oh! Mr. Harding, what a delightful evening we had at the Darcy’s last week; how extremely pleasant. Did you ever see such dancing? Was it not delightful? Miss Pemberton and Mr. Finch; I never saw any thing equal to it.”

“Oh! very delightful indeed; I can say nothing less, for I suppose Miss Pemberton and Mr. Finch are hearing every thing that passes. And (raising his voice still more) I do not see why Miss Vance should not be mentioned too. I think Miss Vance dances very well; and Miss Eleanor Vance is the very best accompanist, without exception, in the county. Now, if your friends have any gratitude, they will say something pretty loud about you and me in return; but I cannot stay to hear it.”

“Oh! Mr. Harding, one moment more; something of consequence, so shocked! Eleanor and I are both so shocked about the missing seedling!”

“What is the matter now?”

“To think of your sending us all your prize camellias. You said you had a great many, and now you have not one left. We really are so shocked! Mr. Dawson may well be angry. William Giles mentioned it here. You should not have done it, indeed you should not. Ah! he is off. He never can bear to be thanked. But I thought he would have stayed now, and it would have been a pity not to have mentioned.... Well,” returning to the room, “I have not been able to succeed. Mr. Harding cannot stop. He is going to Netherfield. He asked me if he could do any thing....”

“Yes,” said Eleanor, “we heard his kind offers, we heard every thing.”

“Oh! yes, my dear, I dare say you might, because you know, the door was open, and the window was open, and Mr. Harding spoke loud. You must have heard every thing to be sure. ‘Can I do any thing for you at Netherfield?’ said he; so I just mentioned.... Oh! Miss Pemberton, must you be going? You seem but just come, so very obliging of you.” Eliza found it really time to be at home; the visit had already lasted long; and on examining watches, so much of the morning was perceived to be gone, that Mr. Finch and her companion taking leave also, could allow themselves only to walk with the two young ladies to the rectory gates, before they set their own course for home.

Eliza, walking arm in arm with Miss Pemberton, felt the crisp autumn air a welcome change after the close warmth of the rectory drawing room. Mr. Finch, ever attentive, strolled a pace or two ahead with Eleanor, making polite conversation about the changing colours of the leaves. The rectory gates, with their sturdy stone pillars, soon loomed into view, marking the inevitable moment of separation.

“Well,” said Miss Pemberton, turning to Eliza with a sweet smile, “it was indeed a most pleasant morning. I confess I had not anticipated such agreeable company when Mr. Finch proposed a call.”

Eliza returned the smile warmly. “Nor I, Miss Pemberton, though I am ever delighted to spend an hour with Eleanor. She is quite the most amiable of companions.”

Mr. Finch, overhearing this, paused at the gate. “Indeed she is, Miss Eliza. A true credit to the rectory, and to the county. Now, we must take our leave. My mother, I apprehend, will be expecting me to convey her regards to Mrs. Philips by three o’clock at the latest, and we have a considerable distance yet to cover.”

“Of course,” said Eleanor, with a slight dip of her head. “Pray, give our respects to your excellent mother, Mr. Finch. And to your own family, Miss Pemberton. We hope to see you again very soon.”

“Undoubtedly,” replied Miss Pemberton, allowing Mr. Finch to open the gate for her. “Perhaps at the assembly next month, or sooner, should the opportunity arise

Eleanor closed the heavy oak gates with a soft thud that echoed the finality of their guests’ departure. She turned to Eliza, a faint smile playing on her lips. “Mr. Finch is ever a man of dispatch,” she observed, “and Miss Pemberton, despite her apparent gentleness, seems quite accustomed to such promptness.”

Eliza nodded, linking her arm once more with Eleanor’s as they began the short walk back up the gravel path to the rectory door. The crispness in the air had sharpened perceptibly since they had stepped outside, and a few golden leaves, loosened by the morning’s breeze, danced across their path. “Indeed,” Eliza replied, “though I confess I found myself wishing their conversation might have been extended. Miss Pemberton has a rather thoughtful turn of phrase when she is not merely agreeing with Mr. Finch.”

“She is not without intelligence, certainly,” Eleanor conceded, “but one feels, with her, that much of it is held in reserve, lest it should disturb the tranquility of her station. Her family, I understand, are most particular about such matters.”

“Ah, the expectations of society,” Eliza murmured, a hint of amusement in her voice. “They do indeed shape us, do they not? Though I imagine a spirit as independent as yours, Eleanor, finds them somewhat… constricting at times.”

Eleanor gave a small, almost imperceptible sigh. “One learns to navigate them, Eliza. What cannot be altered must be endured with grace, or at least with civility.” She paused at the rectory entrance, turning the brass handle. “Besides, there are comforts within these walls that often make the strictures of the world seem less burdensome. Mama, for instance, has a way of making one forget the outside altogether.”

As they stepped into the quiet hall, the warmth of the house enveloped them, a pleasant contrast to the cooler air without. The faint scent of beeswax and dried lavender hung in the air. They removed their bonnets and gloves, placing them on the polished mahogany table by the door.

“I imagine Mama will be eager for a full report of their departure,” Eleanor said, leading the way towards the drawing room. “She always feels a responsibility for the good order of such leave-takings.”

They found Mrs. Philips seated by the window, her knitting needles clacking a steady rhythm against a half-finished shawl. She looked up as they entered, her spectacles perched on the end of her nose. “Well, my dears,” she began, her voice brimming with anticipation, “are they finally gone? I confess, I thought Mr. Finch had almost forgotten his mother’s three o’clock engagement. Though he is, to be sure, a most estimable young man. Did Miss Pemberton seem pleased with her visit? I trust she was not too fatigued by Mr. Harding’s rather boisterous exit.”

Eliza and Eleanor exchanged a glance. “They seemed in good spirits, Mama,” Eleanor replied, taking a seat opposite her. “Mr. Finch was quite concerned with the time, as you predicted, and Miss Pemberton expressed her pleasure at the company.”

“Indeed,” Eliza added, seating herself comfortably. “She spoke of hoping to see us at the assembly next month, or sooner.”

Mrs. Philips nodded approvingly. “Excellent, excellent. It is always good to extend one’s circle of acquaintance, particularly with families of such unimpeachable standing as the Pembertons. Though I have always found Mrs. Pemberton to be a trifle… precise. But then, one must not expect too much from any individual, must one? No, one must not.” She resumed her knitting, her needles clicking with renewed vigour. “And Mr. Harding. Did he indeed rush off as if the hounds of Hades were at his heels? Such a peculiar man. Always so eager to be off, yet so kind-hearted, in his own way, to offer assistance. Though I hardly know what assistance he imagines he could render at Netherfield, beyond perhaps startling poor Mrs. Bennet with his sudden arrival.”

“He seemed to think nothing of the journey,” Eleanor said, “and offered to carry messages or parcels for anyone who required it.”

“Which, as we heard,” Eliza interjected with a smile, “you politely declined, Mrs. Philips, as you had no need of such services.”

Mrs. Philips pursed her lips. “Well, what would I send to Netherfield? A potted plant? A book of sermons? Mrs. Bennet cares for neither, I daresay. No, I had no occasion to trouble him. Though it was good of him to offer. One cannot fault the man for a lack of kindness, only for a want of… well, of repose. He seems incapable of sitting still for longer than five minutes. It is quite trying. And that business with his camellias! Giving them all away, as William Giles informed me. To think! His prize camellias, gone! Mr. Dawson will be furious. Such a waste. Though it does show a certain generosity of spirit, I suppose, however ill-advised.”

Eliza listened, half-amused, half-puzzled. The camellia matter seemed to have been a source of much discussion, but the details remained somewhat vague to her. She thought of the robust, glossy-leaved camellia plants that usually adorned Mrs. Philips’s conservatory, their dark green foliage promising bursts of vibrant colour in the colder months. Mrs. Philips took immense pride in them, tending to them with a devotion usually reserved for a favourite pet.

“Speaking of which,” Mrs. Philips said, suddenly pausing her knitting and peering out the window. “The air has grown quite sharp, has it not? I daresay my prize specimen, the one with the particularly delicate white bloom, will be feeling the chill. I must remember to instruct William Giles to move it further into the conservatory, perhaps nearer the warmth of the main house. Or no, perhaps I should go myself, just to be certain. He has a tendency to be rather… robust in his handling of such fragile beauties.”

She rose with a determined air, laying her knitting carefully aside. “Eleanor, my dear, would you fetch my small gardening shears? The ones with the mother-of-pearl handle. And Eliza, would you care to accompany me? The conservatory is looking particularly well just now, and the fragrance of the early jasmine is quite delightful.”

Eliza, always amenable to a stroll and curious about the rectory’s floral treasures, readily agreed. Eleanor disappeared to fetch the shears, and Eliza followed Mrs. Philips through a side door that led to a long, glass-paned corridor connecting the main house to the conservatory. The air here was noticeably warmer and thicker, humid with the scent of damp earth and verdant leaves.

Mrs. Philips moved with a briskness that belied her years, her gaze sweeping over the rows of potted plants, her hand occasionally brushing a leaf or straightening a stray tendril. “Such a blessing, this conservatory,” she declared. “Even in the bleakest winter, one can find a touch of spring within its walls. It is a constant reminder of nature’s benevolence, Eliza. A source of much quiet contemplation.”

They reached the main section of the conservatory, a spacious, glass-roofed area filled with an exotic array of plants. Eliza admired a towering fern, its fronds unfurling like intricate green lace, and a cluster of vibrant fuchsias hanging gracefully from the ceiling. Mrs. Philips, however, had a specific destination in mind. She headed directly towards a raised bed where her most cherished camellias usually resided.

Her cheerful chatter ceased abruptly. She stopped, her head tilted, her spectacles sliding slightly down her nose. Her eyes, usually so keen, narrowed in an expression of puzzlement, then alarm.

“But… where is it?” she murmured, her voice losing its usual confident tone. “My finest white camellia. The one I have nursed for fifteen years, since it was but a fragile cutting from Lady Dalton’s estate. It should be here. Directly here, among its fellows. Do you see it, Eliza?”

Eliza followed Mrs. Philips’s gaze to the empty spot. There was indeed a noticeable gap in the row, a space where a large, healthy camellia plant, easily identifiable by its glossy, dark leaves, should have been. Only a few scattered fallen leaves and an imprint in the damp soil remained.

“Perhaps William Giles has moved it indoors for the season?” Eliza offered, though the rest of the plants of similar hardiness were still in place.

Mrs. Philips shook her head slowly, a dawning comprehension chilling her features. “No. No, he would not move such a plant without my express permission. And I gave no such permission. Not for that one. That one is too precious to be moved without the utmost care. It is almost ready to bud, you know. The first white blooms of the year.” She looked around frantically, as if the plant might have simply wandered off. “William! William Giles!” she called, her voice rising with each repetition.

Eleanor appeared then, the mother-of-pearl shears in her hand, her brow furrowed in concern at the commotion. “Mama? What is the matter? Is anything amiss?”

Mrs. Philips turned, her face a mask of disbelief and growing horror. “Amis? Amis, Eleanor? My prize white camellia is gone! Vanished! From its very bed! Do you know anything of this?”

Eleanor’s face paled. She looked from her mother to the empty space, her eyes widening. “Gone? Oh, Mama! But… but Mr. Harding… he… he asked for them.”

A dreadful silence descended upon the conservatory, broken only by the gentle drip of water from a nearby fern. Mrs. Philips slowly turned her head towards Eleanor, her gaze fixed and unblinking. “Mr. Harding,” she repeated, each syllable laden with foreboding. “Mr. Harding asked for… for *my* camellias? And you, Eleanor, you gave them to him? To him? My prize specimen?” Her voice had dropped to a whisper, but it was a whisper more terrifying than any shout.

Eleanor wrung her hands. “He said… he said he was in need of a fine plant, Mama. He spoke of having given all of *his* own away, and that he wished to present one to a lady who was particularly fond of them. He said it would be a kindness. And he was in such a hurry! I thought… I thought perhaps you would not mind so much, for such a worthy cause. And you often say how generous Mr. Harding is, how kind-hearted…” Her voice trailed off, overcome by the enormity of her confession.

Mrs. Philips stared at her daughter, her mouth agape. “Eleanor! Are you telling me that Mr. Harding, that peculiar, hurrying man, came here, and upon hearing that *he* had given away *his* plants, you immediately offered him *mine*? My prize camellia? The one William Giles and I have nurtured for fifteen years? The one from Lady Dalton’s? The one I speak of with such reverence to every visitor? The one that is almost ready to bud its first, magnificent white bloom of the season?”

“He was so insistent, Mama,” Eleanor pleaded, her eyes glistening. “And he did not specify which. He only said a camellia. And I thought… I thought it would be a pleasant gesture, a mark of our esteem. He seemed so grateful. He took it with such haste.”

“Haste!” Mrs. Philips exclaimed, her voice finally breaking free from its shocked whisper. “Of course he was in haste! He knew he was taking something that was not his to take, nor yours to give! To think! To think that such a precious, irreplaceable treasure has been carried off by that… that whirlwind of a man, merely because he ‘needed a plant’! And you, Eleanor, my own daughter, the very child I have instructed in the proper care and appreciation of every leaf and blossom in this very conservatory, you allowed it!”

Eliza, feeling the tension in the air thicken to an almost unbearable degree, stepped forward gently. “Perhaps, Mrs. Philips, Mr. Harding did not understand the true value of the plant. He may have simply seen a fine specimen and not realised its particular significance to you. And Eleanor, I am sure, acted with the very best of intentions, thinking to be hospitable.”

Mrs. Philips rounded on Eliza, her eyes flashing. “Intention, Miss Eliza, is a fine thing, but it does not replace a fifteen-year-old camellia! And as for Mr. Harding not understanding its value, why, the man has camellias of his own! Or rather, he *had* them, until he so foolishly gave them all away! He knows precisely the worth of a prize bloom. He knew exactly what he was taking. This is not mere oversight; it is… it is a depredation! A horticultural catastrophe!” She gestured dramatically at the empty space. “My poor, poor camellia! Gone! To Netherfield, no doubt, to be given to some flighty young lady who will probably allow it to wither for want of water, or worse, place it in an unsuitable position where it will never bloom again!”

Eleanor burst into tears, her shoulders shaking. “Oh, Mama, I am so very sorry! I truly did not think! He asked so kindly, and he was so very pressed for time, and I wished to be obliging.”

“Obliging!” Mrs. Philips scoffed, though the sight of Eleanor’s tears softened her expression slightly. “Obliging at the cost of my very finest plant! Do you know how long I have waited for that particular white bloom? The purity of it, the delicate scent! It is an anticipation that has sustained me through many a gloomy day. And now… gone! A mere trifle to Mr. Harding, no doubt, but to me, Eliza, to me it is a loss of considerable magnitude.” She wrung her hands, mimicking Eleanor’s earlier gesture. “What am I to do? What can be done? It is surely too late to send a message to Netherfield. He will be there by now, presenting my camellia as if it were his own generous gift! The sheer effrontery of it!”

“Perhaps,” Eliza suggested, trying to inject a note of practicality into the lamentation, “we might write a letter? Explaining the misunderstanding. Mr. Harding, I am sure, would return it if he understood his error.”

“A letter?” Mrs. Philips repeated, as if the concept were entirely foreign to her. “And what would such a letter say? ‘Dear Mr. Harding, pray return my camellia, for my daughter, in a moment of utter folly, gave away my most cherished possession’? It would sound quite absurd! And besides, by the time a letter reaches Netherfield, and a reply is dispatched, and the plant returned, it will have suffered immeasurably from the journey. A camellia is not a parcel of dry goods, Eliza; it requires care and stability. Its roots will be disturbed, its delicate buds irrevocably damaged. No, a letter is out of the question.”

She began to pace the narrow aisle between the plant beds, her hands clasped tightly before her. “This is all due to his precipitous nature! Had he stayed but five minutes longer, had he not rushed about like a man pursued by a horde of demons, this unfortunate event would never have transpired! I should have insisted he remain. I should have prevented Eleanor from speaking to him alone! But no, he was off, like a shot from a pistol, leaving a trail of horticultural devastation in his wake!”

Eleanor, still tearful, ventured, “I thought… I thought perhaps he was asking for one of the smaller, less important ones, Mama. The ones we often give away to visitors who express an interest.”

“Less important ones!” Mrs. Philips stopped pacing and turned, hands on her hips. “Eleanor, my dear, do you truly believe that any camellia in this conservatory is ‘less important’? Every plant here is nurtured, cared for, loved! But this one! This particular one! It was destined for such magnificent blooms! I had already planned its placement in the drawing room for the Christmas festivities. It was to be the centrepiece! The talk of the parish!”

Eliza felt a pang of sympathy for Eleanor, who looked utterly crushed beneath the weight of her mother’s distress. The rectory, usually a haven of quiet comfort, now vibrated with Mrs. Philips’s indignation. It was clear that this was not merely about a plant; it was about pride, order, and a deeply felt connection to the natural world she so meticulously curated.

“What if,” Eliza ventured again, “William Giles were to go to Netherfield? He could retrieve it, explaining that it was taken by mistake, without Mr. Harding or Miss Eleanor intending any ill. He knows how to handle plants, and could ensure its safe return.”

Mrs. Philips considered this, a flicker of hope mingling with her anger. “William Giles? To Netherfield? It is a considerable distance, Eliza, and he has his duties here. Besides, what an imposition upon Mr. Bennet! To send a servant to reclaim a gift. It is quite beyond the bounds of propriety. No, that would be even more mortifying. Imagine the gossip! ‘Mrs. Philips so mean-spirited as to demand the return of a plant!’” She shuddered at the thought. “No, that will not do. That will simply not do at all.”

She sighed deeply, her shoulders slumping. “No, I daresay it is lost. Irrecoverably lost. Carried off by that… that well-meaning but utterly inconsiderate man, Mr. Harding. Oh, my poor camellia. I shall never see its pure white blooms again. Never again.” She wrung her hands once more, a picture of profound despondency.

Eleanor, seeing her mother’s distress, quickly wiped her eyes and stepped forward. “Mama, please do not despair so entirely. Perhaps it is not as bad as you imagine. Mr. Harding, for all his haste, is a kind man. He would surely ensure it is well cared for. And if it truly is lost to us, then perhaps we might cultivate another. I will personally tend to a new cutting, if you will allow me.”

Mrs. Philips looked at her daughter, a faint light returning to her eyes. “Another cutting? My dear Eleanor, this was not just ‘another cutting’! It was the progeny of Lady Dalton’s prized collection! It possessed a lineage, a history! One does not simply replace such a plant. It takes years, decades, to achieve such perfection.”

“Nevertheless, Mama,” Eleanor persisted, her voice steadier now, “if there is nothing else to be done, then we must make the best of it. And perhaps, when Mr. Harding next calls, we might gently impress upon him the particular significance of the plant he departed with. He may yet regret his haste.”

“Next calls!” Mrs. Philips exclaimed, then softened. “Yes, perhaps. Though I daresay I shall require a considerable period of recovery before I can face him again without some… some residual resentment. It is not every day one’s prize camellia is spirited away from under one’s very nose. No, it is not. I shall write to Lady Dalton. She, at least, will understand the depth of my sorrow. She will know precisely what it means to lose such a specimen.”

Eliza, seeing that Mrs. Philips had settled into a more manageable, albeit still lamenting, state, decided to offer a final, calming observation. “Indeed, Mrs. Philips, Lady Dalton will surely sympathise. And perhaps, in time, this unfortunate incident may even serve to remind us of the transient nature of all things, even the most beautiful camellia blooms.”

Mrs. Philips let out a long, shuddering breath. “Transient nature, Eliza? It is not transient; it is gone! Vanished! But you are right. We must bear it. We must. Come, Eleanor, come, Eliza. Let us return to the drawing room. The very sight of that empty space is quite disheartening. I shall require a cup of my strong herb tea. And perhaps a quiet hour of reflection. This… this loss requires careful consideration. I must decide how best to respond to such an unprecedented breach of horticultural etiquette.”

She cast one last, mournful glance at the empty space in the camellia bed, a profound sadness etched on her features. Then, with a sigh that seemed to carry the weight of a thousand fallen leaves, she turned and led the way back out of the conservatory, Eliza and Eleanor following in her wake, leaving the silent, humid air to hold the memory of the vanished white camellia.

As they walked back through the glass corridor, the warmth and light of the drawing room seemed a distant comfort. Mrs. Philips continued to murmur about the injustice of it all, her voice a low, constant hum of grievance. “To Netherfield, of all places! With all of Mrs. Bennet’s fuss and bother, my poor plant will be quite overwhelmed. And the Bingley sisters, with their talk of London fashions and balls, will not spare a single thought for its delicate needs. Oh, it is a tragedy, a veritable tragedy!”

Eliza exchanged another glance with Eleanor, a shared understanding passing between them. Eleanor offered a small, apologetic shrug. The rectory, it seemed, was about to enter a period of quiet mourning for its lost floral treasure, a period that would undoubtedly be punctuated by Mrs. Philips’s eloquent and detailed expressions of lamentation.
