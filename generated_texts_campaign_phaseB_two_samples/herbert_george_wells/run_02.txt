I cannot explain it. Perhaps the very fabric of sentience has a limited tenure, a period of flourishing before it must inevitably fold in upon itself. It is a common assumption that a civilisation’s worth is measured by its endurance. But those, unversed in the deeper currents of social dynamics, forget that a culture must ultimately consume its own foundations to fuel its continued existence. As these moments of consumption occur, a society may transform into something unrecognisable; and it may be that some prior age had already suffered this fate. Whatever the cause, the fact remains that this city was vastly more placid than we can comprehend.

Well, one particularly serene afternoon, my third, I believe, as I was observing the placid activities of the populace from a balcony of spun-glass and silver, there occurred this curious event. Below me, the city’s inhabitants, a graceful and willowy people, drifted through plazas whose geometries shifted in slow, dreamlike undulations. By contrast with their languid movements, a section of the plaza’s floor suddenly grew opaque, its pearlescent tiles darkening to a matte black. I watched, intrigued, for the change from lustre to void drew the eye. Suddenly I froze, my attention arrested. A faint vibration, almost a hum, became perceptible through the soles of my shoes.

The old scholarly instinct for the unknown rose in me. I abandoned my balcony and descended the silent ramps. I was wary of startling the natives. Then the thought of the absolute peace in which this society seemed to exist settled my nerves. And then I recalled that strange tremor beneath my feet. Overcoming my hesitation to some extent, I stepped onto the darkened patch and knelt. I will confess that my breath was shallow and quick. I laid my hand flat and felt a faint, rhythmic tapping from below. At once a hairline crack appeared, widening silently, and a pale appendage flickered into view. I drew back with my heart hammering against my ribs, and saw a strange, multi-jointed limb, its tip a polished chrome stylus, making swift, intricate adjustments to a crystal node just beneath the surface. It retracted as if startled by the light, and the floor panel sealed itself over, its pearlescent sheen returning in an instant.

My impression of it is, of course, fleeting; but I know it was bone-white, and unnaturally slender, jointed in ways no human arm should be. But, as I say, it vanished too quickly for me to see its owner. I cannot even say if it was organic or mechanical. After a moment’s shock I ran to where another such panel was darkening. I dared not touch it; but, after a time in the profound silence of the plaza, I perceived a pattern to the darkening and lightening of the city’s surfaces. A sudden idea struck me. Could these Beings be living within the very walls and floors? I found a discarded shard of mirror, and holding it at an angle to a newly formed aperture, I saw a thin, elongated creature, with large dark lenses where its eyes should be, which seemed to stare back without seeing me as it worked. It made me recoil. It was so like a human insect! It was bracing itself against the inner walls, and now I saw for the first time a series of magnetic grips and handholds lining the interior of the structure. Then the panel closed, and when another opened elsewhere the lithe artisan had disappeared.

I do not know how long I stood there, watching the city breathe and repair itself. It was not for some time that I could bring myself to accept that the thing I had seen was a form of man. But, gradually, the realisation took hold: that Humanity had not remained a single society, but had stratified into two distinct functions: that my elegant children of the Surface were not the sole inheritors of our endeavours, but that this pale, industrious, subterranean Thing, which had laboured before me, was also a keeper of the world.

I thought of the silent, self-repairing structures and my theory of an automated infrastructure. I began to sense their true mechanism. And what, I wondered, was this Artificer doing in my vision of a post-labour utopia? How was it related to the idle grace of the beautiful Surfacers? And what else was happening down there, within the skin of the city? I sat upon the edge of a fluidly reshaping bench telling myself that, in any case, there was a logic to be discovered, and that there I must look for the answer to my questions. And withal I was utterly daunted by the prospect! As I dithered, two of the beautiful surface people glided past, trailing scents of flowers and ozone in their wake. The first pointed a delicate finger at a blossoming light-form, and the second responded with a note of pure, melodic laughter.

They seemed puzzled to find me, my hand pressed against a vibrating wall panel, listening to the hum within. Apparently it was considered unusual to pay attention to the city’s workings; for when I gestured to the wall, and tried to ask a question in their simple tongue, they seemed sweetly confused and drifted away. But they were fascinated by my notebook, and I sketched a flower to delight them. I tried them again about the wall, and again I was met with pleasant incomprehension. So presently I left them, meaning to return to my archive, and see what I could deduce from my recordings. But my mind was already in turmoil; my theories and assumptions were collapsing towards a new configuration. I had now a clue to the purpose of the humming, to the shifting architecture, to the mystery of this effortless paradise; to say nothing of a hint at the meaning of their social amnesia and the fate of conscious struggle! And very faintly there came a suggestion towards the solution of the philosophical problem that had so occupied me.

Here was the new thesis. Plainly, this second strain of Man was integrated. There were three aspects in particular which made me believe that its complete separation from the surface world was the result of a long-perfected division of purpose. In the first place, there was the pallid look common to creatures of the interior, the colourless things of the deep ocean, for instance. Then, those large, lensed eyes, with their lack of expressive qualities, are common features of beings that see in specific spectra, witness the electron microscope and the thermal camera. And last of all, that immediate, unthinking devotion to its task, that seamless disappearance and complete disregard for my presence, all reinforced the theory of an extreme specialisation of consciousness.

Around me, then, the city must be honeycombed with service channels, and these channels were the domain of the New Man. The presence of access panels and energy conduits on every surface, everywhere, in fact, except in the tranquil gardens, showed how total was its network. What so logical, then, as to assume that it was in this integrated circuit of life that such maintenance as was essential to the leisure of the surface race was performed? The idea was so compelling that I at once adopted it, and went on to speculate on the _why_ of this bifurcation of the human condition. I dare say you can see the outline of my conclusion; though, for myself, I quickly surmised that it was far from a complete picture.

At first, proceeding from the dilemmas of my own time, it seemed as clear as day to me that the gradual separation of the present temporary and cultural division between the Creator and the Consumer was the key to this entire state. No doubt it will seem absurdly simplistic to you, and utterly fantastical!, and yet even now there are trends that point in this direction. There is a tendency to render our technologies invisible, to hide the complexity behind simple interfaces; there is the cloud computing of our global networks, for instance, there are automated factories, there are logistical hubs, there are server farms and dark kitchens that hum with activity unseen and unthought of. Evidently, I thought, this tendency had progressed until the Makers had gradually surrendered their place in the sunlit world. I mean that they had withdrawn deeper and deeper into the systems they created, spending an ever-increasing portion of their lives within the machine, until, at last, ! Even now, does not a systems architect live in such conceptual conditions as to be practically detached from the physical world he administers?

Again, the passive tendency of the user, due, no doubt, to the increasing perfection of the service, and the widening gulf between them and the raw mechanics of the system, is already leading to the closing, by their own disinterest, of vast realms of knowledge. About London, for instance, perhaps not one in a thousand understands the engineering that brings them water and light. And this same widening gulf, which is due to the length and complexity of technical education and the increased ease of and temptations towards a life of frictionless consumption on the other, is already creating two humanities, moving in opposite directions.

This divergence, I reasoned, was not a matter of malice, but of a slow, inexorable logic, the same logic that designs a circuit for maximum efficiency. The human race, in its pursuit of comfort and the elimination of toil, had simply optimised itself into two distinct components. One, a beautiful, passive interface for the experience of life; the other, a hidden, tireless processor for the maintenance of that experience. It was a tragedy, certainly, a profound loss of the wholeness that had defined man, but it was a tragedy of engineering, not of morals. There was a certain grandeur to it, a terrible and sublime conclusion to the grand project of civilisation. Having arrived at this elegant, if melancholy, synthesis, I felt a renewed surge of the scientific spirit. An idea, however compelling, is but a ghost until it is clothed in the flesh of fact. I must have proof; I must gather data to support or refute my grand theory.

My first course of action was to engage once more with the sunlit people. Previously, I had observed them with a sort of bewildered enchantment, as one might watch the flitting of butterflies. Now, I saw them as a data set, the living terminals of a worldwide system. I approached a group of them as they sat by a fountain, weaving garlands of unfamiliar, star shaped blossoms. Their beauty, I noted with my new, colder perspective, was of a disturbingly uniform kind. The same delicate features, the same clear, untroubled eyes, the same slender grace was repeated in each of them, with only minor variations in hair colour or the precise curve of a lip. It was the beauty of a perfected model, not the diverse and often difficult beauty of a species forged in struggle. I sat down near them, and for a time, simply watched. Their conversation was a musical babble, punctuated by soft laughter, but it contained no more substance than the splashing of the water beside them.

Holding up a piece of the succulent fruit they had given me, I attempted an experiment. I pointed to the fruit, then to my mouth, and then made a gesture of enquiry, sweeping my hand across the landscape as if to ask, where does this come from? I pointed to the clothes they wore, a soft, seamless fabric, and mimed the action of weaving or stitching. I pointed to the great, grey edifices that dotted the hillsides and made a gesture of building, of laying stone upon stone. My pantomime, which I felt to be a masterpiece of clarity, was met with a complete lack of comprehension. They smiled at me, their expressions placid and sweet. One of them, a girl with hair the colour of spun moonlight, simply took the fruit from my hand, broke it in two, and offered me a piece, as if the answer to my question was to be found in the act of consumption itself. Another giggled at my imitation of weaving, and then began a game, chasing his companions around the fountain in a dizzying, pointless circle.

Their inability to grasp my meaning was absolute. It was not that they were unintelligent in the way a beast is unintelligent; it was that the entire category of my questions was alien to them. The concepts of origin, of process, of labour, were as foreign to their minds as the colour blue might be to a man born blind. They were the perfect consumers, living in an eternal present, where every need was met as if by magic. The fruit appeared on the trees, the garments clothed them, the shelters stood against the rain. To ask ‘how’ was a question that had long ago lost all meaning, and with it, I realised, had gone the very framework of curiosity that drives progress and understanding. My theory was not merely supported; it was confirmed with a depressing finality. The gulf was not merely one of education or culture; it was a fundamental alteration of the human mind.

My frustration with the surface dwellers quickly turned into a singular obsession with their hidden counterparts. If the true spark of humanity, the intellect and the will to create, still existed, it must be down below, in the dark and humming heart of this world. It was there, with the pallid New Man, that I might find a mind I could speak to, a being who understood the concept of a machine, of a system, of cause and effect. I turned my back on the beautiful, vacant gardens and began a systematic survey of the land, my eyes no longer seeking beauty, but function. I was a detective now, searching for the door to the engine room.

The access panels I had noted before were everywhere, set flush into pathways, on the sides of buildings, even on the trunks of what I realised were not trees but cunningly disguised structural supports. They were all made of the same white, metallic substance and were utterly seamless, without handle or hinge. I pushed at them, I pried with a piece of broken stone, but they would not yield. They were not for me. They were for the specialised beings that served them. I walked for miles, my gaze sweeping the landscape, noting how the ground would sometimes emit a low, almost subliminal thrum, and how the air in certain places was warmer, shimmering with a heat that had no business being there under the cool, high sun. These were the symptoms of the machine, the outward signs of the immense metabolic processes occurring beneath the skin of the world.

My search went on for what must have been two full days. I grew weary of the endless fruit and longed for a crust of bread. The constant, gentle presence of the surface people began to grate on my nerves. Their laughter seemed a mockery of my serious purpose, their placid existence an insult to the striving and suffering that had, until now, defined the human story. It was late in the afternoon of the second day that I found it. It was a structure unlike any other I had seen. Set on a small hillock, partially shrouded by overgrown, purple leaved shrubbery, was a small, circular edifice of a dark, greenish bronze. It was weathered and ancient, looking more like a forgotten mausoleum or a well head from some long vanished age than a part of this polished, modern world.

It had an air of disuse, and yet I was drawn to it. The surface people, I had noticed, never came near this hill. Their games and rambles always seemed to flow around it, as if guided by an unconscious aversion. This, more than anything, convinced me I was on the right track. I pushed through the bushes and stood before it. The building was perhaps twice my height, with a domed roof and no visible windows. On one side, however, there was what looked like a pair of small, ornate doors, also of bronze. There were no handles, but there was a deep vertical line between them. As I ran my hands over the cool, verdigris coated metal, I felt a faint vibration, a deep, slow pulse that seemed to come from the very depths of the earth.

I pushed against the doors with all my might, but they were immovable. I searched the perimeter for any kind of latch or switch, but found nothing. Despairing, I was about to turn away when my foot slipped on a loose stone at the base of the structure. In recovering my balance, my hand slapped against the dome above the doors. I felt, not a solid surface, but a slight give, a section that seemed to depress under my palm. I pressed it again, deliberately this time. There was a low grinding sound, a sound of machinery long unused, and with a great shudder the bronze doors slid slowly apart, revealing not light, but a profound and absolute darkness.

From the opening came a rush of air, but it was not the fresh, clean air of the surface world. It was a thick, warm, and oily draught, carrying with it a complex smell of hot metal, of some strange lubricant, and a faint, almost organic odour that I could not place. And beneath the smell, there was a sound, a deep and continuous humming, the sound of a million sleeping wasps, punctuated by a distant, rhythmic clanging. This was it. This was the throat of the underworld, the gateway to the world of the Makers.

For a long moment, I stood there, hesitating on the threshold of my discovery. The sun was beginning to set, casting long, peaceful shadows across the green landscape behind me. I could hear the faint, bell like laughter of the surface people at their evening play. It was a world of ease, of safety, of simple, thoughtless pleasure. Before me was a black shaft leading into an unknown world of noise, heat, and unimaginable complexity. Every instinct for self preservation screamed at me to turn back, to remain in the sunlight. But the thirst for knowledge, the driving curiosity that had brought me across the gulf of ages, was stronger. My theory demanded verification. I had to know.

Taking a deep breath of the sweet evening air, as if to store it in my lungs, I stepped into the darkness. The bronze doors slid shut behind me with a heavy, final clang, and I was plunged into a total blackness more complete than any night I had ever known. My heart hammered against my ribs. I fumbled in my pockets for the box of matches I always carried, a relic of my own time that I guarded jealously. The first match scraped and failed. The second flared to life, a tiny, brave star in the immense dark.

In its flickering light, I saw that I was at the top of a huge, vertical shaft. A narrow, metal ladder, its rungs slick with a film of condensation and oil, descended into the abyss. The shaft itself was lined with a chaos of pipes and thick, insulated cables, all disappearing into the gloom below. The humming sound was much louder here, a palpable vibration that I could feel in my bones. Taking a firm grip on the box of matches, I held it in my teeth and began my descent.

The journey downwards was a terrifying ordeal. The ladder was seemingly endless, and the rungs were slippery and treacherous. My muscles ached, and my hands, unaccustomed to such exertion, were soon raw. Every few yards, I would pause, light another precious match, and peer into the darkness. Below me, there was only more shaft, more cables, and the unending ladder. Above me, the crack of light where the doors had been was long gone. The air grew progressively warmer and fouler, the rhythmic clanging louder and more distinct. I felt like a diver descending into a monstrous, mechanical ocean.

I do not know how long I climbed down, whether it was an hour or a day. Time, like the light, had ceased to have any meaning in this vertical world. At last, just as my strength was beginning to fail me completely, my dangling foot touched a solid surface. With a final, desperate effort, I lowered myself the last few feet and collapsed onto a metal platform. I lay there for some time, gasping in the thick, hot air, my body trembling with exhaustion. When I had recovered enough to sit up, I lit another match.

The platform was a small landing in a cavern of truly colossal scale. The match flame, so bright in the shaft, was a pathetic speck here, swallowed by a darkness so vast it felt like a solid substance. But it was not entirely dark. Far below me, and stretching away into the distance, were points of light, a dim, reddish glow that seemed to cast everything in a hellish twilight. The humming was deafening now, a physical pressure on my eardrums, and the clangour I had heard from above was revealed to be the sound of immense, pounding machinery somewhere in the gloom. I had arrived. I was in the heart of the machine.

Cautiously, I made my way from the platform down a wide, shallow ramp. The floor was made of iron plates, worn smooth by the passage of ages. The air was thick with smoke and steam that billowed up from unseen chasms. As my eyes adjusted to the dim, red light, the true nature of the place began to reveal itself. I was in a vast, subterranean hall, its ceiling lost in the darkness far above. All around me were gigantic machines, their forms only vaguely discernible. There were huge, rotating wheels, massive, pumping pistons, and great, arching structures that spat sparks into the gloom. It was a vision from a nightmare of industry, a factory floor the size of a city.

And then I saw them. Not one, but dozens. The New Man. My pallid creature of the access panel. They were everywhere, swarming over the machinery like ants in a disturbed nest. In the ruddy glow of what I now saw were furnaces, I could see them much more clearly. They were, as I had first observed, unnervingly pale, with the same dead white skin of things that live without sunlight. Their eyes were large and grey, and seemed to reflect the furnace light with a disturbing, cat like luminosity. They were smaller than the surface dwellers, and stooped, with long arms that seemed ill suited to their bodies. They wore only rudimentary rags around their waists, their glistening white bodies moving with a swift, furtive purposefulness.

They were tending the machines. They scurried up ladders, they pulled great levers, they oiled immense gears, their movements economical and precise. They never spoke, but communicated with a series of low clicks and guttural noises. There was no sign of the high intellect I had imagined, the detached mind of the systems architect. This was not the clean, automated world of a voluntary, scientific priesthood. This was a world of hard, desperate, physical labour. The seamless disappearance I had admired on the surface was not the mark of a higher consciousness, but the ingrained habit of a creature that fears the light. My theory of a neat, social contract, of a bifurcation into Creator and Consumer, began to feel horrifyingly naive.

I hid in the shadow of a colossal, pillar like machine, watching them. A new, more terrible understanding began to dawn in my mind, a chilling reinterpretation of the facts. The leisure of the Eloi, of the surface people. The tireless, subterranean labour of these Morlocks, for so I began to call them in my mind. The complete absence of any agriculture, of any food source, in the underworld. The uniform, plump, and placid nature of the surface dwellers. My mind, trained in the brutal logic of evolution and the struggle for existence, made a connection that turned my blood to ice.

A bell of some kind rang out, its tone cutting through the din of the machinery. The Morlocks stopped their work. From various tunnels and passages, more of their kind emerged, blinking in the furnace light. They began to congregate around a large, flat table of stone that I had not noticed before. One of them, carrying a large, white object, placed it on the table. My scientific curiosity fought a desperate battle with a rising tide of nausea. I had to see. I had to know. I crept closer, keeping to the deepest shadows. The food. What did they eat? I saw them begin to divide their meal. It was meat. And on the stone table, I saw the remains of their repast. It was not the flesh of any beast I recognised. The meaning of the tranquil gardens, the simple games, the boundless leisure of the beautiful people above, crashed down upon me with the force of a physical blow. I had been wrong. Terribly, catastrophically wrong. This was not a division of labour. The surface world was not a resort. It was a pasture.

I reeled back from the scene, my hand flying to my mouth to stifle the cry that threatened to betray me, a sound that would have sealed my fate as surely as the closing of a tomb. The strength went out of my legs, and I found myself collapsing against the cold metal of the pillar, the vibration of the machine a dull, monstrous heartbeat against my back. My scientific detachment, the lens through which I had viewed this entire future world, was shattered. In its place was a primal, animal terror, a revulsion so profound it felt like a physical sickness. I saw not a species, not a sociological curiosity, but a butcher's shop, an abattoir disguised as a civilization.

The Morlocks, oblivious to my presence, continued their grim repast. They tore at the white flesh with their fingers, their large grey eyes, so disturbingly vacant, now gleamed with a focused, animal hunger. The sounds they made, the clicks and guttural grunts, were no longer the language of mechanics, but the sounds of feeding. I felt a surge of bile in my throat, and I turned away, pressing my forehead against the cold, unyielding metal, my eyes screwed shut. But the image was burned onto the inside of my eyelids, the pale limbs on the stone slab, the casual, practiced way they had been portioned. The beautiful, thoughtless people of the upper world. The Eloi. My mind kept repeating the name, and with each repetition the horror deepened. Weena. The thought of her, with her trusting eyes and the flowers she had so innocently placed in my pockets, was a fresh torment. Was this her destiny? To laugh and play in the sun, growing plump and tender, until one night a silent hand reached out from the darkness of a ventilation shaft and drew her down to this subterranean kitchen?

The logic of it was sickeningly perfect. The Eloi had no need for strength, or intelligence, or courage. Such traits would have been bred out of them, like a rancher breeds aggression out of his cattle. Their fear of the dark, their simple, unthinking lives, their very beauty, were all products of this terrible, symbiotic relationship. They were the carefully tended blossoms of a garden, whose only purpose was to be harvested. And the Morlocks, they were not the architects, but the farmers. Driven underground by some long forgotten cataclysm or social shift, they had adapted. They had kept the machinery of the old world running, not for progress, not for knowledge, but for the most basic of reasons, to maintain their food supply. They were the keepers of the farm.

A scraping sound nearby jolted me from my horrified reverie. One of the Morlocks, its meal finished, was shuffling away from the table, its pale form moving through the gloom. It passed uncomfortably close to my hiding place, and for a terrifying moment its large, luminous eyes seemed to sweep over my position. I held my breath, my heart hammering against my ribs so violently I was sure it must hear. The air was thick with the smell of machine oil, of damp earth, and something else, a coppery, metallic odour that now I understood all too well. The creature paused, its head cocked, and it emitted a low series of clicks. It had sensed something. My scent, perhaps, or the faintest sound of my breathing. Another Morlock turned from the table, its head swivelling in my direction. I was discovered.

Panic, cold and absolute, seized me. I pushed myself away from the pillar and scrambled backwards into the deeper darkness of a side tunnel. My foot caught on some unseen pipe and I went sprawling, my hands landing in a slick of grease and filth. A chorus of sharp, inquisitive clicks rose up behind me. They were coming. I pushed myself up and ran, plunging blindly into the labyrinth. I had no idea where I was going. My only instinct was to flee, to put distance between myself and those pale, devouring things. The darkness was absolute, a thick, pressing weight. I ran with my hands outstretched, my fingers brushing against cold, sweating walls and the hard edges of machinery. Behind me, I could hear the patter of their bare feet on the stone, a sound far more terrifying than the roar of any predator I had known. They were at home in this blackness. I was a clumsy, stumbling intruder.

My shoulder struck a metal girder with stunning force, and I cried out in pain, a sound that was immediately answered by a triumphant chittering from my pursuers. They were closer now. I could hear their breath, a dry, rasping sound. I fumbled in my pockets, my fingers numb and clumsy, searching for the one advantage I possessed. The box of matches. My hand closed around it, the flimsy cardboard a totem of a forgotten world of light and safety. I pulled it out, my other hand shielding it as I tried to strike one. The first match head scraped uselessly against the side. The second broke in my trembling fingers. The pattering was almost upon me. I could feel the closeness of them, the heat from their bodies. With a final, desperate effort, I struck a third match.

It flared into life, a glorious, impossible star in the suffocating darkness. I thrust it forward like a sword. The effect was instantaneous and dramatic. The Morlocks, who had been moments from seizing me, recoiled with hisses of pain and surprise. I saw them clearly for a split second, a knot of them, their pale faces contorted, their large grey eyes blinking stupidly in the unaccustomed light. They shielded their faces with their long, spidery arms and fell back, their clicking turning from triumphant to alarmed. They were creatures of the dark. Light was their enemy. It was pain. It was fear.

The match burned my fingers, and I dropped it, plunging us back into blackness. But I had my answer. I had a weapon. I struck another, and another, using the brief, flickering illumination to find my way, running in a stumbling gait from one pool of light to the next. I was leaving a trail of spent matches like breadcrumbs, but it was a trail that led away from death. The Morlocks followed, but more cautiously now, keeping their distance, their hisses and clicks a constant, unnerving chorus in the dark. I did not know if I was heading toward the well shaft I had descended, or deeper into their world. I only knew that I had to keep moving, to keep striking matches until the box was empty.

My lungs burned. My legs were heavy as lead. My mind, however, was strangely, horribly clear. It worked with the cold precision I had once prized, fitting the pieces together. The absence of graveyards or funeral rites among the Eloi. The strange, well like openings that dotted the landscape, their purpose now horrifyingly apparent. They were not just ventilation shafts. They were conduits. They were the channels through which the harvest was brought down. Every aspect of the idyllic surface world was now reframed in my mind as a feature of a well managed, free range farm. The fruit they ate, so plentiful and easy to gather, was to sweeten the meat. Their lack of curiosity, their inability to grasp complex ideas, was a form of blissful ignorance, a mercy, perhaps, granted by their keepers.

I stumbled out of a narrow passage into a larger space, and I saw it. High above, a faint circle of pearly grey light. It was one of the wells. It was my way out. A new wave of desperate energy surged through me. But the shaft was not empty. A group of Morlocks were already there, clambering up the metal rungs and handholds that studded its interior, their white bodies like immense, pale insects crawling up a wall. They were trying to cut off my escape. Behind me, the pursuit was closing in again, emboldened by the dim light of the shaft, which was less alien to them than the sharp flare of my matches. I was trapped.

For a moment, I despaired. But then I looked at the machinery around me. The Morlocks were mechanics, not inventors. They maintained the old world, they did not innovate. The machines were my world. I was a man of science, of engineering. And I was a desperate animal. I saw a great lever, part of some enormous, dormant apparatus. It was thick with rust and disuse, but its purpose seemed clear, a switch of some kind. I had no idea what it would do, but inaction was death. I threw my shoulder against it. It refused to budge. The clicking of the Morlocks was at my back. I set my feet, took a deep breath, and heaved with all the strength that terror could lend me. With a tortured groan of protesting metal, the lever moved.

A deep, grinding roar filled the cavern, far louder than the general din of the furnaces. The floor beneath my feet began to vibrate violently. A section of the wall, a massive iron plate, began to rise, revealing a new tunnel, and from this tunnel blasted a tremendous current of air. It was the main ventilation system. The Morlocks in the shaft shrieked as the hurricane force wind tore at them. Several lost their grip and fell, their pale bodies tumbling into the darkness below with sickening thuds. The ones behind me were knocked off their feet, sent rolling and tumbling back into the passage from which they had come. The wind howled past me, a cleansing, powerful force, tearing at my clothes, trying to pull me from my feet. I clung to the lever, using the brief respite to strike another match and look for a path. The main shaft was a vortex of wind, impassable. But the new tunnel, the source of the wind, was my only other option. I let go of the lever and plunged into the gale.

Fighting the wind was like swimming against a powerful current. It was a clean, dry air, unlike the damp, fetid atmosphere of the lower caverns. It roared in my ears and scoured my skin, but it was pushing the Morlocks back, giving me a chance. I fought my way along the tunnel, which sloped steadily upwards. After what felt like an eternity, the pressure began to lessen, and the tunnel opened into another, smaller chamber. In the centre of this chamber was another well shaft, narrower than the others, a simple, round opening in the ceiling. And from it, I could feel a gentle breeze, and I could smell the sweet, clean scent of grass and flowers. I could see the sky.

With the last of my strength, I began to climb. The handholds were small and far apart, designed for the Morlocks' longer limbs, but I hauled myself upwards, my muscles screaming in protest. I did not look down. I did not want to see if anything was following me. I focused only on the growing circle of blue above, the promise of sunlight and open air. My head and shoulders finally broke through the surface. I was in a small, circular ruin of white stone, overgrown with moss and creepers, some distance from the great lawn where I had first arrived. I dragged my exhausted body out of the shaft and collapsed onto the warm, sun drenched grass, gasping for air.

I lay there for some time, my cheek pressed against the earth, letting the warmth of the sun soak into my chilled bones. The world of the Eloi was achingly, cruelly beautiful. The sky was a perfect, cloudless blue. The air hummed with the buzz of insects and the distant, musical laughter of the surface dwellers. It was a paradise. And I knew, with a certainty that chilled me more than the deepest cavern, that it was a paradise built on a foundation of hell. I sat up, my body aching. I looked at my hands, scraped and bloodied and covered in grime. I looked at my torn and filthy clothing. I was a spectre at the feast, a messenger from the abattoir.

I saw a group of Eloi nearby, weaving garlands of flowers. They were laughing their simple, carefree laughs, their faces empty of all thought, all worry. One of them, a lovely youth with golden hair, looked over at me. His face registered a flicker of mild curiosity, the same look one might give a strange bird, before he turned back to his flowers, his attention span exhausted. I looked at his smooth, unblemished skin, his plump, well fed limbs, and I felt a wave of nausea. I did not see a person. I saw a prize animal, fattened for the slaughter, blissfully unaware of its own doom.

The horror of my discovery was now compounded by a terrible, crushing sense of isolation. Who could I tell? How could I explain it? These beautiful children would not understand. They lacked the very concepts of fear, of struggle, of death, that would allow them to comprehend their situation. To warn them would be like trying to explain the concept of a butcher to a sheep. I was utterly alone in this knowledge.

And then I thought of Weena. I had not seen her since I had gone down the well. A new fear, more personal and immediate than the grand, abstract horror, gripped me. I had to find her. My scientific mission was over. I was no longer an observer, a detached chronicler of the future. I was a man, trapped in a nightmare, with one small, fragile connection to this world. I had to protect her. I did not know how. My only weapons were a handful of matches and a terrible secret. But as I staggered to my feet and began to search for her among the placid, grazing herds of humanity, I knew that I could not simply escape in my Time Machine. I could not leave her to the dark. My journey into the future had just acquired a purpose, a grim and terrible purpose. I had to fight back.

I called out her name, my voice a raw, desperate croak. "Weena!" The sound was an obscenity in this gentle world, a jagged stone thrown into a placid pool. The nearby Eloi, the ones who had been weaving flowers, turned their heads. Their large, soft eyes regarded me with a momentary, placid blankness. They heard the noise I made, but the meaning, the urgency, the terror coiled within that single word, was utterly lost on them. One of them giggled, a sound like silver bells, and turned back to a particularly vibrant crimson blossom. My shout had been nothing more to them than a strange bird call, a meaningless disturbance in the afternoon's golden peace.

I began to run, my protesting muscles screaming with every jarring step. I stumbled through groves of strange, beautiful trees, their branches laden with exotic fruits I now saw as little more than fodder. I pushed past groups of the Eloi, who simply parted before me like a gentle stream flowing around a rock, their faces showing no alarm, no annoyance, only a faint, childish surprise. I was searching for a particular face in a sea of beautiful, vacant faces. It was a maddening, impossible task. They were as similar to one another as flowers in a field, distinguished only by the shade of their tunics or the color of the blossoms tucked behind their ears.

"Weena!" I yelled again, my lungs burning. The fear that had been a general, philosophical horror was now a sharp, personal agony. What if she had wandered near one of the shafts? What if, in her childlike curiosity, she had peered too closely? The thought was a physical blow. The Morlocks were creatures of the dark, I told myself. They feared the sun. They would not have taken her in the light of day. But my rational mind was a small, flickering candle against a gale of primal fear. In this beautiful, treacherous world, I could not be certain of anything.

I reached the great stone hall, the palace where I had first slept among them. It was filled with dozens of the creatures, lounging on silk cushions, feeding one another fruit, their soft chatter filling the vast space. I scanned the crowd, my eyes darting from one golden head to the next. I saw faces like hers, bodies with the same delicate frame, but I did not see her. A cold dread began to solidify in my gut. Had I lost her? Was she already gone, dragged down into the lightless world below while I myself was escaping it? The irony was too cruel to contemplate. My scientific detachment had been a sham, a lie I told myself to cope with the utter alienness of this age. I had left her, my one anchor, to pursue a truth that was now threatening to drown me.

I fled the hall, my hope dwindling. I ran towards the river, the last place I could think to look. The path sloped downwards, through a garden of what looked like enormous, cultivated roses, their scent thick and cloying in the warm air. And then I saw her. She was sitting by the water's edge, alone, trailing her small hand in the clear, cool current. She was watching the small, silver fish that darted just below the surface. She had a garland of white flowers on her head, and another, half finished, lay in her lap.

"Weena," I said, my voice barely a whisper.

She turned. Her face, which had been placid and contemplative in the way a child's is, lit up with pure, uncomplicated joy. She leapt to her feet and ran to me, her little sandals slapping against the stone path. She threw her arms around my waist, pressing her face against my filthy tunic without a hint of hesitation or disgust. She did not seem to notice the blood on my hands, the grime on my face, or the manic, haunted look in my eyes. Or if she did, she attached no meaning to it. I was simply her strange, new playfellow, who had been gone and was now returned.

She pulled back, her wide eyes full of affection. She held up the half finished garland from her lap. It was made of the same white flowers she wore in her hair. With a soft, murmuring sound, she reached up and began to place it around my neck. I stood rigid, a statue of filth and horror being adorned with a symbol of absolute innocence. The petals, cool and soft, brushed against my skin, a grotesque contrast to the memory of the cold, clammy flesh of the Morlocks. I closed my eyes, and for a moment, I felt the world sway. The sweet scent of the flowers mingled with the phantom stench of the underworld, the memory of stale air and freshly butchered meat.

I gently took her small wrists in my hands. Her bones were so delicate. I could feel the flutter of her pulse, the steady, rhythmic beat of a life that had no concept of its own fragility. I looked down at her, at this tiny, doomed creature who had shown me nothing but kindness, and my purpose solidified from a vague notion into a sharp, unyielding resolve. I would not just protect her. I would arm myself. I would make this garden a fortress. I would turn their paradise into a battlefield if I had to.

I led her away from the river, my hand holding hers. She trotted along beside me, chattering her musical, incomprehensible language, pointing out flowers and brightly colored butterflies. I did not understand her words, but I understood her spirit. It was a spirit I had to shield from the truth, even as I fought the source of that truth. My first priority was a weapon. The thought was so alien to this world that it felt like a blasphemy. In a place without conflict, without struggle, the very idea of a weapon was unthinkable. But I was not of this world. I was a man from an age of iron and fire, and I would bring a piece of that age into this fragile future.

I scanned the trees around us. They were mostly slender, ornamental things, with soft wood. But then I saw it, a sturdier tree, perhaps a relic of an older, wilder time, that had somehow survived the Eloi's endless gardening. A low hanging branch, thick as my forearm, extended out over the path. I stopped. Weena looked at me, her head cocked in question. I pointed to the branch. I made a fist and mimed striking something. She simply giggled, not understanding.

I released her hand, found my footing, and gripped the branch with both hands. I put my back into it, pulling and twisting. The wood was green and strong. It resisted, groaning. The Eloi nearby paused in their languid play to watch me, their faces a mixture of mild curiosity and confusion. This exertion, this act of forceful will upon the environment, was utterly foreign to them. To them, a tree was for shade, its fruit for eating, its flowers for wearing. The idea of breaking it, of turning a piece of it into an instrument of violence, was outside the bounds of their imagination.

With a final, grunting heave, the branch snapped with a loud crack that echoed in the quiet air. It was a sound of violation. Several of the Eloi flinched and drew back, their happy expressions dissolving into vague unease. I had done something that was not done. I stood, breathing heavily, holding a four foot length of solid wood. It was crudely shaped and poorly balanced, but it was heavy. It was real. In my hand, I held the first weapon this world had seen in millennia.

I began to strip the smaller twigs and leaves from my new club, my hands working with a grim, methodical focus. Weena watched me, her large eyes wide. For the first time, a flicker of what might have been fear, or at least deep uncertainty, crossed her face. She crept closer to me, not to the other Eloi who were now whispering and pointing from a safe distance. She touched my arm, her touch tentative. I paused and looked at her, forcing my face to soften. I could not afford to frighten her. She was the very thing I was preparing to fight for. I gave her what I hoped was a reassuring smile and patted her head. Her fear seemed to recede, replaced by her boundless trust in me. She did not understand my actions, but she understood that I was her protector.

My next thought was of fire. My matches were a finite resource, precious beyond measure. I had perhaps a dozen left. Each one was a spark of hope, a tiny sun to hold back the unending night of the Morlocks. I could not waste them. I needed to find a way to create a more sustainable fire. I thought back to the books of my youth, to tales of survival. I needed tinder, dry and fine. I needed fuel.

As I worked, the sun continued its slow descent towards the western hills. The perfect blue of the sky began to bleed into shades of orange and pink. The shadows of the great buildings and the ornate trees stretched long and distorted across the lawns, like dark fingers reaching out to reclaim the land. With the fading light, a subtle change came over the Eloi. Their laughter became less frequent. Their movements became less random, more purposeful. They began to drift, in small groups, towards the security of their communal palaces. An ancient, instinctual rhythm was asserting itself. The day was for play. The night was for hiding.

I watched them, my club in one hand, Weena's hand in the other. I saw it then, etched in their communal behavior: a deep, profound, and entirely unconscious fear of the dark. They did not know why they feared it. They had no stories of monsters, no concept of the pale, ape-like cannibals who lived beneath their feet. Their fear was a genetic fossil, a vestigial terror passed down through a thousand generations of pampered, placid prey. They were like sheep who instinctively seek the fold at dusk, without ever having seen a wolf.

This observation gave me no comfort. It only highlighted the tragic hopelessness of their state. But it also gave me an advantage. I knew the wolf. I had seen its face.

We could not stay in the open. And I would not take Weena back to the great hall. It was a trap, a feasting table with a thousand entrances for the butchers to come and go as they pleased. I needed a sanctuary, a place I could defend. My eyes scanned the landscape, now painted in the melancholy hues of twilight. I saw the crumbling white ruin where I had emerged from the shaft. It was small, circular, and built on a slight rise. It was overgrown, but the stone walls were thick. More importantly, it seemed to have only one main point of entry. It was not perfect, but it was better than anything else I could see.

"Come," I said to Weena, my voice low and urgent. I tugged her hand gently.

She resisted for a moment, her eyes fixed on the distant palace where her people were gathering. The instinct to follow the herd was strong in her. She whimpered softly, a sound of childish distress. I knelt down so my eyes were level with hers. I put all the conviction I could muster into my gaze. I pointed to myself, then to her, then towards the ruin on the hill. I made my face as stern and reassuring as I could.

She looked from my face to the darkening sky, then back to the safety of the distant hall. A tremor ran through her small body. She was afraid. But her fear of the growing darkness seemed, for the moment, to be greater than her fear of defying her instincts. She squeezed my hand tightly, her choice made. She would stay with me.

As we began to walk towards the hill, away from the flow of the retreating Eloi, a new sound began to rise from the earth. It was a low, almost subsonic hum, the sound of immense machinery stirring in the deep. The ventilation shafts, which had been silent all day, began to exhale a faint, cool breeze that smelled of damp earth and metal. The Morlocks were waking up. The time of the Eloi was ending. The time of the hunters was about to begin. I tightened my grip on my club. Weena huddled closer to me, her face buried in my side. The night was coming, and for the first time in uncounted centuries, someone on the surface was ready to meet it with fire and steel, or at least, with a box of matches and a broken branch.

The path to the hill was less a path than a suggestion, a faint trace of paler green through the deepening moss. Every step was an effort, not merely of locomotion, but of will. I was pulling Weena away from the warm, familiar hive, towards an unknown and shadowed place. Her small hand in mine was a constant, trembling reminder of the trust she had placed in me, a trust born of a terror that had momentarily eclipsed a lifetime of instinct. The humming from the earth grew more pronounced, a pervasive thrumming that seemed to vibrate in the very bones of my skull. It was the sound of the world’s hidden engine turning over, of the great and terrible industry of the night beginning its shift.

Weena flinched at every new shadow, her breath catching in her throat. The familiar, gentle landscape of her day was transforming into something alien and menacing. The soft curves of the hills now looked like the hunched shoulders of sleeping giants. The delicate, fern-like trees clawed at the sky with skeletal fingers. The air itself, once warm and heavy with the scent of blossoms, now carried a chill that had nothing to do with the setting of the sun. It was a subterranean cold, a damp and lifeless air that smelled of decay and rusted metal, puffed from the grilles of the ventilation shafts in faint, visible sighs.

I registered the change in temperature with a scientist’s detachment, even as my heart hammered against my ribs. It was a clear indicator of a vast subterranean ventilation system, a network of lungs for the underworld. The Morlocks were not merely coming to the surface; they were changing the very atmosphere of the surface to suit their needs, exhaling their world into ours. Weena, of course, understood none of this. She only knew that the air had grown cold and foul, and that the familiar world was being unmade before her eyes. She pressed herself so tightly against my leg that I nearly stumbled, her small body a knot of pure, uncomprehending fear.

The circular walls of the little building rose before us, a ghostly white against the deepening purple of the sky. It was a ruin, certainly, but a solid one. The stones were massive, fitted together with a precision that had long outlasted whatever purpose they had originally served. Thick ivy clung to the walls like a tattered shroud, but the structure itself seemed sound. A single dark opening, a ragged archway, gaped on the side facing away from the great palace of the Eloi. It was our door. Our fortress.

"We are almost there," I murmured, more to myself than to her. The words were a puff of steam in the cooling air. She did not understand the words, but she must have heard the resolve in my tone. She gave a small, answering whimper and held my hand a fraction tighter.

We passed through the archway into the ruin's interior. The space within was small, perhaps thirty feet in diameter. The sky was a dark circle of indigo far above, framed by the crumbling remnants of a domed roof. The floor was a thick carpet of leaf mould, damp earth, and tangled roots. In the centre, a great pile of rubble and ancient, petrified-looking timbers lay where the roof had finally collapsed centuries ago. It was dark, and it smelled of wet stone and rot. For a moment, my heart sank. It was a grim and gloomy shelter. But it was stone. It had one entrance. And it was ours.

Weena’s courage, already stretched to its breaking point, finally failed her. The oppressive darkness inside the ruin was too much. She pulled back, trying to retreat into the twilight, her eyes wide with a new and more immediate panic. The open, dangerous air she knew was preferable to this close, suffocating blackness.

I could not allow it. I let go of her hand, and before she could bolt, I turned and took a step back out of the archway. I knelt again, putting my face close to hers. "No," I said, my voice firm but not, I hoped, unkind. I pointed into the dark interior. Then I took the small, precious box from my pocket.

My hands trembled slightly as I slid it open. The lucifer matches. My last, best link to my own time, to the age of reason and fire. They felt impossibly fragile, impossibly potent. I selected one with the care of a jeweller handling a flawless gem. Weena watched, her whimpering stilled by curiosity. She had seen me produce these from my pocket before, but she had no concept of their function.

I struck the match against the side of the box. There was a sharp, scraping sound, then a flare of brilliant, violent light. A tiny sun bloomed in the space between us. Weena cried out, a sharp gasp of astonishment and fear, and scrambled backwards, shielding her eyes. The flame sputtered, its tiny chemical heart fighting against the damp air. I shielded it with my cupped hands, a fragile spark of my own time, my own world, held against the encroaching dark of this one. It held. The sulphur gave way to the steady, golden burn of the wood.

I stood up slowly and carried my tiny torch into the centre of the ruin. I knelt by the pile of ancient, fallen timbers. They were far too large and dense to burn, but all around them was a thick scattering of dry, brittle twigs and leaves, sheltered from the rains by the debris above. It was a natural pyre, waiting for a spark. I touched the flame to the driest cluster of leaves. For a heart-stopping second, it seemed they would only smoulder. Then a small, greedy tongue of orange fire licked upwards, and then another. A wisp of smoke curled towards the open sky. I began feeding the nascent fire, first with the smallest, driest twigs I could find, then with slightly larger branches broken from the dead wood littering the floor.

The fire grew. It crackled and hissed, a living, breathing creature of light and heat. It pushed back the oppressive gloom, painting the stone walls in dancing shades of orange and red. The damp chill retreated. A circle of warmth and light, a dome of safety, expanded around me. I looked back towards the entrance. Weena was still there, a small, hesitant silhouette against the last of the twilight. She was staring at the fire. Her large, dark eyes, which had been wide with terror, now reflected the dancing flames with a kind of reverent curiosity. I had performed a miracle. I had tamed the darkness.

I beckoned to her. I smiled, a gesture I hoped she would understand. "It is safe," I said softly. "It is warm."

She took one tentative step into the ruin, then another. Her fear of the enclosed space was now warring with her fascination for the fire. The light won. She crept forward, her movements as cautious as a fawn's, until she was standing just inside the circle of light. She stared into the flames, her head cocked to one side. A strange, soft cooing sound came from her throat. She had lived her entire life under the sun, but I doubt she had ever seen a fire like this, a tame star brought down to earth. She reached out a hesitant hand, as if to touch a flame, and I quickly caught her wrist.

"No," I said, shaking my head. I picked up a small twig and thrust it into the fire. It flared up, and I pulled it back out, a tiny, glowing brand. I showed her the heat, the way it consumed the wood. Her eyes grew wider still, but now with understanding. She drew back her hand. I had given her another lesson: the beautiful light could also burn.

She settled down on the earth a few feet from me, tucking her small feet beneath her. She did not take her eyes off the fire. Her fear seemed to have melted away, replaced by a profound sense of wonder and, I think, of security. The fire was a barrier, a guardian. I was the keeper of that guardian. In her simple, direct way, she had transferred her trust from the herd to me, the fire-bringer. A heavy weight of responsibility settled upon my shoulders, heavier than the club that lay beside me.

For a time, there was peace. The only sounds were the crackling of the logs I had added to the fire and the faint, distant hum from the underworld. The circle of our firelight felt like the last bastion of warmth and sanity in a dying world. I looked at Weena, at her childlike face illuminated by the flickering light, her expression one of utter contentment. For all my knowledge, for all my intellectual grasp of the grand, tragic sweep of evolution that had led to this moment, she was in some ways better equipped for it. She lived purely in the present. She was cold, and now she was warm. She was frightened, and now she felt safe. For her, it was enough.

For me, it was not. My mind was a fever of calculation and dread. I was safe for the moment, but the night was young. The hum from the earth was a constant reminder of the beings who had hunted Weena’s people for ten thousand generations. They were down there, in the dark. And they knew we were up here. The fire was a shield, but it was also a beacon.

My vigilance was rewarded sooner than I had expected. It was not a sound that alerted me, but a sudden stillness. The incessant chirping of some nocturnal insect in the ivy outside our walls abruptly ceased. The night had gone silent. I picked up my club, its weight a crude but solid comfort in my hand. I stared into the blackness beyond the archway, my eyes straining to pierce the gloom.

Then I saw it. At the very edge of the fire’s reach, a flicker of movement. Something pale and low to the ground had shifted. I held my breath, my knuckles white on the wood of my club. It moved again, a furtive, scuttling motion. It was a hand. A long, white, spidery hand, pressed flat against the mossy ground. Then a head lifted into the faint, peripheral glow.

My blood ran cold. I had seen them in the darkness of their tunnels, but only in fleeting, nightmarish glimpses. Here, lit, however poorly, by the fire, I could see the creature with a horrifying clarity. It was not an ape, nor was it a man. It was a sickening parody of both, a thing that should not be. Its skin was a dead, fish-belly white, completely hairless and seemingly damp. It was stooped, its long arms giving it a disturbingly simian posture, but its face was a degenerate mask of the human. It had a chinless jaw and a gaping, slack mouth. But the eyes, the eyes were the true horror. They were huge, far too large for the face, and possessed a faint, reddish lustre. They were not designed for the light. They blinked slowly, painfully, and were filmed over like a fish’s, reflecting the firelight with a dead, unnerving lustre.

It stared at the fire, its head twitching from side to side. It seemed both fascinated and repelled. It took a hesitant step forward, its clawed, dirty-white feet making no sound on the damp earth. The firelight caught its face more fully, and a low hiss escaped its lips. It recoiled as if struck, shielding its great, sensitive eyes with one of its long arms.

I had my confirmation. They feared the light. They were creatures of absolute, eternal darkness. The sun was their poison, and fire was its earthly cousin.

Weena, who had been dozing, was roused by my sudden tension. She sat up, saw the direction of my gaze, and followed it. A choked, terrified squeal escaped her throat. It was the sound of a rabbit seeing a weasel. She scrambled to her feet and flung herself behind me, clutching at my coat, her face buried in my back. Her entire body was wracked with violent tremors. This was the wolf. This was the ancient fear made flesh.

The creature at the entrance was not alone. Behind it, in the deeper darkness, other shapes were stirring. I saw the glint of more reddish eyes, pairs of them, floating in the black. There were three, no, four of them. They did not rush us. They were cautious, curious. They were testing our defences. They communicated in low, guttural clicks and soft, cooing whistles that were a revolting mimicry of the Eloi’s own speech.

My mind raced. They were afraid of the fire, but they were intelligent. Or at least, they were possessed of a low, predatory cunning. They would not simply retreat. They would wait. They would test the perimeter. They would look for a weakness.

I had to be proactive. I had to reinforce the lesson of the fire. Acting on pure instinct, I reached into the fire, grabbed the end of a thick, burning branch, and pulled it out. It was a blazing torch. The sudden increase in light, the shower of sparks, sent the closest Morlock stumbling backwards with another hiss of pain and frustration.

I stood up, holding my torch aloft in one hand and my club in the other. I took a step towards the entrance. "Go!" I yelled, the word tearing from my throat, raw and primal. "Get back!"

The Morlocks flinched from the sound of my voice as much as from the light. They were creatures of silence as well as darkness. My aggressive shout was as alien to them as the fire. They shuffled back, melting into the deeper shadows beyond the arch. I saw their eyes, glowing like malevolent embers, watching me from the gloom. They were not gone. They were merely waiting.

I stepped back to the centre of our sanctuary, my heart pounding a wild tattoo against my ribs. I was breathing heavily, the adrenaline singing in my veins. I tossed the torch back onto the fire, where it added its strength to the blaze. Weena was still clinging to me, her small body trembling uncontrollably. I gently pried her hands from my coat and turned to face her. I knelt and put my hands on her shoulders.

"It is all right," I said, trying to keep my voice steady. "They are gone. The fire keeps them away."

She stared at me, her eyes bottomless pools of terror. She did not understand my words, but she saw that I was not afraid, or at least, that I was master of my fear. She saw that I had driven the monsters away. Her trembling lessened slightly. She leaned forward and rested her head against my chest, her small arms wrapping around my neck. It was a gesture of complete and total dependence. I held her for a moment, patting her back, trying to soothe her as one might a terrified child.

In that moment, holding that frail creature, I felt a profound and terrible shift within myself. I was no longer a detached observer, a scientific tourist in a future age. My journey had ceased to be an intellectual exercise. I was a participant. I was a guardian. This small, helpless descendant of my own species, this beautiful, fragile remnant of a humanity that had forgotten how to fight, was my charge. She was the last, frail ember of a humanity that had forgotten how to burn, and I, a ghost from the past, had become her keeper. They were beautiful, the Eloi, and they were doomed; their grace was the grace of the sacrificial lamb. But this one, this one I would not let them have.

After a long while, Weena’s trembling subsided, and her breathing evened out into the slow, deep rhythm of sleep. She had cried herself into exhaustion. I carefully laid her down on the bed of leaves near the fire, pulling a piece of my coat over her for warmth. She curled into a ball, a picture of innocence in a world that had no place for it.

I did not sleep. I could not. I sat with my back against the stone wall, my club across my knees, and watched the fire. The night was long, and the hunters were many. I fed the flames, stick by stick, conserving my precious fuel. The fire was my only true companion, a silent, watchful ally. Each time it dipped, the shadows crept closer, and my own fear stirred. Each log I added was an act of defiance.

My mind, no longer occupied with immediate terror, turned to strategy. This ruin was a temporary refuge, nothing more. I could not stay here forever. My fuel would run out. My matches were finite. And my ultimate goal, my only real hope of escape, was the Time Machine, locked away somewhere beneath the feet of the White Sphinx. To get it back, I would have to go on the offensive. I would have to descend into their world, into the blackness they called home. The thought filled me with a cold, visceral dread.

But dread was a luxury I could not afford. I needed a better weapon. I needed more fire. I thought of the Palace of Green Porcelain, the great museum I had seen in the distance. Museums held artifacts. They held the tools and weapons of past ages. In a world of sticks and stones, even a simple iron bar from a forgotten display case would be a king’s sceptre. It could hold metal. It could hold flammable chemicals. It could hold the keys to my survival, and to Weena's.

That would be my goal. At dawn, when the Morlocks retreated from the hated sun, we would leave this place and make for the museum. It was a long and dangerous journey, but it was a plan. It was a hope.

I looked at Weena, sleeping peacefully in the firelight. Her face, so placid and untroubled in sleep, was a stark contrast to the grim calculations running through my head. I was a man from the nineteenth century, an age of progress, industry, and boundless optimism. And here I was, in the far future, reduced to the state of a primitive caveman, guarding his mate and his fire against the creatures of the night. It was the ultimate irony. Humanity had climbed so high, only to fall so very far. The Eloi were the pampered, witless aristocracy; the Morlocks, the brutalized, monstrous proletariat. And I, I was something else entirely. A ghost, a warrior, a protector.

The fire crackled, sending a fresh shower of sparks up towards the dark, open sky. Outside the archway, I could hear a faint, intermittent scratching, the sound of the Morlocks, patient and persistent, testing the edges of my fortress of light. They would be there all night. I tightened my grip on my club. The night was long, and the hunters were many, but the fire still burned, and I was awake.

The scratching ceased, then began again in a new place, a dry, rasping sound like a rat gnawing through a wall. I did not move. I merely listened, cataloguing the noises, mapping the positions of my besiegers in the darkness. There were at least three, perhaps four. They were not working in concert, not in any intelligent sense that I could discern. Their efforts were individual, born of a singular, bestial hunger. They were not strategists. They were simply a tide of patient malice, waiting for the seawall of my fire to fail.

Hour after hour I sat upon the cold stone. I marked the slow passage of the night by the wheeling of the unfamiliar constellations above. The moon, a pale and hurried sliver, had long since set. My mind, sharp and clear from a lack of sleep, became a strange and lonely place. I found myself thinking of my laboratory back in Richmond, of the comfortable clutter, the smell of shellac and ozone, the warm glow of the gas lamps. I thought of my friends, of the Medical Man and the Psychologist, gathered around my mahogany dining table, listening with polite incredulity to my theories. What would they say if they could see me now? A dirty, half starved creature in ragged clothes, armed with a crude club, presiding over a dwindling fire in the ruins of the eight hundred and second millennium. They would not believe it. I scarcely believed it myself.

Weena stirred in her sleep, a soft whimper escaping her lips. I reached out and gently placed a hand on her shoulder. She settled at my touch, her breathing returning to its peaceful rhythm. This simple, instinctual gesture surprised me. I was her protector, yes, but the connection felt deeper, more primal. In her, I saw not just an Eloi, not just a specimen of a degraded humanity, but a living, breathing soul who had placed her absolute trust in me. It was a terrifying and profound responsibility. My success was her life. My failure was her death, a horrid, unthinkable death in the dark. This thought, more than any other, steeed my resolve. The fire was for me, but it was also for her. The journey to the Palace of Green Porcelain was for me, but it was most certainly for her.

A sudden, sharp clang echoed from the far side of the ruin. It was a different sound from the scratching, a sound of metal on stone. My head snapped up, every nerve ending tingling. One of the Morlocks, more daring or perhaps more foolish than the others, had dislodged a piece of the crumbling masonry. It was followed by a soft thud and a guttural squeal of pain or surprise. Then, silence. Had one of them been injured? I strained my ears, but heard nothing more. They were learning, perhaps. Learning the limits of their prison, and mine. The fire had sunk low. I realised with a jolt that I had been lost in my reverie. I quickly, and with a miser's care, placed another dry branch onto the glowing embers. It caught with a satisfying crackle, and the shadows were once more beaten back to the edges of the archway. The reprieve was brief. I knew the night was a finite resource, and so was my wood.

The dawn came slowly. It was not a sudden burst of celestial fire, but a gradual, almost reluctant seeping of grey into the profound blackness of the sky, a dilution of the night’s ink. As the light grew, the sounds from outside dwindled. The scratching stopped. The faint, unsettling whispers ceased. It was as if the sun itself was a great and terrible weapon, its first rays striking dumb the creatures of the pit. I watched, hardly daring to breathe, as the archway transformed from a square of absolute black into a frame for a world of soft, grey shapes. A profound silence fell over the land, the kind of quiet that follows a storm. They were gone. For now.

I rose stiffly, my joints protesting, my muscles aching from the long vigil. The fire, which had been my saviour, was now just a pile of smoking ash. I looked down at Weena. She was still asleep, one small hand clutching the hem of my tattered coat. I felt a surge of affection so powerful it almost made my knees buckle. I gently shook her awake.

Her eyes fluttered open, large and brown and filled with the immediate, uncomplicated joy of a new day. She sat up, stretched her little arms above her head, and smiled at me. There was no memory of the night’s terror in her face, no lingering dread. For the Eloi, it seemed, fear was a nocturnal ailment, cured completely by the rising sun. She pointed towards the archway, now bright with morning light, and made a series of happy, chirping sounds. She was ready to go out, to play among the flowers.

“Not just yet,” I said, my voice a rough croak. I picked up my club. “We have a long walk ahead of us, Weena. A very long walk.”

She did not understand my words, of course, but she understood my tone. She tilted her head, her brow furrowing in a rare expression of concern. I offered her my hand. She took it without hesitation, her small fingers wrapping around mine. And so, we set off.

The journey to the Palace of Green Porcelain was an odyssey through a graveyard of giants. The structure I had seen from the hilltop was much farther than I had anticipated. We walked for hours under the warm sun, through landscapes of breathtaking beauty and profound melancholy. We traversed great, park like spaces where brilliantly coloured, unfamiliar flowers grew in wild profusion. We followed the broken lines of what must have been magnificent roads, their surfaces shattered and overgrown. Here and there, colossal fragments of architecture lay half buried in the earth, like the bones of some gargantuan, fossilised beast. The scale of it all was overwhelming. This had not been a civilization of men, but of titans. And the utter silence, broken only by the buzz of insects and the calls of strange birds, was the most eloquent testament to their fall.

Weena was a cheerful and resourceful companion. Her fear of the dark was absolute, but in the daylight, she was fearless. She skipped ahead, her white tunic a bright spot against the green and grey of the ruins, pointing out things with childlike wonder. She showed me which of the exotic fruits were good to eat, pulling them from trees with a practiced ease. She seemed to know the land in a way I never could, an instinctual, intimate knowledge passed down not through learning, but through her very blood. Several times, we encountered other Eloi. They were reclining in the sun, weaving garlands of flowers, or chasing one another in aimless, laughing games. They would look up as we passed, their beautiful, placid faces showing a mild curiosity, nothing more. They offered Weena flowers, which she accepted, but none of them seemed to question why she was with me, a strange, grim figure who did not belong. I was invisible to them, a ghost in their midst. It was a deeply unsettling feeling.

The constant reminder of the Morlocks was the network of circular wells, the ventilation shafts for their subterranean world. We passed dozens of them. Each one was a dark, round mouth breathing a faint, cool air that smelled of damp earth and machinery. Weena gave them a wide berth, a flicker of her nocturnal fear touching her face even in the bright sunlight. I, on the other hand, found myself drawn to them. I would pause, peering down into the oppressive blackness, listening. Sometimes, I thought I could hear a faint, metallic clang from the depths, the echo of their hidden industry. The Eloi lived on the surface, a garden of Eden. The Morlocks toiled below, in a self made hell. And they fed on the Eloi. The perfect, brutal economy of this new world never ceased to horrify and fascinate me.

As the sun climbed towards its zenith, the Palace of Green Porcelain grew larger on the horizon. It was not a single building, but a complex, a sprawling edifice of strange, sea green material that glittered in the sun. It was of a completely different architectural style from the other ruins. It was more massive, more angular, and despite its age and dilapidation, it seemed somehow more modern, more functional. Great sections of its walls had fallen away, revealing the dark interiors. Towers, some intact, some broken, pointed towards the sky like accusing fingers. It looked less like a palace and more like a fortress, a last bastion of a forgotten science.

Our progress was slowed when we came to a broad, slow moving river. The bridge that had once spanned it was a wreck of twisted metal and shattered stone, its central arches long since collapsed into the water. There was no way across. Weena, seeing the water, clapped her hands in delight and made to wade in, but I held her back. I had no idea how deep it was, or what might lurk beneath the surface. For a moment, I felt a familiar stab of despair. To have come so far, only to be thwarted by a simple river. But then I looked at the debris of the bridge. Great girders, thick as tree trunks, lay half submerged in the shallows near the bank. An idea formed in my mind. A foolish, desperate idea.

“Wait here,” I said to Weena, pointing to a spot on the grassy bank. I found a long, sturdy piece of fallen masonry and waded into the river myself, using the stone to probe the depth ahead of me. The water was cool and surprisingly clear. The bottom was silty, but firm. I moved slowly, carefully, towards the nearest of the great fallen girders. It was a massive piece of rusted iron, slick with green algae, but it formed a precarious, partial bridge to the next piece of wreckage. It would be dangerous. A slip could mean a broken leg, or worse. But it was possible.

I returned to the bank. Weena watched me with wide, curious eyes. I tried to explain what we had to do, using gestures, pointing from one piece of wreckage to the next. She seemed to understand. I picked her up. She was impossibly light, like a bird. I held her tight against my chest, my club tucked into my belt, and started across.

That crossing was one of the most terrifying experiences of my life. Each step was a trial of balance and nerve. The metal was slippery. The gaps between the pieces of the broken bridge seemed to yawn like open graves. Weena clung to me, her face buried in my shoulder, but she did not cry out. She was perfectly still, trusting me completely. Halfway across, my foot slipped on a patch of wet moss. For a heart stopping moment, we tilted precariously over the flowing water. I gritted my teeth, my muscles screaming with the effort of righting myself. I managed to find a new foothold and, after a moment, continued on. Finally, with a gasp of relief, I stumbled onto the far bank and set Weena down on solid ground. She immediately ran to pick a flower, the ordeal completely forgotten. I sat for a long moment, my heart hammering against my ribs, and simply breathed.

We were closer now. The Palace of Green Porcelain loomed over us, a silent, brooding giant. The final approach was up a grand, shattered staircase, each step the size of a small carriage. Weeds grew in the cracks, and a fine, green dust coated everything. The main entrance was a gaping, shadowed maw where enormous doors had once hung. They were gone now, likely torn from their hinges centuries ago. The air that drifted out was cool and musty, carrying the scent of ages, of dust and decay.

Weena hesitated at the threshold. She tugged on my hand, pointing back towards the sun and the open fields. This was not her world. The Eloi were creatures of light and air; this dark, enclosed space was anathema to them.

“I know,” I said softly, stropping to my knee to be at her level. “It is dark. It is frightening. But we must go inside. There are things in here that can help us. Things that will keep the night away.” I held up my hand, and made a motion of a bright, dancing flame. “Fire,” I said. “More fire.”

She looked from the dark doorway to my face, her own face a mask of confusion and fear. But then she looked at my hand, the one she had held so tightly during our journey. She placed her own small hand back in mine. It was a gesture of trust that shamed my own fears.

“Together,” I said, though she could not understand. I stood up, took a deep breath, and holding Weena’s hand tightly, I led her out of the sun and into the echoing silence of the Palace of Green Porcelain.

The change was instantaneous and profound. The warm sunlight was cut off, replaced by a dim, greenish twilight that filtered down from high, broken windows and gaping holes in the roof. The air was heavy and still, thick with the dust of millennia. Our footsteps, which had been silent on the soft turf outside, now echoed unnervingly in the vast, cavernous space. We stood in what must have been a great hall, a space so large that its distant walls were lost in shadow. Great, dust shrouded shapes loomed in the gloom, the ghosts of ancient exhibits.

Weena pressed close against my leg, her eyes wide with apprehension. This place was the very antithesis of her nature. I gave her hand a reassuring squeeze. My own heart was pounding, but not entirely from fear. There was a sense of excitement, of discovery. I was an explorer, an archaeologist, standing on the threshold of the ultimate museum.

As my eyes adjusted to the gloom, I began to make out details. The floor was made of some polished, dark material, now cracked and covered in a thick layer of green dust. The looming shapes were skeletons. Skeletons of creatures so vast and strange that my mind struggled to comprehend them. There was a colossal, four legged beast with a long, serpentine neck that stretched up into the darkness above, its skull nearly touching the distant, shattered ceiling. Beside it stood the fossilised remains of something that looked like a giant, armoured insect. This was the gallery of palaeontology, I realised, a record of life on Earth. But what age of Earth, I could not say.

We moved forward slowly, my club held ready. The silence was absolute. We passed case after case of shattered glass. Inside, smaller skeletons and fossilised plants lay in disarray, covered in the same ubiquitous green dust. It was a scene of utter neglect and desolation. Yet, for all the decay, there was a sense of order to the ruin. The museum was laid out in sections. We passed from the hall of ancient life into what I took to be a gallery of geology. Huge, cut geodes lay open, their crystalline hearts dulled by time. Mineral samples were arranged in crumbling cabinets, their labels long since faded to illegibility.

Weena, despite her fear, was distracted by a display of brightly coloured crystals. She let go of my hand and went to touch one, a shard of deep, purple quartz. The simple, innocent act was a strange counterpoint to the oppressive gloom.

My goal, however, was not geology. I was looking for chemistry. I was looking for anthropology. I was looking for the armory. I needed tools, weapons, and combustibles. I scanned the vast halls, looking for any sign, any faded placard that might guide me. The layout seemed logical, progressing from the natural world towards the works of man. We pressed on, deeper into the museum.

We entered a new section. The exhibits changed. Here, there were no skeletons or rocks, but machines. Strange, complex contraptions of rusted metal and dulled brass, their purposes a complete mystery to me. They were not machines of my time. They were too sleek, too complex, their forms both elegant and alien. This must be the hall of industry and invention. My hopes rose. Where there is industry, there is chemistry. Where there is chemistry, there is the potential for fire, for explosions, for power.

I examined one of the machines more closely. It was a series of interlocking rings and spheres, covered in levers and dials. It was coated in dust, but beneath it, the workmanship was exquisite. What was its purpose? Did it generate power? Did it bend space? I would never know. It was as mute and mysterious as the Sphinx itself.

It was in this hall that we found the first sign that we were not entirely alone. Lying on the floor, amidst a scattering of broken components, was a single, white bone. It was not a fossil. It was fresh. Too fresh. It was long and slender, and it had been gnawed at one end. I picked it up. My blood ran cold. It was, I was almost certain, the femur of an Eloi.

I held the gnawed bone in my hand, its smooth, polished surface cold against my skin. The implications of it settled upon me not with a sudden shock, but with a slow, creeping dread that tightened its icy grip around my heart. The Morlocks were not merely subterranean creatures who emerged at night. They were here, now, in this vast, silent tomb. This museum, this palace of green porcelain and bronze, was not just a relic of a dead civilisation. It was a larder. The thought was so profoundly grotesque, so utterly alien to any concept of humanity I had ever entertained, that for a moment my mind refused to process it. I looked over at Weena. She was still captivated by the dull gleam of the crystals, her small hand tracing the facets of the purple quartz, entirely oblivious to the horror I held. A wave of fierce, protective anger surged through me. I would not let these creatures have her. I would not let her innocent, fragile existence become another gnawed bone on a dusty floor.

Quietly, so as not to startle her, I tucked the femur into a deep pocket of my coat. She must not see it. Her fear was already a constant, flickering flame, and a sight like this would surely cause it to erupt into a conflagration of terror I could not manage. My own fear was a different beast entirely. It was a cold, calculating thing, a scientist's fear. It sharpened my senses, heightened my awareness. The silence of the great hall was no longer a peaceful void, but a taut, stretched membrane, ready to be punctured by the slightest sound. Every shadow seemed deeper, every recess a potential hiding place for something pale and sightless. The dust on the floor, which before had been a simple testament to the passage of time, now seemed to hold the faint impressions of soft, unshod feet.

My mission had acquired a desperate, terrible urgency. I was no longer an explorer cataloguing a lost world. I was a man hunted, and I needed to become a hunter myself. The club in my hand felt suddenly inadequate, a primitive stick against a monstrous, unseen enemy. We had to press on. To retreat now, back into the open, felt like a fool's errand. The Morlocks owned the night, and the grey twilight of this distant age was little better. Here, within these walls, lay the potential for salvation. Here were the tools of man, the accumulated knowledge of a species, however decayed. I had to find them.

"Weena," I said, my voice a low whisper that seemed unnaturally loud in the stillness. She started, pulling her hand back from the crystals as if caught in some transgression. Her large, dark eyes fixed on mine, wide with question. I forced a reassuring smile, a grimace that felt like a betrayal of my true feelings. "We must keep going." I took her hand again. Her small fingers were cold. She clung to me tightly, sensing the shift in my mood, the new tension that coiled within me.

We moved away from the geology gallery, leaving the silent, glittering crystals behind. The layout of the museum continued to be logical, a silent narrative of a world's history. From geology we passed into a hall that must have been dedicated to botany. Great, fossilised tree trunks stood like pillars, and in shattered glass cases lay the delicate, carbonised impressions of ferns and flowers unknown to my own time. But my eyes were not on the exhibits. I scanned the floor, the corners, the spaces beneath the towering displays. And I found more of what I dreaded. Here, a small, delicate rib, no larger than my finger. There, a scrap of the same bright fabric the Eloi wore, stained and torn. A trail. A gruesome trail of breadcrumbs leading deeper into the labyrinth.

The air began to change. A faint, unpleasant odour, musky and foul, reached my nostrils. It was the scent of a predator's den, a smell of old meat and unwholesome living. Weena wrinkled her nose and pressed closer to my side, her steps faltering. I squeezed her hand, urging her forward. The trail led us towards a darkened archway at the far end of the botany hall. It was not a main entrance to another gallery, but seemed to be a smaller, secondary passage, perhaps for staff. The smell was stronger here. My heart hammered against my ribs, a frantic drumbeat in the oppressive quiet. I knew I should turn back, that to follow this path was to walk directly into the jaws of the beast. But I could not. I had to know. I had to see the full extent of the horror.

I held my club ready, my knuckles white. Weena was whimpering softly now, a continuous, pitiable sound. I drew her behind me, stepping into the darkness of the archway. The passage beyond was short, opening into a small, circular chamber that might have been a storage area or a workshop. The light from the main hall filtered in weakly, illuminating a scene of absolute nightmare.

It was their feasting place. Heaped in a great, disordered pile against the far wall was a mass of bones. Skulls, femurs, ribs, vertebrae, all jumbled together in a horrifying testament to their appetites. They were all small, all delicate. They were all Eloi. Some were clean, picked bare and white. Others were darker, still bearing traces of what I did not want to contemplate. The stench was overpowering. I felt a wave of nausea wash over me, and I choked back the bile that rose in my throat. This was the final, ghastly truth of this future world. The beautiful, childlike Eloi were not the inheritors of the Earth. They were cattle, raised in the sun for the grim harvest of the Morlocks below. The whole elaborate structure of their society, the carefree life, the lack of struggle, it was all a monstrous form of animal husbandry.

Suddenly, from the deep shadows in the corner of the chamber, came a sound. A soft, wet, scraping noise. It was followed by a low, chittering sound, a guttural clicking that was not animal, yet not human. My blood turned to ice. We were not alone in the charnel house.

I did not wait to see what emerged. I grabbed Weena's arm and pulled her back with a force that made her cry out. We stumbled back into the botany hall, my mind racing. They were here. They knew we were here. The chase was on. I could not fight them in the dark, not on their terms. I needed light. I needed fire. The chemistry gallery. It was my only hope.

We ran. Our footsteps echoed unnaturally through the vast, empty spaces. Weena's fear gave her speed, and she scrambled to keep up with my long strides. We blundered through a hall of what looked like astronomical instruments, great brass orreries and telescopes pointing sightlessly towards the shattered roof. I barely gave them a glance. I was looking for a sign, a placard, any clue to guide me. Then I saw it. A wide doorway, and above it, though the paint was peeling and faded, I could just make out the words: "CHEMICAL & PHARMACEUTICAL ARTS".

We plunged into the gallery. It was in even greater disarray than the others. Shelves had collapsed, spilling their contents across the floor. Thousands of glass bottles, jars, and beakers lay in glittering, hazardous piles. The air was thick with the dust of a hundred different powders, a strange, acrid perfume of decay. It was a chaotic mess, but it was also a treasure trove.

"Stay here," I commanded Weena, pushing her gently into a corner behind a large, overturned cabinet where she would be out of sight. "Do not make a sound." She nodded, her eyes like huge, dark saucers in her pale face.

Then I began my frantic search. I was like a madman, scrabbling through the debris, ignoring the cuts on my hands from the broken glass. I needed combustibles. I needed anything that would burn. I knocked aside jars of inert salts and harmless crystallised sugars. I found a container of what looked like sodium, but it had long since oxidised into a useless white powder. I found liquids in sealed flagons, but I dared not open them, not knowing if they were corrosive acids or deadly poisons. The chittering sounds were closer now, echoing from the hall of astronomy we had just fled. They were following us.

My fingers closed around a large, waxy block, wrapped in what felt like oilcloth. I tore the covering away. A strong, pungent, and wonderfully familiar scent filled my nostrils. Camphor. A large block of it, at least a pound. It was highly flammable. A profound sense of relief washed over me. This was a weapon. This was a shield. I broke a piece off and put it in my pocket, then looked for something to carry the main block in. My pockets were already full. I spied a heavy velvet curtain, green with age, covering a shattered window. I tore a large square from it and wrapped the camphor, tying the corners to make a makeshift bag.

My search grew more desperate. What else? What else could I find? My eyes fell upon a row of large, stoneware jars on a high, intact shelf. They were labelled, but the paper was crumbling. I could just make out a symbol on one, a stylised 'S'. Sulphur. I knew it at once. I scrambled up the shelving, my feet slipping on broken vials. The jar was heavy, sealed with a thick cork stopper. I worked it free and peered inside. A mound of fine, yellow powder. Sulphur. An essential ingredient for gunpowder, for matches, for fire in its most violent forms. I could not take the whole jar. It was too heavy, too cumbersome. I looked around wildly. I emptied a small leather satchel that had once held mineral samples and, with my hands, began scooping the yellow powder into it. Dust flew everywhere, making me cough and sneeze, but I did not care. I was arming myself. I was forging the thunderbolts of a new god in this dead world.

A soft scraping sound from the doorway made me freeze. I looked down from my perch. A pale, ape like shape stood there, its large, greyish red eyes blinking in the dim light. It was a Morlock, the first I had seen this close. It was a sickeningly white, dwarfish creature, with a shock of flaxen hair on its head and down its back. It was not looking at me, but sniffing the air, its head turning from side to side. Then its great, luminous eyes fixed on my position. It let out a high pitched, chittering call. An answer came from the hall, and then another. They were gathering.

I slung the satchel of sulphur over my shoulder and scrambled down from the shelf, landing heavily amidst the glass. The Morlock in the doorway flinched back at the noise but did not flee. It stood its ground, a sentinel. I could see more shapes gathering behind it, a mass of pale bodies and shining eyes. They were blocking my only exit.

I brandished my club. My heart was a wild drum. I looked at Weena, huddled in her corner. I had to get us out. I thought of the camphor in my pocket. If only I had a flame. A match. Anything. But I had nothing. My pockets contained the future of fire, but the present was dark and cold.

There was another way. The gallery was long, and I could see another doorway at the far end. It was our only chance. "Weena, run!" I shouted, pointing towards the distant exit. "Run to that door!"

The sound of my voice seemed to startle the Morlocks. They stirred, their chittering rising in pitch. The one in the doorway took a hesitant step into the room. I did not wait for it to take another. I charged, not at the creature, but past it, angling for the space between it and the doorframe. It reached out a long, white arm to grab me, and I swung my club in a low arc, smashing it against the creature's limb. There was a dull crack and a squeal of pain. It recoiled, and I was through.

I ran to Weena, grabbed her hand, and pulled her along. "Now!" I yelled, and we sprinted down the length of the chemistry gallery, our feet crunching on the broken glass. The Morlocks surged into the room behind us, a tide of white flesh and guttural cries. They were faster than I had imagined, their movements unnervingly swift and spider like.

We burst through the far doorway and found ourselves in yet another vast hall. This one was different. It was filled with figures. Lifeless, human figures. For a terrifying second, I thought we had stumbled into a colony of them, but then I realised they were mannequins, arranged in tableaux. We were in the ethnological section. The hall of anthropology. Displays of primitive huts stood beside cases of pottery and tools. And weapons.

My eyes latched onto a large, glass fronted case against the wall. It contained a display of medieval European armaments. Swords, shields, poleaxes, and maces. The glass was thick plate, reinforced with a bronze frame. The Morlocks were pouring into the hall behind us, their numbers swelling. They were fanning out, their intent to surround us clear.

I let go of Weena's hand. "Stand back," I ordered. I lifted my club, the crude weapon that had served me so far, and with all the strength I possessed, I swung it against the glass case. The club bounced off with a loud thud, the glass shuddering but not breaking. The Morlocks paused, their chittering momentarily silenced by the violent noise. I swung again, aiming for the same spot. This time, a network of cracks appeared. On the third swing, the glass exploded inwards with a deafening crash, showering the display with glittering shards.

The noise spurred the Morlocks into action. They began to advance, their initial caution overcome by their numbers. I ignored them for the moment, reaching into the shattered case. I bypassed the swords; I had no training in their use. My hand closed around the haft of a heavy, iron mace. Its head was a brutal sphere of flanged metal. It was perfectly balanced, a tool designed for one purpose: to crush and destroy. Its weight felt magnificent in my hand, a solid and uncompromising answer to the creeping horror of this world.

I turned to face them, the mace held ready. Weena was screaming now, a high, piercing shriek of pure terror. One of the Morlocks, bolder than the rest, detached itself from the pack and scuttled towards me. It was low to the ground, its long arms reaching. I did not hesitate. I did not think. I acted. I brought the mace down in a powerful, vertical arc. There was a hideous, crunching sound, a noise of shattered bone and ruined flesh, and the creature collapsed into a heap of white limbs. It did not even twitch.

The effect on the others was instantaneous and profound. They stopped dead. A wave of agitated chittering passed through their ranks. They had come for an easy meal, for the soft, unresisting flesh of the Eloi. They had not expected this. They had not expected iron. They had not expected a creature that fought back with such sickening finality. They were predators, yes, but they were cowardly predators, accustomed to their prey simply accepting its fate.

This was my chance. The terror I had inspired in them was my shield. I would not, could not, fight them all. "Run, Weena!" I bellowed, scooping her up with my free arm. She was light, a mere featherweight. Holding her against my side, the heavy mace in my other hand, I charged. I did not run back the way we came, towards the deeper parts of the museum. I ran towards the main entrance, back towards the dim, grey light of the outside world.

I became a whirlwind of desperate motion, a force of nature plowing through their ranks. They scattered before me, shrieking and stumbling over one another to get out of my way. They were terrified of the mace, of the brutal death it represented. I was no longer prey. I was a monster to them, as fearsome as they were to me.

We careened through the hall of industry, its strange machines silent witnesses to our flight. We passed the gallery of palaeontology, the great skeleton of the dinosaur a grim reminder of extinction. The Morlocks gave chase, but it was a half hearted pursuit. They stayed in the shadows, their glowing eyes watching us, but they kept their distance. They were creatures of darkness, and I was running towards the light.

Finally, I saw it. The grand entrance hall, and beyond it, the pale rectangle of the open bronze doors. Freedom. With the last of my strength, I burst out of the museum and into the open air. The grey daylight, as dim as it was, felt as brilliant as a summer noon. I stumbled to a halt on the stone steps, panting, my lungs burning. I looked back. The Morlocks were crowded in the darkness of the doorway, a writhing mass of pale bodies and resentful eyes, unwilling or unable to follow us into the light.

I set Weena down. She was trembling uncontrollably, her face buried in my coat. I stood there on the steps of that great, dead palace, the iron mace heavy in my hand, the satchel of sulphur and the block of camphor slung over my shoulder. I was exhausted, terrified, and splattered with the foulness of my enemy. But I was alive. And I was armed. The passive observer from the nineteenth century was gone forever, consumed in the dark halls of the museum. In his place stood something new, something harder. The war against the future had begun.

I held my position for a long moment, my chest heaving, my grip tight on the haft of the iron mace. The world seemed to spin in a slow, grey vortex. Weena clung to my leg, her soft sobs the only sound besides the rush of blood in my ears and the distant, hateful murmuring from the museum's dark maw. The Morlocks did not disperse. They remained just inside the threshold of shadow, a gallery of pale, frustrated faces and luminous eyes. They were waiting. They knew, with an ancient and horrible patience, that the sun, this weak and watery guardian of mine, would eventually abandon its post. The day was already old and tired. The light had the quality of an eclipse, a pervasive dimness that promised no true dawn and a swift dusk.

"We must go," I said, my voice a raw rasp. "We cannot stay here."

I gently detached Weena from my coat, kneeling to look into her face. Her large, innocent eyes were wide with a terror she could not comprehend. She had seen the little people, the Morlocks, before. They were the bogeymen of her race's childhood, the dark things that came at night. But she had never, I am sure, seen one so utterly and finally broken. She had never witnessed such violence. I had not merely saved her; I had corrupted her world with a brutality it had long forgotten. Or perhaps, more accurately, a brutality it had outsourced to the darkness below.

"The sun goes away," she whispered, her tiny hand pointing to the low, grey clouds in the west, where a faint, sickly yellow light struggled. "The dark comes."

"Yes," I said, my heart a cold stone. "Yes, it does. That is why we must find a place. A high place. With fire."

Fire. The idea was a revelation, a beacon in the desperate calculus of my mind. In my own time, fire was a tool, a comfort, a source of industrial power. Here, it was a weapon. It was life. It was the one thing, I felt certain, that the Morlocks, with their centuries spent in subterranean blackness, would truly fear. I had the means. My pockets, which had seemed so ludicrously filled with trinkets from the future’s toy box, now held the keys to my survival. The matches, the camphor, the sulphur. I was a veritable Prometheus in this dying world.

I took Weena's hand. Her fingers were cold and trembled in my own. "Show me," I said, trying to keep my voice calm, trying to project a confidence I was far from feeling. "Show me a high place. Away from the trees. A hill."

She looked at me, her brow furrowed in thought, the immediate terror receding as her mind grappled with my request. The Eloi, for all their simplicity, possessed a faultless intimate geography of their small world. She pointed, not back the way we had come, but sideways, along the great ruined facade of the museum. "There," she said. "The place of the high stones. It is far."

"Far is good," I replied, my gaze fixed on the lingering Morlocks. I hefted the mace, its weight a grim reassurance. "Far is what we need."

I did not turn my back on the museum. I began to walk sideways, like a crab, pulling Weena along with me, my eyes locked on the darkness under the bronze doors. The Morlocks did not move. They simply watched. I felt their collective gaze as a physical pressure, a malevolent weight against my soul. They knew they had lost this skirmish, but the war, as they measured time, was long. With every step I took away from the museum, the pressure lessened. Ten yards. Twenty. Fifty. At a hundred yards, I finally dared to turn my back on them and break into a lumbering trot, pulling the stumbling Weena behind me.

Our path was a treacherous one. We followed the line of a great, shattered roadway, its surface buckled and broken by the roots of strange, grey leaved trees. The vegetation was thick and rampant, choking the life out of the stone bones of the ancient city. Great scarlet blossoms, as large as dinner plates, drooped from fleshy stalks, and fuzzy, moss like growths clung to every surface. The air was thick with the scent of damp earth and decay. It was a botanist's paradise and a sane man's nightmare.

Weena, despite her fear, seemed to navigate this wilderness with an inborn grace. She pointed out the smoothest paths, guided me around treacherous patches of thorny vines, and once, pulled me back from a patch of ground that quivered in a most unnatural way. I was the warrior, the monster with the iron club, but she was the guide, the native intelligence of this hostile land. We were a strange partnership, the last scholar and the last innocent, bound together by a shared and monstrous enemy.

After what felt like an eternity of hurried, anxious travel, the ground began to slope upwards. The dense vegetation thinned, giving way to coarse grass and weathered stone. Weena was right. We were ascending a considerable hill, its summit crowned with a cluster of what looked like enormous, weathered megaliths. They were not natural formations. They were the last, defiant remnants of some colossal structure, worn down by a million rains, standing like broken teeth against the bruised sky. This was the place.

"We stop here," I declared, my lungs burning. The climb had been steep, and the mace and satchel felt as if they were forged from lead.

The summit of the hill offered a commanding view. Below us, the world stretched out in a panorama of grey green decay. I could see the Palace of Green Porcelain, now a distant shape, its bronze doors a tiny dark rectangle. I could see the silver birch forest, the river a dull metallic ribbon, and dotted across the landscape, the familiar circular wells, the gateways to the underworld, puffing their faint, rhythmic plumes of air. There was nowhere for the Morlocks to hide up here. The ground was open for at least two hundred feet in every direction.

But the light was failing fast. The sickly yellow in the west had deepened to a lurid orange, and the shadows in the valleys were growing long and black. The world was holding its breath, waiting for the night.

"Wood," I said to Weena, my voice urgent. "We need wood. Dry wood. Small pieces. Quick!"

I dropped my satchel and the mace, my hands suddenly feeling light and useless without its weight. I began to scour the hilltop, my eyes darting everywhere. There was little to be found. The great stones were bare, and the grass was short. But around the base of the megaliths, lodged in crevices and hollows, were tangles of dried roots and scraps of wind blown branches, brittle and dry as bone. It was not much, but it would have to be enough. Weena, her fear seemingly forgotten in the face of this new, urgent task, was a surprisingly effective forager. She moved like a little butterfly, her small hands gathering tiny twigs and bits of dried grass I would have overlooked.

In the center of the ring of stones, I cleared a small patch of earth down to the bare dirt. This would be our hearth. As the last vestiges of daylight bled from the sky, I arranged our pathetic collection of fuel into a small, hopeful pyramid. My hands were shaking, not from cold, but from a profound, existential dread. Everything depended on this.

I took the box of matches from my pocket. How many had I used? How many were left? I dared not count. I struck one. The tiny flame sputtered, a fragile miracle in the gathering gloom. I shielded it with my hands, my heart in my throat, and brought it to the base of the kindling. A wisp of smoke, a brief, discouraging sizzle, and then, a tiny tongue of orange flame licked at a dried leaf. It caught. The flame grew, nurtured by the dry grass Weena had found. It climbed the twigs, embracing the larger pieces of wood. Within a minute, we had a small, but defiant, fire.

I let out a breath I did not realize I had been holding. The warmth on my face was the most welcome sensation I had ever known. Weena crept closer, her eyes wide with a mixture of fear and wonder. The Eloi, I had observed, had a deep seated fear of fire, just as they did of the dark. It was another one of their strange, childlike traits.

"It is a friend," I told her, my voice softer than I intended. I pointed into the darkness that now completely surrounded our small island of light. "It keeps the dark away. It keeps the Morlocks away."

She looked from the fire to the darkness, and then to me. I do not know if she understood my words, but she seemed to understand the intent. She sat down close to me, her shoulder pressing against my leg, and stared into the flames.

The fire was our shield, but it was also a beacon. I had no doubt that our little flame was visible for miles, a clear signal to every Morlock in the valley. They would come. I settled down for the long watch, the iron mace across my knees. The night was cold, far colder than the mild days. A wind had picked up, whistling a mournful tune through the high stones. Our fire, which had seemed so robust, now seemed small and fragile against the immense, pressing dark.

For a long time, there was nothing. Only the sound of the wind, the crackle of the fire, and Weena’s soft, even breathing. She had fallen asleep, her head resting on my thigh, one hand clutching a fold of my trousers. In sleep, her face was completely untroubled, a picture of perfect, unthinking peace. Looking at her, a fierce, protective feeling rose in me, so powerful it was almost painful. I was no longer a scientist studying a specimen. I was a guardian. My life had acquired a purpose so simple and so absolute it was terrifying. I had to keep this one small, gentle creature safe from the carnivorous dark.

Then, I saw them. At the very edge of the firelight, a flicker of movement. I stared, my muscles tensing. Another flicker. And then, a pair of eyes, reflecting the firelight with a greenish, phosphorescent glow. They appeared and vanished. Then another pair, further to the left. And another. Soon, the entire perimeter of our sanctuary was ringed with these disembodied, watching eyes. They did not make a sound. They simply watched, waiting, their patience an unnerving, predatory thing.

I added more wood to the fire, stoking it until it roared, sending a shower of sparks up into the black sky. The eyes retreated slightly, but they did not disappear. They were testing the boundary, gauging my resolve, measuring the reach of the light.

My mind, freed from the immediate necessity of action, began to race. I saw with sickening clarity the elegant and horrifying symmetry of this future world. The Eloi, the beautiful, frail aristocracy, lived their lives in the sun, a life of leisure and placid stupidity. The Morlocks, the pale, industrious working class, had been pushed underground, toiling in darkness to maintain the machinery that kept the upper world running, their humanity bleached out of them by generations of subterranean existence. And what did the Eloi pay for this service? They paid with their flesh. The cattle had become the ranchers. It was a perfect, stable, and utterly monstrous ecosystem. I, with my fire and my iron mace, was a foreign element, an ecological disruption.

The hours crawled by. The wind grew stronger, making the fire dance and gutter. I had to build a small wall of loose stones on the windward side to protect the precious flames. My supply of wood was dwindling at an alarming rate. I knew I had to conserve it, but every time the fire sank, the ring of eyes crept closer, and a wave of chittering, whispering noises would ripple through the darkness, a sound like dry leaves skittering over stone.

At what must have been the deepest point of the night, they made their move. It was not a charge, not a direct assault. They were more cunning than that. I saw a movement from the corner of my eye, a pale shape darting from one patch of shadow to another, just beyond the effective range of the fire. Then another. They were attempting to surround me, to find a weak point, a moment of inattention.

I stood up, the mace feeling heavy and solid in my hand. "Back!" I roared into the darkness, my voice swallowed by the wind. "Back, you foul things!"

My shout seemed to embolden them. The chittering grew louder, more excited. A clod of earth was thrown from the darkness, landing just short of the fire. Then another. They were trying to extinguish it. My heart hammered against my ribs. If the fire died, we were lost.

This was the moment. I reached into my satchel and my fingers closed around the block of camphor. It felt greasy and cool. I broke off a large chunk and tossed it into the heart of the fire. The effect was immediate and spectacular. The flames erupted with a brilliant, almost explosive flash of white light, roaring upwards with a loud whoosh. The camphor burned with an intense, clean incandescence, turning our hilltop into a stage lit by a magnesium flare.

In that sudden, dazzling light, I saw them clearly for the first time. There were dozens of them, crouched and waiting, their apelike bodies frozen in attitudes of shock. Their eyes, so accustomed to darkness, were blinded. They threw their hands up to their faces, squealing and shrieking in pain and confusion. The brilliant light had stripped them of their menace, revealing them for what they were: pathetic, etiolated creatures, their pallid skin looking sickly and thin.

But they were still dangerous. Before they could recover, I acted again. I grabbed the pouch of sulphur. Tearing it open, I flung a handful of the yellow powder into the fire. It ignited with a foul, blue flame, and a thick, choking cloud of suffocating smoke billowed outwards, carried on the wind directly into their ranks.

The effect was even more profound than the light. The Morlocks gasped and choked, their shrieks turning into hacking coughs. The smell was abominable, the very stench of brimstone and the pit. They broke. The coordinated probe dissolved into a panicked rout. They stumbled over each other, blinded by the light, their lungs filled with poison smoke. I saw them fleeing down the hillside, pale, frantic shapes swallowed by the darkness they so coveted.

I stood panting in the center of the foul smelling, brilliantly lit circle, brandishing the mace at the retreating shadows. I had won. I had held the line. The camphor burned out quickly, and the fire settled back to its normal state, though the acrid smell of sulphur hung in the air for a long time. The ring of eyes did not return.

I sank back to the ground, my body trembling with exhaustion and adrenaline. Weena stirred but did not wake. I gently stroked her hair, my hand still shaking. I had held back the night. But my supply of camphor was finite. My matches would not last forever. And the sun would set again tomorrow. The war was far from over. As the first faint hint of grey began to dilute the eastern sky, I looked out over the sleeping world and made a silent vow. I would get Weena to safety. And I would get to my Time Machine. I would go back. And then, somehow, this future would have to answer for itself.

The weariness in my bones was a profound ache, a testament to the night’s exertions. Each muscle fiber screamed in protest, and my mind, though sharpened by the recent terror, felt brittle and thin, like old parchment. The grey of the false dawn yielded slowly to a wash of the palest rose and gold. The world below began to resolve itself from a mass of indistinguishable shadows into the familiar, gentle landscape of green mounds and flowering trees. It was a scene of such placid beauty, so utterly at odds with the nocturnal horror it concealed, that a fresh wave of revulsion and despair washed over me. This paradise was a mausoleum. These gentle slopes were the hunting grounds for the bleached cannibals of the underworld.

I kept my vigil, the iron mace resting across my knees. The fire, no longer fed by my chemical accelerants, had dwindled to a bed of glowing embers, casting a faint, pulsing light upon Weena’s sleeping face. Her expression was one of perfect peace, a child’s untroubled slumber. One of her small hands was curled near her cheek, the other still clutched a wilted, pinkish flower she had picked the previous day. How could she sleep so soundly after the shrieks and the chaos? Did some merciful, evolutionary dullness protect the Eloi from the full comprehension of their fate? I hoped so. The thought of this tiny, affectionate creature living in a state of constant, lucid terror was more than I could bear.

My supplies. I laid them out on the dew slicked grass beside me. The satchel felt distressingly light. I had perhaps half of the block of camphor remaining. The pouch of sulphur was nearly empty; only a few pinches of the yellow dust remained. And my matches. I counted them with the grim precision of a condemned man counting his final hours. There were seventeen. Seventeen chances to bring forth the gift of Prometheus, to hold the darkness at bay. It was a terrifyingly small number. My only other weapon was the mace, a crude instrument of butchery that I loathed to touch, yet could not afford to abandon.

As the sun climbed higher, a glorious, unfiltered gold, the world came fully alive. The birdsong that I had found so charming on my first day now seemed like a hollow mockery. Other Eloi began to appear, emerging from the great ruined palace in the distance. They came in small, chattering groups, their colorful tunics like scattered petals on the green lawn. They moved with their usual languid grace, their faces empty of all but a simple, bovine contentment. A few of them drifted towards our hilltop, drawn perhaps by the lingering, strange smell or simply by idle curiosity. They looked at the remains of the fire, at me with my grim weapon, and at the sleeping Weena. Their expressions were blank. There was no gratitude, no understanding of the battle that had been fought for their sake, for all our sakes. They simply blinked their large, gentle eyes, offered me a flower or two which I declined, and then wandered away to splash in a nearby stream.

I was utterly alone in my understanding of this world. These beautiful, useless people were not allies; they were dependents, and I could not save them all. I could only save one.

I gently shook Weena’s shoulder. She stirred, her eyes fluttering open. When she saw me, her face broke into a smile of such pure, uncomplicated delight that my heart ached. She sat up, stretched her little arms, and chattered at me in her soft, lilting tongue, pointing towards the newly risen sun and the flowers sparkling with dew. She seemed to have no memory of the night’s terrors, no inkling of the danger that had crouched just beyond the firelight. For her, the world was reborn each morning, fresh and new and full of pleasant things.

I had to make her understand. I stood up and pointed, not towards the sun, but west, towards the distant grey green haze where I knew the Palace of Green Porcelain lay, and beyond that, the White Sphinx. I tried to communicate my intention through gesture. I made walking motions with my fingers, then pointed to her, then to myself, then again to the west. I pointed at the Sphinx in my mind's eye, trying to project the image of the Time Machine, of home, of escape.

Her response was to pick a fresh blossom and try to put it in my hair. I brushed her hand away, perhaps more roughly than I intended. Her face fell, her lower lip trembling. The look of hurt in her eyes was a physical blow. I knelt down and took her small hands in mine. I softened my expression, trying again, more gently. I pointed to the sun, then traced its arc across the sky with my hand, trying to show the coming of another night. I then made a low, guttural noise and pretended to be one of the Morlocks, crouching and shielding my eyes. Her face clouded with fear. She understood that, at least. She pressed closer to me, her small body trembling. Good. Fear was a tool, a necessary one for survival in this dreadful age.

Now, I pointed west again, with an expression of urgency. Walk. Go. There. I tried to convey safety, an end to the frightening dark things. I do not know how much she truly grasped of my strategic purpose, but she understood the essential part: that I, her new protector, wanted us to go in that direction. She nodded, her large eyes fixed on my face, full of a faith that was both my strength and my greatest burden.

We could not linger. I scattered the embers of the fire, grinding them into the damp earth with my boot until not a single spark remained. I gathered my meager supplies, securing the mace to my belt, a grim and constant reminder of the stakes. Weena took my hand, and together we began our descent from the hill. The other Eloi watched us go with a complete lack of interest, already absorbed in their timeless pastimes of eating fruit and weaving garlands. They were the living dead, and I was leaving their sun-drenched graveyard behind.

Our journey was a slow and arduous trek through a world of ghosts. We walked through groves of strange, beautiful trees and across meadows carpeted in alien flowers. But everywhere there were the ruins, the skeletal remains of a civilization so vast and so advanced that my own age seemed primitive by comparison. We passed under the shattered arches of colossal aqueducts, their surfaces now furry with moss. We trod on roads paved with a milky, seamless material, now cracked and heaved by the roots of ancient trees. I saw buildings that must have once scraped the clouds, now reduced to jagged stumps of metal and fused stone, draped in flowering vines as if nature were trying to gently efface the memory of failure.

It was all so quiet. There was no hum of machinery, no roar of traffic, no sound of industry. Only the sighing of the wind, the twittering of birds, and the soft patter of our own footsteps. It was the profound silence of a world that had talked itself to death, that had achieved everything and then, with nothing left to strive for, had simply given up. The Eloi were the result of that cosmic ennui, the pampered heirs who had forgotten how their inheritance was earned. And the Morlocks? The Morlocks were the bill, come due in the darkness.

Weena was a constant, if demanding, companion. Her attention span was that of a small child. She would dart away to chase a brightly colored butterfly, or stop to marvel at a particular flower, or insist we rest by a fountain to splash our feet in the cool water. I had to continually coax her, urge her onward, and keep a vigilant eye on our surroundings. Every shadowed ruin, every dark thicket of trees, seemed to me a potential ambush point. My nerves were stretched taut. The sun, my ally, was also my clock, and its slow march across the sky was a constant source of anxiety.

By midday, we came to the edge of a great forest. It was not like the open, park-like groves we had passed through. This was a dense, ancient wood, a place where the canopy was so thick that the ground below was cast in a perpetual twilight. The trees were enormous, their trunks thick as columns, their leaves a dark, somber green. A chill emanated from the tree line, a damp, earthy smell that spoke of shade and decay. My instincts screamed at me not to enter. This was their territory. In this gloom, the Morlocks would not need to wait for nightfall.

But the forest stretched for miles in either direction, an impassable barrier. To go around it would take days, days I did not have. Seventeen matches. The number echoed in my head. I had to go through.

I checked that the mace was loose at my belt. I held Weena’s hand tightly. "Stay close," I said, the words a useless comfort to her, but a necessary command to myself. She looked up at the towering trees, her happy disposition finally faltering. She whimpered and pressed against my leg, her fear a palpable thing.

We plunged into the gloom. The transition from the bright, open sunlight to the deep shade was instantaneous and disorienting. My eyes struggled to adjust. The air was heavy and still, and the cheerful sounds of the open world were replaced by a profound, listening silence. The ground was a soft carpet of moss and fallen leaves, which muffled our footsteps, making our passage unnervingly quiet.

For a time, nothing happened. We walked deeper and deeper into the forest, the last vestiges of the sunlit world vanishing behind us. The only light came from occasional shafts that pierced the canopy, illuminating small patches of the forest floor in an almost theatrical way. I found myself moving from one pool of light to the next, as if they were stepping stones of safety in a river of darkness. Weena’s fear had subsided into a state of wide eyed apprehension. She did not speak or chatter, but simply clung to my hand, her trust in me absolute.

It was in one of the darkest stretches, between the pools of light, that they came.

There was no sound, no warning. Just a sudden, swift movement in the periphery of my vision. A pale shape lunged from behind a thick tree trunk. I reacted without thinking, shoving Weena behind me and swinging the mace in a horizontal arc. There was a sickening, wet crunch as the iron head connected with something solid. A high pitched shriek of pain cut through the silence, and the shape recoiled back into the shadows.

But it was not alone. From the deeper darkness, I saw them: the pale, luminous eyes. Half a dozen pairs, maybe more. They were surrounding us, using the gloom as their shield. They were more cautious now, but no less menacing. I could hear their soft, padding footsteps and a low, chittering sound, the sound of predators closing in.

Weena was sobbing with terror behind me. I backed away slowly, pulling her with me, keeping my face towards the watching eyes. My back hit the rough bark of a massive tree. I was cornered. They knew it. One of them made a darting feint, and I swung the mace again, but it met only empty air. They were toying with me, testing my reach, my resolve.

My matches. I fumbled in my satchel with my free hand, my fingers shaking so badly I could barely grasp the box. I pulled it out, the sound of the little wooden sticks rattling inside seeming impossibly loud in the stillness. The eyes watched my every move. They knew what fire was. They were waiting for me to fail.

My first attempt was a disaster. My trembling thumb scraped the match head against the striking surface, but it only sputtered and died. A low, guttural sound, almost a laugh, came from the darkness. Panic, cold and sharp, lanced through me. I took a deep breath, forcing my hand to steady. I had to make the next one count.

I selected a single match. I held the box firmly. I looked straight at the nearest pair of eyes, which glowed with a malevolent curiosity. I struck the match. It flared to life with a brilliant, tiny sun.

In its brief, precious light, I saw them clearly. Three of them, crouched barely twenty feet away. Their faces were contorted in a mixture of fear and hatred. The light hurt their sensitive eyes; they flinched and partly turned away. It was the opening I needed. I did not have camphor to create a great flare, but I had dry leaves. The ground was covered in them.

Still holding the lit match, I dropped to one knee, shielding the tiny flame with my body. With my other hand, I scooped up a double handful of the driest leaves and twigs I could find at the base of the tree. I thrust the match into the pile. For a heart stopping second, nothing happened. Then a tiny curl of smoke appeared, followed by a small, hesitant lick of flame.

"More," I muttered to myself, my voice a ragged croak. I piled more leaves on, fanning the nascent fire with my hand. The Morlocks watched, hesitating. The fire was growing, catching on the dry tinder with a satisfying crackle. The flames grew brighter, pushing back the oppressive gloom. The circle of eyes retreated further.

The fire was our shield. It cast a flickering, protective circle of light around the base of the great tree. The Morlocks would not cross it. I stood up, the mace in one hand, feeding the fire with the other. The immediate danger had passed, but we were trapped. We were besieged in a tiny fortress of light, deep inside enemy territory.

The firelight danced on Weena’s tear stained face. She had stopped crying and was now watching the flames with a kind of mesmerized wonder. For her, it was a beautiful, dancing thing. For me, it was a prison. Every branch I threw onto the pile was one less piece of fuel between us and the patient, waiting dark. The sun was continuing its journey across the sky, but in the depths of the forest, it made no difference. Here, it was always twilight, and the monsters were always near.

We waited. The hours crawled by. The Morlocks did not leave. I could see them moving at the edge of the firelight, pale, fleeting shapes. They were content to wait us out. They knew the fire could not last forever. My pile of gathered fuel was finite, and I dared not venture out of the circle to gather more.

My mind raced. I could not stay here. We would be doomed when the fire died. I had to make a break for it. But how? The only path was forward, through them. I looked at the fire, then at the pouch of sulphur at my belt. I had a few pinches left. Not enough for a great cloud like on the hill, but perhaps enough for a diversion.

It was a desperate gamble. I explained none of it to Weena. I simply pulled her close and pointed through the trees, in the direction I hoped was west. "We run," I said, trying to fill my voice with a confidence I did not feel. "When I say, we run."

I took the last of the sulphur powder and held it ready. I scanned the woods, trying to find the thinnest point in their cordon. They seemed more clustered to the left of us. The path to the right looked slightly clearer. That would be our route.

I fed the fire with the last of my nearby fuel, making it roar up as high as I could. Then, with a prayer to whatever gods might still be listening in this dead world, I threw the sulphur into the heart of the blaze.

The effect was a pathetic echo of the previous night’s triumph, but it was enough. A small, noxious puff of blue flame and choking smoke billowed out, not in a great cloud, but in a directed plume. Fortune was with me; a slight draft carried it towards the left, where the Morlocks were thickest. There were immediate howls of consternation and disgust. For a moment, their attention was completely diverted.

"Now!" I screamed, grabbing Weena’s arm and pulling her with me. We plunged out of the circle of light and into the gloom on the right. We ran, stumbling over roots, crashing through unseen undergrowth. Behind us, the shrieks of the Morlocks turned from confusion to rage as they realized our ruse. The hunt was on.

I did not dare to look back. I could hear them coming, their padding footsteps surprisingly swift. I ran with the desperation of a man who sees the gallows being built outside his cell window. My lungs burned. Branches whipped at my face. Weena was light, but dragging her along was a terrible strain. She ran with all her might, her little legs pumping, her breath coming in ragged gasps.

The gloom seemed to lighten ahead. Was it another clearing, or was it the edge of the forest? Hope, fierce and desperate, gave me a new burst of strength. "Almost there!" I gasped.

We burst out of the trees and into the full, glorious light of the late afternoon sun. I blinked, momentarily dazzled. We were free. I turned, the mace held ready, expecting to see the pale horde spill out of the forest behind us. But the tree line was empty. They would not follow us into the direct sunlight. We had escaped.

Panting, gasping for breath, we collapsed onto the soft grass. Weena was trembling violently, but she was unharmed. I lay on my back, my chest heaving, and stared up at the beautiful, blue sky. I had spent two more matches. My sulphur was gone. But we were alive.

When I had recovered enough to sit up, I looked out across the landscape. And there it was. Not two miles distant, rising from a gentle slope, was the White Sphinx. It stood upon its pedestal of bronze, impassive and ancient, its chipped, stone face staring out over the world with an unreadable expression. The sun glinted off its worn surfaces. Inside its base, I knew, were the bronze doors. And behind those doors, my machine. My escape. My way home.

A wave of triumph, so pure and potent it was almost painful, surged through me. I had done it. I had crossed the wilderness. I had protected Weena. My goal was in sight.

But as I looked at the Sphinx, a cold dread began to temper my elation. The bronze doors had been closed and locked to me. The Morlocks had taken my machine, dragged it inside their dark realm. They had set the trap. And now, I was walking right into it. The journey was not over. The final battle was yet to come. And it would be fought on their terms, at the very threshold of their world. As the sun began to dip towards the horizon, casting long shadows that reached like grasping fingers from the east, I knew the next night would be the last. One way or another.

I lifted Weena to her feet. She leaned against me, her small body still quivering with the aftershocks of terror. I smoothed her tangled hair, my own hands unsteady. The sun was a great, bleeding wound on the western horizon, and the sky around it was stained with hues of orange and violet. It was a spectacle of profound and terrible beauty. Each moment the sun lingered above the hills was another moment of reprieve, another second of precious light. But I knew it was a countdown. The shadows, once distinct and sharp, were beginning to soften and merge, bleeding into a universal twilight. The Morlocks were creatures of the dark. They had been baulked, but they had not been defeated. They would be waiting.

"We must go, Weena," I said, my voice hoarse. "To the tall stone house."

She looked from my face to the distant Sphinx, her brow furrowed in a simple, childlike confusion. She could not comprehend the terrible significance of that structure, nor the desperate gamble we were about to undertake. To her, it was simply another of the many ancient and inexplicable features of her world. But she trusted me. She slipped her small hand into mine, her fingers curling around my own with a confidence that was both my strength and my greatest fear. Her trust was a weight, a responsibility more profound than the mere preservation of my own life.

We set off across the rolling turf. The journey was a torment of a different kind from our flight through the forest. Here, there was no immediate, tangible threat, no snapping branches or shrieking pursuers. There was only the vast, silent emptiness of the landscape and the crushing weight of my own thoughts. With every step, the White Sphinx grew larger, its enigmatic face seeming to watch our approach. It was a silent sentinel, guardian of the underworld. I felt like an insect crawling towards a spider’s web, fully aware of the trap, yet compelled to proceed by a force greater than my own fear. What choice did I have? To remain in the open was to invite certain death when night fell. The forest was no safer. The only path forward, the only path home, lay through the heart of the Morlocks’ domain.

My mind, accustomed to grappling with problems of mechanics and physics, worked feverishly. I replayed the events of the past days, searching for any detail, any observation I might use to my advantage. Fire. Fire was my only true weapon against them, and I had no means of making it. My matches were spent, my sulphur a memory of acrid smoke. My only tool was the iron mace I had wrenied from some forgotten museum piece. It had served me well as a club, a blunt instrument of destruction. But against a horde, it was a pitiful defence. I would be overwhelmed in moments.

I needed a plan. Not a desperate scramble, but a calculated strategy. The Morlocks had dragged my Time Machine into the pedestal. They had closed the bronze doors. Why? Not simply to hide it. They could have destroyed it easily enough. No, they had understood, in their own dim, bestial way, that the machine was important to me. They had used it as bait. They expected me to come for it. They would be waiting for me, clustered in the dark, ready to spring their trap the moment I breached their fortress.

And that was the key. They would wait. They would not expect an immediate, aggressive assault. Theirs was the cunning of the predator, the patient ambush. They would expect me to fumble at the doors, to be cornered, to be terrified. But what if I did not play the part of the cornered prey? What if the prey attacked?

The sun had now vanished completely, leaving only a fading, greenish glow in the west. A few early stars pricked the deepening purple of the sky. The air grew cooler. I could feel the subtle change in the world, the shift from the domain of the Eloi to that of the Morlocks. A low, humming sound began to emanate from the ground, the same thrumming of hidden machinery I had noticed before. It was the world breathing, the lungs of the underworld beginning their nightly work. Weena huddled closer to me, her eyes wide as she scanned the darkening landscape. She too could sense the change.

We reached the base of the slope upon which the Sphinx stood. From here, it was colossal, looming over us like a mountain. Its weathered limestone face was a mask of utter indifference, its stone eyes having watched civilizations rise and crumble. I felt a tremor of pure, intellectual dread. I was a man of science, a product of an age of reason and progress, and I was about to engage in a primitive, brutal struggle for survival at the foot of an icon from a dead world. The irony was not lost on me.

We reached the pedestal. The great bronze doors were exactly as I remembered them, vast and imposing, decorated with bas reliefs that had been worn smooth by the passage of countless ages. I ran my hand over the cool metal. They were seamless, shut tight. There was no handle, no knob, no visible lock. How had the Morlocks opened them? Did they possess some key, some hidden mechanism? Or did the doors simply yield to them?

I placed my ear against the bronze. The humming was louder here, a deep, resonant vibration that I could feel in the bones of my skull. And beneath it, I could just make out other sounds. A faint, wet scraping. A low chittering, like insects in a nest. They were in there. Waiting.

"Weena," I whispered, turning to her. I knelt down so I was at her level. The twilight was thick now, and her face was a pale oval in the gloom. "I am going into the house. It will be very dark. You must stay right with me. Do not let go of my hand, not for anything. Do you understand?"

She nodded, her eyes vast and solemn. She placed her other hand on my shoulder, a gesture of comfort that tore at my heart. She had put her entire, fragile existence in my care.

And then, my plan, born of desperation and the dimmest flicker of scientific insight, solidified. It was a terrible risk, a madman’s gambit, but it was all I had. I looked at the mace in my hand. It was heavy, a solid bar of iron. I looked at the seam where the two great bronze doors met. The fit was tight, but it was not perfect. Millennia of weather and shifting foundations had left the tiniest of gaps.

I had no key. But I had a lever.

"Stand back," I told Weena, positioning her behind me, against the relative safety of the pedestal's outer wall.

I took the mace and jammed its narrow, flattened end into the thin crack between the doors. It was a poor fit. I had to angle it, working it back and forth, the grating sound of iron on bronze setting my teeth on edge. Every scrape and screech felt like a trumpet blast, announcing my presence to the creatures within. I expected them to burst out at any moment. But nothing happened. The chittering from within ceased, replaced by an expectant silence that was somehow more terrifying.

Finally, I managed to wedge the end of the bar a good inch into the seam. Now came the true test. Using the edge of the opposing door as a fulcrum, I put my entire weight on the mace. I heaved, my muscles screaming in protest. The bar bent slightly, the iron groaning under the strain. For a moment, nothing happened. The doors were immovable, a solid wall of metal. Despair, cold and sharp, pricked at me.

Then, I heard it. A deep, protesting groan, not from my mace, but from the doors themselves. A shudder ran through the bronze. I leaned into it again, my feet scrambling for purchase on the smooth ground. Sweat poured into my eyes, stinging them. "Move," I grunted, a prayer and a command.

With a shriek of tortured metal, the door shifted. It was only a fraction of an inch, but it was enough. A sliver of profound blackness opened up, and a gust of foul, subterranean air washed over me, thick with the smell of raw meat and human decay. From within that sliver of darkness, I saw a pair of luminous, greyish eyes blink.

The sight gave me a surge of manic energy. I pulled the mace free and jammed it deeper into the now wider gap. I heaved again, and then again. The process was agonizingly slow. The gap would widen to a few inches, and I would have to reset my lever, gaining a painful new purchase. Each time, the screech of metal echoed in the unnerving stillness. The eyes in the darkness were joined by others. I could see them now, pale, shuffling shapes gathering just beyond the opening, their curiosity warring with their instinctual fear of the noise and the light of the open sky.

The gap was perhaps a foot wide now. Wide enough to slip through. It was time. The final part of my plan was the most dangerous. I had to abandon my lever.

"Now, Weena! Hold on to me!" I shouted.

I grabbed her hand, pulled her close to my side, and turned, presenting my back to the opening. The mace I held ready in my right hand. I took a deep breath of the clean night air, perhaps the last I would ever breathe, and plunged sideways through the gap into the utter, suffocating blackness.

The transition was instantaneous and absolute. The cool twilight was gone, replaced by a warm, cloying darkness that was a physical presence. The stench was overwhelming. The air was thick and difficult to breathe. And the sound. The sound was a living thing, a chorus of clicks and whimpers and wet, shuffling noises that surrounded us.

For a second that stretched into an eternity, nothing happened. I think our sudden, aggressive entry surprised them. Then, a shriek of rage and hunger cut through the air, and the darkness erupted.

I did not wait. I knew where my Time Machine had to be, in the centre of this chamber. Dragging Weena with me, I lunged forward into the unseen. My foot struck something soft and yielding, and a creature squealed and fell away. I swung the mace in a wide, horizontal arc, clearing a path before me. I felt it connect with something, a sickening, wet crunch followed by a shriek that was cut short.

"This way!" I yelled, more to myself than to Weena.

I could feel them grabbing at me, their soft, cold hands plucking at my clothes, at my hair. One of them fastened onto my leg, and I kicked back viciously, shaking it free. Weena screamed, a thin, terrified sound beside me. I tightened my grip on her hand until my knuckles were white. I could not lose her.

My eyes, adjusting to the gloom, could just make out shapes. The chamber was larger than I had imagined, a vast, circular hall. And there, in the middle of it, was a form I would recognize anywhere. The faint quartz bars, the brass and ivory framework, the saddle. My Time Machine. It was twenty yards away. It might as well have been a mile.

The Morlocks were swarming now, a pale, writhing tide of bodies. They were no longer cautious. They threw themselves at me, their weight threatening to bear me down. I was a wild animal, cornered and fighting for my life. I swung the mace relentlessly, a machine of destruction. I felt bones break, heard the wet thuds as my blows landed. I was covered in something warm and slick. But for every one I struck down, two more seemed to take its place. They were pulling at Weena now, their small, strong hands grabbing at her dress, her legs.

"No!" I roared, a sound of pure fury. I spun, sweeping the mace in a low arc that cleared the creatures around her feet. We gained a precious few yards. We were halfway there.

I could see the machine clearly now. It seemed to glow with a faint luminescence of its own, a beacon of hope in this hellish place. But the Morlocks were growing bolder. They were learning. They stopped throwing themselves directly at the mace and began to swarm my legs, trying to trip me, to drag me down. A heavy weight landed on my back, and I felt sharp teeth sink into my shoulder. I bellowed in pain and rage, twisting and slamming my back against one of the pillars that dotted the chamber. The creature fell away with a whimper.

We were at the base of the machine. I scrambled onto the low platform, never releasing Weena’s hand. The Morlocks surged up after us, a wave of pale flesh and luminous eyes. I pushed Weena behind me, towards the saddle.

"Get on!" I screamed. "Get on the seat!"

I stood my ground on the edge of the machine, my final redoubt. I was a berserker. My civilized mind was gone, replaced by a primal instinct to kill and to protect. The mace was a blur of motion. The floor around the machine was littered with the twitching, broken bodies of my attackers. But still they came. They climbed over their own dead, their faces devoid of fear, only a blank, ravenous hunger.

I felt a hand grasp Weena’s. I turned. She was on the saddle, but a Morlock had clambered up the side of the machine and was pulling her, its fingers tangled in her hair. I lunged, smashing its skull with the butt of the mace. But another was there, and another. They had her foot.

I reached for her, my left hand grabbing her arm while my right swung the mace. "Hold on, Weena!"

Her eyes met mine, wide with terror. She was trying to hold on to the machine, but they were pulling her, dragging her off the saddle. I pulled back, a desperate tug of war. For a moment, I thought I had her. But a dozen more hands joined the fray, grabbing her legs, her waist. Her small body was ripped from my grasp.

I heard her cry my name. A small, despairing sound that was swallowed by the chittering horde. I saw her disappear into the roiling mass of pale bodies, a flash of white dress, and then she was gone.

Something inside me broke. A roar of anguish and fury tore itself from my throat. I was about to leap in after her, to sell my life dearly in a final, pointless slaughter. But then I saw it. The little bouquet of flowers she had picked for me earlier, the ones she had placed in my pocket, had fallen onto the control panel of the machine. Two small, withered white flowers. A final, heartbreaking token of her affection.

That fragile, pathetic memory of gentleness in this place of utter savagery shocked me back to my senses. To die here would be a betrayal of her. My only purpose now was to escape. To bear witness. To tell the world what I had seen.

With a final, desperate sob, I threw myself into the saddle. My hands found the controls by instinct. I did not look for the lever that controlled the pace. I grabbed the main lever, the one that sent me hurtling through time. I slammed it forward.

There was the familiar sensation of falling, the sickening lurch. The chamber, the writhing Morlocks, the spot where Weena had vanished, all of it dissolved into a grey, indistinct blur. The darkness of the pedestal gave way to the frantic, strobing flicker of days and nights. The sounds of the underworld faded, replaced by the silent, humming rush of temporal acceleration. I was moving. I was free.

But as I hurtled into the future, away from the blood and the darkness, I clutched the iron mace in my hand and wept. I had won. I had escaped. And I had never been more utterly and completely alone.

The dials spun with a dizzying speed, their numbers a meaningless blur. The centuries flickered past my eyes, each day and night a pulse of light and dark so rapid they merged into a continuous, nauseating grey. My body was a dead weight in the saddle, my knuckles white where they gripped the cold, unforgiving metal of the mace. Tears streamed down my face, not hot with immediate grief but cold with the chilling finality of my loss. They were frozen by the wind of my passage, a wind that howled a song of utter solitude. Weena. Her name was a silent scream in my mind, a wound that would not close. I saw her face in the swirling chaos of time, her wide, trusting eyes, the simple delight she took in a flower, the warmth of her small hand in mine. And then I saw her again, swallowed by that pale, heaving sea of Morlocks. The image was branded onto my soul.

I do not know how long I travelled in that state of abject misery. The initial, blind panic of escape gave way to a profound and hollow ache. The scientist in me, the observer, was dead. Only the man remained, and the man was broken. I had no destination. I was simply running. Fleeing a single, unbearable moment, trying to put as much of time as I could between myself and the sound of her last cry. The machine hummed its incessant, monotonous tune, the only sound in my universe besides the frantic beating of my own heart. The world outside was a featureless stream. I was a ghost, haunting the corridors of eternity.

Slowly, almost against my will, a sliver of my old self began to stir. It was not curiosity, not yet. It was a morbid need to see the consequences, to watch the world that had taken her from me decay and die. With a trembling hand, I reached for the lever that controlled the pace of my journey. I did not stop, not completely, but I eased the pressure, slowing the headlong rush to a more deliberate, observable flow. The strobing of days and nights slowed to a visible rhythm. I was moving at a rate of perhaps a year per second. The great hall of the grey stone palace, visible from my pedestal, began to show the signs of age. Cracks spiderwebbed across its facade. The sphinx, that enigmatic gatekeeper, grew weathered, its features blurred by the ceaseless caress of wind and rain. The verdant world of the Eloi began to change. The lush greenery thinned. The fruit-bearing trees withered.

I watched for what must have been centuries in the span of a few minutes. I saw the Eloi, those beautiful, useless children, dwindle. Their numbers lessened. They seemed to grow more frail, their dances less frequent, their laughter a rarer and more fragile sound. I felt no pity for them, only a cold and bitter contempt. They were the cattle, and the butchers were rising. I saw the wells, the dark portals to the underworld, become more numerous. The Morlocks, it seemed, were growing bolder, their population booming in the darkness. On certain nights, which grew more frequent as the ages passed, I could see them swarming the surface in greater numbers, not just as furtive hunters but as conquerors. The Eloi were no longer just prey. They were being eradicated. The last of the surface dwellers vanished. The gardens became a wilderness of strange, scrubby vegetation. The palaces crumbled into picturesque ruins, and then into mounds of rubble, and finally, into dust. The Morlocks had inherited the Earth.

My morbid fascination held me captive. I pushed the lever forward again, accelerating into a deeper future. Now the sun itself was changing. It grew larger in the sky, its brilliant white light shifting to a sullen, orange glow. The moon vanished, or perhaps it had simply receded into the blackness of space. The frantic pulse of day and night slowed, became sluggish. The Earth's rotation was dying. The days grew longer, then immensely long. The sun would hang in the sky for what seemed an eternity, baking the barren landscape, before sinking with ponderous slowness, giving way to a night of equal and terrible length. The sky was no longer a brilliant blue but a deep, bruised purple, and the stars shone with a fierce, unwavering light, even in the twilight hours.

The very shape of the land was changing. Mountains eroded into hills. Seas dried up, leaving vast, salt-caked plains. New oceans formed in sunken basins. It was a silent, geological ballet of immense and terrifying scale. And still I flew onward, drawn by some dreadful compulsion to see the very end of things. The sun swelled further, becoming a vast, crimson disc that filled a significant portion of the sky. Its light was no longer warm but cast a weak, ruddy illumination over a world that was growing colder. The air, I suspected, must be growing thin. The last vestiges of green had long since vanished from the land, replaced by rock and a curious, lichen-like growth of a deep, blood red colour.

I decided, quite suddenly, that I must stop. To see it all rush past was one thing. To stand upon this dying world, to breathe its thin air and feel its moribund chill, was another. It was a penance, perhaps. A way to confront the ultimate loneliness I felt inside by finding its physical manifestation. My grief for Weena had cooled into a hard, dense object in my chest, a permanent part of my own internal geography. The desire to bear witness was now my only guide.

I chose my moment when the great red sun was setting, casting long, spectral shadows across a silent, alien coastline. I eased back on the stopping lever, gently, carefully. The machine bucked and groaned. The blur of the landscape resolved itself into a desolate beach of black sand. The humming ceased, replaced by a silence so profound it was like a pressure on my eardrums. I had arrived. The date on the dials was a staggering number, more than thirty million years from my own time.

I sat there for a long time, the mace still resting across my lap. The air was thin and bitingly cold. I took a hesitant breath, and then another. It was breathable, but there was a metallic tang to it, and I felt a lightness in my head. The sea before me was not the blue or grey I knew. It was a sluggish, oily green, and it lay almost perfectly still, with no tide to speak of, only a slow, almost imperceptible swell that lapped at the shore with a whisper. The sky was a dark violet, and the stars were hard, diamond points of light. The immense, dull red globe of the sun was half sunk below the horizon, and it did not blaze but seemed to glow with the sullen heat of a dying ember. The only vegetation was the reddish, weed-like growth I had seen from above, which clung to the black rocks that dotted the beach. There was no birdsong, no insect hum, no sound of life at all. The silence was the silence of a tomb.

I finally dismounted, my legs stiff and unsteady. The black sand crunched under my boots. I held the mace tightly, a useless comfort in this dead world. My eyes scanned the shore, the rocks, the still, green water. My heart pounded with a mixture of terror and a strange, scientific exaltation. I was here. The first man, and perhaps the last, to witness the senescence of our world. I walked slowly along the water's edge. The silence was unnerving. Every crunch of my boots seemed like a sacrilege. I looked back at the Time Machine, a strange, skeletal framework of nickel and ivory and brass, an impossible artifact on this shore at the end of time. It was my only link to a world of life, of warmth, of Weena.

As I watched, I saw something move. At first I thought it was a trick of the dim, red light. But then I saw it again. A large, dark shape, scuttling out of the water and onto the black sand. I froze, my hand tightening on the mace. It was a crab. But it was a monstrous thing, larger than any I had ever seen, perhaps the size of a small table. Its shell was a mottled, greenish-black, and it had a multitude of legs that carried it sideways with a dreadful, jerky speed. Its great claws were held aloft, and its stalked eyes seemed to glow with a faint, phosphorescent light. It paid me no mind, instead beginning to scavenge along the tideline, probing the red weed with its claws.

Then another appeared. And another. Soon the beach was crawling with them, a host of enormous, armoured creatures moving with a silent, purposeful industry. This was the dominant form of life, it seemed. These ghastly crustaceans, heirs to the planet. I watched them, repulsed but fascinated. They were the ultimate survivors, thriving in a world that had rejected warmth and complexity. As I stood there, a new horror revealed itself. Drifting in the still, green water were pale, globular shapes, some of them a yard in diameter. They looked like immense, spectral jellyfish. But as one floated closer to the shore, I saw it was something else entirely. It was a creature with a large, convoluted body and long, trailing tentacles. And then I saw something that made my blood run cold. Another of the giant crabs, which had waded into the shallows, was seized by one of these tentacles. The crab struggled, its great claws snapping uselessly, but it was drawn inexorably towards the floating mass and then, horribly, appeared to be absorbed by it.

A profound sense of dread and disgust washed over me. This was the end point of evolution's grand experiment on Earth. A desolate shore where giant crabs were devoured by floating horrors under the light of a dying sun. There was no intelligence here, no art, no love. There was only a grim, mechanical cycle of consumption. This was not a world for man. It was an affirmation of my utter solitude. I was an anachronism, a ghost from a time of sunlight and laughter.

A sound broke the silence. It was not the scuttling of the crabs. It was a flapping, leathery noise. I looked up towards the darkening sky. Something was descending. It was huge and white, like a colossal moth or a bat. It circled once, its vast wings beating the thin air with a sound like wet canvas, and then it landed on a tall black rock not fifty yards from me. I could see it more clearly now in the deepening twilight. It was a startling, horrifying creature. It had a soft, white, fur-like covering, and its wings were leathery and vast. But it was its head that held my attention. It had no visible eyes, only two dark pits where eyes should have been. And below them was a beak-like mouth, surrounded by a cluster of writhing, pink tentacles.

It turned its sightless face towards me. I do not know how it sensed my presence, whether by some subtle scent on the dead air or by a sense of vibration, but I knew with absolute certainty that it was aware of me. The tentacles around its beak twitched and writhed. It let out a low, plaintiff cry, a sound that was utterly alien, a bleating, mournful note that echoed across the dead beach. I stood my ground, gripping the iron mace until my knuckles ached. The thing was a nightmare given form, a creature born of the planet's final, senile delirium. It seemed to be considering me, a strange, warm-blooded anomaly in its cold world.

My grief for Weena, which had been a constant, heavy presence, was suddenly joined by a sharp, immediate terror. I had sought the ultimate desolation, and I had found it. But I had no desire to become a part of it, to end my days as a meal for some ghastly, night-flying horror. My purpose, the one I had forged in the fire of my loss, surged back to the forefront of my mind. To bear witness. To tell the story. I could not do that if I died here, thirty million years from anyone who could understand. My testimony was for my own time, a warning of the two paths of devolution that led, ultimately, to this. The gentle, useless beauty and the savage, subterranean industry, both ending here, on this barren shore of monsters.

The great white creature on the rock shifted its weight. It spread its wings slightly, as if preparing for flight, or for an attack. That was my signal. I did not run. I backed away slowly, deliberately, my eyes locked on the thing. My boots made no sound on the soft sand. Step by step, I retreated towards the gleaming framework of the Time Machine. The creature watched my retreat, its blind head tracking my every movement. The crabs continued their silent scavenging, oblivious. The world was holding its breath.

I reached the machine. Without taking my eyes off the winged horror, I fumbled behind me, found the saddle, and clambered aboard. My hands found the levers by touch. My fingers were numb with cold and fear. The creature let out its bleating cry again, louder this time, a note of frustrated hunger. I spared one last glance for this terrible, final world. The dim red sun had almost vanished, and the black beach was awash in the eerie light of the stars and the phosphorescence of the monstrous crabs. It was the last page of a book I never wanted to read.

I had seen enough. My purpose was clear. My journey was not into the future, but back to the past. Back to my own time. I had to tell them. I had to warn them. I had to explain how our triumphs of civilization, our social stratifications, our turning away from the harsh realities of nature, could lead to the Eloi and the Morlocks, and how that, in turn, could lead to this. This utter end.

I grabbed the main lever. I did not ease it gently. I thrust it backward with all my strength. There was the familiar, sickening lurch, the sense of falling upward. The desolate beach, the still green sea, the monstrous white bat-creature, all of it dissolved into a grey, indistinct blur. The stars streaked across the sky. The sluggish red sun began to move again, faster and faster, rising and setting in a frantic pulse. It grew smaller, its colour shifting from red to orange, to yellow, and then to a brilliant, familiar white. The Earth’s rotation sped up. The sky turned blue again. The dead world fell away behind me.

I was hurtling back towards my own time. Back towards Victorian London, with its gas lamps and its horse-drawn cabs, its poverty and its progress. I was returning as a prophet, a messenger from a future no one could imagine. In my pocket, I could feel the faint, dry outline of Weena’s withered flowers. They were a token not just of her affection, but of my failure. And they were the fuel for my resolve. I wept again, but my tears were different now. They were not the tears of hopeless grief, but of a terrible, newfound purpose. I had seen the end of humanity. And I was going home to tell the tale.
