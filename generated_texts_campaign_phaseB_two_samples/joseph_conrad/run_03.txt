Finch would not hear of it. The map, a document of dubious provenance sketched on oilskin, suggested a pass through the escarpment another two leagues ahead, but Finch, with the air of a man consulting a holy text, declared the approaching twilight uncooperative. He was a man for whom the world was a series of scheduled appointments, and the setting of the sun was an infuriating, if predictable, breach of protocol. To attempt the crossing in the dark, he argued, would be to invite disaster; a broken axle, a lame mule, a sprained ankle, any of which would catastrophically derail his timetable. It was all quite logical, I suppose, if one ignored the fact that our water was low and that another night in this kiln of a valley was a far more tangible threat than any of his imagined misfortunes. I felt a hot surge of impatience, a pointless anger that immediately soured into weary resignation. What was one more delay, after all, in a journey composed entirely of delays?

We made our camp in the lee of a great sandstone monolith, a red sentinel that had watched the slow erosion of millennia. The heat of the day did not retreat but rather settled into the rock, radiating back at us as the sky turned a deep, bruised purple. The air grew still, so still that the flame of our single lantern burned without a flicker, a perfect, unwavering teardrop of light. A profound silence descended, a silence that was not empty but full, weighted, as though it were the accumulated quiet of ages. It pressed upon the ears, making you doubt the very mechanism of hearing. I saw Sutton, the expedition’s geologist, tap his own temple as if to clear a blockage. Then, quite suddenly, the night was upon us, a black velvet cloth thrown over the world, and we were blind beyond the small circle of our fire.

Sometime after midnight, a sound began. It was not a sound one could easily place; it was a low, guttural vibration that seemed to rise from the ground itself, a deep thrumming that you felt in your teeth and in the marrow of your bones. Sutton scrambled upright, his eyes wide in the firelight. ‘An earthquake?’ he stammered, his scientific curiosity utterly overwhelmed by pure animal fear. Finch, who had been asleep with his mouth open, snorted awake and sat up, looking offended. ‘Unscheduled,’ he muttered, glaring into the darkness as if it were a subordinate in need of discipline. The sound continued, a rhythmic, pulsing hum, like some vast, subterranean heart had begun to beat. It was a sound that had no place in the known world, and the sheer alien quality of it turned my blood to ice water.

The porters, the half dozen local men we had hired in the last wretched outpost of civilisation, showed no such alarm. They had been murmuring amongst themselves, but at the onset of the sound they fell silent, their faces impassive, their dark eyes reflecting the fire. They were not afraid; they were listening. Their leader, a tall, sinewy man named Kaelen whose age was impossible to guess, rose and walked to the edge of the firelight. He stood there for a long moment, his head cocked, a silhouette against the immense, humming dark. ‘Well?’ I called out to him, my voice sounding thin and absurd. ‘What is it?’

He turned, not to me, but to face the whole of our small, pale group. ‘The rock sings,’ he said, and his voice was as dry as the dust under our boots. ‘It sings for the old ones.’ Finch made a noise of contemptuous disbelief. ‘Nonsense. It’s a geological phenomenon. Sub-sonic frequencies, probably.’ Kaelen’s lips parted in something that was not quite a smile. It was a baring of teeth, ancient and predatory. ‘The rock is hungry,’ he said, and then he simply sat down again, pulling his blanket around his shoulders, the matter apparently settled. A great and terrible stillness fell over our camp, a stillness made all the more awful by the ceaseless, monstrous humming that now seemed to fill the universe. We sat there, the three of us, Finch, Sutton, and I, rigid with a nameless dread, obstinately listening to a silence that was not there.

I found myself looking at Finch and Sutton, at their soft, city-dweller’s flesh, and thinking what Kaelen had really meant. Hunger was a simple word, but here, in this place, it seemed to have a thousand terrible meanings. It occurred to me then that our porters must be thirsty. We had given them a ration, of course, a carefully measured quantity stipulated in their contracts, a contract which I am certain none of them could read. Their chief provision was a bag of dried, leathery dates and a small, stinking goatskin of their own, which must have been nearly empty. For weeks we had watched them chew on woody roots they dug from the ground, their magnificent health slowly eroding, the shine of their skin dulling, the solid muscle of their backs and arms becoming leaner, stringier. Why, in the name of all the parched devils of thirst, they didn’t simply slit our throats in the night and take our water barrels, I could not comprehend. There were six of them, and three of us; and Finch, I knew, could not even fire his revolver without closing his eyes. A kind of terrible restraint, a profound and baffling secret of their humanity, was the only thing that kept us alive. Yes; I looked at them, at their quiet, patient forms, and I felt a sudden, sharp curiosity, a flicker of something that was almost admiration before it was swamped by a more practical, and far more dreadful, thought. I hoped, I truly and sincerely hoped, that I looked tougher and less palatable than Finch. It was a sliver of ghastly vanity, I admit, a perfectly insane notion that felt entirely at home in the vast, humming madness of the night.

The night wore on, each moment a grain of sand dropping into the void, measured out by the monstrous, low-frequency pulse that emanated from the stone. Finch, ever the empiricist, was the first to break the tableau. He gave a short, nervous cough, a sound that was immediately swallowed by the greater hum. ‘It is an acoustic illusion,’ he declared, though his voice lacked its earlier conviction. ‘The shape of the canyons, the strata of the rock. It creates a resonance chamber. A natural organ pipe of continental size.’ He did not seem to be trying to convince us, so much as himself, reciting a catechism of science against the encroaching dark. He fumbled in his pack and produced a tuning fork, a small, ridiculous sliver of metal. He struck it against his boot heel and held it up, his ear inclined towards it. The fork’s pure, clean note was born and died in an instant, utterly annihilated by the oppressive, fundamental tone of the desert. Finch’s face, illuminated by the guttering fire, was a mask of frustration and a dawning, intellectual terror. The universe was not behaving as it should, and it offended him on a deeply personal level.

Sutton, who had been as still as the rocks themselves, finally stirred. He was a simpler man than Finch, a man of ledgers and contracts, and his fear was simpler too. It was the fear of an animal that knows a predator is near. ‘We should post a watch,’ he said, his words clipped. ‘Two hour shifts.’ I looked from his pale, sweating face to the circle of porters. They were our watch. They had been watching since the sun had set, their bodies arranged in a loose perimeter, their backs to us and their faces to the humming darkness. They were not looking at any one thing; their gaze was fixed on the entirety of the landscape, as if it were a single, living entity. Kaelen sat apart from them, closer to the fire, his blanket drawn up to his chin, only his eyes visible. They were not reflective, like an animal’s in the night. They seemed to absorb the firelight, twin pools of utter blackness.

‘They are already watching,’ I said to Sutton, and the simple statement seemed to carry a weight of finality. There was nothing for us to do but endure. The hours that followed were a unique form of torment. Sleep was an impossibility. Every time my eyelids grew heavy, the vibration that thrummed through the ground, up through my bones, would seem to intensify, a physical presence jarring me back to a state of rigid wakefulness. The hum was not static. It changed. It began to acquire a kind of cadence, a slow, abyssal rhythm, like the breathing of a creature whose life spanned millennia. It was the sound of geology, of deep time given a voice, and it was a voice that spoke of things for which humanity had no words. It spoke of pressures and temperatures that would obliterate flesh and bone, of ages passing in darkness, of a hunger that was not of the belly, but of the very stone itself.

And it was then that I felt it. A new sensation, distinct from the vibration and the sound. It was a hollowness inside me, a deep, aching void that began in my stomach and spread through my limbs. It was hunger, yes, but a perverted form of it. I had eaten my ration of desiccated beef and hard biscuit only hours before, and my stomach was not empty. This was a craving of the soul, a profound and sickening desire for something I could not name. My eyes, of their own accord, drifted to Finch. He was slumped now, his head in his hands, his scientific instruments lying forgotten by his side. The firelight played over the soft curve of his neck, the pale skin where his collar was open. The thought from before, my ghastly flicker of vanity, returned, but it was different now. It was no longer about appearing less palatable. It was a cold, speculative assessment. The thought was not mine; it felt alien, an invasive species in the ecology of my mind, and I was utterly, sickeningly horrified to find it there. I wrenched my gaze away, my heart hammering against my ribs, a frantic bird in a cage of bone. I looked at my own hands, at the dirt beneath my fingernails, and I wondered if they were still my own.

Dawn arrived not as a gentle reprieve but as a hostile glare. The sun lifted above the jagged horizon, painting the sky in brutal shades of orange and violet, and the light that fell upon our camp seemed thin and sterile. It did not warm; it merely illuminated the exhaustion etched on our faces. The humming did not cease with the light. If anything, it became more maddening, a constant, grinding noise now that the visual mystery of the night was gone. Sutton was the one who noticed. He was doing a slow, nervous circuit of the camp, his revolver held loosely in his hand, a pointless talisman. ‘Where is Nabil?’ he asked, his voice tight. I looked towards the porters. They were gathering their meagre belongings, their movements slow and deliberate. There were only five of them. Kaelen stood watching them, his expression unreadable.

Finch scrambled to his feet. ‘What do you mean, where is he? He’s probably relieving himself behind a rock.’ But there was a new, brittle edge to his voice. We waited. The silence from our part of the camp stretched, made taut by the unceasing hum. Nabil did not return. He was the youngest of the porters, a boy of perhaps seventeen with a quick, nervous smile we had not seen in weeks. ‘He has deserted,’ Finch announced, seizing on the rational explanation with a kind of desperation. ‘He’s taken his share and made a run for it.’

I walked over to Kaelen. The other porters fell silent and still, their eyes on me. ‘Kaelen. Where is your man?’ He did not look at me. He looked past me, towards the great, central edifice of rock that dominated the landscape, the source of the sound. In the stark morning light, it looked less like a geological formation and more like a piece of cyclopean architecture, a ruined temple built by giants. ‘He heard the song,’ Kaelen said, his voice flat. ‘He went to answer it.’

‘Answer it? What in God’s name does that mean?’ Finch demanded, striding over to join me. Kaelen finally turned his head, his black eyes fixing on Finch. The baring of his teeth was more pronounced this time. It was a look of profound, almost pitying contempt. ‘The rock was hungry,’ he said, as if explaining something to a child. ‘It has eaten.’ A wave of nausea washed over me. The humming seemed to pulse in time with the blood in my ears. I thought of the strange, soulless hunger I had felt in the night. I thought of my gaze upon Finch’s neck.

‘That is the most superstitious, primitive nonsense I have ever heard,’ Finch sputtered, his face flushed with anger and, I think, a deep, foundational fear. ‘Men do not simply get eaten by rocks! He deserted. Or he fell. We must search for him.’ Sutton looked from Finch to Kaelen, his face a mess of confusion and dread. ‘We can’t just leave him,’ he mumbled. I knew what I should do. As the expedition leader, I should have organized a search party, maintained order, adhered to the protocols of civilized men in the wilderness. But I looked at the impassive faces of the porters, and I looked at Kaelen, and I looked at that monstrous, humming bulk of stone, and the protocols of my world seemed like the rules of a children’s game, meaningless in this new, terrible reality. ‘We will go closer,’ I said, my own voice sounding distant to me. ‘We will investigate the formation. But we stay together.’

Kaelen gave a single, slow nod. ‘I will lead you,’ he said. ‘To the threshold. No further.’ The other porters did not move. They watched us, their expressions blank. They would not be accompanying us. They would wait. Whether for our return or for the rock to sing again, I did not know. We left them there, a small, silent congregation in the vast, empty church of the desert.

The walk towards the rock was a journey into the heart of the sound. With every step, the humming grew in power, graduating from an auditory phenomenon to a physical force. It resonated in my chest, a deep, cellular massage. The dust beneath our feet vibrated, creating tiny, dancing patterns. My teeth ached with it. Finch walked ahead of me, his initial outrage giving way to a manic, scientific curiosity. He kept tapping the rock faces we passed with his geological hammer, holding his ear to the stone, a mad physician taking the pulse of a god. Sutton lagged behind, his movements stiff with terror, his eyes scanning the cliffs above us as if expecting them to collapse upon him.

The ground began to change. The soft sand and loose scree gave way to a smooth, black, glassy plain that cracked under our boots like sheets of obsidian. The air grew still and close, heavy with the smell of ozone and something else, something ancient and metallic, like blood that had dried a million years ago. Embedded in the black stone were shapes, faint whorls and fossilized patterns that bore no resemblance to any plant or animal I had ever read of. They were things of nightmare geometry, all angles and spurs and chitinous-looking plates. Finch was ecstatic. ‘Incredible,’ he muttered, running his hand over one of the impressions. ‘Pre-Cambrian, perhaps? But the morphology is all wrong. It’s completely alien.’ He chipped away a piece with his hammer and tucked it carefully into a canvas bag, a trophy from a world not meant for human eyes.

I felt a strange pull, a sense of dreadful familiarity. The humming was no longer just a sound; it was a language, and my basest instincts were beginning to understand it. It spoke of endings and beginnings, of cycles of consumption and silence that dwarfed human history. Part of me, a part I did not recognize, wanted to lie down on the glassy stone, to press my ear to it and listen to its story until the end of time. It was a seductive thought, a siren call to oblivion.

We found what we were looking for, though we did not know it at first. Lying on the black ground, perhaps fifty yards from a deep, vertical fissure in the main rock face, was a single, worn leather sandal. It was Nabil’s. There were no other signs. No tracks, for the glassy stone held no prints. No blood. No sign of a struggle. Just the sandal, a pathetic, solitary testament to a man’s existence. Sutton made a choked sound and pointed. ‘There.’

We stood there for a long moment, the three of us, staring at the sandal. The humming was immense now, a physical pressure that made it hard to breathe. Kaelen, who had led us this far, stopped. He took the heel of his own sandal and drew a long, straight line in a patch of dust that had collected in a crack in the stone. He then squatted, spat deliberately on our side of the line, and rose again. He looked at me, his face grim. ‘Here,’ he said. ‘This is the threshold. The eating place. You go further, you feed it your mind. You feed it your soul.’

Finch let out a laugh, a high, unhinged sound. ‘His mind? His soul? The man fell into a crevasse, you fool. It’s a simple, tragic accident.’ He looked at the deep fissure Nabil’s sandal lay near. It was a wound in the rock, a vertical slice of absolute blackness from which the humming seemed to pour like a torrent. ‘The answers are in there,’ he said, his eyes gleaming with a feverish light. ‘The source of the resonance. The geological structure. This is the discovery of a lifetime.’ Before Sutton or I could stop him, he strode forward, stepping over Kaelen’s ritual line as if it were nothing. He walked right up to the edge of the dark fissure, peering into it. ‘Incredible,’ his voice echoed, strangely thin against the wall of sound. ‘The vibrations are… they are organized. It’s not just noise. It’s patterned. There’s a structure, a… a syntax…’

He fell silent. The humming that poured from the fissure seemed to intensify, to focus on him. It was a physical blow of sound. Finch let out a single, sharp cry, a sound of pure, unadulterated shock, and stumbled backward, his hands flying to his head. He tripped and fell hard on the glassy stone. Sutton and I rushed forward, crossing Kaelen’s line without a thought. We knelt beside him. His body was trembling violently. His geological hammer had flown from his hand and lay shattered on the black surface. ‘Finch?’ I said, grabbing his shoulder. ‘Finch, what is it? What did you see?’

He turned his head and looked at me. But it was not Finch. The intelligence, the arrogance, the scientific curiosity, all of it was gone. His eyes were wide, staring, but they were as empty as the desert sky. They were the eyes of an idiot, a mindless thing. A thin line of drool trickled from the corner of his mouth. His lips began to move, and from them came a noise. It was not a word. It was a low, guttural, rhythmic humming. He was mimicking the song of the rock. He had looked into the source, and the source had scoured him clean, leaving behind nothing but a hollow, vibrating shell that had once been a man. I looked from Finch’s empty face to the dark fissure, and then to Kaelen, who stood on the other side of his line, watching us with an expression of ancient, terrible sadness. The rock was hungry. And now, it had been fed.

Sutton let out a string of curses, his voice raw with disbelief. He shook Finch again, harder this time. ‘Finch, snap out of it. What kind of joke is this?’ But it was no joke. The man beneath his hands was gone. The only response was that dreadful, monotonous hum, a perfect and terrible imitation of the sound still pouring from the fissure behind us. The sound seemed to cling to the air, a thick, syrupy presence that made my teeth ache. Sutton, ever the pragmatist, switched from shouting to doctoring. He felt for a pulse at Finch’s neck, his fingers pressing into the skin. ‘Pulse is rapid, thready. Breathing is shallow. His pupils are dilated, fixed.’ He was reciting symptoms, trying to fit this square peg of cosmic horror into the round hole of his medical knowledge. ‘It’s a seizure. A psychogenic event triggered by the infrasound. We have to get him out of here.’

I looked at Kaelen. He had not moved. His arms were folded across his chest, his face a mask of weary resignation. He looked like a man watching a tide he had warned against, a tide that had now come in and swept away everything he had tried to protect. His line in the dust, that simple, superstitious barrier, seemed to hold more power than all of our scientific equipment combined. It separated his world of consequence from our world of ruin.

‘Help us,’ I said, my voice cracking. ‘For God’s sake, Kaelen, help us get him back.’

Kaelen’s dark eyes met mine. They held no pity, only a profound and bottomless sorrow. ‘You cannot put the water back in the cup once it is drunk,’ he said, his voice low and steady against the overwhelming hum. ‘He went to the well. He drank. The well is inside him now.’

‘What does that mean?’ Sutton demanded, looking up from Finch’s vacant face. His own was pale, beaded with sweat. ‘Speak plainly, man.’

‘It means he is a vessel,’ Kaelen said, his gaze shifting to the humming form on the ground. ‘An echo. He will sing its song until his throat is dust and his lungs are empty. And the song will try to find a home in you, too.’

A cold dread, far colder than the desert night, settled deep in my bones. I could feel it already, the resonance. It was not just in my ears. It was a vibration in my chest, a tingling in my fingertips. The patterned structure Finch had spoken of, the syntax. It was a language, and my body was trying to learn it against my will. I scrambled backward, away from Finch, away from the edge of that dark mouth in the rock. I stopped just short of Kaelen’s line, a part of my rational mind screaming at the absurdity of it, the other, more primal part, desperate for its symbolic safety.

Sutton was made of sterner, more stubborn stuff. ‘Nonsense. It’s an acoustic phenomenon. We’re suffering from exposure. We get him back to the land rover, we drive, we get to a hospital. That’s the plan.’ He tried to lift Finch. It was like trying to lift a statue carved from lead. Finch’s body was limp, but unnaturally heavy, a dead weight animated only by the tremor that ran through him. His head lolled back, his empty eyes staring at the bruised twilight sky. The hum from his lips did not cease. It was constant, a human bagpipe playing a single, maddening note.

‘I can’t do it alone,’ Sutton grunted, his arms straining. He looked at me, his expression a mixture of fear and command. ‘Help me. We are not leaving him here.’

I hesitated. Every instinct screamed at me to get behind Kaelen’s line, to leave this cursed place, to run until the humming was a distant memory. But I could not leave Finch. Not like this. He was my colleague, my friend, for all his arrogance. I took a deep breath, the vibrating air feeling like fine grit in my lungs, and moved back to help Sutton. Together, we managed to haul Finch’s dead weight to his feet. We each took an arm, draping it over our shoulders. His body was cold, clammy. The vibration from his humming traveled through his arm and into my own body, a direct transmission of that terrible song. It felt like holding a massive, living tuning fork.

We staggered with him, our feet scuffing on the black stone. It was a slow, nightmarish retreat. Finch’s legs dragged behind him, his boots scraping with a sound that was almost lost beneath the dual hum of the fissure and his own throat. When we reached Kaelen’s line, I felt a ridiculous, powerful urge to get Finch over it, as if crossing it would somehow contain the horror. We stumbled across the dusty demarcation. Nothing happened. The air was just as thick, the humming just as present.

Kaelen finally moved. He stepped forward, his face inches from mine. His expression was no longer sad. It was hard, flinty. ‘You brought the echo out,’ he said, his voice barely a whisper. ‘You should have left him. It would have been a mercy.’

‘We don’t leave our people behind,’ Sutton shot back, his breath coming in ragged gasps.

Kaelen ignored him, his eyes fixed on me. ‘Now you carry it with you. It will be in your camp. It will be in your sleep. It will call to the place it came from. And the place will answer.’ He looked at Finch, who hung between us like a grotesque puppet. ‘You must keep him from stillness. You must keep him from silence. If his song ever stops, a louder one will begin.’

I did not understand what he meant, not fully, but the core of it struck me with the force of a physical blow. Finch was not just a victim. He was a beacon. A lure.

The journey back to our camp was the longest mile of my life. The sun had completely set, and the desert was plunged into a deep, star-dusted blackness. We had only our headlamps to cut a moving tunnel of light through the oppressive dark. Kaelen led the way, his steps sure and silent on the treacherous ground. Sutton and I followed, wrestling with Finch’s awkward, heavy form. The humming was our constant companion. It was a third presence, an invisible passenger we carried between us. It set my teeth on edge, made my skull feel like it was a bell being softly, perpetually struck.

Sutton was silent, his jaw clenched. I could see the gears turning in his head. He was trying to process, to rationalize. He was a geologist, a man who understood pressure and time, stratum and stress. He was not equipped for this. None of us were. The world he knew was made of measurable forces. This was something else entirely. This was a force that measured you, and found you wanting.

We reached the small circle of our camp, the two tents and the cold fire pit looking impossibly mundane, artifacts from another lifetime. We lowered Finch to the ground near the pit. He lay on his back, his body still trembling, his chest rising and falling with the effort of his endless, monotonous song. It was quieter here, the overwhelming roar of the fissure left behind, but Finch’s personal echo was more than enough to fill the void. It was intimate, personal, and infinitely more disturbing.

Kaelen immediately began to build a fire, his movements economical and precise. He did not speak. Sutton dropped his pack and began rummaging through it, his actions jerky and angry. He pulled out a medical kit.

‘What are you doing?’ I asked.

‘I’m going to sedate him,’ Sutton said, his voice tight. ‘There are powerful tranquilizers in here. It’s the only humane thing to do. Stop this… this fit.’

‘Kaelen said not to let him be silent,’ I countered, the guide’s words echoing in my mind.

Sutton let out a short, bitter laugh. ‘Oh, you’re listening to him now? The man who draws lines in the dirt and talks about hungry rocks? Finch is having a neurological event. His vocal cords are in spasm. He needs medical intervention, not folklore.’ He pulled out a syringe and a small vial.

I stepped between him and Finch. ‘Sutton, stop. Think about this. You saw what that place did. You were standing right there. Science didn't explain that. Finch’s mind is gone. What if Kaelen is right? What if this humming is… I don’t know, a form of protection? A barrier?’

‘A barrier?’ he scoffed, his face illuminated by the flickering, new born flames of the fire Kaelen was tending. ‘It’s a symptom. It’s the sound of a brain short circuiting. We need to give his system a chance to reset. That means sedation. That means silence.’

The fire crackled to life, casting our shadows long and dancing against the rocks. Kaelen looked up from his work, his face a mask of dark lines and orange light. ‘The scientist is a fool,’ he said, not looking at Sutton, but at the fire. ‘He wants to silence the cricket so he cannot hear the snake. The song is not the sickness. It is the sign of it. If you stop his song, you invite the singer.’

Sutton hesitated, the syringe in his hand. The absolute certainty in Kaelen’s voice was unnerving. It was the voice of generations, of a people who had learned to live alongside this terrible power by respecting its rules. We had come here and broken them all in a single afternoon.

‘What are you saying?’ Sutton asked, his own certainty beginning to fray.

‘His mind is gone,’ Kaelen said, turning to look at us. The firelight glinted in his eyes. ‘It has been… unmade. But the thing that unmade it is still looking. It tastes him on the wind. The song he sings, the little echo, it confuses the real voice. It makes this place sound like it is already occupied. If you make him silent, you create an empty space. And it will be filled.’

A gust of wind swept through the camp, kicking up sand and making the fire gutter wildly. For a moment, carried on that wind, I thought I could hear it. A faint, deep thrumming from the direction we had come. The song of the fissure. The real voice.

Sutton slowly lowered the syringe. He looked at Finch, who lay humming in the sand, oblivious. He looked at Kaelen, his face a storm of conflict. Then he looked at me, his eyes wide with a fear he could no longer conceal behind skepticism. ‘My God,’ he whispered. ‘What have we done?’ He capped the syringe and put it away. The argument was over. The science had failed. We were in Kaelen’s world now, and we would have to abide by its terrible, ancient laws.

We sat by the fire, the three of us, a strange and broken fellowship. Kaelen sat cross legged, feeding sticks into the flames, his gaze distant. Sutton stared into the fire as if trying to find answers in the embers. And I watched Finch. I could not look away. He had become the focal point of our dread. The humming was the soundtrack to our vigil. It was a low, resonant note that seemed to vibrate in the very marrow of my bones. After an hour, I felt my own throat muscles twitching, my own breathing wanting to fall into its rhythm. I had to consciously fight it, swallowing hard, clenching my jaw.

Kaelen seemed to notice. ‘Do not listen to the echo,’ he said softly. ‘Listen to the fire. Listen to the wind. Fill your head with other sounds, or it will find room.’

I tried. I focused on the spitting and cracking of the wood, the soft sigh of the night breeze moving over the dunes. But the hum was always there, underneath it all, a baseline of horror. We ate a meager meal of dried rations, the familiar act feeling alien and pointless. The food tasted like ash in my mouth. All the while, Finch hummed. He did not move, did not ask for food or water. He just lay there, a metronome counting down the seconds of our sanity.

Later, Sutton and I took turns trying to get some water into him. We tilted his head and poured a small stream from a canteen into the corner of his mouth. Some of it dribbled in, but most of it ran down his chin, mixing with the thin line of drool. He did not seem to notice. The humming faltered for a second as he swallowed, and in that brief, fractional silence, the night seemed to press in on us. The air grew heavy, and the distant thrum from the fissure seemed to spike in volume, a hungry query. Then Finch’s humming resumed, and the presence receded. Kaelen had been right. The silence was the real danger.

We agreed to take watches. There was no question of all of us sleeping. Kaelen took the first watch, sitting motionless by the fire, a lonely guardian in the vast, uncaring wilderness. Sutton and I retreated to our tent. Sleep was impossible. We lay in our sleeping bags, the thin nylon wall doing nothing to muffle the sound of Finch’s humming. It was a lullaby from hell.

‘He called it a syntax,’ Sutton murmured in the darkness. His voice was hollow. ‘Finch. He said it had a structure. A language.’

‘I know,’ I said.

‘Maybe that’s what it is. A language that reprograms the brain. An acoustic virus. It found his operating system and wiped it clean.’ He was trying again, clutching at the straws of scientific metaphor.

‘Maybe,’ I said, though I did not believe it was that simple. This felt older, more fundamental. It was not a virus. It was a predator.

After what felt like an eternity, it was my turn for watch. I crawled out of the tent into the cold desert air. Kaelen gave me a slight nod and went to his own bedroll, laid out under the stars a short distance away. Now it was just me, the fire, and the humming man. I sat on a rock, my rifle across my lap. The weapon felt useless, a child’s toy. What good was a bullet against a sound?

The stars were brilliant, a cold, distant fire scattered across a black velvet cloth. They offered no comfort. They seemed to be watching, indifferent witnesses to our small, terrestrial drama. The humming continued, relentless. I tried Kaelen’s trick. I listened to the fire. I felt the rough texture of the rifle’s stock under my fingers. I counted my own heartbeats. But the hum was insidious. It wove itself into everything. The crackle of the fire seemed to find its rhythm. The pulse in my own veins seemed to sync with it.

My mind began to wander. I thought of the fissure, that slice of absolute black. I remembered Finch’s cry of shock. What had he seen? Or was it what he heard? That focused, physical blow of sound. He said it was patterned. A structure. I found myself trying to discern the pattern in Finch’s own humming. Was it truly monotonous? Or were there subtle shifts in pitch, in timbre, that I was missing? I leaned closer, straining my ears. The hum filled my head, pushing out all other thoughts. It was a simple note, but it felt incredibly complex, like a single thread from a vast, unknowable tapestry. It felt… meaningful. It promised a kind of order, a sublime and terrible logic that lay beneath the chaos of the world. It was a key, and it wanted to turn in the lock of my own mind.

My head swam. The fire blurred. For a moment, just a moment, I understood the allure. I felt a profound urge to hum along, to join the song, to be part of that perfect, awful pattern. It felt like the most natural thing in the world. It felt like coming home.

A hand gripped my shoulder, hard. I yelped, jolting back to full awareness. Kaelen was standing over me, his face grim in the dying firelight. ‘You were listening,’ he said. It was not a question.

I scrambled to my feet, my heart hammering against my ribs. ‘I… I don’t know. I was just…’

‘It was feeling for a keyhole,’ Kaelen said, his eyes boring into me. ‘And you were showing it the door.’ He gestured toward Finch. ‘Look.’

I looked. Finch had moved. He was no longer on his back. He had pushed himself up into a sitting position. His back was ramrod straight. His head was cocked to one side, and his empty eyes were fixed directly on me. The humming that came from his lips had changed. It was louder now, and it had a new note in it, a lower, resonant tone that harmonized with the first. It was becoming a chord.

‘He hears you listening,’ Kaelen whispered, his voice tight with urgency. ‘He hears the space it is making in you. He is building a bridge.’

The blood ran cold in my veins. Finch, the mindless echo, was reacting to me. The humming intensified again, the two notes weaving together into a sound that was not just unnerving, but physically painful. It felt like a drill boring into the base of my skull. I clapped my hands over my ears, but it did no good. The sound was inside me now. I had opened the door, and the singer was at the threshold, its voice joining the echo.

Then Sutton was there, stumbling out of the tent, his face a mask of sleepy confusion that quickly turned to terror as he saw Finch. Finch, sitting upright, his vacant eyes locked on me, singing his new, terrible, two-part song into the shivering night. The rock was hungry. It had been fed one of us. And now it was calling for the rest of its meal.

Sutton’s shout was a ragged tear in the fabric of the night. ‘What is that? Gods, what is he doing?’

He scrambled away from the tent, his hands flapping uselessly at his sides. The chord coming from Finch drilled deeper. It was not merely sound. It was pressure, a physical weight pushing in on my skull. It found the resonant frequencies of my bones, my teeth, my very soul. And in the center of the pain, there was a terrible, seductive harmony. A voice was trying to be born inside me, and Finch’s throat was its willing cradle. I felt my own jaw go slack, my own throat working, a low hum trying to fight its way past my teeth. I was going to join him. I was going to complete the chord.

‘No,’ Kaelen’s voice was a low growl, a blade of sound cutting through the drone.

He moved with a speed that was shocking. He was not a man stumbling from a cold bedroll. He was a predator, unleashed. In two long strides he was on Finch, his powerful frame eclipsing the sitting man from my view. There was a brief, violent struggle. I heard a choked gasp, a wet, guttural noise. Sutton cried out again, a thin, useless sound. Kaelen’s back was a wall of muscle, straining in the firelight. I saw him rip a length of cloth from one of the nearby packs. Another muffled, desperate sound from Finch. Then silence. The chord was gone.

The absence of it was as shocking as its presence had been. My ears rang with a phantom tone. The pressure in my head vanished, leaving a hollow, aching void. I swayed on my feet, the world tilting violently. The fire, the rocks, the star-dusted sky all smeared together into a meaningless wash of color. I took a gasping breath, and then another. The air was just air again, cold and thin, no longer thick with that awful, perfect pattern.

Kaelen straightened up, his chest heaving. He turned, and in his hand he held the tattered strip of canvas. He had used it as a gag. Finch was slumped over, his head lolling on his chest, secured to his own pack by a length of rope Kaelen must have knotted with brutal efficiency. He was silent. He was still. But his stillness was no longer the stillness of an empty vessel. It was the stillness of a loaded weapon.

‘Is he… is he dead?’ Sutton whispered, his voice trembling. He was hugging himself, shivering uncontrollably.

‘No,’ Kaelen said, his own voice tight, ragged at the edges. He walked over to me, his eyes searching my face. His gaze was intense, analytical, like a physician diagnosing a fatal disease. ‘Are you hurt?’

‘I… I don’t think so.’ My own voice sounded alien. ‘My head…’

‘It was singing to you,’ he stated, his voice flat. ‘Using him as an amplifier. When you started to listen, really listen, you gave it a target. You created a sympathetic vibration. It was tuning you.’

The words made a terrifying kind of sense. Tuning me. Like an instrument. The thought made my stomach clench. ‘Tuning me for what?’

Kaelen’s face was hard as the granite around us. ‘To play its song.’

Sutton took a shaky step forward. His face was pale, his eyes wide with a frantic, animal fear. ‘We have to go. Now. We have to get away from this place. Kaelen, we have to leave him.’ He pointed a trembling finger at Finch. ‘He’s a part of it now. He’s a beacon. It will use him to find us.’

‘We don’t leave our own,’ Kaelen said, his voice dropping to a dangerous low. It was the voice I had heard once before, when he had warned Finch about wandering off. It was a voice that permitted no argument.

‘He’s not one of our own anymore!’ Sutton’s voice rose to a near shriek. ‘Did you not hear that? Did you not see him? He’s a puppet, Kaelen! And the rock is holding the strings. If we take him with us, we are taking it with us!’

‘We take him,’ Kaelen repeated, his jaw set. He turned his back on Sutton, a clear dismissal, and focused on me again. ‘The bridge it was building. Do you still feel it?’

I closed my eyes, trying to look inward. The immediate, overwhelming presence was gone. But there was something left behind. A trace. An echo. It was like seeing the ghost of a bright light after it has been extinguished, a faint shape burned onto the inside of your eyelids. It was a memory of the pattern, a faint hum in the quietest parts of my mind. A single, pure note that waited patiently for its companion.

‘Yes,’ I whispered. ‘It’s quiet. But it’s there.’

A muscle twitched in Kaelen’s cheek. He nodded slowly, as if I had just confirmed his worst fears. ‘It has a hook in you now. It will not let go easily. It will call to you. You have to fight it. Do you understand me? You cannot listen. You cannot let your mind drift. You must keep your thoughts grounded. Think of the trail. Think of the cold. Think of anything, anything but the pattern.’

‘We’re just going to walk out of here?’ Sutton demanded, his voice laced with disbelief. ‘In the middle of the night? With that… that thing?’

‘We are not walking. We are running,’ Kaelen corrected him. ‘And we do not stop until the sun is high in the sky and we are miles from this cursed place. Pack your gear. Water, food, rope. Anything you can carry in one load. We leave the tents. We leave everything else. We have five minutes.’

He did not wait for an answer. He moved to our supplies, his actions economical and swift. He began stuffing dried rations and a coil of rope into his own pack, working with a desperate urgency that was more terrifying than Sutton’s panic. The choice was made. We were abandoning our camp, our expedition, everything, and fleeing into the darkness.

Sutton stood frozen for another second, then a fresh wave of terror seemed to wash over him, and he scrambled to his own pack, his hands shaking so badly he could barely work the buckles. I tried to do the same. I stumbled to my belongings, my mind a confused fog. My fingers felt like clumsy sausages. I fumbled with my waterskin, dropping the stopper twice before I could secure it. My gaze kept drifting toward the high, dark ridge of the rock, a black saw against the stars. I could feel its immense, silent attention. It was no longer singing. It was waiting. It was watching us run.

Kaelen finished his packing and moved to Finch. With a grunt of effort, he hefted the bound man’s pack onto his own front, balancing the load, then crouched and hauled Finch’s limp body over his shoulder in a fireman’s carry. The man was not small, but Kaelen rose to his feet as if carrying little more than a sack of grain. The muscles in his neck and shoulders corded with the strain.

‘Ready?’ he grunted, his eyes sweeping over me and Sutton.

Sutton nodded, his pack haphazardly stuffed and half open. I simply stood there, my own pack on, my mind still reeling. The silence was the worst part now. It was a canvas, and my imagination, poisoned by the memory of that song, was beginning to paint on it. I could almost hear the hum, a whisper on the edge of hearing, carried by the rising wind.

‘Let’s go,’ Kaelen said, and he turned, leading the way out of our small camp, away from the dying embers of the fire.

We moved in a stumbling, frantic line. Kaelen in the lead, bearing the terrible weight of Finch. Sutton followed close behind him, practically treading on his heels as if the small space between them was the only safe place in the world. I brought up the rear, my feet finding treacherous purchase on the loose scree. The moon was a slender crescent, providing just enough light to turn the landscape into a treacherous world of stark highlights and bottomless shadows. Every rock looked like a crouching beast. Every gust of wind sounded like the beginning of a melody.

Kaelen’s words echoed in my head. *Keep your thoughts grounded.* I tried. I focused on the crunch of my boots on the gravel. Left, right, left, right. The burn in my thighs as we climbed a steep embankment. The biting cold on my exposed cheeks. But the song was a persistent lover. It whispered in the spaces between my thoughts. It was so easy to listen. The memory of the pattern was a perfect, beautiful thing in a world of chaos and fear. It promised an end to the struggle. It promised sense.

We cleared the small basin that had sheltered our camp and came out onto a wide, exposed shoulder of the mountain. The wind hit us with full force, a physical blow that made me stagger. It howled around us, a wild and mournful sound. But through the howling, I heard it. A single, low note, weaving through the gale. It was not in my head this time. It was outside. The mountain itself was singing.

I stopped, my blood turning to ice. Kaelen was a few yards ahead. He must have heard it too. He paused, his head cocked, the dark shape of Finch a grotesque burden on his shoulder.

‘Kaelen?’ Sutton’s voice was thin and reedy, almost snatched away by the wind.

The note held for a long, terrible moment, and then it was joined by a second, higher one. The chord. The same agonizingly perfect chord that Finch had sung. It was everywhere now, vibrating through the rock under our feet, carried on the air, pressing in from all sides. The source was not a single point anymore. The whole damned mountain range was the instrument.

‘It’s not just the rock,’ I heard myself say, the words tumbling out. ‘It’s bigger than that.’

‘It has our scent,’ Kaelen said, his voice grim. ‘It is tasting the air. We need to break out of its territory.’

He started moving again, faster now, half running down a treacherous slope. Sutton and I scrambled to keep up. The two-note drone was a constant pressure, a sound that bypassed the ears and went straight to the mind. It was harder to think. It was harder to place my feet. The allure I had felt by the fire was gone, replaced by a raw, primal terror. This was not a sublime order. This was the sound of a predator closing in. This was the sound of a trap springing shut.

My foot slipped on a patch of loose shale. I cried out, pinwheeling my arms for balance, but it was no use. I went down hard, my knee smashing against a sharp edge of rock. A bolt of white hot pain shot up my leg. I lay there, gasping, the chord seeming to intensify, reveling in my weakness.

‘Get up!’ Kaelen yelled back at me over the wind. He could not stop. He could not put Finch down.

I tried to push myself up, but my leg buckled, sending a fresh wave of agony through me. The pain was a new focal point. For a moment, it drowned out the song. I could think again, for a precious second. I looked back the way we had come. The hollow where our camp had been was a pool of deep shadow. But above it, on the high ridge, something was moving. It was not an animal. It was a light. A faint, pulsing luminescence, a sickly green, like foxfire. It grew in intensity, matching the rhythm of the humming chord. The singer was showing itself.

‘Sutton, help him!’ Kaelen commanded.

Sutton hesitated, his form a silhouette of indecision against the pale ground. He looked from me, to Kaelen, to the horrifying light on the ridge. His fear was a palpable thing, a stench on the wind. He wanted to run. He wanted to abandon both me and Kaelen and save himself. I could see it in the taut line of his body.

Then Kaelen’s voice cut through the air again, sharp and cold as ice. ‘Sutton!’

The command broke Sutton’s paralysis. With a sob of terror, he turned and scrambled back to me. He grabbed my arm, hauling me to my feet. ‘Come on, come on, come on,’ he chanted, his voice a frantic prayer.

I leaned on him, my leg screaming in protest. We stumbled forward, a clumsy, three-legged beast, trying to catch up to Kaelen. The humming grew louder still, the pressure unbearable. The light on the ridge was spreading, seeping down the rock face like luminous paint, flowing toward the path we had just taken. It was following us.

We were not just running from a sound. We were being hunted by a conscious, malevolent geography.

We caught up to Kaelen at the entrance to a narrow ravine. He had paused, waiting for us, his breathing coming in harsh, ragged bursts. The weight of Finch was clearly taking its toll.

‘This way,’ he gasped, gesturing into the deep shadow of the ravine. ‘It will break line of sight. It might confuse the sound.’

It was a desperate hope, and we all knew it. We plunged into the gash in the rock. The walls rose up on either side of us, blotting out the sky. The wind died down, and for a glorious moment, the humming seemed to recede. The silence was thick, broken only by our panicked breathing and the scrape of our boots.

‘Did it work?’ Sutton whispered, his voice echoing unnaturally in the confined space.

As if in answer, a new sound began. It was not the chord. It was a scratching, skittering noise. It came from ahead of us, from the darkness deep within the ravine. It also came from behind us, from the entrance we had just passed through. We were trapped.

Kaelen slowly, carefully, lowered Finch to the ground. He leaned the unconscious man against the rock wall. Then he unslung his pack and pulled out his climbing axe. The firelight had made it look like a tool. In the oppressive dark of the ravine, it looked like a weapon.

‘It was herding us,’ Kaelen said, his voice a low whisper. His eyes were wide, scanning the darkness. ‘It drove us in here.’

The skittering grew louder, closer. It was the sound of a thousand tiny claws on stone. I fumbled in my own pack, my fingers closing around the handle of my geological hammer. It felt pathetic, a child’s toy. Sutton let out a choked sound and pulled out a long knife he used for cutting cord.

Then we saw them. Figures began to resolve themselves out of the deeper darkness. They were not large. They were hunched, wiry things, moving with a jerky, unnatural gait. Their limbs were too long, their joints bent at impossible angles. They were the shape of men, but horribly wrong. As they drew closer, the faint light from the ravine’s entrance caught their skin. It was pale, grey, and tight over their bones, like old parchment. Their faces were devoid of features, smooth and blank, except for wide, dark mouths that gaped open. And from those mouths came a faint, monotonous hum. The first note. The key.

They were echoes. Like Finch. But they were not catatonic. They were active. They were moving. They were the mountain’s hands, its teeth. The rock had been fed before. And it had not let its meals go. It had given them a new purpose.

There were dozens of them, blocking the path ahead, blocking our retreat. They shuffled forward, their heads lolling, their humming a rustling, multi-toned whisper that was beginning to build toward the terrible chord.

My heart hammered in my chest. This was it. This was the end of the expedition. The end of everything. We had been analyzed, targeted, isolated, and cornered. The song had found its listeners. And now the choir was coming to welcome us into the fold.

Kaelen’s words were a death sentence. ‘It was herding us.’ He spoke what I had felt in my bones, the cold and certain knowledge that we were not explorers here, we were prey. The mountain was not a passive thing of rock and ice. It was a predator. A sophisticated one. The humming from the pale, featureless things grew, a chorus of individual notes beginning to seek harmony. My geological hammer felt impossibly small, a pebble against a glacier. Beside me, Sutton’s knife seemed to tremble in his grip, or maybe it was just his hand. There was no plan. There was no escape. There was only the closing trap.

‘Back to back,’ Kaelen’s voice cut through the rising hum. It was not a shout, but it had a commander’s edge, a sliver of steel in the suffocating dark. ‘Sutton, with me. You,’ he looked at me, his eyes catching a sliver of light, ‘protect Finch. Whatever happens, keep them off him.’

The command was absurd. Protect him with what? My rock hammer? My body? But it was an order, and in the chaos, an order was an anchor. I stumbled back, planting my feet on either side of Finch’s slumped form. Sutton scrambled to Kaelen’s side, and they formed a tiny island of defiance in the center of the ravine. The skittering was on all sides now, a sound like a million beetles crawling over a stone floor. The echoes shuffled closer, their movements a parody of human walking, a twitching, broken dance. Their eyeless faces were all turned toward us, their gaping mouths a constellation of black holes in the grey dark. The humming intensified, the individual notes beginning to merge. It was like an orchestra tuning up before a dreadful symphony. I felt the sound in my teeth, in my skull. It was a physical pressure, a prelude to the mind breaking chord.

The first one reached Kaelen. It was a stooped thing, its spine bent at a sharp angle. It lurched forward, its long arms ending in pale, claw like fingers. It made no sound other than the low hum from its throat. Kaelen did not hesitate. He swung the climbing axe not like a tool, but like a battle axe, a fluid, brutal arc of motion. The pick end struck the creature in the chest. There was no cry of pain. There was no spray of blood. There was a sound like old wood splitting, a dry, fibrous crack. The creature stumbled, its hum faltering for a second, but it did not fall. It simply took another shuffling step. Kaelen grunted, wrenching the axe free. He swung again, this time with the broad adze of the axe head, aiming for the neck. The blow connected with a wet, pulpy thud, and the thing’s head lolled to the side, held on by only a few strands of what looked like dried sinew. It staggered a few more steps before its legs tangled and it collapsed in a heap.

Then they were on us. It was not a charge, it was an inexorable tide. One came at me, its long arms reaching. Its skin was like vellum stretched over a cage of twigs. I swung my hammer in a wild, panicked arc. The hammer head, designed for splitting rock, met its forearm with a sickening crunch. Bone broke. But it felt dry, brittle, like striking a dead branch. The creature did not react. It did not seem to register the injury. The arm just hung at a new, impossible angle as it continued to press forward. Its other hand grazed my jacket, and the touch was cold, a deep, cellular cold that had nothing to do with the mountain air. It was the cold of the grave.

I screamed, a pathetic, strangled sound, and swung again, this time at its head. The hammer connected with its smooth, featureless face. The impact jarred my arm to the shoulder. The face crumpled inward like a paper mask, and the humming from its mouth stopped with a wet gurgle. The thing fell, and another was there to take its place.

The ravine became a blur of motion and a cacophony of terrible sounds. The scrape and skitter of their feet, the dry crack and wet thud of our weapons, our own grunts and gasps for breath. Over it all, the humming continued to build, a dozen, then two dozen, then fifty notes all vibrating in the narrow space, weaving together. It was getting harder to think. The sound was a hook in my brain, pulling me toward a vast, empty space. I saw Sutton get swarmed. Three of them simply walked into him, their weight and momentum bearing him to the ground. He shrieked, his knife falling from his hand. He was lost. But Kaelen was a whirlwind of controlled violence. He spun, his axe a blur. He brought the pick down into the back of one creature, the adze into the neck of another. He kicked the third away from Sutton’s body and hauled him to his feet in one fluid motion. ‘Get up!’ he roared, his voice raw.

We had made a small circle of broken bodies around us, but they were just obstacles. The echoes climbed over their fallen kin with that same jerky, unstoppable purpose. They were not alive. They did not tire. They did not feel. We did. My arm ached. My lungs burned. Fear was a metallic taste in my mouth, so strong I could almost chew it. I saw a gap. Behind us, toward the entrance of the ravine, the press was thinner. The creatures seemed more focused on pushing us deeper in. It was the herding instinct Kaelen had mentioned.

‘The entrance!’ I yelled, my voice cracking. ‘We can get out the way we came in!’

Kaelen’s head snapped toward me, then toward the ravine mouth. His face was a mask of grime and strain. For a second, I thought he was going to dismiss it, to say we had to go forward. But he saw it too. A chance. A sliver of hope. ‘Sutton, grab Finch’s legs! On three!’ he bellowed. He did not wait for an answer. He turned and drove forward, not toward the deeper dark, but back into the wave we had just fought through. He was a wedge, a prow of a ship breaking a frozen sea. The axe rose and fell. Crack. Thud. Crack.

Sutton, his face pale and smeared with dirt, looked at me with wide, terrified eyes. Then he seemed to find some last reserve of will. He nodded, and together we bent down and grabbed Finch. He was a dead weight, his body limp and cold. ‘One!’ Kaelen roared, felling a creature directly in his path. ‘Two!’ He kicked another one aside, clearing a small space. ‘Three!’

We heaved. We stumbled forward, dragging Finch between us. His boots scraped over the stone and over the bodies of the fallen echoes. It was a nightmare of a retreat. Creatures grabbed at us. A hand closed on my ankle, its grip like iron. I kicked wildly, my boot connecting with something that broke with a snap. The hand fell away. Sutton cried out as a clawed hand raked his arm, leaving three long, pale scratches that did not bleed but seemed to weep a clear, thin fluid. The humming was a physical wall of sound now, the chord almost complete. My mind felt like it was fraying at the edges. I saw colours that were not there. I heard whispers under the hum, my name called from the darkness.

‘Almost there!’ Kaelen yelled. He was at the mouth of the ravine, turning to face the tide, holding them back for a precious few seconds. ‘Go! Get him out!’

Sutton and I half carried, half dragged Finch out of the narrow gash in the rock and back into the howling wind of the open slope. The change was instantaneous and brutal. The confined acoustics of the ravine had focused the humming, but out here, the full, matured chord of the mountain hit us like a physical blow. It was not just a sound. It was a vibration that shook the air, the ground, our very bones. It was a sound that had colour, a sickening, shifting pattern of violet and green that pulsed behind my eyes. It was a sound that had meaning, a terrible, cosmic meaning of utter insignificance. My mind buckled. I fell to my knees, dropping Finch’s legs, my hands flying to my ears as if I could block out a sound that was already inside me.

Sutton collapsed beside me, curling into a fetal position, whimpering. Kaelen stumbled out of the ravine after us, a dark gash on his forehead weeping blood. He did not fall. He stood there, swaying, his chest heaving, the climbing axe held loosely in his hand. He looked back at the ravine. The echoes did not pursue. They milled about at the entrance, a writhing mass of pale limbs and gaping mouths, their hum now just one part of the greater, overwhelming chord. They were a choir. And this was their cathedral.

‘It’s not just in the rock,’ I gasped, the words torn from me. I was a geologist. I understood resonance. I understood how vibrations could propagate through a medium. The rock of the mountain was the tuning fork, the amplifier. But the air, the very space around us, was the final instrument. ‘It’s everywhere.’

Kaelen wiped the blood from his brow with the back of his sleeve, smearing it with grime. His eyes, clear and hard despite the sonic assault, scanned the slopes around us. He was looking for shelter, for a way down. But there was nowhere to go. The chord was a dome of sound, trapping us on this cursed slope. To go down was to stay within its influence.

‘Sutton,’ Kaelen’s voice was strained, but it still held its authority. ‘Sutton, on your feet.’

Sutton just shook his head, his hands clamped over his ears. ‘No more,’ he sobbed. ‘I can’t. The noise. It’s in my head.’

‘It’s in all our heads,’ Kaelen said grimly. He knelt beside Finch. Our friend, our companion, was unchanged on the outside. But his humming had changed. It was no longer the single, monotonous note. He was humming the full chord now, a perfect, miniature replica of the mountain’s song. His body was a small, flesh and blood resonator, perfectly in tune with the horror around us. Kaelen placed a hand on Finch’s chest, then quickly pulled it away. ‘He’s cold. Colder than before.’

My scientific mind, battered and terrified as it was, latched onto that. A detail. A data point. The process, whatever it was, was continuing. Finch was not just a victim. He was becoming something else. He was becoming like them. An echo in training.

Then I saw it. High above us, on a ridge silhouetted against the bruised purple of the dying sky. Figures. Not the hunched, broken forms from the ravine. These were tall. Impossibly tall and thin. They stood motionless, like statues or dead trees. There were three of them. As I watched, a fourth one seemed to resolve from the rock beside them, as if it had been there all along. They were not humming. They were silent. They were watching. The source? The conductors of this terrible orchestra?

‘Kaelen,’ I whispered, my voice hoarse. I pointed a trembling finger. ‘Up there.’

He followed my gaze. His body went rigid. He had seen them too. The silent watchers on the ridge. The humming from Finch grew fractionally louder. The chord from the mountain seemed to sharpen, to focus. The pressure in my skull intensified. It felt like my brain was being squeezed in a vise.

‘They’re not the hands,’ Kaelen said, his voice a low, awed whisper. ‘The things in the ravine. They’re just the tools.’ He looked from the tall figures on the ridge, to the mass of echoes at the ravine mouth, and then down at us, three broken insects scrabbling on a mountainside. ‘We were wrong.’

‘Wrong about what?’ I asked, though I was not sure I wanted to know the answer.

‘We thought the goal was to get down,’ he said, his gaze distant, analytical, even now. ‘We’ve been trying to run from the sound. But it’s a dome. We can’t get out from underneath it by going sideways or down. The signal is weakest at the edges, but the mountain is shaped like a cone. To get to the edge, we have to go a long, long way down.’ He paused, and a terrible, insane logic began to dawn on me. ‘What if the weakest point isn’t at the bottom?’

He looked up toward the ridge. Toward the silent, watching figures. ‘Every broadcast has a source. An antenna. The sound is strongest here, but it’s diffuse. A blanket. The source itself... the signal there might be more focused. More pure. But maybe... maybe it’s also more vulnerable.’

I stared at him. I saw the madness of the idea shining in his eyes, a desperate, feral gleam. ‘You want to go up?’ I croaked. ‘You want to go toward them?’

‘Running down is a slow death,’ he said, his voice flat and certain. ‘Sutton is already breaking. Finch is lost. The chord will grind us down, and then the echoes will come and collect what’s left. It’s a process. A harvest. We can either lie down and wait for the combine, or we can try to break the machine.’

He looked at me, his eyes demanding an answer. The wind tore at us, carrying the soul crushing chord. Below us, the path down was lost in shadow and hostile sound. Above us, silhouetted against the sky, stood the silent, waiting things that commanded this symphony of madness. The ravine behind us was a den of horrors. Finch was humming a prelude to his own damnation at our feet.

It was insane. It was suicide. It was a geologist, a cartographer, and a half mad expedition leader with a climbing axe against the sentient heart of a malevolent mountain. But as I looked at the options, at the slow, inevitable dissolution offered by retreat versus the sharp, terrible finality of an ascent, I found there was no choice at all. There was only one direction that offered even the ghost of a chance, no matter how slim.

I took a deep, shuddering breath, the vibrating air cold in my lungs. I got to my feet, my geological hammer feeling a little heavier, a little more solid in my hand. ‘What about them?’ I asked, nodding toward Sutton and Finch.

Kaelen’s face hardened. He looked at Sutton, curled up and broken. He looked at Finch, a human tuning fork for the abyss. The logic was as cold and hard as the rock under our feet. ‘We can’t carry them both up,’ he said, his voice devoid of emotion. ‘And we can’t leave them for the echoes.’

We gathered all our remaining shards. We piled them onto the milky vein, creating a small cairn of counter resonance. The air around the pile seemed to shimmer, to clear. The pressure lessened in our immediate vicinity.

‘This is it,’ Kaelen said, looking at me. He was not asking if I was ready. He was telling me to act.

I took the geological hammer. My hands were steady. The terror was a cold, hard knot in my stomach, but my mind was clear, focused on the physics of the problem. This was not an act of violence. It was an act of tuning. We were about to strike a tuning fork to shatter a bell.

I placed the largest, flattest shard from our pile on top of the others, creating a solid surface to strike. I raised the hammer. I could see my reflection in its polished head, a small, distorted, and utterly insignificant human figure against the backdrop of an impossible, alien landscape. For a single, lucid moment, the sheer absurdity of our situation washed over me. A geologist, a security consultant, and a graduate student, trying to kill a mountain with a rock hammer.

Then I brought the hammer down.

The impact was not loud. It was a single, perfect, chiming note. The same note our shards had been humming, but amplified a thousand times. The cairn of crystals flared with a brilliant white light, and a wave of pure, clean resonance shot down the vein into the heart of the crater.

For a heartbeat, nothing happened. The great black crystal continued its silent, sickening pulse.

Then, a high pitched whine started, rising in frequency until it was at the very edge of hearing. A single, tiny crack of white light appeared on the surface of the black monolith. Then another. And another. The whine became a scream of tortured physics. The cracks spread, racing across the surface of the black crystal like lightning, a web of pure white energy.

The sentinels around its base began to tremble. They crumbled, one by one, collapsing into piles of black dust, their amplifying function overloaded and destroyed.

The scream reached an unbearable crescendo, and then the colossal black crystal, the heart of the mountain, imploded. It did not explode outwards. It collapsed in on itself, folding into a point of impossible darkness. A wave of absolute silence rushed out from the center, a wave of *nothing*, washing over us, extinguishing the pressure, the whispers, the fear.

The silence was total. Absolute. The mountain was dead.

We stood on the rim of the caldera, panting in the clean, empty air. The exhaustion of the past days hit me all at once, and my knees buckled. Kaelen caught me. We looked at each other, stunned, victorious.

And then the ground lurched.

A deep, groaning sound came from the rock beneath our feet. Not a psychic signal, but a purely physical, geological protest. A tremor shook the rim of the caldera, and a section of the cliff face a hundred yards away sheared off and fell into the abyss. The groaning grew louder, the sound of a billion tons of rock settling into a new, unstable configuration.

We had killed the mountain’s mind. We had broken the strange, impossible force that held it all together in its unnatural state. And now, gravity was reasserting itself. The mountain, free of its animating consciousness, was beginning to collapse. We were standing on the edge of a geological catastrophe we had just created. The ascent was over. The attack was a success. Now, the escape began.

“Move,” Kaelen’s voice was a blade, cutting through my daze. “Now.”

He didn’t need to say it twice. The groan from the deep rock was escalating, a grinding, tectonic complaint of a scale I could barely comprehend. My geologist’s mind, trained to think in millions of years, was struggling to process a mountain dying in mere minutes. Kaelen was already pulling Anya away from the rim, his movements economical and certain. I scrambled after them, my legs feeling like lead, my heart a frantic drum against my ribs. The ground was no longer solid. It was a living thing, shuddering and pitching like the deck of a ship in a typhoon. We ran, stumbling over the familiar gray rock that was now our mortal enemy. A chasm, five feet wide, ripped open in the ground just ahead of us. Kaelen vaulted it without breaking stride, pulling Anya with him. I leaped, my boots scraping the far edge, loose scree skittering into the newly opened darkness. I didn’t look down.

Behind us, the sound was not a simple crash. It was a symphony of destruction. The entire rim of the caldera, a circumference of miles, was beginning to fail. We could hear it happening in a domino effect, a roar of shearing rock that traveled around the abyss, getting closer. We were in a race with the collapse of our own footing. The air, once so clean and silent, was now thick with dust, a choking haze of pulverized rock that stung the eyes and coated the back of the throat. The beautiful, terrifying crater was devouring itself. We reached a spine of darker, more competent rock, an outcropping that seemed, for the moment, more stable than the crumbling rhyolite of the rim. We pressed ourselves against it, gasping for breath, and watched.

It was a spectacle of impossible geology. Entire sections of the caldera wall, acres of rock weighing billions of tons, were slumping inwards. They didn’t just fall. They flowed, liquefying under the sudden, immense stresses. The elegant, impossible architecture of the mountain, held in place by a force we had just extinguished, was surrendering to the simple, brutal physics of mass and gravity. The abyss where the black crystal had imploded was now a voracious mouth, swallowing its own body.

“The way we came up,” Anya said, her voice raspy with dust and exhaustion. “It’s gone.”

She was right. The ridge we had so carefully ascended was simply no longer there. It had been part of a section of the wall that had already peeled away and vanished into the churning dust cloud below. We were stranded near the summit of a disintegrating mountain, with no clear path down.

“The whole structure is failing,” I said, my voice sounding distant to my own ears. I was thinking aloud, the geologist in me taking over as a defense mechanism against terror. “It’s called a catastrophic slump. The crystalline lattice, the vein system, it wasn’t just a nervous system. It was the mountain’s skeleton. It provided a tensile strength that normal rock doesn’t have. We broke it. Now the whole edifice is undergoing isostatic rebound in the worst possible way. It’s trying to find a new equilibrium.”

“In English, Doctor,” Kaelen said, his eyes scanning the treacherous slopes below us, never resting. “Which way do we go that doesn’t kill us?”

I took a deep, steadying breath, the grit in the air scraping my lungs. This was my domain now. Not the alien physics, but the plain, deadly mechanics of falling rock. “We can’t follow the troughs. They’ll be channeling the debris. We need to stay on the ridges, the spines. They’re the most compressed, the most solid rock. Like that one.” I pointed a trembling finger downwards, tracing a jagged line of dark gray basalt that snaked its way down the mountain’s flank. It was a knife’s edge in places, a treacherous looking path, but it seemed more intact than the crumbling slopes on either side. “It’s our only chance.”

Kaelen nodded, his jaw set. “Alright. Anya, you’re between us. Don’t look down. Just look at my heels. Doctor, you take the rear. Call out any warnings. If the ground feels wrong, you shout. Understood?”

We both nodded. There was no time for debate. The very outcrop we were standing on gave a sickening lurch, and a shower of pebbles rained down on us from above. Our moment of respite was over.

The descent was a nightmare traverse. The air was filled with the constant roar of the mountain’s collapse, a bass rumble that vibrated through the soles of our boots and into our bones. The ridge was narrow, barely wide enough for one person, with sheer drops on both sides that fell away into swirling clouds of dust. We moved as quickly as we dared, our focus narrowed to the next three feet of rock. My geological training was both a blessing and a curse. I could see the stress fractures in the rock before they failed. I could identify the unstable strata, the layers of looser conglomerate that were ready to give way. I saw death in every crack, every patch of loose gravel.

“Hold,” I yelled. We froze. Ahead of us, the ridge was bisected by the same crystalline vein we had followed on our ascent. Here, it was a band of shattered, milky quartz about twenty feet across. It looked like a river of broken glass, and it was vibrating, humming with the last echoes of the resonance we had created. The ground on either side of it was visibly shaking.

“It’s a zone of weakness,” I explained, shouting over the din. “The vibrations are probably helping to tear the mountain apart. We have to cross it.”

“It doesn’t look stable,” Anya stated, her voice tight with fear.

“It’s not,” I agreed. “But the slope to the left is all unconsolidated scree, it’s a guaranteed avalanche. The cliff on the right is already starting to shear off. This is the only way.”

Kaelen went first. He didn’t run. He moved with a low, deliberate crawl, distributing his weight, testing each placement of his hands and feet. The glassy shards shifted and chimed under his weight, a discordant, terrifying music. He made it to the other side and turned, waving us on. Anya went next, her face pale. I could see her reciting something under her breath, a mantra to keep her moving. She slipped once, her foot plunging into a gap between larger crystals, and my heart seized. But she recovered, pulling her leg free and scrambling the last few feet to Kaelen.

Then it was my turn. As I stepped onto the vibrating field of crystal, the humming intensified. It wasn't the psychic pressure from before. This was a purely physical vibration, the death rattle of the mountain’s strange bones. The shards were sharp and unstable. I felt a large one tilt under my boot and I froze, balancing on one leg. The entire vein groaned, a sound of a thousand tiny fractures propagating at once. I threw myself forward, half crawling, half falling, and landed in a heap on the solid basalt of the far side. Kaelen hauled me to my feet. We didn’t pause to look back.

Our next obstacle was a long, open slope, angled at a terrifying forty five degrees. It was a shooting gallery. Rocks, from the size of fists to the size of small cars, were constantly breaking free from the cliffs still high above us and bouncing down the slope in lethal, unpredictable trajectories. We had to cross it to reach the next ridge.

“We go one at a time,” Kaelen commanded. “Sprint. Don’t think, just run. I’ll go first. Wait for my signal.”

He pointed to a large boulder about two hundred yards away, our target island of safety. He took a breath, and then he was off, a blur of motion against the gray slope. A rock the size of a microwave screamed past, missing him by inches. He didn’t flinch. He just kept running, his legs pumping, a perfect machine of survival. He reached the boulder and dove behind it, then gave a sharp, two toned whistle.

Anya went next. Her sprint was more of a panicked scramble, but she was fast. A shower of smaller debris kicked up around her feet, but she made it, collapsing behind the boulder next to Kaelen. He whistled again. It was my turn. I pushed off, my lungs burning, my legs screaming in protest. The world became a tunnel. There was only the boulder, the goal. The sound of a projectile grew louder, a whistling shriek. I didn’t look up to see it. I just ran faster, fueled by pure adrenaline. I heard it impact the ground behind me, a heavy, wet thud that shook the slope. I dove the last ten feet, skidding on the gravel, and slammed into the relative safety of the rock.

We lay there for a minute, panting, our bodies slick with sweat and grime. We were maybe halfway down. The roar from the caldera was slightly more distant now, but the ground beneath us was still in constant, trembling motion. We shared a few swallows of water from Kaelen’s canteen. The liquid was warm and tasted of plastic, but it was the best thing I had ever tasted.

“The silence,” Anya said suddenly, her voice quiet but clear in a brief lull between rockfalls. We looked at her. “I was so afraid of the whispers,” she continued, looking not at us, but at the hazy, dust filled sky. “But this is worse. The silence where it used to be. It’s so loud.”

I knew what she meant. The absence of the entity was a palpable thing. The air no longer felt charged, no longer carried that weight of ancient, alien thought. Before, every rock and every gust of wind had felt imbued with a malevolent intelligence. Now, it was just rock and wind. It was mindless, indifferent, and somehow, that was more terrifying. We had slain the dragon, and now we were left to be crushed by the mindless collapse of its corpse.

Our path was blocked again. A massive fissure, a wound in the mountain’s flesh a hundred feet deep and fifty feet across, had opened up, severing our ridge. The groaning of the rock was louder here, the stresses more apparent. There was no way to climb down and up the other side. The walls of the fissure were sheer and constantly shedding rock.

“The rope,” Kaelen said simply. It was our last one, a hundred and fifty feet of high tensile climbing rope. Finding an anchor point was the problem. The rock on our side was fractured and unreliable. I spent ten minutes tapping, examining, and finally found a massive block of gneiss that seemed deeply rooted, separated from the more fractured surface rock. It was a gamble.

“This is the best we’re going to get,” I said. “It feels solid, but if we get a big enough tremor while someone’s on the rope…” I didn’t finish the sentence.

Kaelen worked with grim efficiency, wrapping the rope around the anchor block and securing it with knots I had only seen in textbooks. He tested it with his full weight, bouncing on it, his eyes watching the anchor for any sign of movement. It held.

“I’ll go first and set up a belay on the other side,” he said. “Anya, you follow. Doctor, you’re last. You’ll have to un-tie the anchor and jump the last few feet. I’ll try to swing the rope back to you, but no promises.”

He rappelled into the chasm, disappearing into the dusty gloom. The minutes stretched into an eternity. The rope twitched and swayed. A tremor shook our ledge, and a cascade of rocks fell into the fissure, their impacts echoing from the depths. I watched our anchor, my hand resting on the cool stone, trying to feel for any shift, any sign of failure. Then we heard Kaelen’s whistle from the other side. He was safe.

Anya followed, her movements slow and shaky, but she made it. Now it was just me. The plan was for me to rappel most of the way down, and then push off the wall, swinging across the chasm like a pendulum. Kaelen and Anya would be on the other side, acting as a counterweight, trying to pull me in. It was a desperate, clumsy maneuver.

I untied the anchor knot as instructed, re-tying a simpler loop that would allow Kaelen and Anya to haul the rope back once I was across. I lowered myself over the edge. Dangling in the middle of that raw, new gap in the world was profoundly unnerving. The scale of the forces at work was laid bare. I could see strata, bent and twisted by the mountain’s unnatural growth, now rent asunder. I reached the lowest point of the rope’s arc.

“Now,” Kaelen’s voice echoed across the gap.

I took a breath, kicked off the crumbling rock wall with all my strength, and swung out into the void. For a second, I was flying. The far wall rushed towards me. Hands grabbed at my pack, my jacket. Kaelen and Anya strained, pulling me up onto the ledge. I collapsed, my body screaming with a new collection of bruises. We had made it. We were on the last major ridge leading down to the foothills.

As we descended further, the slope began to gentle. The constant roar from above faded to a continuous, deep rumble. The worst of the collapse was over, leaving the mountain to settle and crumble at a more geological pace. We were no longer in immediate, second by second danger, but we were still moving through a landscape that had been violently remade only an hour before. The foothills were a chaotic jumble of newly deposited boulders and vast fields of scree.

The sky, which had been an unnaturally clear, static-tinged blue, was now darkening with real clouds, heavy and gray. A cold wind whipped around us, the first sign of a normal weather pattern returning to this blasted land. And as we picked our way through a field of debris, I saw something that made me stop.

The collapse had acted like a massive, violent excavation, exposing rock layers that must have been buried for millennia. In the side of a newly carved gully, I saw a strange stratum. It was a dark, glassy layer, about three feet thick, sandwiched between layers of ordinary sedimentary rock. It looked like obsidian, but it was contorted, fused, and shot through with fossilized, hollow tubes, like petrified lightning.

I stumbled over to it, running my hand over its unnervingly smooth surface. Kaelen and Anya came up behind me.

“What is it?” Anya asked.

My mind was racing, putting pieces together. The implosion. The wave of silence, of nothing. The way the black crystal had collapsed in on itself. This glassy layer, it was tektite, but not formed from a meteor impact. It was rock that had been instantaneously melted and then cooled, fused under an impossible pressure. It was the scar of a previous implosion.

“This has happened before,” I whispered, a new and different kind of dread washing over me. “This mountain… this entity… it’s a cycle. It grows, it matures, and then it collapses. Or something makes it collapse. And it leaves a scar like this.”

I pointed to the fossilized tubes. “This is what’s left of an old vein system. Buried. Dormant.”

The implications were staggering. We hadn’t ended a unique threat. We had merely triggered the end of one phase of a recurring, natural, and utterly alien life cycle. How many more of these scars were buried beneath our feet? How many other mountains on this planet, or others, harbored a similar potential? Our victory suddenly felt small, temporary. We hadn’t been giant killers. We had been midwives to a geological rebirth we could not possibly understand.

We stood there for a long moment, staring at the evidence of a timescale and a biology that dwarfed human comprehension. Then the first drop of cold rain hit my face. It was a real, physical sensation that broke the spell. We were still alive. We were still exhausted. And our camp was still a few miles away.

We left the glassy scar behind and trudged on. The land flattened out, and soon we were on the familiar gray dirt of the basin floor. We stumbled the last mile in a state of near delirium, our bodies running on the last fumes of adrenaline.

And then we saw it. The small cluster of domes, our base camp, untouched by the catastrophe. A haven of mundane reality in a world that had come undone. We practically fell through the flap of the main dome, collapsing onto the floor. The sound of the rumbling, dying mountain was a distant, ignorable thunder. The silence inside the tent was absolute, and this time, it was not the silence of an absent mind, but the simple, welcome silence of shelter. We had survived. We had won. But as I closed my eyes, the image of that black, glassy scar was burned onto the inside of my eyelids. The escape was over. But I had a chilling certainty that the story of the crystal mountains was not. We had only turned a single page.

Sleep did not come easily. It was a shallow, fitful thing, a half conscious state haunted by the memory of a world unmaking itself. Every time I drifted towards true oblivion, the silence would press in, not the welcome quiet of the dome but the predatory, cognitive void of the mountain’s final breath. I would surface with a gasp, my heart hammering against my ribs, the synthetic fabric of my sleeping bag slick with sweat. Kaelen snored softly on the far side of the dome, a sound so aggressively normal it was almost an insult. Anya was a still, silent lump in her own bag, but I knew she was not sleeping either. I could feel the tension radiating from her, a quiet hum of wakeful anxiety.

Hours passed. The dim emergency lights of the dome cast long, distorted shadows, turning familiar equipment into crouching beasts. I gave up on rest. I sat up, my muscles screaming in protest, every joint a point of grinding pain. I crawled over to the water dispenser and drank until my stomach ached, the cool liquid a balm on my raw throat. The world outside was quiet now. The deep, planetary rumbling had ceased, leaving behind only the gentle patter of rain on the dome’s taut skin. It was over. The cataclysm had passed.

I found myself at the main console, my fingers hovering over the activation pad. It was an involuntary act, the scientist in me overriding the exhausted survivor. With a soft chime, the screens flickered to life, bathing my face in a cool blue glow. I started with the seismographs. The data was apocalyptic. The primary graph was a solid block of red, an event so far off the scale that the sensors had simply maxed out, recording a flat line at their absolute upper limit for nearly fifteen minutes. The implosion. Then, a vertical drop to absolute zero. A line so flat and perfect it looked like an error. The zone of silence. It had lasted for four minutes and twelve seconds. Then, a chaotic scramble of aftershocks as the planet’s crust settled, gradually tapering off into the low, ambient hum of normal geology.

“You couldn’t stay away, could you.” Anya’s voice was soft, husky with fatigue. She had unzipped her bag and was sitting up, watching me.

“It’s better than staring at the ceiling,” I said, not looking away from the screen.

She crawled over and knelt beside me, her eyes tracing the violent story told by the data. “I keep thinking about the scar,” she said. “That black glass. You said it was a cycle.”

“I think it is,” I replied, my voice low. I pulled up the atmospheric composition logs. A massive, transient spike in argon and other noble gases, vented from the planet’s mantle by the rupture. Then a corresponding dip in everything as the vacuum of the implosion sucked in the local atmosphere, followed by a violent return to equilibrium. It was all there, a neat, clinical record of an impossible event.

Kaelen grunted from his sleeping bag. “Let it go. For one night, just let it go. We’re alive. We did the job. Let’s call that a win and get some sleep.”

“Kaelen’s right,” Anya said, though she didn’t move. Her gaze was still fixed on the screen. “We need to rest. We can debrief properly in the morning.”

“There’s something else,” I said, ignoring them both. My attention had been snagged by a secondary seismic feed, one from a series of deep core sensors we had deployed weeks ago, before we even knew the true nature of the mountain. These were designed to listen for subtle, deep earth tremors, not catastrophic surface events. Most of them had been obliterated, the connections severed during the collapse. But one, the furthest one, number seven, was still online. Its data log was… strange.

It had recorded the main event, of course, a chaos of overwhelming noise. It had recorded the silence. But after the main aftershocks had died down, after the planet’s crust should have returned to a state of relative calm, sensor seven was picking up a new signal. It was incredibly faint, a whisper buried deep beneath the background noise of a living planet. But it was there. And it was rhythmic. A slow, steady pulse. One beat every ninety three seconds.

“What is that?” Anya leaned closer, her exhaustion forgotten.

“I don’t know,” I said, running a diagnostic. The sensor was functioning perfectly. The signal was real. “It’s deep. Very deep. And it wasn’t there before.”

Kaelen sat up, scrubbing a hand over his face. He was a man who valued practicality and procedure above all else. Curiosity was a luxury he rarely afforded himself. But even he was intrigued. He lumbered over, peering at the screen over my shoulder. “A pulse?” he asked. “Like a magma pocket refilling?”

“No,” I said, shaking my head. I filtered the data, isolating the signal, amplifying it. The rhythmic line on the graph became clearer. It was too regular, too clean for any natural geological process I knew of. “Magma convection is chaotic. This is methodical. It’s like a heartbeat.”

A heavy silence descended on the three of us. The only sound was the faint hum of the console and the rain outside. A heartbeat. Deep beneath the basin. Deep beneath the fresh grave of the crystal entity.

“It’s coming from a position roughly under the old summit,” I said, triangulating the signal’s origin point. My finger traced a path on the holographic map. “But the depth is… it’s a good ten miles down. Far below the immediate collapse zone.”

“And it started after the collapse,” Anya whispered.

“Approximately one hour after the final major aftershock,” I confirmed, checking the timestamps.

Kaelen crossed his arms, his expression grim. “So we killed one of them, and it woke up its mother.”

“I don’t think it’s another creature,” I said, my mind racing back to the glassy scar, the tektite stratum. “The scar. The fossilized tubes. What if they’re not just evidence of a past event? What if they’re part of the process?”

“What process?” Kaelen demanded. “We just watched a mountain eat itself. What’s left to process?”

“Rebirth,” I said, the word tasting strange and momentous. “The entity we encountered, the crystal, it grew from something. It developed. It matured. We forced its collapse. The collapse creates immense, localized pressure and heat, melting the surrounding rock into that tektite layer, sealing the… the wound in the planet. But what if the collapse also serves another purpose? What if the energy released, the sheer physics of it, doesn't just vanish? What if it’s focused downwards? A seed of energy, planted deep in the mantle, to begin the cycle anew.”

Anya stared at me, her eyes wide. “So the new pulse… it’s the next one. Starting to grow.”

“It’s a hypothesis,” I said, but I believed it. The elegant, terrifying symmetry of it was undeniable. “The death of one is the conception of the next. And the scar isn't a tombstone. It's a womb. It’s a protective layer of sterile, fused rock under which the new life can gestate for millennia.”

Kaelen was quiet for a long time. He walked over to the dome’s small viewport and stared out into the drizzly darkness. “We need to leave,” he said, his voice flat. “We file the report. All of it. The collapse, the scar, the new signal. We tell them this planet is a nursery for god knows what, and we recommend a permanent, system wide quarantine. Then we get on the first long range transport and never, ever look back.”

He was right, of course. It was the sensible, sane thing to do. Our mission was observation and, if necessary, mitigation. We had done both. Our duty was fulfilled. But as I looked at the pulsing line on the screen, the slow, steady heartbeat of a geological embryo, I knew I couldn't leave. Not yet.

The next forty eight hours were a blur of methodical activity. We ate, we forced ourselves to sleep in scheduled shifts, and we worked. The exhaustion was still a physical weight on our bones, but the mystery was a powerful stimulant. We compiled our data, wrote our preliminary reports, and sent the initial burst transmission to command. It was coded, concise, and gave no hint of the true, paradigm shattering nature of our discovery. It simply reported mission success, catastrophic geological event, and recommended immediate follow up with a full geological survey team. It was a lie of omission, a way to buy ourselves time.

Kaelen argued against it. He wanted to send everything, to wash our hands of the entire affair. “This isn’t our discovery to sit on,” he’d insisted. “This is bigger than us. It’s bigger than the entire xenobiology department. This is a first contact scenario with a planet.”

“And how do we explain that in a preliminary report, Kaelen?” I’d countered. “We have a theory. A wild one. We need more than a single, faint seismic signal. We need physical evidence. We have to go back to the scar.”

He had fought me on it, but Anya had sided with me. Her scientific curiosity, once awakened, was a formidable thing. She saw the logic. A report like the one I was proposing would be buried in committee, dismissed as sensor malfunction or crew psychosis. We needed proof. We needed a sample. Reluctantly, Kaelen had agreed to one more excursion. One.

We set out on the third day. The rain had stopped, and the twin suns of the system burned in a sky scrubbed clean. The air was crisp and carried a strange, metallic tang, the ghost of vaporized rock. We took the rover this time, its heavy treads churning through the gray dirt. The landscape was altered but familiar, until we reached the foothills.

The change was absolute. Where once the crystalline slopes had risen, now there was a crater, a vast basin of shattered, conventional rock. At its center was not a pit, but a plateau, a newly raised landscape of jagged stone and steaming fissures. The implosion hadn't just created a void, it had pulled the mountain down into the planet, simultaneously causing a massive rebound of the surrounding crust. The physics were baffling.

We left the rover at the edge of the devastation and proceeded on foot. The ground was treacherous, a jumble of freshly broken rock, sharp as glass. We moved slowly, our sample kits and sensor packs heavy on our backs. It took us two hours to reach the location where I had seen the stratum of black glass.

It was still there, a stark, unnatural band in the wall of a newly formed canyon. The rain had washed it clean, and it gleamed under the alien suns. It was even more disturbing up close, in the light of day. It looked like a river of polished night, frozen in time. And the tubes, the hollow, fossilized channels that riddled it, seemed more prominent now.

“Okay,” Kaelen said, his voice tight with tension. “You’re here. What are you looking for?”

I didn’t answer. I unslung my pack and pulled out a portable resonance scanner. It was a delicate piece of equipment, designed to measure subtle vibrations in solid materials. I calibrated it and placed the contacts against the smooth, black surface of the tektite. The display remained flat. Zero.

“Nothing,” Kaelen said, a note of satisfaction in his voice. “It’s glass. Dead rock. Let’s get a core sample and get out of here.”

“Wait,” Anya said. She was crouched further down the line, peering intently at one of the fossilized tubes. “This one’s different.”

I moved over to her. Most of the tubes were hollow, their insides coated with a fine, gray dust. But the one she was looking at was not entirely empty. Deep inside, perhaps a foot into the rock, was a blockage. A dark, organic looking mass. It was impossible. Nothing organic could have survived this.

“The scanner,” I said, my voice barely a whisper. Anya moved aside, and I carefully placed the sensor contacts on the rock, right beside the opening of the tube.

The display flickered. A line jittered across the screen, then settled into a pattern. It was faint, almost imperceptible, but it was unmistakable. It was a perfect match for the deep seismic signal. A slow, rhythmic pulse, once every ninety three seconds.

My breath caught in my throat. I moved the scanner a few feet away. The signal vanished. I moved it back. It reappeared. The vibration was not coming from the rock itself. It was coming from whatever was inside that tube.

“It’s in there,” Anya breathed, her face pale. “It’s coming from in there.”

“What is it?” Kaelen asked, his pragmatic armor beginning to crack. He knelt beside us, staring from the scanner to the dark hole in the rock.

“The seed,” I said, my mind reeling. “It’s not a metaphor. The energy of the collapse didn’t just radiate downwards. It must have sent something physical. A particle, a spore, a packet of information on a quantum level, I don’t know. It shot down through the old vein system, the only thing that could survive the transit, and planted itself in the mantle. That’s the deep signal we’re reading. The embryo. But these… these aren’t just fossilized tubes. They’re… roots. Or receivers. They’re still connected.”

I took the scanner and placed it next to another tube, then another. Each one was the same. Dead silence. Only this one, the one with the organic looking blockage, carried the pulse.

We stood there, the three of us, kneeling before a wall of black glass in a shattered canyon on an alien world. We were listening to the echo of a heartbeat broadcast from ten miles beneath our feet, picked up by a single, seemingly fossilized root.

Kaelen finally found his voice. “We need to destroy it.”

“What?” I turned to him, shocked.

“That thing.” He pointed a trembling finger at the tube. “We have a laser drill in the rover. We can melt this entire section of rock. We can cut the root.”

“We can’t do that,” Anya said, horrified. “We don’t know what that would do. It could be completely unstable. We’re scientists, Kaelen, not soldiers.”

“We were soldiers two days ago when we brought a mountain down!” he shot back, his voice rising. “Look at this place! This is what it does. This is the result of one of them maturing. And we just found a new one. A baby. Are you seriously suggesting we just leave it here to grow?”

“He’s right,” I said quietly. Both of them looked at me, surprised. “Killing it might be the only responsible thing to do. But we can’t do it with a hand drill. We don’t know that this is the only connection. There could be thousands of these tubes buried in this stratum, stretching for miles. And we have no idea what would happen if we ‘cut the root’. It could trigger a feedback loop. It could accelerate the growth. It could cause another collapse. The new entity is ten miles deep in the planet’s mantle. We can’t get to it. We can’t kill it. All we can do is watch.”

The terrible logic of my own words settled over us. We were powerless. The scale of this life form, its life cycle, was geological. It operated on a level we could barely comprehend, let alone interfere with. We were like bacteria trying to perform surgery on a human being. Any action we took would be clumsy, ignorant, and likely catastrophic.

Kaelen sank back on his heels, the fight going out of him, replaced by a profound weariness. “So what do we do? We just sit here and listen to it grow?”

I looked at the scanner, at the slow, steady pulse. The heartbeat of a nascent god. I thought about the report we would have to write now. The sheer impossibility of what we had found. Our victory two days ago had been an illusion, a brief and meaningless act in a drama that had been playing out for eons. We had not been giant killers. We had not even been midwives. We were simply witnesses. The first conscious beings to ever understand the process.

“We take a sample,” I said, my decision made. “We take the blockage from the tube. We take a piece of the root. We take it all back. We analyze it. We learn everything we can. We are not its executioners, and we are not its guardians. We are its observers. That is our function now.”

I reached into my own kit and pulled out the remote micro extractor. It was a delicate, precise tool. As I carefully maneuvered its articulated arms into the dark opening of the tube, I knew, with a certainty that chilled me to the marrow, that our escape was a fiction. We had not left the mountain behind. We had just found its deepest, most vital part. And now, we were about to take a piece of it with us. We were no longer turning a page. We were stealing a word from the book, hoping we could learn the language before the author noticed it was missing.

The metal arms of the extractor whined softly, a high pitched counterpoint to the deep, resonant thrum of the planet. I guided them with a practiced steadiness I did not feel. My own heart was a frantic, irregular drum against my ribs. Elara stood beside me, her face illuminated by the extractor’s control panel, her eyes wide and fixed on the monitor that showed the tool’s perspective. Kaelen was a statue of coiled tension a few feet away, his hand resting on the grip of his own drill, a useless talisman against the immensity we faced.

The first target was the blockage itself. On the magnified view, it was not the simple plug of rock or mineral I had expected. It was a complex, layered structure, almost like a fungal bract, but with the crystalline sheen of obsidian. It had a disturbingly organic symmetry. I positioned the primary manipulator, a fine toothed cutting claw, at its edge. The plan was to shear off a small, manageable section.

“Easy,” Elara whispered, her breath fogging slightly in the cold, damp air. “The composition is unknown. Brittle fractures could contaminate the whole chamber.”

I did not need the warning. My hands were slick with sweat inside my gloves. I engaged the claw. It bit into the dark material with a grating screech that echoed unnervingly in the confined space. A fine powder, iridescent and black, puffed from the incision. The extractor’s internal sensors immediately began an atmospheric analysis. Before Elara could read the results, a tremor ran through the rock beneath our feet. It was not a violent quake, just a profound, gut wrenching shudder, as if a sleeping giant had shifted in its bed.

“Hold,” Kaelen said, his voice sharp. “Back it out. Now.”

“No,” I replied, my eyes locked on the monitor. “We’re committed. If we stop now we might get nothing.”

The tremor subsided, but the deep pulse from the scanner seemed to have changed. It was infinitesimally faster. A fraction of a beat per minute, but there. The change was real. I ignored the cold knot of fear tightening in my stomach and focused on the cut. The claw completed its arc. A crescent shaped wedge of the black material detached and floated gently in the low gravity of the extractor’s forward chamber. It was done. Phase one complete.

Now for the root. I retracted the cutting claw and extended a finer, more delicate tool, a biopsy needle designed for geological sampling. The ‘root’ itself was a different substance entirely. Where the blockage was crystalline and hard, the root was fibrous and pliant. It looked like a bundle of dark, thick cables, slick with a colorless fluid that did not bead or run but simply clung to its surface. It pulsed with a faint, internal light, a slow tide of bioluminescence that matched the rhythm of the deep entity’s heartbeat. It was undeniably, terrifyingly alive.

“I’m going in,” I announced, more to myself than to them.

I pushed the needle forward. The moment its tip made contact with the root’s surface, the bioluminescence flared. A wave of light washed down the visible length of the cable, away from us, into the depths. It was a signal. A nerve firing. On my control panel, a dozen small alarms blinked red. Proximity voltage. Static discharge. A spike in local gravitonics.

“It knows,” Kaelen breathed, his voice hollow with awe and terror. “It knows we’re here.”

“Stay calm,” Elara said, her voice tight but controlled. “It’s a reflex action. The system is reacting to a foreign body. Like an immune response.”

Her clinical explanation did nothing to soothe the primal fear that her words conjured. We were a virus. An infection. And the planetary organism was trying to fight us off.

I pushed the needle deeper. There was a soft resistance, like pushing a probe into dense muscle tissue. The needle’s core rotated, shearing off a tiny cylinder of the root material. The light from the root flared again, brighter this time, and a low moan echoed up from the tube, a sound so full of resonant power that I felt it in my bones, in my teeth. The rock around us groaned in sympathy. Small pebbles skittered from the ceiling of the cavern.

“Get it and let’s go!” Kaelen yelled, finally breaking his paralysis. “The whole damn cave is going to come down on us!”

I retracted the needle, the sample secured in its sterile core. I sealed the forward chamber of the extractor, locking our stolen pieces of genesis inside. The moment the seal was complete, the low moan from the tube ceased. The bioluminescence faded back to its faint, rhythmic pulse. The unsettling silence that fell was worse than the noise. It felt like a held breath.

I pulled the extractor back, its articulated arms folding into its housing. I did not look at the dark, violated opening of the tube. I could feel its emptiness like a gaze. I packed the extractor into its case, my movements stiff and automatic.

“We have it,” I said. “Let’s move. Full evac procedure. No detours.”

The journey back to the surface was a nightmare of inches and seconds. Every shadow seemed to writhe. Every gust of wind whistling through the tunnels sounded like a pursuing whisper. The faint, rhythmic pulse we had grown accustomed to was gone from our immediate surroundings, but I could still feel it, a phantom beat in my own blood. The world had not gone silent. It was listening.

We did not speak. The climb was arduous, a vertical ascent up kilometers of ladders and service elevators through the shafts we had drilled. The air grew thinner, cleaner. The oppressive humidity of the deep caverns gave way to the sterile, recycled atmosphere of the upper levels. Yet the feeling of being watched, of being measured, did not dissipate. It clung to us like the fine, black dust from the chrysalis, a particle of which I noticed had clung to the cuff of my glove. I stared at it for a long moment before deliberately wiping it away.

Kaelen’s earlier aggression had been burned away, leaving a brittle, paranoid shell. He kept glancing over his shoulder, his hand never straying from the sidearm holstered at his hip, as if he expected some physical manifestation of the entity to come crawling up the shaft after us. Elara was the opposite. She was turned inward, her mind clearly racing, processing, trying to fit the incomprehensible data into a logical framework. Her silence was more unnerving than Kaelen’s paranoia. It was the silence of someone who was beginning to understand the true scope of the problem and was horrified by the equation.

And me. I carried the case containing the extractor and its priceless, terrifying sample. It felt impossibly heavy, a weight that had nothing to do with its mass. It was a container of stolen fire, a seed of forbidden knowledge. With every step upward, away from the mantle, I felt its presence more acutely. It was a piece of the deep, a living fragment of a god, and we were bringing it into our world.

Hours later, we cycled through the final airlock and stepped into the clean, white corridor of the main habitation module. The familiar hum of the life support system, the bright, even lighting, the sheer mundanity of the station, felt alien and fragile. We had been somewhere else. Somewhere ancient and fundamental. Coming back here was like waking from a dream you could not quite remember, but which left you with a profound sense of loss and dread.

We took the sample directly to the primary containment lab. It was a room shielded by three feet of reinforced alloy and its own independent power and life support systems. Behind a thick pane of polarized glass, a set of heavy duty robotic arms waited. The protocol for handling unknown, potentially bioactive materials was strict. We had drilled it a hundred times. Now, it felt like a child’s game.

I placed the extractor into the transfer lock. We retreated to the observation room, the heavy door hissing shut behind us, sealing us away from it. Elara initiated the sequence. Inside the sealed chamber, the robotic arms carefully removed the sample cartridge and placed it into a large, gel filled biosphere. High resolution cameras zoomed in.

The two pieces lay suspended in the clear gel. The crescent of black chrysalis material was inert, its sharp edges seeming to drink the light. But the piece of the root, the tiny gray cylinder no bigger than my thumb, was not. It was pulsing. A faint, white light throbbed within it, in perfect synchronicity with the data still streaming from the deep range scanner. We were ten miles from the source, yet here was a piece of it, still connected, still alive, still beating with the heart of its parent.

“My God,” Elara whispered, her hands pressed against the glass. “The cellular structure is… it’s reorganizing. Look at the spectrograph.”

A screen to her right flickered with data. Mass was increasing. Not by much, a few micrograms a minute, but it was a physical impossibility. It was converting the suspension gel into its own substance. It was growing.

“It’s feeding,” Kaelen said, his voice rough. He stood at the back of the room, as far from the window as he could get. “We brought a parasite up here. We brought it right into our home.”

“It’s not a parasite,” Elara countered, her scientific curiosity overriding her fear. “It’s a cutting. A graft. It’s behaving exactly as a severed piece of a colonial organism would. It’s trying to establish a new colony. It’s trying to grow into a new entity right here, in this tank.”

The implications of that were staggering. We had not just taken a sample. We had taken a seed.

“Can we destroy it?” Kaelen asked, his voice rising again, a familiar desperation creeping back in. “Incinerate the whole damn lab?”

“And what happens when we do?” I said, my voice flat. I was watching the pulse. Watching the data streams. “What does the parent do when it feels its offspring die? Does it lash out? Does it wake up? We know less than nothing, Kaelen.”

My gaze drifted to the main comms panel in the corner of the observation room. The report. I had to write the report. How could I put any of this into the cold, factual language of a corporate geologist? ‘Item 1: Recovered sample of suspected macro-organism. Item 2: Sample exhibits spontaneous growth and energy production. Item 3: Said organism may or may not be a planetary scale life form capable of causing geological upheaval.’ It was absurd. It was the stuff of madness.

I walked over to the console, leaving Elara and Kaelen to their horrified vigil at the window. I opened a new file, the cursor blinking on the blank page. My fingers hovered over the keyboard. I was supposed to be the one in charge, the calm center. But my mind was a storm. The weight of my decision to take the sample, to bring this ticking bomb aboard, was crushing me.

I typed a single sentence. ‘Preliminary findings from Site Gamma.’

Then I stopped. My eyes were drawn to the bank of monitors displaying long range seismic data. The feed from the new entity, the one we had just disturbed, was on the main screen. The pulse was steady, strong. Faster than before we arrived, but stable. It had found a new rhythm.

But it was a secondary monitor that made the blood freeze in my veins. It was displaying the data feed from our first discovery. From the mountain we had brought down. The entity we had ‘killed’. The site was supposed to be geologically inert now, a dead zone of fractured rock.

A single red light was blinking on the panel. An alert I had not seen in three days.

“Elara,” I said, my voice barely a whisper. “What’s sensor package twelve?”

She tore her eyes from the biosphere and looked at the alert. Her face went pale. “That’s… that’s the primary thermal sensor. The one we drilled into the core of the first entity’s main nerve cluster. The one we used to confirm it was dead.”

On the screen, a graph was slowly building. A gentle, upward curve. The temperature, deep in the heart of the dead mountain twenty miles away, was rising. It was only a few degrees, but it was unmistakable. And next to it, another sensor was activating. A low frequency acoustic monitor. It was picking something up. A sound.

It was a low, resonant thrum.

I looked from the thermal graph of the ‘dead’ parent to the live feed of the biosphere where our sample, the piece of the ‘child’, was pulsing with light. Then I looked at the scanner showing the heartbeat of the new god, ten miles deep. The pieces clicked into place with the terrible, final sound of a tomb door sealing shut.

The moan we had heard in the cavern. The flare of light. It was not a reflex. It was not a warning. It was a cry. A child, wounded, crying out for its parent. And twenty miles away, across a gulf of dead rock and shattered strata, the parent was beginning to stir. It had heard the cry.

We had not stolen a word from the book. We had screamed it. And now, the author was awake. And it was angry. The blinking red light was not an anomaly. It was an answer.
