keys, a strange stillness fell upon him. ‘You seek the source of the music,’ a voice whispered, so close it seemed to be a thought of his own. ‘I am the music, and I am its prisoner.’ Julian’s hands froze above the ivory. ‘You are the Lady of the Spire,’ he breathed, remembering the local tales. ‘Yes, the Spire. The place of endless waiting. To be stone and dust, to feel the wind but not the sun. To see the seasons turn, but never to turn with them. To have no voice, to have no name. To be utterly forgotten. You can help me. You can sound the chord that shatters the spire’s silence, for Art is born of you, and Art is truer than Time is.’ Julian felt a tremor of profound cold, not of the body but of the soul, and the room seemed to shrink around him. It was a waking nightmare, exquisite and terrifying. Then the Lady spoke again, her voice like the rustle of ancient parchment. ‘Have you not seen the verse carved into the rosewood frame?’ ‘Many times,’ whispered the young man, his eyes wide; ‘I have traced the letters with my finger. They are in a strange, flowing script, and nearly worn away. There are only six lines: When the artist’s hand can find Music lost to heart and mind, When the lonely marble weeps, And a waking promise keeps, Then the ancient stone will fall And silence answer to the call. But I have never understood their meaning.’ ‘They mean,’ she said, her voice full of a sorrow that spanned centuries, ‘that you must play for me the song of my heart, for I have no hands, and weep for me for my solitude, because I have no tears, and then, if you have always been true, and kind, and brave, the Spirit of Creation will grant me release. You will hear the mocking of failed ambitions, and the wails of forgotten dreams will claw at your mind, but they cannot touch you, for against the purity of a true artist the phantoms of despair are powerless.’ Julian did not speak, and the Lady seemed to sigh, a sound like the tide receding from a shingled shore as she watched his bowed, thoughtful head. Suddenly he rose, his face pale but resolute, a fire in his dark eyes. ‘I am not afraid,’ he said clearly, ‘and I will play the chord that sets you free.’ A faint ripple of joy, like the chiming of a crystal glass, filled the air, and as he reached for the keys, a cool draft touched his hand, a caress as light as a moth’s wing and as final as a vow. His fingers were as nimble as ever, but the notes that rose from the instrument were strange and dissonant. On the gilded wallpaper were painted cherubs in a pastoral scene. They dropped their tiny lutes and with plump hands gestured for him to cease. ‘Cease! gentle Julian,’ they seemed to plead, ‘cease!’ but a will not his own now guided his hands, and he could not look away from the keys. Hideous gargoyles with leering faces, carved into the legs of a nearby table, seemed to chatter at him and gnash their wooden teeth. ‘Beware! gentle Julian, beware! you may never play again,’ but the strange melody swelled more loudly, and Julian did not hear. When he struck the final, crashing chord, he stopped, and an unearthly silence descended. He looked up, and saw the far wall of the room shimmer and dissolve like smoke, revealing a spiral staircase made of grey, luminous stone. A scent of damp earth and crushed ivy drifted towards him, and he felt a gentle pull on his sleeve. ‘Hurry, hurry,’ whispered the Lady, ‘or the way will be closed,’ and, in a moment, the wallpaper had reformed behind him, and the Music Room was empty.

CHAPTER IX

A QUARTER of an hour later, the great bell tolled for supper, and, as Julian did not appear, his aunt, Lady Ashworth, sent a maid to fetch him from the Music Room. Presently she returned, her face quite pale, and declared that she could not find Mr. Julian anywhere. As he was prone to taking long, solitary walks along the cliffs before sundown, Lady Ashworth was not immediately concerned, but when seven o’clock chimed from the hall clock, and Julian had not returned, she grew deeply alarmed, and sent the footmen out to search the grounds, while she and Sir Thomas checked every chamber in the manor. At half-past seven the footmen returned to report they had found no sign of the young man at all. The whole household was now in a state of considerable panic, and did not know what to do, when Sir Thomas suddenly recalled that, only the day before, he had seen a group of fishermen mending their nets down in the cove. He therefore immediately made for the cliff path, where he knew their cottages were, accompanied by his eldest son and the head groundskeeper. Miss Eleanor Vance, who was quite overcome with worry, begged to be permitted to join them, but Sir Thomas would not hear of it, as he feared the path might be treacherous in the fading light. Upon reaching the cove, however, he discovered the fishermen had already put out for the night tide, and it was clear they had been gone for some hours, as their lamps were but faint specks upon the horizon. Having sent his son and the groundskeeper to search the length of the beach, he hurried back to the manor, and sent a rider to the constabulary in the next town, telling them to keep an eye out for a young man who may have lost his way or been accosted by smugglers. He then had his own mare saddled, and, after ordering his wife and daughter to take some soup, rode off towards the Old Lighthouse Road with a groom. He had scarcely gone a mile, however, when he heard the frantic patter of feet behind him, and, turning, saw Miss Vance running, her hair having come undone and her face streaked with tears. ‘I am dreadfully sorry, Sir Thomas,’ she panted, ‘but I cannot rest while Julian is missing. Please, do not be cross with me; if you had not invited him to this dreary place, he would never have been in such peril. You will not make me go back, will you? I cannot! I will not!’ The Baronet could not help but be moved by the girl’s passionate distress and her loyalty to his nephew, so leaning down from his saddle, he helped her up behind him and said, ‘Well, Eleanor, if you will not be told I suppose you must come along, but I must insist you take my coat at Portsmouth.’ ‘Oh, never mind a coat! I want Julian!’ cried the young lady, weeping, and they rode on to the port town. There Sir Thomas enquired of the harbour master if any man matching Julian’s description had been seen booking passage on a ship, but he could learn nothing of him. The harbour master, however, sent word by lantern to the patrol boats, and assured him that a close watch would be kept, and, after procuring a heavy shawl for Miss Vance from a shopkeeper who was locking his doors for the night, Sir Thomas rode on to Blackmoor, a desolate stretch of marshland which he knew was a common shortcut for travellers on foot. Here they woke the old toll-keeper, but could get no useful intelligence from him, and, after riding all about the marsh’s edge, they turned their horses’ heads for home, and arrived at the manor near midnight, utterly exhausted and with spirits at their lowest ebb. They found the son and the groundskeeper waiting for them at the stables with lanterns, as the courtyard was wreathed in a thick sea-fog. Not a single trace of Julian had been found. The fishermen had been hailed on their return to the cove, but he was not with them, and they explained that the tide had turned early, forcing them to leave sooner than they had planned. Indeed, they were quite concerned to learn of Julian’s absence, as they were very fond of the young man for his gentle manner, and three of their number had offered to stay and help search the caves at first light. The ornamental lake had been dragged, and the entire estate thoroughly searched, but to no avail. It was certain that, for this night at least, Julian was gone from them; and it was in a state of the most profound gloom that Sir Thomas and his son walked back to the house, the groom leading the weary mare behind them. In the hall they found a huddle of anxious servants, and slumped in a wing-backed chair in the drawing room was poor Lady Ashworth, beside herself with grief and terror, and being tended to with smelling salts by her personal maid. Sir Thomas at once insisted she try to eat something, and called for sandwiches and hot tea for the entire party. It was a miserable affair, as no one had the heart to speak, and even the usually boisterous dogs were quiet and subdued, as they had a great affection for the young master. When they had finished, Sir Thomas sent everyone to bed, though he knew they would not sleep, and he sat alone in the great, silent house, listening to the mournful cry of the foghorn, and waiting for the dawn.

The first light of dawn was a cruel and hesitant thing, a mere dilution of the night’s blackness into a uniform and chilling grey. It brought with it no warmth, no promise of a new day, but served only to reveal the full extent of the fog’s dominion, which clung to the house and grounds like a shroud. Sir Thomas had not moved from his chair, save to replenish the fire, and he watched the ghostly transformation of the world outside the long drawing room windows, his mind a turmoil of grim possibilities. The cry of the foghorn, which had been his only companion through the interminable hours, now seemed to fall silent, replaced by the slow, reluctant stirring of the house. He heard a floorboard creak above, the distant clatter of a poker in the servants’ quarters, the soft padding of feet in the hall. The world was waking to his private nightmare.

He rose stiffly, his joints aching with cold and weariness, and made his way to the kitchen. Cook was already there, her face pale and her eyes red-rimmed, moving about her duties with a mechanical slowness. She looked up as he entered, her expression one of profound sympathy, and curtsied. ‘Sir Thomas,’ she murmured. ‘I’ll have coffee on directly. And some breakfast for the men.’ Sir Thomas nodded, unable to summon the words for a proper reply. He was grateful for her quiet competence, for the simple, sustaining routines of life that persisted even in the face of such catastrophe. Soon his son, Arthur, joined him, his young face etched with a sleepless worry that made him look years older. He carried a heavy seaman’s coat over his arm. ‘For the caves, Father,’ he said, his voice rough. ‘The wind will be bitter off the water.’

And then Eleanor appeared in the doorway, a wraith in her travelling dress from the day before, which was now creased and stained. Her hair was unkempt, and dark circles bruised the delicate skin beneath her eyes, but those eyes themselves burned with a fierce, unyielding light. ‘I am coming with you,’ she stated, and it was not a question or a request, but a declaration of incontrovertible fact. Sir Thomas opened his mouth to object, to insist she remain in the warmth of the house with his wife, but the look on her face stopped him. He saw in her the same stubborn refusal to accept defeat that he felt in his own heart, a desperate love that would not, could not, be idle. He simply nodded, and gestured for Cook to pour a third cup of the strong, black coffee.

The party that set out for the cove was a sombre one. Sir Thomas, Arthur, and Eleanor rode on horseback, while the three fishermen, whose names were Jed, Silas, and young Finn, walked alongside, carrying coils of rope, lanterns, and stout wooden poles. The fog was so thick that the world seemed to shrink to a small, moving circle around them, the muffled clopping of the horses’ hooves and the crunch of boots on the gravel path the only sounds in the damp, still air. When they reached the cliffs, the descent to the cove was treacherous, the narrow path slick with moisture and loose stones. The fishermen moved with a practiced ease, their sea-boots finding sure purchase where the others faltered, and they offered their calloused hands to help Eleanor down the steepest parts.

The cove itself was a place of desolate beauty. The tide was out, revealing a wide expanse of wet sand littered with seaweed and pockmarked by rock pools. The sea, a churning sheet of grey, hissed and sighed at the edge of the shore, and the black, jagged mouths of the sea caves dotted the base of the cliffs. The fishermen, who knew these caves as well as they knew their own cottages, began their work with a grim efficiency. ‘We’ll start with the Grotto, sir,’ said Jed, the eldest of the three, his voice a low rumble. ‘It’s the largest. The tide fills it nigh to the roof when it’s high, but there are ledges. A man could climb up to wait it out, if he knew the way of it.’

They left Eleanor at the entrance with a lantern, with strict instructions not to stray, and the four men ventured into the dripping darkness. She stood on the cold sand, the lantern light casting a small, wavering pool of gold around her feet, and listened. She could hear the shouts of the men, their voices echoing strangely from within the rock, mingling with the ceaseless, mournful boom of the waves. It was a sound of profound loneliness, the sound of the world’s great, empty heart beating in the dark. Time stretched and warped. She paced back and forth, her shawl clutched tightly around her, her gaze fixed on the black opening of the cave. Every shadow seemed to take on a familiar shape, every drip of water sound like a footstep. Hope and terror warred within her, each gaining the upper hand for a fleeting, agonising moment.

After what felt like an eternity, the men emerged, their faces grim. ‘Nothing, miss,’ Silas said gently, shaking his head. ‘Not a sign. We’ve checked every ledge, every cranny. If he were in there, we’d have found him.’ They moved on to the next cave, a narrower fissure known as the Devil’s Chimney, and then the next, and the next. Each time the result was the same. The methodical search, the echoing calls, the slow, head-shaking return to the pale light of day. The initial, desperate hope that had sustained them began to wither, replaced by a creeping, cold dread. Sir Thomas’s shoulders seemed to slump with the weight of each failure, and even the fishermen’s stoic resolve began to fray at the edges.

Eleanor, however, refused to be cowed by the grim reality, her jaw set in a line as hard and unyielding as the cliffs themselves. While the men prepared to enter the last and most treacherous of the caves, a low, water-filled passage that was only accessible for a short time at low tide, her eyes scanned the cliff face. She remembered walks with Julian along this very stretch of coast. He had not been one for the dark, wet caves; he had called them the sea’s dreary cellars. He had loved the light, the high places, the precarious paths that wound up the side of the cliffs to the headland, where the wind blew fresh and the gulls wheeled and cried. He had a favourite spot, a small, grassy ledge sheltered by an overhang of rock, where he would sit for hours with his sketchbook, capturing the dramatic sweep of the coastline.

An idea, sharp and insistent, pierced through her fog of grief. She looked at the men, huddled in consultation, their backs to her. They would not allow it; they would tell her it was too dangerous, that she must wait, that she must be sensible. But she could not wait. The need to do something, anything, was a physical ache, a fire in her blood. Slipping away from the group, she began to climb. The path Julian had shown her was barely a path at all, more a series of footholds kicked into the turf and rock. It was steep and slippery, and her long skirts hampered her at every step, but she scrambled upwards, her gloved hands grabbing at tufts of tough grass and outcrops of rock, her heart pounding with exertion and a wild, unreasoning hope.

She did not dare to look down. She focused only on the next handhold, the next patch of solid ground, her breath coming in ragged gasps. Finally, she hauled herself over the last ridge and onto the sheltered ledge. It was exactly as she remembered it: a small, level patch of green, protected from the wind, with a commanding view of the grey sea and the fog-bound horizon. And there, tucked into a crevice in the rock wall, as if placed there for safekeeping, was a leather-bound sketchbook.

With a cry that was half sob, half gasp of triumph, she seized it. It was his. She knew it instantly by the worn corners and the soft, supple feel of the leather. Her fingers, numb with cold and trembling with emotion, fumbled with the clasp. She sank to her knees on the damp grass and opened it. The first few pages were filled with familiar scenes: a quick, charcoal study of the manor’s chimneys against a stormy sky; a detailed pencil drawing of a spray of sea pinks; a loving, if slightly inexpert, portrait of his favourite hound, Hector, asleep by the fire. Then there was a sketch of her, done from memory, her head tilted to one side, a smile playing on her lips. A tear fell onto the page, smudging the delicate pencil work, and she hastily wiped it away.

She turned the page, and her breath caught in her throat. The style changed abruptly. The calm, familiar subjects were gone, replaced by a series of frantic, hurried sketches. There was a drawing of a ship, but it was unlike any of the fishing vessels from the cove or the naval frigates at Portsmouth. It was a dark, foreign-looking vessel with a high, curving prow and a single, tattered sail. It looked ancient, almost piratical. On the next page, there were more sketches of the same ship, drawn from different angles, as if Julian were trying to commit every detail to memory. And then she saw the figure.

He was drawn on a fresh page, a full-length study, and the detail was astonishing, rendered with a nervous, obsessive energy. He was tall and gaunt, with a long, narrow face and eyes that seemed to stare out from the page with a burning intensity, even in a pencil sketch. He wore rough, dark clothing of a foreign cut, and a long scarf was wrapped around his neck and lower face, obscuring his mouth and chin. But it was the man’s expression that held her transfixed. It was not overtly menacing, but there was a deep and unsettling strangeness to it, a look of profound and ancient weariness mixed with a predatory stillness. Beside the drawing, in Julian’s elegant script, were a few hastily scrawled words: ‘The passenger. From the Isolda. He watches.’

Eleanor stared at the drawing, a cold dread seeping into her bones that was far more chilling than the sea air. This was no accident. Julian had not slipped and fallen. He had not been swept away by the tide. He had seen something, someone, he was not supposed to see. This strange man, this passenger from a ghost ship, had been watching the coast, and Julian had been watching him.

Scrambling to her feet, the sketchbook clutched to her chest like a shield, she began the perilous descent. She no longer cared about the danger, the slippery path, the sharp rocks. She was filled with a new and terrible energy, the certainty of a hunter who has found the spoor of her quarry. She half slid, half fell the last few feet onto the beach, landing in a heap on the sand, heedless of the shocked cries of the fishermen who rushed to her side.

‘Miss Vance! Are you hurt?’ cried Sir Thomas, his face a mask of alarm as he helped her to her feet.

‘I’m fine,’ she gasped, pushing them away, her eyes wild. ‘I’ve found something. Not Julian. Something he left.’

She thrust the sketchbook into Sir Thomas’s hands, her finger stabbing at the page with the drawing of the stranger. ‘Here,’ she said, her voice shaking but clear. ‘Look. He was not alone. Someone was with him.’

The men gathered around, their faces pale in the misty light. Sir Thomas stared at the drawing, his brow furrowed in intense concentration. He traced the lines of the strange ship with his finger, then his gaze fell upon the gaunt, scarfed figure. A low growl rumbled in his chest. The despondency that had weighed him down fell away, replaced by a cold, hard fury. All the vague, terrifying spectres of accidental death and lonely drowning vanished, and in their place solidified this one, tangible image: a hawk-faced man on a dark ship.

‘Jed,’ Sir Thomas said, his voice dangerously quiet. ‘Have you ever seen a vessel like this in these waters?’

The old fisherman peered at the drawing, his brow crinkled. ‘No, Sir Thomas. Never. That’s no English ship, nor French neither. Looks more like something from the Baltic, or further. And the name, Isolda… it’s not one I know.’

Sir Thomas snapped the book shut. The search for a body was over. A different kind of hunt was about to begin. He looked from the drawing to the grey, indifferent sea, then to the determined, tear-streaked face of the young woman who had just changed everything. ‘Arthur,’ he commanded, his voice ringing with a renewed authority that made his son stand taller. ‘Ride back to the house. Tell your mother that we have found… hope. Then ride on to Portsmouth. Do not go to the harbour master this time. Go to the magistrate. Tell him I have reason to believe my son has been taken, and I require warrants and men. Give him this description.’ He ripped the page with the drawing of the man from the sketchbook and handed it to his son. ‘Tell him to send word to every port on the south coast. The ship is the Isolda. The man… the man is our only lead.’

He turned to Eleanor, and his expression softened for a moment. He placed a hand on her shoulder. ‘You have done well, my dear. You have done more than all of us. Now, you must come home.’

‘No,’ she said, her chin high, her gaze fixed on the endless grey horizon. ‘I am not going home. Not until we find him.’ Sir Thomas looked at her, at the fire in her eyes that mirrored his own, and he knew he would not win that argument. For this night, Julian was still gone from them; but he was no longer merely lost. He was taken. And they would move heaven and earth to get him back.

The salt spray clung to them, cold and damp, a constant reminder of the sea that had yielded its grim secret. The other men, fishermen and tenants who had joined the search out of duty and respect, stood back, their quiet murmurings silenced by the raw energy now radiating from Sir Thomas and the young woman at his side. The discovery of the book had not brought comfort, not yet, but it had lanced the stagnant abscess of grief, letting loose a torrent of purpose.

Jed cleared his throat, his gaze still on the grey water. ‘If that ship came from the Baltic, Sir Thomas, she would have needed to round the Skaw and come down through the North Sea. Or else through the Kiel, if she were small enough. Either way, it is a long voyage. They would have needed to take on provisions.’

‘Portsmouth,’ Sir Thomas said, his mind racing. ‘They must have put in at Portsmouth. Julian met them there. He sketched them there.’ He looked again at the sketchbook, which he still held. He had ripped out the one page for Arthur, but the rest remained. A history of his son’s last days. He could not bear to look through it now. It felt like a violation, yet it was also his most sacred text.

He turned to the old fisherman. ‘Jed, I need you and your men. Not to search the coves. I want eyes on the water. A watch on every high point from here to Selsey Bill. Any foreign vessel, anything that does not look like a coastal trader or a fishing smack, I want to know of it. Send a boy to me at the house the instant you see anything.’

‘Aye, Sir Thomas,’ Jed said, his weathered face set in grim lines. The hunt for one of their own, lost to the waves, was a task they understood. A hunt for a kidnapper, a foreign villain, was something new, something that stirred an older, more primal anger in their seafaring blood.

Sir Thomas put a firm hand on Eleanor’s arm. ‘Come. We must go back to the house.’ This time, she did not resist. Her body was wracked with shivers, a combination of the cold and the violent adrenaline that had coursed through her. The fire in her eyes remained, but it was banked now, simmering beneath a layer of profound exhaustion.

The walk back up the winding path from the shore was a silent one. The men dispersed, heading to their appointed posts, their steps imbued with a new gravity. The small procession that returned to Ashton Park was just Sir Thomas and Eleanor, two figures moving as one against the bleak landscape. Their steps were no longer the heavy, uncertain shuffle of mourners but the firm, rhythmic tread of soldiers marching from their first, costly skirmish.

The great oak doors of the house swung open before they reached them, framing the anxious face of the butler. Behind him, in the grand hall, stood Lady Ashton. She rushed forward, her hands outstretched, her face a fragile porcelain mask of hope and terror.

‘Thomas? Arthur said… he said you had found hope.’ Her eyes darted to Eleanor, then back to her husband, searching their faces for the news she both craved and dreaded.

‘We have, my dear,’ Sir Thomas said, his voice gentler than it had been all day. He led her into the drawing room, away from the prying eyes of the staff, and sat her down on a velvet chaise. Eleanor followed, a silent shadow.

‘Julian is not… he did not drown,’ Sir Thomas began, choosing his words with care. ‘We believe he was taken. Abducted, from a ship.’

Lady Ashton’s hand flew to her mouth, a small, muffled gasp escaping her lips. The hope she had entertained, a desperate vision of Julian found alive on some deserted stretch of coast, shattered. This new reality was in some ways more terrible. Drowning was an act of God, a tragedy of nature. Abduction was an act of men, an evil, calculated cruelty. Her hope was a fragile, terrified thing, a belief in miraculous survival; it was not the cold, sharp weapon her husband now wielded.

‘Taken?’ she whispered, her eyes wide with horror. ‘By whom? Why?’

‘We do not know why,’ Sir Thomas said, his voice hardening again. ‘But we know who. Or at least, we have his likeness.’ He did not have the drawing to show her, but he described the hawk faced man, the strange ship, the name Isolda.

Eleanor stood by the window, looking out at the gardens where she and Julian had walked just a week before. The manicured lawns and blooming roses seemed to belong to another world, a peaceful, orderly existence that had been utterly annihilated. She could feel Lady Ashton’s gaze upon her, feel the weight of the older woman’s sorrow. Lady Ashton saw her as a fellow victim, a poor girl whose future had been stolen. But Eleanor did not feel like a victim. She felt like a hunter.

‘You must be exhausted, my poor child,’ Lady Ashton said, her voice trembling with sympathy. ‘You should rest. I will have a room prepared.’

‘Thank you, Lady Ashton, but I cannot rest,’ Eleanor replied, her voice quiet but unyielding. She turned from the window. ‘There is too much to do.’

Sir Thomas nodded in agreement, a flicker of pride in his eyes. ‘Eleanor is right, Margaret. This is no time for rest. This is a time for war.’ He strode from the room, his purpose carrying him towards his study, the nerve centre of his estate and, now, of this new campaign.

Eleanor remained with Lady Ashton, a gulf of understanding between them. The mother could only see the loss, the horror of her son in the hands of unknown evils. The woman who loved him saw only the objective: to get him back. The means were secondary. The cost was irrelevant.

Later, as dusk bled into a long and starless night, Eleanor found Sir Thomas in his library. The room smelled of old leather and beeswax, a scent of stability and knowledge. He was seated at his vast mahogany desk, the salvaged sketchbook open before him. Maps were spread out across its surface, charts of the English Channel and the North Sea. He had lit a dozen candles, and their flickering light cast long, dancing shadows on the walls, making the room feel like a general’s tent on the eve of battle.

‘May I come in?’ she asked from the doorway.

He looked up, his face etched with fatigue but his eyes burning with intensity. ‘Of course, my dear. Come.’

She approached the desk and looked down at the sketchbook. He had turned to a new page. It showed a series of detailed studies of knots. To Eleanor, they were just intricate loops and whorls of rope.

‘I never paid his drawings much mind,’ Sir Thomas said, his voice low and regretful. ‘They were a pastime. A pleasant diversion. Now… now they are the only voice he has left. Look at these.’ He pointed to the knots. ‘These are not sailors’ knots, not English ones. I have had Jed look at them. He says they are more akin to the work of a rigger from the Low Countries, or perhaps Scandinavia.’

Every page held a potential clue. A sketch of a seabird, a gannet, but with a note in Julian’s elegant script beside it: ‘Follows the foreign barque as if it were home’. A quick charcoal of a face in a crowd at the Portsmouth market, a man with distinctive tattoos on his hands, seen talking to the hawk faced man near the docks. They were fragments, pieces of a puzzle they did not yet understand, but they were something.

‘Isolda,’ Eleanor said softly, looking at the name scrawled beneath the drawing of the ship. ‘It is the German form of Iseult, is it not?’

Sir Thomas nodded, reaching for a heavy, leather bound book on his shelf. ‘I looked it up. A story of a princess carried across the sea to be a bride, a tragic love, a fated journey. It is a name steeped in romance and sorrow.’ He let the book fall closed with a heavy thud. ‘Perhaps the captain is a man of poetry. Or perhaps it is merely a name.’

They sat in silence for a long time, the only sound the rustle of paper as Sir Thomas turned the pages of his son’s last testament. They were two solitary lighthouses, their beams of concentration fixed on the same dark point in the ocean of uncertainty. They were no longer father and prospective daughter in law; they were allies, bound by a single, sacred objective.

It was well past midnight when the sound of a horse being ridden hard reached them from the long drive. The frantic, galloping rhythm was a sound of urgency, of news. Both Sir Thomas and Eleanor were on their feet in an instant, their shared vigil broken. They met Arthur in the main hall, his face flushed and his clothes spattered with mud.

‘The magistrate will see you in the morning, Father,’ Arthur said, breathless. ‘He was… skeptical at first. But the drawing, and your name, convinced him. He has sent riders to every port from Dover to Plymouth. The description of the man and the ship will be with every harbour master by dawn.’

‘And the warrants?’ Sir Thomas demanded.

‘Granted. And he has assigned men. A dozen constables will be at our disposal by sunrise. But Father…’ Arthur hesitated, his gaze dropping for a moment. ‘He said it is a fool’s errand. The ship, the Isolda, a barque of foreign make with a dark hull, was sighted. It was seen leaving the Solent two days ago, sailing east. The harbormaster’s log confirms it. He said by now it could be anywhere. Holland, Denmark… the sea is wide. The magistrate believes they are long gone.’

A muscle twitched in Sir Thomas’s jaw. The law. Slow, methodical, and already resigned to failure. A dozen constables to scour the ports when the quarry was already on the open sea. It was a gesture, not an action.

‘They may be gone from our shores,’ Sir Thomas said, his voice a low growl. ‘But they are not gone from this earth.’ He turned and walked back towards the library, his son and Eleanor following in his wake. He stood before the maps on his desk, a general reviewing his field of battle.

‘The magistrate is a fool if he thinks I will wait for his constables to write their reports,’ he said, his finger tracing a line across the Channel. ‘They sail east. Jed said their knots were from the Low Countries. They will make for Amsterdam, or Antwerp. A busy port, where one ship can disappear among a hundred others.’

He looked at Arthur, his eyes blazing. ‘Your ride to Portsmouth was but the first step. Now you will ride again. Go to Captain Wallace. Tell him I need his ship, the Sea Serpent. And I need his best crew. Tell him money is no object, and I will pay double for speed. I want to be ready to sail on the evening tide tomorrow.’

Arthur stared at his father, his exhaustion forgotten, replaced by a surge of awe and excitement. This was not a plea for help; this was a declaration of war. ‘Yes, Father.’

‘I will be going with you,’ Sir Thomas stated, his decision absolute.

‘And I,’ Arthur added, his chin set.

A silence fell. Both men looked at Eleanor. She stood straight and tall, her hands clasped before her, her expression unreadable but for the unwavering intensity in her eyes.

‘You will remain here, my dear,’ Sir Thomas said, his tone softening, but still a command. ‘You will be safe here with Lady Ashton. You have done more than enough.’

‘No,’ she said. The word was not loud, but it filled the room, silencing all argument. She stepped forward, closer to the desk, and placed her hand flat on Julian’s sketchbook. ‘I will not stay here to wait and to weep. That is what you asked of me before, and had I listened, we would still be dragging the coves for a body that is not there. It was my eyes that found this.’ Her voice was steady, imbued with a logic as cold and hard as her grief. ‘It was my mind that refused to accept the easy answer of an accident. Julian is out there, on that ship, with that man. I see him. When I close my eyes, I see him. Do not ask me to close my eyes now and wait for news from a distance. My place is with you. On that ship. I found the first clue. I do not believe for a moment that it will be the last.’

Sir Thomas stared at her, at the young woman who had, in the space of a single day, transformed from a gentle bride to be into a figure of iron will. He saw her resolve, her unshakeable certainty. He saw the same fire that burned in his own soul, the same refusal to surrender, the same all consuming love for his son. He saw an asset, a weapon, not a liability. He had told his wife they were at war, and in war, a general needs his best soldiers.

He gave a slow, deliberate nod. ‘Very well. You will come. But you will obey my orders, without question. Is that understood?’

‘Perfectly,’ she said.

He turned back to his son. ‘Arthur. Go now. To Captain Wallace. Tell him to prepare for three passengers.’

Arthur looked from his father to Eleanor and back again, a new respect dawning on his face. He bowed his head slightly. ‘At once, Father.’ He turned and strode out of the room, his steps light and swift.

Sir Thomas looked at Eleanor, a long, appraising look. ‘Get some sleep. We have a long journey ahead.’

‘I will,’ she promised, though she knew sleep was an impossible luxury. Her mind was already on the sea, on a dark hulled ship named for a tragic queen, chasing the horizon.

The house fell into a new kind of quiet. It was not the silence of grief, nor the silence of sleep. It was the charged, humming silence of a machine being coiled, of a spring being wound tight before its violent release. The search for Julian Ashton had ended on the shingle shore among the weeping gulls. The pursuit of the Isolda was about to begin on the vast and treacherous waters of the Channel. And on board the ship that would give chase, a father, a brother, and the woman who loved him would carry not just hope, but a burning, righteous fury.

The promise of sleep was a phantom, a wisp of smoke in the storm of her thoughts. Eleanor did not even attempt to find her bed. Instead, she went to the room that had been prepared for her, the room that was to have been her sanctuary as a new bride. The moonlight, cold and indifferent, streamed through the window, illuminating the carefully folded linens and lace of her trousseau, laid out on a chest in anticipation of a life that now seemed like a foolish dream. She looked at the silk nightgowns, the delicate slippers, the fine wool dresses chosen for country walks and formal dinners. They were the costume for a part she would never play.

With a methodical slowness, she pushed the chest shut. The soft click of the latch was a sound of finality. She turned instead to her own travelling trunk, the one she had arrived with. From it, she pulled not the finery of a future Lady Ashton, but the sturdy, practical garments of Eleanor Vance, a vicar’s daughter who knew the realities of a long journey. She chose her plainest, darkest wool dress, one made for durability not admiration. She found her thickest stockings and her most sensible, stout leather boots, the ones she wore for walking across the moors in any weather. There was no vanity in her preparations, only a cold and clarifying purpose. As she braided her hair, pulling it back from her face in a severe, tight plait, she caught her reflection in the dressing table mirror. The face that looked back was pale and drawn, the eyes huge and dark in their sockets, but they held a light that was not madness, nor fear. It was the hard, glittering light of a forged blade.

A soft knock came at the door. ‘Eleanor?’ It was Lady Ashton’s voice, fragile and thin.

Eleanor opened the door at once. Julian’s mother stood there, wrapped in a heavy shawl, her face a ruin of sleepless grief. But as her eyes took in Eleanor’s severe attire, the packed travelling bag at her feet, a flicker of something else, something akin to understanding, passed over her features.

‘You are truly going,’ she said. It was not a question.

‘I must,’ Eleanor replied, her voice gentle but firm.

Lady Ashton stepped into the room, her hand trailing along the wall as if for support. She looked at the closed chest, then at Eleanor. ‘I was so angry with him,’ she whispered, her voice cracking. ‘With Thomas. For agreeing. For taking you, a girl, into this… this nightmare.’ She paused, taking a ragged breath. ‘But he was right. You are not just a girl, are you? You are the woman my son chose. I see now that he chose with a wisdom beyond his years.’

She came closer, her frail hands reaching out to clutch Eleanor’s arms. Her grip was surprisingly strong. ‘When you find him,’ she said, her voice dropping to a fierce, urgent whisper, ‘you will not have time for sentiment. You will need to be ruthless. For his sake.’ She let go with one hand and reached into the deep pocket of her dressing gown. She pressed a heavy, velvet pouch into Eleanor’s palm. ‘This is gold. Sovereigns. Do not let Thomas know you have it. Men like him believe their will is enough to move the world. But the world often moves for the glitter of coin when it will not move for anything else. Use it if you need to. Bribe a sailor, buy a horse, secure a passage. Do whatever you must. Do not hesitate.’

Eleanor looked down at the pouch, its weight a dense, secret promise in her hand. ‘I will,’ she said, her voice thick with an emotion she could not name.

‘Good.’ Lady Ashton seemed to gather herself, her spine straightening. She looked at Eleanor, a long, searching look from mother to daughter, not in law, but in spirit. ‘There is one more thing.’ She fumbled for a moment at the collar of her own dress and unpinned a small, silver brooch in the shape of a compass rose. It was old and tarnished, but beautifully made. ‘This belonged to my father. He was a captain in the Navy. He said it always guided him home. Perhaps… perhaps it will guide our Julian home too.’

She pinned the brooch to the dark fabric of Eleanor’s dress, just over her heart. Her fingers were cold but steady. ‘Go with God, Eleanor. And bring my son back to me.’

Eleanor could not speak. She simply nodded, the weight of the gold in her hand and the cool touch of the silver on her breast a testament to the trust she now carried. Lady Ashton gave a final, watery smile and slipped from the room, a ghost returning to her lonely vigil.

Eleanor tucked the pouch into a hidden pocket sewn into her petticoat and drew on a heavy, hooded cloak that Arthur’s mother had insisted she take. It smelled faintly of lavender and camphor, a scent of home she was leaving far behind. When she descended the grand staircase, Sir Thomas and Arthur were waiting in the hall. Arthur held a lantern, its yellow light casting long, dancing shadows. Sir Thomas was already dressed for the outdoors in a heavy greatcoat, his hat in his hand. His gaze swept over her, taking in the practical boots, the severe hair, the dark cloak. He did not comment on her transformation, but a single, sharp nod conveyed his approval. She was no longer a guest or a prospective daughter in law. She was a soldier, reporting for duty.

‘The carriage is ready,’ he said, his voice clipped and devoid of pleasantry. ‘There is no time to waste.’

The journey to the port was made in a tense, almost suffocating silence. The carriage rattled over the cobblestones of the town, the familiar sounds of home becoming alien and distant. Eleanor sat opposite Sir Thomas, with Arthur beside her. She could feel the taut energy radiating from the man opposite, a controlled fury that seemed to make the very air in the carriage vibrate. He stared out the window, his jaw set like granite, watching the darkened houses of his neighbours slip by. He was a man leaving his world behind, venturing into a wilder, more brutal one where his name and his title held less currency. Arthur, for his part, was preternaturally still, a coiled spring of readiness, his eyes flicking from his father’s rigid profile to the passing darkness.

They did not speak. There was nothing to say that had not already been said, no comfort to offer that would not be an insult to the gravity of their purpose. Their shared silence was a bond, a silent oath sworn in the dark, jolting confines of the carriage. As they drew nearer the harbour, the air changed. The smell of trimmed lawns and coal smoke gave way to the sharp, briny tang of salt and seaweed, the underlying odours of tar, wet rope, and fish. The sounds changed too, from the distant bark of a dog to the cry of a night gull and the low, resonant boom of a foghorn out in the Channel.

The carriage clattered to a stop on a wide, stone quay. The port was a hive of controlled chaos, a stark contrast to the sleeping town. Men with lanterns moved purposefully, their shouts cutting through the damp air. The vast, skeletal spars of ships rose up against a sky just beginning to pale from black to a deep, bruised indigo. Arthur was out of the carriage before it had fully stopped, holding the door for Eleanor and his father.

A man detached himself from the shadows and approached, touching his cap. ‘Sir Thomas. Captain Wallace is aboard and expecting you.’

‘Good,’ Sir Thomas grunted, and strode towards a gangplank that led up to the dark hull of a ship. It was not a grand vessel, not a proud naval frigate or a sleek clipper. It was a working ship, a brig, broad in the beam and built for endurance, not speed. Its lines were solid, dependable, and utterly without grace. A freshly painted name was just visible on the stern in the shifting lantern light: *The Sentinel*.

Eleanor followed him, her hand gripping the rope rail of the gangplank. The ship smelled of the sea, but also of new timber and fresh paint, and the comforting aroma of hot pitch. As her feet touched the deck, a man stepped forward to greet them. He was of middling height but immense breadth of shoulder, with a weathered face and eyes so pale they seemed almost white in his tanned skin. This had to be Captain Wallace.

‘Sir Thomas,’ he said, his voice a low rumble like stones grinding together. ‘She’s ready to catch the morning tide. My men are the best. They are yours to command.’

‘They are *your* men, Captain,’ Sir Thomas corrected him, his tone sharp. ‘And you are their commander. I am merely the passenger with the most urgent of destinations. Your reputation precedes you. They say you can smell a rogue current a league away and that you know every cove and inlet on the French coast like the back of your own hand. We will need that knowledge.’

Captain Wallace’s grim face did not smile, but a flicker of respect showed in his pale eyes. ‘We’ll find your ship, sir. The *Isolda* is a barque, Hull built, black, with a distinctive gold scrollwork on her bow. She put in for supplies two days ago. My sources say she was taking on extra water and preserved meats. More than she’d need for a simple cross Channel trip. She’s planning a long voyage.’

‘And the man?’ Sir Thomas’s voice was low and dangerous.

‘A passenger came aboard under his own name. A Mr. Thorne. Paid in cash. Kept to himself,’ Wallace reported. ‘As for another passenger, one brought aboard against his will… that sort of thing is harder to hide, but harder to prove. No one I spoke to saw anything, but that means little. A rolled up carpet, a large sea chest… there are ways.’

The image sent a fresh spear of ice through Eleanor’s heart, but she locked her knees, refusing to show weakness. This was not the time for horrified imaginings. It was the time for facts.

‘Where would he go?’ Arthur asked, speaking for the first time since they’d left the house. ‘A long voyage from here could mean anywhere. America? The Indies?’

Captain Wallace shook his head. ‘Not with the winds as they are. Not from this port. My guess, Sir Thomas, is he’ll follow the coast south. Hug the shore, maybe try to lose himself among the shipping traffic bound for Spain or the Mediterranean. Or he could make a dash for the open Atlantic. He has a day’s lead on us.’

‘Then we will have to sail faster,’ Sir Thomas stated, as if it were a simple matter of will.

‘We will, sir,’ Wallace agreed, unperturbed. ‘I’ve stripped her for speed. No unnecessary cargo. Every scrap of canvas is new. Now, if you’ll follow me. Your quarters are prepared. They are not what you are accustomed to, but they are dry.’

He led them below deck. The ceiling was low, the passageways narrow. The air was thick with the smell of wood and brine. He showed Sir Thomas and Arthur to a small, spartan cabin with two narrow bunks. Then he turned to Eleanor. ‘Ma’am, your cabin is separate. The master’s cabin, such as it is. Sir Thomas insisted.’

It was at the stern of the ship, directly beneath the quarterdeck. It was larger than the other, with a single bunk, a small table bolted to the floor, and a thick glass window that looked out on the ship’s wake. It was plain, functional, and utterly solitary. It was a mark of respect from Sir Thomas, a clear signal that he saw her not as a woman to be protected, but as a fellow officer who required her own space.

‘It will do perfectly, Captain,’ she said. ‘Thank you.’

He nodded. ‘We’ll be underway in the hour. The tide is turning.’ He left her, and she was alone with the groaning of the ship’s timbers and the muted shouts of the crew on the deck above. She placed her small bag on the bunk and walked to the stern window. The lights of the port were a string of wavering jewels against the encroaching dawn. This was the last she would see of England, of home, for God knew how long.

She felt a presence at the door and turned. It was Sir Thomas. He stood in the doorway, his large frame filling it completely.

‘The captain is capable,’ he said, as if continuing a conversation. ‘The ship is sound. We have done all we can on land.’

‘And now we are in the hands of the wind and the sea,’ Eleanor finished for him.

‘And our own resolve,’ he added, his eyes boring into hers. ‘That is the one thing over which we still have control. Do not let it waver. Not for an instant.’

‘It will not,’ she promised. The conviction in her own voice surprised her. It felt like another person was speaking, a woman she was only just beginning to know.

He held her gaze for a moment longer, then gave another of his curt nods and was gone. Eleanor remained at the window. Soon, she felt a subtle vibration through the soles of her boots, a deep thrumming as the anchor was weighed. There were shouts, the creak of the windlass, and then a slow, almost imperceptible shift. The ship was moving. The string of lights on the shore began to slide sideways. The gap of dark, churning water between the *Sentinel* and the quay widened, becoming a chasm.

She stayed there as the harbour fell away, as the open sea embraced them. The deck began to rise and fall with the long, slow rhythm of the Channel swell. Above her, she heard the snap of canvas as the sails caught the morning wind, a great, collective exhalation as the ship came to life. The pale light of dawn was now spreading across the sky, revealing a world of grey water and grey cloud, vast and empty. Somewhere out there, in that endless grey, was the *Isolda*. Somewhere out there was Julian.

Eleanor reached up and touched the silver brooch on her dress. Its points were sharp against her fingertips. It was not a compass to guide a ship, but a compass for the soul. Its needle pointed to a single, unalterable heading: Julian. The hunt had begun. She would not weep. She would not wait. She would look forward, into the grey expanse, her eyes fixed on the horizon, until she found what she was looking for. The gentle bride was a memory, drowned in the waters of the Channel. In her place stood a hunter, cold and patient and filled with a righteous, terrible fury. The sea air was cold on her face, but she did not feel it. The fire within her burned hot enough to keep the entire ocean at bay.

The fire did not diminish as the day wore on. It settled deep in her belly, a core of heat that defied the salt spray and the persistent damp. She remained on deck for hours, a solitary figure wrapped in a heavy woollen cloak, her back to the mainmast. The crew, a collection of hard faced men with wind burned skin and knowing eyes, gave her a wide berth. They moved about their duties with a practiced economy of motion, their calls to one another a language as foreign and elemental as the cry of the gulls that followed in their wake. She watched them, learning the rhythm of the ship. She learned the names of the sails not by asking but by listening, associating the shouts with the great sheets of canvas that were unfurled or reefed in. The fore-topsail, the mizzen-royal, the jibs. The words were a catechism, a litany of this new and brutal church in which she now worshipped.

Sir Thomas found her there as the sun reached its zenith, a weak and watery disc in the overcast sky. He carried two tin mugs and a block of hard cheese wrapped in wax paper. He handed one of the mugs to her. It was filled with a weak, lukewarm tea, yet the warmth seeped into her chilled fingers.

‘You cannot stand watch all the way to the Azores, Lady Ashworth,’ he said, his voice a low rumble against the constant sighing of the wind in the rigging. He did not use her first name. They were not friends. They were confederates in a grim enterprise.

‘I am not standing watch,’ she replied, her gaze fixed on the horizon. ‘I am learning.’

He followed her gaze. There was nothing to see but the endless, rolling expanse of grey green water. ‘And what have you learned?’

‘That the sea does not care. It is not our enemy, nor is it our friend. It simply is. We are the intruders here.’

A flicker of something, perhaps approval, crossed his stern features. ‘A lesson many sailors take a lifetime to understand. Good. Indifference is a purer foe than malice. It can be navigated. It can be understood. Malice is a whirlpool, it seeks only to drag you down.’ He tore a piece of cheese from the block with his teeth and chewed it thoughtfully. ‘You will need your strength. Go below. Eat something more substantial. Sleep.’

‘I am not tired.’

‘Then pretend to be,’ he countered, his tone leaving no room for argument. ‘The crew is uneasy. A woman on a ship like this, on a voyage like this. It is a disruption to their order. They see your grief and mistake it for fragility. Let them see you are following a routine. Let them see you care for yourself. It will make them believe you are predictable. It is better they think you a grieving, addled widow than a woman with a purpose they cannot comprehend. Your purpose is your own. Do not share it with them. It is a weapon. You do not show a weapon until you mean to use it.’

She looked at him then, truly looked at him for the first time since they had set sail. His eyes were the colour of the sea, a flat, hard grey. There were no depths to them, no hidden warmth. They were the eyes of a man who saw the world as a series of problems to be solved, of obstacles to be overcome. He was not comforting her. He was instructing her in the art of concealment.

‘I understand,’ she said, and she did. The hunter does not announce her presence to the forest. She moves through it, a shadow among shadows. She finished the tea and handed the empty mug back to him. ‘I will go below.’

He nodded, that same curt, final gesture. As she turned to leave, he spoke again, his voice low. ‘The captain’s name is Davies. He is a good man, but he is a creature of the sea. He trusts what he can see, what he can measure. The wind, the tides, the position of the stars. He does not trust passions. When you are in his presence, be calm. Be steel.’

The days that followed fell into a harsh and unvarying pattern. Eleanor rose before dawn, dressed in the dark of her small cabin, and took her breakfast of hard biscuit and salted beef in the cramped mess, a space that smelled perpetually of stale beer and boiled cabbage. The sailors, after the first few awkward encounters, began to accept her presence as they would accept a spell of bad weather, an unavoidable and unpleasant fact of the voyage. They did not speak to her, and she did not speak to them, but she watched them. She saw the way they mended a frayed rope, the casual courage with which they climbed the rigging in a rising swell, the simple camaraderie in a shared joke or a passed flask. They were not her friends, but they were her vessel. Their lives were tied to hers.

In the afternoons, Sir Thomas would summon her to the captain’s great cabin. It was the largest room on the ship, dominated by a heavy oak table bolted to the floor. A brass sextant lay in a velvet lined box, and rolled charts were stacked in a corner like scrolls in a forgotten library. Captain Davies would be there, a short, barrel chested Welshman with a fringe of white hair and a face that looked as if it had been carved from driftwood. He would nod to her, a gesture of gruff acknowledgement, and then turn his attention to the charts.

It was there, in the heart of the ship, that the hunt took shape. Sir Thomas would unroll a chart, pinning its corners with small lead weights. The vast expanse of the Atlantic Ocean was laid bare, a web of rhumb lines and sounding depths.

‘The *Isolda* cleared the port of Bristol a full day before Julian was taken,’ Sir Thomas explained on the third day, his finger tracing a line south from the English coast. ‘Her captain is a Frenchman, a mercenary named Renaud. A man known for his speed and his ruthlessness. He is not a pirate in the common sense. He does not attack at random. He works for hire.’

‘He was hired to take my husband,’ Eleanor stated, her voice flat.

‘He was,’ Sir Thomas confirmed. ‘By whom, we do not yet know for certain, but the trail leads back to Lisbon, to a consortium of merchants who believe your husband’s work on steam propulsion threatens their shipping interests.’

Captain Davies snorted, a noise like gravel being ground underfoot. ‘Merchants. Worse than pirates. A pirate will kill you for your gold. A merchant will kill you for the gold he thinks you might one day have.’

‘Renaud will not sail directly for Lisbon,’ Sir Thomas continued, ignoring the captain’s commentary. ‘That would be too predictable. He is clever. He will take a southerly route, using the trade winds to make for the Azores. He can resupply there, at Horta or Ponta Delgada, without raising suspicion. A French trading vessel taking on water and provisions. It is a common enough sight.’

‘And how can you be so certain?’ Eleanor asked, her eyes tracing the archipelago of tiny islands on the chart. They looked so small, so lost in the immensity of the ocean.

‘Because I have sailed against Renaud before,’ Sir Thomas said, and for the first time, a sliver of something personal entered his voice. A cold, hard anger that mirrored her own. ‘He is a creature of habit. He has boltholes, safe harbours he trusts. Horta is one of them. We have agents there, watching. They will know if the *Isolda* makes port.’

‘And if she does not?’

‘Then he continues south, towards the Cape Verde islands, and then cuts east for the African coast. A much longer route, but one that avoids the main shipping lanes. But our quarry is not cargo. It is a man. A valuable man. They will want him delivered quickly. No, he will go to the Azores. I would stake my life on it.’

Eleanor looked at the chart, at the ink line that represented their own course. They were following the *Isolda*, a ghost ship pursuing another. ‘So we are sailing into a trap. He will know he is being followed.’

‘We are not following,’ Captain Davies corrected, speaking for the first time directly to her. ‘We are anticipating. We will bypass the main islands and lie in wait here,’ he tapped a point on the chart, a small, uninhabited rock island to the west of the main cluster. ‘From the cliffs there, with a good glass, we can watch the shipping channel into Horta. We will see him before he sees us.’

‘And then?’ she pressed.

Sir Thomas looked at her, his grey eyes weighing her. ‘And then, Lady Ashworth, we will do what must be done.’

He taught her to use the spyglass, to hold it steady against the roll of the ship, to focus the brass lenses until the distant blur of a sail resolved into sharp lines. He made her stand on the quarterdeck for hours, scanning the empty horizon, describing to him the shape of the clouds, the colour of the water, the subtle shifts in the swell. He was training her eyes, honing her senses, forcing her to see the world not as a backdrop but as a source of information. He taught her to tie knots, the bowline, the clove hitch, the sheet bend. Her fingers, once accustomed to embroidery needles and piano keys, grew raw and calloused. She did not complain. Every knot she mastered was a small victory, a piece of this harsh new world that she could claim for her own.

The storm came without warning on the eighth day. The sky, which had been a placid, hazy blue, turned a bruised purple in the space of an hour. The sea lost its gentle rhythm and began to heave in great, oily swells. The wind, which had been a steady companion, began to shriek, a high, keening wail that vibrated through the ship’s timbers.

The crew moved with a frantic, disciplined energy. Shouts were snatched away by the gale. Men scrambled up the rigging like spiders to furl the highest sails, clinging to the wildly swaying masts as the deck pitched beneath them. Eleanor stood near the helm, her hands gripping the cold, wet wood of the gunwale, her cloak whipping around her like a flag. The sea spray was like a volley of needles against her face.

‘Get below, my lady!’ Captain Davies bellowed at her, his face a mask of concentration as he and two other sailors fought the great wheel. ‘This is no place for you!’

She looked at Sir Thomas, who stood near the mizzenmast, his feet planted wide, as immovable as the mast itself. He met her gaze and gave a single, sharp shake of his head. He was not ordering her below. This was another test.

The first great wave broke over the bow. A mountain of churning, white water crashed onto the deck, sweeping away everything not bolted or tied down. The force of it knocked Eleanor from her feet. She slammed into the base of the mast, the air driven from her lungs. Icy water surged around her, pulling at her, trying to drag her towards the open scuppers. For a terrifying instant, she was submerged, the roar of the ocean filling her ears. Then the water receded, leaving her gasping and choking on the canted deck.

A hand, hard as iron, grabbed the collar of her cloak and hauled her to her feet. It was Sir Thomas. His face was inches from hers, his expression unreadable in the maelstrom. ‘Hold on!’ he roared over the wind. He pushed her against the solid wood of the mast and wrapped a loose coil of rope around her waist and the mast itself, securing it with a swift, expert knot. ‘Do not move from here!’

He was gone then, moving across the treacherous deck to help the men struggling with a tearing jib sail. Eleanor was tethered, a part of the ship. She could do nothing but watch as the *Sentinel* fought for its life. The sky and the sea merged into a single, roaring chaos of grey and white. Waves, vast and monstrous, rose up like jagged cliffs before crashing down upon them. The ship groaned, a deep, agonising sound from its very soul. It would rise up the face of a wave, hanging for an impossible moment at its crest, the wind screaming through the rigging, before plunging down into the trough with a sickening lurch that made her stomach climb into her throat.

She was afraid, but it was a cold, clear fear, not the panicked terror she would have once felt. It was the fear of an animal in a trap, a sharp, focused awareness of mortality. She watched Sir Thomas, a dark figure against the fury of the storm, issuing orders, his voice cutting through the din. She saw him bodily pull a young sailor back from the rail as a wave tried to claim him. He was a part of this element, as much as the captain, as much as the sea itself. He was not fighting the storm. He was enduring it.

Hours passed. She lost all sense of time, lost in the deafening roar and the violent motion. Her body was a mass of aches, her hands frozen to the rope that held her. Yet the fire inside her did not go out. It burned with a fierce, defiant heat. This storm, this uncaring, elemental rage, was nothing compared to the storm in her own soul. The sea could kill her, but it could not comprehend her. It could not understand the cold, patient fury that drove her. If she was to die here, she thought with a strange, chilling calm, then she would die looking forward, towards the horizon where Julian was.

Then, as gradually as it had begun, the storm began to recede. The wind’s shriek lessened to a mournful howl. The waves, though still immense, lost their terrifying steepness. A sliver of pale, watery light broke through the clouds. The ship, battered and bruised, had survived.

Men began to move about the deck, assessing the damage. A section of the forward rail was splintered, and the jib sail was shredded beyond repair, but the masts held. The hull was sound.

Sir Thomas came to her. He did not speak. His face was grim, streaked with salt and exhaustion. With his knife, he sawed through the rope that held her. She stumbled, her legs numb and weak, and he caught her arm to steady her.

‘Go,’ he said, his voice hoarse. ‘Get warm. You have seen it now. You know what it is.’

She nodded, unable to form words. She turned and made her way, stumbling and slow, towards the hatch that led below decks. As she reached it, a shout went up from the lookout in the crow’s nest.

‘Wreckage! Wreckage off the starboard bow!’

Eleanor froze, her hand on the hatch. She turned back. Sir Thomas was already at the rail, the spyglass to his eye. Captain Davies joined him. The crew, forgetting their exhaustion, crowded the starboard side, their faces filled with a grim curiosity.

She walked back to the rail, her own small spyglass clutched in her hand. She raised it, her arms trembling with cold and fatigue. She found the spot the men were pointing to, a dark shape rising and falling on the grey swells. It was a piece of a ship’s mast, splintered and broken, with a tatter of canvas still attached to it. Adrift beside it was a wooden crate.

‘It’s from a merchantman,’ Captain Davies said, lowering his glass. ‘A heavy carrack by the look of that spar. The storm claimed more than just our sailcloth.’

Eleanor kept her glass focused on the wreckage. Something about the crate, its colour, the way it bobbed in the water. She adjusted the focus, her heart beginning to pound a slow, heavy rhythm against her ribs. There was stencilling on the side. The letters were faded, partially submerged with each swell, but she could make them out. She had seen that stencilling before, in the warehouses on the docks of Bristol, on the crates being loaded for Julian’s last, fateful tour.

‘Sir Thomas,’ she said, her voice a low, tight whisper. He turned to her, surprised by her tone. ‘Let me see your glass.’

He handed it to her without a word. The captain’s glass was more powerful, the lenses clearer. She focused it on the crate. The letters sprang into sharp relief. A. I. C. Ashworth Imperial Company.

And below it, another, smaller stencil. *Isolda*.

The sea had given them a sign. The hunter was closing in on her prey.

The words tasted of salt and iron on her tongue. *Isolda*. The name was a ghost, a whisper she had carried in the hollow spaces of her heart for months. Now it was here, branded on a piece of flotsam, a tombstone cast upon the waves. The sea had not swallowed the evidence whole. It had offered her a fragment, a first clue.

Sir Thomas lowered his spyglass, his gaze shifting from the distant wreckage to her face. He saw the recognition there, the sudden, terrible stillness that had seized her. He did not need to ask. He had seen that same look on the faces of men who find a fallen comrade’s standard on a battlefield. It was the look of grief given a direction, a purpose.

‘Captain Davies,’ Sir Thomas said, his voice low and hard, cutting through the murmurs of the crew. ‘We are recovering that crate.’

The captain turned, his brow furrowed with professional concern. ‘Sir, the sea is still unsettled. The longboat will have a perilous time of it. My men are exhausted from the storm. To risk them for a waterlogged crate…’

‘It is wreckage from the *Isolda*,’ Eleanor said, her voice quiet but carrying with an unnatural clarity across the deck. The name fell into the assembled crew like a stone into a still pond. The curious chatter died. Men looked at her, then at the grey, heaving water, their expressions changing from simple curiosity to a sombre understanding. They all knew her story. They all knew why they were sailing into these hostile waters.

Captain Davies’s protest withered on his lips. He looked at Eleanor, at her pale, determined face framed by damp strands of hair. He looked at the unwavering iron in Sir Thomas’s eyes. He was a practical man, a man of tides and winds and timbers, but he was not a fool. This was no longer a simple voyage. It was a pilgrimage of vengeance, and he was merely the ferryman.

‘Very well, Sir Thomas,’ he conceded, his tone formal. ‘Mr. Cobb!’ he bellowed. ‘Ready the starboard longboat. Pick your four best oarsmen. I want grapples and a long hook.’

A burly, bearded man, the boatswain, touched his forehead. ‘Aye, Captain.’ He began shouting orders, and the weary crew moved with a renewed, if grim, energy. The business of the ship, the familiar rhythm of ropes and pulleys, was a comfort against the chilling mystery bobbing on the waves.

Eleanor did not move from the rail. She did not return below to the warmth of her cabin. The cold was a part of her now, a second skin. It kept her sharp. She watched as the longboat was swung out on its davits and lowered with creaking precision into the churning sea. It looked terrifyingly small, a leaf tossed upon a vast, indifferent ocean. Mr. Cobb stood in the stern, tiller in hand, while four sailors, their faces set and grim, took to the oars.

Sir Thomas stood beside her, a silent, solid presence. He did not offer her platitudes or comforts. He understood that what she felt was beyond their reach. He simply bore witness with her.

The longboat fought its way across the swells. From the high deck of the *Vengeance*, the rhythm of the waves seemed manageable, but down on the surface, each swell was a rolling hill of grey water that lifted the small boat high, then dropped it sickeningly into a trough. The oarsmen rowed with powerful, synchronised strokes, their backs straining, their skill the only thing keeping the boat from being swamped or turned broadside to a breaking wave.

Eleanor held her small spyglass to her eye again, her knuckles white. She watched the men’s struggle, her breath held tight in her chest. They reached the wreckage. The splintered mast, a great black spear of timber, rose and fell heavily, a dangerous, unpredictable companion to the crate. Mr. Cobb steered them close, shouting instructions over the rush of the water. A sailor in the bow cast a grappling hook. It missed, skittering off the wet wood of the crate. He hauled it in, his movements frantic but sure, and cast again. This time it caught.

They worked to secure a second line, the longboat bucking and sliding on the waves. At one point, a larger swell lifted the broken mast and slammed it down perilously close to the boat, sending a shower of white spray over the men. Eleanor’s heart leaped into her throat. But they held on, their faces slick with seawater, their determination absolute.

Slowly, painstakingly, they began the arduous task of towing the heavy, waterlogged crate back towards the *Vengeance*. It was a stubborn, dead weight, fighting them with every wave. The journey back was even slower than the journey out. The men at the oars were grunting with the effort, their rhythm strained.

On the deck of the *Vengeance*, Captain Davies oversaw the preparations to bring the crate aboard. A net was lowered, along with heavy ropes. The crew lined the rail, their earlier exhaustion forgotten, replaced by a tense, focused anticipation. This was the first tangible piece of the puzzle they had been sent to solve. It was a message from the deep.

When the longboat finally drew alongside, battered but triumphant, the men within were pale and spent. Lines were thrown, and the longboat was secured to the hull of the mother ship. Getting the crate out of the water was another battle. It swung wildly on the hoist, slamming against the ship’s side with a deep, groaning thud. Men shouted, ropes strained, and timbers creaked. Finally, with a great, collective heave, it was hauled over the rail and crashed onto the deck with a sound of finality.

It was larger than it had looked in the water. A heavy sea chest, banded with rusted iron, its wood bleached and softened by the sea. The stencilling was clearer now. A. I. C. *Isolda*. There was no doubt.

The crew gathered around, their faces a mixture of awe and dread. They kept a respectful distance, as if it were a coffin. And in a way, it was.

‘Alright, back to your duties,’ Captain Davies ordered, breaking the spell. ‘The storm’s passed, but the work hasn’t. Check the rigging. Splicing crew to the mainmast. Let’s have the ship right before nightfall.’

He was giving them work to distract them, to restore the normal order of the vessel. He was a good captain.

He turned to Sir Thomas. ‘The deck is yours, sir.’

Sir Thomas nodded. He looked at the two sailors who had remained. ‘Crowbars.’

The men brought the tools. The iron bands were thick, the nails deeply embedded. It took their combined strength, the wood groaning and splintering in protest. Eleanor stood motionless, her hands clasped before her. Every screech of the metal, every crack of the wood, was an assault on her nerves. What would they find? Empty, sodden trade goods? Or something more? Something that would tell her how Julian had died.

With a final, wrenching crack, the lid was pried open. A smell of brine and decay washed over them. The sailors stepped back.

Sir Thomas and Eleanor leaned forward. The crate was packed with bolts of silk, now ruined, their vibrant colours bled into a uniform, muddy grey. They were slimy to the touch, heavy with absorbed seawater. For a moment, a wave of bitter disappointment washed over Eleanor. It was just cargo. A meaningless piece of a merchant ship’s inventory.

‘Wait,’ Sir Thomas said. His hand, reaching into the sodden mess, had found something hard. He pulled aside the ruined fabric. Tucked into a corner, protected by a layer of waxed canvas that had partially held, was a small, leather bound book. A ship’s log.

His eyes met Eleanor’s. This was it.

He lifted it out carefully, as if it were a holy relic. The leather was warped and stiff, the pages swollen. He handed it to Eleanor. Her fingers trembled as she took it. It felt impossibly heavy. The log of the *Isolda*. The last testament of the men who had sailed with her husband.

‘There’s something else,’ the sailor with the crowbar said, pointing.

Buried deeper in the muck of spoiled silk was a smaller object, a pouch made of oilskin, tightly bound with a waxed cord. It was remarkably well preserved, seemingly untouched by the water. Sir Thomas retrieved it. It was addressed in faded but still legible ink. *For Eleanor*.

Eleanor stared at it, her vision blurring. Julian’s hand. She knew his script as well as she knew her own face. It was a letter. A letter he had written for her, a final message that the sea, in its cruel mercy, had decided to deliver.

‘Take them to my cabin,’ Sir Thomas said to her, his voice gentle but firm. ‘We will look at them there. Away from this.’ He gestured to the deck, the curious eyes of the crew, the wreck of the crate.

She nodded numbly and clutched the logbook and the pouch to her chest. He put a steadying hand on her back and guided her towards the hatch. The journey to his cabin, a small, spartan space dominated by charts and books, was a blur. He sat her in a chair, lit a lantern against the fading afternoon light, and poured two small glasses of brandy. He pushed one into her hand.

‘Drink,’ he commanded. She obeyed, the fiery liquid a shock that cut through the cold fog in her mind.

‘Which first?’ he asked, his gaze resting on the two objects on the table between them. The book or the letter. The official record or the personal farewell.

‘The log,’ she whispered. She needed facts before she could face feelings. She needed the skeleton of the story before she could bear to look upon its heart.

Sir Thomas took the logbook. The pages were stuck together. He worked at them with the tip of his knife, painstakingly separating them. Much of the ink had run, turning whole sections into unintelligible blue grey smudges. But some entries, written in a bolder hand with better ink, remained partially legible. He angled the book towards the lantern light.

‘The first weeks are standard,’ he murmured, scanning the pages. ‘Fair winds out of the Channel. Mentions of cargo settling. Routine ship’s business.’ He flipped a few more fragile pages. ‘Here. The weather begins to turn. The captain, a man named Prewitt, writes of a low barometer and a ‘wicked looking sky’.’

He continued to read, his voice a low drone, piecing together the narrative from the fragmented sentences. They had fought the storm for two days, just as the *Vengeance* had. There were entries about a split topsail, a damaged rudder chain. Then the writing became more hurried, the script almost a scrawl.

‘Listen to this,’ Sir Thomas said, his finger tracing a line. ‘…wind beyond measure. Ship taking on water faster than the pumps can clear it. Mainmast is groaning. I fear it will not hold… Another vessel sighted, two points off the port bow. A brig, flying no colours. Strange to see another soul in this hell. She is holding her station in the storm with unnatural ease…’

Eleanor leaned forward. ‘Another ship?’

‘So it seems.’ Sir Thomas’s face was grim. ‘He mentions it again. The final entry.’ The last page was badly damaged, but a few lines at the top were clear, written in a desperate, slanted hand.

*…brig is closing. Not a rescue. God have mercy on our souls. They are not here to help. The cargo… it was a lie… Tell Ashworth… Corbin betrayed…*

The rest was an indecipherable smear of ink and seawater.

Corbin. Eleanor had heard the name. He was one of Ashworth’s junior partners, a man with a reputation for sharp dealing and ambition that bordered on ruthlessness. Betrayed. The word hung in the small cabin, more chilling than the storm’s wind.

The *Isolda* had not just been a victim of the weather. The storm had been a cloak for something far more sinister. It was piracy, or murder. Or both.

For a long time, neither of them spoke. The truth she had suspected, the truth that had been dismissed as a widow’s hysteria, was beginning to take shape. Julian had not been lost to the uncaring fury of nature. He had been taken by the calculated malice of men.

Finally, Sir Thomas pushed the oilskin pouch across the table towards her. ‘Eleanor. There is this.’

Her fingers were stiff as she worked at the cord. It came away, and she unfolded the pouch. Inside was a folded piece of paper, dry and safe. It was dated just three days before the storm hit. She unfolded it. Julian’s writing filled the page, his familiar, elegant script a voice from the grave.

*My Dearest Eleanor,*

*I write this in haste, with a sense of foreboding I can neither shake nor explain. This voyage has not been what I was told. The cargo we carry is not merely silks and spices for the Orient trade. There is something else, hidden in the lower hold, something Sir Richard Ashworth himself charged me to guard. He spoke of it only in whispers, of its great value and the great danger in its transport. He trusted no one else.*

*A man, Corbin, who arranged the final manifests, has been a shadow on this voyage from the start. His men are among the crew, and they watch me. They watch the hold. I do not trust him. I do not trust the feel of this ship.*

*We have altered our course. The captain says it is to avoid the coming storm, but the new heading makes no sense. It takes us towards the Serpent’s Teeth, that forsaken cluster of islands south of the trade routes. I fear we are not avoiding trouble, but sailing directly towards it. It feels like a rendezvous.*

*If you are reading this, it is because my fears have been realised. Know that my last thoughts were of you. My only regret in this life is that our time together was so short. Do not grieve for me, my love. Live. Be happy. That is all I have ever wanted for you.*

*But if your spirit demands justice, as I know it well may, trust Sir Thomas. He will know what to do. The key is the cargo, Eleanor. Find what was in the hold. Find it, and you will find the men responsible. Look for the Nightingale.*

*Forever Yours,*
*Julian.*

The letter fell from her trembling hands onto the table. A single tear, hot and sharp, traced a path down her cold cheek. The Nightingale. The ship in the storm. It had a name. Corbin. He had a name. The Serpent’s Teeth. They had a destination.

She looked up at Sir Thomas. The grief in her eyes was now overlaid with a fire, a cold, burning light. The weeping widow was gone. In her place was something else, something forged in the storm and tempered by this terrible, clarifying truth.

‘The cargo,’ she said, her voice a raw whisper. ‘He said to find the cargo.’

Sir Thomas nodded slowly, his expression hard as stone. ‘Ashworth’s secret. The reason for the betrayal.’ He stood and walked to the great chart of the southern seas pinned to the wall. His finger traced a path south of their current position.

‘The Serpent’s Teeth,’ he said. ‘A vile place. A maze of volcanic islands and treacherous reefs. A known haven for the worst dregs of the sea. Pirates, smugglers, renegades. If Corbin planned a rendezvous, it would be there. He would take the cargo, scuttle the *Isolda*, and let the storm wash away the evidence.’

‘But the storm was worse than he planned,’ Eleanor finished for him. ‘It scattered the wreckage. It sent us this.’ She gestured to the logbook and the letter.

‘The sea does not always keep its secrets,’ Sir Thomas agreed. He looked at her, his eyes searching her face. ‘This changes everything, Eleanor. This is no longer a search for a lost ship. It is a hunt. And the prey we seek is far more dangerous than any storm. We can turn back. No one would blame you.’

Eleanor stood, her legs steady now. She walked to the chart and placed her hand beside his, her small, pale fingers resting on the jagged ink marks that represented the Serpent’s Teeth. The hunter was closing in on her prey. And now she knew where the beast made its lair.

‘Captain Davies will object to the course,’ she said. It was not a question.

‘I am not asking Captain Davies,’ Sir Thomas replied. ‘I am asking you.’

She looked from the chart to his grim, waiting face. The last of her tears had dried. Her grief was a cold, hard stone in her gut, but it was no longer an anchor. It was a whetstone, and it was sharpening her soul to a razor’s edge.

‘Set a course for the Serpent’s Teeth, Sir Thomas,’ she said, her voice devoid of any tremor. ‘We will find the *Nightingale*. And we will find Mr. Corbin.’

Sir Thomas’s hand fell away from the chart. His gaze did not leave Eleanor’s face, searching for any sign of faltering, any crack in the new, hard façade she presented to the world. He found none. There was only the chilling stillness of a woman who had passed through the heart of a tempest and found not peace, but a terrible, singular purpose.

‘I will inform the captain,’ he said, his voice low. He made to move towards the door, a silent dismissal, but Eleanor spoke again.

‘No,’ she said. ‘We will inform him together.’

He paused, one hand on the brass handle of the cabin door. A flicker of something, perhaps surprise or even admiration, crossed his stern features. He gave a single, sharp nod.

Captain Davies was a man carved from the same weathered timber as his ship. He had a face mapped with the lines of a thousand sea voyages, and his eyes, a pale, washed out blue, held the distant look of a man more comfortable with horizons than with people. He stood before them in Sir Thomas’s great cabin, his stocky frame rigid, his hands clasped behind his back. He had been summoned, and he did not like it.

‘Sir Thomas. Lady Ashworth,’ he greeted them, his voice a gravelly rumble. He inclined his head a fraction of an inch towards Eleanor, a gesture that was more protocol than respect. His focus remained on Sir Thomas, the man he perceived as his true employer.

‘Captain,’ Sir Thomas began, his tone formal. ‘There has been a change of plan. We have new information regarding the *Isolda*. We will no longer be making for Cape Town.’

Captain Davies’s bushy eyebrows drew together. ‘Not making for Cape Town? Sir, with all respect, that is our destination. The charter is clear. We are to transport you and Lady Ashworth to the colony with all possible speed.’

‘The charter is amended,’ Sir Thomas said flatly. He gestured to the chart on the wall. ‘Our new destination is here. The Serpent’s Teeth.’

The captain’s weather beaten face lost what little color it had. He took a half step towards the chart, squinting as if he had misheard. He stared at the spidery ink marks for a long moment. A low whistle escaped his lips.

‘The Serpent’s Teeth,’ he repeated, the name a curse on his tongue. ‘Sir, you cannot be serious. That is no place for this ship. It’s a graveyard. A nest of cutthroats and pirates. The currents are devils, the reefs are unmarked, and the only welcome you’ll find is at the end of a cannon’s mouth.’

‘We are aware of the risks, Captain,’ Sir Thomas said, his voice hardening.

‘Are you?’ Davies shot back, his professional composure finally cracking. He turned, his pale eyes blazing with a sudden fire. ‘That place is the sea’s bilge water. Men go there to disappear. To trade in things that cannot be traded in honest ports. What possible business could a gentleman of your standing and… and a grieving widow have in such a hellhole?’ He gestured towards Eleanor, his meaning clear. A place for men, not for delicate ladies.

Eleanor stepped forward. Her movement was slight, but it drew the captain’s full attention. The air in the cabin seemed to crackle.

‘My husband’s business, Captain Davies,’ she said. Her voice was quiet, yet it cut through the tension like a keen blade. It held no plea, no feminine weakness. It was a statement of fact. ‘My husband’s murderer is there. The man who scuttled the *Isolda* and left her crew to drown. His name is Corbin. The ship he stole is called the *Nightingale*. He has something that belonged to my husband, something he valued more than his own life. I mean to have it back. And I mean to see Mr. Corbin answer for his crimes.’

She let the words hang in the air. She did not raise her voice. She did not need to. The sheer, unvarnished truth of her statement was more powerful than any shout.

Captain Davies stared at her. He saw not the pale, fragile woman who had been carried aboard his ship, but the woman standing before him now. He saw the cold fire in her eyes, the resolute set of her jaw. He saw the ghost of her husband’s will living on in her. His belligerence seemed to drain away, replaced by a grudging, astonished respect.

‘The charter,’ he said again, but the fight had gone out of his voice. It was a sailor’s last protest, a defense of his precious order.

‘The charter is mine, Captain,’ Eleanor said softly. ‘I am paying for this voyage. I am paying for your ship, for your crew, for the very air we breathe upon this sea. And I am telling you, as the sole beneficiary and executor of Lord Ashworth’s estate, that our destination has changed. You will take us to the Serpent’s Teeth. Or I will find a captain who will.’

The silence that followed was absolute. It was broken only by the groan of the ship’s timbers and the distant rush of the waves. Sir Thomas stood like a statue, watching, letting her fight her own battle.

Finally, Captain Davies let out a long, weary breath. He ran a hand over his face. ‘The crew won’t like it. They’re good men, but they’re not navy. They signed on for a trade route, not a pirate hunt.’

‘Double their wages,’ Eleanor said without hesitation. ‘From this moment until we return to a civilised port. Hazard pay. Take it from my accounts.’

The captain looked from her to Sir Thomas, who gave a minute, confirmatory nod. This was beyond anything Davies had encountered in thirty years at sea. A lady of high birth, with more iron in her than most admirals he had known.

‘It will take us days to reach the outer islands,’ he said, his voice now resigned, professional. He was already calculating courses, winds, supplies. ‘And once we are there, navigating the channels is a nightmare. We’ll be sailing blind.’

‘We have Julian’s logbook,’ Eleanor said, gesturing to the salt stained book on the table. ‘It has his last known position. And his intended rendezvous.’

The captain walked back to the chart, his seaman’s instincts taking over. ‘Aye,’ he muttered, his finger tracing the treacherous geography. ‘If a man wanted to hide a ship, or meet a buyer for stolen goods… this is where he’d do it. Sheltered coves. False channels. The volcanic haze hides a fleet from a mile out.’ He shook his head. ‘It’s a madman’s errand.’ He looked over his shoulder at Eleanor. ‘But your husband was a fine man, Lady Ashworth. I met him once in London. He deserved better than a traitor’s hand and a watery grave.’ He turned fully back to the chart. ‘Very well. I’ll give the order. God help us all. Set a course for the Serpent’s Teeth.’

The *Dauntless* turned her head south. The change in the ship’s aspect was felt by every man aboard. The familiar, rhythmic roll of a vessel running with the trade winds was replaced by a tauter, more aggressive motion as she cut across the grain of the ocean. The sun still shone, the sky was still a brilliant, depthless blue, but a shadow had fallen over the ship.

The crew, as Captain Davies predicted, did not like it. The news, delivered by the first mate in clipped, non-negotiable terms, spread through the lower decks like a fever. The Serpent’s Teeth. The name was known to every sailor. Whispers followed Eleanor whenever she appeared on deck. The men, who had once looked at her with a sort of distant pity, now watched her with a new expression. It was a mixture of awe, suspicion, and fear. She was no longer just a passenger. She was the architect of their new, dangerous fate. The promise of double pay did much to quell outright mutiny, but it could not buy peace of mind. They were sailing into the dark, and she was the one holding the lamp.

Eleanor, for her part, refused to be confined to her cabin. The small, enclosed space had been a sanctuary for her grief. Now it felt like a cage. The sea was no longer her enemy. It was her accomplice. It had brought her Julian’s last words. It was her path to justice.

Each morning, she was on deck before the sun had fully risen, a cloak wrapped tight against the chill morning spray. She stood by the rail, not as a spectator, but as a student. She watched the men work the ropes, their movements economical and sure. She learned the names of the sails, the points of the compass, the feel of the wind as it shifted.

Sir Thomas became her tutor. He found her one morning, her gaze fixed on the horizon, her face pale but determined in the grey light of dawn.

‘You cannot will the islands to appear any faster,’ he said, his voice a low rumble beside her.

‘I can learn the way to them,’ she replied, not taking her eyes off the endless expanse of water. ‘If something were to happen… I will not be helpless again. Teach me.’

And so he did. He taught her how to read the chart not as a flat piece of paper, but as a three dimensional world of depths and dangers. He showed her how to use the sextant, guiding her hands as she struggled to capture the sun at its zenith. Her first attempts were clumsy, her calculations wild. But she persisted. The cool, precise logic of mathematics was a balm to her wounded heart. It was a problem that had a solution, a system that could be mastered.

‘The sun and the stars do not lie, Eleanor,’ he told her, as they stood on the quarterdeck one evening, the sky a vast canopy of glittering diamonds. ‘They are fixed points in a changing world. Men may betray you. The sea may betray you. But Polaris will always show you the way north.’

She looked from the North Star to his stoic profile, silhouetted against the last vestiges of twilight. In this hard, unforgiving man, she had found her own fixed point. He was her Polaris. A silent guardian who asked for nothing, but who was always there, a bulwark against the chaos.

‘What was the cargo, Thomas?’ she asked one afternoon, as they sat in the great cabin, Julian’s logbook open between them. They had scoured its pages a dozen times. The entries were mostly routine. Wind speed, position, minor repairs. But near the end, the entries became more cryptic. Mentions of ‘the Nightingale’s burden’ and ‘Ashworth’s folly’.

Sir Thomas traced a line in the book with his finger. ‘Julian was a member of the Royal Society. And an advisor to the Admiralty. He was fascinated by botany, by cartography… but his true passion was engineering. New designs for ship’s hulls, for cannon mounts. He believed the future of the Empire lay in technological supremacy at sea.’

‘And the cargo?’ Eleanor pressed.

‘I believe the cargo was not something to be sold, but something to be demonstrated. A weapon, Eleanor. Or the plans for one. Something that would have given our navy an insurmountable advantage. Something worth killing for. Something worth starting a war over, or preventing one.’ He looked up from the book, his eyes grim. ‘Corbin was not just Julian’s first mate. He was a protégé. Julian trusted him implicitly. He would have known every detail of the project.’

A weapon. The word was cold and ugly. Julian had been a builder, a creator. He had spoken of progress, of securing the trade routes, of making the seas safer. But she knew he was also a patriot. He loved his country with a fierce, unwavering devotion.

‘So Corbin did not steal gold or jewels,’ she murmured. ‘He stole the future of naval warfare.’

‘And tried to sell it to the highest bidder,’ Sir Thomas finished. ‘The Serpent’s Teeth would be the ideal marketplace. A neutral, lawless ground where representatives from foreign powers could meet a traitor and inspect his wares.’

The *Nightingale*. A ship carrying a terrible new song. And Julian, her gentle, brilliant Julian, had been silenced to protect its secret. Her grief, which had settled into a cold, hard knot in her chest, now burned with a fresh, acidic anger. This was not just about avenging her husband’s death. It was about protecting his legacy, and the country he had died for.

Days bled into a week. The sea changed. The deep, royal blue of the open ocean gave way to a murkier, greenish tint. The air grew heavy, humid, thick with the smell of salt and something else, something primal and volcanic. Long, oily swells replaced the crisp waves, making the *Dauntless* pitch and yaw uncomfortably. The crew grew quieter, more watchful. Every man’s eyes were turned to the south.

Then, one grey afternoon, the lookout’s cry echoed from the masthead. ‘Land ahoy!’

Eleanor was on deck in an instant, Sir Thomas at her side. She raised the spyglass he had given her, her hands surprisingly steady. At first, she saw nothing but the haze that clung to the horizon. But then, as the ship rose on the crest of a swell, she saw them.

They did not look like islands. They looked like fangs. Jagged black peaks clawing at the sky, wreathed in plumes of white steam that hissed from unseen vents. They tore through the surface of the sea, sharp and brutal, a row of monstrous teeth waiting to devour them. The Serpent’s Teeth. The name was no poetic fancy. It was a literal description.

As they drew closer, the scale of the place became apparent. It was a vast archipelago, a maze of volcanic rock and treacherous water. The currents, as Captain Davies had warned, were devils. The sea boiled and churned around the bases of the islands, creating whirlpools and vicious crosscurrents that threatened to seize control of the ship.

Davies was in his element now. The uncertainty was gone, replaced by the pure, focused concentration of a master seaman navigating a deadly passage. He stood at the helm, his knuckles white, his voice barking a constant stream of orders to the men at the sails and the rudder.

‘Watch that reef to port! Hard to starboard! Bring her about, smartly now!’

The *Dauntless* danced a terrible, intricate ballet through the opening channels. Eleanor stood braced against the mainmast, the spyglass pressed to her eye. She was not watching the sea. She was scanning the coastlines of the islands, looking for any sign of human passage. A sheltered cove. A lookout post. A wisp of smoke from a cooking fire that was not volcanic in origin.

For two days, they saw nothing but rock, steam, and screaming seabirds. The tension on the ship was a palpable thing, a cloying weight in the humid air. They were a single, lonely vessel in a world of hostile stone.

On the third morning, the lookout called again, his voice strained. ‘Wreckage! Dead ahead!’

Eleanor’s heart hammered against her ribs. She trained her spyglass on the water. Floating in a calm, protected bay between two towering spires of rock was a field of debris. Splintered planks, a shattered spar, the tattered remains of a sailcloth.

Captain Davies eased the ship closer, his expression grim. ‘Too recent for the *Isolda*,’ he said, his voice low. ‘This wood hasn't been in the water more than a day or two.’

Sir Thomas, who had been scanning the debris with his own glass, suddenly stiffened. ‘There,’ he said, pointing. ‘Look there, by that rock.’

Eleanor followed his direction. Bobbing gently in the water, caught against a dark, seaweed slicked stone, was a piece of carved wood. It was painted a dark blue, with a flash of gold leaf that caught the weak morning light. Even from this distance, the carving was clear. It was a small bird, its head cocked, its beak open in song. A nightingale.

A silence fell over the quarterdeck, profound and absolute, a void into which the screams of the gulls and the hiss of the sea vanished. The carved nightingale. It was the crest of the Montagues, the family of the man Eleanor had crossed half the world to find. Julian. The image was burned into her memory, the same small bird embossed on his letters, engraved on his signet ring, the symbol of a lineage as old and proud as any in England. To see it here, floating amidst the evidence of such brutal destruction, was a shock that stole the breath from her lungs.

‘The Montagues,’ Sir Thomas breathed, his voice a low murmur beside her. He did not need to ask. He knew.

Captain Davies lowered his own glass, his face a mask of grim pragmatism. ‘So. The gentleman you seek was here. And recently.’ He swept his gaze across the bay, his sailor’s eye judging the currents, the rocks, the narrow entrance. ‘This is a clever little trap. A ship seeks shelter from the main channel, thinks it has found a safe harbor. But the tide could turn this into a cage. There is no easy way out if the wind is wrong.’

‘We have to go in,’ Eleanor said. Her voice was quiet but held a core of unyielding iron. Her gaze was fixed on the bobbing piece of wood.

‘Miss Howard, that is not wise,’ Davies countered, though his tone was more cautionary than dismissive. ‘We do not know what happened here. That other wreckage, the merchantman... it suggests this place is not merely dangerous by nature. Someone, or something, is an active threat.’

‘All the more reason to go ashore,’ she insisted, turning to face him. Her eyes, which had held the distant focus of the spyglass for days, were now sharp and direct. ‘Julian was here. His ship may be lost, but he and his crew may have survived. They may be on one of these islands, hurt, waiting for rescue.’

‘Or they met the same fate as that trader,’ Davies said bluntly. ‘And whoever finished that ship may be waiting on the shore for the next one to send a vulnerable party into their arms.’

‘Captain,’ Sir Thomas interjected, his hand resting lightly on the ship’s rail. ‘We have come this far. We have followed a ghost of a rumor into the mouth of hell itself. Now we have the first tangible proof that we are on the right path. To turn back now, to sail away from this… it is unthinkable. For Eleanor, and for me.’

Davies looked from Sir Thomas’s resolute face to Eleanor’s desperate hope. He chewed on his lip, his gaze returning to the treacherous waters of the bay. He was a man of the sea, a man who respected its power and its unforgiving nature. What they were proposing went against every instinct he had. But he was also a man who had accepted a contract, and the fire in Eleanor’s eyes spoke of a will that would not be denied.

‘Very well,’ he said at last, his voice tight with reluctance. ‘I will not risk the *Dauntless* in that basin. The currents are too fickle. But I will lower a longboat. Mr. Finch, my first mate, will command it. He will take four of my best men. And they will row you in, retrieve what you can, and make a swift assessment of the shore. One hour. Not a minute more. If you are not on your way back by then, I am to assume the worst.’ He fixed Eleanor with a stern look. ‘And you will obey Mr. Finch’s orders as if they were my own. If he says it is time to leave, you leave. Is that understood?’

‘Perfectly, Captain,’ Eleanor said, her heart soaring with a mixture of terror and gratitude. ‘Thank you.’

The lowering of the longboat was a swift, professional affair. Mr. Finch was a wiry, sun-weathered man with pale blue eyes that missed nothing. He did not speak much, but his presence was a reassuring one. He and his four sailors, armed with cutlasses and a single musket apiece, were the picture of calm competence. Eleanor, wrapped in a heavy cloak against the spray, sat in the stern sheets beside Sir Thomas. As they pulled away from the looming safety of the *Dauntless*, she felt a fresh pang of fear. The mother ship seemed a bastion of civilization in this raw, primeval world. The longboat was a tiny, fragile shell.

The journey across the half mile of water was strangely silent. The churning chaos of the outer channel gave way to an eerie, glassy calm inside the bay. The water was unnervingly clear. Looking over the side, Eleanor could see the dark shapes of rocks on the seabed thirty feet below, draped in long, waving fronds of kelp. The place felt ancient, secret. As they drew nearer to the debris field, the scale of the violence became clearer. A section of a ship’s hull floated on its side, the copper sheathing torn away like peeled fruit. A hammock, still knotted, drifted aimlessly.

The sailors maneuvered the boat with quiet strokes of their oars. One of them, a young man named Harris, reached out with a boathook and snagged the precious piece of carved wood. He passed it back, his hand dripping with seawater. The carving was heavier than it looked, made of dense, dark wood. The blue paint was chipped, the gold leaf tarnished, but it was unmistakably Julian’s nightingale. Eleanor took it from him, her fingers tracing the smooth lines of the bird’s wing. It was cold, inanimate, yet it felt like a direct connection to him. She closed her eyes for a moment, clutching it to her chest. He was real. He was here. This was not a fool’s errand.

‘Mr. Finch,’ Sir Thomas said, his voice low. He was pointing at a tangle of rigging and canvas nearby. ‘What do you make of that?’

Mr. Finch leaned over, his brow furrowed. ‘That’s a merchant’s flag. Dutch, I think. The *Haarlem Star*, maybe? Heard she was trading in these waters.’ He pointed with his chin towards the shore. ‘Looks like she ran aground on that submerged reef at the mouth of the bay. Tore her bottom out.’

‘An accident?’ Eleanor asked, her voice tight.

‘Could be,’ Finch said, his gaze scanning the steep, jungle choked cliffs that ringed the bay. ‘Or she was lured in. A false light on the shore, promising a safe anchorage. Old trick. A wretcher’s trick.’

The word hung in the air, ugly and menacing. Wreckers. Men who preyed on ships, who saw the Serpent’s Teeth not as a hazard but as an accomplice. One of the sailors shuddered. The stories were common in every port, tales of monsters who shone lanterns to guide ships onto rocks and then murdered the survivors for their cargo.

A cry from another sailor drew their attention. ‘Over there! By the rocks!’

Bobbing in the gentle swell near the shoreline was a flash of crimson. As they rowed closer, it resolved into a sailor’s sash, tangled in a clump of seaweed. A dark, rust colored stain spread from it, tainting the clear water. Blood.

‘That settles it,’ Mr. Finch said, his voice grim. ‘No accident. We go ashore. But we stay together, and we keep our eyes open.’

He directed the boat towards a small crescent of black sand, the only break in the jagged rock that formed the coastline. Two sailors stayed with the boat, their muskets primed and ready, while Mr. Finch, the other two men, Eleanor, and Sir Thomas stepped onto the island. The sand was hot under their feet, a fine, volcanic grit. The air was thick and humid, heavy with the smell of sulfur from the steaming vents on the high peaks, mingled with the sweet, cloying scent of rot from the jungle that began just yards from the beach. Twisted, skeletal trees, their bark bleached white by the sun and salt, stood like sentinels at the edge of the vegetation.

The signs were immediate and chilling. Footprints marred the black sand. Dozens of them. The heavy tread of booted sailors, the smaller, frantic tracks of men in bare feet. They crisscrossed the beach, signs of a confused, desperate landing. And among them, other tracks. Prints made by rough, hide-wrapped footwear.

‘Someone was waiting for them,’ Sir Thomas murmured, his hand resting on the hilt of the sword at his side.

‘Aye,’ Mr. Finch agreed, gesturing for his men to spread out slightly, their eyes scanning the dense wall of green before them. ‘They came ashore from the wreck, and the welcoming party was already here.’

Eleanor’s eyes searched the tracks, her heart a cold knot of dread. She was looking for a specific print, the elegant shape of Julian’s riding boots. She did not find it. But what she did find sent a fresh wave of hope and fear through her. A set of smaller, shallower tracks, leading away from the main chaos, towards the relative shelter of a rock outcropping. They were not the prints of a panicked man. They were measured, deliberate. The stride of someone thinking, not just reacting.

‘This way,’ she said, her voice a whisper.

They followed the tracks, moving with a caution that bordered on reverence. Behind the outcropping, shielded from the open beach, they found it. Not a camp, but the last resort of desperate survivors. A piece of sailcloth had been draped over a low branch to provide a semblance of shelter. A fire pit, long cold, held a small pile of gray ash. A few discarded tins of salted beef lay scattered about, forced open with a knife.

This was the camp of Julian’s crew. Eleanor felt it in her bones. The order amidst the chaos, the attempt to create a defensible position, it spoke of a disciplined command. Sir Thomas found a broken crate, the stenciling still visible. *L'Étoile du Soir*. The Evening Star. Julian’s ship.

So it was true. His ship had not wrecked. It had put its crew ashore here. But why? Had they seen the Dutch merchant founder and come to investigate? Had they been ambushed as well?

Eleanor knelt by the cold fire pit, her fingers brushing the ash. And then she saw it, tucked beneath a fold of the sailcloth, as if to protect it from the elements. A small, leather bound volume. Her breath caught in her throat. She knew it instantly. It was a book of Percy’s poems, one she had given him the week before he sailed. He had laughed, saying there was little room for poetry on a voyage of exploration, but she had seen the way he slipped it into his coat pocket. He would never, ever have abandoned it willingly.

Her hands trembled as she opened it. The pages were stiff with dried seawater, the ink blurred in places. But it was not a random page that fell open. A small, pressed flower, a wildflower from the English countryside, had been used to mark a specific passage. And beneath the printed words, in faint pencil, a message had been scrawled in Julian’s familiar, elegant hand.

*Eleanor, if you find this, they have taken us. Wretches who use this devil’s island as their lair. They watch from the high places, from the smoke that is not steam. We are being taken inland. Follow the silent falls. My star will guide you. Always. J.*

Eleanor read the words twice, three times, her world narrowing to the small page. He was alive. Captured, but alive. A wave of dizziness washed over her, and Sir Thomas’s strong hand was suddenly on her elbow, steadying her.

‘What is it, my dear?’

‘He’s alive,’ she whispered, showing him the book. ‘They’ve been taken.’

Mr. Finch crowded closer to read the message over Sir Thomas’s shoulder. His expression hardened. ‘Smoke that is not steam,’ he muttered, his eyes lifting to the peaks that surrounded them. The plumes rising from the volcanic vents were white. But now, as he looked, he could discern another smudge against the sky, further inland. It was thinner, grayer. The smoke of a wood fire. ‘The high places…’

As if summoned by the thought, a glint of light flashed from the high ridge overlooking their bay. It was sharp and brief, the sun reflecting off a piece of polished glass. Or metal.

A spyglass.

They were being watched.

The illusion of secrecy shattered. The quiet, overgrown island suddenly felt alive with unseen eyes, every rustle of leaves a potential footstep, every bird call a possible signal. The humid air, once merely oppressive, now felt suffocating, thick with malice.

‘Back to the boat,’ Mr. Finch snapped, his voice a low, urgent command. ‘Now. Move!’

There was no thought of argument. The two sailors fell into position behind him, their muskets raised towards the jungle. Sir Thomas drew his sword, its rasp of steel loud in the sudden silence. He placed himself between Eleanor and the dense foliage, his face set in a grim mask of protection. Eleanor clutched the book and the carved nightingale to her chest, her heart hammering not with fear, but with a new, cold resolve.

Their retreat to the beach was a tense, hurried affair. They imagined figures in every shadow, threats behind every twisted tree. A rock, dislodged by a scurrying lizard, tumbled down a small cliff face, and the sailors spun towards the sound, their knuckles white on their weapons. They scrambled into the longboat, pushing off the black sand with a desperate energy. The two men left to guard it had seen the glint of light as well, their faces pale with anxiety.

As the sailors rowed with all their might, pulling them away from the shore, Eleanor looked back. The island was as it had been before, a wall of rock and impenetrable green. The black sand beach was empty. The steam and the smoke rose into the hazy sky. It looked peaceful, deserted. But she knew better now. It was a liar. It was a predator’s den, a place of false lights and hidden watchers.

She held the wooden nightingale in one hand and the book of poems in the other. One was a symbol of his love, the other a message from his captivity. The search was over. The rescue had just begun. The Serpent’s Teeth were not just a navigational hazard. They were a fortress. And somewhere inside, Julian was waiting. She would not leave him there. No matter the cost. As the longboat reached the shadow of the *Dauntless*, Eleanor looked up at Captain Davies, who was watching their approach with a thunderous expression. She knew what she would have to ask of him, and she knew he would refuse. And she knew, with absolute certainty, that she would find a way to make him agree.

The rope ladder slapped against the hull of the *Dauntless*, a frantic rhythm against the deep thrum of the sea. Below, the longboat pitched and yawed in the swell. Eleanor felt strong hands haul her over the gunwale, her feet finding the solid deck with a jarring thud. The familiar scent of tar and brine filled her lungs, a welcome antidote to the island’s cloying perfume of decay and unseen threat. The rest of the shore party scrambled aboard after her, their faces slick with sweat, their eyes still scanning the green ramparts of the island they had fled.

Captain Davies stood before them, a monolith of disapproval. His arms were crossed over his barrel chest, and his face, weathered by a hundred storms, was a thunderous mask. His gaze flickered over their dishevelled state, the drawn sword in Sir Thomas’s hand, the ready muskets of the sailors, and finally rested on Eleanor, his eyes narrowing.

‘Mr. Hobbs,’ he barked to his first mate, his voice like grinding stones. ‘Prepare to weigh anchor. We are leaving this cursed place forthwith.’

‘No,’ Eleanor said. The word was quiet, yet it cut through the deck’s tense atmosphere like a shard of glass.

Captain Davies turned his full attention to her. It was a formidable force, one that had cowed mutinous sailors and stared down tempests. ‘Miss Ashworth,’ he began, his tone dangerously low, ‘you are a passenger on my ship. You do not give orders.’

‘We cannot leave,’ Sir Thomas interjected, stepping forward to stand beside Eleanor. He had not yet sheathed his sword. ‘Captain, we have confirmation. Julian is on that island. We saw evidence of a settlement. A fire. They are watching us.’

‘All the more reason to put this shore to our stern and never look back,’ Davies retorted, his jaw tight. ‘I am responsible for this vessel and the lives of the forty men aboard her. I was contracted for a search, Sir Thomas, not an invasion. My duty is to the ship, and my ship is in danger. We will be gone by nightfall.’

‘And condemn my brother to whatever fate awaits him in that place?’ Eleanor’s voice trembled, not with weakness, but with a burgeoning fury. ‘You would have us turn and flee like cowards, knowing he is there, alive?’

‘Knowing he is held by persons unknown, who possess spyglasses and the high ground, is precisely why we leave,’ Davies snapped. ‘To remain is suicide. What do you propose we do, Miss Ashworth? Sail the *Dauntless* into that cove and be dashed to splinters on the rocks? Send these men, sailors not soldiers, into a jungle full of hostile natives who know every path and every shadow? It is a fool’s errand.’

Mr. Finch, who had been quietly cleaning his spectacles on his sleeve, stepped forward. ‘They are not natives, Captain. Not in the traditional sense.’

All eyes turned to the naturalist. ‘Explain yourself, Finch,’ Sir Thomas said, his brow furrowed.

‘The spyglass,’ Finch said, placing the spectacles back on his nose. ‘The glint was too refined. And the smoke… it was from a chimney, I would wager. A small one, but properly constructed. The settlement is not some primitive village. It is an outpost. Organized. Deliberate. The question is, an outpost of what?’

‘Pirates,’ a sailor muttered from the rail, his voice filled with dread. The word hung in the air, a poison dart. The Serpent’s Teeth were notorious, a place ships avoided for their treacherous currents and fanged rocks. It was a perfect lair.

‘Pirates or worse,’ Captain Davies said, unmoved. ‘It changes nothing. My orders stand. We are leaving.’ He turned, a clear dismissal.

‘Captain Davies.’ Eleanor’s voice was different now. The tremor was gone, replaced by a cold, level command that made the seasoned captain pause and turn back. She held up the book of poems. ‘This is my brother’s. We found it on the beach. He left it for us.’

She walked towards him, her gaze unwavering. The crew watched in silence, a captive audience to this strange duel of wills. She did not stop until she stood directly before him, forcing him to look down at her.

‘Your contract,’ she said, her voice clear and precise, ‘was to find Julian Ashworth. You have found him. Your contract is not fulfilled until he is standing on this deck. To leave now would be a breach of that contract. My father’s solicitors would take a very dim view of that. They would see to it that you never captained so much as a river barge again.’

A muscle feathered in Davies’s jaw. He was a proud man, and the threat, veiled as it was, landed like a physical blow. ‘Are you threatening me, Miss Ashworth?’

‘I am reminding you of your obligations, Captain,’ she countered smoothly. ‘But that is not my most compelling argument.’ She gestured for Sir Thomas, who opened his oilskin pouch and produced a small, heavy purse. He tossed it onto a nearby barrel, where it landed with a dull, metallic chink.

‘That is a retainer,’ Eleanor said. ‘For the new contract. A rescue. Every man in your crew who volunteers for this undertaking will receive a bonus of one hundred sovereigns upon its completion. You, Captain, will receive one thousand. My father’s resources are not infinite, but for the life of his son, they are close to it. I will sign the letter of credit myself, guaranteed against my own inheritance.’

A stunned silence fell over the deck. One hundred sovereigns was more than a sailor might see in five years of hard service. It was a life changing sum. Men exchanged wide eyed glances. The fear was still there, but now it was mingled with a new, powerful emotion: avarice.

Captain Davies stared from the purse to Eleanor, his expression unreadable. He was a man of principle, but he was also a pragmatist. He knew the effect her offer would have on his crew. To refuse now might sow a different kind of danger aboard his ship.

‘Money does not stop a bullet,’ he said, his voice softer now, almost weary. ‘It does not heal a wound.’

‘No,’ Eleanor agreed. ‘But it can buy loyalty. And it can pay for the risk we are about to ask of these men. We are not asking you to launch a frontal assault. That would be madness, as you say. But we cannot simply sail away. There must be another way. A smarter way.’

The captain’s cabin was small and stuffy, the air thick with the smell of lamp oil and old charts. The ship’s gentle rocking was a constant, unsettling motion. Captain Davies sat at his heavy oak table, his large hands resting flat on its surface, as if to hold it down. Sir Thomas stood by the stern window, staring out at the darkening mass of the island. Mr. Finch was unrolling a chart, weighting its corners with a spyglass and a lead inkwell. Eleanor stood opposite the captain, her hands clutching the back of a chair.

‘A smarter way,’ Davies repeated, his voice devoid of emotion. ‘Enlighten me, Miss Ashworth. What is this grand, intelligent strategy you have conceived between the beach and my deck?’

‘Reconnaissance,’ Mr. Finch said without looking up from the chart. ‘Not an attack. A foray. We do not know their numbers, their fortifications, their routines. To act blindly would be a catastrophe.’

‘The moon is a sliver tonight,’ Sir Thomas added, turning from the window. ‘We would have the cover of darkness. A small party, in a single boat. Land on a different beach, away from the one we visited. Ascertain the layout of their settlement and the disposition of their forces.’

Captain Davies looked at the chart. It was a beautiful piece of cartography, purchased at great expense in London, showing the archipelago in meticulous detail. The main island was a knot of contour lines, indicating steep, almost vertical cliffs on most sides. But Finch was pointing to a spot on the southern coast.

‘Here,’ the naturalist said. ‘This inlet is marked as being choked with mangroves. Unsuitable for a deep draught vessel, but a longboat might pass through. The foliage would offer excellent cover for an approach. From there, a ridge leads inland. It is a more difficult climb, but it would keep a party out of the valley where we presume the settlement lies.’

‘A settlement we know nothing about,’ Davies countered. ‘For all we know, it is a fortress bristling with cannon.’

‘Unlikely,’ Finch said calmly. ‘If they had cannon, they would have fired a warning shot at a vessel of our size by now. The spyglass suggests vigilance, not military might. They are hiding. They do not want to be found. A large, well armed pirate crew would be more brazen.’

‘This is all conjecture,’ Davies sighed, rubbing his temples. ‘Speculation and hope.’

‘It is more than we had this morning,’ Eleanor insisted. She came around the chair and placed her hands on the chart, next to Finch’s. ‘This morning, we were searching for a ghost. Now we have a location. We have proof of life. We have a purpose. All we lack is a plan.’

Davies looked at her, at the fierce, unyielding light in her eyes. He had seen that look before, in admirals and desperate men, and he knew it was not something that could be easily dismissed. He had sailed the world, and he knew that sometimes the most dangerous currents were not in the sea, but in the hearts of determined people.

‘And who,’ he asked, his voice heavy with resignation, ‘would be in this party of yours, Sir Thomas? You, I presume. Mr. Finch, with his maps and his mangroves. Who else?’

‘Two of your best men,’ Sir Thomas said. ‘Steady hands with a musket. Men who can move quietly. I would leave the selection to you.’

‘Four men,’ Davies mused. ‘Against an unknown enemy on their own ground.’ He shook his head slowly. ‘The odds are poor.’

‘They are better than zero,’ Eleanor said. ‘Which is what they will be if we sail away.’

There was a long silence, broken only by the creak of the ship’s timbers. Captain Davies stared at the chart, at the island that sat on the paper like a spider in the center of a web. He thought of the hundred sovereigns, a king’s ransom to his men. He thought of the threat to his reputation, and the iron will of the young woman before him. But most of all, he thought of a man left behind, a situation he, as a captain, could not truly abide.

‘Very well,’ he said at last, the words seeming to be dragged from him. ‘One attempt. You will have your small party. You will have the cover of darkness. You will take the longboat and four of my best men, not two. Mr. Hobbs will be one of them. He is steady and knows how to lead. You will go ashore. You will observe only. You will not engage the enemy unless you are discovered. At the first sign of overwhelming resistance, you will retreat. If you are not back by dawn, the *Dauntless* will weigh anchor and leave you to your fate. Those are my terms. Are they accepted?’

Sir Thomas nodded grimly. ‘They are.’

‘I am going with them,’ Eleanor said.

‘Absolutely not,’ Sir Thomas and Captain Davies said in unison.

‘This is not a debate,’ Sir Thomas said, his voice hardening. ‘Eleanor, you will remain on the ship. It is far too dangerous.’

‘And what use am I here?’ she shot back, her hands balling into fists. ‘Wringing my hands and praying? Julian is my brother. I know him better than anyone. I might see something you miss, a signal, a sign. He might try to communicate in a way only I would understand. He did it once with the book. He might do it again.’

‘She has a point,’ Finch murmured, peering at her over his spectacles. ‘A familiar eye is a valuable tool.’

‘Finch, do not encourage her,’ Sir Thomas warned.

‘I am not a child to be encouraged or forbidden,’ Eleanor declared, her chin high. ‘I will not be a liability. I can handle a boat. I can be quiet. But I will not be left behind. This is my search. My rescue. I will see it through.’

Sir Thomas looked at her, his face a mixture of frustration, fear, and profound admiration. He saw that the young woman he had vowed to protect was gone, replaced by someone stronger, more resolute than he could have imagined. Arguing was futile. She would find a way, with or without his permission. He let out a long, defeated breath.

‘She will stay with the boat,’ he said to Davies, not looking at Eleanor. ‘Under guard. She will not go inland. That is my final offer.’

Eleanor opened her mouth to protest, but a sharp look from Sir Thomas silenced her. It was a compromise, a foothold. She would take it. For now.

Captain Davies gave a curt nod. ‘So be it. Begin your preparations. You leave in two hours.’

The deck of the *Dauntless* was a hive of quiet, purposeful activity. Under the dim light of shielded lanterns, men were checking the flint and powder of their muskets. Blades were being honed on whetstones, the scrape of steel a whispering threat in the night. Mr. Hobbs, the first mate, a tall, silent man with a face like carved oak, was selecting ropes and grappling hooks. There was little talk. The colossal bribe had bought their service, but it had not dispelled the fear. Every man knew the risk. They were venturing into the serpent’s mouth.

Eleanor stood by the starboard rail, the wooden nightingale clutched in her hand. The sea was black, merging with the blacker mass of the island. A few faint stars pricked the humid haze of the sky. The air was unnervingly still. She felt a presence at her side and turned to see Sir Thomas. He was dressed for the expedition, in dark, practical clothing, a pair of pistols tucked into his belt alongside his sheathed sword.

He did not speak for a moment, simply stood beside her, his gaze fixed on the island. ‘I used to think you were like a porcelain doll, Eleanor,’ he said quietly, his voice a low rumble. ‘Something beautiful and fragile, to be kept safe on a high shelf. I have never been more wrong in my life.’

‘We all become what we must, Thomas,’ she replied, her own voice barely a whisper. ‘I did not know I had this in me, until I had no other choice.’

‘Julian is a lucky man,’ he said. ‘To have a sister who would cross the world and brave the devil himself for him.’ He reached into his coat and produced a small, finely made flintlock pistol, much smaller than his own. The stock was polished walnut, the metal a dull silver. ‘I had this commissioned for my own sister once. She found it unladylike.’ He pressed it into her hand. ‘I would rather you did not need it. But I will not have you defenseless.’

The pistol was heavy, cold. A solid, deadly reality. Eleanor curled her fingers around the grip. ‘Thank you.’

‘Stay with the boat, Eleanor,’ he said, his voice pleading now. ‘Promise me.’

She looked from his earnest, worried face to the dark island. A promise was a cage, and she could not afford to be caged. ‘I promise I will do everything in my power to ensure we all return safely,’ she said carefully. It was not the promise he wanted, but it was the only one she could give.

He seemed to understand. He gave a small, sad nod and squeezed her shoulder. ‘Be safe.’

An hour later, the longboat was lowered into the water, its oars muffled with strips of canvas. Six figures descended the rope ladder: Sir Thomas, Mr. Finch, Mr. Hobbs, and three grim faced sailors chosen for their nerve. Eleanor was the last to board, her pistol tucked securely in her belt, the book of poems and the wooden nightingale in a small pouch at her side.

They pushed off from the *Dauntless*, a ghost ship in the dark, and began to row. The only sounds were the soft dip and pull of the muffled oars and the gentle lapping of water against the hull. The island grew before them, a solid wall of blackness that blotted out the stars. As they rounded a headland and entered the channel Mr. Finch had identified, the air grew thick and stagnant, heavy with the smell of mud and rotting vegetation. The tangled roots of the mangroves arched over the water, creating a claustrophobic tunnel. Strange clicks and rustles echoed from the dense foliage around them.

Mr. Hobbs guided the boat expertly through the narrow, winding waterway, his eyes sharp in the gloom. Finally, the mangroves thinned, and the boat’s nose nudged against a muddy bank. This was it.

‘We go on foot from here,’ Sir Thomas whispered. ‘Stay with the boat, Eleanor. You and Ames,’ he gestured to one of the sailors, a burly man with a nervous twitch. ‘If we are not back by the first light of dawn, you row back to the *Dauntless*. Do you understand?’

Eleanor nodded, her heart pounding. The sailor, Ames, checked the priming on his musket, his eyes darting into the oppressive darkness of the jungle.

Sir Thomas, Finch, Hobbs, and the other two men melted into the trees like specters. For a long time, there was only silence. Eleanor sat in the boat, straining her ears, every nerve alight. The jungle was not quiet. It was filled with a thousand tiny sounds, a symphony of insects, the drip of water, the sigh of unseen things.

Time stretched, becoming an elastic, unbearable thing. An hour passed. Then another. Ames, the sailor left with her, was a statue of tension, his musket held at the ready. Eleanor clutched the pouch at her side. She felt useless, a parcel left by the shore. Her promise to Thomas felt like a lead weight.

Then, a sound broke the night’s drone. It was faint, distant, but unmistakable. The sound of a single, muffled gunshot.

Ames swore under his breath, spinning to face the jungle. ‘They’ve been found.’

Panic, cold and sharp, seized Eleanor. They had walked into a trap. Her mind raced. The plan was to retreat if discovered. But what if they could not? What if they were pinned down, or worse?

She could not just sit here. She could not wait for a dawn that might never come for them.

‘Stay here,’ she told Ames, her voice a low command. ‘Keep the boat ready.’

‘Miss, Sir Thomas’s orders…’ he began, his voice a panicked whisper.

‘Sir Thomas may be in need of help he cannot ask for,’ she cut in, her tone leaving no room for argument. She was already climbing out of the boat, her feet sinking into the soft mud. She pulled the pistol from her belt. The cold weight of it was a comfort.

She was no warrior, no soldier. She was a poet’s sister. But the island had taught her that poetry was not enough. The search was over. The rescue had begun. And she would not fail. Ignoring the sailor’s hissed protest, Eleanor Ashworth pushed aside a heavy, wet frond and stepped into the suffocating darkness of the jungle, moving towards the echo of the shot, towards her brother. The predator’s den had her friends. She was coming to get them back.

The jungle swallowed her whole. The damp frond slapped back into place behind her, severing the last sliver of sight connecting her to the boat, to Ames, to the world of reason. The air was a thick, wet blanket, heavy with the perfume of unknown blossoms and the foul breath of decay. It was not mere darkness, for the canopy above was so dense that it blotted out the moon and stars, creating a profound, primal blackness that felt like a physical substance. Sounds pressed in on all sides, a cacophony that had been background noise from the boat but was now an intimate, unnerving assault. The chirr of a thousand insects, the low croak of a corpulent frog, the rustle of something small and fast in the undergrowth, the distant, mournful cry of a night bird.

Eleanor’s foot slipped on a slick root and she went down on one knee, her hand sinking into cool, squelching mud. She bit back a cry, a mixture of pain and frustration. Her fine wool skirt, already stained from the boat, was now ruined, heavy with mud and water. She pushed herself up, her breath coming in ragged gasps. This was madness. She was a scholar’s daughter, more at home in a library than a lethal wilderness. Every instinct screamed at her to turn back, to scramble back to the relative safety of the boat and the twitchy but armed sailor.

But the echo of the gunshot was a brand on her mind, and the image of Thomas’s face, earnest and trusting as he asked her to find Finch, was a fire in her belly. She had made a promise. More than that, these men, her brother, Finch, even the gruff Sir Thomas, had become her expedition, her family in this strange and hostile corner of the world. The predator’s den. The words echoed in her thoughts. She had been the bait, the reason for this ill fated rescue. She could not abandon the rescuers.

Taking a deep, shuddering breath, she tried to impose order on the chaos of her senses. The shot had come from inland. She had been facing the jungle, so it was somewhere ahead, perhaps slightly to her right. It was a flimsy direction, a guess based on a fading memory of sound, but it was all she had. She held the pistol up, not to aim, but to lead, the cold metal a talisman against the encroaching fear. She began to move, not quickly, but with a deliberate, agonizing slowness. She tested each footstep before putting her weight down, her free hand outstretched, brushing against the dripping leaves and rough bark of unseen trees.

Vines, slick with unseen dew, clutched at her skirts and snagged in her hair, pulling the careful coils loose. A spider’s web, thick as thread, broke across her face, and she choked back a scream, frantically wiping at her skin, imagining the crawling touch of its creator. The jungle was not just an obstacle; it felt malevolent, a living entity that resented her intrusion. Time ceased to have meaning. It could have been minutes or an hour that she crept forward, guided only by the slight downward slope of the ground and her own desperate hope.

Then, she saw it. Not a light, but a change in the texture of the darkness. A flicker. She froze, her heart a frantic drum against her ribs. She sank into a crouch behind the broad, waxy leaves of a plant that came up to her shoulders. Peering through a gap, she waited. There it was again. A soft, orange pulse, somewhere ahead and to her left. A fire. Where there was fire, there were people.

Her new direction was no longer a guess. She moved towards the light with renewed purpose, the immediate terrors of the jungle subsumed by the greater dread of what she would find. As she drew closer, the sounds of the wilderness began to fade, replaced by something else. The low murmur of voices. The language was guttural, completely alien, punctuated by sharp clicks and soft hisses. It was the language Finch had tried to mimic, the one spoken by the islanders who had taken him.

She was close now, perhaps only fifty yards from the source of the light. The ground grew firmer, the undergrowth thinner, as if a trail was often walked here. She got down on her stomach, ignoring the damp chill of the earth seeping through her clothes, and began to crawl the final distance. The smell of woodsmoke was strong now, mingled with the scent of roasting meat and something else, something acrid and unpleasant.

She reached the edge of a natural clearing. Before her was a scene that seemed torn from a fever dream. A small village, if it could be called that, was nestled in a clearing carved from the very heart of the jungle. Crude huts of woven branches and mud were arranged in a rough circle around a large, central fire pit. The flickering flames cast dancing, monstrous shadows on the trees and the faces of the people gathered there.

There were perhaps twenty of them, men and women. They were tall and lean, their skin the color of rich earth, decorated with swirling white and red pigments that made their faces into terrifying masks. Some wore simple loincloths of hide or woven grass, while others were draped in fabrics of a surprisingly fine weave, dyed in deep indigos and scarlets. They were not the wild, unclothed savages she might have imagined. There was an order here, a sense of society, which was somehow more frightening.

Her eyes scanned the crowd, desperately searching. And then she saw them.

They were tied to a thick wooden totem pole at the edge of the clearing, away from the fire’s warmth. Sir Thomas, Finch, and the two sailors. Mr. Hobbs, the river pilot, lay on the ground near them, unnervingly still. He was not tied, but a dark stain was spreading on the earth beneath his chest. Eleanor’s breath caught in her throat, a sob of pure horror. The gunshot. It had found its mark.

Sir Thomas was on his feet, bound tightly, his face a mask of stone. His jacket was torn and a cut on his forehead bled freely down his temple, but his eyes were alive with a furious, defiant fire. Finch was beside him, looking smaller and more fragile than ever, his face pale with terror. He kept glancing at the still form of Hobbs, his expression one of disbelief. The other two sailors were a study in contrast; one slumped in his bonds, his head hanging in defeat, while the other strained against the ropes, his face purple with effort and rage.

The islanders seemed to be paying them little attention. They were focused on a figure who stood near the fire, addressing the gathering. He was taller than the others, his body a canvas of intricate white patterns. A headdress of iridescent black feathers cascaded down his back, and in his hand, he held a long, polished wooden staff topped with a piece of carved, luminous green stone. He was clearly the leader, a chieftain or a priest. He spoke, and the others listened with rapt attention, their expressions grim and unreadable.

Panic gave way to a cold, clear focus. Crying would not help them. Screaming would get her caught. Sitting here and watching would see them all dead by morning. Her gaze fell to the pistol in her hand. It was a single shot weapon. She had one bullet. To use it now would be suicide. It would not save them.

She needed a plan. A distraction. Something loud, something chaotic, something that would draw all eyes away from the prisoners and give them a chance, however slim, to fight back. Her eyes swept the periphery of the camp. The huts were made of dried branches and leaves. They were tinder boxes.

The fire pit was the key. Most of the islanders were gathered on the far side, listening to their leader. A smaller, secondary fire burned near the edge of the clearing, not far from where she was hidden. It looked like a cooking fire, with a few spits of meat roasting over it. Beside it, a woman was tending a stack of dry firewood.

An idea, wild and dangerous, bloomed in Eleanor’s mind. It was a terrible risk, but it was better than the certainty of death that awaited her friends. She checked the priming on her pistol, ensuring the powder was dry. This was her only real weapon, and she would save it for a moment when it could truly make a difference. She slid the cold metal back into her belt. Then, she began to move, not back the way she came, but sideways, circling the clearing in the deep shadows of the jungle, her destination the small cooking fire.

Every snapped twig, every rustle of a leaf, sounded like a thunderclap in her ears. She moved in a low crouch, her muscles screaming in protest. A guard, armed with a long, obsidian tipped spear, paced slowly at the edge of the clearing, his path taking him within a few feet of her intended route. She froze behind a thick tree trunk, her heart pounding so hard she was sure he must hear it. He paused, looking directly into the darkness where she hid. Eleanor held her breath, convinced she was discovered. The man grunted, scratched his side, and then continued his patrol, his back to her.

She waited a few more seconds, then darted across the open space to the woodpile. She crouched behind it, her back pressed against the stacked branches. She could feel the heat of the cooking fire on her cheek. The woman who had been tending it had moved away to join the main group, drawn by the leader’s speech.

Eleanor peered around the woodpile. The nearest hut was less than ten feet away. Its thatched roof was low and dry, perfect kindling. She reached into the pile, her fingers closing around a long, thin branch. Carefully, she pushed one end into the edge of the cooking fire. The tip sizzled, smoked, and then caught, blossoming into a small, bright flame. She had her torch.

Now came the most dangerous part. She had to get from the fire to the hut without being seen. The leader was still speaking, his voice rising in a passionate crescendo. All eyes were on him. This was her chance.

Taking a last, steadying breath, Eleanor rose from her crouch. Holding the burning branch low, she moved like a wraith across the short distance to the hut. She reached the wall of woven sticks and thrust the torch deep into the dry thatch of the roof. For a terrifying second, nothing happened. Then, a wisp of smoke curled upwards, followed by a flicker of orange. The dry material caught with a sudden, greedy whoosh.

Flames licked up the side of the hut. Eleanor did not wait to admire her work. She scrambled back into the darkness of the jungle just as the first person shouted in alarm.

The effect was instantaneous and absolute. The leader’s speech was cut short. The mesmerized crowd broke apart, turning to see the pillar of fire now roaring up from the roof of the hut. Shouts of anger and confusion filled the air. The fire spread with terrifying speed, sparks and embers carried on the night breeze, threatening the other huts. The orderly village dissolved into a panicked mob.

In the chaos, Eleanor saw Sir Thomas act. With the guards distracted, he threw his entire body weight against the man next to him, knocking him off balance. ‘Now!’ his voice roared over the din.

It was the signal. The sailor who had been straining at his bonds gave a final, desperate heave, and the rope, perhaps weakened or poorly tied, gave way. He lunged for a spear dropped by a frantic islander and immediately turned on the captor who was trying to subdue Sir Thomas. Finch, seeing his chance, kicked backwards with all his might, catching a man behind him in the shins and then dropping to the ground, wriggling to get out of the way.

Eleanor’s gaze was fixed on the scene, her knuckles white. Two of the islander guards, recovering from their surprise, turned back to the prisoners, their faces contorted with rage. One raised a heavy wooden club to bring down on Sir Thomas’s head.

This was the moment. Eleanor raised her pistol, the front sight a tiny, wavering notch against the firelight. She had never fired a pistol at a living thing. Her hand trembled. She steadied it with her other hand, aimed for the center of the man’s chest, and squeezed the trigger.

The explosion was deafening, a sharp crack that cut through the noise of the fire and the shouting. The pistol bucked in her hand, the recoil jarring her arm to the shoulder. The man with the club stopped, a look of shocked surprise on his face. He looked down at the new, dark hole in his chest, then crumpled to the ground without a sound.

The shot had done more than save Sir Thomas. It had pinpointed the source of the attack for her friends. Sir Thomas’s head whipped around, his eyes finding her silhouette at the edge of the trees. His expression was one of pure astonishment.

‘Eleanor!’

There was no time for reunions. The other guard was upon them, but the freed sailor met his charge with the stolen spear. The fight was brutal and quick. More islanders were turning their attention from the fire back to the prisoners, their initial panic turning to focused rage.

‘To the river!’ Sir Thomas bellowed, cutting the ropes that still bound Finch and the other sailor. He grabbed a fallen club. ‘Eleanor, run!’

She did not need telling. She turned and plunged back into the darkness. She could hear them behind her, crashing through the undergrowth. Sir Thomas, a surprisingly fast runner, was right behind her. ‘This way!’ he grunted, grabbing her arm and pulling her in a slightly different direction. ‘I marked the trail!’

The retreat was a nightmare of blind running. Branches whipped at their faces, roots tried to trip them at every step. Behind them, they could hear the sounds of pursuit, angry shouts and the crashing of many bodies moving through the jungle. They were a pack of wolves, and they were hunting.

Finch was struggling to keep up, his lungs burning. One of the sailors, a man named Green, practically carried him for part of the way. Sir Thomas led the group with an animal instinct for direction, never hesitating, pushing them onward with guttural commands. They ran not towards the boat’s landing spot, but parallel to it, a longer but less direct route that Sir Thomas hoped would confuse their pursuers.

Finally, through the thinning trees, Eleanor saw the glint of open water. The river. They burst from the tree line onto the muddy bank, a hundred yards down from where they had left the boat.

‘Ames!’ Sir Thomas yelled, his voice raw. ‘Ames, bring the boat!’

For a heart stopping moment, there was no reply. The shouts of the islanders were closer now, just inside the tree line. They had run out of time.

Then, a shape detached itself from the deep shadows of the mangrove roots. The longboat, with Ames frantically poling it towards them. The sailor’s face was a pale oval of terror and relief in the gloom.

‘Quickly! In!’ Sir Thomas ordered, pushing Eleanor towards the boat as Ames brought it alongside the bank.

Green and the other sailor scrambled in, then reached back to haul the exhausted Finch over the gunwale. Sir Thomas shoved Eleanor in after them. As he prepared to climb in himself, a spear flew out of the darkness, hissing past his head and embedding itself in the boat’s wooden seat with a solid thunk.

The first of the islanders had reached the riverbank. A tall warrior, his face a mask of white paint, stood poised to throw another. Sir Thomas did not hesitate. He raised the heavy club he still carried and hurled it with all his might. It caught the warrior in the chest, sending him stumbling backward with a grunt of pain.

That second of delay was all they needed. Sir Thomas vaulted into the boat. ‘Row!’ he screamed at Ames. ‘Row for your life!’

Ames and the other sailors needed no encouragement. They dug their oars into the dark water, the boat lurching forward, away from the shore. More figures were appearing on the bank now, a crowd of furious, painted faces. A few more spears were thrown, splashing harmlessly into the water behind them. They shouted, their voices filled with rage, but their prey was escaping.

The boat slid out into the center of the narrow channel, the frantic splashing of the oars the only sound besides their own ragged breathing. Eleanor slumped onto a seat, her body trembling with adrenaline and exhaustion. The pistol, forgotten, fell from her limp hand and clattered on the floorboards.

They were alive. They were clear. She looked back at the shore. The islanders were still there, watching, their forms lit by the distant, angry glow of the fire she had set. The predator’s den was burning. And she, the poet’s sister, had been the one to light the match.

Sir Thomas collapsed onto the bench opposite her. He looked at the spear still quivering in the seat, then at the bodies of his men in the boat, then at Finch, who was being tended to by Green. Finally, his eyes settled on Eleanor. They were filled with a complex mixture of fury, awe, and profound relief.

‘Eleanor Ashworth,’ he said, his voice a low rumble. ‘You have defied my direct orders. You have put yourself in mortal peril. You are reckless, disobedient, and an absolute madwoman.’ He paused, taking a deep breath. ‘And you have just saved all our lives. We are in your debt.’

Eleanor could only stare at him, the words washing over her like the cold river spray. His face, etched with grime and lit by the terrible glow from the island, was a mask of contradictions. Debt. The word hung in the air, heavy and strange. She had never been in anyone’s debt, nor had anyone of consequence ever been in hers. She was William Ashworth’s sister, a creature of libraries and drawing rooms, a quiet observer of other people’s lives. The pistol on the floorboards, the memory of the acrid smoke in her lungs, the phantom heat of the flames on her face, all screamed otherwise.

‘I…’ she started, but her voice was a dry whisper. She swallowed, tasting ash. ‘I did what was necessary.’

It was a feeble reply, wholly inadequate for the magnitude of the night. Sir Thomas’s gaze did not soften. He simply nodded, a sharp, birdlike motion. ‘Indeed. That you did.’ He turned his attention then, his authority reasserting itself like a shroud settling over his shoulders. The moment of shared humanity, of raw emotion, was over. He was the commander once more. ‘Green, how is Finch?’

‘He’s breathing steady, sir,’ Green replied, his voice low. He had torn a strip from his own shirt to fashion a crude bandage for the botanist’s head. ‘Lost a lot of blood, but the bleeding’s slowed. He’s unconscious, though. Has been since they struck him.’

‘And the others?’ Sir Thomas asked, his gaze drifting to the two still forms lying at the bottom of the boat. They were covered now by a spare sail, a small mercy against the indignity of their deaths.

‘Dawes and Miller, sir,’ Ames said, his voice trembling. He rowed with a frantic, jerky rhythm, his eyes fixed on the dark water ahead, as if terrified to look back at the shore. ‘They never stood a chance. Ambushed from the trees.’

Sir Thomas’s jaw tightened, a muscle jumping in his cheek. He reached out and gently pulled back a corner of the sail. In the gloom, the faces were pale and slack, their life’s voyages ended on this forgotten spit of land. He covered them again, his movements deliberate and heavy. The loss of each man was a weight he carried personally. He had chosen them for this shore party. Their deaths were his to answer for.

Eleanor watched him, a strange pang of something akin to pity cutting through her own shock. He was not merely a tyrant of the quarterdeck. He was a man responsible for other men, and he was failing them. Or rather, this island was defeating him. She looked down at her hands. They were black with soot and dirt, her nails broken. She had torn her dress scrambling through the undergrowth. She was a ruin, just like his expedition.

The river widened, the claustrophobic press of the mangrove roots giving way to a broader expanse of water. The sounds of the island receded, the furious shouts fading into the general thrum of the jungle’s nightlife. Above them, the canopy opened up and stars, brilliant and impossibly distant, glittered in the inky sky. The air grew cooler, cleaner, smelling of salt and open water. They had reached the river’s mouth.

Ahead, a single light burned, a tiny yellow star floating in the vast darkness. The *Sea Serpent*. Home. Safety. Civilisation.

‘Signal the ship,’ Sir Thomas ordered. ‘Let them know we’re coming.’

Green fumbled for the small oil lamp kept in the boat’s stern locker. He lit the wick with a shaking hand, the small flame struggling against the breeze before catching hold. He raised it and shuttered the light three times. They waited, drifting on the gentle swell, the only sound the lapping of water against the hull and Finch’s shallow breathing.

A moment later, the light on the ship answered, blinking twice. They were seen.

‘Row,’ Sir Thomas commanded, his voice regaining its iron edge. ‘Ames, you and Jacobs, put your backs into it. Let’s get our wounded and our dead home.’

The two sailors obeyed without a word, their exhaustion momentarily forgotten. The longboat surged through the water, closing the distance to the ship. As they drew nearer, the *Sea Serpent* resolved itself from the darkness, its tall masts and intricate rigging a comforting, familiar silhouette against the star-dusted sky. Figures moved on the deck, and a rope ladder was thrown over the side, its wooden rungs clattering against the hull.

‘Boat ahoy!’ a voice called down. It was Mr. Cobb, the first mate. ‘What’s your status, Sir Thomas?’

‘Heavy casualties, Mr. Cobb,’ Sir Thomas shouted back, his voice carrying clearly over the water. ‘Two dead, one grievously wounded. Prepare the surgeon. We’re coming aboard.’

The news was met with a stunned silence from the deck. Then, a flurry of activity. The task of getting everyone aboard was grim and methodical. A sling was lowered for Finch, and he was hoisted up with an unnerving gentleness, a limp sack of a man. The two dead sailors followed, their shrouded forms a terrible sight for the crew gathered at the rail. Green and the other sailors scrambled up the ladder, their faces grim.

Then it was Eleanor’s turn. Sir Thomas gestured to the ladder. ‘Miss Ashworth.’

She looked at the swaying ladder, then at the dark water below. Her body, which had carried her through the jungle on pure adrenaline, suddenly felt impossibly heavy. Her legs trembled, and a wave of dizziness washed over her. ‘I don’t think I can.’

Sir Thomas’s expression was unreadable. For a moment, she thought he might order her, berate her for her weakness. Instead, he simply said, ‘Yes, you can.’ He placed a hand on the small of her back, a firm, steadying pressure. ‘One rung at a time. I will be right behind you.’

Drawing a shaky breath, Eleanor reached for the ladder. The rope was rough against her palms. She placed one foot on a rung, then another. Up she went, her world narrowing to the wooden rungs, the solid hull of the ship, and the quiet presence of Sir Thomas below. When her hand was finally grasped by a waiting sailor and she was pulled onto the solid planks of the deck, she nearly collapsed.

The ship’s surgeon, a harried looking man named Davies, was already crouched over Finch. The crew stood about in silent, anxious groups, their faces pale in the lantern light. They stared at Eleanor, at her torn and filthy dress, her soot-stained face, her wild hair. They looked at her as if she were a ghost, a creature spat out from the island’s dark maw.

Sir Thomas was the last one aboard. He hauled himself over the rail, his face a granite mask of command. ‘Mr. Cobb,’ he said, his voice ringing with authority that silenced all whispers. ‘Secure the longboat. Double the watch. No one else sets foot on that island. Is that understood?’

‘Aye, sir,’ the first mate replied, his gaze flickering towards the shrouded bodies.

‘Mr. Davies, report to me on Finch’s condition as soon as you have one,’ Sir Thomas continued. ‘The rest of you, get back to your duties. There’s nothing more to be seen here.’

He dismissed them all with a wave of his hand, and the crew, though reluctant, dispersed. His eyes then found Eleanor, who was standing numbly by the rail. ‘Go to your cabin, Miss Ashworth. Get some rest. We will speak in the morning.’ It was an order, not a suggestion.

She could only nod. Turning, she made her way across the deck, acutely aware of the path the crew cleared for her, the way they avoided her eyes. She felt contaminated, marked by the island’s violence. She descended the companionway to the lower deck, her cabin a small haven of order at the end of the narrow corridor.

Inside, she shut the door and leaned against it, the familiar click of the latch sounding like a gunshot in the sudden silence. The cabin was exactly as she had left it. Her journal lay open on the small desk, a book of her brother’s poetry beside it. Her spare dress was hung neatly on a peg. It was a scene from another life, a world away from fire and spears and the wet, heavy weight of a pistol.

She caught her reflection in the small, salt-crazed mirror above her washbasin. The woman who stared back was a stranger. Her eyes were wide and dark, haunted. Her face was a canvas of soot and fear. There was blood, not her own, dried in a dark smear along her jawline. Who was this person? This was not Eleanor Ashworth, the poet’s sister. This was someone else entirely, someone forged in the heart of a burning village.

Mechanically, she poured water from the pitcher into the basin. It was cool against her skin. She scrubbed at her face and hands, watching the water turn grey, then black. She washed away the grime of the jungle, the soot of the fire she had set, but she could not wash away the images burned into her mind: the painted face of the warrior, the terrified eyes of the sailors, the indifferent stare of the idols in the longhouse.

She stripped off the ruined dress, balling it up and shoving it into a corner, unable to look at it. Dressed in her nightgown, she felt a little more like herself, yet the feeling of wrongness persisted. She sat on the edge of her narrow bunk, her body trembling with a fresh wave of reaction. The adrenaline was gone, leaving behind a bone-deep exhaustion and a terrifying, echoing clarity. She had taken a man’s life. Perhaps more than one. She had fired the pistol into the chaos of the longhouse. She had not aimed, had not even truly looked. She had just pulled the trigger. And she had lit the fire, a conflagration that had consumed a village, a home. She had done it to survive, to save the others, but the thought brought no comfort. It was a cold, hard fact, a new and terrible piece of her own history.

A soft knock came at her door, making her jump. ‘Miss Ashworth?’

It was Sir Thomas’s voice. So much for speaking in the morning.

‘One moment,’ she called out, her own voice sounding thin and reedy. She took a deep breath, trying to compose herself, then opened the door.

He stood in the narrow corridor, seeming to fill the entire space. He had cleaned the worst of the grime from his face, but he looked just as weary as she felt. His eyes, clear and piercing, held hers. ‘May I come in?’

She hesitated, then stepped back, allowing him to enter the tiny cabin. He had to duck his head to clear the low ceiling beams. With the two of them inside, the space felt impossibly small. He did not sit, but stood by the desk, his presence dominating the room.

‘Mr. Davies believes Finch will survive,’ he began without preamble. ‘He has a severe concussion and a fractured skull, but the surgeon is optimistic. He will be incapacitated for some time, but he will live.’

‘That is good news,’ Eleanor said quietly.

‘It is the only good news of the night,’ Sir Thomas stated flatly. He picked up the book of poetry from her desk, his thumb tracing the embossed title. ‘William Ashworth. Your brother writes of beauty and truth. Noble pursuits.’ He set the book down. ‘They seem very distant at the moment.’

He turned his full attention to her. ‘I told you that we would speak in the morning. I find I do not have the patience to wait. I want to know everything that happened, from the moment you left Finch to the moment you appeared at the riverbank.’

His tone was that of an admiral demanding a report from a subordinate. She felt a flicker of her old indignation, the resentment of being ordered about. But it was quickly extinguished by the shared memory of the night. They were beyond such petty stances now.

She told him everything. She spoke in a low, steady voice, recounting her observations of the village, the layout of the longhouses, the casual brutality she had witnessed. She described the shrine, the grim trophies, the realisation that they were not dealing with simple fishermen, but with predators. She explained her decision, the cold logic that had driven her. They were cornered. A direct fight was suicide. Their only weapon was chaos.

‘I saw the oil, the dried thatch,’ she said, her eyes fixed on a point on the far wall. ‘I saw a predator’s den, and I knew the only way to drive a predator out is to burn its lair. It was not a plan born of courage. It was an act of desperation. An act of terror.’

When she spoke of the pistol, her voice faltered. ‘I did not aim. I just… fired. Into the noise.’

Sir Thomas listened without interruption, his expression unchanging. When she had finished, the silence in the small cabin was profound. He looked from her face to the book of poetry, then back again.

‘You say it was not courage,’ he said, his voice a low rumble. ‘Perhaps. But it was a remarkable act of strategic thinking under extreme duress. You correctly assessed the enemy. You identified their weakness. You created a diversion that allowed for our escape. In the Royal Navy, we would call that a successful tactical maneuver.’

He paused, his eyes searching hers. ‘You also directly disobeyed my order to remain with Finch. You abandoned a wounded man. You took it upon yourself to act as a soldier when you are a civilian, a passenger. You introduced a level of chaos that could just as easily have gotten us all killed.’

His words were sharp, clinical, a dissection of her actions. ‘You are correct. I did,’ she admitted. ‘If I had stayed, we would all be dead now. Or worse.’

The challenge in her tone was unmistakable, and to her surprise, he did not rebuke it. A faint, almost imperceptible softening occurred around his eyes. ‘That is also correct,’ he conceded. ‘It seems I have a madwoman in my charge. A disobedient, reckless, and dangerously effective madwoman.’

He stepped closer, and she had to tilt her head back to look at him. ‘I came here to tell you two things, Miss Ashworth. The first is that, for your actions tonight, you have my gratitude. You saved my life, and the lives of my men. That is a debt I will not forget.’

He held up a hand to forestall any reply. ‘The second is a warning. This cannot happen again. I am the commander of this vessel and this expedition. My authority must be absolute. Without it, there is only anarchy, and anarchy at sea leads to death. From this moment forward, you will follow my orders to the letter. Is that understood?’

She met his gaze, unflinching. The woman from the drawing room would have recoiled, intimidated. The woman who had walked out of a burning village did not. ‘And if your orders lead to certain death, Sir Thomas? Am I still to follow them?’

A long moment passed. She saw a flicker of something in his eyes, not anger, but a grudging respect. ‘Then, Miss Ashworth,’ he said, his voice barely above a whisper. ‘You will have to pray that I am a better commander than you are a subordinate.’

With that, he turned, ducked under the beam, and was gone, leaving her alone in the silence of her cabin. Eleanor sank onto her bunk, the conversation replaying in her mind. He had thanked her and threatened her in the same breath. He had acknowledged her value while simultaneously reasserting his dominance. She was no longer just a passenger. She was an asset, and a problem. A piece on the board that had suddenly moved of its own accord.

Sleep, when it finally came, was a dark and dreamless void.

She was woken by a tolling bell. It was a slow, funereal rhythm. Morning light, grey and diffuse, filtered through the porthole. She rose and dressed, choosing a simple, dark gown. When she emerged onto the deck, she found the entire crew assembled. The sky was overcast, the sea a rolling expanse of dull grey. In the distance, the island was a dark smudge on the horizon, a thin plume of smoke still rising from its interior like a final, defiant gesture.

In the center of the deck, two long shapes lay upon a plank, shrouded in sailcloth and weighted with cannonballs. Sir Thomas stood before them, a Bible in his hand. He was dressed in his formal uniform, the dark blue wool and gold braid a stark contrast to the grim reality of the ceremony.

He began to read from the book, his voice clear and strong, carrying over the wind and the cries of the gulls. ‘We therefore commit their bodies to the deep, to be turned into corruption, looking for the resurrection of the body, when the Sea shall give up her dead…’

Eleanor stood near the back, listening to the ancient, solemn words. She watched the faces of the crew, their expressions stoic and sorrowful. These were the men who had survived. They had faced the same horrors she had, and now they mourned their comrades. She felt a sense of connection to them she had never felt before, a bond forged in shared terror and loss.

Her gaze fell upon the two shrouded forms. Dawes and Miller. She barely knew their names. They had died in the ambush, swift and brutal. But her fire, the act that had saved the rest of them, had done nothing for those two. It had only been for the living. The price of their survival was written on that plank.

When the reading was done, Sir Thomas closed the book. ‘These men served the Crown with honour. They fell in the pursuit of knowledge, on a hostile shore, far from home. We will not forget their sacrifice.’ He looked up, his eyes sweeping over the crew, and for a moment, they rested on Eleanor. ‘We will honour them by completing our mission and returning home. But we will not do it here. This island has taken enough from us.’

At his signal, the sailors at the end of the plank tilted it. The two bodies slid from beneath their canvas shrouds and slipped into the grey water. They vanished with two quiet splashes, leaving only spreading ripples behind.

The bell tolled a final time. The ceremony was over. The ship, which had been holding its position, began to move. The sails billowed as they caught the wind, and the *Sea Serpent* turned its bow away from the smoking island, pointing towards the open, unknown sea. The expedition would continue. But everything had changed.
