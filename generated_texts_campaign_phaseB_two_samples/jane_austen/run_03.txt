“, and with not so much as a proper letter to explain himself! It is the most shocking behaviour I have ever heard of. To think a man of Mr. Davies’s supposed breeding could treat a sweet girl like your sister so abominably! But there it is, my dear; when a man’s pockets are empty, his heart soon follows, I’ve always said.”

“The lady he has fixed upon then, Miss Beaumont, I believe, is she of great fortune?”

“Thirty thousand pounds, Annelise, and a house in town besides! My cousin, Mrs. Pope, had it from her own maid, whose sister is in service with the Beaumonts, so you may be sure it is the truth. A plain little thing, by all accounts; sallow, and with a slight stoop. I saw her once at the theatre, and I cannot say I was impressed. But thirty thousand pounds! And Mr. Davies, they say, has quite run through his own inheritance. Such a pity! All those fancy waistcoats and trips to Newmarket. Well, it is no use crying over spilt milk; but a gentleman has no right to win a girl’s affections, and then cast her off like an old glove simply because his tailor’s bills have grown too long and a plainer face with a fuller purse happens to cross his path. Why does he not retrench? Sell a carriage, dismiss a valet, live within his means for a year or two? I am certain Miss Catherine would have thought him all the more noble for it. But that is not the way of things now; self-denial is a virtue quite lost on the young men of today.”

“Do you know anything of Miss Beaumont’s character? Is she thought to be a pleasant sort of person?”

“I have heard little enough on that score; indeed, people are generally too busy counting her money to trouble themselves with her manners! But Lady Marks did mention at cards the other night that the girl has a temper, and that she and her own mother cannot pass a day together without a quarrel. What a charming prospect for Mr. Davies!”

“And who is Lady Marks?”

“Her godmother, my dear. But the girl is of age now, and has made her choice. A fine bargain she has made of it!, But listen to me, chattering on,” she said, her tone softening as she glanced towards the door. “Your poor sister must be quite prostrate with grief. It is a mercy she went to her room; I could see at dinner she was on the verge of tears. Shall I send up a plate of the apricot tart? Cook makes it especially well.”

“You are too kind, Ma’am. I think rest is all she requires at present. I shall attend to her myself in a little while.”

“Yes, yes, that is for the best. Poor dear girl! To think, that letter this morning should have contained such cruel news. I saw it arrive, you know, and thought it was from him, of course; another one of his pretty declarations! Lord, how I shall be scolding myself for weeks for teasing her about it at breakfast. But how was I to know? It looked for all the world like a love-letter. My son George and his wife will be quite thunderstruck when they hear of it. I should have stopped at their house on my way back from the circulating library, but my head was full of other things. Well, I shall tell them tomorrow.”

“If I may be so bold, Ma’am, I would ask you to do us a great kindness. It would be a mercy if George and Mrs. Finch could be cautioned against mentioning Mr. Davies’s name, or this whole unhappy business, in Catherine’s hearing. Her spirits are very low, and the least allusion to it gives her the most terrible pain. And indeed, the less it is spoken of to me, the better I shall be able to support her.”

“Oh! My dear child, of course! You need not have asked. It is a dreadful thing for you to have to hear it discussed; and as for Catherine, wild horses would not drag his name from my lips in her presence. You must have seen I avoided the subject entirely during the second course. No more would George, for he has a good heart; and his wife is very feeling, especially when I tell her what she ought to feel. For my part, I always say the best way to deal with such unpleasantries is to pretend they never happened. The sooner it is forgotten, the sooner it is mended, you know.”

“In this case, forgetting will be a difficult task. But you are right that talking of it can only do harm. I must say this in Mr. Davies’s defence, however, he had made no formal proposal to my sister. There was no binding engagement.”

“Law, my dear! Do not take his part. No binding engagement! After giving her the little spaniel, and naming the very spot in the garden where they would build a summer-house!”

Annelise, for her sister’s sake, felt she could not contest the point further, and for Mr. Davies’s, she knew it would do no good. After a moment’s pause, Mrs. Finch’s natural optimism resurfaced with a cheerful burst.

“Well, it is an ill wind that blows nobody any good, as my late husband used to say! This will be the very making of Mr. Sterling. He will have her now, you will see! Mark my words, they will be walking down the aisle before Michaelmas. Lord! how he will brighten when he hears Mr. Davies is out of the picture. I hope he calls this evening. It is a far more suitable match in every respect. A good three thousand a year, and not a penny of debt! The estate is a picture, I can tell you; Ashworth is its name. Exactly the sort of comfortable, well-run place I admire. A walled kitchen garden that produces the earliest peaches in the county, and such a trout stream! Lord! how my nephew and I did feast on the fish the one time we visited! Then there is a charming home farm, a proper dairy, and the house itself is just a mile from the church, with the post-chaise stopping at the crossroads twice a day, so there is never a want of news or company. A thousand times better than some draughty, mortgaged estate where they must send five miles for a joint of beef and have no society to speak of. Well, I shall give Mr. Sterling a little encouragement when next I see him. One suitor, you know, drives out another. If we can only get Mr. Davies out of her thoughts!”

“Ah, if we can do that, Ma’am,” said Annelise, “we shall be quite content, with or without Mr. Sterling.” And rising, she made her excuses and went to find Catherine.

She discovered her, as she had supposed, in her chamber, huddled in a chair before the dying embers of the fire, her only light in the growing twilight.

“I had rather you left me,” was the only greeting she received.

“I will leave you,” Annelise replied gently, “if you will promise to get into bed.”

At first, in the peevishness of her misery, she would not agree. But her sister’s quiet persistence soon won her over, and Annelise had the comfort of seeing her settled, before she returned to the drawing-room.

She had been there but a moment when Mrs. Finch bustled in, carrying a small glass of amber liquid.

“My dear,” she began, “I was just telling my maid about your poor sister, and I remembered I have a bottle of the most excellent sherry that was a gift from the Admiral. My late husband swore by it for settling the nerves! He said it was better than any medicine. Do take it up to her. I am sure it will do her a world of good.”

Annelise accepted the glass, though with a considerable degree of reluctance, her fingers closing around the cold, smooth surface. A cordial of this nature, she felt quite certain, was the very last thing her sister required. A spirit inflamed by grief needed no further inflaming by spirits of another sort. Yet, to refuse Mrs. Finch in her current mood of benevolent command would be to invite a lecture on the ungratefulness of youth and the wisdom of age, a trial for which Annelise had not the strength.

“You are too kind, Ma’am,” she murmured, offering a faint smile that she hoped would suffice. “I will take it to her directly.”

“Do, my dear, do! And insist she drinks it all. Tell her it is from me, and that it will put the roses back in her cheeks. Lord! what a state. To think a man could cause such a commotion and then simply ride away. It is a mercy he is gone, a true mercy.” And with this final declaration, Mrs. Finch turned back towards the fire, already composing in her mind, Annelise was sure, the opening pleasantries she would direct at Mr. Sterling upon his much anticipated arrival.

Annelise left the drawing-room, her steps soft upon the polished floorboards of the hall. The little glass of sherry seemed to weigh a great deal in her hand, a symbol of the well-meaning but utterly misguided remedies the world wished to press upon a wounded heart. She ascended the staircase not to Catherine’s chamber, but past it, to the small linen-closet at the end of the corridor. The window there was narrow and overlooked the less-regarded part of the garden, where the compost heap lay and the gardeners kept their barrows. With a swift, silent movement, Annelise unlatched the window, leaned out into the cool, damp air, and inverted the glass. The amber liquid fell in a dark stream, vanishing into a patch of overgrown ivy. She watched it go without a flicker of remorse, feeling only a small, quiet satisfaction. After closing the window, she wiped the glass meticulously with her handkerchief before placing it, empty, upon a nearby shelf, to be collected later by a maid.

Her duty thus discharged, she returned to Catherine’s door and knocked softly. There was no reply. She entered anyway, finding the room now quite dark, the last of the embers in the grate having expired into a fine grey ash. The form in the chair was still, but Annelise could hear the faint, uneven sound of her sister’s breathing.

“Catherine?” she said, her voice barely a whisper.

A choked sound came from the chair. “Did she send you with one of her remedies? Laudanum, or sherry, or some other such nonsense?”

“No,” Annelise said, moving closer and kneeling beside the chair. “I came of my own accord. I brought nothing.”

For a long moment, there was only silence. Then Catherine spoke, her voice thick with unshed tears. “He is gone, Annelise. Truly gone. I kept thinking, all afternoon, that it was some dreadful mistake. That he would turn back. That a messenger would arrive with a letter explaining everything. But the road is empty. The evening is here. And he is gone.”

“I know,” Annelise said simply, placing a hand on her sister’s arm.

“She does not understand,” Catherine went on, a fresh wave of misery roughening her voice. “Mrs. Finch. She thinks it was his prospects, or his charm. And yes, he was charming, I know he was. But it was not that. It was his mind. The way he saw the world. He spoke of things no one else does. Of books, and ideas, and how things might be different. How a place like his, so burdened with debt and neglect, could be made beautiful again. He had such schemes, Annelise. Such dreams. He believed in them. He made me believe in them.” She finally turned her head, and in the deep gloom Annelise could just make out the pale oval of her face, streaked with tears. “I know he was foolish with money. I know he was impractical. But he was not empty. And now everything feels empty without him.”

Annelise’s heart ached for her. It was as she had suspected. Catherine had not been merely infatuated; she had found a spirit that spoke to her own, a mind that ignited a part of her that the comfortable, predictable society of their acquaintance left untouched. Mr. Davies, for all his faults, had offered her a world of passion and ideas, a stark contrast to the world of well-managed estates and sensible investments that Mrs. Finch so admired.

“I know you loved him,” Annelise said, her voice gentle but firm. “No one can take that from you. But you are exhausted, my dear. You have wept all day and eaten nothing. You will make yourself ill.”

“What does it matter?” Catherine whispered.

“It matters to me,” Annelise replied, and in her tone there was a strength that finally seemed to penetrate her sister’s wall of grief. She helped Catherine to her feet, her arm a steady support, and guided her towards the bed. She helped her undress and slip between the cold sheets, then fetched an extra blanket from the chest at the foot of the bed and spread it over her.

“I will have a tray sent up,” she said. “Just some broth and toast. You must try to take something.”

Catherine did not protest, but only turned her face to the pillow. Annelise smoothed her hair back from her forehead, a gesture of comfort from their shared childhood, and then left the room, closing the door quietly behind her.

The next morning broke clear and bright, a cruel mockery of the gloom that had settled over the household. At the breakfast-table, Mrs. Finch was in a state of high animation, her earlier pronouncements now solidifying into a concrete plan of action. Catherine was present, though she looked as if she had not slept at all. Her face was pale and drawn, and she sat staring into her teacup as if it held the answers to some terrible question.

“A fine morning, is it not?” Mrs. Finch began, addressing the room at large but looking pointedly at Catherine. “The very day for a brisk walk! Nothing clears the head and revives the spirits like a good dose of fresh air. We shall all go out after breakfast. A turn about the park, I think.”

Annelise glanced at her sister, whose shoulders seemed to slump even further at the suggestion. “Ma’am, I do not think Catherine is quite well enough for a walk. Perhaps she had better rest today.”

“Nonsense, my dear, it is the very thing she needs!” Mrs. Finch countered with unassailable cheerfulness. “To be shut up in a dark room is to court melancholy. We cannot have that. Besides,” she added, unfolding her napkin with a flourish, “we may have company.”

Annelise felt a prickle of alarm. “Company, Ma’am?”

“Indeed. I took the liberty yesterday evening of sending a note over to Ashworth. To Mr. Sterling. I happened to mention that we were all feeling a little dull, and that if he had an hour to spare, he might find us walking in the park this morning. It would be a kindness, I said, to bring us a little news of the neighbourhood.” She beamed, entirely satisfied with her own diplomacy.

Catherine’s head came up at last, her eyes flashing with a spark of their old fire, now fuelled by anger. “You did what? You invited Mr. Sterling here? Today?”

“And why not, pray?” Mrs. Finch returned, her tone brooking no argument. “He is a perfectly respectable and amiable young man, and an acquaintance of many years. It is a simple civility. One cannot shut oneself away from the world forever on account of a… a disappointment.”

“He is not welcome! I will not see him!” Catherine cried, pushing her chair back from the table.

“You will do as you are told, Catherine,” Mrs. Finch said, her voice suddenly as sharp and cold as the morning air. “You are a guest in this house, and you will show courtesy to my guests. Mr. Sterling is a gentleman of excellent family and fortune. He is not to be slighted on account of some fleeting fancy for a man who has proven himself to be utterly unreliable. You will compose yourself, and you will join us on our walk.”

Tears of helpless fury welled in Catherine’s eyes. She looked at Annelise, a desperate, silent plea for intervention, but Annelise knew there was nothing to be done. To argue further would only make matters worse. She gave a tiny, almost imperceptible shake of her head. Defeated, Catherine sank back into her chair, her face a mask of misery.

An hour later, they were assembled in the morning-room, awaiting the arrival of their guest. Catherine, coerced into a pale grey walking dress, sat by the window, her back rigid, staring out at the drive as if watching for an enemy. Annelise sat with her needlework, though her fingers were clumsy and her mind was far from the pattern. Mrs. Finch, meanwhile, kept up a steady stream of commentary on Mr. Sterling’s many virtues.

“His greenhouses are a marvel, a true marvel! And he has such a sensible plan for rotating the crops on the home farm. Not one for frivolous expense, Mr. Sterling. He understands the value of a well-managed ledger. A solid, dependable young man. The very best sort.”

The sound of horse’s hooves on the gravel silenced her. A moment later, a groom led away a handsome bay, and Mr. Sterling himself was announced.

He was a gentleman of medium height, with a fair, serious countenance and hair of a sandy brown, meticulously brushed. He was dressed, as one might expect, in a riding coat of excellent cut and boots polished to a mirror sheen. His entire aspect was one of composure and deliberation; he was the very picture of prosperous stability. He bowed to Mrs. Finch, then to the sisters.

“Mrs. Finch,” he began, his voice pleasant but lacking in warmth. “You are too good to invite me over on such a fine morning.”

“Not at all, Mr. Sterling, not at all! We are delighted to see you,” she bustled. “You know my nieces, of course. Miss Finch, and Miss Catherine Finch.”

He turned his gaze to Annelise first. “Miss Finch. A pleasure to see you again.” He then directed a carefully prepared expression of sympathy towards the figure by the window. “Miss Catherine. Your aunt has acquainted me with your… recent indisposition. I am very sorry to hear of it.”

The word ‘indisposition’ hung in the air, a bland and utterly inadequate description of the torrent of feeling that had wrecked her sister’s peace.

Catherine rose slowly from her chair. She did not offer her hand. “You are very good, Sir,” she said, her voice so low it was almost inaudible. She curtsied, a stiff, formal gesture, and then immediately turned back to the window, making it perfectly clear that the conversation, for her part, was at an end.

A brief, awkward silence followed. Mr. Sterling looked momentarily at a loss, his conventional sympathies having met with a wall of glacial reserve. Annelise, seeing that someone must salvage the situation, put down her sewing.

“Mrs. Finch was just telling us of your trout stream, Mr. Sterling,” she said, her tone light and easy. “She claims it produces the finest fish in the county.”

He turned to her with visible relief. “Ah, yes. The stream is in excellent condition this year. The rains in the spring were most beneficial. We have had very good sport. My man landed a fine three-pounder just last week.”

“Indeed!” said Mrs. Finch, seizing the opportunity. “Well, we must not keep you indoors talking of fish. The sun is shining! We were just about to take a turn in the park. You will join us, will you not?”

“I should be delighted,” he replied, and the matter was settled.

The walk was precisely the ordeal Annelise had anticipated. Mrs. Finch, with the subtlety of a military commander, immediately attached Mr. Sterling to her own side, leaving the two sisters to follow behind. As they set off down the great sweep of lawn that led to the woods, she kept up a constant, loud commentary, pointing out features of the landscape and drawing comparisons, always favourable, to Ashworth.

“Your new plantings along the north wall are a great success, I hear, Mr. Sterling! So clever of you to choose the hornbeam. Such an excellent windbreak!”

Annelise walked beside Catherine, who moved like an automaton, her eyes fixed on the ground before her. Her silence was so profound, so absolute, it was a positive weight in the air around them.

“Catherine, please,” Annelise whispered, when they had fallen some distance behind. “You make your feelings too plain. It is discourteous to him. He, at least, means well.”

“He means to purchase me,” Catherine retorted, her voice a venomous whisper. “With Mrs. Finch as his agent. Can you not see it? He looks at the house, at the trees, at me, all with the same eye. The eye of an owner. He is assessing his property.”

“That is unfair,” Annelise murmured, though a part of her feared it might be true. “He is only trying to be pleasant.”

“His pleasantness is an insult. He speaks of the weather and the health of his fish while my heart is breaking. Does he imagine a tour of his well-run estate will mend it?”

Up ahead, Mr. Sterling and Mrs. Finch had paused, waiting for them to catch up. He had a small, polite smile on his face, but his eyes, as they fell on Catherine, held a look of distinct bewilderment. He was a man who understood ledgers and crop rotation, pedigrees and property law. The tempestuous, illogical world of a woman’s grief was a foreign country for which he had no map.

He made another attempt as they drew near. “The bluebells will be quite a sight in these woods in a month or two, Miss Catherine. Ashworth has a fine display, but I believe yours are superior.”

Catherine merely inclined her head in a gesture that might have been acknowledgement or dismissal. It was impossible to tell.

Desperate, Annelise stepped into the breach once more. “Do you attend the assembly at Matlock next month, Mr. Sterling? I hear it is to be a particularly grand affair.”

And so it went on. Annelise and Mrs. Finch maintained a fragile façade of polite society, asking after neighbours, discussing the price of wool, and commenting on the latest news from London, all while Catherine drifted beside them like a ghost at the feast. Mr. Sterling, to his credit, persevered. He spoke of improvements and investments, of local politics and the management of the poor rate. He was sensible, informed, and articulate. He was everything Mr. Davies was not. And Annelise, watching him, understood with a dreadful clarity both why Mrs. Finch was so determined, and why her sister was so repulsed. He was safety. He was prosperity. He was a life without financial worry and social disgrace. But his soul did not catch fire. He spoke of the world as a thing to be managed, not as a poem to be lived.

When they finally returned to the house, the relief was palpable. Mr. Sterling did not linger. He bowed, uttered the correct pleasantries, and expressed a hope that Miss Catherine’s ‘indisposition’ would soon pass.

“A capital start!” Mrs. Finch declared, as soon as the door had closed behind him. “A most sensible and well-bred young man. He was not at all put off by your melancholy, Catherine. He has great steadiness of character, you see. Mark my words, he will call again before the week is out.”

Catherine did not answer. She gave a single, desolate look at Annelise, then turned and fled up the stairs, the sound of her footsteps echoing her flight from a future she could not bear to contemplate. Annelise was left standing in the hall with her aunt, the bright morning sun streaming through the windows, illuminating the dust motes dancing in the air and the profound sense of dread that had settled in her heart.

“It is you who are melancholy, Annelise,” Mrs. Finch said, her voice sharp with a vexation that belied her triumphant words. She began to unpin her bonnet, her movements brisk and decisive. “You see only obstacles. I see a young man of considerable fortune and excellent sense who is willing to overlook a great deal of girlish nonsense for the sake of a pretty face. What more could we possibly ask for?”

Annelise did not know how to answer. To her aunt, the transaction was as simple as the sale of a prize bull at market: a fair price for a sound asset. The asset’s own feelings on the matter were an inconvenience to be managed, not a factor to be considered. “Catherine is not nonsensical, Aunt. She is in genuine distress. Her heart is engaged elsewhere.”

“Engaged!” Mrs. Finch gave a short, unamused laugh. “Engaged to a penniless scribbler of verses who fills her head with foolish notions and has not a single prospect to his name. An attachment to Mr. Davies is not an engagement; it is a liability. It is a slow march towards ruin and disgrace, and I will not stand by and watch it happen.” She placed her bonnet on the hall table with a definitive tap. “Did your dear father, may he rest in peace, not warn you both against men of that sort? Men of feeling but no fortune? He knew the cost of it.”

The mention of their father sent a fresh pang of sorrow through Annelise. He had been a man of feeling himself, a man who loved books and music more than accounts, and whose gentle, unworldly nature had left his daughters in this very predicament. “Papa wished for our happiness above all,” Annelise said softly.

“And is happiness to be found in a damp cottage with six starving children and a husband who pays the butcher with a sonnet?” Mrs. Finch’s voice rose, edged with a desperation that Annelise had not heard before. “Do you have any idea, any idea at all, of our circumstances, Annelise? The investments your father made… they were ill advised. The income from the estate is all that keeps us, and it is not enough. Not for three women to live with any degree of propriety. We are stared at in the village. The dressmaker’s bills are paid late. We have not been to Bath in three years! This match, this sensible, solid, prosperous match with Mr. Sterling, is not a matter of preference. It is a matter of survival. It is Catherine’s duty.”

The word hung in the air, cold and heavy as a shroud. Duty. Annelise felt the blood drain from her face. She had known they were not wealthy, had understood the need for economy, but she had not grasped the true depth of their vulnerability. Her aunt’s fear was suddenly real and tangible, a fourth presence in the sunlit hall. It was the fear of genteel poverty, of slipping slowly down the social ladder until they disappeared from view altogether.

“I did not know it was so very bad,” Annelise whispered.

Mrs. Finch’s expression softened, the hard lines of her frustration giving way to a weary anxiety. “I did not wish to alarm you. But you must understand what is at stake. You are the sensible one. You must make Catherine see reason. Her romantic fancies are a luxury we cannot afford.”

Annelise inclined her head, unable to promise anything, and made her own escape, her steps far heavier and slower than her sister’s had been. She climbed the stairs not in flight, but as if carrying the weight of her aunt’s revelations on her shoulders. The dread she had felt earlier now had a name and a shape. It was the bailiff at the door, the pitying glances of neighbours, the slow, humiliating decay of their world.

She found Catherine not weeping, as she had expected, but sitting on the window seat, gazing out at the very woods through which they had just walked. Her face was pale and set, her hands clasped tightly in her lap around a small, leather-bound book. It was a volume of Mr. Davies’s own poems, privately printed at his own expense. Annelise knew it by heart, for Catherine had read them aloud to her on countless evenings, her voice full of a wondering delight that had made Annelise’s own heart ache with a mixture of love and apprehension.

“He would never have spoken of the bluebells as being ‘superior’ or ‘inferior’,” Catherine said, without turning around. Her voice was quiet, but it vibrated with a low, intense scorn. “He would have said they were a carpet of fallen sky, or a congregation of shy spirits. He would have made me see them, truly see them, not merely assess their value.”

Annelise came to sit beside her. “Mr. Sterling is a practical man, Catherine. It is not his way to speak in poetry.”

“It is not his way to feel anything at all,” Catherine retorted, turning at last. Her eyes, the same soft grey as Annelise’s, were blazing. “He looks at the world and sees only assets and liabilities. He sees me as an asset to be acquired. A tolerable addition to his estate. Did you not see the way he looked at the house? At the portraits? He was calculating its worth, Annelise. He was calculating my worth.”

“He was showing an interest. He was trying to be polite.”

“His politeness is a cage!” Catherine’s voice broke. “He wishes to trap me in a life of ledgers and sensible dinners and discussions of the poor rate. A life where the soul is starved to feed the purse. I cannot do it, Annelise. I would rather die.”

“Do not say that.” Annelise took her sister’s hand. It was cold as ice. “You are unhappy now, but your heart will heal. You are young. In time…”

“In time I will become like his furniture,” Catherine whispered, her gaze falling back to the book in her lap. “Well-made, serviceable, and silent. I will forget what it is to feel. I will forget the colour of the sky. I will forget him.” She clutched the book to her chest as if it were a shield. “And I will not. I will not.”

Annelise’s carefully constructed arguments, the sensible words she had prepared on the stairs, all dissolved in the face of such profound misery. The spectre of their financial ruin seemed a paltry thing compared to the spiritual ruin Catherine described. How could she speak of duty when her sister spoke of death?

“Aunt Finch told me,” Annelise began, her voice faltering, “she told me of our finances. It is worse than I knew, Catherine. She believes this is our only hope.”

Catherine looked at her, a flicker of a new and terrible understanding in her eyes. “So I am to be sold.”

“No. It is not like that. She is frightened. She wishes to see you secure.”

“Secure in my misery,” Catherine said flatly. She opened the book of poems and her fingers traced the handwritten dedication on the flyleaf. ‘For Catherine, who is the poem’. A single tear fell onto the page, smudging the ink. “He understands,” she whispered. “He is the only one who has ever understood.”

The days that followed were a trial of quiet endurance. A tense and unhappy truce settled over the house. Mrs. Finch, having laid out the stark reality of their position, now adopted a strategy of expectant silence, her knitting needles clicking with a pointed and judgmental rhythm. Catherine retreated further into herself, taking her meals in her room and speaking only when directly addressed. She spent her hours walking alone in the most remote parts of the grounds or sitting with her book of poems, her face a mask of resolute sorrow.

Annelise was the fulcrum upon which all this silent conflict balanced. She placated her aunt with vague assurances that Catherine was ‘thinking’, and she comforted her sister with her steady, unquestioning presence. She managed the household accounts with a new and painful awareness, seeing the deficit in every pound of sugar, every yard of ribbon. She felt stretched thin, a diplomat in a war with no foreseeable end, loving both sides and belonging to neither. She understood her aunt’s terror of poverty, but she could not bring herself to sacrifice her sister’s soul on the altar of solvency.

Just as Mrs. Finch had predicted, a note arrived from Ashworth Park four days after the visit. It was addressed to Mrs. Finch, and she read it with a look of smug satisfaction.

“Mr. Sterling is to call upon us tomorrow afternoon,” she announced at luncheon. Annelise was the only other person at the table; Catherine had, as usual, remained upstairs. “He enquires particularly after Miss Catherine’s health. Such delicate consideration! He is a true gentleman.”

Annelise’s heart sank. “What am I to tell Catherine?”

“You are to tell her to come downstairs, put on a respectable dress, and behave with a modicum of civility,” Mrs. Finch said sharply. “I will not have a repeat of the last performance. If she cannot be pleasant, she can at least be present.”

The task of conveying this command fell, as always, to Annelise. She found Catherine arranging a small vase of wildflowers on her bedside table. They were the very bluebells Mr. Sterling had so clumsily admired.

“He is coming again,” Annelise said, without preamble. “Tomorrow.”

Catherine did not look at her. She carefully moved a single bluebell from one side of the arrangement to the other. “Then tomorrow I shall have a headache.”

“Aunt Finch insists that you come down.”

“Does she?” Catherine’s hand stilled. “And what will she do if I refuse? Lock me in my room? It would be a mercy.”

“Catherine, please. For my sake. A short while is all that is required. Be civil, and he will soon leave. To refuse to see him will only create a greater scandal. It will provoke Aunt Finch to more desperate measures.”

A look of weary resignation passed over Catherine’s face. She looked at Annelise, truly looked at her, and saw the anxiety etched around her eyes, the tired set of her mouth. “You are caught in the middle of this, are you not?” she said softly.

“I only wish for a little peace,” Annelise admitted.

Catherine sighed, a long, shuddering breath. “Very well. For you, Annelise. I will come down. I will be present. But I make no promises to be pleasant.”

The next afternoon, Mr. Sterling was announced. Annelise, sitting with her aunt in the drawing room, felt her nerves tighten. Catherine had kept her word. She had come down, dressed in a simple grey muslin that seemed to drain the remaining colour from her face. She sat by the window, her hands lying still in her lap, her expression as remote and unreadable as a winter sky.

Mr. Sterling entered, looking just as solid and sensible as before. But today, he carried something in his hand: a wrapped parcel. He bowed to Mrs. Finch, then to Annelise. His eyes then found Catherine. The polite bewilderment was still there, but there was something else too. A flicker of determination.

“Mrs. Finch. Miss Annelise.” He inclined his head. “Miss Catherine. I am pleased to see you are well enough to join us today.”

Catherine gave the slightest possible nod of her head.

He was not deterred. After a few moments of the usual stiff conversation with Mrs. Finch, he turned his full attention to the silent figure by the window. He walked over to her, his footsteps seeming unnaturally loud in the quiet room.

“Miss Catherine,” he began, his voice low and earnest. “I confess I have been giving our last conversation, or rather, the lack of it, a great deal of thought.” Annelise held her breath. “It occurred to me that my own topics of discourse, matters of estate management and the like, must be of very little interest to a lady. I have observed your fondness for the outdoors, and for… for the more decorative aspects of nature.” He seemed to be choosing his words with immense care. “And so I took the liberty of bringing you this. I hoped it might offer some small diversion.”

He held out the parcel. Catherine looked at it as if it might be a venomous snake. Annelise wanted to prod her, to will her to move, to speak. After a moment that stretched into an eternity, Catherine slowly reached out and took it. Her fingers fumbled with the string.

Inside was a book. It was a handsome volume, bound in green leather and stamped with gold. But it was not poetry. The title on the spine read: ‘An Illustrated Compendium of the Flora of Derbyshire’.

A dead silence fell upon the room. Annelise could feel her aunt’s beaming approval radiating from across the room. It was such a sensible gift. A useful gift. An improving gift. It was, in its own way, a kind gesture, a clumsy attempt to bridge the gulf between them. But Annelise, watching her sister’s face, knew it was a catastrophic failure.

Catherine stared at the book. To her, it must have seemed like a confirmation of her worst fears. He had seen her love of flowers and had translated it into a science, a thing to be catalogued and classified, not felt. He had taken her poetry and turned it into prose.

She raised her eyes to his. Her expression was perfectly, terribly polite. “You are very thoughtful, Mr. Sterling,” she said, her voice clear and cold as winter ice. “A book of lists. How very… useful. I shall add it to my library.”

She did not open it. She did not look at the illustrations. She simply placed the book on the small table beside her, her gesture as final as if she were closing a coffin lid.

Mr. Sterling’s face fell. For the first time, Annelise saw a crack in his composure. It was not anger, but a deep, and genuine, disappointment. He was a man who understood cause and effect, investment and return. He had made a calculated effort, an investment of thought and expense, and it had yielded nothing. He simply did not understand. He looked from the book to Catherine’s frozen face, and the bewilderment returned, stronger than ever.

The rest of the visit was an agony. Mr. Sterling soon retreated to the safe territory of conversation with Mrs. Finch. Catherine remained by the window, a statue of silent rebellion. When he finally rose to leave, he did not even attempt another private word with her. He bowed to the room at large and departed, the sound of the closing door echoing with a note of finality.

The moment he was gone, Mrs. Finch erupted. “Catherine! I have never in my life witnessed such a display of wilful rudeness! The man makes a thoughtful, a generous gesture, and you treat him with the utmost contempt! Do you know what that book must have cost?”

“I do not care what it cost!” Catherine sprang to her feet, her composure finally shattering. “He is trying to catalogue my soul! He thinks my love for a flower can be cured by learning its Latin name! He does not see me, he sees a problem to be solved!”

“He sees a future wife who is in need of a steadying hand!”

“I do not want a steadying hand! I want a hand to hold! I want…”

Her words were cut short by the entrance of the maid, Mary, who was holding a letter on a small silver tray.

“A letter for you, Miss Catherine,” she said, her eyes wide with curiosity at the scene she had interrupted. “It just arrived with the post-boy.”

Annelise’s heart stopped. She knew, with a certainty that defied all reason, who it was from. The handwriting on the front was an elegant, slightly flamboyant script she had seen before on the flyleaf of a certain book of poems.

Catherine snatched the letter from the tray. Her hands were shaking as she broke the seal. Mrs. Finch stood rigid, her face a thundercloud of suspicion. Annelise could only watch, trapped between the two opposing forces.

As Catherine read, the transformation was astonishing. The frozen mask of misery melted away. Colour flooded her cheeks. Her eyes, which had been so dull with despair, lit up with a brilliant, joyful light. She looked as if she had been given a reprieve from a death sentence. She looked up, not at her aunt, but at Annelise, her face radiant.

“He is coming,” she breathed, her voice filled with a rapturous disbelief. “He has secured a temporary post near Matlock. He will be here in a fortnight. He writes that he cannot bear our separation a moment longer.”

She clutched the letter to her heart, the offending botanical encyclopedia forgotten on the table beside her. She was no longer a ghost or a statue; she was vibrant, alive, incandescent with a hope so powerful it seemed to light up the whole room.

“A fortnight,” Mrs. Finch repeated, her voice dangerously low. “He dares to present himself here?”

“He loves me,” Catherine declared, her chin high, ready for battle. “And I love him. And that is all that signifies.”

“We shall see what signifies,” Mrs. Finch said, her lips a thin, white line. “We shall see about that.” She turned her furious gaze on Annelise. “You will put a stop to this. This is your doing as much as hers. You have encouraged her folly. Now, you will end it.”

But Annelise was not looking at her aunt. She was looking at her sister, whose face was alight with a happiness so fierce and pure it was almost painful to behold. And in that moment, the dread in Annelise’s heart shifted, changing from a fear of poverty to a new, and far more terrible fear: the fear of what would happen when that light in her sister’s eyes was finally and irrevocably extinguished.

Mrs. Finch’s command hung in the air, as sharp and cold as a shard of ice. You will end it. The words were a sentence passed not upon Catherine, but upon Annelise herself. It was she who was to be the executioner, she who was to wield the axe that would sever her sister’s only lifeline. She looked at Catherine, who stood clutching the letter to her breast as if it were a shield against the world’s vituperation. Her sister’s face, so recently alight, was now set in a look of defiant terror, her eyes fixed on their aunt. But the light had not gone out, not yet. It was simply burning with a more desperate intensity.

“I cannot,” Annelise whispered, the words barely audible.

“What was that?” Mrs. Finch took a step closer, her entire being radiating a furious, contained energy. She looked like a kettle about to scream.

“I cannot do it,” Annelise repeated, finding a sliver of strength she did not know she possessed. Her voice was still quiet, but it was firm. “She is my sister.”

“She is a fool,” Mrs. Finch corrected, her gaze flicking with contempt towards Catherine. “A fool who would trade her security, your security, and the family’s good name for a book of verses and a few pretty words. This… poet. What is his name? What is his station? He has none, I imagine. He is a fortune hunter, preying on the romantic delusions of a silly girl.”

“His name is Julian Thorne,” Catherine said, her voice ringing with pride. “And he is the kindest, most brilliant man I have ever known. He seeks my heart, Aunt, not my dowry. For he knows perfectly well I have none.”

The statement, intended as a defence, was a fatal admission. Mrs. Finch smiled, a chilling, triumphant little twist of her lips. “Precisely. He has nothing, and you have nothing. It is a perfect union of destitution. You would live in a cottage, I suppose? On what? On the proceeds from his sonnets? You would starve within a month. And what of Annelise? Have you given a single thought to her? When your disgraceful affair becomes common knowledge, who will have her? Who will want a connection to a family so lacking in sense and propriety? You are not just ruining your own life, you are ruining hers.”

The blow landed exactly where it was meant to. Annelise saw Catherine flinch, the radiant certainty in her eyes flickering with the first shadow of doubt. Her sister’s love was a pure and selfish thing, as most first loves are, and she had not truly considered the wider ripples of her actions. Annelise felt a pang of pity, not for her own prospects, but for the terrible burden her aunt had just placed upon Catherine’s joy.

“Go to your room, Catherine,” Mrs. Finch commanded. “You will remain there. Mary will bring your meals. You will have time to contemplate the consequences of your folly. Annelise will speak with you later, and I trust she will be more persuasive than I have been.” She turned and swept from the room, leaving a vortex of silence in her wake. The botanical encyclopedia still lay open on the table, its neat diagrams a monument to a world of order and classification that had just been blown to pieces.

The two sisters were left alone. The silence stretched, thick and uncomfortable. Catherine did not move, her hand still pressed to her heart. Annelise walked over to the window, looking out at the meticulously manicured garden, a place of constrained and ordered beauty. It felt like a prison.

“She is right, is she not?” Catherine’s voice was small and broken. “About you. I have not thought of you.”

Annelise turned. The joy was gone now, replaced by a terrible, anxious guilt. “Do not let her do this to you, Cat,” she said, moving to her sister’s side. “Do not let her poison it.”

“But your future…”

“My future will be what it will be,” Annelise said, taking Catherine’s free hand. It was cold as stone. “But what I cannot bear is to see you as you were before this letter arrived. That is the future I fear. A future of you sitting in this house, fading day by day, married to a man you do not love. If this Mr. Thorne is your happiness, then…” She trailed off, unable to finish the thought. The practicalities were too monstrous to ignore.

“He is,” Catherine whispered, her eyes filling with tears. “Oh, Annelise, he is. When I am with him, the world makes sense. The colours are brighter. I feel… I feel like the person I was always meant to be. Without him, I am just a collection of duties and expectations. A daughter, a niece, a prospective wife. With him, I am Catherine.” She finally unfolded the letter, her hands trembling. “May I read some of it to you? So you can see? He is not what she says.”

Annelise nodded, her heart aching. She sat beside her sister on the chaise lounge, and as Catherine’s voice, soft and reverent, filled the room, the world of Mrs. Finch and Mr. Davies and financial ruin began to recede. The prose was beautiful, of course. It was filled with breathtaking metaphors and turns of phrase that made Annelise understand his appeal. He wrote of their separation not as a mere inconvenience but as a tear in the fabric of the universe. He did not say he was coming to Matlock; he said he was journeying toward the magnetic north of his soul. He did not describe his new post as a temporary job; it was a dreary exile made bearable only by its proximity to her light.

It was glorious, romantic nonsense, and Annelise knew her aunt would have scoffed at every line. But as she listened, she was not hearing the words of a fortune hunter. She was hearing a man who understood her sister’s spirit perfectly. He did not try to catalogue her love for a flower; he compared her soul to the secret life of a rose, something that unfolds in its own time, in its own way. He saw her. Truly saw her. And Annelise knew, with a sinking feeling of complicity, that she would not, could not, be the one to end it.

The next ten days were the longest of Annelise’s life. The house became a fortress, and Catherine its closely guarded prisoner. Mrs. Finch was relentless. She confiscated Catherine’s writing materials, suspecting she might try to reply to her lover. She kept her to her room for all but an hour a day, when she was permitted to walk in the walled garden, always under the watchful eye of Mary, the maid, who had been thoroughly instructed in her duties as a jailer.

Annelise was caught in the middle, a reluctant double agent. To her aunt, she presented a mask of dutiful concern. “I am speaking with her, Aunt,” she would say. “She is beginning to see the unsuitability of it all. It will take time.” These lies were a constant, sour taste in her mouth. To Catherine, she was a lifeline. She smuggled in scraps of paper and a pencil stub so her sister could draft a thousand unsent replies. She relayed descriptions of the outside world, a world Catherine now believed existed only as a prelude to Julian’s arrival.

Catherine, for her part, lived on the memory of that letter. She had been forced to surrender it to their aunt, but she had memorised every word. She would recite passages to Annelise in hushed whispers at night, her eyes shining in the candlelight. She was sustained by a hope so potent it seemed to be a physical presence in their small shared bedroom. Annelise found herself both guarding this hope and being terrified by its fragility. It was like holding a soap bubble in her hands; beautiful, shimmering, and liable to burst at the slightest touch.

Then, a week before the fateful fortnight was to end, Mrs. Finch played her trump card.

“Mr. Davies will be joining us for a few days,” she announced at breakfast. Annelise was the only one at the table, as Catherine was still confined. “He is most eager to spend more time with Catherine. I have explained to him that she has been suffering from a bout of ill health, a nervous complaint, and that he is not to be discouraged by her quietness. I expect you, Annelise, to be a charming hostess and to smooth over any awkwardness.”

The unspoken threat was clear. Mr. Davies was the solution. His solid, dependable presence was to be the antidote to the fever dream of a penniless poet. His arrival felt to Annelise like the drawing up of a siege engine against their fragile fortress wall.

Mr. Davies arrived two days later, his carriage laden with luggage, his face beaming with a good-natured eagerness that was almost unbearable. He was a man utterly without subtlety. He brought with him a box of expensive chocolates, which Mrs. Finch accepted on Catherine’s behalf, and a new, even larger book. This one was on the geological formations of the Derbyshire dales.

“I thought,” he said, his chest puffing out with pride as he presented it to Annelise, “that since Miss Catherine found the botanical guide so… absorbing, she might appreciate an understanding of the very ground we walk upon. It is a foundation of stability, you see. The rocks. They are always there.”

Annelise wanted to scream. He did not see a person; he saw a project. A delicate, nervous creature to be managed with facts and fortified with sensible realities. He was the human embodiment of her aunt’s philosophy.

That evening, Catherine was ordered to join them for dinner. She entered the dining room like a queen going to her execution. She was pale but composed, her gown a simple, unadorned grey that made her seem more ethereal than ever. She greeted Mr. Davies with a cool politeness that was itself a form of defiance.

The dinner was a masterclass in misery. Mr. Davies, bless his oblivious heart, tried his best. He directed all his conversation to Catherine, attempting to draw her out.

“Mrs. Finch tells me you have been unwell, Miss Catherine,” he began, his voice booming in the tense silence. “I am heartily sorry to hear it. Perhaps the air is not agreeing with you. I have found that a brisk walk, followed by the study of local sedimentary layers, can be a wonderful tonic for the spirits.”

Catherine simply inclined her head, her fork pushing a single pea around her plate.

Annelise leaped into the breach. “Catherine has always been of a more delicate constitution, Mr. Davies,” she said, forcing a bright smile. “But she is much improved. Is she not, Aunt?”

“She is present,” Mrs. Finch said dryly, her eyes boring into her younger niece.

Undeterred, Mr. Davies pressed on. “Indeed. Well, when you are feeling stronger, Miss Catherine, we must take a tour. I have identified a particularly fascinating outcrop of limestone not three miles from here. The fossil record is said to be quite remarkable.”

Catherine’s mask finally cracked. She looked up, her eyes wide with a kind of bewildered horror. “I have no interest in the fossil record, Mr. Davies,” she said, her voice quiet but carrying the sharp clarity of a tiny bell. “I find stones to be cold, and hard, and dead.”

A shocked silence fell over the table. Mr. Davies’s cheerful face crumpled in confusion. Mrs. Finch’s hand tightened on her wine glass. Annelise felt a wild, inappropriate urge to laugh. Her sister was not fading; she was fighting. In her own passive, devastating way, she was fighting.

The rest of the meal passed in a strained and painful formality. As soon as it was over, Catherine excused herself, claiming a headache, and fled back to the sanctuary of her room.

Mr. Davies looked after her, his brow furrowed with genuine concern. “She seems… very low,” he said to Annelise, as Mrs. Finch went to give orders to the staff. “I had hoped my company might cheer her.”

“Her ailment is not one that can be cheered by company, Mr. Davies,” Annelise replied, feeling a strange mix of pity for him and loyalty to her sister.

“Is there… is there some other attachment?” he asked, lowering his voice. “Some prior understanding that I have been kept ignorant of?”

The question was so direct, so unexpected, that it caught Annelise off guard. He was not as unperceiving as she had thought. He had seen something was deeply wrong. Here was her chance. She could tell him a version of the truth, hint that Catherine’s heart was taken, and perhaps he, as a gentleman, would withdraw. It would solve everything. The pressure would be off. Mrs. Finch’s plans would be ruined.

But then she looked at his earnest, kind face, and she thought of what would happen next. He would withdraw, yes. But Mrs. Finch’s fury would be redirected, with its full and terrible force, upon Catherine. And upon Annelise for her betrayal. The poet was not yet here. Their hope was still just a whisper, a promise in a letter. To reveal it now would be to expose it to a killing frost.

“No, Mr. Davies,” Annelise lied, the words feeling like stones in her own mouth. “There is no one. She is simply… melancholy.”

He accepted her answer with a nod, but the worry in his eyes did not fade. “Then I must be more persistent in my efforts to bring the sunshine back into her life,” he declared with a renewed, and utterly dreadful, determination.

The day of the fortnight’s end dawned grey and overcast. It was the day Julian Thorne was due to arrive in the area. Catherine was a knot of taut nerves, pacing their room, her face pale with anticipation and fear. Mr. Davies was still in residence, a constant, looming presence. He had announced his plan for the afternoon: a carriage ride to the limestone outcrop. He had insisted Catherine join them. Mrs. Finch had seconded the command.

“I cannot,” Catherine whispered to Annelise, her hands twisting in her lap. “I cannot sit in a carriage with him and look at rocks. Not today. Julian will be here. How will I find him? How will he find me?”

“We will find a way,” Annelise promised, her own heart pounding a frantic rhythm against her ribs. She had no idea how, but she had to make it so. For the past two weeks, she had lived a divided life, placating her aunt while comforting her sister. Now, she had to choose a side. And she knew, without any doubt, which side it would be.

The plan, when it came, was one of desperation. After the midday meal, as Mr. Davies was consulting his geological maps and Mrs. Finch was giving instructions for the carriage, Annelise approached her aunt.

“Aunt, I have a dreadful headache,” she said, pressing her fingers to her temples. It was not entirely a lie; the stress had given her a dull, throbbing pain. “I do not think I can bear a long carriage ride. The jostling would be agony.”

Mrs. Finch looked at her with suspicion. “Do not think you can get out of your duties, Annelise. Mr. Davies needs company.”

“I know,” Annelise said quickly. “And Catherine will go. She has promised me she will make an effort. But perhaps a short walk would be better for my head than the carriage. Just a brief turn about the village. The fresh air might clear it.”

Her aunt considered this. A walk to the village was a small, contained activity. And with Annelise out of the way, Catherine would have Mr. Davies’s undivided attention. It suited her purpose. “Very well,” she conceded. “But do not be long. And Catherine will ride out with Mr. Davies at two o’clock. See to it.”

Annelise’s heart leaped. The first hurdle was cleared. She flew upstairs to Catherine. “You are not going on a carriage ride,” she said, her words coming in a rush. “I have a headache. You are going to stay and tend to me.”

Catherine stared at her, uncomprehending. “But… then how…?”

“While you are tending to my terrible headache,” Annelise continued, pulling her sister’s cloak from the wardrobe, “you are going to feel the need for a special herbal remedy that can only be purchased from the apothecary in the village. A remedy I have found most effective in the past. You will go and fetch it for your loving, ailing sister. You will go alone, because I am too ill to move and everyone else is occupied.”

Understanding dawned on Catherine’s face, and with it, the brilliant, joyful light that Annelise had so feared to see extinguished. “Annelise,” she breathed. “You are a genius.”

“I am a liar and a conspirator,” Annelise corrected, her stomach twisting with nerves. “Now hurry. We have very little time.”

It worked. It worked with a smoothness that was almost frightening. Mrs. Finch, distracted by finalising arrangements with Mr. Davies, accepted the change of plans with an irritated wave. Mr. Davies was disappointed but expressed his hope that Miss Annelise’s headache would soon pass. He was left in the parlour with his maps, a man waiting for a future that was never going to arrive.

Annelise lay on the bed in her room, the curtains drawn, listening. She heard the crunch of the carriage wheels on the gravel as it was brought around. She heard her aunt’s sharp voice, and Mr. Davies’s deeper tones. Then, she heard the front door close, and a moment later, the soft tread of her sister’s feet on the landing.

Catherine peeked into the room, her face a mask of hope and terror. She was wrapped in her dark blue cloak, the hood pulled up. She looked like a figure from a romantic ballad, stepping out into a world of peril and possibility.

“Go,” Annelise whispered from the bed. “Be careful. The post-house is the first place he will stop.”

Catherine nodded, her eyes full of a love so profound it made Annelise’s throat tighten. “Thank you,” she mouthed. Then she was gone.

Annelise lay in the feigned sickness of the darkened room, her head genuinely pounding now. She had done it. She had defied her aunt, deceived a good if misguided man, and set her sister on a path that could lead to utter ruin. She had chosen the shimmering, beautiful bubble of hope over the hard, cold reality of the rocks. She listened to the silence of the house, every nerve ending straining for the sound of the front door opening again, not knowing whether it would bring back a sister radiant with joy, or one whose heart had been broken beyond all repair. The fear was a living thing inside her, but it was mingled with a new, strange feeling: a fierce and thrilling sense of liberation. They had stepped off the precipice together. Now all they could do was pray they had wings.

The minutes stretched into an eternity, each one a leaden weight added to the crushing pressure in Annelise’s chest. The house, usually a place of quiet industry, was now a tomb of suspense. Every creak of a floorboard in a distant room, every tick of the grand clock in the hall below, seemed to mock her with its normalcy. Time was moving on for the rest of the world, while for her, it had stopped at the precise moment Catherine’s footsteps had faded on the landing.

She pressed her fingers to her temples, where the feigned ache had become a genuine, throbbing misery. She had played her part, and now she was trapped within it. She could not rise, could not pace, could not rush to the window to scan the road. She was Miss Annelise, the invalid, confined to her darkened chamber. The role was a prison of her own making. Her only connection to the unfolding drama was her hearing, which she strained until her ears rang. She tried to map the progress of her sister’s flight in her mind. Catherine would have slipped out the kitchen door, avoiding the front of the house where Mr. Davies might still be looking out a window. She would take the path across the west meadow, the one that joined the main road just beyond the first bend, hidden from the view of the house. It was a half mile walk to the village. A half mile of terror and hope.

Annelise pictured her sister, her blue cloak a moving shadow against the green fields. Was her heart pounding as fiercely as Annelise’s own? Was she looking back over her shoulder? Or was she looking only forward, her eyes fixed on the image of Thomas waiting for her, a beacon at the end of this desperate journey? He had promised to be there, at the post-house, with a private chaise ready. He had sworn it in the secret letters that had passed between them, letters Annelise herself had helped to conceal. But promises were breath on the wind. A man could change his mind. A horse could go lame. A thousand small, cruel accidents of fate could conspire to ruin them.

The silence was finally broken by a light tap on her door. Annelise’s heart leaped into her throat. She smoothed her hair against the pillow and composed her features into a mask of weary suffering. “Come in,” she called, her voice a practiced, fragile whisper.

The door opened and Mary, one of ahe younger housemaids, crept in carrying a small tray. On it was a porcelain bowl of cool water and a folded linen cloth.
“Mrs. Finch sent me up, miss,” the girl said, her voice full of sympathy. “She said I’m to put a compress on your head. She said the cool water helps a powerful lot with a sick headache.”
“That is very thoughtful of my aunt,” Annelise murmured, allowing the girl to approach the bed. Mary was young and impressionable, her face an open book of earnest concern. She would be easy to fool.
The girl dipped the cloth into the water, wrung it out with surprising efficiency, and gently placed it on Annelise’s forehead. The chill was a shock, but a welcome one. It grounded her, pulling her back from the dizzying heights of her anxiety.
“Is that better, miss?” Mary asked, her eyes wide.
“Much better, thank you, Mary. You have a gentle touch.”
The girl beamed. “Is there anything else I can get for you, miss?”
Annelise seized the opportunity. “No, thank you. I am only waiting for my sister to return. The apothecary’s remedies are the only thing that truly works. Has she been gone long?” She tried to make the question sound casual, a sick woman’s hazy perception of time.
Mary’s brow furrowed in thought. “Oh, a fair while now, miss. Mr. Davies was just remarking on it to Mrs. Finch downstairs. He said Miss Catherine must be stopping to greet half the village.”
A cold dread washed over Annelise, colder than the compress on her head. Of course. Mr. Davies was still here. Waiting. A living, breathing reminder of the life Catherine had just run from. And he was keeping track of the time.
“He is still here?” Annelise asked, unable to keep a note of alarm from her voice.
“Oh yes, miss. Mrs. Finch insisted he stay for a cup of tea. She said it was the least we could do, what with his journey being for nothing today.” Mary bustled about the room, tidying things that were already tidy. “He’s a very patient gentleman, isn’t he?”
“Yes,” Annelise said, the word tasting like ash in her mouth. “Very patient.”
She dismissed the girl with another word of thanks, and Mary curtseyed and left, closing the door softly behind her. The patience of Mr. Davies was a complication she had not fully considered. She had imagined he would take his leave in polite disappointment. But her aunt, ever the pragmatist, would not allow a potential son-in-law with five thousand a year to simply walk away. She would be managing the situation, smoothing his ruffled feathers, assuring him that this was a minor delay. And all the while, the clock in the hall kept ticking, each tick a hammer blow against the fragile structure of their lie.

Another stretch of time passed, longer than the first. The water on the compress grew warm. The sounds from downstairs changed. She heard the clink of porcelain, the low murmur of voices. Her aunt was serving tea. Then, footsteps on the stairs, heavier than Mary’s. They stopped outside her door. A polite, hesitant knock.
“Annelise? May I come in for a moment? It is Mr. Davies.”
Annelise’s blood ran cold. This was a nightmare. She had to face the man she had so thoroughly deceived. She took a deep breath, clutching the bedsheets under the covers. “Of course, Mr. Davies. The door is not latched.”
He entered the room with an awkward deference, his large frame seeming to shrink in the dim, feminine space. He held his hat in his hands, turning the brim over and over. He looked like a kind, worried sheep, and Annelise felt a fresh, sharp pang of guilt.
“Miss Annelise,” he began, his voice soft with concern. “Your aunt told me of your sudden illness. I was so very sorry to hear of it. I hope you are not in too much pain.”
“It is only a headache, Mr. Davies,” she lied, looking at him through her eyelashes. “But it is a persistent one. I am sorry to have ruined your visit.”
“Good heavens, do not think of it,” he said, taking a step closer to the bed. “A visit can be rearranged. Your health is what is important. I only came up because… well, your aunt and I were hoping for some good news from the apothecary.” His eyes were kind, and for a moment, Annelise felt the terrible urge to confess everything, to throw herself on the mercy of this good man. But that would not only doom her, it would doom Catherine.
“I am hoping for it as well,” she said, letting a note of weariness enter her voice. “My sister is so kind to go for me. I fear she has been gone a long time.” She let the sentence hang in the air, a baited hook.
He took it. “Yes, that is what we were just discussing. It has been over an hour now. The village is not so large. I hope she has not met with any trouble on the road.”
The word ‘trouble’ sent a fresh spike of fear through her. She had been so worried about Thomas not being there, she had not considered the other dangers. A lone woman on the road. A twisted ankle. An overturned cart. Unsavory characters.
“Catherine knows the path well,” Annelise said, more to reassure herself than him. “She is a very sensible girl.”
“Of course, of course,” he said quickly. “I did not mean to imply otherwise. I am merely… concerned. For both of you.” He looked around the dim room, at the drawn curtains, at her pale face on the pillow. “It seems a shame to keep you in the dark like this. Perhaps some fresh air would do you good.”
Before she could protest, he moved to the window and, with a decisive pull, drew back the heavy velvet curtains. Afternoon sunlight flooded the room, painfully bright. Annelise flinched, throwing a hand over her eyes. “Mr. Davies, please! The light!”
“My apologies!” he cried, his face flushing with embarrassment. He made to close them again, but he froze, his hand on the cord, his gaze fixed on something outside. Annelise slowly lowered her hand, her heart hammering. She followed his gaze out the window. The window of her bedroom overlooked the drive and the main road beyond the gates.
And there, coming up the drive at a brisk trot, was the very same apothecary’s boy who delivered their medicines once a month. He was whistling, a small basket swinging from his hand. He was not accompanied by Catherine.
Mr. Davies stared at the boy, then turned his gaze back to Annelise. The expression on his face was no longer one of simple concern. It was a dawning, sorrowful comprehension. He was not a stupid man. He had been told Catherine had gone to the apothecary. And here was the apothecary’s boy, coming to them. The two facts could not exist in the same logical universe.
“He seems to be coming here,” Mr. Davies said, his voice flat. He knew. In that instant, he knew he had been made a fool of. He did not say another word. He simply drew the curtains closed, plunging the room back into its conspiratorial twilight. He walked to the door, his shoulders slumped. He paused with his hand on the knob and looked back at her. The kindness was gone from his eyes, replaced by a deep, profound hurt.
“I wish you and your sister… all the best, Miss Annelise,” he said, his voice barely a whisper. “I will see myself out.”
Then he was gone, the click of the latch echoing in the silent room like a gunshot. Annelise lay rigid in the bed, the lie crumbling around her. She had not counted on the apothecary’s boy. She had not counted on Mr. Davies’s simple, heartbreaking decency. The front door closed downstairs with a soft, final thud. A future had not just been avoided; it had been actively, cruelly slain.

She did not have to wait long. The footsteps that ascended the stairs this time were rapid and heavy, the footsteps of a woman who had just been deeply, publicly humiliated. The door was thrown open without the courtesy of a knock.
Aunt Finch stood in the doorway, her face a thunderous mask of fury. She was no longer the polite hostess. She was a general whose lines had been broken.
“Get up, Annelise,” she said, her voice dangerously low and sharp as a shard of glass.
Annelise stared at her, the pretense of illness falling away. “Aunt…”
“Do not ‘Aunt’ me. I said, get up. Your little performance is over. Mr. Davies has just left. He did not even say goodbye to me. He simply informed me that he had pressing business elsewhere and that he would not be returning. Now, you will tell me where she is.”
Annelise slowly pushed herself up, her limbs feeling strangely heavy. The game was up. There was no point in more lies. “I cannot tell you that.”
“You can and you will,” her aunt snapped, advancing into the room. “Do you have any idea what you have done? Any idea at all? You have ruined this family. That man was our salvation! Five thousand a year, a respectable name, a solid establishment in the north. All of it, gone. And for what? For some childish, romantic fantasy? For a penniless drawing master with ideas above his station?”
Her aunt knew about Thomas. Annelise felt a chill go through her. Of course she knew. Her aunt missed nothing. She had simply deemed him an irrelevance, a nuisance to be swatted away.
“It is not a fantasy,” Annelise said, her own voice rising to meet her aunt’s. “Catherine loves him. He loves her. They deserve a chance to be happy.”
“Happy?” Her aunt let out a short, bitter laugh. “Happiness is a roof over your head and food on the table. Happiness is knowing you will not be thrown out of your home when the creditors come calling, a day which, thanks to you, has just drawn considerably nearer. Love does not pay the bills, you foolish girl. I loved your uncle. Do you think that made our first five years living on bread and cheese in two rented rooms any more pleasant? It is a luxury, a frivolous dessert you might permit yourself after you have eaten a sensible meal. You and your sister have just thrown the whole meal in the fire because you wanted to eat dessert first.”
She was pacing now, her fury a tangible force in the small room. “I came up here, ready to smooth things over. To tell Mr. Davies that Catherine had been taken with a sudden fit of charity and was visiting old Mrs. Gable. I had a story prepared. But he had seen the apothecary’s boy. He looked at me, Annelise. He looked at me with pity. Pity! As if I were the clueless guardian of two scheming, deceitful little vipers.”
“We are not vipers!” Annelise retorted, swinging her legs over the side of the bed. She stood, facing her aunt, her own anger a fierce, protective fire. “We are your nieces. Your dead sister’s daughters. You were supposed to protect us.”
“I was protecting you!” her aunt yelled, her voice cracking. “I was protecting you from precisely this! From poverty, from obscurity, from the foolish whims of your own hearts. Your mother, my sister, she was just like you. She threw away a perfectly good match for your father, a charming man with a brilliant mind for poetry and no mind for money whatsoever. He wrote her sonnets while they sold her mother’s pearls to pay for firewood. He loved her to distraction, and he left her with nothing but two daughters and a pile of debts. I have spent the last ten years trying to clean up their romantic mess. I will not stand by and watch you make the same mistake.”
The words struck Annelise with the force of a physical blow. She had always romanticized her parents’ story. She had never considered the grim reality her aunt was painting.
“So you would have Catherine be miserable?” she whispered. “You would have her marry a man she does not love, just for money?”
“She would not have been miserable,” her aunt said, her anger finally seeming to exhaust itself, leaving behind a cold, hard pragmatism. “She would have been comfortable. She would have been safe. Mr. Davies is a good man. He would have been kind to her. In time, she would have developed an affection for him. That is how the world works, Annelise. That is how it has always worked for women like us. Your grand bubble of love is a beautiful thing, I grant you. But it is still a bubble. The slightest touch of reality, and it pops.”
Aunt Finch stopped pacing and stood before her, her eyes cold and assessing. “I asked you where she is. I will ask you one last time.”
Annelise shook her head, her jaw set. “I will not tell you.”
Her aunt stared at her for a long, silent moment. A strange, almost admiring light flickered in her eyes before it was extinguished. “Very well,” she said, her voice devoid of all emotion. “Then you leave me no choice. You think this is a romantic ballad. It is not. It is a matter of logistics.”
She turned and walked to the door. “John Coachman will have the fast carriage ready in twenty minutes. They will be making for the North Road, trying to get to Scotland. That is the only logical route. The post-chaise your sister has hired will be slow. We will be faster. I will find her, Annelise. And I will bring her back. And when I do, the life you have known here will be over. For both of you.”
She pulled the door closed, and this time, Annelise heard the distinct, final sound of a key turning in the lock. She was a prisoner. Below, she heard her aunt’s sharp voice calling for the coachman, her tone one of absolute command. She heard the house roused into a new, urgent activity. They had stepped off the precipice, praying they had wings. Now, Annelise could only listen to the sound of her aunt’s furies being harnessed, ready to pluck them out of the sky. The liberation she had felt was a fleeting dream. This was the reality: a locked room, a furious chase, and the terrifying, silent question of which carriage would be faster.

The silence in the room was a living thing, a heavy blanket woven from her aunt’s final words and the suffocating scent of dried lavender. Annelise stood frozen in the center of the floral carpet, her ears straining, cataloging the sounds of her own imprisonment. The hurried, heavy tread of John Coachman on the gravel path outside. The sharp, clipped commands of Aunt Finch, a voice that could curdle milk, now organizing a military campaign. The distant, anxious whinny of a horse being bridled in haste. Each sound was a nail being hammered into the coffin of her sister’s happiness.

She ran to the door first, a foolish, animal instinct. She rattled the brass handle, her fingers slipping on the cool metal. It was useless. The lock was solid, ancient, a piece of iron as unyielding as her aunt’s will. She pressed her ear to the polished wood, but the sounds from the hall were muffled, indistinct. She was cut off. A specimen in a jar, left to watch the world through distorted glass.

Panic, cold and sharp, pricked at the edges of her composure. She backed away from the door, her gaze sweeping the room that had been her sanctuary since childhood. It was a lovely room, all pale blues and cream, with a small writing desk by the window and shelves overflowing with books. Her books. Her father’s books. Volumes of poetry, plays, romantic histories. The very fuel for the fire her aunt so despised. Looking at them now, she felt a flicker of doubt. Had she and Catherine been living in a fantasy spun from these pages? Was her aunt’s cold pragmatism the only truth that mattered? The image of her mother, frail and coughing by a meager fire while her father wrote sonnets, rose unbidden in her mind. It was a story Annelise had always polished to a shine, seeing only the beauty of the love, not the chill of the poverty.

No. She shook her head, the motion sharp, violent. Her aunt was wrong. Or rather, she was only half right. A life without means was hard, yes, but a life without love was a prison of a different sort, one with gilded bars and silken chains. Catherine would have withered in a marriage to Mr. Davies. Her light would have gone out. Annelise had not just helped her sister run toward love, she had helped her run from a slow, comfortable death of the soul. That was a truth she would cling to. It was the only raft she had in this churning sea of fear.

The sounds below were growing more organized, more imminent. The carriage would be ready soon. The fast carriage. The words echoed in her mind. We will be faster.

Annelise flew to the window, her heart a frantic drum against her ribs. She fumbled with the latch and pushed the casement open. A gust of cool evening air, smelling of damp earth and coming rain, rushed in to meet her. She leaned out, her palms flat on the rough stone of the sill. Her room was on the second story, overlooking the formal gardens at the side of the house. Below her, the trellised roses her mother had planted climbed the wall, their thorns like tiny, grasping fingers. It was a long way down. A fall would mean a broken leg, or worse. But her gaze was fixed beyond the garden, on the corner of the house around which the stable yard lay. If she could just get there. If she could just do something, anything, to slow them down.

A matter of logistics, her aunt had said. Very well. Annelise would engage in logistics of her own.

She looked at the trellis again. It was old, but the main vines were thick as a man’s wrist. She tested it with her foot, reaching a leg out into the open air. A shower of dried leaves and petals rained down. A section of the wood groaned in protest, but it held. It would have to hold. There was no other choice.

With her breath held tight in her chest, she swung her other leg over the sill. For a terrifying moment she dangled, her fingers scrabbling for purchase on the stone, the thin fabric of her dress snagging on a nail. Then her feet found the lattice, and she began to climb down, her movements clumsy and desperate. Her soft house slippers offered no grip. A thorn tore a long scratch down her arm, drawing a thin line of blood. She ignored the sting. Down she went, her mind a blank slate of pure, focused will. Stop them. Slow them down. Give Catherine time.

Her feet touched the soft earth of the rose bed with a jolt that jarred her teeth. She did not pause. Crouching low, she skirted the edge of the manicured lawn, using the great, shadowy forms of the yew hedges for cover. The sky was a deep, bruised purple, the day bleeding out into night. A few early stars were pricking the gloom. It was perfect. The darkness was her ally.

She reached the corner of the house and peered around it. The stable yard was a hive of controlled chaos, illuminated by the bobbing light of several lanterns. John Coachman, a man whose face was a permanent mask of dour concentration, was overseeing two grooms as they finished harnessing a team of four magnificent black horses to the traveling coach. It was not the family’s usual carriage; this one was leaner, built for speed, its wheels unnervingly tall and slender. Her aunt’s war machine.

Aunt Finch herself stood near the stable door, wrapped in a heavy woolen cloak, her face a pale, determined mask in the lantern light. She was speaking to a footman, handing him a small, heavy purse. Annelise could not hear the words, but the intent was clear. This was a chase with no expense spared.

Annelise’s eyes scanned the scene, searching for a weakness, an opportunity. What could she do that would not be immediately discovered? She was no stable hand. She could not tamper with the harness without being obvious. She could not spook the horses; they were too well trained, and the grooms were right there. Her gaze fell upon the wheels. On the spare wheel strapped to the back of the coach. And then, on the small, unassuming bucket of axle grease and the collection of tools that lay near the mounting block.

An idea, mad and brilliant, sparked in her mind. A carriage wheel was held on by a single pin, a linchpin. A simple piece of metal that prevented the entire assembly from flying off. It was a piece John Coachman would check as a matter of course before a long journey. But what if one were to go missing from the spares? What if a second one, one already in place, were to be… loosened? Not removed entirely, for that would be noticed. But tampered with, so that it might work its way free after a few miles of hard riding on the rough North Road. It was a terrible risk. A wheel coming off at speed could cause a catastrophic accident. But her aunt was a force of nature. She would survive. It would, however, stop her. It would grant Catherine hours.

Her aunt turned her back, walking toward the house to retrieve one last item. The grooms were occupied with the lead horses. It was now or never.

Annelise took a deep breath and darted from the cover of the shadows. Her movements were a silent grey blur against the darkening cobblestones. She snatched up a heavy wrench from the tool pile, her hand closing around its greasy weight. She moved to the rear of the coach, on the side opposite the grooms, her body shielded by the bulk of the carriage. Her heart hammered so loudly she was sure they must hear it.

She found the linchpin on the rear left wheel. It was a thick cotter pin, secured through the axle. It was stiff, coated in grease and road dust. She fitted the wrench around its head, pulling with all her might. It would not budge. Panic began to claw at her again. This was a fool’s errand. She was not strong enough.

She repositioned the wrench, using the ground for leverage, putting her entire body into the effort. The pin groaned, then twisted. Not much, just a fraction of an inch. She pulled again, her knuckles scraping against the wheel spokes. It moved a little more. It was not loose. It would not fall out immediately. But the jolting and rattling of a high speed chase would work on it. The metal would vibrate, the pin would turn. It was a chance. It was more than she’d had locked in her room.

Footsteps. Her aunt was returning.

Annelise dropped the wrench, her nerve failing. She could not risk being caught. She scrambled back, intending to flee into the darkness, but as she rounded the back of the carriage, she saw the spares. There was a small wooden box containing extra tackle. Without thinking, she opened it, her fingers searching in the dark. She found them. Two spare linchpins, smooth and cool. She grabbed one and shoved it into the pocket of her dress. Its absence might not be noticed for some time, not until it was needed. It was another small delay, another grain of sand in her aunt’s perfect machine.

She fled, her heart in her throat, melting back into the shadows of the garden just as her aunt reached the carriage door. She did not stop until she was back under her own window, her chest heaving, her dress stained with grease and dirt. She had done it. She had thrown her small, desperate stone at the giant.

She heard her aunt’s voice, sharp and clear. “Is everything ready, John?”

“Aye, ma’am. The horses are fresh. We’ll make good time.”

“Check everything. I want no delays on the road.”

Annelise held her breath. She pictured John Coachman making his final inspection. Would he notice the slightly turned pin? Would he feel that it was not seated quite right? A long, agonizing minute passed. Then another.

“All seems in order, ma’am,” the coachman called out. “Just as it should be.”

Relief washed over Annelise, so potent it almost buckled her knees. She had done it.

“Good,” said Aunt Finch. The carriage door opened and closed with a solid thud. “To the North Road. Drive them as if the devil himself is at your heels.”

The crack of a whip split the night air, a sound like a gunshot. The carriage lurched forward, its great wheels crunching on the gravel. The four black horses strained, and then they were moving, gathering speed, the rhythmic thunder of their hooves growing fainter and fainter as they swept down the long drive and disappeared into the night.

Annelise watched until the twin points of the carriage lamps were swallowed by the darkness. She had bought her sister time. An hour? Two? She did not know. But it was something. A flicker of hope in the overwhelming darkness.

Slowly, painfully, she began to climb the trellis again. Her muscles screamed in protest, and her hands, slick with grease, kept slipping. It was a far harder ascent. When she finally tumbled back through the window and collapsed onto her bedroom floor, she was shaking, bruised, and utterly exhausted. But she was not defeated.

She lay there for a long time, listening to the house settle into a new, uneasy quiet. The furious energy of the departure had dissipated, leaving a vacuum in its wake. There was only the sound of the wind in the trees and the distant, mournful cry of an owl. The adrenaline faded, and the cold reality of her situation returned. She was still a prisoner. And when her aunt returned, her fury would be a thing of legend. The thought sent a new chill through her, colder than the night air.

A soft scratching sound at her door made her jump. It was not the sound of a key. It was lighter, more hesitant.

“Miss Annelise?” a small voice whispered through the keyhole.

Annelise crawled to the door. “Mary? Is that you?” Mary was one of the younger housemaids, a girl with wide, sympathetic eyes who had always looked at Catherine and Annelise with a kind of gentle adoration.

“Yes, miss. I saw… I saw them lock you in. It ain’t right.”

A surge of gratitude, sharp and overwhelming, brought tears to Annelise’s eyes. “Oh, Mary.”

“Your aunt… she was in a state, miss. Before she left, she came in here. With two of the footmen.”

Annelise frowned. “What are you talking about? I have been here the whole time.”

“No, miss. While you were… well, while you weren’t here,” the girl said, her voice dropping lower, as if she knew exactly what Annelise had been doing. “She used her own key. She came in. She said you were to have no more distractions. No more foolish ideas.”

A dreadful premonition seized Annelise. She pushed herself to her feet and turned to face her room. At first, nothing seemed amiss. But then her eyes fell on the bookshelves.

They were empty.

Every single book was gone. Her volumes of Shakespeare, her treasured Keats, the worn copy of her father’s favorite Donne. The novels, the histories, the leather bound collections of verse. All of it. Stripped away. She went to her writing desk. Her inkwell was gone. Her quills were gone. Her little stack of fine paper, gone. Her lap desk, which had been her mother’s, was gone.

Her aunt had not just locked her in a room. She had hollowed it out. She had taken away her words, her stories, her past and her comfort. It was an act of breathtaking cruelty, more profound than any physical blow. It was an attempt to unmake her, to scour her mind of the romanticism that she embodied.

Annelise sank onto the edge of her bed, a low cry of despair escaping her lips. This was the true beginning of her punishment. Her aunt was not just trying to bring Catherine back; she was trying to break Annelise’s spirit, to prove that her world of love and poetry was nothing but a fragile dream, easily dismantled.

“Miss?” Mary’s voice was a small thread of sound from the other side of the door. “She said… she said she had the footmen burn them. In the kitchen furnace.”

The words hit Annelise with a physical force. Burned. Her father’s books. The marginalia he had scribbled, the pages her mother’s hands had turned. All of it turned to ash and smoke. She pictured the flames consuming the words, the stories, the love they represented. And in that moment, something inside her hardened. Grief gave way to a cold, clear rage. Her aunt had meant to break her. Instead, she had forged her into something new. Something harder.

She went back to the door, her voice low and steady. “Mary. I need your help. It is a great risk. You must not agree if you are afraid.”

There was a pause. “For you and Miss Catherine? I’ll do it.”

“My aunt has taken my paper,” Annelise said. “Is there anything? A scrap from the kitchens? A page from an account book?”

“I can find something, miss. And a bit of charcoal from the hearth.”

“Good. And Mary… one more thing. Do you know the stable boy, Tom, who sometimes runs messages into the village?”

“I do, miss.”

“When you bring me the paper, I will have a note. It is not for the North Road. It is for Mr. Davies.”

There was a sharp intake of breath on the other side of the door. “Mr. Davies, miss? But…”

“My aunt believes love is a weakness,” Annelise whispered, her hand resting on the cold, unyielding wood of the door. “She believes kindness is a transaction. I am going to see if she is right. She thinks this is a race between two carriages. But she has forgotten there are other players on the board.” She paused, a new, steely resolve settling into her bones. Her aunt had called this a matter of logistics. And Annelise was just beginning to fight. “Bring me the paper, Mary. Quickly. The night is not over yet.”

The silence that followed was absolute. Annelise stood with her hand pressed to the door, feeling the faint vibrations of the house settling around her. The creak of a floorboard on the floor below, the distant clatter of a pot in the kitchens, the sigh of the wind against the windowpane. Each sound was a reminder of the world that continued on without her, a world from which she was now surgically removed. She listened for Mary’s footsteps, her ears straining against the quiet. The wait stretched, each second a small eternity. In that time, the cold rage that had filled her did not cool, but rather settled. It became less a fire and more a block of ice in her chest, solid and heavy. It was a foundation.

She paced the length of the room, her bare feet silent on the wooden floorboards. The moonlight, thin and silver, spilled through the window, illuminating the stark emptiness. She saw the pale rectangles on the walls where pictures had hung. She saw the ghostly outlines in the dust on the shelves where her books had stood. It was a room of ghosts, a testament to what had been stolen. Her aunt had not just taken things; she had taken evidence. Evidence of a life lived with feeling, of a mind shaped by beauty and thought. She had tried to erase the person Annelise was. But as Annelise looked at the empty spaces, she did not feel erased. She felt… distilled. All that was left was the core of her, the part that loved Catherine, the part that remembered her parents, the part that would not, could not, break.

A faint scratching sound at the door, as soft as a mouse. “Miss?” Mary’s whisper was barely audible.

Annelise was there in an instant, her mouth close to the keyhole. “Mary. Are you safe?”

“Yes, miss. I have it.”

The sound of something sliding under the door. Annelise knelt. Her fingers found the edge of a rough sheet of paper. It was thick and slightly greasy, likely a sheet of wrapping paper from the butcher, saved for kitchen use. Beside it was a small, brittle stick of charcoal. It was a pathetic substitute for her fine quills and vellum, but in that moment, it was the most precious treasure she had ever held. It was a weapon. It was a voice.

She retreated from the door, carrying the paper and charcoal to the single shaft of moonlight by the window. She had no desk, no chair. She knelt on the floor, spreading the paper out on the bare boards. Her mind, so used to weaving intricate verses and flowing prose, now had to become ruthlessly efficient. Mr. Davies was a man of logic and law, her father’s solicitor and the executor of his will. He was a dry, precise man who had always seemed mildly baffled by her father’s poetic soul, but his loyalty had been absolute. He would not respond to a plea for love’s sake. He would respond to a threat against the estate.

Her fingers, clumsy with the unfamiliar charcoal, began to write. The brittle stick crumbled slightly, leaving a smudge of black on her skin. The words were not elegant. They were stark and urgent.

*Davies,*

*Aunt Eleanor moves against the estate. C. is the instrument. The marriage is a sale. All that Father feared has come to pass. You hold the charter. Intervene.*

*A.*

She read it over. C. for Catherine. The marriage is a sale. A simple, brutal truth that Mr. Davies would understand. And the charter. Her father had spoken of it once, a long time ago. A legal document, drafted by Mr. Davies himself, designed to protect the girls, to protect the integrity of their inheritance from precisely this kind of avaricious maneuvering. It was a failsafe, a final piece of fatherly protection from beyond the grave. Her aunt, in her arrogance, must have forgotten it, or believed Annelise too much of a child to know of its existence.

She folded the rough paper carefully, the grease making it resistant. She went back to the door. “Mary,” she whispered.

“I am here, miss.”

“The note is ready. Can you get it to Tom?”

“His room is above the stables, miss. He will be abed now. But I can make an excuse. Say I need to check on the new mare, that I thought I heard a noise. The head groom trusts me.”

“There is money in the small pot on my dressing table. The one my aunt overlooked. Take it. Give some to Tom. For his trouble and his silence.” Annelise paused. A transaction. Just as her aunt would have it. But this was different. This was payment for loyalty, not a purchase of it. “Tell him Mr. Davies is in the village, at The Gilded Quill inn. He must ride now. Not with the dawn. Now. Tell him the future of this house depends on it.”

“Yes, miss.” Mary’s voice was firm.

Annelise pushed the folded note under the door. She heard Mary retrieve it. Then, a moment of hesitation.

“Miss Annelise,” Mary said, her voice softer. “She will be furious when she learns of this.”

“I am counting on it,” Annelise replied, the ice in her chest seeming to spread through her veins. “Go, Mary. And be careful.”

She heard the soft pad of Mary’s retreating footsteps. And then, once again, she was alone in the hollow room. The adrenaline that had sustained her began to fade, leaving a profound weariness in its wake. She went to her bed, but she did not lie down. She sat on the edge, her hands clasped in her lap, and she stared at the empty shelves.

The burning. The thought came back to her, no longer with the hot sting of grief, but with a cold, analytical curiosity. Her aunt had ordered them burned in the kitchen furnace. Not the grand fireplace in the library, where such a thing might be seen. No, she had relegated them to the servants’ domain, a place of soot and refuse. She had not just destroyed the books; she had insulted them. She had treated them like garbage. Annelise thought of her father’s copy of Ovid, the leather worn as soft as a glove. She remembered the pressed flower, a violet, that had marked a page in a book of sonnets her mother had loved. They were ash now. Part of her wanted to weep, to curl into a ball and surrender to the howling emptiness of that loss.

But she did not. Instead, she began to build a fortress in her mind. She thought of the stories her aunt could not burn. The ones she knew by heart. She began to recite them, silently, to the empty room. She started with a simple ballad, one her nurse had taught her. Then a sonnet. Then a long, complex passage from a play. The words were a comfort, but more than that, they were an act of defiance. Her mind was a library her aunt could not torch. Her memory was a sanctuary her aunt could not breach. She sat there for hours, a still figure in the moonlight, rebuilding her world inside the wreckage of her room. The night wore on, marked only by the slow crawl of the moon across the sky.

Sometime before dawn, she must have drifted off, for she was startled awake by the sound of a key grating in the lock. Her heart leaped into her throat. Had Mary been caught? Was this the end?

The door swung open, and her aunt stood there, silhouetted against the dim light of the hallway. She held a single candle, its flame throwing long, dancing shadows. She did not look angry. She looked… satisfied.

“I thought I might find you awake,” Aunt Eleanor said, her voice smooth. She stepped into the room, her eyes sweeping over the empty spaces with a proprietary air. “It is a great improvement, do you not think? So much less clutter. A mind, like a room, functions best when it is orderly and free of distractions.”

Annelise said nothing. She remained seated on the edge of the bed, her hands still clasped, her face pale and composed. She willed her expression to be blank, a mask of weary submission.

Her aunt glided closer, the candlelight illuminating the sharp planes of her face. She looked at Annelise, her gaze analytical, as if inspecting a piece of merchandise. “Catherine’s carriage leaves within the hour. She is being fitted into her travelling dress now. She cried, of course. A great deal. But she is a good girl, fundamentally. She understands her duty. It is a shame you do not.”

Still, Annelise was silent. She focused on a point on the far wall, a place where a small watercolor of the coastline had once hung. She could see it in her mind’s eye. The blue of the water, the white of the gulls.

Her aunt seemed displeased by her lack of response. She wanted a reaction. She wanted tears, or pleas, or impotent rage. “I have done this for you as much as for her,” she continued, her voice taking on a slightly harder edge. “To show you the consequences of this… romantic affliction. Love does not pay the bills, Annelise. It does not repair a leaky roof or secure a place in society. Lord Ashworth’s fortune will secure this family for a generation. Your cousin’s pretty face is a commodity, and I have simply found the highest bidder. That is how the world works.”

Annelise finally met her aunt’s gaze. Her own eyes were cold and clear. “Yes, Aunt,” she said, her voice a low monotone. “I understand.”

The simple agreement seemed to unnerve her aunt more than any argument would have. She had come for a battle and found a surrender. She frowned. “Good,” she said, though she did not sound pleased. “Perhaps there is hope for you yet. Perhaps once the poetry is burned out of you, the sense will be allowed to grow.” She placed the candle on the bare top of the dressing table. “You will remain here until I am satisfied you are no longer a danger to yourself or this family’s prospects. Your meals will be brought. Do not attempt to speak with the servants.”

She turned and left, the sound of the key turning in the lock a final, definitive click. Annelise listened to her footsteps fade down the hall. Only when she was sure she was gone did she allow herself a small, mirthless smile. Her aunt thought she had won. She thought the game was over. She had paraded her victory, explained her entire strategy, and revealed her entire philosophy, all while believing Annelise was a vanquished foe. It was a fatal miscalculation. Her aunt saw a broken girl. She did not see the fortress Annelise had built in the night. She did not see the message even now making its way through the pre dawn gloom toward The Gilded Quill.

An hour later, the sounds began. The heavy tread of footmen in the hall below, carrying trunks. The crunch of wheels on the gravel of the drive. The whinny of horses. Annelise went to the window. By pressing her face to the glass and craning her neck, she could just see the front of the house. The large black carriage stood waiting, its lamps lit against the grey morning. She saw her aunt, a severe figure in a dark travelling cloak, directing the loading. Then she saw Catherine.

She was a small, forlorn figure in a deep blue dress, her face pale and her shoulders slumped. She moved as if in a dream, her hand held tightly by her mother, a woman who looked as cowed and unhappy as her daughter. Annelise’s heart ached for her. She wanted to scream, to break the window, to let Catherine know she was not alone, that help was coming. But she could not. Any sign of defiance now would alert her aunt, and the entire plan depended on her aunt’s complacency. So she watched, her fingers gripping the window frame, as Catherine was helped into the carriage. Her aunt followed, settling herself opposite her niece, a dragon guarding her treasure. The door was closed. The driver cracked his whip, and the carriage lurched into motion.

Annelise watched until it was just a black shape disappearing down the long drive, taking the North Road. Taking Catherine away. The cold rage in her chest flared, but she banked it, contained it. This was not the end. This was the middle. Her aunt had played her move. Now, it was Mr. Davies’s turn.

The day passed with agonizing slowness. Mary came in the late morning with a tray: a piece of dry bread, a small wedge of cheese, and a cup of water. Her eyes were red rimmed, but she gave a tiny, almost imperceptible nod as she set the tray down.

“He is gone, miss,” she whispered, not looking at Annelise, pretending to straighten the thin blanket on the bed. “Tom. He rode out before the cook was even awake. Said his uncle was taken ill in the village. No one questioned it.”

“Thank you, Mary,” Annelise said, her voice low. “You are very brave.”

Mary risked a quick glance at her, a flicker of fierce loyalty in her eyes, before she curtsied and left, the lock turning behind her.

Annelise ate the bread and cheese, not because she was hungry, but because she knew she needed strength. She drank the water. Then she began to pace again. The fortress of her mind was built; now she needed to furnish it with patience. She waited. For what, she did not know. A sign. A message. Anything. The sun crossed the sky. The shadows in her room lengthened and shifted. The silence was a physical weight.

Late in the afternoon, a new sound intruded. A different sound from the morning’s departure. It was a lighter carriage, pulled by a single horse, coming up the drive at a brisk trot. It was not her aunt returning so soon. Annelise rushed to the window again. A small, unassuming trap pulled up before the door. A man in a dark, sober suit of clothes climbed out. He was neither young nor old. He carried a leather satchel. He looked like a man of business. A clerk.

He walked to the front door and knocked. Annelise’s breath caught in her throat. After a few moments, the door was opened by the butler. There was a short, unintelligible exchange, and then the clerk was admitted into the house.

Annelise’s heart began to beat a frantic, hopeful rhythm against her ribs. It was too soon for it to be a coincidence. It had to be him. It had to be Davies’s opening move. It was not a thunderclap, not a cavalry charge. It was a quiet man with a leather satchel. It was a move on her aunt’s own board, played by her own rules of business and law. Her aunt thought she was on the North Road, racing toward a fortune. She had no idea that a second front had just been opened, right here, in the heart of her own home. Annelise pressed her hand against the cold windowpane, a real smile finally touching her lips. The game was truly afoot.

The minutes stretched into an hour. Annelise sat on the edge of her narrow bed, her hands clasped tightly in her lap, forcing herself into a state of unnatural calm. She had listened to the sounds from below. The low, authoritative murmur of the clerk’s voice, a stark contrast to the higher, flustered tones of Jennings the butler. There was the scrape of a chair in the hall, as if someone had been asked to wait. Then, a longer silence. She imagined the clerk, a Mr. Quill perhaps she named him in her mind, sitting impassively on the hard hall chair, his leather satchel resting on his knees. He would be the embodiment of impersonal law, a force her aunt could not bully or charm or dismiss. He was a fact, delivered to her front door.

After what felt like an eternity, she heard the firm tread of his boots again, the brief, formal exchange of goodbyes, and the solid click of the front door closing. A moment later, the light trap crunched on the gravel as it turned and trotted away, the sound fading into the afternoon stillness. He was gone. The opening move was complete. Now came the backlash. She knew Jennings would not have the courage or the authority to keep such a visit from her aunt. He would send a rider. He would dispatch an urgent message. Her aunt, believing herself to be victoriously en route to London, would be summoned back by this unexpected and alarming development. Annelise calculated the distances. The message would likely intercept her aunt’s carriage at a coaching inn where she planned to stop for the night. She would turn around immediately. She would be back by morning, if not sooner, driving the horses into the ground.

Annelise stood and went back to the window. The world outside was serene, bathed in the soft, golden light of the declining sun. It offered no hint of the war being waged within the walls of the house or the storm that was gathering on the horizon. She thought of Catherine, bundled into that carriage, her face pale with confusion and fear. Mr. Davies’s man had to be on the road. A discreet shadow, keeping pace, ensuring no harm came to her. It was a fragile hope, but it was all she had. It was the bedrock on which she built her resolve.

The house fell into a new kind of quiet. A tense, waiting quiet. The servants would be whispering in the kitchens, in the scullery, in the stables. The arrival of a London clerk with a satchel was a momentous event, an omen. Their loyalties, already strained by Mrs. Croft’s tyrannical reign, would be tested. Annelise knew that most, like Mary, harbored a quiet affection for her and Catherine, the daughters of their former, kinder master. But fear was a powerful motivator, and her aunt was a master of wielding it.

As dusk began to purple the edges of the sky, she heard the familiar soft footsteps approaching her door. The key turned with its usual grating complaint. Mary entered, carrying a tray with a bowl of thin soup and another piece of bread. Her face was pale in the growing dimness, her eyes wide. She set the tray on the small table and, without a word, began her ritual of tidying the already tidy room.

“He served a paper, miss,” she whispered, her back to the door, her hands needlessly smoothing the coverlet. “On the house itself, it seemed. Mr. Jennings tried to refuse it. He said Mrs. Croft was not at home, that he could not accept any legal documents in her absence.”

Annelise leaned forward, her own voice a bare thread of sound. “And the clerk?”

“He was very calm, miss. He said his instructions were to serve the notice upon the property’s senior resident domestic, which was Mr. Jennings. He said refusal to accept would be noted but would not invalidate the service. Mr. Jennings looked like he might faint. The man, the clerk, he just placed the document on the hall table, tipped his hat, and said his business was concluded.” Mary finally risked a look at Annelise, a spark of something like triumph in her eyes. “He asked for a glass of water before he left. While I was fetching it from the kitchen, he… he gave me this for you.”

Her hand, quick as a mouse, darted from the pocket of her apron. She pressed a tiny, tightly folded piece of paper into Annelise’s palm. It was cool and crisp. Annelise’s fingers closed around it, a jolt of electricity shooting up her arm.

“He said to tell you the North Road is well watched,” Mary added, her voice trembling slightly. “And that you should eat. To keep up your strength.”

“Mary,” Annelise breathed, her throat tight with emotion. “I will not forget this. Whatever happens, I will not forget your courage.”

A faint blush touched Mary’s cheeks. She gave another of her quick, jerky curtsies and slipped out of the room, the lock tumbling home behind her with its sound of finality. But it was not final. The note in her hand was a key of a different sort.

Annelise did not rush to read it. She went to the door and listened, her ear pressed against the cold wood. She heard Mary’s footsteps retreating down the hall and then the stairs. She waited. Five minutes. Ten. Only when the house was utterly silent again did she move to the window, using the last of the dying light.

Her fingers, clumsy with anticipation, carefully unfolded the small square of paper. The handwriting was sharp, angular, and blessedly clear.

A,
Injunction served. All estate assets frozen pending review of the will. Your aunt cannot sell, transfer, or borrow against the property.
C is safe. My man follows. She will be lodged comfortably and securely, away from your aunt’s reach, as soon as it is safe to intervene.
The true will exists. Your father spoke of it. He told me he kept his most important papers somewhere you would know. A place from your childhood. Think. Memory is your map. We need that document.
Hold fast. The game is long.
D.

Annelise read the note three times, committing every word to memory. Her heart swelled with a relief so profound it almost brought her to her knees. Catherine was safe. Watched over. That was the first and most important battle. The injunction was the second. Davies had bound her aunt’s hands with the red tape of the law. She was trapped, her plans to liquidate the estate and seize the fortune thwarted before they had even truly begun.

A place from your childhood. Memory is your map.

She closed her eyes, the note still clutched in her hand. Her father. She saw his kind eyes, the way he would lean back in his study chair and tell her stories. He had trusted her, talked to her not as a child but as a confidante, especially after her mother had passed. Where? Where would he hide something so vital?

Her room had once been her nursery. It was furnished simply now, a prisoner’s cell. The bed, the table, a small wardrobe, a washstand. And in the corner, covered with a dust sheet, a piece of furniture that had been deemed too old and unfashionable for any of the grander rooms: a small rosewood secretaire, a writing desk that had belonged to her grandmother. It had been in the nursery when she was a little girl. She and Catherine used to play at it, pretending to be great ladies writing important letters. Her father had often sat there with her, showing her how to draw horses, folding paper into the shape of a bird.

It was too obvious. And yet, it was also perfect. A place from your childhood. Somewhere you would know.

She went to the corner and pulled off the dust sheet. The wood gleamed faintly in the moonlight that now streamed through the window. It was a beautiful, delicate thing, inlaid with mother of pearl. And it was locked. Of course it was locked. The main desk portion, and a series of small drawers above it. She ran her fingers over the smooth wood, searching for a hidden catch, a secret button her father might have shown her. She remembered him tapping it once, a game they played. “Every treasure chest has a secret,” he had said with a wink.

She pressed and prodded along the carved trim, the inlaid patterns, her desperation mounting. Nothing. The lock was small but solid. She had no key. No tools. She felt a surge of frustration. The answer was here, inches away, but sealed from her.

She ate the soup and bread mechanically, her mind racing. She could not break it open; the noise would bring the whole household. She needed a key, or something to pick the lock. A hairpin? Her hair was simply pinned up. She drew one out, a long, steel pin. She went back to the secretaire and knelt before it, inserting the tip of the pin into the tiny keyhole. She had read of such things in novels, but the reality was maddeningly difficult. The pin bent. The lock remained stubbornly closed.

Defeated for the moment, she knew she had to destroy the note. She tore it into the tiniest possible pieces, then, one by one, she chewed and swallowed them, the taste of paper and ink bitter in her mouth. It was a grim, desperate act, but it ensured there was no evidence to find.

She lay on her bed in the darkness, not sleeping, but thinking. The secretaire. The lock. A childhood memory. Her father’s words echoed in her mind. She thought of their games, the stories he told, the rhymes he taught her. A rhyme about a magpie. One for sorrow, two for joy. He had pointed to the inlay on the secretaire. Two small magpies, their beaks holding a pearl blossom. Two for joy. She had forgotten.

She scrambled off the bed and back to the desk, her heart hammering. In the pale moonlight, she found the two inlaid birds. She pressed on the first one. Nothing. She pressed on the second. A faint, soft click, almost inaudible. A tiny, almost invisible panel of wood, no bigger than her thumbnail, sprang open just below the birds. And inside, nestled in a bed of faded velvet, was a small, ornate, brass key.

Her breath hitched. A wave of love and grief for her father washed over her. He had trusted her to remember. He had left her a map, and she had found the treasure.

With a trembling hand, she took the key and fitted it into the main lock. It turned as smoothly and silently as if it had been oiled that very day. The front of the secretaire folded down, revealing a writing surface of worn green leather and an array of small pigeonholes and drawers. Most were empty, filled only with the faint, dusty scent of old paper and woodsmoke. But in the very central pigeonhole, tucked far in the back, was a thick, folded bundle of documents, tied with a faded blue ribbon.

She did not need to read them to know what they were. This was it. The true will. The weapon Davies needed. She held it in her hands, the paper cool and heavy. It felt like the weight of her entire future.

She had just enough time to push the documents into the bodice of her dress, the stiff paper scratching against her skin, and to relock the secretaire, returning the secret key to its hiding place, when she heard it.

The sound she had been anticipating.

The frantic, pounding rhythm of horses’ hooves, driven hard up the gravel drive. The angry crunch of carriage wheels pulled to a jarring halt. A carriage door thrown open with such force it seemed to shudder on its hinges. Then, the heavy, furious stamp of her aunt’s boots on the stone steps and the violent slam of the front door that echoed through the entire house.

The storm had arrived.

Annelise stood in the center of her room, her back straight, her hands clasped behind her. She did not run to the window. She did not hide. She waited. The papers hidden against her heart were a shield. The knowledge she now possessed was her armor.

She heard her aunt’s voice, a furious, cutting shriek from the hall below. “Jennings! What is the meaning of this? Explain yourself!”

The butler’s voice was a terrified, placating murmur. Annelise could picture him, wringing his hands, his face ashen, holding out the legal document the clerk had left. There was the sound of paper being ripped, followed by a torrent of rage so potent it seemed to make the floorboards vibrate.

Then, the footsteps. Heavy, rapid, coming up the stairs. Coming for her.

Annelise took a slow, deep breath. She was no longer a frightened girl in a locked room. She was the keeper of the truth. She was a player in the game, and she held a very powerful piece.

The key slammed into the lock of her door, turned with a savage twist. The door flew inward, crashing against the wall. Her aunt stood on the threshold, her face a mask of incandescent fury. Her bonnet was askew, her traveling cloak was still dusty from the road, and her eyes burned with a cold, malevolent fire.

“You,” she hissed, her voice low and shaking with rage. “What have you done?”

Annelise met her gaze without flinching. She kept her own voice level, cool. “I am not sure what you mean, Aunt. I have done nothing. I have been locked in this room since you left.”

“Do not play the innocent with me, you deceitful little viper,” Mrs. Croft snarled, stalking into the room. She loomed over Annelise, her presence filling the small space with menace. “A man from London. A solicitor. An injunction. These things do not happen by magic. You contacted someone. How?”

Annelise gave a small, calculated shrug. “Perhaps father had more friends than you knew. Perhaps his business partners grew concerned when you began to treat his daughters like prisoners and his estate like your personal bank.”

Her aunt’s hand flew up, and for a second Annelise thought she would be struck. But the hand stopped, trembling, inches from her face. A flicker of uncertainty, of fear, crossed her aunt’s features. Striking her would gain nothing and could be used against her. The law was involved now. There were rules.

“You have no idea what you are meddling in,” her aunt said, her voice dropping to a venomous whisper. “I have powerful friends. This little legal trick will be swept aside. And when it is, you will regret your defiance. You think this room is a prison? I will show you what a real prison is.”

Annelise held her ground, the secret papers a comforting weight against her chest. “You cannot sell the house, can you, Aunt? You cannot touch the accounts. Your great plans have met a small obstacle, it seems. A piece of paper with a fancy stamp.”

Mrs. Croft’s face contorted. For a moment, she was speechless, sputtering with a rage that had no outlet. She had come back here expecting to confront a weeping, terrified girl she could break with a few harsh words. Instead, she found a calm, defiant young woman who seemed to know more than she should.

“Where is Catherine?” Annelise asked, pressing her small advantage. The question was a calculated risk, but she had to know her aunt’s version of events.

Her aunt gave a cruel, mirthless smile. “Your sister is on her way to a new life. A school where they will teach her discipline and obedience. Qualities you so sorely lack. She is far beyond your reach, and far beyond the reach of any meddling solicitor.”

Annelise’s blood ran cold at the threat, but she clung to Davies’s promise. The North Road is well watched. “And yet,” she said softly, “I do not think she is. I think you will find that all your roads are being watched now, Aunt. You wanted a fortune. Instead, you have found yourself in a cage of your own making.”

The pure, unadulterated hatred in her aunt’s eyes was terrifying, but it was the hatred of a cornered animal. Mrs. Croft stared at her for a long moment, her chest heaving. She had lost control of the situation, and she knew it. She had come for a swift, brutal victory and had walked into a stalemate.

Without another word, she spun on her heel, marched out of the room, and slammed the door shut. The key turned in the lock, more gratingly than ever before. Annelise listened as her aunt’s footsteps descended the stairs, slower this time, heavier. The fury was still there, but now it was mingled with the ponderous weight of thought. Of strategy.

Annelise let out a breath she did not realize she had been holding. Her knees felt weak. She sank onto the bed, her hand pressed over the hidden documents. The first confrontation was over. She had survived it. She had even, in a small way, won it.

But she knew, with chilling certainty, that this was only the beginning. Her aunt was not a woman to accept defeat. The injunction was a barrier, but her aunt would seek a way through it, or around it. And her anger, denied a legal outlet, would now be entirely focused on the two people she blamed for her predicament. On Catherine, wherever she was. And on Annelise, locked in the room, holding the key to it all. The game was afoot, but the board had grown infinitely more dangerous.

The silence that followed her aunt’s departure was a different kind of quiet from the one she had grown accustomed to. Before, it had been the quiet of abandonment, of a house holding its breath. Now, it was the charged silence after a declaration of war. It vibrated with unspoken threats, with the furious, calculated thoughts of the woman now descending the stairs. Annelise pressed her forehead against the cool, unforgiving wood of the door, listening until the last echo of her aunt’s heavy tread faded into the vast, tomblike stillness of the ground floor.

She was alone again, but everything had changed. The fear was still there, a cold serpent coiled in her stomach, but it was joined by a fragile, fluttering thing: a flicker of power. It was the power of knowledge. The power of a single, well placed piece of paper. She backed away from the door, her movements slow, almost reverent. She slid the documents from the bodice of her dress. The paper was warm from her skin, the solicitor’s formal script a talisman against the encroaching darkness. She did not need to read the words again; they were etched into her memory. Yet she unfolded the main page, the injunction itself, and smoothed it out on the worn coverlet of her bed. The embossed seal of the court was a small, red sun in the gloom.

This was her shield. But a shield was a defensive weapon. Her aunt had just demonstrated, with terrifying clarity, that she intended to remain on the attack. The threat against Catherine was not idle. Annelise knew her aunt’s capacity for cruelty. A school for discipline and obedience. The words were a euphemism for a place of misery, a private asylum or a punitive workhouse where a young girl’s spirit could be systematically broken, far from any sympathetic eye. Davies had promised the roads were watched, but her aunt was resourceful. What if she had used a different route, a private carriage, an unmarked road? The possibilities multiplied in the silence, each one more dreadful than the last.

The last vestiges of her defiant adrenaline drained away, leaving behind a profound weariness and a chillingly clear understanding of her position. She had won the battle, but the siege was about to begin. Her aunt would not try the door again, not with words. She would now use silence, and time, and deprivation as her weapons.

The first proof of this new strategy came with the setting of the sun. The pale gray light filtering through the grimy window deepened to indigo, then to black. No lamp was brought. No tray with her meager evening meal appeared. The faint, familiar sounds of the house settling for the night, the distant clatter of pots from the kitchen, the murmur of servants’ voices, were entirely absent. It was as if she and her aunt were the only two souls in a vast, empty mausoleum.

Annelise sat on the edge of her bed in the deepening dark, her hunger a dull ache, secondary to the gnawing anxiety for Catherine. She thought of her sister, so timid, so reliant on her for comfort. Was she cold? Was she frightened? Was she calling for a sister who could not come? Annelise squeezed her eyes shut, fighting back the useless tears. Crying would not help. Despair was the enemy, the very thing her aunt wanted to cultivate in this cold, dark room.

She must be practical. She rose and felt her way to the washstand. The ewer was still half full of water. She took a small, careful sip of the stale, metallic liquid. It would have to last. Food she could endure without for a day, perhaps two. But water was life. Her aunt, she realized with a fresh wave of cold dread, knew this too. This was not a simple punishment. It was a methodical, patient assault on her physical and mental fortitude.

The night was the longest of her life. Without light, time became a thick, viscous thing. She lay on the bed, fully clothed, the precious papers tucked back inside her dress, a fragile armor over her heart. Every creak of the old house was magnified, every skittering sound in the walls a potential threat. Was that a footstep on the landing? Was that the scrape of metal against the keyhole? She strained her ears in the darkness, her body a taut wire of apprehension, but the sounds were only the ghosts of a house in decay. Her aunt was playing a longer game.

When morning finally came, a weak, grudging light that did little to warm the room, it brought no relief. The silence remained absolute. The hunger was a sharp, demanding pain now. Her head felt light, and a dull ache throbbed behind her eyes. She forced herself to get up, to move. To surrender to the weakness, to simply lie there and wait, would be to hand her aunt the victory.

She began a systematic exploration of her prison. The window was her first focus. The bars were thick, set deep into the stone mullions. She pushed against them, her full weight having no effect. The glass was filthy, but through a small, clearer patch she could see the tops of the great elms that lined the drive, their branches bare against the white sky. There was no sign of life, no carriage, no rider, nothing. The world outside had forgotten her.

The door was next. She ran her fingers over the thick oak planks, searching for any weakness in the hinges, any give in the frame. It was as solid as the day it was built. The keyhole was the only vulnerability, a tiny portal to the world beyond. She had knelt there many times, trying to see, but the angle was wrong, revealing only a sliver of the opposite wall.

Her gaze fell upon the hearth. The fireplace was small, designed for a nursery, and black with old soot. The chimney was far too narrow for any person to climb, but it offered a different possibility. Soot and water. She could make a basic ink. But what could she write on? Her own clothes felt too precious to tear. Then she remembered. Tucked away in a small, forgotten sewing box from her childhood, a remnant her aunt had not bothered to clear out, were scraps of linen and a few needles.

She found the box in the bottom of the wardrobe, under a pile of old blankets. Her fingers, clumsy with cold, fumbled with the latch. Inside, nestled amongst the faded scraps, was a small, sharp pair of embroidery scissors. A potential tool. A potential weapon. The thought was fleeting, and she pushed it away. She was not her aunt.

She spent the next hour working. She scraped soot from the back of the fireplace onto a plate. She mixed it with a few precious drops of water from her ewer, creating a crude, gritty ink. From the hem of her innermost petticoat, she carefully snipped a small, square piece of fabric. She had a medium, but no message and no messenger. It was an act of faith, a preparation for an opportunity that might never come.

The second day passed much like the first. More silence. More darkness. More hunger. The gnawing in her stomach was a constant, vicious companion. She rationed her water, sip by agonizing sip. She spent the long hours pacing the room to keep warm, her mind a frantic whirl of strategy and fear. She thought of Mr. Davies. He knew she was here. He would not simply abandon her. He was her piece on the board, out in the world. But how to move him? How to direct him?

It was on the second night, as she lay in a state of semi conscious delirium, that she heard it. A sound so faint she thought she had imagined it. A soft, hesitant scratching at the bottom of her door.

She sat bolt upright, her heart hammering against her ribs. "Who is it?" she whispered, her voice a dry rasp.

The scratching stopped. There was a long pause, then a whispered reply, so faint it was barely audible. "Miss Annelise? It is Martha."

Martha. The cook. A woman of sturdy build and even sturdier loyalties, who had been in their family’s service since before Annelise was born. A wave of relief so powerful it made her dizzy washed over her.

"Martha," she breathed, scrambling from the bed and crawling to the door. She pressed her face to the floor, her cheek against the cold, dusty wood. "Oh, Martha. Are you well?"

"Hush, miss," the voice came back, urgent and low. "She is sleeping. The laudanum I put in her evening tea has seen to that. But it will not last forever."

Laudanum. Annelise’s mind reeled. The gentle, law abiding cook had drugged her aunt. The world had truly been turned upside down.

"What is happening?" Annelise whispered, her mouth close to the crack under the door.

"She has gone mad, miss. Truly mad. She dismissed everyone yesterday. Sent them away without wages. It is just me left. She said she needed a cook. I stayed. I had to. For you." There was the sound of a muffled sob. "She watches me like a hawk. I could not get to you sooner. She has taken all the keys. But she is a fool about the kitchen. She does not know the old ways."

A small parcel was being pushed under the door. It was a thick slice of bread and a wedge of hard cheese, wrapped in a napkin. Annelise seized it, her hands trembling. She had never known such gratitude for so simple a thing.

"Eat, miss. You must keep your strength up."

"Catherine," Annelise said, her mouth full of the glorious, life giving bread. "Do you know anything of my sister?"

"I heard her talking to a man," Martha whispered. "A grim fellow with a carriage. She paid him in gold. I heard the name of a place. Blackwood Manor. She called it a school. Up north, she said. That is all I know."

Blackwood Manor. The name was as grim as the man Martha described. But it was a name. It was a destination. It was more than she had had before.

"Martha, I need you to do something for me. It is dangerous."

"Anything, miss." The reply was immediate, firm.

"I have a message. It must get to my solicitor. His name is Mr. Davies. His address is on Fleet Street." She scrabbled back to the bed, found the soot stained square of linen and the small embroidery scissors she had sharpened into a crude stylus. There was no time for soot ink. She would have to scratch the words.

She worked quickly, her fingers guided by desperation. Using the point of the scissors, she painstakingly scratched her message into the fabric, the letters small and cramped.

DAVIES. AUNT HOLDS ME. ATTEMPTING STARVATION. C. AT BLACKWOOD MANOR NORTH. SEND HELP. A.

It was crude, but it was clear. She pushed the small, stiff square of fabric back under the door. "Can you get this to London? To the post?"

"There are ways," Martha said, her voice full of a new, steely resolve. "The butcher’s boy still makes his rounds. He is a good lad. He will see a letter posted for a shilling." A small purse was pushed under the door. "My savings. For the postage, and more if needed."

"No, Martha, I cannot take your money."

"You must, Miss Annelise. This is not about money. This is about what is right. Your father was a good man. I will not see his daughters ruined by that viper."

The exchange was done. The small parcel of food, a treasure beyond price. The vital message, a seed of hope planted in the darkness.

"Go now," Annelise whispered. "Be careful."

"God keep you, miss," Martha whispered back, and then there was only silence.

Annelise ate the rest of the bread and cheese slowly, making it last, the flavors exploding in her mouth. She drank a little water, which now seemed less stale, less metallic. Hope was a potent spice. She had an ally. She had a plan. Her message was on its way. The game had changed once more.

She slept then, a deep and dreamless sleep, the first true rest she had had in days. When she awoke, the morning light seemed brighter. The gnawing hunger was gone, replaced by a patient resolve. She waited.

Hours passed. The mid morning quiet was broken by the sound of footsteps on the stairs, slow and deliberate. Her aunt. Annelise’s heart began to beat faster. She stood up, positioning herself in the center of the room, ready.

But there was no angry tirade, no key scraping in the lock. Instead, a piece of paper was slid silently under the door. Annelise waited until the footsteps receded before she picked it up.

It was not a note from her aunt. It was a thick, cream colored sheet of vellum, bearing the letterhead of a prestigious firm of London solicitors. Her aunt’s solicitors. The language was cold, formal, and utterly terrifying.

It was a formal notice, informing her that due to her "erratic and hysterical behavior," her "unfounded accusations," and her "inability to conduct herself in a manner befitting her station," Mrs. Eleanor Croft was petitioning the High Court to be granted full legal guardianship over the person and estate of Miss Annelise Croft. It stated that her current confinement was a "temporary measure of protective care, undertaken with a heavy heart, to prevent Miss Croft from doing harm to herself or the family name." It ended with a chilling paragraph, suggesting that her cooperation would be looked upon favorably by the court, whereas continued "defiance and fantasy" would only serve as further proof of her mental incompetence.

Annelise read the letter twice, then a third time. The bread and cheese turned to a lump of lead in her stomach. This was her aunt’s new strategy. It was brilliant. It was evil. She was not just trying to break her will; she was trying to erase her legal existence. She was using the very system of law Annelise had relied upon, twisting it into a weapon to declare her insane. The injunction, her only shield, would be meaningless if she was deemed unfit to have sought it in the first place.

She looked from the damning legal document in her hand to the grimy window, to the barred and locked door. The room was no longer just a prison. It was the staging ground for a sanity hearing, and she was the sole, captive defendant. The cage of her aunt's making, she had called it. She had been wrong. Her aunt was not the one in the cage. She was. And the bars were made not of iron, but of paper and lies, far stronger and infinitely harder to break.

The paper trembled in her hand, a slight, almost imperceptible tremor that belied the earthquake erupting within her. She sank onto the edge of the cot, the cheap mattress groaning under her weight. The vellum felt heavy, too heavy for a single sheet, as if it were weighted with the full authority of the Crown and all the gray, bewigged men who served it. A sanity hearing. It was a term from penny dreadfuls, a gothic horror visited upon inconvenient wives and troublesome daughters in stories meant to thrill and chill. It was not something that happened in the bright, modern light of London. It was not something that happened to her.

But it was. Her aunt was a creature of sublime, reptilian cunning. She had seen Annelise’s move, the seeking of an injunction, and had not merely countered it; she had vaulted over it entirely, aiming for the very foundation of Annelise’s identity. If Annelise was not sane, she could not have legally sought an injunction. She could not own property. She could not direct her own life. She would become a ghost in her own skin, a ward of the very woman who was trying to destroy her. Her signature on a document would be meaningless. Her words would be the ravings of a lunatic.

A cold sweat broke out on her brow. She thought of Tom, the boy with the soot on his cheek and the honest eyes. Her message to Mr. Abernathy, her father’s man of business, was her only lifeline. But what good would it be? Abernathy might come, might demand to see her, but her aunt would simply show him this letter. She would put on a mask of sorrowful duty. "I am so glad you are here, Mr. Abernathy. The poor girl has had a complete breakdown. We are seeking help for her. The best care, of course." And he, a man of business and propriety, would bow his head, murmur his condolences, and leave. He would not risk associating himself with a public scandal, with a family whose heiress was being declared mentally incompetent. The word of Mrs. Eleanor Croft, a respectable widow, would always outweigh the frantic protests of a young woman locked in an attic.

The hope that had warmed her just that morning now felt like a cruel joke. It had been a fool's hope, a child's faith in fairy tale rescues. The real world was not made of heroes and villains, but of solicitors and petitions, of paper and ink that could bind a person more tightly than any iron chain. She dropped the letter on the floor as if it had burned her. She stood up and began to pace, the four short steps from the door to the window, the turn, the four steps back. The room, which had seemed a temporary holding cell, now felt like the antechamber to Bedlam.

The sound of footsteps on the stairs came again. Heavier this time. Slower. Her aunt, returning to survey her handiwork. Annelise froze in the middle of the room, her back to the door. She would not give her the satisfaction of seeing her cowering. She would not let her see the terror. She forced her hands to unclench at her sides. She regulated her breathing, pulling the air in slowly, letting it out even more slowly. The accusation was "erratic and hysterical behavior." Very well. She would be a statue of placid reason.

The key turned in the lock with a loud, grating sound. The door swung inward. Her aunt stood on the threshold, dressed in a severe but elegant dress of dark grey silk. Her face was a mask of compassionate concern, an expression so false it was almost beautiful in its artistry.

"Annelise, my dear," she said, her voice dripping with counterfeit sympathy. "I know this must be a shock. Believe me, this was the hardest decision of my life."

Annelise did not turn around. She continued to stare at the grimy windowpane, at the slice of indifferent blue sky beyond the bars. "Was it?" she asked, her own voice startlingly calm and clear. "Harder than stealing my inheritance? Harder than locking me in this room? You seem to have a great capacity for difficult decisions, Aunt."

Eleanor stepped into the room, leaving the door ajar. A calculated move, Annelise knew. It suggested she was not a prisoner, that she could leave if she were only reasonable. "You see? This is what I mean," Eleanor said, her voice taking on a tone of weary sadness, as if speaking to an unseen audience. "These wild accusations. This fixation on money. Your father’s death has unseated your mind, child. It is a grief too great for your delicate constitution."

Now Annelise turned, slowly, deliberately. She looked her aunt directly in the eye. There was no fire in her gaze, only a cool, assessing light. "My constitution is quite robust, Aunt. It is my patience that has grown thin. You have the letterhead of a prestigious firm of solicitors. Do they know that their client has resorted to illegal imprisonment? Do they know that your petition is based entirely on falsehoods you have manufactured to conceal your own criminal actions?"

A flicker of irritation crossed Eleanor's face before being smoothed away. "Illegal imprisonment? Annelise, this is protective care. The letter explains everything. I am trying to save you from yourself. To save our family from the embarrassment of your public outbursts."

"The only outburst I have made was in the office of my solicitor, when I filed an injunction to stop you from selling my father's assets less than a month after his funeral," Annelise stated, each word precise and unhurried. "An action I took on the sound advice of Mr. Abernathy. Is he also suffering from a delicate constitution? Or is it that anyone who stands between you and my father's money is suddenly declared hysterical?"

Her aunt’s smile became a thin, tight line. The mask was slipping. "You will be seen by a doctor, Annelise. A very respected man. He will assess your condition. If you are calm, if you are cooperative, this whole unpleasantness can be managed discreetly. A quiet stay in the country, perhaps. For your health."

A sanatorium. A private asylum. The final nail in the coffin of her freedom. Annelise felt a wave of nausea, but her expression remained unchanged. "And if I am not cooperative? If I tell this doctor the truth?"

"Then you will only prove my case," Eleanor said, her voice now losing its syrupy coating and taking on a hard, metallic edge. "You will be a poor, mad thing, shouting about conspiracies and stolen fortunes. Who do you think he will believe? A respectable, grieving woman trying to care for her unfortunate niece, or the niece herself, locked away for her own safety? The choice, my dear, is entirely yours." She gave Annelise one last, lingering look of triumph, then turned and walked out, pulling the door shut. The key turned, the bolt slid home. The lock was no longer just a sound; it was a verdict.

Annelise stood for a long time, listening to her aunt's footsteps fade away. The choice. It was a liar's choice, a devil's bargain. Cooperate and be quietly erased, or fight and be publicly condemned. But her aunt had made one crucial mistake. She had told her the plan. She had warned her that a doctor was coming. And in doing so, she had given Annelise the one thing she needed: a stage.

The fight was not for Mr. Abernathy to win. It was not for Tom to win. It was for her, Annelise Croft, to win, right here in this dusty, sun-starved room. She was not the defendant in a sanity hearing. She was the chief witness for her own prosecution of her aunt. She had to be flawless.

She went to the small, cracked mirror that hung by the washstand. Her hair was a mess, tangled and limp. Her dress was creased. She looked wild. She looked exactly as her aunt wanted her to look. Methodically, she began to repair the damage. She poured a little water into the basin and washed her face and hands. She used her fingers as a comb, patiently working through the knots in her hair until it fell in some semblance of order over her shoulders. She smoothed her dress as best she could, pulling the fabric taut, brushing away specks of dust. She could not change her clothes, but she could change her presentation. She was no longer a frantic prisoner. She was Miss Annelise Croft, waiting to receive a visitor.

She sat on the single rickety chair, placing it in the middle of the room, away from the barred window and the locked door. She did not want to look like a captive animal. She folded her hands in her lap. And she waited.

It was several hours later, as the afternoon sun began to cast long shadows across the floor, that she heard them. Two sets of footsteps this time. Her aunt’s, and a man’s, heavier and more measured. Her heart hammered against her ribs, a frantic bird beating its wings against a cage of bone. She forced it into submission, taking a deep, slow breath. Calm. Composed. Rational.

The key turned. The door opened. Her aunt entered first, her face once again a study in sorrowful concern. Behind her was a man in his late fifties, tall and silver haired, with spectacles perched on a narrow nose. He carried a leather Gladstone bag and looked around the sparse room with an air of professional detachment.

"Annelise, dear," her aunt began. "This is Doctor Finch. He has come to have a little chat with you."

Dr. Finch stepped forward, giving a slight, formal bow. "Miss Croft. A pleasure." His eyes, magnified by the spectacles, were sharp and observant. They took in everything: her posture, her smoothed hair, her folded hands, the way she sat calmly in the center of the room. He seemed momentarily surprised. He had likely been expecting a disheveled creature, pacing and weeping.

"Doctor," Annelise said, her voice even. She inclined her head but did not stand. She was the lady of the house, receiving a guest, not an inmate being inspected. "Thank you for coming. I must confess, the circumstances are somewhat unusual."

"Indeed," the doctor said, pulling the room's other chair, the one from by the cot, and placing it opposite her. Her aunt remained standing by the door, a self appointed chaperone. "Your aunt has expressed some... grave concerns about your well being."

"My aunt is a woman of many concerns," Annelise replied smoothly. "Most of them financial."

"Annelise, please," Eleanor sighed, shaking her head sadly for the doctor's benefit. "You see, Doctor? This obsession."

Dr. Finch held up a hand, silencing Eleanor without looking at her. His focus was entirely on Annelise. "Miss Croft, your aunt informs me that you have been suffering from delusions. That you believe she is trying to steal your inheritance and that you have been confined here unjustly."

"Doctor," Annelise said, leaning forward slightly, her expression one of earnest reason. "Let us speak of facts, not delusions. Fact: my father, Mr. Richard Croft, passed away four weeks ago. Fact: his will names me as his sole heir, with my aunt as executrix, a role which grants her administrative power but no claim to the estate itself. Fact: within three weeks of my father's death, my aunt began taking steps to liquidate assets belonging to the estate, actions for which she had no authority and which were against my express wishes. Is this a delusion?"

The doctor looked at Eleanor. "Is this correct, Mrs. Croft?"

"It is a gross misinterpretation," Eleanor said quickly. "I was merely consolidating things, for ease of management. The girl does not understand business."

Annelise continued, her gaze fixed on Dr. Finch. "Fact: upon discovering this, I consulted with my father’s long time man of business, Mr. Abernathy, who advised me to seek a legal injunction to prevent my aunt from taking any further action. I did so. The injunction was served to my aunt two days ago. Fact: a few hours after receiving that legal document, my aunt lured me to this room under a false pretext and locked me in. She has held me here since. Is that, in your professional opinion, the action of a concerned guardian, or of someone whose financial ambitions have just been legally thwarted?"

She spoke without hysteria, without anger. Her words were a solicitor’s argument, not a madwoman’s rant. Dr. Finch’s impassive expression did not change, but he steepled his fingers, a thoughtful gesture.

"And this... injunction," he said slowly. "You have a copy of it?"

"I do not," Annelise said. "It is in my personal papers, in my rooms downstairs. Rooms which I am, of course, unable to access. However, a copy can be easily obtained from the High Court, or from my solicitor, Mr. Davies of Lincoln's Inn Fields. I can provide you with his full address."

"Fantasies," Eleanor whispered from the doorway. "She reads too many novels. She has invented all of this."

Dr. Finch ignored her. "Your aunt has also petitioned the court, Miss Croft. She seeks guardianship, on the grounds of your mental instability." He watched her closely, searching for a reaction, for the crack in her composure.

Annelise simply nodded. "Yes. She had a copy of the petition delivered to me this morning. It is a brilliant strategy, you must admit. If I am declared legally incompetent, the injunction I filed becomes invalid. Her 'guardianship' would give her absolute control over the very estate she was trying to appropriate. It is a far more effective way of getting what she wants than simply selling off a few paintings behind my back. It is a move that is as logical as it is monstrous."

There was a long silence in the room. The only sound was the distant clatter of a carriage on the street below. Dr. Finch stared at Annelise, his head tilted. He was a man trained to find the flaws in a narrative, to distinguish between the rational and the irrational. Annelise had not presented him with a frantic, emotional tale of woe. She had presented him with a sequence of events, a chain of cause and effect, grounded in legal and financial motive. It was a story that, however shocking, made a terrifying kind of sense. The delusion her aunt described was chaotic and emotional; Annelise's version was structured and logical.

"Doctor," Annelise said, her voice softening slightly. "I am not mad. I am trapped. My aunt has taken my freedom, and now she is using the law itself to take my identity. She is counting on the fact that a man of your standing will believe a woman of her class over a girl she has locked in an attic. She is counting on you to see what she wants you to see. I am asking you, as a man of science and reason, to see what is actually here."

Dr. Finch slowly took off his spectacles and began to polish them with a handkerchief from his pocket. It was a delaying tactic, a way to break the intensity of her gaze. "Your aunt claims you have been erratic. Hysterical."

"And when was I supposedly hysterical?" Annelise countered. "Was it before or after she locked me in this room? Is it not a natural human response to be distressed when one is unjustly imprisoned? The 'symptoms' she describes are not the cause of my confinement, Doctor. They are the result of it. And her petition is not a remedy. It is the weapon."

He put his spectacles back on. He looked from Annelise’s calm, pale face to Eleanor’s, which was now tight with poorly concealed fury. The tableau was telling. The 'hysterical' patient was serene; the 'concerned' guardian was vibrating with rage.

"Mrs. Croft," the doctor said, turning his head. "You said you found your niece in a distressed state. Was this before or after you were served with this injunction she speaks of?"

"I... I don't recall the exact timing of legal papers," Eleanor stammered. "My concern for her has been growing for weeks. She has been withdrawn, melancholic..."

"But the decision to confine her to this room, and to seek a professional opinion," Dr. Finch pressed, his voice neutral but insistent, "came after you were legally barred from managing her estate?"

Eleanor’s face flushed a deep, unbecoming red. "The two events are unrelated. The injunction was the final straw. It was proof of her paranoia, of how far gone she was."

Dr. Finch stood up. "I see." He turned back to Annelise. "Miss Croft, you present a... compelling narrative. However, these are serious allegations. The situation requires careful consideration."

"Then consider it carefully, Doctor," Annelise said, remaining seated. "And quickly. I do not know what other 'care' my aunt has planned for me."

He gave a curt nod. "I will be in touch." He picked up his bag and walked to the door, pausing to let Eleanor precede him. As she passed Annelise, her aunt shot her a look of pure venom, a silent promise of retribution.

Then they were gone. The key turned, the bolt shot home. Annelise was alone again in the gathering dusk. She did not move from the chair. The performance had taken every ounce of strength she possessed. Her body felt boneless, her mind scoured clean. She had not won. Not yet. But she had planted a seed of doubt. She had turned her aunt’s weapon back on her, forcing her to answer for the convenient timing of her 'concern'.

She had bought herself time. A day, perhaps. Maybe more. She finally allowed her body to tremble, a deep, racking shudder of delayed shock and immense strain. She had faced the monster and held her ground. She looked towards the window, where the last of the light was fading. The game was not over. But now, at least, her opponent knew she was playing against an equal. And somewhere out in the darkening city, a boy named Tom was still carrying her hope. The wait began again, but it was a different kind of waiting now. It was not the passive wait of a victim, but the patient, watchful waiting of a queen in a chess game, anticipating her opponent’s next, desperate move.
