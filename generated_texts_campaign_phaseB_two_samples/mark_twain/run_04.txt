The heat was so thick you could have sliced it with a knife and served it for supper. Not much was stirring in our town, which was mostly just one long, muddy street that had a powerful ambition to be a swamp. The only real excitement we got was when the Queen of the West, which was a steamboat that was more rust than paint, would come wheezing up to the landing. She came that afternoon, and everybody that wasn’t nailed down went to see what she’d brung.

Mostly it was just barrels and bolts of cloth, but then a feller steps off the gangplank like he owned the whole river. He had on a pearl-gray suit and a hat to match, and his shoes shined so bright I reckoned he could see his own face in them. He wasn’t a young man but he moved spry, and he had a smile that looked like it had been practicing in a mirror for forty year. He carried a little folding table under his arm.

“Well now,” one of the loafers on a barrel says, “look what the cattywampus dragged in. He’s either a preacher or a politician.”

Another one spit a long stream of tobacco juice. “Naw, he ain’t got the right kind of sweat for a preacher. I say he’s sellin’ hair tonic.”

The feller in the gray suit paid them no mind. He just set up his little table right there on the dock, pulled out three walnut shells and a little dried pea, and cleared his throat. “Gather ‘round, gentlemen, gather ‘round! A quick eye and a quicker hand is all it takes to turn a pauper into a prince. The eye is faster than the hand, or is it the other way around? A dollar to win five, who’s the sportsman?”

Well, you never seen a crowd form up so fast. He was slick, that feller was. He’d talk and his hands would fly, and the little pea was everywhere and nowhere all at once. He took a dollar off Jim Riley, and then two off a farmhand that was in town for supplies. He’d let a feller win a little, just enough to get his blood up, and then he’d clean him out proper, all the while smiling and chattering like a magpie. I seen him do it three, four times.

Then a man come pushing through the crowd. This was Mr. Blackwood, who owned the sawmill and just about everything else. He was a hard man, and quiet, and he didn’t have a bit of smile in him. He was dressed plain, in work clothes, but everybody made way for him. He stood there for a minute, just watching the feller with the shells.

Mr. Blackwood says, real low, “That’s enough of that.”

The feller in gray looked up and his smile got even wider. “Sir, this is but a friendly game of chance, a diversion for these good people.”

“One of those ‘good people’ works for me,” Mr. Blackwood says, “and that was his week’s wages you just put in your pocket. I want you off this dock. I want you out of this town by the time the six o’clock mill whistle blows.” He didn’t raise his voice, but it was like hearing a rock fall down a deep well.

The gambler, he just laughed. “My good man, I am a free citizen conducting a legal enterprise. I’ll leave when my business is concluded.”

Mr. Blackwood looked at him for a long time. His eyes was like little gray stones. “Six o’clock,” he says again, and then he turned around and walked away, the crowd parting for him like he was Moses and they was the Red Sea.

The crowd got real quiet, but the gambler, he just shook his head and laughed again, loud. “Hard customers in this town, I see! Who’s next, boys? The stakes are higher and the rewards are sweeter!”

But it warn’t the same after that. The fun was gone out of it. People was nervous, and they kept looking over their shoulders towards the sawmill. Some fellers tried to tell him. One says, “Mister, you don’t know Blackwood. If he says six o’clock, you’d best be five miles away at five fifty-five.” The gambler just waved his hand and told them a faint heart never won a fair lady. He kept at it, but he warn’t getting much business.

I slid down to the end of the dock and watched the sun get lower. It was awful quiet, except for the gambler still trying to drum up a game and the river slapping against the pilings. Then I heard it, a long, mournful sound from up the hill. Whoooo-eeeeeee. The six o’clock whistle.

The gambler, he froze with his hands over the shells. He looked up, and his smile was all gone. The whole crowd went dead still.

Down the street come Mr. Blackwood, walking steady. He wasn’t hurrying. He had something in his hand, but I couldn’t see what it was. It was long and dark. The gambler started to fold up his table right quick. He says, “Well, gentlemen, it appears my time here is at an end,” and he tried to make it sound like a joke, but his voice was shaky.

Mr. Blackwood stopped about ten feet away. I seen then he was carrying a peavey, which is a big heavy hook on a stick they use for rolling logs. He didn't say a word.

The gambler held up his hands. “Now, hold on. There’s no need for any trouble here. A misunderstanding, that’s all.” He started to back away towards the steamboat.

Mr. Blackwood took two steps forward and swung the peavey, not fast, but with a kind of awful purpose. It warn’t a wild swing; it was short and flat, like he was swatting a horsefly. There was a sound like a melon breaking, a wet, heavy thud. The gambler’s gray hat flew off his head and he just folded up in the middle and fell down on the dock planks. He didn’t make a sound.

Mr. Blackwood looked down at him for a second, then he let the peavey drop. It clattered on the wood. He turned and walked back up the street, not looking at nobody.

For a minute, nobody moved. Then everybody surged forward, just like they did with Boggs, all pushing and shoving to get a look. I was small, so I squirmed my way up to the front. The gambler was laying on his face, and there was a dark puddle spreading out from under his head, mixing with the river water that was splashed on the dock. One of his shiny shoes had come off. Somebody rolled him over, and his face was a mess.

One of the men that had lost money to him says, “Well, I reckon he ain’t a-gwyne to be leavin’ town after all.” Another feller nodded and says, “Blackwood always did pay his debts.” And then a young feller, he picked up the peavey and showed the others how Blackwood done it, making a short little swing in the air. “Just like that,” he says, proud, like he’d figured out a real puzzle. And two or three people that seen it nodded and said he done it perfect. Nobody said nothing about lynching nobody. They was all too scared of Mr. Blackwood for that.

I got shoved around some more, and another feller took the peavey and tried it, but he swung it too wide and the first feller told him he was doing it all wrong, that Blackwood was economical with it. They started to argue about the proper way a man would kill another man with a log hook, which seemed a particular kind of foolish to me. Pretty soon a man with a tin star pinned to his vest come waddling down the street. He was a big, round man named Beadle, and he was the town constable, though mostly what he done was roust drunks out of the alley behind the saloon and tell us boys to stop swimming naked where the church ladies might see.

He pushed his way through the crowd, puffing. “Alright, alright, break it up now,” he says, like he was talking about a dog fight. He looked down at the gambler, and his face got a little green around the gills. He took his hat off and wiped his forehead with his sleeve, even though it wasn’t that hot. “Sakes alive,” he mumbled. He looked around at the faces, but he didn’t look up the hill where Mr. Blackwood had gone. Nobody did. It was like there was a secret rule that you didn’t look in that direction.

“Who seen it?” Mr. Beadle asked, but his voice was small and didn’t have no authority in it.

A dozen men spoke up at once. “I did.” “Seen the whole thing.” “He deserved it, Constable.”

Mr. Beadle held up his hands. “One at a time.” He pointed at a man who run the livery stable. “You, Silas. What happened?”

Silas, he puffed out his chest. “Well, Constable, this here feller,” he says, pointing at the body with his thumb, “was running a crooked game. A rank cheat, plain and simple. Fleecing good folks.”

“He fleeced me out of two dollars,” another man chimed in, real angry.

“Took my last four bits,” says a third.

Mr. Beadle nodded slow. “I see. A crooked game.” He looked down at the gambler again. He still hadn't asked who done the killing. He knew, and he knew that everybody else knew, and he knew that asking would be the stupidest thing he could do. If he asked, he might have to do something about it, and there wasn’t a man in that town, and probably not in the whole county, who wanted to be the one to do something about Mr. Blackwood.

Up on the steamboat, the captain leaned over the railing. He’d been watching the whole thing. “Constable Beadle,” he hollered down, his voice echoing over the water. “Are we free to cast off? I have a schedule to maintain.”

Mr. Beadle looked up, relieved to have something else to think about. “Hold on there, Captain. This is a, uh, a crime scene.” He said the words like he’d read them in a book and was trying them out.

The captain snorted. “It’s a dead cheat on your dock, Beadle. It ain’t my cargo. I’m pushing off in five minutes, with or without your say so.” He was a river man, and he didn’t have no patience for town business.

This put Mr. Beadle in a fix. He looked at the body, then at the steamboat, then at the crowd. He was sweating for real now. Finally, he turned to two big fellers that worked at the lumber mill, the same ones that loaded the cordwood onto the boats. “You boys. Get him on the cart,” he says, pointing to a dusty handcart that was sitting by the warehouse.

So they did. They rolled the gambler over onto his stomach and each grabbed an arm and a leg. It was a clumsy business. His other shoe fell off, and the shells from his little table were scattered all over, shining in the last of the sun. They flopped him onto the cart like a sack of meal. His head hung down one side, still leaking, and one arm dangled and bumped along the planks as they wheeled him away. Mr. Beadle walked behind them, looking important, like he was leading a parade.

The crowd broke up then. The show was over. The steamboat let out a great blast from its whistle, a short, angry sound this time, not like the six o’clock whistle. The big paddlewheel started to churn, making the water muddy and white, and it pulled away from the dock, heading downriver into the falling dark. I watched its lights get smaller and smaller until they was just a couple of fireflies, and then they was gone.

The dock was mostly empty now. All that was left of the gambler was a dark, wet stain on the wood, his lonely gray hat, and one of his shiny shoes. The peavey was gone. I reckoned that young feller who’d been so proud of figuring out the swing had took it home as a souvenir. I felt a cold knot in my stomach that wasn’t just hunger. It was one thing to see a man shot in a drunken rage like Boggs was. That was hot and loud and stupid. This was something else. This was quiet and cold and sure, like the turning of the seasons or the rising of the river. It was a thing that had to happen, and so it happened. And the whole town just nodded and went on with its business.

I couldn’t stand to be on that dock no more. I slipped away into the alleys as the last of the light faded out of the sky. I crept along, keeping to the shadows. The town was quieter than usual. Lamplight shone out of the windows of the houses, warm and yellow, but there weren’t many people out on the streets. It was like a lid had been put on the town, pressing all the noise down.

I was hungry, awful hungry. I hadn’t eaten since morning, and seeing a man killed is a thing that will scour you out inside. I thought about trying to snitch a pie cooling on a windowsill, but I didn’t have the heart for it. All my nerve had leaked out of me. I ended up down by the cooper’s shop, where there was a lean to full of new barrels that hadn’t been sold yet. They smelled clean and woody, of fresh-cut oak. I found one that was big enough, and I tipped it on its side and crawled in. It was a tight fit, but it was out of the wind, and nobody would think to look for a boy in a barrel.

Lying there in the dark, I couldn't stop seeing it. The swing of that peavey. The sound it made. It wasn't like the crack of a gun; it was softer, more personal. It was the sound of a thing being broken, not just killed. I thought about the gambler. He had a smooth, easy way of talking, and his hands moved so fast with them shells you couldn’t hardly follow. He was a cheat, for sure. But did he know he was going to die for it? When he saw Mr. Blackwood coming, did he think he could talk his way out of it, or did he know his time was up right then? His face when he looked up from his table, that was the thing. All the fun and trickery was gone from it, and there was just this naked, holy terror.

And Mr. Blackwood. I'd seen him around town before. He didn't talk much. He owned the big sawmill up on the hill, and half the timber land for twenty miles around. He was a rich man, but he didn't act like it. He dressed in plain, dark clothes, and he walked like a man who was used to the ground being solid under his feet. He had eyes that was the color of a winter sky, and they didn't seem to blink much. The men who worked for him was scared of him, but they respected him too. They said he was a hard man, but fair. He paid a man what he was worth and didn’t suffer fools. Now I knew what else they meant. He didn’t suffer cheats, neither.

I must have dozed off, because the next thing I knew the moon was up, a big silver dollar in the sky, and it was shining right into the end of my barrel. The town was dead quiet now, except for a dog barking way off somewhere and the chirping of the crickets. My stomach was growling something fierce. I decided I’d risk a trip to the back of the hotel kitchen. Sometimes they left scraps out for the pigs.

I crawled out of my barrel, stiff and sore, and started back towards the main street, sticking to the dark spots. The saloon was still open; a little bit of yellow light and tinny piano music was spilling out, but it sounded tired. As I got closer, I saw a figure sitting on the bench outside the general store, which was all dark. It was a man, just a black shape in the moonlight. I froze behind a rain barrel, my heart thumping.

Then I saw the shape of his hat, and the square set of his shoulders. It was Mr. Blackwood. He was just sitting there, all by himself in the dark, looking out at the empty street. He wasn't smoking. He wasn't drinking from a bottle. He was just sitting, still as a stone statue. He had his hands resting on his knees. The same hands that had swung the peavey. I wondered what he was thinking about. Was he sorry? Was he satisfied? Or did he not feel nothing at all? Looking at him, I figured it was the last one. He’d done a thing that needed doing, like fixing a leaky roof or putting down a sick animal, and now he was just resting from his work.

A shiver went down my spine that had nothing to do with the night air. I was more scared of him sitting there silent than I was when he was swinging that hook. The quiet of him was bigger and more dangerous than any noise. I backed away, slow and careful, not making a sound, and I gave up on the hotel scraps. I went back to my barrel and curled up small, and I didn’t come out again till the sun was well up.

The next morning, the town acted like nothing had happened. The blacksmith was hammering, the wagons was rattling down the street, and the ladies were going to the store with their baskets. But it was different. You could feel it. People didn’t laugh as loud. They walked a little quicker past the dock. When they talked to each other, they kept their voices low. The killing had sunk down into the town, like a stone in a well, and the ripples was still spreading out.

I was wild with curiosity to know the reason for it all. I mean, I knew the gambler was a cheat, but men cheated at cards and shells all up and down the river, and mostly all they got was a black eye or run out of town on a rail. They didn't get their head broke open with a peavey. There had to be more to it.

I went up to the general store and made myself busy rearranging a display of hoes and rakes near the front door, trying to look like a boy hoping for a job. The men were all gathered around the cracker barrel, talking, just like they did every morning.

“Heard the constable buried him out in the potter’s field this morning,” one feller said. He was chewing on a piece of licorice. “No name, no marker.”

“Don’t need a marker,” another one grunted. “His kind is common enough.”

Then a man with flour on his overalls, the baker I reckoned, lowered his voice. “My wife’s sister works for the Blackwoods, cleaning the house. She says it was about the boy.”

Everybody leaned in closer. I stopped pretending to work on the rakes and tried to look like part of the wall.

“Thomas?” the first man asked.

“That’s the one,” said the baker. “You know how he is. Simple. Not right in the head since the fever took him as a baby. Gentle as a lamb, though. Wouldn’t hurt a fly.”

I’d seen the Blackwood boy a few times. He was maybe sixteen or seventeen, with a pale, sweet face and eyes that always seemed to be looking at something far away. The other boys sometimes made fun of him, but they stopped if any of the men who worked at the mill were around.

“Well,” the baker went on, “that gambler, he saw Thomas yesterday afternoon. The boy was just walking, jingling that silver dollar in his pocket. The one his mother gave him before she passed. He’s had it for ten years. Never spends it, just carries it for luck.”

A sick feeling started in my gut. I knew where this was going.

“That slick devil, he stops young Thomas, shows him the shells. Says he can double that dollar. The boy, he don’t know no better. He’s fascinated. The gambler lets him win once, a little penny wager. Then he coaxes him to bet the dollar. And poof.” The baker snapped his fingers. “It’s gone. The boy starts to cry, asking for his dollar back, his mama’s dollar. And the gambler just laughs at him. Laughs and tells him to run along home to his daddy.”

The men around the cracker barrel were silent. They stared down at the floorboards, their faces hard.

“Somebody seen it and went and told Mr. Blackwood at the mill,” the baker finished, his voice barely a whisper. “The whistle hadn't even blown yet. He just took off his apron, picked up a peavey from the rack, and walked down the hill. Never said a word.”

So that was it. It wasn't about the two dollars or the four bits the other men had lost. It was about a simple-minded boy and his mother's silver dollar. It was about a laugh. Mr. Blackwood hadn't been paying a debt; he'd been answering an insult so deep it couldn’t be washed away with nothing but blood. The town understood that. An eye for an eye was one thing, but this was different. This was a man protecting something that couldn't protect itself.

I slipped out of the store. I didn’t feel hungry no more. I felt like I had a stone in my belly. I walked through the town, but I wasn't seeing it. I was seeing Mr. Blackwood’s face, not angry, but set and calm. And I was seeing the gambler's face, smiling his slick smile as he took a silver dollar from a confused boy.

I don’t know why I did it. It was a fool thing to do. But I started walking up the hill, towards the Blackwood house. It was the biggest house in town, painted a stark white, with dark green shutters. It sat on the hill like a judge, looking down on everybody else. There was a neat picket fence around it, and the yard was perfect. Not a blade of grass out of place.

I didn't go to the front. I crept around the side, where a big vegetable garden was growing. I hid behind a row of corn and just watched the house. It was quiet. It looked like nobody was home. Then, I saw a movement in an upstairs window. A curtain was pulled back, and a face looked out. It was Thomas. He was just looking out at the sky, his face pale and empty. He wasn’t crying. He didn’t look happy or sad. He just looked like himself, a boy who lived in a world I couldn't understand.

I watched for a long time. Then I heard a sound behind me, a latch clicking. The back door of the house opened. My blood went cold and turned to ice water. I scrunched down behind the corn, praying he wouldn't see me. Mr. Blackwood stepped out onto the back porch. He had a small wooden bucket in his hand. He walked over to a water pump in the yard, not twenty feet from where I was hiding.

I held my breath. The corn stalks suddenly felt about as wide as a piece of string. He worked the pump handle, his arm moving with a steady, powerful rhythm. The water splashed into the bucket. He didn't look around. He didn't seem to be thinking about anything but the water. But I felt like his eyes were on me, even with his back turned. I felt like he knew the exact location of every bird in the trees, every bug in the dirt, and every scared boy hiding in his corn patch.

He filled the bucket, and for a long second, he just stood there, holding it. The sun glinted off the water. Then, slow, he turned his head and looked right at the place where I was hiding. He couldn't see me, not really, through all the leaves. But his eyes, those cold winter eyes, they found me. We looked at each other for maybe three seconds, him out in the bright sun and me in the green shadows. He didn’t frown. His face didn’t change at all. He just looked. It was a look that said, I see you. I know you’re there. And it doesn’t matter. You don't matter.

Then he turned away, picked up the bucket of water, and walked back into the house, closing the door softly behind him.

I stayed crouched there until my legs was screaming with cramps. Then I scrambled out of that garden and I ran. I didn't run like a boy playing a game. I ran like a rabbit with the hounds on its tail. I didn't stop until I was at the river's edge, my lungs burning, the sound of the water slapping the bank filling my ears.

I looked back at the town, sitting there neat and tidy on the hill. It looked peaceful. But I knew it wasn't. It had a cold, dark spot in the middle of it, a spot shaped like a man with a peavey. And I knew I couldn’t stay there no more. Some places are just too quiet.

Down the bank, a family of shanty-boaters was getting ready to push off. Their raft was a clumsy thing made of logs and rough planks, with a little tent on it. They wasn't much, but they was moving. I watched them for a minute. Then I saw a loose skiff tied to a willow tree a little ways down, half full of rainwater. It didn't look like it belonged to nobody in particular. I waded out, untied the rope, and bailed it out with my hands. It was a leaky, sorry-looking boat. But it would float.

I pushed off into the current. The river grabbed me and started to pull me away. I didn’t look back again. I just kept my eyes on the water ahead, a big, muddy road that went on forever, away from towns and away from men who paid their debts in such a terrible, final way.

The current was a hungry thing. It pulled the skiff along like a dead leaf, turning it slow in the brown water. For the first hour, I did nothing but drift. The town on the hill got smaller and smaller until it was just a smudge, a dark spot against the blueing twilight sky. Then the river bent and it was gone. I was alone. The only sounds was the chuckle of water against the hull and the far off call of a night bird. My arms was sore from bailing but the work wasn't done. Water seeped in through a dozen seams in the old wood. I had to scoop and throw, scoop and throw, just to keep the river on the outside of the boat.

Night fell proper, black and thick. The moon was a sliver, not much help at all. Stars came out, more than I’d ever seen from town, and they looked cold and a long way off. I kept bailing. My stomach was a tight, angry knot. I hadn't eaten since noon and then it was only a biscuit. I thought about the man. I saw his face in the dark water, those eyes like chips of ice. I saw the peavey, dark with something that wasn't river mud. I saw my Pa’s face for a second, not how he was at the end, but how he was in the morning, telling a joke with his mouth full of coffee. The memory was a sharp pain, like touching a hot stove. I pushed it away. Thinking about it wouldn't help. Bailing would help. So I bailed.

I must have fallen asleep sometime deep in the night, slumped over with my hand in the gritty water at the bottom of the boat. A noise woke me. It was a scraping sound. I shot up, my heart trying to beat its way out of my chest. The skiff had drifted into the shallows and was grinding against a gravel bar. The sky was gray, not black anymore. Dawn was coming. I was shivering, my clothes soaked through with river water and dew. I looked around. The world was nothing but water and trees. The river was wider here, a great big sheet of moving mud. My fear from the night before had cooled into something hard and heavy in my gut. It was hunger.

I pushed the skiff off the gravel and let the current take me again. The sun came up, a hot penny in the sky, and burned the mist off the water. It did little to warm me. I had to find something to eat. I watched the banks, looking for a town, a farm, anything. But there was nothing. Just miles of willow and cottonwood trees, their roots tangled up at the water's edge. I saw a fish jump, a flash of silver, and my stomach growled so loud it scared a heron off a log. I had no hook, no line. I had nothing but a leaky boat and the clothes on my back.

I thought I might die out there. The thought didn't scare me as much as it should have. It was a quiet, plain thought. It felt almost peaceful. Maybe I’d just float down the river until the boat sank or I starved. Maybe I’d wash up on a sandbar and the crows would find me. It would be an ending. A quiet one. Better than the ending my Pa got.

Late in the afternoon, when the sun was starting to slant low, I saw smoke. It was a thin gray ribbon curling up from the trees on the west bank, a few miles downriver. Smoke meant a fire. A fire meant a person. My first feeling was fear. My hand tightened on the side of the skiff. It could be anybody. It could be somebody who’d ask questions. It could be somebody who’d take my boat, though it wasn't much to take. But the hunger was a bigger fear now. It was a wolf gnawing on my insides. I started paddling toward the smoke, using my hands to dig into the water. It was slow, hard work. The current tried to keep me in the middle of the river, but I fought it. I had to.

It took me near an hour to get to the bank. I pulled the skiff up into a little cove choked with cattails, hiding it as best I could. I crept through the trees, quiet as I knew how. The smell of woodsmoke got stronger, and underneath it, the smell of cooking fish. My mouth filled with water. I parted a curtain of willow branches and looked.

There was a small clearing next to the river. A man was sitting on a log by a fire. He was old, with a face as wrinkled and brown as a walnut. A dirty white beard covered most of it. He had on a pair of bib overalls that was more patches than original cloth. He was tending to a skillet over the fire. Next to him was a rough-looking shack made of driftwood and tar paper. A trotline with a dozen hooks was stretched out to dry on a rack. He looked like he’d grown out of the riverbank, a part of the mud and the trees. He wasn’t looking my way. He was focused on the fish, humming a tune I didn't know. It was a low, sad sound.

I watched him for a long time. He didn't seem like the man from town. He didn't have that cold stillness. This man was all slow, easy movements. Still, I was scared. But the smell of that fish was making me dizzy. I took a breath and stepped out of the trees.

The old man didn't jump. He didn't even turn his head fast. He just stopped humming and slowly looked over at me. His eyes wasn't cold. They was the color of the river, murky and brown, but they was tired, not mean.

“Lost?” he said. His voice was scratchy, like gravel.

I shook my head. I didn't trust my own voice to work.

“Hungry, then,” he said. It wasn’t a question. He squinted at me, taking in my wet clothes, my skinny frame. “You ain’t a ghost, are you? I see ‘em sometimes on the water at night.”

“No sir,” I managed to say. It came out as a whisper.

He nodded, slow. “Well, ghosts don’t get hungry. Sit.” He pointed with his chin at the other end of the log. I hesitated. “Fish ain’t gonna get no better for you standin’ there gawkin’.”

I sat down. The heat from the fire felt so good it made me want to cry. He used a stick to push a piece of fried catfish onto a tin plate and handed it to me. There was a chunk of cornbread next to it. I took it. My hand was shaking. I didn't wait for him to say anything else. I ate like a starving dog, stuffing the hot fish in my mouth with my fingers, not even caring that I was burning my tongue. It was the best thing I had ever tasted in my life. I ate the whole thing in about a minute. When I was done, I looked at the empty plate, then at him. I was still hungry.

He was watching me, his face showing nothing. “There’s more,” he said, and pushed another piece of fish onto my plate.

I ate the second piece slower. When I was done, the knot in my stomach had loosened. I could breathe again. We sat in silence for a while, just the crackle of the fire between us.

“You runnin’ from somethin’,” he said finally, looking into the flames.

I tensed up. “No sir.”

“Boy,” he said, and he looked at me again. His eyes seemed to see right through me, but it wasn't like the man in town. It wasn't a look that said I didn’t matter. It was a look that said he’d seen it all before. “Everybody on this river is runnin’ from somethin’. Or to somethin’. Most times, it’s both. You got a name?”

I thought about telling him my real name. But it felt like it belonged back in that town, in that corn patch. It felt dangerous. “Don’t have one,” I said.

He grunted, a little sound in his throat. He looked over at my skiff, which he must have seen me hide. “That little boat yours?”

“Found it,” I said, which was half true.

“Found it,” he repeated, and a corner of his mouth twitched. “They leak, them ‘found’ boats. Always do.” He stood up, groaning as his old joints popped. “Gonna need to patch it if you mean to get much further. River’s gonna swallow you whole otherwise.” He pointed at the woodpile. “Bring some of that over. Night’s gettin’ cold.”

I stayed with him for three days. He never asked my name again. He called me Skiff, and the name stuck. He never asked where I came from or what I was running from. I think he knew it was bad, and he knew that some things is better left unsaid. His name was Silas. He’d been on the river for forty years, he said. He’d been born in a house, but the river had claimed him.

He taught me things. He showed me how to heat pine tar in a can and dribble it into the seams of my skiff to seal the leaks. We tipped it over on the bank and worked all one afternoon, the hot smell of tar filling the air. He showed me how to bait a trotline with bits of cut perch and where to set it across the mouth of a slough to catch catfish. He taught me how to read the water, to see the subtle signs of a hidden snag or a deep channel. “The river talks,” he said, pointing at a V shaped ripple. “You just gotta learn to listen. It’s tellin’ you there’s a log under there, waitin’ to rip the guts out of your boat.”

In the evenings, we’d eat fish and cornbread by the fire and he’d tell stories. He told me about the great flood of '27, when the water rose so high it licked the top of his shack and he had to live in a tree for a week. He told me about giant alligator gar that could bite a man's leg off, and about the packet boats with their gamblers and fancy ladies. He spoke of the river like it was a person, a mean and generous old woman who could drown you one day and feed you the next.

For the first time since I ran, I felt my shoulders unclench. I slept deep, not in the bottom of a wet boat, but on a pile of old blankets in a corner of his shack. The nightmares still came. I’d wake up in a sweat, the image of those cold winter eyes burning in my mind. But then I’d hear Silas snoring, a sound like a rusty saw, and the gentle lap of the river, and the fear would slowly drain away. It was a fragile kind of peace. I knew it couldn’t last.

On the fourth morning, a steamboat came up the river. It was the first one I’d seen up close. It was a big side wheeler, white as a wedding cake, pushing a huge swell of water ahead of it. Music was playing on its deck, a tinny piano sound that carried across the water. People was standing at the railing, the men in dark suits and hats, the women with parasols. They looked like they was from another world. A clean world, where debts was paid with money.

Silas and I stood on the bank and watched it pass. It was so close I could see the faces of the people. And then I saw him. It wasn't him, not really. But it was a man who looked like he could have been. He was standing near the stern, a thick man in a dark suit, with a hard, square face. He was looking out at the banks, at the wild trees and the muddy water, with a look of distaste. He looked like he owned things. He looked like the men from my town. The banker. The sheriff. The man who hired my Pa and then paid his debt with a peavey.

My blood went cold. My breath caught in my throat. I felt the same way I had in the corn patch, like a rabbit that just heard the hounds. I knew it wasn't him. I knew he was back in that town, walking its quiet streets. But it didn't matter. The man on the boat was a reminder. A reminder that the world was full of men like him. A reminder that I could never be safe, not really. I could patch my boat, but I couldn't patch the hole he’d torn in the world.

The steamboat churned past, leaving a wake that rocked my little skiff in the cattails. The piano music faded. The river went back to being quiet.

“Time for you to move on,” Silas said, not looking at me. He’d seen the look on my face.

“Yes sir,” I said.

He nodded. He went into his shack and came back out with a small flour sack. He pushed it into my hands. “Some cornmeal. A salt brick. And a line and some hooks. A man can’t live on found fish.” He also gave me a small, heavy knife with a worn wooden handle. “For cuttin’ bait,” he said, but we both knew it was for more than that.

I pushed my skiff, my newly patched skiff, out into the water. It sat higher now, proud. It didn’t leak. I looked at the old man on the bank. He looked small and frail against the big woods.

“Silas,” I said. “Thank you.”

He just nodded, his face a mask of wrinkles. “Keep your eyes on the water, Skiff. And don’t never trust a man in a clean shirt.”

I dipped my hands in the water and started paddling out toward the main current. The river grabbed me, just like it did the first time. I didn’t look back at Silas. I didn’t want to see him get smaller and disappear. I just kept my eyes on the water ahead, that big, muddy road. It was still a lonesome road. I was still alone. But something was different. I had a tight boat, a full sack, and a knife in my belt. I had the things Silas taught me. It wasn't much. But it was more than I had before. The fear was still there, a cold passenger sitting in the stern of my boat. I could feel its weight. But now, I was the one at the oars. I was the one steering. And I pointed my skiff south, toward whatever came next.

The first few days were a blur of water and sky. The river was a patient thing. It did not hurry. It moved with a slow, powerful certainty, and I learned to move with it. Mornings, the mist would be so thick on the water it was like paddling through a cloud. I could not see the banks, only the gray water directly in front of my boat and the gray sky directly above. In those hours, it felt like the whole world had been washed away, leaving only me and the river and the quiet drip of water from my paddle.

I learned the river's language. The V of a ripple that meant a submerged log. The nervous, choppy water that spoke of a sandbar hiding just beneath the surface. The slow, deep eddy behind a point of land where a catfish might be waiting. I used the line and hooks Silas had given me. The hooks were small and sharp. I baited one with a piece of river clam I dug from the mud, and another with a fat grub I found in a rotting log. I would tie the line to a willow branch that hung over the water and leave it, checking it later. Sometimes there would be a silver-sided bream, flapping and pulling. More often, there would be nothing but a clean hook.

The cornmeal from Silas’s sack was my steady friend. I would mix a little with river water to make a thick paste and eat it cold. It was coarse and tasteless but it filled the hollow in my belly. The salt brick was a treasure. I would lick it sometimes, just for the sharp, clean taste. When I did catch a fish, I’d rub it with salt and cook it on a stick over a small fire, a fire I built in a patch of bare dirt on the bank, far from any dry grass. I’d eat the fish slow, making it last, picking the bones clean.

The loneliness was the biggest thing. It was bigger than the river, bigger than the sky. It sat with me in the skiff, a heavier weight than the fear. The fear was a cold, sharp thing that would jab me when I saw a strange shape on the bank or heard a sudden noise. The loneliness was a dull, constant ache. Sometimes I’d talk to the boat. “We’re makin’ good time, skiff,” I’d say, my voice sounding strange and thin in the great quiet. Sometimes I’d talk to my Pa. I’d tell him about the color of the sunset, or the big turtle I saw sunning itself on a log. It felt like he was listening. It felt like if I just kept talking, I could keep him from fading away completely.

I thought about Silas, too. I wondered if he was sitting on his porch, watching the river, his face a web of wrinkles. His words came back to me at odd times. Keep your eyes on the water. Don’t never trust a man in a clean shirt. I held the knife he gave me. The wooden handle was smooth and dark from his hand. I used it to clean the fish I caught, the blade sharp and sure. It felt good in my belt, a solid weight against my side.

After about a week of paddling, the world changed. The sky, which had been a pale, hazy blue, turned the color of a bad bruise in the west. The air got heavy and still. The birds on the bank went quiet. The river itself seemed to be holding its breath. The wind came first, a whisper in the willows, then a low moan that grew into a steady push. It pushed the water into angry little waves that slapped against the side of my boat.

I paddled hard for the bank, my arms straining. The trees looked different. They were taller here, thick with cypress and tupelo, their roots like gnarled knees sticking out of the water. I saw a narrow opening, a little cut that led back into the swampy woods. It looked dark and full of snakes, but it also looked like shelter. I pointed the skiff toward it and dug my paddle in deep.

The rain started just as I slipped into the quiet water of the cut. It was not a gentle rain. It was a hard, mean rain, hitting the water so fast it looked like the whole river was boiling. The drops were cold and heavy. They stung my face and soaked my thin shirt in seconds. Thunder cracked right over my head, a sound so loud it felt like the sky was splitting apart. The skiff rocked, even here in the sheltered water.

I pushed deeper into the trees, the branches scraping the sides of my boat. I found a spot where a huge cypress had fallen, its massive trunk creating a kind of roof over a pool of black water. I slid the skiff underneath it. The rain still found its way through the leaves, but it was a drip now, not a downpour. I was out of the wind.

I sat there, shivering, and watched the storm. Lightning lit up the woods in quick, blinding flashes, making the trees look like skeletons. The thunder was a constant rumble, a growl in the belly of the world. I thought of the patch on my boat. I thought of the river swallowing me whole. I thought of my Pa, and how small a man is against a thing like a storm, or a thing like another man’s hate.

I bailed the water from my skiff with my hands, my fingers stiff and cold. I made sure Silas’s flour sack was as dry as I could keep it, tucked under the small seat in the bow. There was nothing to do but wait. I sat there for hours, listening to the world tear itself apart. The fear was bad. It was the corn patch fear. The feeling that there was nowhere to run, nowhere to hide. I was just a small thing, and the world was big and angry.

When the storm finally passed, it left a strange quiet. The rain softened to a drizzle, then stopped. The only sound was the dripping of water from a million leaves. I pushed the skiff out from under the log. The air smelled clean, washed new. The river had risen. It was faster now, and the color of coffee with no cream. It carried whole trees in its current, their branches reaching up like drowning arms.

I stayed in the cut until the next morning, not wanting to chance the main current in the dark. I was hungry, but I didn't want to risk a fire in the wet woods. I ate another cold paste of cornmeal and water, my hands still trembling a little. The next day, the river had calmed some, though it was still high and swift. I pushed out into the current and let it take me. The world felt bigger after the storm. The woods seemed darker, the river wider. I felt smaller.

Two days later, I saw smoke. It was a thin gray ribbon curling up from the east bank, a few miles ahead. My cornmeal was getting low, and the fish had not been biting in the muddy water. The thought of real food, maybe some bread or a piece of salt pork, was a powerful pull. But Silas’s voice was in my ear. Don’t never trust a man in a clean shirt.

I paddled closer, staying on the far side of the river, using the willows on the bank for cover. It was a camp. Two men sat by a fire. There was a lean-to made of branches and a dirty canvas tarp. They didn’t look like men from town. Their clothes were as ragged as mine. One was a big man, with a thick black beard and arms as big as my legs. The other was small and thin, with a twitchy, ferret-like face. He was the one who saw me first.

He nudged the big man and pointed. “Looky there, Cole. Got us a visitor.”

The big man, Cole, turned his head slowly. He looked at me, then at my skiff. His eyes were small and set deep in his face. He didn’t smile. He just watched me.

I let my boat drift. I was a long way off, but the water was quiet. My voice felt weak when I called out. “Howdy.”

The small man grinned. The grin didn’t touch his eyes. “Howdy yourself, boy. That a fine little boat you got there.”

I didn’t say anything. I just kept my hands on the paddle, ready to pull away.

“You hungry?” the small man called. “We got squirrel stew. Got more than we can eat.”

My stomach rumbled. Squirrel stew. The smell of the woodsmoke and the roasting meat reached me across the water. It was the best smell in the world. Against my better judgment, I started to paddle toward them. I told myself I would stay in the boat. I would just get a little closer.

I brought the skiff in close to the bank, but I didn’t pull it ashore. I kept it in the water, the nose resting on the mud. “Much obliged for the offer,” I said. “But I gotta keep movin’.”

“Nonsense,” the small man said, getting up. He was wiry, and moved quick. “Name’s Jip. That there is Cole. No need to rush off. River’ll be there tomorrow.” He walked to the fire and ladled some stew into a tin bowl. It was thick with meat and maybe some wild onions. He held it out. “Come on. Take it.”

I looked at the stew. I looked at the big man, Cole, who was still sitting by the fire, cleaning a long-barreled rifle. He hadn't said a word. He just watched me. I looked back at Jip’s smiling face. His shirt was not clean. It was stained with sweat and dirt and what looked like old blood. Silas’s warning was about clean shirts. These were not clean shirts. Maybe it was alright.

Slowly, I got out of the skiff, pulling it halfway onto the muddy bank. I kept one hand on the boat. I took the bowl from Jip. “Thank you,” I said. The stew was hot and good. The meat was tender. I ate it fast, standing right there, not taking my eyes off them.

“Where you headed, boy?” Jip asked, watching me eat.

“South,” I said, my mouth full.

“South is a mighty big place,” he said, still smiling that thin smile. “What’s down there for you?”

“Just goin’,” I said. “Away from nothin’. Toward nothin’.”

“A boy with a boat and no destination,” Jip said, looking at Cole. “Ain’t that somethin’.”

Cole grunted. He set the rifle down and picked up a piece of wood. He started whittling at it with a knife. He never looked at me. He just shaved off long, thin curls of wood.

I finished the stew and handed the bowl back to Jip. “That was mighty fine,” I said. “I’m grateful.” I started to push my skiff back into the water.

“Hold on now,” Jip said. He put a hand on the bow of the boat. It was a light touch, but it stopped me. “We been good to you. Shared our fire. Shared our food. Seems only fair you share a little with us.”

My blood went cold. The same cold I felt when I saw the man on the steamboat. “I ain’t got much to share,” I said. My hand moved, slow and easy, to my belt, where the knife was tucked.

Jip’s eyes followed the movement. His smile vanished. “You got a boat. You got a sack of supplies. We ain’t got a boat. Our raft broke up in that storm. We need to get south, too.”

“This boat’s all I got,” I said. My voice was tight.

The big man, Cole, stood up. He was even bigger standing. He was like a tree. He still had the whittling knife in his hand. He took a slow step toward me. “The boy is right, Jip. The boat is all he’s got. It wouldn’t be right to just take it.” He looked at me, and for a second, I thought he was going to help me. His voice was low and gravelly. “We should trade for it.”

“Trade what?” I asked, my heart hammering against my ribs.

“We’ll trade you this,” Cole said. He held up the piece of wood he’d been whittling. He had carved it into the shape of a little bird. “For the boat.”

Jip laughed, a short, ugly sound.

I knew what was happening. It was the same as the man with the peavey. A trade that was no trade. A choice that was no choice. They were going to take my boat. They were going to take my cornmeal and my line and my hooks. They were going to leave me on the bank with nothing but a little wooden bird.

The fear was a wild thing in my chest, a trapped animal. I looked at the river. It was my only way out. It was fast and deep. I looked at the two men. Cole was between me and the water. Jip was still holding the bow of my skiff.

My mind went quiet and clear. I thought of my Pa in the dust. I thought of Silas’s face. Keep your eyes on the water. I looked at Cole. “Alright,” I said. My voice was a whisper. “It’s a fair trade.”

Cole’s face relaxed a little. He thought he had won. He took another step toward me, holding out the wooden bird.

That’s when I moved.

I didn’t go for Cole. I went for Jip. I lunged forward and shoved him, hard, with both hands. He was small and wiry, but he wasn’t expecting it. He stumbled backward, tripping over a root, and fell with a curse.

At the same time, I grabbed the side of my skiff and threw all my weight into it, pushing it off the mud bank and into the current. The river grabbed it instantly. I scrambled in, clumsy, half falling over the side. The boat tipped dangerously, taking on water.

Cole roared. He was faster than I thought. He lunged for the stern of the boat as it swung out into the river. His big hands closed on the wood. He was strong. He started to pull the skiff back toward the bank. I was going to lose.

I grabbed my paddle. There was no time to use it for paddling. I swung it like a club. I didn't aim for his head. I aimed for his hands. The hard wood of the paddle cracked down on his knuckles.

He howled in pain and surprise, and his grip loosened for just a second. It was all I needed. The current took hold again, pulling the skiff away from him, out toward the middle of the river.

Jip was back on his feet, screaming curses at me. Cole stood on the bank, cradling his hand, his face twisted with rage. He still had his rifle, but it was by the fire, too far away to grab.

I didn't wait. I got to my knees and started paddling, digging deep, putting my back and my fear and my anger into every stroke. I didn't paddle across the river. I paddled with it, letting the swift current add to my speed. The camp and the two men got smaller and smaller. Their shouts faded until they were lost in the sound of the water.

I didn't stop paddling for miles. I didn't stop until my arms were numb and shaking, and the bend of the river had long since hidden the place where their fire had been. I finally let the skiff drift into a quiet stretch of water along the bank and collapsed onto the bottom of the boat, my chest heaving.

My hands wouldn’t stop trembling. I had done it. I had gotten away. I had looked at the men who hired my Pa, the men who owned the world, and I hadn’t let them take what was mine. They were not in clean shirts. They were ragged and hungry. But they were the same. They believed the world and everything in it belonged to them for the taking.

I sat up and looked at my things. The flour sack was safe. My line and hooks were safe. I looked at my hands on the paddle. They were scraped and raw. I touched the knife at my belt. I hadn’t even had to pull it. The paddle had been enough. The river had been enough.

The fear was still with me. It sat in the stern, a cold passenger. But its face had changed. It was not a ghost anymore. It was not the memory of the banker in a dark suit. It was the face of Cole, his eyes small and hard. It was the face of Jip, with his ugly, false smile. The fear was real now. It had hands that could grab and a voice that could curse.

I was alone on the big, muddy road. But I was still moving. I had faced the hounds, and for now, I was still running. I ate the last of my cold cornmeal paste. It tasted like victory. When I was done, I picked up my paddle. The wood was smooth in my hands. I pointed my skiff south. The river didn't care. It just kept moving. And so did I.

The sun climbed higher. It beat down on my neck and the back of my head. The river was a sheet of brown glass under the heat, shimmering so bright it hurt my eyes to look at. My throat was dry. The victory paste of cornmeal was long gone, a faint memory in my belly. Thirst was a different kind of hunger. It was sharper. It made my tongue feel thick and my head feel light.

I kept paddling, but the strokes were shorter now, less powerful. I tried to stay in the main current, that deep, fast channel in the middle of the river, but my arms burned with a fire that had nothing to do with the sun. I had to get to the bank. I had to find water that wasn't full of mud and silt. I had to rest. But the fear was a whip at my back. Cole and Jip were back there somewhere. How far back? A few miles? A dozen? I had no way to know. They were on foot, but the river bent and twisted. A man on foot, cutting across the land, could make up time on a man in a boat who had to follow every curve.

I pictured them. Cole, his hand swollen and purple, his face a knot of fury. Jip, his grin gone, replaced by something meaner. They would not give up. I had taken something from them. Not the skiff, not the flour. I had taken their power. I had looked them in the eye and said no, and I had made it stick. Men like that could not let such a thing stand. They would walk until their boots fell apart. They would find a farm, steal a horse, buy passage on a steamer. They would come. The fear told me so.

So I kept paddling. I watched the banks for any sign of them. Every snapped twig, every bird that took flight, made my heart jump into my throat. The world was full of noises, but the only one I was listening for was the crack of a rifle.

By late afternoon, I knew I couldn't go on. My body was giving out. The heat was making me dizzy, and my vision swam at the edges. I guided the skiff toward the western bank, looking for a place to hide. Not a town, not a farm. Just a place where the world could not see me. The bank was a wall of green, a tangle of willow and cottonwood and vines so thick they looked like they were holding the trees together.

I saw a place where a smaller creek fed into the river. Its mouth was almost completely hidden by a fallen log and a curtain of hanging moss. It was a dark, uninviting slit in the green wall. It was perfect. I dug the paddle in, pushing the skiff past the log and into the narrow waterway.

The air changed immediately. It was cooler in here, damp and thick with the smell of mud and rotting leaves. The light filtered down through the canopy, green and dim. The sound of the big river faded behind me, replaced by the buzz of insects and the drip of water. I paddled a hundred yards up the creek, the banks closing in, the branches of ancient cypress trees meeting overhead. I was in a tunnel of green. I found a small cove, barely bigger than the skiff itself, where the roots of a giant cypress knobbed out of the water. I nudged the boat in among them and tied the painter to a thick root. From the creek, my skiff was invisible. From the river, the creek itself was nearly invisible. It was as safe as I was going to get.

For a long time, I just sat there, listening. The silence was a living thing. My hands were shaking again, not from fear this time, but from exhaustion. I looked at them. The skin over my knuckles was scraped away where I had hit Cole. My palms were blistered and raw from the paddle. They were ugly hands, but they were my own.

Thirst was the first thing I had to deal with. I leaned over the side of the skiff and looked at the creek water. It was clearer than the river water, but still brown. I remembered my Pa telling me how to find water. You don't drink from the open. You dig. I climbed out of the skiff, my legs unsteady. The mud on the bank was deep and soft, and it tried to suck my boots off. I found a spot a few feet from the water's edge and started digging with my hands. I dug past the black topsoil, past the thick roots, until I got to sand. Water began to seep into the hole. It was muddy at first, but I scooped it out. I kept digging, kept scooping. Finally, the water that filled the hole was almost clear, filtered by the sand. I waited for it to settle, then I cupped my hands and drank. It was cool and tasted of earth and minerals. It was the best thing I had ever tasted. I drank until my belly was full and sloshing.

Next was food. My flour sack was a comfort, but it was just a sack until I found something to put in it. I took out my line and one of my precious hooks. For bait, I rolled over a piece of rotting wood on the bank. A whole world of pale, fat grubs squirmed in the dark soil underneath. I picked the biggest one and threaded it onto the hook. I didn't have a pole. I didn't need one. I dropped the line into the dark water of the creek and let it sink to the bottom.

Then I waited. The sun went down. The green light in my tunnel faded to gray, then to black. The evening chorus started up, a million frogs and crickets all singing their own songs. A deep loneliness settled over me. Out on the big river, the world felt huge and I felt small. In this tight, hidden place, the world felt small and I felt trapped. I thought about my Pa. I wondered if he had ever been this alone. He had been a quiet man. Maybe he had been alone even when he was with people.

A tug on the line startled me. It was sharp and insistent. I let the fish take it for a second, then I jerked the line hard to set the hook. The fight was on. It wasn't a big fish, but it was strong, pulling the line back and forth in the dark water. I played it carefully, my heart thumping. This was not a sport. This was dinner. This was tomorrow. I finally worked it to the side of the boat and pulled it out of the water. It was a catfish, a foot long, with a wide, ugly head and long whiskers. In the faint starlight that filtered through the trees, its skin was slick and dark.

I killed it quickly with the handle of my knife, a sharp rap on the head. The work wasn't done. A fire was a risk. A big fire was a stupid risk. Light and smoke were beacons. I needed a fire I could hide. I found a small hollow between two thick cypress roots. I lined it with rocks from the creek bed. Then I gathered the driest, smallest twigs I could find in the dark, snapping them into short lengths. I had no matches. I had a flint and steel, a gift from my Pa the last time I saw him. "A man who can't make his own fire is already half dead," he had said.

It took me a long time. My hands were clumsy with fatigue. The flint was small, the steel was cold. I struck and struck, sending pathetic sparks into a small nest of dry moss I had prepared. Nothing. I cursed under my breath. I thought about Cole and Jip. I thought about the banker. I thought about being cold and hungry and alone. Anger made my hands steadier. I focused on the small, dark space between my hands. I struck the flint again. A single spark, brighter than the others, fell into the moss. A tiny orange eye opened. I bent close and blew on it, a soft, steady breath, nursing it, protecting it. The orange eye grew, and a wisp of smoke, almost invisible, curled up. I added the smallest twigs one by one. The flame grew, but it was contained in my little stone pit, hidden from view, casting just enough light for me to see.

I cleaned the catfish with my knife, the blade sharp and sure in my hand. I cut off the head and gutted it, throwing the insides far into the creek. I didn't have a pan. I found a flat, green stick and split it, weaving the fish's body through the fork. I propped it over the tiny fire. The smell of the cooking fish filled my little cove. It smelled like safety. It smelled like I was going to live another day.

I ate it with my fingers, right off the stick. The white meat was flaky and hot. It had a muddy taste from the creek, but I didn't care. I ate every bit of it, then chewed on the bones to get the last scraps of meat. When I was done, I licked the grease from my fingers. I was full. I had found water. I had found food. I had made a fire. I had done it all myself.

I put the fire out carefully, dousing it with water and stirring the ashes with a stick until it was just cold, wet mud. I didn't want any trace left. I climbed back into the skiff. The bottom of the boat was hard, but it was my home. I lay down, my flour sack for a pillow. I put the paddle beside me. I took my knife from my belt and held it in my hand. The cool wood of the handle felt solid. The fear was still there. It lay down with me in the boat. But it was a quiet fear now. It was a watchdog, not a wolf. It listened to the night sounds with me. An owl hooted somewhere deep in the swamp. A heavy splash downstream made me sit up, my hand tight on the knife. A beaver, maybe. Or a gar. I waited. The silence returned. I lay back down. I did not think I would sleep. But the river had taken its toll, and the food in my belly was a warm anchor. My eyes closed.

I woke before the sun. A gray mist hung over the creek, so thick I could not see the opposite bank. The world was silent and damp. I was cold, and my body ached in places I didn't know could ache. I sat up and ate the other half of the catfish, cold. It was still good.

It was time to go. Hiding was safe, but safety was not a destination. I had to keep moving south. I untied the painter and used the paddle to push the skiff out of my little cove and back into the narrow creek. I moved slowly, silently. The paddle made no sound as it dipped into the water. When I reached the mouth of the creek, I paused behind the fallen log, peering out at the main river.

The river was a different beast in the morning. The mist lay on it like a blanket, hiding whatever was downstream. It was a world made of gray water and gray sky. I felt a pang of a different kind of fear. Not the fear of Cole and Jip. The fear of the unknown. I was paddling into a blank space. I had no map. I did not know what towns were ahead, what dangers, what rapids or sandbars. Every bend in the river was a question I did not have the answer to.

I pushed the skiff out into the current. The big river took hold of me again, pulling me south. The mist was cold on my face. For an hour, I paddled through a world without shores, with only the feel of the current to guide me. Then the sun began to burn through. The mist shredded into white rags and drifted away, revealing the river and the green walls of the trees on either side.

The land was changing. It was getting flatter, the trees less dense. I saw a cleared field, brown and fallow, stretching back from the riverbank. I saw a fence, with two posts standing and the rest a line of ghosts fallen in the tall grass. People had been here. Or were here still. I moved the skiff to the center of the river, as far from either bank as I could get. I was a small speck on a huge expanse of water. I hoped it made me harder to see.

Later in the day, I saw a dock. It was little more than a few planks nailed to some posts, sticking out into the water like a broken finger. A shack stood on the bank behind it, a thin curl of smoke rising from a rusty stovepipe. An old man sat on the end of the dock, a cane pole in his hands. He was too far away for me to see his face, but my whole body went tense. My first instinct was to turn and paddle for the other side of the river. But I held my course. To change direction suddenly would be to draw attention. I kept paddling, my strokes even, my eyes pointed forward. I watched him from the corner of my vision. He did not look up. He was part of the river, just like the logs and the birds. He was not looking for a boy in a stolen skiff. As I passed him and the distance grew, the tension in my shoulders eased. Not every person was an enemy. But I would act as if they were. It was safer that way.

The sun was setting again. I had put another day between me and my pursuers. I had survived. I looked at the flour sack. It was still empty. My stomach was empty too. The work was never done. Find a place to hide. Find water. Find food. Survive the night. Repeat. It was the rhythm of my new life.

The river was wider here. The current felt stronger, more determined. It was an old road, a road that had carried traders and explorers and armies. It had carried men in clean shirts and men in rags. Now it was carrying me. I did not know where I was going. I only knew the direction. South. Away from the memory of the banker. Away from the cold fury in Cole’s eyes. I was paddling toward something, but I did not know what it was. A new life. A place where I did not have to run. A place where I could stop looking over my shoulder.

I looked down at the paddle in my hands. The wood was worn smooth. It felt like a part of me. It had saved my life. It had fed me. It was my only tool, my only weapon, my only friend. I dug it into the brown water. The skiff surged forward. The river just kept moving. And so did I.

The night came on slow and thick. The purple of the sunset bled to black. The stars came out one by one, cold and distant. They did not look like the same stars I had known. They looked like holes in the dark, showing a brighter, colder dark behind them. I kept the skiff in the middle of the river. The banks were just long, low shadows. I could not see the trees anymore, but I could hear the wind in them. I could hear the insects, a steady, high pitched hum that was the sound of the night itself.

My arms ached. My back was a knot of fire. But the emptiness in my stomach was the worst part. It was not just hunger anymore. It was a hollow, aching thing that seemed to be eating me from the inside. I thought of the half loaf of bread I had stolen what felt like a lifetime ago. I could taste it. I could feel the dry crumbs on my tongue. The memory was so strong it made me dizzy. I dipped my hand in the river and drank from my cupped palm. The water was cold and tasted of mud. It filled my stomach for a moment, a cold weight that did not ease the hunger. It only made it feel colder.

I had to stop. I could not paddle all night. My body would give out. I needed to find a place to hide, a place to rest. I began to paddle toward the western bank. It seemed less defined, more broken. That might mean creeks or inlets. A place to disappear. The skiff slid through the dark water. The only sound was the dip of my paddle and the gurgle of the water running along the hull. It was a lonely sound.

I found what I was looking for. A narrow cut in the bank, almost hidden by a tangle of low hanging willows. I pushed the skiff through the branches. They scraped against the sides of the boat, a dry, whispering sound. Inside, the water was still. The air was thick and smelled of mud and rot. It was a small creek, no wider than the skiff was long. It snaked back into the trees. I followed it for a hundred feet, until the river was completely gone from sight. I was in a tunnel of darkness. I felt for the bank with my paddle. It was soft mud. I pushed the nose of the skiff into it and was still.

I sat there for a long time, just listening. The river was a distant murmur. The insect noise was louder here. A frog croaked nearby. Something splashed in the water. I was not alone, but nothing sounded human. I tied the skiff’s rope to a thick root sticking out of the mud. I climbed out. My legs were stiff. The mud sucked at my boots.

I had to find something. Anything. Roots. Grubs. I did not care. I stumbled through the undergrowth, my hands out in front of me. Thorns tore at my shirt and my skin. I could see almost nothing. The starlight did not get through the thick canopy of leaves. I felt the ground with my fingers. I found wet leaves and slick mud and gnarled roots. I did not know which roots were safe to eat. I did not know anything. My father had known things. He had known how to track, how to tell one plant from another. He had tried to teach me. But I had been a boy then, more interested in games than in knowledge. The memory of my father was a sharp pain, different from the hunger but just as deep. I pushed it away. It did not help me now.

I found nothing. I was too weak, and it was too dark. I gave up. I went back to the skiff. I curled up on the hard planks of the bottom. The flour sack was a poor pillow. Its emptiness mocked me. I closed my eyes. Sleep would not come. The hunger was a constant companion. It whispered to me. It showed me pictures of food. A roasted chicken. A thick slice of pie. A bowl of stew. The banker’s table had been covered in such things. I had seen it through the window, just before. Just before everything went wrong. I saw Cole’s face then, his eyes narrowed, his mouth a thin, hard line. He was out there. He was hunting me. He would not stop. The hunger and the fear were a pair of wolves, circling me in the dark. I did not know which one would get me first.

I must have slept. I woke to a gray light. A fog lay over the creek, thick and wet. It clung to my clothes and my hair. I was cold to the bone. My whole body ached. The hunger was a dull, constant fire now. I had to get back on the river. I had to keep moving. But first, water. I leaned over the side and drank from the creek. The water was stagnant and warm. I did not care. It was wet.

I untied the rope and pushed the skiff back toward the river. The fog was even thicker on the main channel. I could not see either bank. I could only see a few feet of brown water around me. The world had shrunk to a small, gray circle. It was good for hiding. But it was also dangerous. I could run aground. I could run into another boat. I stayed in what I hoped was the center of the current, letting it do most of the work. My strokes with the paddle were weak. Each one took a huge effort.

After an hour, the fog began to thin. The sun was a pale disk in the gray sky. The world came back into view, soft and blurry at first, then sharp. The river was wider than ever. The banks were low and marshy, covered in tall reeds and stunted trees. It was a desolate looking place. And then I saw it.

It was not on the bank, but in the water, caught on a half submerged sandbar. It was a boat, much larger than my skiff. A houseboat, maybe, or a small barge. It was in a sorry state. It listed heavily to one side, its flat roof tilted at a sharp angle. One corner of it was underwater. The paint was peeling, and the wood was dark with water stains. It looked like a dead thing, a skeleton picked clean by the river.

My heart started to beat faster. My first thought was to paddle away. A wreck could be a trap. It could be the home of river rats, scavengers who would kill a boy for his boots. But my second thought was about supplies. A boat that size, a boat someone had lived on, must have had food. Maybe something was left. Maybe something had survived the water.

Desperation was a stronger master than fear. I turned the skiff and paddled toward the wreck. I moved slowly, silently. I did not let the paddle splash. I held my breath and listened. The only sounds were the lapping of the water against the tilted hull and the cry of a distant bird. The wreck seemed empty. Dead.

I came alongside the high side, the one out of the water. A rotten rope trailed in the current. I grabbed it and tied my skiff to a rusty cleat. The silence was heavy. I was a thief in a graveyard. I pulled myself up onto the deck. It slanted steeply. I had to brace myself against the wall of the cabin to keep my footing. The wood was slick with a thin layer of green algae.

The cabin door was swollen and stuck in its frame. I put my shoulder to it and pushed. It groaned but did not move. I pushed again, putting all my weight into it. My boots slipped on the green deck. I fell to one knee. The hunger made me weak and clumsy. I looked at my paddle, lying in the skiff. A tool. A weapon. A friend. I got back in my skiff, grabbed the paddle, and climbed onto the wreck again. I jammed the blade of the paddle into the crack between the door and the frame. I put my weight on it. I leaned. The wood creaked. It splintered. With a loud crack, the door broke open and swung inward.

The sound was like a gunshot in the silence. I froze, my body rigid, listening. I waited. Nothing. No shout. No movement. Just the river. I let out my breath and peered into the cabin.

The inside was a mess. The tilt of the boat had thrown everything to one side. A table was bolted to the floor, but the chairs were in a heap against the low wall, one of them broken. The floor was covered in a thin layer of brown, silty water. The air smelled of mold and decay. There were bunks built into the wall, the mattresses dark and bloated with water. Papers and books were scattered, their pages swollen into a solid pulp. It looked like someone had left in a hurry. Or had never had a chance to leave at all.

I stepped inside, my boots splashing in the shallow water. I moved carefully, my eyes scanning everything. I looked for a galley, a kitchen. I found it at the back of the cabin. A small stove, a countertop. The cupboards were all open, their contents spilled out. A few jars lay broken on the floor. An empty sack of rice, the grains scattered and mixed with the mud. My heart sank. There was nothing here. I had taken a great risk for nothing.

I kicked at a pile of debris in frustration. Something hard was under it. A metal box. It was an old footlocker, painted green, with rust blooming along its edges. It was jammed under the lip of a bunk. It was heavy. I pulled at it, my fingers straining. It would not budge. I used my paddle again, shoving the blade under the locker and using the bunk for leverage. I heaved. The paddle handle bent, the wood groaning in protest. I was afraid it would break. It was my life. But the hunger was a fire in my gut. I heaved again, a desperate grunt escaping my lips. The locker scraped free.

It was locked. A thick padlock held it shut. I wanted to scream. I looked around the cabin. There had to be a tool. A crowbar. A hammer. I found nothing. There was only the paddle. I looked at the lock. Then I looked at the hinges on the back of the locker. They were rusty. Maybe they were weaker than the lock.

I laid the footlocker on its front. I wedged the blade of the paddle under the lid, right next to a hinge. I stomped on the paddle. A jarring pain shot up my leg, but the metal of the hinge bent. I did it again. The metal groaned. One of the rusty screws tore loose from the rotting wood. I moved to the other hinge and did the same. Stomp. The metal shrieked. Stomp. The hinge ripped free. The back of the lid was loose. I jammed my fingers under it and pulled. With a final shriek of tortured metal, the lid came open.

I stared. I could not believe it. Tins. A dozen of them. Their paper labels were gone, but they were sealed. They were food. My hands were shaking. I grabbed one. It was heavy in my hand. It was real. Under the tins was a folded piece of oilcloth. And under that, something wrapped in a greasy rag. I ignored them. I only cared about the tins.

How to open one? I had no tool. I looked around wildly. Then I saw a metal corner bracket on the broken chair. I picked up a tin and smashed it down on the sharp corner of the metal. Once. Twice. A small dent. I did it again, with all my strength. A gash appeared in the top of the tin. A dark liquid seeped out. I dropped the tin and used my fingers to tear at the hole, to make it bigger. The sharp metal cut my skin, but I did not feel it. I got the hole wide enough to see what was inside. Beans.

I tilted the tin to my mouth. I drank the cold, salty liquid. Then I tipped it up and shook it, stuffing the cold beans into my mouth with my cut, dirty fingers. I did not chew. I swallowed. I ate like an animal. I finished the tin in seconds. I grabbed another one and started smashing it on the chair. This one held some kind of meat, packed in gelatin. I ate it just as fast.

It was the best food I had ever tasted. It was life. It was strength. The fire in my stomach turned to a warm, spreading glow. I sat on the wet floor of the ruined cabin, my back against the wall, and I let the feeling wash over me. I had survived again. I had found a treasure.

After a third tin, my stomach hurt from the sudden fullness, but it was a good pain. My mind was clearer now. The panic was gone. I looked back at the footlocker. The oilcloth package. The greasy rag. I reached in and took them out.

I unwrapped the greasy rag first. My breath caught in my throat. It was a revolver. It was old, the metal worn, but it was heavy and solid. It felt dangerous. There was a small cardboard box with it. I opened it. Cartridges. Gleaming brass and lead. I did not know how to use a gun. I had never held one before. But I knew what it was for. It was for killing. It was a tool of men like Cole. I felt a chill that had nothing to do with the wet cabin. I wrapped the gun and the cartridges back in the rag and set them aside.

I unfolded the oilcloth. Inside was a stack of papers, tied with a string. And a map. It was a river map, hand drawn in ink. It was stained and creased, but it was readable. It showed the river, and the creeks that fed it, and the towns along its banks. I saw names I did not recognize. Port John. Henderson’s Ferry. The Forks. I tried to find where I was, but I could not be sure. I was somewhere on a long, winding stretch between two towns I had never heard of. But the map showed the way. It showed the whole river, all the way down to a wide bay, and then the sea. It was a path. A way out.

I put everything in my empty flour sack. The tins of food. The wrapped gun. The map. The sack was heavy now. It was a good weight. I carried it out of the cabin and back to my skiff. I did not look back at the wreck. I had taken what I needed.

I pushed off from the dead boat and paddled back into the main channel. The sun was higher now. I felt strong. My paddle strokes were deep and sure. My belly was full. I had a weapon. I had a map. I was not just a scared boy in a stolen boat anymore. I was a survivor. I was armed. The fear was still there, a cold stone in my gut, but it was smaller now. It was manageable.

I looked down at the map, spread across my knees. I found a place called The Forks, where a smaller river joined this one. It looked like the next major landmark. I decided that was my goal. I would paddle for The Forks. I had a destination.

The river was quiet. I paddled for another hour. The marshy banks gave way to low, rolling hills. The trees were taller here. I felt a sense of purpose I had not felt since I had fled. I was in control.

Then I heard it. A sound that did not belong. A low, sputtering cough. It was a motor. I looked up. A boat was coming around a wide bend upriver, heading toward me. It was not a skiff. It was bigger, a flat bottomed boat with a crude shelter built in the middle. It moved faster than the current. A thin plume of blue smoke rose from its engine.

There were two men in it. They were too far away to see clearly, but I could see their shapes. One was steering. The other was standing in the bow, looking downriver. Looking right at me.

My blood went cold. The warmth in my stomach turned to ice. I was caught. I was in the middle of the wide river, with nowhere to hide. The man in the bow raised his arm and pointed. At me. There was no doubt. The boat did not change its course. It kept coming, steady and determined. Right for me. I looked at the bank. It was too far. I could never make it before they reached me. I was trapped.

I stopped paddling. The skiff drifted on the current. The sputtering of their engine grew louder. I could hear it over the beat of my own heart. I reached down and touched the flour sack. The hard shape of the gun was there. The tins of food felt like stones, weighing me down. My new hope, my new strength, it was all draining away, replaced by the old, familiar terror. The river was not my road to freedom. It was just a trap. And it had finally closed on me.

The two men in the boat grew larger. The sputtering of their motor was a ragged, angry noise that tore the quiet air. I could see them clearly now. The man in the bow was thin, with a long, horse like face and stringy hair plastered to his skull by sweat and river damp. He wore a dirty sleeveless shirt and his arms were a mess of faded, blurry tattoos. The man at the tiller was bigger, thicker in the shoulders and neck, with a round, red face and small, deep set eyes that seemed to be fixed on me. He did not move, just steered the boat with one hand, a steady pressure on the tiller.

The thin man was the one who had pointed. He did not lower his arm. He leaned forward, squinting. "Well now," he shouted, his voice carrying easily over the water. "Look what the river coughed up."

His voice was high and grating. It scraped at my nerves. I did not say anything. My hands were gripping my paddle so hard my knuckles were white. The skiff drifted. My little boat looked like a toy next to theirs. Theirs was a working boat, a scow, scarred and patched, with a tarp stretched over a wooden frame in the middle. It smelled of gasoline and dead fish.

They did not slow down. They were going to run right into me. At the last second, the big man at the tiller pushed it hard over. The scow swung in a wide, dirty arc, its wake slapping against my skiff and nearly tipping me over. I dropped the paddle and grabbed the sides of my boat, my heart trying to beat its way out of my chest. They cut their engine. The sudden silence was more frightening than the noise. Their boat drifted beside mine, close enough for me to touch.

The thin man grinned. It was not a pleasant sight. His teeth were yellow and crooked. "Scared you a little, boy?" he said. He laughed, a dry, rattling sound. The big man just watched me. His eyes were like two little black stones.

"What do you want?" I managed to say. My voice was a croak.

The thin man’s eyes moved over my skiff. They lingered on the flour sack sitting between my feet. "Just being neighborly," he said. "Seeing who's using our river. You're a long way from anywhere, boy. Where you headed?"

"The Forks," I said, before I could stop myself. The word was out of my mouth before I knew it. A stupid mistake. Never tell them where you are going.

"The Forks," the thin man repeated, drawing the words out. "That's a ways yet. A boy like you, all alone. Must be hard." His eyes fixed on the sack again. "What you got in there? Provisions for your long trip?"

I did not answer. I pulled the sack closer with my foot. The hard corner of a tin dug into my ankle. The weight of the gun was a secret I held against my own body.

"Not much of a talker, are you?" the thin man said. He looked at his partner. The big man gave a slow, deliberate nod. It was a signal. I knew it. The thin man turned back to me. His grin was gone. His face was all sharp angles and hunger.

"Let's make this simple," he said, his voice low and mean. "We're gonna take your boat. And whatever is in that sack. You can swim to shore. It ain't far."

I looked at the bank. It was far. Too far. The current was strong here. I would be swept downstream long before I made it. They knew it. They were giving me a death sentence and calling it a choice.

The old terror was back. It was a flood, wiping out everything. The full belly, the map, the plan. All gone. I was just a boy in a boat again, and the monsters were real. They were right here.

But then I felt it. The hard shape of the gun against my leg. It was still there. I had not just found food. I had not just found a map. I had found teeth. The question was, did I have the guts to use them.

"I said, get out of the boat," the thin man snapped. He reached over, his grimy hand grabbing the side of my skiff. He gave it a shake. The little boat rocked violently.

Something broke inside me. It was not courage. It was just a wire snapping. The part of me that knew how to be scared and do nothing just burned away, and all that was left was a wild, cornered animal. I was not going to drown in this river. I was not going to die for a few cans of beans.

My hand moved so fast I did not even feel like I was controlling it. I plunged it into the flour sack, my fingers closing around the cold, heavy grip of the revolver. The cloth was thin. I did not even need to pull it all the way out.

The thin man saw the movement. His eyes widened. "What you got there, boy? You think..."

He never finished. I ripped the gun out of the sack. It felt huge and clumsy in my shaking hand. I raised it. The barrel wavered. I pointed it at his chest.

For a single, silent moment, nobody moved. The whole world was just the three of us, drifting on the brown water. The thin man's face was a mask of disbelief. The big man at the tiller finally showed an expression. His jaw tightened. He started to move, to stand up.

"Don't," I said. My voice was a stranger's. It was thin and high, but it did not break.

The thin man started to laugh. It was a terrible, mocking sound. "You gonna shoot us, boy? With that old piece of rust? You ain't got the..."

I pulled the trigger.

The sound was the loudest thing I had ever heard. It was not a bang. It was a physical blow, a roar that slammed into my ears and echoed off the hills. The gun kicked back with a force I never could have imagined, a brutal, violent shove that ripped it from my grip. My whole arm went numb. The revolver flew out of my hand, hit the side of my skiff with a crack, and disappeared into the river. It was gone.

My ears were ringing. Acrid smoke filled the air, burning my nose. I stared at my empty hand, then at the men.

The thin man was no longer laughing. He was clutching his shoulder, a dark stain spreading across his dirty shirt. His mouth was open in a silent O of shock and pain. He stared at the blood, then at me. The look in his eyes was not anger. It was pure, raw hatred.

The big man was on his feet now. He had a gun of his own. A short, ugly shotgun. He raised it. I had nowhere to go. I had fired my one shot. My one chance. And all I had done was make them angry. I was dead.

But the shot had done something else. My skiff, shoved by the recoil, had pushed away from their scow. The current caught it. I was a few feet away now, and drifting.

The big man leveled the shotgun at my chest. I stared down the black hole of the barrel. Time stopped.

Then the thin man screamed. A wet, gurgling sound. "The boat, you idiot! He's getting away! Shoot him!"

The big man hesitated. He looked at me, then at the widening gap of water. He could kill me, but I was already moving downstream. He made a choice. He turned and yanked the starter cord on the motor. It coughed, sputtered, and died. He cursed, a low, guttural sound, and pulled again.

I did not wait. I grabbed my paddle. My arm was on fire, a deep, throbbing ache from the recoil, but I ignored it. I dug the paddle into the water. Once. Twice. The skiff surged forward. I was paddling with the current now, my fear a rocket engine on my back.

The motor behind me roared to life.

I did not look back. I just paddled. My strokes were clumsy, desperate. I was not strong and sure anymore. I was a panicked animal fleeing a predator. I could hear their engine getting louder. They were faster than me. It was hopeless.

A loud crack echoed from behind me. A patch of water just to my left erupted. A splinter flew from the side of my skiff. They were shooting.

I hunched my shoulders and paddled harder. My muscles screamed. My lungs burned. The shore. I had to get to the shore. It was my only chance. I angled the skiff toward the north bank. It was closer now. Thick with trees and tangled undergrowth.

Another crack. This one was followed by a white hot pain in my left shoulder. It felt like a hornet sting, a branding iron, and a punch all at once. My arm went weak. The paddle slipped from my grasp and floated away.

I cried out, a short, sharp gasp of pain and shock. I clutched my shoulder. My hand came away wet and sticky. It was blood. Dark red. So much of it.

The skiff turned sideways in the current, drifting helplessly. The motor sound was very close now. I could hear the thin man shouting, his voice thick with pain and rage. "Get him! I want him!"

I was finished. I had lost my paddle. I was shot. I looked at the bank. It was so close. Maybe twenty feet. I could almost touch the overhanging branches.

Without thinking, I stood up. The skiff wobbled dangerously. I did not care. I gathered my legs under me, my eyes locked on a thick root that jutted out from the muddy bank. The motor was screaming in my ears. I could hear the water churning as they closed in.

I jumped.

It was not a graceful leap. It was a desperate, clumsy fling of my body. For a second I was flying. Then I hit the water. The cold was a shock that stole my breath. I went under, the brown water closing over my head. It was dark and silent. The motor noise was gone. My wounded shoulder exploded with fresh agony. My lungs were on fire. I clawed at the water, my good arm flailing. My feet hit something soft. Mud.

My head broke the surface. I gasped for air, for life. I was in the shallows. The water was only up to my chest. The bank was right there. I lunged for the root, my fingers grabbing hold. The mud was slick. The current tried to pull me away. I held on. The pain in my shoulder was a steady, blinding roar.

I heard a shout from the river. I risked a glance back. Their scow was right where I had left my skiff. The big man was staring at the empty boat, then scanning the water. The thin man was slumped against the side. They had not seen where I had gone under.

I used my last ounce of strength. I pulled myself up the bank, my boots slipping in the mud. The roots and branches tore at my clothes and skin. I did not feel it. I just kept climbing, crawling, pulling myself out of the river that had tried to kill me.

I collapsed in the undergrowth, hidden by a thick curtain of leaves. I was shaking uncontrollably. I was soaked. I was bleeding. My skiff was gone. My paddle was gone. But I was alive. And I still had the sack. It was still slung over my good shoulder, heavy with water, but there.

I lay there, my face pressed into the damp earth, and listened. I heard the motor sputter and cough. I heard the men cursing. They circled the area for a few minutes, their voices angry and frustrated. I did not move. I barely breathed.

Then, the motor sound grew fainter. They were giving up. They were heading on downriver. Maybe they thought I had drowned. Maybe they had to get the wounded man to help. I did not know. I did not care. They were gone.

The silence they left behind was vast. All I could hear was the gentle lapping of the river, the buzz of insects, and the frantic, ragged sound of my own breathing. The adrenaline began to fade. The pain did not. It bloomed in my shoulder, a hot, spreading flower of pure agony.

I pushed myself up into a sitting position. My whole body ached. I had to look at the wound. I peeled away the soaked fabric of my shirt. The hole was small and neat on the front of my shoulder, where the bullet had gone in. But the back was a mess. A torn, bloody landscape of flesh. The bullet had gone all the way through. I felt a wave of nausea and dizziness. I leaned my head against a tree trunk and closed my eyes, fighting to stay conscious.

Stay awake. You have to stay awake.

The words were a mantra in my head. I opened my eyes. The world swam for a moment, then came into focus. I had to get away from the river. They might come back. Or someone else might come along. The river was a highway. I needed to disappear into the woods.

I shrugged the heavy, dripping sack off my good shoulder. It landed on the ground with a wet thud. I fumbled with the knot. My fingers were clumsy and stiff. My left arm was useless, just a dead weight hanging at my side. Finally, the sack came open.

The tins were there. The map, a soggy, pulpy mess, was there. But the flour that had lined the sack was now a thick, white paste clinging to everything. I grabbed one of the tins. Beans. I needed to eat. I needed energy. But I had no way to open it. The can opener was in the skiff. Along with everything else. My bedroll. My spare clothes. The little things I had taken for granted. All gone.

Panic began to bubble up again. I pushed it down. Hard. Crying was not going to help. Panicking was not going to help. I had the gun. No, the gun was at the bottom of the river. I had shot a man. I had been shot. My whole world had tilted on its axis in the last ten minutes.

I focused on the map. I carefully unfolded it. The paper was fragile, tearing at the creases. But it was still readable. I found The Forks. It was still miles downriver. Following the river was no longer an option. I looked for roads. There was a thin, dotted line that ran roughly parallel to the river, but a few miles inland. An old logging road, maybe. If I could get to that road, I might have a chance. It might lead somewhere. Anywhere was better than here.

First, the wound. I ripped a long strip of cloth from the bottom of my wet shirt. It was not clean, but it was all I had. I reached back with my good hand, trying to stuff the cloth into the wound on my back to stop the bleeding. The pain was excruciating. Black spots danced in my vision. I grit my teeth until my jaw ached. I did the same for the front. A crude, wet bandage.

Next, the food. I took the tin of beans and found a sharp edged rock. I started scraping and pounding at the lid. It was slow, exhausting work. My good arm trembled with effort. The pain in my shoulder made me want to scream. But I kept at it. Scrape. Pound. Scrape. Pound. After what felt like an hour, a small, jagged hole appeared in the lid. I worked it wider, until I could drink the cold, metallic tasting bean juice straight from the can. It was the best thing I had ever tasted. I drank it all, then used my finger to scoop out the beans. I ate them cold.

I felt a little better. The food gave me a sliver of strength. I packed the other tins back into the sack. It was lighter now, with all the water draining out. I stood up, using the tree for support. The world swayed. My legs were shaky. I took a deep breath.

I looked at the woods. They were dark and dense. Nothing like the open river. There was no clear path. I would have to make my own. I had no idea what was in there. Bears. Snakes. Other people. But the men in the boat were on the river. So I would go where they were not.

I took one last look at the river. My skiff was gone, probably miles downstream by now. The water flowed on, indifferent. It had given me hope, and it had taken it away. It was not a road. It was just a river.

I turned my back on it and walked into the trees. My left arm hung uselessly. My shoulder throbbed with every step. The wet sack banged against my hip. I had a map I could barely read, a few tins of food I could barely open, and a hole in my body that was still leaking my life onto the forest floor. I was no longer a survivor. I was just prey that had managed to escape the trap for a little while. And the hunt was not over. I could feel it. It had only just begun.

The first tree I touched was a pine. Its bark was rough and sticky under my good hand. Sap clung to my fingers. The air in here was different. Thicker. It smelled of damp earth and decay and pine needles. The roar of the river was gone, replaced by a deep, humming silence. Or what I thought was silence. After a minute, my ears adjusted. I could hear the buzz of insects. The rustle of a squirrel in the canopy high above. The drip of water from a leaf onto the forest floor. Each sound was sharp, distinct, and a potential threat.

I tried to walk in a straight line. I remembered the map. The road was inland, roughly east. I looked for the sun, but the trees formed a solid roof of green and black. Light filtered down in hazy shafts, but it gave no sense of direction. I was walking blind. I kept moving. The ground was a tangle of roots and moss covered stones. My wet boots slipped on everything. I fell twice in the first hundred yards. The second time, I landed hard on my good side, but the jarring motion sent a bolt of pure agony through my wounded shoulder. I lay on the damp leaves, gasping, my vision swimming. I thought I might just stay there. Let the forest take me. It would be easier. Quieter.

But the image of the men in the boat rose in my mind. The flat, cold look in their eyes. They were not the kind of men who gave up. They would not be satisfied until they had a body. My body. That thought, more than any hope of survival, got me to my feet. I leaned against a thick oak, my breath coming in ragged sobs. Pain was a constant companion now. A hot, living thing that had taken up residence in my shoulder and was sending out tendrils through the rest of my body.

I had to be smarter. Crashing through the undergrowth was loud and exhausting. It was leaving a trail a child could follow. I started to move more slowly. I watched where I put my feet. I used my good hand to push branches aside instead of letting them snap back. I became a part of the forest's quiet rhythm. Step. Listen. Breathe. Step. Listen. Breathe. My world shrank to the next ten feet in front of me. The ground was my map now. I looked for game trails, the faint paths worn by deer. They were narrow and winding, but they were easier to follow than forging my own way.

I needed water. My mouth was dry again, my throat raw. The bean juice was a distant memory. I licked my lips and tasted salt and grime. My thirst was a dull ache that was quickly growing into a sharp demand. I stopped and listened. Not for men this time, but for the sound of moving water. A gurgle. A trickle. Anything. The forest was maddeningly silent. I pressed on. My left arm was a dead weight. It swung with my stride, bumping against my body, sending fresh waves of pain from my shoulder. I tried to hold it still with my good hand, but I needed that hand for balance. I was a broken puppet, stumbling through an endless green maze.

Hours passed. Or maybe it was minutes. Time had lost its meaning. There was only the pain, the thirst, and the next step. I found a small clearing where a giant tree had fallen long ago. Its roots were a massive, upturned wall of dirt and stone. I sat on the mossy trunk to rest. I pulled the wet sack into my lap. The tins of food were a comforting weight. I knew I should save them, but the hunger was a hollow pit in my stomach. Opening another can seemed like a task for a giant. My good arm ached from the effort of the first one.

I decided to check the map again. I carefully pulled it from my pocket. It was a soggy, pulpy mess. The ink had run. The thin, dotted line of the logging road was now a blurry, blue gray smudge that disappeared into a dozen other smudges. It was useless. A wave of despair so profound it made me nauseous washed over me. I was lost. Truly lost. The river had been a false hope, and the map had been a lie. There was no road. There was only this. This endless, suffocating green.

I almost threw the map away. I crumpled it in my fist. But I stopped. It was still paper. It was dry in some spots. I could use it for fire, maybe. If I ever found anything dry enough to burn. If I ever had the strength to start a fire. I smoothed it out as best I could and put it back in my pocket. You do not throw anything away out here. Everything has a purpose, or it might have one later.

It was then that I heard it. A new sound. A low, steady drone. It was faint, so faint I thought I was imagining it. I held my breath, straining my ears. There it was again. It grew steadily louder. An engine. Not a boat engine. This was higher pitched. A whine. It was coming from the sky. I scrambled to my feet and looked up, searching for a break in the canopy. I saw a patch of blue sky and then, a flash of silver. A small plane. It was flying low, its engine a loud buzz now that it was almost overhead.

They were looking for me.

They had a plane.

The hope that had flared in me, the insane thought that it might be a rescue plane, died as quickly as it came. These were not rescuers. They were hunters, and they were using every tool they had. I dove back under the cover of the trees, my heart hammering against my ribs. I pressed myself against the trunk of a tree, trying to make myself small, invisible. The plane circled once, directly over my position. The sound was deaf-Earing. I felt naked, exposed, even with a hundred feet of forest above me. I could imagine them up there, with binoculars, scanning the woods for any sign of movement. A broken branch. A flash of my torn shirt.

The plane flew on. The sound of its engine faded, leaving a silence that felt deeper and more threatening than before. They knew my general location. The river had told them that much. Now they were methodically searching the woods. The logging road, the one I could not find, would be the most logical place for them to search, to land a vehicle. My desperation to find that road was now matched by my terror of it.

The encounter with the plane changed something in me. The despair congealed into a hard, cold knot of anger. They would not have me. I would not be a trophy. I would not be a story they told over drinks. I got up, my movements no longer just a stumble for survival. There was a purpose in them now. I had to get away from this area. The plane would have radioed my likely position. They would send men in on foot.

I started moving again, faster now, more recklessly. The thirst was a fire in my throat. I stumbled upon a patch of ground that was soft and muddy. I knelt, pushing aside the leaves with my good hand. Underneath, a few inches down, the soil was dark and wet. Water. Not clean, but it was water. I dug with my fingers, creating a small hollow. Muddy water seeped in. I waited for it to clear, but I did not have the patience. I lowered my head and drank. It tasted of dirt and roots and worms. It was thick and gritty. But it was wet. I drank until my stomach cramped. I scooped handfuls of the cold mud and plastered it over the crude bandages on my front and back. The coolness was a relief against my feverish skin. Maybe the dirt would help stop the bleeding. Maybe it would give me a fatal infection. At that moment, I did not care.

I found a new direction, away from where the plane had circled. I walked until the light began to fail. The forest grew darker, the shadows long and menacing. I knew I could not travel at night. I would fall and break my neck. I needed to find a place to hide. A place to wait out the long, dark hours. I found a cluster of massive boulders, a jumble of granite left behind by some ancient glacier. In the center was a small, protected space, almost a cave. It was big enough for me to squeeze into. The ground was cold stone, but it was dry and out of the wind.

I sat with my back against the rock, my legs pulled up to my chest. The coming of night brought the cold. My wet clothes clung to me. I started to shiver, a deep, uncontrollable shaking that rattled my teeth and made my whole body ache. My wounded shoulder throbbed in time with my shivering. I knew I needed to eat. I took out another tin of beans. I had kept the sharp rock. The process was just as brutal as the first time. My hand was shaking from cold and exhaustion. I cut myself twice on the jagged metal lid. I did not care. I finally got a hole big enough and drank the liquid. Then I used my dirty fingers to scoop out the beans. I ate them mechanically, without taste, without pleasure. It was fuel. Nothing more.

As darkness consumed the forest, the sounds changed. The birds went silent. The insects buzzed louder. An owl hooted somewhere nearby, a lonely, mournful sound. Then I heard something else. A snapping twig. It was close. Too close. I froze, every muscle tensed. I held my breath. I was not alone in my little fortress of rock. I peered into the gloom. I could see nothing but shapes and shadows. Another twig snapped, closer this time. A low growl rumbled through the dark. It was not human.

My blood ran cold. A bear. A wolf. I had nothing. No gun. No knife. Not even a decent stick. I was wounded. I smelled of blood and fear. I was an invitation to a predator. I squeezed myself further back into the crevice, trying to disappear into the rock. I could hear it sniffing the air. A low, wet sound. It was circling my hiding spot. I closed my eyes, my hand gripping the empty bean tin. A pathetic weapon.

The growling continued, a constant, menacing vibration. I waited for the attack. For the tearing claws and the crushing jaws. I waited for the end that I had been running from all day. But it did not come. The sniffing and growling went on for what felt like an eternity. Then, I heard it move away. The sound of its heavy body crashing through the undergrowth, receding into the distance. It was gone.

I did not know why it had left me. Maybe I was too well hidden. Maybe it had found an easier meal. Or maybe, just maybe, some small part of the wild recognized another wounded thing and decided to grant it a moment of peace. I did not know. I did not care. I was alive. I let out a breath I did not realize I had been holding for minutes. The shivering returned, worse than before. It was not just from the cold anymore. It was from sheer, unadulterated terror.

I did not sleep. I do not think I could have slept even if I were warm and safe in a bed. I sat in the dark, listening to the forest, my mind replaying every moment of the day. The river. The wound. The plane. The growling in the dark. I was prey. The thought came back, but it was different now. It was not a thought of despair. It was a statement of fact. And if I was prey, I had to learn to think like prey. Prey is quiet. Prey is cunning. Prey knows how to hide. Prey knows its terrain better than the hunter.

When the first gray light of dawn filtered through the trees, I was ready. The cold had seeped into my bones. Every joint was a universe of pain. But my mind was clear. The feverish panic of yesterday was gone, replaced by a cold resolve. I crawled out of my rocky shelter. I ate the last of the beans from the open can. I left the other tins in the sack. They were heavy, and opening them was a risk I could not afford to take often.

I knew what I had to do. The logging road was a trap. The open spaces were a death sentence. The plane would be up again. The men would be on the ground. My only hope was to stay in the deepest, thickest part of the woods. To move like the animal that had spared me. Slowly. Quietly. I had to become a ghost. I abandoned the idea of the road, of the map, of the town miles away. My goal was simpler now. Survive the next hour. Then the one after that.

I found a stream, a real one this time, clear and cold, tumbling over rocks. I drank until my stomach ached. I soaked the strip of cloth from my shirt and washed my wound as best I could. It was an ugly thing. The flesh was red and swollen. But the cold water felt good. I re-tied the bandage, tighter this time.

I started walking again. Not east, toward the imaginary road. But north. Away from the river, away from the direction the plane had been flying. I stayed in the shadows, moving from tree to tree. I walked all day. I did not stop to rest. I did not think about food. I thought only about silence and movement. The forest was no longer my enemy. It was my only ally. Its tangled undergrowth was my cover. Its deep shadows were my cloak. Its confusing landscape was my defense.

Late in the afternoon, I found it. It was almost by accident. A slight depression in the ground, too straight to be natural. I followed it. Two faint ruts, almost completely reclaimed by grass and saplings. The logging road. It was real. I had found it.

I stood in the middle of it, a ghost on a ghost road. It was no wider than a car. The trees pressed in on both sides, forming a long, green tunnel. I looked one way, then the other. It was the same in both directions. An endless, straight path to nowhere. A day ago, finding this road would have felt like salvation. Now, it felt like a death trap. To walk on it was to be seen. It was a path for hunters.

But it was also a path. It was a way forward that was not a fight against thorns and roots. I could move faster on it. I could move quieter. It was a risk. A terrible risk. But staying in one place was also a risk. Everything was a risk. I had to choose one.

I took a deep breath. I listened. The forest was quiet. I turned and started walking down the road. I walked on the grassy strip in the middle, my footsteps soft on the earth. I was exposed. I was vulnerable. But I was moving. I was no longer just prey, hiding and waiting. I was prey that had chosen its own path. The hunt was not over. I knew that. But now, for the first time, I felt like I was no longer just running. I was choosing the ground on which we would meet. And that, in this world of green and pain, was a victory.

The silence of the road was different from the silence of the woods. In the forest, the quiet was a living thing, full of tiny, hidden movements. The rustle of a leaf. The scuttling of some unseen creature. The groan of a branch in the wind. Here, on the road, the silence was empty. It was a vacuum, waiting to be filled by the sound of my pursuer. Every step I took felt like a shout. My heart beat a frantic rhythm against my ribs, a drum calling out my location. I am here. I am here. I am here.

The sun, which had been my enemy in the open expanse of the river, was now a welcome warmth on my back. It filtered through the canopy in shifting patterns of light and shadow, making the road ahead a strobing, uncertain path. I kept my eyes moving. Scanning the trees on my left. Scanning the trees on my right. Glancing behind me every fifty steps. The paranoia was a physical thing, a prickling on my skin, a tightness in my jaw. I saw shapes in the deep woods that were not there. A flicker of movement that was only a falling leaf. The dark bulk of a stump that for a terrifying second looked like a crouching form.

My hunger was a dull, constant ache. My body was running on nothing. I tried not to think about it. Thinking about it made it worse. Thinking about it made me weak. And I could not afford to be weak. Not here. Not now. The wound in my arm throbbed with a life of its own. I could feel the heat of it through the thin, damp cloth. I feared infection more than I feared the animal. An infection was a slow, internal enemy. One I could not run from. One I could not fight with a sharpened stick.

I walked for what felt like hours. The road did not change. It was a perfect, monotonous line cut through the wilderness. It never curved. It never rose or fell in any significant way. It was a testament to the single mindedness of the men who had built it. Get the trees out. That was its only purpose. I wondered when the last truck had rumbled down this path. Ten years ago. Twenty. The saplings growing in the ruts were thick as my wrist. Nature was patient. It was taking back its own.

Then I saw it. I stopped so suddenly I almost fell. There, in the damp earth of the left rut, was a track. It was huge. I knelt, my breath catching in my throat. It was not the paw print of a bear. It was too long for that. Too narrow. It looked almost like a human footprint, but impossibly large and with claws. Three long, deep gouges were dug into the dirt at the front of the print. The impression was deep. Whatever made it was heavy. And it was fresh. The edges were still sharp. Water had not yet pooled in the depression.

My blood ran cold. I stood up slowly, scanning the trees again. It was here. It was using the road. The road was not just my path. It was the hunter's path too. A shiver wracked my body, a violent, uncontrollable tremor. My calculated risk, my choice of ground, it was all a joke. I had not chosen the ground. I had stumbled onto its preferred territory. I was a mouse walking down the center of the cat's favorite hallway.

I had to get off the road. The thought was a physical command. My legs wanted to bolt, to dive back into the thorny, protective embrace of the undergrowth. But I hesitated. I looked at the track again. It was heading in the same direction I was. It was in front of me. Or it had been. How long ago? An hour? A minute? Was it watching me now, from the shadows?

My mind raced. If I go back into the woods, I move slowly. I make noise. He will hear me. He will know where I am. On the road, I am quiet. I can see a long way ahead. Maybe I can see him before he sees me. It was a new kind of calculus. A new and more terrible equation of survival. Stay on the path of the monster, or flee into the unknown and be hunted anyway.

I made my choice. I kept walking. But my posture changed. I was no longer walking in the center. I moved to the absolute edge of the road, my right shoulder almost brushing the leaves of the ferns that lined the woods. I walked on the balls of my feet. I made myself as small as possible. Every sense was on fire. I smelled the air, trying to catch a scent of musk or fur. I listened for a sound that was not the wind or my own breathing. My eyes scanned not just the path ahead, but the canopy above. I remembered the way it had moved. A fluid, dark shape. It could be anywhere.

The sun began to dip lower, casting long, distorted shadows across the road. The world was turning orange and purple. Dusk was coming. I could not be out in the open when darkness fell. Not on this road. My pace quickened, driven by a new desperation. I had to find shelter. A rock overhang. A cave. Anything.

The road continued its relentless, straight path. And then, it changed. Up ahead, the tunnel of green seemed to widen slightly. The light was different. I slowed my pace, my heart hammering. I crept forward, one agonizingly slow step at a time. The road opened into a small, circular clearing. And in the center of the clearing stood a building.

It was a cabin. A small, square shack made of dark, weathered logs. It had a single window, a black, vacant square, and a door that hung slightly ajar. A tin chimney pipe, rusted and bent, jutted from the sloping roof. It was a relic of the logging operation, a place for a watchman or a foreman to sleep. It was the first sign of human construction I had seen since the plane fell from the sky.

Hope surged through me, so powerful it was nauseating. Shelter. Walls. A door to close. But the hope was immediately choked by fear. A trap. It was the most obvious place to be. A perfect place to be cornered. If the creature knew this forest, it knew about this cabin. It might even use it.

I stood at the edge of the clearing, hidden by the last of the trees, and I watched. I watched the cabin for a long, long time. I watched the black square of the window. I watched the dark gap of the open door. I watched for any sign of movement. There was nothing. The clearing was perfectly still. The silence here was deeper than anywhere else. It was the silence of a forgotten place.

To stay outside was to be exposed to the night and to the hunter. To go inside was to walk into a potential cage. I thought about the track on the road. The size of it. The claws. I looked at the darkening sky. I had no choice. I had to risk the cage.

I took a deep breath and ran. I burst from the tree line and sprinted across the clearing, my feet pounding on the hard packed dirt. The ten yards to the door felt like a mile. I did not look left or right. I looked only at the dark opening. I hit the door with my shoulder, shoving it inward, and stumbled into the darkness beyond.

I spun around immediately, my back against the far wall, my eyes trying to adjust. The cabin was a single room. The air was thick with the smell of dust, mildew, and ancient cold. A sliver of fading light from the doorway cut through the gloom. I saw a simple wooden table, a single chair lying on its side, and a small, pot-bellied stove in the corner. Against the back wall was a narrow cot, the mattress a dark, stained rectangle.

I moved to the door and pushed it shut. There was no lock. Only a heavy wooden bar on the inside, resting in two iron brackets. I lifted the bar with trembling hands. It was heavy. Solid. I slid it into place. The sound of the wood settling into the iron was the most reassuring sound I had ever heard. I was safe. For a moment, I was safe.

I leaned against the door, my legs weak with relief. My whole body was shaking. I slid down to the floor, my head in my hands. I stayed there for a long time, just breathing the musty air. I was alive. I was inside.

Eventually, the shaking subsided. I had to be practical. I stood up and surveyed my new home. The floor was rough hewn planking. The walls were solid logs, with dried moss and dirt chinked in the gaps. The window was a gaping hole. There was no glass left. Anyone, or anything, could look in. I found a loose plank near the stove, long and wide, and managed to wedge it into the window opening. It was not perfect, but it covered the hole. The room was now plunged into almost total darkness.

I fumbled my way back to the door and let in a sliver of light again. I needed to search the place. Thoroughly. I started with the cot. The mattress was filled with something that rustled like dried leaves and smelled faintly of mice. Underneath it, I found nothing but dust and a few desiccated insect husks. I righted the chair. It was sturdy. I looked at the table. On its surface were two things. A tin cup, coated in a film of grime. And a book.

The book's cover was gone. The pages were yellow and swollen with damp. I picked it up carefully. It was a ledger. The pages were filled with neat, spidery handwriting. Dates. Numbers. Lists of supplies. It was a record of the logging camp. I flipped through a few pages. The last entry was dated over thirty years ago. We are pulling out. Last truck leaves at dawn. Too many incidents. Peterson is gone. Just gone. The words sent a fresh chill down my spine.

I put the book down. I turned my attention to the stove. It was cold and filled with ash. Beside it, however, was a small, wooden box. My heart leaped. I knelt and pried open the lid. It was not food. It was filled with tools. A hammer. A handful of rusty nails. And a long, heavy piece of iron. A fire poker. It was nearly two feet long, with a hooked end. I picked it up. The weight of it felt good in my hand. It was not a sharpened stick. It was a weapon. I would not let it leave my side.

My search was fruitless until I saw the chest. It was tucked away in the darkest corner, under the cot. It was a simple wooden footlocker, bound with iron straps. It was locked. A small, rusty padlock held it shut. I took the fire poker, wedged the hooked end under the lock's hasp, and threw all my weight into it. The rusty metal groaned. I strained, my muscles screaming, the pain in my arm flaring into a white hot agony. With a sharp crack, the wood around the hasp splintered and broke. The chest was open.

I lifted the lid, my breath held. The smell that wafted out was not of mildew or decay. It was the smell of preserved food. Cans. There were a dozen cans, their paper labels long since rotted away. I picked one up. It was heavy. I shook it. It was a solid, reassuring weight. I did not know what was inside. It did not matter. It was food. It was survival. There was also a small, metal box. A first aid kit. Inside, I found a roll of gauze, still sealed in waxed paper, a small bottle of antiseptic, and a tin of what looked like salve.

I almost wept with relief. It was a miracle. A thirty year old miracle, left in the dark for me. I felt a wave of dizziness, a combination of hunger and overwhelming emotion. I sat on the floor, a can in my lap, the first aid kit beside me. I had to eat.

There was no can opener. Of course there was no can opener. I took the hammer from the toolbox. I placed the can on the floor, held a nail against the lid, and began to tap. The sound was deafening in the small, quiet room. Each strike of the hammer felt like a betrayal of the silence I had worked so hard to maintain. But the hunger was a ravenous beast. It demanded to be fed. After a dozen strikes, I had pierced the lid. I worked my way around the edge, punching a series of holes. Then I used the hook of the fire poker to pry the lid open.

The contents were peaches. Canned peaches in a thick, sweet syrup. I stared at them for a moment. The color was still vibrant. They smelled heavenly. I used my fingers to pull out a slice and put it in my mouth. The sweetness was explosive. It was the most incredible thing I had ever tasted. I ate the entire can, fruit and syrup, scooping it out with my fingers, not caring about the mess. I drank the juice, letting it run down my chin. It was pure energy. Pure life.

After I ate, I tended to my wound. I un-tied the ragged strip of my shirt. The flesh around the claw marks was an angry purple, and hot to the touch. I carefully cleaned it with the antiseptic from the kit. It stung, a clean, sharp pain that was almost welcome. It was the pain of healing, not of injury. I applied the salve and wrapped my arm in the clean, sterile gauze. It felt professional. It felt secure.

With a full stomach and a clean bandage, a profound sense of security settled over me. I had walls. I had a weapon. I had food and medical supplies. I had outsmarted the forest. I had outsmarted the hunter. I barred the door again, wedged the plank back in the window, and lay down on the dusty cot. For the first time in days, I felt like I might actually survive. I closed my eyes, the fire poker resting on my chest, and let exhaustion claim me.

I do not know how long I slept. It could have been minutes or hours. What woke me was a sound. It was a low, heavy scraping sound. The sound of something dragging against the outside wall of the cabin.

My eyes snapped open. The room was pitch black. My heart was a frantic bird in my chest. I did not move. I did not breathe. I listened. Scrape. Drag. Scrape. It was moving slowly around the perimeter of the cabin. Testing the walls. I gripped the fire poker, my knuckles white. The feeling of safety evaporated, replaced by a cold, absolute terror. It knew I was in here. The hammering on the can. It had heard me. I had called it here.

The scraping stopped. Silence descended again. A thick, heavy silence that was worse than the noise. I strained my ears, listening for a footstep, for the sound of breathing. Nothing. Minutes stretched into an eternity. My imagination filled the void. I pictured it standing just outside the door, listening for me, just as I was listening for it. I pictured its huge, dark form silhouetted against the window plank.

Then came a new sound. A soft, wet, snuffling noise, right at the crack beneath the door. It was smelling for me. I could hear the air being drawn in, a deep, guttural inhale. I could almost feel the vibration through the floorboards. I was trapped. I was a rat in a box, and the cat was at the door.

The snuffling stopped. A new sound began. A low growl that seemed to come from the center of the earth. It was a sound of pure menace, of ancient hunger. It vibrated through the logs of the cabin, through the floor, up into my bones. The growl grew in volume, a rising tide of fury. And then came the impact.

The whole cabin shook as something immense threw itself against the door. The wooden bar groaned in protest. Dust rained down from the ceiling. I scrambled off the cot, pressing myself into the far corner of the room, the fire poker held out in front of me like a talisman. It hit the door again. A thunderous boom that echoed in the small space. A crack appeared in the thick wood of the door.

This was it. My chosen ground. The shelter I had run to was now my cage, my tomb. It hit the door a third time, and this time I heard the splintering of wood. The bar was not going to hold. Maybe two more hits. Maybe one.

My mind went strangely calm. The terror receded, replaced by a cold, sharp clarity. I was not going to die cowering in a corner. I looked around the dark room. The stove. The pot-bellied stove was made of cast iron. It was my only other weapon. My only other chance.

I moved to the stove, my feet silent on the floorboards. The creature outside let out another enraged roar and threw itself against the door again. The bar cracked, the sound like a gunshot. One more hit. I grabbed the handle of the small stove door and pulled. It was rusted shut. I jammed the hook of the fire poker into the latch and pulled. The metal screamed, but it opened. The inside was a bed of soft, thick ash.

I did not have time to think. I scooped up a handful of the cold ash. It was fine and soft, like flour. I held it in my left hand, the fire poker in my right. I stood beside the door, my back pressed against the log wall. I listened to its heavy breathing just outside. I listened to it gathering itself for the final charge.

The impact was apocalyptic. The wooden bar shattered, and the door flew open, crashing against the inside wall. A huge, dark shape filled the doorway, silhouetted against the pale moonlight. It was bigger than I had imagined. It stood on two legs, but it was not a man. It was stooped, with long arms and a massive, hunched back. Its head was low, and I could see the glint of its eyes.

It took a step into the room, its head turning, trying to find me in the darkness. In that moment, before it could adjust to the gloom, I acted. I threw the ash. I aimed for its head, a desperate, hopeless gesture. The cloud of fine, grey dust billowed into the air, right in its face.

It gave a roar of surprise and pain, not from injury, but from the sudden blindness. It shook its massive head, snorting and coughing, its eyes momentarily useless. That was my chance. I did not run. I charged. I screamed, a raw, ragged sound torn from my own throat, and I lunged forward, swinging the heavy fire poker with all my strength.

I aimed for its head, but in the dark I missed. The iron poker connected with its shoulder with a sickening, wet crunch. The creature shrieked, a high pitched sound of agony that was terrifyingly unlike its growl. It stumbled back, clutching at its shoulder. It could not see me. It was hurt. It was confused.

It swiped blindly with its long arm, claws extended. I felt the air move as they sliced past my face. I scrambled back, out of its reach. The creature bellowed in frustration and stumbled back out of the doorway, into the moonlight. It stood in the clearing for a moment, a wounded, terrifying giant, shaking its head to clear its vision. It looked at the cabin, then it looked at its shoulder. Then it turned and lumbered off into the darkness of the trees, a trail of guttural moans fading behind it.

I slammed the broken door shut and frantically looked for something to brace it with. I dragged the heavy wooden table, wedging it against the door. It would not hold against another charge, but it was something.

I stood in the middle of the room, gasping for breath, the fire poker still clutched in my hand. It was gone. I had hurt it. I had driven it off. The hunt was not over. I knew that. It would be back. It was intelligent, and now it was wounded. It would be angry. But I had survived the encounter. I had faced the hunter on my chosen ground, and I was the one still standing. In the silence of the ruined cabin, surrounded by the smell of fear and ancient ash, that was more than a victory. It was a resurrection.

I stood there for a long time. The silence that followed the creature’s retreat was heavier than any sound. It was a vacuum, pulling at my senses, making me doubt what had just happened. My heart hammered against my ribs, a frantic drumbeat in the quiet. My arms trembled, not from cold but from the violent aftermath of adrenaline. The iron poker felt like it had fused to my hand. I had to consciously uncurl my fingers, one by one, to let it fall. It hit the wooden floor with a loud clang that made me jump.

The cabin was a ruin. Splintered wood and cold air. The table I had wedged against the door was a pathetic barrier. A child could push it aside. I looked at the doorway, a gaping black mouth that had swallowed the night. I had to close it. I had to secure it. The thought was a single, hard command in my mind. The hunter would be back. Not might be. Would be. Wounded animals are the most dangerous. They do not run to escape. They run to find a place to turn, a place to make their final stand. Or to heal, and to let their rage and pain fester into something even more terrifying.

I moved slowly at first, my legs stiff and unsteady. I picked up the remains of the shattered door. It was heavy, solid oak, but it was in three pieces. Useless. I let it drop. I looked around the small cabin. There was the bed frame, the table, the one chair I had not broken. My eyes fell on the bed. It was heavy, built into the wall on one side. I could break it apart. I could use the timbers to build a new barricade.

I found the axe. It was propped in the corner by the fireplace, where I had left it after chopping kindling. Its head was heavy and sharp. Its handle was smooth and familiar in my hands. I was no stranger to an axe. I had been splitting my own wood since I was a boy. I went to the bed frame and began to work. The sound of the axe biting into the wood was loud and shocking in the stillness. Each swing was a declaration. I am still here. I am still fighting. I worked with a feverish energy, sweat stinging my eyes. I broke the bed into a pile of rough hewn planks.

I took the planks to the doorway. I began to nail them in place, using the long, rustic nails I found in a coffee can on the mantelpiece. I hammered them into the door frame, one after another, layering the planks until the opening was sealed. It was not a door. It was a wall. A crude, ugly wall of splintered wood. There was no way out, but there was also no easy way in. As I hammered the last nail, I noticed the sky through the small, grimy window. It was turning from black to a deep, bruised purple. Dawn was coming.

With the barricade finished, a strange calm settled over me. The immediate danger had been walled off. I had a few hours, perhaps. I went to the fireplace. The embers were still glowing. I carefully added small pieces of wood, coaxing the flames back to life. The fire was my only ally in this. It gave me light. It gave me warmth. It gave me ash. I looked at the pile of grey dust on the hearth. My last weapon. A weapon born of desperation. I had to have more.

I tended the fire, feeding it logs until it roared, a hungry beast in its stone cage. Then I waited. The waiting was the hardest part. The silence returned, but this time it was different. It was not empty. It was filled with my own thoughts. The creature’s face, or what I had seen of it, was burned into my mind. The glint of its eyes. The shape of its head. The sound of its shriek of pain. I had hurt it. I had made it bleed. The thought did not bring me comfort. It brought a cold, hard clarity. This was not a fight for survival anymore. It was a war. And in a war, there is no defense. There is only attack.

I could not stay here. I could not wait for it to return, healed and stronger. I could not wait for it to tear down my new wall, plank by plank. The hunter had become the hunted. And now, the hunted had to become the hunter. The thought was insane. I was one man, with an axe and a fire poker. It was a monster from a nightmare. But the alternative was to sit here and wait for death. My resurrection had to mean something. It could not be a brief, fleeting moment of defiance before the end. It had to be the beginning.

I made my decision. I would not wait for the creature to come to me. I would go to it. I would find it in its lair, while it was wounded and weak. I would finish what I had started.

The sun was a pale sliver on the horizon when I began to prepare. I moved with a new purpose. Every action had a meaning. Every choice was a matter of life and death. I needed weapons. I took the fire poker. Its end was bent from the impact with the creature’s shoulder. I put it in the fire, heating it until it glowed a dull red. I took it out and hammered it straight on the stone hearth. I sharpened the point on the rough stone, grinding it back and forth until it was a crude but effective spear.

I took the axe. It was my primary weapon. Heavy and brutal. I ran my thumb over the edge. It was sharp enough. I found a small utility knife in my pack. It felt like a toy compared to the axe, but I put it in my pocket anyway.

I needed a way to carry the ash. I could not just throw it from the hearth. I found a small leather pouch I used for carrying tobacco. I emptied it and filled it with the finest, lightest ash from the fireplace. It was a grey, dusty bomb. My secret weapon.

I needed supplies. I had no idea how long this would take. I found a small canvas sack. I put in a full canteen of water. I added the last of my dried beef and a handful of nuts. It was not much, but it would have to do.

I dressed in my warmest clothes, layering them against the morning chill. I looked around the cabin one last time. The broken furniture. The sealed doorway. The dying fire. It was a tomb. I was leaving it behind.

To get out, I had to use the axe again. Not on my new wall, but on the back wall of the cabin. It was the farthest point from the front door, from the direction the creature had fled. I chose a spot between two logs and began to chop. It was slow, hard work. The logs were thick and solid. But my resolve was harder. I hacked and splintered and tore at the wood until I had made an opening just big enough to squeeze through.

I stood on the threshold of my own escape. The air outside was cold and clean. It smelled of pine and wet earth. I took a deep breath. I pushed the sack of supplies through the hole first. Then I slid the axe and the poker through. Finally, I squeezed my own body through the rough opening, scraping my back on the splintered wood. I was out.

The sun was just beginning to touch the tops of the trees. The forest was waking up. A bird called out, a clear, sharp note in the quiet air. I stood for a moment, letting my eyes adjust. I was a trespasser here. This was its world.

I retrieved my weapons and my pack. I did not look back at the cabin. It was part of a life that was already over. My new life started here, in the cold morning light, with an axe in my hand and a trail of blood to follow.

I circled around the cabin, keeping to the trees. I found the spot where the creature had stood in the moonlight. The ground was disturbed. The tracks were huge, splayed, and deep. And there was blood. A dark, almost black patch on the pine needles. It was real. I had not imagined it.

I found the first drop of its trail a few feet away. A single, glistening spot on a dead leaf. Then another, a few feet beyond that. And another. The trail was clear. It was heading north, deeper into the forest, toward the rising slopes of the mountain that loomed over the valley.

I began to follow. I moved slowly, carefully, my senses on fire. Every snapped twig, every rustle of leaves, sent a jolt through me. The forest was no longer a place of beauty. It was a place of danger. The trees were not just trees. They were hiding places. The shadows were not just shadows. They were ambushes.

The trail was easy to follow at first. The creature had been bleeding freely, moving quickly and carelessly. It had crashed through the undergrowth, leaving a clear path of broken branches and trampled ferns. It was angry and in pain. I could feel its desperation in the violence of its passage.

I walked for what felt like hours. The sun climbed higher in the sky, filtering down through the dense canopy of leaves. The trail grew steeper. I was climbing the side of the mountain now. The ground was rocky and treacherous. The blood trail became less frequent. The creature was slowing down. Its wound was clotting. Or it was losing less blood. I did not know which was worse.

I found a place where it had rested. There was a large pool of blood on a bed of moss, next to a large boulder. A piece of its dark, coarse fur was caught on the rough bark of a pine tree. I touched it. It was stiff and wiry. I imagined the beast that wore it, and a fresh wave of fear washed over me. What was I doing? I was a man. A simple man. I was hunting a monster. This was suicide.

I leaned against a tree, my breath coming in ragged gasps. The axe felt impossibly heavy in my hand. I could turn back. I could go back to the cabin, reinforce my wall, and pray. But I knew that was a death sentence. The fear would kill me long before the creature did. I looked at the blood on the moss. It was real. It could bleed. And if it could bleed, it could die.

I pushed myself away from the tree. The moment of doubt had passed. I was not the same man who had cowered in the cabin. That man was gone. I was something else now. Something harder. Something colder.

I continued to climb. The air grew thinner. The trees became sparse, replaced by jagged rocks and hardy, windswept shrubs. The trail was almost gone now. Just a single drop of dried blood every twenty or thirty feet. I had to search for each one, my eyes scanning the ground, the rocks, the leaves. I was not just a hunter. I was a tracker.

The trail led me to a narrow ravine, a deep scar in the side of the mountain. The walls were steep and sheer. At the bottom, a small stream trickled over smooth, grey stones. The creature had gone down into the ravine. I followed, my boots slipping on the loose gravel.

At the bottom of the ravine, the trail was gone completely. The water had washed away any trace of blood. I stood in the middle of the stream, the cold water soaking through my boots. I was lost. The creature could be anywhere. It could be behind me. It could be above me, watching from the rim of the ravine.

I listened. I closed my eyes and listened. The only sound was the gentle murmur of the water and the whisper of the wind. I stood perfectly still, trying to hear something else. A breath. A footstep. A growl.

Then I heard it. It was not a growl. It was a low, guttural sound, like stone grinding against stone. It was a sound of pain. It came from somewhere upstream, farther up the ravine. It was close.

I moved toward the sound, my heart pounding in my chest. I held the axe in one hand and the fire poker in the other. I was ready. The ravine narrowed, the rock walls closing in on me. I rounded a sharp bend, and I saw it.

It was a cave. A dark, jagged opening in the rock face, hidden by a curtain of hanging moss. The sound was coming from inside. It was the creature’s lair.

I stopped. I stood before the dark opening, the final door. The smell that came from the cave was foul. It was the smell of a wet animal, of old blood, of decay. It was the smell of death.

This was it. The end of the hunt. I had found it. It was wounded. It was cornered. But this was its home. This was its territory. Inside that cave, it was the king. I was the intruder.

I took a quiet step back, melting into the shadow of an overhanging rock. I needed a plan. I could not just charge in. That would be suicide. I looked at the axe in my hand. I looked at the pouch of ash tied to my belt. I had one advantage. The element of surprise. And I had the ash.

I sat down in the shadows, my back against the cold stone. I waited. I watched the entrance to the cave. I listened to the pained sounds from within. The sun was beginning to set, painting the tops of the ravine walls in shades of orange and red. The night was coming. My night in the cabin had been a nightmare of defense. This night would be a vigil of attack. I would wait for the deepest part of the darkness, the time when all creatures, even monsters, sleep the soundest. I would let it think it was safe. I would let it rest. And then, I would bring the fire and the iron. I would descend into its hell and finish this. The hunted had become the hunter. And the hunter was now waiting at the door.

The moon rose, a sliver of bone in the black sky. It cast a pale, weak light that did little to pierce the profound darkness of the ravine. The air grew colder, biting at the exposed skin on my face and hands. I pulled the collar of my torn jacket tighter around my neck. My stomach growled, a hollow and pathetic sound against the vast silence. I had not eaten since the stale bread in the cabin, an eternity ago. The hunger was a dull ache, a distant cousin to the sharp throb in my leg and the burning fire in my shoulder. I ignored it. Hunger was a weakness I could not afford.

The pained sounds from the cave had subsided. For a long time, there had been a rhythmic, wretched scraping, the sound the creature made when it shifted its weight. Now, there was nothing. The silence was heavier than the sound had been. It was a weighted blanket of suspense. Was it dead? Had it succumbed to its wounds? I did not dare to hope. Hope was a fool’s luxury. It was more likely sleeping. Its monstrous body knitting itself back together, its unnatural vitality overcoming the wound I had given it. I had to assume it was healing. I had to assume it would be stronger than ever when it woke.

I unwrapped the small, half-burnt piece of wood I had saved from the cabin’s fireplace. With my knife, I began to whittle, not to create anything, but for the simple, repetitive motion. Shaving slivers of wood, watching them fall to the gravel. The small, scraping sounds were a comfort, a tiny assertion of my own presence in the overwhelming quiet. My thoughts drifted. I saw the creature’s eyes again, burning with a cold intelligence in the firelight. I saw the splintered door of my cabin. I felt the phantom pain of its claws tearing into my flesh. Fear was a constant companion, a cold knot in my gut. But something else was there now too. A coldness that was not from the night air. It was a hard, still thing in the center of my being. The fear was a fire around it, but the core was ice. I had been a man of quiet books and warm fires. A man who startled at loud noises. That man was gone. He had died in the cabin, or perhaps on the long, bloody trail. The man sitting in the dark, sharpening a piece of wood with a hunting knife, was someone else. Something else.

Hours passed. The moon climbed higher, then began its slow descent. The stars were brilliant, cold points of light in a sea of ink. They offered no warmth, no guidance. They were simply witnesses. I finished with the piece of wood, whittling it down to nothing. I ran a thumb along the edge of my axe. It was not as sharp as I would have liked. I found a flat, smooth river stone and began to hone the blade. Scrape. Scrape. Scrape. The sound was loud in the night. I imagined the creature in its lair, its ears twitching at the sound. Let it hear. Let it know something was out here. Let it wonder. The fear I had felt for myself was gone, replaced by a strange, predatory focus. I was no longer the rabbit. I was the wolf.

I checked my other weapon. The fire poker. It was heavy, solid iron. The end was pointed, blackened from the hearth. It felt good in my hand. It was a straightforward tool. It was made for one purpose. To stab. To pierce. I thought about the cabin, about the life I had lost. The books I would never read. The quiet mornings I would never again enjoy. There was a pang of something, a brief flicker of grief, but I smothered it. That life was over. This was my life now. This ravine. This night. This hunt.

The deepest part of the night arrived. The time between three and four in the morning, the hour of the wolf. The world seemed to hold its breath. The moon was gone. The only light came from the indifferent stars. The air was still and frigid. If the creature was going to sleep soundly, it would be now. The silence from the cave was absolute. It was time.

I rose to my feet, my joints cracking in protest. The cold had settled deep in my bones. I moved slowly, deliberately, forcing blood back into my numb limbs. I walked to the stream and splashed the icy water on my face. The shock was electric, chasing away the last vestiges of fatigue. I was awake. I was ready.

I approached the cave mouth, my boots making no sound on the damp stone. The curtain of moss was a black veil in the darkness. The smell was still there, thick and cloying. I untied the small leather pouch from my belt. The ash. The last remnant of my home, of the fire that had almost been my tomb. I poured a small amount into my palm. It was soft and fine. I walked a few paces back from the entrance and, with a steady hand, began to pour a thin, unbroken line of ash on the ground, creating a wide semicircle around the mouth of the cave. An old superstition. A desperate prayer. A line of demarcation. On one side, the world I had left. On the other, the hell I was about to enter. If it tried to escape, it would have to cross the ash. I did not know if it would work, but it was all I had. It was a symbol. I was trapping it. I was a judge drawing a line.

With the line complete, I took a final breath of clean, cold night air. I held it in my lungs, a final taste of the living world. Then, I exhorted, and stepped forward. I pushed aside the hanging moss. The smell became overwhelming, a physical force that made my eyes water and my stomach churn. I pushed through it. I stepped into the blackness.

The darkness was total. It was a physical presence, pressing in on me from all sides. I stood just inside the entrance, my back to the rock, letting my eyes adjust. It was a slow, painful process. Shapes began to emerge from the gloom, faint and ghostly. The cave was not a straight tunnel. It sloped downward, turning sharply to the left. The air was thick with humidity, and the sound of dripping water echoed from somewhere deep within. Drip. Drip. Drip. It was a maddeningly slow clock, ticking away the seconds of my life.

I took a step. My boot slid on something slick and viscous. I caught my balance, my heart hammering against my ribs. I did not need to see it to know what it was. Blood. Old and new. I moved forward, one hand against the rough, damp wall for guidance. The axe was in my right hand, the poker in my left. I held them out in front of me, probing the darkness. The passage was narrow, the rock scraping against my shoulders. I was a worm crawling into the guts of the earth.

I rounded the bend. The passage opened up into a larger chamber. It was not a huge cavern, but bigger than my cabin’s main room. Here, some faint light filtered down from a crack in the ceiling, a fissure high above that revealed a sliver of the star-dusted sky. It was just enough to see. And what I saw would be burned into my memory forever.

The floor was a carpet of bones. They were everywhere. Piled in corners, scattered across the ground. Some were clearly animal. The skulls of deer, the ribcages of wolves. But others… others were not. I saw a slender bone that looked horribly like a human femur. I saw a small, shattered ribcage that could have belonged to a child. This was not just a lair. It was a slaughterhouse. It was a tomb.

And in the center of the chamber, I saw it.

It was not sleeping. It was awake. It was hunched over, its back to me. It was making a low, wet, rhythmic sound. A sound of tearing. A sound of eating. Before it was the fresh carcass of a young buck, its body ripped open, steam rising from its entrails into the cold air. The creature was feasting. It had gone out while I waited, a silent hunter in the night, and it had made a kill. It was not weak. It was not dying. It was replenishing its strength.

My blood ran cold. My plan, my patient vigil, had been for nothing. I had underestimated it. I had sat outside like a fool while it healed and fed. Rage, hot and pure, burned away the last of my fear. It was a clean, purifying fire. I was not going to die cowering in a cave. If I was to die, I would die fighting.

I took a step forward, and a loose stone skittered under my boot. The sound was a gunshot in the cavern.

The creature froze. The tearing sound stopped. Its long, pointed ears swiveled in my direction. Slowly, horribly, it began to turn. The head came around first, its neck impossibly long and flexible. The glowing eyes found me, locking onto my own. There was no surprise in them. There was no fear. There was only a cold, ancient hatred. It rose to its full height, dwarfing me. It was bigger than I remembered, a towering silhouette of muscle and sinew against the faint light. The wound in its side was still there, a dark, weeping gash, but it did not seem to slow it down. It let out a low growl, not of pain, but of annoyance. A sound that said, you. The intruder. The nuisance.

It took a step toward me, and I reacted. I did not think. I simply moved. I lunged forward, not at the creature, but to the side. I swept the poker in a wide arc, sending a shower of bones clattering across the floor. The distraction was momentary, but it was enough. The creature’s eyes flickered to the sound, and in that instant, I was on it.

I brought the axe down with all my strength, aiming for its head. It was impossibly fast. It ducked, the axe blade scraping harmlessly against the stone of its skull with a shower of sparks. The impact jarred my arm to the shoulder. It lunged, its jaws snapping shut inches from my face. I could smell its breath, the fetid stench of rot and fresh meat. I stumbled back, bringing the poker up between us. It swiped at me with a clawed hand, and I parried with the iron shaft. The clang of claw on iron rang through the cave.

We circled each other, a grim dance in the bone-strewn arena. It was a predator, confident in its own territory. I was a cornered rat, running on adrenaline and rage. It lunged again, and this time a claw caught me, tearing a long gash down my thigh. The pain was blinding, a white-hot fire. I cried out, falling to one knee. This was it. This was the end.

The creature loomed over me, a triumphant snarl on its lipless mouth. It raised a clawed hand for the killing blow. I looked up into its merciless eyes, and I saw not a monster, but a killer. And a killer could be killed. With my free hand, I scooped up a handful of dirt and bone dust from the cave floor and flung it into its face.

It roared in surprise and fury, shaking its head, trying to clear its eyes. It was the opening I needed. I surged up from the ground, ignoring the fire in my leg. I drove the pointed end of the fire poker forward with every ounce of strength I had left. I did not aim for its chest or its head. I aimed for the wound. The one I had given it. The one it thought was healing.

The iron point sank into the soft, torn flesh of its side. It went deep. The creature let out a sound I had never heard before. It was not a roar of anger or a growl of pain. It was a high pitched, piercing shriek of pure agony. It was the sound of its very being coming undone. It thrashed wildly, trying to dislodge the poker, but it was stuck fast, wedged between its ribs.

It swatted at me, a blind, desperate blow that sent me flying across the chamber. I landed hard against the far wall, the axe falling from my numb fingers. My head hit the rock, and the world exploded into a starburst of pain. I lay there, dazed, my vision swimming. I could see the creature, a great, dark shape thrashing in the center of the cave. It was trying to pull the poker out, its claws tearing at its own flesh. Blood, thick and black, poured from the wound.

Slowly, I pushed myself up. My body was a symphony of pain. Every muscle screamed. But I was alive. I looked for the axe. It lay a few feet away, its blade gleaming faintly. I crawled toward it, my movements slow and agonizing. The creature was weakening. Its movements were becoming slower, more erratic. It staggered, took a step, and then its legs gave out. It collapsed onto the floor with a ground shaking thud.

It was still alive. Its chest rose and fell in ragged, shuddering gasps. Its eyes, no longer burning with hatred, were wide with shock and confusion. They were the eyes of a dying animal. It looked at me, and I looked back. In that final moment, the roles were reversed. It was the helpless thing on the ground, and I was the one standing.

I picked up the axe. I walked over to the creature, my shadow falling over its twitching form. I stood over its head. It looked up at me. There was no more fight in it. There was only the end. I raised the axe high above my head. It was heavy. My arms trembled with exhaustion. But my resolve was steel. This was for the cabin. This was for the quiet life I had lost. This was for the man I used to be.

I brought the axe down.

The sound echoed in the sudden, absolute silence of the cave. And then there was nothing. Only the slow, steady drip of water. Drip. Drip. Drip. I stood there for a long time, the axe in my hand, my body covered in filth and blood. I looked at what I had done. The hunter was dead. The hunt was over. But as I stood in the darkness, surrounded by the bones of the dead, I did not feel like a victor. I did not feel like a survivor. I felt like the new king of hell. And I was alone in my kingdom.
