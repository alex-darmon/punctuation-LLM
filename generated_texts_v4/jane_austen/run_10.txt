"Reserved, yes," Clara conceded, "but not, I think, in an unfriendly manner. More in a thoughtful one. He listens intently, even to the most insipid of conversations, as if seeking some hidden meaning."

"Perhaps he is merely bored," Eleanor suggested with a mischievous glint in her eye.

Clara shook her head gently. "I do not believe so. There is a quality about his attention, a certain gravity, that suggests something deeper than mere polite endurance. But tell me, Eleanor, have you had the opportunity to converse with him?"

"Briefly, a mere exchange of civilities as he passed," Eleanor replied. "He inquired after my comfort, and complimented Lady Gresham's hospitality. Perfectly proper, perfectly polite, and perfectly unrevealing. He offers little to grasp, does he not? Like attempting to hold water in one's hand."

Clara considered this. "Perhaps he chooses to reveal little. Some minds, I find, are like complex mechanisms, requiring careful study to comprehend their workings."

"Or perhaps he is merely a very quiet man, Clara," Eleanor countered with a light laugh, "and you, with your penchant for philosophical inquiry, are attributing him with depths he does not possess."

Clara merely smiled, unwilling to concede the point entirely. She felt a quiet conviction that there was more to Mr. Ashworth than met the superficial eye. The cotillion was now in full swing, the music filling the room with a lively, rhythmic pulse. Clara observed the dancers, her gaze occasionally drifting back to Mr. Ashworth, who was now engaged in a rather animated, though still formal, discussion with Mr. Davies. She wondered what subject could possibly animate Mr. Davies to such an extent, and felt a moment of pity for Mr. Ashworth, who was enduring it with such stoicism.

Lady Gresham, seeing Clara and Eleanor standing together, now approached them, her expression one of solicitous concern. "My dear Clara, Eleanor, are you not dancing? The cotillion is quite delightful this evening."

"We were just discussing the merits of observation, Lady Gresham," Eleanor said with a charming smile, "and enjoying the music from a distance."

"Nonsense, my dears," Lady Gresham declared with a benevolent firmness. "One must participate. Clara, I believe Mr. Henderson is looking for a partner, and Eleanor, Colonel Fitzwilliam would be delighted, I am sure."

Clara suppressed a sigh. Mr. Henderson was indeed advancing towards them, his usual air of hopeful anticipation upon his features. "Lady Gresham," he began, bowing slightly, "might I have the honour of this dance with Miss Albright?"

Lady Gresham beamed. "Indeed, Mr. Henderson. Clara, off you go."

Clara offered Mr. Henderson a polite, though somewhat forced, smile and allowed him to lead her onto the dance floor. The prospect of an entire cotillion with Mr. Henderson, whose conversation would undoubtedly revolve around the latest agricultural yields or the merits of a new plough, filled her with a familiar sense of resignation. As they took their places, she cast a quick glance towards Mr. Ashworth, only to find him still cornered by Mr. Davies. A fleeting moment of shared commiseration, perhaps, crossed her mind, before she focused on the intricate steps of the dance.

The cotillion, with its precise formations and elegant movements, demanded a certain degree of concentration, even from practiced dancers like Clara. Mr. Henderson, though a well-meaning partner, was not particularly graceful, and Clara found herself subtly guiding him through some of the more complex figures, all while maintaining a polite smile and an air of effortless charm. His conversation, as predicted, quickly turned to the state of the harvest and the fluctuating prices of corn, punctuated by occasional, rather heavy-handed compliments on her dress or her dancing. Clara responded with the appropriate polite phrases, her mind, however, often drifting. She found herself mentally comparing Mr. Henderson's somewhat plodding movements and predictable conversation to the quiet intelligence and reserved grace she had observed in Mr. Ashworth. The contrast was stark, and it deepened her nascent curiosity about the newcomer.

The dance progressed, and with each turn and promenade, Clara found her gaze inadvertently drawn towards the corner where Mr. Ashworth still stood. He seemed to have managed to extricate himself from Mr. Davies at last, and was now conversing, rather more animatedly it seemed, with Colonel Fitzwilliam. The Colonel, a man of worldly experience and genuine intellectual curiosity, was a far more suitable conversational partner, Clara mused, and she imagined their discourse would be of a significantly higher caliber. She wondered what subjects they might be discussing. Politics, perhaps, or military campaigns, or even the latest literary works. She wished she were privy to their conversation.
