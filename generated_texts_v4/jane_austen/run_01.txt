The drawing-room at Mrs. Elmwood’s, though not extensive, was on this particular morning quite alive with conversation, and seemed scarcely capable of holding all the eager sentiments poured forth within its walls. Mr. Finch, lately arrived in the neighbourhood, and paying his first formal call, was holding forth on the superior merits of the London season, a subject which, while it entertained the placid Mrs. Elmwood with its novelty, somewhat perplexed her niece, Miss Charlotte Grey, whose experience extended no farther than the annual assembly at Northwood, and a summer spent once at Bath. He seemed resolved that every young lady present should be made aware of the advantages of metropolitan life, and scarcely allowed Miss Eleanor Thorne, a visitor from some more fashionable quarter of the country, to interpose a word.

“Indeed, ma’am,” said he, turning to Mrs. Elmwood with an air of profound satisfaction, “the very air of London seems to invigorate the faculties; one finds oneself perpetually engaged in some pursuit of taste or intellect, quite unlike the rather languid existence one is often condemned to in the provinces. Do you not find it so, Miss Grey? Does not your spirit long for a broader sphere, for the constant stimulus of refined society?” Charlotte, whose spirit at that moment longed only for the safe arrival of the postman with her weekly magazine, coloured slightly, and replied, “I confess, sir, that I have ever found contentment within my present sphere; and though I do not doubt the charms of London, I apprehend they might prove too overwhelming for one accustomed to simpler pleasures.” Mrs. Elmwood nodded approvingly; Miss Eleanor, however, gave a little cough, and Mr. Finch, perceiving it, turned his attention to her with renewed vigour.

“Ah, Miss Thorne, you, I am sure, comprehend my meaning entirely. You have lived amidst the fashionable world; you know the very pulse of society.
