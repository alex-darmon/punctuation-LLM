The morning call had lingered, as morning calls were apt to do when new acquisitions lent fresh impetus to conversation. Mr. Pelham, a young gentleman whose fortune, though modest, seemed to inflate his self-importance beyond measure, had just finished assembling the new music stand. It was an elaborate affair of polished mahogany and brass, recently arrived from London, a gift from Mrs. Albright's distant cousin, Mr. Thorne. He stood back, surveying his handiwork with a self-congratulatory air, wiping his brow with a silk handkerchief that smelled faintly of verbena.

“There,” he declared, addressing Mrs. Albright, “it stands complete. A most excellent mechanism, one must admit. The craftsmanship, though perhaps not of the very first order, shows a certain earnestness of endeavour. One may imagine Mr. Thorne himself poring over catalogues, selecting with the utmost care.”

Mrs. Albright, a lady of effusive gratitude and rather less discernment, clapped her hands together. “Oh, Mr. Pelham, you are too good! What an invaluable help you have been. I declare I should never have managed it without your clever fingers. My dear Clara,” she turned to Miss Annesley, who sat quietly observing from a small armchair, “is it not quite the most elegant thing? And to think of Mr. Thorne’s kindness, remembering us so. I must write to him at once. Do you imagine he tasked his London agent with the purchase immediately upon deciding, or had he merely a general wish, a direction indefinite as to time, to depend upon a happy coincidence of availability?”

Miss Annesley, a young woman whose composure was rarely ruffled by such effusions, replied with a forced calmness, “Till I have read Mrs. Thorne’s account of its sending, I can imagine nothing with any confidence. It must be all conjecture.”

“Conjecture, yes, sometimes one conjectures right, and sometimes one conjectures wrong,” Mr. Pelham rejoined, bending to tighten a screw. “I wish I could conjecture how soon I shall make this final brace quite firm. What nonsense one talks, Miss Annesley, when hard at work, if one talks at all. Your real workmen, I suppose, hold their tongues; but we gentlemen labourers, if we get hold of a word. Miss Annesley said something about conjecturing. There, it is done. I have the pleasure, madam, I address Mrs. Albright, of presenting your music stand, assembled for the present.”

He was very warmly thanked by Mrs. Albright, and by Mrs. Davies, a friend of the family, who seemed merely polite. To escape a little from the former’s unrestrained praise, he went to the pianoforte and begged Miss Annesley, who was still sitting nearby, to play something.

“If you are very kind,” said he, “it will be one of the airs we heard last night; allow me to live them over again. You did not enjoy them as I did; you appeared tired the whole time. I believe you were glad we ceased playing; but I would have given worlds, all the worlds one ever has to give, for another half hour.”

She played. “What felicity it is to hear a tune again which has made one happy! If I mistake not that was played at the assembly in Upper Cross.” She looked up at him for a moment, coloured deeply, and played something else. He took some sheet music from a chair near the pianoforte, and turning to Mrs. Davies, said, “Here is something quite new to me. Do you know it? Vivaldi. And here are a new set of Scottish ballads. That, from such a quarter, one might expect. This was all sent with the stand. Very thoughtful of Mr. Thorne, was not it? He knew Miss Annesley could have no new music here. I honour that part of the attention particularly; it shows it to have been so thoroughly from the heart. Nothing hastily done; nothing incomplete. True affection only could have prompted it.”

Mrs. Davies wished he would be less pointed, yet could not help being amused; and when on glancing her eye towards Clara Annesley she caught the remains of a smile, when she saw that with all the deep blush of consciousness, there had been a smile of secret delight, she had less scruple in the amusement, and much less compunction with respect to her. This amiable, upright, perfect Miss Annesley was apparently cherishing very reprehensible feelings. He brought all the music to her, and they looked it over together. Mrs. Davies took the opportunity of whispering, “You speak too plain. She must understand you.”

“I hope she does. I would have her understand me. I am not in the least ashamed of my meaning.”

“But really, I am half ashamed, and wish I had never taken up the idea.”

“I am very glad you did, and that you communicated it to me. I have now a key to all her odd looks and ways. Leave shame to her. If she does wrong, she ought to feel it.”

“She is not entirely without it, I think.”

“I do not see much sign of it. She is playing *My Love is Like a Red, Red Rose* at this moment, *his* favourite.”

Shortly afterwards Mrs. Albright, passing near the window, descried Mr. Henderson on horseback not far off. “Mr. Henderson, I declare! I must speak to him if possible, just to thank him. I will not open the window here; it would give you all cold; but I can go into the antechamber, you know. I dare say he will come in when he knows who is here. Quite delightful to have you all meet so! Our little room so honoured!” She was in the adjoining chamber while she still spoke, and opening the casement there, immediately called Mr. Henderson’s attention, and every syllable of their conversation was as distinctly heard by the others, as if it had passed within the same apartment.

“How d’ye do? How d’ye do? Very well, I thank you. So obliged to you for the ride last night. We were just in time; my cousin just ready for us. Pray come in; do come in. You will find some friends here.” So began Mrs. Albright; and Mr. Henderson seemed determined to be heard in his turn, for most resolutely and commandingly did he say, “How is your niece, Mrs. Albright? I want to inquire after you all, but particularly your niece. How is Miss Annesley? I hope she caught no cold last night. How is she to day? Tell me how Miss Annesley is.” And Mrs. Albright was obliged to give a direct answer before he would hear her in any thing else. The listeners were amused; and Mrs. Davies gave Clara a look of particular meaning. But Clara still shook her head in steady scepticism.

“So obliged to you! So very much obliged to you for the ride,” resumed Mrs. Albright. He cut her short with, “I am going to Ashworth. Can I do any thing for you?”

“Oh! dear, Ashworth, are you? Mrs. Proctor was saying the other day she wanted something from Ashworth.”

“Mrs. Proctor has servants to send. Can I do any thing for *you*?”

“No, I thank you. But do come in. Who do you think is here? Miss Annesley and Mrs. Davies; so kind as to call to hear the new music stand. Do put up your horse at the George, and come in.”

“Well,” said he, in a deliberating manner, “for five minutes, perhaps.”

“And here is Mrs. Weston and Mr. Frank Churchill too! Quite delightful; so many friends!” This was an accidental slip, referring to a party not present. “And here is Mr. Pelham too! Quite delightful; so many friends!”

“No, not now, I thank you. I could not stay two minutes. I must get on to Ashworth as fast as I can.”

“Oh! do come in. They will be so very happy to see you.”

“No, no; your room is full enough. I will call another day, and see the music stand.”

“Well, I am so sorry! Oh! Mr. Henderson, what a delightful party last night; how extremely pleasant. Did you ever see such conversation? Was not it delightful? Miss Annesley and Mr. Pelham; I never saw any thing equal to it.”

“Oh! very delightful indeed; I can say nothing less, for I suppose Miss Annesley and Mr. Pelham are hearing every thing that passes. And, raising his voice still more, I do not see why Mrs. Davies should not be mentioned too. I think Mrs. Davies has a very good understanding; and Mrs. Albright is the very best hostess, without exception, in the county. Now, if your friends have any gratitude, they will say something pretty loud about you and me in return; but I cannot stay to hear it.”

“Oh! Mr. Henderson, one moment more; something of consequence, so shocked! Clara and I are both so shocked about the damson jam!”

“What is the matter now?”

“To think of your sending us all your store damson jam. You said you had a great many jars, and now you have not one left. We really are so shocked! Mrs. Finch may well be angry. William Giles mentioned it here. You should not have done it, indeed you should not. Ah! he is off. He never can bear to be thanked. But I thought he would have staid now, and it would have been a pity not to have mentioned. Well, returning to the room, I have not been able to succeed. Mr. Henderson cannot stop. He is going to Ashworth. He asked me if he could do any thing.”

“Yes,” said Clara, “we heard his kind offers, we heard every thing.”

“Oh! yes, my dear, I dare say you might, because you know, the door was open, and the window was open, and Mr. Henderson spoke loud. You must have heard every thing to be sure. ‘Can I do any thing for you at Ashworth?’ said he; so I just mentioned. Oh! Miss Annesley, must you be going? You seem but just come, so very obliging of you.” Clara found it really time to be at home; the visit had already lasted long; and on examining watches, so much of the morning was perceived to be gone, that Mrs. Davies and her companion taking leave also, could allow themselves only to walk with the two young ladies to the High Street corner, before they set off for their respective homes.

Clara and Miss Annesley continued their walk, having bid farewell to Mrs. Davies and her companion. The High Street was now busier than it had been when they first set out, and the prospect of returning home, though necessary, carried with it a quiet satisfaction for Clara, whose nature, while sociable, found a peculiar comfort in the domestic sphere after any prolonged excursion into the more demanding regions of social discourse. Miss Annesley, on the other hand, seemed to possess a more robust constitution for such engagements, her spirits remaining uniformly cheerful and her observations ever ready to spring forth, untainted by the slightest hint of fatigue.

"Mr. Henderson is certainly a most excellent man," observed Miss Annesley, breaking the short silence that had fallen between them, "and so very generous. To part with all his damson jam! It is almost too much to conceive of such selflessness."

Clara smiled faintly. "Indeed it is. My aunt was quite overcome with the news. She could speak of little else but the injustice to Mr. Henderson and the probable displeasure of Mrs. Finch. Though I must confess, the thought of Mrs. Finch's anger gives me a certain amusement, for she is a woman who finds occasion for displeasure in almost every circumstance, and her pronouncements are always so very decided."

"Oh, she is a character, is she not?" Miss Annesley responded with a light laugh. "But truly, Mr. Henderson's kindness is beyond compare. One rarely meets with such an open-handed disposition. It speaks volumes of his heart, does it not

It speaks volumes of his heart, does it not, for truly, to consider another's need before one's own, especially when it concerns so fine a preserve as damson jam, is a rare quality indeed.”

Clara, allowing a moment for the soft cadence of Miss Annesley’s voice to dissipate in the gentle breeze of the High Street, replied, “It does, Miss Annesley, it most certainly does. My aunt, as I mentioned, was quite struck by it. She declared him a saint, though I fancy she reserves that particular epithet for those who perform acts of kindness directly benefiting herself. Still, even her more measured pronouncements concerning Mr. Henderson always carry a note of profound respect. He is a man much esteemed in the neighbourhood, as you know, and this latest instance of his benevolence will surely only serve to heighten that regard, whatever Mrs. Finch may have to say on the matter.”

“Ah, Mrs. Finch!” Miss Annesley laughed again, a light, melodious sound that drew a momentary glance from a passing gentleman. “She is a constant source of entertainment, is she not? One never quite knows what new grievance she will find. But to be deprived of her usual share of Mr. Henderson’s jam, which I understand she considers almost her due, will surely provoke a lamentation of the grandest order. I confess I am almost eager to hear the tale of her disappointment, though I should never wish ill upon anyone, of course.”

“Nor I,” Clara agreed, her lips curving into a faint, knowing smile. “But one cannot help but anticipate the dramatic flourish with which she will recount the injustice. She will speak of scarcity, of impropriety, of the sudden and alarming disappearance of what she considers a staple of refined society. Her sense of personal injury, even when it is entirely vicarious, is truly something to behold. I imagine she will feel as though the very fabric of social order has been rent asunder, merely because a few dozen jars of preserve have been diverted from their accustomed path.”

They continued their walk, the High Street growing increasingly lively with carriages and pedestrians, the windows of the shops displaying their morning wares. The sun, though not yet high, promised a fair day, and the air held the crisp freshness of late spring. Clara felt a pleasant weariness beginning to settle upon her, a desire for the quiet domesticity of home, where the demands of social intercourse were less stringent and the familiar rhythms of daily life offered a comforting predictability. Miss Annesley, however, seemed invigorated by the bustle, her eyes sparkling as she took in the scene.

“It makes one quite desirous of a little excitement, does it not, Clara?” she ventured, turning her gaze from a milliner’s window to her companion. “All this talk of Mr. Henderson’s astonishing generosity and Mrs. Finch’s impending wrath. One wonders what will happen next. Will Mr. Henderson return from Ashworth with some exotic new variety of fruit to replace the lost damsons? Or will Mrs. Finch, in a fit of indignation, declare a boycott of all things Hendersonian, if one might coin such a term?” She giggled at her own whimsy.

Clara shook her head gently. “I doubt either extreme will come to pass, Miss Annesley. Mr. Henderson is a man of remarkable constancy, and while his generosity is indeed exceptional, I cannot imagine him attempting to replicate the damson supply on such short notice. As for Mrs. Finch, her indignation, though formidable, rarely translates into actions that inconvenience her in any significant way. She will lament, she will complain, she will probably send a servant to inquire after the exact quantity and destination of the lost jam, but she will not, I apprehend, cease to hold Mr. Henderson in high regard, especially given his generally amiable disposition and his readiness to assist with other matters when called upon. She may even, in time, see this as an opportunity to receive some other favour, a fresh trout perhaps, or a particularly well-grown cucumber from his garden, as a kind of compensatory offering.”

“You are too practical, Clara,” Miss Annesley chided playfully. “One must allow for a little drama in life, especially when it is of so harmless a nature as this. Think of the conversation it will provide at every tea table for weeks to come! Indeed, one might almost be grateful to Mr. Henderson for giving us such a topic.”

They had by now reached the intersection of the High Street and the lane leading to Clara’s aunt’s dwelling, a neat, modest house with a small, well-tended garden. As they turned the corner, Clara felt a true sense of relief.

“Well, here we are,” she said, slowing her pace slightly. “I imagine my aunt will be eager to hear all the particulars of Mrs. Davies’s report, though I suspect she has already drawn her own conclusions regarding the enormity of the affair.”

“And so she should,” Miss Annesley declared. “It is not every day that one hears of such a complete emptying of a larder, is it? One must commend Mr. Henderson’s spirit, even as one marvels at the sheer scale of his benevolence. I shall call upon you soon, Clara, to ascertain the latest developments. Perhaps Mrs. Finch will have issued a formal protest by then.”

With a final, cheerful farewell, Miss Annesley took her leave, promising a prompt return. Clara watched her go for a moment, admiring her unwavering good spirits, before opening the gate and proceeding up the short path to the front door. She let herself in, the familiar scent of beeswax and lavender immediately greeting her.

Her aunt, Mrs. Selby, was in the parlour, seated by the window with her needlework, though the threads lay idle in her lap. Her spectacles were pushed up onto her forehead, and her expression was one of thoughtful disquiet. The moment Clara entered, her aunt looked up, her eyes bright with a mixture of curiosity and perturbation.

“Ah, Clara, my dear, you are returned at last,” Mrs. Selby exclaimed, letting her needlework fall entirely. “I have been quite beside myself with speculation. What did Mrs. Davies say? Did she truly confirm the dreadful news? Is Mr. Henderson indeed entirely bereft of his damson preserves?”

Clara walked across the room and sat in an armchair opposite her aunt, untying the ribbons of her bonnet. “Indeed, Aunt, Mrs. Davies did confirm it all. She had it from Mr. Henderson himself, as he was leaving for Ashworth. Not a single jar remains, it would seem.”

Mrs. Selby pressed a hand to her chest, her countenance expressing a profound sense of shock. “Not one! Oh, the poor man! To be stripped clean, as it were, of a year’s labour and enjoyment. It is truly an astonishing thing to consider. And all for those poor folk, those unfortunates in Ashworth. Not that I begrudge them a single spoonful, mind you, for charity is a virtue, and Mr. Henderson is a man of God, I am sure. But to part with everything! One does wonder if it was entirely necessary. Surely a dozen or two jars would have sufficed? One must consider proportion, after all. There is such a thing as overdoing it, even in kindness.”

Clara smiled inwardly at her aunt’s dilemma, caught between admiration for generosity and a deeply ingrained sense of practical economy. “Mrs. Davies seemed to think Mr. Henderson acted without much deliberation, Aunt. She implied he simply gave them all, without counting, when the request was made.”

“Without counting!” Mrs. Selby gasped. “Well, that does explain it, I suppose. A man of such good heart, often forgets the practicalities. But still! And what of Mrs. Finch, Clara? Did Mrs. Davies have any intelligence as to her probable reaction? For I assure you, my dear, she will not take this lightly. Not lightly at all. She considers those preserves almost her birthright, or at least a solemn annual privilege. She values them above all other gifts, save perhaps the early asparagus from Mr. Davies’s own garden, which is, I grant you, quite excellent. But damson jam! It is a particular favourite of hers, and she prides herself on serving it at her morning calls, often remarking, with a significant glance in my direction, that Mr. Henderson’s damsons are incomparable.”

“Mrs. Davies, I am afraid, could only speculate as to Mrs. Finch’s reaction,” Clara replied, “though Miss Annesley and I indulged in a little conjecture on the way home. We both agreed that her displeasure would be considerable. Miss Annesley even suggested she might issue a formal protest.”

Mrs. Selby’s eyes widened further. “A formal protest! Well, I should not be surprised, not in the least. Mrs. Finch is a woman of decided opinions, and she does not hesitate to voice them, often with a vehemence that quite startles one. I recall once, when the baker, poor Mr. Jenkins, inadvertently sold her a loaf that was not quite up to her exacting standards, she sent it back with a servant and a note of such stern remonstrance that he almost considered closing his shop. And this is far more serious, Clara, far more serious than a mere loaf of bread. This is Mr. Henderson’s damson jam!”

The gravity with which her aunt pronounced the words, as if they held the weight of nations, amused Clara greatly. She rose to remove her bonnet and shawl, hanging them on the peg by the door. “Well, we shall doubtless hear the full extent of her feelings in due course, Aunt. Perhaps we should prepare ourselves for a visit, or at least a lengthy communication from her.”

“Indeed we should,” Mrs. Selby agreed, picking up her needlework again, though her gaze remained fixed on the middle distance, clearly contemplating the social ramifications of the great damson scarcity. “And to think, Mr. Giles mentioned it so casually. As if the disappearance of two hundred jars of jam were a matter of no consequence! Men are so very different, Clara. They do not comprehend the nuances of such things. The implications, you see. The ripple effect. It is not merely the jam itself, my dear, but the disruption it causes to established custom, to expectation, to the very fabric of our polite society.”

Clara listened with a fond indulgence, knowing that her aunt, like Mrs. Finch, found a certain relish in dissecting social intricacies, however minor the originating event. She moved towards the kitchen, thinking of luncheon, when a knock at the front door interrupted their reflections.

“Now who can that be?” Mrs. Selby wondered aloud, her brow furrowed. “It is rather early for callers, unless it is Mrs. Finch herself, come to deliver her lamentation in person. Oh dear, Clara, do you think you should answer it? I confess, I am not quite prepared to face her wrath just yet. My nerves, you know, are not what they once were.”

Clara, accustomed to her aunt’s occasional dramatic pronouncements about her nerves, nevertheless moved to the door. She opened it to find not Mrs. Finch, but a young boy, perhaps twelve years of age, dressed in a neat but slightly worn livery, holding a small, sealed letter. He touched his forelock respectfully.

“Good morning, Miss Selby,” the boy said, his voice a little breathless. “A letter for Mrs. Selby, if you please, from Mrs. Finch.”

Clara took the letter, the paper feeling crisp and slightly embossed under her fingers. “Thank you, young man. Is there an answer required?”

“No, Miss, only to deliver it with all speed,” he replied, already turning to depart.

Clara closed the door and returned to the parlour, holding the letter aloft. “Well, Aunt, your premonitions were correct, though it is not a personal visit, but a letter.” She handed it to Mrs. Selby, who took it with a trembling hand, as if it contained news of a national disaster.

“A letter!” Mrs. Selby exclaimed, peering at the elegant script of the address. “Indeed! She wastes no time. Open it, Clara, quickly. My fingers are quite stiff with the shock of it all. I can barely feel them.”

Clara gently broke the seal, a faint scent of lavender and something vaguely medicinal emanating from the paper, a characteristic blend Clara always associated with Mrs. Finch. She unfolded the single sheet, her eyes quickly scanning the familiar, somewhat ornate handwriting.

“It begins, ‘My Dearest Mrs. Selby’,” Clara read aloud, her voice calm and even. “‘I trust this missive finds you in tolerable health, though I confess my own spirits have been quite dashed by a most lamentable occurrence, the particulars of which have only just reached my ears, and which, I apprehend, must surely by now have occasioned you equal dismay. It concerns, I need hardly add, Mr. Henderson’s damson preserves.’”

Mrs. Selby nodded vigorously, a look of grim satisfaction settling upon her features. “Just as I thought! She goes straight to the point, does she not? A woman of purpose, whatever her faults. Continue, my dear, continue.”

Clara resumed reading: “‘I was informed this very morning, by my maid, who had it from Mr. William Giles’s housemaid, that the entirety of Mr. Henderson’s prodigious store of damson jam has been unceremoniously, and I might add, quite precipitously, dispatched to Ashworth. The sheer magnitude of this decision, not to mention its timing, has quite stunned me. I had, as you are aware, been eagerly anticipating my usual quarterly consignment, a tradition of some years standing, and one which Mr. Henderson has always, with the utmost civility, honoured without fail.’”

Mrs. Selby interjected, “Consignment! She makes it sound like a commercial transaction! Oh, Mrs. Finch, you are too much!” But there was a note of admiration in her voice, for the audacity of the phrasing.

Clara continued: “‘One must, of course, commend the charitable impulse, for compassion is a virtue no less important than prudence. However, to divest oneself of every single jar, without thought for the long-standing obligations, or indeed, the severe inconvenience to one’s regular beneficiaries, seems to me an act of considerable, indeed, almost wilful, improvidence. Where, I ask you, is the foresight? Where is the consideration for those whose domestic arrangements have been, for so many seasons, harmoniously aligned with the expectation of this particular delicacy? I assure you, my dear Mrs. Selby, my own breakfast table this morning felt quite bereft, a certain air of scarcity hanging over the buttered toast, a subtle but palpable sense of deprivation.’”

Clara paused, a small laugh escaping her lips. “A subtle but palpable sense of deprivation, Aunt! She truly is quite the dramatist.”

Mrs. Selby shook her head, though a faint smile played on her own lips. “Oh, she is quite extraordinary. But she has a point, Clara. It is a disruption. One does become accustomed to these things. It makes one feel unsettled. Go on, my dear.”

Clara cleared her throat and read on: “‘Moreover, the manner of this announcement, conveyed through the channel of servants’ gossip, rather than by Mr. Henderson himself, or by some more formal means, adds insult to injury, if I may be so bold as to express it thus. It suggests a want of proper decorum, a disregard for the sensibilities of those most directly affected. I confess myself quite perplexed by Mr. Henderson’s actions in this instance, though I have always held him in the highest regard, a gentleman of unimpeachable character and, until now, impeccable social judgment. This oversight, however, casts a somewhat perplexing shadow upon his otherwise sterling reputation.’”

“Oh, she is laying it on thick now,” Mrs. Selby murmured, evidently enjoying the performance. “Casting a shadow upon his sterling reputation! As if the man had committed some dreadful crime against the community. She does not mince words, does she?”

“She rarely does, Aunt,” Clara agreed, reading the concluding paragraph: “‘I feel it incumbent upon me to discuss this regrettable affair with you, my dearest Mrs. Selby, as I know you share my deep appreciation for order and propriety in all matters, great and small. I shall send my carriage for you tomorrow morning at eleven o’clock, that we might converse at length upon this bewildering turn of events and consider what, if anything, can be done to restore a sense of equilibrium to our domestic economy. Until then, I remain, with every assurance of my most sincere regard, Your devoted friend, Arabella Finch.’”

Clara finished reading and folded the letter, a thoughtful expression on her face. “So, Aunt, it seems you are summoned to a council of war.”

Mrs. Selby sighed, a complex sound that mingled resignation with a hint of anticipation. “Indeed. A council of war over damson jam. Only Mrs. Finch could elevate such a matter to the level of international diplomacy. But I suppose I must go. One cannot ignore such a direct summons, especially when it concerns, as she says, the restoration of equilibrium to our domestic economy. It is very kind of her to send the carriage, of course. It saves one the trouble of walking, and the road to her house is rather dusty at this time of year.”

“You will, no doubt, hear all the details of the maid’s account of Mr. Giles’s housemaid’s account of Mr. Henderson’s departure,” Clara observed dryly.

“Oh, I daresay I shall,” Mrs. Selby affirmed. “And I shall be expected to offer my own opinions on the matter, which, you may be sure, will be carefully considered and, if I may say so myself, rather more balanced than Mrs. Finch’s. Though I confess, I am quite as perturbed as she is by the suddenness of it all. One likes to be prepared for such changes, Clara. One likes to know where one stands with one’s damson jam.” She paused, a new thought seeming to strike her. “I wonder if Mr. Henderson’s cook, poor woman, was informed beforehand. Imagine her consternation, to have all her carefully made preserves spirited away without warning! She must have been quite aghast. It is the cooks, Clara, who truly bear the brunt of these sudden decisions.”

Clara agreed that the cook’s feelings were certainly worthy of consideration. The conversation then shifted to the practicalities of tomorrow’s visit to Mrs. Finch, discussing what Mrs. Selby might wear, and what she might politely say to temper Mrs. Finch’s more extreme pronouncements, without appearing to condone Mr. Henderson’s perceived imprudence. Clara knew that her aunt would navigate these social waters with a seasoned grace, capable of expressing both sympathy for Mrs. Finch’s plight and a gentle defence of Mr. Henderson’s benevolent intentions, all while subtly advancing her own views on the importance of proportionality in charitable acts.

The afternoon passed in its usual quiet routine. Clara assisted with some household tasks, ensuring everything was in order, while her aunt continued her needlework, occasionally punctuating the silence with a sigh or a murmured reflection on the damson crisis. The more Clara thought about it, the more she found herself drawn to the character of Mr. Henderson. His abrupt, unthinking generosity, while perhaps socially inconvenient, spoke of a genuine kindness that she found deeply appealing. In a society often governed by rigid rules and subtle calculations, his impulsive benevolence was a refreshing, almost startling, deviation. She wondered what sort of man he truly was, beyond the pleasantries and the occasional charitable act. Was his life a solitary one, allowing him to act on such impulses without consulting others? Or did he have a family, perhaps, whose opinions he simply disregarded in his haste to do good? Mrs. Davies had mentioned him going to Ashworth, but not why, or with whom.

As the day began to wane, and the first shadows lengthened across the parlour floor, a young man arrived, bearing a small basket from the greengrocer. He set it down with a polite nod and handed Clara a small, sealed note. This time, the handwriting was bolder, more masculine, and less ornate than Mrs. Finch’s. Clara recognised it as Mr. Henderson’s.

“Another communication, Aunt,” Clara announced, a note of surprise in her voice. “From Mr. Henderson himself, it seems.”

Mrs. Selby put down her spectacles again, her eyes wide with renewed interest. “From Mr. Henderson! Well, this is a turn. What can he possibly have to say? Has he heard of Mrs. Finch’s dismay already? Or is it about the damson jam itself? Perhaps he intends to send some other fruit in compensation! Open it, Clara, without delay!”

Clara broke the seal, a faint, clean scent of fresh paper and ink replacing Mrs. Finch’s more cloying perfumes. She unfolded the note.

“It is rather brief,” she observed. “‘My Dear Mrs. Selby’,” she began reading. “‘I trust this finds you well. I must beg your pardon for any inconvenience or surprise occasioned by the sudden disposition of my damson preserves. It was an unforeseen necessity, and I regret any disappointment this may have caused. I felt it only right to apprise you directly, however briefly, before my departure. I shall return from Ashworth within a fortnight, by which time I hope to have made arrangements for a fresh supply of a most excellent variety of plum, which, I am assured, makes a preserve almost as fine as the damson, and which I intend to distribute as soon as it is prepared. I trust this will go some way to ameliorating any sense of loss. With renewed apologies, I remain, your most obedient servant, Arthur Henderson.’”

Clara finished, a faint smile playing on her lips. “Well, Aunt, it seems Mr. Henderson is not entirely oblivious to the social repercussions of his actions. He apologises, and promises a replacement, of plums.”

Mrs. Selby sat back, a look of thoughtful assessment on her face. “Plums, you say? A fresh supply of plum jam? Well, that is certainly very good of him. Very thoughtful. And to send a note directly, before his departure, shows a proper regard. Though he calls it an unforeseen necessity. I wonder what necessity that could have been? One cannot imagine such an urgent need for damson jam, however dire the circumstances in Ashworth. Still, to promise plums. It is a gesture, Clara, a very handsome gesture. He clearly understands the importance of these things.”

“And he will be back in a fortnight,” Clara added. “So, not a prolonged absence.”

“No, not prolonged,” Mrs. Selby agreed. “A fortnight. That gives Mrs. Finch just enough time to exhaust her indignation and prepare herself for the offer of plum jam. I shall certainly mention this to her tomorrow, though perhaps not immediately. One must allow her to have her say first, of course. But the news of plums will, I apprehend, go a long way towards restoring her equilibrium, as she so delicately put it.” Her eyes twinkled. “Indeed, it might even reconcile her to the temporary absence of the damsons. For plums, Clara, if they are of a good variety, can be quite delightful. Though not, of course, quite the same as damson. But one must make allowances, must one not, especially for a gentleman of Mr. Henderson’s truly kind and considerate disposition?”

Clara nodded, a sense of quiet contentment settling over her. The small drama of the damson jam had found its gentle resolution, at least in prospect. Mr. Henderson, it seemed, was not merely an impulsive philanthropist, but also a man of delicate social understanding, capable of anticipating and addressing the ripples his actions might cause. His promise of plum jam, however minor a detail it might appear to an outsider, spoke volumes to Clara of his thoughtfulness, confirming her growing impression of him as a truly excellent man. The thought of his return in a fortnight, and the eventual arrival of the plum preserves, now provided a pleasant, if subtle, point of anticipation in her otherwise orderly and predictable domestic sphere. She wondered if the plum jam would indeed be as fine as the damson, and whether Mrs. Finch would truly be appeased. The prospect of such future social observations offered a quiet satisfaction.
It speaks volumes of his heart, does it not, for truly, to consider another's need before one's own, especially when it concerns so fine a preserve as damson jam, is a rare quality indeed.”

Clara, allowing a moment for the soft cadence of Miss Annesley’s voice to dissipate in the gentle breeze of the High Street, replied, “It does, Miss Annesley, it most certainly does. My aunt, as I mentioned, was quite struck by it. She declared him a saint, though I fancy she reserves that particular epithet for those who perform acts of kindness directly benefiting herself. Still, even her more measured pronouncements concerning Mr. Henderson always carry a note of profound respect. He is a man much esteemed in the neighbourhood, as you know, and this latest instance of his benevolence will surely only serve to heighten that regard, whatever Mrs. Finch may have to say on the matter.”

“Ah, Mrs. Finch!” Miss Annesley laughed again, a light, melodious sound that drew a momentary glance from a passing gentleman. “She is a constant source of entertainment, is she not? One never quite knows what new grievance she will find. But to be deprived of her usual share of Mr. Henderson’s jam, which I understand she considers almost her due, will surely provoke a lamentation of the grandest order. I confess I am almost eager to hear the tale of her disappointment, though I should never wish ill upon anyone, of course.”

“Nor I,” Clara agreed, her lips curving into a faint, knowing smile. “But one cannot help but anticipate the dramatic flourish with which she will recount the injustice. She will speak of scarcity, of impropriety, of the sudden and alarming disappearance of what she considers a staple of refined society. Her sense of personal injury, even when it is entirely vicarious, is truly something to behold. I imagine she will feel as though the very fabric of social order has been rent asunder, merely because a few dozen jars of preserve have been diverted from their accustomed path.”

They continued their walk, the High Street growing increasingly lively with carriages and pedestrians, the windows of the shops displaying their morning wares. The sun, though not yet high, promised a fair day, and the air held the crisp freshness of late spring. Clara felt a pleasant weariness beginning to settle upon her, a desire for the quiet domesticity of home, where the demands of social intercourse were less stringent and the familiar rhythms of daily life offered a comforting predictability. Miss Annesley, however, seemed invigorated by the bustle, her eyes sparkling as she took in the scene.

“It makes one quite desirous of a little excitement, does it not, Clara?” she ventured, turning her gaze from a milliner’s window to her companion. “All this talk of Mr. Henderson’s astonishing generosity and Mrs. Finch’s impending wrath. One wonders what will happen next. Will Mr. Henderson return from Ashworth with some exotic new variety of fruit to replace the lost damsons? Or will Mrs. Finch, in a fit of indignation, declare a boycott of all things Hendersonian, if one might coin such a term?” She giggled at her own whimsy.

Clara shook her head gently. “I doubt either extreme will come to pass, Miss Annesley. Mr. Henderson is a man of remarkable constancy, and while his generosity is indeed exceptional, I cannot imagine him attempting to replicate the damson supply on such short notice. As for Mrs. Finch, her indignation, though formidable, rarely translates into actions that inconvenience her in any significant way. She will lament, she will complain, she will probably send a servant to inquire after the exact quantity and destination of the lost jam, but she will not, I apprehend, cease to hold Mr. Henderson in high regard, especially given his generally amiable disposition and his readiness to assist with other matters when called upon. She may even, in time, see this as an opportunity to receive some other favour, a fresh trout perhaps, or a particularly well-grown cucumber from his garden, as a kind of compensatory offering.”

“You are too practical, Clara,” Miss Annesley chided playfully. “One must allow for a little drama in life, especially when it is of so harmless a nature as this. Think of the conversation it will provide at every tea table for weeks to come! Indeed, one might almost be grateful to Mr. Henderson for giving us such a topic.”

They had by now reached the intersection of the High Street and the lane leading to Clara’s aunt’s dwelling, a neat, modest house with a small, well-tended garden. As they turned the corner, Clara felt a true sense of relief.

“Well, here we are,” she said, slowing her pace slightly. “I imagine my aunt will be eager to hear all the particulars of Mrs. Davies’s report, though I suspect she has already drawn her own conclusions regarding the enormity of the affair.”

“And so she should,” Miss Annesley declared. “It is not every day that one hears of such a complete emptying of a larder, is it? One must commend Mr. Henderson’s spirit, even as one marvels at the sheer scale of his benevolence. I shall call upon you soon, Clara, to ascertain the latest developments. Perhaps Mrs. Finch will have issued a formal protest by then.”

With a final, cheerful farewell, Miss Annesley took her leave, promising a prompt return. Clara watched her go for a moment, admiring her unwavering good spirits, before opening the gate and proceeding up the short path to the front door. She let herself in, the familiar scent of beeswax and lavender immediately greeting her.

Her aunt, Mrs. Selby, was in the parlour, seated by the window with her needlework, though the threads lay idle in her lap. Her spectacles were pushed up onto her forehead, and her expression was one of thoughtful disquiet. The moment Clara entered, her aunt looked up, her eyes bright with a mixture of curiosity and perturbation.

“Ah, Clara, my dear, you are returned at last,” Mrs. Selby exclaimed, letting her needlework fall entirely. “I have been quite beside myself with speculation. What did Mrs. Davies say? Did she truly confirm the dreadful news? Is Mr. Henderson indeed entirely bereft of his damson preserves?”

Clara walked across the room and sat in an armchair opposite her aunt, untying the ribbons of her bonnet. “Indeed, Aunt, Mrs. Davies did confirm it all. She had it from Mr. Henderson himself, as he was leaving for Ashworth. Not a single jar remains, it would seem.”

Mrs. Selby pressed a hand to her chest, her countenance expressing a profound sense of shock. “Not one! Oh, the poor man! To be stripped clean, as it were, of a year’s labour and enjoyment. It is truly an astonishing thing to consider. And all for those poor folk, those unfortunates in Ashworth. Surely a dozen or two jars would have sufficed? One must consider proportion, after all. There is such a thing as overdoing it, even in kindness.”

Clara smiled inwardly at her aunt’s dilemma, caught between admiration for generosity and a deeply ingrained sense of practical economy. “Mrs. Davies seemed to think Mr. Henderson acted without much deliberation, Aunt. She implied he simply gave them all, without counting, when the request was made.”

Clara had finished untying her bonnet and placed it carefully on a small table beside her. She observed her aunt with a mild amusement that never quite reached her lips. Mrs. Selby’s concern, while genuine, was often an elaborate performance of anxiety, designed to explore every conceivable facet of a piece of village gossip.

“Perhaps, Aunt,” Clara offered, “he has other preserves. Apple jelly, or blackberry jam. Or perhaps he simply prefers the taste of fresh fruit, and will content himself with that until the next harvest.”

Mrs. Selby waved a dismissive hand. “Nonsense, Clara. You know as well as I do that damson preserves are a particular delicacy, a staple of any respectable larder, especially one belonging to a gentleman of Mr. Henderson’s standing. Mrs. Selby sighed, a profound, theatrical exhalation. Her spectacles, still perched on her forehead, seemed to twitch in sympathy with her agitated thoughts.

Clara considered this. “Mrs. Davies did mention that the request came suddenly, Aunt. A deputation from Ashworth, seeking assistance for families recently afflicted by the unfortunate accident at the mill. “Impulse!” Mrs. Selby repeated, her voice rising slightly. “Impulse is a dangerous master, Clara. It leads to all manner of regrettable decisions, particularly where one’s larder is concerned. One must always exercise a degree of prudence, even in the most benevolent of acts. To give all one has, without thought for tomorrow, without leaving so much as a jar for a rainy day, or a sudden unexpected guest, or even for one’s own legitimate needs, is simply not sensible. Clara found herself picturing Mr. Henderson, a man she knew only by sight and reputation. He was a quiet gentleman, perhaps a little stooped, with a kindly but somewhat distracted air. He was known for his extensive library and his love of horticulture, but rarely for any grand social gestures. This act of wholesale charity was entirely out of character for the reserved scholar she imagined him to be. It was precisely this incongruity that fuelled the village’s fascination.

“Perhaps, Aunt, Mr. Henderson felt it was a small price to pay to alleviate genuine suffering,” Clara suggested gently. “The reports from Ashworth have been quite grim, have they not? Many families truly destitute.”

“Oh, indeed, indeed,” Mrs. Selby agreed, momentarily distracted from her economic concerns by the plight of others. “A dreadful business, that mill accident. Three men lost, and so many injured. Clara suppressed a smile. Clara smiled. The Finches were the village’s unofficial rivals to everyone, always striving to outdo their neighbours in matters of social standing and charitable giving, but only within carefully calculated limits. Mrs. Finch’s competitive spirit was legendary.

The thought of a larder emptied of its winter stores did indeed evoke a sense of vulnerability. It was a tangible link to the changing seasons, to the cycle of work and provision. For Mr. Henderson, a man whose life seemed so ordered and predictable, this act was a rupture.

Mrs. Selby’s eyes widened, as if she had just considered this particular angle with the appropriate gravity for the first time. “Mrs. Green! Oh, the poor woman. She must be in a state of utter disarray. She is known for her meticulous attention to detail, her spotless cupboards, her perfectly labelled jars. To have her careful arrangements overturned in such a wholesale fashion! It would be a blow to her professional pride, Clara. A true affront to her sense of order. She will be the one, I imagine, who truly feels the pinch of this benevolent folly. For it is she who manages the day-to-day provisions, is it not? It is she who will have to explain to Mr. Henderson that there is no damson preserve for his breakfast. Or perhaps he has not even thought of that himself yet! Oh, dear. Not to replace the damsons, of course, for nothing can truly replace them, but as a temporary balm. Just a little something to remind him that not all larders are quite so bereft. And perhaps, to show Mrs. Green that we sympathise with her predicament. Though I imagine she will be quite too mortified to accept such an offering without a great deal of fuss.”

Clara nodded. Such small gestures, ostensibly acts of kindness, were often deeply coded messages within the village’s intricate social fabric. A pot of quince jelly sent to Mr. Henderson would speak volumes: an acknowledgement of his generosity, a gentle reproof for his excess, and a show of commiseration for his housekeeper.

“I shall write a little note to accompany it, Clara. Something suitably delicate. ‘A small token, dear Mr. Henderson, in appreciation of your boundless spirit, and with the fervent hope that providence will soon replenish your stores.’ No, that sounds too pointed. Perhaps something simpler. ‘With our warmest regards, and a small offering for the coming winter months.’ Yes, that is better. Less direct, more subtle.”

Mrs. Selby began to plan the wording of her note with the same earnest concentration she usually reserved for balancing her household accounts. Clara, watching her, understood that for her aunt, these social nuances were as important as any grand event. The incident of the damson preserves was not merely a story; it was a living, breathing drama, playing out in the minds and conversations of the villagers, each person adding their own interpretations, their own judgments, their own gestures of response.

The afternoon light began to fade, casting longer shadows across the parlour. A maid entered quietly to light the lamps, their soft glow adding a comforting warmth to the room. The familiar scent of beeswax and lavender was now mingled with the faint, pleasant aroma of lamp oil.

“I do hope Miss Annesley calls soon,” Mrs. Selby mused, her thoughts returning to their earlier conversation. “She will have heard more, I am certain. Her network of acquaintances is quite extensive, and she has a remarkable knack for ferreting out the most interesting details. Clara pictured Miss Annesley, with her bright eyes and irrepressible cheerfulness, making her rounds through the village, collecting anecdotes and distributing good humour. If anyone could elicit a protest from Mrs. Finch, it would be Miss Annesley, with her innocent questions and artful observations.

“Excellent, excellent,” Mrs. Selby declared, brightening considerably. “We shall have to ensure our own intelligence is up to date, then. “There,” Mrs. Selby declared, holding up the card for Clara’s inspection. “Simple, elegant, and to the point. ‘With the compliments of Mrs. Selby, a small provision for the winter. Best wishes.’ I think that conveys the sentiment without being overly intrusive. And the quince jelly is quite delightful this year, if I do say so myself. Clara took the card and read it. It was indeed perfectly pitched, a model of polite, understated concern. “It is excellent, Aunt,” she agreed, handing it back.

“And the pot,” Mrs. Selby continued, presenting the small, gleaming jar. “Wrapped in my finest linen napkin, with a little sprig of dried lavender tucked into the bow. One must always attend to presentation, Clara. It shows respect, both for the recipient and for the gift itself.”

Clara admired the careful wrapping. Her aunt’s attention to detail, even in these small matters, was unwavering. “It is very thoughtful, Aunt.”

“Thoughtful, yes,” Mrs. Selby agreed, her gaze fixed on the pot as if contemplating its journey. “But also a necessary response. One cannot simply stand by when such an event occurs without acknowledging it in some appropriate fashion. Otherwise, one risks appearing unconcerned, or worse, unsympathetic. And that, Clara, would never do.”

She carefully placed the pot and the card on the small side table, ready for Thomas to collect in the morning. Clara knew that this small act of sending jelly, this carefully worded note, would contribute another layer to the complex narrative unfolding in the village. It would be noted, discussed, and interpreted. It would add another thread to the intricate tapestry of social interactions, all woven around the central, surprising fact of Mr. Henderson’s empty larder.

The incident was far from over. It was, Clara realised, only just beginning to truly ripple outwards. The conversation would continue, the speculations would mount, and the social responses would unfold, all in their own unhurried, characteristic village fashion. And she, Clara, would be there to observe it all, a quiet participant in the gentle drama of ordinary life, made extraordinary by a sudden, sweeping act of damson preserve benevolence.

A deputation from Ashworth, seeking assistance for families recently afflicted by the unfortunate accident at the mill. It sounds as though Mr. Henderson acted on impulse, out of immediate sympathy, rather than a calculated disbursement of his stores.”

“Impulse!” Mrs. Selby repeated, her voice rising slightly. “Impulse is a dangerous master, Clara. It leads to all manner of regrettable decisions, particularly where one’s larder is concerned. One must always exercise a degree of prudence, even in the most benevolent of acts. To give all one has, without thought for tomorrow, without leaving so much as a jar for a rainy day, or a sudden unexpected guest, or even for one’s own legitimate needs, is simply not sensible. It is, dare I say, almost irresponsible. Though of course,” she hastened to add, catching herself, “one must admire his heart. His very, very generous heart. But a heart, however noble, requires a head to guide it, does it not? Otherwise, one finds oneself in the wilderness without a single piece of toast to sustain one.”

Clara found herself picturing Mr. Henderson, a man she knew only by sight and reputation. He was a quiet gentleman, perhaps a little stooped, with a kindly but somewhat distracted air. He was known for his extensive library and his love of horticulture, but rarely for any grand social gestures. This act of wholesale charity was entirely out of character for the reserved scholar she imagined him to be. It was precisely this incongruity that fuelled the village’s fascination.

“Perhaps, Aunt, Mr. Henderson felt it was a small price to pay to alleviate genuine suffering,” Clara suggested gently. “The reports from Ashworth have been quite grim, have they not? Many families truly destitute.”

“Oh, indeed, indeed,” Mrs. Selby agreed, momentarily distracted from her economic concerns by the plight of others. “A dreadful business, that mill accident. Three men lost, and so many injured. And the winter promises to be a harsh one, I hear. So yes, the need is great. And Mr. Henderson’s contribution will undoubtedly be a blessing. But still! All of it! It is the ‘all’ that vexes me, Clara. It points to a certain lack of judgment, or perhaps a sudden fit of… what shall I call it? Excessive philanthropy? One hopes he has not given away the silver spoons next, or the very roof over his head, in a moment of similar enthusiasm.”

Clara suppressed a smile. Her aunt’s imagination, once set loose, rarely failed to explore the most dramatic and unlikely of possibilities. “I believe Mrs. Davies specifically mentioned the preserves, Aunt, and nothing else. I do not think Mr. Henderson’s generosity extends to his personal belongings, nor his property.”

“Well, one lives in hope,” Mrs. Selby declared, giving a sharp nod of her head. “But it does make one wonder about his general state of mind. Is he perhaps a little… eccentric? We have always known him to be a quiet man, devoted to his books and his garden, but never before has he demonstrated such an extreme form of… what is the word? Disregard for his own creature comforts. One does not expect a man of his age and settled habits to suddenly turn into a veritable saint of improvidence. Unless,” she paused, her eyes narrowing as a new theory began to form, “unless there is more to it. Could he be in financial straits, Clara? Is this a desperate measure, a way to appear generous when in fact he is simply trying to rid himself of surplus that he can no longer afford to maintain? Though that makes little sense. Preserves are not so burdensome as to require such drastic measures.”

“I think that is unlikely, Aunt,” Clara said, shaking her head. “Mr. Henderson has always been considered a man of comfortable means. His house is substantial, his garden well-kept, and he keeps a small but competent staff. I have never heard any whispers of financial difficulties concerning him.”

“No, you are right, you are right,” Mrs. Selby conceded, leaning back in her chair and picking up her needlework, though her hands remained idle. “It does not fit. So then, it must be pure, unadulterated, unthinking generosity. And that, in itself, is a thing to marvel at. It certainly sets a precedent, does it not? One wonders how Mr. Finch will react. He is not known for his open-handedness, particularly where his own stores are concerned. And Mrs. Finch, you recall, is quite particular about her husband’s reputation for civic responsibility. She will feel compelled to respond, I am sure. Perhaps a donation of blankets? Or a subscription to a relief fund? But to match Mr. Henderson’s gesture, to empty their own larder of its carefully stocked jellies and jams, that would be an extraordinary sight indeed. I cannot imagine Mrs. Finch ever permitting such a thing. She takes such pride in her Michaelmas gooseberry jam, made according to her grandmother’s recipe.”

Clara smiled. The Finches were the village’s unofficial rivals to everyone, always striving to outdo their neighbours in matters of social standing and charitable giving, but only within carefully calculated limits. Mrs. Finch’s competitive spirit was legendary.

“Miss Annesley seemed to think Mrs. Finch would issue a formal protest, Aunt,” Clara reminded her, recalling their earlier conversation.

Mrs. Selby gasped lightly. “A protest! Good heavens, Clara, that is a delightful thought! Imagine Mrs. Finch marching up to Mr. Henderson’s door, demanding to know if he has considered the implications of his actions for the rest of the village’s charitable efforts. Or perhaps she would write a strongly worded letter to the Ashworth Committee, suggesting that while Mr. Henderson’s spirit is laudable, his method lacks… organisation. Oh, it would be magnificent. One must hope it comes to that. It would certainly liven up the weekly tea parties.”

She paused, a thoughtful expression on her face, then picked up her needlework, her fingers finally beginning to move, albeit slowly, as she stitched a delicate rose onto a piece of linen. “But it does bring to mind, Clara, a most peculiar incident from my own youth. Not a drop was to remain in the cellar, she declared, for it was too astringent, too unpalatable for any civilised palate. Her housekeeper, poor Mrs. Giles, was quite beside herself.
