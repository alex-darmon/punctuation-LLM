The day, a Tuesday of remarkable insipidity, promised little beyond the usual round of domestic quietude for Miss Elara Finch, whose existence, it must be admitted, was ordered with an almost surgical precision. Her drawing-room, a sanctuary of faded chintz and meticulously dusted porcelain, awaited the afternoon callers with a sort of polite resignation; even the very air seemed to hold its breath in anticipation of polite platitudes and the delicate clinking of teacups. Miss Finch herself, a woman of forty-seven years and an unwavering adherence to social protocol, presided over the silver tea service with an air of quiet determination, her hands, rather surprisingly robust for one of such delicate mien, arranging the cucumber sandwiches with an exactitude that bordered upon the scientific.

One might have thought, given her somewhat austere bearing, that Miss Finch harboured a secret disdain for these social rituals, yet the truth was far more complex. She regarded them, perhaps, as one might a necessary yet slightly tedious scientific experiment, each guest an unpredictable variable whose reactions, however minute, merited close observation. It was not, after all, the conversation itself which engaged her so much as the intricate dance of human interaction, the subtle nuances of insincerity, the often-ludicrous posturing of those who imagined themselves to be of consequence.

The first to arrive was Mr. Alaric Thorne, a gentleman whose bulk suggested a life of considerable ease and whose opinions, once expressed, were rarely withdrawn. He entered with a puff of wind and a rather loud declaration regarding the regrettable state of the local turnip crop, a subject he deemed, quite erroneously, to be of universal interest. His waistcoat, a garment of considerable floral exuberance, stretched tautly across his expansive frame, suggesting that its owner had partaken rather too liberally of Miss Finch's excellent shortbread on previous occasions. He settled himself, with a ponderous sigh, into the most comfortable armchair, an act which invariably discomfited Miss Finch who preferred her furniture, like her life, to remain precisely as she had left it.

"A trying morning, Miss Finch, quite trying," he boomed, accepting a cup of Darjeeling, which he promptly began to stir with an almost aggressive circularity. "The market, you understand, is in an absolute turmoil. One simply cannot rely on anything these days, cannot rely at all."

Miss Finch merely inclined her head, a gesture which could be interpreted as either polite agreement or profound disinterest; Mr. Thorne, being rather self-absorbed, naturally assumed the former.

Next, Mrs. Periwinkle made her entrance, a woman whose every movement seemed designed to create a rustle, whether of her voluminous skirts or her equally voluminous conversation. She was, as was her habit, accompanied by a rather nervous-looking terrier named Pip, a creature of indeterminate breed and incessant scratching. Mrs. Periwinkle possessed a remarkable talent for the digression, a conversational style which resembled nothing so much as a runaway carriage, veering wildly from one subject to another with no discernible logic or intended destination.

"My dear Elara, how perfectly charming everything looks," she gushed, depositing herself upon the sofa with a sigh that threatened to deflate the very cushions. "Such a dreadful journey, the dust, you know, absolutely everywhere. And young Thomas, my great-nephew, insisted upon telling me a most peculiar tale about a cow that could sing, imagine! One rather wishes children would confine their imaginations to more sensible matters, does one not?"

She then proceeded, without pausing for breath or indeed for an answer, to inquire after Miss Finch's hydrangeas, to comment upon the lack of suitable seamstresses in the district, and to express a vague, yet persistent, anxiety regarding the imminent arrival of a comet, about which she had read a most disturbing article in the evening gazette. Mr. Thorne, momentarily distracted from the trials of the turnip market, offered a gruff dismissal of comets as "mere astronomical trifles," a pronouncement which left Mrs. Periwinkle looking vaguely offended.

It was at this juncture that young Master Barnaby, Miss Finch's nephew, a boy of ten years and an inexhaustible supply of youthful exuberance, burst into the drawing-room. He was, as always, slightly dishevelled, his hair falling across his brow in a manner that Miss Finch considered lamentably untidy. He held aloft a rather grubby toad, an object which he clearly considered a prize of great value.

"Aunt Elara, look what I've found! He's quite splendid, is he not? I call him Bartholomew."

A collective gasp, faintly perceptible, rippled through the room. Mrs. Periwinkle recoiled, uttering a small, horrified squeak. Mr. Thorne, in a rare display of agitation, nearly upset his teacup. Miss Finch, however, maintained her composure with admirable fortitude. She merely fixed Barnaby with a steady gaze, a gaze which had, over the years, quelled many a youthful insurrection.

"Barnaby," she said, her voice betraying not a tremor, "Bartholomew, however splendid he may be, is not, I fear, an appropriate guest for afternoon tea. Perhaps he would be more comfortable in the garden, awaiting your return."

Barnaby, recognising the unwavering authority in his aunt's tone, mumbled a slightly disgruntled apology and retreated, toad in hand, leaving behind him a faint odour of damp earth and childish adventure. The moment of crisis averted, the polite platitudes resumed, though the brief interlude had served to highlight, for the discerning observer at least, the fragile veneer of civility that often thinly cloaks the wilder aspects of human, and indeed amphibian, nature. Miss Finch, pouring fresh tea for her now somewhat flustered guests, reflected that Tuesday, despite its initial promise of insipidity, had offered its own peculiar, if minor, excitements.

Miss Finch, with a quiet efficiency that belied the recent perturbation, extended the porcelain pot towards Mrs. Periwinkle's cup, observing, as she did so, the slight tremor in the older lady's hand, a tell-tale sign of the residual shock occasioned by Master Barnaby's amphibian presentation. The incident, though brief, had evidently made its mark, much like an unexpected pebble in an otherwise smooth path, causing a momentary, if inconsequential, jolt. Mrs. Periwinkle, having recovered a semblance of her former equilibrium, though perhaps not entirely, patted her voluminous skirts and took a restorative sip of tea, her gaze flitting about the room as if expecting another creature of the wild to emerge from behind the antimacassars. Pip, her perpetually itchy companion, seized upon this lull in the conversation as an opportune moment for a particularly vigorous scratching session behind his left ear, a performance which, though entirely natural for a creature of his disposition, nevertheless added a certain restless counterpoint to the room's re-established decorum.

"Such vivacity, young Barnaby possesses," Mrs. Periwinkle declared, her voice still bearing a faint inflection of something akin to startled wonder, "though one does wish it might be directed towards pursuits of a less... squelchy nature. One recalls, not so very long ago, a similar incident involving my cousin Agatha's prize-winning gladioli and a rather determined colony of earwigs. A truly ghastly affair, the infestation, though not, I must concede, quite so directly confrontational as a toad

Mrs. Periwinkle, having delivered her anecdote concerning the gladioli and their unfortunate invertebrate inhabitants, paused, perhaps expecting a comparable tale of horticultural woe or, at the very least, an expression of profound sympathy for her cousin Agatha’s botanical misfortunes. Miss Finch, ever adept at navigating the delicate currents of drawing room discourse, offered a measured hum of acknowledgement, a sound which, while entirely noncommittal, nevertheless conveyed a suitable degree of polite commiseration without inviting an extended dissertation on the life cycles of earwigs. Mr. Thorne, who had, throughout Mrs. Periwinkle’s recounting, maintained an admirable, if somewhat rigid, posture, now cleared his throat with a subtle, almost imperceptible, rumble. He then ventured, in a voice carefully modulated to suggest both concern and a gentle redirection of the conversation, that indeed, the garden, while a source of much aesthetic pleasure, could also present its unexpected trials, particularly during a season such as this, when the humidity seemed to encourage a plethora of such small, creeping concerns. He alluded, rather vaguely, to certain challenges he himself had faced with his prize winning fuchsias, though he refrained from specifying the nature of these challenges, preferring, it seemed, to maintain a certain dignified reticence regarding his struggles against the forces of nature.

Pip, having completed his vigorous scratching, now settled with a contented sigh, his small, beady eyes observing the company with an air of profound, if utterly canine, wisdom. His tail, a fluffy plume of white, gave a single, languid thump against the carpet, a silent commentary on the agreeable re establishment of drawing room tranquility. Miss Finch, perceiving that the immediate threat of further amphibian or entomological digressions had receded, decided it was an opportune moment to introduce a topic of more general and, she hoped, less unsettling interest. She enquired, with a graceful inclination of her head towards Mrs. Periwinkle, whether the latter had, by any chance, received word regarding the upcoming village fête. The annual fête, an institution of considerable antiquity and importance in the social calendar of Little Paddocks, invariably provided a safe harbour for conversation, its myriad arrangements, committees, and inevitable minor controversies offering an inexhaustible wellspring of genteel discussion.

"Ah, the fête!" Mrs. Periwinkle exclaimed, her eyes brightening perceptibly. "Indeed, Miss Finch, I have just this morning received a rather lengthy missive from dear Miss Arabella Crump, outlining in quite exhaustive detail the arrangements for the cake stall. One does hope, though perhaps without undue optimism, that her lemon drizzle cake, however celebrated, will not again suffer the ignominy of being mistaken for a rather robust specimen of rock bun, as was, alas, the case two years ago. A most unfortunate misunderstanding, which, I am told, led to a certain degree of consternation amongst the judges, particularly poor old Colonel Fitzwilliam, whose dentures, one understands, were subjected to a quite unexpected strain."

Mr. Thorne permitted himself a faint, almost imperceptible, smile at this recollection. Colonel Fitzwilliam’s dentures were, in Little Paddocks, a subject of some local renown, having been the unintended protagonists in several village anecdotes. Miss Finch, however, merely offered a sympathetic murmur, her attention already turning to the broader implications of the fête. "And the Fancy Dress Competition?" she enquired, her tone suggesting a keen, though entirely proper, interest in the more theatrical aspects of the communal gathering. "One trusts that young Master Puddifoot, with his predilection for rather elaborate historical tableaux, will not again attempt to arrive dressed as a full sized Roman galley, a feat which, though undeniably ambitious, proved somewhat impractical for navigating the confines of the village hall entrance last year."

A shared chuckle, delicate and refined, rippled between Miss Finch and Mrs. Periwinkle, a testament to the enduring amusement provided by Master Puddifoot’s overzealous sartorial efforts. Mr. Thorne, for his part, offered a more robust, yet still entirely gentlemanly, laugh. "Indeed, Miss Finch," he affirmed, "one rather hopes that practicality might, this year, take precedence over pure theatricality. Though, one must concede, young Puddifoot’s interpretation of Britannia, complete with a rather precariously balanced shield and an authentic if somewhat damp trident, was certainly a sight to behold, particularly when he became entangled in the bunting."

The conversation drifted pleasantly on, touching upon the merits of various jam recipes, the perennial debate over the most suitable location for the coconut shy, and the ever present challenge of persuading Mrs. Higgins to part with her prize winning dahlias for the floral display without causing her undue distress. It was, Miss Finch reflected, precisely the sort of innocuous chatter that served as the social mortar of village life, binding together disparate personalities in a shared anticipation of communal festivity. The brief shadow cast by Barnaby’s amphibian offering had quite dissipated, replaced by the gentle light of amiable gossip and the comforting predictability of familiar routines.

As the afternoon light began to soften, casting longer shadows across the lawn visible from the drawing room window, Miss Finch observed a certain restlessness stirring within Mrs. Periwinkle. The older lady’s gaze, which had been fixed with an almost mesmerized intensity upon a particularly persistent fly buzzing against the windowpane, now shifted towards the mantelpiece clock. Its ornate hands, in their slow, deliberate peregrinations, indicated that the hour for departure was drawing nigh. Pip, sensing the impending change in the domestic landscape, stirred from his slumber, emitted a small, anticipatory yawn, and stretched his front paws out with an almost balletic grace, before settling once more, albeit with an air of readiness.

"My dear Miss Finch," Mrs. Periwinkle began, her voice tinged with that particular blend of regret and necessity that accompanies the polite conclusion of a social engagement, "I fear the hour grows late, and I must, alas, prepare to take my leave. Young Timothy, my driver, will no doubt be fretting by the gate, convinced, as he always is, that some dire calamity has befallen me or, worse yet, that I have been persuaded to attend a second sitting of tea, an indulgence he views with a suspicion bordering upon outright disapproval." She gathered her voluminous handbag, a veritable treasure trove of handkerchiefs, smelling salts, and an alarming number of small, rather hard peppermints, and began the slow, deliberate process of rising from her armchair, a manoeuvre that always seemed to require a considerable expenditure of both physical and mental effort.

Mr. Thorne, with the gallantry of a gentleman of an earlier, perhaps more chivalrous, age, immediately rose to assist her, offering a steadying hand and a reassuring murmur. Miss Finch, equally attentive to the rituals of departure, stood poised to accompany her guests to the front door, already mentally arranging the removal of the tea things and the airing of the drawing room.

"It has been, as always, a most delightful afternoon, Miss Finch," Mrs. Periwinkle declared, having successfully achieved a vertical posture. "So very good of you to receive us. And do give my very best to young Barnaby, once he has, as it were, returned Bartholomew to his natural habitat. One trusts he will not again attempt to introduce the garden’s more... untamed inhabitants to your otherwise perfectly appointed drawing room." There was a faint, lingering note of amusement, perhaps even a hint of a fond exasperation, in her voice as she uttered this final, cautionary remark.

"I shall indeed convey your regards to Barnaby, Mrs. Periwinkle," Miss Finch responded, a faint, almost imperceptible smile playing about her lips. "And I assure you, we shall endeavour to maintain a strict demarcation between the drawing room and the wilder reaches of the shrubbery."

With a final exchange of polite farewells, and a rather effusive scratch behind Pip’s ear from Mr. Thorne, the two guests were escorted to the front door. The sound of Mrs. Periwinkle’s rather grand motorcar sputtering into life, a sound that always struck Miss Finch as remarkably akin to a particularly dyspeptic dragon clearing its throat, drifted in through the open door, followed by the crunch of tyres on the gravel drive as it departed.

Miss Finch stood for a moment in the quiet hallway, a faint scent of lavender and old roses still clinging to the air, mingled now with the faintest whisper of exhaust fumes. The house, having briefly hummed with the gentle thrum of social interaction, now settled back into its accustomed stillness, a stillness broken only by the distant caw of a rook and the soft tick tock of the grandfather clock in the hall. She felt a familiar, quiet satisfaction. The afternoon tea, despite its momentary amphibian diversion, had been, by all accounts, a success. Decorum had been maintained, pleasantries exchanged, and the delicate fabric of social convention had, for another day, remained reassuringly intact.

However, as she turned to make her way back to the drawing room, her eye caught a small, rather insignificant object lying half hidden beneath the console table in the hall, a table adorned with a rather charming porcelain shepherdess and a bowl of dried potpourri. It was a single, solitary gumdrop, of a rather vibrant green hue, and decidedly sticky to the touch. A gumdrop, moreover, of a variety known to be particularly favoured by young Master Barnaby.

A flicker of something akin to exasperation, quickly masked, crossed Miss Finch’s usually serene features. Barnaby, bless his energetic heart, was not known for his tidiness, nor for his strict adherence to the household rule that sticky confections were to be consumed solely in the nursery, or, failing that, in the garden, far from the polished surfaces of antique furniture. This small, emerald disc, testament to a secret indulgence, was a minor infraction, certainly, but it represented a larger, more persistent challenge: the subtle but incessant battle against the forces of youthful chaos that periodically threatened to overwhelm the carefully ordered universe of her home.

She stooped, with a slight sigh, to retrieve the offending item, her fingers gingerly avoiding its sticky surface. As she did so, her gaze fell upon a faint, almost imperceptible smudge on the polished mahogany leg of the table, a smudge that, upon closer inspection, revealed itself to be a barely discernible pattern of tiny, somewhat muddy, circular prints. Amphibian prints. Bartholomew, it appeared, had not been quite so swiftly or entirely removed from the premises as Barnaby’s swift retreat had suggested. The toad, it seemed, had left his own, rather less polite, calling card.

A small, wry smile touched Miss Finch’s lips. The wilder aspects of nature, indeed. Barnaby, in his boundless enthusiasm, had merely served as Bartholomew’s unwitting accomplice. The fragile veneer of civility, she mused, could be breached by rather more than mere words. It could be breached by sticky sweets and by the faint, muddy impressions of a toad’s peregrinations.

She straightened up, the offending gumdrop now carefully deposited in a waste paper basket, the faint muddy prints duly noted for later, more thorough cleaning. The drawing room awaited, its cushions still bearing the faint indentations of her departed guests, the lingering aroma of Ceylon tea and polite conversation still present. But a new thought, an altogether more practical and perhaps slightly troubling one, had begun to form in Miss Finch’s mind. If Bartholomew had managed to linger, even for a moment, in the hall, what other small, uninvited visitors might have found their way into her carefully maintained abode, perhaps even into the drawing room itself, while her attention had been otherwise engaged with the pleasantries of afternoon tea?

The thought, while not precisely alarming, was certainly disquieting. Her house, The Laurels, was her sanctuary, a bastion of order and propriety against the occasional unruly tides of the outside world. The idea of an unseen, unsanctioned presence, however small or amphibian, within its hallowed walls was one that demanded immediate, if discreet, investigation. She surveyed the drawing room with a new, more critical eye. Everything appeared to be in its usual, impeccable order. The antimacassars were perfectly aligned, the porcelain figurines on the mantelpiece gleamed, and the floral arrangements stood serenely in their crystal vases. Yet, the memory of those faint, muddy prints, a subtle betrayal of her nephew’s obedience, gnawed at the edges of her composure.

She walked slowly around the room, her gaze sweeping over every surface, every shadow. The afternoon sun, now lower in the sky, cast long, golden shafts through the windows, illuminating motes of dust dancing in the air, but revealing nothing untoward. She ran a gloved hand along the polished surface of the grand piano, then smoothed a stray fold in the heavy velvet curtains. Her inspection was thorough, almost ritualistic, a quiet affirmation of her dominion over her domestic realm.

But as she approached the large, somewhat imposing potted fern that stood in a corner by the bay window, she noticed something. It was not a gumdrop, nor a muddy print. It was a subtle disturbance in the soil at the base of the fern, a faint, freshly dug depression, as if something small had

begun to make itself rather too much at home. Miss Finch, ever the meticulous observer, knelt slowly, her knees protesting only a fraction, to examine this new, rather more unsettling breach of decorum. The gumdrop, a mere sticky smudge, and Bartholomew's fleeting muddy prints were, she now reflected, almost charmingly uncouth in comparison. This, this was a deliberate act, a conscious reordering of her meticulously maintained botanical display.

She leaned closer, adjusting her spectacles with a precise movement of her forefinger and thumb. The disturbance was indeed fresh. A small mound of displaced earth, dark and rich against the paler, more settled soil, testified to recent activity. And there, just at the edge of the terracotta pot, almost hidden beneath a broad, fronded leaf, was a tiny, precisely circular opening, a miniature tunnel, barely wide enough for a plump bumblebee, yet unmistakably a burrow. A burrow, in her drawing room fern. The very notion was preposterous.

A sigh, soft and almost inaudible, escaped her lips. The wilder aspects of nature, indeed. Bartholomew, with his amphibian wanderings, had been merely a fleeting, damp indiscretion. This, however, suggested something more permanent, a tenant, as it were, uninvited and assuredly without benefit of a lease. Her sanctuary, The Laurels, a bastion of order and propriety, was undergoing a rather insidious siege.

She reached out a gloved finger, not quite touching the disturbed soil, but hovering inches above it, as if to gauge the very temerity of the invisible intruder. What creature, she mused, would possess such audacious disregard for the sanctity of indoor horticulture? It could not be Bartholomew again. Toads, while given to unexpected appearances, were not, to her knowledge, burrowing creatures, certainly not with such architectural precision. A beetle, perhaps? No, a beetle would merely scuttle, not excavate with such industriousness. A mouse. The word, unbidden, formed itself in her mind, sending a faint shiver, not of fear, but of profound irritation, down her spine. A mouse, in her drawing room. The thought was utterly abhorrent.

Mice, she recalled from certain regrettable childhood encounters in the less fastidious environs of her Aunt Mildred's country cottage, were not merely small and scurrying. They were destructive, prone to nibbling, to leaving tell-tale droppings, and to generally undermining the very foundations of domestic tranquility. Her Aunt Mildred, bless her chaotic soul, had always merely exclaimed "Oh, dear!" and left saucers of milk about, a practice Miss Finch had always considered rather counterproductive. No, Miss Finch would not merely exclaim "Oh, dear!" Her response would be rather more considered, and decidedly more effective.

She straightened up, her gaze now moving from the fern to the immediate surroundings. If there was a mouse, it had not simply appeared within the pot itself. It must have entered The Laurels from somewhere, and then, with an astonishing lack of discretion, chosen her prized fern as its temporary domicile. The audacity of it.

Her eyes swept over the gleaming parquet floor, the intricate pattern of the Persian rug, the polished brass fender of the inactive fireplace. Nothing seemed amiss. No tell-tale trails, no tiny droppings, thank goodness. But then, mice were notoriously discreet, at least until their numbers became rather too numerous for discretion to be maintained. This, she surmised, was likely a single, pioneer mouse, emboldened by some unforeseen opportunity.

What opportunity? Her mind, ever analytical, began to construct a series of possible scenarios. The back door, perhaps, left ajar for a moment too long by young Thomas, the gardener's boy, when delivering the cuttings. Or a ventilation grate, perhaps slightly loosened by the recent high winds, allowing a tiny ingress. Or, and the thought was a sour grape in her mouth, Barnaby's precipitate, if rather enthusiastic, exit with Bartholomew. Had the door been held open for that fraction of a moment longer than necessary? Had the very disruption of the toad's presence somehow created an opportunity for another, more terrestrial intruder to slip past the usual formidable defences of The Laurels?

She dismissed the thought as unworthy, at least for the moment. Blaming Barnaby, while occasionally satisfying, would not solve the immediate problem of a potential murine resident. Practicality, Miss Finch reminded herself, always trumped conjecture, however tempting the latter might be.

Her methodical inspection resumed, but with an increased intensity, a sharpened focus. She began with the immediate vicinity of the fern, moving with the quiet grace of a predator, though a rather elegantly dressed one, armed only with her formidable powers of observation. She knelt again, this time to inspect the skirting board that ran along the base of the wall behind the fern. Not a crack, not a crevice, not a whisper of a gap. The Laurels, built in a more substantial era, was remarkably well sealed. The mouse, if it was indeed a mouse, was either incredibly resourceful or incredibly fortunate.

She ran her gloved hand along the heavy velvet curtains that framed the bay window. No tiny gnaw marks, no disturbed folds. The sunlight, though now fading into a softer, more golden hue, still illuminated the occasional dust motes, but nothing else. Her gaze dropped to the thick pile of the Persian rug beneath the window. She smoothed a stray tassel, her fingers exploring the intricate pattern. And then, there it was. Not a mouse, not a burrow, but a faint, almost microscopic scattering of tiny, dark specks. Indistinguishable to the untrained eye, perhaps, but to Miss Finch, whose domestic vigilance was legendary, they were unmistakable. Mouse droppings. Small, dark, and utterly, unequivocally unwelcome.

A tiny gasp, quickly suppressed, escaped her. This was not a potential murine resident. This was an actual, confirmed, and undeniably present murine resident. And one, moreover, that had clearly been rather lax in its choice of latrine. The thought sent a fresh wave of irritation through her. Her drawing room, a place of refined discourse and impeccable cleanliness, had been defiled.

She stood up, slowly, her back aching just a fraction more than usual. The problem had escalated. From a subtle disturbance to an undeniable presence. And not just a presence, but one that had already begun to leave its rather unsavory calling cards.

Her gaze now swept over the entire drawing room with the cool, analytical assessment of a general surveying a battlefield. Every piece of furniture, every decorative object, every cushion and curtain, now seemed a potential hiding place, a possible route of ingress or egress for the tiny, unwanted invader. The very air, once redolent with the gentle aroma of Ceylon tea, now seemed to carry a faint, imaginary hint of something wilder, something untamed, something utterly out of place.

She walked towards the Chippendale escritoire, its inlaid mahogany gleaming dully in the diminished light. It was a beautiful piece, a gift from her late mother, and usually kept locked, its contents of bills and correspondence neatly categorised. She ran her hand along its smooth, cool surface, then knelt to inspect the base. Nothing. Not a scratch, not a crumb.

Next, the grand piano. Its polished surface reflected the last vestiges of the afternoon sun. She lifted the lid, revealing the pristine white and black keys. No sign of intrusion there. But what about underneath? She peered into the shadowy depths beneath the keyboard, her eyes searching for any tell-tale sign. The dust, while present, was undisturbed. No scurrying shapes, no rustling sounds.

The entire room, once a picture of tranquil domesticity, was now transformed in her mind's eye into a labyrinth of potential hideaways. The elegant tapestry screens, the heavy oak chest, the various occasional tables laden with porcelain figurines and leather-bound books. Each presented a new challenge, a new possibility.

"Indeed," she murmured to herself, her voice a low, precise whisper. "A proper investigation is clearly in order."

She considered her options. Summoning Mrs. Higgins, her redoubtable housekeeper, was an immediate thought. Mrs. Higgins, a woman of stout resolve and practical solutions, would undoubtedly march in with a broom and a determined expression, perhaps even a rather theatrical sigh. But Miss Finch prided herself on discretion. Such matters, while undeniably vexing, were best handled with a certain quiet efficiency, lest the very fabric of The Laurels' serene reputation be, however subtly, sullied. And besides, Mrs. Higgins had a tendency to narrate such events to the butcher, the baker, and indeed, the entire village, with a colourful embellishment that Miss Finch found entirely unsuited to the gravity of the situation.

No, this was a task for Miss Finch alone, at least for the initial reconnaissance. She retrieved a small, silver-handled magnifying glass from the drawer of a side table. It was usually reserved for examining particularly fine embroidery stitches or deciphering faded inscriptions on antique jewellery. Now, it was pressed into service for a rather less glamorous, though no less important, purpose.

Armed with her magnifying glass, Miss Finch began her systematic sweep. She started at the far corner of the room, near the fireplace, and moved clockwise, her eyes meticulously scanning every surface, every join, every shadow. She paid particular attention to the baseboards, the often-overlooked frontiers where the domestic realm met the wall, and where, she knew, tiny breaches could often occur.

She moved the velvet armchair slightly, just enough to peer behind it. The dust bunnies, of course, were present. A perennial challenge, even for Mrs. Higgins's most diligent efforts. But no sign of any other, more animate, residents. She ran her gloved hand along the back of the armchair, feeling for any rough edges, any suspicious textures. Nothing.

The bookcase, filled with leather-bound volumes of poetry, history, and botany, stood tall and imposing. Miss Finch ran her finger along the spines of the lower shelves. No nibble marks on the bindings. No tiny tunnels bored into the pages of "The Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire." A small mercy. She peered behind the bookcase

behind the bookcase, where the accumulated dust motes danced in the fading light, presenting a miniature galaxy of domestic neglect. She ran her gloved hand along the exposed portion of the skirting board, feeling for any rough edges, any tell tale gouges that might betray a clandestine gnawing. Nothing. The wood was smooth, unblemished, a testament to the diligent craftsmanship of a bygone era and the periodic attentions of a diligent, if occasionally distracted, housemaid.

Her perambulation continued. The small occasional table, laden with an assortment of polished silver photo frames and a delicate porcelain shepherdess, received her meticulous scrutiny. She lifted each frame, inspecting the felted undersides, then carefully replaced them. The shepherdess, a fragile heirloom from her grandmother, was likewise examined. No minute paw prints, no dislodged fragments of its painted skirt. It stood as serene and untroubled as ever.

Next, the pair of elegant tapestry screens, placed strategically to shield a draft from the window. These were perhaps more promising, with their intricate folds and the slight space between their panels and the wall. Miss Finch gently nudged the first screen, creating a gap wide enough for her to peer into the shadowed recess. Her magnifying glass was raised, her eye pressed close to the lens. The dust here was certainly more profound, resembling a fine, grey fur, but it was inert. No signs of disturbance, no compacted pathways, no stray hairs or droppings. The second screen yielded a similarly unhelpful blank.

"Most perplexing," she whispered, the sound absorbed by the heavy drapes and the hushed atmosphere of the room. The initial confidence in her detective abilities, born of a logical mind and an orderly disposition, was beginning to waver slightly. The absence of concrete evidence, while superficially reassuring, was in fact more troubling. It implied an adversary of considerable cunning, or perhaps, a fundamental misapprehension on her part. But no, the crumb. The definite, undeniable crumb. And the swift darting motion, glimpsed in the periphery of her vision. Her senses, while perhaps not as sharp as they once were, were certainly not entirely to be dismissed.

She moved towards the window, where the last vestiges of the afternoon sun were now indeed fading into a soft, bruised purple twilight. The heavy velvet curtains, drawn back and tied with silken cords, offered another potential hiding place. She reached behind one, feeling the thick, dust catching folds of the fabric. Her fingers, accustomed to the smooth silk of her own gloves, detected only the familiar texture of aged velvet and the occasional, almost imperceptible, snag. The window ledge itself was pristine, polished daily by Mrs. Higgins, a battleground won in the eternal war against dust. No tiny tracks across the sill, no tell tale smudges on the glass.

The grand oak chest, standing sentinel against the far wall, was a more formidable challenge. Its heavy lid, adorned with intricate carvings, suggested an impregnable fortress. Yet, Miss Finch knew that appearances could be deceiving. She knelt once more, her knees protesting slightly against the thick Persian rug, and began her inspection of its base. The wooden feet were solid, resting firmly on the floor. She ran her hand underneath, feeling for any crevices or hollows. The wood was smooth and uninterrupted. A faint scent of beeswax and old wood emanated from the chest, a reassuring aroma of permanence and domestic stability.
