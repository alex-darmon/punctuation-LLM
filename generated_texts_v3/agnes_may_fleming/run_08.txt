It was upon a Tuesday, a day of customary quietude and punctual observances within the snug confines of The Laurels, that Miss Millicent Plimpton, a lady of some sixty years and of sensibilities hitherto unruffled by undue modern impertinences, found her morning entirely, if subtly, disarranged. The breakfast china, a delicate pattern of tiny blue forget-me-nots, had been cleared with Mrs. Gable's usual methodical efficacy, and Miss Millicent had but settled herself in her favourite winged armchair, preparing to embark upon the morning's embroidery, when a most unlooked-for interruption occurred. A vehement rap, indeed more of a thud, reverberated through the highly polished front door, startling the placid Miss Millicent and causing her to drop a bead of cerulean silk upon the Persian rug.

Presently, Mrs. Gable, whose stout figure and forthright manner betokened a lifetime of diligent service, appeared in the doorway of the drawing-room, her face bearing a peculiar mixture of vexation and bewilderment. 'Beg pardon, Miss Millicent,' she announced, her voice a little sharper than was her wont, 'but there's a lad from the village, young Thomas Higgins, standing at the door with a package. And what a package it is, too, quite the size of a small coffer, I should say, wrapped in brown paper most clumsily, and smelling faintly of peat smoke.'

Upon her small, somewhat bird-like countenance, there settled a look of mild consternation. Miss Millicent had no relations who were wont to send such unwieldy parcels, nor was she accustomed to receiving items of such magnitude through the common post. Her purchases were invariably dispatched from the respectable establishments in the county town, arriving in neat, manageable boxes, carefully addressed and quite free of any olfactory suggestion of the rural hearth. 'A package, Mrs. Gable? For me? Are you quite certain of the address?' she enquired, though knowing full well Mrs. Gable's punctilious habits precluded any error in such a matter.

'Oh, entirely certain, Miss. 'Tis writ in a rather flourishing hand, I grant you, "Miss M. Plimpton, The Laurels, Little Upton," plain as day. And the lad says 'tis come all the way from Scotland, if you please, though he knows no more than that.'

Scotland. The word hung in the air, redolent of heather and a certain distant, somewhat rugged ancestry about which Miss Millicent knew little save for a few faded daguerreotypes of stern-faced uncles she had never met. She sighed faintly. 'Well, then, we had best admit the boy, Mrs. Gable, and see the object safely deposited in the hall. One cannot leave a delivery boy lingering upon the step, however preposterous his burden.'

So it was, after a short while, that a truly prodigious parcel, wrapped in several layers of coarse brown paper secured with what appeared to be industrial twine, came to rest upon the sturdy oak chest in the front hall. Thomas Higgins, a rosy-cheeked youth whose boots left faint, earthy smudges upon the pristine linoleum, accepted his threepence with a bob of his head and departed, leaving behind a faint, lingering aroma of damp wool and village life.

Miss Millicent surveyed the behemoth with a gaze both apprehensive and profoundly curious. It was an irregular shape, not rectangular like a book or a garment, but possessed of an odd protuberance here, a hollow there, as if containing some peculiarly structured entity. 'What on earth,' she mused aloud, addressing the package directly, 'can you possibly contain?'

Mrs. Gable, armed with a formidable pair of dressmaker's shears, approached the mystery with rather more resolve. 'Shall I, Miss Millicent? 'Twould be a shame to let it sit there all day, obstructing the light from the fanlight.'

'Yes, do, Mrs. Gable,' Miss Millicent acquiesced, stepping back a pace, her embroidery work for the moment entirely forgotten. 'But do exercise caution. One never knows what modern innovations one's more distant kin might be tempted to dispatch. It might be some newfangled contraption for the extraction of honey, or perhaps a device for measuring the rainfall, though I confess I cannot conceive of a need for either.'

With a series of determined snips and tugs, Mrs. Gable began to dismantle the outer layers. The paper, thick and unyielding, gradually yielded to reveal an inner casing of straw and yet more twine, all meticulously packed. A faint scratching sound, almost like the rustling of dry leaves, accompanied the unveiling. Miss Millicent leaned forward, her spectacles perched upon her nose, a delicate hand pressed to her breast. The suspense was, she felt, quite uncharacteristic of her usually ordered temperament.

Finally, with a grand sweep, Mrs. Gable pulled away the last vestiges of the wrapping, exposing the true contents. There, perched somewhat precariously upon its base, was a birdcage. Not a simple, elegant little cage suited to a canary or a finch, such as the one in the drawing-room, but a truly extravagant structure. It was fashioned from dark, polished wood, intricately carved with fantastical birds and curling vines, and adorned with gilt trimmings that glittered rather brazenly in the soft morning light. Its dome-shaped roof was surmounted by a miniature turret, and within its bars, three tiny, porcelain birds, one blue, one green, and one rather astonishing yellow, sat frozen on their perches, their beady eyes staring blankly into the void.

Miss Millicent stared, first at the opulent cage, then at the cheerful, unblinking faces of the ceramic inhabitants. Her initial look of mild consternation had given way to one of utter, if polite, astonishment. 'Good heavens, Mrs. Gable,' she murmured at length, 'it is a birdcage. And such a birdcage. I declare, I have never seen its like. It appears to be, indeed, a veritable aviary for inanimate fowl.'

Mrs. Gable, meanwhile, had begun to chuckle, a low, rumbling sound. 'It's a beauty, Miss, for all that. Though our dear Pip, with his simple needs, might find it a touch overwhelming, perhaps. A grand gesture, if nothing else, from those distant Scots.'

Miss Millicent, after a moment's contemplation, a slight smile beginning to play upon her lips, nodded slowly. 'Indeed, a grand gesture. And one which, I apprehend, must be accommodated. Perhaps,' she mused, surveying the vast proportions of the hall, 'it might find a suitable home upon the landing, affording a rather unique vista for any who ascend the stairs. A small touch of the unexpected, you understand, to enliven the otherwise unyielding predictability of life at The Laurels.' And with that, the morning's disarrangement was, if not entirely resolved, then at least gracefully, and quite charmingly, incorporated into the fabric of Miss Millicent Plimpton's impeccably ordered world.

The decision made, and the initial surprise gracefully absorbed into the domestic landscape, Miss Millicent Plimpton turned her attention, with characteristic resolve, to the logistical challenge at hand. The birdcage, for all its ornamental grandeur, was an object of considerable weight and bulk, and the prospect of conveying it from the hall to the upper landing presented itself as a task requiring both careful planning and no small measure of physical exertion.

"Now, Mrs. Gable," Miss Millicent began, her voice retaining its customary calm, though a faint hint of strategic contemplation could be detected, "I believe the landing offers the most judicious placement. It receives a pleasing northern light for much of the day, yet avoids the direct glare that might, one apprehends, render the gilt trimmings quite excessively ostentatious. Moreover, its elevated position will permit a comprehensive view of the entire structure, allowing its unique qualities to be fully appreciated by any who might ascend or descend the stairs."

Mrs. Gable, who had by this point deposited the last of the brown paper and twine into a convenient basket destined for the kitchen fire, wiped her hands on her apron and surveyed the elaborate cage with a rather more pragmatic eye. "It's a grand thing, Miss, no doubt about it. But moving it will be a business. It's heavier than it looks, for all its fancy bits. And those little birds, they look as if they'd tumble right off their perches if one gave it too much of a jolt."

"Indeed, Mrs. Gable," Miss Millicent affirmed, a slight frown momentarily creasing her brow. "Utmost care must be exercised. I suggest, perhaps, a slow and steady progression. We shall consider it a delicate operation, akin to conveying a particularly fragile piece of porcelain or a newly potted fuchsia. One step at a time, you understand, with frequent pauses for repose and adjustment."

The undertaking commenced shortly thereafter. Mrs. Gable, with her robust constitution and a lifetime of handling the various weighty accoutrements of a well appointed household, took the primary burden. She bent her knees, a technique she had perfected over many years of lifting coal scuttles and hefty laundry baskets, and grasped the base of the birdcage firmly. Miss Millicent, meanwhile, positioned herself on the opposite side, her slender hands supporting a section of the ornate carving, more, it must be admitted, for moral support and directional guidance than for any significant distribution of the physical load.

The ascent of the main staircase at The Laurels was usually an unhurried affair, marked by the gentle creak of aged timber and the quiet dignity of a house accustomed to its own rhythms. On this particular morning, however, the procession was punctuated by the occasional grunt from Mrs. Gable, the soft rustle of Miss Millicent's linen dress, and a series of low, murmured instructions.

"A little to the left, if you please, Mrs. Gable. Mind the newel post. We mustn't scuff the varnish, not after Mr. Hemlock's excellent work last spring."

"Just a moment, Miss. My grip needs readjusting. This thing's got more corners than a country lane."

At each landing, a brief respite was called, during which Miss Millicent would meticulously inspect the birdcage for any sign of dislodge or damage, and Mrs. Gable would take a restorative deep breath, her face a little flushed from the exertion. The three porcelain birds, however, remained impassive, their painted eyes staring ahead with an unblinking tenacity that seemed almost to mock the human effort expended on their behalf.

Finally, after what felt to Miss Millicent to be a journey of considerable duration, though in reality no more than ten minutes had elapsed, the extravagant structure reached its intended destination. The landing, spacious and airy, seemed to welcome its new inhabitant. Miss Millicent directed Mrs. Gable to place it precisely in the corner nearest the large arched window, where it would catch the morning sun but not be overwhelmed by its intensity.

With a final, gentle heave, the birdcage settled into its appointed place. A sigh of relief, audible and deeply felt, escaped Mrs. Gable's lips. "There we are, Miss. Right as rain. Though I daresay it's the heaviest birdcage I've ever had the pleasure of not putting a bird in."

Miss Millicent stepped back, critically assessing the effect. The dark polished wood, the intricate carvings, the brazen gilt, and the turreted roof now formed a striking counterpoint to the more subdued tones of the wallpaper and the simple elegance of the mahogany console table that usually occupied that corner. The three porcelain birds, blue, green, and astonishing yellow, seemed to observe the quiet domestic drama with an air of detached superiority.

"It is quite remarkable, Mrs. Gable," Miss Millicent pronounced, her initial astonishment now tempered by a growing appreciation for the object's unique character. "A truly bold statement, one might say. And it does, I apprehend, rather enliven the landing. It introduces an element of the unexpected, a certain whimsicality, which I believe is not altogether unwelcome in a household where order and routine are, for the most part, paramount."

She walked around the cage, examining it from various angles, her head tilted slightly. "The light catches the gilt in a rather pleasing fashion from this perspective, don't you think? And those little birds, though undeniably inanimate, possess a certain peculiar charm. One wonders what narratives they might observe from their lofty vantage point, witnessing the daily ebb and flow of life at The Laurels."

Mrs. Gable merely nodded, already beginning to dust her hands and straighten her apron. Her appreciation of the birdcage, while genuine, was rooted in a more practical understanding of its novelty. "It'll be a sight for sore eyes, Miss, that it will. And a talking point, I've no doubt. Mrs. Higgins next door will have something to say about it, won't she now?"

Miss Millicent smiled at the thought of Mrs. Higgins, whose pronouncements on matters of interior decoration were generally delivered with an authoritative air, irrespective of her actual expertise. "Indeed, Mrs. Gable. I daresay she will. One must always be prepared for the observations of one's neighbors, particularly when one introduces an object of such... unusual provenance."

The arrival and subsequent placement of the extravagant birdcage became, over the following days, a subtle but persistent alteration in the quiet rhythm of The Laurels. Miss Millicent found herself pausing more frequently on the stairs, her gaze drawn to the intricate details of the carving, the glint of the gilt, and the unblinking stares of the three ceramic inhabitants. She developed a habit of offering a silent, rhetorical question to the porcelain birds as she passed, a gentle, harmless eccentricity. "And how are we this morning, my silent observers? Have you noted any particular changes in the atmospheric pressure, or perhaps a new bloom in the rose garden that has escaped my own notice?" The birds, of course, offered no reply, which was precisely what Miss Millicent expected, and indeed, preferred. Their stillness was a comfort, a counterpoint to the small, ceaseless anxieties of the living world.

Her own canary, Pip, ensconced in his more modest, yet perfectly adequate, bamboo cage in the drawing room, seemed entirely oblivious to the arrival of his grand, inanimate counterparts upstairs. He continued to trill his cheerful, unvarying melodies, peck at his millet, and flutter his wings with an innocent disregard for the dramatic shift in avian interior design that had occurred within the house. Miss Millicent sometimes wondered if Pip, with his simple needs and uncomplicated joys, might find the ornate structure a touch overwhelming, as Mrs. Gable had suggested. Perhaps, she mused, there was a quiet wisdom in Pip's lack of curiosity, a tacit understanding that true contentment lay not in ostentation, but in the small, familiar comforts of a well ordered life.

A week later, Mrs. Higgins, as predicted, paid her customary Tuesday afternoon call. She was a woman of firm opinions and an unwavering belief in the superiority of her own judgment, particularly concerning matters of taste and decorum. Her entrance was, as always, announced by a rather vigorous rap upon the front door, followed by her brisk stride into the hall, a process that invariably seemed to suggest she was on a mission of vital importance.

"Millicent, my dear," Mrs. Higgins declared, sweeping into the hall, her plum coloured hat adorned with an alarming profusion of artificial grapes, "I trust you are well. I was just passing, you understand, and thought I simply must pop in. The roses in your front garden are looking particularly robust this season. Though I do wonder if that rambler might benefit from a more aggressive pruning. It is quite encroaching upon the path, you see."

Before Miss Millicent could offer a reply concerning the rambler's assertiveness, Mrs. Higgins's gaze, sharp and inquisitive, fell upon the new occupant of the landing. Her pronouncements on the rose garden were momentarily forgotten. Her eyes widened, her mouth formed a small, perfect 'o' of surprise, and she stopped dead, one hand clutching her reticule.

"Good heavens, Millicent!" she exclaimed, her voice rising in pitch. "What, pray tell, is that extraordinary contraption? It appears to be a pagoda for particularly exotic parrots, or perhaps a miniature temple of some kind! I do declare, I have never witnessed its

equal in all my seventy-two years, and I have, as you well know, Millicent, seen a great many things, both remarkable and utterly bewildering, in my time." Mrs. Higgins, whose pronouncements were often delivered with the unwavering conviction of a seasoned orator addressing a bewildered multitude, took a step back, her gaze sweeping over the intricate structure as if it were a newly discovered species of flora, perhaps something venomous, or at the very least, profoundly out of place in a respectable English hallway. Her plum coloured hat, with its rather unfortunate cluster of artificial grapes, seemed to quiver slightly with the intensity of her appraisal.

Miss Millicent, who had been on the verge of offering her customary pleasantries regarding Mrs. Higgins's apparent good health and timely arrival, found herself momentarily bereft of speech. The suddenness of Mrs. Higgins's exclamations, combined with their characteristic theatricality, had a tendency to disarm her. She felt a familiar prickle of apprehension, a sensation not unlike the apprehension one might feel before a particularly rigorous inspection of one's pantry, or perhaps a sudden, unexpected downpour on the day of a much anticipated garden party. "Why, Mrs. Higgins," she began, attempting to infuse her voice with a lightness she did not entirely feel, "it is merely a birdcage, you see. A rather substantial one, I grant you, but a birdcage nonetheless." She gestured vaguely towards the pagoda, a gesture which, she realised almost immediately, did little to clarify matters.

Mrs. Higgins, however, was not one to be easily mollified by such a simple designation. "A birdcage?" she echoed, her voice tinged with an incredulity that suggested Miss Millicent had just proposed the installation of a full sized carousel upon the landing. "My dear Millicent, I assure you, I have

My dear Millicent, I assure you, I have seen birdcages, a great many birdcages, from the humble wicker confines of a canary's abode to the gilded extravagances favoured by certain ladies of title who harboured a penchant for the more flamboyant varieties of finch, but never, and I do mean never, have I beheld a structure so architectural, so overtly ornamental, so utterly, undeniably pagan in its design, that it could possibly be mistaken for the simple dwelling of a feathered creature. Are you quite certain, my dear, that it is not, in fact, some recently unearthed artefact from an archaeological dig, perhaps a rather miniature temple dedicated to a particularly obscure deity of the Orient, or even, dare I suggest it, a rather avant-garde garden folly mistakenly installed within the confines of your very respectable hallway? The intricacy of those carvings, my dear, and the curious little upturned eaves, why, they practically scream of foreign climes and rituals best left to those who understand such things, far, far away from the sensibilities of an English landing, however spacious.

Millicent felt a familiar flush creep up her neck, a sensation she always associated with Mrs. Higgins's more robust interrogations, which, while ostensibly framed as expressions of concern or curiosity, invariably felt rather more like a cross-examination conducted by a particularly tenacious barrister intent on exposing some fundamental flaw in one's domestic arrangements. She wished, with a silent but fervent urgency, that the floorboards might open up beneath her, or that a passing delivery van might, with a perfectly timed shriek of its horn, provide a much needed distraction. It was always thus with Mrs. Higgins, whose enquiries, once initiated, possessed the relentless, inexorable momentum of a particularly well greased locomotive, gathering speed and an impressive volume of steam until every conceivable detail had been thoroughly scrutinised, dissected, and ultimately, delivered back to its unfortunate owner encased in a new and rather unflattering interpretation.

"But Mrs. Higgins," Millicent ventured, her voice a little breathier than she would have liked, "it truly is a birdcage. For a parrot, you see. My Uncle Alistair, bless his rather eccentric soul, bequeathed it to me in his will, along with its occupant. He was always rather fond of the exotic, you will recall, though I confess I had quite forgotten the sheer scale of his enthusiasms until this arrived, rather unexpectedly, upon my doorstep just yesterday morning. A very large crate it was, requiring no fewer than three rather brawny chaps from the railway to manoeuvre it into the hall. Quite an undertaking, I assure you, and rather disruptive to the milkman's usual schedule."

Mrs. Higgins, however, appeared to be only partially listening, her gaze still fixed with an almost mesmerised intensity upon the elaborate structure, her mouth now pursed into a thoughtful line that suggested the diligent application of considerable intellectual horsepower. "Uncle Alistair," she murmured, as if the name itself provided a sudden, illuminating piece of the puzzle, "yes, I recall Alistair. A man of peculiar tastes, certainly, and one whose sense of proportion was, shall we say, rather uniquely his own. He once attempted to cultivate miniature pineapples in his conservatory, did he not, convinced they would fetch a handsome price at the village fete? And there was that regrettable incident with the ornamental goldfish, which, I am reliably informed, grew to an alarming size in his pond and devoured all the smaller, more charming specimens. But even Alistair, with all his quirks, my dear, to commission a birdcage that so strikingly resembles a Buddhist shrine, well, it does rather suggest a new level of extravagance, does it not? One might almost imagine him attempting to achieve a state of enlightenment by communing with his feathered friend." She paused, then tilted her head, her plum hat with its quivering grapes momentarily obstructing Millicent's view. "And the occupant, you say? A parrot? One trusts it is not one of those frightfully loud Macaws, Millicent, which are quite capable, I understand, of stripping wallpaper with their beaks and repeating rather colourful phrases learned from sailors, thus rendering one's drawing room quite unsuitable for afternoon callers."

It was at this precise juncture, just as Mrs. Higgins was preparing to launch into a detailed disquisition upon the peculiar architectural features of various ancient civilisations, or perhaps the social implications of avian profanity, that a distinct and rather unexpected sound emanated from within the supposed "birdcage." A sound not entirely melodious, nor particularly delicate, but rather a robust, somewhat guttural squawk, followed by a peculiar rustling that suggested a creature of more than passing acquaintance with the art of considerable commotion. A moment later, a flash of brilliant emerald green, accented with vibrant cobalt blue and fiery scarlet, darted across one of the pagoda's intricate openings. The creature, for such it clearly was, then settled upon a perch, its head cocked at an angle, revealing a formidable, hooked beak and a pair of intelligent, obsidian eyes that seemed to regard Mrs. Higgins with a distinct air of amused appraisal. It was, beyond any shadow of a doubt, a parrot, and a rather magnificent, indeed rather imposing, specimen at that.

"Good heavens!" Mrs. Higgins exclaimed, taking another step back, her hand flying to her throat as if to quell a sudden surge of vocalisation. The grapes on her hat performed a brief, agitated dance. "It lives! And what a creature! It is not merely a birdcage, Millicent, it is a veritable avian monarch's palace, and its inhabitant, a feathered potentate! Look at the colours, my dear, quite blinding, like a tropical sunset come to life. And those eyes, so unnervingly perceptive. It looks as though it is assessing my hat, Millicent. I do hope it is not considering it a potential nesting site, or perhaps a rather flamboyant dessert. What kind of parrot is it, pray tell? It is certainly not one of those rather drab grey ones that merely utter polite good mornings and occasional imitations of the door chimes."

Millicent, somewhat relieved that the parrot's sudden appearance had at least diverted Mrs. Higgins's architectural analysis, felt a faint stirring of affection for the colourful creature, despite the considerable logistical challenges it presented. "He is, I believe, a Green Wing Macaw, Mrs. Higgins," she explained, attempting a tone of knowledgeable proprietorship, though inwardly she still felt rather overwhelmed by the bird's sheer presence. "His name, according to Uncle Alistair's rather scrawled note, is 'Commodore'. And yes, he is rather large, I confess. But I am assured he is quite a gentle soul, despite his formidable appearance. Though he does have a particular fondness for walnuts, which he cracks with a truly impressive force."

The Commodore, as if in response to this introduction, let out another squawk, this one rather more resonant, and then, to Millicent's profound mortification and Mrs. Higgins's wide-eyed astonishment, it distinctly articulated a word. "Botheration!" the parrot declared, in a voice remarkably clear and surprisingly deep, a voice that carried the distinct timbre of a grizzled, if perhaps slightly disgruntled, gentleman of advanced years. It then ruffled its magnificent plumage, as if rather pleased with its pronouncement.

Mrs. Higgins gasped, a small, choked sound that was quite unlike her usual resonant exclamations. Her hand, which had briefly descended from her throat, shot up again, this time to grasp her reticule with a white knuckled intensity. "Botheration!" she repeated, her voice a mere whisper, "Did that bird just say, 'Botheration!'? Millicent, my dear, are you quite certain this creature is merely a parrot? It possesses an astonishing command of the King's English, if a somewhat, shall we say, *robust* vocabulary for a drawing-room companion. Good heavens, what an astonishing turn of events! I confess, I had anticipated a delightful tea, perhaps a quiet discussion about the new vicar's rather ill advised sermon on the perils of procrastination, but I certainly did not expect to be addressed by a feathered philosopher of such articulate, if somewhat exclamatory, persuasion!"

Millicent felt the familiar prickle of heat return to her cheeks. "Yes, well," she began, floundering a little, "Uncle Alistair was rather fond of teaching him phrases. He had a great many naval acquaintances, you see, and I believe the Commodore spent rather a lot of time in their company. There are, I am told, a good many other phrases he knows, though I have yet to discover the full extent of his repertoire. I do hope, Mrs. Higgins, that you will forgive his rather... direct manner of speaking. He means no offence, I assure you."

Mrs. Higgins, however, appeared utterly captivated by the Commodore, her usual critical faculties momentarily suspended in the face of such an extraordinary spectacle. Her lips moved silently as she replayed the parrot's utterance, her expression a mixture of profound shock and burgeoning fascination. "No offence, my dear Millicent," she finally managed, her voice regaining some of its customary vigour, "but a parrot that says 'Botheration!' with such conviction is certainly a topic of considerable conversational merit. It quite eclipses the vicar's sermon, I assure you, however ill advised. This is, without a doubt, the most remarkable acquisition I have ever witnessed in a private residence, and that includes the time Lady Griselda bought that rather unfortunate stuffed anteater from an expedition to Brazil, which, despite her assurances, never quite managed to look anything other than profoundly uncomfortable in her otherwise exquisite conservatory. But a talking parrot, Millicent, a talking parrot that says 'Botheration!' It is quite beyond belief!"

The Commodore, perhaps sensing the shift in Mrs. Higgins's demeanour from initial alarm to intense interest, let out a soft chuckle, a sound so utterly human it sent a shiver down Millicent's spine. It was a rather knowing chuckle, she thought, the kind one might hear from an old sea captain observing the bewilderment of a landlubber.

"Well," Millicent said, attempting to steer the conversation back to more conventional social channels, "perhaps we should take tea in the drawing room, Mrs. Higgins? I am sure the Commodore would appreciate a moment of quiet contemplation after his arduous journey, and I have made those rather delightful lemon drizzle scones you are so fond of." She offered a hopeful smile, a silent plea for the resumption of normalcy.

Mrs. Higgins, however, was not so easily swayed from her current preoccupation. She glanced briefly at Millicent, then back at the bird, her eyes still wide with wonder. "Tea, yes, tea would be delightful, Millicent. But tell me, does he say anything else? Anything more, shall we say, *descriptive*? Perhaps a short poem? Or an opinion on the current political climate? One hears such dreadful things about parrots, you know, capable of repeating entire conversations, even those of a most private and delicate nature. Imagine the consternation if he were to repeat, for instance, Mrs. Henderson's rather unflattering remarks about the village fête committee's choice of floral arrangements! The scandal, Millicent, the sheer, unadulterated scandal!" Her voice rose in pitch with the delicious anticipation of potential gossip.

Millicent felt a wave of despair wash over her. She had hoped to avoid precisely this line of enquiry. Uncle Alistair, she knew, had been a man of many secrets, and the Commodore, by all accounts, had been privy to a great many of them. The thought of the bird inadvertently broadcasting some long buried family indiscretion, or worse, some of Uncle Alistair's more colourful opinions on local dignitaries, filled her with a profound sense of dread.

"I am quite certain," Millicent stated, perhaps a little too emphatically, "that the Commodore is far too discerning to repeat anything of a truly compromising nature. He is, after all, a parrot of noble lineage, I am quite sure, and would understand the sanctity of private conversations. I have, in fact, been attempting to teach him to say 'Please pass the sugar', a phrase which I believe would be of far greater utility in a domestic setting."

The Commodore, as if understanding the delicate nature of the discussion, let out another squawk, this one followed by a distinct, if somewhat garbled, rendition of "Shiver me timbers!" It then commenced a vigorous preening session, as if the topic of conversation had become entirely too terrestrial for its elevated sensibilities.

Mrs. Higgins clapped her hands together, a sound surprisingly sharp and sudden. "Shiver me timbers!" she exclaimed, a delighted laugh bubbling up from her ample bosom. "Oh, Millicent, this is quite, quite wonderful! I do believe he has a sense of humour, a rather robust, nautical sense of humour, to be sure. This changes everything, my dear. One cannot simply have tea with a parrot who says 'Shiver me timbers!' without first establishing the full extent of his conversational capabilities. We must, I insist, endeavour to coax more out of him. Imagine the entertainment at the next bridge club! Mrs. Plummett's parrot, a dreary little thing that only squawks 'Hello, dolly!' when offered a seed, will be quite eclipsed, quite utterly eclipsed!"

Millicent, envisioning her drawing room transformed into a veritable theatre for avian performance, felt a tremor of apprehension. The prospect of Mrs. Higgins encouraging the Commodore to display his full, potentially scandalous, repertoire filled her with a sense of impending doom. However, to refuse Mrs. Higgins's insistent curiosity would be considered a social transgression of the highest order, akin to serving lukewarm tea or, worse still, running out of milk.

"Well," Millicent conceded, sighing inwardly, "perhaps after tea, Mrs. Higgins, we might... observe him for a while. But first, the scones are cooling, and I daresay a cup of Earl Grey would be most welcome after such an... eventful morning." She tried to imbue her voice with a persuasive, hostess like charm, hoping to appeal to Mrs. Higgins's ingrained sense of proper social order.

Mrs. Higgins, after a final, lingering glance at the now preening Commodore, whose emerald head was tucked beneath a sapphire wing, seemed to reluctantly assent. "Very well, Millicent, tea first. Though I confess, my mind will be rather preoccupied with the prospect of your feathered orator. One cannot simply dismiss a parrot that says 'Botheration!' and 'Shiver me timbers!' as a mere domestic pet, can one? It is a conversationalist, Millicent, a genuine conversationalist, albeit one who communicates through a rather flamboyant avian interpreter. I must say, Alistair always did have a knack for the unexpected, though I believe this particular surprise quite surpasses all his previous eccentricities. The miniature pineapples pale into insignificance, truly they do."

With a rustle of her plum coloured skirt and a renewed quiver of her grape adorned hat, Mrs. Higgins finally allowed Millicent to usher her towards the drawing room. As they passed the formidable pagoda, the Commodore, as if unable to resist one last dramatic flourish, lifted his head, fixed his intelligent eyes upon Mrs. Higgins, and with a distinct and perfectly enunciated clarity, declared, "Polly wants a cracker, you scallywag!"

Mrs. Higgins stopped dead in her tracks, her mouth forming another perfect 'o' of astonishment, larger this time, and remained frozen for a beat, two beats, before a slow, delighted smile spread across her face. "You scallywag!" she echoed, her voice filled with a profound and almost girlish glee. "Oh, Millicent, this is truly a marvel! A genuine, bona fide marvel! We simply must tell Mrs. Henderson. She will be absolutely green with envy. Her wretched budgerigar, a flighty little thing, hardly even chirps!"

"A truly momentous occasion! One feels the very air hum with the weight of your presence! So very much the hero, one imagines, of countless nautical adventures, tales of daring and derring do on the high seas!"

The Commodore paused, his grey eyes fixed upon Mrs. Higgins, a faint, almost imperceptible frown creasing his brow. His silence was far more potent than any spoken rebuke. Mrs. Higgins, sensing perhaps, for the first time, a truly unyielding audience, faltered slightly, her dramatic flourish losing some of its accustomed vigor.

"Madam," the Commodore finally stated, his voice devoid of any warmth or theatricality, "my adventures, such as they were, involved a great deal of waiting, precise navigation, and the occasional, rather dull, bout of mechanical repair. Derring do, as you term it, was largely confined to ensuring the proper functioning of the engine room and avoiding unpleasant encounters with unusually large squid. I assure you, the reality is considerably less romantic than the theatrical imaginings of those who have never left dry land." His gaze then drifted to Mrs. Higgins's hat, a look of profound, almost surgical, disapproval etched upon his face. "And may I enquire, madam, if you are attempting to cultivate a small, portable garden upon your person? One might mistake you for a particularly well stocked horticultural display, rather than a guest at a drawing room tea."

A collective gasp, discreet and immediately suppressed, rippled through the room. Mrs. Higgins's face, beneath its powder and rouge, flushed a delicate shade of crimson. Her hand flew, not to her bosom, but to her hat, a gesture of instinctive, almost childlike, defense. Lady Agatha Ponsonby, for her part, allowed herself a small, triumphant smile, the corner of her lips twitching upwards in a fleeting moment of undisguised satisfaction. Uncle Alistair let out a genuinely amused snort, an unrepentant sound that drew a momentary glare from the now thoroughly mortified Mrs. Higgins. Miss Plum, however, merely observed the scene with an almost scientific interest, a flicker of appreciation for the Commodore's unapologetic candor dancing in her green eyes. Even Mr. Henderson, who had apparently decided to abandon his horticultural detachment, offered a barely perceptible nod of agreement with the Commodore's assessment, perhaps finding a kindred spirit in such unvarnished truth.

Millicent, feeling a sudden surge of something akin to exasperated admiration for the Commodore's brutal honesty, took a deep, steadying breath. "Commodore Armitage," she interjected, her voice carefully modulated to convey both authority and a subtle plea for decorum. "Perhaps we can discuss the fascinating world of naval engineering and unusually large cephalopods over a cup of tea. I assure you, the tea itself is rather less adorned than some of our more, shall we say, spirited guests." She offered him a cup of steaming Earl Grey, strong and dark, precisely as she imagined a man of his unyielding disposition would prefer it.

The Commodore took the cup from her hand, his large fingers dwarfing the delicate porcelain. He took a slow, deliberate sip, his eyes, still holding that unblinking directness, meeting Millicent's. A faint, almost imperceptible nod followed, a silent acknowledgement of her diplomatic efforts. "Very well, Millicent. Tea, then. And perhaps we can discuss the regrettable tendency of some individuals to mistake a social gathering for a botanical exhibition. Or perhaps," he added, his gaze returning, with an almost inevitable force, to Mrs. Higgins, "a rather badly planned theatrical tableau."

Mrs. Higgins, utterly vanquished, merely emitted a small, choked sound, her eyes wide with a mixture of outrage and profound, theatrical self pity. The stage, Millicent knew, was now truly set. The players, no longer unwitting puppets, were now fully engaged, each revealing their true colors under the penetrating, unblinking gaze of the Commodore, whose arrival had ignited the spark of scandal into a full blown, albeit politely contained, conflagration. The afternoon, Millicent realized, was going to be even more eventful than she had dared to imagine. She took a fortifying sip of her own tea, preparing for the inevitable social battle that was only just beginning. The delicate porcelain, usually a symbol of quiet refinement, now felt like a fragile shield against the onslaught of unvarnished truth and unabashed performance.
