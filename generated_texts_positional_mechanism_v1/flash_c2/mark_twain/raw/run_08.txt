The sun was beating down that day till the dust on the main street looked like gold, and you could see the heat rising off the river like a ghost. Wagons was hitched all along the general store and a few of the boys was swimmin’ down at the bend, but mostly folks just sat on their porches sippin’ their lukewarm cider and fannin’ themselves with palmetto leaves. It was a slow kind of day, the kind where you hear every fly buzz, and the only sound was the creakin’ of the general store sign in the little breeze there was. I was sittin’ on a barrel outside Jed’s Dry Goods, whittlin’ a stick and watchin’ a big ol’ hog scratch itself against a post when all of a sudden a cloud of dust came up the road, and then a wagon. It warn’t no ordinary farm wagon either. It had a big painted sign on the side that said “Brother Ezekiel’s Gospel of Fire and Brimstone” and it was pulled by two scrawny mules that looked like they hadn’t seen a decent meal in a month of Sundays. Brother Ezekiel himself was standin’ up in the wagon, a tall skinny man with a long black coat that looked too hot for this kind of weather, and he was yellin’ at the top of his lungs, “Repent ye sinners, for the hour is at hand!”

He kept on a-comin’ right to the center of town, where the church used to be before it slid into the river last spring, and he pulled them mules up short with a jerk. Folks started driftin’ out of their houses and off their porches, rubbin’ their eyes like they’d just woke up. Brother Ezekiel stood there, taller than a polecat on a fence post, and he begun to preach. He talked about sin and fire and damnation, and how this here town was full of idle hands and lazy hearts, and he looked right at old Man Tibbs, who was just then comin’ out of his little bait shop, carryin’ a bucket of minnows. Old Man Tibbs was a quiet sort, never said much, but everybody in town respected him, knowed he was honest and could catch a fish when nobody else could. He was about seventy year old, with a face like a dried apple and eyes that always seemed to be lookin’ at somethin’ far off. He didn’t pay the preacher no mind, just went to set his minnow bucket down by his porch swing. But Brother Ezekiel warn’t havin’ none of that. He puffed himself up and pointed a long, bony finger right at old Man Tibbs and he hollers, “There he is, a man of the river, a man of the flesh, spendin’ his days with worms and fishes whilst the good Lord’s work goes undone!”

Old Man Tibbs just stopped, put his bucket down, and looked at the preacher. He didn’t say nothin’, just kept lookin’. The crowd was gettin’ bigger now, and nobody was laughin’ or talkin’ much. You could feel the air gettin’ tight. Brother Ezekiel, he seemed to take that quiet as a challenge. He climbed down from his wagon and walked right up to Tibbs’s porch. “What say you, old man? Don’t you know the good book says, “Cast not your pearls before swine” and yet you cast your days before the fish of the river?” He was yellin’ so loud a dog under a wagon woke up and gave a little yelp.

Old Man Tibbs took a slow breath, and then he says, quiet-like, “The Lord made the fish, Brother, and He made the river too. And He made a man’s belly to be filled.”

Brother Ezekiel laughed, a real loud, brayin’ kind of laugh. “Filled with the spirit, old man, not with catfish and mud-turtles! You’re a man of sloth, a man of idleness, and I tell you, your soul is as muddy as that river you love!” He spit on the ground right near Tibbs’s foot.

The crowd stirred. I seen a couple of the younger fellers clench their fists. Old Man Tibbs’s eyes got a little darker, like the river just before a storm. He looked down at the spit, and then he looked back up at Brother Ezekiel. He didn’t raise his voice a bit, just spoke clear as a bell. “Brother Ezekiel,” he says, “I ain’t much for book learnin’ but I reckon I know a thing or two about the Lord’s creations. And I reckon the Lord give a man a brain to use, and hands to work with, and a mouth to be careful what comes out of it.” Then, quick as a flash, quicker than a snake strikes, Old Man Tibbs reached into his bucket of minnows. He pulled out a big, live, wrigglin’ mud-turtle, about the size of a dinner plate. And he held it up right in front of Brother Ezekiel’s face. The turtle was kickin’ its legs and tryin’ to bite.

Brother Ezekiel let out a shriek like a scalded cat. He jumped back, trip’d over his own feet, and fell right into the dusty street with a thud. The mud-turtle went flyin’ out of Tibbs’s hand and landed right in Ezekiel’s lap, its little head dartin’ out. Ezekiel was yellin’ and flailin’ his arms, tryin’ to shake the turtle off, and the mules hitched to his wagon got spooked and started to back up. The wagon wheels scraped on the street, and Ezekiel was still yellin’, “A beast! A devilish beast!”

Old Man Tibbs just stood there on his porch, lookin’ down at the preacher and the turtle. He didn’t say another word. But a slow, sort of tired smile came onto his face, like the sun comin’ out after a long rain. The crowd burst out laughin’, a big, roaring sound that filled the whole street. Some of the men started clappin’ and stompin’ their feet. Brother Ezekiel finally got the turtle off him, threw it in the dust, and scrambled to his feet, brushin’ himself off and lookin’ wild-eyed around at the laughin’ crowd. He jumped back into his wagon and snatched up the reins. He gave the mules a good whack, and they started off, kickin’ up dust all the way down the road, with Brother Ezekiel still standin’ and yellin’ about the devil’s creatures and the sin of laughter, till he was just a speck in the heat haze. Old Man Tibbs just walked back into his bait shop, quiet as ever. The laughter died down some, and folks started goin’ back to their porches, but I reckon everybody felt a bit better after that, and nobody was talkin’ about fire and brimstone anymore, just about Old Man Tibbs and his mud-turtle.

Sherburn, that was the name. And the crowd, they took up the cry like a pack of hounds after a fox, only this fox was a man, and he’d just done what they all seen. Sherburn ought to be lynched, they said, and then they all started saying it, louder and louder, like a storm brewing, and pretty soon they were moving, a whole river of folks, pouring down the street toward the Colonel’s house. I was scared, I tell you. My heart was in my throat, and my legs felt like water, but I couldn’t help myself, I just had to see what was a-going to happen. So I joined the tail end of the mob, keeping to the shadows, like a little minnow trying to keep up with the big fish.

The noise was something fierce, all that yellin’ and hollerin’, and the feet a-trampin’ on the dusty road, raising a cloud that hung in the air like a fog. Women were there, too, and even some children, all of them carried along by the current of the crowd, their faces flushed with excitement and something else, something mean, like dogs when they smell blood. Some of them had ropes, big thick ones, coiled over their shoulders, and others had guns, not like Sherburn’s fancy pistol, but old rusty shotguns and squirrel rifles. They wasn’t aiming them, but just the sight of them made a shiver run down my spine. They was all excited, and talking loud about justice, and how a man couldn’t just kill another man in cold blood in front of everybody and get away with it, not in their town. It was a terrible thing to see, all these people, most of them just regular folks, acting like a single, monstrous creature, moving with a single mind, and that mind was set on violence.

Colonel Sherburn, he lived in a big house, the biggest in town, set back from the street a ways, with a white picket fence around it, and a garden out front that always looked neat and trim. It was a fine house, painted white, and it looked like it didn’t belong in a town like this, where most of the houses were leaning and saggin’ like old men after a long day’s work. The mob, they stopped outside the fence, sort of milling around, the noise dying down a little, like a wave that hits the shore and then pulls back, hesitating. You could feel the tension, thick as the dust in the air. They were all lookin’ at the house, waiting. Nobody made a move to open the gate or climb the fence. It was like they expected the house itself to do something, to give up the man inside.

Then, the front door of the house opened, slow and quiet, and out stepped Colonel Sherburn. He wasn’t a bit surprised, not a lick. He just stood there on his porch, a tall figure in the doorway, framed by the white paint of his house. He didn’t have his pistol, and he didn’t have a weapon of any kind that I could see. He just had his two hands stuck into his pockets, and his head held high, like he always did. His face was pale, not with fear, but like marble, and his eyes, they just looked out at the crowd, calm and steady, like a hawk looking down at a bunch of chickens. He didn’t say a word, just stood there and looked at them, and it was a look that seemed to go right through them, right to their bones.

The crowd, they started to get uneasy. The yelling and hollering had died down almost to nothing, and all you could hear was the shuffling of feet and a cough here and there. Some of the men in front, the ones who had been loudest a minute ago, they started to shift their weight, and look at each other, and then look down at their feet. It was a funny thing, how one man, standing alone, could stop a whole mob of angry people dead in their tracks, just by looking at them. It warn’t fear that was stopping them, not exactly, but something else, something like shame, or maybe just confusion. They had come to lynch a man, but the man they had come to lynch didn’t seem to be afraid, and that took all the fun out of it for them.

Then Sherburn, he took his hands out of his pockets and folded his arms across his chest. He still didn’t say a word, just looked them up and down, and a slow, contemptuous smile spread across his lips. It wasn’t a friendly smile, not by a long shot. It was a smile that said he thought they were all worms, and not even worth the trouble of stepping on. And when he finally spoke, his voice was clear and cold, and it carried over the crowd without him even seeming to raise it much.

“The idea of you lynching anybody!” he says. “It’s amusing. The idea of you people doing anything but sneaking around in the dark and doing your dirty work under cover of night. There isn’t a man in this town, from the biggest bully to the smallest runt, who wouldn’t run a mile if he thought there was a fair fight waiting for him. You come here now, a hundred of you, maybe two hundred, all of you hiding behind each other’s backs, because you haven’t got the guts to face one man alone. You’re a mob, that’s what you are. A pack of cowards, that’s what you are. And you call yourselves men.”

He paused then, and just looked at them, letting his words sink in. The crowd, they didn’t know what to do. Some of them looked like they wanted to say something back, to argue, but they couldn’t find the words. Others just shuffled their feet and looked down, their faces getting red. It was clear that Sherburn had hit a nerve. He wasn’t just talking about their actions, he was talking about their very souls, telling them what they really were.

“You’re brave enough to come here in a crowd,” he went on, “and you think that makes you strong. But a mob ain’t strong, it’s just big. And it’s only as brave as the weakest among it. You’re a pitiful lot. You’re the kind that’ll hang around a saloon all day, talkin’ big, and then go home and beat your wives. You’re the kind that’ll take advantage of a drunk man, but you won’t stand up to one when he’s sober. You’re all mouth and no trousers, every single one of you.”

He was giving them a tongue-lashing, a real dressing down, and nobody dared to interrupt him. It was a strange sight, a whole mob being lectured by one man, and them taking it, too. He was telling them the truth, a harsh, ugly truth that they didn’t want to hear, but couldn’t deny.

“You talk about justice,” he says, and he scoffed, a little sound of pure disgust. “You wouldn’t know justice if it bit you. You don’t want justice; you want excitement. You want a show. You want to feel important, for once in your miserable lives. You want to see blood. But you want to see it from a distance, where you’re safe, where nobody can point a finger at you alone. You’re too yellow to act like men, too yellow to be anything but a rabble.”

He took another pause, and you could feel the words hanging in the air, thick and heavy. The silence was louder than any of the noise had been. Nobody moved. Nobody spoke. It was like they were frozen, trapped by his gaze and his cutting words.

“There’s not a man in that crowd who has got the grit to face a man in broad daylight,” he continued, and his voice got a little louder now, carrying more authority. “Not a single one of you. You’re all afraid. You’re afraid of me, yes, but you’re mostly afraid of each other. You’re afraid to step out of the crowd, afraid to be seen as different. You’re a disgrace to the very name of man. Now get out of here. Go home. Go back to your whiskey and your worthless lives. The law will take care of me, or it won’t. But it won’t be you. Not today. Not ever.”

And with that, he turned, slow and deliberate, and walked back into his house. He didn’t slam the door, he just closed it quiet, and then he was gone.

The crowd stood there for a minute, maybe two, maybe even longer, just staring at that closed door. The dust settled around them, and the sun beat down, and everything felt suddenly quiet and still. It was like a spell had been broken. The angry, unified creature they had been dissolved back into a bunch of individual men and women, all of them looking confused and a little bit ashamed. Nobody said anything for a while. Then, one man, a big fellow who had been carrying a rope, he sort of coughed and shifted his feet, and then he turned and started to walk away, slow, not looking at anybody. Another one followed, and then another, and pretty soon, the whole mob was just melting away, drifting off in different directions, some of them kicking at the dust, some of them looking over their shoulders, like they expected Sherburn to jump out and say something else. But he didn’t.

I stayed put for a long time, watching them go, until there was only a handful left, standing around looking stupid, and then they too wandered off. I never seen anything like it. A man, all by himself, had faced down a whole mob and made them turn tail just with words. It made me think that there was more to people than just what you seen on the outside, and sometimes the biggest show was just that, a show, and the real strength was in something else entirely.

I walked back down the street, my legs feeling a bit shaky, but my mind spinning with what I had seen. The excitement and the meanness had drained out of the air, and the town was going back to its usual lazy hum. People were gathering in small knots, talking in low voices, not about lynching anymore, but about what Sherburn had said, and about how he had faced them all down. Nobody was laughing now, not like they had been with Boggs. It was like they had all been slapped in the face, and it had woken them up a bit.

I seen the lanky man, the one who had been re-enacting the shooting, standing by himself, his fur stovepipe hat pushed back on his head, his crooked cane hanging from his hand. He looked like he’d lost his appetite for performance. He just looked bewildered, like someone had stolen his thunder and then kicked him in the pants besides. He wasn’t drawing circles in the dirt anymore. The street was quiet, like a graveyard after a funeral.

I wandered over to the drug store where they’d taken Boggs. The crowd wasn’t pushing and shoving anymore, but there was still a knot of people inside, and some lingering outside, talking low. I edged my way up to the window again, where I’d been before. They’d covered Boggs with a sheet now, but his daughter was still there, sitting on the floor beside him, her shoulders shaking with silent sobs. Two women were trying to comfort her, but she just seemed lost in her own sorrow. It was a sad sight, and it made the whole business with the mob seem even uglier, because for all their bluster and talk of justice, they had forgotten about the real sorrow of what had happened. They just wanted a show, like Sherburn had said.

I didn’t stay long at the drug store. The feeling in there was too heavy, too sad. I went down to the river, where I always went when I needed to think things over. The river, it just kept rolling along, minding its own business, not caring about the troubles of men. I sat on the bank for a long time, watching the water, and thinking about Boggs and Sherburn, and the mob, and how strange people were.

Boggs, he was a loudmouth, a drunk, and everybody made fun of him, but he never hurt nobody. Sherburn, he was quiet, proud, and he killed a man, but then he stood up to a whole town. And the town, they were all hot and bothered about lynching him one minute, and then they melted away like snow in summer just because he called them cowards. It didn’t make much sense to me, none of it. It made me wonder about right and wrong, and how people decided what was what. It seemed like whatever was loud or exciting at the moment was what folks believed in, until something louder or more exciting came along.

The sun started to go down, painting the sky with colors like a spilled paintbox, all red and orange and purple. A steamboat whistled in the distance, a long, mournful sound that echoed over the water. I figured I ought to be getting back to where I was staying. My stomach was growling a bit, and the thought of some supper sounded good.

As I walked back through the town, the air had cooled some, and a few lights were starting to flicker on in the windows. The main street was mostly empty now, just a few stragglers heading home. It was like nothing much had happened, but I knew it had. It had happened for me, and I reckoned it had happened for a lot of others, too, whether they wanted to admit it or not. The town had seen itself in a mirror that day, and it warn’t a pretty sight.

I seen a couple of men sitting on a bench outside the general store, talking low. As I passed, I heard one of them say, “He warn’t wrong, though, about us. We did look like a bunch of fools.” The other man just grunted, and didn’t say anything. It seemed like Sherburn’s words had stuck with them, burrowed down into their consciences like a tick.

I thought about Colonel Sherburn, locked up in his big house, and I wondered what he was thinking. Was he proud of himself? Was he just tired of it all? He was a strange man, a powerful man, in his own way. He didn’t care what people thought of him, and that made him different from almost everybody else. Most folks, they spent their whole lives trying to get people to like them, or to think they were important, or to be afraid of them. But Sherburn, he just seemed to be himself, for better or worse, and let the chips fall where they might.

I also thought about Boggs. He was gone now, just a body in a drug store, and his daughter left to cry over him. For all his bluster and his monthly drunks, he hadn’t seemed like a bad man, just a foolish one. And the way he died, in the street, after being tormented by the crowd and then shot down by a man who looked like he didn’t even break a sweat doing it, it seemed like a hard end for a man who never meant no real harm. It made me wonder if sometimes the people who seemed the weakest were actually the ones who needed the most protection, and the ones who seemed the strongest were the ones who carried the most danger.

The whole day was a mess of contradictions, a tangle of right and wrong that didn’t sort itself out easy. It wasn’t like the stories in books, where the bad man was always clearly bad, and the good man always clearly good. In real life, it seemed like things were all mixed up, and people could be a little bit of everything all at once. And that was a thing that made the world a lot more complicated than I ever thought it was.

I figured I’d better not be seen loitering too much, or folks would start asking questions, and I didn’t have any good answers. I just wanted to forget the whole thing, or at least put it away in a corner of my mind for later, when I could make more sense of it. But I knew I wouldn’t forget it. It was one of them days that burned itself into your memory, like a brand. It showed me things about people that I hadn’t known before, and some of them things weren’t pretty. It showed me how a crowd could be a terrible thing, and how one man, even a bad one, could sometimes stand taller than all of them put together.

I kept walking, and pretty soon I was out of the main part of town, heading for the edge. The smells changed, from dust and people and cooking food to wet earth and river smell. The air got a bit cooler, too, and the stars started to pop out in the darkening sky, one by one. They looked a long way off, and quiet, and they didn’t seem to care about any of the trouble down here on the riverbank. They just shone on, steady and bright, like they always did, and that was a comfort, somehow.

I thought about Jim, and how he always had a sensible way of looking at things. I wished he was there, so I could talk it all over with him. He might not have all the answers, but he’d listen, and sometimes just talking it out was enough. But he warn’t there, and I was on my own, with nothing but the river and the stars for company. And that was just how things were sometimes, out here in the world. You seen things, and you had to figure them out for yourself, whether you liked it or not.

I kicked a loose stone in the road, and it skittered off into the darkness. The whole day had been like that stone, skittering off into a place I didn’t expect, and landing in a pile of questions that I didn’t know how to answer. But I reckoned that was part of living, too. You just kept going, and you kept seeing things, and maybe, little by little, you started to understand a bit more of it all. And maybe, just maybe, that was enough.

I found my little skiff where I’d left it tied to a willow branch, half-hidden by some thick bushes and looking all peaceable and small. It was good to see it, like seeing an old friend after a long time away, even if it warn’t that long. I untied the rope and shoved her out into the current, careful and quiet, and then I clumb in. I pushed off from the bank with a paddle, and the river took me, slow and easy. The town was still making a kind of hum, a low sound of a lot of people talking and moving and being restless, but it got fainter and fainter as I drifted out, and pretty soon it was just the river sounds, the water gurgling and whispering against the sides of the skiff, and the crickets tuning up their fiddles in the reeds.

I laid down in the bottom of the skiff, and I looked up at the stars, and they seemed to swim a little, the way they do when your eyes are tired. My head was still full of what I seen that day, the shooting and the crowd, and Boggs’s poor girl screaming and crying over him. It warn’t a pretty sight, not by a long chalk, and it stuck to your mind like burrs to a dog’s tail. I thought about that Sherburn, standing there all stiff and proud, telling that mob what was what, and how they was all cowards and didn’t have the sand to face a man straight up. He was right, too, about them. They was brave enough when it was one old drunk man, but when it was a man with a gun and a cold eye, they tucked their tails and run, every last one of them. It made me feel a kind of sickness, seeing how quick people could change from being loud and threatening to being scared and quiet. It made you wonder what was real about them, and what was just put on for show.

I reckon I drifted for a good hour or two, just letting the current do its work, and my mind kept turning over all them things. I seen a good many strange things since I left home, and a good many bad things, and some things that warn’t neither one nor the other, just odd, like a hen laying a square egg. But this here Sherburn business, it felt different. It felt like it was getting at something deep down in folks, something ugly and something strong, all mixed up together. It made me think about Pap, and how he could be cruel and mean, and how folks around him just put up with it, or laughed about it till he got too bad. It made me think about how easy it was for people to be riled up, and how much trouble that could cause.

I found a quiet little bend in the river, where the bank was soft and sandy, and I pulled the skiff up onto it. I built a small fire, just big enough to boil a little coffee and warm up a piece of corn pone I had stashed away. The fire snapped and popped, and its light danced on the water, making the shadows stretch and shrink like living things. It was a good feeling, the fire, warm and friendly, a comfort against the big, dark night. I sat there for a long time, sipping my coffee and watching the embers glow, and I felt a bit better, a bit steadier in my mind. The river was a good place for thinking, better than any town, that was for sure. The river didn’t care what you thought, or what you done. It just kept on rolling, steady and sure, and it always seemed to know where it was going, which was more than I could say for myself most of the time.

I thought about what that man said, the one who told me Boggs didn’t mean nothing, that he was the best naturedest old fool in Arkansaw. And then he was dead, shot down in the street, with his daughter screaming over him. It just went to show you, I reckoned, that you never knew what was coming. One minute a man was a-whooping and a-hollering and saying he was on the warpath, and the next minute he was lying flat on his back, with a Bible on his chest. It was a quick kind of justice, and it warn’t like the kind they talked about in Sunday school. It was the kind that just happened, like a storm, and then it was over, and you was left to pick up the pieces.

The way that crowd had been, all eager and laughing, and then all sober and quiet, and then all ready to lynch a man, it just twisted my insides. It was like they didn’t have a mind of their own, just a big shared one that could turn on a dime. And Sherburn, he seen it, he knowed it. He called them out, and he was right. They was cowards, most of them, when it came to really facing up to something. They liked to talk big, and they liked to stir up trouble, and they liked to see a show, but when it came to standing their ground, they scattered like chickens. It made me wonder about all the big talk I heard in towns, all the bluster and the bragging. How much of it was real, and how much was just smoke?

I laid back, with my hands behind my head, and watched the stars. They seemed so far off and so cold, and yet they was there every night, steady as a rock. It made you feel small, looking at them, and it made your troubles seem small, too, in a way. Not that they went away, but they got put in their place, like. You was just a little speck on a big river, under a big sky, and the world was a heap bigger than any one town or any one man’s troubles. That was a comforting thought, sometimes, when things got too heavy.

I must have dozed off for a bit, because when I opened my eyes again, the sky was a bit lighter in the east, and the stars warn’t so bright. A faint mist was rising off the water, soft and ghostly, and the air was chill. I shivered, and then I stretched, and my bones cracked a bit. Time to move on, I reckoned. I couldn’t stay in one place too long, not with Jim waiting somewhere downriver, and not with the kind of ruckus I’d left behind. It was best to put some miles between me and that town, and between me and the memory of Boggs and Sherburn and that crazy crowd.

I doused the fire, making sure every last spark was out, and then I pushed the skiff back into the current. I paddled a little, just enough to get myself out into the main channel, and then I let the river take me again. The sun was just beginning to peek over the trees, painting the sky with streaks of pink and gold, and the mist was burning off, curling up into nothing. It was a fresh new morning, and it felt like a new start, even if my head was still cluttered with old thoughts.

I thought about Jim again, and a kind of warmth spread through me. Jim, he was steady, like the river. He didn’t get all riled up easy, and he didn’t go for all that bluster and show. He was just Jim, plain and honest, and he was a good friend to have. I missed him, and I reckoned I ought to try and find him soon. We had a plan, we did, and it warn’t a plan that involved getting mixed up in town squabbles and shootings. It was a plan about freedom, and about making our way in the world, quiet-like, without bothering nobody, and nobody bothering us. That was the best kind of plan, I reckoned.

As the sun climbed higher, the river came alive. A steamboat passed by, churning up the water, its whistle letting out a long, mournful sound that echoed across the bends. Log rafts floated by, slow and heavy, with men on them shouting and pushing with their poles. Fishermen in little skiffs sat patiently, their lines dangling in the water, and sometimes you’d see a family on a flatboat, their clothes hanging out to dry, and a dog sitting on the deck, watching the world go by. It was a whole world on the river, and it was a different world from the one in the towns. It was a world of movement, and of nature, and of folks mostly minding their own business.

We talked more, then, out in the quiet of the fields or on the porch swing at dusk. She told me about her husband, who’d passed on a few years back, and how she’d been managing the farm all by herself ever since. Her name was Mrs. Sally, she said, but most folks just called her Miss Sally. She never asked me about my past, not directly, just listened when I talked about the river, and about towns, and sometimes, when I was quiet for too long, she’d just say, “Trouble has a way of working itself out, if you give it enough space.”

I begun to feel like myself again, or a new version of myself anyway, one that knew how to split wood and mend fences and pull weeds, and one that didn’t jump at every sudden sound. The fear from the town started to fade, like a bad dream you can’t quite remember when you wake up. Oh, it was still there, in the quiet corners of my mind, but it warn’t as loud, and it warn’t as pressing. I found myself thinking about the feel of the axe in my hands, or the warmth of the sun on my back, instead of the sight of that man falling, or the sound of the girl screaming. It was a kind of healing, I reckon, the steady work and the quiet days and the kind presence of Miss Sally.

One afternoon, about a week after I’d arrived, Miss Sally sent me to the creek to check on the fence again, just to make sure no new pig had decided to make a break for it. The sun was hot, and the air was thick with the hum of bees, and I was whistling a little tune to myself, feeling content. As I got closer to the creek, I heard voices, and my stomach give a little lurch. It warn’t the kind of lonesome farm noises I’d got used to. These were men’s voices, loud and rough, and they sounded like they was comin’ from just around the bend in the creek, down by the old sycamore tree where the water ran deepest. My first thought was the mob, come to find me, and my heart started to pound like a drum. I stopped dead in my tracks, ducking behind a thick bush, and tried to make myself small, listening hard.

“I tell you, Sheriff, that’s what I heard down in town,” one voice said, a gravelly one. “They say that Sherburn fellow, he ain’t even been charged, not proper. Just let go, like it warn’t nothing.”
“Well, now, Jed, you know how these things go,” another voice, calmer and more official-like, replied. “There was a whole town full of witnesses, and they all seen it was self-defense, after Boggs had been makin’ threats all day. Folks was just lookin’ for an excuse to get riled up.”
“Riled up or not, a man was shot down in cold blood, Sheriff. And there’s talk of a young fella, a boy, seen with the Sherburn fella just before, maybe even helped him get away,” the first voice, Jed, insisted.
My breath caught in my throat. They was talkin’ about _that_ town. About _that_ shootin’. And about _me_.

I pressed myself tighter against the bush, trying to stop the trembling in my hands. I listened with all my might.
“Don’t you go listenin’ to every rumor that floats down the river, Jed,” the Sheriff said, sounding a bit tired. “Boggs had it comin’, sad to say, and Sherburn, he just had enough. And as for any boy, well, the river’s wide, and folks travel on it. Ain’t no call to go trackin’ down every wanderer. We got enough real trouble hereabouts, with that gang of rustlers been stealin’ hogs from old Man Peterson’s farm.”
“Rustlers or not, Sheriff, there’s folks in town who ain’t forgotten that day,” Jed grumbled. “They say the law ain’t doin’ its job. Some fellas, they was talkin’ about settin’ things right, if you catch my meanin’.”
My heart was thumping so hard I thought they might hear it. Settin’ things right. That sounded an awful lot like lynchin’ to me.

They talked some more about the rustlers, and about crop prices, and about the weather, and then I heard the jingle of harnesses and the splash of horses in the creek, and the voices started to fade as they rode away, probably back to some nearby town. I stayed hidden for a long time after the sounds had completely died down, my mind spinning like a top. They warn’t after me specifically, not yet anyway, but the talk about the shooting, and about folks wanting to “set things right,” that sent a cold shiver down my spine that went all the way to my toes. It meant the whole thing warn’t over, not for Sherburn, and maybe not for anyone who had been there. It meant trouble, like Miss Sally said, had a way of following a body.

I didn’t go to the fence. I just turned around, walking slow and careful, keeping to the tall grass, making my way back to the cabin. My mind warn’t clear anymore. It was all cloudy with fear again, and the quiet of the farm, which had been so peaceful, now felt like a place where things could sneak up on you unawares. When I got back, Miss Sally was on the porch, shelling peas into a big tin pan. She looked up when I came near, her eyes, like always, seeing right through me. “You didn’t fix that fence,” she says, not a question, just a plain statement.
I couldn’t meet her gaze. I just looked at my muddy boots. “No, ma’am. I… I heard some fellas talkin’ down by the creek.”
She stopped shelling, and the little clatter of peas ceased. “Talkin’, was they? About what, pray tell?”
I swallowed hard. “About the… the shootin’ in town. And about Colonel Sherburn. And about a boy that was there.”
She just looked at me for a long minute, her face unreadable, and then she sighed, a deep, weary sound. “So the trouble has caught up to you, then, or at least the shadows of it have. I figured as much. You got the look of a boy with a history, though you ain’t said much on it.”
I squirmed, uncomfortable. “I ain’t done nothin’ wrong, Miss Sally, not truly. I just… I was there.”
“I didn’t say you did, child,” she said, her voice soft. “Sometimes just bein’ there is enough to get a body tangled up. It’s a hard world, full of hard people. So, what are you thinkin’ to do?”
I didn’t know what to think. My peaceful week had been shattered. I had begun to feel safe, had begun to forget. Now it was all rushing back, the image of Sherburn, cold as stone, and Boggs falling.
“I don’t know, Miss Sally,” I says, my voice barely a whisper. “I thought… I thought I was safe here.”
“You are safe here,” she says, firm, and her eyes held mine. “For now. But peace of mind is a different thing, ain’t it? And if the peace of your mind is gone, then the safest place in the world won’t feel safe no more.”

She went back to shelling her peas, the little green rounds popping into the pan. I sat down on the steps, feeling the warm wood under me, but it didn’t bring no comfort. The thought of moving on, of going back out on the road, filled me with a fresh kind of dread. This place, this farm, this quiet woman, it had become a kind of home, a place where I could breathe easy. To leave it, to go back to wandering, that felt like stepping back into the shadow. But to stay, knowing that folks were still talkin’, still lookin’, still maybe hopin’ to “set things right”… that was a different kind of fear.

That night, I had the dream again, the one about the shooting. I saw Boggs’s face, red and yelling, and then Sherburn, cool as a cucumber, raising that pistol. I heard the shots, loud as thunder, and the girl’s scream, and then Boggs falling, heavy and solid, his arms spread out like a cross. I woke up with a gasp, sweat pourin’ off me, and for a minute I didn’t know where I was, just darkness and the sound of my own heart hammerin’ against my ribs. Then I remembered the little cabin, and the soft bed, and the steady breathin’ of Miss Sally in the next room, and slowly, the dream faded, leaving only a cold knot of dread in my gut. I lay awake for a long time after that, staring at the ceiling, trying to decide what to do.

The next morning, I was quiet at breakfast. Miss Sally, she just watched me, not pressing. Finally, I cleared my throat. “Miss Sally,” I says, “I reckon… I reckon I ought to be goin’ on.”
She nodded slow, like she’d known it all along. “I figured as much, child. A body can only outrun their past for so long before it catches up, even if it’s just in the tellin’ of others. It’s a good thing you got a strong pair of legs, and a bit of sense in your head, even if you’re young.”
“I’m sorry to leave you,” I says, feeling a pang of real sadness. “You been mighty kind to me, kinder than most.”
“Ain’t no call to be sorry,” she says, and she reached across the table and patted my hand, her skin rough but gentle. “A body does what a body must. You got skills now, boy. You can split wood and mend fences and even fix a contrary pump. Them’s good things to know, and they’ll serve you well, wherever you go.” She stood up and went to a small wooden chest by the fireplace. She opened it and pulled out a small leather pouch. “Here,” she says, handing it to me. “It ain’t much, but it’s some honest pay for your honest work. And a bit extra, for the road.”
I looked inside. There was a few silver coins, enough to buy food for a week or more, and maybe even a new shirt. My eyes widened. “Miss Sally, I can’t take this. I was just earnin’ my keep.”
“Nonsense,” she says, her voice firm. “You earned it, and then some. I ain’t had this place lookin’ so spry since my old man passed. Take it. It’ll buy you a meal, or a place to sleep, or maybe even a new beginning.”

I put the pouch in my pocket, feeling the weight of it, and the warmth of her kindness. “Thank you, Miss Sally,” I says, and my voice was a bit thick. “Truly. Thank you.”
I packed my few things, mostly just the clothes on my back and the small bundle I’d carried before, and then I stood on the porch, looking out at the farm that had been my refuge. The sun was rising, painting the sky with soft colors, and the chickens were cluckin’ in the coop, and the old cow was mooing softly in the pasture. It looked peaceful, just like it had when I first came, but now I knew the world outside could reach even here.
Miss Sally stood beside me. “You take care, boy,” she says. “And remember what I told you. Sometimes, a change of scenery is all you need to shake off the dust. But other times, you got to face what you’re runnin’ from, eventually.”
I nodded. “I reckon so, Miss Sally. I reckon so.”
I looked at her, at her kind, weathered face, and I felt a lump in my throat. I wanted to say more, but I didn’t know what words to use. So I just turned, and started walking down the path, the same path I’d taken to mend the fence, the one that led to the creek, and then to the road, and then to wherever the road would take me next.

I didn’t look back. I just walked, putting one foot in front of the other, feeling the dust rise around my boots, and the sun on my face. The talk I’d overheard, it had stirred up everything again, and now I knew I couldn’t stay. The river was calling me, the big, wide, ever-changing river, the only thing that had ever truly felt like home for long. It warn’t about running from trouble no more, not entirely. It was about finding a place where I belonged, a place where I didn’t have to worry about folks lookin’ to “set things right,” or about the shadows of other men’s sins falling on my head. I walked for a long time, till the farm was just a distant speck, and then it disappeared altogether behind a rise in the land. The sounds of the countryside faded, and the road got wider, and pretty soon I could hear the faint, distant rumble of a steamboat whistle. The river. It was always the river. And I knew, deep down, that it was where I had to go.

I reached the river by late afternoon, a wide, brown, majestic thing, flowing steady and strong, like it always had and always would. The sun was starting to dip, making the water glitter like a million scattered diamonds. There warn’t many folks about, just a few fishermen sittin’ silent in their little skiffs, and a couple of flatboats tied up at a muddy bank further down. No bustling town, no crowds, no shouts. Just the river, and me, and the wide-open sky. It was a comfort, a deep, silent comfort, that flowed right through my bones. I walked along the bank for a spell, watching the water, feeling the cool breeze on my face, and letting the old, familiar feeling of being near the river wash over me. It felt like coming home, even though I warn’t sure what home really meant anymore.

I saw a small raft, not much more than a few logs lashed together, tied up near a cluster of willow trees. It looked like it had been abandoned, or maybe just left by some traveler for a short spell. My mind started to work, quick and keen. A raft. The river. A way to travel without having to worry about roads, or towns, or folks talking. It warn’t much, but it was a start. I walked over to it, checking the ropes, feeling the rough wood under my hands. It looked sturdy enough, if I was careful. And it was free. I reckon there warn’t no better way for a body like me to travel. I could float by day, and tie up by night, and nobody would be the wiser. Nobody would ask me where I was from, or where I was going, or what I’d seen. The river, it didn’t ask questions. It just flowed.

I sat down on the bank, watching the sun sink below the horizon, painting the clouds in fiery colors. The air grew cool, and the crickets started their nightly song, and the frogs begun to croak in the shallows. I pulled out the small leather pouch Miss Sally had given me, and I counted the coins again, just to be sure. It was enough. Enough to buy some food, some supplies for the raft, maybe a blanket if the nights got too cold. I felt a surge of hope, a small, quiet kind of hope, growing in my chest. It warn’t the kind of wild, reckless hope I might have felt a few weeks ago, but a steadier, harder kind, built on the solid ground of my own two feet, and the wide-open expanse of the river.

I knew there would be dangers on the river too. Snags and storms, and river pirates, and all manner of other trouble a body could get into. But it felt like a different kind of danger, a cleaner kind, somehow, than the fear of a mob, or the terror of a man shot down in the street. This was the wild, natural danger, the kind you could fight with your own wits and strength. That felt better. It felt like I had a chance. I lay back on the grass, using my bundle as a pillow, and watched the first stars begin to prick the darkening sky. The river flowed on, a steady, murmuring presence, carrying its secrets and its travelers downstream, and I knew that tomorrow, I would be one of them. The road was done for a spell. The river was calling. And I was ready to answer. I closed my eyes, and for the first time in a long, long time, I felt a sense of peace that wasn’t just a trick of my mind, but a real, solid feeling, like the ground under my back, and the steady, endless flow of the water.I reckoned she was right, because as soon as I started swinging that axe, the world began to shrink down to just me and the log and the sharp glint of the steel, and the whack it made when it hit, a solid, satisfying sound that seemed to chase away all the other noises in my head. My shoulders begun to ache pretty quick, because I warn’t used to that kind of steady work, not like the old days back home, but I kept at it, because the woman had said it would clear my mind, and I was mighty keen on having a clear mind, seeing as how mine had been a regular mess for the last few days, all jumbled up with shootin’ and yellin’ and fear. The axe was well-balanced, like she said, and it bit deep into the oak, splitting it clean in two sometimes, and other times just sendin’ off a shower of splinters that stung a bit but didn’t do no real harm. I set the pieces aside, and picked up another log, and then another, and the pile of split wood begun to grow, looking like a little brown mountain beside the woodpile. The sun climbed higher, and the air got warmer, and pretty soon my shirt was stickin’ to my back with sweat, and it felt good, a different kind of good than anything I’d felt in a long spell. It felt like I was doing somethin’ real, somethin’ that mattered, not just runnin’ or hidin’ or watchin’ bad things happen.

After I’d split a heap of wood, enough to last a week maybe, I put the axe away and went to find the woman. She was out by the chicken coop, talkin’ low to her hens like they was her kinfolk, and they was cluckin’ back at her, seemed like. “I done the wood, ma’am,” I says, wiping my brow with the back of my hand. She turned, and that little smile was on her lips again. “Well now, that’s a sight, a real sight for sore eyes, all that good wood piled up. Reckon you’ll earn your keep yet, boy. Now, about that fence. It’s down by the creek, just follow the path past the pigpen, and you’ll see where the old sow broke through.” She pointed with a gnarled finger, and I nodded, and off I went.

The path was overgrown in places, but easy enough to follow, winding down a gentle slope toward the sound of the creek. Sure enough, where the path met the water, there was a section of fence, all busted up, with posts leaning every which way and wire saggin’ into the mud. A big old sow, all muddy and happy-looking, was rooting around on the other side, grunting contentedly. “Well, you’ve made a mess, old girl,” I says to her, and she just grunted back, like she agreed but didn’t care none. I looked around for tools, and sure enough, hangin’ on a nail in an old tree stump was a hammer and some wire cutters, and a roll of new wire laying next to it. The woman, she thought of everything.

It was harder work than the axe, because the ground was soft and muddy, and the posts was heavy and rotten in places. I had to pull out the old, broken bits, and find some good, strong branches to use as temporary supports, and then try to stretch the new wire tight enough so the sow couldn’t push through it again. My hands got scraped and my clothes got muddy, and a mean-lookin’ wasp decided to buzz around my head for a spell, but I just kept at it. There was a kind of satisfaction in getting a post straight again, and hearing the wire hum tight when I pulled it taut and hammered it into place. The sow, she watched me for a while, seemed like, and then decided I warn’t too interesting and went back to her rooting. It took me a good long while, but by the time the sun was starting to get low, paintin’ the sky orange and purple, that fence was standin’ proud again, not as pretty as new, but stout enough to hold any pig in Arkansaw.

I went back to the cabin, all tired and muddy, and the woman was waitin’ for me on the porch, a bucket of warm water and a scrub brush by her feet. “Well, you look like you wrestled a whole mudhole, boy,” she says, and laughed, a soft, dry laugh like leaves rustling. “But that fence is looking mighty fine, I took a peek. I ate like I hadn’t seen food in a week, and it tasted better than any fancy meal I ever had, because it was earned, and it was warm, and it was in a safe place.
