The little town of Willow Creek warn't much for excitement, not unless a stray hog got into Miz Prudence's prize petunias, or the preacher's eldest boy run off with a peddler's daughter. Sally, she still sat there, quiet as a mouse, but her eyes, they was on the old man, taking in every word, I reckoned. It warn’t just words to her, it was like he was telling her the truth of things, and she was storing it up. I thought about what he said, about folks on land and their troubles, and it made sense to me. I’d seen it with Miss Watson, always trying to civilize me, and the Widow Douglas, always fretting about my salvation. And Pap, he warn’t much for civilization, but he had his own kind of trouble, chasing after money and whisky and hating everything that warn’t him. They was all tied down to something, to places and ideas and what other folks thought of them, and I never liked it. The river, she was different. She was freedom, and I understood that better than anything.

The old man, he took another puff from his pipe, and the smoke curled up again, white in the firelight. “You take a land man,” he went on, his voice a low rumble, “he builds his house on solid ground, he thinks, and he plants his crops, and he sets his roots down deep, and he thinks he’s safe. But then a flood comes, or a fire, or a sickness, or the market goes bust, and everything he built up, it crumbles. And he ain’t got nothing left to hold onto, because he put all his faith in things that stand still. But a river man, he knows nothing stands still. He knows the ground under his feet is always shifting, and the water is always moving, and the sky is always changing. That’s why we’re better off, me and Sally, even if we ain’t got much to show for it but this old flatboat and the clothes on our backs. We got each other, and we got the river, and that’s more than a lot of rich land folk can say.”

He paused then, and looked out over the dark water. The moon had risen higher now, and its light made a silvery path across the river, shimmering and dancing. You could hear the crickets chirping in the reeds, and the distant hoot of an owl, and the soft, steady gurgle of the current against the bank. It was a peaceful sound, the river at night, and it made a body feel small but also a part of something big and old, something that had been there before you and would be there after you. Sally, she had stopped whittling, and she had her head tilted back, watching the moon. Her face, in the pale light, looked even softer, like a ghost almost, and I wondered what she was thinking. She didn’t say much, that girl, but I reckoned she saw a lot, and felt a lot too, though she kept it mostly to herself.

“We’ll push off before sun-up,” the old man said, suddenly, breaking the quiet. “There’s a good current running tonight, and we can make some miles before the day gets too hot. Got a load of timber to deliver down to Pikesville, and the sooner we get there, the sooner we can turn it over and maybe get us some provisions. Always looking ahead, the river man is. Never looking back, 'cept to learn from what you left behind.” He stood up, stretching his old bones, and give a grunt. “You hungry, boy? Got some dried meat and some hardtack left. Not much, but it’ll fill a corner.”

I told him I could eat, and he rummaged in a canvas bag by the side of the fire, pulling out some strips of jerky and a couple of hard biscuits. Sally, she reached for a leather canteen and offered it to me. The water was cool and fresh, tasting of earthen jar and river-edge spring, and it went down good. We ate in silence for a bit, just the sounds of the night and the crackle of the fire keeping us company. The jerky was tough, but flavorful, and the hardtack, it took some chewing, but it settled in the stomach. When we was done, the old man put away the leftover provisions and then stretched out on a blanket by the fire, pulling another one over him. Sally, she did the same, not too far from him, her back to the fire, looking like a small bundle under the stars.

“You can take the other blanket, boy,” the old man said, his voice already getting thick with sleep. “And keep an ear out. River’s mostly peaceful, but you never know what might drift by.”

I thanked him, and took the blanket he pointed to, a rough woolen thing that smelled of woodsmoke and damp earth, but it was warm. I laid down on the ground, not too close to the fire, but close enough to feel its heat, and I looked up at the stars. There was so many of them, more than I ever saw in St. Petersburg, and they seemed to hang so low you could almost reach out and touch them. The Milky Way was a hazy band across the sky, like smoke from a giant’s pipe, and I laid there for a long time, just watching them twinkle, and listening to the river. It was a good feeling, being out there, with nothing but the sky above and the river beside, and no one to bother you, or tell you what to do. I thought about Jim, and how he liked to look at the stars too, and how he said they spoke to him sometimes. I wondered where he was now, and if he was looking at the same stars, and if he was free.

I must’ve drifted off, because the next thing I know, the old man is stirring the fire and the sky is just beginning to lighten in the east, a faint gray turning to a pale pink. Sally was already up, rolling up her blanket and stowing it away in a watertight barrel. She moved quiet and quick, like a shadow, and I didn’t even hear her get up. The old man, he had a small pot of coffee brewing over the coals, and the smell of it was mighty good. “Morning, son,” he said, his voice a little raspy from sleep. “Time to earn your keep. We got to unmoor the boat.”

I jumped up, feeling a bit stiff but ready to go. The air was cool and damp, and a thin mist hung over the river, making the opposite bank look blurry and far away. We ate some more hardtack and had a cup of the strong, black coffee, and then we went down to the flatboat. It was a simple affair, just a big raft with a small cabin built on it, and a long sweep-oar at the back for steering. The old man called it the ‘River Sprite,’ though it looked more like a stubborn old mule than a sprite. We untied the ropes that held it to the bank, and the old man took the sweep-oar, while Sally and I stood ready with long poles to push us off the shallow bank and into the current.

“Easy now, easy,” the old man grunted, leaning into the oar. “Let the river do the work. We just got to guide her.”

We pushed off, and the current caught the flatboat gentle-like, pulling it out into the main channel. The mist was still thick, but you could see a little further now, and the first rays of the sun was just beginning to break through the trees on the eastern bank, painting the river with streaks of gold and orange. The old man, he steered with a sure hand, his eyes scanning the water ahead, watching for snags or sandbars. Sally, she stood by the side, looking out into the mist, her face calm and serious. I stood near the front, feeling the slight sway of the boat under my feet, and the cool breath of the river on my face. It was a grand feeling, floating down the river, like you was part of it, and nothing else mattered.

We drifted for a good spell, maybe an hour or two, and the sun rose higher, burning off the mist and turning the river into a shimmering sheet of silver. The banks was lined with thick woods, and sometimes you’d see a small clearing with a cabin, or a cow grazing in a field, but mostly it was just wild country. The old man pointed out a big cottonwood tree that had fallen into the river, its branches sticking up like a bony hand, and told me how it could rip the bottom out of a boat if you warn’t careful. He told me about the different currents, and how to read the water, and how to tell if a sandbar was hidden just under the surface. He knowed the river like the back of his hand, and he taught me a thing or two, though I already knowed a fair bit myself.

Around mid-morning, we heard the sound of a steamboat coming upriver. It was a deep, throbbing sound that grew louder and louder, and pretty soon we could see the plume of smoke rising above the trees, and then the boat itself, big and grand, with its fancy decks and its churning paddlewheels. It was a sight to behold, those steamboats, all white and shining, with their flags flying and their calliopes playing sometimes. This one warn’t playing no music, but it was a fine looking boat, pushing a big wave in front of it.

“Best get out of her way, boy,” the old man said, giving a tug on the sweep-oar. “She don’t care for little folk like us.”

We steered the flatboat closer to the bank, letting the steamboat pass us by. It went by fast, its engines puffing and groaning, and the wake it threw was considerable. Our flatboat rocked and swayed like a cradle, and Sally grabbed onto the cabin wall, while I held onto a rope. The old man, he just braced himself, grinning a little. “See that, boy? That’s progress, they call it. Comes roaring by, stirring up the water, and then it’s gone, leaving a mess behind for us to deal with.” He spat into the river. “Too much fuss and noise for my liking. Give me a quiet current and a steady drift any day.”

We spent the rest of the day drifting, sometimes poling to keep us in the channel, sometimes tying up to the bank for a bit while the old man fished or gathered firewood. Sally, she helped with everything, fetching water, mending ropes, or just sitting quiet, watching the river go by. She had a keen eye for things, I noticed. She’d point out a patch of wild onions on the bank, or a bird’s nest hidden in the reeds, things I might have missed. She didn’t talk about them, just pointed, and then went back to her quiet watching.

As the sun began to set, painting the western sky in hues of fiery red and purple, we pulled the flatboat into a small cove, hidden by a thick stand of willows. “Good spot here,” the old man said, tying us up. “Sheltered from the wind, and good ground for a fire.” We built a fire, smaller this time, just enough to cook our supper. The old man had caught a couple of catfish, and Sally cleaned them with her quick, sure hands, scaling them and gutting them in a flash. We cooked them over the fire, the smell of sizzling fish filling the air, and ate them with some more hardtack and a few wild greens Sally had found. It was a fine meal, and I was hungry enough to eat a whole ox.

After supper, as the last light faded from the sky and the first stars began to pop out, the old man pulled out his pipe again. “You ever hear tell of the Widow’s Light, boy?” he asked me, his voice low, as if telling a secret.

I shook my head. “No sir, can’t say I have.”

“Well,” he said, puffing on his pipe, “some folks say it’s just swamp gas, or fireflies, but I know better. It’s a real light, flickering out in the deepest parts of the bayous and sloughs, mostly seen on dark nights, when there ain’t no moon. They say it’s the spirit of a widow woman who lost her man and her children in a flood, way back when. She was looking for them, carrying a lantern, and when she finally succumbed to the water, her spirit kept on looking, her lantern still lit. And if you see it, they say, it means a change is coming. Could be good, could be bad. But a change, for sure.”

Sally, she was sitting cross-legged by the fire, poking at the coals with a stick, but I seen her lift her head a bit when he started talking about the Widow’s Light. She warn’t looking scared, just curious, like she was listening to a familiar story but still wanted to hear the end of it.

“Now, I ain’t seen it myself in a good many years,” the old man continued, “but my pa, he seen it once. Said it was a little blue flame, dancing in the air, just beyond reach. And sure enough, a few days later, a big storm blew in, worse than any he’d ever seen, and it changed the river for miles around, tearing out banks and making new channels. So, you see, the river, she’s full of mysteries, and some of them ain’t got no explanation, no matter how much those land folks try to explain everything with their science books and their logic.”

He fell silent then, and we all just listened to the sounds of the night, the rustle of the leaves, the distant croak of a frog, the gentle lapping of the water against the boat. I thought about the Widow’s Light, and it sent a little shiver down my spine. I’d seen strange things on the river myself, things that warn’t easy to explain, and I reckoned there was more to it than just what you could see with your eyes.

The next morning, we set off again, a little earlier this time, before the sun was fully up. The air was crisp and cool, and the river was smooth as glass, reflecting the pale sky like a mirror. We saw a family of ducks paddling through the water, and a big turtle sunning itself on a log. The old man, he was in a good mood, whistling a tuneless sort of song under his breath as he guided the boat.

“Pikesville ain’t much further now,” he said, sometime after breakfast. “Maybe another day and a half’s drift, if the current holds up. Then we offload this timber, maybe pick up some goods for the trip back north. Ain’t nothing better than a good trade, a fair exchange for honest work.”

We passed by a few other flatboats and rafts, mostly lumbering downstream like us, and sometimes we’d wave to the people on them, or exchange a few words about the weather or the river conditions. There was a sort of brotherhood among river folk, I reckoned. You was all in it together, out on that big, wide, indifferent river, and you looked out for each other, mostly.

Around noon, we came to a stretch of river where the banks was a little higher, and there was a few more houses, scattered amongst the trees. We could hear the faint sound of a sawmill in the distance, a high-pitched whine that rose and fell. “Getting close to the settlements now,” the old man observed. “Best keep a sharp eye out. Folks can be friendly, but they can be mighty suspicious too, especially of strangers drifting through.”

He glanced at me, and then at Sally, who was, as usual, quiet and watchful. “Now, I got my ways of dealing with people, and Sally, she knows how to keep to herself. You, boy, you look like a decent sort, but you got to be careful what you say, and who you say it to. Some folks ain’t got no kindness in them, and they’ll take advantage of a body if they think they can get away with it. Just keep your eyes open, and your mouth shut, mostly. And if trouble comes, you come to me, or you stick with Sally. She’s small, but she’s got a good head on her shoulders, and she’s quicker than a bobcat when she needs to be.”

I nodded, taking his words to heart. I’d learned a thing or two about suspicious folks myself, and I knowed how to keep my own counsel. But it was good to hear him say it, like he was looking out for me, and that was a feeling I warn’t used to. It made me think of Jim again, and how he always looked out for me.

We tied up the boat for the night a little earlier than usual, in a thick patch of woods on a high bank, well out of sight of any houses. The old man said it was best not to draw attention to ourselves when we was this close to town. We had a simple supper of cornmeal mush and leftover fish, and then we turned in. I laid there again, listening to the river, but this time I was a little more awake, a little more aware of the sounds around us. The old man’s words about trouble and suspicious folks was rattling around in my head, and I found myself wondering what kind of folks we’d run into in Pikesville.

The next morning, we was up before dawn again, and we pushed off into a thick fog that had rolled in overnight. You couldn’t see ten feet in front of the boat, and the old man had to steer by the sound of the current and his memory of the river. Sally and I stood at the front, poling gently, trying to keep us away from the bank and any hidden snags. It was a spooky feeling, drifting through that fog, like we was on a cloud, or in a dream, and you couldn’t tell where the water ended and the sky began.

“Keep your ears open for other boats,” the old man called out, his voice muffled by the fog. “And if you hear anything, sing out.”

We drifted slow, and the silence was deep, broken only by the drip of water from the trees and the soft gurgle of the current. After a while, I thought I heard something, a faint splash out in the fog, but I couldn’t be sure. I strained my ears, trying to catch it again. Then Sally, she pointed, her finger a pale shadow in the mist. “Look,” she whispered, her voice barely audible.

The old man swung the sweep-oar, bringing the flatboat around towards the dark shape. As we got closer, the fog thinned a bit more, and we could see it plain. It was indeed a skiff, upside down, and it looked like it had been battered by something fierce. One of the oars was broken, sticking up like a snapped bone, and there was a dark stain on the bottom, where the wood was visible.

“Looks like someone had a rough night,” the old man grunted, his face grim. “Might be a good idea to see if there’s anything salvageable. Or any poor soul clinging to it.”

We guided the flatboat alongside the overturned skiff. The old man reached out and grabbed onto it, pulling it close. Sally and I looked over the side, our hearts thumping a little faster. There warn’t no one there, no one clinging to it, and no one under it. Just the empty, broken boat. But as we looked closer, I saw something else, caught under one of the thwarts. Sally and I leaned closer, trying to see. Inside the bag, nestled amongst some wet rags, was a small, heavy pouch, made of buckskin. The old man opened it, and out tumbled a handful of coins, shining dully even in the dim light. Gold coins, by the look of them. And not just a few, but a good many.

“Looks like our friend had a bit of a treasure with him,” the old man said, his voice still low, but with a new edge to it. He looked around, peering into the fog, as if expecting the owner to suddenly appear. But there was nothing but the silence and the swirling mist. “No sign of him, though. Must’ve gone down quick. Or got carried off by the current.”

He closed the buckskin pouch and put it back in the canvas bag. He didn’t seem excited, not like Pap would’ve been, or any of the river rats I’d known who’d found a windfall. He just looked serious, and a little troubled.

“What do we do with it?” I asked, my voice a whisper.

The old man looked at the bag, then out into the fog again. “Well, boy, that’s the question, ain’t it? This here river, she gives and she takes. He looked at Sally, and she looked back at him, her eyes calm and steady. She didn’t say nothing, but I reckoned she was thinking the same thing as him, and maybe as me. What was the right thing to do with a dead man’s money, or a soon-to-be dead man’s money, found floating in the fog?

“We ain’t going to just leave it here,” the old man finally said, deciding. “That ain’t right. If we find the owner, we give it back. If we don’t, well, then it’s providence, I reckon. This kind of find, it can bring more trouble than it’s worth.”

We continued on our way, the fog slowly lifting as the sun climbed higher. The discovery of the gold had changed the mood on the flatboat. We was still drifting, still watching the banks, but there was a new sort of quiet amongst us, a quiet that warn’t just peaceful, but watchful. I kept looking over my shoulder, and into the water, half expecting to see a face staring up at me from the depths, or another skiff, with a desperate man in it, hailing us. But there was nothing. By afternoon, the fog was completely gone, and the sun was hot overhead. We saw more signs of civilization now. A ferry crossing, with a flat-bottomed boat pulling itself across on a rope. It warn’t a big town, not like St. Petersburg, but it had a steamboat landing, and a few stores, and a church steeple sticking up above the trees. There was a fair bit of activity on the landing, with men loading and unloading goods, and wagons waiting to carry them away.

“Well, here we are, River Sprite,” the old man said, patting the side of the boat. “Time to do some business.” He steered us towards the landing, finding a spot between a couple of barges that was tied up. Sally and I got the lines ready, and we skillfully brought the flatboat alongside the rickety dock.

Just as the bony feller reached for the old man, a sound drifted through the air, faint at first, then gettin’ clearer. It was the chugging of a steamboat, and it sounded like it was comin’ from downriver, around the bend. Silas and his men froze, their heads cocked, listenin’.

“What in tarnation is that?” Silas mumbled, his eyes narrowin’ as he tried to peer through the trees downriver. The steamboat sound grew louder, and soon we could hear the splash of its paddles and the hiss of its steam. It was comin’ fast.

The bony feller looked nervous. “That ain’t no ordinary flatboat, Silas. Sounds like a big one. Could be the law, or some government boat.”

The stocky feller spit again. “Or that revenue man from Baton Rouge. He’s been makin’ rounds lately, lookin’ for folks without proper licenses.”

Silas’s face was a mixture of anger and worry. He still had his rifle pointed at the old man, but his attention was split. He knew a steamboat, especially a government one, meant trouble for his operations. They was probably runnin’ a little ferry or a tollbooth without permits, or maybe doin’ worse things than just that.

“Blast it all!” Silas growled. “They always show up at the worst time.” He looked from the old man to the sound of the steamboat. He couldn’t be seen holdin’ us at gunpoint if it was the law. But he also didn’t want to let us go, thinkin’ we might run straight to that Colonel Sherburn he mentioned.

The old man, quick as a flash, seen his chance. He didn’t say a word, just gave a little nod to me, a tiny movement of his head that only I could catch. Then, with a mighty push of his pole, he shoved the raft hard away from the bank, right into the current.

“Hey! What in the devil!” Silas roared, caught off guard. He lurched forward, tryin’ to grab the rope tied to the raft, but he was too slow. The raft was already movin’, pickin’ up speed, pulled by the river.

The bony feller, he raised his shotgun. “Should I shoot ‘em, Silas? Should I shoot ‘em down?”

“No, you fool!” Silas yelled back, his face crimson with fury. “Don’t you dare! You want to draw attention to us right before that steamboat comes around the bend? They’ll hear the shots, and they’ll come pokin’ around!”

He was right. A gunshot would certainly attract the steamboat’s attention, and that was the last thing Silas wanted. So he just stood there on the bank, stompin’ his foot and cussin’ under his breath, watchin’ us drift away. His two men stood beside him, lookin’ just as mad.

The steamboat’s whistle blew then, a loud, mournful sound that echoed through the trees. It was gettin’ real close. We was already a good distance from Silas and his gang, and the current was carryin’ us along pretty good now.

I looked back. Silas, he was shakin’ his fist at us, and I could hear his cussin’ carryin’ over the water, mixed with the sound of the steamboat. He looked like a mad dog, all teeth and snarls. I didn’t know what he would’ve done to us if that steamboat hadn’t come along, but I reckoned it warn’t gonna be pretty.

The old man, he kept his eyes on the river ahead, steerin’ us carefully around a snag. Sally was still shakin’, but she was startin’ to raise her head a little, watchin’ the receding figures of Silas and his men.

“We’re safe, child,” the old man said, his voice a little shaky now that the danger was past. He took a deep breath, like he was tryin’ to calm his own nerves. “Thanks to that steamboat, we are.”

I felt a wave of relief wash over me, so strong it almost made me weak in the knees. I slumped down on the raft, my back against a bundle, and just watched the woods go by. The steamboat came into view then, a big, white side-wheeler, puffin’ smoke and makin’ a grand racket. It passed us by, further out in the channel, headin’ upriver, its passengers on deck lookin’ like ants from where we was. It didn’t even seem to notice us, a small raft full of weary travelers, but it had saved us all the same.

We drifted for a long time after that, the sun gettin’ lower in the sky, turnin’ the river to a deep, fiery orange. Nobody spoke much. Sally, she finally stopped shakin’ and just sat there, quiet, starin’ at the water. The old man, he kept steerin’, his face grim. I reckoned he was thinkin’ about what might have happened, and how close we came to bein’ caught up in somethin’ awful.

After a while, when the light was just about gone and the first stars was beginnin’ to peep out, the old man finally spoke. “That warn’t a good patch of river, boys and girls,” he said, his voice low. “That Silas, he’s a mean one. And he ain’t alone. We’ll have to be extra careful from now on. These riverbanks, they got all sorts of folks hidin’ in them, just waitin’ for a chance to make trouble.”

I nodded. I’d seen it before. The river was free, but the land on either side warn’t. And there was always somebody tryin’ to claim a piece of it, tryin’ to set up their own laws and their own tolls. It made a body feel mighty small and mighty vulnerable, floatin’ along on a few logs with nothin’ to protect you but your wits and a bit of luck.

We pulled into a quiet cove later that night, a place where the trees grew thick down to the water and there warn’t no sign of human habitation. We built a small fire, just big enough to boil some coffee and warm up a little. The old man, he kept lookin’ out into the darkness, listenin’ to the sounds of the woods. He didn’t sleep much that night, I don’t think, and neither did I. Every creak of the branches, every hoot of an owl, made me jump, thinkin’ Silas and his men might be comin’ after us, that they might have decided to take their chances with the steamboat after all, just to get back at us.

But the night passed without incident, and the sun rose again, paintin’ the sky with soft pinks and oranges. We ate our meager breakfast, and then the old man pushed us back out into the current. The river looked peaceful then, all smooth and shining under the morning sun, but I knew better. Underneath that peaceful surface, and behind those quiet trees, lay all sorts of dangers.

The old man, he told us a bit about Silas. He said he’d heard talk of him before, a feller who’d set up a rough and ready kind of toll station along that stretch of the river. He warn’t the law, and he warn’t a government man, just a bandit with a few fellers workin’ for him, tryin’ to skim off what they could from passing boats. He’d heard Silas had a nasty temper, and that he wouldn’t think twice about roughin’ somebody up if they didn’t pay.

“He’s got no real authority, mind you,” the old man said, as we drifted along. “But out here, away from the towns and the sheriffs, a man with a gun and a mean streak can make his own authority. And if you ain’t got nothin’ to give ‘em, well, sometimes they just take what they can, or make trouble just for the sport of it.”

I thought about what Silas had said, about Colonel Sherburn. It was strange that he’d mentioned that name. Made me wonder if there was some kind of feud goin’ on between river gangs and town folk, or if Sherburn himself had some dealings further down the river. The world felt bigger and meaner the further we got from St. Petersburg, and the more I seen of it. It warn’t just Pap that was mean, or the Grangerfords and Shepherdsons. It seemed like meanness was everywhere, just waitin’ for a chance to grab hold of you.

We kept a closer watch after that, the old man especially. He didn’t want to stop at any more banks that looked deserted or too wild. He wanted to stick to the main channel, where there was more traffic, even if it meant comin’ across steamboats more often. He reckoned it was safer to be around folks, even if they was strangers, than to be alone with men like Silas.

A few days later, we passed by a small town, just a cluster of houses and a general store clingin’ to the riverbank. It warn’t much, but it looked peaceful compared to what we’d been through. We didn’t stop, just drifted by, watchin’ the folk on the shore. They looked ordinary, just goin’ about their business, and for a moment, I almost forgot about Silas and his rifle. But then I remembered the way his eyes had glinted, and the sound of his laugh, and I knew that even in ordinary places, trouble could be waitin’. The river was a long, long road, and we had a heap more travelin’ to do. And every bend could bring somethin’ new, somethin’ dangerous, or somethin’ that would make you wonder about the goodness of folks. I just hoped we’d have enough luck and enough sense to keep out of its way. We kept movin’ down, down the mighty river, with every passing mile takin’ us further from what we knew and deeper into what we didn’t, and every now and then I’d look back upriver, half expectin’ to see Silas’s grim face appear from around the bend, still lookin’ for a reason to cause us harm.

So we kept on down the river, the old man and me, keepin’ to the middle channel like he said, and watchin’ out for anything that looked like trouble. The sun was hot and the air was still, and the river was wide and lazy. You could see for miles and miles, and sometimes you’d spot a steamboat way off, just a plume of smoke on the horizon, movin’ slow and grand, like it owned the whole river. The old man would point ‘em out, and we’d tie up to a snag and lay low in the skiff till they’d passed, just to be safe. He said there warn’t no tellin’ who was on ‘em, or what they might be up to. He was mighty careful, that old man, and I reckoned he had good reason to be. All that talk about Silas had put a scare in him, and it warn’t just a scare, it was a kind of a sad look in his eyes too, like he was thinkin’ about all the bad things in the world and wonderin’ if there was any way to get away from ‘em.

We talked a good deal about Silas, and about Colonel Sherburn, and how some folks just got a mean streak in ‘em that warn’t natural. The old man said it was all because they didn’t have no proper raisin’, or maybe they’d seen too much trouble themselves and it had soured ‘em. I didn’t know much about sourin’, but I knew Silas was a sight to behold, with his rifle and his scowl, and I was glad to be puttin’ miles between him and us. The river just rolled on, day and night, and we was just little specks on it, driftin’ and wonderin’ where we was goin’ to fetch up next. We fished a good deal, and the old man was a master at catchin’ catfish, big fat ones that we’d fry up over a small fire on a sandbar when it was safe to stop. He’d tell stories too, mostly about folks he’d known, and some of them was funny, and some of them made you think, and some of them just made you feel a bit lonesome for a spell.

After a few more days of floatin’, we come around a bend where the river widened out considerable. It warn’t a town exactly, not like the one where Boggs got shot, but it was a regular little settlement, stretched out along a low bank where the land warn’t so steep. There was a few cabins, built mostly out of logs and rough-cut timber, with smoke curlin’ out of the chimneys. And there was a landing, just a simple dock made of planks nailed to some pilings, and tied up to it was a flatboat, a real big one, with a sort of little house built on top of it. It looked like a regular river shanty, like some folks lived on the river all the time, just driftin’ and stoppin’ when they pleased. The old man squinted at it for a long time, and I could tell he was thinkin’ hard. Our supplies was gettin’ low, and we hadn’t seen a decent place to buy salt pork or flour in a good spell.

“Reckon we ought to risk it, Huck?” he says, finally. “Looks like a peaceable enough place. Might be able to get some vittles.”

I looked at the flatboat again. There was some clothes hangin’ on a line out front, swayin’ in the breeze, and a dog was layin’ curled up on the deck, sleepin’ in the sun. It didn’t look nearly as mean as Silas’s place, and I was gettin’ mighty tired of hardtack and fish. “Might as well,” I says. “Worst they can do is tell us to shove off.”

So we guided the skiff over, easy and quiet, and bumped up against the side of the dock. The dog on the flatboat woke up, lifted its head, and just give a little sniff, then put its head back down. It warn’t a barkin’ dog, which was a good sign. As we tied up, a man come out of the little house on the flatboat. He was a tall, bony feller, with a long, thin face, and a patch over one eye. He had a pipe stuck in his mouth, but it warn’t lit, and he was wearin’ a faded calico shirt and britches that looked like they’d seen a heap of river water. He just stood there, leanin’ against the doorway, watchin’ us tie up, without sayin’ a word.

“Howdy,” the old man says, tryin’ to sound friendly. “Any chance a feller could get some flour and maybe some smoked ham around these parts?”

The man took the pipe out of his mouth, real slow, and spit a little tobacco juice into the river. “Depends,” he says, his voice raspy like dry leaves. “Who’s askin’, and what’s the price?”

“Just travelers, lookin’ to move on,” the old man says. “Got a few coins, not much, but honest coin.”

The one-eyed man chuckled then, a dry, rattlin’ sound. “Honest coin don’t buy much these days, friend, not with all the scoundrels floatin’ up and down this river. But come aboard. We can talk about it. Name’s Jedediah, but most folks just call me Jed.”

So we climbed onto the flatboat. It was wider than I thought, and seemed pretty sturdy. The deck was worn smooth by years of foot traffic, and there was a smell of fish, and old wood, and somethin’ sweet like fermentin’ fruit. Inside the little house, it was dark, but I could make out a table, a couple of stools, and a bunk along one wall. There was a woman sittin’ at the table, cleanin’ fish, with a greasy-lookin’ knife. She was a stout woman, with hair pulled back tight in a bun, and a face that looked like it had been carved out of stone. She didn’t look up when we come in, just kept scrapin’ at the fish.

“This here’s my wife, Martha,” Jed says, not soundin’ like he cared much if we knew her name or not. “She don’t talk much, especially to strangers.”

Martha just snorted, but she still didn’t look up.

“So, what kinda vittles you lookin’ for?” Jed says, pullin’ out a stool for the old man. I just stood by the door, feelin’ a bit uneasy. That flatboat felt like a trap, somehow, though I couldn’t say why. It was just the way Jed looked at us, with that one eye, like he was measurin’ us up for somethin’.

The old man told him what we needed, and Jed stroked his chin, thinkin’. “Got some flour, yes, and some good smoked ham, if you’re willin’ to pay a fair price. But my ham, it ain’t cheap. It’s special ham, from hogs raised right here on the riverbank, fed on acorns and whatever else they can root up.”

He talked a lot about that ham, makin’ it sound like it was the best ham in the whole wide world, and I reckoned he was tryin’ to talk up the price. The old man, he just listened, patient like, and when Jed finally got around to namin’ a price, it was a steep one, even for good ham.

“That’s a mighty high price, Jed,” the old man says, still calm. “We ain’t got that much coin. Could we maybe trade for somethin’?”

Jed’s one eye narrowed a bit. “Trade what? You got anything worth tradin’?” He looked over at our skiff, like he was wonderin’ what might be hid in it.

The old man, he thought for a moment. Then he says, “I’m a fair hand with a fishing line, and I know where the big ones bite. I could bring in enough fish to feed your whole crew for a week, if you was to let me have a bit of that ham and flour.”

Jed let out another one of them dry chuckles. “Fishin’, eh? We do our own fishin’. Don’t need no help with that. But I’ll tell you what. I got a little job needs doin’, a bit of heavy liftin’ down the bank. If you and your boy here was to help me with that, I might be inclined to cut you a deal on the vittles.”

I didn’t like the sound of “heavy liftin’”. It always meant trouble, or somethin’ dirty. But the old man, he looked at me, and I knew we was hungry, so I just nodded. “What kinda liftin’?” he asks.

Jed smiled then, a slow, thin smile that didn’t reach his one eye. “Just some crates. Got a new shipment in, and it’s a bit heavy for an old feller like me and Martha here.” He gestured to Martha, who still hadn’t looked up, just kept choppin’ at that fish like she was mad at it.

So we agreed. Jed led us off the flatboat, down a narrow path that wound through some brush, away from the settlement and further downriver. The old man kept his eyes peeled, lookin’ left and right, and I did too. It felt like we was walkin’ into somethin’ we oughtn’t. We come to a small cove, hid pretty well by some overhanging trees, and there, pulled half onto the bank, was another flatboat, smaller than Jed’s, and lookin’ a bit run-down. And piled on the bank next to it was a stack of crates, big wooden ones, bound with rope, and all marked with somethin’ I couldn’t rightly make out from where I was.

“There she is,” Jed says, pointin’ with his pipe. “Just need to get these crates onto my boat. They’re a bit heavy, mind you, so mind your backs.”

We got to work, the old man and me. The crates was heavy, all right, heavier than they looked. It took the two of us to lift each one, and even then, we had to grunt and strain. I could feel the sweat startin’ to roll down my back, and my arms ached. We was about half done when I noticed somethin’ peculiar. One of the crates, as I was liftin’ it, shifted a little, and a corner of the wood broke off. And inside, where the wood had splintered, I could see somethin’ that warn’t supposed to be there. It warn’t flour, and it warn’t ham. It was a glint of metal, and somethin’ that looked like fancy cloth.

I glanced at the old man, but he was strainin’ with another crate, his face red with effort. Jed was just standin’ there, smokin’ his pipe now, watchin’ us, his one eye followin’ our every move. I didn’t say nothin’, but my mind started workin’. That metal, it looked like a part of a clock, maybe, or some other fancy machine. And that cloth, it looked like silk, or somethin’ expensive like that. It warn’t the kind of stuff you’d find in a normal river shipment of flour and ham.

We finished movin’ the crates, every last one of ‘em, onto Jed’s flatboat. By the time we was done, the sun was startin’ to dip towards the horizon, and the air was gettin’ cooler. I was tired, and my muscles was sore, but I was mighty curious about them crates.

“Well, you done good, boys,” Jed says, slappin’ the old man on the shoulder. “Come on back to the boat. Martha’s got some supper on, and we can talk about your payment.”

So we went back to the main flatboat. Martha had a pot of somethin’ stewin’ on a little stove, and the smell was good, though I still felt uneasy. We sat down at the table, and Martha, she put out three bowls, and filled ‘em up with a thick, meaty stew. It was the best stew I’d had in a long time, and I was so hungry I almost forgot about the crates. Almost.

While we was eatin’, Jed started talkin’ again, about the river, and how hard life was, and how a man had to make his own way. He kept lookin’ at us with that one eye, like he was tryin’ to see into our heads. I didn’t say much, just ate my stew and listened. The old man, he answered Jed’s questions, but he kept his answers short, not givin’ away more than he had to. He was always careful, that old man.

After supper, when the bowls was cleared away, Jed says, “Now, about that ham and flour. I reckon you earned it, fair and square. But I got another proposition for you, if you’re inclined to hear it.”

My stomach tightened up a bit. I knew this was comin’. The old man, he just looked at Jed, waitin’.

“You boys look like you know your way around the river,” Jed says, leanin’ forward, his voice a bit lower now. “And you’re strong. I got some business further down, a few days’ journey from here. Some valuable cargo needs transportin’. It’s good pay, real good pay, for a few days’ work. What do you say?”

I looked at the old man. His face was still and unreadable. He warn’t sayin’ nothin’, just thinkin’. I knew what he was thinkin’. These crates, they warn’t ordinary cargo. And Jed, he warn’t no ordinary river trader.

“What kind of cargo?” the old man asks, his voice soft.

Jed chuckled again, that dry rattle. “Valuables, friend. Things that some folks would pay a pretty penny for. Nothin’ illegal, mind you, just… specialized goods. And the pay would be enough to buy you all the ham and flour you could eat, and more besides.”

I knew it was illegal. Or if it warn’t illegal, it was somethin’ mighty close. Fancy clocks and silk cloth, in heavy crates, bein’ moved by a one-eyed man in the dead of night, away from regular channels. That just spelled trouble.

The old man sighed, a long, slow sigh. “Jed,” he says, “we’re just simple travelers. We ain’t lookin’ for no trouble, and we ain’t lookin’ to get involved in no specialized goods. We just want our vittles and to be on our way.”

Jed’s face darkened then, and that one eye of his seemed to glow a bit in the dim light. “You moved my crates, boys. You seen what was in ‘em, didn’t you?”

I swallowed hard. He knew I’d seen. I hadn’t said a word, but he knew.

“It warn’t nothin’ but old farm equipment, Jed,” the old man says, lyin’ through his teeth like he’d been doin’ it his whole life. “Just heavy old iron. Didn’t pay it no mind.”

Jed leaned back in his chair, and for a long moment, nobody said nothin’. The only sound was the river water lappin’ against the side of the flatboat, and the distant hoot of an owl. Martha, she was sittin’ there like a statue, her back to us, starin’ at the wall.

“Well,” Jed says, finally, his voice a bit colder now. “It’s a shame you ain’t interested in a bit of honest work. But a deal’s a deal. You helped with the liftin’, so you get your payment.” He reached into a small canvas bag he had hung by the stove, and pulled out a small sack. He tossed it onto the table. It landed with a soft thump, and the old man picked it up. He opened it, and inside was a generous amount of flour, a small smoked ham, and a little bit of salt. It was more than we’d expected, more than what was offered earlier for our coin.

“Thank you kindly, Jed,” the old man says, lookin’ a bit surprised.

“Don’t thank me yet,” Jed says, with that thin smile again. “You best be on your way now, and don’t look back. And forget what you seen today, understand? There warn’t nothin’ but old farm equipment in them crates. Nothin’ at all.” His one eye was fixed on me now, and I felt a chill run down my spine. He meant it.

We gathered our provisions, said our goodbyes, and quickly made our way back to our skiff. As we pushed off from the dock, the moon was comin’ up, a big yellow ball hangin’ low in the sky, castin’ long, spooky shadows on the water. Jed stood on the deck of his flatboat, watchin’ us, his figure outlined against the glow of the moon. I didn’t look back again, just kept my eyes on the water ahead, helpin’ the old man paddle us out into the middle of the river.

We rowed for a long time, not sayin’ a word, just puttin’ distance between us and Jed’s flatboat. The current was strong, and we was makin’ good time. After what felt like an hour or two, when we couldn’t see the lights of the settlement no more, the old man finally spoke.

“That warn’t no honest man, Huck,” he says, his voice low. “That was a river pirate, or somethin’ worse. Stealin’ goods and movin’ ‘em downriver. Good thing we didn’t get mixed up in it.”

“He knew I seen it,” I says, my voice still a bit shaky. “He knew I seen the metal and the cloth.”

“Aye, he did,” the old man says. “That’s why he give us a good deal on the vittles, and let us go. He didn’t want us hangin’ around, talkin’ about what we seen. He just wanted us gone, and quiet.”

We floated on through the night, the old man takin’ his turns sleepin’ while I steered, and then me sleepin’ while he steered. I kept thinkin’ about them crates, and about Jed’s one eye, and how Martha hadn’t said a single word the whole time. It was a strange place, that flatboat, full of secrets and silent dangers. The river, it just kept carryin’ us away from it, and I was mighty glad.

The next few days was quiet, and peaceful. We cooked up some of that ham, and it was good, just like Jed said, but it had a funny taste to it now, a taste of danger. We saw more steamboats, and a few other flatboats, but we kept our distance, stickin’ to the old man’s rule about the main channel and not gettin’ too close. We was learnin’ that the river warn’t just water and fish and sun. It was full of folks, and some of them was good, and some of them was bad, and some of them was just plain peculiar, and you never rightly knew which kind you was dealin’ with till you was already in deep.

We run low on wood for our fire, and the old man says we ought to stop and gather some on a little island we was passin’. It looked uninhabited, just thick with trees and brush, and a nice sandy bank to pull up on. So we did. We pulled the skiff up, and started lookin’ for some dry branches. I was out a little ways from the old man, deeper into the trees, when I heard somethin’. A sound, like a low moanin’, comin’ from somewhere in the thicket.

I froze, listenin’. It come again, a soft, mournful sound, like somebody in pain. I looked around for the old man, but he was out of sight, further down the bank. I didn’t know what to do. My heart was thumpin’ in my chest, but I was mighty curious.

I crept forward, real slow and quiet, pushin’ through the bushes, tryin’ to see what was makin’ the sound. The moanin’ got a little louder, and now I could hear somethin’ else, a faint whisperin’, like someone talkin’ to themselves. I parted a thick clump of vines, and there, in a small clearing, was a sight that made me stop dead in my tracks.

There was a man sittin’ on the ground, leanin’ against a tree. He was a skinny feller, with clothes that was all torn and muddy, and his hair was long and matted. He was holdin’ his leg, and it looked like it was bent at a funny angle, all swelled up and purple. He was moaninc’, and whisperin’ to himself, and as I got closer, I could hear what he was sayin’. He was prayin’, but it warn’t like any prayin’ I’d ever heard. He was talkin’ to God, but he was cussin’ Him at the same time, beggin’ for help and then accusin’ Him of abandonin’ him.

He looked up then, and his eyes was wild and glassy, like he had a fever. He seen me, and he jumped, tryin’ to stand up, but his leg give out and he fell back with a cry.

“Who’s there?” he rasps, his voice rough. “Are you one of them? Did they send you to finish me off?”

I backed away a step. “No, sir,” I says, my voice a whisper. “Just me and an old man, travelin’ down the river. We heard you moanin’.”

He looked at me for a long time, his eyes still wide, tryin’ to make sense of me. Then, slowly, the wild look in his eyes faded a bit, and a look of pure misery settled in. “My leg,” he says, his voice weak. “They broke my leg. Left me here to die.”

“Who did?” I asks, my heart thumpin’ hard.

“The river rats,” he says, his teeth clenched. “Jedediah and his crew. They stole my boat, and everything in it. My family heirlooms, my mother’s locket, everything. I tried to stop ‘em, but there was too many. They broke my leg and throwed me onto this island, said if I made a fuss, they’d come back and finish me.”

My blood ran cold. Jedediah. The one-eyed man. So that’s what was in them crates. Stolen goods, and this poor feller was the one they stole ‘em from. And he looked mighty near death.

“We seen Jedediah,” I says, careful not to say too much. “He made us help him move some crates.”

The man let out a bitter laugh, which turned into a cough. “Crates, you say? He’s always movin’ crates. Full of other folks’ misery, that’s what they are.” He winced, holdin’ his leg. “Please, boy, if you’re not with them, can you help me? I ain’t got much time. My name is Samuel. I just need water. And if you could set my leg, or at least bind it up tight.”

I didn’t know nothin’ about settin’ legs, but I knew I couldn’t just leave him there. I ran back to where I’d last seen the old man, yellin’ for him. He come crashin’ through the brush a minute later, lookin’ worried.

“What is it, Huck? What’s all the fuss?”

“There’s a man here, old man,” I says, my words comin’ out in a rush. “His leg’s broke. Jedediah, the one-eyed feller, he did it. Stole his stuff and left him here to die.”

The old man’s face went grim. He followed me back to the clearing, and when he seen Samuel, he knelt down right away, inspectin’ the leg. He was good with doctorin’, the old man was, knew a thing or two about bones and cuts. He shook his head, a slow, sad shake.

“It’s a bad break, son,” he says to Samuel, his voice gentle. “Ain’t no way to set it proper out here. But I can bind it up for you, try to keep it from gettin’ worse. And we got water.”
