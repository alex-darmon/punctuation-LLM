The old mill stood sentinel amongst the gnarled oaks, a hunched and rotting beast, its timbers groaning softly on windy nights. The grown folks, they called it Widow’s Weep, and swore that on moonless evenings you could hear the mournful cries of old Widow Hemlock, who’d lost her entire family to the grinding gears. But Elms, he wasn’t one to take such talk seriously. He’d seen a good many things in his ten summers, and most of them had proved to be no more than shadows and the creak of old boards.

“Ain’t nothing but rats and bats, I tell you, Jasper,” Elms declared, spitting expertly to hit a pebble a foot away. “All that haint talk is just to keep us from playing in the best place in the whole county.”

Jasper, a year younger and a good deal more prone to sudden shivers, picked at a loose thread on his overalls. “My Pa says there’s queer goings-on in there; he says old Widow Hemlock, she never truly left.”

“He says! My grandma says the moon is made of cheese, but you don’t see me climbing up there with a cracker, do you?” Elms scoffed, puffing out his chest a little. “What kind of explorer are you, anyway, if a little ghost story turns your knees to jelly?”

“My knees ain’t jelly,” Jasper retorted, though his voice was a bit squeaky. “I’m just saying, it’s… spooky. Why can’t we just go fishing?”

“Fishing!” Elms snorted. “Fishing is for babies, Jasper. This is adventure! This is something to tell old man Peabody down at the general store, something that’ll make his jaw drop right onto his suspenders. Imagine it, Jasper. ‘We went right into Widow’s Weep,’ I’ll say. ‘Explored every dusty corner, we did. Ain’t nothing but squirrels and spiders.’ He’ll be green with envy.”

“He probably wouldn’t believe us,” Jasper mumbled.

“He will if we bring him a souvenir!” Elms’ eyes gleamed with mischief. “Something that’s been in there a hundred years. A rusty bolt, maybe. Or a piece of that old grinding stone.”

This idea, a tangible proof of their daring, seemed to stiffen Jasper’s spine a bit. “A grinding stone piece? That’d be heavy, Elms.”

“Not a whole one, you ninny. Just a chip! A little chip, mind you. But think of the glory, Jasper, think of the glory!” Elms was already striding towards the mill, his bare feet kicking up dust. Jasper, after a moment’s hesitation, shuffled after him, casting nervous glances at the darkening woods around them. The air was still and heavy, and a strange quiet had settled, broken only by the buzz of summer flies.

The mill itself was even more imposing up close. Gaps in the boarded-up windows looked like empty eye sockets, and a broken vane on the roof groaned in the faint breeze, a sound that might indeed be a mournful wail if you were expecting one. The door hung askew on one hinge, revealing an opening like a gaping mouth into utter blackness.

“See?” Elms whispered, though his voice held a trace of awe. “Nothing to it.” He pushed the door further inward, sending a shower of rotten wood and dust cascading to the ground. A cloud of disturbed spiders, small and scurrying, scattered from the frame.

Jasper jumped back, startled. “Ugh! Spiders!”

“Just brave little eight-leggers,” Elms said, trying to sound nonchalant. “Come on, now. Don’t be a chicken.” He took a deep breath, and stepped into the gloom. Jasper followed, his heart thumping like a drum against his ribs.

Inside, the smell of damp earth and decay hung thick in the air. Dust motes danced in the sparse shafts of sunlight that pierced the grimy windows. Gears, immense and rusted, loomed like sleeping iron monsters, their teeth sharp and silent. The boys moved with exaggerated stealth, their footsteps echoing unnaturally in the vast, empty space. Every shadow seemed to stretch and writhe, every creak of the old building sounded like a stealthy footfall.

“Nothing,” Elms whispered, louder than he intended. “See? Just… old stuff.” He tried to sound confident, but his voice was tight.

Suddenly, a scuttling sound came from the floor above them, sharp and distinct. Jasper let out a yelp and clutched Elms’ arm. “What was that?”

“Just… a rat,” Elms stammered, his eyes darting towards the rickety wooden stairs. “A big one, maybe.”

Another sound, a hollow thud, followed by a faint, distant groan. This time, Elms’ bravado evaporated like morning mist. His face, usually ruddy with youthful confidence, had gone a shade paler. “Maybe it’s… maybe it’s the wind, Jasper. Just the wind playing tricks.”

Then a slow, deliberate scrape, as if something heavy was being dragged across the floorboards upstairs. It was too regular, too heavy to be a rat. Too mournful to be the wind. Both boys stood rooted to the spot, their eyes wide with a terror that made their earlier boasts seem like a forgotten dream.

“Elms,” Jasper whimpered, tears starting to well in his eyes. “I don’t like it. I really don’t like it.”

Elms swallowed hard, his throat dry as bone. His earlier grand pronouncements about “squirrels and spiders” now seemed laughably foolish. The mill, which had been merely old, now felt ancient and malevolent, pressing down on them with its shadowy weight. His whole being screamed at him to run, but his feet would not obey. He imagined a spectral hand reaching out, bony and cold.

Just then, a sharp, piercing shriek tore through the silence from somewhere deep within the mill, a sound so inhuman, so filled with agony, that it froze the blood in their veins. It was followed by a crash, like a thousand brittle bones shattering.

“Run!” Elms finally found his voice, a raw, strangled cry. He yanked Jasper’s arm and they stumbled blindly towards the open doorway, their earlier caution forgotten. They didn’t look back, their hearts hammering like war drums. They burst out into the bright afternoon light, gasping for air, their lungs burning, and did not stop running until they reached the edge of the creek, their familiar sanctuary.

They collapsed by the water’s edge, panting, their chests heaving. The cool air was a balm to their heated faces, but the horror of the mill still clung to them like a shroud. Elms looked at Jasper, his face still pale and streaked with dust. Jasper, in turn, looked at Elms, his eyes red-rimmed but full of a shared, unspeakable dread.

“That… that wasn’t no rat,” Jasper finally managed, his voice barely a whisper.

Elms nodded, speechless. His chest ached, not just from the running, but from the raw, gut-wrenching fear he had felt. His grand plan, his brave talk, all of it seemed insignificant compared to the sheer, terrifying reality of that shriek.

“We ain’t… we ain’t going back there, are we, Elms?” Jasper asked, conviction in his tone.

“No,” Elms said, his voice flat and shaky. “No, we ain’t.” He looked at the familiar sparkle of the creek, the green of the trees, and the warm, comforting sun. Never had the ordinary world seemed so precious, so safe.

That evening, when Pa asked what they’d been up to, Elms just mumbled about skipping stones at the creek, and Jasper quickly agreed. They sat quieter than usual at supper, picking at their food, and neither of them mentioned Widow’s Weep, nor the rusty bolt, nor the piece of grinding stone. The glory, they found, was rather heavy to carry, and not worth the fright it brought. They knew, without needing to say it aloud, that some adventures were best left untried, and some legends were best left alone. They had learned a lesson, an unspoken one, and the memory of that shriek would surely last them till their beards grew gray.

The days that followed were not like the days before. Elms and Jasper, usually as boisterous as a pair of bullfrogs in a summer pond, moved with a subdued air, as if a great weight had settled upon their shoulders, or perhaps upon their very souls. The sun still shone, the creek still babbled its familiar song, and the dusty roads still invited bare feet to scuff along them, but the zest, the pure, unthinking joy, had been wrung out of them like water from a dishcloth. They spoke little of Widow’s Weep, for the memory of that shriek was a thing too raw, too sharp, to be brought into the light of common conversation. It lay between them, an unspoken pact, a secret burden. Sometimes, in the quiet of the evening, when the crickets began their fiddling and the shadows lengthened into monstrous shapes, one would catch the other looking out towards the distant, dark silhouette of the mill, and a shiver, cold and swift, would run down their spines. They would hastily turn away, pretending interest in a stray dog or a cloud shaped like a dragon, anything to avoid the accusing gaze of that silent, knowing dread.

Their mothers, keen-eyed as all mothers are, noticed the change. Mrs. Perkins, Jasper’s mother, remarked to Mrs. Miller, Elms’s mother, over a fence post one afternoon, that “Jasper ain’t been himself lately, quiet as a mouse and jumps like a cat on a hot stove at the least little sound. I reckon he’s got the summer doldrums, or maybe he ate too many green apples.” Mrs. Miller, a woman of more pragmatic bent, merely nodded, thinking to herself that Elms, too, had lost some of his usual sparkle; his eyes, usually alight with some mischief or other, now held a shadow, a sort of wary watchfulness that had not been there before. She put it down to growing pains, or perhaps a touch of malaria that hadn’t quite blossomed into a full fever, for boys were odd creatures, prone to strange humors.

For a week, they stuck close to the familiar paths, the sun-drenched patches of grass behind the barn, the shallow, mud-bottomed stretch of the creek where tadpoles wriggled in the warmer water. But even these once-cherished territories seemed to have lost a measure of their charm. The thrill of turning over a flat stone to find a wriggling salamander felt less exciting, and the joy of skipping a perfectly flat pebble across the water’s surface seemed a rather tame amusement after the dreadful music of the mill. Boredom, that relentless fiend of summer days, began to creep in, a slow, insidious poison that worked its way through their newly acquired caution. The fear was still there, a knot in the pit of their stomachs, but the restless energy of boyhood demanded an outlet, a new channel for its boundless flow.

One sweltering afternoon, finding themselves with no other occupation than watching a beetle struggle with a crumb twice its size, Elms sighed, a sound of profound despondency. “Jasper, I swear, if I have to watch that beetle another minute, I’ll turn into one myself and try to carry off a whole biscuit.”

Jasper, who had been contemplating the beetle’s plight with a solemn air, grunted. “It ain’t much of a life, is it? Just pushin’ crumbs. We could be doin’ somethin’.”

“Like what?” Elms asked, kicking idly at a loose stone. “Ain’t nothin’ to do. We can’t go to the mill, that’s for sure.” He shivered at the unspoken memory. “And there ain’t no pirates on the creek, nor no wild Indians to fight.”

“Maybe there’s somethin’ else,” Jasper mused, squinting at the shimmering air above the fields. “Somethin’ hid. Like treasure.”

Elms sat up straighter, a faint flicker of his old self returning to his eyes. “Treasure? What kind of treasure? Old man Potter ain’t got no treasure, just a cellar full of pickled onions. And Widow Higgins only treasures her cats, which ain’t worth a bent nail.”

“No, not *their* treasure,” Jasper said, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper, as if the very air might carry his words to disapproving adult ears. “I heard Pa talkin’ to Mr. Abernathy last night, down by the general store. Mr. Abernathy was mighty put out, he was. Said he’d lost his ‘lucky piece,’ a silver dollar from the year eighteen-ought-three, with a eagle on one side and a lady with a torch on the other. Said it was worth more’n gold to him, more’n all the gold in California, even. And he said he’d give a whole dollar, a brand new shiny dollar, to anyone who found it.”

Elms’s eyes widened. A whole dollar. That was a fortune, a king’s ransom. Enough to buy a whole barrel of ginger ale, or perhaps even a pair of those fancy spectacles like Professor Dimwitty wore, though what one would do with them was another question entirely. “A dollar? For just findin’ a coin?”

“That’s what he said,” Jasper affirmed, nodding vigorously. “He was wringin’ his hands, too, like a woman who just dropped her last good thimble down the well. Said he must’ve lost it somewhere between his house and the store, or maybe on his walk down to the old fishing hole where he likes to sit and think.”

A treasure hunt, Elms thought, his mind already spinning with possibilities. This was different from the mill. This was tangible, a thing of silver, not of shadows and shrieks. This was a quest with a clear reward, and without the chilling uncertainty of the supernatural. “Well, what are we waitin’ for?” Elms sprang to his feet, the beetle and its crumb instantly forgotten. “A dollar’s a dollar, and Mr. Abernathy’s a fair man. We could be rich, Jasper, rich as Croesus!”

Jasper scrambled up, a grin spreading across his face, pushing away the lingering gloom. “Yeah! And we wouldn’t have to tell nobody, not even our folks, till we had it in our hands. Just a surprise, like. And then we could buy that fancy fishing lure we saw in the store window, the one with the little spinner that looks like a real minnow.”

“And a whole bag of Jawbreakers!” Elms added, his imagination already spending the dollar several times over. “Now, where’d he say he might’ve lost it?”

“Between his house and the store, or down by the fishin’ hole,” Jasper repeated, trying to recall the exact words. “He stops at Mrs. Gable’s to look at her petunias sometimes, and he always leans on the old rail fence by the blacksmith’s shop, and then he cuts across Deacon Smith’s field to save time, unless he’s feelin’ lazy and sticks to the road.”

This was a good deal more complicated than Elms had first imagined. Mr. Abernathy, known for his meandering habits and his tendency to stop and contemplate every weed and pebble as if it held the secret to the universe, could have dropped his coin anywhere in a mile-wide radius. “Well,” Elms declared, putting his hands on his hips with an air of seasoned detective work, “we’ll have to be systematic. First, we map out his usual routes. Then, we search ‘em, inch by inch, like bloodhounds on a trail.”

The boys spent the rest of the afternoon in earnest consultation, drawing crude maps in the dust with sticks, marking out Mr. Abernathy’s known stopping points. His house, a ramshackle affair with a porch that sagged like a tired old man, was their starting point. Then, the path along the creek bank to the fishing hole, a quiet bend where willow branches dipped into the water like green fingers. Then, the main road that led to the general store, with its various diversions: Mrs. Gable’s petunias, the blacksmith’s shop, the shortcut across Deacon Smith’s field (a route fraught with peril, for Deacon Smith was known to chase boys with a broom for merely breathing on his prize pumpkins).

The next morning, armed with a shared sense of purpose and two empty flour sacks for any treasure they might find, they set out. The sun beat down with summer’s relentless fervor, baking the dust into a fine powder that rose in little clouds with every step. Their first target was the path to the fishing hole. It was less traveled, and therefore, they reasoned, the coin would be easier to spot.

They walked slowly, their eyes scanning every patch of dry earth, every tangle of roots, every glint of sunlight that might be reflected from silver. The path wound through tall grass, tickling their bare legs, and over uneven ground where loose stones threatened to turn an ankle. Bees hummed lazily among wildflowers, and dragonflies darted above the creek. It was peaceful, a world away from the shuddering terror of the mill, and the very act of searching, of being on a quest, began to restore a measure of their lost sprightliness.

“Look!” Jasper exclaimed, pointing to a flattened patch of grass near an old sycamore. “Mr. Abernathy sits here sometimes. He always says it’s the best spot to watch the turtles.”

They dropped to their hands and knees, sifting through the dry leaves and twigs, peering under roots. Nothing but a rusty nail, a few smooth creek stones, and a particularly fat caterpillar. “He must’ve been thinkin’ too hard about the turtles to lose his coin here,” Elms observed, wiping dust from his forehead. “No matter. Onward, to the fishing hole itself!”

They reached the bend in the creek, a quiet place where the water flowed slower and deeper, shaded by ancient willows. The air was cooler here, and the scent of damp earth and growing things was thick. They searched the muddy banks, poked under fallen logs, and even waded into the shallow edges of the water, disturbing a school of minnows that scattered like silver confetti. Hours passed. The sun climbed higher, beating down on their heads, and their stomachs began to rumble with the insistent call of lunchtime.

“I reckon he didn’t lose it here,” Jasper declared, flopping down onto a patch of moss. “My feet are commencin’ to ache, and my eyes feel like they’ve been rubbin’ against sandpaper.”

Elms, though equally weary, refused to be discouraged. The thought of that shiny dollar spurred him on. “A true treasure hunter ain’t deterred by a little sun, Jasper. And his eyes, they grow keener with every missed opportunity. We just ain’t lookin’ in the right spot yet. We got to think like Mr. Abernathy. Where would *he* lose somethin’ he prized so much?”

They decided to head back towards town, focusing on the main road and its diversions. This was more public territory, and they had to be more discreet, pretending to be merely strolling or playing some complicated game involving counting fence posts. They walked past Mrs. Gable’s house, casting furtive glances at her prized petunias, but saw no glint of silver among the blossoms. The blacksmith’s shop was next, a noisy, clanging place where sparks flew from the anvil like tiny stars. They lingered by the old rail fence, pretending to watch the smithy at work, while their eyes scoured the dusty ground beneath the rails. The ground was hard-packed and barren, however, and yielded nothing but a stray horseshoe nail and a few discarded bolts.

“He probably had it in his pocket when he was here,” Elms muttered, shielding his eyes from the sun. “Too much noise, too much goin’ on. A man holds on tight to his valuables when there’s a crowd, even if it’s just old Mr. Henderson gettin’ his plow fixed.”

Their final option for the day was Deacon Smith’s field. This was a daring choice, a territory fraught with risk. Deacon Smith was a man whose piety was matched only by his fierce possessiveness of his land and crops. His cornstalks stood taller than any man, and his pumpkins swelled to monstrous sizes, each one seemingly guarded by an invisible, watchful eye. To trespass was to invite the wrath of a righteous man, a wrath often accompanied by a swift broom.

“We gotta be careful,” Jasper whispered, as they approached the rickety wooden fence that marked the edge of the field. “He’s likely out there, somewhere, checkin’ his squashes.”

“We’ll stick to the edge,” Elms strategized, his voice low. “Just along the fence line. He wouldn’t have cut too far across if he was just savin’ a few steps. And if we see him, we just pretend we’re lookin’ for a lost ball, or somethin’.”

They slipped through a gap in the fence, their hearts thumping a little faster, not from supernatural dread this time, but from the very real and immediate threat of Deacon Smith’s broom. The ground in the field was rougher, clumpy with dried earth and the stubble of last year’s crops. They moved slowly, their eyes darting not only for the coin, but also for any sign of the Deacon. The corn rustled in the gentle breeze, sounding like a thousand whispered warnings. Every shadow seemed to hold the lurking figure of the old farmer.

Suddenly, Jasper stopped dead, pointing a trembling finger. “Elms! Look!”

Elms’s eyes followed Jasper’s gaze. There, partially hidden by a clump of weeds, glinting faintly in the afternoon sun, was a small, round object. His heart leaped. It was too small for a button, too flat for a stone. It had to be it.

With a shared gasp of excitement, they dove towards it, scrambling through the weeds. Elms reached it first, his fingers closing around the cool metal. He pulled it out, brushing away the dirt. It was a coin, alright, a silver one, but it was thinner than he expected, and the image on it was not an eagle or a lady with a torch. It was a rather comical looking pig, with the words “Good for One Ice Cream Cone” stamped beneath it.

“Well, I’ll be,” Jasper breathed, his initial excitement deflating like a pricked balloon. “It ain’t Mr. Abernathy’s lucky piece. It’s just one of them tokens from Mr. Henderson’s ice cream parlor.”

Elms turned the token over in his hand, a wry smile playing on his lips. “So it is. And not even a good year on it. Just a pig. A very fat pig, at that.” He tossed it lightly into the air and caught it. “Still, it *is* treasure, of a sort. One free ice cream cone. That ain’t nothin’ to sneeze at.”

Just then, a gruff voice boomed from behind them. “What in tarnation are you two scallywags doin’ in my cornfield? Spectin’ to harvest my crop for me, are ya?”

They both froze, turning slowly to face Deacon Smith, who stood there, indeed, with a broom in his hand, his face red with indignation. “No, sir, Deacon Smith, sir!” Elms stammered, holding up the ice cream token. “We was just lookin’ for this. It fell out of my pocket, sir. A very important token.”

Deacon Smith eyed the token, then them, his brow furrowed in suspicion. “An ice cream token, eh? Well, see that you keep your ice cream tokens, and yourselves, out of my field. Next time I catch you traipsin’ through my corn, I’ll tan your hides till you look like a pair of sun-dried tomatoes!”

They didn’t wait for a second invitation. They scrambled back through the fence with surprising speed, not stopping until they were well out of broom-swinging range, their chests heaving with exertion and suppressed laughter.

Later, sitting by the creek, sharing their single ice cream cone, a tangible reward for their efforts, Elms looked at Jasper. “Well, that wasn’t exactly a dollar, was it?”

“No,” Jasper admitted, licking a drip of vanilla. “But this here ice cream tastes mighty fine. And we didn’t see no… you know… ghosts.” He shivered slightly, even in the warm afternoon.

Elms nodded, a thoughtful expression on his face. The hunt for Mr. Abernathy’s coin had been a different sort of adventure. It had its challenges, its frustrations, and even its moments of genuine peril, though of a decidedly earthly sort. But the fear had been a familiar, manageable one, the kind that made your heart pound but didn’t freeze your blood. And the reward, though small, felt earned, a solid, sweet satisfaction. The ghost of Widow’s Weep still lurked in the deeper corners of their minds, but the sunlight felt warmer, the creek’s babble more cheerful, and the world a little less menacing. They had learned that not all adventures needed to be terrifying to be worthwhile; some, indeed, were sweeter for being merely human. And as they walked home, the last of the ice cream a pleasant memory on their tongues, they already began to concoct plans for the next great search, perhaps for the legendary lost fishing lure that old Man Peabody swore was snagged at the bottom of the mill pond, a new quest for a different sort of treasure.

The sun dipped low, painting the western sky in streaks of fiery orange and soft lavender, as Elms and Jasper, their bellies content with the sweet, if fleeting, memory of ice cream, ambled along the dusty path leading back towards home. The ice cream token, now a mere memory, had proven that even the most mundane of treasures could yield unexpected delights. But the real adventure, the one that held the true promise of glory and bragging rights, still lay before them.

“You reckon old Man Peabody was tellin’ the straight goods about that lure, Elms?” Jasper asked, kicking a loose pebble that skittered into the tall weeds. He still felt a shiver run down his spine at the thought of ghosts, though the ice cream had done a remarkable job of banishing the most immediate terrors.

Elms, ever the more pragmatic of the two, though no less prone to flights of fancy, considered this. “Old Man Peabody, he ain’t never been one for outright lyin’, not in my experience. Exaggeratin’, maybe, now that’s a different horse. He’ll stretch a fish story till it’s thin as a spider web, but he usually starts with a true enough thread.” He scuffed the toe of his worn shoe in the dirt. “And he swore on his mama’s grave, he did, that he lost it right there by the old mill pond, the one just past Miller’s Woods.”

Jasper’s eyes gleamed, reflecting the last of the sunlight. “Two bits. That’s a powerful lot of money for a lure, Elms. With that, their pace quickened, propelled by the twin engines of hunger and anticipation. The path twisted through a patch of woods, where the shadows were already growing long and deep, making the trees look like silent, watchful sentinels. They burst out of the woods and onto the familiar dirt road that led to the edge of the town, just as the last sliver of the sun vanished below the horizon, leaving behind a sky of fading twilight. The air grew cooler, carrying the scent of woodsmoke and damp earth. Their homes, small and unpretentious, were just visible in the gathering gloom, their windows beginning to glow with the warm, inviting light of lamps.

Supper that night was a hurried affair

Supper that night was a hurried affair, devoured with the quick, unceremonious motions of boys whose minds were elsewhere. Elms and Jasper sat at their respective kitchen tables, their forks clanking against plates, eyes distant, taste buds dulled to the familiar flavors of corn bread and salt pork. Their mothers, accustomed to the fits and starts of boyish appetites and the sudden enthusiasms that swept through their young lives like summer storms, noticed little amiss beyond a certain distracted air, a peculiar restlessness that seemed to hum just beneath the surface of their quiet mien. Neither boy spoke much, for the secret was too grand, too weighty, to risk even a whispered breath.
