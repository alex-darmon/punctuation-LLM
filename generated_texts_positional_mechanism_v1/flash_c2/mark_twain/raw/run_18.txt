The sun was hot enough to cook a possum on a fence post, and the dust on the main street was as fine as flour, rising in little puffs with every wagon that rolled through. Most folks was taking it easy, sitting on their porches or under the awnings of the general store, swatting flies and watching the river roll by. This here town, called Willow Creek, warn't much for excitement, usually, just folks living their lives, and the biggest thrill was when the steamboat whistled a half-mile away. But this day was different, you could feel it in the air, a kind of buzzing like a hornet’s nest just before it gets kicked.

There was a big canvas tent set up on the patch of grass next to old man Hemlock’s blacksmith shop, and a feller was outside it, shouting. He was a real flashy dresser, all shiny buttons and a vest that looked like a rooster’s tail, and he had a tall hat perched on his head, a silver snake coiled around the crown. He was hollering about his "Elixir of Life", which he said could cure anything from consumption to a broken heart, and he had a bottle of it in each hand, shaking them so the brown liquid sloshed around. He looked like he’d been doing this a long time, his voice was rough as a worn-out file, but he knew how to get a crowd, and by the time he’d been at it for ten minutes, there was a good thirty people gathered round, some laughing, some looking mighty serious.

“Step right up, ladies and gentlemen!” he sang out, his voice cracking a little, “Don’t be shy now, for the ailments of the body and the woes of the spirit, I bring you salvation in a bottle! Only one dollar, folks, a mere dollar for everlasting youth and vigor!”

Old Silas, who was known to sit on the same cracker barrel outside the store every day for forty years, he squinted at the feller. Silas warn’t one to believe much in miracles, unless it was a good fishing day.

“Say, Doc,” Silas yelled out, “Whar’s your diploma from? You look more like a river pirate than a medical man to me.”

The feller, who called himself Professor Tiberius, he just grinned, a big, wide grin that showed a missing front tooth. “My diploma, good sir,” he said, pointing a finger to the sky, “is writ in the very stars, and my knowledge comes from the ancient sages of the Orient! My patients, they speak for my cures, not some dusty piece of paper!”

He pulled out a small glass vial, full of red liquid, and held it up. “Now, who here suffers from the ague? Or perhaps a touch of the rheumatiz? This here is the genuine, one hundred percent, bona fide snake oil, guaranteed to make you dance a jig even if your knees is locked tighter than a bank vault!”

A young woman in the crowd, her face pale and pinched, she stepped forward. “My Pa, he’s got a cough, sir, somethin’ fierce. Says it feels like a cat scratching his insides.”

Professor Tiberius brightened up. “Ah, a common affliction, my dear, easily remedied by a dram of my miraculous elixir!” He poured some of the brown stuff into a tiny cup and offered it to her. “Just a sip, and his lungs will be clear as a mountain stream!”

But before she could take it, Silas, he pushed his way forward, a scowl on his wrinkled face. “Hold on there, girl! Don’t you go puttin’ that mystery brew down your Pa’s throat. Last time a feller like him come through here, he sold a bottle of colored water that near poisoned old Widow Higgins’ pig.”

Professor Tiberius’s grin tightened, and his eyes got a bit shifty. “Now, hold on, old man, you are slandering a reputable professional! My elixirs are distilled from rare herbs and potent minerals, gathered from the highest peaks and the deepest jungles!”

Silas just snorted, and spat a stream of tobacco juice into the dirt. “Highest peaks and deepest jungles, eh? Looks to me like it’s mostly Mississippi River mud and a touch of redeye whiskey. I seen that kind of ‘elixir’ before, and it always leaves a bad taste, one way or another.”

The crowd started murmuring, some looking at Silas, some at the Professor. The young woman, she looked scared and pulled her hand back from the cup.

Professor Tiberius he got all red in the face, and he stomped his foot. “Why, you old curmudgeon! You’re just jealous of my scientific advancements! I challenge you, sir, to a test of my remedies!”

Silas straightened up, his eyes bright with mischief. “Alright, Professor, I’ll take that challenge. You got anything that can cure a man of his fool notions? Because if you do, you just might be a real miracle worker.”

The Professor was sputtering, but Silas didn’t let up. “Or how about this, Professor? You say this stuff cures everything. I got a wart on my left thumb here, bigger than a bumblebee. If your elixir can make that wart vanish by sundown, I’ll buy a whole dozen bottles for the whole town, and I’ll even spread the word myself.” He stuck out his thumb, a truly ugly, dark wart sitting proud on it.

Professor Tiberius he looked at the wart, and then at the crowd, and then at the sun which warn’t nowhere near sundown. His face lost some of its rooster-tail color, and his big grin looked more like a nervous twitch. He mumbled something about needing a special concoction for warts, a different formula altogether, and he started to pack up his bottles mighty quick. The crowd started laughing, a slow rumble at first, then a full-blown roar, and Silas, he just stood there with his wart still on his thumb, a satisfied look on his face. Professor Tiberius, he scrambled into his tent, and by-and-by his wagon was rumbling out of town, leaving nothing but a cloud of fine dust and the smell of stale whiskey behind. Silas, he just winked at the young woman, and went back to his cracker barrel, and the buzzing in the air settled back down to a lazy summer drone.

The dust from the Professor’s wagon warn’t hardly gone from the road before the town felt like it was catchin’ its breath again, slow and easy. The sun was high up, baking the planks of the general store and making the air shimmer above the riverbank. Silas, he just sat there on his cracker barrel, whittling a piece of wood and looking like nothing much ever bothered him, not even a traveling snake-oil man or a big wart on his thumb. The young woman who had been with Boggs, she was still standing near the empty spot where her pa had fallen, looking a bit lost and pale, but nobody was paying her much mind anymore. People had gone back to their own business, some of them kicking at the dirt with their toes, some leaning against posts and chewing on straws, just waiting for the next thing to happen. It warn’t noon no more, but it warn’t late neither, and the afternoon promised to be a long one with nothing but the heat to look forward to.

I was leaning against a hitching post, watching a fly crawl across a horse’s nose, when a low, mournful sound came from downriver. It was a deep, rumbling groan at first, like a giant sigh, then it swelled up into a proper bellow. It was the whistle of a steamboat, sure as anything, and that always meant a bit of excitement for a town like ours, even if it warn’t much to look at most days. Heads started to lift, and folks straightened up, their eyes all turnin

 all turnin’ to the bend in the river where the sound was coming from.

It warn’t just a regular steamboat, neither, not by the sound of it. This one had a deep, throaty whistle, a sound that carried across the water like a big ol’ bull alligator calling for its mate. And pretty soon, around the bend she came, churning the muddy water with her big paddle-wheel, a grand sight to see. She was a packet boat, a big, white beauty with two tall smokestacks puffing black smoke into the clear sky, and her decks was crowded with people, all leanin’ over the rails to get a look at our little town. The name painted on her side, I could just make it out, was the “Belle of Natchez”, and she looked like she could carry a whole town if she had a mind to. The pilot, he was a-tootin’ that whistle somethin’ fierce, like he wanted to announce himself to the whole state of Arkansaw, and then he pulled her up against the bank with a mighty splash and a rumble, the gangplank swung down with a clang, and pretty soon the passengers was a-streaming off, all fresh and curious and looking like they’d just stepped out of a picture book, some of them in fine city clothes, and some with big carpetbags, and all of them with a sort of excited look on their faces, as if they’d come to a place where somethin’ was always happening.

Our town folks, they gathered round the landing, curious too, though a bit more quiet about it. It was like two different worlds had just bumped into each other, the fine steamboat folks and the dusty river town folks, and both sides was a-sizing each other up. The air was still thick with the smell of gunpowder and that strange, quiet fear that had settled in after Sherburn shot Boggs, but the steamboat arrival, it kinda pushed that to the back of people’s minds for a spell. For a minute, it seemed like the general stir of things was about to settle down, just like a pot of boiling water after you take it off the fire. But that ain’t how it was going to be, not today.

One feller, a big, red-faced man with a shiny black coat and a diamond pin on his cravat, he steps off the gangplank like he owned the whole river, and he looked around with a mighty important air. He caught sight of a group of men still talking about the shooting, still gesticulating and whispering, and he walked right up to them, bold as brass. “What’s all this here commotion?” he booms out, his voice like a preacher’s, “I ain’t never seen a town so agitated. Was there a fire? A fight, perhaps? I hear tell of rough ways in these parts, but this here seems to be a mite more than a common brawl.”

One of the townsfolk, a scrawny feller named Jed, he always was a bit too eager to tell a tale, he pipes up quick. “A fight, you say? Why, it warn’t no fight, mister. It was murder, plain and simple! Colonel Sherburn, he shot old Boggs in cold blood, right there in the street, right after he promised not to touch him till one o’clock. Didn’t even give him a chance, he didn’t!”

The big man’s eyes got wide, and he puffed out his chest even more. “Murder, you say? A cold-blooded killing? Why, this is an outrage! An absolute outrage! Is there no law in this God-forsaken place? No justice for a man cut down in the prime of life, no matter how much he might have been a-carryin’ on?” He looked around at the crowd, and a few of the townsfolk started nodding, their eyes a-lightin’ up with the same indignation. It ain’t hard to stir up a crowd, once you get a good, righteous indignation going.

Another feller from the steamboat, a lean, sharp-eyed man with spectacles, he steps forward. “I am a lawyer, sir, by profession,” he says, his voice a bit thinner than the first man’s, but with a sharp edge to it. “And if what this good man says is true, then this Sherburn feller ought to be brought to justice. The laws of man, and the laws of God, demand it!”

This was all the spark the folks needed. The talk of lynching, which had died down some when the steamboat arrived, it started right up again, louder than before. It was like a brush fire that someone had thought they’d put out, only to find a new gust of wind fanning the embers. “That’s right!” somebody yelled, and then another, “Let’s string him up!” And another, “He ain’t above the law, no matter how much money he thinks he’s got!” The big man from the steamboat, he looked mighty pleased with himself, like he’d just started a revival meetin’. He began to stride amongst the crowd, clappin’ men on the back and spouting off about justice and the rights of the common man, and pretty soon he had a good dozen or so of the steamboat passengers all stirred up and ready to join in the clamor.

I slipped away from the landing, feeling a cold knot in my stomach. I’d seen what a mob could do to a man, how they’d howl and spit like dogs, and it warn’t a pretty sight. Old Boggs, he was a pest, sure, but he didn’t deserve to get shot down in the street like that, and Sherburn, he was a proud man, but I didn’t reckon he deserved to be lynched by a bunch of folks who just wanted to make a show of it. The whole town was a-buzzin’ now, and the noise was building, a deep, angry hum that warn’t natural. The cry of “Lynch him! Lynch him!” was starting to take hold, passin’ from mouth to mouth like a fever.

I saw the lanky man, the one who’d re-enacted the shooting, he was right in the thick of it, waving his crooked-handled cane and shouting louder than anyone. “He’s a coward!” he hollered, his voice cracking, “Shot a man when he warn’t looking, that’s what he done!” Which warn’t strictly true, Boggs was a-reelin’ towards Sherburn, but the truth don’t matter much when a mob gets to goin’. Pretty soon, the whole mass of people started to move, not just a few stragglers, but a proper river of folks, all surging up the street towards where Colonel Sherburn lived, which was in a big, fine brick house on the edge of town, with a proper fence and a well-kept yard.

It was a fearsome sight, that mob, all those angry faces and clenched fists and the air just thick with hatred. They warn’t thinkin’ about law or justice no more, just about letting out all the meanness and excitement they had bottled up inside them. I saw a few fellers with ropes coiled over their shoulders, and others with pistols and shotguns, though they kept them pointed at the ground, mostly. There was a sort of wildness to it all, like a pack of wolves on the scent of a wounded animal, and a thrill went through me, half fear and half a strange kind of excitement, because I warn’t never seen nothin’ like it before.

I ran along with them, but kept to the edges, not wanting to be part of it, but not wanting to miss it neither. The steamboat passengers, they was right up front, especially the big red-faced feller and the lawyer with the spectacles, all puffed up with their own importance, like they was leading a crusade. They got to Sherburn’s house, and it was a solid looking place, with all the curtains drawn. There warn’t a sign of life, but the front door was closed and bolted tight.

The crowd milled about in the yard, trampling the flowerbeds and shoutin’ insults and threats. “Come out, Sherburn!” they yelled. “We got a rope for you, Colonel! Your time has come!” The big red-faced man from the steamboat, he pushed his way to the front and pounded on the door with his fist, a mighty thud that echoed in the sudden quiet that followed. “Open this door, sir! We are here for justice!”

And then, just as he said that, a window on the second floor of the house slid open, slow and deliberate, and there was Colonel Sherburn, standing there as calm as you please, holding a double-barreled shotgun in his hands. He warn’t aiming it, just holding it, but you could tell by the way he held it that he knew how to use it. He looked down at the mob, his face pale but hard, and there was a sort of contempt in his eyes that made the bravest of them fellers take a step back.

He stood there for a long moment, just looking, and the crowd got quiet, a sort of shamed quiet, like a bunch of schoolboys caught stealing apples. Then he spoke, his voice clear and sharp, cutting through the silence like a razor. “The idea of you lynching anybody!” he says, and there was a sort of snarl in his voice. “It’s amusing. The idea of you a-coming for _me_! You’re a pack of loafers and cowards, every last one of you. You don’t have the sand to face a man in broad daylight, one on one. No, you hide in the dark, and you come in a mob, and you think you’re brave. You ain’t brave. You’re just a lot of noise and nothing else.”

He paused, and the silence was so thick you could cut it with a knife. Not a single man in the crowd dared to utter a sound, or even move much. They just stood there, their eyes fixed on him, like he was a rattlesnake about to strike.

“The trouble with you fellers,” he goes on, his voice still low but with a sting to it, “is that you ain’t got no manhood. Not a one of you, not truly. You come out here by night, a thousand strong, and you’ll scare a sick old man to death, or burn down a widow’s house, or run off a poor family with your shouting and your threats. But you ain’t got the grit to do a thing in the light of day. You’re brave only when you’re hidin’ in a crowd. You don’t have the decency of a dog, and you don’t have the courage of a mouse.”

He raised the shotgun just a little, not aiming it, but just enough to remind them of what it was. “Now, I give you fair warning,” he says, and his voice was cold as ice. “The first man that crosses that fence line, the first man that takes one step towards this house, he’s a dead man. And I’ll take down as many of you as I can before you get me. Now, go home, you rabble. Go home and tell your wives you finally found something worth doing, which was running away from a single old man with a shotgun.”

He stood there, still as a statue, the shotgun held steady. The crowd, which had been so full of bluster and fury just a few minutes before, now looked like a bunch of whipped dogs. Their anger seemed to drain out of them, leaving behind only shame and a sort of pathetic confusion. The big red-faced man from the steamboat, he tried to mumble something about laws and justice, but his voice was weak and it died in his throat. The lawyer with the spectacles, he just looked down at his shoes, all his sharp edges gone dull.

Then, slowly at first, one feller turned around, and then another. A whisper started up, “He’s right, you know,” and then a cough, and then a general shuffling of feet. The crowd started to break up, not with a rush, but with a sort of defeated slump. They slunk away from the house, not even looking back, their heads down, like they was ashamed to be seen. The lynching warn’t going to happen. Sherburn had faced them down, one man against a mob, and he had won. It was a mighty powerful thing to see, and it made me think about all sorts of things about people and their courage, or lack thereof.

The noise died down quicker than it had started, and soon the street was empty again, save for the trampled flowerbeds and the dust that was a-settlin’ back down. Colonel Sherburn, he closed his window with a soft click, and the house looked as still and unmoving as it had before.

I walked back down to the river, the whole scene a-playin’ over in my head. It warn’t long before I saw Silas, the feller with the wart, sittin’ on his cracker barrel again, whittling away. He looked up at me, a slow, knowing smile on his face. “Well, boy,” he says, “reckon that showed ‘em, didn’t it? Some folks, they just need a good dose of truth to set ‘em straight.”

I nodded, still a bit dazed by it all. “He warn’t scared a bit, Silas, not a bit.”

“Scared?” Silas chuckled, a dry sound. “Sherburn? That feller ain’t got a scared bone in his body. He knows men, that’s his secret. Knows what makes ‘em tick, and what makes ‘em run. He knows a mob ain’t nothin’ but a bunch of individuals, each one of ‘em a coward when he ain’t got the others to hide behind. A smart man can always break a mob, if he’s got the nerve.”

Just then, I saw her, the young girl, Boggs’ daughter, the one who’d screamed so. She was sitting on the ground near the general store, her back to me, her shoulders shaking with quiet sobs. She warn’t crying loud no more, just a deep, weary kind of sorrow. The crowd had forgotten about her in their rush to go lynching, and now she was just there, alone with her grief. I felt a pang in my chest, seeing her. She looked so small, and so lost.

Silas followed my gaze. “Poor thing,” he murmured. “Lost her pa, she did. And in such a way, too.” He got up, slowly, his old joints creaking. “Somebody ought to look after her.” He walked over to her, hesitant at first, and then he sat down on the cracker barrel next to her, not saying a word, just sitting there, a silent comfort. She didn’t look up, but she leaned just a little towards him, like a wilting flower looking for a bit of shade. It was a small thing, but it was the first bit of true kindness I’d seen since Boggs got shot, and it made me feel a little better, even if it didn’t change what had happened.

The steamboat, the Belle of Natchez, she was a-blowing her whistle again, ready to depart. The big red-faced man and the lawyer, they was already back on board, looking a bit deflated and not so important as they had just a short while ago. The other passengers, they was all looking at our town with a sort of mixture of awe and disgust, like they’d just witnessed some savage ritual. The gangplank swung up, the paddle-wheel started churnin’ again, and the Belle of Natchez pulled away from the bank, leaving a long trail of white foam in her wake. The noise of her engines and her whistle faded into the distance, and the quiet settled back in, a heavier quiet this time, filled with the memory of all that had happened.

The sun started to dip low in the sky, painting the clouds with streaks of orange and purple. The heat of the day began to lessen, and a gentle breeze came up from the river, rustlin’ the leaves on the trees. People started to gather their wagons and horses, heading back to their farms in the country, their faces still thoughtful, still a bit stunned by the day’s events. There warn’t no more laughter, no more loud talking, just a sort of solemn departure. The three fights I’d seen earlier felt like a lifetime ago, a distant memory from a more innocent time, before the shooting, before the mob, before Colonel Sherburn had faced down a whole town.

I watched Boggs’ daughter and Silas for a while longer. He was still sitting there, quiet, and she had stopped shaking so much. I reckoned she’d be alright, with somebody like Silas looking out for her. He warn’t much for words, but he had a good heart, and sometimes that’s all a body needs.

I started to walk along the riverbank, kicking at loose stones, thinking about everything. It seemed like people could be so mean, and then so cowardly, all in the space of an afternoon. And then there was Sherburn, who was mean in a different way, a cold, hard way, but he warn’t a coward. He stood up to a whole town and shamed them into running away. It was all so mixed up, I tell you. Good and bad, brave and scared, all jumbled together like clothes in a laundry basket.

I kept walking, past the last houses, out into the quiet of the cottonwoods. The river flowed on, same as always, unconcerned by the comings and goings of men, or their arguments, or their shootings. It was a constant thing, the river, always moving, always the same, but always changing too, just like the people who lived along its banks.

The moon was just starting to show itself, a sliver of silver in the darkening sky. A lone dog barked in the distance, and the crickets began their nightly song. The air smelled of damp earth and river water and something else, something I couldn’t quite put my finger on, a sort of lingering tension, like the quiet after a thunderstorm, when you know the air is still charged, and another one could break any time.

I laid down on the grass, looking up at the stars as they started to prickle through the twilight. I tried to make sense of it all, but it was like trying to catch water in a sieve. People, they was just too complicated. One minute they’re laughing at an old drunk, the next they’re howling for blood, and then they’re running away with their tails between their legs, all because one man had the courage to call them out for what they were. And the man who called them out, he was the one who’d just shot another man down in the street, not an hour before. It warn’t right, but it was real. And I reckoned that was how things often went in the world, messy and mixed up, and nobody ever seemed to know which end was up, or which way was right. It made you wonder about a lot of things, it did. It made you wonder if there ever was such a thing as real justice, or if it was just whatever the strongest feller with the coldest eye decided it was.

The world seemed a mighty big place, and a mighty confusing one, and I was just a boy, and I warn’t sure I was ever going to figure it all out. But I knew one thing for sure: I was glad I warn’t Colonel Sherburn, and I was glad I warn’t Boggs, and I was mighty glad I warn’t any of them folks in that mob. I was just me, and that was enough, for now anyway. I pulled my knees up to my chest and wrapped my arms around them, feeling the cool night air on my face, and I just watched the river flow by, and waited for tomorrow. And I reckoned tomorrow would bring its own set of troubles, because it always did, especially in a place like this, on the banks of the mighty Mississippi. There warn’t no peace to be had for long, not with people being people, and rivers being rivers, and towns being towns, always a-shifting and a-changing, and always a-throwin’ up surprises when you least expected ‘em. It was a hard world, but it was the only one we had, and you just had to make your way through it the best you could, and try not to get chawed up along the way. That’s what I figured, anyway, and I reckon that’s all a body can do.

I knew I had to tell someone, someone in authority, about what was happenin' on that wreck. But who could I trust? And how could I make them believe a raggedy boy with a wild story?

The sun began to set, and the cargo hold grew dim again. I was gettin' hungry once more, but I didn't dare risk goin' to the galley again. I hoped the boat would dock somewhere soon, and I could slip off, maybe find a town, and look for a sheriff or a constable.

Just then, I heard footsteps comin' into the cargo hold, heavy, regular footsteps. My heart jumped, and I pressed myself flat against the floor, pullin' the burlap sack tight around me. I held my breath, listenin' to the footsteps get closer, closer, till they stopped right near my hidin' spot.

"Well now, what have we here?" a gruff voice said, right above me.

My blood ran cold. I knew I was caught. I slowly pushed the burlap sack away from my face and looked up. Standin' over me was a big, burly man, with a thick beard and a stern face. He had a lantern in his hand, and he was shinin' it right in my eyes. He was the watchman, I reckoned, or maybe the first mate. He wore a uniform, dark and official.

"Look at this, a little river rat," he says, not unkindly, but with a note of weary resignation in his voice. "Stowaway, are you? Thought I smelled somethin' young and scared in here."

I couldn't speak, my tongue felt thick and useless in my mouth. I just stared at him, my eyes wide with fear.

He sighed, a deep, rumblin' sound. "Ain't no use hidin' now, boy. Come on out. What's your name? Where you from? And what in tarnation are you doin' on my boat?"

I slowly got to my feet, my legs still a bit wobbly. "My name's... well, folks just call me Jim," I mumbled, my voice barely a whisper. "I ain't from nowhere specific, just up and down the river." I didn't want to tell him about the wreck, not yet, not till I knew if he was a good man.

He looked me over, his eyes sharp and observant. He saw my torn clothes, my bare foot, the dirt and mud on me. "Jim, huh? And a sorry sight you are, Jim. Look like you've been sleepin' with the hogs. Where's your folks?"

"Don't got none," I said, hangin' my head. It warn't a lie, not exactly. My folks was long gone.

He scratched his beard, thinkin'. "Right. So, you snuck on board. Any money for passage?"

I shook my head. "No, sir. I ain't got a cent."

He sighed again. "Thought so. Well, we can't have you ridin' for free, Jim. That ain't how things work on the river. You gotta earn your keep." He looked at my small frame, then back at the stacks of cargo. "You look a bit skinny for haulin' wood, but I reckon you can sweep decks, or peel potatoes, or somethin'. What do you say, Jim? You willing to work?"

A wave of relief washed over me. He warn't goin' to throw me overboard, or turn me over to some mean authority. He was offerin' me work, a chance. "Yes, sir! Yes, I can work! I can work real hard, sir!" I said, my voice crackin' with eagerness.

He gave a small, grim smile. "Alright then, Jim. Follow me. We'll get you somethin' to eat, a bit cleaner, and then we'll find you a broom. You'll be workin' for your supper, and your bed, for as long as you're on this boat."

I followed him out of the cargo hold, feelin' like a new man, even with my bare foot and my dirty clothes. He led me through a maze of corridors, past more cabins and storage rooms, until we reached a small, cramped cabin near the stern, right next to the engine room, where the hum was loudest. It was a humble place, with a single cot, a small table, and a wash

basin on a shelf, with a bucket underneath for slop water. The air in there was thick and warm, smellin' of oil and somethin' like old sweat and metal, but it was dry, and it had a window with a thick glass pane lookin' out onto the churnin' water behind the boat. It warn't much, but after that hold, it looked like a king's palace to me.

"Here you are, Jim," the watchman says, his voice a bit louder to cut through the engine noise. "This'll be your bunk. You keep it tidy. There's a mess hall for grub; I'll show you to it after you've splashed some of that river mud off you. There's a pump in the hall for fresh water, and a soap, if you ain't used it all up yet." He pointed to a small, worn piece of lye soap on the shelf next to the basin. "I'm Captain Jedidiah, but most folks call me Cap'n Jed. Don't you forget it. We're a tight ship here, and everybody pulls their weight. You do your part, and you'll eat well and sleep sound. You don't, and you'll find yourself on the next shore quicker than a catfish can swallow a fly, and without your passage money."

He didn't sound mean, not exactly, but his eyes was like chips of flint, and they didn't miss nothin'. I nodded, tryin' to look eager and capable. "Yes, sir, Cap'n Jed. I understand, sir. I'll work hard, I will."

"See that you do," he grunted, and then he turned and left, his heavy boots clompin' down the narrow passageway.

I stood there for a minute, just lookin' around my new home. The cot was hard, made of canvas stretched over a frame, but it was a bed. The table was scarred and stained, but it was a table. And that window, lookin' out at the churnin' water, that was somethin'. It meant I was still on the river, still goin' somewhere. I went to the wash basin and pumped some water, cold and clean, and I splashed it on my face and neck. It felt good, mighty good. I scrubbed my hands with the lye soap till they was red and clean, and then I took off my shirt and tried to scrub the worst of the dirt off my arms and chest. My clothes was rags, but they was mine, and I hung them on a hook by the window to dry a bit. My bare foot, the one that had been cut, was still tender, but it warn't bleedin' anymore.

After I felt clean as I could get without fresh clothes, I stepped out into the passageway. It was narrow and lit by oil lamps that swung gently with the boat's motion. I could hear the rumble and hiss of the engine, a steady beat that vibrated through the floorboards. Cap'n Jed was waitin' for me, leanin' against the bulkhead with his arms crossed.

"Ready for some grub, Jim?" he asked, without much expression.

"Yes, sir, Cap'n Jed," I said, my stomach rumblin' just thinkin' about it.

He led me to a larger cabin forward, which was the mess hall. There was a long wooden table, scarred and polished by years of use, and benches on either side. A few men was already there, big rough-lookin' fellows, mostly, with beards and calloused hands. They looked up as we came in, and some of them gave me a quick, curious glance, then went back to their food. It was a stew, thick and hearty, with big chunks of meat and vegetables. The smell made my mouth water.

Cap'n Jed told me to sit at the end of the table and a cook, a burly man with a white apron tied around his middle, slapped a generous helping of stew onto a tin plate for me and handed me a hunk of fresh bread. I didn't waste no time. I dug in like I hadn't eaten in a week, which warn't far from the truth. It was the best stew I'd ever tasted, rich and warm and fillin'.

As I ate, Cap'n Jed stood by the door, watchin' me and the other men. He didn't eat with us, I noticed. He just watched. When I was done, he nodded for me to

follow him, and so I did, my belly full and warm, feelin’ a sight better than I had in days. He didn’t say nothin’ till we was back in the passageway, the oil lamps swingin’ and the engine thumpin’ under our feet like a big, slow heart.

“You said you was willin’ to work, Jim?” he asked, lookin’ at me from under his bushy eyebrows.

“Yes, sir, Cap’n Jed,” I said, standin’ as straight as I could. “I’ll work hard, I will. Whatever you need.”

He grunted. “Good. We got plenty to do on a boat like this. Ain’t no place for idlers. You’ll be a deckhand, mostly. Swabbin’ decks, carryin’ coal, haulin’ lines, whatever needs doin’. You got strength in them skinny arms?”

I puffed out my chest a little. “Yes, sir, I reckon I do.”

“We’ll see,” he said, and then he turned and walked forward, past the mess hall, towards the front of the boat. I followed him, my old tattered clothes feelin’ a little less ragged now that I had a purpose. The passageway opened up into a wider deck area, and beyond that was the bow of the steamboat, where the big paddlewheel was just makin’ its steady splashin’ noise. It was dark out now, the moon was high and bright, throwin’ a shimmery

light on the water and the river banks, which was just a blur of trees and dark shadows goin’ by. The big wheel made a soft churnin’ sound, and the smokestacks was puffin’ out their black breath against the sky. It was a grand sight, I tell you, and I felt mighty small standin’ there, a new hand on a big boat bound for I didn’t rightly know where, but it felt like somewhere important.

Cap’n Jed pointed a thick finger towards a pile of ropes coiled near the rail. “See them lines, boy? They ain’t coiled right. A good coil holds its shape, ready to run when you need it, not all tangled up like a rat’s nest. You get to work on them. And see that rust on the deck? Get a wire brush from the bosun’s locker, the one with the big padlock on it, and start scrubbin’. We ain’t got no idlers on this boat, like I said. You keep busy till I tell you different. Any questions?”

I shook my head. “No, sir, Cap’n Jed.” My stomach was full, but my arms already felt a little weak just lookin’ at them ropes.

“Good,” he grunted, and then he turned and walked off, leaving me alone under the big moon and the puffin’ smokestacks. I looked at the ropes, then at the rust, and then at the dark water sliding by. It warn’t exactly a clear night, the moon kept dodgin’ behind thin clouds, but it was bright enough to see what I was doin’.

I found the bosun’s locker, just like he said, down a short ladder near the paddlewheel. It smelled of tar and old canvas. I fumbled with the padlock, which was a heavy brass thing, till I got it open, and inside there was all sorts of tools and gear, shovels and buckets and brushes and rolls of canvas. I found a couple of wire brushes, hard and bristly, and then I went back up to the deck.

The ropes was thick and stiff, and they had a rough feel to them. I picked up one end and started tryin’ to coil it like I thought it ought to be, neat and round, but it kept twistin’ and snaggin’. It warn’t as easy as it looked, I tell you. My fingers got sore quick, and a few times the rope slipped and smacked me in the arm. I could hear the engine throbbin’ and the water rushin’ past, and every now and then a distant bell would ring out, or a whistle would blow somewhere far off down the river. The boat rocked gentle, but steady.

After a spell, maybe an hour or so, and I had only got one rope coiled half decent, a big man with a long grey beard and a chew of tobacco in his cheek came by. He had a lantern swingin’ in his hand, and he stopped and looked down at me. “New hand, ain’t you, boy?” he asked, his voice low and rumbly.

“Yes, sir,” I said, standin’ up straight and wipin’ my hands on my britches.

He spit a long stream of tobacco juice over the rail, and it hissed as it hit the water. “That ain’t the way to coil a line, son. You’ll have it tangled worse than a catfish in a trotline if you keep on like that.”

My face felt hot. “I’m tryin’, sir,” I mumbled.

He chuckled, a dry sound like leaves skitterin’ over a hard road. “I reckon you are. But tryin’ ain’t the same as doin’. Here, let old Silas show you. You gotta feed it, see? Let the rope tell you how it wants to lay. Don’t fight it. You gotta make your turns even, not too tight, not too loose, and let it fall into itself. Like this.” He took the rope from me, and his big, calloused hands moved over it like they had a mind of their own. He worked quick and smooth, and in no time at all, that rope was coiled up pretty as you please, layin’ flat and ready for use.

“There,” he said, givin’ it a pat. “That’s how you do it. Takes practice, boy. Everything does. You just keep at it. Don’t get discouraged.”

“Thank you, Mr. Silas,” I said, feelin’ a little bit better, but still a bit shamefaced.

He just grunted and moved on, his lantern swingin’, disappearin’ into the shadows. I took the next rope and tried to do it just like he showed me. It was still hard, but I could see what he meant about lettin’ the rope tell you. It warn’t a fight, it was more like a dance, a slow, patient kind of work. I got a little better with each one, and my hands started to learn the feel of it.

After I had got about half the ropes coiled up, I remembered the rust. I picked up a wire brush and started scourin’ away at the deck. It was slow work, and my back started to ache. The boat kept on movin’, steady as a clock, cuttin’ through the dark water. The air was cool and smelled of the river and coal smoke. I was tired, but I felt good too, like I was earnin’ my keep.

The moon rose higher, and the stars came out clearer in between the clouds, big and twinklin’ like scattered diamonds. I thought about the little shanty I’d left behind, and the old woman who’d taken me in, and how she’d always told me to work hard and be honest. I wondered if she was thinkin’ of me. I wondered where this river was goin’ to take me. It was a big world out there, and I was just a small piece of it, but for the first time in a long while, I felt like I was goin’ somewhere, like I had a purpose.

I kept scrubbin’ till my arms was tired and the rust was mostly gone from the spots I’d tackled. Then I went back to the ropes, and by the time I had coiled all of them, the eastern sky was startin’ to get a little bit lighter, just a faint grey peepin’ through the dark blue. It was almost dawn.

Cap’n Jed came back then, silent as a ghost, and he looked at the ropes and the deck. He didn’t say much, just nodded his head once. “Good enough for a greenhorn,” he mumbled. “Go get some sleep, boy. You’ll be on watch with Silas after breakfast. He’ll show you the ropes, pardon the pun. You eat with the crew.”

I just nodded, too tired to say much more, but I felt a warmth spread through me. “Thank you, sir,” I managed.

He just waved a hand and turned away. I went back to the mess hall, which was mostly empty now, and found a quiet corner on one of the benches. I was so tired I could have slept standin’ up. The engine’s throb was a lullaby, and the gentle rockin’ of the boat was like a cradle. I closed my eyes and fell asleep almost before my head hit the table.

I woke up to the smell of coffee and fried bacon. The mess hall was bustling now, men talkin’ and laughin’ and clatterin’ plates. The sun was up, pourin’ a bright light through the small portholes. I rubbed my eyes and stretched, my muscles feelin’ stiff, but not near as sore as I thought they would be.

The cook, the big burly man, seen me and called out, “Mornin’, sleepyhead! Come on and get some grub before it’s all gone.” He slapped a plate of bacon and eggs and a thick slice of fried bread in front of me, and a mug of strong, black coffee. It was just what I needed. I ate it all up, and the coffee warmed me right down to my toes.

After breakfast, I found Silas on the main deck, lookin’ out at the river. The banks was closer now, and I could see cows grazin’ in green pastures and a few farmhouses tucked away amongst the trees. The river itself was wide and shinin’ under the morning sun.

“Mornin’, Mr. Silas,” I said.

He turned and nodded. “Mornin’, son. Cap’n Jed says you’re with me for watch. Come on, I’ll show you where everything is. You gotta know the boat like the back of your hand, because when trouble comes, and it always does on the river, you ain’t got no time to be scratchin’ your head wonderin’ where the fire ax is, or how to shut off a valve.”

Silas was a patient man, and he took his time showin’ me everything. We went from bow to stern, above deck and below. He showed me the lines and the cleats, the steam pipes and the pressure gauges, the pumps and the fire buckets. He pointed out the different parts of the engine, though I didn’t understand much of that. He even showed me the wheelhouse, where the pilot stood at the big wheel, steerin’ the boat. The pilot was a grizzled old man with eyes that seemed to have seen every ripple on the river, and he gave me a quick nod, but didn’t say a word.

“The river’s a livin’ thing,” Silas told me, leanin’ on the rail as the sun climbed higher. “It shifts and changes, always movin’, always somethin’ new. Snags, sandbars, whirlpools, other boats, storms, fog. You gotta be ready for it all. And you gotta respect it. The river don’t care for no man’s pride or foolishness. It’ll take you down just as quick as it’ll carry you along.”

He showed me how to read the markers on the banks, the different lights on other boats, and how to tell the depth of the water by the color and the currents. He taught me how to tie a proper knot, one that would hold when it needed to, and then come undone easy when you wanted it to. It was a lot to take in, but I listened hard, knowin’ that every bit of it was important.

The day passed by quick. I hauled coal for the firemen, sweatin’ and grimy, but I felt strong. I helped clean out the ashes from the furnaces, which was a hot and dirty job. I learned how to swab the deck proper, pushin’ the water and the grime off the sides, makin’ the wood shine. Silas kept an eye on me, and every now and then he’d give me a word of advice, or a quiet nod of approval.

In the afternoon, we passed a small town, much like the one where I’d seen Boggs get shot. It was just a cluster of wooden buildings by the river, with a few people standin’ on the landing, watchin’ us go by. I saw a young girl wave, and I waved back, feelin’ a little bit of homesickness tug at me. But then the town was gone, disappearin’ behind the trees, and all there was again was the wide river and the movin’ banks.

Towards evening, the sky started to cloud up, and the air got heavy. Silas looked worried. “Looks like a squall comin’,” he said, squintin’ at the western sky. “River gets nasty when she’s mad. We better batten down the hatches, boy. Make sure everything’s tied down tight.”

We worked fast, me and Silas, runnin’ around the deck, pullin’ tarps over goods, makin’ sure the lines was secure. The wind started to pick up, whistlin’ through the rigging, and the boat began to pitch a little. The river, which had been so smooth and calm, now had little whitecaps dancin’ on its surface.

Soon enough, the rain started, first just a few big drops, then a steady downpour. The sky turned a dark, angry grey, and thunder rumbled in the distance. The steamboat rolled and swayed, and the engine seemed to groan under the strain. I got soaked through in minutes, my clothes stickin’ to me, but I kept workin’, tryin’ to keep up with Silas.

A sudden gust of wind tore at one of the tarps we had just secured, flappin’ it wildly. “Hold it, boy!” Silas yelled over the roar of the wind and rain. “Don’t let it go!”

I grabbed a corner of the tarp and held on with all my might, the canvas tuggin’ and fightin’ like a wild thing. The rain was comin’ down so hard I could barely see, and the wind was pushin’ me towards the rail. Silas was on the other side, strainin’ to pull it back into place.

Suddenly, a big wave slapped against the side of the boat, sendin’ spray high into the air, and the boat gave a lurch. My feet slipped on the wet deck, and for a heart-stopping second, I thought I was goin’ over. I let out a yelp, but I held onto that tarp like my life depended on it, which it probably did.

Silas, seein’ my trouble, yelled, “Stay low, boy! Get a better grip!” He scrambled over, quick as a cat for a big man, and grabbed hold of me, steadyin’ me with one arm while he wrestled the tarp with the other. Together, we finally managed to pull it back and tie it down tight with a thick rope.

“Close one,” Silas said, pantin’, his face stream-soaked. “You did good, boy. Didn’t let go.”

I was shiverin’ from the cold and the fright, but I felt a burst of pride too. I’d faced the river’s anger and I hadn’t backed down.

The storm raged on for another hour or so, but then, as sudden as it started, it began to die down. The rain eased off, the wind lessened, and the clouds started to break apart, showin’ patches of clear sky. A big, bright rainbow stretched across the eastern horizon, touchin’ the river at both ends. It was the prettiest thing I ever seen.

The boat kept on goin’, steady as ever, though a bit slower than before. The deck was littered with leaves and small branches, and there was puddles everywhere. Me and Silas spent the next while cleanin’ up the mess, sweepin’ the debris overboard.

When night came again, I was tired down to my bones, but it was a good tired. I’d worked hard, and I’d learned a lot. I’d even faced a storm and come out the other side. My bunk was a small cot in a corner of the crew’s quarters below deck, a place I hadn’t seen yet. It was cramped, with maybe eight or ten other cots, but it was dry, and it was mine. I pulled off my wet clothes and found a coarse blanket, wrapin’ it around myself.

The other men in the quarters was mostly asleep, or talkin’ in low voices. I heard somebody snorin’ like a buzzsaw, and another fella coughin’ a deep, rumblin’ cough. The air was thick with the smell of sweat and tobacco and the faint, sweet smell of the river.

I lay there for a while, listenin’ to the sounds of the boat and the men, thinkin’ about the day. It had been a long day, full of new things. I thought about Cap’n Jed, who didn’t say much but seen everything. I thought about Silas, who was rough on the outside but kind underneath. And I thought about the river, big and powerful and always movin’. It felt like a lifetime since I’d left that town where Boggs was shot. I closed my eyes, and the gentle rockin’ of the boat carried me off to sleep, dreamin’ of wide rivers and clear skies and the churnin’ sound of a paddlewheel.

The next few days was much the same, a steady rhythm of work and watch. I learned more and more about the boat and the river. I learned to splice a rope, to grease the capstan, to chip paint from the railings without stoppin’ to scratch myself every minute. I learned the names of some of the other crewmen, too. There was Old Man Hemphill, the engineer, a fussy sort with spectacles and oil on his hands, always tinkerin’ with the engine. And there was a young fella named Pete, about my age, who was the cabin boy, and he had a sharp tongue but a kind heart. He told me stories about all the places the boat had been, and all the strange folks he’d seen. He said he’d seen a man try to pay for his passage with a sack of rattlesnakes once.

We passed through more towns, some big and bustling, with wharves crowded with steamboats and warehouses piled high with goods, and some just tiny hamlets, hardly more than a few shacks and a muddy landing. People was always comin’ and goin’, gettin’ on and off the boat with their luggage and their goods, their hopes and their troubles. The river was like a big, movin’ road, carryin’ everyone and everything along.

One afternoon, a few days after the storm, the sky was clear and bright, and the river was smooth as glass. Cap’n Jed called me to the wheelhouse. He was standin’ next to the pilot, lookin’ at a big map spread out on a table.

“Jim,” he said, without lookin’ up from the map. “You done well so far. You ain’t shirked your duties, and you listen to Silas. That’s a good start.”

My chest swelled a bit with pride. “Thank you, sir.”

He finally looked at me, his eyes sharp and serious. “But there’s more to the river than scrubbin’ decks and coiling ropes. There’s somethin’ else I need you to do, somethin’ a bit more… delicate.” He paused, and I waited, wonderin’ what he was talkin’ about.

“We’re carryin’ some… valuable cargo,” he went on, his voice lower now. “And there’s talk of… well, of folks who might want to relieve us of it. We need extra eyes, quiet eyes, eyes that don’t look like they’re lookin’. You understand?”

I thought about it for a moment. He wanted me to be a lookout, but a secret one. “You want me to watch out for trouble, sir?”

“Precisely,” he said, a faint smile touchin’ his lips for just a second. “You got young eyes, and you can blend in. Nobody expects a young deckhand to be payin’ close attention to things that ain’t his direct business. Your job is to keep your ears open, and your eyes wider than usual. If you see anything suspicious, anything out of place, you come straight to me or Silas. Don’t talk to anyone else. Understand?”

“Yes, sir,” I said, feelin’ a sudden chill run down my spine. This was different from scrubbin’ decks. This was important, and maybe a little dangerous.

“Good,” he said. “Now, this ain’t somethin’ you talk about. Not to Silas, not to Pete, not to anyone. This stays between us. Your regular duties still stand, but this is your main job now, even if nobody knows it. You keep a close watch, especially when we’re puttin’ in at a town, or slowin’ down near a remote landing. There are bad sorts on the river, Jim, always lookin’ for an easy mark. And this boat ain’t goin’ to be one.”

He turned back to his map, dismissin’ me. I left the wheelhouse with a new feeling in my belly, a mix of excitement and a little bit of fear. I was still a deckhand, still haulin’ coal and scrubbin’ decks, but now I was somethin’ more too, a secret pair of eyes for the Cap’n. It felt like I had a heavy responsibility on my shoulders.

I spent the rest of the day lookin’ at everyone and everything with a new kind of attention. I watched the passengers as they came and went, men in fancy suits and women in bonnets, farmers with their bundles and peddlers with their wares. I tried to see if any of them looked like “bad sorts,” but they all just looked like regular folks to me. It was hard to tell.

When we stopped at a woodyard to take on fuel, I watched the men who came aboard to load the cordwood. They were rough-lookin’ fellas, with dirty clothes and hard hands, but they were just doin’ their job. I watched the riverbank too, lookin’ for anything unusual in the trees or on the path leadin’ down to the landing, but there warn’t nothin’.

Later that night, as the boat churned its way upriver, I was on watch with Silas again. The moon was full and bright, makin’ a silver path on the water. I stood by the rail, pretendin’ to just be lookin’ at the stars, but my eyes was dartin’ everywhere, checkin’ the shadows, listenin’ to every creak and groan of the boat.

Silas came up beside me, quietly, and leaned on the rail. “Clear night tonight, Jim,” he said, his voice soft. “Good for travelin’.”

“Yes, sir,” I answered, still keepin’ my eyes movin’.

He looked at me for a long moment, then he looked out at the river. “You seem a bit jumpy tonight, boy. Somethin’ on your mind?”

I remembered Cap’n Jed’s words, “Don’t talk to anyone else.” So I just shook my head. “No, sir. Just… thinkin’ about things. All the places we’re goin’.”

Silas grunted, and I couldn’t tell if he believed me or not. But he didn’t press it. He just stood there for a spell, quietly smokin’ his pipe, lookin’ out at the movin’ water.

I kept up my watch, feelin’ the weight of Cap’n Jed’s secret. The river felt different now, more mysterious, more full of hidden dangers. It warn’t just a path anymore; it was a place where things could happen, good or bad, and it was my job to see the bad before it got too close. And for a young fella like me, that was a mighty big job, and I reckoned I was up to it. I looked at the moon, shinin’ down on the big river, and I took a deep breath, ready for whatever might come.
