It seemed like a long time ago that we was floatin’ down the river, just the two of us, and the biggest trouble we had was tryin’ to get enough to eat. Now it seemed like trouble was everywhere, and it just kept a-comin’.

My stomach started to rumble a little, and I realized I hadn’t eaten since the day before. I wished I had a piece of corn pone, or maybe some fish I could cook over a little fire. But I didn’t have nothin’, so I just took a drink from the river, cuppin’ my hands and bringin’ the cool water to my mouth. It tasted a little muddy, but it was wet, and it helped some.

After a while, I heard the sound of a steamboat whistle, far off down the river, a long, mournful sound that always made me feel a little lonesome. It was a good sound, though, a sound that meant things was still a-movin’ in the world, even if everything else felt like it was stuck. I wondered where that boat was a-goin’, and who was on it, and if they knew what had happened in this little town, or if they was just sailin’ on, happy and unaware. It was funny how the world kept a-turnin’, even when some folks’ worlds had just crashed to pieces.

I stayed there till the sun was a good ways up, feelin’ the warmth on my face, and then I reckoned it was time to move on. I couldn’t stay by the river forever, and I had to figure out what I was a-goin’ to do next. I walked back up the path towards town, but I didn’t go straight into the main street. I kept to the back roads, walkin’ along behind the houses and the stores, where there warn’t so many folks. I wanted to see what was a-happenin’, but I didn’t want to be seen myself, not yet anyway.

As I come closer, I could hear the town startin’ to wake up for real. Doors was a-openin’ and shuttin’, and I could hear the clatter of pots and pans from kitchens, and the murmur of voices, still quiet and low, like folks hadn’t quite got their speakin’ voices back yet. I slipped into an alleyway between two stores, a dark, narrow place that smelled of stale beer and garbage, and I peered out from behind a pile of empty barrels.

The street looked different now in the full light of day. It warn’t packed with wagons and horses like it had been the day before. There was a few here and there, but mostly it was just empty, and the dust was still thick on the ground, stirred up from

all the stompin’ and the yellin’ of the day before. It laid thick in the street, like a blanket, and there warn’t no breeze to stir it up none, so it just sat there, quiet and still, like a memory of all the fuss. The sun was gettin’ higher, but it still felt cool in the alley, and I could hear the flies buzzin’ around the garbage cans, a lazy, sleepy sound that warn’t like the wild noise of yesterday at all.

I stayed put for a good while, just watchin’ and listenin’. Pretty soon, a couple of men come out of one of the stores, stretchin’ and yawnin’, and they leaned up against the porch posts. One of them was a big fella with a red beard, and the other was thin and wore spectacles. They started talkin’ low, and I strained my ears to catch what they was sayin’.

“Well,” says the red-bearded fella, “he put ’em in their place, didn’t he? That Sherburn, he ain’t a man to trifle with.”

The thin one nodded. “Never seen such a thing. That whole crowd, a-yellin’ and a-whoopin’ like a pack of wild dogs, and then he just steps out, cool as a cucumber, and talks ’em down like they was children. It was a sight to behold, it truly was.”

“He knowed ’em, that’s what it was,” says the red-bearded fella. “He knowed they warn’t no account, all bluff and bluster when they was together, but not a one of ’em with the gumption to stand alone. He called their bluff, and they shrunk like wet rags.”

They jawed on about it for a bit, and I listened close, tryin’ to piece together what had happened after I lit out. It seemed like that crowd that had been so keen on lynchin’ Sherburn had indeed gone right for his house. The way they was talkin’, it sounded like a regular riot, with folks spillin’ out from every street, all fired up and hollerin’ for justice, or what they called justice anyway. I could almost see it in my mind, that angry mob, torches a-flickerin’ in the dark, and all the faces twisted up with hate and excitement. It was the kind of thing that makes your skin crawl, just thinkin’ about it.

They said the crowd got to Sherburn’s place, and they was just about to break down the door, or maybe burn the house down, when Colonel Sherburn himself stepped out onto his porch, bold as brass. He had a double-barreled shotgun in his hand, and he just stood there, tall and straight, lookin’ out at that sea of faces. And then, he started to speak.

Now, these two fellas in front of the store, they couldn’t quite remember every word he said, but they got the gist of it. They said he looked down at that crowd, all sneerin’ and scornful, and he called them cowards. He said they warn’t men, but just a pack of white-livered curs, brave only when they was a thousand strong against one. He said they warn’t even a proper mob, just a bunch of shirkers and nobodies that would scatter like chickens if one real man stood up to ’em. He told ’em to look at themselves, all drunk and mean, and then he dared ’em to come and get him, one by one. He said he had his shotgun, and he warn’t scared, and they was just a disgrace to the human race. He stood there for a long minute, just starin’ ’em down, and not a single soul moved. They all just kind of sagged, like a balloon when the air goes out of it. The yellin’ died down to a murmur, and then to silence. And then, slowly, they just started to melt away, a few at a time, until the whole street was empty again. The red-bearded man said it was the most shameful thing he ever seen, a thousand men turned tail by one.

I reckoned it was pretty brave of Sherburn, even if he was a cold, hard man. It made me think about how folks can act so big and brave when they’re in a crowd, all pushin’ and eggin’ each other on, but when it comes right down to it, and they got to face somethin’ real, they ain’t got no backbone. It was kind of disappointin’ in a way, seein’ how easily they backed down. It made you wonder what all the fuss was for in the first place, if they warn’t really willin’ to go through with it.

After a bit, the two fellas went back inside, and the street was quiet again, mostly. I decided it was safe enough to move, so I slipped out of the alley and started walkin’ along the side street, tryin’ to look like I belonged there, though I surely didn’t. My stomach was a-growlin’ somethin’ fierce, and I was hopin’ to find an open back door somewhere, maybe a kitchen where they’d just finished up breakfast and left somethin’ out. I knew I couldn’t just walk into a store and buy somethin’, not with no money, and not lookin’ like I did.

I passed by a general store, and then a blacksmith shop, where the fire was just bein’ stoked and the hammer was startin’ to clang on the anvil, a steady, rhythmic sound that was a comfort, somehow. Then I come to a small buildin’ that had a sign painted on it that said “BAKERY.” The smell that was comin’ out of there was enough to make a dead man sit up and beg. It was warm and sweet, like fresh bread and cakes, and my mouth just started to water. I crept around to the back, real quiet-like, and sure enough, the back door was open a crack, lettin’ out a plume of that good smell.

I peered inside, and there warn’t nobody there, just stacks of fresh loaves on a wooden table, and some pies on a shelf. My heart started to beat a little faster. It warn’t right to steal, I knowed that, but I was so hungry my head was swimmin’. I thought about what Pap always said, that if you’re hungry, you take what you can get. And then I thought about Jim, and how he always tried to do what was right, even when it was hard. I stood there for a minute, arguin’ with myself, while that good smell just kept pullin’ me in.

Finally, I couldn’t stand it no longer. I slipped inside, quick as a cat, and grabbed a small loaf of bread and a couple of those sweet rolls from the shelf. My hands was tremblin’ a little. I shoved ’em under my shirt and backed out, careful not to make a sound. I got back into the alley, and then I just kept goin’, away from the main street, towards the edge of town where there was some woods. I didn’t want to be seen eatin’ my stolen goods, and I wanted to be out of sight.

I found a good spot under a big old oak tree, where the sun made dappled patterns on the ground, and I tore into that bread like I hadn’t eaten in a week, which warn’t far from the truth. It was warm and soft, and it tasted like heaven. The sweet rolls was even better, full of sugar and cinnamon. I ate till my stomach stopped growlin’, and then I felt a lot better, and not so much like a scared rabbit.

While I was eatin’, I got to thinkin’ again about all the things I’d seen in this town. It warn’t like Pikesville, where I first seen the King and the Duke. That was a sleepy place, mostly. This town was different. It had a kind of wildness to it, a meanness that could boil up real quick. It made me feel uneasy. Folks was quick to laugh, quick to get angry, and quick to back down. They warn’t steady, like the river. The river always knew where it was a-goin’, and it always kept on its way, but these people, they just swayed with every wind that come along.

I reckoned Sherburn was a hard man, but he was a steady one. He knowed his own mind, and he didn’t care what nobody else thought. That made him different from all the rest. It warn’t a good kind of steady, maybe, but it warn’t wishy-washy either. He killed Boggs, and that was a terrible thing, but he done it out in the open, and then he stood his ground. He didn’t sneak around in the dark. That made him seem more honest in a way, even if he was a murderer. It’s a funny thing, the way people are.

I stayed under that tree for a long time, just listenin’ to the birds and the wind in the leaves. I didn’t want to go back into town. I felt like I’d seen enough of that place for a lifetime. It made my head ache, all that noise and fuss and then the quiet shame. I started to think about Jim again, and how he was out there somewhere, maybe still on the raft, maybe hidin’ in the woods like me. I missed him, and I wished I could just float away on the river and forget all about these towns and their troubles.

But I couldn’t just sit there forever. I had to figure out what I was a-goin’ to do next. I couldn’t stay in this town, that was for sure. Someone might recognize me, or ask questions, and I didn’t want no part of that. I needed to get back to the river, and try to find the raft, or at least a way to get downstream and look for Jim.

So, I got up and stretched my legs. My belly was full now, and I felt a bit more like myself. I started walkin’ through the woods, away from the town, tryin’ to head towards the riverbank where I’d left the water that mornin’. It was a long walk, and the sun was gettin’ hotter, beatin’ down through the trees. The woods was quiet, just the rustlin’ of leaves and the chirp of crickets. It was a good kind of quiet, not like the uneasy quiet of the town.

After a while, I come to a clearing, and I seen a little cabin tucked away amongst some trees. Smoke was comin’ out of the chimney, and there was a small garden patch out front, with corn and beans a-growin’. It looked peaceful, but I warn’t keen on bein’ seen, so I just skirted around it, keepin’ to the trees. I didn’t want to bother nobody, and I didn’t want nobody to bother me.

I walked some more, and then I heard the sound of an axe choppin’ wood, a steady _thwack, thwack_ sound. I got down low and crept a little closer, peerin’ through the bushes. I seen an old man, thin and bent, choppin’ wood near a small lean-to. He had a long white beard, and he was wearin’ overalls and a straw hat. He looked harmless enough. I reckoned he was just a hermit, livin’ out in the woods, away from everybody.

I watched him for a bit. He didn’t seem to be in any hurry, just choppin’ away, stackin’ the wood neat and tidy. I thought maybe I could ask him for some water, or directions to the river, but then I thought better of it. It was always best to keep to yourself, especially when you was on the run. You never knowed who might be lookin’ for you, or who might just be a busybody.

So I kept on my way, tryin’ to be as quiet as I could. The choppin’ sound faded behind me, and pretty soon, I started to hear the soft murmur of the river again, a sound that always made me feel like I was comin’ home. It was a deeper, wider sound now, not just the lappin’ of the water, but the big, slow breath of the whole river. I pushed through a last thicket of bushes, and there it was, spread out before me, wide and shinin’ under the afternoon sun.

I walked down to the bank and dipped my hands in the cool water. It felt good on my face, washin’ away some of the dust and the worry. I looked out across the river, tryin’ to spot the raft, but there warn’t no sign of it. It had been tied up a good ways downstream, and I reckoned it had probably drifted off or somebody had found it and took it. That was a hard thought, because that raft was my home, and Jim’s home too. It was all we had.

A heavy sigh escaped me, and I sat down on the bank, feelin’ pretty low. What was I a-goin’ to do now? No raft, no Jim, stuck near a town full of folks that was quick to anger and quick to shame. It felt like the whole world was a-closin’ in on me. I watched a steamboat paddle by in the distance, churnin’ up the water, and I felt a fresh wave of lonesomeness. That boat was a-goin’ somewhere, and I warn’t. I was just stuck.

I sat there till the sun started to sink low in the sky, turnin’ the clouds all colors of red and orange and purple. It was a pretty sight, and it always made me feel a little better, just bein’ out there with the river and the sky. But it didn’t make the problems go away. I had to get a plan.

I reckoned my best bet was to just walk along the riverbank, headin’ downstream. Maybe I’d come across the raft, or maybe I’d see Jim, or maybe I’d find some other way to get on the water. It was a long shot, but it was all I had. I couldn’t just stay put and let things happen to me. I had to keep movin’, that was the only way to stay ahead of trouble.

So, when the last of the sun had faded, and the first stars was just startin’ to pop out, I got up. The air was gettin’ cool, and a little mist was risin’ from the water. I didn’t have no blanket, and I didn’t have no fire, but I had a full stomach, thanks to that stolen bread, and I had my own two feet. That was somethin’, anyway.

I started walkin’ along the bank, quiet-like, tryin’ to see in the dim light. The sounds of the night was startin’ up, crickets and frogs and the occasional hoot of an owl. It warn’t scary, just natural. I felt a little bit of that old freedom come back, the feelin’ of just bein’ out in the world, with nobody tellin’ you what to do. It was a good feelin’, even if I was a bit lost and didn’t know what tomorrow would bring. I just kept on walkin’, puttin’ one foot in front of the other, listenin’ to the river, and hopin’ for the best. I reckoned I’d find my way, one way or another. I always did. The night was dark, but the river was there, and that was somethin’ to hold onto. I kept my eyes peeled, lookin’ for anything familiar, a broken branch, a sandbar, anything that might tell me where I was and where Jim might be. Every shadow seemed to move, every rustle of leaves made me jump a little, but I tried to keep my nerve. I knowed the river, and the river knowed me, and I reckoned we’d get by together, somehow. It warn’t no use frettin’ over what was done; what was done was done, and all I could do was keep on goin’.

The riverbank was a tricky place to walk in the dark, full of roots and snags and soft spots where the mud seemed to want to swallow your feet whole. I had to go slow, feelin’ my way with my bare feet, sometimes stumblin’ over a fallen log or a clump of bushes. The moon warn’t up yet, or else it was hid behind a thick bank of clouds, so the world was mostly just shades of black and gray, with the dark line of the river shinin’ a little bit like polished lead whenever the sky cleared for a second. The air got cooler and cooler, and I pulled my rags tighter around me, though they didn’t do much good. My stomach was full from that bread, but a cold wind can find its way right through a fella, no matter how much he’s et.

I kept thinkin’ about Jim. Where was he? Was he still on the raft, driftin’ along? Or had he got ashore too, maybe further down? He warn’t the kind to just give up, I knowed that. He was smart, Jim was, and he had a good head on his shoulders. But he warn’t used to bein’ alone like this, not in these parts. My heart felt heavy when I thought about him, out there in the dark, maybe scared, maybe hungry. It was my fault, all of it, for lettin’ them fellas talk me into goin’ ashore. I should’ve stuck to the raft, like Jim always said. He

warn’t never used to talkin’ like that, Jim warn’t. He was a good nigger, the best I ever knowed, and he stuck by me through thick and thin, and now here I was, alone again, and it was all my fault. I shouldn’t never have let them fellas talk me into goin’ ashore, not with the raft tied up like that, and Jim tellin’ me it was a bad idea. But I was young and foolish, and I wanted to see what was happenin’, and now look at me, wanderin’ in the dark, with Jim likely driftin’ down the river without me, or maybe caught, or maybe somethin’ worse. My heart ached somethin’ awful, and I wished I could just lay down and cry, but that warn’t no use. A fella couldn’t get nowhere by cryin’, and I had to keep movin’, had to find Jim, no matter what.

The riverbank got rougher the further I went, full of gully-washes and snags where trees had fell in the high water and got themselves tangled up. The mud was deep in places, suckin’ at my bare feet, and sometimes I’d trip over a root or a stone that I couldn’t see in the dim light. The wind picked up a little, whisperin’ through the willows that hung over the water, and it sent a shiver right through my thin shirt. I was cold, and hungry again, though I’d et that bread not so long ago. But walkin’ in the dark like that, with your nerves all jangled up, it burned through your vittles quick as anything. I kept my eyes fixed on the river, hopin’ to catch a glimpse of the raft, or even a piece of it, somethin’ to tell me Jim was alright, or at least that he’d made it off. But there warn’t nothin’ but the black water slippin’ by, smooth and silent, just like it was laughin’ at me for bein’ so stupid.

I walked on for what felt like hours, though I couldn’t tell time in the dark like that. My legs was achin’ and my feet was sore from all the rocks and roots. I was startin’ to think I’d never find him, or that I’d walked right past him in the gloom. Then, as I rounded a bend where the bank was a little higher and more sandy, I seen somethin’. It was a dark shape, half in the water, half out, and it looked like a piece of wood. My heart jumped into my throat, and I broke into a stumbling run, splashin’ through the shallow water at the edge. When I got closer, I seen it warn’t just any piece of wood. It was a section of one of the raft’s logs, splintered at one end where it looked like it had been tore loose, and stuck in a little eddy. There was a bit of rope still tied to it, too, a piece of the lashing that held the logs together.

My breath caught in my chest, and a cold dread went through me that was worse than the night air. The raft was broke up. Jim could be hurt, or worse. But then I thought, if it was broke up, maybe he got to shore. Maybe he was further down, safe in the woods somewhere, waitin’ for me. That thought put a little bit of fire back in my belly, and I pushed on, my eyes scanin’ the bank with a fierce eagerness now. I wanted to yell Jim’s name, but I knowed better. This warn’t no time for yellin’. There was always folks on the river, good folks and bad folks, and I didn’t know which kind was about in this part of the country.

The bank grew steep again, and I had to scramble up a little rise, grabbin’ at roots and bushes to pull myself along. When I got to the top, the trees was thicker, mostly old sycamores and cottonwoods that growed right down to the water’s edge. The ground was softer here, like a path had been made by animals or by folks walkin’ it steady. I followed it, keepin’ close to the river but away from the immediate bank, where it was too easy to fall in or get caught in the mud. The moon was tryin’ to peek through the clouds now, and sometimes a silver patch would appear on the water, makin’ the shadows of the trees dance and sway. It warn’t much light, but it was enough to see the path, and to see that it was leading me further from the river, into a thicker patch of woods.

I hesitated then, wonderin’ if I should stay by the water or follow this path. But somethin’ in my gut told me to go for the path. It felt like it might lead to somethin’, maybe a cabin, or at least a clearing where I could rest till mornin’. As I walked, I started to hear new sounds, sounds that warn’t just the river and the night critters. It was a faint barkin’ in the distance, a dog, and then a different sound, like wood bein’ chopped, but very far off. My heart started thumpin’ like a drum. That meant people. People could mean trouble, but they could also mean food, or help for Jim, if he was with them. Or they could be slave-catchers. I knowed I had to be careful.

I left the path and melted into the trees, movin’ slow and quiet, like an Injun. I kept my head down and my eyes peeled, makin’ sure not to snap any twigs underfoot. The barkin’ got a little louder, and I could hear the chop-chop-chop of an axe more clearly now. It sounded like it was coming from a little ways inland, not right on the river. I crept on, goin’ deeper into the woods, towards the sounds. My stomach was twistin’ itself into knots now, both from hunger and from fear. What if it was Pap? What if he’d caught up to me? No, I reckoned Pap warn’t smart enough to track me like this. But then, there was always somebody lookin’ for somethin’, and I knowed I was a runaway myself, and I had a runaway slave with me. That was a bad combination, right enough.

After a bit, I seen a faint glow through the trees, a yellow light, like from a window. I stopped dead still and pressed myself behind a thick oak, my breath hitched in my throat. I waited a long time, listenin’. The axe had stopped, and the dog warn’t barkin’ no more. All I could hear was the crickets and the whisper of the wind, and the thumpin’ of my own heart. I squinted through the branches. The light was steady, so it warn’t a fire in the open. It had to be a cabin, with a lamp inside.

I knowed I couldn’t just march up to it. Not at night, and not with what I was doin’. I needed to see who was there, and what kind of folks they was. So I started movin’ again, makin’ a wide circle around the light, tryin’ to get a view of the place without showin’ myself. The woods was dark and spooky, and every shadow looked like it might jump out at me. I kept tellin’ myself, *don’t be a fool, Huck, you been through worse than this*. But still, my skin felt crawly, and I wished Jim was there with me, his big hand on my shoulder, tellin’ me not to worry.

I found a spot behind a thicket of blackberry bushes, where I could see the cabin clear enough. It warn’t much, just a small log cabin, sort of ramshackle, with a little porch tacked onto the front. There was a thin curl of smoke comin’ from the chimney, and a faint yellow light shinin’ from the single window. Near the cabin, there was a small pen with a pig snortin’ softly, and a rickety shed that looked like it might fall down any minute. The dog, a scrawny hound, was layin’ curled up on the porch, fast asleep. It looked like poor folks lived here, not fancy ones. And poor folks sometimes was good folks, and sometimes they was just as mean as anybody else.

I settled down behind the bushes, shiverin’ a little from the cold and the tension. I decided I’d wait till mornin’. I couldn’t risk showin’ myself in the dark. It was better to see what kind of people they was in the light of day. I leaned my back against a tree, tryin’ to get comfortable, but it warn’t no use. My mind kept goin’ over everything, about Jim and the raft, and what I was doin’. The Widow Douglas, she’d always preached about right and wrong, and Miss Watson, she was always talkin’ about Providence. Well, Providence hadn’t done much for me or Jim lately, it seemed. Here I was, helpin’ a nigger run away, and everybody in town would call me a low-down abolitionist if they knowed it. But Jim was my friend, and he’d never done me no harm, and he always treated me good, better than Pap ever did. So I reckoned I was doin’ the right thing, even if it warn’t what the law said. My conscience give me a bit of a tweak, but I pushed it down. I warn’t thinkin’ about that now. I was thinkin’ about Jim.

The night wore on, slow and endless. I must have dozed off a few times, because the next thing I knowed, the sky was startin’ to lighten, turnin’ a pale gray in the east. The sounds of the night began to fade, and new sounds took their place: birds chirpin’ and twitterin’ in the trees, a rooster crowin’ somewhere further off, and the pig in the pen gruntin’ louder, expectin’ his breakfast. I stretched my stiff limbs and rubbed my eyes. I was hungry, my stomach groanin’ and rumblin’ like thunder.

I looked at the cabin again. The smoke was thicker now, meanin’ they was makin’ a fire for cookin’. I decided I’d wait a little longer, till they come out, so I could get a better look at them. I wanted to see their faces, how they moved, before I made any decisions. Pretty soon, the cabin door opened, and a man stepped out onto the porch. He was a tall, skinny fella, with a long, scraggly beard that looked like it hadn’t seen a comb in weeks. He wore patched-up overalls and a faded red shirt, and he had a old, beat-up hat on his head. He yawned wide, stretchin’ his arms over his head, then he scratched himself under his armpit and went to the edge of the porch, where he spit a long stream of tobacco juice into the dirt.

He looked around, squintin’ at the risin’ sun, then he said somethin’ I couldn’t rightly hear, mumbled-like, and went back inside. A few minutes later, he came out again, this time with a tin bucket in one hand and a hoe in the other. He shuffled over to a small patch of ground near the cabin that was all dug up, and started choppin’ at the weeds. He moved slow and easy, like he had all the time in the world. He didn’t look like no slave-catcher, or nobody mean. Just a poor, old farmer, tryin’ to get his vittles from the ground.

While he was workin’, another person came out of the cabin. It was a woman, smaller than the man, with her hair pulled back tight in a bun. She wore a simple calico dress, all faded, and she had a shawl wrapped around her shoulders. She carried a slop bucket, and she went over to the pigpen, whistlin’ a little tune as she poured the slops in for the pig. The dog woke up and wagged its tail, then got up and stretched, and went over to sniff at the woman’s heels. They both seemed quiet and peaceable.

I watched them for a good while, tryin’ to make up my mind. My stomach was groanin’ louder now, and the smell of cookin’ drifted on the air, makin’ my mouth water. I reckoned I could risk it. These folks didn’t look like they’d harm nobody. They looked like they’d been here a long time, just scratchin’ out a livin’. I figured I could make up some story about bein’ lost, or separated from a flatboat, and maybe they’d give me somethin’ to eat, and tell me if they’d seen anybody else on the river, or any signs of a broken-up raft.

I waited till the man went back inside for a minute, and the woman was still by the pigpen. I straightened up, took a deep breath, and started walkin’ out of the bushes, tryin’ to look as innocent and lost as I could. The dog seen me first. It let out a soft whine, then a low growl, and started to walk towards me, its tail droopin’. The woman heard it and looked up. She seen me standin’ there, a small, raggedy boy, lookin’ like I’d been through a ringer. Her eyes got wide for a second, then she put her hand to her mouth, like she was surprised.

“Well, bless my soul,” she said, her voice a little shaky. “Who in tarnation are you, child? And what are you doin’ out here in these woods all by yourself?”

I shuffled my feet, lookin’ down at the ground, tryin’ to seem pitiful. “I’m Huck Finn, ma’am,” I said, my voice crackin’ a little. “I got separated from my pap. We was on a flatboat, and a big storm come up last night, and it tore the boat all to pieces, and I got washed ashore, and I don’t know where he is, or where I am.” I made sure to put a little tremble in my voice, like I was about to cry.

She looked at me for a long moment, her eyes scanin’ me up and down. Then she lowered her hand and came a little closer. “A flatboat, you say? Well, I declare. A big storm, you say? And it’s a good piece from here, on de mainland. Dey ain’t gwyne to be lookin’ dere for a runaway, not after all dis time.”

I thought about the Grangerford place. It warn’t far from where we’d lost the raft, but that was a long time ago. It was an old, abandoned plantation, a place of ghosts and sad memories. Nobody went near it. It was far enough that it’d be hard for dogs to pick up a trail if Jim was careful.

“That’s a good thought, Jim,” I said. “The old smokehouse, it might do. But it’s a good walk from here, and you got to do it all in the water, as much as you can. And what about food?”

“I got some corn dodgers and a bit of dried fish in dat bundle, Huck. Enough for a day or two.”

“Alright then. Here’s the plan. You take your bundle, and you wade out, right now. Walk downstream, close to the bank, as deep as you can go without makin’ a splash. Don’t come out till you’re well past the island, maybe even a mile or two. Then, when you do come out, be careful, Jim. Try to stick to rocky ground, or even muddy places where a scent gets washed away easy. And head for the Grangerford smokehouse. Hide there, and don’t come out for nothing. I’ll come find you as soon as I can, after I throw them off the scent here.”

Jim stood up, gathering his bundle. He looked grim, but determined. “You be careful too, Huck. If dey catch you, dey gwyne to ask questions, and den it all be over.”

“I know, Jim. I’ll be slicker than a greased pig. You just worry about yourself. Now go on, and good luck.”

He nodded, and then, with a last look at me, he stepped into the dark water. I watched him go, a silent shadow moving through the moonlit river, his head barely visible above the surface. It was a terrible thing, sendin’ him off like that, alone in the dark, with dogs on his trail. But it was the only way.

I waited until I couldn’t see him anymore, then I got back in the skiff. I rowed it back to the Phelps’ landing, my arms achin’ from the effort. I pulled it up, hid it as best I could under the willows, just like I’d found it. Then I walked back towards the house, tryin’ to look like I’d just been gatherin’ firewood, and not like I’d just sent a man off into the wilderness to escape slavery and huntin’ dogs.

When I got back, Silas was still on the porch, puffin’ his pipe, but Aunt Sally was there now, sittin’ in a rocker, her hands busy with some knittin’. She looked up as I came up the steps.

“Well, there you are, Tom. Did you get that firewood, dear?” she asked, her voice soft and gentle.

“Yes ma’am,” I said, tryin’ to sound tired but accomplished. “Stack was a bit low, but I found some good dry pieces.” I was carryin’ a few small logs I’d picked up near the woodshed, just for show.

“That’s a good boy,” she said, smilin’. “Supper’s almost ready. Go on and wash up.”

I went inside, my heart still poundin’. I ate supper, tryin’ to act natural, listenin’ to Silas talk about the weather and the crops. He mentioned the runaway again, talkin’ about how important it was to catch them, for the good of society and the law and all that. My stomach turned over, but I just nodded and kept on eatin’.

That night, I couldn’t sleep a wink. I lay in bed, listenin’ to the crickets and the night sounds, thinkin’ about Jim out there, wanderin’ in the dark. I wondered if he was cold, if he was safe, if he’d found the Grangerford smokehouse. And I wondered about them dogs. Would Silas really send them out in the morning? He sounded like he meant it.

The next morning, I woke up before anyone else. I crept out of bed, dressed quiet-like, and then snuck out of the house. I had to do something to throw them off, if Silas was a-gwyne to send them dogs out.

I went straight for the river bank, to the spot where Jim had entered the water. I looked around carefully, makin’ sure there warn’t any tracks that might lead back to the island. Jim was smart, he’d gone right into the water. But what if a dog could still pick up a general scent of a person havin’ been there?

I got an idea. I pulled off my shoes and socks, and then I waded into the water myself, right where Jim had gone in. I walked a little ways downstream, careful not to step on anything too soft, then I came out and walked back upstream, just for a little bit, then back into the water, and out again. I tried to leave my own scent around the area, messin’ up any faint traces Jim might have left, makin’ it seem like maybe it was just me, a boy playin’ by the river.

Then I went into the woods, a little ways from the river, and walked around, makin’ a few circles, and then headin’ back towards the house. The idea was to leave a confusing trail of my own, so if the dogs picked up a scent, they’d get mixed up. Maybe they’d think it was just a boy wanderin’ around, not a runaway slave. It warn’t much, but it was all I could think of.

When I got back to the house, Aunt Sally was up, startin’ breakfast. “Well, you’re an early bird today, Tom,” she said, smilin’. “What were you up to, runnin’ around so early?”

“Just went for a little walk, ma’am,” I said, tryin’ to look innocent. “Felt good to get some fresh air.”

She just nodded, and went back to flippin’ pancakes. Silas came down a little later, lookin’ serious.

“Well, I reckon it’s time to send them boys out,” he said to Aunt Sally, chompin’ on a piece of bacon. “That runaway ain’t gonna catch himself. And that reward money ain’t gonna fetch itself, neither.”

My heart stopped. This was it.

“You want me to go with them, Mr. Phelps?” I asked, tryin’ to sound eager, like a boy who wanted to help.

Silas looked at me, considerin’. “Well, now, Tom, you ain’t much of a tracker. But maybe you could help keep an eye out. And it might be good for you to see how it’s done.”

“Yes sir! I’d like that!” I said, tryin’ to put a lot of enthusiasm into it. It was a dangerous game, but if I was with them, I might be able to steer them away from Jim’s real trail, or at least learn how good those dogs really were.

After breakfast, Silas went out to the barn and fetched two men, young fellers, strong and quick, and then he brought out the dogs. Oh, they were magnificent beasts, big hounds with long, floppy ears and eyes that looked like they knew everything. There were five of them, all lean and muscled, pantin’ and strainin’ on their leashes, eager to go.

Silas knelt down, rubbin’ one of the dogs behind the ears. “Alright, boys,” he said to the men. “You know what to do. Take ‘em down to the river bank, down near that sandbar, where we heard he might have been seen. And then let ‘em loose. They’ll pick up the scent.”

My stomach churned. This was it. Jim’s life depended on me.

“Mr. Phelps,” I spoke up, as casually as I could manage, “I was down by the river bank this mornin’, walkin’ around. I went right down near that sandbar. Maybe my scent will confuse them dogs? They might think it was me.”

Silas frowned. “Hmm, that’s a thought, Tom. Might make ‘em wander a bit. But these dogs, they’re smart. They know the difference between a white boy and a runaway nigger. Still, it ain’t ideal.” He looked at the men. “Alright, boys, you make sure to tell them dogs what they’re lookin’ for. Tell ‘em ‘runaway, runaway.’ They understand. And if they get confused, bring ‘em back to the house, and we’ll try again from a different spot. But start down by the sandbar.”

The men nodded, and then, with the dogs pullin’ hard, they set off. I watched them go, my heart in my mouth. I had messed up my own scent there, so maybe it would make the dogs hesitate. But what if it warn’t enough? What if they were so good that they could still tell Jim’s scent from mine?

I walked along behind them, pretending to be all excited. We got down to the river, and the men let the dogs off their leashes. The hounds sniffed around, their noses to the ground, tails waggin’ a little at first, then they started to whimper, and then one of them let out a low bay, a sound that sent chills down my spine. They’d picked up something.

They started to move, not fast at first, just slow, sniffin’ and workin’ the ground. They went right to the spot where Jim had entered the water, and where I had gone in too. They dipped their noses to the water, then back to the bank, back and forth, confused. They whined, and looked at the men, then back at the water. They didn’t know what to do.

“What’s wrong with ‘em?” one of the men asked, scratchin’ his head. “They ain’t never been this confused. They usually just light out.”

“It’s that boy’s scent, like he said,” the other man grumbled, glarin’ at me. “Messed ‘em up. Damn fool kid.”

I just tried to look sheepish, like I was sorry I’d caused trouble. But inside, I was shoutin’ for joy. It had worked! My little trick had worked!

The dogs milled around for a while, sniffin’ at the bank, then at the water, then back up into the woods a little, where I’d walked. They couldn’t make up their minds. They’d follow a scent for a bit, then lose it, then pick up another one, and it just led them in circles. It warn’t a clear trail, not like they were used to.

Finally, after about an hour of this, one of the men blew a whistle, and called the dogs back. “Ain’t no use,” he said, shakin’ his head. “They’re all mixed up. We need a fresh start. We’ll take ‘em back to the house and try somewhere else later.”

My relief was so great I almost stumbled. I had bought Jim some time. But it warn’t over yet. They were still a-gwyne to look. They were just confused for now.

I went back to the house with the men, tryin’ to act all downcast about the dogs not bein’ able to track. Silas was disappointed when they told him.

“Well, that’s a bother,” he said, puffin’ out a sigh. “That boy’s gamblin’ around the river bank, messin’ up the scent. We’ll have to try further downriver, maybe, or up in the woods. These dogs, they ain’t never failed me before. But a boy’s scent, it’s a tricky thing.” He looked at me, a little annoyed, but not truly mad.

“I’m sorry, Mr. Phelps,” I said, hangin’ my head a bit. “I didn’t mean to mess things up.”

“It’s alright, son,” he said, wavein’ his hand. “You didn’t know no better. We’ll just have to be more careful. We’ll rest the dogs for a bit, and then we’ll take ‘em out again this afternoon, or maybe first thing in the mornin’. We’ll find that runaway. They always do.”

My heart sunk a little. Not over yet. But I had a few hours, maybe a whole day. I needed to get to Jim, and we needed to make a new plan.

I spent the rest of the morning tryin’ to act normal, helpin’ Aunt Sally with chores, listenin’ to Silas talkin’ about the crops. My mind was racein’ the whole time. How was I a-gwyne to sneak off again without raisin’ suspicion?

Around noon, Aunt Sally sent me to the springhouse to fetch some butter. That was my chance. The springhouse was a little ways from the main house, down a path that led towards the woods. I took my time, lookin’ around, makin’ sure nobody was watchin’. When I was out of sight, I moved as fast as I could.

I didn’t go back to the river. I went straight into the woods, tryin’ to follow a path that would lead me towards the Grangerford place. It was a good piece, maybe three or four miles. I had to be careful, couldn’t get lost. The woods were thick, and the sun was high now, makin’ dappled light on the forest floor.

I walked and walked, careful to step light, tryin’ to leave as little sign as I could. I thought about Jim, waitin’ in that old smokehouse, wonderin’ if I’d ever come. I thought about what we’d do. We couldn’t stay at the Grangerford place for long, either. It was just a temporary hideout. We needed to get to the raft.

After what felt like forever, I started to see signs of the old plantation. The woods thinned out a bit, and there were some overgrown fields, where the cotton used to grow. Then I saw the crumbling brick chimney of the big house, stickin’ up through the trees. It looked sad and lonely, haunted.

I went around the main house, keepin’ to the edge of the woods, until I found the outbuildings. There was the old kitchen, half fallen in, and then the smokehouse, a small, sturdy brick building, with a heavy wooden door. It looked like it had seen better days, but the roof seemed mostly intact.

“Jim!” I whispered, my voice hoarse from the walk. “Jim, you in there?”

There was a muffled thump from inside, and then the door creaked open a crack. Jim’s face, dark and worried, peered out.

“Huck! You made it! Oh, I was so scared! I thought dey got you too!” he whispered, his voice shaky with relief.

I pushed the door open the rest of the way and slipped inside. It was dark and smelled of old smoke and damp earth. Jim was sittin’ on an old crate, wrapped in his blanket.

“I told you I’d come, Jim. Are you alright? Are you cold?”

“I’s alright, Huck. Just mighty scared. Dis place, it’s spooky. I been hearin’ noises, thinkin’ it’s ghosts.”

“It’s just the wind, Jim. Or maybe some critters. Nobody comes near here.” I sat down on the dirt floor beside him. “The dogs, they got confused this morning. My tracks messed ‘em up. But Silas, he ain’t a-gwyne to give up. He’s a-gwyne to send them out again, maybe this afternoon, maybe tomorrow.”

Jim groaned. “Oh, Lord. What we gwyne to do, Huck? We can’t stay here.”

“I know. We got to get to the raft. But how? It’s upriver, a good piece, and if we take the skiff, they’ll know for sure. And if we walk, them dogs might still pick up a trail.”

We sat in silence for a bit, thinkin’ hard. The sun cast a few shafts of light through cracks in the old brick, illuminatin’ dust motes dancin’ in the air.

“What if,” Jim said, slow and thoughtful, “what if we get de raft tonight, after dark? It be harder for dogs to track in de dark, ain’t it?”

“It would, Jim, but it’s a long way upstream to where we buried it. And we can’t take the skiff. That’d raise the alarm straight off.”

“No, no, not de skiff. We swim, Huck. We swim upriver to where de raft is. Dat way, dey ain’t no tracks on de land.”

My eyes widened. Swim upriver? That was a long, hard swim. The current was strong. But it was a good idea, a mighty good idea. No land tracks for the dogs to follow.

“That’s smart, Jim! That’s real smart!” I said, my voice full of excitement. “But it’s a powerful long swim, and mighty cold at night. Are you sure you can do it?”

“I can do it, Huck. I swam before. And it’s better dan gettin’ caught by them dogs. We go after de moon is down, when it’s darkest. And we swim quiet, close to de bank.”

“Alright then. That’s the plan. Tonight, after it’s good and dark, and the moon is down, we’ll swim upriver to where we buried the raft. We’ll get it dug up, and then we’ll float on down the river, quiet as ghosts. But first, I got to get back to the Phelps’ place, so they don’t suspect nothin’. I’ll bring you some more food if I can, and maybe an extra blanket.”

Jim looked at me, a flicker of hope in his eyes. “You a good feller, Huck. A real good feller. I owe you ever’thing.”

“Oh, shucks, Jim, don’t talk like that. We’re in this together. Now you stay hid, and don’t make a sound. I’ll be back as soon as I can.”

I slipped out of the smokehouse, makin’ sure the door was closed tight, and then I headed back through the woods. The sun was startin’ to dip again, and I had to hurry if I was a-gwyne to get back before supper.

When I got back to the Phelps’ place, Aunt Sally was ringin’ the supper bell. I made it just in time, walkin’ up to the house as if I’d just been wanderin’ around nearby. Nobody seemed to suspect nothin’.

That evening, the talk at supper was all about the runaway and the dogs. Silas was sayin’ how he’d try a new spot in the mornin’, maybe further down the river, or across in the fields. My heart was pounding, but I just kept my face calm, chewin’ on my food. I listened closely, makin’ sure they warn’t a-gwyne to do any searchin’ tonight.

After supper, I managed to slip a few pieces of cold cornbread and a chunk of bacon into my pockets when Aunt Sally warn’t lookin’. I also snagged an old, thin blanket from the shed, foldin’ it up small. I waited till everyone was asleep, then I crept out of the house.

The night was dark, just like Jim said it would be. The moon had set, and the stars were bright, but they didn’t give much light down in the thick woods. I moved slow and careful, usin’ all my woodsman skills, makin’ sure I didn’t step on any dry branches or rustle any leaves too loud.

It was a long walk back to the Grangerford place, and it felt even longer in the dark. I stumbled a few times, barkin’ my

legs, and once I nearly tumbled head over heels into a gully, but I caught myself just in time. The woods were full of creaky noises, and hoot owls, and sometimes a rustle in the leaves that made me hold my breath and listen, thinkin’ it might be a dog or a man, though I knew it warn’t likely, for they warn’t searching at night. Still, a body can’t help but be jumpy when he’s doin’ somethin’ he knows the law, and Aunt Sally, would say was wrong, even if his heart says it’s right. My conscience was a-gnawing at me somethin’ fierce, a-tellin’ me I was a-stealing Jim, and that warn’t right by what Miss Watson and the Widow Douglas had always taught me. But then I’d think of Jim, and how he looked when he was talkin’ about bein’ free, and how he trusted me, and then I’d reckon that all the preachin’ in the world warn’t as important as one good, free man. It was a mighty tangle, that feelin’, but I kept on through the dark.

After what seemed like a powerful long time, I finally seen the outline of the old smokehouse. I crept up to it, quiet as a mouse, and tapped on the door. “Jim?” I whispered, my voice hardly more than a breath.

The door opened a crack, and Jim’s face, dark against the darker inside, peered out. “Dat you, Huck?” he whispered back, his voice thick with relief.

“It’s me,” I said. “Got you somethin’ to eat, and a blanket.” I handed him the cornbread and bacon, and the thin old blanket. He took them with a grateful sigh, and then he was outside, closing the door softly behind him.

“Bless you, Huck, bless your heart,” he said, his voice low and shaky. “I bin a-thinkin’ and a-thinkin’ and ever’ minute I feared dey’d come back, or de dogs’d find me out.”

“Well, they didn’t, and they ain’t a-gwyne to now,” I said, trying to sound braver than I felt. “They’re all asleep back at the house, and Silas is a-gwyne to try farther downriver tomorrow, or in the fields. We got to move quick.”

“Yaas, we got to move,” Jim said, already pullin’ the blanket around his shoulders. “De river be cold, Huck.”

“I know it, but we ain’t got no choice,” I told him. “You ready?”

He nodded, his eyes wide and serious in the dark. “Ready as I ever be. Dis de night, Huck. Dis de night we break free, or we don’t.”

We started off for the riverbank, movin’ slow and careful, listenin’ to every sound. The night air was chill, and a thin mist was a-risin’ off the water. When we got to the bank, the river looked like a big, black, movin’ sheet, with only the stars a-sparklin’ on its surface. It looked powerful cold and deep.

“You shore you can do this, Jim?” I asked, a shiver runnin’ down my spine that warn’t just from the cold.

“I got to, Huck. Ain’t no choosin’,” he said. He took off his ragged coat and set it on the bank with his food, wrapped tight in the blanket. “We ain’t a-gwyne to take dis wid us. Too much weight. We wrap de raft in dat blanket and food, and when we get it up, we get a fire going and eat.”

“Good idea,” I said, and I shed my own outer shirt, though I kept my breeches on. The water was awful cold when we first stepped in, and it took my breath away. It felt like a thousand needles a-prickin’ my skin, but after a minute, it went numb. The current was strong, jest like I’d reckoned, and we had to fight it right from the start.

“Keep close to de bank, Huck,” Jim whispered, his voice strainin’ a little. “Dey don’t look so close to de bank. And de snags, dey worse in de middle.”

So we stuck to the shallows, feelin’ our way with our feet, and pushin’ off from the bottom sometimes when it warn’t too deep. It was hard goin’. Ever’ stroke was a struggle against the powerful river, and the water was so dark you couldn’t see your hand in front of your face. All you could do was swim by feel, and by the faint outline of the trees against the sky. Jim was a strong swimmer, a powerful man he was, and he kept a steady pace. I tried my best to keep up, but my arms and legs started to ache pretty quick, and my teeth began to chatter. I kept thinkin’ of the dogs, and Silas, and how they’d be out searchin’ at first light. That thought was like a lash across my back, a-drivin’ me on.

The cold was the worst part. It went right through you, down to your bones. I could feel my fingers and toes gettin’ stiff, and my breath was comin’ in ragged gasps. Ever’ now and then, I’d look over at Jim, and he’d be swimmin’ with his head low, just his eyes and forehead above the water, lookin’ like a big, dark log a-movin’ upstream. He warn’t makin’ a sound, just breathin’ hard. I knew he was a-sufferin’ too, but he warn’t a-complainin’. He had too much ridin’ on this.

We must have swum for a good hour or more, maybe even two, though it felt like a lifetime. My arms felt like lead, and I was sure I couldn’t lift them for another stroke. I wanted to give up, to just let the current take me, but then I’d think of Jim, and his hope, and I’d dig deep and find a little more strength. We passed places I recognized from when we’d been on the raft, old stumps and fallen trees that had been landmarks. I knew we were a-gwyne in the right direction.

Finally, Jim put his hand out and touched my arm. “Huck,” he whispered, his voice hoarse, “I think we mighty near it. Keep a-lookin’ for dat big sycamore, de one wid de crooked branch.”

My eyes ached from strainin’ in the dark, but I peered ahead, tryin’ to make out the shapes of the trees. The bank here was a little higher, a steeper climb. Then I seen it, a darker, bigger shape than the rest, with a branch that stuck out sideways like a broken arm. “Thar it is, Jim!” I hissed, a fresh burst of energy runnin’ through me. “The sycamore!”

We pushed hard for the bank, clawin’ our way up the slick mud. When I finally dragged myself onto solid ground, I just laid there for a minute, pantin’, my whole body shiverin’ and achin’. Jim was next to me, just as worn out, but his eyes were bright with triumph.

“We made it, Huck, we made it!” he breathed, and he even managed a weak grin.

“Now for the hard part,” I said, pushin’ myself up to a sit. “Remember where we buried it?”

“Yaas, I remember,” Jim said, pointin’ a shaky finger further up the bank, where a patch of willow trees grew thick. “It be near dat clump, under de roots of dat big willow, whar de ground warn’t so soft.”

We started crawlin’ on our hands and knees through the undergrowth, feelin’ around for the spot. The ground was cold and damp. We scraped away the leaves and loose dirt with our bare hands, and pretty soon, my fingers hit something hard and smooth.

“Here it is, Jim!” I whispered excitedly. “I think I got it!”

We started diggin’ in earnest then, clawin’ and scratchin’ at the earth with our hands, tryin’ to be as quiet as we could. The ground warn’t too hard, thank goodness, but it was slow work. Ever’ little rustle of leaves or snap of a twig sounded mighty loud in the still night. We listened hard for any sounds of dogs or people, but all we heard was the gentle lappin’ of the river and the hum of night insects.

After a long spell of diggin’, our fingers were sore and our arms were tired, but we started to uncover the side of the raft. It was wrapped tight in a canvas tarp, jest like we’d left it. “We got it, Jim, we got it!” I said, my voice full of pride.

“Easy, Huck, easy now,” Jim warned, though his own voice was tremblin’ with excitement. “We don’t want to rip de canvas. We need dat.”

It took us another good while to fully uncover the raft. It was a powerful big thing to move, even buried in the soft earth. We had to work together, grunting and strainin’, to pry it free from the suction of the mud. We rolled it onto its side, and then, with a mighty heave, we managed to slide it down the bank and into the water. It floated like a dream, silent and steady, a big, dark shape on the black river.

“Now, we gotta get de paddle, and de rest of de stuff,” Jim said. We had buried a paddle and a few other things a little ways off, near an old stump. We fetched them quick, our hearts poundin’ with relief and the thrill of escape. We laid the paddle on the raft, and then carefully unwrapped the blanket with the food.

“You wait here, Huck,” Jim said. “I’m a-gwyne to get us a little fire started. We need to warm up before we get too far downriver.”

He was right. We were both shiverin’ something fierce. Jim was a good hand at makin’ a fire, even with wet hands and cold wood. He found some dry tinder under a fallen log, and with a few strikes of his flint and steel, he had a tiny blaze cracklin’. We huddled close to it, holdin’ our hands out to the warmth, the smoke stingin’ our eyes a little. The cornbread and bacon tasted like the best meal I’d ever had, even cold, and the warmth of the fire started to seep into our chilled bones.

“Feels good, don’t it, Jim?” I asked, munchin’ on my bacon.

“Oh, Lord, it feel like heaven, Huck,” he said, his face eased for the first time that night. “And dis raft, dis raft feel like de chariot of de Lord, takin’ us away from all dat trouble.”

After we’d warmed up a bit and finished our meager meal, we doused the fire carefully, makin’ sure not a single spark was left. We pushed the raft out into the current, Jim takin’ the paddle. He held it steady, makin’ sure we drifted quiet and slow, close to the bank where the shadow was thickest.

“We free now, Huck,” Jim whispered, his voice full of wonder. “We really free.”

I laid back on the raft, lookin’ up at the stars. The river felt different now. It warn’t a prison no more, it was our road, our escape. The fear was still there, a little bit, tucked away in the back of my mind, but it was mixin’ with a powerful sense of relief and a kind of wild, joyful hope. We were on the run, yes, but we were on the run *together*, and for a good cause.

“Where we a-gwyne to head, Jim?” I asked, my voice soft.

“Downriver, Huck, always downriver,” he said, his paddle makin’ a soft dip in the water. “Down to de free states. We keep our eyes peeled, and we keep low. And we don’t stop for nothin’ till we hear de sound of freedom a-ringin’ in our ears.”

We floated along, the current carryin’ us steadily downstream. The silence of the river was a comfort, broken only by the chirpin’ of crickets and the occasional splash of a fish. Jim kept a careful watch, his eyes darting from side to side, ever’ now and then leanin’ over to listen. I laid there, feelin’ the gentle sway of the raft, the cold river water a-slappin’ softly against the logs beneath me. It was a peaceful sound, a sound of freedom.

I reckoned we must have floated for several hours before the first hint of dawn began to lighten the eastern sky. The trees on the banks started to take on shape, and the black river turned to a dark gray. Jim steered the raft towards a small, heavily wooded island in the middle of the river, a place where the trees grew thick down to the water’s edge.

“We hide out here for de day, Huck,” he said. “Too dangerous to be out in de open when de sun up. We rest, and we plan, and we keep a-movin’ at night.”

We pushed the raft into a thicket of willows, where it was completely hidden from the river. We covered it with some branches and leaves, makin’ sure it looked like jest another part of the natural growth. Then we settled down amongst the trees, away from the river’s edge, where we could keep watch and feel safe.

“You sleep, Huck,” Jim said, his voice softer now. “I watch. You done good, boy. Real good.”

I was powerful tired, and his words were a comfort. I curled up on the hard ground, pullin’ the thin blanket around me. The thought of the Phelps’ place, and Silas’s search, and the dogs, all seemed far away now, like a bad dream. We were on the river, and the river was our friend, carryin’ us away from all that trouble. I closed my eyes, and pretty soon, I was fast asleep, the sound of the river still a-singin’ in my ears.

When I woke up, the sun was high, filterin’ through the leaves in dappled patterns. Jim was sittin’ a little ways off, watchin’ the river with a careful eye. He turned when he heard me stir, and gave a small, quiet smile.

“Mornin’, Huck,” he said. “You sleep good?”

“Like a log,” I said, stretchin’ my stiff muscles. “Any sign of trouble?”

He shook his head. “Naw, nothin’ yet. Jest de river, and de birds. Dis a good spot. Nobody gwyne to find us here.”

We spent the day on the island, talkin’ in low voices, makin’ plans. Jim told me about his wife and children again, and how he dreamed of buyin’ them out of slavery, takin’ them up North to be free. He spoke of the North like it was a promised land, a place where a black man could walk tall and nobody would own him. It made me feel a little funny inside, hearin’ him talk like that, for it warn’t what I’d been taught. But then I looked at his face, so full of hope and determination, and I reckoned that if anybody deserved to be free, it was Jim.

We ate the last of the cornbread and bacon for our midday meal, savin’ a little for the night. The thought of bein’ hungry didn’t bother me none, not when we were free. We watched the steamboats and flatboats go by on the river, keepin’ well hidden. It was strange to see them, so close and yet so far away, a whole other world a-movin’ along, with people who had no idea we were there, hidin’ in the bushes, tryin’ to escape.

As the sun began to sink in the west, paintin’ the sky with streaks of orange and purple, a new kind of quiet fell over the island. It warn’t the worried quiet of the night before, but a peaceful quiet, full of the promise of another journey. Jim checked the raft, makin’ sure the fastenings were secure and the paddle was ready.

“We leave soon as it dark,” he said. “We got a long way to go, Huck. But we gwyne to make it. I feel it in my bones.”

I believed him. Out here on the river, with Jim, away from the lies and the fear, anything seemed possible. We were jest two souls on a raft, driftin’ down a big, wide river, with the whole world spread out before us. And for the first time in a long while, I felt truly happy. It was a powerful good feelin’, that freedom, even if it warn’t legal. I reckoned I’d take that feelin’ over any amount of properness any day. And so, as the last light faded from the sky, we pushed off from the island, back onto the dark, movin’ water, and our journey downriver began anew. The stars came out, one by one, a-sparklin’ like diamonds in the black velvet sky, and the river carried us on, a-whisperin’ its secrets to the night. It was a grand, lonesome, free kind of a feelin’, and I wouldn’t have traded it for all the gold in creation. We had no map, no compass, only the river and our hope, but that was enough. The door opened a crack, and Jim’s face, dark against the darker inside, peered out. “Dat you, Huck?” he whispered back, his voice thick with relief.

“It’s me,” I said. “Got you somethin’ to eat, and a blanket.” I handed him the cornbread and bacon, and the thin old blanket. He took them with a grateful sigh, and then he was outside, closing the door softly behind him.

“Yaas, we got to move,” Jim said, already pullin’ the blanket around his shoulders. “De river be cold, Huck.”

“I know it, but we ain’t got no choice,” I told him. “You ready?”

He nodded, his eyes wide and serious in the dark. “Ready as I ever be. Dis de night, Huck. Dis de night we break free, or we don’t.”

We started off for the riverbank, movin’ slow and careful, listenin’ to every sound. The night air was chill, and a thin mist was a-risin’ off the water. When we got to the bank, the river looked like a big, black, movin’ sheet, with only the stars a-sparklin’ on its surface. It looked powerful cold and deep.

“You shore you can do this, Jim?” I asked, a shiver runnin’ down my spine that warn’t just from the cold.

“I got to, Huck. Ain’t no choosin’,” he said. He took off his ragged coat and set it on the bank with his food, wrapped tight in the blanket. “We ain’t a-gwyne to take dis wid us. Too much weight. We wrap de raft in dat blanket and food, and when we get it up, we get a fire going and eat.”

“Good idea,” I said, and I shed my own outer shirt, though I kept my breeches on. The water was awful cold when we first stepped in, and it took my breath away. It felt like a thousand needles a-prickin’ my skin, but after a minute, it went numb. The current was strong, jest like I’d reckoned, and we had to fight it right from the start.

“Keep close to de bank, Huck,” Jim whispered, his voice strainin’ a little. “Dey don’t look so close to de bank. And de snags, dey worse in de middle.”

So we stuck to the shallows, feelin’ our way with our feet, and pushin’ off from the bottom sometimes when it warn’t too deep. It was hard goin’. Ever’ stroke was a struggle against the powerful river, and the water was so dark you couldn’t see your hand in front of your face. All you could do was swim by feel, and by the faint outline of the trees against the sky. Jim was a strong swimmer, a powerful man he was, and he kept a steady pace. I tried my best to keep up, but my arms and legs started to ache pretty quick, and my teeth began to chatter. I kept thinkin’ of the dogs, and Silas, and how they’d be out searchin’ at first light. That thought was like a lash across my back, a-drivin’ me on.

The cold was the worst part. It went right through you, down to your bones. I could feel my fingers and toes gettin’ stiff, and my breath was comin’ in ragged gasps. Ever’ now and then, I’d look over at Jim, and he’d be swimmin’ with his head low, just his eyes and forehead above the water, lookin’ like a big, dark log a-movin’ upstream. We must have swum for a good hour or more, maybe even two, though it felt like a lifetime. My arms felt like lead, and I was sure I couldn’t lift them for another stroke. I wanted to give up, to just let the current take me, but then I’d think of Jim, and his hope, and I’d dig deep and find a little more strength. We passed places I recognized from when we’d been on the raft, old stumps and fallen trees that had been landmarks. I knew we were a-gwyne in the right direction.

Finally, Jim put his hand out and touched my arm. “Huck,” he whispered, his voice hoarse, “I think we mighty near it. Keep a-lookin’ for dat big sycamore, de one wid de crooked branch.”

My eyes ached from strainin’ in the dark, but I peered ahead, tryin’ to make out the shapes of the trees. The bank here was a little higher, a steeper climb. Then I seen it, a darker, bigger shape than the rest, with a branch that stuck out sideways like a broken arm. “Thar it is, Jim!” I hissed, a fresh burst of energy runnin’ through me. “The sycamore!”

We pushed hard for the bank, clawin’ our way up the slick mud. When I finally dragged myself onto solid ground, I just laid there for a minute, pantin’, my whole body shiverin’ and achin’. Jim was next to me, just as worn out, but his eyes were bright with triumph.

“We made it, Huck, we made it!” he breathed, and he even managed a weak grin.

“Yaas, I remember,” Jim said, pointin’ a shaky finger further up the bank, where a patch of willow trees grew thick. “It be near dat clump, under de roots of dat big willow, whar de ground warn’t so soft.”

We started crawlin’ on our hands and knees through the undergrowth, feelin’ around for the spot. The ground was cold and damp. We scraped away the leaves and loose dirt with our bare hands, and pretty soon, my fingers hit something hard and smooth.

“Here it is, Jim!” I whispered excitedly. “I think I got it!”

We started diggin’ in earnest then, clawin’ and scratchin’ at the earth with our hands, tryin’ to be as quiet as we could. The ground warn’t too hard, thank goodness, but it was slow work. Ever’ little rustle of leaves or snap of a twig sounded mighty loud in the still night. We listened hard for any sounds of dogs or people, but all we heard was the gentle lappin’ of the river and the hum of night insects.

After a long spell of diggin’, our fingers were sore and our arms were tired, but we started to uncover the side of the raft. It was wrapped tight in a canvas tarp, jest like we’d left it. “We got it, Jim, we got it!” I said, my voice full of pride.

“Easy, Huck, easy now,” Jim warned, though his own voice was tremblin’ with excitement. “We don’t want to rip de canvas. We need dat.”

It took us another good while to fully uncover the raft. It was a powerful big thing to move, even buried in the soft earth. We had to work together, grunting and strainin’, to pry it free from the suction of the mud. We rolled it onto its side, and then, with a mighty heave, we managed to slide it down the bank and into the water. It floated like a dream, silent and steady, a big, dark shape on the black river.

“Now, we gotta get de paddle, and de rest of de stuff,” Jim said. We had buried a paddle and a few other things a little ways off, near an old stump. We fetched them quick, our hearts poundin’ with relief and the thrill of escape. We laid the paddle on the raft, and then carefully unwrapped the blanket with the food.

“You wait here, Huck,” Jim said. “I’m a-gwyne to get us a little fire started. We need to warm up before we get too far downriver.”

He was right. We were both shiverin’ something fierce. Jim was a good hand at makin’ a fire, even with wet hands and cold wood. He found some dry tinder under a fallen log, and with a few strikes of his flint and steel, he had a tiny blaze cracklin’.
