The sun beat down hard on the dry plain that day, making the dust rise up in clouds every time a wagon came lumbering in from the country, and folks was all sweating and fanning themselves with their hats and anything else that came to hand. The little town of Mud Creek warn’t much to look at, just a scatter of wood buildings along the crick bank, but on market day it fair bustled, and you could hear the noise and the hawkin’ of goods a mile off. I was just loafing by the general store, watching the folk and the horses, when a big, gaudy wagon, painted up with all sorts of bright colors and pictures of smiling women, pulled up in the square. A skinny feller with a long, greased-down mustache and a red vest jumped down, all smiles and bluster, and he was Silas Grindle, the medicine man, come back to town after a long spell.

He started right in, setting up his little stand with bottles of his “Elixir of Life” and a whole heap of other cures for what ailed you, and he had a spiel that would charm a snake right out of its skin. He talked fast, and he waved his hands, and he told stories about all the folks he’d cured, and pretty soon a crowd gathered around him, mostly just gawkers, but some was looking mighty interested in his bottles. I seen a widow woman with a bad cough come up, and Silas told her his elixir would clear her lungs cleaner than a spring rain, and he poured her a free sample, though he measured it mighty careful.

He went on for a good while, selling his bottles and telling his tales, and he was getting a fair pile of coins on his table, but then he started to get a mean glint in his eye, and he squinted over at Jedediah Finch’s hardware store, which stood right across the square. Jedediah was a quiet feller, owned most of the town, and he warn’t one for show or for loud talk, just sat behind his counter looking at his books, and he always wore a clean, starched shirt, even on the hottest days. Silas Grindle hitched up his pants and sang out, loud enough for the whole town to hear, “Well now, looky there, folks! If it ain’t Jedediah Finch, the honest merchant! Least, that’s what folks call him, but I reckon a heap of us knows better, don’t we, boys?”

The crowd got real quiet, and some folks shifted their feet, and others just looked at each other with their eyes big. Jedediah Finch didn’t look up from his books, not at first, but Silas warn’t about to let it drop. “Yessir, Jedediah Finch, who sold old man Potter that leaky roof tin, and charged him double for it! And who bought up Widow Perkins’ land for pennies on the dollar when her husband passed, claiming it was for the good of the community, and then turned right around and sold it to the railway for a fat profit!”

A few uneasy chuckles went through the crowd, and some of the loafers looked like they was ready for a show. Silas got bolder, and he pointed a skinny finger at the hardware store. “Come on out, Jedediah! You ain’t got the guts, have you? You just hide in your fancy store and count your stolen money, don’t you? You ain’t nothing but a yellow-bellied cheat, and I’m a-gwyne to tell the whole world!”

Still Jedediah didn’t move, but then his store door opened slow, and there he stood, tall and thin, with his clean shirt and his spectacles on his nose. He didn’t say nothing, just looked at Silas with a gaze as steady as a stone. Silas Grindle puffed up, pleased with himself that he’d roused the man. “Ah, there he is, folks! The great Jedediah Finch, finally come out to face the music! What’s it gwyne to be, Jedediah? A confession? A plea for mercy?”

Jedediah Finch took off his spectacles and folded them slow, and then he put them in his breast pocket. His voice was low, but it carried across the square clear as a bell. “Silas Grindle, I told you last year, and I tell you again now. You got till the sun is past that church steeple, which ain’t but ten minutes from now, to pack up your wagon and be gone from my town. If I hear your voice or see your face here after that, you’ll regret it, and that’s a promise you can take to your grave.” Then he turned around and went back into his store, quiet as he come out.

The crowd stood silent for a minute, and Silas Grindle’s big smile had faded some. He tried to laugh it off, but it sounded thin. “Oh, listen to the big man talk! He thinks he owns the whole blamed world, don’t he? Well, he ain’t gwyne to scare Silas Grindle! I got as much right to be here as any man, and I ain’t leaving till I done sold all my bottles, so there!” He pounded his fist on his table, but his eyes kept darting to the church steeple, and you could see the sun was moving.

He started up his spiel again, but his heart warn’t in it, and the crowd warn’t listening like before. He was still trying to hawk a bottle to a fella when the sun’s shadow finally crept past the top of the church steeple, and just then, Jedediah Finch stepped out of his store again. He had a shotgun in his hand, and he didn’t say a word. He just stood there, holding the gun level, not pointing it at Silas, but just holding it, and his face was calm as a summer lake.

Silas Grindle saw the gun, and his face went white, and he fair scrambled. He knocked over his bottles, and they smashed on the dry ground, and he jumped in his wagon, grabbed the reins, and whipped his old horse so hard it near flew. The wagon lurched, and Silas Grindle went a-rattling out of town in a cloud of dust, not looking back, not once. Jedediah Finch just stood there till the wagon was out of sight, then he lowered his shotgun, went back into his store, and closed the door. Nobody said a word, and the silence in the square was thick enough to cut with a knife. The crowd broke up then, and folks went about their business, but everybody walked a little softer, and nobody looked at Jedediah Finch’s store for a while. It warn’t much of a show, not with all that talk, but it was enough to make you think, and I reckon Silas Grindle didn’t come back to Mud Creek for a good long time.

The mob was like a river, I tell you, a-swelling and a-roaring, and every fella in it felt bigger and braver than he ever had by himself. They went a-tearing down the street, bumping into each other, and shouting all sorts of things, most of it cuss words and threats against Colonel Sherburn. They was like a pack of dogs that had finally got the scent and warn’t gwyne to let go till they had their quarry, and the way they was a-going you’d a-thought they was all ready to jump through a brick wall if the Colonel was behind it.

I reckon I was a young fella then, and I ain’t never seen nothing like it before, not with that many people all bent on the same thing, and that thing being something as awful as a lynching. My stomach was all twisted up, and I felt a cold knot in my gut, but I couldn’t help but follow along, drawn like a moth to a flame, even though I knew it warn’t right. There was old men with their whiskers a-flying, and young bucks with their fists clenched, and women too, some of them yelling just as loud as the men, egging them on. It was a sight, let me tell you, one that sticks with you long after the dust settles.

They headed straight for Colonel Sherburn’s store, which warn’t much of a store at all, really, just a kind of general goods place with a residence above it,

They was a-swarming, a-stomping, and a-yelling, and every fella in that mob had a face twisted up with something that looked like courage but was mostly fear and meanness boiled together. They had picked up sticks and stones, and some had old guns they didn’t rightly know how to shoot, but they was a-waving them about like they was seasoned soldiers. The air was thick with their dust and their noise, and it felt like the whole town was shaking under their feet. They come to a halt in front of Colonel Sherburn’s place, which warn’t much more than a plain wooden building, a bit weathered, with a store downstairs and living rooms above, and a porch running across the front. The windows was dark, and the door was shut tight, and for a minute, with all their noise, it seemed like the place was empty, like the Colonel had just packed up and gone. But that warn’t his way, not from what I’d seen.

The noise kept on, growing louder if anything, as more folks piled up from behind, all pushing and craning their necks, wanting to be part of something big. They was yelling for Sherburn to come out, to show himself, calling him names that ain’t fit for print, and threatening him with all manner of bad ends. Some of them started throwing little pebbles at the windows, but they was too scared to break anything serious, not yet anyway. They just wanted to make noise and feel important, and they was feeding off each

other, like hogs at a trough. The noise was something fierce, a real din, and it kept on building, with fellows pushing and shoving and some even starting to climb the porch posts, though none of them got very high. They was hollering for him to come out, calling him every name under the sun, and the womenfolk was shriller than the men, pointing their fingers and shrieking like banshees. It felt like the very air was a-humming with all that hate and excitement, and I felt my own heart thumping hard against my ribs. I was back a ways, tucked in behind some taller men, but I could see well enough, and I was holding my breath, waiting for something to happen. It seemed like it could go on forever, that yelling and cussing, but then all of a sudden, like a storm cloud busting open, the noise just died down a little. Not all at once, mind you, but it started with a few fella’s at the front, and then it spread back through the crowd like a ripple in a pond, till it was mostly just a low grumble. And then, there he was.

The door to the store, that had been shut so tight, it opened slow and quiet, and Colonel Sherburn stepped out onto the porch. He didn’t hurry, didn’t seem to be in any kind of a sweat. Just stepped out, cool as you please, and closed the door behind him with a little click that you could hear even over the last few murmurs of the crowd. He was dressed in his good clothes, the ones he wore for town, and he had his hat on, pulled down just a little over his eyes, so you couldn’t quite make out his expression. He stood there for a moment, on the top step, and just looked out at the whole pack of them, his hands tucked easy into his pockets. He didn’t say a word, not a single one, but his eyes, when he finally lifted his head just a fraction, they seemed to bore right through every fella in that crowd, and I could feel them from where I was.

The whole place got quiet then, mighty quiet. You could’ve heard a pin drop, if there had been any pins to drop, and every man and woman in that crowd, they was suddenly still. All that bluster and bravado just seemed to drain right out of them, like water out of a leaky bucket. They was all looking at him, and he was looking back at them, and it felt like a long, long minute that passed like that, with nobody moving and nobody saying a thing. He was a tall man, Colonel Sherburn was, and he carried himself like a king, even just standing there on his own porch. He didn’t seem to have a weapon in his hand, not that I could see anyway, and that made it all the more strange. They had come to lynch him, to tear him limb from limb, and here he was, standing there as calm as if he was just waiting for the postman to come by with the mail.

Then he slowly took his hands out of his pockets. In his right hand, he held a double-barreled shotgun, one of them fine ones, with polished wood and dark steel. He didn’t raise it, didn’t point it at nobody, just held it easy, letting it rest against his hip. And then, he spoke. His voice was quiet, too, not a shout, not a bellow, but it carried, oh, it carried, and every single person in that street heard every single word, clear as a bell. It was a voice that commanded respect, and it was full of scorn.

“What’s the matter with you people?” he says, and his voice was slow and drawn out, like he was talking to a pack of dull-witted children. “Ain’t you got no sense of shame? Coming here, a whole mob of you, a-howling and a-cursing and a-threatening, all because one man stood up to another. You think you’re brave, don’t you? You think you’re doing something mighty and righteous, a-ganging up like this. But I tell you, you ain’t nothing but a pack of curs. A single dog, he’ll tuck his tail and run, but put a hundred of ‘em together, and they’ll bark and snarl at a lion. That’s what you are. A hundred curs, a-snapping at a man who ain’t even interested in biting back, not unless he has to.”

He paused then, and looked across their faces, and I could see the sweat on some of the men’s brows, even though the sun warn’t that hot anymore. Nobody dared to speak, nobody dared to even shift their feet. It was like they was all frozen, caught in his gaze, and I could feel the power of it myself, even as an outsider.

“Oh, you’re brave enough when there’s a whole lot of you, ain’t you?” he went on, and his voice got a little sharper, like a whip cracking. “You’ll shout and stomp and throw your pebbles. You’ll hang a man when he ain’t got no chance, when he’s alone and friendless. That’s your kind of courage, ain’t it? To take a man by surprise, ten to one, or a hundred to one, and then claim you’re doing justice. I tell you, there ain’t a man among you that’s got the guts to face me alone. Not a single one.”

He lifted the shotgun then, just a little, and let the barrels gleam in the afternoon sun, but he still warn’t pointing it at nobody particular. It was just a show of what he had, a reminder, and it was enough. “You come here to lynch me, do you?” he says, and there was a sneer in his voice now, plain as day. “You want to take the law into your own hands. Well, I tell you, the law’s right here, in my hands, and you ain’t strong enough to take it from me. Not as a mob, and certainly not as individuals. Because you ain’t individuals. You’re just a crowd. A lot of scared sheep, following the loudest bleat, without a thought in your heads.”

He took a step forward, right to the edge of the porch, and I saw a few fellas in the front ranks flinch and take a half-step back, though they tried to hide it. “Look at you,” he says, and he was almost whispering now, but it was a whisper that carried more menace than any shout. “Every one of you, deep down, knows you’re a coward. You know you wouldn’t dare to lay a hand on me if I met you in a dark alley, just you and me. But here, with your friends and neighbors all around, all puffed up with each other’s false courage, you think you can do anything. You think you can murder a man and call it justice.”

“I’m an honest man,” he says, and he drew himself up taller, “and I did what any man would do when a drunken fool threatens his life. He was warned, wasn’t he? He was told to stop, wasn’t he? And he kept on, didn’t he? So I put him down. And I’d do it again, to any man who threatened me. And I’ll do it to any one of you who steps foot on this porch with murder in your heart. You want to see courage? You want to see what a man does when he’s pushed? Then watch me. Because I ain’t afraid of a thousand of you, not when you’re just a pack of dogs that’ll scatter the moment a single one of you gets hurt.”

He raised the shotgun then, slow and steady, and pointed it just a little above their heads. And then he barked, “Now, get out of here! Go home! Go back to your dirty little lives and remember that you were too scared to do what you came to do! Go on, run!”

And that was it. It was like somebody had popped a balloon. All that air went right out of the crowd. The fella’s in the front, the ones who had flinched before, they just turned around, slow at first, and then faster, and started to melt back into the crowd. And the ones behind them, they didn’t want to be at the front, not anymore, and they started to push and shove their way back too. It wasn’t a wild stampede, not exactly, but it was a quick, sudden unraveling. The shouts and threats didn’t come back. Instead, there was a kind of murmuring, a shuffling of feet, and then the sounds of wagons starting up, and horses being turned around.

I watched it all, my mouth a little open, hardly believing my own eyes. All that fury, all that bloodlust, just melted away by a man’s words and his cold, steady gaze. It was a lesson, let me tell you, about what kind of stuff people are made of, and how easy it is for a crowd to lose its nerve when it’s faced with real courage. They was slinking away now, not even looking back, and the ones who had been the loudest before, they were now trying to make themselves small, trying to disappear into the general shuffling and going.

Colonel Sherburn just stood there on his porch, watching them go, the shotgun still held easy in his hand, and he didn’t say another word. He just watched, his face as cold and hard as stone, till the last of them was turning the corner down the street, and the dust they had kicked up was starting to settle back down. Then, with a little sigh that I couldn’t have heard but I imagined it, he turned around, opened his door, and stepped back inside, closing it softly behind him. I had seen a lot of things since I left home, and I had seen men do brave things and foolish things, but I never seen anything quite like that. A single man, standing against a whole town, and making them turn tail and run, just with words and a shotgun. It made you think about what courage really was, and what a crowd really was, and how one could be so strong and the other so weak, all at the same time.

I started walking, slow and easy, down the street, my hands in my pockets. The sun was still high, beating down on the dust and the clapboard houses, making everything look bleached out. I passed the general store where Boggs had been yelling, and then the drug store where they laid him out with the Bible on his chest. It looked all shut up now, the windows dark. It was a strange feeling, walking through a place that had been so packed with people and noise just minutes before, and now it was like a ghost town. All that fuss, all that wanting to do murder, and then it just blew away like smoke. I reckoned folks had gone home to chew on what had happened, or maybe to cuss out Sherburn in their own houses, where he couldn’t hear them. It seemed to me that a town that could get itself all worked up into a lynching frenzy, and then get scared off by one man, warn’t a very reliable place to be. It put a thought in my head that maybe I ought to get out of there, too, before the next thing happened. Because something always happens in a town like that, where folks got too much time on their hands and too little sense in their heads.

I stopped by the riverbank, where the ground sloped down to the water. The river just kept on rolling, wide and muddy, not caring one bit about Boggs or Sherburn or any of the doings on the land. It made me feel small, and the troubles of the town seemed to shrink a bit, too, when you looked at that big old river just going about its business. I sat down on a log, watching a steamboat paddle along far off in the distance, its smoke trailing behind it like a feather. It was a peaceful kind of sight, after all that ruckus. I started thinking about Boggs, lying there dead in the drug store. He warn’t much good, I reckoned, always drunk and threatening folks, but he never hurt nobody, as that man had said. And now he was dead, and his daughter was screaming, and it all seemed like a waste. Sherburn, he was a cold one, all right. He warn’t going to back down for nobody, and he showed that crowd who was boss. But I didn’t know if that made him a good man, or just a hard one. I didn’t know much about what made a man good or bad anyway; it seemed to me like most folks were a mix of both, just depending on the day and what they had for breakfast.

After a spell, I got up and stretched. My stomach was starting to rumble, and I figured I ought to find something to eat. I walked back towards the main street, which was starting to get a little life back into it, but it warn’t the same kind of life as before. Folks were moving about, but they seemed quieter, like they were walking on eggshells. The wagons that had come in from the country were mostly gone, and the streets warn’t nearly as crowded as they had been at noon. I could see a few men standing in little knots, talking low and glancing towards Sherburn’s house. I sidled up close to one of these groups, trying to look like I was just admiring a puddle, but I was listening with all my might.

“Well, I tell you,” one fella was saying, his voice a gravelly whisper, “he had it coming, old Boggs did. Always jawing and threatening. Sooner or later, someone was bound to shut him up.”

Another man, taller and with a long, drooping mustache, shook his head. “Maybe so, Jim, maybe so. But to shoot him down in cold blood like that, after he was already turned to go, and with his daughter coming, too. That warn’t right. That ain’t how men settle things.”

“Settle things?” the first fella scoffed, and spit a stream of tobacco juice into the dust. “What was there to settle? Boggs was a nuisance. Sherburn gave him fair warning. More warning than most men would give. And when a man gives warning, and the other fella don’t heed it, well, then

Some were saying he ought to be strung up, right quick. Others were saying that Boggs warn’t no saint, and Sherburn had a right to protect himself and his good name. But even them ones looked a little uneasy, like they knew deep down that this whole business was fixing to boil over.

Pretty soon, the knot of men started to unravel and stretch out into a line, and the line started to move, slow at first, then picking up a kind of shuffle. More and more men came out of the stores and the saloons, drawn by the low murmur of voices and the way the crowd was heading. It was like a big river, collecting little streams as it went, all flowing in one direction. And that direction was towards Colonel Sherburn’s house.

I reckon there was about two or three hundred men by the time they reached the end of the street where Sherburn lived. A few boys, like me, had snuck along, curious and a little bit scared. We kept to the edges, ready to bolt if things got too hot.

They had come to his door with all their bluster and their whisky courage, and he had stripped it all away with a few words and the sight of that rifle.

I tell you, it was a sight to see. All those men, big and brawny some of them, standing there like whipped dogs, not daring to meet his eye. I recognized that voice. My blood ran cold. It was the tall, skinny man from the island, the one with the long beard. And then, I saw the other man, the short, round one, standin’ in the doorway of the cabin, lookin’ around like he was guardin’ something.

Jim nudged me. “Huck, dem’s de same fellers from de island. De ones we took de bacon from.”

“I know,” I whispered, my heart sinkin’. This was bad. Really bad. We were right in the middle of it now.

We watched through a gap in the bushes. Inside the cabin, the tall man had the woman by the arm. She was a little thing, with long, dark hair all messed up. She was tryin’ to pull away from him, but he was too strong. He shoved her onto a wooden chair, and then he started to rummage through a chest in the corner of the cabin, throwin’ clothes and trinkets onto the floor.

“What they doin’, Jim?” I asked, my voice shaky.

“Sound like dey tryin’ to take her place, Huck,” he whispered back. “Maybe dey squatter, or worse. Maybe dey tryin’ to rob her.”

The tall man pulled out a small cloth bag from the chest. He untied it and looked inside. His eyes lit up. “Well, well, what have we here? Looks like somebody’s got a little stash of somethin’ precious.” He poured out a handful of old, tarnished silver coins onto the table.

The woman gasped. “No! Those are my mother’s! You can’t take those!” She jumped up and tried to grab them, but the tall man pushed her back onto the chair, hard.

“Stay put, little bird,” he growled. “These here coins are mine now. Payment for all your troubles, you might say.”

The woman started to sob, her face buried in her hands. The short man came in from the doorway, lookin’ at the coins.

“Looks like we struck it rich, Hank,” he said, grin’in’.

“Ain’t rich, Jed,” the tall one, Hank, said. “But it’s a good start. This little cabin ain’t worth much, but this here silver will buy us a new flatboat. And then we can really get down to business.”

They continued to ransack the cabin, throwin’ things about, and the woman just sat there cryin’. My blood was boilin’. It warn’t right. It just warn’t right. These men were clearly bad ones, takin’ advantage of a helpless woman.

“We gotta do somethin’, Jim,” I whispered, my voice firm.

Jim shook his head frantically. “Huck, we can’t! Dey got guns, I bet. An’ dere’s two of dem. We ain’t got no chance.”

“But what about her?” I hissed. “They’re gonna take everything she’s got. Maybe even hurt her.”

I thought hard and fast. We couldn’t fight them directly. But maybe we could scare them off, or trick them. My mind went back to the Sherburn business. Those town folks, they were cowards till Sherburn shamed them. These fellers, they were just plain mean.

“I got an idea, Jim,” I whispered. “But you gotta do exactly what I tell you.”

Jim looked at me like I was crazy, but he was listenin’.

“You make a big noise on the other side of the cabin, like a whole bunch of people comin’,” I said. “Holler loud, like you’re a sheriff or somethin’. Make it sound like a crowd.”

Jim’s eyes got even wider. “You crazy, Huck. Dey gwyne to know it’s just me.”

“Naw, they won’t,” I insisted. “It’s dark out here, and they’re busy. And I’ll be on this side, makin’ a fuss too, so they won’t know where to look. They’ll think they’re surrounded.”

Jim still looked scared, but I could see him thinkin’ it over. “What you gwyne to do?” he asked.

“I’m gonna bang on the side of the cabin, and then I’m gonna yell somethin’ scary,” I said. “Like, ‘Sheriff’s here! We got you surrounded!’ You do the same, just louder, and from the other side.”

Jim trembled a little. “Dis be de death of us, Huck.”

“Only if we don’t do nothin’,” I said. “Now, go on. Get around the other side. When I bang, you holler.”

Jim nodded slowly, still lookin’ like he was about to faint. He crept off through the bushes, makin’ as little noise as he could. I waited till I heard him on the far side of the cabin, then I took a deep breath.

I picked up a good-sized rock and threw it hard against the side of the cabin, right near the door. It made a loud thwack that echoed in the quiet night. Both men inside jumped.

“What in tarnation was that?” Hank yelled.

“Sounds like somethin’ hit the wall,” Jed replied, reachin’ for somethin’ in his belt, probably a knife.

Then I let out a yell, tryin’ to make my voice sound deeper than it was. “Sheriff’s here! We got you surrounded, you scoundrels! Drop your weapons and come out with your hands up!”

Right after I yelled, Jim’s voice came from the other side of the cabin, loud and booming. “Yeah! Dis ain’t no place for no thieves! Come out now, or we shoot!” He sounded like a whole posse of men, his voice echoing through the woods. He must have been beat in’ on somethin’ too, because I heard a loud thumpin’ sound.

The two men inside the cabin froze. Hank dropped the silver coins, and they scattered on the floor.

“Surrounded?” Hank whispered, his voice suddenly not so tough. “Who’s out there?”

“It’s the whole county posse!” I yelled again, bangin’ my fist on the wall to make more noise. “You ain’t got no way out! Give up now!”

Jim let out another loud yell, and then another thump, like someone fallin’ down, makin’ it sound like a struggle was happenin’ out there, like we were fightin’ and gettin’ ready to rush in.

Hank and Jed looked at each other, their faces pale in the dim light of the cabin. The woman, who had stopped cryin’, looked up at them with a glint of hope in her eyes.

“There’s too many of ‘em, Hank,” Jed said, his voice tremblin’. “I ain’t gettin’ shot for a few measly coins.”

Hank looked out the window, then back at Jed. “Blast it all! We ain’t got time to argue. Let’s get out of here through the back. They ain’t coverin’ the whole place.” He grabbed a sack and shoved the silver coins into it, leavin’ the rest of the woman’s belongings scattered on the floor.

They rushed to the back door, the same one I’d used to sneak in before. I quickly moved back into the thick bushes, makin’ myself as small as possible. The door burst open, and the two men came tearin’ out, lookin’ wild-eyed and scared. They didn’t even see me, they were so intent on gettin’ away. They plunged into the dark woods, crashin’ through the underbrush, headin’ away from the river, makin’ enough noise to scare every animal for a mile around.

I waited until I couldn’t hear them anymore, then I crept back to the cabin. Jim was already there, standin’ by the front door, lookin’ all proud and relieved.

“Huck, we done it!” he whispered, a wide grin on his face. “We done scared dem rascals clean away!”

The woman was standin’ in the doorway now, lookin’ from us to the woods where the men had disappeared. She looked confused, but mostly she looked relieved.

“Who… who are you?” she asked, her voice still a little shaky.

“Just friends,” I said, steppin’ forward. “We heard you were in trouble.”

She looked at me, then at Jim, her eyes still a little scared. “But… I thought I heard a whole bunch of men. Sheriffs, you said?”

Jim chuckled. “Naw, ma’am. Just us. We ain’t no sheriffs. Just me an’ Huck. We jes’ made a heap o’ noise, dat’s all.”

She looked at us for a moment longer, then a small smile started to spread across her face. “Well, I’ll be. You two… you saved me. They were gonna take everything. They were awful mean.”

“They were,” I agreed. “And they were the same fellers who were on that island upriver. The ones with the flatboat.”

Her eyes widened. “Island? They had a flatboat? Oh, those rascals! They were watchin’ me, I bet. Been seein’ that boat driftin’ around lately, but I didn’t think nothin’ of it.”

We went inside the cabin. It was a small place, but cozy, even with the mess the men had made. There was a little fireplace, and a bed in the corner, and a few rough chairs. The woman, she straightened up her belongings as best she could, gatherin’ the scattered silver coins from the floor.

“My name’s Sarah,” she said, offerin’ us a grateful smile. “Thank you, both of you. I don’t know what would have happened if you hadn’t come along.”

“Don’t mention it, ma’am,” I said, feelin’ a little shy but proud. “We just happened to be floatin’ by.”

“You must be hungry,” she said, lookin’ at our ragged clothes. “Please, sit down. I ain’t got much, but I can make you some coffee and maybe some johnnycakes.”

My stomach rumbled a polite response. Jim and I sat down, glad to be out of the night air. While she tidied up and started a fire to heat water, she told us her story. She lived alone in that cabin since her ma had passed on last year. Her pa had gone downriver a long time ago and never come back. She made a livin’ by sellin’ herbs and medicines she gathered from the woods, and sometimes traded with the passing riverboats. She had a little patch of garden out back where she grew vegetables.

“Those men, they were watchin’ me, I just know it,” she said, shakin’ her head. “They’d been hangin’ around the area for a few days, I saw their smoke once or twice from the island. They must have been plannin’ to jump me. I heard talk of river pirates sometimes, but I never thought they’d come to my door.”

Jim and I looked at each other. River pirates. That was a scary thought. It warn’t just a couple of mean fellers, it was somethin’ more organized, somethin’ dangerous.

She poured us hot coffee and served us a plate of fresh johnnycakes with a little bit of homemade syrup. It was a feast, better than any bacon we’d stolen. We ate it all, tellin’ her a little bit about ourselves, but mostly keepin’ mum about our real story, just sayin’ we were travellin’ downstream and got separated from our steamboat.

After we finished eatin’, Sarah looked at us with a concerned expression. “You two can’t go back out on the river tonight,” she said. “Those men might be gone for now, but they’re likely to be back. And they saw you here. You can stay here, in my cabin. I got a little loft where you can sleep. It ain’t much, but it’s safe.”

Jim and I looked at each other. It was risky, stayin’ put. But the thought of those river pirates bein’ out there, and us bein’ on the open river, it warn’t a good thought. And Sarah was a kind woman. We couldn’t just leave her alone after what we’d done.

“We’d be mighty grateful, ma’am,” Jim said. “But we don’t want to be no trouble.”

“Nonsense,” she replied, wavin’ her hand. “You saved me from a heap of trouble. It’s the least I can do.”

So we stayed. Sarah showed us to the little loft, a small space up a ladder, with a couple of old blankets and straw mattresses. It was warm and smelled of dried herbs. Before we climbed up, she handed me a small, shiny stone.

“Here, Huck,” she said. “This is a good luck charm. It’s supposed to ward off evil. You keep it close. And you, Jim, if you need anything, just call.”

I took the stone, feelin’ its smooth coolness in my palm. It was a kind thing for her to do. We climbed up into the loft, and settled down on the mattresses. It felt strange to be in a real house again, even a little shanty, after so long on the raft. But it was a good feeling, too. A safe feeling.
