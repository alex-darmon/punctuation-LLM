It was one of them days where the sun jest seemed to bake the dust right into the air, and a man’s throat was dry before he even got up from his bunk. Most folks was gathered down by the river, watchin’ some flatboats unloadin’ corn and whiskey from up north, and the air was thick with the smell of sweat and muddy water, and a kind of excitement that always come when new things rolled into town. I was perched up on a cracker barrel outside of Old Man Hemple’s General Store, watchin’ it all, and feelin’ pretty good for a boy what ain’t had to work all mornin’.

Pretty soon a big, loud feller come ridin’ in from the east, settin’ up a cloud of dust that follered him right down the main street. He was dressed in clothes too fine for this part of the world, all flashy and bright, and had a big ol’ top hat on his head that looked like it had seen better days, but he wore it proud. He had a big chest, and a voice that could carry over the clatter of a whole blacksmith’s shop, and he wasn’t shy about usin’ it. He pulled up right in front of the store, and swung down from his horse with a flourish, like he was expectin’ a cheer. His horse was a scrawny thing, looked like it needed a good meal, but the feller patted its neck like it was a prize-winner, and then he started hollerin’ at the top of his lungs: “Howdy, folks! I am Professor Phileas Phipps, purveyor of cures, conjurer of marvels, and master of the invisible arts! Step right up, step right up, and witness wonders beyond your wildest dreams!” He had a big wooden chest strapped to the back of his saddle, and he started to unstrap it, talkin’ all the while, and tryin’ to get folks to gather round. Some of the loafers already was, drawn by the noise, and a few kids like me come closer, but most of the sensible folks was still down by the river.

He pulled out bottles of colored liquid, and some shiny trinkets, and a pack of cards that he shuffled faster than a rattlesnake’s tail. He was a-givin’ a regular show, explainin’ how his ‘Elixir of Everlasting Youth’ could cure everything from ingrown toenails to a broken heart, and how his ‘Miracle Magnet’ could draw out all the bad humors from a body. He even made a silver dollar vanish from one hand and appear in the other, and the kids all gasped, and one old woman clapped her hands.

But then, Old Man Hemple, he comes out of his store, lookin’ sour as a pickled onion. He was a quiet man, always wore a clean apron, and never liked no fuss or bother on his property. He stood there on the porch, his hands on his hips, and his eyes was narrow, and he didn’t say nothin’ for a spell, jest watched the Professor do his jig. The Professor, he was still carryin’ on, tellin’ folks about his next big trick, when Old Man Hemple finally spoke up, real slow and steady.

“Say now, friend,” Old Man Hemple says, his voice not much more than a whisper, but it cut through the Professor’s bluster like a sharp knife, “you aimin’ to sell them snake oils and fancy lies on my doorstep all day, or you fixin’ to move along?”

The Professor stopped dead in his tracks, and his big smile kinda wilted around the edges. He looked at Old Man Hemple like he hadn’t seen him standin’ there, and he give a little nervous laugh. “Why, good sir,” he says, tryin’ to put on his grand voice again, “I am but bringin’ the blessings of science and spectacle to this fine town. Surely you wouldn’t deny these good people a chance at betterment?”

Old Man Hemple didn’t bat an eye. “I don’t deny nobody nothin’ they want to pay for,” he says, “but I ain’t havin’ no hocus-pocus and outlandish claims right where folks come to buy honest goods. You got a license for this peddlin’? You got a permit to disturb the peace?”

The Professor looked around at the small crowd that was growin’, and he seen that folks wasn’t laughin’ no more. They was lookin’ at Old Man Hemple with respect, and at the Professor with a kind of uncertain curiosity. The Professor, he puffed out his chest a bit, and tried to rally. “A license?” he booms. “My license is the open road, my permit the good will of the populace!”

“Your permit is about to expire, mister,” Old Man Hemple says, and he took a step down from the porch, and then another, walkin’ right up to the Professor, who was a head taller, but suddenly looked a lot smaller. Old Man Hemple wasn’t a big man, but he had a kind of quiet authority that most big men didn’t, and he wasn’t afraid of nobody. “I reckon you got about five minutes to pack up your wagon and be on your way, or I’ll be callin’ for Sheriff Beauregard, and he ain’t got much patience for folks what don’t know when to leave.”

The Professor’s face went a little pale under his bravado. He opened his mouth like he was a-gwyne to say somethin’ smart, but then he seen the look in Old Man Hemple’s eyes, and he must’ve known it warn’t no use. He mumbled somethin’ under his breath, somethin’ about small-minded provincials, and then he started to gather up his bottles and his trinkets, his movements not so grand and flowery no more, but quick and clumsy. He tossed his stuff back into the wooden chest, and he strapped it back onto his horse, and then he pulled himself back into the saddle, his big hat lookin’ kinda crooked on his head.

He give one last glare at Old Man Hemple, who was still standin’ there silent and unmoving, and then he spurred his scrawny horse, and away he went, down the dusty street and out of town, leavin’ behind a trail of dust and a faint smell of peppermint oil. The crowd kinda dissolved, folks shakin’ their heads or chucklin’ quiet-like, and Old Man Hemple, he jest turned around and went back into his store, without so much as a word. I reckoned he was satisfied, and I learned somethin’ that day about quiet men and loud men, and which ones was worth listenin’ to. It warn’t always the one with the biggest hat that had the most to say, or the most to back it up with, and that was a lesson worth knowin' in them parts.

The heat of the afternoon wasn’t somethin’ to be trifled with, not in July, and after Old Man Hemple went back into his store the street seemed to settle down even more, like the dust itself was too tired to stir. I reckon the Professor’s wagon had kicked up enough of it for one day anyhow. I wasn’t much for settin’ still, so I drifted on down towards the levee, where the riverboats tied up. That river, she was a grand thing to watch, always rollin’ and churnin’, pullin’ at the land like a hungry dog at a bone. Sometimes you’d see a whole piece of bank just slough off into the water, trees and all, and then folks would say, “Well, the river’s had her supper tonight.” It made you think, how somethin’ so big and powerful could jest keep on goin’ without a thought for all the little folks livin’ along her banks, and how they had to live with her temper.

I watched a paddle-wheeler, the ‘Belle of St. Louis,’ loadin’ up a fresh cargo of cotton bales, the roustabouts singin’ and sweatin’ like it warn’t ninety degrees in the shade. The cap’n, he was a big man with a voice like a foghorn, stood on the upper deck yellin’ orders, and a bunch of fancy dressed folks was already settin’ on the verandy, fannin’ themselves and sippin’ on what I reckoned was lemonade, though it might’

was likely somethin’ a sight stronger, somethin’ that fetched a higher price than what a body could squeeze from a lemon, even if they had a whole orchard of them. The sun was beatin’ down considerable, makin’ the air shimmer above the hot planks of the levee, and the tar in the cracks was soft and sticky underfoot. Them fancy folks on the boat didn’t seem to mind it much, settin’ there fannin’ themselves with big palmetto fans, the ladies in their bonnets and parasols lookin’ like they was made of spun sugar, and the men with their vests unbuttoned a bit, moppin’ their brows with white handkerchiefs.

The roustabouts kept on singin’ their songs, a sort of mournful chant that went with the heave and strain of the cotton bales, each one bigger than a fat man. They was strong, them fellers, muscles jumpin’ under their dark skin, and sweat shinin’ on them like they’d just been dipped in the river. Every now and then one of them would let out a whoop, and the others would laugh, and then they’d settle back into their rhythm, loadin’ up that boat till she seemed to be sittin’ lower in the water than when she first come in. The big paddle-wheel at the back was still, waitin’ to churn the muddy water, and the smokestacks was lettin’ out a thin wisp of smoke, a sign that they was gettin’ ready to fire her up good.

I watched a while, leanin’ against a pile of old ropes that smelled of river water and somethin’ like fish, and it warn’t long before a feller come by, a deckhand I reckoned, with a bucket of water and a dipper, offerin’ drinks to the roustabouts. He seen me standin’ there, lookin’ a bit dusty and not much account, and he give me a nod, a kind of tired nod, and said, “Thirsty, son?” I told him I was, and he let me have a dipperful. It was cool and fresh, tasted like well water, and I thanked him and watched him go on.

The cap’n on the upper deck was still yellin’ his orders, his voice carryin’ clear across the water, and he had a big gold watch he kept pullin’ out of his pocket and lookin’ at, like time was a thing he could hold in his hand and make go faster or slower. Pretty soon he yelled out, “Last call for passengers! We’re pullin’ out in ten minutes!” And then the big bell on the boat started to clang, a deep, mournful sound that echoed down the river.

People on the levee started hurryin’ up the gangplank, some carryin’ carpetbags, some with boxes and baskets, all in a rush like they was afeared to be left behind. There was a fat woman with about five children clingin’ to her skirts, all of them lookin’ tired and cross, and a couple of young fellers with fancy clothes and shiny shoes, smokin’ cigars and lookin’ like they had important business upriver. I seen all sorts, and I reckoned each one of them had a story, though I warn’t likely to hear any of them.

Then I heard a commotion further down the levee, away from where the boat was tied up, and I looked over. There was a small crowd gatherin’ around somethin’, and I could hear a voice, loud and booming, cuttin’ through the general noise of the riverfront. It warn’t the cap’n, this voice was different, more theatrical, like a feller tryin’ to sell somethin’ from the back of a wagon. My curiosity got the better of me, like it usually does, and I walked on over to see what the fuss was about.

It was a man, standin’ on an upturned barrel, with a long, flowing black coat that looked too hot for the weather, and a big, shiny top hat that reminded me a bit of the Professor who’d just got run out of town, only this hat was even grander, with a feather stickin’ out of it. He had a big, bushy black beard that looked a little too neat, like it might’ve been glued on, and spectacles perched on his nose, makin’ his eyes look twice their size. In his hand he held a bottle, glintin’ in the sun, filled with a dark, oily-lookin’ liquid. Around him was a table covered with a faded velvet cloth, and on it was more bottles, some with colored liquids, some with dried roots and herbs, and a few little velvet bags tied with string.

“Ladies and gentlemen,” he boomed, his voice carryin’ far, “I present to you the marvel of the age! The one, the only, Dr. Aloysius P. Bumble’s Universal Elixir of Life! A single swallow of this miraculous tonic, brewed from ancient herbs found only in the darkest jungles of the Amazon, can cure what ails ye! Is it a crick in the back that keeps you from your labors? Is it the ague that chills your bones? The fever that burns your brow? The rheumatism that cripples your joints? Or perhaps a melancholy of the spirit, a deep and abiding sadness that no earthly pleasure can dislodge? Fear not, for Dr. Bumble is here!”

He paused for dramatic effect, sweeping his hand across the bottles on the table, and the small crowd murmured a bit. There was a few old folks, their faces wrinkled and tired, lookin’ at him with hopeful eyes, and some younger fellers, leanin’ against posts, smirkin’ and passin’ comments. A couple of women with their market baskets stood listenin’, their eyes wide.

“Yes, my friends,” Dr. Bumble continued, holdin’ up the bottle again, makin’ it catch the sunlight, “this very elixir, passed down through generations of healers and mystics, can restore the vigor of youth! It can turn a withered hag into a blushing bride! A stooped old man into a spry youth! It can clear the mind, strengthen the heart, and put a spring in your step that would shame a mountain goat! And all this, for the paltry sum of one dollar a bottle! A mere pittance, my friends, for a lifetime of health and happiness!”

He took a big gulp from the bottle he was holdin’, smackin’ his lips and makin’ a show of feelin’ instantly invigorated. “Ah, the very nectar of the gods!” he declared, puffin’ out his chest. “Feel the life-giving essence coursing through my veins! Feel the wisdom of the ancients permeating my very being!”

I looked at the bottle he drank from, and it looked suspiciously like the same dark liquid as the others. I reckoned he warn’t drinkin’ nothin’ more than colored water with a bit of sugar and maybe some strong-smellin’ herbs to give it a kick. I’d seen this kind of thing before, fellers tryin’ to sell snake oil to country folk. Pap always said they was worse than any common thief, ‘cause they stole a body’s hope as well as their money.

An old woman, bent over with a cane, pushed her way to the front of the crowd. Her face was a mass of wrinkles, and her eyes were watery. “Doctor,” she quavered, “I got a pain in my hip that’s been botherin’ me for nigh on ten year. Sometimes I can’t hardly walk a step. Can your elixir help me?”

Dr. Bumble beamed down at her, a most benevolent smile on his face. “My dear madam,” he said, his voice softer now, full of a kind of oily sympathy, “it would be a privilege to relieve you of such suffering! A single dose, taken three times a day, and I assure you, you’ll be dancin’ a jig within the week! Come, step right up, don’t be shy!”

He helped her climb a small step up to the barrel he was standin’ on, and poured a small amount of the dark liquid into a tiny glass. The old woman looked at it doubtfully, but then she took a sip. She coughed a bit, and her eyes watered more. “It’s… strong,” she said, her voice still weak.

“A potent brew, my dear, for a potent affliction!” Dr. Bumble declared, clappin’ her on the back a little too hard. “Feel it now, working its magic? Feel the warmth spreading through your system, chasing away the cold grip of disease?”

The old woman stood there for a moment, swayin’ a little, and then she took another hesitant step. She winced, but then she took another one, and then another. She straightened up a tiny bit, her eyes wide. “Well, I do feel… somethin’,” she said, her voice a little stronger. “A warmth, like you said. And the pain… it ain’t quite so bad right now.”

The crowd murmured again, this time with a bit more awe. The young fellers stopped smirkin’. Even I felt a flicker of wonder, though I told myself it was likely just the shock of the strong taste or the power of her own wishin’ it to be true.

“See there, my friends!” Dr. Bumble cried, holding up the bottle again. “The proof is in the potion! A miraculous recovery, right before your very eyes! Who else suffers from the torments of the flesh? Who else wishes to cast off the shackles of infirmity and embrace a new life of vibrant health?”

He started sellin’ bottles like hotcakes after that. The old woman, she bought two, payin’ with shaky hands and a few silver coins she pulled from a little pouch. A burly riverman with a hacking cough bought one, and a pale-faced young woman who looked like she might be with child bought another, sayin’ she hoped it would help with her morning sickness. Dr. Bumble was countin’ his money and lookin’ pleased as punch, his spectacles glintin’ in the sun, and the bell of the Belle of St. Louis was still clangin’ in the distance, a reminder that time was passin’.

I was about to turn away, reckonin’ I’d seen enough of that particular show, when a man pushed his way through the crowd, a big feller with a red face and a dirty shirt, smellin’ strongly of stale whiskey. He had a mean look in his eye, and he was swayin’ a bit, even more than Dr. Bumble on his barrel.

“Hold on there, you fancy talkin’ charlatan!” the man slurred, pointin’ a shaky finger at Dr. Bumble. “I seen your kind before! Sellin’ colored water to honest folk! I say you’re a fraud, and you oughta be run out of town like the last one!”

Dr. Bumble’s smile didn’t falter, but his eyes got a little colder behind the spectacles. “My good man,” he said, his voice still smooth but with an edge to it, “I believe you are mistaken. I am a purveyor of health, a bringer of wellness, not a mountebank! And I resent your accusations, sir!”

“Accusations, is it?” the drunk man roared, takin’ a step closer, makin’ the crowd draw back a bit. “I say you’re a liar and a cheat! And I ain’t afeared to call you out on it! What’s in that bottle, eh? What’s really in it?” He lunged forward, tryin’ to grab one of the bottles from the table.

Dr. Bumble was quicker than he looked. He sidestepped the lunge, and with a surprising move, he brought the big bottle he’d been holdin’ down on the drunk man’s head. It warn’t a hard blow, more of a thud, and the bottle didn’t break, but it was enough to stun the feller. The drunk man stumbled back, shakin’ his head, and Dr. Bumble, without missin’ a beat, pulled a small, shiny pistol from inside his long coat. It warn’t a very big pistol, but it looked mean enough.

“I warn you, sir,” Dr. Bumble said, his voice now like cold steel, “I am a man of peace, but I will not tolerate violence or false accusations! Stand back, or you will regret it!”

The drunk man blinked, saw the pistol, and his eyes got wide. He muttered somethin’ under his breath, somethin’ about not worth the trouble, and then he stumbled away, pushin’ through the crowd, lookin’ a good deal less brave than he had a moment before. The crowd parted for him, and then closed up again, lookin’ at Dr. Bumble with a new respect, or maybe it was fear.

Dr. Bumble tucked the pistol back into his coat, and his benevolent smile returned, though it seemed a bit more forced now. “As I was saying, my friends,” he continued, his voice a little shaky, “the Universal Elixir of Life! One dollar a bottle! A small price to pay for eternal health and boundless energy!”

But the magic was gone. The crowd started to disperse, some still lookin’ back at him with wary eyes, others shakin’ their heads. The old woman with the hip pain was still standin’ there, lookin’ confused, holdin’ her two bottles like they might turn into snakes any minute. I reckoned the show was over. Dr. Bumble didn’t sell any more bottles. He started packin’ up his things, his movements not so grand and theatrical anymore, but quick and efficient. He tossed the bottles into a wooden chest, folded up his velvet cloth, and then climbed off the barrel, lookin’ a bit smaller without the height. He glanced around, and his eyes met mine for a second. I quickly looked away, pretendin’ to be interested in a loose plank on the levee. He didn’t say nothin’ to me, just grumbled to himself and pulled the chest onto a small handcart, then pushed it away, meltin’ into the general bustle of the levee.

The bell of the Belle of St. Louis gave a final, long clang, and then the whistle blew, a mournful, drawn-out sound that made the air tremble. The big paddle-wheel at the back started to turn, slow at first, then faster, churnin’ up the muddy river water into a creamy froth. The gangplank was pulled up, and the boat slowly started to move away from the bank, the roustabouts cheerin’ and hollerin’. The fancy folks on the upper deck waved their handkerchiefs, and a few people on the levee waved back. The current caught her, and she swung out into the main channel, her smokestacks puffin’ out thick black smoke now, makin’ a great cloud that drifted lazily over the town.

I watched her go, gettin’ smaller and smaller as she headed upriver, and I felt a bit lonesome. I always did feel that way when a big boat left, like a piece of the world was goin’ on without me. It made me think about all the places they went, and all the people they saw, and all the stories they carried with them. This town was just a speck, a stop along the way, and then they was gone.

The sun was startin’ to dip lower in the sky now, turnin’ the clouds above the river all red and orange and purple, a sight that always made me feel a bit peaceful, even after all the fuss with Dr. Bumble. The air was gettin’ a little cooler, and the mosquitoes was startin’ to come out, buzzin’ around my ears. I scratched at a bite on my neck and reckoned it was time to find some place to settle for the night. I didn’t have much to my name, just the clothes on my back and a few coins in my pocket, but I always managed. The river always provided somethin’, one way or another.

I walked away from the levee, back towards the main street of the town. The wagons and horses that had been thick in the street earlier was mostly gone now, headin’ back to their farms, kickin’ up dust that hung in the still air like a golden haze. The stores was startin’ to close up, their proprietors pullin’ down the awnings and puttin’ up the shutters. A few lamps was bein’ lit inside, castin’ warm, yellow light onto the dusty street.

I passed a saloon, and I could hear the tinny sound of a piano playin’ a lively tune, and men’s voices laughin’ and talkin’. A whiff of whiskey and tobacco smoke drifted out, and for a moment I thought about goin’ in, just to see what was happenin’, but then I remembered Pap and how he was in places like that, and I thought better of it. I warn’t lookin’ for trouble, not tonight.

I seen a stray dog, a scrawny hound with ribs stickin’ out, sniffin’ around a garbage barrel outside a butcher shop. He looked up at me with sad eyes, and I wished I had somethin’ to give him, but I didn’t. He went back to his sniffin’, and I walked on.

I came to the edge of town, where the houses got sparser and the fields began. There was an old, abandoned shed just beyond the last fence, its roof half caved in, and a couple of its walls leaning precariously. It warn’t much, but it looked like it might offer a bit of shelter from the night air, and maybe a place to lie down where nobody would bother me. I figured I could stretch out on the dirt floor, maybe find some old sacks to sleep on. It warn’t the Ritz, but it was better than nothin’.

As I got closer, I seen a small flicker of light comin’ from inside the shed. My heart gave a little jump. I stopped, hidin’ behind a big oak tree, and peered around the trunk. Someone was already in there. I could see a faint glow, like from a candle or a small lantern. I wondered who it could be. Maybe a tramp, or another boy like me lookin’ for a place to sleep. Or maybe somethin’ worse.

I waited a bit, listenin’. I couldn’t hear no voices, just the crickets chirpin’ and the distant croakin’ of frogs from down by the river. The light stayed steady. Curiosity was a powerful thing, and I crept a little closer, tryin’ to be quiet as a cat. I peered through a crack in the rotted wood of the shed wall.

Inside, hunched over a small, flickering lantern, was a man. He was old, with white hair, and he was wearin’ clothes that looked like they’d seen better days, patched and faded. He had a small, worn carpetbag beside him, and a half-eaten piece of dry bread on a rag.

He looked peaceful, not like he was up to no mischief. Just an old man, readin’ by a lantern in an old shed. It warn’t what I was expectin’. I thought about just movin’ on, findin’ another place, but then I figured if he warn’t bothered by me, I warn’t bothered by him. There was room enough in the shed for two.

I decided to make my presence known, rather than just sneakin’ in and scarin’ him. I stepped out from behind the tree, and walked slowly towards the shed door, makin’ just enough noise so he’d hear me comin’. He looked up quick, his head snappin’ around, and his eyes behind the spectacles was wide with surprise, or maybe a little fear.

“Evenin’,” I said, as polite as I could. “Didn’t mean to scare you. Just lookin’ for a place to get out of the night air.”

He squinted at me for a moment, then his eyes softened. “Well, good evening to you too, young man,” he said, his voice a bit raspy, but gentle. “Come in, come in. There’s room enough here for another soul. It gets a bit lonesome out here all by one’s self.” He gestured with a frail hand to a clear spot on the dirt floor.

“Pleased to meet you, Huck,” he said, givin’ me a small smile. “My name’s Silas. Just travelin’ through. Like yourself, I reckon.”

I nodded. “Somethin’ like that.” I looked at the book in his hands. “What you readin’?”

He held it up a bit. “It’s a book of poetry, son. Old tales, from a long time ago, from lands far away. Keeps an old man company.” He chuckled softly.

He had a big, bushy black beard that looked a little too neat, like it might’ve been glued on, and spectacles perched on his nose, makin’ his eyes look twice their size. In his hand he held a bottle, glintin’ in the sun, filled with a dark, oily-lookin’ liquid. Around him was a table covered with a faded velvet cloth, and on it was more bottles, some with colored liquids, some with dried roots and herbs, and a few little velvet bags tied with string.

“Ladies and gentlemen,” he boomed, his voice carryin’ far, “I present to you the marvel of the age! The one, the only, Dr. Aloysius P. Bumble’s Universal Elixir of Life! A single swallow of this miraculous tonic, brewed from ancient herbs found only in the darkest jungles of the Amazon, can cure what ails ye! Is it a crick in the back that keeps you from your labors? Is it the ague that chills your bones? The fever that burns your brow? The rheumatism that cripples your joints? He paused for dramatic effect, sweeping his hand across the bottles on the table, and the small crowd murmured a bit. There was a few old folks, their faces wrinkled and tired, lookin’ at him with hopeful eyes, and some younger fellers, leanin’ against posts, smirkin’ and passin’ comments. A couple of women with their market baskets stood listenin’, their eyes wide.

“Yes, my friends,” Dr. Bumble continued, holdin’ up the bottle again, makin’ it catch the sunlight, “this very elixir, passed down through generations of healers and mystics, can restore the vigor of youth! It can turn a withered hag into a blushing bride! A stooped old man into a spry youth! It can clear the mind, strengthen the heart, and put a spring in your step that would shame a mountain goat! He took a big gulp from the bottle he was holdin’, smackin’ his lips and makin’ a show of feelin’ instantly invigorated. “Ah, the very nectar of the gods!” he declared, puffin’ out his chest. “Feel the life-giving essence coursing through my veins! I looked at the bottle he drank from, and it looked suspiciously like the same dark liquid as the others. I reckoned he warn’t drinkin’ nothin’ more than colored water with a bit of sugar and maybe some strong-smellin’ herbs to give it a kick. I’d seen this kind of thing before, fellers tryin’ to sell snake oil to country folk. Pap always said they was worse than any common thief, ‘cause they stole a body’s hope as well as their money.

An old woman, bent over with a cane, pushed her way to the front of the crowd. Her face was a mass of wrinkles, and her eyes were watery. “Doctor,” she quavered, “I got a pain in my hip that’s been botherin’ me for nigh on ten year. Sometimes I can’t hardly walk a step. Can your elixir help me?”

Dr. Bumble beamed down at her, a most benevolent smile on his face. “My dear madam,” he said, his voice softer now, full of a kind of oily sympathy, “it would be a privilege to relieve you of such suffering! A single dose, taken three times a day, and I assure you, you’ll be dancin’ a jig within the week! Come, step right up, don’t be shy!”

He even got a couple of young women, all gigglin’ and blushin’, to buy his "beauty tonic," which he claimed would make their skin glow like a new-minted silver dollar and keep the wrinkles away till they was a hundred year old. Are your joints creaky and complaining? This’ll oil ‘em up like a well-greased wagon wheel! Do you feel a general listlessness, a lack of gumption, a spirit that’s down in the dumps? Why, a good swig of this, and you’ll be ready to dance a jig, sing a song, and tackle any chore life throws at you, with a smile on your face and a twinkle in your eye!”

He went on like that for a good spell, pullin’ out every stop, tellin’ tales of folks who was on their deathbed and sprung back to life, of young lovers who was too shy to speak and found their voice, of farmers whose crops withered until they sprinkled a little of his elixir on the soil and suddenly had the biggest harvest in three counties. He even claimed it helped folks find lost items, and improved their luck at cards, though he quickly added he warn’t condonin’ gamblin’, oh no, not him, but if a fella happened to have a run of good fortune, well, who was he to argue with Providence?

The crowd was gettin’ real worked up now. Their eyes was gleamin’ with something like hope, and their hands was fidgetin’ in their pockets, and a low murmur started to go through ‘em, like a hive of bees just before they swarm. Dr. Bumble, he was a master, he could feel it, and he knew just when to bring it home.

“Now, I know what you’re thinkin’,” he boomed, “you’re thinkin’, ‘Dr. Bumble, this sounds too good to be true! This must cost a king’s ransom!’ And you’d be right, it ought to! For a medicine of such potent qualities, of such life-givin’ power, you’d expect to pay a pretty penny. Why, in the big cities, a single bottle of this golden fluid, this liquid sunshine, would fetch you a dollar, two dollars, maybe even five, if the need was great enough!” He paused, lettin’ that sink in, lettin’ ‘em chew on the idea of how valuable this stuff was. And I reckon some of ‘em was calculatin’ what they had in their pockets and if it would be enough.

Then he leaned forward, confidential again. “But I ain’t here to get rich off the sufferin’ of my fellow man! No, friends, my mission is to spread health and happiness far and wide, to bring the blessings of nature to every hearth and home! And so, for today only, right here, right now, to you, my good friends of this fine town, I am offerin’ this miraculous elixir, this blessed balm, not for five dollars, not for two, not even for one dollar! No, friends, I am offerin’ it to you for the unbelievably low, almost charitable, price of just fifty cents a bottle! Yes, you heard me right, fifty cents! Half a dollar! A mere two bits, for a lifetime of health and vigor!”

And that did it. The crowd surged forward like a dam had broke. Hands was shootin’ up, coins was clinkin’, and folks was shoutin’ out, “Me, Dr. Bumble, me! I need one!” “Here’s my money!” “Gimme two bottles, one for my wife!” The doctor, he had a young fella, skinny as a rail and quick as a squirrel, that was his helper, and the helper was already there, openin’ a crate and pullin’ out bottles and takin’ money. Dr. Bumble, he just stood there, beaming, occasionally pointin’ to someone in the crowd and sayin’, “Yes, sir, you look like you need a good dose of life! Comin’ right up!” or “Madam, you’ll be dancin’ by nightfall, I guarantee it!”

I seen an old woman, her face like a crumpled piece of paper, her back bent low, she dug into her apron pocket and pulled out a worn purse, and her hands was shakin’ as she counted out the fifty cents. She took a big swig, and her eyes watered, and she coughed a bit, and then she let out a sigh, and she kinda straightened up a little, just a little, but enough for her to look surprised. “Why,” she croaked, “I do believe… I do believe I feel a bit… warmer.” And the folks around her, they all nodded, like that was proof enough.

I reckoned it was mostly the whisky, or whatever other strong spirits he put in that bottle, and the sugar, and the pepper, and maybe a dash of something else to give it a kick. But the old woman, she wanted to believe, and that was half the cure, I suppose. It put a kind of shine in her eye, a little spark of hope that hadn’t been there before, and that was somethin’ to see, even if it warn’t real.

The sales went on for a good half hour, and the pile of coins in the helper’s bucket got higher and higher, and the crate of bottles got emptier and emptier. Dr. Bumble, he was just smilin’ and noddin’, occasionally clappin’ a fella on the back, or offerin’ a word of encouragement to a lady who looked particularly downhearted. He never seemed to lose his breath, or his smile, and he had a way of lookin’ every single person in the eye, makin’ ‘em feel like he cared about their personal ailments.

But then, just as the last few bottles was bein’ sold, and the crowd was startin’ to thin a little, a big, burly fella, with a red beard and a mean look in his eye, pushed his way to the front. He warn’t none of the hopeful ones, you could tell that right away. He had his arms crossed, and his jaw was set, and he looked like he’d chewed nails for breakfast.

“Hold on there, Doc!” he growled, his voice like rocks rubbin’ together. “Just hold on a minute! What is this bilge you’re peddlin’ here? I bought a bottle of your ‘elixir’ last year, when you passed through Memphis, and all it did was give me the runs for three days and make my teeth ache!”

A hush fell over the remaining crowd. Some of the folks who’d just bought bottles looked a little nervous, and the old woman who’d felt “warmer” shrunk back a bit. Dr. Bumble, he didn’t even flinch. He just kept that easy smile on his face, though I seen a little glint in his eye, like he was enjoyin’ the challenge.

“My good sir,” Dr. Bumble said, his voice smooth as silk, “I assure you, I have never set foot in Memphis in my life! And my Elixir, the Invigorating Life-Restorer, has never, to my knowledge, caused such… digestive disturbances, nor dental distress! You must have been misled by some imposter, some unscrupulous scoundrel who dares to copy my good name and product!”

The red-bearded man snorted. “Imposter, my foot! He looked just like you, wore a coat just like yours, talked just like you! And he called himself Dr. Bumble, too!”

“Ah, but there’s the rub, my friend!” Dr. Bumble exclaimed, throwin’ his hands up in a gesture of dismay. “There are many who would ape the master, many who would try to steal a good man’s reputation! But there is only one true Dr. Bumble, and you are lookin’ at him! I have my papers, my bona fides, my attestations of authenticity, if you doubt me!” He gestured to his helper, who was already rummagin’ in a satchel. “No, my good sir, you fell victim to a charlatan, and I deeply sympathize with your predicament. But I assure you, my product is pure, wholesome, and beneficial in every way!”

He turned back to the crowd, his voice rising again. “This is the curse of the truly effective remedy, friends! The fakes, the counterfeits, they always follow success! But don’t let a bad experience with a fraud spoil you for the genuine article! Think of it like this: if someone sold you a rotten apple, would you swear off all apples forever? No! You’d seek out a good, wholesome, juicy apple, and enjoy its benefits! And so it is with my Elixir!”

The crowd, they looked at the red-bearded fella, then back at Dr. Bumble, and I reckon the doctor’s smooth talk was winnin’ ‘em over. The red-bearded fella still looked mad, but he was outnumbered, and he didn’t seem to know what to say next. He just grumbled a bit and faded back into the thinnin’ crowd, defeated.

Dr. Bumble, he gave a little bow to the space where the fella had been, as if dismissin’ a minor interruption, and then he spread his arms wide, his face full of triumph. “Now, who else wishes to partake of the genuine article, the true fountain of youth, before I must depart for other towns, to spread health and happiness to all corners of this great land?”

A few more bottles was sold, and then the crate was empty. The helper counted the money, and the doctor rubbed his hands together, satisfied. He gathered his things, and with a final grand bow and a promise to return, he climbed into his wagon, his helper already drivin’ it slowly through the now much smaller crowd. The people dispersed, some clutchin’ their bottles, some lookin’ a bit disappointed, others just wanderin’ off, the spell broken.

I stood there for a spell, watchin’ the dust settle where the wagon had been. It was strange, how quick folks could get caught up in something, and how quick they could forget it, too. They’d been all excited about gettin’ healthy and livin’ long lives, but that excitement, it just fizzled out, like a wet firecracker.

The sun was bearin’ down hot now, hotter than ever, and the air was thick with dust and the smell of horse sweat and unwashed people. The general hum of the town was different now, too. It warn’t the lively, hopeful hum of a crowd at a medicine show. It was lower, more ominous, like a distant rumble of thunder.

I started to notice it, the way people warn’t just dispersin’ and goin’ about their business. They was driftin’ in a certain direction, mostly towards the center of the street, where the bigger stores was. And they warn’t talkin’ about Dr. Bumble anymore, or their aches and pains. They was talkin’ in low, rumblin’ voices, and the word I kept hearin’, over and over, was “Sherburn.”

My stomach went cold, real sudden-like. I remembered the talk from earlier, after Boggs got shot, about lynchin’ Colonel Sherburn. I’d almost forgot it, what with all the excitement of Dr. Bumble’s show, but now it came rushin’ back, stronger than before, like a bad taste in my mouth.

I seen a few of the men who’d been listenin’ to Dr. Bumble, their faces still a bit flushed from the heat and maybe the whisky in the elixir, but now they had a different look in their eyes. It warn’t hope, it was somethin’ harder, somethin’ meaner. They was joinin’ up with other men, men who looked like they hadn’t left the street since Boggs got shot. They was millin’ around, pushin’ and jostlin’, and their voices was gettin’ louder.

“Where’s he at now?” somebody yelled, and it sounded like a challenge, not a question.

“He’s still in his store, they say!” another fella shouted back, and a growl went through the crowd.

I shrunk back into the shade of a general store awning, tryin’ to make myself small, tryin’ to be invisible. My heart was poundin’ in my chest like a drum. This warn’t no medicine show fun. This was somethin’ serious, somethin’ dangerous. They warn’t just talkin’ about it anymore, they was movin’.

The crowd was swellin’ and pushin’ forward, a dark, ragged wave of men, and some women and boys, too, all caught up in the fury of it. They moved with a kind of slow, terrible momentum, like a river risin’. I seen the lanky man, the one who’d mimicked the shootin’ earlier, he was right up front, his crooked-handled cane swingin’ like a weapon, his fur hat still on the back of his head. He was shoutin’ somethin’, but I couldn’t quite make it out over the general roar.

The sound was like a fever, growin’ hotter and more intense. It was a mixture of angry shouts, grumbles, the scuffin’ of boots on the dusty road, and the nervous brayin’ of a mule tied to a post. It was a sound that told you trouble was brewin’, and it warn’t the kind of trouble you could just walk away from.

I seen the local sheriff, a big, red-faced man named Bell, tryin’ to push his way through the crowd near the front. He was shoutin’ somethin’ about law and order, tryin’ to reason with ‘em, but they just ignored him, or pushed past him, like he was a gnat. He looked helpless, just one man against a whole town gone mad. He warn’t gwyne to stop ‘em, I reckoned. Nobody was gwyne to stop ‘em once they got an idea fixed in their heads.

The crowd was aimin’ for Sherburn’s store, the very same one he’d stepped out of to face Boggs. It was a big, respectable-lookin’ place, with a wide front and a couple of windows. Now, it looked closed up, the curtains drawn, like it was hidin’ from the storm that was comin’.

My legs felt like jelly, but I couldn’t tear my eyes away. It was awful to see, but I couldn’t help but watch, like when you see a snake coiled up to strike, you know it’s bad, but you just got to see what it’s gwyne to do. I wondered if Sherburn was still in there, or if he’d slipped out the back, or maybe he’d just stay put, like he done before. He was a proud man, that Colonel Sherburn, and he warn’t one to run from a fight, not even a fight against a whole town.

The sun beat down, makin’ the air shimmer above the street, and the dust rose in little clouds with every step the mob took. The noise was a dreadful thing, a mix of animalistic growls and human shouts, all blended together into a single, ugly sound that made the hair stand up on my arms. I seen men with pick-axe handles, and one fella had an old musket, though I reckoned it warn’t loaded, or at least I hoped it warn’t. They warn’t lookin’ for justice, not really. They was lookin’ for blood, for revenge, for a way to feel powerful, all of ‘em together, against one man.

They reached the front of Sherburn’s store, and the leading edge of the mob just stopped, all of a sudden, like they’d hit a wall. There was a moment of silence, a strange, tense quiet that fell over ‘em, only broken by the pantin’ of some of the men and the restless murmurs from the back. And then, a voice, clear and strong and steady, cut through the quiet like a knife. It was Colonel Sherburn, and he was standin’ on the roof of his porch, right above the store entrance.

He warn’t yellin’, he warn’t screamin’, he just spoke, and his voice carried over the whole crowd, every word distinct. He had a shotgun in his hands, not aimed, just held easy, pointed down at the ground in front of him. His face was pale, but his eyes was dark and hard, and he looked down at that mob like they was a pack of mangy dogs.

“You are cowards!” he said, and the words echoed in the sudden silence. “You come here, a thousand strong, to murder a man who has wronged you. And why? Because one drunken fool got what was comin’ to him? Because he threatened a man’s life, and got shot for it, like any man would shoot a mad dog?”

The crowd stirred, some of ‘em looked down at their feet, some tried to talk back, but his voice just rode right over ‘em.

“You call yourselves men? You are not men! You are curs! You come here under the cover of darkness, or in the middle of a hot afternoon when all decent folks are in their homes, to do your dirty work. You pick on a single man, because you are afraid to face him one on one. You are afraid to face the law. You are afraid to face your own consciences!”

He paused, and swept his gaze over every single face in that mob, and I swear, every fella felt it, that look, like he was lookin’ right through ‘em and seein’ the rotten core of ‘em.

“I know you. I know your kind. You are the mob. You are the nameless, faceless crowd, that hides its cowardice behind numbers. You will never dare to do a thing alone. You will always wait for the crowd, for the mob, to gather, to give you courage. You are a pack of animals, followin’ your lowest instincts, without thought, without reason, without a shred of dignity!”

He shifted the shotgun just a little, barely a movement, but every eye in that crowd fastened on it. He warn’t aimin’, but it was there, a silent promise of what would happen if they tried to move.

“Now, go home!” he commanded, and his voice was like thunder. “Go home, every last one of you, and look yourselves in the mirror, if you dare. And if you still want to come for me, then come, but come one by one, like men, not like a pack of rabid wolves! Because if you come again, as a mob, I will stand here, and I will meet you, and I will send a few of you to hell before I fall!”

The words hung in the air, hot and heavy, and the silence that followed was even heavier than before. The mob, they didn’t move. They just stood there, their faces slack, their anger slowly drainin’ out of ‘em, replaced by a kind of shame, and a real fear. He had called ‘em out, every one of ‘em, and he had done it with a kind of cold, hard truth that they couldn’t deny.

I seen the lanky fella with the cane, his shoulders sagged, and his fur hat slipped a bit further down his head. The sheriff, he looked like he’d been let off a hook, and a little smirk played on his lips. The mob, it started to waver, to break up, like a big cloud gettin’ pulled apart by the wind. A fella here, a fella there, started to shuffle his feet, then turn around, then walk away, slow at first, then a little faster. The roar died down to a murmur, then to just the general street noise again.

In just a few minutes, the whole thing was over. The great, terrible mob, that had swelled and threatened to burst, it just melted away, like snow in the sun. They didn’t even say goodbye, they just went, ashamed and defeated, each one of ‘em probably feelin’ like he’d been called out in front of God and everybody.

Colonel Sherburn, he just stood there for a spell, watchin’ ‘em go, his face still grim and hard. Then, without another word, he turned and went back inside his store, the shotgun still in his hand, and the door closed behind him with a soft thud.

I let out a breath I didn’t even know I was holdin’. My knees was still shaky, but the cold dread in my stomach was gone, replaced by a kind of wonder. I’d never seen a man face down a whole crowd like that, with nothing but his words and his courage, and win. It was somethin’ to remember, that was for sure. It showed you that a single man, if he had enough grit and a clear head, could stand against all the foolishness and meanness of a whole town.

I started to walk away, my mind buzzin’ with what I’d seen. The streets was still full of folks, but they was talkin’ in low voices now, and nobody was lookin’ at anybody else’s eye, and a lot of ‘em was headin’ for the saloons, I reckon, to wash down their shame. The excitement had passed, and all that was left was the hot sun, and the dust, and the memory of a close call. The town, it was quiet again, but it was a different kind of quiet, a quiet that had seen somethin’ ugly and then recoiled from it, like a burned hand. I reckoned they wouldn’t forget Colonel Sherburn’s words for a long time, and maybe, just maybe, some of ‘em would think twice before joinin’ another mob. But then again, folks is mostly foolish, and a crowd can make even the best of ‘em do things they wouldn’t ever dream of alone, so I warn’t holdin’ my breath. Let’s get him!” Their faces was all twisted up, some with excitement, some with a kind of bloodlust, and their eyes was wild and shiny. They was sweating and breathing hard, and the air around them felt thick with their anger and their mean intentions. I seen a young fella, no older than me really, with a brand new rope coiled over his shoulder, his chest puffed out like a rooster, and he was shouting louder than anybody, even though I reckon he hadn’t seen more than fifteen summers. And then there was old man Hooper, who usually spent his days sleeping in a chair outside the general store, now he was shuffling along with a rusty old musket he’d probably hadn’t fired since the Mexican War, his jowls a-quiverin’ with some kind of fierce, false courage he’d stirred up from somewhere deep down.

It was a sight to behold, all them folks that just a minute ago was standing around gawking at Boggs, now they was all united for one purpose, and that purpose was to do violence, to take a life, not out of justice, not out of anger really,

but out of some kind of mean, ugly sport, like setting a pack of dogs on a stray cat. It wasn’t right and I knowed it, but there warn’t nothing I could do, not a scrawny boy like me amongst all them grown men with their blood up. I just stood there, pressed against a storefront, watching them go, the sound of their feet on the dusty street and their hollerin’ a-growin’ fainter as they rounded the corner towards the better part of town, where Colonel Sherburn had his big house.

I felt a cold shiver go down my spine, even though the sun was hot enough to bake a biscuit. It was one thing to see a man shot down in the street, like Boggs was, and another to see a whole town, or a big part of it, turn into a howling, stomping monster with murder in its heart. I seen plenty of ugly things on the river, and heard tell of more, but this here was different. This was folks I’d seen just a few minutes ago, laughin’ and jawin’ and drinkin’ their whisky, now all of a sudden they was transformed, possessed by a terrible spirit that made them forget all about right and wrong. It made me feel sick to my stomach, and I wished I was back on the raft with Jim, out on the wide, peaceful river, where the only thing you had to worry about was snaggin’ a fish or keepin’ clear of a steamboat.

But I warn’t on the raft, I was in that town, and my feet just seemed to take me along with the rest of the current, not as fast as the mob, but following in their wake. I wanted to see what would happen. I knowed it was dangerous, but there was a powerful pull to it, like a moth to a candle flame. I figured if I kept to the edges, and stayed out of the way, I’d be safe enough.

The mob was making a powerful racket now, their shouts echoing off the buildings. They was a-swarmin’ towards Colonel Sherburn’s place, and I could hear them yellin’ his name, and sayin’ all kinds of ugly things about him, and what they was a-gwyne to do to him. They was all bunched up together, a great ugly mass of humanity, with their shirtsleeve arms a-waving and their fists a-shakin’. It was like a river of angry people, flowing straight towards that big white house with the green shutters.

When I got around the corner and could see the house, the mob had already reached it. They was milling around in the street, some of them trying to climb over the low fence that surrounded the yard, others just standin’ and shoutin’ and kickin’ up dust. The noise was something awful. I seen one fella pick up a rock and heave it at a window, but it just bounced off the glass and didn’t break it. The house looked quiet and still, like nobody was home, but I knowed better. Colonel Sherburn warn’t the kind of man to run away.

Then, all of a sudden, the front door of the house opened, and there stood Colonel Sherburn. He was dressed just like he was when he shot Boggs, with his clean clothes and his proud way of holdin’ himself. He had a double-barrel shotgun in his hand, but he warn’t pointin’ it at nobody. He just stood there on his porch, lookin’ out at the mob, as calm as a summer morning. The sight of him, so steady and unmoving, seemed to suck some of the noise right out of the crowd. Their yellin’ died down a little, though some of the fellas in the back was still whoopin’ and carryin’ on.

Sherburn just stood there for a minute, lookin’ at them. He warn’t scared, not one bit. You could tell that just by the way he held his head, and the way his eyes just kind of swept over them, like they warn’t worth more than a pile of dirty laundry. Then he lifted the shotgun, not aiming it, but just raising it up a little, and he says, his voice cutting through the remaining noise like a sharp knife:

“The idea of you lynching anybody! It’s amusing. The idea of you a-trying to lynch _me_! You don’t know what you’re about, you people. I know you. I know you all and every one of you. I have lived among you for thirty years, and I know what a genuine first-class coward you are. You do not dare to look a man in the face and kill him. You have to have the darkness and a crowd. You have to have the bottle and a crowd. You have to be a majority, and you have to be drunk, and you have to be mean. And then you are brave, and then you are going to lynch somebody. You will kill a man in the dark, but you would not kill him in the day. You will kill a man from behind, but you would not face him. You will kill a man when you are safe, but you would not put yourselves in danger. You think you are brave. You are not brave. You are cowards, every one of you. You are nothing but a pack of curs, and I spit upon you. You are not men, you are dogs. And you act like dogs. You slink and you snarl and you growl, but when a man stands up to you, you run. You are not brave enough to face a single man, but you think you are brave enough to face me. You are wrong. You are mistaken. You are fools.”

He paused then, his eyes still sweeping over them, and the silence had spread through the crowd now like a sickness. Even the ones in the back had hushed up, and you could feel their eyes on him, and on his shotgun. He warn’t shoutin’, he was just talkin’, but every word was like a whip-crack.

“I’m not a brave man,” he says, “but I know how to deal with cowards. And you are all cowards. Every man in this crowd, from the biggest to the smallest, from the oldest to the youngest, is a coward. You came here to lynch me, did you? You came here to commit murder, did you? You came here thinking you were safe in your numbers, thinking that no one would know who fired the shot, or who threw the stone, or who tied the knot. But I know you. I know your faces. I know your names. And I know what you are. You are a shame to the human race. You are a disgrace to your state. You are a blot upon the land. You are nothing. You are worthless. You are less than the dirt under my feet.”

He lowered the shotgun then, just a little, and pointed it at the ground in front of him, but his eyes never left them. He says:

“Now, listen to me, and listen good. You think you’re a big, strong mob, don’t you? You think you’re a force to be reckoned with. You think you’ve got me cornered. But you’re wrong. You’re not a mob. You’re a collection of individuals, each one of you a-shakin’ in your boots, each one of you a-hopin’ that some other fella will do the dirty work. You’re a pack of sheep, and you’re followin’ the loudest one in the flock, even if he’s a fool. And you know what happens to sheep when a wolf comes? They scatter. They run. They bleat. And that’s what you’re a-gwyne to do now. Because I am not a sheep. And I am not a coward. I am a man, and I will stand my ground. And I will tell you, plainly and clearly, that if any one of you, any single one of you, steps one foot over that fence, or tries to lay a hand on my door, you will be a dead man. I will shoot you down like the dog you are. And I will keep shooting, till my gun is empty, and then I will reload, and I will keep shooting, till there is not a single one of you left standing. Do you understand me? Do you hear me? Do you believe me?”

He waited, just looking at them, and the silence was so thick you could almost taste it. The men in the front of the crowd, the ones who had been the loudest and the boldest, was now lookin’ down at their feet, or shifting their weight from one leg to the other. Their faces was no longer red with anger, but kind of pale and ashamed. The young fella with the new rope, his chest wasn’t puffed out no more, and he was tryin’ to hide the rope behind his back. Old man Hooper, he just stood there with his rusty musket, lookin’ like he wished the ground would open up and swallow him.

“You came here in a mob,” Sherburn says, his voice a little softer now, but still sharp, “because you are afraid. You are afraid to face me alone. You are afraid to stand up for your own actions. You are afraid of the law. You are afraid of God. You are afraid of your own shadow. You are nothing but a bunch of hicks, and you are not fit to lynch a dog, let alone a man. You are a disgrace, all of you. Now get out of my sight. Go home. Go back to your dirty little lives. And never again, not one of you, ever think to come near my house with such a foul purpose in your hearts. Do you hear me? Now go. Begone. Be gone from my sight, you miserable curs!”

He took a step forward on the porch, raising the shotgun a little, not aiming, but just like he was ready, and the effect was like a physical blow. The men in the front of the crowd flinched, and then they started to back away. Slowly at first, just a shuffle, and then a little faster, and then they was turning, stumbling over each other, trying to get away. The ones in the back, who hadn’t heard every word, but had felt the change in the air, they started to drift away too, looking confused, or just glad to be going. The mob didn’t break all at once, it just kind of dissolved, like a lump of sugar in a hot cup of coffee. One by one, and then two by two, the men turned their backs and started to walk away, not running, but hurrying, not looking at each other, just wanting to get gone.

The noise of their going was different now, it warn’t a roaring mob noise, but a shuffling, mumbling, ashamed kind of sound. It was the sound of a crowd that had been broken, that had been told what it was, and didn’t like the truth. I seen some of them cast a quick glance back at Sherburn’s house, their eyes full of a mixture of fear and something like wonder. He hadn’t fired a shot. He hadn’t even truly threatened them with one. He had just stood there, and talked, and he had broken them with his words.

I stood there for a long time, watching them all disappear down the street, until the last one was out of sight. Sherburn just stayed on his porch, holding his shotgun, watchin’ them go. He didn’t go back inside until the street was completely empty, and the dust had started to settle again. Then he turned, and went back into his house, and closed the door as quietly as he had opened it. It was like nothing had happened.

But something had happened. Something big. I had seen a mob, full of power and fury, just melt away in front of a single man. And it warn’t because he had a gun, though that helped. It was because he knowed them. He knowed what they was made of. He knowed they warn’t brave at all, just a bunch of loudmouths hiding in a crowd. And he called them on it. He shamed them.

I begun to walk away, my mind a-buzzin’ with what I’d seen. It was a mighty strange thing, this human nature. One minute they was all a-whoopin’ for blood, the next they was slinking off with their tails between their legs, just because one man had stood his ground and spoke some hard truths. It made me think about all the times Jim had told me about folks, and how you gotta understand what they’re like inside. Sherburn, he understood. He knowed just what words to use, and just what kind of look to give, to cut through all their bluster and get right down to their yellow core.

I kept walking, not really knowing where I was going, just needing to put some distance between myself and that awful scene. The sun was getting lower, casting long shadows down the empty street. The air felt cleaner somehow, now that the mob was gone. The whole town seemed to be breathing a sigh of relief, though nobody was saying anything out loud. Folks started to come out of their houses again, careful like, and look around. But they didn’t look at each other, not really. They just looked at the ground, or off in the distance. They all seemed to have seen something they didn’t want to remember, and they was all a little bit ashamed.

I found myself back down near the river, where the steamboats was still tied up, and the roustabouts was still a-loadin’ and a-unloadin’ cargo. The regular sounds of the river town was back, the creak of ropes, the splash of water, the distant hoot of a steamboat whistle. It was a comfort to my ears after all that shoutin’ and carryin’ on. I sat down on a pile of lumber and just watched the river flow by.

It warn’t long before I seen a few of the men from the mob, them that had been loudest, sneak past me, headin’ for the taverns. They didn’t look at me. They just hurried along, still with that hangdog look on their faces. I reckoned they was a-gwyne to try and drown their shame in whisky. But I doubted it would work. Some things, they just stick with you, like a burr under your saddle.

I thought about Jim again. He had always said the river was a teacher. And today, this town, it had taught me something big. It taught me that a crowd ain’t always brave. Sometimes, a crowd is just a big bunch of cowards, all scared of each other, and scared of being found out. And it taught me that one man, with courage and a clear head, can sometimes stand against a whole tide of foolishness and meanness and make it turn back. It was a powerful thing to see, and it made me think a lot about what courage really was. It warn’t about having a gun, or having a mob behind you. It was about standing alone, and facing down what you knowed was wrong, even when it looked like you was a-gwyne to lose.

I laid there for a long time, watching the water, and the stars started to come out, one by one, in the darkening sky. The river kept on flowin’, steady and strong, like it always did, no matter what kind of foolishness folks was a-gwyne to get up to on the shore. And I reckoned that was a good thing. The river, it didn’t care about mobs or lynchings or proud colonels. It just kept on doing its job, carrying its water and its boats, and me and Jim, further and further away from all that trouble. And I was glad of it. I was mighty glad of it. I had seen enough of civilization for one day, and I was hankering for the lonesome, free feel of the raft, and the wise, quiet talk of Jim, out under the wide, open sky, where the only judge was God and the only law was the current. I had seen enough of what men could do when they were together, and I was ready to be apart again. The river was calling, and I was ready to answer.

I got up off that lumber pile, feeling a heap lighter than I had when I sat down. The last bits of daylight was fading out, and the river was turning that deep, dark purple color you see just before the stars get bright enough to hold their own. I could hear the calls of the night birds starting up, and the croak of frogs along the bank, and it was a comfort, like the world was setting itself right again after all the fuss. I struck out along the bank, heading for where I reckoned Jim would have the raft tied up, a little ways downstream from the town. My feet was glad to be moving, and I was eager to put some distance between me and all that ugly business. I kep' thinking about Colonel Sherburn, standing there, cool as a cucumber, facing down that whole mob. It was a sight, I tell you. And then Boggs, dead on the ground, and his daughter screaming. It was a powerful mix-up of feelings, and I was glad to be getting away from it, back to the simple, honest ways of the river.

I walked along, kicking at loose stones and watching the shadows get longer and longer. The air was getting cooler, and a little breeze come up, rustling the leaves in the trees overhead. It felt good, a whole lot different from the hot, dusty, crowded streets of that town. I could still hear the distant hum of voices, but it was fading out, turning into just another background noise, like the river itself. I reckoned the excitement was still high back there, with folks jawing about what all had happened, and that lanky fellow probably still acting out the shooting for a new crowd of gawkers. But it warn't my business no more, and I was mighty relieved of that.

It warn't long before I seen the faint glow of Jim's little fire, tucked away in a thicket of willows down by the water's edge. My heart give a little jump, a good kind of jump, like when you've been away from home for a spell and you see the light in the window. I walked a little faster, and pretty soon I could make out the shape of the raft, tied up snug, and Jim himself, sitting there by the fire, maybe tending a hook, or just enjoying the quiet.

“Jim!” I sung out, and he started up a little, then seen it was me and grinned, a big, wide grin that showed off all his white teeth.

“Huck! Mah goodness, boy, I 'uz gittin’ to worry 'bout you. You been gone a heap o’ time. What all happened up dah?” he says, his voice low and comforting, like the murmur of the river itself.

I slid down the bank and come over to the fire, plopping myself down on a log. “Jim, you ain’t a-gwyne to believe what I seen today. It was the beat of all.”

And so I told him, from start to finish, about Boggs a-coming to town, drunk as a biled owl, and a-carrying on, and how Sherburn warned him, and then how Sherburn shot him down, just like that. And then about the mob, and how they was a-gwyne to lynch Sherburn, and how he faced them down, calling them cowards, and shaming them till they broke and run. I didn’t leave out a single thing, about the daughter, and the Bible, and the lanky man acting it all out. Jim listened, his eyes wide and serious, nodding his head now and then. He didn’t interrupt me once, just listened till I was done.

When I finished, there was a quiet spell, with just the crackle of the fire and the hoot of an owl far off.

“Well,” Jim says, slow and thoughtful, “dat's a powerful heap o’ trouble for one day, Huck. A powerful heap o’ trouble, indeed.” He shook his head. “Dat Colonel Sherburn, he a brave man, or he a foolhardy one, I don’t know which. Mebbe both. But he spoke de truth ‘bout dat crowd, he did. Dey ain’t nuthin’ but a pack o’ sheep, when you git ‘em all together, one pushin’ de odder.”

“That’s just what I was thinking, Jim,” I says. “I seen ‘em, Jim, they was all puffed up and loud, but when he stood up to ‘em, they just wilted. It warn’t courage they had, it was just… noise.”

Jim nodded again. “Dat’s right, Huck. Dey ain’t got no backbone of dey own. Dey jes’ borrows it from de feller next to ‘em. And if dat feller runs, dey all run. But dat Boggs now, dat’s a sad thing. To be shot down like dat, and his daughter. Dat ain’t right, Huck, no how you look at it. He was a fool, a drunken fool, but he warn’t a bad man, from what you says.”

“That’s what that man told me, Jim. Said he was the best-natured old fool in Arkansaw, never hurt nobody. But Sherburn shot him all the same.”

“Some folks, dey got too much pride, Huck,” Jim says, stirring the fire with a stick. “Too much pride to let a fool talk to ‘em. Dey think dey got to prove somethin’. But de good Lord, he don’t make us to go ‘round shootin’ down every fool dat gits too loud. He makes us to have patience, and to forgive, and to understand dat sometimes folks is jes’ folks, and dey ain’t meanin’ no real harm.”

He looked up at the stars, which was shining bright now, a million little pinpricks in the black cloth of the sky. “Dis river now,” he says, “she don’t hold no grudges. She jes’ keeps on flowin’. She don’t care if you a big colonel, or a drunken fool, or a runaway slave. She jes’ carries you along, same as she does de logs and de leaves. And dat’s de way I like it. Out here, ain’t no crowds, ain’t no mobs, ain’t no men shootin’ odder men down for talkin’ too much. Jes’ de river, and de sky, and us.”

I laid back on the log, feeling the warmth of the fire on my face, and listened to the sounds of the night. Jim was right. The river was a whole lot better than the town. It was honest, and it was peaceful, and it didn’t ask no questions, and it didn’t make no judgments. It just was. And I was mighty glad to be back on it.

We stayed there by the fire for a long time, not talking much more, just letting the quiet soak in. After a while, Jim says we better turn in, so we doused the fire and climbed onto the raft. The moon was up now, shining a silver path across the water, and the raft drifted along, soft and easy, rocking us like a cradle. I laid there on my back, watching the stars wheel overhead, and feeling the soft bump of the current against the bottom of the raft. All the noise and the anger and the sadness of the day seemed to fade away, swallowed up by the vast, quiet expanse of the river night. I reckoned Jim was the wisest man I knowed, for all he was a runaway slave. He knowed things about people and about the world that them town folks, with all their schooling and their fancy clothes, they didn’t know at all.

The next few days was pure bliss, I tell you. We drifted along, mostly by night, tying up in quiet coves during the day, where we could fish and cook and just take it easy. The weather was fine, warm and sunny, and the river was wide and lazy. We caught plenty of catfish, and Jim cooked them up on a little fire on the raft, and they tasted better than any fancy meal I ever had in a house. We swam in the river, cooling off in the current, and sometimes we’d just lie on our backs on the raft, watching the clouds drift by, or counting the stars at night. Jim would tell me stories, old conjure tales and songs from home, and I’d listen, sometimes falling asleep with his voice murmuring in my ears.

It was the kind of life a boy was meant to live, free and easy, with no schooling or chores or no Pap to worry about. Just the river, and Jim, and the wide-open world. I almost forgot about that town and its troubles. Almost. Every now and then, a shiver would run through me when I remembered Boggs falling, or Sherburn’s cold eyes, but then I’d look at Jim, peacefully sleeping, or humming to himself, and the feeling would pass.

One morning, just as the sun was burning off the mist from the river, we was drifting around a big bend. The trees on the banks was thick and green, and the air was fresh and sweet. Jim was up front, poling us along, keeping us out of the snags, and I was just lounging, feeling lazy and content. Suddenly, Jim stopped poling and just stood there, looking ahead, his head cocked a little.

“Huck,” he says, low and soft, “you see dat?”

I squinted, trying to make out what he was looking at. The mist was still hanging low over the water in patches, but I could see something floating in the distance. It looked like a lump, a dark shape against the shimmering water.

“What is it, Jim? A log?” I says, sitting up.

“Don’t look like no log to me, Huck,” he says, and there was a strange note in his voice, not exactly fear, but a kind of wary curiosity. “Look like… like somethin’ else. And dat ain’t no natural place for it to be, in de middle o’ de current like dat.”

We drifted closer, and as the mist cleared a little more, I seen it plain. It warn’t a log. It was a small, broken-down flatboat, just barely staying afloat. And on it, there was a heap of rubbish, and on top of that, what looked like a man. Or what used to be a man.

“Jim,” I says, my voice a whisper, “what in the world?”

“Shhh,” Jim says, waving his hand at me. “Let’s git a little closer, but careful now. You don’t know what kind o’ trouble dat could be.”

We eased the raft alongside the derelict boat. It was a pitiful sight. The boat was old and rotting, one side stove in, and half full of muddy water. And the man, he was lying face down, tangled up in some old blankets and rags. He looked like he’d been there a good long while. His clothes was ragged and soaked, and his skin was all puffed up and pale.

“He’s dead, Jim,” I says, a little sick to my stomach. I’d seen dead folks before, but never quite like this, all alone on the river.

Jim reached out with his pole and nudged the flatboat, pushing it gently closer to our raft. “Looks like it, Huck. But we better make sure. And see if dere’s anythin’ we can do, or any papers on him. Somebody gwyne to miss him, mebbe.”

We tied the two boats together, and Jim, being bigger and stronger, carefully clambered onto the flatboat. He turned the man over, real gentle-like. The man’s face was awful to look at, bloated and a ghostly white, with his eyes closed and his mouth a little open. He had a straggly beard and matted hair, all stuck with mud and leaves.

“He done been dead for a while, Huck,” Jim says, shaking his head. “Poor feller. Don’t look like he got much to his name.”

We looked through the few things on the boat: a rusty tin cup, a half-eaten piece of hardtack wrapped in a dirty cloth, and an old, waterlogged sack. Jim opened the sack, and inside there was nothing but a few bits of moldy tobacco, a broken pocket knife, and a small, worn leather wallet. He opened the wallet. Inside was a couple of bent coins, and a folded piece of paper, all damp and crinkled.

Jim carefully unfolded the paper. It was a letter, but the ink was all run and faded. He tried to make out the words, but he couldn’t read much of it, being uneducated like he was. He handed it to me.

I took the letter and tried to read it. It was hard to make out, but I could see a few words clear enough. It was from a woman, writing to her “dearest William,” talking about how she missed him and hoped he was well, and how she prayed for his safe return. It was signed, “Your loving wife, Mary.” There was a date, too, from about two weeks ago.

“Looks like his wife was waiting for him, Jim,” I says, feeling a pang in my chest. It always got me, thinking about folks waiting for somebody who ain’t never a-gwyne to come home.

“Dat’s a sad thing, Huck,” Jim says, his voice soft. “A very sad thing indeed. She gwyne to wait and wait, and she ain’t gwyne to know. What we gwyne to do, Huck? We can’t jes’ leave him here to drift.”

We talked it over for a spell. We couldn’t take the body to the next town. If we showed up with a dead man, there’d be questions, and we was trying to lay low, especially Jim. And we couldn’t just cut him loose. It warn’t right.

“I reckon we got to bury him, Jim,” I says. “Find a spot on the bank, a nice quiet place, and put him to rest.”

Jim agreed. It was the only decent thing to do. We towed the flatboat over to a secluded spot on the shore, a little clearing under some big cypress trees. The ground was soft there, good for digging. We didn’t have no shovels, just a couple of old pieces of wood we used as paddles sometimes. It was slow work, digging with those. But we kep’ at it, taking turns, sweat dripping down our faces in the morning sun.

It took us a long time, longer than I thought it would. The hole wasn’t very deep, but it was enough. We wrapped the poor man in the blankets he was tangled in, and laid him in the hole, gentle as we could. Jim said a little prayer over him, talking to God in his low, soft voice, asking for mercy on the man’s soul and comfort for his grieving wife. I stood there, hat in hand, feeling solemn and quiet. It warn’t much of a funeral, but it was all we could do.

We covered him up, piling dirt and stones on top to keep the wild critters away. Then we broke up his old flatboat, scattering the pieces in the current so it wouldn’t be a danger to other boats. We put the wallet and the letter in a tin box Jim had, figuring maybe someday we could send it to his wife, though it seemed a mighty long shot.

When we was done, the sun was high in the sky, and we was tired and hungry, but a little bit lighter in our hearts. We’d done a good deed, I reckoned.

“Well, Huck,” Jim says, wiping his brow with his sleeve, “dat’s anudder piece o’ trouble we done got ourselves into, but dis one, it warn’t de bad kind. Dis one, we helped a poor soul.”

We got back on the raft and pushed off, letting the current take us downstream again. The river looked different now, somehow. It warn’t just a path to freedom anymore; it was a graveyard, too. A place where folks lived and died, sometimes all alone, with nobody but the water to see it. It made me think about how big the world was, and how small a man could be in it.

That evening, as the sun was setting, painting the sky in colors I ain’t got names for, we seen a steamboat coming upstream, a big, grand one, all lit up, with music drifting across the water. It was a pretty sight, and for a minute, I felt a longing for the noise and the lights, the crowds and the bustle. But then I looked at Jim, sitting there quiet, watching the steamboat pass, and I knew where I belonged. Out here, with him, under the stars, away from all that fuss and bother.

The next day brought its own bit of excitement, though of a different sort. We was rounding a sharp bend, and all of a sudden, we seen a camp on the bank. It warn’t just a few men, but a whole crowd, maybe twenty or thirty, gathered around a roaring fire. They had tents set up, and horses tied to trees, and wagons scattered about. It looked like a big traveling party, or maybe a camp meeting.

“Who dat, Jim?” I says, sitting up straight. “You reckon they friendly?”

Jim looked worried. “I don’t know, Huck. Dem kind o’ crowds, dey can be good, dey can be bad. Mebbe we should try to sneak past ‘em, keep close to de far bank.”

But it was too late. One of the men seen us, and he sung out, “Halloo, there! Come ashore, boys! We got company!”

There warn’t nothing for it but to go ashore. Jim steered us over, and we tied up the raft. As we got closer, I seen that these warn’t just ordinary folks. They was a rough-looking bunch, most of ‘em with long hair and beards, and wearing buckskin and homespun. They had rifles leaning against trees, and pistols tucked into their belts. A big man, with a red bandana around his head and a patch over one eye, steps forward. “Well now, look what the cat dragged in! A couple of river rats, eh? Where you boys headed?”

Silas, he sat next to me and Jim for a spell. “You boys got any skills?” he asks. “We could use some good hands, building a town. Hard work, but honest. And a man gets his share.”

“Well now, that’s good to hear,” he says, clapping Jim on the back. “A good carpenter is worth his weight in gold out where we’re going. You ever think about settling down, making a life for yourselves?”

“We like the river, sir,” I says, trying to be polite but firm. “It’s been good to us.”

It sounded good, in a way, but it also sounded like walls and fences and rules, and all the things we was trying to get away from.

Jim was already asleep, snoring soft, but I couldn’t sleep for a long time. And I knew one thing for sure, I warn’t ready to set down no roots just yet. Not with Jim counting on me, and freedom just around the next bend of the big river. We had to keep moving, had to keep drifting. That was our home, the river, and it warn’t gwyne to tie us down.
