The wagon rolled into town like a thunderclap, a big blue thing with gold letters painted on the side that said “PROFESSOR MARVEL’S MIRACLE ELIXIR,  CURE ALL AILMENTS, STRENGTHEN THE BODY, QUICKEN THE MIND!” It wasn't much of a town, jist a dusty stretch of road with a general store and a blacksmith’s shop, but a wagon like that always drew a crowd. Folks came out on their porches, dogs stopped scratchin’ and looked up, and before long there was a little knot of people gathered, mostly loafin’ men and curious kids like me, standin’ around in the heat.

Out of the wagon steps a fella in a shiny black suit, with a tall top hat and a smile that looked like it was painted on. He was thin as a rail, but he had a voice that could carry all the way to the next county. He set up a little folding table, then started haulin’ out bottles, red and green and blue, all glintin’ in the sun. He cleared his throat, and the sound was like a church bell, loud and clear. “Friends, neighbors, fellow sufferers!” he sings out, “I am Professor Ezekiel P. Marvel, and I have traveled the four corners of this great land to bring you the greatest boon to mankind ever conceived by science and nature!”

He went on like that for a good ten minutes, spouting words I didn’t rightly understand, all about humors and vitality and the ancient secrets of the Orient. He said his elixir could cure the ague, the consumption, the rheumatism, the melancholia, and even the common stubbed toe. He even claimed it would make a man strong as an ox and smart as a college professor, and a woman beautiful as a spring mornin’ and young as a newborn babe. The crowd was a-gawkin’ and a-mutterin’, some lookin’ skeptical, some lookin’ like they already felt better jist from hearin’ him talk.

Then old Jedediah Stone pushed his way through to the front. Jed was a big man, built like a tree trunk, and his face was always set in a frown, like he’d jist smelled somethin’ foul. He never much cottoned to fancy talkers or things that sounded too good to be true. “Professor, you say?” Jed rumbled, and his voice was like gravel rollin’ down a hill. “You got a cure for a broken back, do ye? Professor Marvel’s smile didn’t falter none, though I seen a little flicker in his eyes. “My good sir,” he says, “for such grievous injuries, one might need a full course of treatment, but I assure you, for the general malaise and the creeping aches of age, my Elixir is without peer!”

Professor Marvel puffed up his chest a bit. “Sir, I take offense at such an implication. My Elixir is pure, potent, and proven! It has been blessed by the wise men of Tibet and distilled in the purest mountain springs!”

“Prove it then,” Jed says, quiet-like, but everybody heard him. “You say it makes a man strong? There’s a barrel of molasses over at the general store, full to the brim. Weighs a good three hundred pounds. You want to prove your elixir ain’t snake oil, you lift that barrel over your head.”

A ripple went through the crowd. That molasses barrel was a local legend, a test of strength that only a few men had ever managed, and those was big, strapping farmhands in their prime. Professor Marvel, thin as he was, looked like he might snap in half jist tryin’ to push it. It was old and dark, sticky on the outside, and heavy enough that it took two men to even get it rollin’. You ain’t. Not one of you, standing alone, would dare face a man who’s got a gun and means business. You’d tuck your tail and run. The crowd, it started to rustle a little, like leaves in a light breeze, but it warn’t a movement of anger, it was more like a movement of uneasiness. The men in the front, who had been so brave to clamber over the fence and shout for a rope, they was starting to look around, like they hoped someone else would speak up, or maybe they just wanted to melt into the shadows and disappear. The children, who had been a-skittering and a-shouting, they had stopped their playing, and they was clinging to their mothers’ skirts, their eyes wide and scared.

“Go on,” Sherburn says, and he made a little gesture with his free hand, like he was shooing away a flock of chickens. “Go on home, every last one of you. Go home and think about what you are. Think about how you let yourselves be stirred up by a pack of idle talkers, and how you came here, like mad dogs, to do a thing you wouldn’t dare dream of doing by yourselves. You’re a disgrace to this town, and a disgrace to yourselves. Now get out of my sight.”

He didn’t raise the shotgun, he didn’t even point it directly at them, but it was there, and his words, they was like whip cracks, each one stinging the hide off their pride. The man who had boasted about the steamboat rope, he dropped his eyes and shuffled his feet, and then he slowly, quietly, turned around and started to edge back towards the fence. And then another one, and then another. It warn’t a rush, but a slow, grudging movement, like a big, muddy river that’s trying to find a new path. They didn’t run, not exactly, but they backed away, and they turned away, and they started to melt back into the street, their shoulders hunched, their heads down. The roar, it had gone completely now, and it was replaced by a sort of shuffling, murmuring sound, like a thousand crickets chirping in the dark.

I watched them go, just like Sherburn had told them to, and I couldn’t believe my eyes. A whole town, a whole mob, that had been so wild and fierce and ready to kill just a minute ago, they was now just a bunch of shamed, retreating figures, beaten by the words and the courage of one man. It was a sight to see, a real eye-opener, and it made me think a good deal about how people act, and how they can be one thing when they’re alone, and a completely different thing when they’re all together in a crowd. It warn’t bravery that made them come, no sir, it was something else, something that made them feel safe in their numbers, and when that safety was called out for the lie it was, they just withered up and blew away.

Colonel Sherburn, he watched them go, too, and when the last of them had disappeared around the bend in the road, he just turned around, slow and easy, and went back into his house, without so much as a backward glance. He didn’t slam the door, he didn’t even make a sound, it just closed soft behind him, like a breath. The sycamore tree stood there, quiet and still, and the fence was mostly intact, save for a few bent pickets where the first rushers had tried to break through. The moon was high now, casting long, spooky shadows, and the whole street was empty, except for me. I stood there for a long time, just thinking about what I’d seen. My heart was still thumping, but it warn’t from fear anymore, it was from a kind of wonder, a wonder at the power of a man who warn’t afraid to stand his ground against a whole town.

I reckon I walked home in a daze, my head full of the things Sherburn had said, and the way the crowd had acted. The streets was quiet, strangely quiet, like the town was holding its breath. Every now and then, I’d hear a dog bark, or a baby cry, but it warn’t the usual night sounds, it was too quiet, too still. It was like everybody was back in their houses, sitting in the dark, thinking about what they had done, or what they had been about to do. I wondered if they was ashamed, or if they was just mad at Sherburn for making them look like fools. I figured it was a bit of both, mostly the mad. People don’t like to be called out for what they are, especially when what they are ain’t too pretty.

The next morning, the town was a different place. The sun was shining, and the river was still flowing, and folks was going about their business, but there was a sort of quietness to them, a kind of subdued air. Nobody was talking much, at least not out loud, and the usual joking and carrying on, it warn’t there. I seen a few men from the night before, going into the stores, and they kept their eyes down, and they didn’t look at anybody else. It was like they all had a secret, a bad secret, and they didn’t want anyone to know they was a part of it. The street where Sherburn lived, it was just like any other street, but it felt different, like a place where something big had happened, and the air hadn’t quite cleared yet.

I went down to the river, just to get away from the quietness of the town, and I sat on a log and watched the steamboats go by. The river, it always had a way of making things feel normal again, no matter what had happened on land. It just kept flowing, indifferent to all the troubles of men. I thought about Boggs, and his poor daughter, and how quick life could change. One minute, he was whooping and yelling, and the next, he was dead on the floor of a drug store, with a Bible on his chest. And then I thought about Sherburn, and how he faced down a whole mob, and how he called them out for their meanness and their cowardice. It warn’t a thing I’d ever forget, that’s for sure.

I stayed in that town for a few more days, just watching and listening, but the fun had gone out of it for me. It was like the air had been sucked out of a balloon. The excitement, the wildness, it was all gone, and what was left was just a sort of drab, gray feeling. Folks was still doing their trading, and the wagons was still coming in from the country, but the spirit, it warn’t there. I overheard some talk, here and there, about Sherburn, and how some folks thought he was a hero, and some thought he was a devil, but mostly, they just didn’t talk about it much at all. It was like they wanted to forget it, to push it down and pretend it never happened. But you could see it in their eyes, that they hadn’t forgotten.

I figured it was time for me to move on. This town, it had showed me something important, something about people and mobs, and how easy it is for folks to get caught up in something mean when they’re all together, and how hard it is for them to stand alone. I packed up my few things, which didn’t take long, and I made my way down to the steamboat landing. You’d see a town, and you’d see its ways, and then you’d be off to the next one, with new sights and new sounds, and new stories to hear. But the Professor was all excited, and Bartholomew was already pulling down a different map, tracing lines with his finger.

I thought about all the things I'd seen on the boat, and all the things I hadn't yet seen. I thought about the man who stood alone in the fog, and the family with the guitar, and the old woman with the parrot. Every one of them was a story, and I was supposed to write them down. It was a heavy responsibility, but it also felt like a great privilege. I rolled over and looked out the window, at the dark river rushing by, and the stars blazing in the black sky. I was going to see a lot more stars, I reckoned, in them foothills. And a lot more people. And I was going to write it all down. It warn't going to be easy, but it warn't going to be boring, that was for sure. The sun was coming up then, just a sliver of light on the horizon, and the air was fresh and cool. I knew it was going to be a long day, and a long journey, but I was ready for it. I reckon.

Squirrels chattered in the trees, and once I saw a deer dart across a clearing, its white tail a flag in the green.

They warn’t mountains, not yet, but they were bigger than anything I’d ever walked up, or ridden over. Trees grew thicker here, big oaks and maples, and the underbrush was tangled and wild.

It was strange, being alone. On the boat, there was always somebody around, a familiar face, a voice, the sounds of the engine, the splash of the paddlewheel. Here, it was just the rustle of the leaves, the chirping of insects, the creak of the saddle leather, and the soft plodding of the mare’s hooves. I thanked him, and went to take care of my mare, giving her some water and a good rub down, and then I climbed the ladder to the loft. It was dusty and smelled of old hay, but it was warm. They talked about the weather, about hunting, about folks in other settlements, about the price of goods. Sometimes they’d complain about the government, or about outsiders. The road was just a track at first, winding through some scrubby woods that bordered the river. The air was different here, not so damp, and smelled of pine and dry earth. The sun was getting high, and it was warm. I kept my eyes peeled, trying to see everything, just like Bartholomew said. Squirrels chattered in the trees, and once I saw a deer dart across a clearing, its white tail a flag in the green.

The track got narrower as I rode further, turning into a proper trail, then sometimes just a faint path that I had to follow by instinct, and by looking at the Professor’s map. The land began to roll, gently at first, then rising into proper hills, the foothills Bartholomew had talked about. They warn’t mountains, not yet, but they were bigger than anything I’d ever walked up, or ridden over. Trees grew thicker here, big oaks and maples, and the underbrush was tangled and wild.

It was strange, being alone. On the boat, there was always somebody around, a familiar face, a voice, the sounds of the engine, the splash of the paddlewheel. Here, it was just the rustle of the leaves, the chirping of insects, the creak of the saddle leather, and the soft plodding of the mare’s hooves. “Well, old girl,” I’d say, “looks like it’s just you and me out here, and the crickets, and maybe a snake or two.” She’d just twitch an ear, mostly, and keep on walking, like she knowed exactly where we was going and I didn’t, which was probably true, seeing as how I was just following the Professor’s map, and it warn’t much more than a few lines and squiggles on a piece of greasy paper. The sun, it started to dip down low, painting the sky with all sorts of reds and oranges and purples, and the trees, they looked like black cut-outs against it, tall and still. I was getting hungry, and my backside was getting sore from all that riding, and I started thinking about the boat, and Bartholomew’s cooking, and the soft cot I had down below deck. But then I recollected what Bartholomew said about being a chronicler, and a quiet observer, and I straightened up in the saddle, and told myself that a true chronicler warn’t thinking about no soft cots when there was a whole world to see.

We found a little clearing by a creek just as it got good and dark, and I got down and unsaddled the mare. She rolled in the grass, snorting and kicking up dust, and then she went to drink from the creek, and I poured some oats into a canvas bag for her. I built a small fire, just enough to warm some beans in a tin cup and make a little coffee, and I sat there watching the flames dance, and listening to the crickets and the frogs, and the hoot owl off in the distance. It was peaceful, mostly, but I kept my rifle close, and I kept an eye on the edges of the clearing, just in case. Bartholomew had told me stories about folks out in the wilderness, some good, some not so good, and I warn’t keen on meeting the not-so-good kind unprepared. I spread my blanket on the ground and lay down, staring up at the stars, which were brighter than I ever seen them on the river, like somebody had shaken a whole bag of diamonds across a big black sheet. I thought about the Professor, and his big words, and I wondered if he was right about every man being a book. If that was true, then I was just a little pamphlet, not much writing in me yet, but maybe I was out here to fill some pages.

The next morning, the mist was hanging low in the trees, and everything was wet with dew, and it was colder than I expected. I ate the rest of my cold beans and drank some more coffee, and then saddled up and we started off again. The trail was getting harder to find, sometimes disappearing altogether, and I had to stop and check the map a lot, tracing my finger along the lines, trying to figure out which way was north, and which way was just more trees. The hills were getting steeper too, and the mare, she had to work harder, and sometimes I’d get off and walk beside her, leading her up the inclines.
