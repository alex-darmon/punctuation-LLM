The sun beat down on Rattlesnake Creek something fierce that summer, baking the dust so deep your boots sank with every step you took, and the only shade you could find was under the sagging awnings of the general store or the saloon, where the men folk mostly congregated to chew their tobacco and complain about the heat. There warn't much to do in Rattlesnake Creek, not really, save for watching the dust devils dance in the distance or seeing a new wagon train roll through, which was always a sight to behold and brought a bit of excitement, but they never stayed long. We mostly lived on tales of old prospectors and the occasional brawl that broke out over a poker game, and everybody knew everybody else’s business, whether they wanted to or not.

One day, just when the shadows were starting to stretch a little and the heat was losing some of its bite, a strange contraption rolled into town, drawn by two scrawny mules that looked like they’d seen better days, and perched atop it was a man with a booming voice and a hat so tall it looked like it scraped the sky. He had a big cage on the back of his wagon, too, covered with a tarp, and the mules looked mighty unhappy pulling it. Kids came running from all directions, and even the old timers hobbled out of the saloon, squinting against the late afternoon glare. This fellow, he jumps down from his wagon with a flourish, bowing to the empty street like he was in a grand opera house, and then he hollers out, "Gather 'round, folks! Gather 'round and witness the marvel of the age! Prepare to be astonished by Bartholomew, the Beast of the Badlands!"

He pulls back the tarp with a dramatic yank, and there, sitting inside that cage, was the biggest, mangiest bear I ever did see, all matted fur and sleepy eyes, but with paws the size of dinner plates. It gave a low rumble, and some of the smaller children squealed and jumped back behind their mamas. The showman, Bartholomew he called himself, he starts rattling on about how this bear could do sums, and dance a jig, and even tell your fortune, though I reckon he was making most of it up right there on the spot. He was a skinny man, all talk and no muscle, but he had a way with words that made you want to believe him, even if your common sense was telling you otherwise.

People were laughing and pointing, all happy for a distraction, when all of a sudden a shadow fell over the crowd, and there stood Big Jed Perkins, all six foot five of him, with a chin like a granite rock and a temper that was quicker than a rattler's strike. He was the kind of man who’d just as soon punch you as look at you, and he always wore a frown like the world had personally offended him. He had his hands jammed in his pockets, and he just stared at Bartholomew and that bear with a sneer on his face. “Well, well, what do we got here?” he growls, his voice rumbling like an old wagon wheel over stones. “Another city slicker trying to sell snake oil to honest folks, is it? And that mangy mutt in the cage, you call that a beast?”

Bartholomew, he tried to keep his smile on, but I seen his eyes dart over to Jed, and he swallowed hard. “Why, good sir,” he says, trying to sound all proper, “this is no mere mutt. This is Bartholomew, a specimen of unparalleled strength and intelligence! He has wrestled grizzlies in the mountains and outsmarted trappers in the deepest woods!” Jed just snorts. “Looks like he’s wrestled a barber, more like. He ain’t got no hair left on his hind end. I reckon I could arm wrestle that thing with one hand tied behind my back.” The crowd chuckled a bit, a nervous kind of sound, because everybody knew Jed was strong, stronger than any man in Rattlesnake Creek, but a bear was a different matter.

“Come on now,” Jed says, stepping closer to the cage, “let’s see this beast perform a real feat. Let’s see him try to pull that wagon with one paw. Or better yet, let’s see if he can take a punch, like a real fighter.” Bartholomew’s face went pale. “My dear fellow, that would be highly ill-advised! Bartholomew is a performer, a gentle giant, not a brawler! He responds to kindness, not coercion!” Jed just grins, a mean sort of grin that showed his crooked teeth. “So, he’s a coward, then? Just like his master?” He reaches a big hand through the bars of the cage, intending to poke the bear.

Now that bear, Bartholomew, he warn't doing much before, just swaying a little and blinking slow, but when Jed’s hand went through them bars, that bear let out a roar that shook the dust off the awnings, and he swiped out with one of them dinner-plate paws. It warn't a full-on attack, more like a warning swat, but Jed warn't expecting it, and it caught him right across the arm, tearing his sleeve and leaving three long, red scratches from wrist to elbow. Jed yelled, a surprised sort of yell, and stumbled back, clutching his arm, his face redder than a fresh-picked tomato. The crowd gasped, and then there was a sudden quiet, like everybody forgot to breathe.

Bartholomew, the showman, he starts stammering, “Oh, dear me, I told you, I warned you, he’s temperamental!” But nobody was listening to him anymore. Jed was holding his arm, glaring at the bear, and then he looked at Bartholomew with eyes like burning coals. “You… you let your animal attack me!” he roared, and then he lunged for the cage door, ignoring his bleeding arm. The crowd, they started shouting now, some for Jed, some against, and it turned into a right old ruckus. “Get him, Jed!” one fellow yelled. “That bear ain’t right!” another one hollered. Bartholomew, he knew trouble was brewing, and he looked like a squirrel caught in a trap. He jumped back into his wagon, fumbling with the reins, and with a desperate crack of the whip, he tried to urge his scrawny mules forward. But the wagon was too heavy, and the mules too tired.

Jed, with his arm bleeding and his face contorted in rage, reached the wagon and started trying to unhitch the mules, yelling all the while about justice and how Bartholomew was gonna pay. Other men were rushing forward, some trying to calm Jed down, others egging him on, and it was a mess of flailing arms and angry shouts. Bartholomew, seeing there warn't no escape, he snatched up a small bag from his wagon seat and then, quick as a flash, he bolted into the gathering dusk, leaving his wagon and the growling bear behind. The crowd split then, some running after Bartholomew, hollering for him to stop, and others gathered around Jed and the wagon, still shouting about the bear. It was a good fifteen minutes before things quieted down, and then all that was left was the growling bear in its cage, and Jed sitting on a water trough, nursing his arm, looking madder than a wet hen, and everybody whispering about Bartholomew and how he’d just abandoned his beast. I seen some fellows shaking their heads, saying it warn't right to leave an animal, and others saying Bartholomew got what was coming to him, and it showed you never could trust a traveling showman with a funny hat. I just stood there, watching the dust settle, and reckon that bear was probably gonna be Rattlesnake Creek's newest resident, whether it wanted to be or not.

The bear, he warn't bothered by all the fuss, just sat there in his cage, sniffing at the air, every now and then letting out a little huff that made the timbers of the wagon creak. It was getting proper dark now, and the street lamps weren't much good for seeing clear into that dark corner where the wagon sat, just throwing long, wobbly shadows that made the cage look bigger and the bear look like a proper monster. Folks, they stood around for a spell, gawking and whispering, and then some of the men, the ones who warn't busy chasing Bartholomew or trying to tell Jed how to fix his arm, they started talking about what to do with the critter.

"Well, we can't just leave it there," old Silas said, he being the storekeeper and a man who liked things tidy. "It'll be scaring away all my customers come morning, and Lord knows we need all the trade we can get."

"No, we can't," another fellow agreed, "but what you aim to do with it, Silas? That ain't no house cat, that's a proper grown bear, and a mean one at that, judging by Jed's arm."

Jed, he was still sitting on the water trough, grumbling and trying to tie a torn piece of his shirt around his bleeding arm, but it warn't doing much good. He looked up, his eyes narrow. "Mean, you say? He's a wild beast, that's what he is, and that Bartholomew ought to be hung for bringing such a thing into a Christian town." He spat on the ground. "Someone ought to put a bullet in that varmint, is what I say. Before it gets loose and rips someone else up."

There was a murmur through the crowd, some agreeing with Jed, some looking a bit uneasy. Putting a bullet in a bear, even a caged one, warn't something folks did every day, and it felt a mite like murder, even if it was just an animal. Besides, who was going to do it? And what if the first shot didn't kill it and it got even madder? No one wanted to be the one to get mauled by a dying bear.

"Now hold on, Jed," spoke up Preacher Barnes, who had just come out of the church, looking all solemn like he always did when there was trouble brewing. "We can't be rash. This creature, though it be wild, is God's creation, and we ought to treat it with some measure of respect, even in its captivity."

"Respect?" Jed snarled. "It clawed me, Preacher! Showed me all the respect a bear knows, and that ain't much."

"Still," the Preacher said, adjusting his spectacles, "it's an animal, not a man. Killing it in cold blood seems…unnecessary, if there be another way."

"What other way, Preacher?" a young fella named Billy asked. Billy was always full of questions and not much sense. "You aim to send it to Sunday school?"

A few folks chuckled, even with the tension in the air. The Preacher sighed. "No, Billy, of course not. But perhaps we can find a way to transport it, to a zoo maybe, or release it far, far away in the wilderness where it belongs."

"Zoo?" Silas scoffed. "And who's going to pay for that, Preacher? And who's going to go asking around for a zoo that wants a half-wild river bear? No, we got a problem on our hands, and we need a proper solution."

While they was arguing, a couple of the men who'd gone chasing Bartholomew came back, looking winded and mad. "He got away," one of them puffed. "Slipped into the woods like a ghost. Warn't no catching him in this light."

"And a good thing too, I reckon," the other one said, wiping sweat from his brow. "He had a pistol on him, and I warn't keen on seeing what he'd do if we cornered him."

So that was that for Bartholomew. He was gone, and the town was stuck with his bear. The arguing went on for a good long while, with more and more folks joining in, some saying shoot it, some saying keep it, some even suggesting they could charge admission to see it, which Jed thought was a fine idea if they was aiming to see more folks get clawed. I just listened, watching the bear, who seemed mighty calm for all the fuss, just every now and then giving a soft growl low in its throat, like it was talking to itself.

Finally, old Sheriff Tate, he being a slow but steady man, pushed his way to the front. "Now hold on, folks, hold on!" he said, raising his hands. "No use everyone talking at once. We ain't going to shoot this bear tonight, not without a proper plan. Too dark, too dangerous, and liable to cause more trouble than it solves. And we ain't going to release it in the woods nearby, not when it's already had a taste of town life and might just come back looking for more folks to scratch."

He looked around, then he looked at the bear, then at the wagon. "This wagon here, it's strong, but it ain't exactly a fortress. We need to move this animal somewhere safe, somewhere secure, till we can figure out what to do. The old livery stable, down by the creek, it's got those reinforced stalls for the meanest horses. That'll hold it for a night, I reckon."

There was a general grumble of agreement, mostly because no one else had a better idea, and the Sheriff was the law, after all. So, a dozen men, with the Sheriff directing, started trying to figure out how to move the wagon. It warn't easy. Bartholomew's mules had long since wandered off, likely looking for a patch of grass and some peace and quiet.

The mules had run off, so there warn't nothing for it but for the men to put their shoulders to the wagon and push. It was a big, heavy thing, what with the bear inside it and all the wood it was made of, and the wheels sinking into the dusty road a bit. Sheriff Tate, he barked out orders, saying "Heave-ho!" and "Now, all together!" but it warn't much use. They strained and they grunted, their faces getting red in the dim light of the lanterns folks had brought out, and the wagon moved about a foot, then stopped again. One of the fellas, a short, stocky man named Pease, he slipped on a loose rock and nearly went under a wheel, but someone caught him by the arm. The bear, it just sat there in its cage, looking out through the bars, its eyes shining a bit like wet stones in the light, not making a sound, just watching them like they was all part of some show put on for its amusement.

"We need more hands!" the Sheriff yelled, wiping sweat from his brow. "And a better way to get a purchase on this thing!"
A few more men came forward, mostly young fellas looking to prove something, and they grabbed hold of the wheels and the back of the wagon bed. But it was still a mighty hard job. The road wasn't exactly smooth, and every now and then a wheel would hit a rut, and the whole contraption would lurch, making the bear stir a little inside its cage. It let

out a low rumble, a sound like distant thunder that seemed to come from its very guts, and that made a shiver run right down my spine, though I tried to act like it warn’t nothing. The men pushing on the wagon, they all stiffened up a bit, and a couple of them dropped their hold for a second, looking back at the cage with wide eyes, and the lantern light made their faces look pale and a bit sickly.

“Now, now, boys, keep at it!” Sheriff Tate called out, his voice a little strained, but he kept his own hands on the back of the wagon, pushing with the rest of them. “That critter ain’t going nowhere, not with that cage built the way it is. Just let it grumble, it’s earned the right after being jolted around like this.” He said it like he was trying to convince himself as much as he was trying to convince the others, and I reckon he was, for a bear that size, even caged, could give a man the shakes.

They tried again, all together, and this time they managed to move it a few more feet, maybe a yard or so, before it got stuck again, one wheel sunk deep into a soft patch of earth where a drainage ditch used to be before it got filled in with rubbish and rain-soaked dirt. The sweat was running freely now, even in the cool night air, and I could hear some of the older men panting like spent hounds after a long chase. Their shirts were sticking to their backs, and the dust from the road was making streaks on their foreheads and noses. Pease, the short fella, he was puffing and blowing something awful, looking like he was about to burst a blood vessel, and he kept muttering under his breath about Bartholomew and his “danged bear” and his “no-good mules.” He warn’t saying anything too loud, mind you, for fear the Sheriff might hear, but you could tell he was fit to be tied.

Another fella, a tall, stringy man named Silas, who was known more for his ability to spin a yarn than his strength, he suddenly had an idea. “Sheriff,” he said, wiping his hands on his britches, “we ain’t never going to get this thing moving right without some kind of leverage. We need a good stout pole, or maybe a couple of them, and some rocks to put under the wheels.” He looked around, his eyes darting through the darkness as if expecting a ready-made solution to just appear out of the ground.

Sheriff Tate considered this for a moment, scratching his chin. “Well, Silas, that ain’t a bad notion, not a bad notion at all. But where we going to find stout poles at this time of night, without tearing down somebody’s fence, which I ain’t keen on doing, not with the morning coming and folks finding their property gone?”

Just then, a voice piped up from the back of the crowd. It was old Man Hemlock, who usually just sat on his porch whittling and watching the world go by. He had a reputation for being a bit of a hermit, but he knew more about the land and the river than any ten men put together. “There’s that old cottonwood that fell by Miller’s Mill, Sheriff,” he said, his voice raspy like dry leaves. “Been lying there for a month, and it’s a good stout trunk, already got some limbs trimmed off it. You could break off some of them smaller branches, make good levers. And there’s plenty of flat river stones down by the bank.”

The Sheriff’s face brightened a bit. “Hemlock, you’re a godsend! Alright boys, a few of you, go with Hemlock. Grab some of those branches, the sturdiest ones you can find, and some big flat rocks. The rest of us will try to clear some of this soft dirt from around the wheel.”

So, about half a dozen men, led by old Man Hemlock, headed off into the darkness towards the direction of Miller’s Mill, their lanterns bobbing like fireflies. The rest of us, with the Sheriff still barking orders, started kicking at the dirt and scraping at the mud around the stuck wheel with our boots and any sticks we could find. It warn’t much of a plan, but it was something to do, and it felt like we were making progress, even if it was slow. The bear, it still sat there, watching, its head occasionally turning to follow the movements of the men, but it made no more sounds. It was a big, dark, silent lump in the middle of all our huffing and puffing.

It felt like a long time, maybe twenty minutes or more, before Hemlock and his crew came back, dragging a couple of thick, heavy cottonwood branches behind them. They warn’t perfectly straight, but they looked strong enough, and they had a stack of flat, gray river stones piled in someone’s arms.

“Alright, now!” the Sheriff announced, rubbing his hands together. “Let’s see if we can’t get this wagon unstuck.” He directed two of the stronger men to slide one of the branches under the axle of the stuck wheel, using a large rock as a fulcrum. “Now, all together, push down on that lever! And a couple of you, be ready to shove those stones under the wheel as it comes up!”

It was a delicate business, and it took a few tries. The first time, the branch slipped off the rock with a loud crack, nearly taking a man’s foot off. The second time, the wagon groaned and lifted a bit, but then settled back down before anyone could get a stone under it. The third time, however, with everyone pushing and pulling and yelling instructions, the wheel finally came free of the mud with a squelch, and a couple of men quickly jammed the flat stones underneath it. The wagon was level again, and free. A cheer went up from the crowd, a tired, relieved sort of cheer, and everyone slapped each other on the back, feeling like they’d just conquered a whole army, even if it was just a patch of mud.

“Now, for the pushing,” the Sheriff said, catching his breath. “We’ll put these levers to good use to get us started, and then we’ll keep shoving till we reach the stable. It ain’t far now, just down the main street and then turn left at the old dry goods store.”

They got the levers in place again, and with a mighty heave, the wagon started to roll, slowly at first, then picking up a bit of speed as they got it onto a firmer patch of the road. It was still heavy, but with the added leverage and the combined strength of about twenty men, it moved along steadier than before. I walked alongside, not really helping much, being just a boy, but I wanted to see it all, every bit of it. The street was pretty quiet now, most folks having gone back to bed after the initial excitement, but a few lights were still on in windows, and you could see faces peeking out from behind curtains as we passed.

The lanterns cast long, dancing shadows on the ground, making the men look like giants struggling with some monstrous, shadowy beast. The bear, inside its cage, seemed to be enjoying the ride now, its eyes still gleaming, but it was quiet, maybe a bit bored, or maybe just resigned to its fate. It was a strange procession, a wagon carrying a wild beast, being pushed through the sleeping streets of a small river town by a bunch of tired men under the pale light of the moon and a few flickering lanterns.

We slowly made our way down the main street, past the saloon where a few late-night drinkers were probably still at it, past the general store, and then we turned down the lane that led to the livery stable. The stable was a big, dark building, smelling of horses and hay and old wood. It was built low and stout, with thick walls and heavy doors, made to keep even the wildest horse from getting out. The reinforced stalls the Sheriff had mentioned, they were made for the real troublesome ones, the kickers and the biters, with iron bars set deep into the wood and thick planks that even a mule couldn’t splinter.

“Alright boys, this is it,” Sheriff Tate said, his voice a low rumble now. “Let’s get it in here. We’ll back it up to that big stall door there, the one with the extra-heavy latch. Then we’ll figure out how to get this critter from the wagon to the stall.” This part, I reckon, was going to be trickier than just pushing the wagon.

They grunted and shoved, angling the wagon just right, backing it up as close as they dared to the open door of the reinforced stall. The bear, it let out another low growl as the wagon came to a stop, perhaps sensing that something new was about to happen. Its head moved from side to side, sniffing the air, its nostrils flaring a bit.

“Now, here’s the plan,” the Sheriff said, his voice grave. “We can’t just open the cage door and hope it walks in. This ain’t no trained poodle. We’re going to have to use a ramp, and we’re going to have to do it quick. Bartholomew, you got any kind of heavy planks back there in your wagon, anything we can use as a ramp?”

Bartholomew, who had mostly been standing back and wringing his hands, looking more worried about his lost mules than the bear, he finally spoke up. “Well, Sheriff, I got that loading ramp I use for my heavier crates, but it ain’t designed for a beast like this. It might hold, it might not.”

“It’s all we got,” the Sheriff said, not wasting time arguing. “Get it out.”

So, a few men helped Bartholomew pull out a heavy, splintered wooden ramp from the back of the wagon, groaning with the effort. It was a stout piece of work, but you could see the wear and tear on it, the places where it had been scuffed and scarred by years of heavy use. They positioned it carefully from the back of the wagon to the opening of the stall, making sure it was as steady as they could get it.

“Now,” the Sheriff said, looking at the bear, which was starting to pace a bit inside its cage, bumping against the bars. “We need to get this cage door open, and then encourage it to walk down the ramp. We’ll need a few brave souls with pikes, or anything long and sturdy, to sort of guide it from behind. Nobody gets too close, mind you. If it bolts, we scatter. We do *not* try to be heroes. We just get it in that stall.”

A few men stepped forward, grabbing long pitchforks and other tools that were lying around the stable. Their faces were grim, and I could see their hands shaking a bit in the lantern light. This warn’t like pushing a wagon; this was dealing with a wild animal, and there was a real sense of danger hanging in the air now.

“Alright, Bartholomew,” the Sheriff said, “you built this cage, you know how that latch works. You open it, nice and slow. The rest of us, we’ll stand ready.”

Bartholomew, looking pale as a ghost, slowly made his way to the back of the cage, his hands trembling as he reached for the heavy iron latch. It clicked open with a loud, ominous sound that echoed in the quiet stable. The bear let out a low growl, a deeper one this time, and it bumped its head against the now-unlocked door.

“Easy, boy, easy,” the Sheriff murmured, though it warn’t clear if he was talking to the bear or to Bartholomew.

The bear pushed the door open a crack, and then a bit more, and it stuck its great snout out, sniffing the air with a deep, rumbling inhale. Its eyes, those wet, dark stones, darted around the stable, taking in the dim corners, the shadowy stalls, the nervous men holding their pikes.

Then, with a sudden, surprisingly quick movement for such a big beast, it stepped out of the cage, its heavy paws thudding onto the wooden ramp. The ramp groaned under its weight, but held. The men with the pikes were ready, gently nudging its hindquarters, trying to steer it towards the stall. It moved slowly, ponderously, its great bulk swaying from side to side. Its fur, which looked shaggy and dark in the night, seemed to ripple with a strange power.

For a moment, it paused at the entrance to the stall, its head held high, and it let out a loud, snorting sound, like it was questioning the whole arrangement. The men held their breath. Then, with another nudge from a pike, and a soft, coaxing voice from the Sheriff, it finally lumbered forward, across the threshold, and into the dark confines of the reinforced stall.

As soon as its tail was clear of the doorway, two men sprang forward and slammed the heavy wooden door shut with a resounding thud that echoed through the stable. Another fella quickly dropped the thick iron bar into place, securing the door with a clanking sound that sealed the bear’s fate, at least for the night.

A collective sigh of relief went through the men, and several of them sank to the floor, panting, their faces streaked with sweat and grime. Their hands, which had been clutching the pikes so tightly, were now limp and shaking. The danger was past, at least for now.

“Well, boys,” Sheriff Tate said, leaning against a post, wiping his brow with the back of his hand, a tired smile spreading across his face, “that’s that. We got him. Good work, all of you. That critter ain’t going anywhere till morning, and then we’ll figure out what in the blazes Bartholomew’s going to do with it.” He looked at Bartholomew, who was still leaning against the wagon, his chest heaving, his face still pale.

Bartholomew just nodded, too exhausted to speak. His eyes, however, held a look of profound relief, mixed with a deep, abiding weariness. He’d had a night to remember, and not in a good way, I reckon.

The men started to disperse, some heading for home, eager for their beds, others talking in low voices about going to the saloon for a well-earned drink. Pease, the short fella, he just shook his head and mumbled something about needing a long soak and a shot of something strong. Silas, the storyteller, he was already starting to elaborate on the tale, saying how the bear had nearly broken free and how they’d wrestled it into submission, which warn’t exactly how it happened, but it made for a better story. I reckon he’d have that tale going for weeks, getting grander with each telling.

I lingered a bit, watching the stable door where the bear was now confined. I could hear it rustling around inside, a low huffing sound, and then a great thud as it probably settled down. It was a strange feeling, knowing that wild thing was in there, just a few feet away, breathing and moving. It made the air feel thick and heavy, even after all the men had gone.

Sheriff Tate, he was the last one to leave, after making sure the stable was locked up tight and posting a young fella, a deputy named Billy, to keep watch for a few hours till morning. Billy looked a bit nervous, sitting on a hay bale with a shotgun across his lap, and I reckon he warn’t going to get much sleep that night, not with that bear growling in the darkness.

I walked home by myself, the street quiet and empty now, save for the occasional dog barking in the distance. The moon was high, casting a silver light on everything, and the air was cool and fresh, but I could still feel the lingering tension of the night’s events. My mind was full of the bear’s dark eyes, its heavy thudding steps, and the collective sigh of relief when it was finally locked away. It was a night that would be talked about for a long time in that little town, that’s for sure.

The next morning, the sun rose bright and early, casting long shadows down the dusty streets. The town was abuzz with talk of the bear. Folks who had slept through the night, they gathered in knots on street corners, listening to the exaggerated accounts of those who had been there. Silas, he was holding court in front of the general store, waving his hands about and describing the bear’s “fiery breath” and “claws like butcher knives,” which was a good deal more colorful than the truth, but it certainly kept the crowd entertained.

Sheriff Tate, he looked tired but relieved. He was standing outside the livery stable, sipping coffee and talking to Bartholomew. Bartholomew, for his part, looked like he hadn’t slept a wink, his eyes red-rimmed and his clothes still rumpled from the night before. He was still worried about his mules, he told the Sheriff, but even more worried about what to do with the bear.

“It ain’t a pet, Bartholomew,” the Sheriff said, shaking his head. “And it ain’t exactly a common sight around these parts. We can’t just keep it in the stable indefinitely. It’s a wild animal, and it needs to be in the wild.”

“But if I turn it loose nearby, Sheriff,” Bartholomew pleaded, his voice cracking, “it’ll just come back, or worse, it’ll cause trouble for some farmer’s livestock. I can’t have that on my conscience. And taking it all the way back to the mountains, that’s a journey I ain’t equipped for now, not with my mules gone.”

“Well, you brought it here, Bartholomew,” the Sheriff said, sighing. “It’s your problem, but it’s also become the town’s problem now, seeing as it’s in our stable. We need a solution, and quick.”

Just then, old Man Hemlock came ambling up, carrying his whittling knife and a piece of pine wood. He listened for a bit, then cleared his throat. “Sheriff, Bartholomew, if you ask me, there’s only one sensible thing to do with a bear that’s been brought to town for folks to see. You got to make a spectacle of it.”

Both the Sheriff and Bartholomew looked at him, puzzled. “A spectacle, Hemlock? What in tarnation are you talking about?” the Sheriff asked.

“Well, Sheriff,” Hemlock said, whittling away at his pine wood, tiny curls of wood falling to the ground, “there’s plenty of folks around here, and in the next county over, who ain’t never seen a live bear up close. Bartholomew here, he brought it for that very purpose, even if it warn’t in the way he planned. Why not charge a nickel, or maybe a dime, for folks to come have a look at it? Set up a little show. Get some planks, build a fence around the stall, make it safe. Folks’ll come. And Bartholomew, he can use the money to get himself some new mules and take that bear a good long way from here, where it belongs.”

Bartholomew’s eyes widened a bit. “A show? You mean, like a carnival?”

“Something like that,” Hemlock nodded, pleased with himself. “Just for a day or two, mind. Long enough to make some coin. It’s better than just turning it loose, and it’s better than letting it sit there costing the town hay. And folks, they’d like a good diversion, especially after that business with Boggs and Sherburn yesterday. Something to take their minds off things, something a bit less… permanent.”

One of them had a big Bowie knife tucked into his belt, and the other one had a bludgeon that looked like a hickory limb, mighty stout. This warn't no Professor with his little bottles; this was a whole different kind of show, and it felt a lot more dangerous, too.

I sidled up close enough to see the cage better. The growling was coming pretty steady now, and I could hear a sort of snorting sound, too. The dark shape, it was long and low, and it had a rough, scaly hide, or so it looked in the dimness of the cage, and I thought I could make out a tail, thick and powerful. It was bigger than any catfish I ever seen, that's for sure, and even bigger than the biggest alligator I'd ever heard tell about, though I hadn't seen many myself. The showman, he was a-watching the money box fill up, and when he judged there was enough folks willing to pay, he let out another big yell.

"Alright, folks, the moment you've all been a-waiting for! Step right up, pay your dues, and witness the eighth wonder of the world! But a word of warning, folks, keep your hands to yourselves, and whatever you do, don't you be a-sticking your fingers through the bars! This here critter, he's got a temper like a rattlesnake and a bite like a steel trap, and he ain't particular about what he gnaws on!"

The crowd surged forward, and I got swept along with them, handing over a nickel I'd been saving for a plug of tobacco. It was worth it, I reckoned, just to see what all the fuss was about. As I got closer, the smell hit me, a thick, musky, fishy smell, but stronger, more like a swamp that had been baking in the sun for a month. And the growling, it was deeper now, a low rumble that vibrated through the planks of the scow.

When I finally got a good look inside the cage, my eyes had to get used to the shadows. It was a big, lumpy thing, alright, long and greeny-brown, with what looked like big, bony plates on its back. It had a long snout, and I could see glints of teeth in the gloom. It was an alligator, sure enough, a mighty big one, bigger than any I'd seen pictures of, but not a "man-eater" from the bayous. It looked more like a plain old alligator, probably caught right here in the Mississippi or one of its side rivers, and then they'd been a-starving it to make it mean, or maybe just a-poking it with sticks to make it act ferocious. It was moving slow and sluggish, though, not like it was all that angry, more like it was tired, or maybe just bored.

The showman, he had another spiel ready. "Now, folks, observe the sheer power of this creature! See the muscle in that tail, the glint of those razor-sharp teeth! Why, a single snap of his jaws could sever a man's leg clean off, without so much as a second thought!" He pulled a piece of raw meat, a big slab of something dark and bloody, from a bucket, and held it up with a pair of long tongs. The alligator didn't really react, just sort of twitched its head a little. The crowd was silent, all eyes on the cage.

"Now, for a special demonstration," the showman announced, "I will show you the hunger of this beast!" He swung the meat in front of the bars, but the alligator just laid there, not even opening its mouth all the way. The showman poked it a couple of times with the tongs, making a frustrated grunt under his breath, but loud enough for me to hear. Then he let out a fake cough, and said, "Ah, he's a shy one, this fellow, a bit camera-shy with all you fine folks a-watching! But fear not, his hunger is ever present!"

He then slammed the meat down onto the deck in front of the cage. "There, you see! He smells the fresh meat, he knows his dinner awaits!" He waited a moment, but the alligator still didn't move much. A couple of men in the crowd started to grumble a little. The showman quickly grabbed a bucket of water and splashed it on the alligator, and then it did stir, opening its mouth a little wider, letting out another low growl. The showman quickly threw the meat into the cage, and the alligator, with a slow, deliberate motion, finally snapped its jaws down on it, not with any great ferocity, but more like a lazy old dog taking a bite.

"There! You see! The power! The speed! A truly fearsome beast!" the showman yelled, wiping a hand across his brow. He was sweating now, I noticed. The crowd, they was mostly impressed, though some of the smarter-looking ones, like me, could tell it warn't quite the terrifying spectacle he was making it out to be. They paid their money, though, and they got to see a big alligator eat a piece of meat, and that was enough for most folks, who hadn't seen such a thing in a cage before.

But then, a new voice spoke up from the back of the crowd, a loud, clear voice, and it sounded mighty unimpressed. "Now hold on a minute there, partner! You call that a Mississippi Sea Serpent? Why, I've seen bigger mud turtles than that sorry critter! And faster, too!"

All heads turned to see who was talking. It was a man, tall and lean, with a long grey beard that hung down to his chest, and a wide-brimmed hat that shaded his eyes. He had a look about him like he'd spent more time on the river than on dry land, and he was chewing on a plug of tobacco, spitting a brown stream into the water every now and then. He warn't nobody's fool, I could tell that right off.

The showman's gold tooth stopped flashing, and his smile tightened. "And who might you be, old timer, a-questioning the wonders of nature, and the hard work of an honest showman?" he said, his voice taking on a harder edge.

"Name's Silas, and I ain't questioning nature, sonny," the old man replied, pushing his way through the crowd, "I'm questioning your nature, which looks mighty like a fraud to me. That ain't no man-eating leviathan, that's just a common alligator, likely caught with a hook and line down in the sloughs, and then dressed up with a fancy name to fleece these honest folks of their hard-earned coin!"

A ripple went through the crowd. Some folks mumbled, some looked at the alligator with new eyes, and some looked at Silas with admiration. The showman, he puffed up his chest, his face getting red. "Why, you old river rat! You got no call to be a-spoiling my show! This here is a genuine specimen, a rarity, a marvel!"

"Marvel, my foot!" Silas snorted, spitting again. "I've wrestled bigger gators than that out of my trotlines myself! Why, that one looks half-starved and half-asleep! It ain't even got the spirit to chase a minnow!"

The showman's two rough-looking helpers started to move towards Silas, their hands resting on their weapons. But Silas didn't flinch. He just stood there, looking the showman square in the eye. "And what's more," he continued, "I reckon I know where you got him, too. Down near the old cypress bend, didn't you? Where that fella down there, he's been a-missing his prize gator, said he was a-training him to pull a little cart for his grandkids. Said he had a name for him, too. What was it now? Oh, yes, 'Snapper'!"

A snicker went through the crowd. "Snapper!" someone repeated, and then more folks started to laugh. The showman's face went from red to purple. The alligator, as if on cue, let out a sort of whimper, not a growl, and then settled down to sleep, looking for all the world like a big, tired dog.

"You're a liar, old man!" the showman roared, taking a step towards Silas. "You're just trying to ruin my business! My show is the talk of the river, and you're just jealous!"

"Jealous of what?" Silas retorted, a dry chuckle rumbling in his chest. "Jealous of you calling a sleepy gator a sea serpent? I got more sense than that, sonny. Now, you tell these good folks the truth, or I'll tell it for you, and it won't be as polite."

The showman hesitated, looking from Silas to the crowd, and then to his two menacing helpers, who seemed a bit less eager to jump into a fight with a respected old river man. The crowd was clearly on Silas's side now; there was a murmur of agreement, and some folks were starting to demand their money back.

"Alright, alright!" the showman yelled, throwing his hands up in exasperation. "So he ain't a man-eater! So he ain't from the deep bayous! But he's a mighty big alligator, ain't he? And you paid your nickel to see a big alligator, didn't you? What's the harm in a little showmanship?"

"The harm," Silas said slowly, "is in calling something a terror when it ain't, and making folks believe a lie. That's the harm. And stealing another man's pet gator, that's harm too, plain and simple."

The showman looked trapped. He tried to bluster a little more, but his heart warn't in it anymore. The crowd was turning ugly. People were shouting, "Give us our money back!" and "Hoax!" and "Thief!" The two rough men by the money box suddenly looked very small and nervous.

In the midst of the shouting, something unexpected happened. The alligator, "Snapper," maybe stirred by all the commotion, or maybe just wanting to stretch, suddenly pushed hard against the back wall of its cage. The rusty bars, already weakened, groaned and then, with a loud CRACK, one of the iron rods snapped. The alligator gave a slow heave, and the broken bar, now hanging loose, left a gap wide enough for its snout.

A collective gasp went up from the crowd. The showman's eyes went wide. "The gator! He's a-getting out!" he shrieked, his showman's voice now replaced by a genuine panic.

The alligator, perhaps sensing freedom, pushed its head through the gap, then its shoulders. It warn't moving fast, but it was moving. Folks screamed and started to scatter, pushing and shoving to get off the scow and back onto the solid ground of the levee. The two rough helpers, instead of trying to contain the beast, just turned tail and ran, trampling over folks in their haste. The showman, he let out a yelp and jumped overboard into the river with a big splash, swimming for dear life towards the opposite bank.

I was quick, I reckon. When I saw the cage starting to buckle, I was already making my way to the edge of the scow, not waiting for the crowd to stampede. I climbed over the side and dropped onto the levee, just as the alligator, with a final surge, slithered completely out of its cage. It landed on the deck with a soft thud, then turned its head, looking around with slow, blinking eyes, seeming just as bewildered as anyone.

It didn't go chasing after the fleeing people, though. It just sat there for a moment, then it yawned a wide, toothy yawn, and then, with another slow, deliberate motion, it slid gracefully into the river. A few brave souls who hadn't run as far stood on the levee, watching as the alligator, "Snapper," swam off into the muddy waters of the Mississippi, free as a bird and probably mighty glad to be rid of the showman and his loud hollering.

Silas, the old river man, he stood there, leaning on a post, a small smile playing on his lips, watching the alligator disappear. "Well, I reckon that takes care of that," he mumbled, and then he let out a deep, satisfied chuckle. "Good for you, Snapper, good for you."

The crowd was a mess. Some folks was still running, some was standing around, panting and laughing nervously, and some was complaining about their lost nickels and dimes. The showman was nowhere to be seen, still swimming his way across the river, or maybe he'd found a log to cling to. His scow, with its raggedy awning and broken cage, floated idly by the bank, a monument to a failed hoax. The money box, I noticed, was overturned and empty.

I watched the last of the commotion die down, and then I turned away. It warn't much of a show, I reckoned, but it had ended with more excitement than it had started with, and that was something. I thought about how easy it was for folks to believe something, if it was told to them with enough bluster and shouting, and how quickly they'd turn on you when the truth came out. It reminded me a little of that Boggs business, how folks loved to see a spectacle, even if it was a tragic one.

I decided to head towards the steamboat landing. The sun was getting low, and the air was getting cooler, and I figured a good long look at the river would clear my head of all the nonsense and tomfoolery I'd seen that day. The river, it was always honest, always moving, always showing you things as they really was, without all the fancy words and the pretending.

As I walked, I saw the Professor and his two men again, just coming out of the saloon. The Professor's shoulders was slumped a little, and he had a glum look on his face, and his two men, they was still a-grumbling to each other. They must have heard about the alligator show and its ignominious end, or maybe they was just nursing their own sorrows. It seemed like this town warn't a good place for folks trying to make a quick buck off other people's gullibility, not today anyways. Or maybe it was just a bad day for tricksters in general.

I got to the landing just as a big, fancy steamboat, the 'Sultana Queen,' was a-pulling away from the shore, its paddlewheel churning the water into a white foam. The whistle let out a long, mournful blast that echoed across the river, and the smoke from its stacks drifted like white clouds across the sky. People on the deck was waving to folks on the shore, and it looked like a mighty grand way to travel. I stood there for a long time, watching it go, thinking about where it was going, and all the places it would see, and all the folks it would carry.

The river was wide and powerful, carrying its secrets and its stories downstream, never stopping, never changing its mind. It warn't like people, always a-trying to fool each other, always a-making things out to be what they warn't. The river just was. And as I stood there, watching the last sliver of the 'Sultana Queen' disappear around a bend, a new feeling started to stir in me, a feeling like it was time for me to be moving on too, time to leave this town and its spectacles behind, and find out what other adventures the river had in store for me, the ones that warn't bought and sold for a nickel or a dime, but was just there, waiting to be seen.

I watched the sun dip below the horizon, painting the sky in fiery oranges and purples, and the river turned to gold, reflecting the last light of the day. The air grew still, and the sounds of the town faded into a distant hum. Only the soft lapping of the water against the bank, and the occasional croak of a frog, broke the silence. It was a peaceful sound, a comforting sound, and it reminded me that for all the fuss and bother of human doings, the big world kept on turning, and the river kept on flowing, and there was always a new day, and a new bend, and a new story just around the corner.

I started to walk back towards the outskirts of town, where I had left my raft hidden amongst some thick willows. I figured I'd fetch some supper, maybe a piece of cornbread and some dried meat I had stashed away, and then I'd just settle down for the night, listening to the river. Tomorrow, I'd push off early, with the first light, and leave this place behind. It had been an interesting day, with its share of laughter and sadness and outright foolishness, but I was ready for something simpler, something quieter.

As I passed the dark, empty scow where the alligator show had been, I saw a couple of young boys, daring each other to jump aboard and see if "Snapper" had left any treasure behind. They was whispering and giggling, their voices carrying clear in the twilight. They didn't find anything, of course, just the broken cage and the muddy deck. They was still young enough to believe in wonders, even after seeing a hoax exposed. I reckoned that was a good thing, to keep a bit of that belief alive, even if you had to poke at it with a stick every now and then to make sure it warn't just a trick.

I reached my raft, nestled deep in the shadows, and pulled away the branches that hid it. It felt good to touch the rough wood, to feel the gentle sway of the water beneath it. This was my home, out here on the river, where things was plain and honest, and a man knew where he stood. I lay down on the deck, my hands behind my head, and looked up at the stars, which was beginning to prickle the darkening sky. They was countless, and they was real, and they didn't try to sell you anything or tell you no lies.

A cool breeze stirred the leaves of the willows, and the river whispered its endless song. I thought about Boggs, and Sherburn, and the Professor, and the showman with his alligator, and how all their troubles and their triumphs seemed so small, so fleeting, against the vastness of the river and the sky. It made a body feel mighty humble, and mighty free, all at the same time. I closed my eyes, and soon enough, I was asleep, dreaming of faraway lands and the open road, or in my case, the open river, waiting just beyond the next bend. The river, it always had a way of sorting things out, of washing away the worries of the day, and making ready for whatever was coming next. And I was ready for it, too. I was ready for whatever the good old Mississippi had in store.

The next morning, the sun was just starting to poke its head over the trees when I woke up. The air was cool and damp, smelling of mud and green things, and the river was going along quiet-like, not making much of a fuss. I stretched myself out on the raft, feeling the knots in my back, and watched the mist curl up off the water. It was a sight that always made me feel like things was right with the world, no matter what kind of foolishness I’d seen the day before. The stars had faded out, leaving just a pale blue overhead, and pretty soon the whole eastern sky was turning pink and gold, like somebody had spilt a painter’s bucket right up there.

I untied the raft from the willow branches, careful not to make much noise, and gave a push with my pole. The current caught us gentle, and we started drifting down, past the sleepy banks where the trees stood tall and dark against the growing light. It was just me and the river again, and that was how I liked it best. No shouting, no fighting, no folks trying to swindle you or shoot you. Just the steady, soft chuckle of the water against the logs, and the occasional splash of a fish jumping, or a turtle sliding off a half-sunken branch.

I had a piece of cornbread left, and I gnawed on that for breakfast, washing it down with a scoop of river water that tasted mighty fresh in the cool morning. I watched the banks slide by, one after another, all looking mostly

the same to a body that warn't looking for nothing particular. But I was, in a way, always looking for something, though I never knew what it was till it showed up. The sun climbed higher and higher, and the mist burnt off, and pretty soon the air was getting warm, and the flies started to buzz around my ears, which was a mighty nuisance, but a body gets used to such things on the river. I fished for a spell, with a line I'd rigged up from some twine and a bent nail, but the fish warn't biting, or maybe they was too smart for my bait, which was just a piece of old cornbread. So I laid back down and just let the river do its work, carrying me along like I was just another stick floating down to the sea.

The day wore on quiet and peaceful, and I was just about to doze off again when I heard a sound, a sort of low humming, that slowly got louder and louder. It warn't a steamboat, because I knew the sound of them, and this was different, more like a lot of folks talking low, all at once. There was smoke coming up from some of them, and washing hanging out to dry, and folks moving about, and dogs barking, and children playing, and it was a sight to behold, it surely was.

I wanted to watch them for a spell, because a body can learn a powerful lot just by watching, and I had learned that lesson good back in the town where Boggs got shot. You never knew what sort of trouble a new crowd might bring, or what sort of fun, for that matter, so it was always best to be careful.

The flotilla came nearer and nearer, and I could hear the voices plainer now, and a fiddle playing a jig, and a woman singing, a high, reedy sound that carried over the water. I seen men fishing from the sides of their boats, and women tending fires in little clay ovens, and children splashing in the shallows, chasing after minnows. There was a big flatboat in the middle of the string, and it looked like the main one, with a sort of canvas awning stretched over it, and a sign painted on the side, but I couldn't make out what it said from where I was.

Or maybe he just felt like his business was finished there, and it was time to move on to the next place. I didn’t know. There was a lot of things about Colonel Sherburn that I didn’t understand, and I reckoned I probably never would. He warn’t the kind of feller you could just figure out easy.

I finally moved, my legs feelin’ stiff and cold. I walked over to where the skiff had been, and there was just a faint trace in the mud where it had rested. Our skiff was gone. Well, not our skiff, but *my* skiff, the one I’d borrowed from that feller up the river. And now Sherburn had taken it. It warn’t somethin’ I could complain about, not after what he’d just done. Besides, I reckon he needed it more than I did, seein’ as he was leavin’ town and all. I could always find another skiff, or just swim across to the raft if I had to.

I felt a little bit relieved, to tell you the truth. It warn’t my problem no more, the town and its troubles. Sherburn was gone, Boggs was dead, and the crowd had scattered. It was just me and the river again, and the thought of Jim waitin’ for me, safe on the raft. That’s where I belonged, out on the river, floatin’ free, away from all these tangled-up human doings.

I made my way down to the water’s edge, where I’d left the big raft hidden under some willows. I pushed aside the branches, and there it was, a dark shadow on the water. I climbed aboard, careful not to make no noise, and felt the familiar sway under my feet. It was like comin’ home, a real home, after bein’ in a strange and dangerous place.

I found Jim curled up under his blanket, snorin’ soft. I didn’t wake him. I just sat down beside him, pullin’ my own blanket around me. The mist was gettin’ thicker now, clingin’ to everything, and the air was damp and cold. I looked out into the white fog, where the river flowed silent and unseen. It felt like the world had shrunk down to just me and Jim and the raft, floatin’ in a little pocket of quiet.

I laid there for a long time, starin’ up at the patch of sky I could see through the willows. The stars was gone, swallowed up by the mist. I thought about all the things I’d seen that day. The excitement, the anger, the fear, the courage, the shame, and finally, the quiet, empty aftermath. It was a lot for a feller to take in, and it made me feel older than my years, somehow. It made me realize that there’s more to folks than what you see on the surface, and that the biggest noises ain’t always the most powerful things. Sometimes, it’s the quiet ones, the ones who don’t say much but mean what they say, that make the most difference.

I closed my eyes then, the pictures of Boggs and Sherburn still playin’ in my mind, and the sound of that girl cryin’ in the alley, and the silent, steady pull of Sherburn’s oars disappearin’ into the mist. It was a mighty strange world, full of mighty strange people, and I was just a boy floatin’ through it, tryin’ to make sense of it all. But I reckon I was learnin’ somethin’, too, with every bend in the river, and every town we passed by. I was learnin’ about folks, and about what makes them tick, and what makes them turn. And maybe, just maybe, that was a kind of education that warn’t found in no schoolhouse, but was just as important, or maybe even more so. I just hoped I’d live long enough to learn it all, and not get myself killed in the process, like old Boggs. With that thought, I finally drifted off to sleep, the gentle rockin’ of the raft a comfort against the wildness of the day that was past.
