The dust in Harmony Gulch was always a-churning, whether it was the wind kicking it up from the dry creek bed or the wagons a-rolling through on market day. Most days, it was just the sun beating down and folks moving slow, like they was saving up their energy for some big to-do that never quite come along. But one morning, when I was sweeping out the front of Jed’s General Store, I seen a sight that made me drop my broom right there in the road.

A fancy yellow wagon, with red wheels and pictures of a smiling man on the side, come a-rattling into town, pulled by two tired-looking bay horses. On the side of the wagon it said, “Professor Phineas Quill’s Universal Life Elixir! Cures All Ills!” And perched up high on the driver’s seat was a little man, all spruced up in a black suit and a hat with a feather in it, a-waving to the few folks who had stopped to stare. He pulled up right smack in front of the saloon, which was usually the busiest spot, and begun to set up his stand.

He hopped down with a flourish, like a frog jumping off a lily pad, and started a-pulling out bottles and little glass cups and a big banner that said, “Witness the Miracles!” He was a little fella, with a mustache that curled up at the ends like a couple of pig’s tails; and his eyes moved faster than a hummingbird’s wings. By-and-by, a small crowd had gathered, mostly idlers and a few women who’d come out to see what the fuss was about. The Professor clapped his hands together and sang out, “Ladies and gentlemen, boys and girls, step right up and witness the marvel of the age! I am Professor Phineas Quill, and I hold in my hands the very essence of health, bottled for your convenience!”

He went on like that for a good half hour, talking faster than a rattler can strike, telling stories of folks cured of everything from the consumption to a bad case of the blues. He held up a bottle of dark, syrupy stuff, thick as molasses, and declared, “This here elixir, made from rare roots and mountain spring water, will set your humors straight, clear your mind, and put the spring back in your step! Why, I seen a man so feeble he couldn’t lift a spoon, take but one sip of this, and by gum, he was out a-chopping firewood the very next morn!”

The crowd was a-muttering and a-nodding, some looking like they believed every word, others just enjoying the show. Then, a voice boomed out from the edge of the crowd, deep and gravelly like stones rolling down a hill: “Hold on there, Professor! What’s all this balderdash you’re a-spouting?”

It was Old Man Hemlock, of course. He was a big fella, built like an oak tree, with a beard that could’ve housed a family of squirrels, and eyes that pierced you like ice picks. He warn’t one to suffer fools, and he had a reputation for calling a spade a spade, no matter who was a-holding it. The Professor looked a mite startled, but he puffed up his chest and smiled wide.

“My good sir,” he says, “I perceive you are a man of discerning taste, a true connoisseur of the finer things in life! Step closer, and allow me to demonstrate the unparalleled efficacy of my Universal Life Elixir!”

Old Man Hemlock pushed his way through the crowd, making folks scatter like chickens, and stood right in front of the Professor’s wagon. He put his big, calloused hands on his hips and squinted. “I ain’t no connoisseur of snake oil, Professor. I’m just a man who’s seen more hucksters than hairs on your chin, and every one of ’em was a-gwyne to make a fortune off folks’ gullibility.”

A nervous titter went through the crowd. Folks reckoned there was going to be trouble. The Professor’s smile didn’t quite reach his eyes anymore. “Sir, I assure you, my elixir is no mere ‘snake oil,’ as you so crudely put it. It is a scientific marvel, distilled with care and precision!” He poured a spoonful of the dark liquid into a glass and held it out. “I challenge you, good sir! Take but one taste, and feel the vigor return to your aging bones!”

Old Man Hemlock snorted. “Aging bones, is it? Why, I can still out-chop any man in this town, and I ain’t touched a drop of your bottled swamp water. Tell me, Professor, if this here elixir cures all ills, can it make a bald man sprout a full head of hair, or give a fella back a leg he lost to a runaway wagon?”

The Professor stammered a bit, wiping his brow with a silk handkerchief. “Well, uh, some conditions are, shall we say, beyond the natural healing powers, even with the aid of my elixir. But for the general ailments of the body and spirit, it is unmatched!”

“Unmatched for draining a man’s purse, I reckon,” Old Man Hemlock shot back. “I seen your type before, Professor. You come to town, spin your yarns, sell your rotgut, and then you’re off before anyone can properly examine your claims.” He took a step closer, and the Professor instinctively took a step back, bumping into his wagon. “Now, I ain’t saying you’re a liar, Professor, but I am saying you’re a-gwyne to be run out of this town if you don’t pack up that yellow wagon and take your ‘elixir’ somewhere else.”

The crowd went quiet. You could hear a pin drop, if there had been any pins to drop in the dusty street. The Professor’s face went red, then pale. He looked around at the faces in the crowd, and I seen that they wasn’t all laughing or believing anymore. Some looked downright mad, knowing Old Man Hemlock spoke for a lot of them.

“Why, you… you uncouth ruffian!” the Professor finally squeaked out, his voice not so booming anymore. “You dare impugn my professional integrity? I am a man of science! A healer of the people!”

Old Man Hemlock just crossed his arms, his big frame casting a shadow over the Professor. “Science? You ain’t got no more science in that bottle than a frog has in its hind leg. Now, I’ll say it plain: you got till sundown to be out of Harmony Gulch, or me and

or me and a right smart parcel of my neighbors here will help you roll that fancy wagon of yours right out of town, wheels or no wheels, and maybe give you a little send-off you won’t soon forget, a send-off that ain’t got nothing to do with polite goodbyes, neither.” Old Man Hemlock’s voice was low, but it carried a weight that made even the dust seem to settle heavier on the street, and folks shifted their feet a bit, looking from Hemlock to the Professor, and then back to Hemlock again, like they was watching a slow-motion train wreck you couldn’t rightly look away from. The Professor, he looked like a chicken whose head had just been chopped off, flapping his arms a little and making these little gasping sounds like he’d swallowed a fishbone. His face was all blotchy, purple and red, and his little fancy spectacles was slipping down his nose, and he was wiping at his brow with that silk rag of his, but it warn’t doing no good because new sweat was just a-sprouting faster than he could dab it away.

He looked around at the crowd, his eyes darting like a trapped bird, and I seen him trying to find a friendly face, or at least one that looked like it warn’t just a-gwyne to stand by and let him get a proper drubbing. The Professor, he didn’t like that kind of grin none, and he started a-sweating even harder, little beads running down from his temples and catching in his scraggly beard. He tried to puff himself up again, like a pigeon trying to look bigger than a cat, but his voice come out all croaky, like a frog with a sore throat.

“Now see here, good sirs! This is an outrage! An affront to free enterprise, a dastardly assault on the very foundations of scientific inquiry!” he squeaked, waving his little hands around, but nobody was much impressed. Old Man Hemlock just stood there, arms still crossed, looking like a big oak tree that had decided to grow right in the middle of the street and warn’t going to move for no fancy talks.

“Scientific inquiry, you say, Professor?” Hemlock’s voice was as calm as a deep pool, but you knew there was something dangerous lurking under the surface. “My inquiry says your bottle of rotgut ain’t cured nothing but a thirst for more rotgut, and it’s taken good money from folks that could ill afford to lose it. Sundown, Professor. That’s my deadline. And my terms is plain: either you and that contraption of yours is gone, or we’ll assist you in your departure, and it won’t be polite, not by a long shot.”

The Professor’s eyes darted around again, quick as a lizard. He looked at the faces, searching. He seen Silas Hooper, who was always a-complaining about his rheumatism, and Silas just grunted and looked away. He seen Miz’ Gable, who swore his elixir had made her hair grow back, and she just pursed her lips, looking down at her shoes. He seen young Timmy Miller, who had a bad cough and his mama had bought a bottle for him, and Timmy was just staring with wide eyes, looking scared but also a little bit like he was hoping to see a good show. The Professor warn’t getting no help from nobody. The air was thick with the dust and the quiet, and it was the kind of quiet that meant a storm was brewing, not a thunder-and-lightning storm, but a man-storm, and the Professor was right in the middle of it.

He stammered something, about how he was a respected man, how he had testimonials, how this was unjust, but his voice just kept getting smaller and smaller, like a fly buzzing inside a jar. Old Man Hemlock didn’t say another word, just stood there, big and solid, letting the silence do its work. And it did work. The Professor finally slumped a little, his shoulders caving in, and he let out a long, shaky breath.

“Very well,” he said, his voice barely a whisper, not booming at all now. “Very well. I see… I see my talents are not appreciated in this… this benighted hamlet. I shall depart. Under protest, mind you, under strenuous protest!” He tried to sound indignant, but it just sounded like a whimper.

A low murmur went through the crowd, like folks finally letting out a breath they’d been holding. Some of the grins widened, and a few men started to chuckle, low in their chests. Old Man Hemlock gave a slow nod, a sort of satisfaction in his eyes, but he didn’t let up his stance.

“You got till sundown, Professor,” he repeated, his voice still even. “And I’ll have a few of my neighbors here keep an eye on you, just to make sure you get a good start. No dilly-dallying, no trying to sell one last bottle on the sly. Just pack up and go.”

The Professor looked like he was about to cry. He turned around, his fancy coat a little askew, and started stumbling towards the back of his wagon. He fumbled with the latch, his hands shaking so bad he could barely open the little door that led to his makeshift laboratory and living quarters. The crowd watched him, silent again, like they was watching a rabbit caught in a snare, waiting to see what it would do.

He climbed into his wagon, and we could hear a clatter and bang inside, like bottles being thrown around, and then a grunt. Pretty soon, the flap on the back of the wagon was flung open, and the Professor started tossing things out onto the dusty ground. First came a stack of those brightly colored handbills, the ones with the fancy drawings of happy, healthy people. He threw them out like they was trash, and they fluttered down into the street, some getting caught in the breeze and skittering away. Then came some glass bottles, empty ones mostly, and they clinked together, some of them breaking. Then a small, wobbly stool, then a stack of what looked like dirty clothes, then a tarnished brass trumpet that had probably announced his arrival in every town he’d ever graced. He was just chucking things out haphazardly, not caring where they landed, his face redder than ever now with shame and anger.

“What in tarnation is he doing?” whispered one of the women near me.

“Looks like he’s trying to lighten his load for a quick getaway,” a man next to her muttered, and he chuckled.

Then the Professor himself emerged, carrying a few small, wooden crates, grunting with the effort. He set them down beside the wagon, then went back in. He come out again with a big roll of canvas and some wooden poles, which looked like the makings of his fancy stage setup. He threw these down too, in a heap. It was clear he was dismantling his whole operation, not just packing it up. He was making a show of it, maybe hoping to gain some pity, but nobody was biting. Old Man Hemlock just stood there, watching, his expression unreadable.

He worked in a frenzy, pulling down the banners from the sides of his wagon, the ones that proclaimed “Professor P.T. Marvel’s Miraculous Medicinal Elixir,  Cures All Known Ailments!” He tore them down with a vicious yank, ripping the fabric in places, and then he stomped on them for good measure, his little shiny boots kicking up dust. He was muttering to himself the whole time, something about barbarians and philistines, but nobody could quite make out the words, and if they could, they likely wouldn’t have cared.

Finally, he closed up the back of his wagon with a slam, and then he went around to the front, where his skinny old horse was still tied, looking bored and half-asleep. He untied the reins with jerky movements, and then he climbed up onto the driver’s seat. He looked out at the crowd one last time, his face a mask of fury and humiliation. He opened his mouth, like he was going to say something, maybe a final curse, but then he just snapped his mouth shut, gave a violent flick of the reins, and shouted, “Hyaaah!”

The old horse, startled, lurched forward, and the yellow wagon groaned and creaked as it slowly started to roll down the street. It was a sorry sight, that fancy wagon, now stripped of its banners and looking forlorn, rattling away with its disgraced driver slumped on the seat, head down. The Professor didn’t look back. He just kept driving, slowly at first, then picking up a bit of speed as he got to the edge of town, disappearing in a cloud of dust down the road that led out towards the prairie.

The crowd watched him go, a few of them still chuckling, others just shaking their heads. Old Man Hemlock finally uncrossed his arms, and a faint smile touched his lips. “Well,” he said, his voice carrying clearly in the sudden quiet, “that’s one less snake oil salesman in Harmony Gulch. Maybe now folks will start trusting real doctors, or at least common sense.”

A few people clapped, a couple of men hooted, and then the crowd started to break up, folks heading back to their stores or their wagons, talking amongst themselves about what had just happened. It was a good show, that it was, better than most, and it had cleared out the street of that yellow wagon and its lying owner. I felt a bit of satisfaction myself, seeing as how he’d tried to trick good folk, though I admit a part of me was a little disappointed not to see him get a proper drubbing. But Hemlock had handled it clean, and that was something.

I walked on down the street, thinking about it all. It warn’t every day you seen a fellow run out of town by just words, but Hemlock’s words had more weight than most men’s fists, when he put his mind to it. It got me thinking about how towns work, and how some folks can just stand up and speak for everybody, even when nobody elected them to do it. It was a powerful thing, that kind of respect, or fear, or whatever it was that made folks listen to Old Man Hemlock.

The sun was starting to dip lower in the sky now, painting the clouds orange and purple. The air was getting cooler, and the dust was settling a bit. Folks was starting to light lamps in the stores, and the smell of supper fires was drifting through the air from the houses on the edge of town. It was a peaceful kind of evening, after all that excitement.

I decided to head down to the river, just to watch the water for a spell. The river was always there, always moving, always the same and always different. It had seen a heap more things than any town or any man, and it just kept on going, never caring much about the comings and goings of folks on its banks. As I walked, I seen a little knot of men gathered outside the saloon, talking low and earnest. I figured they was still hashing over the Professor and Hemlock, or maybe they was just planning their next poker game. Men always found something to talk about.

I got to the bank, where the cottonwoods leaned over the water, their leaves rustling in the evening breeze. The river was wide and slow here, reflecting the last of the sunlight in long, shimmering streaks. A flatboat was tied up at a rickety dock a little ways upstream, its owner probably inside the saloon, spending his day’s earnings. Everything felt quiet now, like the town was taking a deep breath after holding it all afternoon.

I sat down on a smooth, grey rock, watching the current. A few fish jumped, making rings in the water that spread out and disappeared. I thought about Boggs and Sherburn, and then about the Professor, and it seemed like there was always something stirring up trouble in every town, one way or another. Whether it was a drunk man with a gun, or a smooth-talking fellow with a bottle of colored water, folks always found something to get riled up about. And then, just as quick as it started, it would be over, and the town would settle back down, waiting for the next thing to happen.

It made a fellow wonder what was next. This Harmony Gulch warn’t much different from other places I’d seen. There was good folks and bad folks, smart ones and simple ones, and everybody just trying to get by, one day at a time. The river kept flowing, and the sun kept setting, and the world kept turning, no matter what kind of ruckus happened in a dusty street.

I sat there for a long time, watching the stars start to prick through the darkening sky, feeling the cool air settle around me. Pretty soon, the moon would be up, big and round, casting shadows on the water. It was a fine night for thinking, and I done a fair bit of it. I thought about how easy it was for a crowd to get whipped up, whether it was for a lynching or just to run a peddler out of town. People was funny that way, always looking for a spectacle, always ready to join in, for good or for bad.

As the night got darker, I heard the faint sound of a fiddle playing somewhere up in town, a lively tune that drifted down to the riverbank. It was probably from the dance hall, or maybe just someone playing on their porch. It sounded cheerful, a contrast to the earlier fuss, and it made me feel a bit lighter. Life just moved on, like the river.

I decided it was time to head back towards where I was staying, a little room above the blacksmith’s shop, where I traded some odd jobs for a cot and a meal. As I walked back up the dusty street, the town seemed different in the twilight, softer, less sharp-edged than it had been in the harsh afternoon sun. The lamps in the windows cast a warm, yellow glow, and the sounds of folks talking and laughing drifted out from the open doors of the saloon and the general store. It was like the town was putting on its night clothes, getting ready to rest.

I passed by the general store, where Old Man Hemlock was just locking up. He saw me and gave a slight nod. “Evening, boy,” he rumbled, his voice still deep but softer now.

“Evening, Mr. Hemlock,” I replied.

He looked down the road where the Professor had gone. “Might be a bit quieter around here for a spell,” he mused, more to himself than to me. “Though trouble always finds a way, don’t it?” He pulled his hat down a little lower, gave the lock a final tug, and then headed off towards his own house, his big frame disappearing into the shadows.

He was right, I reckoned. Trouble always found a way. Just like the river always found its way to the sea, and the sun always found its way back up in the morning. It was just the way things were. And I figured I’d be around to see whatever came next, same as I always was. It warn't a bad life, just watching and learning, and sometimes getting a little scared, but mostly just seeing how folks went about their business in this big, wide world.

I climbed the creaky stairs to my room above the blacksmith shop. The smell of coal smoke and horse sweat was always strong up there, but I was used to it. I laid down on my cot, listening to the crickets chirping outside and the distant sound of the fiddle. My mind kept going over the day’s events, playing them back like a magic lantern show. Boggs getting shot, Sherburn standing his ground, the crowd getting riled up, then the Professor getting run out of town. It was a full day, that it was.

I thought about Boggs’ daughter, the way she screamed and threw herself on him. That was a sad thing to see, a real sad thing. And Colonel Sherburn, just tossing his pistol down and walking away like it warn’t nothing at all. He was a cold one, that Sherburn. But then again, Boggs had been asking for it, in his own drunken way. It warn’t simple, none of it was.

And the Professor, he warn’t much of a man, not really. All bluster and fancy words, but when it come down to it, he folded faster than a cheap deck of cards. But then, he didn’t have a gun, and Old Man Hemlock had the whole town behind him, or at least enough of it to make his words stick. Power warn’t always about who had the biggest muscles or the fastest gun, sometimes it was just about who could get folks to listen, and believe.

I turned over on my side, trying to get comfortable. The mattress was thin, and the springs creaked under my weight. Sleep warn’t coming easy tonight, not with all that had happened. My mind was still buzzing, like a bee in a jar.

I remembered the lanky man who acted out the shooting, getting treated to drinks. People loved a good story, especially one they’d just seen unfold right in front of them. It was like they needed to re-live it, to make sure it was real. And the way that man did it, perfect they said, made it even more real for them. It was a funny thing, how folks could be so quick to anger, so quick to want a lynching, and then just as quick to enjoy the show, even when it was about something as serious as a man getting killed.

It made me think that maybe people warn’t so different from a pack of dogs, all baying at the moon one minute, then chasing a rabbit the next, then settling down for a nap in the sun. It was all just a kind of instinct, I reckoned, something deep down that made them move and holler and get excited. And then it passed, and they went back to their lives, until the next thing come along to stir them up.

I closed my eyes, but the images kept playing. The red face of Boggs, the calm face of Sherburn, the terrified face of the Professor. The way the dust rose from the street, the way the

sun was setting behind the far bank of the river, painting the sky all red and orange, it all come back to me clear as day, even in the dark. I heard a dog bark outside, and then another one, further off, and then everything was quiet again, just the crickets and the whisper of the wind through the cracks in the walls. I laid there for a good long spell, just thinking about all the fuss and bother.

They was yelling, "Lynch him! Lynch him!" and off they went, a whole passel of them, swarming down the street like hornets out of a busted nest. I slipped out of my room and down the stairs, quiet as a mouse, and went out the back way. I didn't want to be right in the middle of it, but I sure as shooting wanted to see what would happen. It warn't right, a lynching warn't, but folks was all fired up, and when folks get like that, there ain't no stopping them, not with words anyway. They went hurrahing and pushing, bumping into each other, and some of them had old muskets and shotguns, and some just had sticks and pitchforks, and a few of them was carrying torches, though it warn't dark enough for them yet, just getting on towards sundown. The air was full of their yelling and their heavy footsteps, and the women and children was hanging back on the stoops and porches, looking scared but not wanting to miss a thing.

I kept a little ways behind them, skirting the edge of the crowd, keeping to the shadows of the buildings. They warn't going in any particular order, just a big, shapeless blob of angry folks, all pointed in the same direction, which was towards Colonel Sherburn’s house. He lived in a fine place, a big two-story white house with a wide porch and tall pillars, set back a bit from the main street amongst some shade trees. It stood out amongst the other houses, being so grand and all, and I reckoned that was part of why folks didn't like him much. He was too proper, too proud, too much a man of his own mind to suit a lot of them common sorts.

When they got to the house, the noise got louder, if that was even possible. They stopped in the street and started shaking their fists and hollering his name. "Sherburn! Come out, you murderer! Come out and face us!" Some of the younger fellas, all full of fire and whisky, started throwing stones at the windows, but most of them just stood there, yelling. There was a big gate, but it warn't locked, and a path leading up to the house. Nobody seemed to want to be the first one through that gate. They was brave in a crowd, but each one of them by himself seemed to have a little bit of sense left.

Then, just as the sun dipped down behind the horizon, throwing a last red glow on the house, the front door opened, slow and quiet, and out stepped Colonel Sherburn. He warn't wearing no hat, and his white hair was all neat and brushed back. He had a shotgun in his hand, a double-barreled one, and he just stood there on the porch, as calm as you please, looking out at that whole yelling, seething mob. He didn't say a word, didn't move a muscle, just stood there, and the way he looked at them, so steady and cold, it was like he was looking right through them, seeing each one for what he was.

A hush started to fall over the crowd, spreading out from the front of the mob like a ripple in a pond. The yelling died down, then the pushing stopped, and pretty soon you could hear a pin drop. All those angry faces was turned up towards him, but they warn't yelling no more. They was just standing there, shifting their weight, some of them still gripping their weapons, but not so tight as before.

Sherburn let them stand there in silence for a minute or two, letting the quiet settle in good and proper. Then he raised his shotgun, not pointing it at anyone in particular, just sort of raising it a little, so they all seen he had it. And then he spoke, and his voice warn't loud, but it cut through the evening air like a razor.

"The idea of you lynching anybody," he says, "is a howling comedy. The idea of you a-laying your hand on a man that's got some grit in him, is a'most more than a body can stand. Why, a man's safe in the hands of ten thousand of your kind, as long as it's daylight and no hollering goes on. You think to scare me with your numbers? You think to come here, a pack of you, and demand what you reckon is justice? What is justice to a pack of sheep that ain't got the gumption to stand alone?"

He paused, and swept his gaze over them, and some of the men in the front ranks looked down at their feet, and some of them shifted their weight uneasy, and some of them even started to back up a little, just a step or two, trying to blend into the folks behind them.

"The average man's a coward," he went on, his voice still low, but every word hitting home like a bullet. "In the daylight, you're safe, because every man of you knows that if he starts anything, the law will get him. But you come in the dark, you come in a crowd, because you ain't got the sand to do anything alone. You ain't men, you're just a mob. And a mob's a coward. A mob ain't got no brains, no heart, no conscience. A mob's just a big, shapeless mass of fear, pushed on by the loudest mouths and the weakest wills."

He took another step forward, just one step, but it seemed to make the crowd flinch. The shotgun was still in his hand, held easy, but you knew what it meant.

"There's not a man here," he says, "that ain't afraid to take that first step. There's not a man here that's got the nerve to stand up by himself and face me, or any other man who ain't afraid to stand his ground. You talk about lynching. You talk about justice. What you really want is to hide in the anonymity of the crowd, to let the mob do your dirty work, because you're too yellow to do it yourself."

He looked at them, steady and contemptuous, and the quiet was so thick you could almost taste it. Nobody moved. Nobody spoke. Even the dogs in the distance seemed to have stopped their barking.

"Go home," he says, "every last one of you. Go home and take a good look at yourselves in the mirror. See the cowards you are. And don't you ever come back here again, not as a mob, not unless you can bring a man who's got the backbone to stand alone."

He didn't move, just stood there. The crowd started to break up, first just a few men on the edges, then more and more, melting away into the shadows. They didn't run, not exactly, but they walked away fast, not looking back, not saying a word. The torches went out, one by one, dropped in the dust or left to smolder. Pretty soon, there was nobody left but a few stragglers, and even they was hurrying to be gone.

Sherburn watched them go, and then, without another word, he turned around and went back into his house, closing the door as softly as he had opened it. The whole thing was over, just like that.

I stood there for a spell, hidden behind a big oak tree, watching the empty street and the big, dark house. My heart was thumping a little, and a shiver went down my spine, even though it warn't cold. I had seen a lot of things in my life, but I ain't never seen a man face down a whole mob like that, and win, just with words and a cold stare. It made me think about a lot of things, about what courage really meant, and how easily folks could be swayed, either to anger or to shame.

I walked back towards the main street, feeling a bit dazed. The town was quiet now, eerily so after all the ruckus. A few lights was on in windows, but most folks was probably holed up in their houses, thinking about what had just happened, and maybe feeling a bit foolish. I reckoned they wouldn't be talking about lynching for a good long while after this.

Back at the hotel, I crept up the stairs and into my room. I didn't feel sleepy no more. My mind was racing, trying to sort out all the pieces of the day. Old Boggs, a nuisance, but harmless, dead. Colonel Sherburn, cold and proud, a killer, but also brave in a way I hadn't seen before. And the crowd, shifting from one thing to another, first laughing at Boggs, then wanting to lynch Sherburn, then running away with their tails between their legs. It was a strange world, full of strange people.

I laid down on my cot again, pulling the thin blanket over me. The crickets was chirping again, louder now that everything else was quiet. I thought about what Sherburn said, about the average man being a coward. Was it true? I reckoned a lot of them folks down there was good enough people on their own, but put them in a crowd, and they turned into something else, something meaner and dumber. It was like they lost their own minds and just took on the mind of the mob. It was a scary thought, that.

The next morning, the town was different. It warn't bustling like it had been the day before. Folks moved slower, talked quieter. You could tell that something big had happened, something that left a mark. Nobody was yelling or hooting, and the streets was mostly empty for a good part of the morning. The feeling was like after a big storm, when everything is calm but there’s still a kind of tension in the air, like folks are waiting for the next gust of wind.

And them fellers, they just folded like a wet rag. Never thought I’d see the day.” He shook his head, a kind of admiration mixing with his earlier fear and approval. He wasn’t sure how to feel about it all, and neither was most folks, I reckon.

The wagon with the family that had fetched their dinner, they were still there, eating their cold vittles, but now they were quiet, chewing slow and watching the goings-on. The whisky drinking had stopped, and nobody was fighting. It was like the whole town had had the wind knocked out of it, and they were all just trying to catch their breath.

I looked at Boggs’s daughter. She was still there, sitting on the steps of the drug store, where they had carried her father. She had stopped screaming and crying, and was just sitting there, huddled, looking at nothing, her face pale and her eyes red-rimmed. People had left her mostly alone, maybe out of a kind of respect, or maybe because they were too busy gawking at the aftermath of the lynching that wasn’t.

Pretty soon, a few men started to move off, kind of sheepishly, and then a few more, and pretty soon the street started to clear out, like the tide going out from the shore. The wagons and horses that had been thick in the streets, they started to move, going back to the country, or to the river landings. The noise of the town started to come back, but it was a different kind of noise, a softer, more usual noise, of work and talk, not of anger and a mob.

Robert, he clapped his hands together, a little too loud in the returning quiet. “Well, that was certainly an experience. Miss Adeline, perhaps we should endeavor to find some refreshment. I believe the general store, despite its recent dramatic interlude, might still offer a palatable selection of ginger ale, or perhaps a sarsaparilla.”

Miss Adeline just nodded, still looking a bit dazed, and Robert, he took her arm and started to lead her towards another, smaller store down the street, still muttering about sociological phenomena. I didn’t follow them. I just stayed where I was, watching the last of the crowd trickle away. It was a strange thing, seeing all that anger and meanness just evaporate under the gaze of one man. It made you wonder about people, and what made them tick.

I thought about old Boggs, dead in the drug store with the Bible on his chest. He was a fool, sure, but he was harmless, mostly. And then I thought about Colonel Sherburn, a hard man, a proud man, who wasn’t afraid of a thousand angry fellers. And I thought about the crowd, so full of rage and then so full of shame. It was a jumble of things, and I didn’t rightly know what to make of it all.

The sun was getting lower in the sky now, and the shadows were getting long. The air was still hot, but there was a little breeze starting to stir, bringing with it the smell of the river. I decided to walk down to the levee, just to get away from the memory of all that fuss and bother. The river always had a way of making things seem smaller, somehow, less important.

When I got to the levee, it was mostly empty. A few old rivermen were sitting on barrels, smoking their pipes and watching the steamboats go by, their faces as weathered as old planks. The river was wide and brown, rolling by steady, same as it always did. You could see where it had gnawed away at the banks in places, whole chunks of land gone, just like the passage talked about, and you could feel its power, quiet and strong.

I sat down on an old log, just watching the water. A big steamboat came by, its paddle wheel churning up the water, and its whistle blowing a long, mournful sound that echoed across the river. It was a grand sight, all lit up with its lanterns, carrying folks up and down the country, to places I’d never seen. I wondered where they were all going, and what kind of adventures they were having.

After a while, one of the old rivermen, a feller with a long white beard and eyes that seemed to have seen a hundred rivers, he spoke up, without even turning his head. “Seen a sight in town today, did you, boy?”

I nodded, even though he wasn’t looking at me. “Yes, sir. There was a big hullabaloo. A feller got shot, and then there was a mob.”

He grunted, and took a long drag on his pipe. “A mob, eh? Them things come and go, like the river floods. All full of noise and fury, and then they leave nothing but a bit of mud behind. Seen it a hundred times.”

“But the Colonel, he stood up to them,” I said, kind of proud of it myself, even though I hadn’t done nothing.

The old man nodded slow. “Sherburn, he’s a hard nut. Always has been. Got a way about him, he does. Don’t back down from nothing. That’s why he’s still standing, and Boggs ain’t. Boggs, he was a good feller at heart, just too much of a blusterer and a drinker. Always picked the wrong fights, he did.”

He seemed to know a lot about everybody, these old rivermen. They just sat there, quiet, but they seen everything that happened in the towns along the river, and they knew the ways of men, good and bad.

“The river, she’s a lot like folks, sometimes,” the old man mused, still looking out at the water. “Can be calm and pretty, smooth as glass. And then she can get angry, all wild and tearing, pulling down the banks and drowning everything in her path. But she always goes back to being herself, sooner or later. Just like folks. They get riled up, they get mad, they do foolish things. But then they settle down, and they go back to their own ways. Most of them, anyway.”

I thought about that, and it made a kind of sense. The town had been like the river in a flood that day, roaring and tearing, and then it had settled down again, back to its usual flow. But some things had changed, just like the river changed the banks. Boggs was gone, and Sherburn had showed his hand, and the town had seen itself, naked and ugly, and then seen itself pull back from the brink.

I stayed there till the stars came out, and the moon started to climb up the sky, painting a silver path on the water. The air got cooler, and the crickets started their nightly song. It was a peaceful sound, after all the noise of the day. I thought about sleeping on the levee, but then I remembered Jim, and how he’d be waiting for me back at the raft, maybe a little worried. So I got up and stretched, and started to walk back towards where we had hidden the raft, down a little cove a ways off from the town.

The town itself was quieting down for the night. The lights in the windows were starting to go out, one by one. I seen a few fellers still standing around outside the general store, talking low, maybe still trying to figure out what had happened, or what it meant. But mostly, it was quiet.

As I walked, I kept thinking about Sherburn. He wasn’t a good man, not by a long shot, but he had a kind of power that was something to see. He knew how to look a mob in the eye and make them back down. And that was a thing I didn’t think I’d ever forget. It wasn’t courage like a feller fighting fair, but it was courage of a different kind, the kind that came from knowing yourself and knowing other folks, and not being afraid to stand alone against them all.

I found the raft hidden good amongst the willows. Jim was there, stirring a little fire, and the smell of fish cooking was in the air. He looked up when I came crashing through the bushes.

“Lawd a’mighty, Huck, you back! I was startin’ to fret some. What all happened in dat town? I heared a powerful lot o’ noise, and den it jes’ died down.”

I sat down by the fire, glad to be back on the raft, away from the town and its troubles. “Jim, you wouldn’t believe it. Old Boggs got shot by Colonel Sherburn, right there in the street. And then the whole town, they decided they was going to lynch Sherburn, a big mob, a thousand of them, all yelling and carrying on.”

Jim’s eyes got big in the firelight. “No! Dey sho’ didn’t!”

“They did too,” I said. “But Sherburn, he came out on his balcony with a shotgun, and he just talked to them. Told them they was cowards, a pack of animals, and he shamed them right into turning tail and running. You never seen nothing like it, Jim. They just melted away.”

Jim shook his head, amazed. “Well, I’ll be blessed. Dat Sherburn mus’ be a powerful man. To talk down a whole mob. Most folks, dey jes’ run.”

“He didn’t run,” I said. “He stood his ground, and he made them run.” I ate some of the fish Jim had cooked; it tasted good and solid after all that excitement.

We sat there for a long time, just talking about it, and about other things, the usual kind of talk we had on the raft. The river flowed by, quiet and steady, and the stars hung bright overhead. It felt good to be on the water again, away from all the fuss and bother of the land.

The next morning, we decided it was best to keep moving. That town had seen enough excitement for a while, and it was probably best if we were a good ways downriver before anybody started thinking too much about a runaway boy and an escaped slave. So we untied the raft, and pushed off into the current, letting the river carry us along.

The mist was still lying low on the water, and the sun was just starting to peek over the eastern bank, painting the sky with streaks of orange and pink. The sounds of the town faded behind us, and soon there was nothing but the gentle lapping of the water against the raft, and the calls of the birds waking up in the trees along the shore.

Jim, he was humming a low tune, and I was just lying on my back, watching the sky change color, and listening to the river. It was a peaceful feeling, a good feeling. But even as the town disappeared behind a bend, I kept thinking about Sherburn, and about Boggs, and about that mob. And I knew that no matter how far we drifted downriver, there’d always be something new to see, and something new to make you wonder about folks and their ways. The river, she just kept on rolling, and the world just kept on turning, and there was always another bend, and another town, and another set of troubles or wonders waiting around the corner. We didn’t know what was coming next, but we knew the river would take us there. And that was enough for now.

The river flowed on, mighty and wide, and it was a comfort to be on it again, Jim and me, with nothing but the sky above and the water all around. The sun came up fine and painted the clouds every color you could think of, and then it got high in the sky and made the water sparkle, and then it went down again, just as pretty, leaving long shadows on the banks and turning the trees into dark shapes against the light. We drifted along, mostly by night, and tied up by day in some quiet place, maybe a little bend where the willows hung down and touched the water, or a sandbar where nobody was likely to bother us. Jim, he was always careful to keep the raft hid, because of him being a runaway, and me, well, I didn’t much care for being seen neither, not after all that ruckus back in the town where Sherburn shot Boggs. It was good to be free of it all, just the two of us, with our fishing lines out and our pipes smoking, or Jim humming one of his old tunes while I watched the clouds go by.

We had a good spell of it, a week or maybe ten days, and nothing much happened, just the river and the quiet, and that was the best kind of happening for us. We’d catch catfish and fry them up on a little fire on the raft, and Jim, he was mighty particular about his coffee, brewing it strong and black, and it smelled powerful good. Sometimes we’d see another raft go by, or a steamboat

or a steamboat, puffing and splashing along, and we’d always lay low when they come near, Jim especial, seeing as how he was a runaway and had to keep mighty dark. But mostly it was just the river and us, and the quiet was so deep sometimes you could hear your own thoughts rattling around in your head. It was a comfortable sort of quiet, though, not the kind that makes you uneasy. We was getting low on coffee and meal, and Jim, he allowed as how his pipe was starting to taste of ashes instead of tobacco, which was a sure sign we needed to put in somewhere and trade a coon skin or two.

So one morning, just as the mist was lifting, we seen a little town off to the left bank, looking mighty peaceful, with smoke curling up from a few chimneys and a couple of flatboats tied up at a rickety landing. It warn’t a big place, just a scattering of houses, a store, and maybe a church house or something like that. It looked quiet enough, not like the last place, so we decided to risk it. We edged the raft over towards the bank, keeping a sharp eye out, and found a little cove where we could tie up amongst some willows, out of sight mostly, but close enough to walk to the town.

“You wait here, Jim,” I says, “I’ll go poke around and see what’s what. You keep the raft hid good, and if anybody comes nosing, you just lay low and don’t make no sound.”

Jim nodded, his big eyes looking solemn. “Dat’s de ticket, Huck. You be keerful, now. Dey’s all kin’ o’ folks on de sho’, some good, some bad, an’ some wuss’n bad.”

I tucked a couple of coon skins under my arm, figuring they’d be worth enough for our needs, and started up the path towards the town. The path was dusty and winding, and the sun was already getting warm. As I got closer, I could hear a kind of droning sound, like a big bumblebee, but too regular for that. It got louder as I walked, and pretty soon I realized it was human voices, a whole passel of them, singing or chanting, I couldn’t rightly tell.

I come out onto the main street, which warn’t much more than a wide dirt road, and there was a crowd gathered in front of what looked like the biggest building, a plain wooden structure with no steeple but a cross nailed above the door, so I reckoned it was a church. The droning sound was coming from inside, and a few folks was standing outside, talking low. There was a big sign tacked to a post near the door, and it read, in big, shaky letters: “REVIVAL MEETING! REPENT, YE SINNERS! FIRE AND BRIMSTONE! TONIGHT AT SUNDOWN!”

I didn’t much care for revival meetings, having seen a few and mostly found them tiresome, but I was curious about the crowd, so I sidled up to the edge of it and tried to look inconspicuous. There was all sorts of people, farmers mostly, with their wives and children, and a few men who looked like they’d been drinking a bit too much corn liquor. They all had a serious, worried look on their faces, except for the children, who was mostly bored or squirming.

A man was standing on a makeshift platform in front of the church, waving his arms around and shouting at the top of his lungs. He was a big, beefy man, with a red face and hair slicked back with grease. He had on a suit that looked like it had seen better days, but he wore it with an air of importance. He was yelling about sin and damnation, and how the fires of hell was waiting for everyone who didn’t repent right then and there. His voice was hoarse, but he kept on hollering, pausing every now and then for breath and to wipe his brow with a big, sweaty handkerchief.

“Oh, brothers and sisters!” he roared, “Can’t you feel it? The wrath of the Almighty bearing down on this wicked generation! The river itself, a-flowin’ with the tears of the damned! Your very souls are in peril, folks, in peril! But there’s a way out! A way to salvation! Just open your hearts, open your pocketbooks, and let the good Lord guide you to redemption!”

He went on like that for a good half hour, and the crowd got more and more agitated. Some of the women started to cry, and a few men shouted “Amen!” and “Hallelujah!” I seen one old woman faint dead away, and two men had to carry her off to the side, fanning her with their hats. It was quite a spectacle.

After a while, the preacher started talking about a special offering, a “love offering” he called it, to help him spread the word of God to other lost souls. He said the Lord loved a cheerful giver, and that the more you gave, the more the Lord would bless you. He had a big velvet bag, all purple and with gold fringes, and he passed it around the crowd. Folks was digging into their pockets, pulling out nickels and dimes, and even some silver dollars, all looking mighty earnest. I saw a poor farmer, whose clothes were patched and threadbare, put in what looked like his last two bits, his face wrung with worry.

I thought about the coon skins I had, and how they were for coffee and meal. I didn’t feel like contributing to the preacher’s “love offering,” not when I knew how much Jim and me needed our supplies. I reckon the Lord understood that.

While the bag was going around, I slipped away from the edge of the crowd and went looking for the store. It was a little further down the street, a plain building with a porch and a couple of barrels out front. Inside, it smelled of sugar and coffee and dry goods. An old man, with spectacles perched on his nose and a long white beard, was behind the counter, reading a newspaper.

“Howdy,” I says.

He looked up, pushing his spectacles higher. “Howdy yourself, young feller. What can I do for you?”

“Got a couple of coon skins here,” I says, laying them on the counter. “Wanta trade ‘em for some coffee and meal, maybe some tobacco.”

He picked up the skins, felt the fur, and nodded. “Good prime skins, these are. All right, I reckon I can give you a fair price. What all you need?”

I told him our needs, and he started weighing out the meal and coffee, and got a plug of tobacco. While he was doing that, I asked him about the revival meeting.

“Oh, that,” he said with a sigh, not looking up from his scales. “That’s Reverend Silas, he comes through here every few months. Stir folks up good, he does. Takes a tidy sum with him when he leaves, too. Folks around here, they’re simple. They mean well, but they’re easily swayed by a good talker, especially when he’s shouting about hellfire.”

“He sounds powerful serious,” I says.

“Serious about his own pocketbook, I reckon,” the old man muttered. He weighed out the last of the coffee. “There you go, son. That’ll be two dollars and forty cents. I’ll give you three dollars for the skins, so you got sixty cents coming to you.”

He pushed the pile of goods towards me and counted out the change. I put the money in my pocket, feeling good that we had what we needed and even a little extra. I thanked him and was about to leave when the door opened and a man came rushing in, his face pale and his eyes wide.

“Elder Stubbs!” he cried out, “They’re saying young Billy Jones done fell in the river! Down by the bend, just below the old mill!”

The old storekeeper, Elder Stubbs, dropped his newspaper and looked alarmed. “Billy Jones? The miller’s boy? Is he hurt?”

“They don’t know, Elder! Some say he hit his head on a rock! Some say he’s drowned already! They’re down there now, trying to find him!”

Elder Stubbs immediately started to move from behind the counter. “Oh, dear, oh, dear. That poor boy. I must go see.” He grabbed his hat and headed for the door, then turned back to me. “You young feller, you best stay out of the way. It’ll be a mess down there.”

I nodded, though I had no intention of staying out of the way. A drowning, that was something. I went out after him and followed at a distance. People were already running down towards the river, their faces full of worry. The revival meeting had broken up entirely, the preacher looking annoyed that his sermon had been interrupted, but even he was now hurrying to see what had happened.

I got down to the bend, and sure enough, there was a crowd gathered on the bank, all looking into the churning water. The old mill was a ramshackle building, with its wheel long since broken, but the water around it was deep and swift. A few men had poles and were poking around in the water, and others were calling out Billy’s name. His pa, the miller, was standing on the bank, his face white as flour, wringing his hands and crying.

“My boy! My Billy!” he kept saying. “Oh, Lord, don’t take my boy!”

The preacher, Reverend Silas, came bustling up, pushing his way through the crowd. He put a hand on the miller’s shoulder, looking mighty solemn. “Have faith, brother! The Lord works in mysterious ways! Perhaps this is a test of your devotion! Have you prayed for your boy, truly prayed with all your heart and soul?”

The miller looked at him, bewildered. “I been prayin’ since he was born, Reverend! What good’s that now?”

“Ah, but a communal prayer, brother! A prayer of all these good folks gathered here! And perhaps a special offering, a token of our collective faith, to show the Lord how much we truly desire this boy’s salvation!”

I just stood there, listening, and thinking how quick that preacher was to turn every misfortune into another chance for a collection. It seemed mighty low-down to me.

Just then, a commotion broke out further upstream, a little past the mill. A man started shouting, “Here he is! I see him!”

Everybody rushed to that spot, and sure enough, there was Billy, not drowned at all, but clinging to a snag of driftwood, looking mighty scared but otherwise all right. Two men waded out and pulled him in, and his pa rushed to him, hugging him tight and crying with relief. The crowd cheered and clapped, and a big wave of relief went through everybody.

Billy was a scrawny boy, about ten years old, and he was shivering and soaked, but he was alive. He’d just slipped on a muddy bank and fell in, and the current had carried him a bit before he managed to grab onto the snag.

“Thank the Lord! Thank the Lord!” the miller kept saying, over and over again.

Reverend Silas, he stepped forward, looking like he’d organized the whole thing himself. “See, brothers and sisters! The power of prayer! The Lord heard your cries! He responded to your faith! And now, in this moment of divine grace, let us show our gratitude with another offering, a thanksgiving offering for this miracle!”

He pulled out his purple velvet bag again, and started passing it around. And wouldn’t you know it, folks, they started digging into their pockets again, some even more eagerly than before, because they felt so relieved and grateful. It was enough to make a body sick, seeing how easily these good-hearted folks were taken in. I saw the same poor farmer who’d given his last two bits before, now pull out another coin, a nickel this time, and drop it in the bag, a look of profound peace on his face.

I just shook my head and started to walk away. This town was too much like the last one, always someone trying to take advantage of folks. I reckoned it was time to get back to Jim and the raft, and put a good piece of river between us and this place.

I walked back towards the store, figuring I’d pick up something for Jim, a little treat maybe, with my sixty cents. But as I passed the alley next to the store, I heard voices. I stopped and peeked around the corner, and there was Reverend Silas, talking to a couple of men. He didn’t look so pious now. He had a flask in his hand, and he was taking a big swig.

“Well, boys,” he said, wiping his mouth with the back of his hand, “That was a mighty fine haul today. That drowning scare, that was a stroke of pure genius, warn’t it? Put the fear of God in ‘em, and then give ‘em a miracle to be grateful for. Old Man Miller, he practically cried tears of joy as he emptied his pockets.”

One of the men, a skinny fellow with shifty eyes, chuckled. “Aye, Reverend, you got a way with words. And with folks’ money. I just hope that little rascal Billy don’t go blabbing how he just played along for a couple of dimes and a candy bar.”

“He won’t,” said Reverend Silas, taking another swig. “He knows what’s good for him. Besides, who’d believe a runny-nosed boy over a man of God? They’ll just think he’s confused from his ordeal.”

My stomach churned. So the whole thing was a setup. Billy hadn’t just accidentally fallen in; he’d been part of the scam. And the preacher, he warn’t no man of God at all, just a common swindler, playing on people’s fears and hopes. I felt a cold anger building up inside me. To take advantage of folks like that, especially the poor ones, it just warn’t right. It was worse than any of Boggs’s drunken bluster, because it was sly and mean-spirited.

I watched them for a minute longer, then I quietly backed away. I didn’t want them to see me. I didn’t know what I could do, but I knew I couldn’t stay there. I went back to the store, bought a small sack of candy for Jim with some of my change, and then hurried back down the dusty path to the river.

When I got back to the raft, Jim was sitting there, whittling on a stick, looking contented. He looked up when he seen me.

“Well, Huck, what kinda folks dey got up dere?” he asked.

I sat down, feeling all heavy inside. “Oh, Jim,” I says, “They’re the same kind of folks as everywhere else, I reckon. Good and bad, and some who ain’t what they seem. Got the coffee and meal, and tobacco, and some candy for you too.” I handed him the sack.

He grinned, his eyes lighting up at the candy. “Dat’s mighty good, Huck. You a good boy.”

“Jim,” I says, “You know that revival meeting they was having? And that boy who fell in the river?”

Jim nodded, his grin fading a bit. “Heard some folks talkin’ ‘bout it. Sounded like a mighty scare. But de Lord he spared de chile, eh?”

“Warn’t the Lord, Jim,” I says, “Warn’t the Lord at all. It was all a big con. That preacher, he put that boy up to it, and then he took a whole passel of money from them folks, telling them it was God’s will and all.”

Jim’s eyes got wide. “No, Huck! You don’ tell me dat!”

“It’s true, Jim. I heard him myself, bragging about it in an alley. And that boy, Billy, he got paid off.”

Jim shook his head slowly, his face full of sorrow. “Dat’s a powerful mean trick, Huck. To play on folks’ feelin’s like dat. Dey ain’t no good in dat man’s heart.”

“No, there ain’t,” I agreed. “And all them poor folks, giving away their last money, thinking they was doing God’s work, when they was just filling that swindler’s pockets.”

We sat in silence for a bit, just listening to the river. The sun was getting higher, and the air was getting muggy.

“We best be gettin’ on, Jim,” I says. “This town ain’t for us.”

Jim nodded. “Dat’s de truf, Huck. De river, she don’ lie. But folks, dey can trick you ever’ way.”

We untied the raft and pushed off, letting the current take us again. I didn’t look back at the town. The sight of it, and the memory of that preacher, just made me feel sour. It warn’t the kind of trouble that made you want to stay and fight, like with Boggs, but the kind that made you want to just float away and forget about folks and their meanness.

We drifted along for the rest of the day, quiet for the most part. Jim smoked his pipe, and I thought about all the different ways people found to be low-down. It seemed like every time we put in to shore, there was some new example of it, some new thing to make you shake your head and wonder. The river, she just kept on rolling, clean and honest, but the land, that was where the trouble was.

As evening came on, we pulled over into a little eddy behind a wooded island. The air was still and peaceful, with the smell of wet earth and growing things. We made a small fire and cooked some fish we’d caught. The stars started to come out, one by one, until the sky was thick with them, shining down on the river and the quiet land.

Jim, he was mighty thoughtful. He looked up at the stars and said, “Huck, you ever think ‘bout de stars? How dey just up dere, shinin’ and shinin’, don’ bother nobody, don’ try to trick nobody. Dey just be what dey be.”

“Yeah, Jim,” I says, “I reckon they’re a lot like the river. Just doing their own thing, not caring much about what folks are up to down here.”

“Dat’s right,” Jim said, nodding slowly. “An’ dat’s why I like it on dis raft, Huck. Just us, an’ de river, an’ de stars. Ain’t nobody tryin’ to get yo’ money out here, ain’t nobody lyin’ an’ cheat-in’. It’s de Lord’s truth out here.”

I knew what he meant. Out on the river, things felt simpler, cleaner. The dangers were natural ones, storms, snags, getting lost, but they warn’t born of human meanness. That was a different kind of danger, one that Jim and I found a sight harder to navigate.

We slept soundly that night, rocked by the gentle movement of the raft. The next morning, the fog was thick, like a blanket, and you couldn’t see ten feet in front of you. It was a good thing we had tied up, because trying to navigate in that would have been a fool’s errand. We just waited it out, eating some cold fish and leftover corn pone from the store.

By mid-morning, the fog started to lift, slowly, like a curtain being drawn back. And as it cleared, we seen something we hadn’t expected. Right there, not twenty yards from our island, was another raft. But this one warn’t like ours. It was bigger, and it had a little cabin built on it, and clothes were strung out on a line to dry. And sitting on the deck, smoking a pipe, was a man.

He was a big, burly fellow, with a tangled beard and a cap pulled low over his eyes. He looked like he’d been on the river a long time. He seen us about the same time we seen him. He took his pipe out of his mouth and grinned, a big, wide grin that showed yellow teeth.

“Well now,” he boomed, his voice echoing across the water, “Look what the cat dragged in! Another raft, and a couple of river rats, eh? Mornin’ to ya, boys!”

Jim and me, we just looked at each other. We hadn’t expected company, and Jim especial warn’t too keen on it. But this man, he seemed friendly enough.

“Mornin’,” I says back, feeling a little wary.

“Name’s Silas,” he called out, “Silas Dobbs. Been workin’ this river since I was knee-high to a grasshopper. You boys just driftin’?”

“Yessir,” I says, “Just driftin’ along.”

“Well, you picked a fine spot to lay up,” he said, waving a hand at the island. “Good fishing here, if you know where to cast. Got any coffee brew-in’? I could use a cup. Mine’s gone cold.”

I looked at Jim. Jim was still wary, but he was always one for hospitality, especial if it meant a cup of coffee. He nodded just a little.

“Got some on,” I called back. “If you want to pull over, you’re welcome to a cup.”

“Now that’s mighty neighborly!” Silas Dobbs boomed again, and he started pushing his raft with a long pole, bringing it slowly towards our island.

Jim whispered to me, “Huck, you be keerful. He look friendly, but you can’ tell about folks.”

“I know, Jim,” I whispered back. “But we can’t just turn him away. That ain’t right.”
