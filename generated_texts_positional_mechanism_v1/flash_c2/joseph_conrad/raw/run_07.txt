The corrugated iron roofs of the mining station shimmered with an unbearable heat, reflecting the unforgiving sun directly into my eyes; this place of profound desolation clung to the sheer face of the mountain like some desperate, metallic lichen. I had come to assess the output, to make certain that the company’s investment, already considerable, was not being squandered by idleness or worse. My predecessor, a man of stout constitution and relentless energy, had departed weeks prior without a word, leaving only a cryptic note about the ‘humming silence’ of the rock. This silence, I soon discovered, was a heavier presence than any clamour.

The men, a motley collection of European labourers and native hands, moved with a listless, almost mechanical rhythm. Their faces, caked with red dust and sweat, betrayed little emotion; only in their eyes, occasionally meeting mine with a flicker of something indefinable, could one discern the prolonged strain of their existence. There was a particular fellow, a German by his accent, with a ragged beard and eyes that seemed perpetually startled, who watched the jungle’s edge with an intensity that verged on mania; he muttered often to himself, soft, urgent phrases that dissolved into the general drone of insects.

One evening, as the oppressive twilight fell, painting the vast, indifferent peaks in hues of bruised purple, a sound began to emanate from the deeper recesses of the valley, a sound unlike any I had ever heard. It was not a roar, nor a shriek, nor the wind’s wail through the crags; it was a rhythmic, almost deliberate thrumming, low and resonant, carrying with it a strange, almost hypnotic quality. The German, Herr Vogel, dropped his plate with a clatter, the tin object bouncing on the hard-packed earth. His eyes, wide and bloodshot, were fixed on the impenetrable curtain of trees. ‘It has begun,’ he whispered, his voice thin and reedy, ‘the mountain calls.’

I confess I felt a prickle of unease, a cold sensation that ran down my spine, quite unbidden by any rational cause. This was, after all, merely a sound, an echo perhaps of some natural process, or the work of some local tribes; yet, the way it seemed to permeate the very air, to vibrate within the bedrock itself, suggested something more profound, something ancient and patient. The other Europeans had grown pale, their conversations dying abruptly; even the native men, usually so stoic, shifted nervously, their grunts and low murmurs a stark contrast to their earlier composure. Their headman, a tall, sinewy individual named K’Tiga, whose face was usually a mask of calm, now had his brow furrowed, his eyes darting towards the source of the sound, a deep apprehension settling upon his features.

‘What is it?’ I asked, perhaps more sharply than intended, addressing K’Tiga. He turned slowly, his gaze meeting mine, and I saw there not fear, but a weary knowing, a recognition of something beyond my comprehension. ‘The Voice,’ he said, his own voice a guttural whisper, ‘the mountain speaks its hunger.’ I dismissed it, of course, as superstition, as the primitive interpretation of an unknown phenomenon; yet, the cold certainty in his eyes, the undeniable tremor in his tone, planted a seed of doubt, a tiny, insidious tendril of dread in the rational soil of my mind. The thrumming continued, unchanging, indifferent, like the heartbeat of some colossal, slumbering beast.

Hours passed, or so it felt, though the crude clock on the wall indicated only minutes. The thrumming intensified, or perhaps it was merely my perception sharpening, my nerves stretched taut by the unwavering pressure of the sound. The air grew heavy, thick with an unspoken dread, a collective apprehension that stifled breath and thought alike; the lamp-light, usually a beacon of human presence, now seemed to cast longer, more menacing shadows, transforming familiar objects into grotesque caricatures. Vogel began to weep, silently at first, then with shuddering gasps, rocking himself back and forth on his bunk. The others stared, their faces immobile, their eyes wide with a peculiar, blank terror.

‘We must investigate,’ I declared, the words sounding hollow and artificial even to my own ears. It was an attempt at assertion, at maintaining some semblance of order and rationality in the face of the encroaching, irrational fear. No one moved. The thrumming pulsed, now accompanied by a faint, almost subliminal vibration in the very ground beneath our feet. K’Tiga, however, slowly rose, his movements deliberate, almost ceremonial. He picked up a long, crudely fashioned spear, its tip blackened by fire, its shaft adorned with feathers. ‘It does not want us to know,’ he said, his voice strangely calm, ‘but it will take what it desires, in its own time.’

The words of K’Tiga, uttered with that peculiar, grave finality which often accompanies pronouncements from those whose lives are lived closer to the elemental forces of existence, echoed in the confined space of the hut, a space made suddenly small and insignificant by the vast, unyielding presence he seemed to invoke. “It does not want us to know,” he had said, “but it will take what it desires, in its own time.” The very simplicity of this assertion, stripped bare of any European metaphysical adornment, of any pretense to scientific inquiry or the comfortable abstractions of theological dogma, struck me with a force that was almost physical. It was a statement of raw, inexorable will, a will that predated and would outlast any human endeavor, any fleeting ambition of conquest or understanding. My rational mind, that carefully cultivated instrument of Western thought, struggled to reject it, to label it as mere primitive animism, the desperate attempt of an untutored intellect to ascribe agency to the indifferent workings of nature. Yet, the thrumming, that ceaseless, subterranean pulse which now seemed to vibrate not only in the earth but in the very marrow of my bones, lent to K’Tiga’s words an unsettling resonance, a dark, undeniable truth that gnawed at the edges of my skepticism.

The air, already thick with the damp heat of the jungle, grew heavier still, a palpable weight that pressed upon the chest and seemed to impede the very act of respiration. The lamplight, flickering weakly against the wooden walls, cast shadows that danced like malevolent spirits, stretching and shrinking with an unnerving plasticity. Familiar objects—the crude table, the piled boxes of supplies, the bunks where men lay or sat in silent, petrified dread—were transformed into menacing shapes, their outlines blurred, their ordinary forms distorted by the encroaching darkness and the internal chaos of our minds. Vogel, poor man, had subsided into a low, guttural whimpering, a sound more animal than human, his face buried in his hands, his body swaying with a rhythm that seemed to mimic the relentless thrumming of the unknown. I observed them, detachedly, as one might study an interesting but ultimately inconsequential biological phenomenon. My own fear, though certainly present, manifested not as outright panic but as a cold, analytical dread, a creeping recognition of my own precarious position on the precipice of an immense and indifferent reality. His eyes, though still bearing that burden of weary knowing, were now fixed, not on the terrified Europeans, nor on the lamp, nor on anything within the hut, but seemingly on something beyond the walls, beyond the immediate confines of our perception. He was listening, I realized, with an intensity that transcended mere auditory perception, listening with every fiber of his being to the voice that the mountain spoke, a voice that was perhaps not meant for my ears, not yet at least, but which was, undeniably, *there*.

“We must go,” I said, the words feeling foreign, almost disembodied, as they left my lips. It was not a command, not really, but an acknowledgment, an internal necessity to confront the source of this oppressive mystery. Besides, a peculiar, morbid curiosity, that insidious human drive to peer into the abyss, was beginning to assert itself. It was the same impulse, I suspected, that drew moths to a flame, a fascination with destruction, with the unravelling of order. No one responded, no one stirred. Their eyes, glazed and vacant, reflected only the trembling lamplight. They were lost, already, to the wilderness that had swallowed their courage, their reason, their very sense of self. They were no longer men, but merely receptacles for fear, waiting to be filled to overflowing.

“K’Tiga,” I addressed the headman, my voice perhaps a shade too loud in the stifling silence, “lead the way. We will investigate.” He turned his head slowly, his gaze meeting mine once more. There was no surprise, no question, merely that deep, ancient understanding. He nodded, a slow, deliberate movement, and then, with a grace born of countless generations spent navigating the primordial depths of the forest, he moved towards the flimsy door, his spear held lightly, almost reverently. I followed him, snatching up my own rifle, its cold metal a momentary comfort against the clammy air, a futile gesture of defiance against the overwhelming immensity that lay outside. The others watched us go, their faces registering no emotion, no flicker of interest or protest. Stepping out into the oppressive night was like plunging into a viscous, warm fluid. The fog, which had been lurking, I now realized, like a patient predator, had thickened considerably, transforming the familiar path into an opaque, shifting labyrinth. It was not merely a physical obstruction; it was a sensory deprivation, a deliberate erasure of the known world. The light from the hut, a dim, yellow smear behind us, was quickly swallowed, leaving us in a world of absolute, profound darkness, a darkness made all the more unsettling by the ceaseless thrumming that now seemed to emanate from every direction, from the very air we breathed. It was an auditory hallucination, perhaps, or a manifestation of the vibration that ran through the very ground, through the soles of my boots and up into my legs, a sensation that made my teeth ache and my jaw clench.

K’Tiga moved with an uncanny certainty, a silent wraith melting into the featureless grey of the fog. I could barely discern his silhouette a few paces ahead, a darker smudge against the lighter grey, but I trusted him implicitly, for he was, in that moment, the only anchor to any semblance of purpose in a world that had suddenly dissolved its accustomed certainties. The jungle pressed in, unseen but undeniably present, its humid breath heavy with the scent of decay and lush, untamed growth. Every rustle of a leaf, every faint creak of a branch, was magnified, distorted, transformed into something menacing, something alien. My imagination, that treacherous faculty of the human mind, began to people the unseen spaces with shadowy forms, with lurking presences, with eyes that watched from the impenetrable gloom. It was a reversion, I mused, to an earlier, more primitive state of being, where every shadow held a potential threat, every unknown sound a harbinger of danger. The thin veneer of civilization, of intellectual superiority, peeled away, revealing the raw, exposed nerve of instinctual fear.

We walked, or rather, drifted, for what felt like an eternity, though the crude clock in my mind suggested only a matter of minutes. The thrumming grew steadily, inexorably, in intensity, filling the void left by the absence of other sounds. It was no longer a distant vibration but a resonant hum that seemed to penetrate the very fabric of my being, resonating within my skull, my chest, my very blood. My ears throbbed with it, my heart felt as though it beat in sync with its alien rhythm. It was a sound that annihilated thought, that dissolved the boundaries of individual consciousness, replacing them with a vast, collective apprehension, a primal recognition of something immense and utterly indifferent to human existence.

Suddenly, K’Tiga stopped. I almost bumped into him, startled by his abrupt halt. He raised a hand, a gesture of absolute stillness, and then pointed, slowly, deliberately, into the opaque curtain of fog ahead. There was nothing, only the swirling grey, the relentless thrum. But as I strained my eyes, straining to perceive what his more attuned senses had already registered, I began to see it. A faint, diffuse glow, a luminescence that seemed to have no discernible source, no clear boundary, but simply *was*. It was a light, yet not a light, for it illuminated nothing, casting no shadows, merely existing as a shimmering distortion in the thick, unmoving air. It pulsed, I realized, with the same slow, immense rhythm as the thrumming, a visual manifestation of the sound, a silent, internal echo made visible.

As we drew closer, the light did not brighten, nor did it assume a more definite shape. Instead, the fog itself seemed to become the source, a luminous shroud wrapped around an unseen core. The air grew perceptibly colder here, a damp, chilling cold that seeped into the bones and made the breath catch in the throat. The thrumming reached a crescendo, a vast, booming resonance that felt as though the very earth were groaning under some immense, internal pressure. It was no longer a sound that could be heard with the ears; it was felt, a wave of pure, raw vibration that overwhelmed all other sensations, all other thoughts.

Then, K’Tiga spoke, his voice a low, reverent murmur, barely audible above the monstrous thrum. “The Eye of the Mountain,” he said, his words seeming to dissolve into the vibrating air. “It watches. It waits.” I peered into the glowing fog, my mind reeling, struggling to impose some rational framework upon this utterly irrational phenomenon. An optical illusion? A peculiar atmospheric effect? The glow of phosphorescent flora? All these explanations, so neatly packaged by scientific reason, felt utterly inadequate, flimsy constructions against the immense, unyielding reality before me. This was not merely an effect; it was a *presence*, a vast, inchoate intelligence that permeated the very fabric of the air, of the earth, of the silent, watching trees. It was the heart of the wilderness, revealed in a grotesque and terrifying apotheosis.

The light pulsed again, a slow, deliberate beat, and for a fleeting instant, I saw it, or thought I saw it. Not a distinct object, not a shape, but a colossal, indeterminate form within the luminous mist, a swirling vortex of energy and darkness, an absence that devoured light, yet somehow emanated it simultaneously. It was a contradiction, a paradox made manifest, utterly beyond human comprehension. It was ancient, I knew, with a certainty that bypassed logic, predating humanity, predating the very concept of time. It was the source, the origin, the vast, primordial power from which all life, and all death, sprang. And it was hungry.

Their Winchesters lay forgotten, scattered on the deck like so many useless toys.

The sun, or what passed for it through the lingering white shroud, began to assert itself, a faint, diffuse glow that promised the eventual lifting of the obscuring vapours. The jungle, which had been reduced to a mere suggestion of form, began to re-emerge, its monstrous density asserting itself once more. But it was not the same jungle. It carried with it the echo of the "Eye," a lingering sense of immense, patient power, of a profound, unyielding indifference that permeated every leaf, every branch, every creeping tendril.

K’Tiga finally turned to me, his eyes, though still distant, holding a flicker of something akin to pity, or perhaps merely a recognition of my own profound disorientation. He said nothing, simply raised his hand, pointing again towards the direction of the receding light, now merely a slightly brighter patch within the still-dense fog. There was no need for words. The message was clear. It had come. It had fed. And now it was retreating, back into the impenetrable depths of the wilderness, to wait for another time, another offering.

I felt a tremor run through me, a belated surge of an animalistic terror, now that the immediate, overwhelming presence had receded. My knees felt weak, my hands trembled uncontrollably. I leaned heavily against the rail, trying to steady myself, trying to reassemble the scattered fragments of my mind. The world, though slowly returning to a semblance of its former self, was forever altered. The thin veneer of order, of meaning, that I had draped over the chaos of existence, had been ripped away, revealing the terrifying, formless truth beneath.

The white fog, now slightly thinner, still clung to the water, but above it, a faint, bruised sky began to show. The sound of the river, the gentle lapping against the hull, began to reassert itself, a small, comforting murmur after the monstrous thrum. But the memory of the "Eye," of its hunger, of its profound, annihilating indifference, was etched deep within me, a wound that no amount of time or distance could ever truly heal. It was a vision of the void, glimpsed through a momentary tear in the fabric of reality, a truth that once perceived, could never be un-perceived. I had looked into the heart of darkness, and it had looked back, not with malice, but with an ancient, silent, all-consuming hunger. And a part of me, I knew, had been taken, irrevocably, into its vast, unyielding depths. The steamer, so small, so fragile, continued its journey, not towards a goal, but deeper, always deeper, into the heart of an incomprehensible truth.—not in the way the men in the hut were afraid. His was a different kind of respect, a recognition of power beyond the confines of human understanding, a reverence born of an ancient, unbroken communion with the very breath of the earth. He simply stood, a dark, motionless silhouette against the luminous, pulsing mist, his spear a useless stick in the face of such immensity, yet he seemed no less secure for its lack of efficacy. He seemed, in fact, more secure, more rooted than I, who felt myself dissolving, my very being becoming porous, absorbing the cold, the sound, the unsettling light. My own mind, accustomed to the neat categories of science and the comforting illusions of control, wrestled with the vast, formless thing. It was an assault on reason, an affront to the very structure of the logical universe I had always inhabited. I felt the foundations of my sanity begin to crumble, not with a sudden, catastrophic collapse, but with a slow, insidious erosion, like a river eating away at its banks, grain by grain, until the entire edifice stood on air.

The thrumming intensified, or perhaps it was merely my own heightened perception of it, a vibration that resonated not just in my ears, but in my chest, my teeth, the very marrow of my bones. It felt like the heartbeat of a sleeping titan, a slow, ponderous pulse that promised, with every beat, to quicken, to awaken, to unleash some unimaginable force. The fog, no longer merely opaque, seemed to thicken into a tangible substance, pressing against my eyes, filling my lungs with its chilling dampness. I could taste it, a metallic tang, like old blood, or perhaps the taste of fear, raw and unadulterated. The light, that formless luminescence, appeared to grow not brighter, but deeper, pulling my gaze inward, into its swirling, unfathomable depths. It was like staring into the abyss, and feeling the abyss stare back, not with malice, but with a profound, indifferent hunger.

I tried to speak, to ask K’Tiga what he meant, what this "Eye" truly was, but my throat felt constricted, my voice a mere croak lost in the vast, booming resonance. What words could encompass such a phenomenon, such an utterly alien presence? My English, even my most carefully chosen phrases, seemed inadequate, childish, a mere clatter of pebbles against the face of a mountain. His own words, "It watches. It waits," though simple, resonated with a terrible truth, a sense of patient, primordial power that transcended all human concepts of time and purpose. He had not said "It sees" or "It thinks," but "It watches," implying a passive, yet immensely powerful, awareness, an eternal vigilance rooted in the very core of this wilderness. And "It waits," for what, I dared not ponder, for the thought itself was a doorway to a darkness I instinctively recoiled from.

The pilgrims, clustered further back on the deck, were not, I noted, looking in our direction. Their fear, perhaps, was too self-referential, too focused on their own imagined perils. Their Winchesters, still clutched in trembling hands, pointed aimlessly into the swirling grey, impotent symbols of a power that could not penetrate this veil. Their faces, pale and drawn, were contorted not with awe, but with a visceral, animal terror, the fear of the known unknown, the lurking savage, the poisoned arrow. They could not conceive of *this*, of a hunger that had no teeth, a threat that had no form, a power that mocked their very existence by its sheer indifference. They saw only the fog, the oppressive silence (which to my ears was anything but silent), and in it, the phantom shapes of their own dread.

One of them, the corpulent man with the sandy hair and pink pyjamas, let out a whimper, a sound so small, so utterly out of place in the face of such vastness, that it struck me as profoundly pathetic. He fumbled with his rifle, dropping it with a clatter that was instantly swallowed by the omnipresent thrum. He then clutched his head, swaying slightly, as if struck by a sudden vertigo. His companions, equally unnerved, shuffled restlessly, their eyes darting, their mouths agape, a tableau of horrified helplessness. They were, I realized with a chilling clarity, completely unprepared for the true nature of this journey, for the unveiling of a reality that lay beyond their carefully constructed world of ledgers, ivory, and the imagined superiority of their race. Their "unwholesome" appearance, which I had noted earlier, now seemed to me a superficial manifestation of a deeper internal corruption, a spiritual sickness that left them utterly vulnerable to the raw, untamed forces of this land. They were soft, like overripe fruit, waiting to be consumed.

I glanced at the black fellows of our crew. Their alertness had not diminished, but it had undergone a subtle transformation. It was no longer merely interest, but a profound, almost ritualistic attention. They did not appear frightened, not in the way the white men were frightened. Their faces, usually expressive, were now remarkably still, their eyes fixed on the luminous fog, not with apprehension, but with a deep, almost ancient understanding. They grunted, softly, to one another, the same guttural sounds I had heard before, but now they carried a different inflection, a note of quiet communion, of shared knowledge that excluded us, the intruders, the unbelievers. Their headman, K’Tiga, remained beside me, perfectly immobile, his head still slightly bowed, his posture a blend of respect and silent contemplation. He seemed to draw strength from the phenomenon, rather than being diminished by it. His steady presence was, paradoxically, both reassuring and terrifying, for it implied a level of acceptance, a familiarity with the monstrous, that I could not begin to fathom.

The thrumming, which had been a relentless, booming resonance, now began to fluctuate, to shift in its timbre, deepening, then rising, like the breathing of some colossal beast. The light within the fog also seemed to pulse with this altered rhythm, expanding and contracting, a vast, luminous heart beating in the primordial darkness. I felt a strange dizziness, a sense of disassociation, as if my own perception of reality was being stretched and warped. Was the steamer swaying, or was it merely my own internal equilibrium that had been disturbed? The world outside, the familiar solidity of the deck, the cold brass rail beneath my hand, seemed to lose its coherence, becoming fluid, intangible, a mere illusion against the overwhelming reality of the "Eye."

K’Tiga slowly straightened, his gaze still fixed on the luminous core of the fog. He raised his hand, not in alarm, but with a slow, deliberate gesture, pointing towards the pulsating light. His voice, when it came, was a whisper, yet it cut through the thrumming with an uncanny clarity, a tone of profound, almost sorrowful resignation. "It takes," he murmured, his eyes wide, reflecting the eerie glow of the mist. "It takes and does not give back."

My blood ran cold. The word "hunger," which had resonated in my mind earlier, now took on a new, horrifying dimension. Not the hunger of the body, of the empty stomach, but a hunger of the spirit, a consumption of essence, of meaning, of existence itself. I remembered the words that had formed in my own mind: "It was the source, the origin, the vast, primordial power from which all life, and all death, sprang. And it was hungry." K’Tiga's simple utterance confirmed my darkest apprehension. This was not merely a physical threat; it was an existential one.

I looked down at my own hands, at the familiar lines and creases, trying to reassure myself of my own physicality, my own independent existence. But even my hands seemed alien, almost transparent in the shifting light, as if they, too, were on the verge of dissolution. The air grew heavier, thick with an unseen pressure, a weight that bore down on the steamer, on the river, on my very soul. I felt a growing sense of emptiness, a draining sensation, as if something vital, something ineffable, was being drawn out of me, absorbed by the immense, insatiable presence lurking within the fog. It was not pain, not precisely, but a subtle, pervasive diminishment, a gradual fading of self.

The silence that followed K’Tiga’s words was not the simple absence of sound. It was a profound, active silence, a void that swallowed all noise, all thought, all sensation, leaving only the relentless, internal thrum and the pulsing, hungry light. Even the faint groans and whimpers of the pilgrims ceased, their terror reduced to a mute, petrified stillness. They stood like statues, carved from their own fear, their mouths open, but no sound issuing forth. Their Winchesters, held forgotten, pointed downward, no longer symbols of power, but of an utter, pathetic futility.

Suddenly, a shift. Not a sudden movement, but a gradual, deliberate change in the very fabric of the luminous fog. It began to coalesce, to draw inward, its shimmering periphery receding, revealing, or seeming to reveal, a greater density, a more profound darkness at its heart. The light, instead of

radiating outwards, seemed to coil upon itself, drawing all luminescence into a vortex, a spiral of ever-deepening shadow. It was not a dimming, but a concentration, as though the light itself, once dispersed and benevolent, was now being consumed by some profound and unimaginable density at its core. The edges of the fog, once solid and blinding, now became diaphanous, fading not into transparency, but into an almost ethereal shimmer, revealing nothing of the world beyond, only a further reach of the same indistinct, pulsating ether. The thrumming, which had been a constant, insidious companion, now seemed to deepen, to vibrate not in the air, but within the very marrow of my bones, a low, resonant hum of cosmic digestion, a sound that spoke of infinite appetite and inexorable process.

My breath caught, not in a gasp of terror, but in a strange, almost meditative suspension. The draining sensation intensified, becoming a perceptible thinning of my very being, as if my consciousness, that most intimate possession, were being drawn out, attenuated, like a filament of spun glass, stretching towards the central darkness. It was a terrifying intimacy, a violation of the self far more profound than any physical assault. I was being unmade, not by violence, but by a form of exquisite, abstract dissolution. The weight that bore down on the steamer, on the river, on my soul, was no longer a pressure, but an emptiness, a vacuum that threatened to pull all substance into its maw. The steamer itself seemed to shrink, its timbers groaning not with stress, but with a kind of weary resignation, its very form seeming to soften, to blur at the edges, as if contemplating its own imminent disappearance.

K’Tiga, his hand still raised, his eyes wide and reflecting the diminishing glow, let out a sound that was less a word and more a deeply resonant vibration, a guttural hum of ancient sorrow. He was watching the heart of the fog, not with fear, but with a terrible, knowing sadness, as if witnessing a tragedy that transcended all human understanding, a ritual played out countless times through the aeons. His earlier whisper, “It takes,” reverberated in my mind, now imbued with a chilling clarity. This entity, this "Eye," did not merely kill; it absorbed, it subsumed, it erased. It was not death in the familiar sense, but an obliteration of the very memory of existence, a silent, ravenous hunger for all that was, all that had been, and all that might ever be.

The pilgrims, those foolish, self-important men, remained fixed in their tableau of petrified horror. Their mouths, still agape, had begun to sag, their muscles yielding to a profound, almost catatonic paralysis. Their Winchesters, those pitiful tokens of their imagined power, had slipped from lax fingers to clatter softly on the deck, the sound swallowed immediately by the overarching silence, a silence so complete that it seemed to possess a physical density. They were no longer screaming inside; their very capacity for internal clamor had been consumed. Their fear, once an active, repulsive thing, had ossified into a grotesque monument to their utter helplessness, a testament to their profound ignorance of the true nature of this terrifying wilderness. Their faces, once etched with a desperate, self-preserving terror, now bore an expression of serene, almost empty acceptance, as if the very force of the unknown had finally scoured away their resistance, leaving only blank, passive vessels. It was a transformation more unsettling than any scream, any struggle; it was the quiet surrender of the individual to the unanswerable, the final, absolute triumph of the incomprehensible.

My own intellect, that fragile construct of logic and inherited wisdom, strained against the impossible, seeking anchors in a reality that was rapidly unmooring itself. I tried to think, to rationalize, to impose some familiar framework upon the unfolding horror, but my thoughts, like smoke, dissipated before they could fully form. The very categories of my understanding—space, time, cause, effect—seemed to lose their meaning, to become quaint, irrelevant relics in the face of this profound, existential entropy. Was this an illusion, a fever-dream induced by the oppressive atmosphere, by hunger, by the pervasive sense of dread? My hand, which I had clenched on the cold brass rail, now felt alien, insubstantial, as if the connection between my will and my flesh was weakening, fraying at the edges. I tried to focus on the sensation, the coldness, the metallic tang, but even that tactile input seemed distant, mediated, filtered through an ever-thickening veil of unreality.

The coalescing darkness in the heart of the fog was no longer merely a denser shadow; it began to acquire a subtle, internal luminescence of its own, a deep, indigo glow that pulsed with a slow, deliberate rhythm, like the beating of some monstrous, unseen heart. It was not a light that illuminated, but a light that absorbed, drawing all other light, all other forms of energy, into its inscrutable depths. It was the antithesis of creation, a void that was not merely an absence, but an active, consuming presence. And with each slow pulse, the thrumming deepened, resonating through my chest, through the deck, through the very hull of the steamer, a vibration that threatened to loosen the very bonds of matter.

I looked at K’Tiga again, his broad chest, once a symbol of his quiet strength, now seemed to rise and fall with a shallow, almost imperceptible rhythm. His fierce nostrils, which had flared with a primal anger just moments ago, were now still, his face a mask of profound, ineffable grief. He was no longer simply watching; he was witnessing, and in his witnessing, there was a profound sense of communion with the inevitable. He understood, I realized with a sudden, chilling certainty, that this was not merely a threat to our lives, but a challenge to the very principle of life itself, a cosmic dissolution that transcended all petty human concerns of survival or conquest. He had called it an "Eye," and now, as the indigo heart of the fog pulsed with its slow, internal light, I began to perceive not a physical eye, but a vast, conscious presence, an intelligence of such ancient and alien magnitude that it defied all comprehension, all categorization. It was not evil, for evil implies intent, and this seemed to operate on a scale far beyond such human distinctions; it was simply, terrifyingly, other.

A profound, melancholic resignation settled upon me, eclipsing even the immediate terror. What was the point of resistance, of struggle, against a force that could simply unravel the very fabric of existence? My earlier fleeting thought of being eaten by the black fellows now seemed quaint, almost comforting in its simple brutality. That was a death I could comprehend, a cessation of breath, a return to the earth. This, however, was a dismemberment of the soul, a scattering of consciousness to the cosmic winds, a complete and utter erasure. It was the ultimate nightmare, not of pain, but of non-being, of having never been.

The very air grew heavy, thick with an unseen pressure, a weight that bore down not physically, but psychically, upon every living thing. The leaves on the jungle trees, once vibrant with the incessant murmur of unseen life, now hung still, unnaturally so, as if frozen in a moment of perpetual dread. The river, which had flowed with such swift, smooth indifference, now seemed to hesitate, its current slackening, its surface becoming unnaturally placid, mirroring the glassy surface of a dead eye. Even the faint, insistent hum of insects, a constant backdrop to the jungle's breath, had vanished, leaving only the profound, resonant thrum of the pulsating darkness.

Suddenly, a sound. Not a cry, not a shriek, but a low, sustained groan, originating not from any single point, but seeming to emanate from the very depths of the jungle itself, a sound of ancient, boundless suffering, a lamentation not of a creature, but of the very earth, of the very fabric of being itself. It was a sound that transcended language, transcended species, a raw, primal expression of cosmic anguish that resonated with the thrumming, harmonizing with it in a dreadful symphony of dissolution. It was the sound of everything that was, everything that ever had been, giving way, succumbing to the inexorable pull of the void.

The pilgrims, those poor, deluded creatures, their faces frozen in that ghastly serenity, began to stir, not with renewed life or terror, but with a slow, deliberate swaying, a synchronized, almost ritualistic movement. Their arms, once stiff, now hung limply, their heads lolling, their bodies responding not to their own will, but to the deep, resonant thrum, to the lamentation, to the insidious pull of the coalescing darkness. They were like puppets, their strings manipulated by an unseen, incomprehensible force, their movements devoid of purpose, of humanity. It was as if their spirits, their very selves, had already been drawn out, leaving behind only the shells of their bodies, animated by some external, sinister energy.

My gaze, drawn inexorably to the indigo heart of the fog, perceived a further transformation. The pulsing light, while remaining concentrated, began to extend, not outward, but inward, forming within its depths a swirling vortex, a maelstrom of luminous darkness. It was a visual representation of the consumption, the insatiable hunger that K’Tiga had spoken of. It was the mouth of the void, opening slowly, deliberately, not to devour flesh, but to ingest the very essence of reality.

And then, with a slow, deliberate movement, K’Tiga raised his other hand, pointing not at the swirling vortex, but at himself, then at me, then at the swaying pilgrims. His lips moved, but no sound emerged, only a silent, profound understanding in his eyes. He was not warning us, for warnings were useless now. He was simply acknowledging, silently, grimly, that we were all part of the same inevitable process, caught in the grip of a force that had no beginning and no end, only an eternal, consuming present. His expression was not of despair, but of acceptance, a quiet dignity in the face of cosmic annihilation.

I felt a surge of something akin to rebellion, a futile, sputtering flicker of the human will, a desperate need to assert my individuality, my precious, ephemeral self, against this overwhelming tide of nothingness. I tried to speak, to scream, to utter some defiance, some word of protest against this unmaking, but my throat was tight, my voice lost, swallowed before it could even form. The sound of the jungle's lamentation and the deep, resonant thrum filled my ears, filling my mind, leaving no space for resistance, no room for thought.

The steamer, which had been gently listing, now began to tremble, not with the familiar vibration of its engines, but with a deep, internal shudder, as if its very components, its rivets and plates, were struggling against an unseen force that sought to tear them asunder. The water around us, which had been unnaturally still, began to ripple with a strange, rhythmic agitation, not from the current, but from an invisible pulsation emanating from the heart of the fog. It was as if the river itself, the very earth beneath, were responding to the call of the consuming void, beginning to soften, to dissolve, to prepare for its own ingestion.

My fever, that constant companion in this land of impossible realities, now flared, not with heat, but with a chilling lucidity, an unholy clarity that stripped away all illusion, all comforting self-deception. I saw, with terrifying precision, the utter insignificance of all human endeavor, all human ambition, all human suffering, in the face of this cosmic hunger. Kurtz, his grand designs, his descent into primal darkness, seemed utterly trivial, a child's tantrum in the face of an apocalypse so vast, so profound, that it rendered all human struggles meaningless. He had sought to penetrate the heart of darkness; but this darkness, I now understood, had always been there, not within the human soul, but at the very core of existence itself, patiently waiting, endlessly consuming.

The white men, the pilgrims, now swayed with an increased tempo, their movements becoming more pronounced, more frantic, yet still utterly devoid of self-will. Their bodies seemed to stretch, to lengthen, their forms blurring at the edges, as if their physical solidity was dissolving, becoming tenuous, ethereal. Their eyes, once glazed with fear, were now wide, black, reflecting the indigo vortex with a terrible, empty fascination. They were being pulled, not by a physical rope, but by an unseen, irresistible current of cosmic suction, their substance being drawn out, thinned, until they were mere phantoms, outlines against the shimmering fog.

His eyes, though reflecting the terrifying light, held a flicker of something else, something almost like an appeal, a silent question. It was a question not of escape, for escape was impossible, but perhaps of understanding, of recognition, of acknowledging the shared doom. The light, which had been promised with the lifting of the mist, seemed reluctant to penetrate the verdant canopy, filtering down in broken shafts, leaving the undergrowth perpetually shrouded in a sombre, greenish twilight, a realm of perpetual damp and shadow where the very air seemed to thicken with unspoken secrets.

My hands, still faintly trembling from the lingering effects of that unsettling cry and the subsequent silence, gripped the helm with a renewed tenacity. The river, a deceptively smooth, brown expanse, revealed its hidden dangers with subtle, treacherous indications: a barely perceptible swirl, a sudden eddy, a glint of submerged wood. Each mile gained was an act of vigilance, a constant negotiation with the capricious will of the stream and the unseen obstacles that lay beneath its deceptively placid surface. The manager, a man whose face seemed permanently etched with the anxieties of his absurd responsibilities, stood beside me, his eyes, small and quick, darting from bank to bank, from the swirling currents to the dense, impenetrable mass of foliage. He offered no word, no advice, merely a shared, silent tension that hummed between us, a recognition of the precariousness of our enterprise. The pilgrims, those grotesque, almost comical figures of colonial enterprise, were huddled on the deck, their Winchesters, still clutched in nervous hands, gleaming incongruously in the dim light. Their fear, which had been so palpable in the fog, now manifested itself in a restless, almost animal anxiety, a constant shifting of weight, a furtive glance towards the impenetrable wall of green that seemed to watch us with myriad, unseen eyes. Their white faces, already sickly, seemed to acquire a more pronounced pallor in the oppressive green light, their movements jerky, almost spasmodic, as if they were perpetually on the verge of some unmanly shriek.

The black crew, however, presented a stark contrast. Their bodies, accustomed to the sun and the raw elements, moved with a quiet, efficient grace. They handled the long poles used for sounding the depths or pushing off from hidden snags with an ease that belied the immense physical effort involved. Their faces, dark and smooth as polished ebony, betrayed little of the fear or disquiet that afflicted their white counterparts. There was a certain alertness in their eyes, a watchful awareness, but it was an awareness born not of panic, but of an intimate, almost primal understanding of this world. They were strangers here, yes, as I had observed, but they were strangers of a different kind, their ancestral memories, perhaps, stirring within them a more profound, a more instinctive connection to the vast, untamed wilderness. I watched their headman, the broad-chested man with the fierce nostrils, as he deftly coiled a rope. There was a profound dignity in his movements, a silent, almost disdainful composure that seemed to mock the agitated nervousness of the white men. He caught my eye for a moment, a brief, fleeting glance that held within it a flicker of something ancient, something both knowing and utterly alien, before his gaze returned to the river, to the ceaseless, hypnotic flow of the brown water. I wondered then, as I often did, what thoughts truly stirred behind those dark, unreadable eyes, what ancient wisdom or savage instinct guided their placid demeanour in the face of such overwhelming, insidious forces.

The day wore on, an interminable succession of identical stretches of river, of dense, unyielding jungle. The sun, when it did break through the oppressive canopy, beat down with a fierce, suffocating intensity, turning the air into a humid, enervating soup that clung to the skin like a second, clammy garment. The heat was relentless, a physical presence that pressed down, sapped strength, and blurred the edges of thought. The constant vibration of the steamer, the rhythmic churning of the paddles, the ceaseless throb of the engine, all combined to create a monotonous, almost hypnotic drone that invaded the very core of one’s being, inducing a state of weary torpor. The river itself seemed to exert a strange, mesmeric power. Its surface, often smooth and reflective as polished bronze, would suddenly erupt in a boil of unseen currents, or be disturbed by the sudden, silent passage of some unseen creature beneath. The banks, though seemingly immutable, were in a constant state of subtle, insidious change. A tree, undermined by the relentless current, would suddenly lean precariously, its roots exposed like a skeletal hand, before giving way with a soft, watery sigh, to be swept downstream, a silent, spectral voyager on the brown tide. The jungle itself was a symphony of unseen life, a constant, low hum that was punctuated by sudden, startling cries: the shriek of a bird, the guttural roar of some hidden beast, the insistent, almost mocking chatter of monkeys. These sounds, though always present, never quite became familiar; they remained alien, unsettling, hints of a vast, unseen world that lived and died with a brutal indifference to our presence.

My "fever" lingered, a persistent, unwelcome companion, manifesting itself in a dull ache behind my eyes, a slight unsteadiness in my hands, and a peculiar lightness in my head, as if my thoughts were no longer entirely my own, but belonged to some wider, more pervasive consciousness that permeated the very air we breathed. It was not a violent fever, no burning delirium, but a subtle, insidious disquiet, a constant whisper of unease that colored every perception, every thought. It allowed for moments of strange clarity, sudden insights into the nature of things, but just as often it blurred the lines between reality and illusion, making the familiar seem strange and the bizarre seem almost commonplace. I found myself staring for long stretches at the twisting roots of some massive tree, at the intricate patterns of the lianas that bound the vegetation together in an almost suffocating embrace, trying to discern some hidden meaning, some secret language in their silent, vegetal struggle for existence. I felt as though I was slowly, imperceptibly, being absorbed by the immensity around me, my own individuality dissolving into the vast, indifferent green. It was a curious sensation, at once terrifying and strangely liberating, as if the superficial concerns of my former life were being stripped away, leaving only the raw, fundamental elements of existence.

We passed a clearing, a brief, unnatural break in the otherwise unbroken wall of vegetation. There was a collection of dilapidated huts, their roofs thatched with dried leaves, almost swallowed by the encroaching jungle. A few figures, dark and skeletal, moved with a slow, aimless lethargy amongst them, their eyes, when they turned towards us, holding no discernible expression, only a profound, ancient weariness. They were like apparitions, ghosts of a vanished past, or perhaps, spectres of a future we could not yet comprehend. The manager, for once, allowed a flicker of something almost akin to pity to cross his otherwise impassive features, but it was quickly suppressed, replaced by that familiar, hard-bitten resolve. He mumbled something about "savages" and "progress," words that sounded hollow and meaningless in the face of such utter desolation. The pilgrims, those paragons of supposed civilization, simply stared, their faces a mixture of disgust and a strange, almost voyeuristic fascination. One of them, the little fat man with sandy hair and red whiskers, let out a nervous titter, quickly stifled by a stern look from the manager. It was a moment of profound, uncomfortable silence, broken only by the relentless churning of our paddles, as we passed these forgotten remnants of humanity, leaving them once more to the indifferent embrace of the wilderness.

The river narrowed in places, becoming a constricted, shadowy channel where the jungle canopy met overhead, forming a green, echoing tunnel. The air grew heavy, thick with the scent of damp earth and decaying vegetation, a rich, fecund smell that was both repellent and strangely intoxicating. The sounds of the jungle intensified here, amplified by the close confines, transforming into a cacophony of rustles, chirps, and unseen movements that pressed in on us from all sides. It was here, in these tight passages, that the sense of being an intruder, an alien presence, became most acute. The steamer, our fragile bastion of steel and steam, seemed to shrink, its mechanical throb a puny defiance against the overwhelming, organic pulse of the primeval world. The native crew, usually so composed, seemed to become even more alert in these narrow stretches, their eyes scanning the dense banks with an almost preternatural intensity, as if expecting something, some primordial menace, to emerge from the tangled shadows. I too felt a quickening of the pulse, a prickling sensation on the nape of my neck, a purely atavistic response to the palpable sense of danger that hung in the humid air like a physical shroud.

We spoke little, the commonality of our apprehension creating a strange, almost animalistic bond amongst us, a shared silence that was more eloquent than any words. The manager would occasionally issue a terse command, his voice strained, almost a whisper, despite the roar of the engine. The pilgrims, for their part, seemed to have retreated into their own private worlds of fear and petty resentment, occasionally exchanging uneasy glances, their grip on their rifles tightening with every sudden rustle from the bank. I found myself thinking of Kurtz more and more as we pressed deeper into this green inferno. What had become of him in this place? What transformation had this profound, inimitable wilderness wrought upon his soul, upon that "exceptional talent," that "universal genius" the Company spoke of with such reverence and envy? Was he still a man, or had he, too, become merely another echo in the endless, suffocating chorus of

the wilderness itself, its primordial song having claimed him entirely, leaving behind only the husk of what he once was, or perhaps, an amplified, distorted version of his true, hidden self, stripped bare by the overwhelming indifference of this ancient land. This thought, like a particularly venomous insect, burrowed deep into my mind, its insidious presence stirring a disquiet that no amount of rationalization could entirely appease. We continued our slow, painstaking ascent, the river, a serpentine path winding through an eternity of green, seemed to resist our passage with a subtle but formidable will of its own, its current a constant, murmuring challenge to our little engine’s strained exertions. The sun, when it managed to pierce the tangled canopy, struck the water in blinding shafts, transforming the humid air into a shimmering, oppressive haze, yet even in these moments of temporary illumination, the shadows, deep and impenetrable, lurked everywhere, clinging to the banks, concealing untold secrets within their inky depths.

The silence that often descended upon us was not the tranquil silence of peace but a heavier, breathing stillness, pregnant with unseen life and unheard menace, a silence so profound that it seemed to absorb all sound, leaving only the dull throb of our machinery, an artificial pulse against the immemorial heart of the jungle. The pilgrims, those self-proclaimed agents of light in a land they considered dark, continued their vigil, their faces etched with a mixture of fear and an almost childish impatience, their Winchesters held with an absurd sense of purpose, as if mere steel and powder could hold back the vast, indifferent power of this primeval world. They whispered amongst themselves, their voices low and urgent, their eyes darting from bank to bank, seeing threats in every rustle of leaves, every splash in the water, their imaginations, untethered by reality, painting monstrous images upon the canvas of the unseen. I observed them with a peculiar detachment, noting how quickly the veneer of civilization peeled away under the relentless pressure of the unknown, revealing a raw, primitive fear that was, in its own way, as unnerving as the wilderness itself. Their fear, however, was of a different quality than that of our native crew; it was a fear born of ignorance, of a profound inability to comprehend, whereas the men who worked our ropes and shovelled our fuel seemed to possess a deeper, more ancestral understanding of the forces at play, their alertness tinged not with terror, but with a wary respect.

The manager, meanwhile, maintained his characteristic composure, his thin lips pressed into a tight line, his gaze fixed on the river ahead, as if by sheer force of will he could command the swirling currents and the hidden snags to yield to his predetermined course. He barked orders occasionally, his voice cutting through the thick air with a practiced authority, though I noticed a subtle tremor in his hand as he consulted his charts, a barely perceptible sign of the strain that was beginning to tell even upon his iron constitution. He spoke little of Kurtz now, his previous pronouncements about the man’s brilliance replaced by a terse, almost grudging acknowledgement of the necessity of reaching him, as if the entire expedition had become a burdensome obligation rather than a quest for profit and glory. He seemed to fear Kurtz, I thought, not with the fear of a lesser man before a greater, but with the fear of an administrator confronted by an anomaly, by something that defied classification and control, something that threatened to unravel the neat, precise order of

his meticulously constructed world. He was a man of ledgers and regulations, of invoices and inventories, and the wilderness, with its unsettling silences and its sudden, inexplicable cries, was an affront to his very soul, a force that resisted categorization, that refused to be neatly entered into any column of profit or loss, and Kurtz, the brilliant, the formidable Kurtz, was becoming an extension of that very wilderness, a wild entry in the ledger of his European sensibilities, an unknown quantity that portended only disruption. The fog, still stubbornly clinging to us, seemed to be an accomplice in this conspiracy of disarray, a shroud drawn over the face of the discernible, reducing the vast, formidable jungle to a mere whisper of shapes, a suggestion of menace without outline, leaving the mind to furnish its own terrors, a task which the pilgrims, with their heightened sensitivities to all things dreadful, accomplished with an almost artistic flair, their imaginations, unconstrained by the mundane, conjuring visions of unimaginable horrors lurking just beyond the two-foot strip of watery reality that still bound us to the world of the tangible.

The sun, an absentee lord, gave no hint of its position, its warmth only a faint, oppressive presence that seeped through the clammy whiteness, making the air thick and difficult to breathe, a substance rather than a void, a suffocating blanket that pressed down upon the steamer, silencing the very pulses of the living world. Not a bird cried, not a branch rustled; the immense, matted jungle, which we knew to be there, though unseen, held its breath, a vast, primeval beast frozen in an act of profound, watchful patience. One felt, in that unearthly stillness, as though the very fabric of time had been stretched thin, almost to breaking, suspending us in an eternal present, a moment pregnant with unknown possibilities, where the distinction between what was and what might be had dissolved into the pervasive mist, leaving only a sense of profound, unsettling uncertainty. The low thrum of the engine, which we kept barely alive, for we could not, in this blind opacity, risk movement, was the only tangible link to the world of purpose and industry, a fragile, vibrating thread against the immense, indifferent quietude of the primeval.

The pilgrims, poor, bewildered souls, had by now abandoned all pretence of their previous, theatrical bravado. The Winchesters, which had earlier been clutched with such fierce, almost comical determination, now drooped in hands that trembled with an unceasing, almost rhythmic tremor, their barrels occasionally knocking against the deck with a faint, metallic clatter that seemed shockingly loud in the profound silence. Their faces, pale and drawn, were studies in abject terror, a terror rendered all the more pitiable by its utter lack of direction; they knew not what they feared, only that fear itself had become a palpable presence, a chill that seeped into their very bones, a shadow that clung to their souls. They huddled together, a pathetic cluster of white linen and European artifice, their whispered consultations now reduced to incoherent mumbles, punctuated by spasmodic glances into the featureless void of the fog, as if expecting some spectral shape to suddenly coalesce out of the whiteness, some monstrous manifestation of their collective dread. One of them, the little fat man with the pink pyjamas, who had so eloquently stammered a plea for meaning, now merely whimpered, a low, continuous sound, like a distressed puppy, his red whiskers, normally so assertive, now seemed to wilt, defeated by the sheer overwhelming power of the unseen.

In stark contrast, our native crew, those "black fellows" whose understanding of time and consequence was, according to my own musings, so fundamentally different from ours, maintained a remarkable, almost unnerving composure. They moved about with a quiet efficiency, tending to the boilers, tightening a rope here, checking a valve there, their movements fluid and unhurried, as if the fog, far from being a terrifying anomaly, was merely another facet of the river's ever-changing temperament, a mood to be observed and navigated with the same calm fatalism with which they approached the treacherous currents or the lurking snags. Their eyes, though watchful, held no trace of the frantic, darting fear that contorted the faces of the pilgrims; instead, there was a deep, ancestral alertness, a knowingness that spoke of generations spent in intimate communion with this untamed land, an understanding that transcended the superficial anxieties of men who measured their lives in brass wire and European goods. The headman, that broad-chested figure with the artfully coiffed hair and the fierce nostrils, now stood leaning against the rail, his dark-blue fringed cloths blending with the grey opacity of the fog, his gaze, though fixed on the same blank whiteness that so tormented the Europeans, seemed to penetrate beyond it, to discern patterns and portents that remained utterly invisible to our less attuned sensibilities. He grunted occasionally, a deep, resonant sound, to a fellow crewman, a communication of shared perception, a silent acknowledgement of a reality far more complex and primal than the manager's neat regulations could ever encompass.

As for myself, I felt a peculiar, almost clinical detachment from the burgeoning panic around me, a sensation perhaps exacerbated by that persistent "little fever" which clung to me like a second skin, blurring the edges of immediate reality, lending an ethereal, dream-like quality to the entire absurd undertaking. The world, as seen through the shifting veils of the fog, and through the haze of my own mild delirium, seemed less a concrete place and more a series of dissolving impressions, a canvas upon which the frantic anxieties of men played out in futile pantomime. I found myself observing the pilgrims not with pity, but with a detached curiosity, as one might observe the frantic fluttering of insects trapped in a jar, their desperate struggles against an invisible barrier. Their terror, I mused, was a purely European construct, a fear of the unknown that stemmed from a profound belief in the absolute knowability of the world, a belief brutally shattered by the impenetrable mystery of the jungle. Their souls, I thought, were not robust enough for this kind of emptiness, this void of meaning that the wilderness so casually offered. Mine, perhaps, had already been sufficiently hollowed out by months of futile striving and sickening observation, rendering me immune, or at least less susceptible, to the acute paroxysms of dread that now afflicted my companions. There was a certain perverse satisfaction in this immunity, a grim sense of having, perhaps, seen too much already to be shocked by mere shadows.

The hours dragged on, each one an eternity unto itself, measured not by the indifferent hands of a clock, but by the slow, inexorable turning of the steamer's existence, a suspension of purpose in a featureless world. The fog, a persistent, suffocating entity, neither thinned nor dispersed, maintaining its absolute dominion over our immediate universe. We existed in a bubble of clammy whiteness, cut off from all that was familiar, all that defined the boundaries of the known world. It was a silence that was not empty, but pregnant with the unexpressed, a stillness that hummed with the latent energy of millions of unseen lives, a brooding, watchful presence that permeated the very air we breathed. The only sounds were the occasional drip of condensation from the rigging, the low, mechanical heartbeat of the engine, and the intermittent, almost imperceptible whimper of the pilgrim with the pink pyjamas, a sound which, for its very insignificance, somehow amplified the profound and terrifying quiet. The world, for us, had shrunk to the dimensions of the steamer, a fragile vessel adrift in an ocean of absolute, primordial mystery, and the thought occurred to me, with a certain chilling clarity, that this was perhaps how the very beginnings of time had felt, a formless void awaiting the first spark of creation, or indeed, the final, utter dissolution.

First, a faint thinning, a ghostly translucence appearing directly above us, a promise of something beyond the immediate, then, a gradual, agonizing dissolution, as if the white substance were not disappearing, but merely becoming less substantial, less opaque. The air, still heavy and humid, began to feel less like a solid wall and more like a vast, unroofed chamber, with invisible currents at play within its vastness. Shapes began to emerge, first as mere suggestions, indistinct and fleeting, then slowly, with increasing definition, the dark, towering mass of the jungle banks reappeared, like monstrous, slumbering creatures rousing from a deep, primeval sleep. The effect was not one of relief, but of a renewed, more focused anxiety, for the sheer overwhelming scale of what had been hidden was now revealed, a vast, green immensity that dwarfed our fragile vessel, a silent, watchful host awaiting our inevitable progression.

The jungle, when it finally allowed itself to be seen, was even more formidable than memory had suggested, an impenetrable wall of green, colossal and ancient, reaching skywards with an almost insolent disregard for human presence. The towering multitude of trees, the immense, matted jungle, with the blazing little ball of the sun now hanging directly overhead, a searing, implacable eye in the vast expanse of the sky, all perfectly still, seemed to absorb sound and light, casting our tiny steamer into an immediate, oppressive shadow. The oppressive quiet, however, was now replaced by a myriad of subtle, unidentifiable sounds, a low hum that was the breathing of the jungle itself, a rustle here, a distant bird call there, all woven into a dense tapestry of life that felt both vibrant and menacing. The manager, his face now more deeply lined, his jaw set with renewed grimness, barked orders to the helmsman, his voice betraying a hint of suppressed urgency, a desperate attempt to reassert control over a reality that had, for a time, utterly escaped his grasp. And for a fleeting moment, I wondered if this, the utter stripping away of everything acquired, everything taught, everything civilized, was not, in fact, the ultimate, terrifying wisdom that Kurtz, the formidable, the inexplicable Kurtz, had himself stumbled upon, in the deep, echoing silence of this immemorial land. The thought was a chilling one, a cold, insidious suggestion that burrowed itself into the recesses of my mind, a seed of dread planted in the fertile ground of my own growing disquiet.his meticulously constructed world. The fog, still stubbornly clinging to us, seemed to be an accomplice in this conspiracy of disarray, a shroud drawn over the face of the discernible, reducing the vast, formidable jungle to a mere whisper of shapes, a suggestion of menace without outline, leaving the mind to furnish its own terrors, a task which the pilgrims, with their heightened sensitivities to all things dreadful, accomplished with an almost artistic flair, their imaginations, unconstrained by the mundane, conjuring visions of unimaginable horrors lurking just beyond the two-foot strip of watery reality that still bound us to the world of the tangible.

The sun, an absentee lord, gave no hint of its position, its warmth only a faint, oppressive presence that seeped through the clammy whiteness, making the air thick and difficult to breathe, a substance rather than a void, a suffocating blanket that pressed down upon the steamer, silencing the very pulses of the living world. Not a bird cried, not a branch rustled; the immense, matted jungle, which we knew to be there, though unseen, held its breath, a vast, primeval beast frozen in an act of profound, watchful patience. One felt, in that unearthly stillness, as though the very fabric of time had been stretched thin, almost to breaking, suspending us in an eternal present, a moment pregnant with unknown possibilities, where the distinction between what was and what might be had dissolved into the pervasive mist, leaving only a sense of profound, unsettling uncertainty. The low thrum of the engine, which we kept barely alive, for we could not, in this blind opacity, risk movement, was the only tangible link to the world of purpose and industry, a fragile, vibrating thread against the immense, indifferent quietude of the primeval.

The pilgrims, poor, bewildered souls, had by now abandoned all pretence of their previous, theatrical bravado. The Winchesters, which had earlier been clutched with such fierce, almost comical determination, now drooped in hands that trembled with an unceasing, almost rhythmic tremor, their barrels occasionally knocking against the deck with a faint, metallic clatter that seemed shockingly loud in the profound silence. Their faces, pale and drawn, were studies in abject terror, a terror rendered all the more pitiable by its utter lack of direction; they knew not what they feared, only that fear itself had become a palpable presence, a chill that seeped into their very bones, a shadow that clung to their souls. They huddled together, a pathetic cluster of white linen and European artifice, their whispered consultations now reduced to incoherent mumbles, punctuated by spasmodic glances into the featureless void of the fog, as if expecting some spectral shape to suddenly coalesce out of the whiteness, some monstrous manifestation of their collective dread. One of them, the little fat man with the pink pyjamas, who had so eloquently stammered a plea for meaning, now merely whimpered, a low, continuous sound, like a distressed puppy, his red whiskers, normally so assertive, now seemed to wilt, defeated by the sheer overwhelming power of the unseen.

In stark contrast, our native crew, those "black fellows" whose understanding of time and consequence was, according to my own musings, so fundamentally different from ours, maintained a remarkable, almost unnerving composure. They moved about with a quiet efficiency, tending to the boilers, tightening a rope here, checking a valve there, their movements fluid and unhurried, as if the fog, far from being a terrifying anomaly, was merely another facet of the river's ever-changing temperament, a mood to be observed and navigated with the same calm fatalism with which they approached the treacherous currents or the lurking snags. Their eyes, though watchful, held no trace of the frantic, darting fear that contorted the faces of the pilgrims; instead, there was a deep, ancestral alertness, a knowingness that spoke of generations spent in intimate communion with this untamed land, an understanding that transcended the superficial anxieties of men who measured their lives in brass wire and European goods. The headman, that broad-chested figure with the artfully coiffed hair and the fierce nostrils, now stood leaning against the rail, his dark-blue fringed cloths blending with the grey opacity of the fog, his gaze, though fixed on the same blank whiteness that so tormented the Europeans, seemed to penetrate beyond it, to discern patterns and portents that remained utterly invisible to our less attuned sensibilities. He grunted occasionally, a deep, resonant sound, to a fellow crewman, a communication of shared perception, a

tacit understanding, perhaps, of the intricate tapestry of the forest, which to us, remained an impenetrable, shrieking mystery, but to them, was a living entity, with moods and humours, with signs and whispers, known intimately since the beginning of time. It was as if their very being was rooted in this earth, their senses finely tuned to its every vibration, while we, with our flimsy clothes and our grand, self-serving narratives, were mere ephemeral spectres, lost and utterly vulnerable in its vast, indifferent embrace. The headman’s eyes, fixed upon the blank whiteness, held a certain gravity, a patient watchfulness that seemed to suggest not merely expectation, but rather an acute awareness of what *must* come, an acceptance born of an ancient, undeniable wisdom. He muttered again, a guttural sound, and the other man, who had been coiling a rope with a quiet diligence that suggested the profoundest concentration, straightened up, his own eyes, dark and fathomless, scanning the impenetrable veil of mist with an identical, profound stillness. They were not, I perceived, searching for the source of the earlier clamour, nor for any threatening apparition, as the pilgrims were; no, their attention was directed towards something far more subtle, a change in the very texture of the air, a minute shift in the pervasive dampness, a fleeting variation in the profound silence itself, which, to our dull European ears, remained uniformly, terrifyingly absolute.

The fog, that blind, warm, clammy shroud, continued to press in upon us, holding us captive in a world reduced to the immediate, the tangible, and the terrifyingly unknown. It was a complete obliteration of perspective, a wiping clean of all familiar landmarks, a dissolution of the very concept of distance. The steamer, our precarious ark of industry and ambition, floated in this aqueous void like a forgotten toy, its metallic hull sweating with the humidity, its machinery thrumming a low, despairing note against the enormous, voiceless indifference of the world. The river itself, which had been our only pathway, our thread of connection to the world of men and their enterprises, had ceased to exist as a navigable reality; it was now but a darker shade within the general greyness, a liquid, invisible presence, felt only by the imperceptible swing of our anchor chain and the silent, swift current that tugged at our hull, a current whose direction and destination were now, in this absolute blindness, matters of pure, unsettling conjecture. The men, the poor, flailing pilgrims, had by now utterly collapsed into themselves, their frantic energy having dissipated into a profound, almost catatonic apathy. They sat, or slumped, or lay upon the deck, their Winchesters, those proud symbols of their supposed superiority, lying discarded beside them, testament to the utter futility of brute force against an adversary that was neither tangible nor comprehensible. Their whispers had ceased entirely, replaced by the occasional sigh, a low moan, or the dry, rasping sound of fear-choked breathing. The little fat man with the pink pyjamas was now curled into a fetal position, his head buried in his arms, his body shaking with a slow, continuous tremor that seemed to emanate from the very core of his being, a vibration of pure, unadulterated dread. They were, in essence, lost children, stripped bare of their comforting illusions, confronted by a reality that defied all their acquired knowledge, all their carefully constructed notions of order and control. And in that raw, exposed state, they were, I mused, perhaps closer to a profound truth than they had ever been in their gilded drawing-rooms or their bustling counting-houses; a truth of irreducible vulnerability, of the vast, terrifying indifference of the world to the puny aspirations of man.

I found myself, not for the first time, pondering the peculiar resilience, or perhaps the profound ignorance, of the human spirit. How quickly, and yet how slowly, does one shed the accumulated layers of custom, of expectation, of self-importance, when faced with the raw, elemental fact of existence in such a place? These men, these ‘pilgrims’ as they so ironically called themselves, had come to civilise, to profit, to conquer, and yet here they were, reduced to trembling blobs of European flesh, their civilised veneers peeled away by a mere shift in atmospheric conditions. What a magnificent, if terrifying, demonstration of the sheer impertinence of our species, to believe that a few paltry ideas, a few scraps of paper, a few lengths of brass wire, could somehow tame the primordial forces that held sway here. It was a grand, cosmic joke, played out with exquisite cruelty upon the stage of this immense, ancient river. I felt a strange detachment, a curious sense of being both participant and observer in this slow-motion unraveling. My own pulse, I noted, was calm, my breathing even. The fever, that insidious companion, seemed for the moment to have receded, leaving me with a cool, almost clinical clarity of perception. Perhaps it was the very intensity of the situation, the absolute immersion in the immediate, that had momentarily cauterised the deeper anxieties, the persistent, nagging questions about Kurtz, about the true nature of this ‘enterprise,’ about the darkening corners of my own soul.

Then, imperceptibly at first, a change came upon the air. It was not a lifting of the fog, nor a sudden shift in the current, but rather a subtle alteration in the quality of the silence. It became, if such a thing is possible, even *more* profound, more weighty, as if the very air itself had thickened, absorbing even the faintest residual hum of the steamer’s engine, the soft lapping of the water against the hull. And then, a sound. Not a cry, not a shriek, but a low, sustained vibration, deep and resonant, rising from the unseen depths of the jungle, a sound that seemed to speak directly to the ancient, pre-human part of the brain, bypassing all the accumulated refinements of civilisation. It was a sound that was not loud in its initial manifestation, but carried an immense, disturbing power, a suggestion of forces vast and unyielding. The native crew, those quiet, watchful figures, remained motionless, their eyes still fixed on the whiteness, but I saw a faint, almost imperceptible tension in their shoulders, a slight widening of the headman’s fierce nostrils, as if he were scenting the very breath of this unknown thing.

The pilgrims, however, those unfortunate specimens of European sensitivity, reacted with a renewed paroxysm of terror. One man, the one who had been whimpering, suddenly sprang to his feet with a strangled gasp, his eyes wide and vacant, as though he had seen some unspeakable horror manifest itself in the opaque air. He stumbled, then fell, knocking over a pail with a clatter that rang out with shocking loudness in the intensified silence. Another began to sob, a dry, wracking sound, devoid of tears, simply an expression of pure, unmitigated despair. Their fear was like a contagion, a palpable wave of panic that threatened to engulf the very deck of our vessel, transforming it into a floating asylum of unreasoning dread. And yet, the sound, that deep, resonant vibration, continued, unwavering, growing in intensity, acquiring a quality that was both deeply melancholic and utterly primal, like the lament of a forgotten god, or the birth-cry of a world still shaping itself from chaos. It was not human, not animal in any familiar sense, but rather something elemental, a voice perhaps of the very earth itself, groaning under the weight of its own immensity.
