The air, thick with the scent of old paper and the humid breath of the jungle beyond the warped windows, clung to one like a shroud. I had sought out Professor Volkov in his forgotten eyrie, a crumbling observatory perched precariously above the winding river, hoping that its distance from the clamour of men might lend a certain clarity to the thoughts that had begun to consume me. He sat, a gaunt figure outlined against the faint glow of an oil lamp, his spectacles glinting, a map of the heavens spread before him like a sacred text. He spoke, not of my journey, nor of the trials that had brought me to his door, but of a comet, lost to the sight of man for a century, that he believed would soon return to its appointed place.

He looked up then, his eyes, deep-set and ancient, finding mine with a disconcerting directness. ‘“It is a peculiar thing, the pursuit of light, is it not, my young friend? To chase a glimmer across the void, to chart its course through an eternity of darkness, and to hold that momentary blaze as the very essence of truth.”’ He pronounced ‘truth’ with a soft hiss, as if it were a delicate insect he might crush. ‘“I have spent my life in this endeavour. I have seen the stars born, and I have seen them die. And yet, the human heart, it remains the most baffling constellation of all.”’ He gestured vaguely towards the window, where only the impenetrable blackness of the night lay beyond.

I had come to him, driven by an unshakeable conviction, a newly found theory, a grand design born of solitary labour and fervent hope, which I believed would redefine the accepted wisdom of my field. My own intellectual ‘specimen’, if you will. I expected enthusiasm, perhaps even a challenge, but certainly not the weary, knowing gaze that now met mine. I began to speak, my voice, to my own ear, sounding strangely thin in the vast quiet of his study. I spoke of years of research, of obscure texts painstakingly translated, of sleepless nights fueled by an almost desperate ardour. When I had finished, a silence descended, broken only by the chirping of unseen insects from the jungle outside.

‘“Ach so,”’ he murmured, his face, etched with countless wrinkles, a landscape of profound thought. He took a long drag from a strange, silver pipe, its smoke curling upwards like a wispy nebula. ‘“A grand edifice you have built, my friend. A towering lighthouse, shining its beam into the darkness. But tell me, this light, this truth you have pursued with such admirable vigour, for whom does it shine?”’ His eyes, though still fixed on me, seemed to gaze through me, into some distant, unseeable horizon.

I stammered, feeling the proud structure of my arguments begin to crumble under the weight of his quiet scrutiny. ‘“For the advancement of knowledge, Professor. For the clarification of the unknown. For humanity.”’

He gave a small, dry chuckle that held no humour, only a profound sense of irony. ‘“For humanity. Yes, always for humanity. And humanity, my dear boy, is a creature of endless contradictions. It builds its observatories to pierce the heavens, and its prisons to confine its own kind. It dreams of charting the stars, and yet it stumbles blindly in the mud of its own making. It seeks absolute truth, but it cannot bear the burden of its own reflection.”’ He tapped the stem of his pipe against the ancient desk, a sound as sharp as a small stone dropped into a deep well.

‘“I too, once, sought to bring light into the world,”’ he continued, his voice dropping to a near whisper, ‘“when I was young, like you, and full of the fire of discovery. I believed that if men only knew the patterns of the cosmos, the sublime order of the universe, they would rise above their petty squabbles, their transient passions. I thought knowledge, pure and unadulterated, would be the great liberator. I had, in my youthful arrogance, charted a course for mankind, a grand, rational trajectory towards enlightenment. I thought to give them the stars. And for a time, yes, for a time I succeeded. I unearthed forgotten calculations, predicted the path of a new nebula, I saw my name in the annals of science, and felt the exhilarating throb of a dream fulfilled.”’ He paused, and a profound weariness settled upon his features.

‘“But then,”’ he resumed, his voice growing fainter, ‘“the world, the real, messy, glorious, heartbreaking world, began to assert itself. My predictions were accurate, my charts precise, but the human heart, ah, the human heart followed its own erratic orbit. I saw men use the very tools of my science to forge new instruments of destruction, to navigate ships of war, to measure the distances for new devastations. The light I had sought to cast upon the universe, they turned upon each other, burning rather than illuminating. And the great truth, the universal order I had so tirelessly revealed, remained for them but a pretty abstraction, a curiosity, a footnote in their own ceaseless, self-made dramas.”’ His face twitched once, a fleeting grimace. ‘“Light, truth, dreams,”’ he said slowly, gazing at the burning ember of his pipe--“phoo!” The ember died, leaving a wisp of smoke. He sighed and turned again to the astronomical charts on his desk. The delicate lines of the constellations seemed to quiver faintly, as if his breath had for an instant called back to life that gorgeous object of his dreams.

‘“The truth,”’ he began suddenly, pointing to a faded star chart, and in his usual gentle, almost mournful tone, ‘“is a terrible burden. I have seen this grand design, this intricate tapestry woven of light and shadow, described. . . . Na! And what is your grand, illuminating truth?”’

‘“To tell you the truth, Professor,”’ I said with an effort that surprised me, for my own confidence had been subtly undermined, ‘“I came here to present a truth. . . .”’

‘“A stellar truth?”’ he asked, with an unbelieving and humorous eagerness.

‘“Nothing so distant,”’ I answered, feeling suddenly dispirited with all sorts of doubts. ‘“A human one!”’

‘“Ach so!”’ he murmured, and his smiling countenance, turned to me, became grave. Then after looking at me for a while he said slowly, ‘“Well--I am a man too.”’

Here you have him as he was; he knew how to be so generously encouraging as to make a scrupulous man hesitate on the brink of confidence; but if I did hesitate it was not for long.

He heard me out, sitting with crossed legs. Sometimes his head would disappear completely in a great eruption of smoke from his silver pipe, and a sympathetic growl would come out from the cloud. When I finished he uncrossed his legs, laid down his pipe, leaned forward towards me earnestly with his elbows on the arms of his chair, the tips of his fingers together.

‘“I understand very well. He is idealist.”’

He had diagnosed the case for me, and at first I was quite startled to find how simple it was; and indeed our conference resembled so much a medical consultation--Volkov, of learned aspect, sitting in an arm-chair before his desk; I, anxious, in another, facing him, but a little to one side--that it seemed natural to ask--

‘“What’s good for it?”’

He lifted up a long forefinger.

‘“There is only one remedy! One thing alone can us from being ourselves cure!”’ The finger came down on the desk with a smart rap. The case which he had made to look so simple before became if possible still simpler--and altogether hopeless. There was a pause. ‘“Yes,”’ said I, ‘“strictly speaking, the question is not how to get cured, but how to live with the knowledge.”’

He approved with his head, a little sadly as it seemed. ‘“Ja! ja! In general, adapting the words of your great poet: That is the question. . . .”’ He went on nodding sympathetically. . . . ‘“How to be! Ach! How to be with the crushing weight of what you know.”’

He stood up with the tips of his fingers resting on the desk.

‘“We want in so many different ways to be,”’ he began again. ‘“This magnificent star, it sits still in its appointed place, a beacon of predictable light; but man he will never on his heap of stardust keep still. He want to be so, and again he want to be so. . . .”’ He moved his hand up, then down, as if weighing two invisible burdens. ‘“He wants to be a saint, and he wants to be a cynic--and every time he shuts his eyes he sees himself as a very fine fellow--so fine as he can never be. . . . In a dream. . . .”’

He lowered the glass lid of a small, antique astrolabe, the automatic lock clicked sharply, and taking up the instrument in both hands he bore it religiously away to its place, passing out of the bright circle of the lamp into the ring of fainter light--into shapeless dusk at last. It had an odd effect--as if these few steps had carried him out of this concrete and perplexed world. His tall form, as though robbed of its substance, hovered noiselessly over invisible things with stooping and indefinite movements; his voice, heard in that remoteness where he could be glimpsed mysteriously busy with immaterial cares, was no longer incisive, seemed to roll voluminous and grave--mellowed by distance.

‘“And because you not always can keep your eyes shut there comes the real trouble--the heart pain--the world pain. I tell you, my friend, it is not good for you to find you cannot make your truth come true, for the reason that you not strong enough are, or not clever enough. . . . Ja! . . . And all the time you are such a fine fellow too! Wie? Was? Gott im Himmel! How can that be? Ha! ha! ha!”’

The shadow prowling amongst the graves of forgotten theories laughed boisterously.

‘“Yes! Very funny this terrible thing is. A man that is born falls into a dream like a man who falls into the sea. If he tries to climb out into the air, into the pure, dry atmosphere of absolute truth as inexperienced people endeavour to do, he drowns--nicht wahr? . . . No! I tell you! The way is to the turbulent element submit yourself, and with the exertions of your hands and feet in the water make the deep, deep sea keep you up. So if you ask me--how to be?”’

His voice leaped up extraordinarily strong, as though away there in the dusk he had been inspired by some whisper of knowledge from the void. ‘“I will tell you! For that too there is only one way.”’

With a hasty swish-swish of his slippers he loomed up in the ring of faint light, and suddenly appeared in the bright circle of the lamp. His extended hand aimed at my breast like a pistol; his deepset eyes seemed to pierce through me, but his twitching lips uttered no word, and the austere exaltation of a certitude seen in the dusk vanished from his face. The hand that had been pointing at my breast fell, and by-and-by, coming a step nearer, he laid it gently on my shoulder. There were things, he said mournfully, that perhaps could never be told, only he had lived so much alone that sometimes he forgot--he forgot. The light had destroyed the assurance which had inspired him in the distant shadows. He sat down and, with both elbows on the desk, rubbed his forehead. ‘“And yet it is true--it is true. In the turbulent element immerse.”’ . . . He spoke in a subdued tone, without looking at me, one hand on each side of his face. ‘“That was the way. To follow the truth, and again to follow the truth--and so--ewig--usque ad finem. . . .”’ The whisper of his conviction seemed to open before me a vast and uncertain expanse, as of a crepuscular horizon on a plain at dawn--or was it, perchance, at the coming of the night? One had not the courage to decide; but it was a charming and deceptive light, throwing the impalpable poesy of its dimness over pitfalls--over graves. His life had begun in sacrifice, in enthusiasm for generous ideas; he had travelled very far, on various ways, on strange paths, and whatever he followed it had been without faltering, and therefore without shame and without regret. In so far he was right. That was the way, no doubt. Yet for all that, the great plain on which men wander amongst graves and pitfalls remained very desolate under the impalpable poesy of its crepuscular light, overshadowed in the centre, circled with a bright edge as if surrounded by an abyss full of flames. When at last I broke the silence it was to express the opinion that no one could be more of an idealist than himself.

He sighed deeply, a sound that seemed to carry the weight of centuries of human yearning within its simple exhalation. The pipe, still unlit, was raised to his lips, and he drew on it with an absent-minded earnestness, as if seeking solace from the unsmoked tobacco. His eyes, though still fixed on that distant point, seemed now to hold a deeper melancholy, a reflection of the very “world pain” he had spoken of earlier.

“And when the dream,” he continued, pulling the pipe from his mouth and gesturing with it, a small, decisive movement, “when the dream is not met, when the reality of the world, with its sharp edges and its unforgiving laws, pricks the shimmering bubble of his aspiration, then comes the real suffering. This is the heart pain, the soul’s deep wound, the profoundest disappointment that a man can know: to find his own essence wanting, to realize that the ‘fine fellow’ he sees when he shuts his eyes is but a phantom, a whisper that vanishes in the harsh light of day. He shook his head slowly, a gesture of profound disagreement with his own rhetorical question. His gaze returned to me, piercing and direct, and I felt as though he were reading the very unformed thoughts in my own mind, anticipating the objections, the confusions, the naive hopes that still flickered within me. His face, usually so placid, was now etched with a deeper intensity, the lines around his eyes and mouth seeming to deepen with the weight of his conviction.

“No, *nicht wahr*! That is not the way for man. For man, to cease to dream is to cease to be, in any meaningful sense. It is to surrender to the dust before the dust has claimed him, to accept a living death. very tumult itself. It is to embrace the very difficulty, the very impossibility, the crushing weight of that reality which forever strives to pull one down from the bright heavens of his aspirations. He must, as I have told you, submit to the destructive element, not with resignation, but with an active, willing surrender of the self, a casting away of the illusion of control, to find control in the very act of letting go.”

He paused, a slight tremor passing through his hands, which were still gesturing with the unlit pipe, as if conducting an invisible orchestra of profound truths. His eyes, fixed on some far point beyond my shoulder, seemed to gather a distant light, a hard, cold glint that spoke of battles fought and insights dearly bought. The air in the room, usually thick with the faint, sweet scent of dried leaves and old paper, now seemed charged with the unspoken weight of his words, a heavy silence that pressed in upon me, demanding not just attention, but a deeper, more visceral understanding. I felt a chill that had nothing to do with the temperature of the room, but with the stark, unadorned truth he was laying bare. It was a truth that stripped away the comforting illusions, the pretty falsehoods by which most men lived, leaving only the raw, arduous path of engagement with the very forces that threatened to undo one. My own specimen, the man I had come to describe, suddenly seemed less a unique anomaly and more a stark illustration of this universal human struggle, a testament to the inescapable dilemma of existence.

“For what is man, my friend,” Stein continued, his voice now lower, a murmur that seemed to rise from the depths of his own long contemplation, “but a creature suspended between the infinite sky of his desires and the hard, unyielding earth of his limitations? He builds castles in the air, he charts courses to distant stars, he paints visions of perfection that mock the squalor of his daily life. And all of this is good, is necessary, for without these dreams, he is less than the butterfly, a mere lump of clay without the vital spark. But the true wisdom, the difficult wisdom, is to know that these castles, these stars, these visions, are not meant to be possessed in the way a collector possesses his specimen, pinned and lifeless in a glass case. No, they are meant to be pursued, to be grappled with, to be fought for, to be sacrificed for, and in that perpetual striving, in that unending combat with the inevitable imperfections of the world and of his own nature, he finds his true humanity. He took a long, deliberate drag on his unlit pipe, the gesture habitual, meditative. The bowl of the pipe was cold, but I imagined it imparting some phantom warmth to his lips, a small comfort in the vastness of his philosophical landscape. His early career had been marked by an almost fanatical dedication to the precepts of efficiency and justice, a belief that through the application of pure reason and unblemished law, the tangled skein of native custom and colonial exploitation could be untangled, purified, and ultimately, made to shine with the austere glow of universal truth. He saw himself, I believe, not as a mere instrument of imperial power, but as an architect of a new, more equitable order, a builder of bridges between disparate cultures, guided always by the unerring compass of his moral conviction. His reports, dispatched with tireless regularity to the distant metropolis, were marvels of lucidity and logical coherence, outlining reforms that, on paper, appeared unimpeachable, promises of prosperity and peace that seemed, to the detached observer, almost within grasp. He was hailed, in certain intellectual circles, as the very embodiment of the conscientious administrator, a man whose principles were as unyielding as the granite cliffs of his native land.

But the colonies, as I have come to know, are not merely territories on a map, nor are their inhabitants figures in a meticulously compiled census. They are vibrant, living organisms, throbbing with their own immemorial rhythms, scarred by centuries of human passion and human frailty, resistant to the neat categories of administrative logic. And it was against this untamed, unruly reality that Henderson’s pristine dream began its inexorable collision. He encountered corruption, not as an aberration, but as a deeply embedded, almost organic, component of the system he sought to dismantle. He faced resistance, not merely from the venal and the self-interested, but from the very traditions he hoped to uplift, from the profound, unarticulated wisdom of a people whose understanding of justice was rooted not in abstract principles, but in the intricate web of kinship and ancient grievance. He saw his carefully constructed plans unravelled by indifference, sabotaged by petty jealousies, and, most cruelly of all, rendered utterly irrelevant by the sheer, unyielding weight of human inertia. The grand, luminous edifice of his ideals began to crumble, not with a dramatic collapse, but with the insidious erosion of countless small disappointments, each one a minute yet agonizing wound to his steadfast spirit.

He refused, however, to acknowledge the failure. To immerse himself in the destructive element, to accept the compromise, to dirty his hands with the necessary impurities of the practical world, would have been, for him, a betrayal, a capitulation to the very forces of chaos and unreason he had vowed to combat. He clung, with increasing desperation, to the purity of his vision, retreating further into the fortress of his own unassailable rectitude. His reports, once beacons of hopeful reform, became increasingly abstract, increasingly remote from the grim realities they purported to address. His administrative decisions, once celebrated for their impartiality, now struck even his most loyal subordinates as rigid, unbending, and ultimately, profoundly unsuited to the fluid, unpredictable exigencies of colonial life. He became known, not for his reforms, but for his inflexibility, for his inability to bend, to yield, to adapt. He was a man who, in his unyielding pursuit of a perfect form, had become a prisoner of his own perfection, watching, helpless and horrified, as the world, indifferent to his immaculate blueprint, simply flowed past, leaving him isolated on his promontory of principle. He was, in Stein’s profound metaphor, the man who, having fallen into the sea, refused to submit to its embrace, desperately striving to climb out into the air, only to find himself, slowly, inexorably, drowning in the vastness of his own unfulfilled dream.

Stein’s lips curved into a faint, knowing smile, a smile that held within it the accumulated wisdom of countless observations of human folly and grandeur. “Ach, the light,” he mused, his gaze once more wandering towards the lamp, which cast its steady, unwavering glow upon the surrounding objects. “It shows us what we wish to see, and also, what we fear to see. A lesson.” He turned his head slightly, his eyes still fixed on the entombed specimen, and a profound melancholy settled upon his features, a fleeting shadow of the immense losses he had once articulated. “For itself,” he repeated, echoing his earlier words, “and for others.”

The implications of his words, layered and rich with meaning, began to unfold within my mind, like the slow, intricate blooming of a rare and complex flower. To immerse oneself was not simply to confront the external world, but to plunge into the depths of one’s own being, to accept the full, often uncomfortable, spectrum of one’s humanity. It was an act of profound self-acceptance, a courageous confrontation with the totality of existence, both within and without.

“So,” I began, my voice hesitant, almost tentative, as if treading on sacred ground, “the struggle is not to conquer the sea, but to become one with it. To learn its ways. To find one’s place within its immensity, even if that place is turbulent, uncertain.”

“Precisely so!” Stein exclaimed, his voice suddenly infused with a renewed vigor, his eyes sparkling with a rare, almost youthful enthusiasm.
