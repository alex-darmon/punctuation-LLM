Each sound, each fleeting sensation, seemed amplified, imbued with a significance it would never possess in the ordered world of brick and pavement. The very air seemed to hum with unseen energies, with the latent power of a world indifferent to human endeavor, a world that tolerated our presence only as long as it pleased, or perhaps, as long as it remained amused.

Suddenly, a slight tremor ran through the deck, almost imperceptible, a vibration that seemed to emanate not from the engines, but from the very river itself. The fog, which had been a solid, unmoving wall, began, infinitesimally, to thin. It did not lift, not yet, but rather thinned, as though stretched by an invisible hand, allowing for a fraction of a degree more light to penetrate its murky depths. Then, a new sound, faint at first, then growing, a distant, rhythmic thumping, deep and resonant, like the beating of a gigantic, primeval heart. It was not the clamour of before, not the shrieks, but something older, more organic, a sound that seemed to rise from the very belly of the earth. The pilgrims, their faces pale and drawn, exchanged nervous glances, their Winchesters suddenly seeming like pathetic toys against this new, formidable presence. Even the headman, whose composure had been absolute, turned his head slightly, his fierce nostrils flaring, his eyes, still fixed on the unseen, betraying a flicker of something, perhaps curiosity, perhaps an ancient recognition.

The rhythmic thumping grew louder, filling the oppressive air, pulsating through the deck, through my very bones. It was a drum, or rather, a multitude of drums, played with a slow, deliberate power, a heartbeat that spoke of something immense, something patient, something profoundly alien. And then, woven into the drumbeat, a chant began, low and guttural, a series of elongated vowels and harsh consonants that had no discernible rhythm, no apparent melody, yet coalesced into a deeply unsettling, hypnotic drone. It was not a language in any sense I understood; it was a sound, a pure emanation of some primal energy, a voice from the very core of this untamed continent. The white men, those who were still capable of thought, seemed utterly paralyzed, their minds struggling to reconcile this ancient, savage music with their preconceived notions of order and progress. It was as if the jungle itself had found a voice, a monstrous, collective utterance that spoke of unspeakable rites, of timeless mysteries, of a profound, unyielding power.

The drums, those invisible, indefatigable instruments of some ancient, unholy ceremony, pulsated with a growing, almost unbearable intensity. They were no longer merely rhythmic; they were a living, breathing entity, a heart that beat with a terrible, indifferent power, a pulse that resonated through the deck, through the very hull of the steamer, through the water beneath, and ultimately, through the bones and sinews of every man on board. It was a sound that knew no quarter, that acknowledged no resistance, a sound that bypassed the ear and went straight to the very core of one's being, stirring there ancient, forgotten instincts, awakening a fear that was not rational, not born of any immediate, definable danger, but elemental, deep, primordial, a fear of the unknown, of the unutterable, of the vast, indifferent emptiness that lay at the heart of all existence. It was not a language, I was certain of that, not in any sense that could be translated or understood by human intellect; it was a pure emanation of primal energy, a raw, unfiltered expression of something profoundly alien, something that had existed long before the advent of human reason, long before the forging of concepts and words, a voice from the very core of this untamed continent, a voice that threatened to unravel the very fabric of our precarious sanity.

I found myself leaning against the rail, my hand gripping the cold metal with an unconscious ferocity, my gaze fixed on the receding tendrils of fog, on the emerging, formidable contours of the jungle, and on the impassive faces of the black crew. There was a profound, almost chasm-like difference between their reaction and ours, a gulf that no amount of shared experience, no common purpose, however fleeting, could ever hope to bridge. The pilgrims, those pitiable specimens of European enterprise, were collapsing inwards, their fear a visible, almost tangible thing, gnawing at their already fragile composure, reducing them to whimpering caricatures of themselves. Their Winchesters, held with a desperate, almost pathetic tenacity, served only to underscore their ultimate impotence, their pathetic reliance on the crude implements of a world that was utterly irrelevant here. But the black fellows, the "savages" whose minds, by the convenient consensus of our civilization, were supposed to be undeveloped, primitive, closer to the animal, these men were not collapsing. They were, if anything, expanding, absorbing the immense, overwhelming presence of the jungle, of its ancient, sonorous voice, into themselves, becoming one with it, as if they were merely hearing a familiar, long-awaited message. Their eyes, previously so quiet in their attention, now held a glint, a peculiar, almost luminous depth, as if they were seeing not with the limited vision of their physical selves, but with some deeper, inherited understanding, a vision that penetrated beyond the immediate, beyond the visible, into the very essence of the primeval.

The headman, whose cryptic utterance of "Eat 'im!" had previously struck me as an instance of grotesque, if understandable, hunger-driven savagery, now seemed to embody something far more complex, far more unsettling. His earlier nonchalance, his almost artistic pose against the rail, had been a mask, perhaps, a thin veil drawn over an ancient, slumbering knowledge. Now, as the drums intensified, as the chant reached a crescendo of terrifying, hypnotic power, he straightened slowly, his fierce nostrils flaring once more, not in ferocity, but in a kind of profound, almost religious inhalation, as if he were breathing in the very spirit of the jungle, allowing it to permeate his being. He turned his head, a slow, deliberate movement, and his eyes, those bloodshot, widening eyes, met mine for a fleeting, disquieting moment. There was no longer the faint flicker of curiosity, no longer the wistful dreaminess. There was only a profound, ancient knowing, an almost chilling certainty that seemed to reach across the vast, unbridgeable gulf of centuries, of cultures, of fundamental ways of being. And in that moment, in the throbbing, all-encompassing sound, in the almost palpable presence of the untamed wilderness, I understood, or rather, I felt, with a profound, visceral certainty, that his "Eat 'im!" had not been merely a crude expression of hunger, but a statement, a declaration, of a profound, existential truth about this land, about its inhabitants, about the eternal cycle of consumption and absorption that lay at its heart. It was not a threat, not to him, not to them; it was a recognition, a fundamental principle of existence, distilled into two stark, chilling syllables.

The sun, which had been struggling heroically against the lingering oppression of the fog, now burst forth with a sudden, blinding ferocity, tearing through the last vestiges of the aqueous grey. It was a violent, almost angry light, illuminating with an unsparing clarity the terrible beauty of the jungle, its myriad shades of green, its colossal forms, its suffocating, abundant life. The white mist, banished at last, retreated entirely, revealing the full, uncompromising grandeur of the river reach, a vast, emerald corridor carved through the heart of the wilderness. And there, upon a slight rise of land, just discernible through the dense foliage, a flicker of movement, a brief, tantalizing glimpse of colour—red, or perhaps ochre—against the impenetrable green. It was gone as quickly as it appeared, swallowed again by the immense, matted jungle, leaving behind only the impression of something fleeting, something almost supernatural, a momentary rupture in the fabric of the all-encompassing green. The drums, however, did not cease. If anything, their power intensified, becoming a physical presence, a vibration in the very air, in the earth beneath, in the water around the boat. The chanting, too, reached a peak, a terrifying, guttural roar that seemed to tear at the very foundations of the world, dissolving the boundaries between sound and matter, between the external and the internal, between sanity and a profound, primal madness.

I felt a strange lightness in my head, a peculiar detachment, as if my consciousness had lifted slightly, hovering above the scene, observing it with a morbid, almost scientific curiosity. The "little fever" I had previously acknowledged, that "playful paw-stroke of the wilderness," now seemed to assert itself with a quiet, insidious power, blurring the edges of my perceptions, lending a dream-like quality to the overwhelming reality. The pilgrims, those absurd figures with their tattered finery and their useless weapons, now seemed even more ludicrous, their fear a grotesque, almost comic spectacle against the backdrop of this immense, indifferent wilderness. They were clutching at each other, some whimpering softly, others staring with wide, unseeing eyes at the impenetrable wall of green. One of them, the little fat man, stumbled backwards, tripping over a coil of rope, and fell with a soft thud onto the deck, his pink pyjamas a splash of garish colour against the grime of the planks. He lay there, a crumpled heap of European impotence, his hands feebly batting at the air, as if trying to ward off an invisible assailant. The others paid him no mind, lost in their own private hells of disoriented terror.

The black crew, in stark contrast, stood like ancient statues, their dark forms sharply delineated against the blinding green of the jungle. Their grunts had entirely ceased, replaced by an absolute, profound silence, a stillness that was not the stillness of fear, but of an intense, almost devotional concentration. Their eyes, now gleaming with an unnerving intensity, were fixed on the jungle, not searching, but waiting, listening. It was as if they were expecting a signal, a specific, undeniable summons that only they, with their deep, inherited understanding, could truly perceive. Their bodies, previously so slight in their everyday movements, now seemed to possess a hidden strength, a coiled energy, a readiness that transcended the mere physical. The headman, still standing by my side, seemed to have undergone a subtle transformation. His rigid adherence to the artificial drapery of his dark-blue fringed cloths, which had earlier seemed an affectation, now acquired a deeper significance, becoming a kind of ritual attire, a demarcation of his ancient authority. His artfully arranged oily ringlets, previously a sign of an almost foppish vanity, now suggested a deliberate, ceremonial preparation, a readiness for a ritual older than time. He was not afraid; he was not even merely attentive. He was, in a profound, unsettling sense, ready.

I felt a chill, not of fear, but of an intellectual apprehension, a sudden, chilling insight into the abysmal gulf that separated us, the men of "progress" and "reason," from these beings who still belonged to the "beginnings of time." Our "inherited experience," our long accumulation of history, of science, of philosophy, seemed utterly worthless here, a fragile edifice crumbling before the raw, elemental power of the wilderness, before the profound, unmediated consciousness of these men. They were not merely present in this landscape; they were the landscape, its living, breathing extensions, its ancient, whispering voices. And the "human secrets that baffle probability," the "something restraining" that had prevented them from "going for us" in their profound hunger, now seemed less a testament to some innate moral quality, and more a function of their own intricate, incomprehensible system of being, a part of a larger, older order that we, in our simplistic, self-serving definitions of "savagery" and "civilization," could never hope to penetrate. Their patience, their stillness, their profound listening, was not a passive endurance; it was an active participation in a reality that transcended our understanding, a reality that pulsed with the incessant beat of the drums, with the guttural roar of the chant.

The steamer, our fragile vessel of European intrusion, seemed to shrink, to become even more insubstantial, its outlines blurred not by the fog, which had now fully retreated, but by the sheer, overwhelming presence of the jungle, by the immense, indifferent scale of the continent. It was a speck, a transient disturbance on the surface of an ancient, brooding power. Our very purpose, our desperate, half-understood quest for Kurtz, for ivory, for profit, for some elusive notion of "progress," seemed to dissolve, to become utterly meaningless in the face of this vast, unyielding reality. The thought, vaguely disquieting, occurred to me that we were not merely visitors here, not merely explorers, but intruders, trespassers upon a sacred, primordial ground, our very presence an affront, a desecration. And the drums, the ceaseless, throbbing drums, and the rising, falling, terrifying chant, were not a warning, not a threat, but a lament, a deep, ancient sorrow for the violation, for the inevitable encroachment of a force that understood nothing, respected nothing, and consumed all in its path. It was the wilderness itself that grieved, that roared, that beat its colossal heart in an unending rhythm of protest and despair.

Suddenly, without warning, the chanting ceased. The silence that followed was not merely the absence of sound; it was a profound, almost physical entity, a vacuum that sucked the very breath from one's lungs. It was heavier, more oppressive, more terrifying than any noise, a silence pregnant with unutterable meaning, a silence that seemed to vibrate with a thousand unspoken threats, a thousand unvoiced promises. The drums, however, continued their slow, deliberate beat, a solitary, relentless pulse that now seemed amplified, each thud resonating with a terrible, inescapable finality. It was the sound of a heart, stripped bare of all adornment, beating in the absolute, terrifying stillness of the universe. The white men, released from the immediate assault of the clamour, now reacted with a fresh wave of panic. the immensity of the surrounding stillness, a stillness that pressed in from every side like an invisible, suffocating shroud. The air, thick and humid, now felt heavy with an unspoken threat, a burden on the chest, a pressure on the very tympanum of the soul. The cessation of the chanting, that tumultuous and mournful uproar, had not brought relief; on the contrary, it had merely stripped bare the raw nerve of our predicament, exposing it to a colder, more penetrating fear. The drums, however, persisted, a relentless, primal throb that seemed to originate not from any particular point in the shrouded jungle but from within the very fabric of the earth itself, a deep, persistent pulse that echoed the terrifying rhythm of some colossal, slumbering heart. The pilgrims, those pathetic, self-appointed guardians of a dubious civilisation, now presented a spectacle of abject terror. Their earlier bluster, their frantic brandishing of Winchesters, had dissolved into a paralysis of the will. The little fat man, his pink pyjamas a grotesque splash of colour against the grey deck, still whimpered, a sound like a small, lost animal. This silence, however, was a void, a monstrous, hungry emptiness that threatened to swallow not only our sounds but our very identities.

And then there were the black fellows, our crew, those men whose inscrutable patience and silent endurance had so recently baffled me. Their reaction to this new phase of stillness was, as ever, a study in contrasts. They stood, for the most part, as if carved from the very wood of the jungle itself, their forms motionless, their faces devoid of the twitching terror that convulsed the white men. Their eyes, however, were not blank. They possessed a depth, a keenness that suggested an internal landscape far removed from our own frantic internal monologues. I looked at them again, my earlier quickening of interest now deepened into something akin to awe, a recognition of a profound, unbridgeable chasm between their way of being and ours. They were not merely surviving in this wilderness; they were part of it, an organic extension of its ancient, brooding intelligence. The manager, meanwhile, had recovered some semblance of his usual composure, a brittle facade of authority that seemed more ludicrous than ever in the face of such overwhelming reality. His concern, I noted, was not for our immediate safety, not for the palpable dread that gripped his pilgrims, but for the disruption, the inconvenience, the sheer impertinence of it all. He saw it not as a manifestation of a vast, indifferent power, but as a deliberate act of insolence, a challenge to his precarious authority, to the very order he believed he represented. His mind, accustomed to the petty machinations of the trading post, the squabbles over ivory and percentages, was utterly incapable of encompassing the magnitude of this primal confrontation. He was a man of ledgers and regulations, a creature of the superficial, and the profound, untamed heart of the wilderness was a concept entirely beyond his grasp, a mere obstacle to be overcome, an irritation to be endured. He still harboured the delusion that this world could be charted, exploited, brought to heel by the sheer force of European will, a delusion that now seemed more tragic than arrogant.

The fog, that warm, clammy shroud, still clung about us, dense and unyielding. The steamer, our supposed refuge, felt less like a vessel of command and more like a derelict adrift in an infinite, formless void. Time, too, seemed to have lost its meaning. My own thoughts, which usually darted with a certain restless energy, now moved with a slow, almost ponderous deliberation, like heavy barges on a sluggish river. The usual anxieties, the mundane concerns of navigation, of fuel, of the elusive Kurtz, receded into a hazy background, replaced by a more fundamental contemplation of existence itself, of the thin, precarious thread that connected us to life, to sanity. The dream-sensation, that pervasive unreality that had coloured my days since venturing into this heart of darkness, now intensified, taking on a clarity, a sharpness that was almost hallucinatory. It was a subtle shift, one that might have gone unnoticed by ears less attuned to the nuances of dread, but it was there, a growing intensity, an increasing urgency that permeated the heavy air. The manager, still standing beside me, seemed oblivious, his mind perhaps still grappling with the indignity of his situation. But I saw a flicker in the eyes of the black headman, a slight tightening of his jaw, a minute tensing of his powerful frame. It was a sound that was at once musical and monstrous, a collective vibration, a chorus of deep, primal voices, chanting, humming, rising and falling with a hypnotic, almost unbearable intensity. It was the voice of the wilderness, speaking not in individual utterances, but in a vast, undifferentiated moan, a lament, a summons, an assertion of its own boundless, indifferent power.

The pilgrims, those pitiable specimens of European enterprise, were reduced to a state of near-catatonic terror. Their Winchesters, held so bravely just moments before, now trembled violently in their hands, some even clattering to the deck with a metallic clang that seemed shockingly loud in the midst of that resonant hum. Their faces, already pale, turned an ashen grey, their eyes wide and unseeing, fixed on nothing in particular, yet conveying an impression of having gazed into some unspeakable abyss. The little fat man, who had questioned the meaning of the earlier cries, now merely whimpered, a thin, reedy sound lost in the great, sonorous drone. He swayed on his absurd side-spring boots, his pink pyjamas, so ludicrously out of place, now seemed only to emphasize his utter vulnerability, his pathetic disconnect from the brutal reality that enveloped him. His whiskers, once so neatly kept, now seemed to bristle with an unkempt fear. Another, a gaunt, dark-haired man with perpetually nervous hands, began to babble incoherently, his words a stream of meaningless syllables that dissolved into the humid air, like smoke. They were, in their raw, unadorned terror, exposed for what they truly were: not agents of civilization, not bringers of light, but merely small, frightened animals caught in a snare too vast, too complex for their limited comprehension.

I, too, felt a tremor, a cold, disquieting sensation that stole through my veins, but it was not the same abject fear that gripped the others. Mine was a fear tinctured with an almost intellectual curiosity, a detached, almost clinical fascination with the unfolding drama, as if my own existence, my own safety, were merely secondary concerns. It was the peculiar detachment of one who has journeyed too far beyond the boundaries of the familiar, whose sensibilities have been irrevocably altered by the incessant pressure of the extraordinary. The hum grew, a tide of sound washing over us, through us, encompassing us entirely. It was not threatening in the conventional sense; there was no aggression, no discernible malice in its cadences, only an immense, undeniable presence, a voice speaking from the very dawn of time. It was the sound of the primeval consciousness, awakened, stirring, asserting its timeless authority over the fleeting intrusions of man.

The black crew, those men whose skins were no longer glossy, whose muscles, though still powerful, had lost some of their youthful hardness, responded with a different kind of intensity. They did not babble or whimper; they did not drop their tools or their weapons. Instead, a profound stillness settled upon them, a stillness that was not born of terror, but of a deep, ancestral communion. Their grunting phrases, which had earlier seemed to settle matters to their satisfaction, were now replaced by an almost religious silence. They stood as one, their bodies subtly tensed, their eyes, not wide with panic, but narrowed, watchful, their gazes seemingly drawn towards a single, invisible point in the dense white curtain. The headman, his elbow still resting on the rail in that profoundly pensive attitude, straightened slowly, his fierce nostrils now flaring more visibly, drawing in the humid air as if sensing some invisible emanation. His eyes, though still bloodshot, now held a glint of something deeper, something ancient, an understanding that transcended mere human language. He seemed to listen, not just with his ears, but with his entire being, to the very marrow of his bones, to the silent reverberations that pulsed beneath the audible hum.

Then, he moved. Not with a sudden jerk, not with panic, but with a deliberate, almost ritualistic slowness. He raised a hand, his dark, powerful fingers splayed, and pointed directly into the thickest part of the fog, towards the unseen bank. No words were spoken, but his gesture, pregnant with meaning, needed none. It was a pointing that conveyed not a specific direction, but an ominous *presence*, a revelation of something that had now emerged from the shrouding whiteness. My gaze followed his, straining against the impenetrable density, trying to discern what he, with his superior, primal senses, already knew to be there. And then, slowly, agonizingly slowly, a shape began to coalesce in the formless void, a darker shade within the pervasive white, a monumental, indistinct mass that seemed to float, not on the water, but in the very air itself. It was immense, shapeless, yet undeniably *there*.

The hum deepened, swelling to a resonant crescendo, and as it did, the form solidified, its outlines becoming marginally clearer, though still veiled by the clingy mist. It was a collection of vertical lines, a multitude of them, rising from the water’s surface, impossibly tall, impossibly slender, yet possessed of a solidity that seemed to defy the ethereal nature of the fog. It was not a single object; it was many, a phalanx, an impenetrable barrier of living things, or things that had once lived. As the hum began to recede, dwindling to a low, sustained murmur, the fog, as if commanded by some unseen force, began to thin, to peel away from this emerging vision, like a shroud reluctantly withdrawn.

What was revealed sent a shiver through my very soul, a tremor that had nothing to do with physical danger, but everything to do with the profound, unsettling nature of existence itself. It was a mass of human figures, hundreds of them, perhaps thousands, standing shoulder to shoulder on the bank, their bodies painted with red earth and white clay, adorned with feathers and leaves, their faces masks of fierce, hypnotic concentration. They were men, yes, but men transformed, men who seemed to have shed the last vestiges of individual personality to merge into a single, collective entity, a pulsating, breathing wall of primal humanity. They held long spears, their tips gleaming dully in the diffuse light that now struggled to penetrate the lifting fog, and they gazed at us, not with animosity, not with curiosity, but with an immense, unfathomable indifference. It was the gaze of eternity, of the wild, untamed spirit of the continent, looking upon a fleeting anomaly, a momentary disruption in its ancient, unchanging flow.

Their hum, which had now subsided to a continuous, deep thrum, was the only sound, a sound that seemed to speak of an infinite patience, an unyielding presence. There was no aggression, no movement, no sudden charge; only this immense, silent contemplation, this massed gaze that seemed to strip away all pretence, all superficiality, exposing the raw, fragile core of our intruder’s existence. The white men, seeing them, were struck dumb. The babbling ceased, the whimpering died, the Winchesters, still held, were forgotten. They stood as if frozen, their eyes, once darting with fear, now fixed in a horrified fascination. Their faces were no longer merely pale; they were grey, cadaverous, drained of all vitality, all illusion of superiority. They were pilgrims no longer, but mere spectres, ghosts of a civilization whose power had evaporated in

that vast, untamed indifference of the primeval world.

Their Winchesters, held with a clumsy, almost ritualistic reverence only moments before, now hung forgotten, their polished stocks gleaming faintly in the oppressive whiteness that still clung like a shroud to the river’s edge. The man with the pink pyjamas, who had so vociferously demanded to know the meaning of the uproar, now stood with his jaw slack, his sandy hair seeming to bristle with a kind of static horror, his eyes wide and vacant, staring, not at the multitude, but through it, as if gazing into some personal, internal abyss. The others, the two who had rushed out with such frantic, almost comical bravado, were equally paralysed, their faces now grey and drawn, their hands, which had so recently gripped their weapons with a spurious air of competence, now hung uselessly, trembling perhaps with a silent, unseen tremor. They were no longer the agents of a powerful, industrial civilization, intent on carving their will upon the wilderness; they were simply men, vulnerable, exposed, stripped bare of their comforting illusions by a gaze that acknowledged their presence without granting them any significance, a gaze that seemed to penetrate to the very core of their being and find it wanting, or perhaps, simply irrelevant.

I found myself, for a moment, caught in that same chilling current of insignificance, that same profound sense of being utterly out of place, an ephemeral speck against an immense, unyielding backdrop. It was not a physical fear, not precisely, but rather a deeper, more insidious dread, a feeling of the ground of one’s very existence being subtly, imperceptibly undermined. The hum, that deep, resonant thrum, persisted, a continuous, almost tactile vibration in the dense air, and it seemed to emanate not just from the massed figures, but from the very banks of the river, from the impenetrable jungle beyond, from the heavy, humid atmosphere itself. It was the sound of the wilderness breathing, a slow, patient, immeasurable breath that dwarfed our anxious, hurried gasps. It was the sound of eternity, I thought, or at least, of a time so vast, so ancient, that our fleeting human concerns were less than ripples on its surface.

My own black crew, I observed, had not succumbed to this universal paralysis, though they too were silent, watching. Their faces, as before, held that alert, naturally interested expression, though now perhaps tinged with something more, a flicker of ancestral understanding, a recognition of forces far older than any European dogma or colonial decree. The headman, the one who had so matter-of-factly offered to "eat 'im," stood beside me, his fierce nostrils still flaring slightly, his eyes, bloodshot and watchful, fixed on the figures on the bank. He did not grin now, as some of the others had, but his posture, his very stillness, conveyed a profound lack of surprise, a certain grim expectation. He was not afraid, I realized; he was merely observing, calculating, perhaps, the next turn of an ancient, predictable dance.

The manager, meanwhile, had managed to retain a semblance of his customary composure, though it was a thin, brittle shell. His jaw was set, his lips compressed into a thin, ungenerous line, and his eyes, though narrowed, darted back and forth between the silent multitude and the utterly bewildered pilgrims. He was a man of regulations, of proper procedures, of the precise application of force and influence, and this situation, this vast, unnerving tableau of passive, yet immensely powerful, opposition, was utterly beyond the scope of his meticulously constructed world. He cleared his throat, a small, rasping sound that was instantly swallowed by the omnipresent hum, and then, finding his voice, though it was a mere whisper, he addressed me.

“Marlow,” he said, the word barely audible above the thrumming, “What do they want? What is their intention?”

I turned my gaze from the bank, from the living wall of humanity, to his pale, anxious face. “Intention?” I murmured, my own voice sounding strangely hollow in my ears. “I doubt they have an ‘intention’ as you or I would understand it, sir. They simply *are*. They exist. And we, it seems, have intruded upon that existence.”

He bristled slightly at my philosophical evasion, his brow furrowing with a mixture of annoyance and genuine apprehension. “But the screaming, Marlow! That dreadful noise! And now this… this display! Surely they mean something by it, some demand, some warning?”

“The scream,” I replied, my eyes drawn once more to the massed figures, “was perhaps a lament, or a cry of profound, unutterable joy, or perhaps a mere expression of the unhinged spirit of this place. As for ‘this display,’ as you call it, it seems to me less a display and more a revelation. They are not performing for us, sir; they are simply being. It is we who are observing, and in observing, perhaps, learning something about ourselves, something we would rather not know.”

He shuddered visibly, a quick, involuntary spasm that betrayed the depth of his unease. He was a man accustomed to direct action, to clear objectives, to the comforting illusion of control. This was a situation devoid of all such comforts, a realm where the familiar rules of engagement, the very grammar of interaction, seemed to dissolve into an unsettling ambiguity. His hand, I noticed, strayed to the revolver he kept tucked into his belt, but he did not draw it. The futility of such an act, against such a mass, against such an overwhelming, silent presence, was self-evident, a grim truth hanging unspoken in the heavy air.

The fog, meanwhile, had continued its slow, grudging retreat, not dissipating entirely, but thinning, forming swirling, serpentine currents that revealed more of the towering trees, the dense, matted jungle, and the grey, featureless sky beyond. The light, though still diffused and weak, seemed to cast the figures on the bank into starker relief, making their painted bodies, their feathered adornments, their impassive faces, seem even more formidable, more ancient. There was no movement amongst them, no shift of weight, no restless shuffling; they stood as monoliths, as carvings brought to terrifying life by some forgotten magic. Their eyes, dark and fathomless, were fixed upon our steamer, upon us, not with hostility, but with an immense, unblinking awareness, as though they were watching a peculiar, temporary insect scuttle across the face of a stone.

And then, as if by some invisible signal, the low, sustained hum began to change. It did not cease, nor did it intensify, but rather, it fractured, subtly, imperceptibly at first, into a multitude of individual voices, a low, guttural murmur that was, if anything, more unsettling than the unified thrum. It was a sound devoid of melody, of rhythm, a chaotic chorus of indistinguishable whispers and grunts, a primeval symphony of raw, unarticulated human sound. It was the sound of a vast, collective consciousness stirring, of a slumbering beast slowly, tentatively, awakening.

The pilgrims, if they could still be called that, were utterly undone by this new development. The fat man with the pyjamas made a small, choked sound, a sort of gurgle, and his eyes, which had been fixed and vacant, now began to dart about wildly, like those of a cornered animal. Another of them, a lanky individual with a sallow complexion and a perpetually anxious air, began to tremble uncontrollably, his Winchester clattering against the deck as he tried, unsuccessfully, to bring it to bear. He pointed it, vaguely, at the bank, but his finger seemed unable to find the trigger, his mind unable to issue the command. They were caught, I thought, in the insidious trap of their own making, lured by the promise of easy gain into a realm where their comfortable certainties, their very definitions of reality, were dissolving like sugar in a cup of boiling water.

I felt, strangely enough, a fleeting, almost perverse satisfaction at their distress. Their unwholesome, self-serving greed, their casual cruelty towards the very men who toiled for them, had always struck me as a peculiarly repulsive form of blindness, a moral myopia that prevented them from seeing anything beyond the narrow confines of their own appetites. Now, confronted with something that refused to conform to their commercial ledgers, something that transcaked their paltry schemes, they were revealed for what they truly were: weak, frightened, utterly inadequate. Their illusions of superiority, so carefully constructed and maintained in the artificial light of distant European cities, were now laid bare, exposed to the pitiless, indifferent gaze of the true masters of this land.

The headman beside me, whose presence I had almost forgotten in my contemplation, suddenly grunted, a low, almost contemptuous sound. He lifted a hand, a broad, powerful hand, and pointed a finger, not at the multitude on the bank, but at the quivering, almost catatonic pilgrims. His eyes, though still bloodshot, held a glint of something akin to amusement, a dark, sardonic intelligence. He then turned his gaze back to the bank, and his lips, thick and dark, parted to emit a series of soft, resonant clicks and guttural murmurs, sounds that were utterly alien to my ears, yet seemed to carry an undeniable weight of meaning, of communication.

The collective murmur from the bank intensified, subtly, almost imperceptibly, becoming more organised, more responsive. It was like a forest stirring, a million leaves rustling in a rising wind, each sound distinct yet blending into a vast, coherent whole. And then, from somewhere within the mass, a single voice rose, clear and piercing, a wailing, mournful cry that seemed to rip through the dense air, carrying with it a profound sense of loss, of lamentation. It was a sound that tugged at something deep within me, something ancient and forgotten, a chord of shared human sorrow that transcended all cultural barriers, all superficial differences. It was the cry, I suspected, not of rage or aggression, but of immense, unutterable suffering, of a history of pain woven into the very fabric of the land.

This wail, instead of terrifying the pilgrims further, seemed to shock them out of their paralysis, but not into action. It shocked them into a deeper, more profound despair. The man in pink pyjamas, his eyes now welling with tears, began to whimper, a small, pathetic sound, like a child lost in a vast, terrifying darkness. He looked, I thought, like a grotesque parody of a civilized man, his finery, his pampered existence, now utterly meaningless in the face of this primal reality. His companion, the sallow man, dropped his Winchester with a metallic clatter, which, for a moment, cut through the pervasive hum, a jarring, artificial sound in a world now dominated by the organic, the primeval. He collapsed onto the deck, a heap of trembling flesh, his face buried in his arms, his body convulsing with silent, uncontrollable sobs.

The manager watched this display of abject terror with a grim, almost detached disgust. His own fear, I suspected, was of a different order, a cold, calculating apprehension for the loss of property, for the failure of his mission, for the unraveling of the carefully constructed edifice of his authority. He glanced at me, his eyes now holding a question, a desperate appeal for some form of solution, some rational course of action in this utterly irrational landscape.

“Marlow,” he began, his voice still hushed, but with an edge of steel returning to it, “we cannot remain here. We must proceed. What do you suggest?”

I looked at him, then at the abject figures on the deck, then at the immovable, silent multitude on the bank. “Proceed where, sir?” I asked quietly. “To what destination? And by what means? The river, you observe, is still partially obscured, and the very air seems to hold us captive.” I swept my hand in a slow, encompassing gesture towards the fog and the river, towards the impenetrable jungle that seemed to press in from all sides. “They are waiting,” I added, my voice almost a whisper. “They have been waiting, I suspect, for a very long time.”

My words, though delivered without overt malice, seemed to further discomfit him. He ran a hand through his thinning hair, his face a mask of frustration and growing desperation. He was a man out of his depth, a minor cog in a vast, rapacious machine, now confronted with the terrifying reality that the machine itself was a fragile, artificial construct, liable to crumble into dust at the slightest touch of genuine wilderness.

Suddenly, the headman spoke again, this time to me, his voice low and urgent, yet still imbued with that strange, sardonic amusement. “They hungry,” he said, his eyes still fixed on the bank. “Much hungry.” He then gestured towards the collapsed pilgrim, his lips twisting into something that was not quite a smile, but a grimace of profound, ancient understanding. “Him fat. Good.”

I felt a shiver run down my spine, not of fear, but of a chilling recognition. His words, delivered with such blunt, brutal honesty, cut through the layers of my own detached observation, reminding me of the precariousness of our position, of the stark, undeniable realities that governed this land, realities that the pilgrims, in their blinkered greed, had so blithely ignored. I recalled my earlier thought, my fleeting, almost humorous apprehension that I myself might be considered "unappetizing." The humor, I realised, had been a thin, fragile shield against a truth far more profound, far more unsettling. We were, all of us, merely flesh and blood, organisms in a vast, complex ecosystem, and in this ecosystem, some organisms were prey, and others were predators, and the lines between civilization and savagery, between progress and primal survival, were astonishingly thin, perhaps even non-existent.

The white fog, as if on cue, began its final, decisive retreat, revealing now the full, overwhelming majesty of the riverbanks. The sun, a pale, anemic disk, struggled to penetrate the lingering haze, casting a sickly, yellowish light upon the scene. And what was revealed was not a continuous wall of human figures, but rather, a series of crude, roughly hewn canoes, dugouts, long and narrow, resting on the mud at the water’s edge, each manned by a silent, watchful figure, their paddles poised, ready. They were the outer perimeter, the silent guard, and the massed figures behind them, still indistinct in the partial gloom, were the true, immeasurable heart of this ancient, unyielding presence.

A sound, a new sound, distinct from the hum, from the wail, now reached our ears. It was a rhythmic, guttural chant, a low, hypnotic chorus that rose and fell, rising with the slow, deliberate movement of a collective breath, falling with a long, drawn-out sigh. It was a sound that seemed to speak of centuries, of generations, of a deep, unbroken connection to the earth, to the river, to the very spirit of the jungle. It was a sound that spoke, not of aggression, but of possession, of an inherent, unassailable right to this land, to this moment, to this profound, undeniable presence.

The pilgrims, however, heard only menace, only the savage prelude to their inevitable demise. The manager, his face now a ghastly shade of grey, turned to me, his eyes wide with a desperate, almost pleading terror. “Marlow! What do we do? We must… we must defend ourselves!”

I looked at the Winchesters, lying scattered or held weakly in trembling hands, symbols of a power that had evaporated in the face of true, unyielding reality. “Defend ourselves,” I repeated slowly, the words tasting like ash in my mouth. “Against what, precisely? A chant? A gaze? The unassailable right of existence?” I paused, letting the silence and the rising, hypnotic chant fill the space between us. “And with what, sir? With our paltry instruments of destruction, our thin veneer of civilization, our hollow claims of superiority?”

He stared at me, his eyes unblinking, his mouth slightly agape, as if struggling to comprehend the profound, unsettling implication of my words. He was a man whose entire worldview was based on the premise of his inherent right to dominate, to control, to exploit. Now, that premise was being systematically dismantled, piece by painful piece, by the very wilderness he had sought to conquer.

The headman, who had been listening to our exchange with a curious, almost academic interest, then made a small, decisive gesture with his hand towards the bank. Immediately, a low, rumbling response came from his crew, a series of short, grunting phrases, their own ancient language, their own pragmatic understanding of the situation. Their alert faces, though still quiet, now held a certain, almost grim, resolve. They were not, I realised, simply observing; they were preparing. They were ready, in their own way, to navigate this complex, treacherous passage, ready to confront the ancient, hungry spirit of the land, ready to make their own choices, to follow their own instincts, unbound by the fragile, collapsing illusions of their white employers.

I looked at the white men, at their cadaverous faces, their trembling hands, their eyes, which had lost all trace of their former arrogance, their former self-assurance. They were reduced to their most basic, most vulnerable selves, stripped bare by the pitiless gaze of the wilderness, by the indifferent immensity of the continent. They had come to conquer, to extract, to impose their will, but the wilderness, vast and ancient, had merely contemplated them, and in that contemplation, had revealed their utter fragility, their profound insignificance. The chant from the bank rose in intensity, a slow, inexorable wave of sound, and the silent figures in the canoes began, almost imperceptibly, to stir, their paddles dipping gently into the dark, swirling water, not in aggression, but in a slow, deliberate movement of anticipation, of an ancient, waiting patience.

It was then, in that moment of profound, unnerving stillness, punctuated only by the rising chant and the gentle lapping of water, that I understood the true, terrifying nature of what we faced. It was not a battle, not a confrontation in the usual sense, but a test, a brutal, unforgiving examination of the very core of our being, of our humanity, of our right to simply exist in this vast, indifferent, and profoundly ancient land. And I knew, with a certainty that chilled me to the bone, that many, if not all, of those who now stood trembling on our deck, clinging to their useless symbols of power, would fail that test, their souls dissolving into the pervasive, consuming darkness that lay at the heart of this formidable, untamed wilderness. The knowledge hung in the air, thick and heavy, like the fog itself, a premonition of dissolution, of a reckoning long overdue.

The chant, a sustained, guttural thrumming that seemed to rise from the very soil and water, was not music in any sense I had ever known; it was a rhythmic pulse, a primeval heartbeat that resonated not in the ears alone, but deep in the chest, a vibration of the air itself, laden with meaning older than thought. It was a lament, perhaps, or a summons, or both, but above all it was an affirmation of a reality that utterly negated our own, a declaration of sovereignty by powers we had long since forgotten, or perhaps, in our colossal vanity, had never even acknowledged. The white men on deck, those figures of European conquest and commercial aspiration, writhed inwardly under its pressure. Their faces, already pale with confinement and the general unhealth of the tropics, now seemed to drain of what little vitality remained, becoming cadaverous masks of fear. The little fat pilgrim, whose pink pyjamas and side-spring boots had seemed so absurdly out of place earlier, now appeared grotesquely tragic, his eyes rolling back in their sockets, his mouth a quivering, shapeless cavity from which no sound emerged, though his whole being seemed to shriek in silent agony. His hands, which had gripped his Winchester with such impotent confidence, now trembled so violently that the polished wood of the stock rattled against the brass fittings of the ship, a pitiful, insignificant counterpoint to the profound, all-encompassing bass of the chant.

Another, a man I had taken for a clerk, with thin, precise lips and spectacles perched precariously on his nose, began to whimper softly, a high, reedy sound that was quickly swallowed by the fog and the relentless, rising chorus. He huddled against the deckhouse, his arms wrapped around himself as if to hold his very essence together, his gaze fixed on nothing, or perhaps on the terrifying emptiness that now seemed to yawn before him, within him. They were dissolving, these men of law and commerce, these bearers of progress and enlightenment; their rigid structures of self, built on generations of acquired superiority and the comforting certainties of a distant civilization, were crumbling under the raw, unmediated assault of the wilderness. It was not a physical threat that undid them, not yet, but a spiritual one, a profound and absolute negation of their perceived significance, a reduction to mere quivering flesh in the face of an immemorial force. They had come to exploit, to extract, to impose; but the wilderness, vast and indifferent, had merely contemplated them, and in that contemplation, had revealed their utter fragility, their profound insignificance, the thinness of the membrane separating their ordered world from the engulfing chaos. The fog, dense and white and suffocatingly warm, pressed in on us, a tangible representation of this dissolution, blurring the edges of the steamer, making her seem less a vessel of solid iron and wood and more a phantom ship, poised on the brink of fading entirely from existence. The world beyond her narrow, misty circle of visibility had ceased to exist, swallowed by an immeasurable, indifferent void.

I, too, felt the insidious creep of that primal dread, that awareness of cosmic indifference, but it was overlaid by a peculiar, almost clinical detachment, as if I were observing a scientific experiment on the limits of the human psyche. My fever, that familiar companion of those desolate waterways, seemed to quicken its pulse, sharpening my senses while simultaneously blurring the lines between external reality and internal perception. The chant now seemed to whisper directly into the fevered convolutions of my brain, a language without words, conveying not threats, but simply being, an ancient, undeniable presence. I looked towards the riverbank, or where I imagined it to be, through the opaque curtain of the fog. The stirring of the canoes, almost imperceptible at first, became more pronounced, a subtle, undulating motion that hinted at a latent power, a coiled readiness. They were not approaching aggressively, not yet, but rather positioning themselves, like predators observing their prey, not with malice, but with a cold, pragmatic calculation born of necessity and an intimate knowledge of their domain. These were not the hurried, desperate movements of attack, but the deliberate, assured motions of those who understood the rhythm of their world, who moved with the patience of geological time.

The headman, that formidable figure of quiet strength, remained near me, his dark-blue fringed cloths hanging almost motionless in the heavy air. His fierce nostrils, which I had noted earlier, now seemed to flare ever so slightly, catching the scent of something in the fog, something perhaps imperceptible to our dulled European senses. His eyes, though still essentially quiet, held a light I could only describe as ancient, a spark of intelligence that seemed to comprehend all that was unfolding, not as a sudden crisis, but as a predestined phase in a much larger, slower cycle of existence. He was not afraid; that much was clear. He was observing, waiting, and in his stillness, there was an immense, unspoken authority, a primal wisdom that made the frantic, pathetic gestures of the white pilgrims seem like the meaningless flutterings of insects caught in a vast, invisible web.

“They prepare,” he murmured, his voice a low rasp that cut through the chant without disturbing its flow, a voice that spoke as if from the very heart of the fog itself. I looked at him, startled by the unexpected utterance, by the precise, almost laconic nature of his observation. “For what, headman?” I asked, my own voice, though I tried to steady it, sounding thin and brittle in comparison. He turned his head slowly, his gaze meeting mine with an intensity that seemed to peel away layers of accumulated convention and pretense. “For the spirit,” he said, simply, the words hanging in the air like carved symbols, each one heavy with an immeasurable weight of meaning. “The great spirit of the river. It calls. It judges.”

His words, simple as they were, struck me with the force of a physical blow. The "spirit of the river." It was not a metaphor for them, not a poetic flourish, but a living, breathing entity, a consciousness woven into the very fabric of this land, as tangible and undeniable as the fog itself. And it called, and it judged. This was the test, then, the reckoning. Not a fight against men, but a confrontation with the very essence of the primeval world, a trial before a court whose laws were written in the currents and the trees, whose judgment was absolute, inexorable. The white men, in their narrow-minded pursuit of ivory and profit, had never even conceived of such a court, such a law. They had brought their own laws, their own petty rules of acquisition and governance, into a domain where such things were meaningless, utterly without purchase. They were children

They were children, these men with their ridiculous boots and their absurd notions of profit and dominion; children lost in a wilderness far older than their history, a wilderness that neither acknowledged nor cared for their puny aspirations. They brought their trinkets, their ledgers, their rifles, believing these paltry extensions of their will could tame a force as immense and indifferent as the primeval forest itself, or compel a spirit whose very breath was the mist that enveloped us. They had not the faintest glimmer of understanding for the nature of the entity they had unwittingly provoked, the profound, slow, undeniable power that was gathering its forces in the impenetrable veil of the fog. Their fear, which contorted their faces into masks of impotent rage and despair, was the fear of a nursery, a childish terror of the dark corners, of imagined monsters beneath the bed; but here, the monsters were not imagined, and the dark was not a corner, but the very fabric of existence, vast, ancient, and utterly merciless in its judgment.

The headman’s words, heavy as they were with an immeasurable weight of meaning, continued to reverberate within the peculiar silence that followed the last shrieks; they were not simply sounds, but a kind of knowledge, a pronouncement from the very heart of this immense and unyielding mystery. “For the spirit,” he had said, “The great spirit of the river. The pilgrims, those representatives of a civilization so certain of its own supremacy, reacted with a frantic, almost ludicrous disarray, a tableau of unmasked, unreasoning fear. The little fat man with the sandy hair and pink pyjamas, who had stammered his question about the meaning of it all, had by now retreated into a corner, his face a ghastly white, his eyes darting with the wild, desperate appeal of a trapped animal. He muttered incessantly to himself, a low, unintelligible stream of words, like a prayer forgotten or a curse half-formed. The others, the two who had rushed out with their Winchesters, now stood frozen, their rifles still clutched in trembling hands, their faces streaked with sweat and fear, their mouths agape, as if attempting to swallow the very air, or perhaps to utter a scream that refused to escape their throats. They looked less like men armed for defense and more like grotesques in a macabre pantomime, their civilized pretensions stripped bare by the raw, brutal touch of the untamed. Their fear, I observed with a detached, almost scientific curiosity, was not merely of physical danger; it was the deeper, more insidious terror of the unknown, the fear of a world suddenly refusing to conform to their preconceived notions, a reality too vast, too indifferent, too utterly alien for their limited, comfortable understanding. Their very souls seemed to shrivel, exposed and vulnerable, before the indifferent majesty of that unnameable sound and the suffocating silence that now embraced us.

My own condition, compounded by the constant, dull ache of the fever, had long since ceased to be one of mere observation. I was no longer an external viewer of this unfolding drama, but an integral part of its fabric, woven into its very threads, my perceptions distorted, my judgments clouded, my grip on the familiar tenuous at best. The distinction between myself and the enveloping wilderness had blurred to the point of extinction. I felt myself permeable, open to the whispers of the immense forest, to the subtle vibrations of the hidden river, to the very pulse of the land itself. The world was not merely seen or heard; it was felt, tasted, absorbed, a continuous, seamless flow of sensations, impressions, and profound, unsettling insights. The rational, analytical mind, that fragile instrument of human conceit, had receded, giving way to a more primitive, intuitive mode of being, a state closer to that of the men who lay sprawled on the deck, exhausted from their communion with the ineffable.

The white fog, meanwhile, remained, a motionless, impenetrable wall, seemingly denser now, more solid, as if the air itself had congealed into a tangible substance, a cosmic cotton wool pressed against our very existence. It did not merely obscure the view; it annihilated it, wiping away all contours, all distances, all familiar landmarks, leaving us suspended in a featureless void, a kind of existential limbo where time and space lost their meaning. There was no sky, no horizon, no river bank; there was only the immediate, clammy pressure of the mist, the faint, disembodied groans of the steamer, and the almost imperceptible tremor of the water beneath us. It was a perfect isolation, a separation not merely from the rest of the world, but from the very concept of a world beyond the immediate, suffocating confines of our momentary prison.

Hours passed, or perhaps mere minutes; the passage of time had become a matter of purely subjective experience, a distortion of the fevered mind. The initial terror of the pilgrims had given way to a kind of stunned, catatonic despair. They sat or lay, huddled together, their faces grey and slack, their eyes vacant, like dolls discarded after a child’s game. Their Winchesters, once symbols of their supposed mastery, now lay forgotten, useless artifacts in a world that had suddenly rendered all such instruments of control utterly meaningless. They were no longer men of purpose or ambition; they were simply flesh, vulnerable, exposed, awaiting a fate they could neither comprehend nor influence.

I, too, felt the insidious creep of despair, a cold, wet tendril reaching into the heart of my being. But it was not the despair of fear, not a dread of personal annihilation, for that particular, self-serving concern seemed a triviality in the face of the vast, impersonal force that encompassed us. My despair was rather that of an understanding too profound, too unsettling, a glimpse into the ultimate insignificance of all human endeavor, all human striving, against the immense, indifferent backdrop of eternity. The whispers of the wilderness, which I had welcomed, had now become a clamor, a cacophony of truths too stark, too ancient, too utterly devoid of comfort. They spoke of the vanity of progress, the fragility of order, and the enduring, immutable power of the primeval.

Then, slowly, almost imperceptibly at first, the sounds began again, not the earth-shaking roar, nor the primal chants, but a softer, more insidious symphony of the unseen. It was a rustling, a slithering, a kind of vast, collective breathing, as if the very jungle had awakened, not to reveal itself, but to stir, to shift, to expand in the suffocating embrace of the fog. It was a sound that suggested an immense, unseen multitude, a vast, living tapestry of creeping things and hidden beasts, all moving with a synchronized, silent purpose just beyond the veil of white. My fevered imagination conjured images of eyes, a thousand, a million eyes, green and yellow and black, all fixed upon our helpless steamer, observing us with an ancient, predatory patience, waiting for some signal, some moment of weakness, some opportune lapse in the precarious balance of our existence.

The headman, whom I had observed earlier in his profound stillness, now stirred. His eyes, though still unfocused, began to move, scanning the impenetrable white. A low grunt escaped his lips, a sound of recognition, or perhaps of renewed resolve. He lifted his hand, slowly, deliberately, and pointed a finger, not at anything visible, for nothing was visible, but into the thickest part of the fog, towards the invisible bank that we knew lay somewhere to our left. His movement was not frantic, not the wild gesticulation of fear, but the precise, almost ritualistic gesture of one who perceives a truth beyond the immediate, who acknowledges a presence that defies the senses.

“They come,” he said, his voice a low, guttural murmur, but clear enough in the enveloping silence. His eyes, now regaining a flicker of their fierce intelligence, rested briefly upon me, a gaze that seemed to penetrate the layers of my European consciousness and touch upon the nascent, primitive understanding that had begun to blossom within me. “They come for Kurtz. They hear the voice. The voice calls.”

His words, simple as they were, pierced through the haze of my fever, igniting a fresh spark of apprehension, though its nature was subtly different now. It was no longer the crude fear of a physical assault, but a more profound dread, a terror born of the realization that Kurtz, that enigmatic, distant figure, was not merely a man to be found, but a nexus, a focal point, a magnet drawing to himself the very essence of this terrifying wilderness. He was not merely a victim to be rescued, nor a renegade to be judged; he was, perhaps, the ultimate initiate, the one who had descended further, understood more, and perhaps, embraced the monstrous truth with a terrifying, unholy fervor. The thought, unbidden and chilling, occurred to me then that we were not pursuing Kurtz, but being drawn to him, inexorably, irresistibly, like iron filings to a powerful, unseen force, a force that originated not from a place on a map, but from the depths of a soul.

The pilgrims, however, understood nothing of this. Their eyes followed the headman’s pointing finger, searching frantically for a tangible threat, a visible enemy, and finding none, their confusion deepened into a kind of desperate, helpless rage. “Who comes, man? Speak plainly!” one of them, the manager’s uncle, a gaunt, sickly looking individual with a perpetual air of aggrieved superiority, demanded, his voice thin and reedy, devoid of its usual arrogant bluster. “What is he babbling about? Savages! Always with their incomprehensible grunts!” He cast a disdainful, yet unmistakably fearful, glance at the headman, a futile attempt to assert a dominance that had long since evaporated.

The headman, without so much as a flicker of acknowledgement, turned his gaze back to the fog, his expression returning to that profound, pensive state, as if communing with forces far beyond the grasp of the European intellect. His comment had been for me, I realized, for the burgeoning, primitive awareness within me, not for the deafened ears of these hollow men. His words were a warning, yes, but also an acknowledgement, a shared understanding of a reality that transcended the superficialities of language and custom.

The rustling and slithering sounds intensified, creating a continuous, low murmur that seemed to fill the very air, a symphony of unseen life, of movement without form, of presence without visibility. It was a sound that spoke of vast numbers, of countless beings, all existing in the opaque, featureless expanse, drawn by the invisible call, moving with a silent, purposeful stealth. And then, woven into this tapestry of subtle sound, a new note emerged, distinct, yet strangely muted by the fog: a rhythmic thumping, a deep, resonant beat, faint but persistent, like the slow, steady pulse of a colossal heart, or the measured tread of an immense, unseen drum. It was not threatening in the conventional sense, not a war-cry or a challenge, but something far more disturbing: an affirmation, a declaration of presence, a solemn, ancient ritual unfolding just beyond the reach of our sight.

My fever, meanwhile, continued its slow, insidious work, painting the edges of my vision with shimmering distortions, filling my ears with phantom echoes, and dissolving the hard certainties of the world into a viscous, shifting dreamscape. The boundaries of my own body seemed to soften, to expand, to merge with the surrounding immensity. I felt a strange lightness, a sensation of disembodiment, as if I were drifting, a mere wisp of consciousness, through a vast, formless sea. The steamer, the pilgrims, even my own hands resting on the cold rail, seemed unreal, spectral illusions projected onto the greater, more profound reality of the fog and the unseen wilderness it concealed.

The rhythmic thumping grew stronger, louder, though still muffled, still possessing that eerie, disembodied quality. It was not a single drum, I now realized, but many, a multitude of beats, all synchronized, all contributing to a vast, pulsating rhythm that seemed to emanate from the very heart of the fog, from the very core of the unseen jungle. It was a primal, insistent rhythm, a sound that spoke not of articulate thought or complex emotion, but of sheer, unadulterated being, of life in its most elemental, unadorned form, a powerful, magnetic pull that vibrated deep within the primitive chambers of my own ancestral memory. It was the drumbeat of the earth itself, an echo from a time when the world was young, when the distinction between man and beast, between consciousness and instinct, was yet to be drawn.

I watched the headman. He had closed his eyes again, his head thrown back slightly, his nostrils flaring, as if inhaling the very essence of the sound, drawing it into his being. A faint smile, not of joy, but of a profound, almost tragic understanding, touched his lips. He was listening, not with his ears alone, but with his entire being, with every fibre of his muscle and bone, with the very spirit that animated him. He was listening to the voice of his ancestors, to the call of a truth that had been buried deep within the collective consciousness of his people for countless ages, now awakened by the terrible magnetism of Kurtz.

The pilgrims, however, were not listening; they were reacting, their fear escalating into a barely controlled hysteria. “What is that noise? What are they doing?” screeched one of them, a man whose face was now slick with perspiration, his eyes wide and bloodshot, fixed on the impenetrable fog as if expecting a legion of demons to materialize at any moment. He fumbled with his Winchester, bringing it up uncertainly, his hands shaking so violently that the barrel wobbled precariously. The manager, roused from his own nervous stupor, tried to bark orders, his voice cracking, impotent against the enveloping roar of the unseen. He clutched at his chest, his face pale and contorted, his carefully constructed facade of authority crumbling into a pathetic display of terror.

Their fear, I realized, was not for their lives in the immediate, physical sense, though that was undoubtedly a component. It was a deeper, more fundamental terror, the terror of civilization confronted by its own primitive origins, the fear of the carefully constructed edifice of their self-importance collapsing into the boundless, formless abyss from which it had sprung. They saw not the intricate tapestry of a living, breathing wilderness, but merely a dark, chaotic threat to their comfortable, well-ordered world. Their minds, conditioned by generations of inherited experience to believe in their own absolute superiority, were utterly unprepared for a reality that refused to acknowledge their existence, a force that swept away their pretensions with an indifferent, majestic contempt. They were confronted, not by a simple enemy to be overcome, but by the very immensity of the unanswerable, the profound, unsettling truth that their carefully curated lives were but a fleeting illusion, a shimmering mirage in a desert of eternal, untamed being.

And in that moment, as the rhythmic drumming permeated the very air, as the unseen rustling surrounded us, and as the white fog pressed in with its suffocating weight, I felt a strange, detached empathy for their suffering. Their terror was genuine, profound, and utterly overwhelming, a complete dissolution of their comfortable selves. But it was a terror born of ignorance, a fear of the unknown that had not yet been transmuted into the awful, illuminating clarity of understanding. They were blind, deaf, and mute to the profound, terrifying symphony that was unfolding around them, prisoners of their own limitations, their own self-imposed illusions.

My own journey, I understood then, had taken me far beyond such limited suffering. I had, in a sense, made my peace with the abyss, or at least acknowledged its boundless, eternal presence. The fever, the whispers, the immense, indifferent roar, the primal chants, the suffocating fog, the rhythmic drumming, all of it had conspired to strip away the layers of my own inherited illusions, leaving me bare, vulnerable, but also, paradoxically, more fully alive, more intensely aware of the vast, terrible beauty of existence. I was no longer merely on a river, navigating a course; I was adrift on an immense ocean of primal consciousness, a consciousness that contained both the monstrous and the sublime, the terrible and the divine, a consciousness that had found its most potent, most terrifying voice in the enigmatic figure of Kurtz.

The steamer, a mere shell of iron and rivets, continued its almost imperceptible rocking, its engines a faint, distant throb, a futile attempt at a mechanical heartbeat in a world dominated by ancient, organic rhythms. It was a monument to human arrogance, a testament to the misplaced belief in the power of technology to conquer the untamed, a fragile barrier against an overwhelming tide that threatened to engulf not just the vessel, but the very minds of its inhabitants. And I, standing amidst the terrified pilgrims and the silently communing crew, felt myself poised on the very edge of revelation, ready to step, or perhaps to fall, into the ultimate, terrifying embrace of the truth that awaited us, a truth far more profound, far more unsettling, than any mere horror conjured by the fleeting fears of men. The journey, I knew, was nearing its culmination, not in a destination, but in an absolute, transformative surrender to the boundless, unfathomable heart of the wilderness itself, a heart that now beat with the slow, resonant throb of a thousand unseen drums, calling us deeper, ever deeper, into its dark, magnetic embrace. The mist, I felt, was finally beginning to thin, not to reveal a familiar landscape, but to expose something far more ancient, far more monstrous, than anything human eyes were ever meant to behold. It was no mere animal outcry, no storm wind, no geological tremor; it was, rather, the voice of the world itself, stripping away the thin, comforting veil of human reason and revealing the terrible, naked heart of an older, hungrier existence. They were like children, stripped of their comforts, confronted by a reality that defied their neat, ordered world. Their fear was contagious, yes, but also curiously uninteresting, a predictable response to the monstrous, magnificent indifference of the wilderness. My own fear, if fear it was, was of a different kind, a profound apprehension not of physical harm, but of a deeper, more unsettling truth, a truth whispered by the headman, a truth woven into the very fabric of this alien land. Kurtz. The voice. The understanding that we were being drawn, not pursuing. This was the terror that resonated within me, a terror born not of immediate danger, but of a dawning, unwelcome insight.

The chanting grew louder, more insistent, and then, slowly, inexorably, it began to resolve into something else. Not language, not words, but a sound that was unmistakably human, a human cry, a wail of infinite sorrow, and then, rising above it, a piercing, almost agonizing shriek, a sound that seemed to tear at the very fabric of the air, a sound filled with both immense suffering and an almost triumphant, savage exhilaration. It was the cry of the jungle made manifest, the voice of the wilderness itself, echoing in the human throat. My hair, indeed, stirred under my cap, and a cold shiver ran down my spine, but it was not the shiver of pure, unadulterated fear. It was the shiver of recognition, of a chilling, profound understanding. This was the sound the headman had foretold, the sound that had drawn us here, the sound that heralded the approach of Kurtz, or at least, the monstrous influence that surrounded him.

The shriek, having reached its crescendo, lingered in the air for a moment, vibrating, echoing, then ceased as abruptly as it had begun, leaving behind a silence so absolute, so profound, that it was almost more terrifying than the clamor itself. The pilgrims, caught in a variety of awkward, frozen postures, remained motionless, their eyes wide, their breath held, as if daring not to disturb the fragile, horrifying quiet.

“Good God!” whispered the manager, his voice barely audible, “What was that? What in heaven’s name was that?” His eyes, usually shrewd and calculating, were now wide with a terror that seemed to have bleached all color from them.

I looked at the headman. He was still gazing into the fog, his expression unchanged, but I thought I detected a faint, almost imperceptible tremor in the set of his jaw, a slight tightening of the lips. He knew. He understood. His knowledge, however, remained his own, a secret shared only with the inscrutable forces of the wild.

The silence persisted, pressing down upon us, suffocating, until the manager, unable to bear it any longer, suddenly shrieked, “Fire! Fire, you fools! Shoot at something! Anything!” His command was less an order than a desperate, hysterical outburst, a plea for action, any action, to break the unbearable stasis.

And, as if on cue, the pilgrims, galvanized by his raw panic, began to fire, a ragged volley of shots that tore through the fog, harmlessly expending lead into the empty, oppressive white. The flashes of their rifles momentarily illuminated their contorted, terror-stricken faces, their desperate, futile gestures. It was a pathetic display, a final, despairing attempt to assert control over a reality that had utterly overwhelmed them. The black crew, however, stood their ground, or continued their work, their faces impassive, their eyes reflecting a quiet, watchful understanding. They knew, perhaps, that such actions were meaningless, that the true battles were not fought with bullets, but with the spirit, with an understanding of the forces that truly governed this land.

I watched the manager, his face twitching, his eyes darting. He was a man utterly unequipped for this confrontation, his carefully constructed world of ledgers and petty power crumbling before the indifferent grandeur of the wilderness. He had come here for profit, for advancement, for the superficial gleam of success, and now he was confronted with a reality that mocked all such ambitions, a reality that stripped him bare to his trembling, vulnerable core. The others were no better, each of them a testament to the hollowness of their self-proclaimed superiority, their thin skin of civilization easily pierced by the sharp, cruel truths of the jungle.

The firing died down, replaced once more by the heavy, waiting silence. The fog remained, dense and impenetrable, a solid wall of white that seemed to press in on us from all sides, isolating us, shrinking our world to the small, vibrating deck of the steamer. The jungle, the vast, matted jungle, was there, I knew, just beyond the veil, waiting, watching, its countless eyes fixed upon our pitiful, isolated vessel.

And then, very faintly at first, almost imperceptibly, I heard it again. A sound. Not the chant, not the shriek, but a low, melodious hum, a sound that seemed to emanate from the very heart of the fog, from the very center of the unseen world. It was a sound that was both hauntingly beautiful and deeply unsettling, a sound that seemed to promise both solace and madness. It was a human voice, yes, but a voice so profoundly altered, so infused with the essence of the wilderness, that it was barely recognizable as such. It was a voice that whispered of ancient secrets, of forbidden knowledge, of a communion with forces that transcended the human.

The headman, his gaze still fixed on the fog, exhaled slowly, a long, soft breath that seemed to carry with it a profound weight of understanding. He turned his head slightly, his eyes, dark and knowing, meeting mine again. He did not speak, but his look was a confirmation, a shared recognition. This was it. This was the voice. This was the culmination of the journey, the magnet drawing us in, the ultimate initiation into the heart of this terrifying, magnificent land.

The humming continued, growing stronger, more distinct, weaving itself into the fabric of the mist. It was a sound that seemed to bypass the ears and resonate directly in the soul, a sound that spoke of a deep, unsettling peace, a peace born of utter surrender, of a complete absorption into the monstrous, indifferent embrace of the wilderness. I felt a strange, almost dizzying pull, a perverse fascination drawing me towards that disembodied sound, towards the source of that unearthly melody. It was the siren call of the abyss, the song of the void, and I, like a lost mariner, felt an inexplicable urge to answer its summons, to plunge into the heart of that impenetrable fog and confront the truth that lay within.

The pilgrims, however, heard only the strangeness, the alienness, the threat. Their faces were studies in raw, uncomprehending terror. They huddled closer, their Winchesters lowered now, their futile bravado exhausted, replaced by a desperate, silent plea for deliverance. They looked at me, at the manager, at the headman, with wide, imploring eyes, seeking an explanation, a reassurance, an escape from the overwhelming, formless horror that had swallowed their carefully constructed world. But there was no explanation I could give them, no reassurance that would assuage their fear, no escape from the relentless, profound truth that was slowly, inexorably, being revealed. We were in the heart of it now, truly in the heart of it, and the very air vibrated with the presence of Kurtz, of the voice, of the unspeakable knowledge that awaited us in the impenetrable white. The very steamer, our small, mechanical bastion of European order, seemed to shiver and hum in response to that unseen, unheard melody, a fragile, trembling entity caught in the immense, indifferent embrace of the wild.

The white fog, dense and heavy, finally began to thin, not lifting entirely, but becoming translucent, revealing hints of the colossal trees that lined the riverbank, immense, dark shapes that loomed like silent, ancient sentinels. And through that thinning veil, I saw them. Shapes. Figures. Not attacking, not rushing, but standing, still and silent, along the invisible bank, their forms indistinct, shadowy, like ghosts materializing from the mist. They were dark, countless, and utterly motionless, their presence a silent, overwhelming affirmation of the chants, the shrieks, the humming voice that had filled the air. And then, through a momentary shift in the mist, I saw something else, something that stood apart, slightly ahead of the others, on a small, elevated mound of earth. A figure. Taller than the rest, almost spectral in its stillness, draped in something dark, its form indistinct, yet radiating an undeniable, magnetic presence. And from that figure, I felt, rather than heard, the source of the humming, the melody of the abyss, the voice that had called us to this moment. It was Kurtz. I knew it with a certainty that transcended sight or sound, a primal recognition that settled deep within my soul. He was there, at the heart of the darkness, waiting, the ultimate initiate, the terrifying, unholy guru of this vast, indifferent realm.

The manager gasped, a choking sound, and pointed a trembling finger. “Look! Look!” he croaked, his voice thick with terror. The pilgrims followed his gaze, their faces blanching further, their earlier panic replaced by a frozen, wide-eyed horror. They saw the figures now, the silent, watchful multitude, and the central, commanding presence. They saw, perhaps, the terrifying truth of their vulnerability, the absolute, crushing insignificance of their white skins and their petty weapons against the overwhelming, organic power of this alien land. They were not just facing savages, I realized, they were facing a force, an idea, a truth that their European sensibilities had utterly failed to prepare them for. And Kurtz, the enigmatic, distant Kurtz, was the embodiment of that truth, the terrifying, magnificent nexus where the two worlds met, where civilization crumbled and the primeval asserted its ancient, terrible reign.

The headman, standing beside me, finally moved. He raised his hand, slowly, deliberately, not in a gesture of fear or warning, but of profound reverence, a gesture that seemed to acknowledge a powerful, ancient spirit. He turned to me, his dark eyes meeting mine, and a faint, almost imperceptible smile played upon his lips, a smile that held both grim understanding and a subtle, almost mischievous triumph. “The voice calls,” he murmured, his voice soft, almost a sigh. “It calls to him. And it calls to us all.” His words were a prophecy, a warning, and a shared initiation, a recognition of the profound, unsettling connection that now bound us all to this place, to this moment, and to the chilling, magnetic presence of Kurtz, who stood, silent and still, at the edge of the visible world, waiting to claim his due. The fog, as if on command, began to thicken again, gently, smoothly, sliding in greased grooves, once more obscuring the figures, returning them to the realm of the unseen, leaving us once more isolated, enveloped, and utterly alone with the profound, unsettling echoes of that haunting, unearthly voice. The profound, terrifying intimacy of that moment, the shared knowledge of the headman, the sickening fear of the pilgrims, and my own strange, feverish fascination, settled upon the deck of the steamer like the oppressive, clammy air itself, a weight that promised to crush or transform us all.
