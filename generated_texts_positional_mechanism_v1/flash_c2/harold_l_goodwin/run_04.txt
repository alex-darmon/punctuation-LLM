What we heard!" CHAPTER I The Whispering Light Jonas, the old fisherman, had no difficulty in establishing a connection between the strange lights and the legend of Silas Croft. He made a quick trip to the general store early that morning, and reported that the shimmering beacon was seen every night from the lighthouse. "No doubt about that, son," was Jonas's comment. Then, because they had not talked to Jonas at any length, Leo questioned the fisherman about the sounds he reported coming from the tower. "I've heard it four or five times, not counting the night it really howled," the fisherman said. "Funny thing about the night it sounded like a wolf." "What was funny?" Clara asked. "It was alone." "But it's always alone," Leo exclaimed. "Nope. It's alone when it lights up, but when it whispers there are other noises with it. Sometimes a groan, sometimes a rattle. Only heard it alone that once--the night it howled." This was a new angle. Leo and Clara looked at each other, puzzled. "You've heard the others?" Clara asked. "Sure have. Not close to, you can bet. Got no wish to tangle with spirits, not me. But I heard them. They sound in the old tower room, and they sound from the lantern room. They were lookin' for somethin'." "How do you know?" Leo demanded. "They'd groan, then stop, and rattle. Like they were searchin' the old place. Bet one of 'em lost a hand and is huntin' for it." "Did you hear where they came from, or went to?" "Not me. I got curiosity, but not the kind that killed that cat they tell about. Like I say, me and spirits don't mix, none to speak of." Leo pondered the information. "Are these strange sounds at midnight?" "No. Mostly around sundown." Leo turned to Clara. "What do you make of that?" "Nothing," Clara replied. "Not a thing. You say you've heard as many as three distinct noises plus the main whispering?" "That's correct. None of them are as loud as the main whisper, though. Most curious thing I ever heard was the night they were dragging something, collectin' the invisible junk from the upper levels." Leo's hair had an impulse to stand on end. The calm, factual way in which the old fisherman piled mystery on mystery was incredible. "You mean you heard ghosts dragging a ghost cart?" the boy asked incredulously. "Like I said. More a scraping than a dragging, I suppose you'd say. They hauled it back and forth, and the echoes trailed out behind it. Once in a while they'd stop and gather and listen to the floor. Clara asked, "Where were you while all this was going on?" "In my boat, scared pink, but not so scared as curious." A man of real courage, Leo thought. Believed in ghosts, but had the nerve to listen to them in action. "Mr. Jonas, you said none of them were as loud as the main whisper. Did the others sound solid?" Jonas's comment brought Leo's carefully built up assumptions tumbling down around his ears. "Oh, it knows about the ghost, all right. By the time the last rays of sun started to disappear from the horizon, the cousins were in their chosen position, on the rocky cliff at a point where they couldn't fail to see all who approached the tower, but where no one could see them through the thick screen of sea-grass. Leo thought an hour was probably more than the ghost producer needed to get ready for its appearance. Only a few minutes might be needed. They either had faith in their assumptions or they didn't. "Wait until the show is almost over," Leo whispered. A voice from behind them called, "Better get out of there, you two. Leo’s mind raced. So Henderson was their man, the ghost producer. This was both a relief and a terrifying realization. They had a human culprit, but he was armed and clearly desperate to protect his secret.

As they descended the worn spiral staircase, leaving the powerful, true light of the lighthouse to sweep its lonely vigil, Leo glanced back at the now-exposed lantern room. The hum of the lighthouse motor was still present, a mechanical heartbeat in the silence. But the ethereal whisper was gone. The Blue Ghost would haunt these fields no more. The mystery, however, was far from over. It had only just begun to reveal its true, golden depths.

The Coast Guard station was a flurry of activity when they arrived. Their story, told in detail, was met with a mixture of disbelief and grudging acceptance as the evidence mounted. The broken equipment, the testimony of Henderson himself once he realized his game was up, and the reports of strange lights and sounds from the local residents, including Belsely’s earlier reports, all began to paint a cohesive, if fantastic, picture.

Leo and Clara were debriefed for hours, recounting every detail of their pursuit, the fight in the lantern room, and Henderson’s confession about the gold mine. They insisted on the connection to the Sons of the Old Dominion and the annual feast. The officers, though initially skeptical about the gold mine aspect, promised to investigate further. The immediate priority was Henderson and the unauthorized use of the lighthouse.

It was well past midnight when their parents, frantic with worry, finally arrived. The relief on their faces, quickly turning to anger when they heard the abbreviated version of the night’s events, was palpable. Leo exchanged a look with Clara. They knew their parents were right to be angry, but they also knew they had done something important. Something that Rick and Scotty, if they had only known, would have been proud of.

The next morning, exhausted but wired, Leo called Rick. He knew Rick and Scotty would be eager for news, especially after their own planned stakeout at the mine.

"Rick," Leo began, trying to keep his voice calm, "you won't believe what happened last night."

A murmur went through the crowd. “What was that?” someone whispered. “It flickered!”

The momentary imperfection in the illusion was enough. It broke the spell. People who had been lost in the eerie beauty of the apparition now began to scrutinize it, their belief shaken. Some began to squint, trying to see the source of the light.

“Up there!” Rick shouted again, his voice cracking with the effort. “Above the water! There’s a machine!”

Now, spurred by the boys’ shouts and the undeniable flaw in the ghost’s performance, many of the Sons began to push forward, craning their necks, their eyes searching the dark, shadowed shelf above the pool. The blue light, now regaining some of its stability, continued to project the ghost, but the sense of mystery was gone, replaced by a growing suspicion.

A burly man in a wide-brimmed hat, one of the elder Sons, pushed through the crowd, his face grim. “What in tarnation are you boys jabbering about?” he demanded, his gaze fierce.

“It’s Jethro Collins!” Scotty blurted out, pointing directly at the blue ghost. “He’s projecting it from up there!”

The man, clearly skeptical, scoffed. “Nonsense! That’s the Blue Ghost, boy, an apparition from the battlefield!”

But another man, younger and more nimble, had already begun to clamber up the rocks at the side of the mine entrance, driven by a sudden, intense curiosity. He was struggling, but determined, his hand reaching for a purchase on the rough stone.

Inside the mine, Collins must have realized his deception was failing. The blue ghost, which had been holding its form, suddenly vanished altogether, plunging the pool and the mine entrance into near darkness, save for the faint glow from the Sons’ gathering. The sudden disappearance was more damning than any flicker. The crowd gasped again, this time in astonishment, then a roar of confused anger began to ripple through them.

“He turned it off!” Rick cried out, sensing their advantage. “He heard us! He knows we know!”

Just then, the clambering man at the side of the mine entrance let out a triumphant yell. “I see it! There’s a light up here! A contraption!” His voice echoed, confirming Rick and Scotty’s claims.

The Sons surged forward, their confusion quickly turning into outrage. They had been fooled, their cherished local legend exposed as a mere trick. Many of them, their faces flushed with anger, began to converge on the mine entrance, demanding answers.

Suddenly, from the darkness of the mine tunnel, a figure burst forth. It was Jethro Collins, his lantern now miraculously relit and swinging wildly. He looked disheveled, his face pale and contorted with a mixture of fear and fury. He hadn’t expected to be discovered, certainly not tonight, and certainly not by two boys.

“Stop them!” Collins yelled, his voice hoarse, pointing at Rick and Scotty. “They’re trying to ruin everything!”

But it was too late. The Sons had seen him emerge, not from the ethereal depths of the spirit world, but from the very mine tunnel from which the ghost had been projected. His appearance, following the sudden vanishing of the ghost and the discovery of the "contraption" by the other man, was the final, irrefutable piece of evidence.

The burly man in the wide-brimmed hat, his face now a mask of cold fury, stepped forward. “Collins, what is the meaning of this?” he demanded, his voice low and dangerous. Other Sons began to surround Collins, their expressions mirroring the elder man’s anger.

Collins, trapped and exposed, looked around desperately, like a cornered animal. He took a hesitant step back towards the mine.

“Don’t let him get away!” Scotty shouted, pointing to the canvas bag Collins still clutched. “He has the chemicals! That’s how he makes the mist!”

This revelation further inflamed the crowd. The Blue Ghost was not only a trick, but a chemical one. The elder Son, with surprising speed for his bulk, grabbed Collins by the arm, his grip like iron. “You’ve got some explaining to do, Jethro,” he said, his voice quiet but firm. “And it better be good.”

Rick and Scotty watched from a slight distance, the exhilaration of their success warring with a residual tremor of fear. They had done it. The Blue Ghost, the fearsome spectre of the Old Dominion, had been unmasked, reduced to a projector and a real-estate agent with a criminal scheme.

But even as Collins was being held, his face a picture of defeat, Rick couldn’t shake the memory of Belsely’s words: the other men, the dark shadows, the cart, searching for something. Collins had been alone tonight, setting up his show. Where were the others? What were they searching for? The projector explained the ghost, but it didn't fully explain the *reason* for the ghost, nor the true extent of Collins’s operation. This was only the first layer of the mystery peeled back. There was still a deeper secret, a more intricate plot, waiting to be uncovered, beneath the haunted hills of the Old Dominion. The Sons of the Old Dominion might have their trickster, but Rick suspected the true treasure, and the true criminals, were still at large, lurking in the shadows, waiting for the smoke of the Blue Ghost to clear.

The clamor of the Sons of the Old Dominion was a churning wave of indignation. Their annual tradition, their local lore, defiled and cheapened by a huckster with a lantern and a projector. Rick and Scotty, standing a little apart, felt the tension thrum in the air. The burly man, whose name Rick now recalled as Mr. Abernathy, held Collins firmly. Other Sons crowded around, their questions sharp and overlapping.

“What’s the meaning of this, Collins?”
“A trick, all this time?”
“The Blue Ghost, a mere machine?”

Collins, though still gripped, managed a defiant sneer. “What’s it to you? It was a good show, wasn’t it? Kept the tourists coming.”

Abernathy’s grip tightened. “Tourists? This is our heritage, Jethro. You’ve profaned it.”

Rick stepped forward, Scotty close beside him. “Mr. Abernathy,” he said, his voice cutting through the noise, “we think Mr. Collins is involved in more than just a ghost hoax.”

Collins’s eyes darted to Rick, a flicker of fear replacing the defiance. “Nonsense! These boys are just troublemakers. They saw my contraption, that’s all.”

“Your contraption, yes,” Scotty chimed in, “but what about the chemicals? Belsely saw you with them. What were they for, Mr. Collins? To make the mist? Or to hide something else?”

The mention of Belsely seemed to throw Collins off balance. He visibly flinched. The Sons, picking up on this, pressed closer.

“Chemicals? What chemicals?” Abernathy demanded.

Collins licked his lips. “Just… just a little dry ice, for atmosphere. It’s part of the show.”

Rick shook his head. “Dry ice makes mist, but not the kind that swirls and hangs so eerily, not the way the Blue Ghost mist did. Belsely mentioned you were selling ice cream to the girl campers, and he saw you with the ghost. Perhaps the chemicals were for more than just a show.”

The Sons, momentarily distracted from their anger, looked at Collins with renewed suspicion. The real estate agent seemed to shrink under their gaze.

“He’s hiding something,” Scotty declared, pointing again at the canvas bag Collins still clutched, though now with less conviction. “He tried to keep that bag. What’s in it, Collins?”

Abernathy, with a grunt, snatched the bag from Collins’s weakening grasp. He upended it. Out spilled not dry ice, but an array of small, dark objects wrapped in faded cloth, alongside several empty glass vials and a small, heavy trowel. The objects clattered onto the rocky ground.

“What are these?” Abernathy asked, picking up one of the wrapped bundles. He carefully unwrapped it. Inside lay a tarnished silver button, ornate and clearly very old. He unwrapped another. A rusty, twisted musket ball. And another, a fragment of an old buckle.

A hush fell over the Sons. These were not items one would find in a theatrical prop kit. These were relics.

“He’s been looting the battlefield,” a quiet voice from the crowd stated, laden with disgust.

Collins, seeing his attempt to play off the ghost as a mere show was failing, tried a new tactic. “They’re worthless! Just old junk I found. I was going to sell them as souvenirs, to fund the annual feast. A little extra income for the Sons, you see.”

“Souvenirs?” Abernathy’s voice was dangerously low. “These are artifacts. From the battlefield where our ancestors fought. You were digging them up, weren’t you, Jethro? Using your ‘Blue Ghost’ to scare people away while you plundered the site.”

The Sons roared with anger anew. This was an affront far greater than a mere ghost hoax. This was desecration. Rick and Scotty exchanged a glance. They had been right. There was more to it.

“But what about the ‘others’?” Rick whispered to Scotty, while the Sons surrounded Collins, who was now beginning to sweat profusely. “Belsely said he saw Collins with other men, searching the fields, pulling a cart.”

Scotty nodded, his eyes scanning the faces of the Sons. “And the ‘invisible dead’ he claimed they were collecting. What do you suppose those were?”

“Not dead, I’d wager,” Rick mused. “But perhaps… more artifacts. Maybe more valuable ones. Collins only had a few small things. Maybe the others are the ones finding the real treasure.”

They watched as Abernathy handed the bag and its contents to another Son, then dragged Collins towards a waiting vehicle. “We’ll get to the bottom of this, Jethro. And when we’re done, the law will be involved.”

The Sons began to disperse, some still grumbling, others discussing the implications of Collins’s actions. The mine entrance, once a source of spectral dread, now looked merely like a dark hole in the hillside, its magic stripped away by projector light and petty greed.

Rick and Scotty, however, felt a renewed sense of purpose. Collins was caught, but the bigger picture remained hazy. They had a lead now: the plundering of artifacts. And a new line of questioning: who were these “others” Belsely had seen, and what were they truly searching for?

“We need to talk to Belsely again,” Rick said to Scotty. “He might have seen something he didn’t understand at the time, something that makes sense now.”

Scotty agreed. “And we need to look at the mine. If Collins was digging, where was he digging? And why did he need the ghost to do it?”

The night air, no longer charged with the electricity of a spectral appearance, felt cool and crisp. The sounds of the Sons’ cars receding down the road left the mine hill in a quieter, more thoughtful silence.

They walked back towards their bikes, the conversation from moments ago replaying in their minds. The fragments of history, the tarnished silver, the musket ball. These were not random curiosities; they were pieces of a puzzle far older than Jethro Collins.

“If he was collecting things from the battlefield,” Scotty began, “then the ‘others’ Belsely saw, searching the cornfield and the field by our plane, they were probably looking for the same thing, weren’t they?”

Rick nodded slowly. “And the cart… Belsely said they were pulling a cart. If they were collecting artifacts, especially larger ones, a cart would make sense. But he said they were collecting ‘invisible dead.’ He believed they were spirits. What could make him think that?”

“Maybe they were very careful,” Scotty suggested. “Working at night, dressed in dark clothes, moving silently. From a distance, in the dark, with Belsely already believing in ghosts, it wouldn’t take much to convince him they were something supernatural.”

“That’s it,” Rick said, a sudden clarity in his voice. “They were using the belief in the Blue Ghost to their advantage. Collins’s ghost show wasn’t just a cover for him to dig; it was a psychological weapon to keep others away, to let the *real* diggers, the ‘others,’ work unmolested.”

“So Collins was just a small-time operator, then?” Scotty asked, a touch of disappointment in his voice. “Just picking up scraps?”

“Perhaps,” Rick considered. “Or perhaps he was part of a larger team, but his specific role was to operate the ghost projector and maybe to do some smaller-scale digging. The ‘others’ might be the ones going after the bigger prize.”

The thought sent a shiver down Rick’s spine that had nothing to do with the cool night air. If Collins was merely a pawn, then the true players were still out there, likely more dangerous and more organized.

They reached their bicycles and began to pedal towards Belsely’s house, their minds racing. The farmer was the key witness, the one who had seen the more complex, more sinister part of the operation.

When they arrived at Belsely’s small, neat farmhouse, a single light burned in the kitchen. They knocked, and after a moment, Belsely’s weathered face appeared at the door, framed by the yellow light. He looked surprised, but not unwelcoming.

“Well now, boys,” he said, opening the door wider. “What brings you back

to see me again so soon?”

Rick stepped forward, hat in hand. “Mr. Belsely, we apologize for bothering you at this hour, but some of the things you said earlier, they’ve really got us thinking. Especially about the other figures you saw in the fields, the ones with the wagon.”

Belsely peered at them, his eyes, though tired, held a sharp intelligence Rick hadn’t fully appreciated earlier. “Ah, them. The spirits at their work. Disturbin’ sight, that. Come on in, boys, don’t stand out there in the night air.”

They stepped into the warm, cluttered kitchen, the air thick with the scent of woodsmoke and something faintly sweet, like baking. Belsely motioned them to sit at a sturdy pine table, then busied himself with a kettle on the stove. “Might as well have some coffee. Helps the mind stay sharp, even when it’s chasing after ghosts.”

“Thank you, sir,” Scotty said, accepting a steaming mug. Rick took his, the warmth a comforting contrast to the chill outside.

“Now, about those other figures,” Rick began, choosing his words carefully. “You said they were dark shadows, and they were pulling a cart, searching the ground. Can you tell us more about that? Everything you remember, no matter how small.”

Belsely took a slow sip of his coffee, his gaze distant, lost in memory. “Well, like I said, it wasn’t always the Blue Ghost out there alone. Not when he was in the fields. Sometimes he had his retinue with him, I suppose you’d call ‘em. Dark shapes, they were. No shine to ‘em like the main one. Just… shadows moving in the night. Always in the cornfield, or the field near your plane.”

“And they had a wagon?” Scotty prompted.

“A cart, more like. A small thing, pulled by two of ‘em, usually. And the Blue Ghost, he’d be leading the way. Like a lantern for the others. He’d glow, you see, and they’d follow him, up and down the rows. Then they’d stop, like I said, and bend over. Sometimes one, sometimes all of ‘em, pokin’ at the ground.” Belsely paused, stirring his coffee with a spoon. “And that mist… it trailed out behind the cart. Like a fog, but a thin, wispy thing. Almost like… breath, coming off the ground.”

Rick leaned forward. “You mentioned they were searching for the invisible dead. What made you think that?”

“Well, what else would a wagon be for in a field, with spirits searchin’ the ground?” Belsely said, as if it were the most obvious conclusion in the world. “And the way they’d stop, and gather close, and look down. Like they’d found something, but it was too delicate to touch, or too… gone. I always figured they were tryin’ to gather up the pieces of the fallen, the poor souls that had been scattered by the war. A sad business, even for spirits.”

“Did you ever see them put anything in the cart?” Rick asked, trying to keep his voice neutral.

Belsely frowned. “Never saw ‘em put anything *in* it, no. Not in the way you’d put a potato in a sack. But sometimes, after they’d bent down and gathered, that mist behind the cart, it would seem to thicken. Just a bit. And the air would feel… colder, around it. Like they’d captured something invisible, something that sucked the warmth right out of the night.”

Scotty glanced at Rick, a flicker of understanding passing between them. Belsely’s interpretation was supernatural, but his description of the *phenomena* was precise. The thickening mist, the coldness. These were physical effects.

“And where did they take this cart, Mr. Belsely?” Rick pressed. “Where did they go with it?”

Belsely hesitated, scratching his chin. “Now that’s a good question. I never followed ‘em, not that far. Like I said, I got my limits. But they always seemed to head towards the mine. Once they got to the edge of the cornfield, near the old road that leads to the mine entrance, they’d sort of… dissipate. Just like the Blue Ghost does when he’s done with his show. The glow would dim, the shadows would fade, and the mist would just… vanish into the night.”

“Always towards the mine?” Scotty asked.

“Every time I saw ‘em with the cart, yes. Never went the other direction. Always back to the mine, like that was their home base.”

Rick considered this. The mine. It was the center of everything. Collins operated his ghost show there. The Sons of the Old Dominion held their annual feast there. And now, these “others” were always heading back there with their mysterious cart.

“Mr. Belsely, you mentioned that they seemed to be searching for things that had been scattered,” Rick continued. “Did you ever notice them looking for anything specific? Or in any particular pattern?”

“Just seemed to be lookin’ generally for… remnants. Pieces of things. I couldn’t tell you if it was metal, or bone, or just some other kind of spirit dust. But they were thorough. Walkin’ slow, eyes on the ground, turnin’ over clods of dirt with their feet sometimes, or pokin’ with a stick.”

“A stick?” Scotty asked, eyes wide. “Ghosts with sticks?”

Belsely shrugged. “Well, a shadow with a stick. Hard to tell what it really was. But they’d use it to prod at the ground, like they were feeling for something.”

This was critical. Ghosts didn’t prod at the ground with sticks. People did. People looking for something buried or hidden.

“And these searches,” Rick said, picking up on Scotty’s thought. “Were they random, or did they seem to follow a line, or a grid?”

“More like a sweep,” Belsely replied, again lost in recollection. “They’d go one way, turn around, come back the other. Like a farmer plowing his field, only slower. And they’d go over the same spots sometimes, if they hadn’t found what they were lookin’ for the first time. They were persistent, you gotta give ‘em that. Even spirits are particular about their lost items, I reckon.”

Rick felt a surge of excitement. A systematic search. This wasn’t random scavenging. This was organized.

“You said you saw them in the cornfield and the field near our plane,” Rick confirmed. “Are those the only places?”

“Those are the main places, yes. Those fields, they’re part of the old battlefield, you see. Lots of history buried in that ground. And the mine hill itself, that’s where some of the fiercest fightin’ happened, long ago. Seems natural the spirits would be drawn to those spots.”

“Did you ever see them digging?” Scotty asked, a direct question this time.

Belsely looked thoughtful. “Digging… not proper digging, no. Not with shovels. But they’d disturb the earth, certainly. Like I said, pokin’ and scrapin’. Never saw ‘em actually *dig a hole* you could fall into, if that’s what you mean.”

Rick absorbed the information. No overt digging, but systematic searching and disturbing the ground. If they were looking for artifacts, they might be using metal detectors or other non-invasive methods, or perhaps only digging small, discreet test pits. Belsely’s perception would be of “poking and scraping.”

“And the Blue Ghost,” Rick pressed. “He was always with them when they had the cart?”

“Always. Leading the way, shining like a beacon. Like he was the boss, telling them where to look, where to go. And he’d disappear with them towards the mine.”

“But the night we chased him, he was alone,” Rick remembered. “You said he was alone at the mine that night.”

“That’s right,” Belsely affirmed. “Just him. No retinue, no cart, no mist trailin’ behind. He came out of the mine, got the alarm going, and then you boys chased him. Just him.”

This was a key distinction. The ghost show, with the alarm, was a solo performance. The field searches, with the cart and the “others,” were a group effort, led by the Blue Ghost. This supported Rick’s theory that Collins was one part of a larger operation, and his solo appearances might be meant to distract or misdirect.

“Mr. Belsely,” Rick said, “you said you sometimes saw one, sometimes two or three other figures. Was there ever a specific number with the cart?”

“Usually two pulling the cart, and the Blue Ghost leading. So three, generally. Sometimes I thought I saw more, shadows moving further off, but it was hard to be sure in the dark. But the core group, with the cart, was always three.”

Three. Rick’s mind immediately went to the idea of a small, organized team.

“And you mentioned they were dark shadows,” Scotty interjected. “Could you tell anything about their clothing? Or if they had any equipment, besides a possible stick?”

Belsely shook his head. “Too dark for that, son. Just shapes. Dark, human-shaped forms. Didn’t seem to be wearing anything bright. Just blends into the night, they did. Like I said, you got to remember, I’m seeing this from a distance, sometimes through the corn rows, or from the edge of the orchard. I ain’t exactly walkin’ up and shakin’ their hands.” He chuckled dryly. “And I sure ain’t tryin’ to get close to a ghost wagon full of invisible dead, no sir.”

“What about the ice-cream man, Collins?” Rick asked, shifting focus slightly. “You said he knew about the ghost. Did you ever see him with these other figures, or with the cart?”

Belsely’s brow furrowed in thought. “No, can’t say I have. I only saw him with the ghost that one time, when he was selling ice cream. And he looked scared stiff, just like anyone else would. Always just him selling ice cream, or him coming out of the mine for the show. Never saw him with the others, or the cart. If he was involved with them, he kept it separate from his other business, far as I could tell.”

This was crucial. If Collins was the solo ghost producer, and these “others” were also involved, it suggested a compartmentalized operation. Collins might be providing the cover, the “ghost story” that kept people away, while the others did the actual work.

“Mr. Belsely,” Rick said, his voice earnest, “this information is incredibly important. We think there’s more going on out there than just a ghost hoax. We think people are using this ghost story as a cover for… well, for something illegal. Like stealing historical artifacts.”

Belsely’s eyes widened slightly. “Stealing, you say? From the ground here?”

“Yes, sir,” Scotty added. “The things they’re searching for, the old battlefield, the way they’re going about it. It points to a systematic search for valuable historical items.”

Belsely leaned back, considering this. “Hmm. Now that puts a different light on things, doesn’t it? I always just figured they were mournful spirits. But people… people are always after somethin’, aren’t they? Gold, glory, old bits of rusty metal. It’s a sad thing, to steal from the past.” He shook his head slowly. “So you think the Blue Ghost, he ain’t no spirit at all. Just a man puttin’ on a show.”

“That’s our theory,” Rick confirmed. “A very clever show, designed to keep people away and to create a believable cover for the real activity.”

“And the others,” Scotty continued. “The ones with the cart. They’re the ones actually looking for things. And the cart, it’s not for invisible dead, but for the actual artifacts they find.”

Belsely tapped his fingers on the table. “Well, I’ll be. It makes a kind of sense, I suppose. More sense than ghosts pullin’ a wagon, though I’ve seen stranger things in my time, let me tell you. But a man behind it all… that’s a different kind of spooky.” He paused, then looked at them directly. “So what are you boys gonna do about it?”

Rick exchanged a look with Scotty. This was the moment. They needed Belsely’s help, or at least his continued cooperation.

“We intend to stop them, Mr. Belsely,” Rick said firmly. “But we need more information. You’ve been invaluable tonight. Do you think you could… keep an eye out? If you see them again, the ‘others’ with the cart, can you try to get a better look? Or notice anything new?”

Belsely rubbed his jaw. “Keeping an eye out, that I can do. I’m out in those fields all the time, day and night. And I ain’t afraid of livin’ men, even if they are bein’ sneaky. I just don’t like tanglin’ with spirits, like I said. If they’re just men, well, that’s another matter.” A glint came to his eye. “It wouldn’t be the first time I’ve helped a lawman, or someone tryin’ to catch a scoundrel. If they’re stealing from this land, that’s a different story.”

“Thank you, Mr. Belsely,” Rick said, genuinely relieved. “That would be a great help. Anything you see, any patterns, any new locations, even if it seems insignificant to you. Call us.” He handed Belsely a small card with his father’s phone number. “If you can’t reach me, my father will know how to find me.”

Belsely took the card, tucking it into his shirt pocket. “I’ll do what I can, boys. This land’s been in my family a long time. I don’t like the thought of folks pilferin’ from it, be they man or spirit.” He paused, then added, “But be careful, you two. If these folks are as organized as you say, and they’re doin’ somethin’ illegal, they might not take kindly to two young fellas pokin’ their noses in.”

“We will, sir,” Scotty assured him. “We appreciate the warning.”

As they stood to leave, Rick had one last question. “Mr. Belsely, when the ‘others’ with the cart dissipated, as you said, towards the mine, did they go into the main entrance? Or somewhere else?”

“Always seemed to head that general direction,” Belsely pondered. “But sometimes, it looked like they might have veered off towards the old service entrance. You know, the smaller one, around the back of the hill, where they used to bring in supplies for the mine office. It’s mostly overgrown now, but there’s still a path of sorts.”

Rick’s eyes lit up. A secondary entrance. That would explain why they didn’t always seem to go directly into the prominent mine opening. Collins might be using the main entrance for his ghost show, but the others, the real treasure hunters, might be using a more discreet entry point.

“Thank you, Mr. Belsely,” Rick said, his mind already spinning with possibilities. “You’ve given us a lot to work with.”

They walked back out into the cool night, the farmhouse light fading behind them. The information from Belsely had been even more valuable than they had hoped. The methodical search, the cart, the systematic approach, and the two distinct modus operandi,  Collins’s solo ghost show and the group’s artifact hunt,  all painted a clearer, more disturbing picture.

“The old service entrance,” Scotty mused as they pedaled their bikes down the gravel road. “That’s definitely something new. And it makes sense. Why use the flashy front door if you’re trying to be discreet?”

“Exactly,” Rick replied, his voice buzzing with renewed energy. “And the three figures. A small, efficient team. The Blue Ghost leading, two others doing the actual work with the cart. It sounds like a well-oiled machine, carefully planned to avoid detection.”

“So, Collins is still our target,” Scotty said. “But he’s probably just the front man, the distraction. The ‘ghost producer’ for the real operation.”

“Right,” Rick confirmed. “He keeps everyone’s attention focused on the main mine entrance, on the supernatural spectacle, while the real action happens quietly, elsewhere. He’s creating the illusion, the stage magic, while the others are plundering the historical site.”

The idea gnawed at Rick. Not just the theft, but the desecration. A historical battlefield, the resting place of soldiers, being systematically stripped of its past for profit. It was a cold, calculated act.

“So our plan for Collins still holds,” Scotty stated. “We still need to figure out his connection to the projector, and how he gets in and out. But now we also need to consider the others. And that service entrance.”

“We need to find out when the others operate,” Rick mused. “Belsely said they were ‘mostly around midnight,’ but he didn’t say how often. It probably isn’t every night. It’s too risky. They’d likely operate intermittently, when they feel it’s safe.”

“We can’t just camp out at the service entrance every night,” Scotty pointed out, ever practical. “We’d draw too much attention. And those insects were bad enough tonight.”

“No,” Rick agreed. “But we can scout it. Get a feel for the terrain. See if there are any signs of recent activity. And Belsely will be keeping an eye out. He’s our best informant right now.”

They continued in silence for a few minutes, the crunch of their bike tires on the gravel road the only sound. Rick’s mind was racing, trying to put all the pieces together. The tarnished silver, the musket ball. These weren’t just random finds. They were clues to a systematic looting operation. And the “invisible dead” the cart supposedly carried. Rick now pictured small, dark objects, carefully packed into the cart, hidden by the very mist the ghost projector created.

“Think about it, Scotty,” Rick said, pulling his bike to a stop by a familiar oak tree. “If Collins is producing the ghost with a projector, and the mist is essential for that, then the others could be using that mist as cover too. Not just a spooky effect, but an actual physical screen.”

“Like a smoke screen?” Scotty suggested.

“Exactly,” Rick affirmed. “Belsely said the mist trailed behind the cart, and thickened when they supposedly collected things. What if that’s a controlled release? To obscure what they’re actually putting in the cart? Or to make it harder to see the cart itself?”

“That’s pretty elaborate for some guys just looking for old coins,” Scotty admitted.

“Which means they’re either very good, very professional, or what they’re looking for is extremely valuable,” Rick countered. “Or both. And if Collins is in on this, then he’s more than just a ghost hoaxer. He’s a crucial part of a criminal enterprise.”

They decided their next step would be to investigate the service entrance Belsely had mentioned. They couldn’t do it tonight; it was too dark and they were already tired. But first thing in the morning, after their usual chores, they would go.

The following morning, after a hurried breakfast and a quick check of the weather, which promised a clear, hot day, Rick and Scotty set off. They made a point of taking a circuitous route, avoiding the main road that led to the mine entrance. Instead, they followed a series of overgrown farm tracks and old logging paths, aiming for the back of the mine hill.

The path Belsely had described was indeed there, faint but discernible, winding its way through a thicket of pines and brambles. It was much narrower than the main road, barely wide enough for a jeep, and clearly hadn’t seen regular use for years. Overhanging branches clawed at them as they pushed their bikes through, the air thick with the smell of pine needles and damp earth.

“This is it,” Scotty whispered, pointing ahead.

Through a gap in the trees, they saw it. Not a grand entrance like the main mine shaft, but a smaller, less imposing opening. It was partially covered by a crude, wooden shed-like structure, its roof sagging, its walls scarred by time and weather. The shed itself was half-hidden by a thick curtain of ivy and Virginia creeper.

They dismounted their bikes and approached cautiously. The shed looked abandoned, a relic from a forgotten era of the mine’s operation. But as they drew closer, Rick noticed something. The ground in front of the shed, though covered in dead leaves and pine needles, seemed… disturbed. Not freshly dug, but recently walked upon. The leaves were compressed, and a few had been scuffed aside, revealing the dark earth beneath.

“Someone’s been here,” Rick murmured, pointing to the disturbed ground. “And not too long ago.”

Scotty knelt, examining the marks. “Looks like boot prints. Big ones. And there’s a faint impression here,” he indicated a wider, smoother track, “like something was dragged across the ground. The cart, maybe?”

Rick’s heart pounded. This was concrete evidence. This wasn’t Belsely’s ghostly interpretation; these were physical signs.

They moved closer to the shed. The wooden door, though old, had a new, heavy-duty padlock on it, shining faintly against the weathered wood. It was an obvious sign that this entrance, despite its appearance, was still in use and being deliberately secured.

“A brand-new lock on an old, dilapidated shed,” Rick observed. “That’s a classic cover-up.”

They tried the door, but it was solid. The padlock held firm. They circled the shed, looking for any other points of entry. The walls were mostly intact, made of sturdy, rough-hewn planks. There were no windows.

“It’s well-fortified,” Scotty noted, running his hand over a particularly thick plank. “And built to last, even if it looks like it’s falling apart.”

Rick ran his fingers over the ivy, pulling some away from the wall. Beneath it, he found more scuff marks, and a faint, almost invisible, scratch on one of the planks. It looked like metal on wood.

“They’re using this entrance,” Rick said, certainty in his voice. “This is where the ‘others’ with the cart are coming and going. And Collins knows about it, or is even directing them.”

“So, what now?” Scotty asked. “We can’t break in. And we can’t just sit here all day.”

Rick thought for a moment. They couldn’t force their way in, not without proper tools and risking being caught. But they now knew the secret entrance. They knew *where* to watch.

“We need to observe it,” Rick decided. “But smartly. We can’t just camp out. We need to figure out their schedule, their patterns. Belsely said ‘around midnight.’ That’s a start. We also know that the main ghost show happens at nine o’clock. Is there a connection?”

“Maybe they use the ghost show as a bigger distraction for an even bigger operation,” Scotty suggested. “While everyone is watching the main entrance for the ghost, they’re coming and going from here, doing their real work.”

“It’s possible,” Rick agreed. “It would be an ingenious double-bluff. The spectacular, public ghost show draws all attention to one place and time, while the real crime happens quietly, simultaneously, at a hidden location.”

They spent another hour meticulously examining the area around the service entrance. They found faint tire tracks in the soft earth further down the overgrown path, leading away from the shed and eventually connecting to a larger, unpaved road that wound through the woods. This suggested they weren’t just walking in; they had some form of vehicle for transport. The cart itself might be hand-pulled, but it was likely loaded into a vehicle nearby.

“A truck, or a van,” Scotty speculated. “Something that can handle rough terrain, but isn’t too conspicuous.”

“And it would have to be parked somewhere discreet,” Rick added. “Not too close to Belsely’s farm, and not visible from any main roads.”

They sketched a rough map in the dirt with a stick, marking the main mine entrance, Belsely’s house, the fields, and now the service entrance and the likely vehicle path. The pieces of the puzzle were slowly but surely fitting together.

“We need to watch this entrance,” Rick said, finally. “But we need to be prepared. Not just for bugs, but for actual people. People who are involved in something illegal, and who won’t want to be discovered.”

Scotty nodded, his face grim. The game had definitely changed. It wasn’t just a ghost story anymore; it was a real-life mystery with tangible stakes and potentially dangerous players.

They returned to their bikes, making sure to disturb the ground as little as possible. As they rode back, Rick’s mind was already formulating a new plan. They couldn’t confront Collins or these “others” directly without more proof, and without a better understanding of their operations. But they could observe. They could gather evidence.

“We’ll need our sleeping bags,” Rick announced to Scotty as they reached a more open stretch of the path. “And some supplies. We’re going to stake out that service entrance.”

Scotty didn’t flinch. “When?”

“Tonight,” Rick replied, a determined glint in his eye. “If they operate around midnight, and if their ghost show is a distraction, then tonight is as good a night as any to confirm our theories. We’ll set up positions where we can see the entrance, but remain hidden. And we’ll watch for the truck, for the people, for the cart, and for anything they might be carrying.”

The thought of spending a whole night hidden in the woods, waiting for criminals, was daunting, but also exhilarating. This was what real detective work was all about. It wasn’t just chasing a spectral light; it was uncovering a human conspiracy.

As the sun began its descent later that day, casting long shadows across the fields, Rick and Scotty prepared. They packed their small knapsacks with water bottles, energy bars, a flashlight each, and Rick even brought a small notebook and pencil to record observations. The biggest items were their sleeping bags, rolled tight, which they strapped to their bike racks.

Their parents, accustomed to their adventurous forays, didn’t question them too closely when they said they were going to explore some old trails and might be back late. They had a degree of freedom born from a reputation for responsibility, a reputation Rick knew they were now putting to the test.

They took the same circuitous route as that morning, arriving at the mine hill just as dusk was deepening into night. The air was cooler now, and a gentle breeze rustled through the trees. They found a good vantage point, a small rise covered in dense scrub, about fifty yards from the service entrance shed. It offered a clear line of sight, but the foliage was thick enough to provide excellent cover.

They laid out their sleeping bags, more for comfort during the long wait than for actual sleep, and settled in. The silence of the woods was profound, broken only by the chirping of crickets and the rustling of unseen creatures in the undergrowth.

“Think they’ll show up tonight?” Scotty whispered, adjusting his position to peer through a gap in the leaves.

“I hope so,” Rick replied, his voice barely audible. “Belsely said ‘mostly around midnight.’ And if they were at the Sons’ feast last night, they might be back at work tonight, especially if they found something interesting.”

The hours crept by. Eight o’clock, then nine, the time when the Blue Ghost would usually make his appearance at the main entrance. They heard nothing from that direction, only the distant, faint sounds of the town. Ten o’clock, then eleven. Rick’s eyes were strained from constantly scanning the dim outline of the shed. Scotty was equally vigilant, his head resting on his pack, his gaze fixed.

Just before midnight, a new sound cut through the stillness. Not the roar of a truck, but the quieter, distinct thrum of an engine on a rough track. It was coming from the direction of the hidden road they had discovered that morning.

Rick’s senses sharpened. “Get ready, Scotty,” he breathed, his heart thumping against his ribs. “Something’s coming.”

The engine sound grew louder, closer, then suddenly cut out. A few moments of silence, then the faint click of a car door opening and closing. They heard hushed voices, low and indistinct, but definitely human.

Two figures emerged from the tree line, walking towards the shed. They were dark, indistinct shapes against the slightly lighter sky, but they were definitely men, and they were solid. No ghostly shimmer, no luminous blue glow.

Rick gripped Scotty’s arm. “These are Belsely’s ‘others,’” he whispered. “The real ones.”

They watched, breathless, as the two men approached the shed. One of them produced a key, and with a soft click, the new padlock was opened. The shed door creaked open, revealing a deeper darkness within. The two men disappeared inside.

A few minutes later, they re-emerged, pushing something before them. It was low to the ground, with two small, sturdy wheels, and covered by a dark tarp. The cart. It was exactly as Belsely had described.

And then, a third figure appeared from the shed, carrying what looked like a long, slender pole. This figure, though not glowing, moved with an air of authority, gesturing to the others. He seemed to be leading.

“That’s the ‘Blue Ghost,’ or at least the man who plays him,” Rick thought, a cold certainty settling in his stomach. He wasn’t glowing now, but his presence, his demeanor, commanded attention.

The three men moved quickly and efficiently. One of them pulled the cart, another walked beside it, and the third, the leader, held the pole, which Rick now recognized as a metal detector, sweeping it expertly before him.

They were not heading towards the main mine entrance. Instead, they took a winding path into the cornfield, the same field Belsely had described. They moved slowly, methodically, the leader sweeping the metal detector in wide arcs, the other two pushing the cart behind him.

Rick and Scotty watched, utterly still. This was it. The real operation. The actual artifact hunters, at work under the cover of night, using the ghost story as their ultimate camouflage. The methodical sweep, the metal detector, the cart for collecting. Belsely’s observations, filtered through a rational lens, were proving to be chillingly accurate.

They disappeared into the tall rows of corn, their figures swallowed by the darkness. Rick and Scotty knew they couldn’t follow them into the field without giving away their position. But they had seen enough. They had seen the men, the cart, the metal detector, and the method.
