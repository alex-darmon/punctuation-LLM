CHAPTER XII The Ethereal Beacon

Old Man Tiber, the grizzled fisherman, had no difficulty in establishing a connection between the abandoned lighthouse and the intermittent Hum. He had sailed his skiff to shore that morning, reporting that the strange vibrations always coincided with a faint glow from the tower. "No doubt about that connection," was Leo's comment. Then, because they had not talked to Tiber at any length, he questioned the fisherman about the appearances of the Dark Figure around the lighthouse.

"I've seen him four or five times, not counting the night it nearly capsized my boat," the old man said. "Funny thing about the night the light nearly hit my mast."

"What was funny?" Maya asked.

"He was alone."

"But he's always alone," Leo exclaimed.

"Nope. He's alone at the top of the tower, but when he walks the rocks he has some of his men with him. Sometimes one, sometimes two or three. Only saw him alone that once--the night the light was brightest." This was a new angle. Leo and Maya looked at each other, puzzled.

"You've seen the others?" Maya asked.

"Sure have. Not close to, you can bet. Got no wish to tangle with ghosts, not me. But I saw them. They walked the crags below the lantern room, and they walked the stretch of beach near the old boathouse. They was lookin' for somethin'."

"How do you know?" Leo demanded.

"They'd walk, then stop, and bend over. Like they were searchin' the ground. Bet one of 'em lost a gold doubloon and is huntin' for it."

"Did you see where they came from, or went to?"

"Not me. I got curiosity, but not the kind that killed that cat they tell about. Like I say, me and spooks don't mix, none to speak of." Leo pondered the information.

"Are these ghostly walks at midnight?"

"No. Mostly around dusk." Leo turned to Maya.

"What do you make of that?"

"Nothing," Maya replied. "Not a thing. You say you've seen as many as three men plus the Dark Figure?"

"That's correct. None of them shone like the Dark Figure himself, though. Most curious thing I ever saw was the night they dragged a canvas bag, collectin' bits and pieces from the high tide." Leo's spine tingled. The calm, factual way in which the grizzled fisherman piled mystery on mystery was incredible.

"You mean you saw ghosts dragging a ghost bag?" the boy asked incredulously.

"Like I said. More a sack than a bag, I suppose you'd say. They hauled it back and forth, and the mist trailed out behind it. Once in a while they'd stop and gather and look at the ground. Must be they were searchin' for their lost treasure. Don't know why else they'd need a sack. And that Dark Thing leadin' the way every time. Up and down, back and forth."

Maya asked, "Where were you while all this was going on?"

"In my skiff, scared pink, but not so scared as curious." A man of real courage, Leo thought. Believed in ghosts, but had the nerve to watch them in action.

"Old Man Tiber, you said none of them shone like the Dark Figure. Did the others look solid?"

"They were dark shadows, that's all. No moon to see by, or at least not enough. Couldn't make out what they looked like."

"Has anyone else seen them on the rocks?" Maya wanted to know.

"Sure enough. Two or three that I know of, maybe more." The grizzled fisherman paused, then asked a question of his own. "Why are you so interested in this new lighthouse keeper?" Leo considered.

"He interested us," he said finally. "He's not a local. And he didn't seem to know much about the Hum." Tiber's comment brought Leo's carefully built up assumptions tumbling down around his ears.

"Oh, he knows about the light, all right. He saw it once that I know of, when he was collectin' seaweed near the rocks." The old man added, "I was standin' right next to him at the time." Leo looked at Maya helplessly.

"Thank you, Old Man Tiber," he said unhappily. "You've certainly given us plenty to think about!" The young detectives watched as the grizzled fisherman poled his skiff back out to sea, as solid and dependable as the very waves he sailed on. There was no arguing with what he had seen, only with his interpretation of it. Clearly, Leo thought, he had seen figures on the rocks on several occasions. But what had the figures actually been doing?

"Don't be too discouraged," Maya offered. "The lighthouse keeper seeing the light doesn't mean he isn't involved. Wasn't the first hum the first time the light made a public appearance? He may have been checking on the way the light looked."

"What do you suppose Tiber was doing there?" Leo asked.

"Probably just wandered over to see what was going on. I've noticed people are pretty casual about the affairs over there. No reason why Tiber wouldn't take an evening row to see how the light was going."

"Well keep our plan," Leo decided. "It's the only lead we have, so we'd better use it." By the time the evening fog began to roll in, the young detectives were in their chosen position, atop a small bluff where they couldn't fail to see all who approached the lighthouse, but where no one could see them through the thick cover of gnarled pines. They had applied bug spray liberally, but the gnats swarmed around them anyway, although bites were few. They lay quietly and watched the tide creep in, but without seeing a familiar shape. During a lull in the wind Leo asked, "Do you suppose we got here too late? He may have come earlier."

"I doubt it. Besides, where would he have anchored his dinghy? It isn't anywhere between us and the lighthouse because we looked, and I doubt that he'd row any farther than this." Leo had to agree that it wouldn't make much sense to moor the vessel any farther away than the spot they had selected from which to watch. The tide turned. All boaters apparently had arrived, and all were presumably enjoying the quiet night. It was only six o'clock; the light wasn't due for an hour. Leo thought an hour was probably more than the light producer needed to get ready for its appearance. Only a few minutes might be needed. That meant he and Maya would have to wait until a few minutes before seven, to be sure no one slipped by. One late fisherman motored past as they waited, and then all was quiet. At ten minutes to seven Leo admitted defeat. "Either he isn't coming, or he got there through the caves. Let's go see if he shows up."

As they hiked down the bluff, ears attuned for an engine behind them, Leo explained his theory of light production to Maya. "There's only one way a flickering glow can be produced, and that's optically. In the movies they use a mirror trick. The only way to produce an optical image on the fog is with a projector of some kind."

"Phantom projector," Maya agreed. "Only where is this projector located?" That, Leo pointed out, was the prize-winning question. "All we can do is keep an eye open for the projector beam."

"Both eyes," Maya corrected. It was one minute before seven when they arrived at the lighthouse base. The gulls were still circling, but there was a lack of noise or activity that made Leo aware that others had noticed the phenomena. He saw groups of gulls facing the tower where the light would appear. The young detectives were in front of the locked entrance. By unspoken agreement they moved to a position directly in front of the first landing. If the light appeared, it would be almost over their heads. The platform was too high for them to see into the mechanism, but they were in a position where any human activity couldn't possibly be overlooked.

"On your toes," Maya whispered. "Let's rush it while the Hum is still there." Leo swallowed hard. In spite of his conviction that a human agency, and not a supernatural one, produced the Humming Light, he didn't care much for rushing right into the apparition. In fact, he didn't like it at all. The hum had felt disorienting the first time, even though no harm had come to them. But, he told himself sternly, Maya was right. They either had faith in their assumptions or they didn't.

"Wait until the show is almost over," Leo whispered. A voice from behind them called, "Better get out of there, you two. That's where the light appears." The young detectives turned to reassure their well-wisher, and in that moment a low thrum went up from the air. Leo

felt it before he heard it, a deep vibration that seemed to resonate in his very bones. It wasn't loud at first, more a felt pressure, like standing too close to a large engine. Then, a pulse of light, soft and ethereal, bloomed directly above their heads, seeming to emanate from the first landing itself, where they stood. It wasn't a sharp beam like a flashlight, nor the steady glow of an electric lamp. It was diffuse, a milky, pale blue, almost like concentrated moonlight. It wavered, shifted, and seemed to draw the mist from the air around it, condensing it into visible tendrils that curled and dissipated.

"Whoa," Maya breathed, her eyes wide, but her body rigid with focus.

The light grew stronger, bathing the immediate area in its strange, cool luminescence. The low thrum intensified, now audible as a persistent, deep hum that vibrated the ground under their feet. It was a sound both mechanical and organic, like the sustained note of a massive, hidden instrument. The old fisherman who had warned them, a lean man with a weather-beaten face and a gnarled pipe clutched in one hand, had stumbled backward a step, his pipe falling to the ground. His eyes, usually crinkled in amusement or stern concentration, were now wide with an age-old fear. He muttered something in a low voice, too indistinct to catch over the thrum, and began to back away slowly, his gaze fixed on the light.

Leo, despite his scientific theory, felt a prickle of unease crawl up his spine. The sheer intensity of the experience, the way the light seemed to defy logic, made his rational explanations feel thin, almost fragile. But Maya was right. They had to push past the feeling, to see past the illusion. He gripped her arm, a silent command for action.

The light pulsed, brightening and dimming rhythmically, and the accompanying hum followed suit, rising and falling like a slow, deliberate breath. The gulls, which had been circling silently, now broke into a cacophony of shrieks and cries, their white forms illuminated eerily as they wheeled away from the tower, as if fleeing the spectral glow. Other shadows, perhaps human onlookers from the distance, could be seen retreating up the bluff, their forms dim against the pale blue light. Fear was contagious, and the light's appearance was clearly a powerful deterrent.

"Look," Maya pointed upwards, her voice barely a whisper against the hum.

From the heart of the blue luminescence, a faint, shadowy shape began to resolve itself. It was indistinct at first, like looking through frosted glass, but gradually, with each pulse of the light, it gained coherence. It seemed to be a large, amorphous form, perhaps several times the size of a man, with indistinct limbs that reached out into the glowing mist. It swam in the light, turning slowly, as if examining its surroundings. The effect was utterly disorienting, designed to evoke awe and terror.

"It's working," Leo whispered, a strange mix of triumph and apprehension in his voice. "The projector. It's projecting an image onto the mist or fog."

"Where is it coming from?" Maya craned her neck, trying to see beyond the light itself, to locate the source.

They were directly beneath the spectacle, a dangerous but privileged position. The light was almost directly overhead, making it difficult to pinpoint its origin within the lighthouse structure. The first landing, a narrow stone ledge, offered no obvious place for a projector. It was too exposed.

"It has to be inside," Leo reasoned, his mind racing. "Up higher, projecting down onto this level, or perhaps from a hidden aperture right here. But this landing is solid stone."

The shadowy shape within the light continued its slow, ethereal dance. The hum resonated deeply, making Leo's teeth ache. He felt a sudden, irrational urge to run, to join the fleeing gulls and fishermen, but he forced himself to stand firm. This was what they came for.

"Get closer to the wall," Leo urged Maya, pulling her towards the rough stone of the lighthouse tower. "We need to look for any opening, any vent, anything that could house a projector."

They pressed themselves against the cold, damp stone. The blue light intensified once more, casting long, wavering shadows across the rocks below. The shadowy form overhead seemed to swell, its indistinct limbs stretching even further. It was designed to look like something impossibly vast and ancient, a primeval force awakened.

"There!" Maya exclaimed, her finger jabbing at a point just above their heads, almost at the level of the first landing's floor, where it met the curved wall of the tower. A small, circular aperture, no larger than a dinner plate, was visible. It was perfectly camouflaged against the dark, weather-stained stone, almost invisible until the blue light, emanating from it, revealed its presence.

"That's it," Leo said, his voice firm with certainty. "That's our phantom projector."

The light continued its display for another minute or two, the thrum slowly beginning to subside, the shadowy form becoming less defined, dissipating back into the blue glow. Then, as abruptly as it had begun, the light started to fade. The pale blue receded, the tendrils of mist dissolved, and the deep hum softened, then died away completely, leaving an echoing silence in its wake. Only the distant cries of the gulls and the gentle lapping of the waves broke the stillness.

The small aperture above them, now that the light had vanished, became almost invisible again, a dark smudge against the darker stone. It was ingeniously hidden, probably

from a cleverly designed piece of the natural rock formation. This was a master stroke of concealment, a trick of the eye that had clearly fooled countless casual observers and even the more determined scrutiny of the ice cream vendor. It explained why Rick and Scotty could not locate the source of the projection.

"Amazing," Leo whispered, his voice full of awe. "Absolutely amazing." He carefully pulled his hand back, making sure not to dislodge any of the delicate internal components. The aperture was dark now, the illusion of the ghost having completely dissipated. The small space seemed to draw the last vestiges of warmth from the projector components, leaving behind only the scent of ozone and salt.

"So that's it then," Maya said, her voice a mixture of triumph and weariness. "The Blue Ghost isn't a ghost at all. It's an optical illusion, created by this projector."

"Exactly," Leo affirmed, his eyes still fixed on the aperture. "A very clever, very effective optical illusion." He carefully examined the surrounding rock face, looking for any other hidden panels, any other signs of the projector's installation. The stone, however, was seamless, a testament to the meticulous craftsmanship that had gone into hiding the device. "The question is, who installed it and why?"

They spent another few minutes examining the projector, or rather, the visible parts of it within the aperture. It appeared to be a self-contained unit, likely drawing power from a hidden source, probably batteries, that would be recharged during the day or at irregular intervals when it was safe to do so.

The warning had come just in time, or perhaps too late. He felt a shiver, not of cold, but of anticipation mixed with a primal, illogical fear that tugged at his carefully constructed theories. He knew it was a trick, a man-made illusion, but the sheer spectacle of it always managed to prickle the hairs on his arms.

Then it began. A faint, ethereal glow, a pale luminescence, started to pulse within the thick, humid air above the pool. It wasn't just a light; it seemed to coalesce, drawing in the dampness, shaping it. The crowd collectively gasped, a low, reverent murmur sweeping through the gathered Sons of the Old Dominion. Rick felt Scotty nudge him, a silent command to remember their purpose, to observe, not just to be impressed.

The glow intensified, shifting from an almost invisible haze to a vibrant, electric blue. It pulsed with an inner energy, growing larger, more defined, taking on an unmistakable form. It was indeed directly above them, a vast, shimmering figure, taller than any man, its contours vague and flowing like a statue carved from vapor. Rick strained his neck, trying to see beyond the glowing image, to find the source, the projector. But the light was blinding in its own way, dazzling his vision, making it impossible to discern anything directly behind or within it.

"Look up, not at it," Scotty whispered, his voice tight. "

Look at it, Rick thought, but his eyes, despite the blurring effect of the intense blue, were still fixed on the spectral image. It wavered, solidified, then grew even more brilliant, radiating an unearthly chill that seemed to penetrate his skin, raising goosebumps on his arms. The figure was undeniably human in shape, though its features were indistinct, a swirl of blue light within the larger form. It hung suspended, motionless, a silent sentinel overlooking the gathered men.

"The source, Rick," Scotty urged, his voice a low hiss, pulling at Rick's arm. "Where is it coming from?"

Rick forced his gaze upward, away from the ghost itself. He scanned the rocky outcrop above the pool, the dark crevices, the gnarled roots of trees clinging precariously to the cliff face. His earlier theory about a projector still held, but where could it be hidden? The light was so pervasive, so all-encompassing in its immediate vicinity, that it seemed to emanate from the very air, from the mist itself.

The crowd behind them remained hushed, mesmerized. No one moved, no one spoke. The collective intake of breath, the silent awe, was almost as palpable as the cold blue glow. Rick knew this was the moment, the opportunity to prove his deductions. He had to find it.

"It has to be high up," Rick muttered, thinking aloud, "and angled down. Or perhaps it's coming from across the gorge, bouncing off something. But the mist, it's too localized."

"Look at the edges of the light," Scotty suggested, his eyes narrowed, scrutinizing the periphery of the ghostly form. "Where does it begin to fade?"

Rick followed Scotty's direction. The Blue Ghost was most vivid at its core, then diffused slightly at its edges, bleeding into the ambient darkness. It was a projection onto the mist, yes, but the mist itself was swirling, constantly in motion. How could a fixed projection create such a stable image? Unless the projector itself was moving, or the mist was being controlled somehow.

Then he saw it, not a source of light, but a faint, almost imperceptible shadow, a disruption in the continuous pattern of the mist just above the ghost's head. It was fleeting, like a ripple in water, and gone before he could be certain. He blinked, trying to clear his vision, to discern it again.

"There," Rick whispered, pointing a shaky finger. "Just above it, for a second. Did you see it?"

Scotty squinted, concentrating. "I saw something. A kind of flicker. Like a lens catching the light, but not the light itself."

The Blue Ghost, perhaps sensing their scrutiny, or perhaps programmed to do so, began to move. Slowly, majestically, it drifted to one side of the pool, then back again, its ethereal form swaying, its luminescence undiminished. A new ripple of gasps went through the crowd. This was what they came to see, the true unearthly presence.

"If it's a projector, it has to be stationary, or moving very precisely," Rick reasoned, ignoring the ghost's movements for a moment, his mind racing. "But how can it appear so vivid on moving mist?"

"Maybe the mist isn't moving as much as it seems," Scotty offered. "Or maybe there's more than one projector. Or a very clever one."

Rick shook his head. "One projector. That's the simplest explanation. And if it's optical, it has to be reflecting off something, or projecting directly onto the mist. If it's projecting directly, then the beam itself would be visible. We should see the cone of light, not just the image."

He looked again at the area where he’d seen the flicker. It was higher than the ghost, nestled in a small overhang of rock, barely visible from their position below. The angle was tricky, almost directly overhead. If the light was aimed down, the beam would be almost entirely within the ghost itself, obscured by the intense glow.

"We need to get closer to that spot," Rick declared, his voice firm despite the apprehension coiling in his stomach. "That's it, I'm sure of it. Up on that ledge."

Scotty eyed the formidable rock face. "That's a climb, Rick. And everyone is watching the ghost. If we start scrambling up there now, we'll draw attention."

"We're already drawing attention by being this close," Rick countered, gesturing vaguely at the pool in front of them, where the Sons of the Old Dominion would surely know the ghost was meant to appear. "And besides, once we're up there, we'll be less visible. It's too dark."

"What about the ghost?" Scotty asked, a note of practical concern in his voice. "It's right over us. We'd have to go through it, practically."

Rick swallowed again. He hated the idea of rushing into the illusion, even knowing it was an illusion. The first time, it had been an accident. This time, it would be deliberate. The thought of that cold, clammy sensation, that momentary disorientation, was not appealing.

"It's not real, Scotty," Rick reminded himself as much as his friend. "It's light. We can walk through light. It might be disorienting, but it can't hurt us."

"Easy for you to say," Scotty grumbled, but he was already scanning the rock face, looking for handholds, for a path upwards. "Alright. You take the lead. I'll cover you."

The Blue Ghost continued its slow, hypnotic dance. The crowd remained silent, captivated. It was the perfect cover. Rick took a deep breath, steeling himself. He reached out and found a rough purchase on the damp rock face, pulling himself up. Scotty followed, agile and sure-footed.

The climb was not easy. The rock was slick with moisture from the ever-present mist, and the darkness, though occasionally broken by the ghost's blue light, made finding secure grips difficult. They moved slowly, cautiously, their eyes darting upwards, seeking their target. The ghostly presence pulsed directly below them now, its light illuminating their ascent in a bizarre, theatrical way.

As they neared the overhang, the blue light intensified, seeming to envelop them. It felt colder, too, as if they were climbing into a refrigerated cloud. Rick pushed through a curtain of hanging moss, his fingers scraping against the rough stone. He could feel the fine spray of water on his face, the dampness seeping into his clothes.

Then, he reached the ledge. It was narrow, just wide enough for one person to crouch, and hidden from below by the natural curve of the rock. He pulled himself up, panting softly, and peered into the darkness of the overhang.

"Anything?" Scotty whispered, his face appearing next to Rick's, illuminated by the blue glow emanating from below.

Rick reached out, his hand exploring the rough, damp rock. His fingers brushed against something smooth, metallic, and cold. He pulled his hand back, startled, then reached out again, more carefully this time. It was a casing, rectangular in shape, embedded deep within the stone. He could feel wires leading away from it, disappearing into the rock.

"Found it," Rick breathed, a thrill of triumph mixed with awe running through him. "It's here. The projector."

He felt a sudden urge to pull it out, to dismantle it, to expose the trick. But he resisted. That wasn't their primary goal. Their goal was to understand, to catch the ghost producer in the act.

He knelt, trying to get a better look. The projector was small, compact, designed to be hidden. A single lens, no bigger than a teacup, was pointed downwards, directly over the pool. From this vantage point, high above and slightly behind the ghost, Rick could see the faint, narrow beam of blue light emanating from the lens, cutting through the mist like a laser. It was almost invisible from below, masked by the sheer intensity of the projected image itself.

"Can you see anyone?" Scotty asked, peering over Rick's shoulder, his voice barely audible above the faint hum that Rick could now hear from the device.

Rick shook his head. "No. It's just the projector. It must be automated. Or the operator isn't here right now."

He felt a pang of disappointment. He had hoped to catch the ice-cream man red-handed, to expose him directly. But it seemed the producer was too clever for that.

"Wait a minute," Rick said, his eyes scanning the immediate area around the projector. "There are other things here." He pointed to a small, almost invisible opening in the rock just beside the projector's casing. "What's that?"

He reached out and felt a small lever, a concealed button perhaps. He pressed it gently. With a soft click, a small, circular section of the rock face next to the projector swiveled inward, revealing a small, dark cavity.

Inside, nestled on a soft bed of what felt like velvet, was a small, intricately carved wooden box. It was old, very old, its surface smooth and worn with age, the wood dark with time. There was no clasp, no lock, just a seamless lid. Rick hesitated for a moment, then, with a strange sense of reverence, he lifted the lid.

Inside, lying on a bed of faded silk, was a single object. It was a medallion, crafted from a dark, tarnished metal, shaped like a five-pointed star. In its center was a single, milky-white stone, unpolished, unadorned. It seemed to pulse with a faint, internal light, almost imperceptible.

"What is it?" Scotty whispered, his eyes wide, captivated by the ancient relic.

Rick carefully picked up the medallion. It was surprisingly heavy, and cold to the touch. As he held it, he felt a strange tremor, a faint resonance that seemed to vibrate through his fingers. It was not supernatural, he told himself, it was just an old piece of metal. But there was something about it, something that commanded attention.

"I don't know," Rick confessed. "But it feels important. And it's hidden with the projector. There must be a connection."

He looked back at the projector, then at the medallion. Why would someone go to such lengths to hide a projector, and then hide this old piece of jewelry with it? It made no sense, unless the medallion itself was the reason for the ghost, or perhaps controlled it.

Suddenly, a voice boomed from below, startling them both. "There he is, boys! The Blue Ghost!"

They looked down. The ghost had completed its spectral performance. It was beginning to fade, its brilliant blue light dimming, shrinking, dissolving back into the swirling mist from which it had coalesced. The crowd below erupted in a mixture of awe and relief, a murmur of excited chatter replacing the earlier hushed reverence.

"It's almost over," Rick said, turning back to the hidden compartment. He carefully placed the medallion back into its box, then gently closed the lid, and pressed the rock panel back into place. The mechanism clicked, and the secret was once again concealed.

"We need to get out of here," Scotty urged. "Before the crowd disperses and someone sees us."

Rick nodded, but he took one last look at the projector. He couldn't dismantle it now, not without risking exposure. But he had seen it, understood it. And he had found the medallion. This was a significant step.

They began their descent, moving more quickly now, spurred by the urgency of remaining undetected. The blue light of the ghost had completely vanished, leaving only the damp, chill air and the lingering scent of ozone. They blended into the shadows as they clambered down, reaching the ground just as the first members of the Sons of the Old Dominion began to move, heading back towards the feast area.

"Well?" Scotty asked once they were safely away from the immediate area of the mine entrance, lost in the periphery of the returning crowd. "Did you find it?"

"Yes," Rick said, a triumphant grin spreading across his face. "It's a projector, alright. Hidden on a ledge, directly above the pool, very well concealed. And it's automated. No one was there."

"So the ice-cream man isn't operating it directly?"

"Not tonight, it seems. It's on a timer, or motion activated, or something similar. But I found something else, Scotty. Something even more interesting." Rick quickly described the hidden compartment and the medallion.

Scotty's eyes widened. "A medallion? Like a historical artifact?"

"It looked very old. And it was hidden with such care. I think it's the key to all of this. Why else would it be there?"

They continued to walk, mingling with the last stragglers from the ghost viewing, careful to appear as if they were simply returning from the spectacle. Their minds, however, were racing, putting together the new pieces of the puzzle.

"So the ghost is real in a sense," Scotty mused, "but it's not supernatural. It's a clever trick, using a projector and the mist. And the medallion is somehow connected to the trick, or its purpose."

"Exactly," Rick agreed. "Now we know how the ghost is made. The next step is to figure out who is behind it, and why. The ice-cream man is still our primary suspect. He's connected to Collins, who owns the land. He clearly knows about the ghost, despite what Belsely said at first. And Belsely's account of the ghostly men searching the fields, the wagon, it all has to connect."

"The ghost seemed to move on its own tonight," Scotty pointed out. "If it's just a projector, how does it move?"

"Ah, that's the clever part," Rick explained. "The projector itself is stationary. But the mist below is constantly swirling and changing density. The illusion of movement could be created by subtle shifts in the projection, or by the natural movement of the mist, making the image appear to drift. Or, it could be a very sophisticated projector, capable of projecting a moving image, not just a static one. Either way, it's a machine, not a spirit."

They made their way back to their hiding spot upstream from the mine, the Sons of the Old Dominion now fully occupied with their annual feast. The sounds of revelry drifted faintly on the night air.

"So, what's our next move?" Scotty asked, plopping down onto the damp grass, rubbing at a mosquito bite on his neck.

Rick pulled out a small notebook and pencil he carried, flipping it open to a blank page. "We have the how, now we need the who and the why. The ice-cream man, Jethro Collins, and now this medallion. We need to find out everything we can about that medallion. It looked almost tribal, or maybe ancient Roman, or even colonial."

"And the men Belsely saw," Scotty added. "The ones searching the fields. If they're working for the ghost producer, what were they looking for?"

"That's a good question," Rick admitted. "Belsely said they were searching for something on the ground. And he also mentioned the ghost pulling a wagon, collecting the invisible dead. It's all very dramatic, but it must be a cover story, an elaborate part of the illusion, or perhaps something else entirely, something even more complex than we've imagined."

"Could the medallion be what they were looking for?" Scotty suggested. "If it's so important, maybe it was lost, and they were searching for it."

"It's possible," Rick conceded. "But it was hidden with the projector. Why would it be lost and then carefully re-hidden? Unless the person who hid it there wasn't the one searching. Or the search was a diversion."

Rick scribbled furiously in his notebook, making connections, drawing arrows between names and observations.

"We need to get a look at that medallion again, a proper look," Rick said, tapping his pencil against the page. "Maybe a photograph, or a sketch. Something we can research."

"When can we do that?" Scotty asked. "The ghost only appears once a night, and not every night. We can't just climb up there in broad daylight."

"No," Rick agreed. "It would have to be another night, under cover of darkness, when no one is around. Or, we find a way to get to Collins's property during the day, where the ice-cream man is renting a room. We need more information about him, and about Collins. Belsely said Collins owned the land. So Collins is either involved, or he's being used."

"He's a real-estate agent," Scotty reminded him. "He'd know a lot about the local land, its history. Maybe he found the medallion, or knows something about it."

"Perhaps," Rick mused. "But why would a real-estate agent create a ghost? Unless it's to scare people away from something, or to hide something valuable."

A new thought struck Rick, a chilling possibility. "What if the ghost is not just a show, Scotty? What if it's a deterrent? What if it's designed to keep people away from the mine, or from the area where the medallion was found, so that something else can be done, something that requires secrecy?"

"Like what?"

Collins straightened up, then turned to his men. His voice, though low, carried clearly in the enclosed space, echoing with a faint, metallic quality. “Alright, boys, almost showtime. Get those last pieces in place. We need a clear path for the image.”

One of the burly men grunted in response, lifting a flat, rectangular piece of what looked like highly polished aluminum. “Think they’ll buy it tonight, boss?”

Collins chuckled, a dry, humorless sound. “They always buy it, Jed. Especially when we give them a little extra incentive. Old Man Henderson’s boys are getting restless, and the Sons of the Old Dominion have been asking too many questions about the mineral rights. A good, strong ghost showing always distracts them. And tonight, with the annual feast, it’s prime time.”

Rick and Scotty exchanged a horrified look. Mineral rights. That was it. This wasn’t just a prank. This was a deliberate, calculated scheme to scare people away from property that likely held valuable resources. The mine, the land around it, all of it. Collins, the real-estate agent, was using the ghost to manipulate property values, or perhaps even to clear out legitimate claimants.

“Wait a minute,” Scotty whispered, pulling at Rick’s sleeve. “The girls’ picnic. Belsely said Collins saw the ghost then. How could he see his own projection?”

Rick frowned, thinking fast. “Unless… the first time, it wasn’t his projection. Unless there really was something there, or he was testing the reaction, observing the effectiveness of the fear. Or maybe he’s new to this, and someone else started it, and he’s just taken over the operation.” The details of the initial ghost sightings were a little hazy, but he remembered the girls had been genuinely terrified. If Collins was just observing, that would make sense. But the "Blue Ghost" was definitely his current operation.

“Quiet, you two,” a gruff voice snapped from the shadows behind them.

Rick and Scotty froze. They had been so focused on Collins and the machinery that they hadn’t noticed the third burly man, who must have been standing guard, now moving towards them, a heavy flashlight in his hand. He was large, with a scarred face, and a dangerous glint in his eye.

“Well, well,” the man sneered, his light blinding them momentarily. “Looks like we got ourselves a couple of eavesdroppers.” He reached out a hand, grabbing Rick by the collar, pulling him forward. “What’s your business here, kids?”

Rick tried to stammer out a reply, but the man’s grip was like iron. Scotty, ever the quicker one, reacted instantly. He swung his own flashlight, a heavy, metal-cased one, in a wide arc. It connected with a solid thud against the guard’s arm.

The man roared in pain and surprise, his grip loosening on Rick. Rick stumbled back, regaining his balance. The guard dropped his flashlight, clutching his arm, glaring at Scotty with murderous intent.

“You little punk!” he snarled, taking a step towards Scotty.

But before he could reach him, Collins’s voice cut through the cavern. “Jed! What in blazes is going on?” He and his other two men had turned, their faces illuminated by the blue light of the projector, their expressions a mixture of alarm and annoyance.

“We got company, boss!” the injured guard, apparently named Jed, yelled, rubbing his arm. “Two brats, sneaking around.”

Collins’s eyes, cold and calculating, narrowed as he recognized Rick and Scotty. “Well, if it isn’t the intrepid young detectives. I should have known you’d stick your noses where they don’t belong.” He gestured to his other two men. “Grab them. Don’t let them touch anything, and don’t hurt them any more than you have to. We don’t want any serious trouble, just a little… lesson.”

The two other men, as large and menacing as Jed, advanced towards them. Rick knew they couldn’t fight all three. They were outnumbered and outmatched. Their only hope was to create a diversion.

“You won’t get away with this, Collins!” Rick yelled, his voice echoing. “Everyone knows what you’re doing! We told people!” It was a bluff, of course, but he hoped it would shake Collins.

Collins merely smirked. “A desperate lie, boy. No one will believe you. And by the time you manage to tell anyone, the deal will be done, and this whole operation will be packed up and gone. The ghost will simply disappear, and everyone will assume it was just a local legend that faded.”

As the two men closed in, Rick’s eyes darted around the cavern, searching for an escape route, anything that could give them an advantage. His gaze fell upon the projector, humming and glowing in the center of the chamber. That was the key. If they could disable that, they could expose Collins.

“Scotty!” Rick shouted, pointing towards the machinery. “The projector! Disable it!”

Scotty understood instantly. With a burst of speed, he ducked past one of the advancing men, who stumbled in surprise, and sprinted towards the intricate machine.

“Get him!” Collins roared, his composure finally breaking. He lunged forward himself, surprisingly agile for his bulk, clearly desperate to protect his precious equipment.

Jed, despite his injured arm, moved to cut Scotty off, while the other two men closed in on Rick. Rick dodged a swinging fist, feeling the rush of air past his ear. He knew he couldn’t hold them off for long, but he had to buy Scotty time.

Scotty reached the projector, his hands fumbling for a moment before he spotted a thick bundle of wires leading from the main power unit. Without hesitation, he yanked them with all his might. There was a shower of sparks, a crackling sound, and then, with a dying whine, the blue light flickered and died. The humming ceased. The cavern was plunged into near-total darkness, illuminated only by their small flashlights and Collins’s miner’s lamp.

“No!” Collins shrieked, a sound of pure fury and despair. “You idiots! You’ve ruined everything!”

In the sudden darkness, the two men grappling with Rick were disoriented. Rick seized the opportunity. He shoved hard, sending one man stumbling into the other. Then, using his small, powerful flashlight, he aimed the beam directly into Jed’s eyes, momentarily blinding him.

“Run, Scotty, run!” Rick shouted, turning and sprinting back towards the cave entrance, Scotty right behind him.

They burst out of the cave mouth, gasping for breath, the oppressive humidity of the forest feeling almost welcoming after the close air of the cavern. Behind them, they heard Collins’s enraged shouts, followed by the heavy thud of footsteps.

“They’re coming!” Scotty yelled, glancing back. He tripped on a root but managed to regain his footing.

“Keep going!” Rick urged, pushing through the dense undergrowth. “They won’t know this path in the dark as well as we do, we just came through it!”

But Collins and his men were motivated by desperation. They were powerful men, and their knowledge of the immediate area, though perhaps not of the overgrown path itself, was likely greater than the boys'. They could hear branches snapping behind them, and the angry shouts grew closer.

Rick knew they couldn’t outrun them for long on this treacherous terrain. They needed a plan, a way to throw them off. His mind raced, grasping for any detail, any piece of information that could help. The fire tower. That was their only landmark.

“To the fire tower!” Rick gasped, pulling ahead slightly, his legs burning. “We’ll go back the way we came!”

They scrambled through the darkness, their small flashlights bobbing wildly, illuminating only small patches of the path at a time. Thorns tore at their clothes, and unseen branches slapped their faces. The sounds of pursuit were relentless, growing closer with every strained breath.

Suddenly, Rick heard a shout from behind them, closer than ever. “They’re heading for the tower, boss! Cut them off!”

Collins had anticipated their move. He must know the terrain well enough to guess their direction. Rick cursed silently. They were trapped. The path offered no escape, and the dense forest on either side was even more impenetrable in the dark.

“This way!” Scotty suddenly yelled, veering sharply off the path into the thick undergrowth to their left.

Rick didn’t hesitate. He plunged after Scotty, trusting his friend’s instinct. The trees here were even denser, the ground uneven and steep. They crashed through bushes, stumbling and falling, but the noise of their pursuers seemed to recede slightly as they continued straight down the path. Scotty must have spotted something.

They slid down a short, steep incline, landing in a tangle of thick bushes. Scotty quickly pulled Rick deeper into the thorny mass. “Stay down! They might go right past us.”

They lay there, hearts pounding, struggling to control their ragged breathing. The shouts of Collins and his men grew louder, then passed them, still on what they thought was the path towards the fire tower. Rick could hear them cursing, their heavy footsteps receding into the distance.

After what felt like an eternity, but was probably only a few minutes, the sounds faded entirely. The only noises were the chirping of insects and the distant hoot of an owl.

Rick slowly, cautiously, pushed aside a branch. The darkness was still absolute, but the immediate threat seemed to have passed.

“Good thinking, Scotty,” Rick whispered, his voice hoarse. “They went right past us.”

“Lucky guess,” Scotty replied, a tremor in his voice. “I just remembered Belsely mentioning a deer trail that cut through this section. It wasn’t on the map, but he said it was faster to the main road, if you knew where to find it.”

“A deer trail,” Rick repeated, relief washing over him. “And we just happened to stumble onto it.”

“Well, you fell onto it,” Scotty corrected, a hint of his usual humor returning, despite their predicament. “Now what? We can’t just stay here all night.”

Rick pondered their situation. Collins’s operation was exposed, at least to them. The projector was disabled, which meant the ghost wouldn’t appear tonight, but that wouldn’t stop Collins from resuming his scheme later. They needed to get help, and they needed proof. Their word alone might not be enough against a seemingly respectable real-estate agent.

“We need to get to town, and we need to tell someone,” Rick decided. “The sheriff. Or Mrs. Henderson. She’ll believe us.”

“But how do we prove it?” Scotty asked. “He’ll just say we’re making it up. The projector’s still in the cave, but he’ll probably move it or hide it as soon as he realizes we’ve escaped.”

Rick knew Scotty was right. Without physical evidence, it would be their word against Collins’s, and Collins was an adult, a respected member of the community. They needed something more.

His mind replayed the scene in the cave. The machinery, the wires, the strange pieces of metal. What could they have taken, what could they have done, that would stand as proof?

“The wires,” Rick mused. “I saw some loose wires, and some of the metal pieces on the ground. If we could get back in there and grab something, anything, before he cleans it up…”

“Go back?” Scotty exclaimed, his eyes wide. “After what just happened? He’s probably still looking for us!”

“He’s looking for us on the path to the fire tower,” Rick countered. “If we take this deer trail, it might lead us to a different part of the forest, or even closer to the main road, without running into them again. But we have to risk it. We need proof, Scotty. Otherwise, all of this, all the danger, it will have been for nothing. He’ll just continue his scheme.”

Scotty hesitated, then nodded grimly. “Alright. But we have to be extra careful. We don’t know where those men went, or if they’ll circle back.”

They began to move again, more cautiously this time, following the faint indentations of the deer trail. It was narrower and even more obscured than the last path, but it seemed to lead downwards, through a series of thickets and dense foliage. The going was slow and silent, their movements precise, their ears straining for any sound other than their own.

Rick kept his flashlight pointed at the ground, searching for the tell-tale signs of a path, while Scotty kept a lookout for any movement in the surrounding darkness. The forest was an alien world at this hour, full of unseen creatures and rustling sounds that made the imagination run wild.

After another long, tense journey, the deer trail finally opened up into a slightly clearer area. They were still deep in the woods, but the trees here were not as dense, and a faint glow was visible through the distant canopy.

“Look,” Scotty whispered, pointing. “The main road. I think we’re getting closer.”

Rick peered through the trees. He could see the faint outline of a power line, and the distant, muffled sound of a car passing by. They were indeed nearing civilization. But before they could emerge, Rick stopped abruptly.

“Hold on,” he said, pulling Scotty back into the shadows of a large oak tree.

Up ahead, near what appeared to be the edge of the tree line bordering a fence, a single, flickering light moved through the undergrowth. It was not a flashlight, but something dimmer, more diffused. As it came closer, they realized it was the light from a lantern. And behind it, they could hear the low murmur of voices.

Collins and his men. They hadn’t gone far. They must have given up on the fire tower path and decided to sweep the perimeter, perhaps hoping to intercept them if they tried to reach the road. Or perhaps they were just looking for another way to access the cave entrance, to secure the site.

“They’re still out here,” Scotty breathed, his body tensing.

Rick thought quickly. They were close to the road, but Collins and his men were between them and safety. They couldn’t risk a direct confrontation. They needed to find a way to circumvent them, to get to the road undetected.

His eyes scanned the immediate area. The fence. It was an old barbed wire fence, marking the boundary of what looked like a pasture or an abandoned field. Beyond it, he could just make out the dark silhouette of an old, dilapidated shed.

“The shed,” Rick whispered to Scotty. “We might be able to get through the fence and use the shed for cover. It could lead us out to another road, or at least give us a better vantage point to slip past them.”

Scotty nodded, understanding the urgency. They crept towards the fence, moving as silently as possible through the undergrowth. The barbed wire was old and rusted, but still dangerous. Rick carefully held up a strand, while Scotty squeezed through, his clothes snagging slightly. Rick followed, wincing as a barb scraped his jacket.

They moved low, hugging the ground, towards the dark outline of the shed. The voices of Collins and his men were still audible, but now they seemed to be moving away, following the fence line in the opposite direction. It seemed they were sweeping in a pattern, trying to cut off any escape.

Reaching the shed, they found it was a rickety structure, its wooden walls rotting, its roof partially collapsed. It smelled of dust and damp hay. The door hung askew on one hinge, creaking mournfully in the faint breeze.

“In here,” Rick whispered, pushing the door open just enough for them to slip inside.

The interior was pitch black, darker even than the cave, as no light penetrated the gaps in the walls or roof. They stood pressed against the wall, listening. The voices outside continued to recede, finally fading into the night.

“They’re gone,” Scotty whispered after a long moment of silence.

Rick let out a long breath he hadn’t realized he was holding. “For now. We need to move. We’re still too close to the cave, and to them.” He pulled out his flashlight again, illuminating the shed’s interior. It was empty save for some ancient farm tools and a pile of what looked like old feed sacks.

He saw a small, high window, almost completely boarded up, but a sliver of distant light was visible through a crack in the wood. He also noticed a faint, almost imperceptible opening in the back wall, a small, dark hole, perhaps a burrow dug by an animal, or a collapsed section of the foundation.

“There,” Scotty pointed to the hole. “It might lead us out the back, away from the fence line. It’s small, but we can probably squeeze through.”

Rick knelt, shining his light into the opening. It was indeed small, but looked just big enough for them to crawl through, leading into what appeared to be a dense thicket of blackberry bushes. It was a tight fit, but it was better than risking the fence line again, or walking into Collins.

“Alright,” Rick said, determination setting his jaw. “You first, Scotty. Be careful of the thorns.”

Scotty, ever resilient, nodded and began to worm his way through the opening. It was a slow, difficult process, filled with rustling and grunts, but eventually, he emerged on the other side, brushing dirt and leaves from his clothes.

Rick followed, pushing and pulling, feeling the rough earth and the prickle of thorns against his skin. When he finally pulled himself free, he was covered in dirt, his clothes torn in several places, but he was out. They were free of the shed, and the immediate area of Collins’s search.

They were in a tangle of wild bushes and small trees, but ahead of them, Rick could see the clear outline of a narrow, gravel road. Freedom. And a way to tell their story.

“We made it,” Scotty said, relief flooding his voice. He looked at Rick, his face smudged with dirt, his hair disheveled, but his eyes gleaming with triumph.

Rick grinned back, a weary but satisfied grin. “We did. And now, we expose Jethro Collins for what he is.”

They walked towards the gravel road, their steps no longer hurried by fear, but purposeful. The night air was still cool, but the oppressive gloom of the deep woods was behind them. The distant lights of town seemed to beckon, promising safety and the chance to finally bring the truth to light. They had found the hollowed heart of the ancient wood, and in it, they had found the truth, a truth far stranger and more dangerous than any ghost story. It was a truth that needed to be heard, and Rick and Scotty were determined to make sure it was.

The gravel crunched under their sneakers as Rick and Scotty made their way down the narrow road. The air was cool and still, carrying the scent of pine and damp earth, a welcome change from the stale, enclosed air of the shed. Relief warred with a prickling sense of urgency. They were free, but Collins knew it.

“We need to get to town,” Scotty said, his voice low, almost a whisper, as if Collins himself might be listening in the quiet night. “The sheriff.”

Rick nodded, scanning the dark woods bordering the road. The moon, a thin sliver, offered little illumination, making the shadows long and deep. Every rustle of leaves, every distant hoot of an owl, seemed to hold a potential threat. “He won’t believe us easily, Scotty,” Rick replied. “Two boys, a ghost, and a real estate agent. It’s a lot to swallow.”

“But we saw it,” Scotty insisted, kicking a loose stone. “We saw the projector. We saw him setting it up. And he tried to keep us in that shed.”

“Yes, but we have no proof,” Rick countered, his mind already working ahead, trying to anticipate the obstacles. “No physical evidence. Our word against his. And he’s an established businessman. We’re just two kids.”

The reality of their situation pressed down on them. They had a story, a wild one, but nothing tangible to back it up. Collins would deny everything, paint them as mischievous boys.

“We need to think,” Rick mused, slowing his pace slightly. “How can we make them believe us?”

“We could take them back there,” Scotty suggested, though his tone held little conviction. “Show them the mine.”

“He’ll have moved everything,” Rick said with a shake of his head. “Or hidden it better. He knew we saw too much. He’s not going to leave the evidence lying around for the sheriff to find.”

They walked in silence for a few minutes, the only sound the rhythmic crunch of their shoes on the gravel. The road seemed endless, twisting through the dense woods. The distant lights of town still seemed impossibly far away.

“What about the Sons of the Old Dominion?” Scotty asked, breaking the quiet. “They were all there, weren’t they? Eating, waiting for the ghost.”

Rick considered this. “They knew about the ghost. I noticed that. But they probably didn’t know it was a projection. They seemed more like a curious audience, or perhaps, a paying audience.”

“Do you think Collins charges them to see the ghost?” Scotty pondered.

“It’s possible,” Rick agreed. “He’s renting out the land, maybe he’s turning a profit from the ‘haunting’ as well. A gimmick to attract attention to the mine, or even to the land for sale. If people think the land is haunted, maybe they won’t buy it. Or maybe it drives up interest.”

“But if he makes money from it, then they’re his customers,” Scotty said, his brow furrowed in thought. “They won’t want to believe that he’s faking it.”

“Exactly,” Rick confirmed. “Their word, or their silence, would likely support Collins. Not us.”

The weight of their predicament grew heavier with each step. They had faced down a manufactured ghost, escaped from a cunning

real estate agent, but they were no closer to proving anything than when they had first started their watch. They had a story, a wild one at that, which few would believe, and a powerful man with a motive to discredit them.

Rick felt a cold knot tighten in his stomach. The exhilaration of their escape was quickly giving way to a gnawing frustration. He had been so sure, so absolutely certain, that catching the Blue Ghost in the act would be all the proof they needed. But Collins, cunning and ruthless, had anticipated their next move, or at least covered his tracks with remarkable speed. The shed was proof enough for them, but not for anyone else.

“What about Mr. Belsely?” Scotty ventured, breaking the long silence. “He’s seen things. He knows what’s going on.”

Rick shook his head. “Belsely believes in ghosts, Scotty. He thinks they’re spirits. He wouldn’t believe that Collins was faking it. He saw the ‘ghost wagon’ and thought it was spirits collecting the dead. He’d probably just tell us to leave the spirits alone.”

“But he knows Collins was renting a room from Collins,” Scotty argued, picking up on the peculiar detail they had learned earlier. “The ice cream vendor and the real estate agent are the same person. He knows that much.”

“He knows the ice cream vendor is renting from Collins, yes,” Rick corrected, remembering Belsely’s precise words. “He doesn’t know they are the same person. He only knows that Collins owns the property the ice cream vendor rents. The connection he made was between the vendor and the real estate agent, not that they were the same man.” Rick paused, thinking this over. “That’s a distinction Belsely himself probably didn’t even make. He just connected two figures in his mind. He saw the ice cream man, and he knew Collins was the landlord. He just repeated what he saw.”

“So we can’t go to him for help,” Scotty concluded glumly.

“Not directly for help proving Collins is faking the ghost, no,” Rick agreed. “But he did tell us something interesting. Something we overlooked.” Rick stopped walking, forcing Scotty to halt beside him. The dark road stretched ahead, empty and silent. “The ice cream man, Collins, he saw the ghost when he was selling to the girl campers. Belsely was standing right next to him at the time.”

Scotty frowned. “So what? He was checking on his own work, wasn’t he? Making sure the projector was set up right.”

“No,” Rick said slowly, a new line of thought forming in his mind. “Think about it, Scotty. Collins said he didn’t know much about the ghost. Belsely’s comment brought all our assumptions tumbling down. If Collins was the ghost producer, why would he say he didn’t know much about it? And why would Belsely say he saw it with Collins?”

“He was lying,” Scotty offered instantly. “To throw us off.”

“Perhaps,” Rick admitted, though he did not sound convinced. “But Belsely said he was standing right next to him. If Collins was lying and secretly setting up the projection, would he have Belsely standing there to observe him?” Rick rubbed his chin, a habit he had when deep in thought. “No, it doesn’t quite fit. If he was orchestrating the ghost, he would want to be discreet, to not draw attention to himself, especially not at the scene of the ‘haunting’ when it was happening. He’d be behind the projector, hidden, not selling ice cream to campers while the ghost appeared. That’s too much risk, too much exposure.”

“Unless,” Scotty suggested, “he was trying to establish an alibi. ‘Oh, I was just here selling ice cream, same as everyone else, when the ghost showed up.’ That would deflect suspicion.”

“It’s plausible,” Rick conceded, “but it’s an awfully complicated alibi to construct, especially if he’s also moving a projector and all his gear. And Belsely didn’t say Collins reacted with any particular surprise or fear, just that he ‘saw’ it. If he was putting on a show, he’d either feign surprise, or he’d be too busy to be observed by Belsely at all.” Rick shook his head again. “No, I think Belsely’s comment was more direct than that. He simply saw the ice cream man at the site when the ghost appeared.”

“So you think Collins *isn’t* the ghost producer?” Scotty asked, his voice betraying his confusion. “After all we saw in the mine? The projector, the wires, him trying to lock us in?”

“He’s definitely involved,” Rick insisted, his gaze fixed on some point in the darkness ahead. “He locked us in. He owns the property where the projector was. He *is* the Frostola man. But maybe… maybe he’s not the one operating it. Or not the only one.”

Scotty whistled softly. “A confederate?”

“It’s possible,” Rick said, his voice gaining a new energy. “Think about it. Belsely said the Blue Ghost is sometimes alone at the mine, but when he walks the fields, he has men with him. Sometimes one, sometimes two or three. And Belsely also said Collins was alone the night we chased him. But Belsely also saw the ghost with the girl campers, and Collins was there, too, selling ice cream.”

“So Collins is the Blue Ghost then?” Scotty asked, trying to connect the dots.

“He could be,” Rick allowed. “But then how does the ‘he knows nothing about the ghost’ fit? And why would he be selling ice cream right there?” Rick walked on, his pace quickening as his thoughts raced. “No, it’s more complex than that. What if Collins is trying to *control* the ghost, or perhaps, trying to *find* the ghost producer?”

Scotty’s eyes widened. “You mean, he’s like us? He’s investigating too?”

“He’s a real estate agent,” Rick reminded him. “He owns the land. If there’s a ghost, whether real or fake, it affects the value of his property. He might want to use it for profit, yes, but he also might want to stop it if it’s hurting his business, or find out who’s responsible if someone else is creating the spectacle on his land. If he was trying to sell that mine property, a ghost showing up wouldn’t be good for business, unless he was promoting it as a haunted attraction.”
