The sheer vulnerability of these surface-dwellers, their almost childlike innocence, was now tinged with a tragic understanding; they were, in their indolent beauty, but cattle to the subterranean horror, a living larder for the monstrous descendants of our industrial age. My rational self, however, argued with fierce urgency. My life, my very ability to return to my own time, depended upon this lever. Without it, I was marooned, a relic in a future that had no place for me. To remain on the surface was to await a slow, ignoble end, perhaps becoming, like the Eloi, a creature of mere pleasure, a succulent morsel for the subterranean dwellers. No, I must retrieve it.

My first task, I decided, was to re-establish contact with the Eloi, or at least to ascertain their whereabouts. Perhaps Weena, with her peculiar attachment to me, might yet be found. I shouted her name, my voice hoarse in the still air, the sound swallowed by the vastness of the ruins. There was no reply. I walked towards the nearest cluster of what I knew to be their sleeping chambers, remnants of once-grand architectural complexes. The silence persisted. It was unsettling. Usually, even in the heat of the day, some faint murmur of their languid activity could be detected, a soft melody, a distant splash. Today, there was nothing. It was as if the world above ground had paused, holding its breath. This unusual quietude did not reassure me; rather, it heightened my sense of foreboding. Were they simply deeper within their shaded sanctuaries, avoiding the sun's fiercest rays, or was there some other, more sinister reason for their complete absence? The thought that the Morlocks might have ventured forth in the daylight, emboldened by their success, and taken some of the Eloi, briefly flickered through my mind, but I quickly dismissed it. Their extreme sensitivity to light, their clumsy movements in the glare, seemed to preclude such an audacious raid. Yet, the possibility remained, a chilling undertone to my already considerable anxieties.

I searched for perhaps an hour, exploring the nearby ruins and the verdant glades between them. Still no sign of Weena or any of her kind. It was as if they had melted away, leaving me utterly alone. This isolation, this complete severance from any form of human companionship, even the limited sort offered by the Eloi, gnawed at me. I was a man of my own time, accustomed to the bustle of London, the constant hum of intellectual discourse. Here, there was only a vast, indifferent silence, punctuated by the rustling of leaves and the occasional shriek of some unseen, distant bird. My heart, I confess, began to sink. The task before me, already daunting, now seemed even more so without the slightest hope of succour or assistance from the surface dwellers. I was truly on my own.

My immediate need was for a source of light, and perhaps a weapon. I remembered the box of matches, now sadly depleted by my previous encounters, but still containing a few precious sticks. These would be my primary means of illumination, a fragile shield against the abyssal gloom. For a weapon, I looked about me. The ruins offered an abundance of choice, but I needed something both portable and effective. A loose slab of granite, too heavy to wield, was useless. A splintered beam of wood, too unwieldy. My eyes fell upon a heavy, iron-bound club, part of some ancient, rusted mechanism. It was corroded, but still stout, and of a size that I could manage with one hand. Its balance was imperfect, but its weight was considerable, and I judged it capable of inflicting a telling blow. I hefted it, testing its feel, and decided it would serve.
