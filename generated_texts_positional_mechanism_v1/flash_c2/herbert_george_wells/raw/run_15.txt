The more he could give Griffin, the more he might placate him, or at least buy himself time. Time was a precious commodity now, more valuable than gold or jewels. He needed time to think, time to formulate a strategy against an enemy he could not perceive, an enemy who could strike from any angle, at any moment.

As he closed the pantry door, his gaze fell upon the telephone in the hall, a relatively new addition to his household, its black, polished surface glinting innocently in the faint light. He moved towards it, his heart quickening. Could he make a call? A silent, swift call to the police? But Griffin was upstairs, perhaps listening. He could be anywhere, poised to strike. The risk was immense. If Griffin heard the click of the receiver, the muffled words, Kemp knew he would pay a terrible price. He imagined Griffin descending the stairs with terrifying speed, seizing him, throttling him before he could utter a single syllable of warning. The phone remained untouched, a symbol of tantalizing, dangerous hope.

He continued his search for the requested "suitable articles for your journey." Griffin had spoken of Port Stowe, a coastal town some fifty miles distant, and a remote cottage. The journey would require discretion, warmth, and sustenance. He went to his study, a room filled with books and scientific instruments, a place of quiet contemplation now invaded by frantic, desperate urgency. He opened a large mahogany chest where he kept old clothes for charity, or for gardening. He found a thick woollen cloak, a pair of sturdy walking boots, and a wide-brimmed felt hat that could be pulled low. He also found a large, stout leather valise, a relic from his own student days, now dusty and unused. These would serve. He imagined the absurd spectacle: an empty cloak, walking boots seemingly treading on air, a hat floating eerily above the ground. It was beyond grotesque; it was profoundly unsettling.

His mind raced through possibilities. A trap? Could he lure Griffin into a confined space? Could he spray him with something visible, like paint or flour? The thought of flour made him pause. A ridiculous notion, perhaps, but a physical means of detection. If he could see Griffin, even for a moment, he could act. But how to do it without tipping his hand? And how to contain a man who could simply shed the visible substance and vanish again? The problem was intractable.

Could he bring himself to use it? Against a man, even an invisible one, who was his guest, however unwelcome? More importantly, could he even aim it effectively at an unseen target? The image of himself firing wildly, blindly, into the air, while Griffin perhaps stood inches away, mocking him, was humiliating and terrifying. He dismissed the idea. Violence, he suspected, was Griffin’s forte, not his own.

He collected the cloak, boots, hat, and valise, placing them on the large tray with the food and drink. The weight of the tray was nothing compared to the crushing burden of his predicament. He looked around the kitchen one last time, a desperate hope lingering that some ingenious solution would present itself. But there was nothing. Only the mundane objects of a normal household, utterly useless against the extraordinary horror that had descended upon him.

He began to ascend the stairs, each step an ascent into a deeper layer of his personal hell. The silence upstairs was profound, oppressive. He listened intently, every muscle tensed, for any sound, any indication of Griffin’s presence. Kemp started, nearly dropping the tray. “No, no, of course not. Just gathering what you asked for. Food, drink, some warmer clothes for your journey.” He forced a casual tone, though his voice was strained.

The unseen mouth worked with a savage hunger that made Kemp’s stomach churn.

The gnawing paused, and the dressing gown seemed to shift, as if Griffin was turning his head towards Kemp. “A process, yes. And a damnably difficult one, I assure you. Years of tireless work, endless experimentation, untold suffering. “Brilliance? It’s monstrous!” Kemp retorted, unable to control the tremor of disgust in his voice.
“Monstrous to the uninitiated, perhaps,” Griffin replied calmly, the gnawing resuming. “But to the true seeker of knowledge, it is the ultimate liberation. Imagine, Kemp, the possibilities! The ultimate freedom from constraint, from observation, from the petty limitations of the visible world!”

The gnawing stopped abruptly. A dangerous substance, certainly, but in the right dose, it could render even a man of Griffin’s constitution unconscious. The thought, once formed, took root in his mind with an almost irresistible force. This was it, the desperate expedient he had been searching for.

The bitterness of the coffee, he hoped, would mask the slightly acrid taste of the chloral hydrate. There was a sound of vigorous chewing, a greedy, almost animalistic sound, as Griffin devoured the food.

Griffin chuckled again, a sound that grated on Kemp’s nerves. “Ah, Kemp, ever the eager student. Always seeking to unravel the mysteries of the universe, are you not? He paused, a faint gurgling sound indicating he was taking another gulp of coffee. “The difficulty, you see, lay not merely in the theoretical understanding, but in the practical application. Kemp listened, his scientific mind, despite the terror, grappling with the implications. It was, theoretically, a sound explanation, albeit one that pushed the boundaries of known science to an almost unimaginable degree. The sheer audacity of the achievement, the intellectual brilliance required, was undeniable. And that, Kemp realized with a fresh surge of dread, was what made Griffin so dangerous. His genius was unbridled, unfettered by moral considerations.

Griffin laughed, a full-throated, almost maniacal sound that chilled Kemp to the bone. “Permanent? My dear fellow, do you imagine I would condemn myself to an eternity of invisibility? He paused, and Kemp heard the clink of the coffee cup being placed back on the saucer, somewhat heavily. “But these are mere technicalities, Kemp. He, a man of reason, an academician, was now embroiled in a situation so fantastical, so utterly beyond the bounds of ordinary human experience, that he himself still struggled to fully comprehend it. He wrapped the rope repeatedly, ensuring it was taut, immovable. Then he moved to the ankles, pulling them together, binding them securely. He finished by running a longer length of rope around Griffin’s torso, securing him to the back of the heavy, wooden chair.

Once the task was done, Kemp stood back, panting slightly. Griffin was a bound, invisible lump in the chair, his snoring still echoing faintly. It was a bizarre, almost surreal tableau. An invisible man, tied with visible rope, in a quiet, gas-lit kitchen.

Kemp’s immediate sense of relief was profound, but it was swiftly followed by a wave of exhaustion. The tension of the last several hours had taken its toll. He needed to think, to plan the next step. Reversing the invisibility. Griffin had said it was a process, not devilry. A scientific process. There must be a way.

He made his way to the study, pulling a thick volume on chemistry from his shelf, then another on optics. He needed to understand the underlying principles of Griffin’s transformation. He remembered fragments of conversation from their university days, Griffin’s wild theories about refractive indices, about manipulating the very fabric of light. But those were abstract, theoretical concepts then, the musings of a brilliant but eccentric student. Now, they were terrifying reality.

He brought the books back to the kitchen, placing them on the table near the still-snoring Griffin. He pulled up another chair, a comfortable armchair from the drawing room, and settled into it, determined to begin his research. But before he could even open the first book, a new thought struck him, a cold, hard realisation that pierced through his momentary sense of triumph.

Griffin had been injured. Shot. The blood-stained bed, the blood on the bandage. He had left Griffin in a cold kitchen, tied, and in pain. The chloral hydrate would mask the pain for a while, but it was still there. And what if the drug wore off before Kemp could find a solution? What if Griffin woke, bound but still invisible, still powerful, and furiously enraged?

Kemp shivered. He needed help. This was too much for one man, too dangerous, too complex. He needed to contact someone, someone in authority, someone who would believe him. But who?

He considered Dr. Cuss, the vicar, Mr. Marvel, the innkeeper. No, they were the very people who had ridiculed Griffin’s story that morning. They would think Kemp had gone mad, perhaps even that he was Griffin’s accomplice.

There was only one person he could truly trust, a man of science, a man of reason, a man with influence. Colonel Adye, the Chief of Police in the nearby town of Port Burdock. Adye was a rational man, not prone to flights of fancy, but he was also pragmatic. If presented with irrefutable evidence, Adye would act.

But how to present the evidence? How to convince a seasoned police officer that an invisible man was tied up in his kitchen? Kemp imagined the scene, the incredulous stares, the dismissive smiles.

He needed to make Griffin visible. That was the key. Only then would his story be believed, only then could he secure help without being deemed insane. But how? He opened the chemistry book, flipping through its pages. Refraction, reflection, absorption, the visible spectrum, molecular structure. Griffin had spoken of changing living tissue. A complex organic process. It wasn’t a simple matter of a prism or a mirror.

Hours passed. The gas jet hissed softly, casting long, wavering shadows across the kitchen. The books lay open before Kemp, filled with diagrams and formulae that seemed utterly inadequate to explain the phenomenon before him. He drank several cups of coffee himself, trying to ward off the fatigue that pressed down on him, trying to keep his mind clear. He occasionally checked on Griffin, placing a hand near his mouth to feel the faint warmth of his breath, confirming he was still unconscious. The dressing gown, with its dark, irregular stains, looked like a macabre, empty garment hanging in the chair.

Towards dawn, a new sound began to emanate from the Invisible Man. A low moan, then a restless stirring. The chloral hydrate was beginning to wear off.

Panic seized Kemp. He had not found the answer. He had not even come close. He had wasted precious hours in theoretical study when he should have been preparing for this eventuality. Griffin would wake, furious, and he would be free in minutes if Kemp did not act.

He looked around desperately. He needed to keep Griffin sedated, or at least incapacitated. He had no more chloral hydrate, and even if he did, he couldn’t administer it to a potentially conscious, struggling invisible man.

His gaze fell on a large, heavy sack of flour in the pantry. Flour. It was light, powdery. If he could throw it, cover Griffin...

The idea was crude, desperate, but it might work. If Griffin was covered in flour, he would become visible. A grotesque, flour-dusted outline of a man. Then he could be dealt with.

He snatched up the sack, his heart pounding once more. He returned to the kitchen, his eyes fixed on the faintly stirring dressing gown. Griffin was beginning to twitch, his low moans growing more frequent, more agitated.

Kemp lunged, grabbing the neck of the sack and swinging it with all his might. The sack burst open mid-air, and a cloud of fine white flour exploded outwards, engulfing the invisible form in the chair.

There was a choked gasp, a sudden convulsion from the now-visible, flour-coated figure. Griffin. He was there. A grotesque, vaguely human shape, covered in white dust, struggling against the ropes. His head, arms, torso, legs, all outlined in the pristine white powder. It was an appalling sight, like a statue come to life, or a ghost made manifest.

“Kemp!” roared the Invisible Man, his voice now clear, sharp with rage and terror. “What have you done, you imbecile! You traitorous fool!” He thrashed violently against the bonds, the ropes groaning under his struggle. The chair rocked, threatening to topple.

Kemp stood frozen for a moment, staring at the visible horror he had created. He had made him visible, but at what cost? Griffin’s fury was palpable, a chilling wave of pure hatred that radiated from the flour-covered form.

“You fiend!” Griffin shrieked, his voice hoarse, distorted by the flour that must have entered his mouth. “You think you can bind me, can make a spectacle of me! You will regret this, Kemp, I swear it! I will make you regret the day you were born!”

Kemp snapped out of his trance. He couldn’t let Griffin break free. He rushed forward, grabbing a heavy poker from beside the fireplace. It was a crude weapon, but it was all he had.

“Silence, Griffin!” Kemp shouted, his own voice cracking with strain. “You are bound! You are visible! Your reign of terror is over!” He raised the poker defensively, wary of the flailing, flour-dusted limbs.

Griffin, however, possessed a strength born of desperation and the lingering effects of the drug. He strained against the ropes, a frantic, almost superhuman effort. The old rope, weakened by time, began to fray. A strand snapped with a sharp crack.

“Fool! You cannot hold me!” Griffin snarled, and with a mighty heave, one of his flour-coated hands tore free. It was a terrifying sight, a disembodied white hand, now moving with shocking speed towards Kemp.

Kemp cried out, instinctively recoiling. The hand, though still coated in flour, was invisible at its core, and its grip was as strong as iron. It lashed out, catching him a glancing blow across the temple. The impact sent a jolt of pain through his head, and he staggered back, dropping the poker with a clatter.

Griffin was still partially bound, but with one hand free, he began to work frantically on the remaining knots. The flour-covered form twisted and bucked, a grotesque dance of liberation.

“You made a mistake, Kemp!” Griffin gasped, his voice raspy. “A grave, fatal mistake! No one makes a fool of the Invisible Man!”

Kemp knew he had only moments. He had to call for help, to get someone here, *anyone*, before Griffin was fully free. He turned and bolted from the kitchen, his heart hammering, the image of the flour-dusted fiend burned into his mind. He burst into the hall, his eyes scanning for the telephone. It was in the study, just opposite.

He sprinted towards it, his breath ragged. He fumbled with the receiver, his fingers shaking as he tried to dial. He heard a crashing sound from the kitchen, the distinct splintering of wood. Griffin was breaking free of the chair.

“Police! Get me the police!” Kemp shouted into the receiver, his voice desperate. “This is Dr. Kemp! I need immediate assistance! I have... an invisible man in my house! He’s dangerous!”

He heard a guttural yell from the kitchen, closer this time, and then the distinct thud of heavy footsteps on the wooden floor. Griffin was coming.

Kemp didn’t wait for a response. He dropped the receiver, leaving it dangling, its tinny voice still squawking. He knew he couldn’t face Griffin alone, not now that he was enraged and partially free. His only chance was to escape, to lead Griffin away from the house, to draw attention to him, to make his visibility a public spectacle that would demand intervention.

He dashed towards the front door, pulling it open with a desperate yank. The cold pre-dawn air hit him, sharp and bracing. He paused for a fraction of a second, glancing back. The flour-covered outline of Griffin filled the doorway of the kitchen, a looming, terrifying silhouette. He was still partially coated, a ghostly apparition of white, a living nightmare made manifest. His eyes, though unseen, felt like burning coals fixed upon Kemp.

“You won’t escape, Kemp!” Griffin’s voice echoed through the house, a chilling promise of retribution. “No one escapes the Invisible Man!”

Kemp burst out into the street, not stopping to close the door. He ran, blindly, fueled by sheer terror, his lungs burning with effort. He could hear Griffin’s footsteps behind him, light and swift, yet strangely ponderous, due to the remaining flour perhaps. He heard the whisper of fabric, the faint scuff of feet on the pavement. The street was deserted, wrapped in the last vestiges of night.

He pounded down the lane, towards the village square, towards any possible sign of life. He knew the way by heart, every twist and turn, every shortcut. But Griffin, invisible and relentless, seemed to be gaining on him. The fear was a physical presence, a cold hand squeezing his heart.

He heard a low chuckle behind him, a terrifying sound that seemed to float on the cold morning air. “Running, Kemp? A pathetic display. Did you truly believe you could outwit me, could trap me, could expose me? I am beyond your petty schemes, beyond your limited understanding.”

Kemp risked a glance over his shoulder. He saw nothing, only the empty street, but he felt the presence, the chilling proximity of his pursuer. He imagined the flour, now perhaps dissipating, leaving Griffin once again a phantom, a deadly, unseen hunter.

He heard a snarl, a furious expletive, then a thud. Had Griffin stumbled? Kemp seized the moment, pumping his legs harder, pushing himself to his absolute limit. He reached the edge of the square, where a few early risers might be stirring, where the first hint of morning activity might offer a chance.

But the square was still quiet, desolate. A single gas lamp flickered feebly in the gloom. No one. He was alone, utterly alone, with the invisible monster at his heels.

He heard another sound behind him, a chilling click, a metallic scrape. Griffin was armed. Had he found something in the house, a knife, a gun? Kemp’s blood ran cold.

“Turn around, Kemp,” Griffin’s voice commanded, closer now, directly behind him. It was no longer the voice of a scientist, or a former colleague, but the voice of a predator, cold and utterly merciless. “Turn around and face your reckoning. You will pay for your betrayal. You will regret the day you ever encountered the Invisible Man.”

Kemp spun around, his hands raised instinctively, though he knew it was futile. He saw nothing, felt only the icy grip of terror. Then, a sharp, searing pain exploded in his side, a brutal impact that knocked the wind from his lungs. He gasped, clutching his side, his fingers coming away wet and warm. Blood. Griffin had stabbed him.

He crumpled to the ground, the street lights swirling crazily above him. He heard Griffin’s triumphant, ragged laugh, a sound of unholy glee.

“A trifle, Kemp,” the voice hissed, close to his ear. “A mere warning. I could have ended it then and there, but I wish you to suffer. I wish you to understand the true nature of my power, the magnitude of your error.”

Kemp tried to push himself up, but his strength was failing. The pain in his side was excruciating, a hot, throbbing agony that spread through his entire body. He felt a boot connect with his ribs, a brutal, deliberate kick that sent another wave of nausea washing over him.

“You will be my first example, Kemp,” Griffin continued, his voice devoid of any human compassion. “A lesson to all who would dare to stand against me, to all who would seek to expose my genius. Kemp felt a faint stirring of hope, a desperate, fading glimmer. He had to attract attention. He had to make noise. He had to scream. But the pain, the shock, the terror, they were overwhelming him. His throat felt constricted, his lungs refused to obey.

He heard the distinct sound of a cart rumbling in the distance, a faint, almost imperceptible noise in the stillness of the pre-dawn. It was a milkman, perhaps, or an early farmer. Someone was coming.

Griffin must have heard it too. There was a sudden tension in his unseen presence. “No,” he muttered, a low growl of frustration. “Not yet. Not like this. You will remain silent, Kemp. Absolutely silent.”
