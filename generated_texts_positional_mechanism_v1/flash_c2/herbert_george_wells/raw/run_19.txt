Aris squeezed his eyes shut, wishing he could unsee, unhear, un-know everything that had transpired in the past hour. The world, which he had always understood as governed by predictable laws, had been irrevocably shattered. He was caught between an unimaginable horror and his own desperate desire to survive. The pressure finally lifted, leaving Aris feeling light-headed, his body weak and trembling. He was a collaborator. A facilitator. And the price of his own survival was the consciousness of others. The laboratory, once his sanctuary, was now his cage, and he, Aris Thorne, renowned biochemist, was now inextricably bound to a monstrous, evolving entity, a being that fed on the very essence of life itself. The thought made him retch, a dry, bitter spasm that wracked his exhausted frame. He could feel the slow, steady pulse of the Chrysalis radiating from the plinth, a silent, insistent beat that promised both unparalleled knowledge and unspeakable horror. His new purpose, forced upon him, loomed like a shadow, long and dark, over the rest of his life. He was no longer merely a scientist; he was an accomplice, his hands already stained, not with blood, but with the invisible, unbearable burden of complicity. He slowly lifted his head, his eyes fixing on the shimmering, alien presence on the plinth, a gaze of pure, utter despair. His life, he knew, would never be his own again.

You haven’t got any idea, have you, of what it means,” the Invisible Man continued, his voice losing some of its earlier petulance, taking on a more reflective, almost mournful quality, though the underlying current of demand remained, “to be without, to be utterly unprovided for, to be an outcast, a hunted beast of the night? How does he pay for a lodging? How does he even purchase a simple meal without revealing his secret, without inciting panic, without being hunted like a beast?”

Kemp remained silent, allowing the torrent to flow. He could not deny the practical difficulties, the sheer, unimaginable hardship of such an existence. Yet, the tone, the underlying current of self-pity and self-justification, grated upon him.

“My initial attempts were, I admit, clumsy,” Griffin went on, a note of bitter amusement in his voice. “I thought I could simply walk into shops, take what I needed. But the jostle of the crowd, the sudden turns, the unexpected obstacles, they were a constant torment. And the fear, Kemp, the ever-present, chilling fear of being touched, of being felt, of being discovered. I learned quickly that the world was not a stage for my grand experiment, but a hostile environment, a labyrinth of invisible threads waiting to snare me. The first time I was truly hungry, truly desperate, I found myself in a small baker’s shop. The smell of fresh bread, Kemp, it was maddening. I stood there, watching the baker’s wife, a fat, complacent woman, serve her customers, her hands moving with a slow, deliberate rhythm that seemed designed to torment me. I waited for her to turn her back, to leave the counter unguarded. And when she did, I snatched a loaf, a simple loaf of bread, and fled. But even that was not simple. The sudden movement of the loaf, apparently by itself, caused a cry, a shout of alarm. I heard the scurry of feet, the clatter of a falling tray. I ran, Kemp, I ran as if the hounds of hell were at my heels, clutching that warm loaf to my chest, my heart pounding like a drum against my ribs.”

Griffin paused, a long, drawn-out sigh escaping him. “And so it went. A snatch here, a pilfering there. A scarf from a market stall, a coin from an open purse. Each act a desperate measure, each discovery a fresh terror. I found that I could not sleep in public places; the slightest brush against a passer-by, the accidental contact with a dog, would lead to instant panic. I slept in deserted sheds, in overgrown gardens, in the damp chill of London’s alleyways. I became a creature of the night, a furtive shadow in a world of light. And the cold, Kemp, the biting, ceaseless cold of late autumn and winter. To be naked, perpetually naked, in the English climate, it is a torment beyond description. I took clothes, of course, when I could, but they were always too large, too small, ill-fitting, and always, always a danger. A coat might snag, a hat might fall off, a shoe might catch. Each article of clothing became a potential betrayer, a visible marker of my invisible presence. Kemp listened, his scientific mind cataloguing the practicalities, even as his moral sense recoiled from the implications. “So, you survived by theft,” he stated, not as an accusation, but as a summary.

“I survived by necessity!” Griffin retorted, his voice rising again. “What other choice had I? To starve? To freeze? To simply cease to be? I tell you, Kemp, society drove me to it. The very structure of your visible world offered me no alternative. It was either become a phantom criminal or a phantom corpse. And I, by God, chose life. And then, there was the intellectual isolation. To be surrounded by people, yet utterly alone. To hear their conversations, their laughter, their mundane concerns, and to be utterly cut off, to be a ghost in the machine of humanity. It breeds a certain… bitterness, Kemp. “And the bandages?” Kemp asked, seizing on a practical detail, hoping to steer the narrative away from Griffin’s increasingly self-serving philosophy. “The bandages and the dark glasses, the disguises you employed in Iping?”

“Ah, Iping,” Griffin sighed, a sound that seemed to carry both nostalgia and loathing. “A fool’s errand, that. My desperate attempt to re-enter the visible world, to find a modicum of peace, a place where I might continue my experiments, where I might even, by some miracle, reverse this accursed process. I had saved a little money, money pilfered from a theatrical box office, ironically enough, and I thought, a quiet village, far from the prying eyes of London, would be my sanctuary. I had learned, through bitter experience, that some means of manifesting myself, however grotesque, was essential for any sort of stable existence. The bandages, the dark glasses, the false nose, the wig, they were all an attempt to construct a semblance of a visible man, to trick the world into accepting me. But the people of Iping, those yokels, those gossiping, superstitious, impertinent brutes, they saw through it, or rather, they imagined things. They were terrified by my oddity, infuriated by my reclusiveness, and utterly convinced I was some devil or madman. They pestered, they pried, they whispered. And when I ran out of money, when my funds dwindled, and I could no longer pay for their overpriced lodgings and their insipid food, they turned on me like a pack of curs. They tried to break into my room, to seize my belongings, to expose me. And when I resisted, when I fought back with the only weapon I possessed, my invisibility, they became truly hysterical. It was chaos, sheer, unadulterated chaos, and all because of their provincial stupidity and their fear of the unknown. I had to reveal myself, partially, to escape them, to demonstrate my power, to scatter them like chaff before the wind. And then I was hunted again, a naked, invisible fugitive, with nothing but the clothes I had worn and the few shillings I had in my pocket. And that fool, Marvel, damn his eyes, he was the only one I could trust, or rather, the only one I could coerce into assisting me. And he betrayed me too, the simpering idiot. He took my money, my books, my very identity, and ran off like a frightened rabbit. That is why I was in London, Kemp, tracking him, searching for him, for my things, for the means to reclaim my life. And that is why I blundered into your house, bleeding, exhausted, and at my wits’ end.”

Griffin’s voice had risen to a crescendo of rage and self-pity, and he ended with a choking sound, as if he were on the verge of tears or further violence. The dressing-gown quivered violently in the basket chair.

Kemp absorbed all this, his mind racing. The narrative was coherent, disturbing, and filled with details that painted a picture of a man driven to the brink, but also a man whose moral compass had spun wildly into self-justification. “So, these were not petty crimes,” Kemp said slowly, choosing his words with care, “but acts of necessary self-preservation, dictated by your unique condition and the hostility of an uncomprehending world. Is that your contention, Griffin?”

“Precisely!” the Invisible Man snapped, his voice sharp with validation. “You understand, Kemp! You, a man of science, a man of intellect, you can see the logic, the undeniable imperative of my actions. I am not a common criminal; I am a victim of circumstance, a pioneer whose discovery has turned into a curse, a man forced to live by rules not of his own making, rules imposed by the very condition that makes me unique!”

“And the shooting?” Kemp pressed, remembering the detail mentioned earlier. “You said there was shooting. Whose confederate was this man? And why did he try to steal your money?”

Griffin grumbled, a low, guttural sound. “That was a devilish complication. After I left Iping, after that debacle, I needed a way to recover my notes, my books, my gold. Marvel had them, the wretched cur. I traced him to a small seaside town

  “Marvel was a poor, weak-willed creature,” Griffin continued, the sound of gnawing on a cutlet resuming, though with less ferocity than before. “He had no strength of character, no conception of the magnificent thing that had been placed in his care. I had to force him, naturally. He was a tramp, a simpleton, easily cowed by the unknown. I found him near Port Burdock, an ideal cover for my operations. I convinced him, through certain persuasive demonstrations, that he was to be my eyes and hands in the visible world. He carried my books, my notebooks, my experimental apparatus, and a small fortune in gold I had managed to secure from my landlord’s petty cashbox. He was my confidant, my only link to sanity, or so I believed then. But the moment he felt he could escape, that moment the foolish constable, Jaffers, attempted to arrest me in Iping, Marvel saw his chance. He bolted, Kemp. He bolted like a hare, with all my worldly possessions, my life’s work, my very future, clutched in his trembling hands.”

  Kemp listened, his eyes fixed on the empty dressing-gown and the floating cutlet. The tale, though self-serving, held a desperate logic. It was a logic of survival, twisted by an almost monstrous egocentrism. “So, you tracked him to this seaside town,” Kemp prompted, recalling the earlier fragment.

  “Indeed. To Port Burdock. A wretched hole, full of gaping holidaymakers and officious little shopkeepers. Marvel, the idiot, had tried to make a life for himself there, believing himself safe from me. He had even tried to open a small, pathetic business, a sort of curiosity shop, selling my instruments as ‘rare scientific antiques.’ Can you imagine the effrontery, Kemp? My precious apparatus, the very tools of my monumental discovery, hawked like common trinkets!” Griffin’s voice rose, a gust of indignation, and a faint clinking sound suggested he had reached for the whiskey again.

  “And you found him,” Kemp said, stating the obvious.

  “I did. Not without difficulty. He was cleverer than I gave him credit for, for a time. He knew I was after him. He made attempts to disguise himself, to hide his tracks. But he could not hide the gold. He began to spend it, foolishly, ostentatiously. I saw him, or rather, I saw the effects of his spending, the new clothes, the ill-fitting spectacles he wore as a disguise. He was a walking advertisement for his own betrayal. I cornered him in a boarding-house lodging, a squalid little room above a fishmonger’s. I demanded my property back.”

  Griffin paused, and the sound of his drinking was distinct. “He refused. The audacity of the man! He claimed they were his, a gift from some ‘wealthy benefactor.’ He even tried to raise an alarm, to shout for help. It was then that a man, a local ruffian he had evidently befriended, burst into the room. A great brute of a fellow, with fists like hams and a mind like porridge. He thought he would protect his newfound associate, or perhaps he thought to share in Marvel’s ill-gotten gains. He lunged at me, Kemp, at thin air. I had no choice. I defended myself.”

  “And the shooting?” Kemp pressed, his jaw tight.

  “Ah, the shooting. A regrettable incident. This oaf, this confederate of Marvel’s, was not alone. There were two of them, it turned out. The second, a smaller, weasely man, had a pistol. He was quite startled, I imagine, by the invisible force that was wrestling his companion to the ground. He fired, not at me, but blindly, in the direction of the struggle. He hit his own friend, I believe. The brute cried out, a dreadful sound, and dropped. I managed to disarm the second man, the weasel, and in the confusion, I fled. I recovered some of my notes, a fraction of the gold, but not all. The rest, along with my more delicate apparatus, remained in Marvel’s possession, or was lost in the ensuing panic. The police were summoned, the alarm was raised, and I was once again a hunted man. The wound in my arm, Kemp, is from that pistol shot. A mere graze, but it bled profusely, and the loss of blood, combined with the exertion, left me weak. I had to continue my flight, invisible, through the streets, dodging the constabulary, past the gawking crowds, who, of course, saw nothing but a commotion. I had to reach a place of safety, a place where I could recover, rethink, and plan my next move. And then, I remembered you, Kemp. Old Kemp, the only man of science I knew who might comprehend the magnitude of my achievement, who might offer a brief respite from this cursed existence.”

  Kemp was silent for a moment, absorbing the narrative. It was a story of desperate acts, certainly, but also of profound moral compromise. Griffin’s constant self-exoneration was chilling. Every act of violence, every theft, every betrayal, was framed as a necessary consequence of his unique predicament, never as a choice. “So, you arrived here, injured, exhausted, and bleeding, after a fight in Port Burdock, not many hours ago.”

  “Precisely. Three hours I mentioned, was it not? Three hours of excruciating pain, of flight, of hiding in ditches and behind hedges. Three hours since I found refuge in your study, quite by chance. The window was open. A fortuitous circumstance, indeed.”

  “Fortuitous for you, perhaps,” Kemp muttered, more to himself than to Griffin. He watched the dressing-gown again, as a faint movement indicated Griffin was settling himself more comfortably in the basket chair. The thought of this invisible, wounded, and evidently dangerous man having been in his house for hours, unnoticed, was deeply unsettling. The scientific marvel, the intellectual puzzle, was now overshadowed by a growing sense of personal threat. This was not a phenomenon to be observed from a safe distance; this was a man, a desperate and ruthless man, occupying his private space, demanding his sustenance, and now, no doubt, his assistance.

  “And now that I have explained everything, Kemp, now that you grasp the enormity of my situation, you must assist me. You are a man of intellect, a Fellow of the Royal Society, not some superstitious bumpkin. You can appreciate the scientific marvel, and you can understand the practical necessities.” Griffin’s voice was softer now, almost wheedling, a shift from his previous belligerence.

  Kemp, however, found himself growing colder, his initial shock giving way to a more pragmatic appraisal of the situation. “Assist you in what, precisely, Griffin? In recovering your gold? In avenging yourself on this Marvel character? In continuing your career of, shall we say, unconventional self-preservation?”

  The dressing-gown stiffened perceptibly. “Kemp! You wound me! Do you think I wish to continue this wretched existence of hiding, of scavenging? No! I have a plan, a magnificent plan, a plan that will elevate me, and perhaps humanity, to a new era of power and control. But I need resources. I need a base of operations. I need a confidant, a partner, a man of scientific understanding who can appreciate the profound implications of what I have achieved.”

  “A partner?” Kemp asked, a dry note in his voice. “One who is visible, I presume, and thus able to procure the ‘resources’ you require, and to face the ‘uncomprehending world’ you so despise.”

  “Exactly!” Griffin exclaimed, the subtle shift in his tone suggesting he believed he had made a significant breakthrough in Kemp’s understanding. “You perceive the genius of it! You, Kemp, with your connections, your established reputation, your freedom of movement. We could achieve anything! Imagine the power, the absolute, unassailable power, of a man who can move through the world unseen, unheard, untouchable, with a mind like yours to guide him!”

  Kemp felt a shiver run down his spine, but it was not entirely from fear. It was the chill of intellectual fascination battling with profound moral revulsion. He, a man of order, of societal norms, was being asked to become the enabler of a dangerous, perhaps psychopathic, genius. “Power, Griffin? To what end? What is this ‘magnificent plan’?”

  Griffin leaned forward, the basket chair groaning under the unseen weight. “To rule, Kemp! To establish a Reign of Terror! Think of it! A man, invisible, with the capacity to sow discord, to punish injustice, to take what is due, to enforce a new order! No more the petty squabbles of politicians, the corrupt machinations of finance, the absurd inefficiencies of government. We, Kemp, we could be the unseen hand that guides the destiny of nations! We could impose our will, our superior intellect, upon the common herd!”

  Kemp stared. The sheer megalomania of it, the chilling detachment from any ethical consideration, left him breathless. “You mean to be a dictator, Griffin? An invisible tyrant?”

  “A benevolent dictator, of course,” Griffin corrected, a touch of testiness in his voice. “One who knows what is best for humanity. One who possesses the means to enforce that knowledge. Think, Kemp! The possibilities are endless! We could infiltrate governments, expose corruption, redistribute wealth, eliminate our enemies with impunity. No one would ever know! They would see only the effects, the inevitable progression towards a more rational, more ordered society, guided by our unseen wisdom!”

  “And if someone resists your ‘benevolent’ guidance?” Kemp asked, his voice low.

  “Then they would be dealt with,” Griffin replied, the words hanging in the air with a terrible finality. “Swiftly. Invisibly. Without trace. Resistance would be futile, Kemp. Utterly futile.”

  Kemp rose from his chair, walking slowly to the window and peering out into the early morning light. The quiet street, the sleeping houses, represented the orderly world Griffin sought to dismantle. He felt a profound sense of responsibility, a sudden, crushing weight. He, Kemp, a respected man of science, was now privy to a secret that could shatter the very foundations of society. And the man who held that secret, who embodied that power, was a wounded, hungry, and morally unhinged individual sitting invisible in his living room.

  “This is madness, Griffin,” he said, turning back into the room. “Absolute madness. You speak of a Reign of Terror, of imposing your will. You are not a god, you are a man. A man who has become invisible, yes, but a man nonetheless, with all the frailties and corruptions of human nature. You speak of benevolence, but your actions, by your own admission, have been born of desperation and vengeance. You steal, you assault, you justify violence against those who merely resist your bizarre demands.”

You have not endured the indignities, the suffering, the utter loneliness of this existence! To be perpetually naked, perpetually cold, perpetually hungry, perpetually hunted! To be denied human contact, human recognition! To be a ghost, a phantom, yet with all the needs and passions of a living man! It changes one, Kemp. It warps one’s perspective. What you call madness, I call necessary adaptation. What you call crime, I call survival and the assertion of my rightful place!”

  The intensity of Griffin’s outburst made Kemp take a step back. The dressing-gown quivered with indignation, and Kemp imagined the invisible man’s face, contorted with rage. He knew he had to tread carefully. This man was dangerous, unpredictably so. His earlier, somewhat detached scientific curiosity was now entirely eclipsed by a visceral fear.

  “Very well, Griffin,” Kemp said, striving for a calm, rational tone he did not feel. “Let us put aside the grand schemes for a moment. You are wounded, you are tired, you are hungry. Let us address those immediate concerns. What do you need to recover?”

Uninterrupted, restorative sleep, which has been a stranger to me for weeks.”

  “That is where you come in, my friend,” Griffin said, a hint of his earlier wheedling tone returning. “You can procure them for me. Discreetly, of course. No one must suspect. You are a man of means, a man of reputation. A few purchases would raise no eyebrows. A new suit, ostensibly for yourself, but perhaps a size larger. Some medical supplies for a minor ailment.   Kemp considered this. It was a reasonable request, on the surface, given the extraordinary circumstances. But it was also the first step down a very dangerous path, a path that led to complicity. He would be aiding and abetting a man who openly admitted to theft and violence, and who harboured ambitions of global terror.

  “And in return?” Kemp asked, testing the waters.

  “In return, Kemp, you will be privy to the greatest scientific discovery in human history! You will be my trusted lieutenant, my intellectual equal, the one man who truly understands. His concern was for the integrity of his world, the safety of his society. He also felt a rising tide of indignation. Griffin spoke of him as a lieutenant, an aid, when Kemp knew, with a chilling certainty, that he would be nothing more than a tool, discarded once his utility had passed.

  “Your scientific training also demands a recognition of undeniable fact!” Griffin retorted, his voice rising again. “And the undeniable fact, Kemp, is that I am here, I am invisible, and I am in urgent need of your assistance.   “Very well, Griffin,” Kemp said, turning back to face the invisible man. “Let us assume, for a moment, that I accept your premise, that I am willing to entertain your ‘plan.’ Tell me more about this invisibility.   Kemp paced the room, his mind racing, connecting the dots. The perpetual nakedness, the cold, the hunger, the constant threat of discovery.   “You expect me to help you build an apparatus to reverse or control this condition, while you simultaneously plot to destabilize society with it?” Kemp asked, stopping to face the empty chair.

  “Why not?” Griffin challenged, his voice sharp. “The two are not mutually exclusive. Indeed, they are complementary! The power I wield now, the means to enforce my will, will give me the resources, the unhindered access, to complete my scientific work.   Kemp sank back into his own chair, feeling utterly overwhelmed. This was not a simple matter of aiding a friend in distress. This was a moral precipice. His duty as a citizen, as a man of conscience, screamed for him to reject Griffin’s insane propositions. What would happen if he refused Griffin? This man had demonstrated his willingness to use violence, his complete disregard for others. An invisible assailant in his own home. We deal in facts, in power, in the relentless pursuit of knowledge and its application. You are a scientist, Kemp. Do not deny your true nature. Do not let archaic morality cloud your judgment.   Kemp remained silent, staring at the floorboards, listening to the insistent, persuasive, utterly terrifying voice of the Invisible Man. The air in the room felt heavy, charged with an invisible tension. He was trapped, he realised, as surely as Griffin himself was trapped in his invisible state. He was trapped between his conscience and his fear, between the rational world and the unimaginable. The sun was now fully up, streaming through the window, illuminating the dust motes dancing in the air, the very light that passed unseen through the monstrous genius sitting before him. He had to make a choice, and he knew, with a sickening certainty, that either choice would lead him down a path from which there was no return. And you haven’t got the sense to offer a man in my condition a place to sit, or a proper meal, or even to listen with the common courtesy due to a man of science.”

Kemp, still holding the empty glass for his own drink, felt a prickle of indignation at the reprimand, but the sheer absurdity of the situation, the invisible presence, the disembodied voice, held his tongue. He had indeed fetched a glass, but his own thoughts were in such a ferment that the offer of a seat to an unseen visitor had quite escaped him. He placed the glass on the dressing-table, a spot where it might be seen, and then looked vaguely at the invisible occupant of his own armchair, which continued to creak faintly under the unacknowledged weight. He felt his head spinning, a sort of intellectual vertigo at the overturning of all known laws.

“A place to sit, you say,” Kemp murmured, more to himself than to the other. “And a proper meal. You’ve had cutlets, and whiskey, and a dressing gown. What more, in heaven’s name, does an invisible man require?”

“Company, Kemp, intelligent company, which you, for all your wit, seem loath to provide at this moment,” the Invisible Man retorted, a new note of injured pride entering his voice. “And understanding. Do you think this is a simple matter, a conjuring trick to amuse the idle rich? I tell you, Kemp, it is the most marvellous discovery of all time, the very key to power, and all you can do is gape and ask silly questions about bandages.”

Kemp flushed. “Silly questions? When I find a bandage floating in my room, and then a man I knew to be corporeal claiming he is now without substance, and when that same man then proceeds to throttle me and shove a sheet in my mouth, what, pray tell, am I to ask? About the weather?”

“That was a necessary preliminary, Kemp, a practical measure to ensure your attention,” said the Voice, with a tone that implied a great deal of self-justification. “You were behaving like a hysterical old woman. But now, if you are quite composed, or as composed as you are likely to be, I will tell you. But first, the drink. You fetched it, now pour it.”

Kemp hesitated. The idea of pouring a drink for an empty space, for a hand he could not see but knew was there, was profoundly unsettling. He reached for the decanter of whiskey, his fingers fumbling slightly. He poured a measure, and then, with a strange half-bow, held it out into the air before him.
