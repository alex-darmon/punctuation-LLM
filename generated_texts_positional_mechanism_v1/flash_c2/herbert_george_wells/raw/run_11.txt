What are the possibilities for a man, a truly intelligent man, possessed of such a gift? Kemp felt a cold sweat break out on his forehead. This was not the broken scientist appealing for help. This was something far more dangerous. He could only see the dark, shifting contours of an unimaginable future, and he was powerless to stop it. The clock on the mantelpiece chimed three times, a hollow, mournful sound in the suffocating silence. Dawn, and Griffin’s demands, would come soon enough. And with them, perhaps, the end of the world as Kemp knew it. He would have to decide, and the choice, he knew, would be between his conscience and his very survival. And Griffin, the invisible tyrant, would be watching him make it. The whiskey, strong as it was, did little to numb the icy dread that had settled deep within his bones. He was truly and utterly alone with this terrifying, impossible guest.

Kemp sat, or rather slumped, on the edge of his bed, the cold glass of whiskey still clutched in his hand, a chilling counterpoint to the heat that flared sporadically behind his eyes. The clock had long since finished its dismal chime; the silence that followed felt heavier, more oppressive, than any noise. It was a silence filled with the palpable presence of the Invisible Man, a silence punctuated only by the occasional clink of cutlery against porcelain – Griffin, no doubt, still devouring the cold cutlets with an insatiable hunger that seemed to extend beyond mere physical need. He closed his eyes tighter, pressing the rim of the glass into his forehead, but the images persisted, vivid and insistent.

His mind, usually a fortress of logic and scientific reason, was a chaotic battlefield. Every impulse screamed for action, for escape, for the immediate summoning of authority, but what authority, he asked himself, could possibly comprehend such a tale? “An Invisible Man.” The phrase, so recently dismissed as the stuff of cheap fiction and public house gossip, was now the terrifying, undeniable truth of his existence. He was trapped, utterly and hopelessly, in his own home, a prisoner to a phenomenon that defied every known law of nature, every principle he held dear. It was the hum that truly unnerved him, however, a low, satisfied vibrato that seemed to emanate from the very air itself. Kemp’s breath hitched. He had to think. What had Griffin said? “Dawn it is. But let there be no mistake, no foolish notions of resistance or escape.” And, “My eyes, though unseen, are everywhere within this room.” The threat was clear, implicit in the very air he breathed. But what could he do? He was a man of intellect, not of physical prowess. His training was in the meticulous dissection of theory, not in the brutal art of self-preservation against an invisible assailant. He pictured himself attempting to run, to shout, to struggle, and he could almost feel the unseen grip, the swift, terrifying retaliation. The memory of being tripped and flung onto the bed, the sheet thrust between his teeth, was fresh and nauseating. A sudden, sharp rap from the direction of the basket chair shattered the oppressive silence. It was the whiskey tumbler, Griffin’s empty glass, being set down with an aggressive finality.

I require more than cutlets. I require a full complement of clothing. These rags are hardly suitable for a man of my – our – impending stature.”

Kemp flinched, startled by the sudden intrusion into his tormented thoughts. He had forgotten, in his panic, that his every movement, every subtle shift of emotion, was likely under invisible scrutiny. He swallowed hard, the whiskey doing little to soothe his parched throat.

A low chuckle, a truly unpleasant sound, emanated from the chair. “Ah, Kemp, ever the pedant. Always a step behind, aren’t you? I implied no such thing. I am invisible, regardless of what I wear. It is the tissue of my body that is changed, not the fabric of my garments. Though I confess, I have grown accustomed to the liberation of nudity. A minor detail. Kemp blinked. “But—if you wear clothes, won’t they just float about, empty?”

“Precisely!” said the Voice, with a renewed, almost joyful, rap of the empty tumbler on the floor. “Imagine the effect, Kemp! A suit of clothes, walking about, speaking, directing! The perfect disguise! No one will dare question a man whose very presence is a defiance of reality. No one will dare lay a hand upon him, for fear of touching nothing, or worse, of being struck by nothing. The possibilities are endless, truly endless!” The hum returned, louder now, a buzzing undertone of manic excitement.

Kemp felt a fresh wave of despair wash over him. Griffin’s mind was not merely brilliant; it was profoundly, terrifyingly deluded. The vision of an empty suit of clothes stalking through the streets, issuing commands, was not one of authority but of grotesque absurdity, a nightmare made flesh, or rather, made fabric. And he, Kemp, was to be a part of this monstrous charade.

“Ah, a practical consideration!” Griffin conceded, a note of grudging approval in his voice. “A temporary measure, then. My current garments, what remains of them, are downstairs, along with my other effects. My bag. It contains my notes, Kemp. My invaluable notes. The culmination of years of relentless toil, of sacrifice, of—of unimaginable suffering!” The last words were delivered with a sudden, chilling intensity, a flicker of the immense pain and desperation that must have driven Griffin to his incredible feat. “I need those. Kemp stared, aghast. “Venture out? You mean into the town? At this hour? What possible reason—?”

I need to gauge the mood, the temper of the populace. My initial forays were... ill-conceived. They reacted with fear, with violence. Kemp’s mind raced, desperate for an opening, a glimmer of hope. This was it. This was his chance. If Griffin left the house, even briefly, it might be possible to alert someone, to raise the alarm. But how to do it without Griffin knowing? Without raising the Invisible Man’s suspicion? The man was clearly paranoid, capable of extreme violence.

“You can’t just walk into a shop, Griffin,” Kemp said, forcing a note of reasoned objection into his voice. “An empty suit of clothes will cause a panic. It’s an outrage. The police—they’ll be called immediately.”

“Rubbish, Kemp! A little discretion, a touch of ingenuity, and the matter is solved. Besides,” the Voice lowered, becoming conspiratorial, almost purring, “I have methods. Methods of persuasion. Methods of—discouragement. There are ways to acquire what one needs without undue fuss. But for now, the immediate task. My bag, downstairs. Retrieve it. And my clothes, such as they are. They are in the hall, near the door, a blood-stained bundle, most likely. Fetch them.”

Kemp hesitated. This was his house. Griffin was ordering him about as if he were a servant. The sheer audacity of it, coupled with the profound helplessness of his situation, stirred a spark of defiant anger within him, quickly extinguished by a cold splash of fear. He remembered Griffin’s grip, his strength, the ease with which he had been overpowered. This was not a negotiation. This was an order.

Slowly, carefully, Kemp rose from the bed. His legs felt stiff, his movements sluggish. He walked towards the door, his eyes scanning the empty room, trying to penetrate the invisible barrier between himself and his tormentor. He felt the weight of Griffin’s unseen gaze, a constant, chilling pressure.
“And be quick about it, Kemp,” the Voice added, a sharper edge now apparent. “Time is precious. The dawn is but a prelude to a new era. Our era.”

He descended the stairs, each step a deliberate, almost ritualistic act. His heart pounded in his chest, a frantic drumbeat against his ribs. Kemp’s fingers brushed against the cold, smooth wood of the umbrella stand, and then, a little lower down, encountered a coarse fabric. It was the bundle. To drop the bundle, to feign a stumble, to make a noise that might alert Mrs. Hall, the housekeeper, who might, by some miracle, be stirring. But the house was silent, wrapped in the profound quiet of the early morning, broken only by the faint creak of the floorboards under his weight. Griffin, he knew, would be listening.

Kemp’s breath hitched in his throat as he reached the landing, the stairs groaning a final protest beneath his weight. The door to his bedroom stood ajar, a rectangle of deeper shadow in the dim hallway. He pushed it open with his foot, the old wood scraping softly on the carpet, and stepped into the room. The air was heavy, charged with the inexplicable presence of the Invisible Man, a tangible pressure that settled upon Kemp’s skin like an icy film. He still held the bundle and the bag, the damp, metallic scent of the blood-stained linen filling his nostrils, a constant reminder of the grim reality. He cast a glance towards the bed, where he imagined Griffin to be sitting, though nothing but the crumpled sheets and the impression on the pillow hinted at a form. “Good,” said Griffin, the sound of the chair creaking as he presumably shifted his weight. “Now, draw the curtains. The light grows too strong. I have enough to contend with without the early morning sun making a spectacle of me.” Kemp crossed the room, his eyes still scanning, trying to pinpoint the source of the voice, to catch some fleeting distortion in the air, anything to give form to the formless. Kemp stood near the lamp, his hands clasped tightly behind his back, his shoulders hunched. “There,” Kemp said, rising slowly, his knees aching. “That should hold you for a while. You’ll need to keep weight off that ankle. Perhaps I can fashion some sort of crutch, or—”

“Later, later,” Griffin interrupted, dismissing the suggestion with an invisible wave of his hand, a movement which caused a faint ripple in the air. “Now, the sustenance. My strength ebbs. This conversation, this exertion, they drain me. I need food, Kemp. Substantial food. And then we talk. We truly talk.”

Kemp felt a weariness settle over him, both physical and mental. He had just dressed two wounds on a man he could not see, a man who, only hours before, he had ridiculed as a figment of village gossip. He walked to the door, opened it, and descended the stairs to the kitchen, his mind a jumble of scientific inquiry and personal dread. The sheer audacity of the situation, the impossible reality of it, hammered at his composure. He fumbled in the larder, bringing back cold meats, bread, cheese, and a jug of milk. He also remembered Griffin’s earlier demand for whiskey, and brought the bottle and a clean glass. He placed the items on the small table near the bed.

“Here you are, Griffin. All I have in the house at this late hour. Cold fare, I’m afraid.”

He watched as the chair creaked again, and then, with an almost imperceptible tremor in the air, the bottle of whiskey seemed to levitate, moving towards the chair. A gurgling sound followed as liquid was poured into a glass, which also appeared to float into existence, then settled into an unseen hand. A gulping sound followed, swift and decisive.

“Ah, that’s better,” Griffin’s voice resonated, a little stronger now. “The immediate physical demands attended to. Now, sit down, Kemp. We have much to discuss. And do not stare so fixedly at my dressing gown. It is merely a covering, not a portal to another dimension. Though, in a sense, it is. But not in the way your common mind interprets it.”

Kemp pulled up another chair, facing the empty dressing gown, and sat down. He found himself unable to tear his gaze from the scarlet robe, which seemed to possess a life of its own, rising and falling with unseen breath, occasionally shifting as if its wearer adjusted his position. He watched as a piece of cold meat, a slice of ham, detached itself from the plate, floated into the void, and vanished, followed by a sound of vigorous chewing.

“You asked about my predicament,” Griffin began, his voice now steadier, more deliberate, less tinged with the desperation of a wounded man. “You called it precarious. And indeed, it is. The world, Kemp, is a profoundly unaccommodating place for the unseen. The very air is a constant friction, every surface a potential betrayer. One cannot walk through a crowded street without being jostled, stumbled upon. One cannot eat in public without causing a sensation. One cannot sleep in peace without the risk of being discovered by some careless touch or sudden gust of wind. And that is merely the pedestrian aspect of it. The deeper, more insidious danger, lies in the human capacity for fear and avarice.”

He paused, a faint clinking sound indicating he was refilling his glass. “This ‘confederate’ of whom I spoke, that snivelling wretch, Thomas Marvel, was his name. A man of low intellect, but possessed of an avarice so profound it bordered on the sublime. I had, foolishly, taken him into my confidence, revealed the initial stages of my work, thinking to find in him a suitable, if rudimentary, assistant. I believed his greed would bind him to my service. I was wrong, Kemp. Utterly, tragically wrong. He saw only an opportunity for crude gain, not the sublime potential of my discovery.”

Another piece of ham vanished. “He was to be my intermediary, my visible agent in the world. To procure the necessary funds, the tools, the materials I required, without drawing undue attention to my, shall we say, unique situation. But the fool, the absolute, incorrigible fool, attempted to abscond with a portion of my funds. He fled, Kemp, fled like a cur, and in doing so, drew the attention of the local constabulary, and indeed, the entire ignorant populace of Iping. They, of course, suspected him of some unknown felony, and in their pursuit, stumbled upon me. The ensuing chaos, the shouting, the threats, the utterly senseless violence, resulted in my current unfortunate state.”

Griffin’s voice grew colder, a harsh edge entering his tone. “The imbeciles, they tried to corner me. They threw stones. They fired shotguns. Shotguns, Kemp! At a man who, by all rights, should have been utterly beyond their crude comprehension. And all because of the blundering idiocy of one contemptible wretch. My genius, my life’s work, reduced to a desperate scramble in a country inn, dodging the pellets of enraged rustics. It is an indignity, Kemp, an absolute outrage!”

Kemp listened, a cold knot forming in his stomach. The indignation in Griffin’s voice was palpable, but it was not the indignation of a wronged scientist. It was the furious resentment of a man who felt entitled to unchallenged supremacy, whose grand scheme had been disrupted by the common herd. This was not the Griffin he remembered, the brilliant but shy student. This was a man consumed by his own creation, a man whose morality seemed to have vanished along with his visibility.

“So you were shot at, by these villagers, after Marvel betrayed you,” Kemp summarised, trying to maintain a clinical detachment. “And you sustained these injuries. And now you have come to me. Why me, Griffin? Why not simply vanish, seek refuge elsewhere?”

“Vanish?” Griffin scoffed, a dry, rasping sound. “My dear Kemp, where does one vanish to, when one is already vanished? The world is not so large when one is perpetually hunted, constantly on the alert. And my condition, as I have explained, demands a constant replenishment of energy. I cannot simply subsist on air. I need resources, a place of safety, a trusted confidante. And you, Kemp, are that man. An old acquaintance, a fellow man of science, one whose intellect I can, to some degree, rely upon. You know me, or at least, you knew the man I was. And your house, remote as it is, offered a temporary sanctuary. A lucky chance, my stumbling upon your doorstep, wounded and starving.”

He shifted in the chair, and the dressing gown rustled again. “Besides, Kemp, I have a plan. A grand design. This invisibility, it is not merely a scientific curiosity, a parlour trick. It is a tool. A weapon. Imagine the possibilities, Kemp! Imagine the power! The capacity to move unseen, unheard, unhindered. To observe, to influence, to command. The world, my friend, is ripe for the taking. And with your assistance, your practical, grounded mind, we can achieve wonders beyond the comprehension of these simpletons.”

Kemp felt a chill that had nothing to do with the night air. “Wonders? Or horrors, Griffin? What exactly is this ‘grand design’? What do you intend to do with this power?” He leaned forward slightly, his gaze fixed on the empty space where Griffin sat. “You mentioned taking. Taking what? And from whom?”

Griffin laughed, a short, sharp bark that was devoid of humour. “Ah, the ethical questions. So typical of the conventional mind. But you, Kemp, with your keen intellect, you must see past such mundane considerations. This is beyond good and evil. This is the dawn of a new era. Imagine, Kemp, a world where one man, an invisible man, can shape events, can exert influence without accountability. The chaos I could unleash, or the order I could impose! It is ultimate freedom, ultimate power. And it requires but a few simple steps. The first, as I have said, is survival, and the second, organisation. I need to gather resources, to establish a base of operations, to secure my position. And for that, I need help. Your help, Kemp.”

“My help in what, precisely?” Kemp asked, his voice tight. “In some scheme of unseen manipulation? Some reign of terror, perhaps, with an invisible hand guiding the strings? Is that what you envision for your ‘new era’?”

“Terror?” Griffin repeated, a note of pique in his voice. “A strong word, Kemp. Unnecessary, perhaps. Or perhaps, entirely appropriate, for those who would oppose me. Consider the advantages, my friend. No lock can hold me, no guard can stop me, no secret is safe from my gaze. I can walk into any bank, any vault, any government office, any private chamber, and simply take what I desire. The world will be at my fingertips. And those who dare to resist, well, they will find themselves grappling with a force they cannot comprehend, a foe they cannot see. Imagine the fear that will grip them, the utter helplessness. They will bend, Kemp. They will all bend.”

The sheer arrogance, the chilling detachment in Griffin’s voice, sent a wave of nausea through Kemp. This was not merely an ambitious scientist; this was a megalomaniac, unhinged by his own discovery. The horror was not just the physical impossibility of Griffin, but the moral vacuum within him. He was a man utterly liberated from the constraints of society, not by a philosophical awakening, but by a physical transformation that allowed him to act with impunity.

“You speak of taking, of bending, of terror,” Kemp said, his voice now steadier, laced with a growing resolve. “This sounds like nothing short of anarchy, Griffin. Or despotism. You would use your invisibility to subjugate, not to enlighten.”

“Enlighten?” Griffin scoffed again, the unseen lips forming a sneer. “My dear Kemp, the world is not interested in enlightenment. It is interested in power. And I, by virtue of my genius, have discovered the ultimate form of it. Do not be so naive. My years of struggle, of poverty, of being ignored, ridiculed, and thwarted by the petty minds of lesser men, have taught me a profound lesson: the world respects only force. And now, I possess it in its purest, most unassailable form.”

He paused, and Kemp heard the distinct sound of the whiskey bottle being set down. “My plan, Kemp, is simple. First, I need to secure my position. I need a place where I can work, undisturbed, unseen. Your house, for the moment, serves that purpose. But it is merely a temporary haven. Second, I need resources. Funds, equipment, a library of scientific texts. And I need a visible agent, someone who can act in the world on my behalf, without drawing suspicion. Someone who can purchase what I need, procure what I lack. Someone like you, Kemp.”

Kemp felt a cold dread begin to solidify in his chest. “You mean for me to be your accomplice, Griffin? To aid you in your schemes of unseen robbery and, what else, exactly? Political manipulation? Covert assassinations? What monstrous path do you intend to tread?”

“Accomplice is a rather vulgar term,” Griffin retorted, a slight irritation in his tone. “I prefer to think of it as a partnership. A collaboration between two brilliant minds. You, with your practicality, your common sense, your established position in society. I, with my unparalleled discovery, my visionary insight. Together, Kemp, we could reshape the world. We could be the true architects of the coming age. Imagine the glory, the influence, the sheer, intoxicating power!”

Kemp stood up, slowly, deliberately. The room felt stifling, the air thick with Griffin’s chilling pronouncements. He looked at the empty dressing gown, at the floating bandages, at the hovering glass and the mysteriously diminishing food, and a terrible clarity settled upon him. This was not a scientific marvel to be studied and admired; this was a terrifying force to be contained.

“I am a man of science, Griffin,” Kemp said, his voice firm, though his heart pounded in his chest. “And a man of law. I cannot, and will not, be a party to such a monstrous scheme. Your vision, if I understand it correctly, is one of unbridled tyranny. And I cannot countenance it, not for any glory, any power.”

A sudden, sharp movement, a rustle of the dressing gown, indicated Griffin’s displeasure. The air around the chair seemed to vibrate with suppressed fury. “You cannot countenance it, Kemp? You, a mere medical doctor, would presume to judge the trajectory of a genius such as myself? You would stand in the way of progress, in the way of power, simply for the sake of some antiquated moral code?”

“Antiquated or not, it is my code,” Kemp retorted, taking a small step back, his hand instinctively reaching for the heavy brass poker by the fireplace. “And I find your intentions, Griffin, utterly repugnant. You have become a monster, not merely an invisible man. And monsters, for the good of humanity, must be stopped.”

There was a moment of absolute silence, a stillness so profound it seemed to suck the air from the room. Then, a low, menacing growl emanated from the empty dressing gown. “Stopped, Kemp? You speak of stopping me? You, a solitary, visible man, against the power of invisibility? Do you truly understand the implications of what you are saying?” The voice was no longer conversational; it was cold, deadly. “You believe you can oppose me? I, who can move amongst you unseen, unheard, untouchable? I, who can strike at will, with absolute impunity? You are a fool, Kemp. A deluded, self-righteous fool.”

A chill, far colder than any in the night air, gripped Kemp. He felt the imminence of danger, the palpable threat emanating from the unseen presence. He gripped the poker tighter, his knuckles white. His scientific mind, for all its rationality, now screamed an ancient, primal warning. He was alone, in his own house, with a power beyond his comprehension, a power that had just declared him an enemy. The full, terrifying reality of his predicament had finally dawned.

“I am not a fool, Griffin,” Kemp said, forcing a steadiness into his voice. “I am a man who understands the consequences of unchecked power. And I will not stand by and watch you unleash your brand of chaos upon the world.”

“Then you shall suffer the consequences of your defiance,” Griffin’s voice hissed, closer now, a faint, chilling breath on Kemp’s face. “You have chosen your path, Kemp. And it is a path that leads to your destruction. I offered you a partnership, a chance at true greatness. You chose instead to align yourself with the common, the mundane, the weak. So be it. You will be the first to taste the true, unyielding power of the Invisible Man. You will regret this moment, Kemp. You will regret it with every fibre of your being.”

A sudden, inexplicable gust of wind seemed to sweep through the room, though all the windows were closed. The gas lamp flickered wildly, casting dancing shadows that seemed to writhe and contort on the walls. Kemp’s heart hammered against his ribs. He knew, with a certainty that transcended all logic, that he had just signed his own death warrant. The conversation, the explanations, the scientific curiosity, all melted away, leaving only the stark, terrifying reality of a fight for his life, against an enemy he could not see, an enemy who was once his friend. The full gravity of his situation, the utter isolation and the monstrous nature of the force he faced, settled upon him with a crushing weight. He was trapped, and the horror had only just begun.

The gust of wind that had swept through the room was no mere draft. It was a tangible force, cold and malevolent, pressing against Kemp’s face, chilling his very blood. The flickering gas lamp, which had for a moment danced wildly, was suddenly extinguished, plunging the room into a suffocating darkness, broken only by the faint silver glow of the moon filtering through the windowpanes. Kemp, his heart a frantic drum against his ribs, stood utterly still, the heavy brass poker clutched in his trembling hands. The darkness amplified his other senses, and he heard, or perhaps felt, a subtle shifting of air, a displacement that was too deliberate, too close.

“Kemp,” the voice hissed again, closer still, a sound that seemed to originate from the very air around his ear. “You have made a grave error.”

A sudden, sharp blow struck his wrist, not with the force of a fist, but with a peculiar, jarring impact, as if something hard and bony had scraped against him. The poker, heavy as it was, was almost wrenched from his grip. He cried out, a guttural sound of surprise and pain, and instinctively swung the poker wildly in the direction of the sound, a desperate, futile gesture into empty space. The heavy brass struck nothing but air, whistling past his ear with a mournful sound.

Then came another blow, swift and merciless, against his side, just above the hip. He gasped, the wind knocked from his lungs, and stumbled backwards, colliding with the dressing-table with a resounding thud
