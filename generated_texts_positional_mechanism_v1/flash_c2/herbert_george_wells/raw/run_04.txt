I avoided the open, sunlit spaces, preferring the broken ground and tangled undergrowth that offered both concealment and a modicum of defence. My boots, though sturdy, made little sound upon the soft earth, a fortunate circumstance indeed, for I had no desire to announce my presence to either the listless Eloi, whose alarm, however fleeting, might attract more dangerous attention, or to the Morlocks themselves, whose subterranean network might extend closer to the surface than I dared to imagine.

The thought of the Time Machine, standing exposed and vulnerable, preyed upon my mind with an almost unbearable persistence. It was not merely a device, a marvel of engineering; it was my only tether to sanity, my sole hope of returning to a world where reason still held sway, where the very fabric of society had not yet unraveled into this ghastly dichotomy. I pictured it, a gleaming anachronism in the heart of this decaying future, a silent sentinel awaiting its master. Had the Morlocks, in their nightly forays, discovered it? Had their brutish curiosity, their crude intelligence, been sufficient to discern its nature, to tamper with its controls? The possibility was a cold dread in my stomach, a knot of apprehension that tightened with every hesitant step. For if the machine were damaged, or worse, irretrievably lost, then I, the Time Traveller, would be nought but a stranded anachronism myself, a living relic condemned to witness the final, ignominious chapter of humanity's descent.

I recalled the eloquent speculations of my own era, the grand pronouncements regarding humanity's inevitable progress, its mastery over nature, its ascent to ever-higher forms of civilisation. How hollow those words now rang in this desolate future! Here, the pinnacle of human achievement had culminated in a species divided against itself, the one half living a life of childlike ignorance and fear, the other dwelling in a perpetual night of bestial appetites. The intellectual, scientific, and moral triumphs of my age, which we held so dear, had merely served to accelerate this grim differentiation, to carve a deeper chasm between the exploiters and the exploited. The "economic problem," as I had so glibly termed it, had not been solved, but merely mutated, festered, and at last erupted into this biological schism. The comfortable assumption of a single, unified humanity, progressing hand in hand, was a cruel delusion, shattered irrevocably by the harsh realities I had now encountered.

As I advanced, the ruins grew larger, more frequent, and more fragmented. I began to recognise certain features, the distinctive curve of a colossal arch, the shattered remains of what might have been a dome, or the jagged, tooth-like silhouette of a once-proud tower. These were the landmarks I had observed during my initial days in this distant future, the very structures from which I had first surveyed the tranquil, deceiving beauty of the Eloi world. And then, at last, through a break in the trees, I saw it: the familiar, weathered grey of the White Sphinx, its features eroded by aeons, its enigmatic gaze fixed upon a horizon that had long forgotten its meaning. And beneath it, or rather, beside it, within the confines of that small, intricately carved building, was where I had left my hope, my escape.

A fresh wave of trepidation, cold and acute, washed over me. The pedestal building, so serene on my arrival, now seemed fraught with unseen perils, a potential trap. I remembered the heavy bronze doors, the peculiar lock, the sense of security I had felt in entrusting my machine to such a structure. But what was security to the Morlocks, who moved through the very fabric of the earth? My theory, now solidified into grim certainty, was that these buildings, these pedestals, these "sphinxes," were not merely decorative elements but rather the entrances to the deeper Morlock tunnels, perhaps even the ventilating shafts for their subterranean realm. The bronze gates, then, were not for keeping things out, but for keeping things in, or rather, for regulating the passage between their world and the surface. The thought chilled me to the bone.

I crept closer, my movements slow and deliberate, my senses acutely tuned to every whisper of the wind, every rustle of the leaves. The path was overgrown, the paving stones beneath my feet broken and uneven, testament to the long centuries of neglect. The air grew heavier here, perhaps a localised effect of the decaying structures, or perhaps, a more sinister emanation from the earth beneath. A faint, almost imperceptible odour, not unlike that of damp earth mingled with something faintly animalistic, seemed to cling to the air, a scent that prickled the hairs on my neck with an almost primal warning. This, I thought, was the true smell of the future, not the perfume of the Eloi’s flowers, but the musky breath of the abyss.

Reaching the edge of the clearing, I paused, concealed by a thicket of what appeared to be some ancient, resilient species of rhododendron, their blossoms a dull, purplish hue. Not an Eloi in sight, no sign of their playful activities, their aimless wanderings. The silence was absolute, a heavy, suffocating blanket that pressed down upon the landscape. This absence, this unnatural stillness, was far more unsettling than any presence, for it bespoke a profound, disquieting emptiness, a vacuum that might at any moment be filled by a horror.

My gaze fell upon the bronze doors of the pedestal building. They were ajar. Not wide open, not flung apart in disarray, but slightly, unmistakably ajar, creating a narrow, vertical slit of blackness that seemed to gape like a hungry mouth. A shiver ran down my spine, despite the relative warmth of the afternoon. This was not the work of the Eloi, of that I was certain. Their strength was insufficient, their inclination for such an act utterly absent. No, this spoke of a different presence, a different agency. The Morlocks. They had been here. They had breached the sanctuary.

The Time Machine awaited, or rather, its fate awaited me. I took a deep breath, clenched my jaw, and moved forward, crossing the clearing with a new, grim determination. Each step was a defiance, a challenge to the unseen masters of this benighted age.

The gap was wide enough for a slender body to slip through, perhaps even for one of the Morlocks, with their peculiar gait and flexible forms. I peered into the blackness, but it revealed nothing save an impenetrable void, a continuation of the underground world from which I had so recently emerged. The air that issued from the opening was cool, carrying that same faint, earthy, animalistic scent, confirming my fears.
