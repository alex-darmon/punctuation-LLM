“Stronger?” I echoed, a bitter laugh escaping me. “Do you imagine, Mrs. Reed, that I wished for strength born of such desolation? That I desired a path strewn with hardship and the constant struggle against the world’s indifference, when a simpler, kinder fate might have been mine? You confuse stoicism with contentment. You mistake the endurance of suffering for the embracing of it.”

Her eyelids fluttered, her gaze shifting slightly, as if the effort of maintaining her defiance was becoming too great. The colour, what little remained, seemed to drain further from her face, leaving it a stark, yellowish white against the dark pillow. “It was necessary,” she murmured, her voice growing fainter, barely audible. “Necessary for my peace. Necessary to sever the last link. He was gone; she was gone. You were a remnant, an unwanted shadow. I did what was required.”

“Required by whom, Mrs. Reed?” I pressed, leaning even closer, determined to extract every confession, every dark truth from her before the veil of silence fell forever. “By your pride? By your resentment? By the narrow confines of your own bitter heart?”

She did not answer immediately. Her eyes closed, and for a moment, I feared she had passed beyond the realm of human inquiry. But then, with a profound sigh, she opened them again, and in their depths, I saw something that startled me: not contrition, not remorse, but a deep, abiding weariness, an exhaustion that transcended physical illness. It was the weariness of a spirit that had fought its battles, won its skirmishes, and was now utterly spent, with no energy left for further conflict.

“All of them,” she whispered, the words barely breathed. “All of them, Jane. They demanded it. My heart, my peace, my understanding of what was right and just. He adored your mother. He showered her with affection, with gifts, with a devotion he never showed to me, his wife. He risked his family’s honour for her, countenancing her low marriage, her disgraceful independence. And when she died, he mourned her with a grief he never evinced for any other. He spoke of her with a tenderness that lacerated my very soul. And then, he brought you, her child, into my home, a constant, living reminder of his misplaced affection, of my own diminished standing. You, with her eyes, her stubbornness, her very spirit. How could I bear it? How could I tolerate such a perpetual affront to my senses, my dignity, my rightful place?”

Her voice trailed off, lost in a fit of coughing, a dry, rattling sound that seemed to tear at her frail frame. I watched her, transfixed, the cold rage within me momentarily suspended by the raw, almost desperate honesty of her confession. It was not merely prejudice, then, nor even solely malice, but a complex, twisted skein of envy, wounded pride, and a profound sense of injustice that had driven her actions. A wife’s resentment, nursed and festered over years, had found its outlet in the innocent child who bore the physical and spiritual imprint of her rival. It was a tragedy of human passion, dark and destructive, and I, the child, had been its innocent victim.

When the paroxysm subsided, she lay back, utterly spent, her face shining with a cold sweat. I offered her a sip of water from a glass on the nightstand, but she waved it away with a feeble hand.

“There,” she said, her voice a mere thread. “It is out. All of it. Now, Jane. Do you still wish to forgive?”

The question hung in the air, a challenge, a final test of my spirit. To forgive such a litany of wrongs, such a deliberate and prolonged cruelty, seemed a superhuman feat. Yet, looking at her, seeing the stark reality of her imminent dissolution, the utter futility of her triumphs, I felt the rage, the indignation, begin to subside, replaced by that earlier, softer sentiment: ruth. Not for her crimes, but for her wretchedness. For the barrenness of a heart that had found no joy save in the infliction of pain, no peace save in the nursing of resentment. What a terrible, desolate existence it must have been.

“I cannot say that I forgive the wrongs, Mrs. Reed,” I replied, choosing my words carefully, for even in this final hour, truth was paramount. “Those were grievous, and their consequences have shaped the entirety of my life. But I can say that I pity you. I pity the torment that must have consumed you for so long, that led you to such cruelties. And seeing you thus, on the threshold of eternity, I can feel no triumph in your suffering, nor any desire to prolong your agony with my own bitterness. I would wish you peace, if peace is to be found for such a spirit.”

Her eyes, still open, regarded me with a long, unblinking stare. There was no softening, no melting, but perhaps a fleeting shadow of comprehension, of a dim understanding of the vast chasm between our two natures. She made no reply. Her gaze seemed to wander, to lose its focus, her breath becoming shallower, more irregular.

The door opened softly, and Bessie peered in, her face etched with concern. “Missis, the doctor is here again. Shall I send him in?”

Mrs. Reed stirred faintly, a restless movement. “No,” she breathed, her voice so weak it was almost lost. “No. It is too late. There is nothing more he can do.”

Bessie hesitated, then looked at me, a silent question in her eyes. I shook my head almost imperceptibly, indicating that her mistress was beyond the aid of earthly physicians. Bessie withdrew, closing the door as quietly as she had opened it.

The silence that followed was profound, broken only by the clock ticking on the mantelpiece, marking the swift passage of these last moments. I remained by the bed, watching, a strange sentinel at the gateway of death. The presence in the room had changed. The formidable, oppressive will that had animated the body now lay inert, powerless. There was a profound quietude, a release, as if the very atmosphere, once charged with resentment, had been purged. I closed her eyes, gently pressing down the lids that now offered no resistance. Her face, in death, seemed strangely altered, smoothed by the absence of that characteristic frown, that imperious expression. It was the face of a woman who had lived a life of struggle, of thwarted desires, of bitter pride, and who had at last found her ultimate rest.

My mission, then, was accomplished. I had come, I had seen, and I had heard. The truth, however painful, however late in its revelation, was mine. It was a heavy burden, but also a strange liberation. The phantom of my past, the mystery of my abandonment, was finally laid bare.

I rose from the chair, a stiffness in my limbs, and moved away from the bed. The impulse to remain was gone. There was nothing more to be done, nothing more to be said. I left the room, closing the door softly behind me, stepping out into the hushed landing. Bessie was waiting, her face anxious.

“She is gone, Bessie,” I informed her quietly.

Bessie’s hands flew to her mouth, a stifled cry escaping her. Tears welled in her eyes, and she wrung her hands. “Poor missis! God rest her soul! She suffered so much, Miss Jane, these last days. We all knew it was coming, but it’s always a shock, isn’t it, when it finally comes?”

I nodded, though I knew Bessie’s sympathy for her mistress was rooted in a very different experience than my own. She had known Mrs. Reed as a mistress, an employer, a figure of authority, but not as the instrument of a child’s sustained suffering.

“Will you inform the others?” I asked, gesturing vaguely towards the rest of the house.

“Oh, yes, Miss Jane! Immediately. Poor Mr. John, and the young ladies. They will be so distressed.” She hurried away, her footsteps echoing faintly down the corridor.

I stood there for a moment longer, contemplating the closed door, the silence within. Then, I turned and walked towards my own chamber. The household, which had seemed so indifferent, so self-absorbed only hours before, would now be plunged into the solemn rituals of mourning. My presence, which had been met with such coldness, would now assume a different significance. I was not merely a guest; I was a witness, and perhaps, now, a part of the family in a way I had never been before, however reluctantly.
