He knew that Dr. Vaneyke, Barney, and Pomp would be scanning the darkness, their eyes trained on his progress, their hands ready to unleash the ice ship’s formidable power should he signal for aid.

The cleft opened onto a narrow shelf of ice, a frozen path that ran along the shore. To his left, the Red Eric loomed, a dark, silent hulk resting low in the water. Its masts, stripped bare of canvas, pointed like skeletal fingers at the star-studded sky, and its hull, encrusted with years of ice and grime, presented a formidable barrier. The open water around the whaler was surprisingly still, reflecting the glint of distant stars, a stark contrast to the rough ice under his feet. The very air seemed to hold its breath, pregnant with the unspoken threat of what lay within the ship.

Frank moved with the cautious tread of a hunter, his senses acutely aware of every sound, every shadow. He kept close to the

shore of the bay, where the ice met the dark, still water, and where the hulking form of the Red Eric rested, as if asleep. He advanced cautiously along the edge of the frozen stretch, his gaze fixed upon the whaler. The open water around the ship was an irregular oval, a dark mirror reflecting the Arctic stars, and the Red Eric, low in this liquid cradle, presented a formidable barrier of tarred timber and iron-bound hull. A single hawser, thick and frayed, led from her bow to an ice-anchor firmly set in the frozen shore, and a second, equally stout, secured her stern. There was no gangplank, no ladder, nothing to suggest easy access.

Frank cursed silently. Ben Bolt was clearly taking no chances. He skirted the open water, keeping to the shadows cast by the ice cliffs, until he reached a point abreast of the whaler’s stern. Here, a heavy, iron-runged ladder, coated with rime and ice, hung precariously from the stern railing, its lower rungs disappearing into the water. It was the only way aboard. Frank tested his weight on the lowest visible rung, and finding it surprisingly stable, began his ascent. The cold iron bit into his gloved hands, and the crunch of his boots on the rime-covered steps sounded unnaturally loud in the vast stillness. He moved with the practiced ease of one accustomed to danger, his body pressed close to the frigid hull, his pneumatic pistols held ready beneath his parka.

Reaching the deck, he paused, crouching low behind a stack of coiled ropes, and surveyed his surroundings. The deck was a desolate expanse, littered with harpoons, blubber-hooks, and the general detritus of a working whaler, all frozen solid under a thin layer of snow. The air was still, heavy with the stench of whale oil and stale brine. No watchman was visible; indeed, there was no sign of life whatsoever. It was as if the crew had abandoned the ship, leaving it to the silent embrace of the Arctic night. Yet, Ben Bolt was not one to abandon his prizes, nor his prisoners. Frank crept forward, his eyes scanning every shadow, every hatchway.

He made his way to the main hatch, a heavy wooden affair, secured by a stout iron bar. With a grunt, he lifted the bar and swung open the hatch, revealing a dark, yawning abyss below. A cold, damp draft rose from the depths, carrying with it the faint scent of human habitation, a mix of cooking odors, stale tobacco smoke, and unwashed bodies. Frank drew one of his pneumatic pistols, its silent discharge a boon in such an undertaking, and descended into the gloom. The ladder was slick with moisture, but he made his way down without a sound, landing softly on the deck below.

He was in the main cargo hold, a vast space, dimly lit by a single, flickering lantern hanging precariously from a beam. The air was thick with the oppressive smell of a ship that had not been aired in weeks, and the cold was bone-deep. Along the bulkheads, a series of crude bunks were built, some still containing a tangle of bedding, others empty and bare. This was clearly the crew’s quarters. No one was present, however, and Frank’s brow furrowed in a puzzle. Where was Ben Bolt’s crew? Did the Red Eric travel with a skeleton crew, or had they all gone ashore?

Moving with the utmost stealth, Frank advanced through the deserted quarters, his senses alert. He found the galley, cold and empty, a half-eaten meal congealed on a table. He found the captain’s cabin, surprisingly opulent for a whaler, but equally deserted, its logbook lying open on a desk, its instruments covered with dust cloths. It was unsettling, this absence of life. It felt like a trap, carefully set, waiting for him. But his mission was clear: find Walter Grey.

He descended to the lower deck, where the ship’s stores and prisoners, if any, would be kept. The silence here was even more profound, broken only by the creaking of the ship’s timbers and the gentle lapping of water against the hull. He passed rows of barrels, sacks of provisions, and stacks of crates, until he came to a small, iron-bound door set into a thick bulkhead. This door, unlike the others, was secured by a heavy padlock, glinting dully in the weak light filtering from the deck above. This was it.

Frank knelt, examining the padlock. It was an old, sturdy mechanism, but Frank, always prepared, drew a set of slim, steel picks from a hidden pocket in his parka. His fingers, deft and accustomed to delicate work, probed the lock’s inner workings. A few moments passed, punctuated only by the faint clicks and scrapes of the picks against metal. Then, with a soft thud, the tumbler yielded, and the padlock sprang open. He carefully removed it, placed it on the deck, and slowly, cautiously, swung open the heavy door.

The air that emerged from the dark cell was even fouler, a mixture of despair and confinement. Frank peered into the darkness. A small figure was huddled in a corner, wrapped in a threadbare blanket, seemingly asleep. “Walter?” Frank whispered, his voice barely audible.

The figure stirred, then slowly raised its head. Two wide, frightened eyes, belonging to a boy of about ten years, stared back at him. It was Walter Grey, pale and gaunt, but unmistakably himself. His face was streaked with grime, and his clothes were torn, but a spark of intelligence, of hope, flickered in his weary gaze.

“Who… who are you?” Walter croaked, his voice raw from disuse.

“I am Frank,” he answered, stepping fully into the cell, closing the door softly behind him. “I’ve come to rescue you. Your friends sent me.”

A sudden, overwhelming sob racked the boy’s small frame. He tried to speak, but only choked noises emerged. Frank knelt beside him, placing a reassuring hand on his shoulder. “It’s all right, Walter. You’re safe now. We’re going to get you out of here.”

“Ben Bolt,” Walter whispered, finally finding his voice, though it trembled violently. “He… he’s going to sell me to the Eskimos. He told me. He said my father wouldn’t pay the ransom.”

“Ben Bolt is a liar,” Frank said firmly. “Your father’s lawyer is in prison, and your stepmother, Mrs. Grey, is in possession of his fortune. We are here to see that you get what is rightfully yours.” He paused, looking around the small, miserable cell. “But where is Ben Bolt? And his crew? This ship seems deserted.”

Walter shuddered. “They went to shore. To hunt seals, he said. They left me here. They left me for days, with only a bit of bread and water.”

Frank’s jaw tightened. The callousness of the man was sickening. “How long ago did they leave?”

“Yesterday morning, I think. It’s hard to tell the time in here.”

This was unexpected, and it presented both an opportunity and a new danger. An opportunity to get Walter out unmolested, but a danger that Ben Bolt and his men could return at any moment. Frank quickly explained his plan to Walter, keeping his voice low and calm. “We must move swiftly and silently. Can you walk?”

Walter nodded, pushing himself up, albeit with difficulty. His legs felt weak and stiff from prolonged confinement. Frank helped him to his feet, and the boy leaned heavily upon him. Together, they made their way out of the cell and back towards the main deck, Frank’s pistol still clutched in his hand.

They climbed the ladder to the upper deck, the cold air a welcome, if biting, change from the foul atmosphere below. Frank glanced towards the ice cliffs, trying to spot the Ranger, but the ship was well-hidden. He knew his companions would be watching, but signaling now would alert anyone on shore. They had to reach the ice first.

As they neared the stern ladder, a sudden, sharp crack rent the air, followed by a gruff shout. “What’s this? A rat caught in my trap, eh?”

Frank spun around, pushing Walter behind him. Standing by the main hatch, silhouetted against the dim starlight, was a hulking figure, a man of immense proportions, with a grizzled beard and a cruel glint in his eye. It was Ben Bolt. He held a massive blunderbuss, its barrel gleaming ominously, pointed directly at Frank. Behind him, two other rough-looking seamen emerged, armed with whaling knives.

“Ben Bolt,” Frank stated, his voice calm, though his heart hammered against his ribs. “I presume you are Captain Bolt.”

“Aye, that I am, and you, my fine fellow, have walked right into my parlor,” Ben Bolt snarled, a sneer twisting his coarse features. “Thought you could just waltz in and steal my prize, did you? Fool! I knew someone would come sniffing around for the boy. Left a few men to keep watch, I did. My crew’s out hunting, but these lads are enough for you.” He gestured with the blunderbuss. “Now, drop that toy pistol, and hand over the boy.”

“I’m not dropping anything,” Frank retorted, his hand tightening on his pneumatic pistol. “And Walter Grey is coming with me.”

“He is, is he? We’ll see about that!” With a roar, Ben Bolt raised his blunderbuss, clearly intending to fire. Frank, quick as a flash, leveled his pistol and fired first. A soft hiss, and a small, needle-like projectile zipped through the air, striking Ben Bolt squarely in the arm that held the weapon. The captain cried out, not in pain, but in surprise, his hand going numb, and the blunderbuss clattered to the deck.

The two seamen charged forward, their knives glinting. Frank fired his second pistol, striking one in the leg. The man stumbled, howling, but the other, a wiry fellow, pressed his attack. Frank, with a swift movement, drew his knife and met the seaman’s blade with a clash of steel. The man was unskilled, driven by brute force rather than technique, and Frank, trained in various forms of self-defense, easily parried his wild thrusts. After a few rapid exchanges, Frank disarmed him with a flick of his wrist, sending the knife spinning onto the deck, and then, with a sharp blow to the jaw, knocked him unconscious.

Ben Bolt, meanwhile, had recovered from the numbing shock of the pneumatic dart and was scrambling for his blunderbuss. Walter, seeing his opportunity, darted forward, snatched the weapon, and threw it overboard with surprising strength. It splashed into the dark water, sinking from sight.

“Why, you little devil!” Ben Bolt bellowed, his face contorted with rage. He lunged at Walter, his massive hands reaching for the boy’s throat. Frank, however, was quicker. He seized Ben Bolt from behind, locking him in a powerful hold. The captain struggled, thrashing like a harpooned whale, but Frank’s grip was like iron.

“Get to the ladder, Walter! Go!” Frank urged, straining against the captain’s immense strength.

Walter, tears streaming down his face but with a newfound determination, scrambled towards the stern ladder. Just as he reached it, the first seaman, recovering from Frank’s dart, staggered to his feet, grabbing a loose harpoon.

At that moment, a blinding flash erupted from the ice cliffs, followed by a whistling sound, and then a heavy thud on the deck. A grappling hook, attached to a thick rope, embedded itself in the Red Eric’s mainmast, pulled by an unseen force. Then, the rhythmic thrum of the Ranger’s gyroscopes echoed across the bay, growing louder by the second.

“Hold on, Frank! We’re coming in!” Vaneyke’s voice, amplified by a speaking trumpet, boomed across the water.

Ben Bolt, momentarily distracted by the sudden arrival of the ice ship, lessened his struggle. Frank took advantage, slamming the captain against the bulkhead, stunning him. “Now, Walter!” he shouted, grabbing the rope that now stretched from the Ranger to the Red Eric. “Climb! Get to the Ranger!”

Walter, with adrenaline surging through him, quickly scaled the rope, reaching the deck of the Ranger just as Barney and Pomp rushed forward to help him aboard.

“Well, bless me sowl, the lad’s safe!” Barney cried, patting Walter roughly on the back. “And what about Frank, docthor?”

“He’s still engaged with the villain, Barney! See, he’s coming now!” Vaneyke exclaimed, pointing.

Frank, having secured Walter’s escape, released the now groggy Ben Bolt, and with a swift kick, sent him sprawling to the deck. He then clambered onto the rope, beginning his ascent. The wounded seaman, however, recovered his senses and lunged at the rope, intending to cut it with his knife.

But before he could reach it, a deafening report echoed from the Ranger, and a powerful stream of water, shot from one of the ice ship’s main cannons, struck the seaman squarely, knocking him off his feet and sending him sliding across the icy deck. Pomp, manning the water cannon with a grin, yelled, “Dar! Dat’ll teach yo’ to mess wid Cap’n Frank!”

Frank reached the Ranger’s deck, pulling the rope up behind him. He quickly checked on Walter, who, though still shaken, was clearly unharmed. “Well done, Walter! You saved us a lot of trouble by getting rid of that blunderbuss.”

“And you, Frank, performed admirably,” Dr. Vaneyke added, his expression one of proud relief. “But what now? We have the boy, but Ben Bolt is still aboard his ship.”

“He will not pursue us immediately,” Frank predicted, glancing back at the Red Eric, where Ben Bolt and his men were slowly getting to their feet. “His men are injured, and his ship is not ready for a chase. We have a head start.”

“Indeed,” Vaneyke agreed. “But we cannot remain here. He knows where we are. We must make haste for our rendezvous point.”

“Faix, an’ a grand fight it was, all the same,” Barney declared, a wide grin splitting his face. “Sure, Ben Bolt won’t be forgetting this day in a hurry, will he?”

“No, Barney, he certainly will not,” Frank said, a grim line settling around his mouth. “And he will likely seek revenge. We must be prepared.”

As the Ranger pivoted on its gyroscopes, ready to depart, a furious Ben Bolt staggered to the rail of the Red Eric, shaking his fist and roaring curses into the night. His words were lost to the wind and the powerful hum of the Ranger’s engines, but his intent was clear.

“That man holds a grudge like no other,” Vaneyke mused, watching the captain’s impotent rage. “We must put as much distance between us and him as possible.”

“And quickly too,” Frank added. “Nordenskjold Bay is not as deserted as it seems, if Bolt has chosen it for his hideout. There may be others, sympathetic to his cause, or simply opportunists. We need to be away from here and back to the true wilderness.”

The Ranger, a marvel of polar engineering, began to pick up speed, gliding silently over the ice, leaving the Red Eric and its irate captain behind. Walter, still clinging to Frank’s side, watched the receding whaler with a mixture of fear and relief. He was safe, for now.

Their journey northward continued, though now with a renewed sense of urgency. The vast, desolate landscape, once merely a backdrop to their quest, now seemed to hold hidden dangers at every turn. Frank and his companions knew that Ben Bolt was not one to easily give up, and his pursuit would be relentless. They had won a battle, but the war for Walter Grey’s safety was far from over.

The ice ship sped through the night, its powerful searchlights cutting swathes through the gloom, revealing the stark, magnificent beauty of the Arctic. They skirted immense glaciers, whose frozen peaks pierced the starlit sky, and navigated through fields of pressure ridges, where the ice groaned and fractured under the immense strains of the shifting polar cap. Barney and Pomp, ever vigilant, kept watch from the turret, their eyes scanning the horizon for any sign of pursuit.

“Dat Ben Bolt, he be a bad man, Cap’n Frank,” Pomp observed, his voice tinged with the gravity of their recent encounter. “He ain’t gonna let dis go. Not him.”

“You’re right, Pomp,” Frank acknowledged. “He’ll be on our trail as soon as he can muster his strength. We must be faster, and smarter.”

Dr. Vaneyke, consulting his charts, pointed to a series of remote islands further north. “There is an old observation post, long abandoned, deep within the archipelago. It’s well-hidden, and the currents around it are treacherous. If we can reach it, we might be able to lose him for a time, or at least gain some respite.”

“A good plan, Doctor,” Frank agreed. “We will make for the observation post. Set our course, and push the Ranger to her limits.”

The ice ship, obedient to its captain’s command, surged forward, its powerful engines throbbing, its steel runners slicing through the ice with a low, resonant hum. The air grew colder, and the landscape became even more barren, a monochrome world of white and gray under the perpetual twilight of the Arctic night. Walter, recovering from his ordeal, was given warm clothes and a hot meal, and though still quiet, the spark in his eyes had returned, replaced by a cautious optimism.

As the hours passed, Frank found himself pondering Ben Bolt’s strange behavior. Why had the ship been so deserted, and why had Bolt left Walter so poorly guarded, expecting him to be found? It was almost as if he desired a confrontation, or perhaps, he had underestimated Frank and his companions, believing his remote location was sufficient protection. Frank suspected the latter; Bolt was a brute, not a master strategist.

Suddenly, a cry from the turret shattered the relative calm. “Cap’n Frank! Look! Somethin’ comin’ up fast behind us!” It was Pomp, his voice laced with alarm.

Frank rushed to the observation deck, Dr. Vaneyke and Barney close behind. Through the powerful spyglass, he scanned the vast, icy expanse in their wake. In the distance, a dark smudge was rapidly growing larger against the white. It was the Red Eric.

“He’s found us,” Frank said, his voice grim. “And he’s moving faster than I expected. That old whaler has more speed than I gave her credit for.”

“The devil take him!” Barney exclaimed, pounding his fist on the railing. “He must have more men, or else he’s driving that old tub like a madman!”

“Both, most likely,” Vaneyke mused. “Ben Bolt is not one to let an injury deter him. He thrives on malice.”

“We must increase our speed, Doctor,” Frank ordered. “Bring us to maximum, now! And prepare the stern cannon, Pomp.”

Pomp, eager for action, disappeared into the turret. The Ranger’s engines roared, and the ice ship surged forward, its speed increasing by the second. The gap between them and the pursuing Red Eric slowly began to widen, but Ben Bolt’s whaler, surprisingly nimble for its size, held its course with grim determination, a dark predator on their tail.

“He means to ram us, or board us,” Frank deduced, watching the Red Eric through the spyglass. “He’s not interested in a distant cannon fight. He wants revenge, up close and personal.”

“Then we must not allow him the chance!” Dr. Vaneyke declared. “Barney, assist Pomp with the cannon. We will give him a taste of our resolve!”

Barney, delighted by the prospect of another skirmish, saluted smartly and hurried to the turret. Frank remained at the helm, his hand steady on the controls, guiding the Ranger through the treacherous ice, knowing that the chase had only just begun, and the Arctic, with all its hidden perils, was now their battleground.

The Ranger, thus unleashed, hurled itself across the frozen expanse with a speed that defied the imagination of any mariner accustomed to the plodding pace of conventional vessels. Its mighty engines, working at their very utmost, sent a deep, resonant thrumming through the very hull, a sound that spoke of immense power barely contained. The steel runners, polished to an uncanny sheen, sliced through the crystal ice, carving twin furrows that streamed behind them like lines of molten silver. The Arctic air, already keen and biting, rushed past the observation deck in a frigid gale, yet Frank, his gaze fixed resolutely forward, felt only a burning determination. Beside him, Dr. Vaneyke gripped the railing, his spectacles catching the pale, diffuse light, his brow furrowed in a mixture of apprehension and scientific fascination.

“A remarkable turn of speed, Frank,” the Doctor remarked, his voice just audible above the roar of the ship. “The Ranger certainly lives up to her name.”

“She will need to, Doctor,” Frank replied, his eyes narrowing as he glanced back through the spyglass. “He is still there. The Red Eric, for all her age and common design, is making an unholy haste. Bolt must have every man at the pumps and every sail drawing, even if the canvas be stripped.”

Indeed, the dark smudge on the horizon, though receding slowly, remained a persistent threat. The Red Eric, a veritable ghost ship of the Arctic, seemed to possess a demonic tenacity, refusing to be outdistanced. Smoke billowed from her single stack, thick and black against the icy sky, a testament to the furious stoking of her furnaces.

“He is a tenacious devil, that Bolt,” Barney declared, joining them, his face grim. “Sure, he’s got the rage of a thousand banshees in his heart, he does. But we’ll shake him yet, we will.”

“We must, Barney,” Frank affirmed. “For Walter’s sake, and for our own. Pomp, is the stern cannon ready?”

A voice, muffled by the turret’s thick walls but vibrant with eagerness, drifted up to them. “Aye, Cap’n Frank! She’s loaded an’ sighted, ready to give dat ole whaler a taste of de Ranger’s thunder!”

“Good, Pomp. A few warning shots, perhaps, to remind him of our capabilities. Aim for the ice ahead of his bow, if you can. We do not wish to sink him outright, merely to discourage his ardour.”

“Will do, Cap’n! A little splashin’ won’t hurt nobody, dat’s fo’ sure!”

Moments later, a deafening report shattered the relative silence of the Arctic night. A thick cloud of acrid smoke momentarily obscured the stern, and a dark projectile, wreathed in fiery vapor, hurtled across the vast distance. The air vibrated with the concussion, and for an instant, all eyes were fixed on the Red Eric. A geyser of ice and water erupted some fifty yards ahead of the pursuing whaler’s bow, a violent, spectacular display.

“A near thing, Pomp!” Frank shouted, a grin momentarily breaking through his stern composure. “Excellent marksmanship!”

The Red Eric, for its part, swerved violently to port, narrowly avoiding the spray and the shattered ice, her crew visible on deck, a flurry of hurried movement. A second shot followed, then a third, each sending up its own magnificent cascade of ice. The chase had transformed into an artillery duel across the frozen wastes, a spectacle never before witnessed in those desolate latitudes.

Ben Bolt, however, was not easily deterred. His ship, despite its cumbersome nature, continued its relentless pursuit, adjusting its course to avoid the Ranger’s bombardment. Then, to their surprise, a flash of orange light erupted from the Red Eric’s foredeck, followed by a dull thud.

“He has a gun!” Dr. Vaneyke exclaimed, pointing. “A small swivel gun, by the look of it, but dangerous at this range!”

A moment later, a harpoon, tipped not with its usual barb but with a crude explosive charge, arced through the air towards them. It fell short, exploding harmlessly on the ice some distance astern, but the message was clear: Bolt intended to fight fire with fire, however primitive his means.

“He means to damage us, Frank,” Vaneyke observed, his voice now laced with genuine concern. “Even a slight impairment to our runners could prove disastrous.”

“Then we must not give him the opportunity, Doctor,” Frank declared. He put his hand firmly on the wheel, adjusting their course with practiced ease. “We are approaching the broken ice fields I noted on the chart. If I recall correctly, there is a labyrinthine passage through them, one which a ship of the Red Eric’s bulk would find exceedingly difficult to navigate.”

With a surge of renewed power, the Ranger plunged into a region of bewildering complexity. Jagged floes, torn asunder by ancient tectonic forces, rose like monstrous battlements. Narrow channels, barely wider than the Ranger’s beam, twisted and turned, forcing Frank to pilot with the utmost precision. Pressure ridges, towering walls of ice, loomed on either side, threatening to crush them should the ship stray even a hair’s breadth from its prescribed course. The Ranger, with its sleek design and powerful engines, danced through this frozen maze like a phantom. Its steel runners scraped and groaned against the formidable obstacles, but its superior maneuverability allowed it to weave through the treacherous passages where a lesser vessel would have been hopelessly ensnared.

The Red Eric, however, possessed neither the grace nor the speed for such intricate navigation. They could hear her, a distant, frustrated bellowing, as she struggled to follow. The grinding of her wooden hull against the unyielding ice, the frantic shouts of her crew, and the desperate blasts of her steam whistle echoed through the cold, still air. Frank, peering through his spyglass, witnessed Bolt’s increasing frustration. The whaler, caught between two enormous floes, struggled for precious minutes, her engines straining, her timbers groaning ominously. Men with long pikes could be seen frantically attempting to push away the encroaching ice, their figures tiny and desperate against the vast, white backdrop.

“He’s stuck!” Pomp shouted from the turret, his voice jubilant. “Ha! Dat’s what he gets fo’ chasin’ de Ranger!”

“Do not celebrate too soon, Pomp,” Frank cautioned. “Bolt is resourceful. He will free himself, mark my words. But this gives us a momentary advantage. Doctor, Barney, keep a sharp lookout. These ice fields are not without their own perils. Walter, how fares our young guest?”

Walter Grey, who had been quietly observing the dramatic chase from the comparative safety of the cabin, emerged, his youthful face alight with a mixture of fear and excitement. The initial shock of his rescue had given way to a cautious fascination with the Ranger and its daring crew.

“I am well, sir,” Walter replied, his voice still a little weak but gaining strength. “It is… quite an adventure.”

“Aye, laddie, that it is!” Barney boomed. “And you’re safe with us, though ol’ Bolt wishes you weren’t, the rogue.”

The momentary lull allowed Frank to consult his charts. He noted a specific landmark: a series of towering, almost geometric ice formations, known to previous explorers as ‘The Towers of Boreas,’ which marked the approach to the deep, sheltered cove he intended to reach. This cove, rarely free of ice, would provide a natural bottleneck, a perfect location for his plan.

As if on cue, the Arctic, ever a fickle mistress, decided to cast a new veil over the proceedings. A thick, ethereal fog, rising from some unseen body of water, began to roll in with astonishing rapidity. It swirled around the Ranger, transforming the jagged ice formations into ghostly, indistinct shapes. Visibility plummeted to mere yards, and the world outside became a swirling vortex of white, gray, and utter silence, broken only by the hum of their engines.

“Well, this complicates matters,” Dr. Vaneyke mused, wiping his spectacles. “Visibility is practically nil. It could be to our advantage, or to our detriment.”

“It is a double-edged sword, Doctor,” Frank agreed. “But I mean to make it work in our favour. Bolt relies on brute force and visual pursuit. In this fog, he is blind. We, however, have the benefit of our experience with these treacherous waters and the keen ears of our crew. Pomp, listen for the Red Eric’s whistle, her engine sounds. Barney, keep an eye on the ice ahead, however little you can see.”

The Ranger, her speed slightly reduced, became a spectral ship, gliding through the milky opacity. Frank steered by instinct and sound, anticipating the unseen obstacles. He could feel the proximity of monstrous icebergs, their cold bulk chilling the air even further, their unseen presence betrayed only by subtle changes in the ship’s vibration or a sudden, localized drop in temperature.

From the stern, Pomp’s voice, now hushed with concentration, cut through the fog. “Cap’n Frank! I hear ‘im! Off to port, dat’s his whistle! He’s comin’ up slow, soundin’ for de channel.”

“Excellent, Pomp!” Frank confirmed. “He’s trying to follow our wake, but the fog scrambles his senses. He cannot know our exact position.”

Frank executed a daring maneuver. Instead of continuing straight, he veered sharply to starboard, describing a wide, silent arc that brought the Ranger almost back on its previous course, but unseen. The Red Eric, guided by the echoes of the Ranger’s passage, steamed laboriously forward, her foghorn bellowing like a lost beast. Frank, holding his breath, brought the Ranger to a near standstill, allowing the pursuing whaler to pass them by, a dark, ghostly shadow glimpsed only for an instant through the swirling mist.

“He went right past us, Cap’n!” Pomp whispered, his excitement barely contained. “He’s still headin’ for de Towers, thinkin’ we’re ahead!”

“Just as I planned,” Frank replied, a grim satisfaction in his voice. “Now to spring our trap. Doctor, Barney, Pomp, listen carefully. We shall follow him at a distance, allowing the fog to continue playing its deceitful game. Our destination is the Narrow Pass of Boreas, just beyond the Towers. It is a choke point, barely wide enough for a ship like the Red Eric to squeeze through, and prone to sudden shifts in the ice.”

Frank then outlined his audacious scheme. Barney and Pomp, armed with carefully placed explosive charges, were to disembark under the cover of the fog and scale the towering ice cliffs flanking the Narrow Pass. Their mission: to trigger an icefall directly in the path of the Red Eric once she was fully committed to the passage. Dr. Vaneyke and Walter would remain aboard the Ranger, ready to provide covering fire with the stern cannon, should Bolt somehow manage to extricate himself.

“It is a perilous task, lads,” Frank cautioned, looking from Barney’s eager face to Pomp’s determined one. “But it is our best chance to cripple him without risking a direct boarding action, which would surely result in needless loss of life.”

“Perilous or no, Cap’n,” Barney declared, thumping his chest, “if it means puttin’ that scoundrel Bolt out of commission, consider it done! Sure, an’ I’ve climbed worse cliffs than these back in the ould country!”

Pomp nodded vigorously. “Don’t you worry, Cap’n! We’ll make dat ice sing a sad song for ol’ Ben Bolt!”

The Ranger, moving with renewed purpose, now pursued the Red Eric, guided only by the distant sounds of her struggle against the ice. The fog, a benevolent ally in Frank’s intricate plan, remained thick and impenetrable. The Red Eric’s crew, meanwhile, believing they were hot on the Ranger’s trail, pressed onward, their ship crashing and grinding its way through the increasingly narrow channels.

As they approached the Narrow Pass, the sounds from the Red Eric grew louder, more frantic. The creaking of her timbers, the shouts of command, and the desperate chipping of axes against the ice echoed ominously. Frank brought the Ranger to a halt in a concealed cove, its entrance shrouded by a thick curtain of fog and a massive, overhanging iceberg.

“Now, lads,” Frank whispered, his voice low and intense. “The time is ripe. May good fortune attend you.”

Barney and Pomp, equipped with ropes, climbing spikes, and satchels containing the explosive charges, melted silently into the swirling fog. They were to ascend the western side of the pass, where a particularly precarious overhang of ice, weakened by centuries of glacial movement, awaited their handiwork. The journey continued for several days without further incident, a monotonous progression through an endless panorama of ice and snow. Walter, despite his weakness, showed surprising agility, driven by the urgency of their escape. Frank kept a hand on his back, guiding him, protecting him. They reached the base of the cleft, and Frank pushed Walter ahead towards the open water where the Ranger awaited.

“Swim, Walter! Swim for the Ranger!” Frank commanded, then turned to face their pursuer. The watchman was just descending the cleft, his heavy frame making the ice groan. Frank knew he couldn’t outrun the man. He had to delay him.

He reached for one of his pneumatic pistols. It was a silent weapon, firing compressed air pellets, but it packed enough punch to stun an opponent. As the watchman neared the bottom, Frank raised the pistol and fired. A soft hiss, and a pellet struck the man squarely in the chest. He grunted, stumbling back against the icy wall, momentarily disoriented. It was not enough to incapacitate him, but it bought them precious seconds.

“Go, Walter, go!” Frank shouted, and with a final glance at the dazed watchman, he plunged into the icy water, swimming strongly towards the distant silhouette of the Ranger. Walter, already in the water, was struggling, his weakened state making the cold an even greater shock.

On board the Ranger, Barney and Pomp, along with Dr. Vaneyke, had been keeping a tense vigil. They had seen Frank and Walter scramble down the cleft, and then the sudden appearance of the watchman.

“Look! They’re in the water!” cried Pomp, his voice filled with alarm.

“Lower the gangplank! Quickly!” ordered Dr. Vaneyke, already moving to assist. Barney, his face grim, began to work the machinery that controlled the retractable gangplank. The Ranger, true to its design, was equipped for quick rescues.

Frank reached Walter, who was gasping for air, his teeth chattering uncontrollably. Frank put an arm around the boy, supporting him, and continued to swim, kicking powerfully towards the boat. The water was agonizingly cold, threatening to sap their strength, but the thought of escape fueled their efforts.

The gangplank splashed down into the water just a few feet from them. Strong hands reached out, pulling Walter aboard first, then assisting Frank. As soon as they were both on deck, Barney and Pomp rushed them towards the heated cabin.

“Good heavens, Walter, you’re half frozen!” exclaimed Dr. Vaneyke, immediately wrapping the boy in a thick blanket. “Get him by the stove, Pomp. Barney, warm some whiskey for Frank.”

Frank, though shivering violently, managed a weak smile. “He’s safe. That’s all that matters.” He looked back towards the Red Eric. The watchman had recovered and was now shouting, his cries echoing across the bay. Other figures were appearing on the whaler’s deck, drawn by the commotion.

“Captain Bolt will know,” said Frank, his voice grim. “We need to get out of here, and fast.”

Dr. Vaneyke, ever practical, was already issuing orders. “Barney, Pomp, prepare to get underway! Frank, get those wet clothes off and get some warmth into you. We’ll be pursued.”
