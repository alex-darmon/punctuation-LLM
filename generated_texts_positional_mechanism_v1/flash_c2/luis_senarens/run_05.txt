The leads of open water grew narrower, forcing Barney to navigate with utmost precision, the powerful hull of the ice ship occasionally scraping against the jagged edges of the floes with a shuddering groan.

“Radar shows increasing turbulence ahead, Frank,” Dr. Vaneyke announced, his voice steady despite the growing din of the wind and the creaking of the ice. “The passage is narrowing significantly, and the ice formations are becoming dangerously unpredictable. Sonar indicates complex subsurface currents, which could shift the floes at a moment’s notice.”

“Then we must rely on our speed and the *Ranger’s* strength, Doctor,” Frank replied, his eyes fixed on the path ahead, his hands hovering over the intricate controls. “Maintain maximum surveillance. Pomp, report anything, no matter how small, that seems out of place.”

The blizzard, true to Barney’s prediction, descended upon them with a sudden, ferocious might. The fine snowflakes turned into a blinding white maelstrom, obscuring vision to mere feet. The wind howled like a legion of banshees, tearing at the superstructure of the *Ranger*, rattling the windows of the cabin, and coating everything in a rapidly thickening layer of rime. The temperature plummeted, and the ice beneath them groaned and shrieked under the relentless pressure of the storm. Navigating became a desperate struggle, a constant battle against an invisible, all-encompassing foe.

Barney wrestled with the wheel, his muscles bulging as he fought to keep the *Ranger* on course, peering through the small clearings on the storm-lashed windows. “Begorra, Frank, it’s a proper white-out we’ve got here! Can’t see a hand in front of me face, let alone an iceberg. We’ll be runnin’ aground if we don’t slow down.”

“We cannot slow down, Barney,” Frank countered, his voice sharp with urgency. “The radar shows the passage is too constricting here. To stop would be to risk being crushed by the shifting ice. We must push through! Trust the instruments, and trust the *Ranger*.”

Indeed, the mighty ice ship proved its worth. Its powerful engines hummed a deep, steady bass note beneath the shrill treble of the gale, driving it forward. The specially designed steel runners, heated by an internal system, sliced through the accumulating snow and thin ice, maintaining traction. Yet, the danger was palpable. Massive blocks of ice, torn from the towering walls of the passage by the tempest, crashed into the water or scraped against the hull with frightening force, causing the entire vessel to lurch and tremble.

Suddenly, Pomp’s voice, strained but urgent, cut through the clamor. “Massa Frank! Somethin’ ahead! Big, dark shape! Not ice! Looks like… like a wreck!”

Frank’s head snapped up. “A wreck, Pomp? Are you certain?”

“Aye, sah! Seen it for a flash when de snow cleared a bit. Dis ain’t no iceberg, it’s man-made! And it’s right in de middle ob de passage!”

Dr. Vaneyke quickly consulted his radar. “Pomp is correct, Frank. There’s a large, solid object directly in our path, showing distinct metallic signatures. It’s not moving. Estimated length is over a hundred feet. It appears to be an old steamer or a whaler, heavily damaged.”

A thrill, a mixture of dread and grim satisfaction, shot through Frank. Could it be Ben Bolt? Unlikely, given the Red Eric was still intact and anchored. But what if it was a scout ship, or a trap? Or perhaps a clue?

“Barney, full reverse, careful now!” Frank commanded, his mind racing. “But prepare to put her hard to port if needed. Dr. Vaneyke, analyze its structure for any signs of recent activity. Pomp, keep that observation going, tell me everything you see!”

The *Ranger’s* engines roared in protest as Barney wrestled with the controls, attempting to slow the massive vessel in the teeth of the storm. The ice ship shuddered, groaning as its momentum fought against the sudden braking. For a terrifying moment, it seemed they would collide with the mysterious obstruction. But Barney, with the practiced skill of a master mariner, brought the *Ranger* to a halt just yards from the dark, ghostly form looming out of the blizzard.

The snow, for a brief, blessed interval, thinned slightly, revealing the grim spectacle. It was indeed a wreck, an ancient, wooden steam whaler, its mast broken, its hull stove in on one side, and its entire structure encrusted with centuries of ice. It lay half-submerged in a narrow lead of water, completely blocking the passage. Its once proud bowsprit pointed forlornly towards the sky, a skeletal finger reaching out from a watery grave. It was a testament to the unforgiving nature of the Arctic.

“By the great polar bear, what in the blazes is that doing here?” Barney exclaimed, crossing himself. “Looks like it’s been here since before the flood!”

“It must have been caught in some ancient ice shift or storm,” Dr. Vaneyke speculated, peering through his binoculars. “Remarkable how well preserved it is, despite the damage.”

Frank, however, was not interested in historical preservation. His mind was already calculating the implications. “This means we cannot proceed through the passage. It’s completely blocked. We’ll have to find another way around, or through.” He looked at the wreck, a frown creasing his brow. “But wait… what’s that?”

Pomp, his eyes like those of an eagle despite the swirling snow, instantly spotted what Frank had seen. “Lights, Massa Frank! Faint, but dey is lights on dat old wreck! And… and dat ain’t no whale oil lamp, sah. Dat’s electric!”

A sudden tension gripped the crew. Lights on an ancient wreck in the middle of a blizzard-swept passage? This was no natural phenomenon. Frank grabbed his own powerful telescope and focused it on the spectral ship. Indeed, faint flickers of light emanated from within the frozen hulk, suggesting occupation. Dr. Vaneyke adjusted his spectacles, his brow furrowed with concern. “But why would he be here, Frank? The Red Eric is anchored in Nordenskjold Bay. Why would he occupy this ancient hulk?”

“Perhaps it is a secondary base, a secret rendezvous,” suggested Frank, pacing the bridge of the Ranger. “Or a temporary refuge if his main vessel is compromised. He is a wily fox, and it would be just like him to have multiple lairs. More likely, however, he intended to meet someone here, or to hide something, or someone, within its decaying hull. And if he hides someone, it could only be Walter.”

Barney shivered, a superstitious dread flickering in his eyes. “A haunted ship, it is, Massa Frank, full of ghosties and ghouls. Better we leave it be and find another path.”

“Nonsense, Barney,” Frank retorted, though a shiver of his own, born of anticipation rather than fear, ran down his spine. “Ghosts do not operate electric lamps. This is the work of flesh and blood, and I mean to find out whose. We cannot simply bypass it. This wreck blocks our way entirely, and if Bolt is truly here, we must confront him.”

Pomp, ever the pragmatist when danger loomed, grasped his trusty cutlass. “What yo’ plan, Massa Frank? We go in all guns blazing?”

Frank shook his head. “Not yet, Pomp. We do not know their numbers, nor their exact intentions. A frontal assault would be foolhardy. My plan is to approach cautiously, reconnoiter the wreck, and ascertain their strength and purpose. If Walter is indeed aboard, we must extract him with as little fuss as possible.”

“And how do we approach without being seen in this infernal storm?” asked Dr. Vaneyke, peering out at the swirling white maelstrom. The wind shrieked like a banshee, and snow plastered itself against the thick glass of the turret windows.

“The blizzard, while a hindrance, is also our ally,” Frank mused. “It provides cover. We will moor the Ranger as close as prudence allows, then proceed on foot, using the driving snow and the chaotic landscape of ice hummocks as concealment. Barney, ever confident in his seamanship, nodded. “Aye, Massa Frank. I can put her as close as a lover’s whisper, if ye give me the word. The ice is thick enough here, and I can find a pocket where the wind won’t thrash us about too much.”

“Excellent,” Frank declared. “Then make it so. Pomp, prepare the grappling hooks and a coil of strong rope. Dr. Vaneyke, I shall require your sharp eyes and steady hand with a rifle, should matters escalate. I will lead the way.”

The Ranger, a marvel of ice-navigation, slowly pivoted. Barney, with meticulous care, guided the massive ship through the treacherous ice-choked waters, the great steel runners grinding softly against the floes. The whaler wreck, a colossal shadow, grew larger, its broken mast a jagged finger pointing into the blizzard. Its hull, riddled with the scars of ages, was a mosaic of splintered wood and ancient ice.

As they drew nearer, the details of the wreck became clearer, even through the swirling snow. It was a leviathan, truly, far larger than the Red Eric, and its timbers groaned and creaked with every gust of wind, a mournful dirge echoing across the frozen waste. A narrow lead of dark, churning water surrounded the hulk, preventing a direct approach on foot from the ice.

“We’ll need the launch, Massa Frank,” Barney observed, bringing the Ranger to a gentle halt alongside a towering ice floe that offered some shelter from the direct blast of the gale. “The water’s too open here for a direct crossing.”

“Indeed,” Frank agreed. “Pomp, Dr. Vaneyke, prepare the small launch. Barney, stand by the Ranger. Keep a sharp lookout. If we are not back within two hours, assume the worst and prepare for a rescue operation, but do not engage unless absolutely necessary.”

“Aye, Massa Frank,” Barney replied, his hand already on the wheel, his eyes scanning the blizzard.

The small ice-reinforced launch was quickly lowered into the water, its electric motor humming softly. Frank, Dr. Vaneyke, and Pomp, bundled in heavy furs and armed with their pneumatic pistols and rifles, climbed aboard. The wind whipped at their faces, stinging their exposed skin with ice crystals. The visibility was barely a ship’s length, a chaotic dance of white flakes.

“Keep close to the ice floes, Pomp,” Frank instructed, his voice muffled by the wind. “Use them for cover. Dr. Vaneyke, keep your eyes peeled for any movement on the wreck. We must assume they have sentries.”

The launch puttered stealthily through the dark water, hugging the jagged edges of the surrounding ice. The wreck loomed larger and larger, a black silhouette against the grey-white chaos. The faint, yellowish glow of electric lamps could now be discerned from several shattered portholes and a gaping hole in the superstructure. The lights pulsed faintly, suggesting a rudimentary, perhaps even jury-rigged, power source.

As they neared the ancient hull, the stench of stale oil, brine, and something metallic, like burnt electrical wiring, assaulted their nostrils. The ship was a wreck, but it was a recently inhabited wreck. The water lapped against its barnacled sides with a morbid sound.

“There,” Pomp whispered, pointing towards a section of the hull that seemed less damaged, where a broken gangway, or perhaps a section of a mast that had fallen, extended downwards towards the water, coated in ice. “We can climb up dere, Massa Frank.”

“Good eye, Pomp,” Frank acknowledged. “Dr. Vaneyke, cover us. Pomp, tie us off to that floe, then secure the launch. We go aboard.”

With practiced efficiency, Pomp secured the small craft. Frank, agile as a cat, was the first to ascend the treacherous icy incline, his boots finding purchase on the frozen splinters of wood. The climb was perilous, the surface slick with ice and spray, but his strong hands found purchase, and soon he stood on a precarious ledge of the wreck’s deck, concealed by a broken davit. Dr. Vaneyke followed, his movements more deliberate but equally steady, his rifle held ready. Pomp, with the agility of a trained sailor, was soon beside them.

The deck was a chaotic jumble of snapped rigging, rusted machinery, and thick, ancient ice. The wind howled through the skeletal remains of the superstructure, creating a cacophony of groans and whistles. Snow swirled in dizzying eddies, obscuring and revealing the desolate scene in turn.

“Lights seem to be coming from the midships section, and possibly below deck,” Frank murmured, gesturing. “We’ll proceed cautiously. Stick to the shadows, and listen for any sound above the storm.”

They moved like specters across the ruined deck, their footsteps muffled by the snow and the gale. The wreck was vast, a labyrinth of shattered beams and twisted metal. They discovered a gaping hole near what would have been the main mast, leading down into the ship’s interior. A faint glow emanated from it, casting flickering shadows on the ice-encrusted bulkheads.

“This looks like our entrance,” Frank whispered, drawing one of his pneumatic pistols. “Be ready for anything.”

He was the first to descend, lowering himself carefully into the gloom. The air grew stiller below deck, though the ship’s constant groaning and creaking was magnified. The cold was bone-chilling, a damp, pervasive chill that seemed to seep into their very marrow. The light below was indeed electric, powered by a crude generator whose intermittent sputtering could now be faintly heard.

They found themselves in what must have been the main cargo hold, now largely clear of its original contents, save for some rusted barrels and a few splintered crates.
