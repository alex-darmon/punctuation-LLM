THE SHINY MYSTERY

One warm afternoon, Sleepy Badger, who was usually content to nap in his cozy sett, decided to take a stroll near Farmer Giles’s overgrown garden. He shuffled along, his nose to the ground, humming a tune to himself. Suddenly, his paw nudged something hard and smooth. He stopped. "What's this?" he mumbled, blinking his small eyes. It was a fragment of something white and curved, with a thin line of gold painted around its edge. It shone dully in the dappled sunlight. Sleepy Badger had never seen anything quite like it. He picked it up carefully. "A most peculiar stone," he decided. "Quite shiny, too." He tucked it into a pouch he carried for interesting finds and ambled back towards his home.

As he was nearing his sett, Chatty Magpie swooped down from a nearby elm. "Sleepy Badger!" she chirped excitedly. "What have you there? I saw a glint from afar!" Sleepy Badger slowly produced his find. "It is a shiny stone," he explained. "I found it near Farmer Giles's pumpkins." Chatty Magpie peered at it, her beady eyes wide with curiosity. "A stone?" she repeated, her voice already rising in pitch. "It looks like a piece of the _moon_! Or perhaps a fragment of a lost star! Farmer Giles must have dropped it!" Sleepy Badger merely shrugged. "It is just a shiny stone," he said again, feeling a bit sleepy already.

But Chatty Magpie was already off, flitting from tree to tree, eager to spread her news. First she found Busy Beaver, who was gnawing on a willow branch. "Busy Beaver, have you heard?" she squawked. "Sleepy Badger has found a magical artifact! A piece of the moon, left by Farmer Giles!" Busy Beaver, who was always practical, just grunted. "The moon, you say? How did it get to Farmer Giles's garden?" But Chatty Magpie didn't wait for an answer. She darted off to tell Fidgety Squirrel.

By the time the story reached Fidgety Squirrel, it had grown considerably. "Sleepy Badger has found Farmer Giles's secret treasure!" Fidgety Squirrel chattered to Old Mother Mole. "A golden goblet, left by the moon-folk, filled with sparkling dew that grants wishes!" Old Mother Mole, who was nearly blind but still loved a good story, gasped. "Wishes, you say? How many wishes?" Fidgety Squirrel paused for a moment. "As many as there are stars!" he declared, improvising wildly. He then scurried off to tell his cousins.

The story continued its journey, bouncing from one creature to another, growing grander and more absurd with every retelling. It was Tiny Mouse who finally carried the most extravagant version to Old Man Owl. "Old Man Owl!" Tiny Mouse squeaked, breathless from running. "You won't believe it! Sleepy Badger has found Farmer Giles's treasure chest, bursting with gold coins and silver jewels, and a magic cup that makes sweet berries appear on demand! He's going to share it with everyone and make himself King of Pleasant Glade!" Old Man Owl, who had been dozing on his branch, opened one wise eye. "Is that so?" he hooted, a hint of amusement in his voice. "A treasure chest, you say? And sweet berries on demand? This sounds like a story that needs a closer look." He spread his great wings and glided silently towards Sleepy Badger's sett.

Sleepy Badger was indeed in his sett, peacefully polishing his "shiny stone" with a bit of moss. He was quite pleased with his discovery. It was a most agreeable shiny stone; it made a pleasant, smooth sound when he rubbed it. Old Man Owl perched on a nearby branch, his eyes fixed on the badger. "Good evening, Sleepy Badger," he hooted. "I hear you have found quite the treasure. A chest of gold, a magic cup, and endless sweet berries, if I am not mistaken." Sleepy Badger looked up, blinking slowly. "Treasure?" he repeated. "Oh, no, Old Man Owl. Just this." He held up the polished fragment of ceramic teacup. "It is a most agreeable shiny stone."

Old Man Owl looked from the small, chipped piece of pottery to the earnest, slightly bewildered badger. He couldn't help but chuckle. "A shiny stone, indeed," he said. "And the treasure chest, and the magic cup, and the endless berries?" Sleepy Badger frowned. "I know nothing of such things," he stated. "I found only this. It is pleasant to hold." "It seems--" Old Man Owl observed--"that some stories have a way of growing far beyond their humble beginnings." He then flew back to his branch, chuckling all the way. He knew that the tale of Sleepy Badger's treasure would soon shrink back to its true size, once the truth was known. And he made a mental note to remind Chatty Magpie that not every shiny object was a piece of the moon.

XIII THE MISSING SCROLL

Old Man Owl sat on his branch in the great oak at the edge of Pleasant Glade. The sun was just beginning to touch the highest leaves, and the early morning air was fresh and cool. He thought about Sleepy Badger and his agreeable shiny stone, and how such a small thing could become a tale of gold and magic cups. He remembered his mental note. It was time, he decided, to have a word with Chatty Magpie. He stretched his great wings and launched himself into the air, gliding silently towards the cluster of trees where Chatty Magpie often held her morning conversations.

He found Chatty Magpie, true to form, perched on a low branch and chattering excitedly to a group of sparrows about a particularly plump beetle she had seen. Her black and white feathers gleamed in the sunlight, and her bright eyes darted this way and that. Old Man Owl landed softly on a branch above her, making the sparrows scatter with a nervous chirp. Chatty Magpie, however, merely twitched her head.

"Good morning, Chatty Magpie," Old Man Owl hooted.

"Oh, good morning, Old Man Owl!" Chatty Magpie chirped. "Did you see that beetle? It was the largest beetle I have ever seen! It must have been at least as big as my thumb!"

"Indeed," said Old Man Owl, though he had not seen any such beetle. "I have come to speak with you about something else. About stories, and how they tend to grow, sometimes beyond the truth."

Chatty Magpie ruffled her feathers. "Oh, that old thing! The Sleepy Badger's treasure? Everyone knows about that now. It was just a little piece of broken cup, after all. But you know," she added, lowering her voice conspiratorially, "I think he still has secrets. Perhaps another treasure, a real one, hidden away."

Old Man Owl sighed. It seemed his lesson would be harder to teach than he thought. "No, Chatty Magpie," he said patiently. "Sleepy Badger has only his shiny stone. What I mean is that we must be careful with what we see and what we hear, for a small bit of truth can become a very grand untruth if it is not handled with care."

"Oh, but it is much more fun when the stories are grand!" Chatty Magpie declared. "Why, just yesterday, I saw something very grand indeed! Farmer Green's boy, Johnnie, was playing near the cross-roads, not far from your very oak, Old Man Owl. And he had a scroll!"

Old Man Owl blinked. "A scroll, you say?"

"Yes! A magnificent scroll!" Chatty Magpie exclaimed, already forgetting about beetles and Badger's secrets. "It was rolled up tight, and tied with the brightest red ribbon you ever saw! And Johnnie Green dropped it! He dropped it right there in the grass, and he didn't even notice! He just skipped away, singing a tune. It must be very important, Old Man Owl. Very important indeed!"

"And what was this scroll, Chatty Magpie?" Old Man Owl asked, trying to keep his voice calm. He suspected another small object was about to become a great legend.

"Well, I don't know what it *was*," Chatty Magpie admitted. "But it looked like an old, old map! Perhaps a map to the secret garden of Farmer Green, where he grows all his extra sweet berries! Or perhaps it is a map to the buried treasure that all the old tales speak of, hidden by pirates centuries ago! It was so mysteriously rolled and tied with that bright red ribbon. It simply *must* be a treasure map!"

Before Old Man Owl could offer a word of caution, Chatty Magpie had darted off, her wings beating furiously. "I must tell Fidgety Squirrel!" she cried over her shoulder. "He loves a good treasure hunt!" Old Man Owl watched her go, shaking his wise old head. Some lessons, he mused, must be learned through experience.

Chatty Magpie found Fidgety Squirrel scampering up and down an elm tree, burying nuts. "Fidgety Squirrel! Fidgety Squirrel!" she chattered. "Stop burying those tiresome nuts! There is a real treasure to be found! A secret scroll! A map to untold riches!"

Fidgety Squirrel stopped, his nose twitching. "A map, you say? What sort of map?"

"The best sort of map!" Chatty Magpie declared, puffing out her chest. "Johnnie Green, Farmer Green's boy, dropped it near the cross-roads. It's an ancient scroll, tied with a bright red ribbon. I just know it leads to pirate gold! Why, it must be the very map the pirates left behind when they sailed away from Swift River, leaving their gold in Pleasant Glade!"

Fidgety Squirrel's eyes grew wide. "Pirate gold! And a map! But if it is so ancient," he mused, "it must be written in a secret code! Only the bravest, or perhaps the cleverest, can read such a map! And there are sure to be traps! Old pirate traps, guarding the treasure with invisible ink and riddles that speak only to the wind!" He imagined himself, the cleverest of squirrels, deciphering the ancient symbols. This was far more exciting than mere nuts.

He thanked Chatty Magpie and immediately scurried off to tell Barnaby Bear. "Barnaby Bear! Barnaby Bear!" Fidgety Squirrel chattered, finding the large bear lumbering through the woods. "Wake up from your nap! There's a treasure map! A real one, with secret symbols and ancient riddles, left by pirates long ago!"

Barnaby Bear blinked his sleepy eyes. "Treasure map, you say? To what sort of treasure?" His mind immediately turned to honey and ripe berries.

"To untold riches!" Fidgety Squirrel insisted. "Pirate gold! But I fear it is also a map to something more. A curse, Barnaby Bear! The pirates always left curses to guard their loot! And the map, it is written with strange signs, perhaps signs of terrible spells and enchantments that only the most powerful can break!" Barnaby Bear, who was usually very brave, felt a shiver run through his thick fur. A curse did not sound like honey at all.

Barnaby Bear, now wide awake and a little nervous, decided he must warn everyone. He told Rosie Rabbit, who was munching calmly on a clover leaf. "Rosie Rabbit! Rosie Rabbit!" Barnaby grumbled. "There is a cursed map! A map left by ancient pirates, full of strange symbols and evil spells! Fidgety Squirrel says it will bring a curse upon Pleasant Glade!"

Rosie Rabbit dropped her clover. "A curse? Oh dear! What kind of curse?"

"I heard," Barnaby Bear whispered, leaning closer, "that it is a map to the last remaining carrot patch in the valley! And once someone finds it, a terrible dragon will awaken from its sleep and guard the patch forever! No rabbit will ever taste a carrot again!" Rosie Rabbit's whiskers twitched with fright. No carrots! This was a disaster of the gravest kind.

Rosie Rabbit, in turn, darted to tell Old Mother Mole, who was busy tunneling beneath the roots of a giant pine. "Old Mother Mole! Old Mother Mole!" Rosie squeaked, her voice trembling. "There's a terrible map! A map of evil curses and dreadful dragons! It will take away all our carrots!"

Old Mother Mole paused her digging. Her eyesight was poor, but her hearing was excellent. "A map, you say? To dreadful dragons and lost carrots?" She had not heard this much excitement since the tale of Sleepy Badger's treasures. "What else does this map say?"

"Barnaby Bear says it has secret symbols and terrible spells!" Rosie Rabbit cried. "And it was left by ancient pirates! It must be a map to the center of the earth, where all the lost gems and treasures are hidden! And only those with true courage, those who are not afraid of dragons or curses, will ever find them!" Old Mother Mole, who loved a good underground adventure, felt a thrill. Gems! And the center of the earth!

Sandy Chipmunk, who was tidying up his burrow, overheard a particularly excited young mole telling his sister about the "map to the center of the earth, guarded by a dragon, and filled with sparkling gems." Sandy paused, a beechnut halfway to his cheek. Another story, he thought. Another grand tale, starting from who-knew-what. He remembered the mail-box, and the "six haystacks" of letters, and the "twelve horses," and how it had all turned out to be a single bag of mail and one horse. He felt a familiar prickle of curiosity, mixed with a healthy dose of suspicion.

Just then, Mr. Crow landed heavily on a branch outside Sandy's burrow, making the leaves rustle. "Sandy Chipmunk!" he cawed. "Have you heard the latest? A map! An ancient map, dropped by Farmer Green's boy! It leads to treasure!"

Sandy Chipmunk poked his head out. "Treasure, Mr. Crow? What sort of treasure?"

"The very best sort!" Mr. Crow declared, puffing out his chest. "Gold! Gems! Riches untold! And they say it's written in an ancient tongue, a secret code that only the wisest among us can decipher!" Mr. Crow looked pointedly at Sandy, implying that *he* was among the wisest. "I've heard some foolish talk about dragons and curses and lost carrots, but I assure you, Sandy, that is mere childish fancy. This is serious business. This is about *wealth*."

"And who told you about this map, Mr. Crow?" Sandy asked, remembering how many times the mail-box story had changed.

"Why, everyone is talking about it!" Mr. Crow flapped his wings dismissively. "Chatty Magpie saw it first. A rolled scroll, she said, tied with a bright red ribbon. It can only be a treasure map. Now, I, of course, have studied many ancient writings. I know a thing or two about deciphering cryptic symbols. So I shall be the one to interpret this map, once it is found."

"And who is to find it, Mr. Crow?" Sandy inquired.

"Why, you, of course, Sandy Chipmunk!" Mr. Crow exclaimed. "You are small and quick. You can slip through the grass and search where others cannot. Find this scroll for me, Sandy. Bring it to me, and I shall tell you what it says. Perhaps it will lead to a new field of corn! Or perhaps some truly valuable shiny things that *I* could use." He did not mention sharing.

Sandy Chipmunk remembered Mr. Crow's demands about the corn, and how he had tried to make Sandy pay Frisky Squirrel with his *own* share. He remembered being called stupid, and then Mr. Crow taking back his words. "I'm afraid, Mr. Crow," Sandy said carefully, "that I am still too stupid for such important work. And I have many beechnuts to look after."

Mr. Crow narrowed his eyes. "Nonsense, Sandy! You are not stupid at all. Why, you found that letter, did you not? You are perfectly capable. Besides, what if this map leads to something dangerous? I, with my vast wisdom, would be able to guide you." He still did not offer to go himself.

Sandy Chipmunk, though, was tired of being a tool for Mr. Crow's grand schemes. He knew that if the map was found, Mr. Crow would claim all the credit, and likely all the treasure too. "Thank you, Mr. Crow," he said, "but I think I shall attend to my own affairs today. The forest is full of rumors, and sometimes the simplest explanation is the true one."

Mr. Crow scowled. "Very well, Sandy Chipmunk! Miss your chance for greatness! But do not come crying to me when I am feasting on pirate gold and you are merely gnawing on stale beechnuts!" With that, Mr. Crow flapped away, cawing loudly, perhaps to impress the other animals, or perhaps to hide his annoyance.

Sandy Chipmunk watched him go. He decided he would investigate, but on his own terms. He would not be hurried by Mr. Crow, nor would he let the exaggerated tales sway him. He went to the cross-roads, the place where Chatty Magpie had said Johnnie Green dropped the scroll. He moved quietly through the tall grass and clover, his bright eyes scanning the ground. He looked for anything that might be rolled, or tied, or even a little bit shiny, like the red ribbon Chatty Magpie had described.

He searched for a long time. The sun climbed higher, warming the earth. Other animals were also out, spurred on by the exciting new rumor. Fidgety Squirrel was digging enthusiastic holes around the roots of a maple tree, convinced the pirate treasure was buried there. Barnaby Bear was sniffing loudly at every large rock, hoping to detect the scent of dragon or curse, or perhaps a hidden honey pot. Rosie Rabbit and her family were huddled together under a thorny bush, nervously twitching their noses, worried about the approaching carrot-devouring dragon. Even Old Mother Mole had made her way to the surface, her tiny eyes peering about, hoping to catch a glimpse of the "center of the earth."

Sandy Chipmunk saw no scroll. He found no ribbon. He began to feel a bit foolish, wondering if Chatty Magpie had been mistaken once more. Just as he was about to give up and return to his beechnuts, he saw Johnnie Green, Farmer Green's boy, skipping along the path, humming a tuneless little song. Johnnie was playing with a small, rolled-up piece of paper. He swung it playfully as he walked.

Then, just as he reached the spot near the cross-roads where Chatty Magpie had said he dropped the original "scroll," Johnnie tripped slightly on a loose stone. He stumbled, and the rolled-up paper, tied with a bright red ribbon, flew from his hand and landed softly in a patch of dandelions. Johnnie merely picked himself up, rubbed his knee, and continued on his way, humming his tune, completely unaware that he had dropped anything.

Sandy Chipmunk watched, his heart thumping a little. This was it! The scroll! He waited until Johnnie Green was well out of sight, down the path towards Farmer Green's house. Then, cautiously, Sandy crept towards the patch of dandelions. The rolled paper lay there, tied neatly with a bright red ribbon. It looked exactly as Chatty Magpie had described.

He nudged it with his nose. It felt like paper. He carefully began to untie the red ribbon, his paws fumbling slightly with the knot. Finally, the ribbon came loose, and the paper unrolled. Sandy Chipmunk peered at it.

It was not a map. It was not ancient writings. It was not a secret code or a recipe for Farmer Green's wife. It was a drawing. A child's drawing. There was a wobbly house with a plume of smoke coming from its chimney, a bright yellow sun with radiating lines, and a stick figure with a big smiling face. It was signed "Johnnie."

Sandy Chipmunk looked at the drawing, then back at the forest animals still searching frantically for dragons and curses and underground gems. He couldn't help but chuckle softly. The great pirate treasure map, the ancient scroll, the bringer of curses and the guide to the center of the earth, was nothing more than a child's picture.

Just as he rolled up the drawing and tied the ribbon back on, a loud "Caw! Caw!" sounded nearby, and Mr. Crow landed with a thump not far from Sandy. "Aha, Sandy Chipmunk! I knew it! I knew you would find it! Give me the scroll! Let me see the ancient writings!" Mr. Crow demanded, his eyes gleaming with anticipation.

Sandy Chipmunk held out the rolled drawing. "Here it is, Mr. Crow."

Mr. Crow snatched it impatiently and quickly untied the ribbon. He unrolled the paper, expecting to see cryptic symbols. Instead, he saw the wobbly house, the yellow sun, and the stick figure. He stared at it, his beak slightly open. He turned it this way, and then that way.

"What is this?" Mr. Crow finally squawked. "This is no map! This is... this is nonsense!"

"It is a drawing, Mr. Crow," Sandy Chipmunk explained. "Johnnie Green's drawing, I believe."

"A drawing!" Mr. Crow scoffed, though he still peered at it intently. "Impossible! Such an important scroll could not be a mere drawing! Perhaps it is a coded message! The house represents the location of a secret vault! The sun means it can only be opened at dawn! And this... this smiling person... perhaps it is a guardian, or a key to the riddle!" He was trying very hard to make it sound important, even to himself.

"I think, Mr. Crow," Sandy said, feeling a surge of confidence, "that it is simply a picture. Like the picture of the chipmunk on my letter, which was just a picture, not a secret message from the moon-folk."

Mr. Crow glared at Sandy. "You are being impertinent, Sandy Chipmunk! And you are mistaken! The world is full of wonders and mysteries, and sometimes the simplest things hold the deepest secrets! Besides," he added, trying to regain his dignity, "how could *you* know? You are just a chipmunk."

"And you, Mr. Crow," Sandy replied, a twinkle in his eye, "are just a crow who is very often mistaken about what he sees and what he hears."

Mr. Crow bristled, his feathers ruffled. He was about to retort with a particularly sharp insult when a familiar "Hoo! Hoo!" sounded from above. Old Man Owl glided down and perched on a nearby branch. He had watched the entire exchange, having followed the frantic search of the other animals.

"It seems, Mr. Crow," Old Man Owl hooted softly, "that Sandy Chipmunk has indeed found the truth. May I see the scroll?"

Mr. Crow, somewhat deflated, handed over the drawing. Old Man Owl gazed at Johnnie's picture for a moment. "A charming piece of art," he observed. "And a perfect example of how quickly a small kernel of truth can blossom into a most extraordinary tale. Is that not so, Chatty Magpie?" he added, looking at the black and white bird who had just arrived, drawn by Mr. Crow's loud cawing.

Chatty Magpie, seeing the drawing for herself, looked rather sheepish. Her feathers drooped. "A drawing?" she chirped faintly. "It was just a drawing all along?" But before she could dwell on her embarrassment, a particularly large, juicy worm wriggled past her branch. "Oh, look!" she exclaimed, forgetting everything else and darting after it.

The other animals, hearing Old Man Owl's wise words, slowly gathered around. Fidgety Squirrel stopped digging. Barnaby Bear ceased sniffing. Rosie Rabbit cautiously emerged from her bush. When they saw Johnnie Green's simple drawing, a collective sigh went through the glade. Some animals felt a pang of disappointment, for the idea of pirate gold and dragons had been rather thrilling. Others felt a blush of embarrassment, realizing how foolish they had been.

Sandy Chipmunk, however, felt a quiet sense of satisfaction. He had not found gold or gems, but he had found the truth, and he had done it on his own. He realized he was not so stupid after all, and that sometimes, paying close attention to the small details was more valuable than chasing after grand, exaggerated stories.

Mr. Crow, seeing that the glory of "deciphering ancient scrolls" had utterly vanished, let out a disgusted "Caw!" He snatched the drawing from Old Man Owl, crumpled it into a ball with his beak, and tossed it to the ground. "A waste of time!" he grumbled. "All of you, always believing such silly tales! I told you it was nonsense! I could have been finding real corn!" He then flapped his wings and soared away, still trying to make it seem as if *he* had been the only one who saw through the charade.

Old Man Owl merely smiled, a knowing twinkle in his eyes. He gently picked up Johnnie Green's crumpled drawing. "It is not a waste of time, Mr. Crow," he hooted softly, though Mr. Crow was already too far away to hear. "For every story, no matter how grand, begins with a small beginning. And learning the difference between the two is a wisdom worth finding."

Sandy Chipmunk retrieved the drawing. He smoothed it out carefully and folded it, tucking it gently into his cheek pouch. It was a charming picture, and it would serve as a good reminder. He thought about how quickly stories could spread, and how easily they could change from a simple drawing to a map of hidden treasures and fierce dragons. He shook his head slightly, a small smile on his face. The forest returned to its usual quiet sounds, the rustle of leaves, the chirp of birds, and in the distance, the faint, tuneless hum of Johnnie Green, unaware of the great adventure his little drawing had caused.

Sandy Chipmunk, feeling quite cross and foolish, tossed the letter away and watched it flutter down into a pile of dry leaves. He watched it fall without a single word. Mr. Crow, seeing his success, swelled his chest and gave a triumphant "Caw!" He was pleased that Sandy had done as he suggested, for Mr. Crow liked nothing better than to have his own way, especially when it came to making others follow his advice, even if that advice was not always the wisest. Sandy Chipmunk, however, did not feel triumphant at all. He felt rather empty, as if he had lost something important, even if Mr. Crow had convinced him it was not his to keep.

"There now," Mr. Crow said, preening a feather on his wing. "That's better, isn't it? No use keeping something that doesn't belong to you, my boy. Besides, I told you it was a foolish letter, full of other people's pictures. What good is a letter like that to a chipmunk? It's much better off scattered in the leaves, where no one will bother with it again."

Sandy Chipmunk did not reply. He merely stared at the fallen envelope, a tiny splash of white against the browns and golds of the autumn floor. He had been so proud to find it. He had been so sure it was meant for him. And now, at Mr. Crow's urging, he had cast it aside. A small tear, which he quickly blinked away, gathered in the corner of his eye. He did not like

Sandy Chipmunk did not like to be told what to do, especially when he knew, deep in his small chipmunk heart, that what he had done was not right. He had been so proud, so truly proud, of that letter, with the picture of a chipmunk that looked so very much like himself. And now it lay there, a crumpled piece of white paper, a sad little smudge against the cheerful autumn leaves. Mr. Crow, however, saw nothing of Sandy's sadness. He was too full of his own cleverness. He fluffed his black feathers and took a step closer to Sandy, eyeing him with a look that was meant to be wise, but only succeeded in being a bit too self-satisfied.

"You see, Sandy," Mr. Crow began, in a voice that was full of the sound of dry leaves skittering across a hard road, "I told you it was a foolish thing. A waste of your time, and certainly no good to you. Other people's pictures, indeed! Why, that's almost as bad as beechnuts for my breakfast!" Mr. Crow laughed a harsh, grating laugh, as if he had made the cleverest joke in the world. He did not notice Sandy's lip trembling ever so slightly, or the forlorn way the little chipmunk kept looking at the discarded paper.

Sandy longed to pick it up, to smooth it out and hold it tight, but Mr. Crow's gaze was upon him, and Sandy was, at that moment, rather afraid of Mr. Crow. Mr. Crow, for all his boasting and his loud caws, was a large bird, and Sandy was a very small chipmunk. It seemed easier, for now, to let the paper lie.

"Now, Sandy," Mr. Crow continued, pacing back and forth with important strides, "you listen to old Mr. Crow. I have seen many winters. I have seen many silly things happen in Pleasant Valley. And I can tell you that the best thing to do with things that don't belong to you is to cast them aside. No good ever comes of keeping what is not rightfully yours. And that letter, my boy, was certainly not yours, no matter how many chipmunks were drawn upon it."

Sandy wanted to argue. He wanted to say, "But it *had* my picture on it!" He wanted to say, "And the man left it open, so it must have been meant for anyone who found it!" But the words stuck in his throat like a dry beechnut. He merely nodded, a very small, reluctant nod.

Mr. Crow took this as agreement. "Excellent!" he boomed. "A wise decision, Sandy. Now, I must be going. I have important business to attend to. Perhaps I shall find a nice patch of corn that Farmer Green has overlooked. A clever crow knows where to look for such things, you see." And with another triumphant "Caw!" Mr. Crow launched himself into the air, his black wings beating a steady rhythm against the clear autumn sky. He flew off towards Farmer Green's fields, leaving Sandy Chipmunk alone with his feelings of regret and the discarded letter.

Sandy waited until Mr. Crow was but a black speck in the distance. Then, slowly, cautiously, he crept towards the spot where the paper lay. He looked at it again. It was just a piece of paper, crumpled and a little smudged. But in his mind, it was still a letter, a personal message, meant just for him. He reached out a tiny paw, hesitated, and then drew it back. Mr. Crow's words, about not keeping what was not rightfully his, echoed in his ears. Sandy was a good chipmunk, and he did not wish to steal, even if he believed the letter was truly his. But oh, how he wished he had kept it!

He sat there for a long time, watching the paper. The wind, a mischievous little breeze, came skipping through the leaves and nudged the crumpled letter. It rolled over once, twice, and then, catching a stronger gust, it lifted slightly and fluttered away, dancing like a tiny white ghost among the browns and reds of the forest floor. Sandy watched it go, feeling a pang of loss. He knew he should not follow it. He knew he should leave it alone. But something tugged at his little chipmunk heart. It was *his* letter, after all, no matter what Mr. Crow had said. And it felt wrong to simply let it disappear.

So, despite his earlier hesitation, Sandy Chipmunk found himself scampering after the fluttering paper. It was not easy. The wind was playful, and the letter seemed to delight in leading him on a merry chase. It would settle for a moment beneath a dry oak leaf, only to be whisked away again just as Sandy almost reached it. He darted this way and that, his little paws padding softly over the crunchy leaves, his bright eyes fixed on the white speck.

The chase led him deeper into the woods, further away from the cross-roads and the familiar oak tree with the mailbox. He ran

through a thicket of thorny bushes, their sharp points snagging at his fur. He scrambled up a small, rocky incline, his claws scraping for purchase. The wind, it seemed, delighted in the chase, for the letter would dance just out of reach, sometimes settling for a tantalizing moment on a low branch, only to be snatched away again just as Sandy gathered himself for a leap. His little lungs burned, and his powerful chipmunk legs, though accustomed to much running, began to ache. He was farther from his burrow than he had ever been, deeper into the part of the woods where the trees grew taller and more ancient, their roots coiling across the forest floor like slumbering giants.

The air grew cooler here, and the light, already dim beneath the dense canopy of autumn leaves, seemed to recede altogether. The familiar chirps and rustles of the day gave way to a deeper hush, broken only by the persistent whisper of the wind and Sandy’s own hurried breathing. He was starting to feel a prickle of unease. This was not his usual territory, nor the comfortable hunting grounds of Frisky Squirrel or Fatty Coon. It felt wilder, older, full of unseen eyes.

Suddenly, the letter, as if tiring of its flight, began a more determined descent. It spiraled downward, slower now, towards a patch of particularly dark earth at the foot of an enormous, moss-covered oak. Sandy, with a burst of renewed energy, darted forward, his eyes fixed on the white speck. He slid to a halt just as the letter settled gently on a cushion of thick, emerald-green moss. It lay there, perfectly still, a beacon in the twilight gloom.

Sandy crept forward, cautiously, almost reverently. He reached out a tiny paw, his heart thumping in his chest like a frantic drum. This time, he thought, this time it was truly his. No wind to snatch it away, no Mr. Crow to mock his efforts. Just him, and his letter.

But as his paw stretched towards the paper, a low, guttural growl rumbled from beneath the mossy oak. Sandy froze. His fur bristled, and his whiskers twitched nervously. He had heard that sound before, deep in the woods, and it was never a friendly one.

From the shadows, a large, dark shape began to stir. It was a badger, old and grizzled, its keen eyes narrowed into slits, fixed directly upon Sandy. The badger emerged slowly, its broad head, striped with black and white, turning towards the audacious chipmunk.

"Well, well, what have we here?" the badger rumbled, its voice like stones tumbling down a hill. "A young, foolish chipmunk, trespassing on my sleeping grounds. And reaching for my private papers, no less."

Sandy squeaked, a high-pitched sound of pure terror. "Y-your papers, sir?" he stammered, backing away slowly. "I... I beg your pardon, but this is a letter. It fell, and I was merely attempting to retrieve it."

"Fell, you say?" the badger sniffed, nudging the paper with its long, powerful nose. "It fell right here, into my cozy nook. And anything that falls into my cozy nook, little fellow, is mine. By right of discovery."

Sandy looked at the badger, then at the letter. He wanted to argue. He wanted to say, "But it had my picture! Mr. Crow said it wasn't yours!" But the words caught in his throat. The badger was much larger than he was, and its claws looked very sharp indeed.

"But... but it's a letter," Sandy tried again, his voice trembling. "It's not... it's not a nut, or a berry. It's just paper."

The badger let out another low growl. "Paper or not, it is here. And I do not appreciate small, chattering creatures disturbing my slumber, or attempting to pilfer from my private collection of curiosities. I have many such things, buried deep within my sett. Leaves of unusual shape, particularly smooth stones, even a lost button from Farmer Green’s coat. This paper shall join them."

Sandy knew he was defeated. He could not fight a badger. And even if he could, the badger’s claim, by right of discovery, was as old as the woods themselves. Mr. Crow’s earlier words about what truly belonged to whom echoed in his ears, but this time, the lesson felt much harsher, much more immediate.

"I... I understand," Sandy whispered, backing away further, until he felt the cold bark of a tree against his back. "I apologize for disturbing you, sir."

The badger grunted, satisfied. It bent its head, and with a surprisingly delicate movement of its nose and paws, it scooped up the letter, then turned and disappeared back into the dark shadows beneath the oak. The moss seemed to close around it, leaving no trace of its passage.

Sandy stood there for a long moment, trembling not just from fear, but from the crushing weight of disappointment. He had chased the letter for so long, faced the scorn of Mr. Crow, braved the icy stream, and now, it was gone, lost to a grumpy badger who would merely add it to its collection of useless trinkets. It was not a nut, it was not a berry, it was not even a delicious mushroom. It was just paper. And now it was gone, forever.

He felt a deep, profound weariness settle over him. He was tired, cold, and utterly disheartened. The sun had completely set, and the forest was now shrouded in true darkness. The hoot of an owl echoed in the distance, a mournful sound that seemed to mirror Sandy’s own feelings. He knew he had to get home. He had to find his way back through the unfamiliar woods.

The journey back was slow and fraught with anxiety. Every rustle of leaves, every snap of a twig, made Sandy jump. He imagined unseen eyes watching him, silent predators lurking in the gloom. He missed the familiar scents, the familiar landmarks. He scurried, pausing often to listen, to sniff the air, trying to orient himself. It seemed to take hours, though in truth it was probably only a short while. The moon, a thin sliver of white, began to rise above the treetops, casting long, eerie shadows.

Finally, after what felt like an eternity, he caught a familiar scent—the earthy smell of his own burrow. He stumbled towards it, exhausted, and collapsed inside, relieved beyond words to be home. He curled into a tight ball amongst his stores of nuts, shivering, trying to banish the memory of the grumpy badger and the lost letter.

He awoke the next morning feeling stiff and out of sorts. The cold he had caught from his swim in the stream had settled in his bones, and a tiny cough ruffled his fur. He spent the day huddled in his burrow, nibbling on beechnuts, too tired and dispirited to venture out.

Later that afternoon, a familiar chirping sound heralded the arrival of Frisky Squirrel. Frisky, always full of boundless energy, scampered into Sandy’s burrow, his bushy tail twitching excitedly.

Sandy merely groaned. "I know, Frisky. Jasper Jay told me."

Sandy sat up, a tiny flicker of hope, quickly extinguished by despair, passing through him. "Invitations?"

"Yes! Mr. Crow said they love to write letters! And he said they especially love to put pictures of animals on the envelopes! He even said they specifically asked Farmer Green about good places to plant beechnut trees!"

Sandy felt a sudden, sharp pang in his chest. Everything Jasper Jay had said, everything Frisky was now repeating, confirmed his worst fears. The letter had been an invitation. A genuine, honest-to-goodness invitation, meant for him and all his friends. And he had, first, nearly ruined it in the stream, and then, allowed a grumpy badger to claim it.

the only, invitation meant especially for him. Sandy Chipmunk felt a cold prickle of dismay, not unlike the chill that still lingered in his bones. He had been so proud, so certain, that the letter with the chipmunk’s picture was his alone. And in his foolish pride, he had dismissed Mr. Crow’s wise (though perhaps a trifle mocking) advice. Now, the grand winter feast, about which Frisky Squirrel chattered with such excitement, seemed a distant, unattainable dream.

“A winter feast!” Sandy repeated to himself, the words tasting like ashes in his mouth. “And I threw away my chance for a warm meal and good company, all because of a grumpy badger and my own pigheadedness.” He sighed, a small, wheezing sound that made his chest ache.

His reputation, he realized with a sinking heart, was probably ruined. He would find out if there were more invitations, and if so, he would make sure to secure one, honestly this time. It was not a grand house like Farmer Green’s, but a smaller, cozier cottage, set a little further back from the road, almost nestled into the edge of the woods. He watched them bring out small dishes, not brimming with a grand feast as he had imagined, but with handfuls of seeds for the birds, or occasionally, a few cracked nuts for the squirrels. Sandy’s stomach rumbled at the sight of the nuts, but he held back. “Patience, Sandy, patience,” he told himself, remembering Mr. Crow’s counsel. Sandy felt a pang of his old desire, the urge to gather, to hoard, but he resisted. He had a message, a personal message, and it asked him not to take.

It was a path to a mission, a mission of stealth and cunning.

He kept to the deepest shadows, darting from behind one tree to the cover of another, his head swiveling constantly to scan the surroundings. He was, as Mr. Crow had reminded him, very good at stealth, and he took great pride in his ability to move unseen. Today, however, the stakes felt higher. Farmer Green was not just a general threat, a large bipedal being to be avoided; he was a specific threat, a man who remembered a chipmunk with a letter, and who had thrown stones. Sandy did not wish to experience another hail of pebbles, however inaccurately aimed.

Soon, he could see the edge of the woods and the clearing where the great oak tree stood, its ancient branches reaching out like welcoming arms. The mail-box, a dark, boxy shape, was still nailed firmly to its trunk. Sandy paused at the treeline, his little nose twitching, testing the air. He listened with all the keenness of a wild creature, sorting through the sounds of the forest for any hint of Farmer Green’s heavy footsteps or the distant rumble of the letter-carrier’s wagon. The air was quiet, save for the whisper of the wind through the leaves and the distant

hum of a honeybee searching for late blossoms. Sandy waited. He did not move a muscle, save for the slight twitch of his whiskers. He was a small brown statue, perched at the very edge of the deep green woods, his eyes fixed upon the distant oak. He waited so long that the shadows began to stretch themselves across the clearing, growing longer and longer, like thin, dark fingers reaching out from the trees.

The sun, which had been high and bright, now began its slow descent towards the western hills, painting the sky in shades of orange and purple. Still, Sandy waited. He had learned from long experience that patience was often a chipmunk’s best friend. Haste, as Mr. Crow had so often said, might catch a berry, but patience caught the whole bush.

At last, just as the first few stars began to prick holes in the darkening sky, a sound reached Sandy’s ears. It was a familiar sound, a clatter and a rumble, not loud, but steady and rhythmic. It was the sound of a wagon, drawn by a single horse, and driven by the very man he was waiting for. It was the letter-carrier.

Sandy pressed himself closer to the ground, his heart giving a little flutter, like a trapped moth. He watched as the wagon pulled up to the cross-roads, just as it always did. The man, a tall, thin fellow with a broad-brimmed hat, climbed down from his seat and walked directly to the mail-box. He was whistling a cheerful tune, quite unaware of the small brown eyes watching him from the edge of the woods.

The man opened the lid of the mail-box, dropped in a few more letters, and then, with a satisfied nod, he closed it firmly. Sandy breathed a sigh of relief. This time, the lid was closed. Farmer Green’s warning, it seemed, had been taken to heart. The letter-carrier then climbed back into his wagon, gave a gentle tug on the reins, and with another clatter and rumble, he drove away down the road, leaving Sandy alone in the gathering twilight.

Now was his chance. Sandy waited until the sound of the wagon had faded entirely, until the last whisper of its wheels had been swallowed by the vast silence of the evening. Then, with a sudden burst of energy, he darted out from the woods. He ran across the clearing, his tiny legs a blur against the fading light, and scrambled up the rough bark of the great oak tree.

He reached the mail-box, a dark, rectangular shape against the rough grey bark. He sniffed at the closed lid, testing it, trying to find a way in. He pushed and he pulled, but the lid was firmly shut. It was a metal box, and a chipmunk, no matter how clever or determined, could not open a metal box that had been snapped shut.

Sandy Chipmunk sat on top of the mail-box, feeling a wave of disappointment wash over him. He had come all this way, braved the fearsome memory of Farmer Green’s stones, and all for nothing. He looked at the letter in his paw, the one with the picture of a chipmunk on it. It had been his prize, his special discovery. But now it seemed useless, a mere scrap of paper if he could not exchange it for something more substantial. Mr. Crow’s words about the "worthless scrap" echoed in his mind.

“Worthless, indeed!” Sandy muttered to himself, not quite believing it. He looked down at the other papers he had tucked under his chin, the ones Mr. Crow had called "prizes." They were crumpled and a bit damp from his journey, but they were still there. He pulled them out, one by one, and spread them on the lid of the mail-box. They were printed papers, just like his chipmunk letter, but without any pictures he could recognize. He could not read them, of course, but they felt important. They were crisp and had a particular smell, a smell of ink and paper that seemed to speak of distant places and hidden meanings.

He sat there for a long time, contemplating his predicament. The night grew darker still. The stars shone brightly above him, like tiny diamond chips scattered on a velvet cloth. A cool breeze rustled through the leaves of the oak, whispering secrets only the trees understood.

Then, a thought, small and bright, like one of those very stars, flickered in Sandy’s mind. Mr. Crow had said Farmer Green was "predictable." He would surely come to his favorite oak tree. He would surely come to check his mail. And when he did, he would open the box.

Sandy’s disappointment began to fade, replaced by a new feeling, a feeling of anticipation. He looked at the mail-box, then at the papers, and then back at the mail-box. A plan, cunning and a little mischievous, began to form in his clever chipmunk brain. It was a plan that required more patience, and a touch of daring.

He carefully tucked the chipmunk letter back into his cheek pouch, next to a few beechnuts he had stored there for later. Then, with the other papers, the ones Mr. Crow had called "worthless," he began to work. He did not tear them, nor did he chew them. Instead, he folded them, very carefully, into small, compact bundles. He worked quickly, but with precision, for Sandy Chipmunk was very good with his paws when he put his mind to it.

When he was finished, he had several small, neat packets of paper. He took one of these packets, the largest one, and slipped it into a tiny crack between the lid of the mail-box and the box itself. It was just wide enough to hold the paper, but not so wide that it would be easily seen. He tucked it in deep, so that it would not blow away in the night wind.

Then, with the remaining papers, he climbed higher into the oak tree. He found a snug hollow in one of the thickest branches, a place where he often rested during his busy days. He pushed the remaining folded papers deep into the hollow, covering them with a few fallen leaves and a bit of moss. He wanted to be sure they would be safe, and dry.

"There," Sandy whispered to himself, feeling a surge of satisfaction. "Now I shall wait."

And wait he did. He waited through the long night, curled up in his hollow, listening to the night sounds. He heard the hoot of an owl, the rustle of a mouse in the leaves below, and the gentle sighing of the wind. He slept a little, but his sleep was light and easily broken, for he was filled with the excitement of his secret plan.

He cleaned his whiskers, stretched his little legs, and then, with cautious movements, he made his way down the oak tree to a branch overlooking the mail-box. He hid himself amongst the thick green leaves, so well hidden that even the sharpest eye would not have noticed him.

"What's this?" Farmer Green muttered to himself.

Sandy held his breath, his little heart thumping against his ribs.

Farmer Green stared at the paper. His frown deepened. "Aha!" he exclaimed. "Another one of those pesky critters! Still trying to get into my mail, are they?" He looked around, his eyes scanning the tree branches, but Sandy was too well hidden to be seen.

Farmer Green crumpled the paper into a ball and threw it onto the ground with an angry grunt. He then opened the mail-box, took out a few letters and a newspaper, and after a moment of hesitation, he closed the lid firmly. Farmer Green had taken the bait. The paper, once "worthless" in the eyes of Mr. Crow, had served its purpose. It had confirmed to Farmer Green that someone was still trying to meddle with his mail. It had given him reason to be vigilant, to close the box extra tightly.

But Sandy knew a secret that Farmer Green did not know. He knew that the letter-carrier, in his haste, often did not push the lid all the way down, leaving a tiny, almost invisible gap. It was not enough for a chipmunk to slip into, but it was enough for something else.

Sandy carefully unfolded the crumpled paper. He smoothed it out as best he could with his tiny paws. Then, with a glint in his eye, he began to work again. He took the paper, and using the sharp edges of his teeth, he began to tear it into thin, narrow strips. He worked methodically, patiently, until he had a small pile of paper ribbons.

He took one of these paper ribbons, the longest one, and very carefully, he pushed it into the almost invisible gap at the top of the mail-box lid. He pushed and he pushed, wriggling it back and forth, until a small portion of the paper ribbon was hanging inside the mail-box. He did the same with another strip, and then another, until there were several paper ribbons dangling temptingly just inside the box.

"Now," Sandy thought to himself, a triumphant grin on his face, "we shall see."

He then retreated back up the oak tree, to his hidden perch amongst the leaves. He wanted to observe, to see what would happen next. He knew that Mr. Crow would have approved of his cunning. He knew that this was a gamble, but a calculated one. He was not trying to get *into* the mail-box this time. He was trying to get something *out* of it.

Hours passed. The sun climbed higher in the sky. The forest was alive with the busy sounds of the day. Bees buzzed amongst the wild flowers. Birds sang their cheerful songs. But Sandy kept his eyes fixed on the mail-box, waiting.

And then, he saw it. A flash of movement, a flicker of grey. A tiny mouse, no bigger than Sandy’s thumb, was scurrying along the base of the oak tree. It was a field mouse, one of the many little creatures who lived in the vicinity of Farmer Green’s house. This particular mouse, whose name was Minnie, was always on the lookout for interesting things.

Minnie Mouse stopped at the base of the oak. She twitched her nose, sniffing at the ground. She saw the crumpled paper that Farmer Green had thrown away. She cautiously approached it, sniffed it again, and then, finding nothing of interest, she began to move on.

But just as she was about to leave, her sharp little eyes caught sight of something else. Something hanging from the mail-box. Little paper ribbons. Minnie Mouse loved ribbons. She loved anything new and interesting, and these paper ribbons, dangling so tantalizingly, seemed very interesting indeed.

She scurried up the trunk of the oak tree, her tiny claws finding purchase on the rough bark. She reached the mail-box, and with a squeak of delight, she began to tug at one of the paper ribbons. She pulled and she pulled, her tiny paws working furiously. The ribbon, caught by the slight friction of the metal lid, resisted at first. But Minnie Mouse was determined. She gave a mighty tug, and with a faint ripping sound, a long strip of paper came free.

Minnie Mouse squeaked with triumph. She held the paper ribbon in her mouth, her whiskers quivering with excitement. She then scurried down the tree, eager to take her prize back to her nest.

Sandy watched her go, a slow smile spreading across his face. "Excellent!" he thought. "She found one!"

But he knew one ribbon would not be enough. He watched as Minnie Mouse disappeared into the undergrowth. Then, he continued to wait. Other mice came by. A tiny shrew, always hungry, paused at the mail-box, but finding no food, hurried on. A beetle, shiny and black, crawled over the box, oblivious to the dangling paper.

It was almost midday when Sandy saw a larger mouse, one of Minnie’s brothers, named Mortimer. Mortimer Mouse was a bit bolder than Minnie, and also a bit more curious. He, too, noticed the paper ribbons. He climbed the tree, and with a confident tug, he pulled out another one. And then, surprisingly, he pulled out a second one. Mortimer, it seemed, was a collector.

As the day wore on, Sandy watched several more small creatures discover his paper ribbons. Not all of them took one. Some were too timid. Some were too busy looking for food. But enough of them did. By late afternoon, Sandy’s pile of paper ribbons had almost completely disappeared into the insides of the mail-box.

Sandy waited a little longer, until the sun began to cast long shadows once more. He wanted to make sure that his ingenious plan had fully played out. When he was certain no more tiny creatures would be interested in his paper strips, he scampered down the tree. He pulled on the mail-box lid. It was still closed, still tight. But he knew his work was done.

He scampered back to his secret hollow in the tree. He retrieved the remaining packets of folded papers, the ones he had hidden so carefully. These, he thought, might be for another purpose. He then made his way back through the woods, his heart lighter than it had been in days. He still had his chipmunk letter, tucked safely in his pouch, and he still had his hidden treasures. And he had, he believed, outwitted Farmer Green.

He reached his burrow just as the last glow of sunset was fading from the sky. He was tired, but very satisfied. He carefully stored his remaining papers in a dry corner of his burrow, along with his hoard of beechnuts. Then, he curled up in his nest of soft leaves, and was soon fast asleep, dreaming of clever plans and daring adventures.

The next morning, Sandy woke with a refreshed spirit. He decided to pay a visit to Mr. Crow. He found the old bird perched on a high branch of a tall pine tree, surveying his domain with a keen eye.

"Good morning, Mr. Crow!" Sandy chirped, scrambling up a nearby branch.

"Ah, Sandy Chipmunk," Mr. Crow replied, turning his head slowly. "You are up early. Did you succeed in your mission? Did you retrieve any interesting 'prizes' from the mail-box?" There was a hint of a twinkle in Mr. Crow's sharp black eyes.

Sandy recounted his adventure, telling Mr. Crow all about his waiting, about Farmer Green’s vigilance, and about his cunning plan with the paper ribbons. He described how the mice and other tiny creatures had, unknowingly, helped him.

Mr. Crow listened intently, bobbing his head slowly from time to time. When Sandy had finished, there was a moment of silence. Then, Mr. Crow let out a loud, delighted "Haw-haw-haw!" that echoed through the quiet woods.

"Well, I'll be!" Mr. Crow exclaimed, wiping a tear from his eye with a claw. "That was indeed a clever trick, little chipmunk! You used your brains, just as I knew you would. So, you retrieved some of Farmer Green’s mail, did you?"

Sandy shook his head. "No, Mr. Crow. I did not retrieve any of Farmer Green's letters. I merely put my own 'worthless scraps' into the box, and then watched as the little mice took them out."

Mr. Crow blinked. "You did what, now?"

"I tore up the paper I had, the one Farmer Green threw away, and I used the strips to lure the mice," Sandy explained again. "I did not want to touch Farmer Green's mail. It is not mine, after all. Mr. Crow stared at Sandy for a long moment. Then, he let out another, even louder "Haw-haw-haw!" He was so amused that he almost fell off his branch.

"Yes, Mr. Crow," Sandy replied, looking a little confused by Mr. Crow's outburst of laughter. "Was that not clever?"

He will have found his mail-box empty, or nearly so, and he will assume that the 'meddlesome creatures' have been at it again. And he will be even more determined to keep his mail safe. Sandy’s ears drooped a little. "Oh," he said, a note of disappointment in his voice. "So my plan did not work?"

Sandy looked at the papers, then at Mr. Crow. "But what secret could these hold, Mr. Crow? I cannot read them."

Sandy was puzzled. "Missing, Mr. Crow?"

"Precisely," Mr. Crow replied. "Consider this: why would a letter with a picture of a chipmunk be in Farmer Green's mail-box? And why would it cause him such a fuss?"

Sandy’s brow furrowed in thought. He had not considered the chipmunk letter from Farmer Green’s perspective. He had only thought of it as _his_ letter.

"Perhaps," Mr. Crow continued, "that letter is not quite what it seems. Perhaps it is a clue to something else. And perhaps," he said, looking pointedly at the other folded papers Sandy held, "these 'worthless scraps' might hold another part of the puzzle."

Sandy looked at the chipmunk letter, and then at the advertisements. He felt a new sense of purpose stirring within him. He had always been a gatherer of nuts and berries, a creature of simple needs. But now, it seemed, he was becoming a gatherer of mysteries. And with Mr. Crow’s cryptic advice, he felt quite certain that this new hobby would be far more interesting than merely hoarding beechnuts.

"What shall I do, Mr. Crow?" Sandy asked, his eyes bright with excitement.

"For now," Mr. Crow said, with a wise nod of his head, "keep your treasures safe. Observe. Listen. And perhaps, my clever little chipmunk, you might discover that the greatest adventures are not always found in chasing after what you want, but in understanding what you already have."

With that, Mr. Crow launched himself into the air and flew off, leaving Sandy Chipmunk perched on his branch, surrounded by the rustling leaves and the warming sun, contemplating the strange and wonderful mysteries of paper, pictures, and the unpredictable nature of Farmer Green’s mail-box. There was a secret in that mail, he felt, and Sandy Chipmunk, with the help of his clever mind and the wise old Mr. Crow, intended to discover it. He had a feeling that the coming winter, which he had once dreaded, might turn out to be the most interesting season of all. And perhaps, just perhaps, he would even learn to read. So, Sandy Chipmunk, with renewed purpose, scurried back to his burrow. He gathered all the letters and advertisements, careful to keep them dry and safe. The next day, he brought them one by one to Mr. Crow, who examined each picture with a feigned air of deep concentration. "This shovel, Sandy," Mr. Crow declared, tapping a picture with his beak, "this tells us that there is a treasure buried near the big oak tree by Farmer Green's apple orchard. A treasure of _very_ special worms, quite plump and juicy, much larger than ordinary worms." Sandy's whiskers twitched. Worms were not exactly a chipmunk's first choice, but plump and juicy worms did sound rather enticing, especially if they were _special_.

Mr. Crow then pointed to the stout boots. "These boots, Sandy, they show us where Farmer Green walks after the rain. And where Farmer Green walks, there he sometimes unearths the most marvelous, sweet-smelling mushrooms, quite unlike any you have ever tasted. They grow in a hidden grove, near the old stone wall." And so Mr. Crow went on, fabricating tales of hidden delights, always connecting them to Farmer Green's supposed "maps." He did not forget the strange, toothy contraption. "Ah, this one," Mr. Crow said, a sly gleam in his eye, "this is for catching _flying corn_! Farmer Green has a special, secret breed of corn that flies through the air, and this device is for gathering it." Sandy, though slightly bewildered by the notion of flying corn, was too excited by the prospect of such exotic fare to question it too deeply.

Mr. Crow sent Sandy on many a fruitless errand. Sandy dug near the old oak, his small paws working furiously, but found nothing but common earthworms, which, while edible, were certainly not the "special worms" Mr. Crow had promised. He searched near the stone wall for the "marvelous mushrooms," but found only toadstools, which he knew were not good for eating. And he spent an entire afternoon looking for "flying corn" near the device that Mr. Crow had so craftily misinterpreted. But alas, there was no flying corn, and the contraption stood silent and empty. Sandy returned to Mr. Crow, dusty and disappointed. "There were no special worms, Mr. Crow," he complained. "And no marvelous mushrooms, either. And the flying corn, it does not fly at all!"

Mr. Crow pretended to be very perplexed. "No? Are you certain, Sandy? Perhaps you did not dig deep enough, or search in quite the right spot. Farmer Green is a very clever fellow, and he hides his treasures well. You must be more diligent, Sandy. A true treasure hunter never gives up!" And Mr. Crow encouraged Sandy to try again, sending him to new locations based on new interpretations of the pictures, always with the promise of delicious food and, of course, a share of the promised corn for Mr. Crow. Sandy, ever hopeful, kept trying. He scampered back and forth, from the oak to the stone wall, from the garden to the edge of the woods, his paws sore and his whiskers drooping.

But as the days grew shorter and the air grew colder, Sandy began to feel a growing suspicion. He had spent so much time on Mr. Crow's "treasure hunts" that he had neglected his own winter preparations. His store of beechnuts and acorns was not as full as it should have been. And Mr. Crow, though he still promised vast amounts of hidden corn, had not once offered to share any of the ordinary corn from Farmer Green's fields, nor had he done any gathering himself. Sandy began to think that perhaps Mr. Crow was not as wise or as generous as he pretended to be. He began to look at the "maps" again, and then at Mr. Crow, with new eyes.

One afternoon, while Sandy was dutifully searching for "giant, sweet-smelling root vegetables" near Farmer Green's tool shed, he saw Farmer Green himself. Farmer Green was not wearing his stout boots, nor was he carrying his shiny shovel. Instead, he was walking slowly, carrying a small metal object in his hand. Sandy flattened himself against a fallen log, watching carefully. Farmer Green walked to the edge of his field, near the old fence post. There, he knelt down and placed the metal object on the ground, carefully baiting it with a piece of dried corn. Then he straightened up, gave a satisfied nod, and walked back towards his house.

Sandy waited until Farmer Green was well out of sight. Then, very cautiously, he crept towards the fence post. His heart gave a frightened leap when he saw the object Farmer Green had placed there. It was the "special collecting device" that Mr. Crow had called a trap for "flying corn." But there was no flying corn. There was only a single, ordinary kernel of corn, sitting invitingly inside the spring-loaded contraption. Sandy's fur prickled with a sudden, chilling understanding. This was no collecting device for flying corn. This was a _trap_, plain and simple. And the corn was _bait_.

A cold, hard knot formed in Sandy's stomach. Mr. Crow had lied to him. Mr. Crow had known all along what the device was, and he had sent Sandy to look for "flying corn" near it, perhaps hoping Sandy would get caught. And all those "maps" to hidden treasures – the special worms, the marvelous mushrooms – they were all just tricks to keep Sandy busy, to keep him from his own gathering, and perhaps even to put him in danger. A deep anger began to simmer within Sandy, an anger he had never felt before. He had been a fool, a stupid chipmunk indeed, to trust Mr. Crow.

Sandy returned to his burrow, his paws no longer nimble, his heart heavy. He looked at the chipmunk letter, the one he had believed was so proudly his, and then at the other advertisements. He pulled out the paper with the picture of the trap. It was the very image of the device Farmer Green had just set! The shovel, the boots, the "collecting device" – they were not maps to treasure. They were pictures of Farmer Green's _tools_, and one of them was a _tool for catching small animals_. Sandy felt a new kind of cold, a cold that seeped into his very bones. Farmer Green was not just a farmer who lost his mail; he was also a danger.

Sandy knew he had to do something. He could not keep these dangerous "maps" in his burrow, and he certainly could not keep them from Farmer Green any longer. But he also knew he could not simply return them, for that would mean facing Farmer Green, and Farmer Green had already chased him and thrown stones. And he certainly would not ask Mr. Crow for help. Mr. Crow could not be trusted. Sandy sat for a long time, chewing on a particularly tough acorn, thinking harder than he had ever thought before. He had to be clever. He had to be _smarter_ than Mr. Crow, and even smarter than Farmer Green, if he was to keep himself safe and still do what was right.

The solution came to him as he looked at the mail-box again in his mind's eye. The mail-box, that strange, open maw on the tree at the crossroads. Farmer Green expected things to be put _into_ it, not taken _out_ of it. And the mailman came every day, or so Mr. Crow had said, to put things in. If Sandy could put the letters back, without being seen, and without getting caught, then all would be well. But how to do it safely? He couldn't just walk up to the mail-box in broad daylight. Farmer Green was always about, and the mailman came at a regular time.

Sandy waited for dusk, when the shadows grew long and everything became indistinct. He waited until the air was sharp with the promise of frost, and most sensible creatures were already nestled in their burrows. Then, very carefully, he gathered all the letters, rolling them into a tight bundle that he could carry in his mouth. He slipped out of his burrow, moving like a whisper through the rustling leaves, keeping to the deepest shadows. He had to be quick. He had to be unseen. This was a mission of great importance, not just for Farmer Green's mail, but for Sandy's own peace of mind, and perhaps even for his very safety.

He reached the oak tree at the cross-roads. The mail-box stood there, a dark, rectangular shape against the darkening sky. Its lid was, thankfully, still ajar, just as the mailman often left it. Sandy paused, listening intently. The only sounds were the soft sighing of the wind through the branches and the distant hoot of an owl. No sign of Farmer Green. No rumble of a wagon. Now was the time. Sandy scrambled up the tree trunk, his claws finding purchase in the rough bark. He was swift and silent, a mere blur against the trunk. He reached the box, balanced precariously on the edge, and with a quick flick of his head, he dropped the entire bundle of letters back inside.

He did not linger. The moment the last paper rustled into the box, Sandy turned and raced back down the tree, across the open ground, and vanished into the shadows of the woods. He did not stop until he was safely back inside his burrow, panting, but with a strange sense of accomplishment. The letters were returned. The dangerous "maps" were no longer in his possession. He had been clever, after all. And Mr. Crow, with all his cunning, had not succeeded in making a fool of Sandy Chipmunk quite so easily this time.

The next morning, when the mailman came by, he was quite surprised to find a large bundle of mail, mostly advertisements, already inside the mail-box. "Well, isn't that something!" he muttered to himself, scratching his head. "Farmer Green must have been tidying up and decided to put his old papers back in for collection. Never seen him do that before!" The mailman shrugged, added Farmer Green's new letters, and closed the lid firmly. He would, of course, tell this strange tale at the post office later that day.

Meanwhile, Mr. Crow, perched high in his oak, watched the mailman. He also watched for Sandy Chipmunk, expecting him to come with news of new "treasures" or perhaps a complaint about the lack of "flying corn." But Sandy did not appear. Mr. Crow waited and waited. "That Sandy," he cawed to himself, "he must still be out searching for those worms. Or perhaps he _did_ find some flying corn and is keeping it all to himself!" Mr. Crow, ever suspicious, felt a pang of annoyance. He decided he would go to Sandy's burrow later and demand his share of the supposed bounty.

But Sandy Chipmunk was not hunting for "treasures." He was back to his usual, sensible work, gathering beechnuts and acorns with renewed vigor. He had learned a valuable lesson about trusting the motives of others, especially when those others stood to gain from his efforts. He had also learned that the human world, with its strange mail-boxes and mysterious pictures and dangerous traps, was best understood from a safe distance. He would stick to what he knew best: the rustle of leaves, the scent of the forest floor, and the simple, honest work of preparing for winter. And he would always keep a sharp eye on Mr. Crow, for one could never be quite certain of Mr. Crow's intentions. Winter was coming, and Sandy Chipmunk intended to be well-fed and wise, no matter what Mr. Crow might try to tempt him with next. He still had his one special letter, the one with his own picture on it, and that was all the mail he needed.

The crisp autumn air carried the scent of fallen leaves and distant wood smoke, hinting at the winter that was fast approaching. Sandy Chipmunk, feeling quite content with his decision regarding the peculiar human mail, was busy. His cheeks were puffed out with a generous store of beechnuts, his little paws worked with admirable speed, and his eyes, bright and bead-like, darted everywhere, noting every ripe acorn, every plump hazelnut. He had, as he told himself more than once, learned a valuable lesson. The world of humans, with its puzzling boxes and paper treasures, was not for him. And as for Mr. Crow, well, Sandy knew now that a kind offer from Mr. Crow often came with a hidden price, a price usually paid by Sandy himself.

Mr. Crow, however, had not forgotten Sandy Chipmunk. Not at all. After the mailman had driven off that morning, leaving him with no explanation for the sudden reappearance of Farmer Green's letters, Mr. Crow had nursed a growing suspicion. He had watched the chipmunk's burrow for a long time, expecting Sandy to emerge, perhaps with a triumphant chitter or a new, exciting piece of gossip. But Sandy had remained out of sight, deep in the earth. Mr. Crow, perched high on his favorite oak branch, shifted his weight from one scaly foot to the other. "He's hiding something," Mr. Crow cawed to himself, his head cocked to one side. "That little rascal, he's found a new treasure, I'

...I'll wager anything he has," Mr. Crow finished, his voice a low, gravelly rumble that was meant only for his own ears. He peered down at the ground below, where Sandy Chipmunk’s snug little burrow lay hidden amongst the roots of an old maple tree. Not a sign of Sandy. Not a whiskered nose, not a twitching tail, not even the faintest rustle of dry leaves. It was, to Mr. Crow's way of thinking, far too quiet. Far too secret.

Mr. Crow was, as you know, a bird of many opinions, and most of them tended to be rather strong. He was also, it must be admitted, a bird of considerable self-interest. He had spent a good deal of time that very morning, after his brief encounter with Farmer Green's mail-box, pondering the odd habits of chipmunks and squirrels, and indeed, of all small folk who scuttled about on the ground. They were forever burying things, he observed. Forever tucking away little caches of food, as if the world might suddenly run out of everything good to eat. It seemed a dreadful waste to Mr. Crow, all that digging and hiding. Why, if he were to find a particularly juicy grub, he would eat it then and there, or perhaps, if it were truly enormous, he might carry it off to his nest for a second helping later. But to bury it? To forget where it was, perhaps, and let some other creature stumble upon it? That was simply unthinkable.

And yet,

other creatures did it. They burrowed and hid, and then, if they were lucky, they remembered. Mr. Crow, for his part, had never forgotten a single thing he had put away for safekeeping. But then, he had never hidden anything in the ground, where dirt might get into it, and where all manner of crawling, burrowing things might claim it for their own. No, Mr. Crow preferred the sturdy branch of an oak, or the hollow of a tree, or even a cranny in Farmer Green’s barn, where the sparrows nested, and where, if he were careful, he might sometimes find a stray kernel of corn that had fallen from a passing wagon.

But Sandy Chipmunk was different. Sandy Chipmunk was a burrowing creature, and Mr. Crow was quite certain that Sandy had buried something. Not just the beechnuts that he saw Sandy carrying, day after day, in those ridiculously puffed-out cheeks of his. No, Mr. Crow suspected something far more interesting, far more valuable. Something that had been hidden since the morning of the mail-box, when Sandy had come scampering away with that strange piece of paper in his mouth, the very same piece of paper that Mr. Crow had then persuaded him to throw away. Perhaps, Mr. Crow reasoned, Sandy had not thrown it away at all. Perhaps he had merely pretended to, and had then doubled back, after Mr. Crow had flown off, and retrieved it. Or, perhaps, Mr. Crow mused, Sandy had found something else in that curious box. Something that

had been *left* there by mistake, something not meant for Farmer Green at all, but for a smaller creature perhaps, one with sharp eyes and quick paws. Mr. Crow’s mind, which was always busy plotting and planning, began to weave a most interesting theory. Sandy Chipmunk, he mused, was a simple fellow, easily swayed by a sharp word or a clever argument. But even simple folk could stumble upon great treasures. And Sandy, with his innocent face and his diligent habits, was just the sort to find something truly remarkable and then, being a chipmunk, bury it for safekeeping.

“Yes,” Mr. Crow said to himself, tilting his head and looking very wise indeed, “that must be it.”

He remembered the excitement in Sandy’s voice when the little chipmunk had proudly displayed his letter. The sheer, unadulterated joy of finding something _addressed_ to him, as it were. And Mr. Crow, in his usual, somewhat selfish way, had managed to spoil it. He had pricked Sandy’s balloon of happiness, as one might say, and sent the letter tumbling into the underbrush. But what if Sandy, in his innocent zeal, had actually found _two_ things? What if there had been, not one, but _two_ envelopes in the mail-box? One with a chipmunk’s picture, which Sandy had taken for himself, and another, perhaps, without a picture at all, but containing something far more interesting. Something that Farmer Green himself would never miss, being quite accustomed to receiving all sorts of dull papers and catalogs.

Mr. Crow stretched his wings and ruffled his feathers, then hopped to a higher branch of the oak tree where he had been observing Sandy’s burrow. From this vantage point, he could see a good deal of Farmer Green’s property, including the mail-box at the cross-roads. The very mail-box that had caused so much fuss and excitement. It stood there, a plain, unremarkable tin box, silent and unassuming, quite innocent of all the rumors and misinterpretations it had spawned. Mr. Crow regarded it with a new sort of respect. It was, he now realized, a source of potential riches.

He thought of the mail-carrier, the man who drove the one horse, not twelve, and who carried but a single bag, not six haystacks. This mail-carrier, Mr. Crow reasoned, was a busy fellow. He hurried from house to house, dropping letters and papers into boxes, always in a rush to finish his route. And a hurried man, Mr. Crow knew, was an absent-minded man. It was entirely possible that a small, shiny thing, perhaps a stray button or a tiny, gleaming coin, might have slipped from his fingers, or out of a torn envelope, and landed, unnoticed, in the bottom of the mail-box. Or perhaps, and this thought made Mr. Crow’s black eyes gleam, a valuable stamp, one of those especially colorful ones that humans seemed to prize, had come loose from a letter.

“A stamp!” Mr. Crow cawed softly. “That would be a fine thing indeed to add to my nest!”

He imagined the bright, jewel-like colors of a stamp, glimmering amongst the dull twigs and feathers of his home. It would be a conversation piece, a rarity, something to show off to Jasper Jay, who was forever boasting about his own collection of shiny odds and ends. Jasper Jay, as Mr. Crow well knew, had a deplorable habit of collecting bottle caps and bits of colored glass, which Mr. Crow considered quite common. But a stamp, a genuine, unused stamp, that would be a treasure of a different sort entirely.

So, with this new and exciting theory firmly lodged in his clever head, Mr. Crow decided that he must make a visit to the mail-box himself. Not to wait for the mail-carrier, of course, for Mr. Crow had no interest in dull letters. But to inspect the box, thoroughly, for anything that might have been overlooked. Perhaps Sandy, in his eagerness to find his own picture, had quite missed something else, something far more valuable in the eyes of a discerning bird like Mr. Crow.

He flew down from the oak, his powerful wings beating a steady rhythm against the morning air. He did not, however, fly directly to the mail-box. That would be too obvious. Mr. Crow was nothing if not subtle, at least in his own estimation. He landed first in Farmer Green’s apple orchard, where he pretended to be searching for fallen apples (though he cared little for apples, preferring corn, as you know). Then he hopped from tree to tree, making a leisurely path toward the cross-roads, all the while keeping a sharp eye on Sandy Chipmunk’s burrow. Not a ripple of movement. Not a sign of the little chipmunk. Good, Mr. Crow thought. Sandy was no doubt still sulking, or perhaps, more conveniently, preoccupied with his beechnuts.

When he finally reached the old oak tree at the cross-roads, Mr. Crow perched on a low branch, just above the mail-box. He peered into its dark interior. It was, as he expected, quite empty. The mail-carrier had already been and gone, leaving a fresh batch of letters for Farmer Green, which Farmer Green had, in turn, already collected. Mr. Crow was quite certain of this, for he had seen Farmer Green trudge past earlier, his large hands grasping a small bundle of white envelopes.

“Empty,” Mr. Crow muttered, a hint of disappointment in his voice. “Just as I feared.”

But then, being Mr. Crow, he did not give up so easily. He hopped down onto the edge of the mail-box, his talons clicking faintly on the tin. He leaned forward, craning his neck, and peered into the very deepest corners. His sharp, black eyes missed nothing. And there, tucked away beneath a loose flake of rust, in the very bottom corner, something caught his attention. It was not a stamp, alas, nor a shining coin. But it was, nevertheless, something small and colorful. Something that glimmered just a little in the faint sunlight that struggled into the box.

With a cautious peck of his beak, Mr. Crow dislodged the object. It was a very small, very bright piece of ribbon. Red, it was, and silken, quite soft to the touch. It had perhaps come from a package, tied around a gift, and fallen out when the mail-carrier had opened the box. Or perhaps, it had been a marker, placed inside a letter by Farmer Green’s granddaughter, who often sent him drawings. Whatever its origin, it was now Mr. Crow’s.

He picked it up delicately in his beak, admiring its vibrant color. It was certainly more interesting than Jasper Jay’s dull bits of glass. And it would look quite splendid in his nest. Perhaps, he thought, he would even tie it around a particularly plump kernel of corn, just for decoration.

Just as Mr. Crow was about to fly off with his new treasure, a sudden rustle in the leaves below made him freeze. It was Sandy Chipmunk! The little chipmunk, looking rather forlorn, was creeping cautiously toward the base of the oak tree. He kept glancing nervously about him, as if expecting Farmer Green to spring out from behind a bush.

Mr. Crow quickly decided what to do. He would not let Sandy see his new find. That would only make Sandy jealous, and Mr. Crow was quite tired of Sandy’s complaints. So he swiftly tucked the red ribbon under his wing, concealing it from view.

“Well, Sandy Chipmunk!” Mr. Crow cawed, trying to sound as if he had just arrived. “What brings you back to this curious contraption?”

Sandy Chipmunk jumped, startled, and nearly tumbled over a root. “Oh! Mr. Crow! You quite surprised me!” he exclaimed, placing a paw over his rapidly beating heart. “I—I was just looking.”

“Looking, eh?” Mr. Crow said, looking very knowing. “Looking for what, pray tell? Another letter with your picture on it?” He let out a soft caw, a rather condescending sound that was meant to make Sandy feel foolish.

Sandy’s ears drooped. “No,” he said quietly. “I was just… thinking about that letter. And how you said it wasn’t mine.”

Mr. Crow shrugged a wing, as if the matter were of no consequence. “Truth is truth, Sandy. And a letter meant for Farmer Green is a letter meant for Farmer Green. Even if it has a chipmunk’s picture on it, which, I might add, was a most peculiar thing indeed.”

“But it _was_ my picture!” Sandy insisted, his voice rising in volume. “It looked just like me!”

“Perhaps so,” Mr. Crow conceded, though he cared little for the truth of the matter. “But I daresay Farmer Green has many pictures of chipmunks. Perhaps he is fond of drawing them. Or perhaps he collects pictures of all the creatures in Pleasant Valley.” Mr. Crow thought this a very clever explanation, one that would put an end to Sandy’s silly notions.

Sandy, however, still looked unconvinced. He glanced up at the mail-box, then down at the ground. “I just… I wish I hadn’t thrown it away,” he mumbled.

Mr. Crow, who was feeling quite pleased with his new red ribbon, felt a pang of something akin to sympathy. But only a very small pang. “Well,” he said, trying to sound helpful, “what’s done is done. And besides, there are always more letters. Who knows what the mail-carrier might bring tomorrow? Perhaps a letter for _me_, for a change. One with a picture of a handsome black bird, perhaps?” He laughed at his own joke, a rather grating sound that made Sandy wince.

Sandy ignored the joke. He was still staring at the ground, right where he had tossed the letter. “I just wanted to make sure,” he said, almost to himself. “To make sure it was really gone.”

Mr. Crow, whose curiosity was now piqued, looked down. “Gone? Of course it’s gone. A foolish thing, to throw away a perfectly good piece of paper.” He watched as Sandy began to poke around in the leaves with his nose, turning them over, his whiskers twitching.

“What are you doing?” Mr. Crow demanded, his voice suddenly sharp. He wondered if Sandy had actually *not* thrown it away, but merely hidden it, as Mr. Crow himself had suspected earlier.

“Just looking,” Sandy replied, without looking up. “For the letter. I thought… perhaps it landed here.”

Mr. Crow hopped down from the mail-box, a frown on his beak. He watched Sandy’s industrious searching. “You mean to say,” he said slowly, “that you think you can find it again?”

“Maybe,” Sandy mumbled, his paws now digging into the soft earth. “It wasn’t very far. And I didn’t throw it very hard.”

Mr. Crow’s mind raced. If Sandy found the letter, he would no doubt parade it about again. And Mr. Crow would have to endure another bout of chipmunk pride. But more importantly, if Sandy *had* found something else in the mail-box, something more valuable, he might have buried *that* too, perhaps right here.

“Sandy Chipmunk,” Mr. Crow said, his voice taking on a new, wheedling tone. “Stop that foolish digging. What use is a letter to you? You cannot read the words, and the picture, as I told you, is most likely meant for Farmer Green.”

“But it _looked_ like me!” Sandy protested, pausing his digging to look up at Mr. Crow. “And the other pictures, they looked like Frisky Squirrel and Mrs. Mouse’s son, and Rinaldo Rat! They were my friends!”

Mr. Crow scoffed. “Pictures in a letter are of no importance. What truly matters is what one can _eat_. Or what one can _use_.” He thought of his red ribbon, tucked safely under his wing. Now, *that* was something useful.

Sandy sighed, then resumed his digging. “I just want to see it again,” he said.

Mr. Crow decided on a new tack. He would offer a distraction. “Tell me, Sandy,” he began, “have you gathered enough beechnuts for the winter? I heard young Frisky Squirrel complaining that you had asked him for help, but then offered him none of _your_ share.”

Sandy stopped digging again, his small nose twitching. “That’s not true! I offered him some, but he said he had already found his own.”

“Hmm,” Mr. Crow said, rubbing his beak thoughtfully. “And what about your own supply? Is it ample? Are you sure you have enough to last through the snows?”

Sandy looked worried. “I think so. I’ve been very busy. But it takes a great many beechnuts, Mr. Crow, a very great many.”

“Indeed it does,” Mr. Crow agreed. “And do you know, Sandy, I was just thinking about those beechnuts. They are, after all, a fine food. Though they do not agree with _me_, personally, I am aware that many creatures find them quite delightful.” He paused, allowing his words to sink in. “And I was also thinking, Sandy, about how much work it is to gather them. All that scurrying and digging. Perhaps, Sandy, you have too many beechnuts. Perhaps you have more than you need.”

Sandy looked confused. “Too many? Is there such a thing as too many beechnuts?”

“For a sensible chipmunk, perhaps not,” Mr. Crow said, “but for a chipmunk who wastes his time digging up old letters, perhaps so.” He lowered his voice, making it sound very confidential. “Now, Sandy, I have a most generous offer for you. A proposition, as it were.”

Sandy’s eyes widened. Mr. Crow’s propositions were often interesting, though sometimes they left Sandy feeling a little… used.

“You see, Sandy,” Mr. Crow continued, “I am in need of a certain quantity of corn. As you know, _corn_ is what I want. And you, Sandy, have a great many beechnuts. More than enough, I daresay. What if, Sandy, we were to make a trade?”

Sandy looked from Mr. Crow’s gleaming eyes to the spot where he had been digging for his letter. “A trade? What kind of trade, Mr. Crow?”

“Why, a simple one,” Mr. Crow said, spreading his wings slightly, as if to encompass the entire forest. “You bring me a goodly quantity of your beechnuts—say, a whole pawful, or perhaps even two. And in return, I will relieve you of the burden of this… searching for a lost letter. I will search for it for you. And if I find it, Sandy, I will bring it to you. And you will not have to waste any more time digging for it. Think of the time saved! Time you could use to gather _more_ beechnuts, or to enjoy the sunshine.”

Sandy’s little brow furrowed. “You would look for my letter, Mr. Crow? For my lost letter?”

“Indeed I would!” Mr. Crow declared, feeling rather pleased with himself. “I have very sharp eyes, as you know. Much sharper than yours, Sandy. I can see things you might miss. And a letter, a mere piece of paper, is a simple thing for me to spot.”

Sandy hesitated. He knew Mr. Crow was clever, and his eyes were certainly sharp. And he really did want to see his letter again. But he also knew Mr. Crow was fond of making deals that mostly benefited Mr. Crow.

“And what about the beechnuts?” Sandy asked, cautiously.

“Ah, the beechnuts!” Mr. Crow chirped. “A mere token, Sandy. A friendly exchange. You provide me with a few, just a few, of those delicious nuts that you have in such abundance. And I, in my generosity, will perform this service for you. It is a very fair trade, I assure you.”

Sandy thought about it. He had indeed spent a good deal of time gathering beechnuts. And he had a good store of them, tucked away in his burrow. And searching for a lost letter in the leaf litter seemed like a tedious business. Perhaps Mr. Crow, with his keen eyesight, could truly find it more easily.

“How many beechnuts, Mr. Crow?” Sandy asked, a hint of capitulation in his voice.

“Let us say,” Mr. Crow began, peering down at Sandy, “a very respectable pile. Enough to fill your two cheek pouches, perhaps. A generous offering for a generous service.”

Sandy considered the offer. It seemed a lot of beechnuts. But finding his letter again, the one with his picture and the pictures of his friends, that was very important to him. And Mr. Crow’s words had also made him remember his earlier worry about having enough beechnuts for winter. If Mr. Crow took some, that meant he would have to gather more, and that would keep him busy.

“Alright,” Sandy said at last, with a sigh. “I will bring you two cheek-pouches full of beechnuts, Mr. Crow. But you must promise to really look for my letter. And if you find it, you must bring it right back to me.”

“A promise freely given!” Mr. Crow cawed triumphantly. He was thinking of the beechnuts, of course, but also of the excellent opportunity this gave him. He could now poke around in the leaves with a legitimate purpose, and perhaps, if he was truly lucky, he might stumble upon something even _more_ valuable than the lost letter. A truly useful object, perhaps, or another piece of interesting ribbon, or even a real, honest-to-goodness stamp!

“Go on then, Sandy Chipmunk!” Mr. Crow urged, flapping his wings impatiently. “Hurry now! Time is precious, and the sun is high!”

Sandy, ever obliging, scurried off toward his burrow. Mr. Crow watched him go, then, once Sandy was out of sight, he dropped to the ground himself. He began to turn over the leaves, not with the quick, efficient movements of a chipmunk, but with the more deliberate, methodical pecks of a crow. He searched the spot where Sandy had been digging, and then widened his search to the surrounding area. He was not truly looking for Sandy’s letter, of course. Not really. He was looking for *anything* interesting. Anything small and shiny. Anything that might have fallen out of the mail-box.

He poked and he prodded, his black eyes scanning every twig and every pebble. He found a rather dull brown feather, which he dismissed. He found a small, desiccated beetle, which he also dismissed, for he preferred his beetles plump and juicy. He found a small, somewhat rusty nail, which he considered for a moment, thinking it might make a fine ornament, but then decided against it. Too heavy, and too prone to rust.

After a good deal of searching, Mr. Crow found nothing of interest. No shiny buttons, no lost coins, no precious stamps. And, more importantly, no sign of Sandy’s letter. It seemed the wind or some other creature had carried it away.

Just as he was about to give up, feeling a bit disappointed, he heard a rustle. Sandy Chipmunk was returning, his cheek pouches bulging. He looked like he had swallowed two small apples whole.

“Here you are, Mr. Crow!” Sandy chirped, his voice muffled by the beechnuts. He carefully deposited the nuts in a neat pile at Mr. Crow’s feet. It was a goodly amount, indeed. Many a meal for Mr. Crow, if he chose to eat them, though he still preferred corn.

Mr. Crow looked at the pile of beechnuts. He felt a flicker of triumph. He had gained a good meal, and Sandy had gained nothing but a false hope. “Excellent, Sandy! An admirable collection indeed!” he cawed, bending down to peck at a nut, just to show he was pleased. He picked one up, then another, then another, and then, with a satisfied gulp, swallowed them whole. They were still not corn, but they would do for a snack.

“And my letter, Mr. Crow?” Sandy asked, looking hopeful.

Mr. Crow shook his head, looking mournful. “Alas, Sandy! I have searched high and low, with the utmost diligence, as I promised. But the letter is quite gone. Vanished! Swept away, no doubt, by a mischievous breeze, or perhaps carried off by some other creature, thinking it a fine leaf for a nest.” He made a show of looking very regretful, though in truth, he felt rather pleased. One less silly letter to worry about.

Sandy’s face fell. He looked utterly crestfallen. “Gone?” he whispered. “My letter is gone forever?”

“I’m afraid so, Sandy,” Mr. Crow said, trying to sound as sympathetic as he could manage. “But do not despair! For you have these fine beechnuts, which will keep you well-fed through the winter. And you have learned a valuable lesson, Sandy: never throw away a letter, no matter what an old Crow might tell you!” Mr. Crow let out a sly caw, then winked.

Sandy, however, did not find it very funny. He looked at the empty spot where his letter had been, then at the cheerful Mr. Crow, who was now contentedly eating his beechnuts. A slow understanding began to dawn in Sandy’s small, chipmunk brain. He remembered Mr. Crow’s words about the corn, and how he had said he didn’t mean what he said. And now, he had promised to find the letter, but hadn’t.

“You didn’t really look for it, did you, Mr. Crow?” Sandy said, his voice surprisingly firm.

Mr. Crow stopped chewing, a beechnut halfway to his beak. “Why, Sandy! What a thing to say! Of course I looked! I searched with the greatest care!”

“No, you didn’t,” Sandy insisted. “You just wanted my beechnuts.”

Mr. Crow sputtered. “That is a most ungrateful accusation, Sandy Chipmunk! I am a bird of my word!”

“But you told me you didn’t mean what you said about the corn,” Sandy retorted. “So how do I know you mean what you say about anything?”

Mr. Crow was, once again, quite speechless. Sandy Chipmunk had a surprising way of turning Mr. Crow’s own arguments against him. He stared at Sandy, his black eyes narrowed. The little chipmunk was not as foolish as Mr. Crow sometimes liked to pretend.

“Well,” Mr. Crow finally managed, clearing his throat. “Even if the letter is gone, Sandy, you still have your beechnuts. And I still have these… delightful… nuts.” He took another peck, a bit less enthusiastically this time.

Sandy, however, had turned his back on Mr. Crow. He felt a deep sense of disappointment. Not just that his letter was gone, but that he had, once again, been tricked by Mr. Crow. He had given away his hard-earned food for a false promise. He sat down, his tail twitching, and stared at the ground.

Mr. Crow, seeing Sandy’s genuine sadness, felt a flicker of something almost like guilt. But it passed quickly. After all, he had gotten his beechnuts. And Sandy would just have to gather more. That was the way of the world.

Just then, a loud, cheerful voice broke the silence. “Hello, Sandy! What are you doing sulking by the road?”

It was Frisky Squirrel, bounding down from a nearby tree. Frisky was a lively fellow, always full of energy and chatter. He skidded to a halt beside Sandy.

“Oh, hello, Frisky,” Sandy said, his voice still low.

“And Mr. Crow!” Frisky exclaimed, noticing the black bird perched nearby. “What a gathering this is!”

Mr. Crow managed a polite, if somewhat strained, caw. He did not particularly like Frisky Squirrel, who was always so boisterous.

“What’s the matter, Sandy?” Frisky asked, nudging his friend. “You look as if you’ve lost your favorite acorn.”

“Worse than an acorn,” Sandy sighed. “I lost my letter. The one from the mail-box. Mr. Crow said he would help me find it, but he didn’t. He just wanted my beechnuts.”

Frisky Squirrel looked from Sandy to Mr. Crow, his bushy tail twitching. “Is that true, Mr. Crow?” he asked, a hint of suspicion in his voice.

Mr. Crow ruffled his feathers indignantly. “Nonsense, Frisky Squirrel! I searched most thoroughly! The letter was quite gone!”

“Well, that’s a shame,” Frisky said, shaking his head. He was a good friend to Sandy, and he didn’t like to see him upset. “But never mind, Sandy. There are always other letters. And besides, letters are just paper. They don’t taste nearly as good as beechnuts.”

“But it had my picture on it!” Sandy wailed. “And pictures of you, and Mrs. Mouse’s son, and Rinaldo Rat!”

Frisky’s eyes widened. “It did? My picture? And Mrs. Mouse’s son? And Rinaldo Rat?” He looked at Mr. Crow, his suspicion deepening. “You told him the pictures didn’t matter, Mr. Crow?”

Mr. Crow bristled. “They were merely illustrations, Frisky! Of no great consequence! The important thing is the _words_, and Sandy cannot read them!”

“But pictures are important too!” Frisky insisted. “Especially if they’re of your friends! Oh, Sandy, I wish I had seen that letter!”

Sandy looked up, a glimmer of an idea in his eyes. “Do you think,” he began, “do you think that perhaps another letter might come? One with _your_ picture on it, Frisky?”

Frisky Squirrel brightened immediately. “Oh, I do hope so! That would be a grand thing indeed!” He scampered closer to the mail-box, peering at it with great interest. “So this is the famous mail-box! I’ve heard so much about it! Do you think it will have a letter for me tomorrow?”

Mr. Crow, seeing a new opportunity to exert his influence, cleared his throat. “It is possible, Frisky. Entirely possible. But the mail-carrier, as you know, is a very busy man. And he does not always leave the lid open. One must be very quick, Frisky, and very clever, to get to the mail-box before Farmer Green.”

Frisky Squirrel puffed out his chest. “I am very quick, Mr. Crow! And I am very clever!”

“Indeed you are,” Mr. Crow said, a sly gleam in his eye. “Perhaps, Frisky, if you were to keep a very close watch on the mail-box tomorrow, you might find something interesting. Something perhaps even _more_ interesting than a mere letter. Something shiny, perhaps?”

Frisky’s ears perked up. “Shiny, Mr. Crow? What kind of shiny thing?”
