XVII THE TALL BUSH

Nimblefoot, a young deer, was feeling rather pleased with himself. He had spent the morning nibbling sweet clover in Farmer Griggs' meadow, and now he was ready for a new adventure. He pranced lightly along the edge of the woods, his small hoofs barely disturbing the fallen leaves.

"Good morning, Nimblefoot!" a deep voice rumbled from a hollow log. Old Bramble, the badger, poked his grizzled snout out into the sunlight. "You seem to be in a hurry this fine day."

"I am, Old Bramble!" Nimblefoot declared, pausing to twitch his ears. "I mean to find the very best late-autumn berries. Lily Griggs was talking about them yesterday to her father. She said they are hidden high up, on a bush with very bright red leaves."

Old Bramble chuckled. "Ah, the hawthorn berries. They are indeed a treat. But they are often guarded by prickles, young one. And sometimes, they are quite difficult to reach."

"I am very tall for my age," Nimblefoot boasted, stretching his neck. "And I am very nimble, too."

He trotted on, leaving Old Bramble to blink in the sun. Soon, Nimblefoot found a hawthorn bush, its leaves a brilliant crimson, just as Lily had described. The berries clustered invitingly, but they were indeed high. He tried to stand on his hind legs, stretching as far as he could. But the highest, juiciest berries remained just out of reach. In his eagerness, he pushed too close to the bush and a thorny branch snagged his ear.

"Ouch!" Nimblefoot cried, shaking his head. He tried to back away, but another branch caught his flank. He began to feel a bit tangled. "Oh dear," he whimpered. "I do believe I am stuck!"

Just then, Hoot, the grumpy owl who lived in the old oak, opened one sleepy eye. "Stuck again, Nimblefoot?" he hooted, his voice dry as old bark. "Why do you always try to reach what is just beyond your grasp?"

"It's not fair!" Nimblefoot complained. "The berries are right there! And I am very hungry."

Old Bramble, having followed at a slower pace, now arrived. He peered at Nimblefoot with a knowing look. "Patience, my young friend, patience," he said calmly. "And a bit of cleverness, too. You see, these bushes have roots. And if one nibbles around the roots, the bush will sometimes bend just enough."

Nimblefoot watched as Old Bramble, with surprising agility, began to scratch and dig at the base of the bush. Soon, the hawthorn leaned slightly, and a cluster of ripe berries dropped within easy reach of Nimblefoot's nose.

"Oh, thank you, Old Bramble!" Nimblefoot exclaimed, eagerly beginning to munch. "You are very wise."

"One learns a few things in many seasons," Old Bramble replied, settling down for a nap. "And sometimes, the easiest way is not always the direct way."

Hoot merely snorted from his branch. "Some creatures never learn," he mumbled, closing his eye again.

Nimblefoot, however, ignored the owl. He was too busy enjoying the sweet, tart berries, grateful for the wisdom of his old friend. He knew that even a nimble deer could sometimes use a helping paw.

The white mare tossed her head. She had a rather scornful look in her eye. "A grown pony, indeed!" she neighed. "Why, you're hardly bigger than a goat. And you tremble like a leaf in a gale. I've seen more courage in a newborn foal."

Ebenezer, who had been listening with a quiet dignity, now spoke. "Every creature has its first time, madam," he said in his deep, calm voice. "And there is no shame in a little apprehension when one faces something entirely new. Twinkleheels is a brave little pony, and he will learn."

The mare snorted. "Brave? He looked as though he would bolt clean out of the county when the smith so much as tapped his hammer. It takes a proper horse to stand steady in this place."

"And a proper horse you are, I have no doubt," Ebenezer replied, his tone perfectly even. "But even the strongest oak was once a sapling. Let us not forget our own beginnings."

At this, the blacksmith, a large, kindly-looking man with strong arms and a leather apron, stepped forward. He had been quietly observing the exchange while he finished shaping a shoe for Ebenezer. "Now, now, Mollie," he said to the white mare, his voice rumbling like distant thunder, "no need to tease the youngster. We all have to start somewhere. And you, little fellow," he added, turning to Twinkleheels, "you'll be as steady as a rock by the time I'm through with you. Just watch old Ebenezer here. He knows the drill."

The blacksmith then began to work on Ebenezer. He lifted one of Ebenezer's large hooves, examining it with practiced eye. Twinkleheels watched, fascinated despite himself. The smith took off the worn shoe with a few deft tugs, then began to trim the hoof with a sharp tool. It looked rather like a giant pair of nippers. Twinkleheels winced inwardly, imagining how that must feel. But Ebenezer stood perfectly still, his eyes half-closed, as if enjoying a pleasant dream.

"Does that not hurt him?" Twinkleheels whispered to Ebenezer.

"Not at all," Ebenezer murmured back. "It's like having one's fingernails trimmed, only for our feet. It actually feels quite good to have the old, worn parts taken away."

Twinkleheels felt a little better. He watched as the blacksmith took a new shoe, heated it again in the forge until it glowed a cheerful orange, and then fitted it against Ebenezer's trimmed hoof. There was a faint hiss and a puff of smoke. Twinkleheels jumped.

"What was that?" he gasped. "Is he burning his foot?"

Ebenezer chuckled softly. "No, no, little friend. That is just the hot metal searing the very outer layer of the hoof. It cleans it and makes a perfect fit. It doesn't reach the sensitive parts. See how I stand quite calmly?"

Indeed, Ebenezer stood as solid as a statue. The blacksmith then cooled the shoe in the tub of water, which caused another hiss, though this one Twinkleheels was now prepared for. Then, with a series of careful taps, the smith began to nail the shoe into place. Each tap sounded loud in the smithy, echoing off the stone walls. Twinkleheels watched the nails emerge from the other side of Ebenezer's hoof, curling slightly. It looked very alarming.

"Does that truly not hurt?" Twinkleheels asked again, his voice barely a squeak. He was still worried about the nails.

"Not a bit," Ebenezer assured him. "The outer part of the hoof, where the nails go, has no feeling, just like your hair or the very tips of your fingernails. If a nail went too deep, into the quick, it would hurt, but a good smith never does that. And this one is the best in the village."

Twinkleheels took a deep breath. He tried to believe Ebenezer. He watched as the blacksmith finished one hoof, then moved to the next, until all four of Ebenezer's feet were shiny with new shoes. Ebenezer stamped his feet a few times, as if testing them. "Ah, that feels much better," he said contentedly. "Thank you, Master Cooper."

The blacksmith, Master Cooper, wiped his hands on his apron and then turned his attention to Twinkleheels. "Now, little fellow, it's your turn," he said with a smile. "Are you ready for your dancing shoes?"

Twinkleheels suddenly felt very small and very nervous again. He glanced at Johnnie Green, who stood nearby, holding his halter rope. Johnnie gave him an encouraging pat on the neck. "It's alright, Twinkleheels," Johnnie said softly. "You'll be fine."

Master Cooper reached for Twinkleheels' front leg. Twinkleheels instinctively pulled it back. He didn't like the idea of anyone holding his foot up in the air.

"Whoa there, little one," Master Cooper said gently. "Just let me have it."

Ebenezer spoke up. "Just relax, Twinkleheels. Let him hold your foot. It's quite easy once you get used to it."

Twinkleheels tried to obey. He looked at Ebenezer, then at Master Cooper. He allowed the smith to lift his front left hoof. It felt very strange to have his leg bent backward and held firmly. He wobbled a little on his three remaining legs. Johnnie Green put a reassuring hand on his shoulder.

Master Cooper chuckled. "A bit wobbly, are we? Don't worry, you'll get your balance. Now, let's see these little feet." He examined Twinkleheels' hoof. "Nice, clean hooves. Not much to trim here, you've been mostly on soft ground, haven't you?"

Twinkleheels nodded, though he wasn't sure if the blacksmith could see him. He tried to remember everything Ebenezer had told him. He watched as the smith pared away a tiny bit of the hoof, then rasped it smooth with a long, rough file. It tickled slightly, but it did not hurt.

Then came the moment he had been dreading. Master Cooper took a small shoe, much smaller than Ebenezer's, and held it up to Twinkleheels' hoof. He heated it in the forge, and Twinkleheels braced himself for the hiss and the smoke. It came, just as Ebenezer had described, and though it made him flinch, he did not pull his foot away.

"There, that's a good boy," Master Cooper praised him. "See? Nothing to it."

Then came the nails. Twinkleheels shut his eyes tightly as the first nail was driven in. He felt a series of taps, a slight vibration, but no pain. He opened one eye cautiously. Master Cooper was working calmly, carefully tapping each nail. Twinkleheels watched the shining points emerge from the hoof wall. It still looked peculiar, but he realized it truly didn't hurt. He felt a little thrill of pride that he was standing so still.

The white mare, Mollie, who had been watching with a critical eye, seemed to shift her weight. "Well, he's not as skittish as I thought he'd be," she conceded grudgingly.

Twinkleheels felt a tiny surge of satisfaction. He was proving her wrong.

One by one, Master Cooper shod all four of Twinkleheels' feet. Each time Twinkleheels flinched slightly at the initial press of the hot shoe or the first tap of the hammer, but each time he quickly settled. He found that if he focused on watching Master Cooper's strong, kind hands, it was not nearly so scary. Finally, the last nail was clinched, and Master Cooper lowered Twinkleheels' last foot to the ground.

"There you go, little champion," the blacksmith said, patting Twinkleheels' neck. "Four brand new shoes. Try them out."

Twinkleheels took a tentative step. It felt very odd. His feet felt heavier, and there was a strange, hard click-clack sound on the stone floor of the smithy that he had never heard before from his own steps. He lifted his feet higher, trying to get accustomed to the new sensation. He felt taller, somehow, and more substantial.

"How do they feel, little friend?" Ebenezer asked, a kind smile in his eyes.

"Strange," Twinkleheels admitted, taking another experimental step. "But... good! They feel very firm." He took a few more steps, trotting a small circle within the smithy. The click-clack was quite distinct. He rather liked it. He looked down at his new shoes. They were shiny and black, fitting perfectly around the edge of his hooves.

Johnnie Green untied his halter rope from the ring in the wall. "Good boy, Twinkleheels!" he exclaimed, patting him. "You were very brave."

Farmer Green paid Master Cooper, and after a few more words of thanks, they prepared to leave. Mollie, the white mare, was still waiting for her turn to be fully shod. She looked at Twinkleheels as he passed. "Don't go thinking you're a full-grown draft horse just because you have iron on your feet," she said, though her tone was less sharp than before.

Twinkleheels merely held his head a little higher. "I am a pony with shoes," he replied with dignity. "And I feel quite ready for anything."

Outside, the air felt crisp and cold. Twinkleheels trotted behind the buggy, his new shoes making a cheerful, rhythmic sound on the hard-packed snowy road. Click-clack, click-clack. It was quite different from the soft thud of his unshod hooves. He felt a spring in his step, a new confidence.

"They feel wonderful, Ebenezer!" he called out to the old horse pulling the buggy. "I feel as though I could travel all the way to the next county and not feel a thing!"

Ebenezer chuckled. "That's how it feels at first, my boy. New shoes are a grand thing. They protect our feet from stones and rough ground. You'll find you can go much further with less weariness."

"And the noise!" Twinkleheels added. "It's such a fine sound. Click-clack! Click-clack! It sounds important, doesn't it?"

"It does indeed," Ebenezer agreed. "Many a traveler has been cheered by the sound of well-shod hooves on a lonely road."

Twinkleheels was so proud of his new shoes that he wanted to show them to everyone. He imagined galloping through the pasture, his hooves making a glorious racket, impressing the bays and perhaps even getting a nod of approval from old Ebenezer. He tried trotting a little faster, just to hear the sound. But Farmer Green kept Ebenezer at a steady pace, and Twinkleheels had to match it.

As they neared the farm, old dog Spot came trotting out to meet them, wagging his tail. "Well, well! Look who's back!" he barked happily. He sniffed at Twinkleheels' feet. "What's that odd smell? And that shiny stuff?"

"They're my new shoes, Spot!" Twinkleheels announced proudly. He lifted a foot slightly so Spot could see. "I went to the blacksmith's shop and Master Cooper put them on me."

Spot sniffed again. "Shoes? What are they for?"

"They protect my feet," Twinkleheels explained patiently. "And they make a wonderful sound when I walk. Listen!" He took a few deliberate steps. Click-clack, click-clack.

Spot wagged his tail harder. "That's a very grand sound indeed!" he agreed. "Much better than your old quiet steps. Now everyone will hear you coming!"

Twinkleheels felt his chest swell with pride. He felt like a proper, important pony now.

Soon they were back in the barn. Johnnie Green led Twinkleheels to his stall. As he unhitched him, Twinkleheels heard a snort from the next stall. It was one of the bays, a young, sleek horse named Dapple.

"Well, well, if it isn't little Twinkleheels, all fancy with his new iron boots," Dapple scoffed. "What do you need those for? Are you going to pull a plow now?"

Twinkleheels felt a familiar prickle of annoyance at Dapple's teasing. He had hoped for admiration, not mockery. "They are for protection," Twinkleheels said stoutly. "And for speed. And for strength. Farmer Green himself said that oxen are strong and sure-footed. And they have very strong feet, I'm sure."

"Oxen!" Dapple snorted again. "You're no ox, little pony. You're just a small pony who got scared of a little snow."

"I was not scared!" Twinkleheels retorted, stamping one of his newly shod feet. The click-clack echoed nicely in the barn. "I merely got stuck. And now, with these shoes, I shall never get stuck again!"

Ebenezer, who had just been led into his own stall by Farmer Green, spoke calmly from behind Twinkleheels. "There is no need for such bluster, Dapple. Twinkleheels is quite right to be proud of his new shoes. They are a sign of

responsibility and growing purpose in life." Ebenezer finished, his voice a low, rumbling sound that seemed to fill the barn with quiet authority. "You will find, Dapple, that there comes a time for every creature when the frivolousness of youth gives way to the usefulness of maturity. These shoes are not merely for show; they are tools, just as your harness is a tool, for the work that Farmer Green requires."

Dapple snorted again, but there was less fire in it this time. He shuffled his feet in his stall, a restless movement that betrayed a flicker of unease beneath his usual bravado. "Work? Twinkleheels barely pulls a light cart in summer. What great work will his new iron boots do? Lead the plough?"

"Perhaps not the plough," Ebenezer conceded, "but every journey begins with a single step, and every pony, no matter how small, has his part to play. Twinkleheels will now find his footing surer, his steps more confident, and his capacity for travel much increased. There is pride in that, Dapple, a just and honest pride."

Twinkleheels felt a warmth spread through his chest. Ebenezer always knew just what to say. It was a comfort to have such a friend, especially when Dapple and the other bays seemed to delight in making him feel small. He still wished he could gallop off and prove them wrong with a magnificent clatter of his new shoes, but Ebenezer’s words were a better balm than any swift flight could provide.

Just then, the other bay, a younger, even more boisterous horse named Flash, poked his head over the stall divider. "What's all this talk of old age and usefulness? We're young, Ebenezer! We're for speed and spirit, not for dragging carts and wearing iron on our feet!"

"And there is a place for that, too," Ebenezer replied with a gentle sigh. "But even speed and spirit benefit from sturdy feet. How many times have you, Flash, had to stop short on a stony path, or grown footsore after a long day's run? These shoes, you see, prevent such discomforts. They are not a hindrance but an aid."

"An aid to clanking and making a racket, perhaps!" Flash neighed, tossing his head. "I like my silent steps, thank you very much. I can sneak up on a field mouse better without all that noise."

Twinkleheels bristled. "It is not a racket!" he declared. "It is a fine, strong sound, a sound of purpose! And I do not wish to sneak up on field mice. I wish to carry Johnnie Green and help Farmer Green, and now I can do it better than ever before!" He stamped a front hoof for emphasis, and the clear, ringing click-clack filled the barn once more.

Ebenezer watched the young pony with a knowing twinkle in his eye. "You are right, Twinkleheels," he said. "It is a sound of purpose. And it is a sound that will serve you well. Now, perhaps, it is time for some rest. Tomorrow will be a new day, and there will be tasks awaiting for strong, well-shod feet."

Johnnie Green, having finished with Ebenezer, now gave Twinkleheels a final pat and tossed a fresh measure of hay into his manger. "You're a grand pony, Twinkleheels," he murmured. "And those shoes look just right on you." He then left the barn, whistling a merry tune.

Twinkleheels ate his hay with renewed gusto, the sweet scent of it mingling with the faint, metallic tang of his new shoes. He felt important, grown-up, and eager for the morrow. He settled down to sleep, dreaming of adventures on hard roads and the satisfying rhythm of his new gait.

The next morning dawned bright and clear, with a crisp, cool air that promised a fine day for work. Farmer Green was in the barn earlier than usual, his face set with a thoughtful expression. He looked from Bright to Broad, the two sturdy oxen, and then to Ebenezer, and finally to Twinkleheels.

"Johnnie!" he called, "we need to fetch some firewood from the upper woods. The snow has melted enough in places, but there are still drifts. I think it's too much for the horses alone, but a wagon full of wood is too heavy for the little cart. What do you say to hitching up Ebenezer and Twinkleheels?"

Twinkleheels' ears perked up. A trip to the upper woods! And with Ebenezer! This was a real task, not just a casual ride in the pasture. He felt a thrill of anticipation. Dapple and Flash, in their stalls, let out disgruntled snorts. They were seldom chosen for such practical work, preferring to spend their days in the pasture or pulling the light buggy for pleasure rides.

Johnnie Green came bounding into the barn. "That's a grand idea, Father! Twinkleheels has his new shoes; he'll be fine."

"Indeed," Farmer Green nodded. "His new shoes will be a great help on the uneven ground and over any remaining ice patches. They will give him better grip and protect his feet."

Soon, Ebenezer and Twinkleheels were hitched to a medium-sized wagon. Ebenezer took the lead, his broad shoulders and steady stride setting the pace. Twinkleheels, with his smaller frame, was hitched behind him, ready to add his strength to the pull. Farmer Green took the reins, and Johnnie Green sat beside him, his eyes alight with excitement.

As they moved out of the barn, Twinkleheels listened to the rhythmic click-clack of his shoes on the packed earth of the farmyard. It was a proud sound, a purposeful sound. He imagined Dapple and Flash hearing it from their stalls, perhaps even grudgingly admitting that it did sound rather important.

The path to the upper woods was indeed challenging. The earlier snowmelt had left puddles and soft, muddy patches, alternating with stretches of frozen ground and stubborn drifts that still clung to the shaded areas. Ebenezer, with his long experience, navigated these varied terrains with practiced ease, his heavy hooves finding purchase where lighter ones might slip.

Twinkleheels, for his part, felt remarkably secure. His new shoes seemed to grip the ground with surprising tenacity. Where before he might have hesitated, fearing a stone or a slippery patch, he now stepped out with confidence. He could feel the solid connection between his feet and the earth, a reassuring firmness that made him feel stronger than ever.

"See, Twinkleheels," Ebenezer rumbled, his voice low and comforting, "these shoes are earning their keep already. You feel the difference, do you not?"

"Oh, yes, Ebenezer!" Twinkleheels panted, a little breathless from the effort, but mostly from exhilaration. "I feel as if I could climb a mountain! The ground does not feel so hard, and I do not slip nearly so much."

Farmer Green smiled. "Good pony, Twinkleheels. You're doing splendidly."

They continued their journey deeper into the woods. The trees grew thicker here, their branches heavy with the remnants of winter's snow, which dripped in silvery curtains when disturbed by a passing breeze. The air was cool and filled with the fresh, earthy scent of damp soil and awakening pine.

Eventually, they reached a clearing where Farmer Green had already felled some trees earlier in the season. There were stacks of cut logs waiting to be loaded. Farmer Green and Johnnie Green set about loading the wagon, their axes making sharp, ringing sounds as they trimmed a few stray branches.

Twinkleheels stood patiently, enjoying the peacefulness of the woods. He looked at Ebenezer, whose eyes were half-closed, enjoying a brief respite. Even the wise old horse seemed content in this quiet corner of the world.

As the wagon grew heavier with logs, Twinkleheels felt the strain increase. He braced himself, digging his new-shod hooves into the soft earth. The weight was considerable, but he found he could manage it. His shoes gave him excellent traction, allowing him to pull with all his might without slipping backward.

Finally, the wagon was full. "Ready, boys?" Farmer Green called, picking up the reins.

"Ready!" Johnnie Green echoed, scrambling onto the seat.

"Hup!" Farmer Green encouraged the horses.

Ebenezer leaned into his harness, his powerful muscles bunching. Twinkleheels, not to be outdone, put his own strength into the pull. For a moment, the wagon resisted, its wheels sunk slightly into the soft ground. Then, with a groan of wood and a creak of leather, it began to move.

Twinkleheels felt a surge of pride. He was truly helping! He was part of a team, and his new shoes were making a real difference. The journey back was slower, the loaded wagon a weighty burden, but Twinkleheels felt a satisfying fatigue, a sense of accomplishment he had rarely known before. His hooves made a steady, reassuring rhythm on the path, a testament to his effort.

As they emerged from the woods and started down the main road towards the farm, the sound of their approach carried ahead. Old dog Spot, ever vigilant, barked a greeting from the porch. Flash and Dapple, who were now enjoying the morning sunshine in the pasture, lifted their heads, their ears twitching.

"Here they come!" Flash neighed. "And listen to that clatter! Twinkleheels sounds like a whole brigade of soldiers!"

Dapple, however, looked more thoughtful. He watched Twinkleheels, pulling his share of the heavy wagon, his small frame straining but resolute. "He looks... strong," Dapple admitted, a grudging note in his voice. "And he is certainly not slipping in the mud."

Twinkleheels, overhearing their remarks, held his head a little higher. He didn't say anything, but he felt a quiet triumph. Actions, he realized, spoke louder than words, or even than the most satisfying click-clack.

When they reached the barn, Farmer Green and Johnnie Green carefully unhitched the team. Twinkleheels, though tired, felt a vibrant energy coursing through him. He had done well.

"Good work, Twinkleheels," Farmer Green said, patting his flank. "You pulled your weight today, and those new shoes were just the thing for the uneven ground."

Twinkleheels whinnied softly in response, a happy, tired sound. He walked into his stall, his steps still firm and steady. He looked across at Ebenezer, who gave him a gentle nod of approval.

"Well done, my boy," Ebenezer said. "You have begun to understand the true value of your new adornments. They are not merely for appearance, but for aiding you in your purpose."

Twinkleheels then drank deeply from his water trough, feeling the cool water slide down his throat. He felt like a proper worker now, a valuable member of the farm. He had helped. He had contributed.

Later that afternoon, Johnnie Green decided to take Twinkleheels out for a short ride. The sun was warmer now, and much of the snow had vanished, leaving behind only the damp earth and occasional puddles. Twinkleheels trotted along, his hooves making their familiar, cheerful sound. Johnnie Green, perched on his back, hummed contentedly.

"You like your new shoes, don't you, Twinkleheels?" Johnnie asked, patting the pony's neck.

Twinkleheels whinnied in agreement. He did like them. He liked the feeling of security, the added grip, and yes, he even liked the sound. It was a part of him now, a symbol of his growing maturity.

As they passed the edge of the pasture, Flash and Dapple were there, grazing lazily. Flash looked up. "Still making all that noise, little pony?" he called out, a hint of his usual teasing in his voice.

But Twinkleheels merely smiled to himself. He didn't feel the need to retort. He knew his worth. He knew what he had accomplished that morning. He was no longer just a small pony prone to getting stuck; he was a pony with purpose, a pony with strong, sure feet.

Johnnie Green, however, had an idea. "Twinkleheels, let's show them what these shoes can do!" He nudged Twinkleheels gently. "A little gallop, perhaps?"

Twinkleheels’ ears flicked forward. A gallop! That was exactly what he wanted. He broke into a joyful canter, then a full gallop, his new shoes pounding a rapid, rhythmic tattoo on the soft earth. Click-clack-clack-clack-clack! It was a glorious sound, a drumbeat of freedom and strength.

Flash and Dapple watched, their jaws momentarily slack. Twinkleheels wasn't just running; he was running with a new sort of confidence, his steps sure and unbroken. He galloped across the pasture, a streak of brown and white, before turning and slowing to a walk, slightly winded but beaming.

"Well!" Dapple exclaimed, recovering his voice. "He certainly makes a lot of noise when he runs!"

"But he runs well!" Flash added, a note of surprise in his tone. "He did not stumble once!"

Twinkleheels felt a profound sense of satisfaction. He had not needed to say a word. His feet, and the sound they made, had spoken for him. He rejoined Johnnie Green, his head held high, his new shoes shining in the afternoon sun. He was Twinkleheels, the pony with the purposeful feet, and he was ready for whatever adventure lay ahead.

The days that followed brought new experiences for Twinkleheels. His new shoes proved invaluable in many small ways. When Johnnie Green’s ball rolled into a shallow, muddy ditch, Twinkleheels was able to retrieve it, his hooves gripping the slippery banks firmly. When a sudden rainstorm turned the farm lanes into slick, treacherous paths, Twinkleheels remained steady, carrying Johnnie Green safely to the barn. Even fetching the mail from the village, a task usually reserved for Ebenezer or the bays, became an easy trot, the well-shod feet making light work of the stony road.

Old Ebenezer observed Twinkleheels with quiet approval. "He is growing into himself," he mused to old dog Spot one afternoon, as Twinkleheels trotted by, pulling a small cart filled with freshly picked apples. "Those shoes have given him not only protection, but also confidence. A pony with confidence can achieve much."

Spot wagged his tail. "He certainly makes a grand sound now," he barked. "I always know when he is coming. It is a good sound, a strong sound."

One crisp autumn morning, a few weeks after Twinkleheels had received his shoes, Farmer Green approached the barn with a concerned look on his face. "Johnnie," he said, "I have a problem. The old bridge over Miller’s Creek, the one we use for the south pasture, has a rotten plank. It's too risky for the heavy oxen or even Ebenezer to cross with the wagon, and the bays are too skittish on shaky ground. But I need to get some tools and fencing supplies over there today."

Johnnie Green frowned. "How will we do it, Father?"

Farmer Green stroked his chin. "We could carry them by hand, but it would take all day. Unless..." His eyes fell upon Twinkleheels, who was listening intently from his stall.

Twinkleheels held his breath. Could it be? Was he, the smallest of the farm animals, about to be entrusted with such an important, and perhaps even dangerous, task?

"Twinkleheels," Farmer Green said slowly, "do you think you could manage to carry a small load, a few bags of tools, across that bridge? You are light, and your new shoes give you good footing."

Twinkleheels whinnied eagerly, stomping a foot. Click-clack! He felt a surge of excitement, mingled with a tremor of nerves. A shaky bridge! This was a real test!

"He can do it, Father!" Johnnie Green declared, his face shining with pride for his pony. "He has been so sure-footed since Master Cooper put his shoes on."

"Very well," Farmer Green decided. "We will try it. Johnnie, load up the small pack-saddle with the lighter tools and a coil of wire. Not too much, mind you. Twinkleheels is still a young pony."

Soon, Twinkleheels stood by the unstable bridge, a small but significant load upon his back. The bridge consisted of a few rough-hewn planks spanning a narrow, but fast-flowing, creek. One of the planks indeed looked quite precarious, sagging noticeably in the middle. Ebenezer stood nearby, watching with a calm, reassuring gaze. Flash and Dapple, from the safety of their pasture, snorted their usual warnings and dire predictions.

"He'll surely fall in!" Flash neighed.

"That bridge will splinter like dry twigs under his weight, shoes or no shoes," Dapple added, though he too sounded a little less certain than usual.

Twinkleheels ignored them. He focused on Farmer Green’s words, "Slow and steady, Twinkleheels. Watch your step. You can do it."

He took a deep breath, or as deep a breath as a pony can take, and placed his first new-shod hoof onto the bridge. The plank creaked ominously, but his shoe gripped firmly. He placed another hoof, carefully, deliberately. The bridge swayed slightly beneath him.

He felt a moment of fear, a memory of sinking into the snow drift, but then he remembered the solid feel of his new shoes, the confident advice of Ebenezer, and the trust in Farmer Green’s voice. He pushed forward, one careful step after another. Click-clack, click-clack, went his hooves, a soft, deliberate rhythm that seemed to steady the very planks beneath him.

And then the sharp prickle of the nails, quickly followed by the dull thud of the hammer, then the cool splash of water, and finally, the solid feeling of the shoes themselves. It had been quite a day indeed, full of firsts and discoveries, and a good many lessons learned. Twinkleheels had learned that even the wisest of friends could not truly banish fear, but they could make it easier to face. He had learned that sometimes what seemed terrifying at first was merely a part of the everyday work of the world. And he had learned that there was a deep satisfaction in overcoming a challenge, in having a purpose and fulfilling it with all his might.

The barn was settling down for the night. The gentle rustle of straw as the horses shifted, the soft munching of hay, the occasional sigh or snort. From a far corner, a small, squeaking sound indicated the presence of the barn mice, going about their nightly business, fearless of the great beasts who shared their home. Outside, the wind had picked up, and Twinkleheels could hear it whistling around the eaves, a mournful sound that spoke of the cold, deep winter night. He was glad to be inside, warm and dry, with his new shoes to protect his feet, and his wise old friend Ebenezer in the next stall.

Twinkleheels drifted into a peaceful sleep, dreaming of crisp, cold air, the crunch of snow beneath sturdy, iron-shod hooves, and the kind, encouraging voice of Ebenezer. He dreamed of trotting purposefully, not just playing, but helping, making a real difference.

When he woke the next morning, the barn was still steeped in a quiet grey light. The air was colder than the previous night, hinting at a fresh fall of snow. He stretched, feeling the gentle ache in his muscles, a good ache that spoke of honest effort. His hooves felt different, solid, capable. He took a tentative step, then another. The clink of his new shoes on the floor of his stall was a pleasing sound, a sort of musical declaration of his new status.

He looked over at Ebenezer, who was already awake, steadily munching his morning hay. “Good morning, Ebenezer!” Twinkleheels called out, his voice bright with a fresh enthusiasm.

“And a good morning to you, Twinkleheels,” Ebenezer replied, pausing his chewing for a moment. “You sound quite spry this morning. Did the shoes work their magic in your sleep?”

Twinkleheels pranced a little in his stall. “I feel quite wonderful, Ebenezer! Strong and ready for anything. I feel as though I could pull a whole cart full of sap by myself today, even through the deepest drifts.”

Ebenezer chuckled, a low, rumbling sound that made the stall vibrate faintly. “Steady on, little friend,” he advised gently. “Confidence is a good thing, a very good thing indeed, but it must be tempered with wisdom. Even Bright and Broad, as strong as they are, need to choose their path carefully. And you, Twinkleheels, are still a pony, for all your new shoes.”

Twinkleheels felt a tiny flutter of disappointment, but he knew Ebenezer spoke the truth. “You’re right, of course,” he admitted, lowering his head slightly. “But I do feel much more capable than yesterday.”

“And so you should,” Ebenezer said, his voice softening. “For you are. You faced your fears, and you have come out of it stronger. That is a kind of strength that goes deeper than mere muscle.”

Just then, Johnnie Green came into the barn, carrying a bucket of oats. He moved with the quiet efficiency of a boy used to farm chores, his breath pluming in the cold morning air. He greeted Ebenezer first, then moved to Twinkleheels’ stall, a smile spreading across his face as he saw the pony.

“Well, Twinkleheels, my fine fellow!” Johnnie exclaimed, reaching over to stroke the pony’s soft muzzle. “Ready for another day? Your new shoes look quite splendid.” He carefully poured the oats into Twinkleheels’ feed bin.

“He’s ready for anything, Johnnie,” Ebenezer put in from his stall. “He’s even claiming he could pull a sap cart all by himself through the deep snow.”

Johnnie laughed. “Oh, could you now, Twinkleheels? That’s quite a boast for a little pony. But I suppose those new shoes make you feel very grand.” He patted Twinkleheels’ neck once more. “Farmer Green says we might have some more sap to gather today, especially if the sun comes out. But I think Bright and Broad will be the ones doing the heavy hauling. You might get to come along, though, for good company.”

Twinkleheels munched his oats, feeling a renewed sense of purpose. Even if he couldn’t pull a sap cart alone, being allowed to come along with Bright and Broad would be a privilege, a sign that he was now considered more useful than before.

As the morning light grew stronger, the other horses in the barn began to stir. Bay Boy and Bay Girl, in their stalls across the aisle, whinnied and stamped their feet, eager for their own morning meal. They were a handsome pair, sleek and restless, but Twinkleheels knew now that their beauty was not their only measure.

When Johnnie brought their oats, Bay Boy snorted, tossing his head. “Well, well, if it isn’t the little workhorse,” he said, casting a glance at Twinkleheels’ stall. “Still clanking about on those heavy feet? I suppose you feel very important.”

Bay Girl added, with a toss of her mane, “One would think he was an ox, with all the fuss he made yesterday. Imagine, a tiny pony thinking he could work like a full-sized horse.”

Twinkleheels felt a familiar wave of annoyance, but it was not as sharp as it once would have been. He had Ebenezer’s words, and Farmer Green’s actions, and the solid feel of his new shoes to bolster him.

“I am simply ready to do my part,” Twinkleheels stated calmly, meeting Bay Boy’s gaze. “And these shoes help me do it. Perhaps you two might find them useful if you ever decided to do something more strenuous than pulling a fancy buggy on a paved road.”

“He’s got you there, Bay Boy,” Johnnie said, unable to hide his amusement. “Twinkleheels worked harder yesterday than you two have all winter.”

The bays turned away in a huff, their elegant tails swishing indignantly. Twinkleheels felt a small thrill of victory. He had not been frightened by their teasing, nor had he cowered. He had spoken his mind, and he had been right.

Twinkleheels’ heart swelled with pride. Farmer Green had remembered! He had seen Twinkleheels’ efforts and appreciated them. This was exactly what he had hoped for.

Johnnie’s face lit up. “That’s a grand idea, Pa! Twinkleheels will be very useful. He’s much quicker on his feet than the oxen, for lighter loads.”

Bright and Broad, who had just been led out of their stalls by Farmer Green to be harnessed, let out a deep, rumbling sound. They were mighty beasts, slow and steady, but immensely strong. They accepted their harnesses with a patient dignity that only oxen possess.

Twinkleheels merely nodded, a slight smile on his face. He knew they weren’t trying to tease him, merely stating facts from their years of experience. He was ready to learn, and ready to work.

He had learned that true pride came not from mere appearance, but from competence and contribution. The scrubby mare at the smithy, with her unkind words, would have been surprised to see him now, working diligently in the snow, a very useful pony indeed.

The barn, with its familiar smells and comforting sounds, awaited him, and he knew a good meal and a well-earned rest would follow. It was, he decided, a very fine day to be a pony with new shoes. The world, he realized, was much larger than his pasture, and full of opportunities for a pony brave enough to explore it, especially with a wise friend like Ebenezer and a kind master like Johnnie. He wondered what adventures tomorrow might bring. But for now, the satisfaction of a day well spent, and the promise of a warm barn and a good meal, was enough. Ebenezer chuckled softly. “That’s fine, Twinkleheels,” he said. “I knew you’d do well. A good shoe, properly fitted, makes all the difference. And a willing heart, too, of course.”

“I wasn’t even afraid of the snow,” Twinkleheels continued, unable to contain his enthusiasm. “I just trotted along, pulling the sled, and it wasn’t heavy at all. Well, it was a little heavy when it was full, but I managed it easily. And I saw Bright and Broad. They were pulling a great big sled, much bigger than mine. But I think I was faster than them.” He paused, then reconsidered. “Well, not faster, perhaps, but certainly more nimble. I could get in among the trees better.”

Ebenezer gave a knowing nod. “Every animal has his strengths, Twinkleheels,” he observed. “Bright and Broad are strong, as strong as any two creatures you’ll find. They can break through deep drifts and move mountains of wood, if need be. But you, you have your quickness and your small size. You can go where they cannot, and you can carry out tasks that are best suited to a pony. It is a good thing to understand one’s own gifts, and to use them well.”

“I suppose so,” Twinkleheels admitted, feeling a tiny bit deflated at Ebenezer’s mild correction. He had wanted to boast more, but Ebenezer’s quiet wisdom always had a way of putting things into perspective. “But it was good work. I felt important. I felt useful.”

“And that, my young friend, is the best feeling of all,” Ebenezer said. “To know that you have contributed, that you have done your part. That is where true pride lies, not in strutting or in comparing oneself to others, but in the honest effort and the satisfaction of a job well done.”

Twinkleheels thought about this as he munched his hay. He had indeed felt truly satisfied. Not just proud of his shoes, or proud that he had overcome his fear of the blacksmith. But proud of what he had *done*. He had worked. He had helped Johnnie. He had gathered sap for the sugar-house, sap that would soon be boiled down into sweet maple syrup. He

had contributed to the very essence of the farm’s spring bounty, and the thought warmed him even more than the plentiful oats in his manger. He licked his lips, savoring the last few grains, and then looked at Ebenezer, whose eyes were now closed in what appeared to be a peaceful doze.

“Ebenezer,” Twinkleheels said softly, not wishing to disturb the old horse too much.

Ebenezer opened one eye slowly, a flicker of amusement in its depths. “Yes, young man?” he rumbled. “Still pondering the mysteries of honest labor?”

“I was just thinking,” Twinkleheels began, “how good it feels. Better than anything, really. Better than running a race, even. And my shoes helped so much. I could never have done it without them. And without what you told me about not being afraid.”

“Ah, the shoes are merely tools, Twinkleheels,” Ebenezer replied, closing his eye again, though his voice remained clear. “And courage is not the absence of fear, but the willingness to proceed despite it. You have both now, and that is a fine thing. You are growing up, Twinkleheels. Each day brings a new understanding.”

Twinkleheels felt a swell of pride again. Ebenezer’s words were always so encouraging, and yet so wise. He munched on a stray piece of hay thoughtfully. The barn was quiet now, save for the occasional rustle of straw from the stalls and the soft breathing of the animals. Outside, the last light of the winter day was fading, casting long shadows across the snow-covered fields. Soon, Johnnie Green would be back to give them their evening water and to make sure all was well before bedtime.

The next morning dawned clear and crisp, with a hint of warmth in the air that spoke of the approaching spring. The snow, which had been so deep and troublesome only days before, now seemed softer, beginning to melt in patches where the sun touched it directly. Icicles dripped from the barn eaves, their music a cheerful counterpoint to the chirping of the early birds.

Twinkleheels awoke with a new energy. His hooves, still feeling firm and secure in their new shoes, tingled with the desire to be put to good use. He nickered happily when Johnnie Green came into the barn, carrying a pail of fresh water.

“Good morning, Twinkleheels!” Johnnie said, patting his neck. “Ready for another day’s work?”

Twinkleheels nodded his head eagerly, though Johnnie, of course, did not understand horse-language. But the boy seemed to grasp his enthusiasm.

“Father says we might not need to go to the sugar-bush today,” Johnnie continued, filling Twinkleheels’ water trough. “The sap’s been running slower, and the weather’s changing. He says there’ll be plenty of other things to do around the farm now that the snow’s beginning to melt.”

This news brought a small pang of disappointment to Twinkleheels. He had rather enjoyed his work in the sugar-bush. But then, the thought of new tasks, new challenges, quickly replaced it with excitement. What would Farmer Green have him do? Would it be something important, something where his new shoes would prove their worth again?

Later that morning, Farmer Green himself came into the barn, carrying a coil of rope and a small wooden box. “Johnnie,” he called, “I need you and Twinkleheels to help me clear some of the fallen branches from the edge of the north pasture. The wind storm last week brought down quite a few, and they’ll be in the way when we start turning the soil for spring planting.”

Twinkleheels’ ears perked up. Clearing branches! That sounded like important work. He could pull a small cart, perhaps, or drag the branches himself. He felt a surge of pride, thinking of how strong and useful he was becoming.

“Yes, Father!” Johnnie replied, already moving to fetch Twinkleheels’ halter.

As Johnnie led Twinkleheels out of the barn, Ebenezer watched from his stall. “Be careful, young friend,” the old horse advised in his deep voice. “The ground will be soft and uneven with the melting snow. Watch your footing.”

“I will, Ebenezer!” Twinkleheels called back, already trotting a little too quickly. “My new shoes will keep me safe!”

Ebenezer merely sighed a gentle sigh. He knew that enthusiasm often outran caution in young creatures.

The north pasture was indeed a sight to behold. Large branches, heavy with melted snow, lay scattered across the partially thawed ground. Some were still covered in a crust of ice, making them slippery. The snow was a patchy carpet, revealing muddy earth in places, and forming treacherous slush in others.

Johnnie Green, armed with a small axe and a pair of stout gloves, began to chop the larger branches into manageable pieces. Twinkleheels stood by patiently at first, eager to be given a task. Farmer Green directed him to a small, two-wheeled cart which had been left near the pasture gate.

“We’ll load the smaller pieces onto this, Twinkleheels,” Farmer Green explained, his voice kind but firm. “And you can pull it to the brush pile at the far end of the field. Johnnie will lead you.”

Twinkleheels felt a thrill. Pulling a cart! That was a grown-up job. He puffed out his chest a little, feeling the sturdy harness settle comfortably on his back. He watched as Johnnie and Farmer Green began to gather the branches, the air filled with the sharp scent of damp earth and fresh-cut wood.

The first few trips were easy enough. The cart was light, and the branches were not too heavy. Twinkleheels trotted along, his new shoes gripping the muddy, half-frozen ground with surprising sure-footedness. He felt strong, capable, and very proud. Johnnie walked beside him, praising him with soft words and gentle pats.

“You’re doing wonderfully, Twinkleheels!” Johnnie said. “What a good pony you are!”
