"Oh, yes!" Fatty Coon said, brightening. "It became much more interesting! It started with just one horse, and then it had twelve horses! And the mail-bag became as big as six haystacks! And anyone could take a letter! It was a grander story, Jasper. Much grander. And I think it was better for it."

"Better?" Jasper squawked, nearly losing his perch on the branch. "But it was not true, Fatty! The story grew and distorted the truth until it was almost unrecognizable! And that, my friend, is a warning. A story can become so embellished, so altered by each retelling, that the original truth is lost entirely. And then, what good is it? It can no longer teach us a moral, or convey accurate information. It becomes merely a pleasant fiction, and sometimes, not even pleasant."

"But a pleasant fiction is often nicer than a dull truth, Jasper," Fatty observed

"But a pleasant fiction is often nicer than a dull truth, Jasper," Fatty observed, his eyes half-closed in contemplation of this profound statement.

"Nicer, perhaps, for the moment," Jasper Jay retorted, fluffing his blue feathers in a show of disapproval. "But what good is 'nicer' if it leads one astray? What good is it if it causes one to mistake a seed catalog for a personal message, and then steal it from its rightful owner? No, Fatty, truth has a value that mere pleasantness can never match. Truth provides a foundation upon which one can build understanding. Fiction, however pleasant, can be a shifting sand."

Fatty Coon merely yawned. "I suppose that depends on what one is building, Jasper. If one is building a good nap, then a pleasant fiction is often preferable to a harsh reality. And if the truth is dull, why should I bother with it? I like stories that make me feel good, or make me laugh, or make me wish for something grander. Like twelve horses and six haystacks of letters! That was a much better story than the one about one horse and one small bag."

Jasper Jay hopped along the branch, his irritation growing with every one of Fatty's contented sighs. "But the mail-man did not have twelve horses, Fatty! He had but one! And his mail-bag was not as big as six haystacks! It was a simple, ordinary mail-bag, just like any other. The truth was not 'dull'; it was merely what it was. And Sandy Chipmunk, because he believed the embellished tale, was disappointed when he saw the reality. He was disappointed, Fatty, because he had been led to expect something grander than the truth could offer!"

"Oh, he was only disappointed for a moment," Fatty replied, waving a paw dismissively. "And then he found a letter with his picture on it, and he was very proud. So it all turned out well, did it not? He felt important, which is a good feeling to have. And a good story can often make one feel important, even if it is not entirely true."

"But it was not his letter!" Jasper cried, exasperated. "And Farmer Green was quite upset that his letter had been stolen. It caused trouble, Fatty! It caused trouble because the truth had been obscured by fancy and exaggeration!"

"Well, Farmer Green should not have left his mail-box open, then," Fatty said with a shrug. "It is his own fault if a curious chipmunk takes what is not meant for him, especially when it has a picture of the chipmunk on it. One must be careful with one's possessions, Jasper. That, I think, is a moral we can all agree upon."

Jasper Jay paused, considering this. It was true that Farmer Green might have been more careful. But that did not, in Jasper's opinion, excuse the distortion of facts. "Perhaps there is a grain of truth in what you say, Fatty," he conceded, "regarding Farmer Green's carelessness. But my point remains: the spreading of inaccurate stories causes misunderstanding and mischief. And I fear that sometimes, those who spread such stories do so not for amusement, but for more mischievous purposes."

Fatty Coon opened his eyes wide. "Mischievous purposes? Who would do such a thing, Jasper? And why?"

"Some," Jasper began, lowering his voice conspiratorialy, "delight in the confusion of others. Some find it amusing to lead others astray. And some, I dare say, even profit from it. Think of Mr. Crow, for instance."

Fatty Coon's ears perked up at the mention of Mr. Crow. "Mr. Crow? What has Mr. Crow to do with pleasant fictions and dull truths?"

"Mr. Crow," Jasper explained, "is a master of twisting tales to suit his own ends. Do you remember how he tricked Sandy Chipmunk into gathering beechnuts, pretending they were corn? He knew full well that Sandy would not know the difference, and he hoped to get his own larder filled without expending his own energy. He misrepresented his desires, and then blamed Sandy for the misunderstanding."

"Oh, yes, I remember that," Fatty chuckled. "Sandy was quite cross about it. But Mr. Crow was quite cross himself when he found he had a pile of beechnuts instead of corn! So it worked out evenly, did it not? A bit of mischief for a bit of mischief."

"But that is precisely my point, Fatty!" Jasper exclaimed, shaking his head. "It all stems from a lack of clarity, a distortion of the truth! Had Mr. Crow been plain and honest, Sandy would have known exactly what was required of him, and there would have been no misunderstanding, no anger, and no disappointment. The truth, however unglamorous, is always the most efficient path to understanding and cooperation."

"But cooperation is not always so interesting," Fatty Coon observed. "Sometimes a little confusion makes things more lively. And Mr. Crow does seem to like things lively, does he not?"

"Lively, perhaps, for Mr. Crow," Jasper grumbled, "but often troublesome for everyone else. And it is not merely Mr. Crow who is prone to such behavior. There are others in Pleasant Valley, who, through carelessness or intent, allow stories to grow beyond all recognition. And sometimes, these growing stories cause a great deal of trouble for those who believe them."

Just then, a small, flitting shadow passed overhead. It was Mrs. Squirrel, scolding her children from a high branch for dropping too many nuts. Her voice, though meant for her young ones,

was somewhat sharp, reached the two friends below.

"You see, Fatty," Jasper Jay continued, lowering his own voice slightly, "even Mrs. Squirrel's plainest words can be misinterpreted by those who hear them without paying proper attention. Imagine, then, the confusion that arises when the words themselves are twisted and embellished before they ever reach a listener's ear."

Fatty Coon blinked his eyes, which had grown rather heavy with the afternoon's warmth and the sound of Jasper's steady lecturing. "It does sound like a great deal of trouble, Jasper," he murmured, "all this talk of truth and untruth. But what is one to do, when stories simply seem to grow of their own accord, like weeds in Farmer Green's garden?"

"One must cultivate vigilance, Fatty," Jasper replied, puffing out his chest with an air of importance. "One must strive to discern the kernels of fact from the chaff of invention. And, most importantly, one must be careful not to perpetuate the untruths, however entertaining they may seem."

Just then, Frisky Squirrel chattered past them, darting up an oak tree with a sudden burst of energy. He had been listening, as squirrels often do, with one ear open, and he had caught a snippet of Jasper Jay's last pronouncements.

"Untruths?" Frisky chattered excitedly, pausing on a low branch and flicking his tail. "Are you talking about the story of the mail-box, Jasper? The one about Farmer Green and his new trap?"

Jasper Jay sighed, a sound that rustled a few leaves on the branch above. "Precisely, Frisky. A perfect example of a story that grew so monstrously out of proportion that it caused Sandy Chipmunk a great deal of unnecessary excitement, and Farmer Green a great deal of inconvenience."

"Inconvenience?" Frisky repeated, his bright eyes widening. "Why, I thought Sandy was rather clever to get a letter for himself! And Mr. Crow said it was all very amusing!"

"Amusing, perhaps, for Mr. Crow, who delights in others' predicaments," Jasper said dryly. "But let me tell you, Frisky, Farmer Green was far from amused. He was quite cross, as a matter of fact. He had been expecting an important circular in the mail, a catalogue, I believe, with pictures of new seeds and farming implements. And when he went to retrieve his mail, he found the box open and one of his envelopes gone."

Fatty Coon, now fully awake and intrigued, pushed himself up on his forepaws. "A catalogue, you say? With pictures?"

"Indeed," Jasper affirmed. "And one of those pictures, as it happened, was of a rather plump chipmunk, holding a tiny plough. It was this picture, no doubt, that led Sandy Chipmunk to believe the letter was addressed especially to him."

"Oh, dear," Fatty said, shaking his head slowly. "So the letter Sandy took was not meant for him at all, but for Farmer Green. And Sandy thought it was for him because of a picture! And then Farmer Green was cross because his catalogue was gone!"

"Exactly so," Jasper nodded. "And the consequences of such misunderstandings can be far-reaching, Fatty. Farmer Green, you see, was planning to order a new variety of plump, sweet corn seeds from that catalogue. He had high hopes for this corn, for it was said to grow taller and yield more kernels than any other kind. And now, without his catalogue, he fears he has missed the opportunity to order them in time for spring planting."

Frisky Squirrel looked thoughtful for a moment. "But what does Farmer Green mean by 'missed the opportunity'?" he asked. "Surely, if he wants corn, he can simply go to the village and buy some seeds?"

"Ah, but this was a special offer, Frisky," Jasper explained. "A limited time offer, available only to those who ordered through the mail by a certain date. And that date, I believe, was only a few days hence. Without the catalogue, Farmer Green does not know the exact name of the corn, nor the special ordering instructions. He is quite put out, I assure you."

"So, Sandy's mistake has caused Farmer Green to lose his chance at special corn," Fatty Coon summarized, his eyes wide. "And all because a story got twisted about a mail-box and a chipmunk mistook a picture for a personal address."

"Precisely," Jasper said again, quite pleased that Fatty seemed to be grasping the gravity of the situation. "And what is more, Farmer Green has sworn to Jasper Jay that he will take extra precautions with his mail-box from now on. He plans to rig it with a small, harmless, but startling device that will give any unauthorized opener a fright, just enough to deter them from tampering with his letters again."

Frisky Squirrel shuddered slightly at the mention of a "startling device." He remembered the exaggerated stories of Farmer Green's "trap," and though Jasper assured him this was harmless, the idea of a surprise was not to his liking. "A device, you say? What sort of device?"

"I heard tell it involves a spring and a bell," Jasper mused. "Something that will ring loudly and startle any curious creature who dares to open the box without knowing the proper way. It is not a trap, mind you, but a deterrent. And I fear that if Sandy Chipmunk, or any other of us, should forget and try to peek inside the mail-box again, they will receive a most unpleasant surprise."

Fatty Coon let out a low whistle. "A bell, eh? That would certainly give one a start! I must remember to warn everyone. This is much more troublesome than a mere misunderstanding about corn and beechnuts, isn't it?"

"Indeed it is, Fatty," Jasper agreed. "For a simple mistake, fueled by careless storytelling, has now led to a situation where an innocent peek could lead to a fright. All because the truth was not told plainly from the outset, and Sandy Chipmunk was led to believe something that was not so."

As if summoned by the conversation, Mr. Crow himself flew lazily overhead, circling once before alighting on a branch not far from Jasper and Fatty. He landed with a ruffle of his black feathers, peering down at them with his beady eyes.

"Hark, hark," Mr. Crow cawed, a glint of amusement in his eye. "Such serious talk for such a beautiful afternoon. What profound philosophical musings are disturbing the peace of Pleasant Valley today?"

Jasper Jay eyed Mr. Crow suspiciously. He knew Mr. Crow's penchant for mischief and twisting tales, and he suspected that Mr. Crow was well aware of the situation with Farmer Green's mail-box and Sandy's "stolen" letter.

"We were discussing the consequences of spreading untruths, Mr. Crow," Jasper stated, perhaps a little too pointedly. "And how a simple misunderstanding can lead to a chain of unfortunate events."

Mr. Crow let out a loud "Haw-haw-haw!" that made a few leaves tremble. "Untruths, you say? Why, Jasper, you make it sound as if all the world should be as dull and predictable as a straight line. Where is the fun in that, I ask you? A little embroidery, a touch of exaggeration, it adds spice to life, does it not?"

"It adds trouble, Mr. Crow," Jasper countered, "as Farmer Green has discovered. He is quite upset about his missing seed catalogue, you know. And he plans to make sure no other creature interferes with his mail again."

Mr. Crow feigned surprise. "Is that so? Farmer Green is upset? Why, I am sure Sandy Chipmunk meant no harm. He merely thought the letter, with its charming picture, was meant for him. One cannot fault a creature for believing what their eyes tell them, now can one?" Mr. Crow's eyes twinkled, as if he knew something Jasper did not.

"And you, Mr. Crow," Jasper pressed, "did you not encourage Sandy in his belief that the letter was his? Did you not, in your usual fashion, mock him for thinking it was not?"

Mr. Crow ruffled his feathers again. "I merely asked him to open it, Jasper. To solve the mystery! For what good is a letter if its contents remain hidden? And what a fine sight it was, to see Sandy's face when he saw all those other creatures pictured inside, in his very own letter! It was a lesson for him, I daresay, a lesson in not being so presumptuous."

Fatty Coon, who had been listening with rapt attention, frowned. "But it was not his letter at all, Mr. Crow. It was Farmer Green's seed catalogue. And now Farmer Green might not get his special corn, all because of Sandy's mistake."

"Ah, the corn!" Mr. Crow exclaimed, as if suddenly remembering. "Yes, the corn. Always the corn. Farmer Green is quite obsessed with his corn, is he not? But perhaps this is for the best. Perhaps this will teach Farmer Green to be less dependent on mere catalogues and more resourceful in finding his own seeds. There is a moral in every story, you see, if one looks closely enough."

Jasper Jay shook his head in exasperation. "You twist everything to suit your own convenience, Mr. Crow. The simple truth is that Sandy took something that did not belong to him, misled by an exaggerated story and his own wishful thinking. And now Farmer Green suffers for it, and other creatures risk being startled by a bell, all because no one spoke plainly."

"Plainly, plainly," Mr. Crow repeated with a sneer. "How dreadfully dull. But speaking of suffering, Jasper, I heard a most interesting tale today. It seems that Mrs. Squirrel is quite beside herself. She believes some of her finest, most perfectly ripened beechnuts have gone missing from her winter store. She is quite distraught, fearing some thieving creature has made off with her hard-won sustenance."

Fatty Coon gasped. "Beechnuts missing? Oh, dear! That is a serious matter, Mr. Crow. For a squirrel to lose her winter store is a tragedy indeed."

"Indeed," Mr. Crow agreed, his eyes gleaming. "And she was quite agitated, calling all sorts of names, I understand, to any passing shadow. Some, I believe, even heard her accusing a certain young chipmunk of being too greedy for his own good."

Jasper Jay's eyes narrowed. He knew Mr. Crow was up to no good. "And who, pray tell, might have put such an idea into Mrs. Squirrel's head, Mr. Crow? For Sandy Chipmunk is a diligent gatherer, and not one to steal from his neighbors."

"Oh, I wouldn't know," Mr. Crow said innocently, preening a feather. "Stories, you see, have a way of spreading. And sometimes, when one hears a tale of a chipmunk taking what is not his, one begins to wonder if other things might have gone missing as well. Such is the nature of cause and effect, is it not?"

Jasper Jay flew into a rare fit of genuine anger. "You are deliberately sowing discord, Mr. Crow! You are using the incident of the mail-box to cast suspicion upon Sandy Chipmunk for a completely unrelated matter! You know Sandy is honest in his gathering!"

"Do I?" Mr. Crow cawed, tilting his head. "I merely report what I hear, Jasper. And what I hear is that Mrs. Squirrel is missing beechnuts, and that a certain chipmunk has recently taken something that was not his. The two facts, while perhaps unconnected, do tend to make one ponder, do they not?"

Fatty Coon, however, was now very concerned. "But if Mrs. Squirrel thinks Sandy stole her beechnuts, there will be a terrible quarrel! And Sandy has so many beechnuts of his own, gathered in place of corn for you, Mr. Crow!"

Mr. Crow chuckled. "Ah, yes, the beechnuts. A fine feast for a chipmunk, but quite disagreeable to my own constitution, as you know. But that is beside the point, Fatty. The point is, perception is a powerful thing. If Mrs. Squirrel perceives that her beechnuts are gone, and if she perceives that Sandy might be the culprit, then that, for her, becomes a sort of truth."

"But it is not THE truth, Mr. Crow!" Jasper exclaimed, hopping agitatedly on his branch. "It is a lie, a fabrication, a mischievous insinuation! And you are the one making it!"

"Me?" Mr. Crow looked genuinely offended. "I merely repeated what I heard. It is not my fault if Mrs. Squirrel is prone to... shall we say, imaginative interpretations of events. Besides, what harm can a little suspicion do? It will make the valley folk more vigilant, more careful with their belongings. Perhaps even more honest!"

Jasper Jay was too indignant to reply. He merely glared at Mr. Crow, who, having successfully stirred the pot, took flight with another "Haw-haw-haw!" and vanished into the blue sky.

"Oh, dear, oh, dear," Fatty Coon lamented, shaking his head. "This is worse than Farmer Green's corn. This is a real predicament. Poor Sandy! He will be so upset if Mrs. Squirrel thinks he stole her beechnuts."

"And it is all Mr. Crow's doing," Jasper said, his voice grim. "He uses a sliver of truth--Sandy taking the catalogue--to create a mountain of untruth and suspicion about something entirely different. This is how true mischief is made, Fatty, not by simple blunders, but by deliberate distortion."

Frisky Squirrel, who had heard all of this, suddenly looked alarmed. "But if Mrs. Squirrel is missing beechnuts, and she thinks Sandy took them, what if she tells her children? And what if her children tell their friends? And what if that story grows and grows, until everyone in Pleasant Valley believes Sandy is a thief?"

"That, Frisky," Jasper said gravely, "is precisely the danger. A false story, once it takes root, is very difficult to dislodge. And it can do untold damage to one's good name and reputation."

Fatty Coon began to wring his paws. "We must do something, Jasper! We cannot let Mr. Crow get away with this. Sandy is our friend, and he is not a thief."

"Indeed we must, Fatty," Jasper agreed, already planning. "But we must be careful. Confronting Mr. Crow directly only encourages him. We must find a way to expose the truth, and clear Sandy's name, without giving Mr. Crow any further opportunity for his sly machinations."

"But how?" Frisky asked, his tail twitching nervously. "How does one fight a story that has already begun to spread like wildfire?"

"One fights it with the truth, Frisky," Jasper declared, puffing out his chest once more, but this time with a sense of purpose. "One finds the real story, and one spreads it even faster and more widely than the lie. But first, we must find out if Mrs. Squirrel's beechnuts are truly missing, or if that, too, is merely one of Mr. Crow's clever inventions."

And so, with a shared sense of urgency, Jasper Jay and Fatty Coon decided to investigate. Frisky Squirrel, still somewhat nervous about the potential for further complications, nonetheless joined them, for he was fond of Sandy and knew in his heart that his friend was no thief. They began by flying and lumbering, respectively, towards Mrs. Squirrel's home, which was in a grand old oak tree on the far side of Farmer Green's meadow. They hoped to arrive before Mr. Crow's seeds of suspicion had fully blossomed into a thorny thicket of misunderstanding.

Meanwhile, back at his own cozy burrow, Sandy Chipmunk was quite unaware of the storm brewing around him. He was happily munching on one of his perfectly gathered beechnuts, thinking how pleasant it was to have such a generous store for the winter. He had, as yet, not heard a whisper of Mrs. Squirrel's supposed missing nuts, nor of Mr. Crow's latest round of mischief. He only knew that the "letter" he had found was full of interesting pictures, though he still felt a little put out that so many other creatures had their likenesses within "his" document. He had, in fact, taken to looking at the pictures of the various farm implements in Farmer Green's catalogue, wondering what peculiar uses they might have. There was a particularly fearsome-looking contraption that he imagined might be useful for cracking the toughest of acorns, though he could not quite fathom how to operate it. He had even shown it to a passing field mouse, who had scurried away in fright at the sight of the drawing, convinced it was a new kind of mouse-trap. Such was the innocent, though sometimes misguided, life of Sandy Chipmunk, a life that was about to become considerably more complicated due to the idle words of a certain black bird.

As Jasper and Fatty approached Mrs. Squirrel's tree, they could indeed hear a great deal of chattering and scolding coming from within its leafy branches. Mrs. Squirrel's voice was quite distinct, and she sounded very cross indeed.

"My precious darlings!" she exclaimed, her voice shrill. "How could they have vanished? I marked them carefully, every last one, the plumpest, the sweetest, the ones I saved for the truly lean days! And now, they are simply GONE!"

Her children, a quartet of small, nervous-looking squirrels, huddled together, looking utterly bewildered. "But Mother," one of them squeaked, "we haven't touched them! We've been gathering all morning, just as you told us!"

"Yes, Mother," another chimed in, "we wouldn't dare. You know how cross you get when we so much as look at the winter store!"

Jasper Jay and Fatty Coon exchanged worried glances. It seemed Mr. Crow had not fabricated the story of Mrs. Squirrel's missing nuts entirely, though his insinuation about Sandy remained a cruel and deliberate twist. Mrs. Squirrel truly believed some of her beechnuts were gone.

"Perhaps," Jasper called out, flying closer to the tree, "perhaps there has been some mistake, Mrs. Squirrel?"

Mrs. Squirrel poked her head out from a knot-hole, her whiskers twitching with agitation. "Mistake, Jasper Jay? There is no mistake! My beechnuts are gone! A whole cache of them! And I can only think of one explanation, one very greedy explanation indeed!" She glared pointedly in the general direction of Sandy Chipmunk's burrow, though she could not see it from her perch.

Fatty Coon lumbered forward, trying to soothe her. "Now, now, Mrs. Squirrel, let us not jump to conclusions. Sandy Chipmunk is a good neighbor. He would never steal your nuts."

"Wouldn't he?" Mrs. Squirrel sniffed. "I heard a tale, just this morning, a very disturbing tale, about a certain chipmunk taking what was not his from Farmer Green's mail-box! And if he would take a letter, why not a few beechnuts, I ask you?"

Jasper Jay felt a wave of despair. Mr. Crow's words had indeed taken root, and quickly too. "Mrs. Squirrel," he said, speaking slowly and clearly, "the matter of the mail-box was a complete misunderstanding. Sandy thought the letter, a catalogue with a picture of a chipmunk, was for him. It was a mistake, not a theft. He meant no harm."

"A mistake that caused Farmer Green to lose his special corn seeds!" Mrs. Squirrel retorted, repeating a detail she had clearly picked up and embellished. "And now you ask me to believe that *my* missing beechnuts are also a 'mistake'? I think not, Jasper Jay! I think there is a pattern here! A pattern of one particular chipmunk helping himself to the belongings of others!"

Frisky Squirrel, who had been listening intently, finally spoke up. "But Mrs. Squirrel, Sandy has so many beechnuts of his own! Mr. Crow even made him gather them, thinking they were corn, and then Mr. Crow didn't want them! Sandy's burrow is practically overflowing with beechnuts!"

Mrs. Squirrel paused, her small eyes widening slightly. "Mr. Crow made him gather them? And then didn't want them?" This was a new piece of information, one that had not been part of the story she had heard.

"Indeed," Jasper affirmed, seizing the opportunity. "Mr. Crow, in his usual misleading fashion, asked Sandy to gather 'food' for him, but refused to specify 'corn.' Sandy, thinking to do a good deed, gathered the finest beechnuts. Then Mr. Crow rejected them, leaving Sandy with an abundance. He has more than enough for himself, Mrs. Squirrel, and certainly no need to take from you."

Fatty Coon nodded vigorously. "It is quite true, Mrs. Squirrel. I saw Sandy's store just the other day. It is a magnificent collection of beechnuts, piled high."

Mrs. Squirrel looked from Jasper to Fatty, and then at her own children, who were now looking less frightened and more intrigued. The idea of Sandy having such a vast surplus of beechnuts, an amount Mr. Crow had so recently rejected, certainly cast a different light on things. She still believed her nuts were missing, but the motive for Sandy to take them now seemed very thin indeed.

"But still, my nuts are gone!" she insisted, though with less conviction. "If not Sandy, then who? Or what?"

Jasper Jay, seeing a chance to steer the truth, decided to take a bold step. "Perhaps, Mrs. Squirrel, you simply misplaced them. Or perhaps, in your great diligence, you hid them so well that even you cannot remember their exact location?"

Mrs. Squirrel bristled. "I do not misplace my nuts, Jasper Jay! I am exceedingly organized!" But even as she spoke, a tiny flicker of doubt crossed her sharp features. She was, it must be admitted, a very enthusiastic gatherer, and sometimes, in her zeal to secure her provisions, she would create small, secret caches in unusual places, only to forget them later.

"Perchance," Fatty Coon suggested gently, "the very children you are now scolding, in their eagerness to help, may have moved them for safekeeping to an even better spot, one you have yet to discover?"

At this, the squirrel children exchanged nervous glances. One of them, the smallest, nudged his brother. "Remember, Pip? That excellent hollow log by the great stone? The one Mother said was too exposed, but we thought it would be perfect?"

Pip's eyes went wide. "Oh, no! We never told Mother! We moved just a few, to see if they'd be safe there!"

Mrs. Squirrel, hearing this, turned sharply to her offspring. "You moved my carefully sorted beechnuts? Without telling me? To an exposed hollow log?"

The children confessed, in a rapid burst of chattering, that they had indeed, in a fit of well-intentioned but misguided enthusiasm, moved a portion of the "missing" beechnuts to a spot they deemed superior, hoping to surprise their mother with their ingenuity. Alas, they had only surprised her with their disappearance, and then, in their fear of her scolding, had kept silent.

Mrs. Squirrel's anger quickly shifted from Sandy Chipmunk to her own well-meaning but disobedient children. "Oh, you naughty, naughty little squirrels! What possessed you to interfere with my perfect system? Now I must go and retrieve them, and reorganize everything!"

As Mrs. Squirrel flew off in a flurry of scolding and chattering, her children trailing behind her, Jasper Jay looked at Fatty Coon and Frisky Squirrel with a triumphant gleam in his eye.

"You see, my friends?" Jasper said, preening a little. "The truth, however hidden or obscured, always has a way of coming to light. And those who spread untruths, whether out of carelessness or malice, are often hoist by their own petard."

"So, the beechnuts were never stolen at all!" Fatty Coon exclaimed, quite relieved. "And Sandy's name is cleared!"

"Indeed," Jasper confirmed. "And all because we sought the facts, rather than simply accepting the twisted tales of others. It is a valuable lesson for us all, I daresay. One must always question the stories that fly about, especially when they seem to confirm one's suspicions or delight one's mischievous inclinations."

Frisky Squirrel, who had learned a great deal that day, chattered in agreement. "I shall remember that, Jasper. I shall try my best to tell stories exactly as they happened, without adding a single extra horse to the wagon, or a single extra haystack to the mailbag."

Jasper Jay smiled. "That is an excellent resolve, Frisky. For the world, after all, is quite interesting enough with its plain truths, without needing the embellishments of fanciful fictions. And clear communication, as we have seen, spares us all a great deal of trouble."

As for Mr. Crow, he never quite learned this lesson. He continued to enjoy his little games of exaggeration and insinuation, always finding new ways to stir up amusement or vexation amongst the forest folk. But after the incident with Mrs. Squirrel and the beechnuts, the animals of Pleasant Valley grew a little wiser. They still listened to Mr. Crow's tales, for he was an entertaining old bird, but they listened with a new caution, always seeking to discern the small, mischievous kernel of truth from the larger, more elaborate husk of his invention. And whenever a new, improbable story began to spread, one of them, usually Jasper Jay, would say, "Now, let us not be too quick to believe this. Let us investigate, and find the true matter of it, before we allow ourselves to be led astray." And in this way, though mischief never entirely left Pleasant Valley, its sting was often dulled, and its power to cause genuine harm considerably lessened. For even the cleverest of crows cannot prevail against a community that has learned the value of truth and the wisdom of clear communication. And Sandy Chipmunk, though he still cherished Farmer Green's mail-order catalogue with its charming pictures, never again attempted to open the mail-box. He had, at last, learned that some things, however tempting, were best left undisturbed. He had learned that the truth, however unglamorous, was often simpler and less troublesome than the most exciting of fictions. And he kept his beechnuts safe, and enjoyed them thoroughly, knowing they were truly his own.

Now, with Sandy Chipmunk having settled into a comfortable rhythm of honest foraging and Mr. Crow having been gently rebuked for his exaggerations, a quiet period descended upon Pleasant Valley. The spring bloomed into summer, and the days grew long and warm. The forest folk went about their usual business, some gathering, some exploring, and others simply enjoying the sunshine. Sandy Chipmunk, with his well-stocked burrow, found himself with more leisure than ever before. He spent his mornings nibbling on his stored strawberries and beechnuts, and his afternoons watching the world go by from the entrance of his burrow. It was a contented life, free from the worries of hunger or the complications of exaggerated tales.

One hot afternoon, as Sandy was enjoying a nap in the cool shade of a fern, he was startled awake by a most unusual sound. It was a high-pitched, insistent chirping, not like any bird he knew. It was faint at first, then grew louder, carrying a note of distress.

A stunned silence fell over the assembled animals. Jasper Jay's feathers drooped. Fatty Coon opened his mouth, then closed it. Sandy and Frisky, from their hiding place, exchanged a look of triumphant understanding.

Mr. Crow, however, was aghast. "An advertisement? For _feed_? And pictures of animals merely for _advertising_? Impossible! This is a document of profound importance! Jasper Jay himself deciphered it! He said it was a call for 'services'!"

Farmer Green burst into a hearty laugh. "Jasper Jay, reading an advertisement? Bless your hearts, you silly creatures. This paper is nothing but an offer to sell me more expensive feed for my chickens! And it's addressed to 'Occupant,' not to you, Mr. Crow, nor to any of your 'able assistants'." He held up the paper, turning it so all could see the heading clearly. "Happy Critter Chow! See? No secret instructions, no hidden bounties. Just plain old selling, that's what it is."

Mr. Crow's face, or what could be seen of it beneath his ruffled feathers, turned a dark shade of crimson. He felt foolish, utterly and completely outwitted, not by another bird, but by a piece of paper and Farmer Green's simple explanation. The grand scheme he had envisioned, the delicious rewards he had promised himself, all dissolved into thin air.
