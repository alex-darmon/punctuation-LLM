Whiskers Mouse scurried through Farmer Brown's new cornfield, his whiskers twitching with every rustle of dry leaves. It was late autumn, and the air held a crisp promise of winter. Whiskers was always a careful little creature; he made it a rule never to venture too far from the safety of his burrow. But this day was different, for he had sniffed out an extra-large kernel of corn, lying quite by itself near the edge of the field.

As he reached for his prize, his nose brushed against something cold and smooth. "What in the world is this?" he whispered to himself. It was a round, flat object, gleaming like a tiny sun, and it hummed ever so faintly. Whiskers nudged it with his paw; it didn't move. He tried to nibble it; his sharp teeth made no mark. "This is certainly a mystery!" he exclaimed, his little heart beating fast with a mix of curiosity and fear. He thought it might be a new kind of nut, but it felt much too hard for that. All the while, Sandy Chipmunk and Frisky Squirrel kept their eyes peeled, watching for any sign of the mysterious watch. They saw Farmer Green take it out several times. Sometimes he merely glanced at it, as if to reassure himself it was still there, a comfortable weight in his hand. Other times, he held it up to his ear, listening intently to its soft tick-tock, a sound that the little animals, even from their perch, could not quite discern. But no matter how many times he looked at it, no matter how many times he listened, his actions did not seem to be directly dictated by its little hands. He did not suddenly drop his axe and run to the chicken coop. He did not abandon his fence-mending to rush to the well for water. His movements were slow and deliberate, the steady rhythm of a man who knew his farm and knew his chores.

"This is most perplexing!" Sandy whispered to Frisky, his tail twitching with impatience. The sun was high in the sky now, warming the oak leaves, but Sandy's mind was anything but warm. It was filled with cold, hard confusion. "Mr. Crow made it sound so simple. He said the watch would tell Farmer Green what to do. But all Farmer Green does is his usual work, and he looks at the watch now and then, just as if it were nothing more than a pretty silver stone."

Frisky Squirrel munched thoughtfully on a stray acorn he had found nestled in the bark. "Perhaps," he mused, "the watch only tells him what to do when there is something *unusual* to be done. Perhaps it is a watch for emergencies. Or perhaps," he added, with a shake of his head, "perhaps it is a watch for *thinking*. Mr. Crow did say that Farmer Green 'thinks to himself' when he looks at it. Maybe the watch simply helps him think."

Sandy frowned, a tiny furrow appearing between his bright, curious eyes. "But what does it help him think *about*? And how do the 'big hand' and 'little hand' help him think? It makes no sense, Frisky. Mr. Crow said they point to his duties. But they seem to point to nothing at all, save the small

marks on its face, which, after all, moved slowly, slowly, and seemed to point to nothing of great consequence.

Sandy sighed, a little puff of air that ruffled the tiny hairs on his nose. "It is just as I thought," he said. "Mr. Crow is a very clever bird, and he knows many things, but sometimes I think he has a way of knowing things that are not quite so. Or perhaps," he added, scratching thoughtfully behind an ear, "perhaps he means what he says, but what he says does not mean what *we* think it means."

Frisky Squirrel nodded. He had often noticed this peculiar quality in Mr. Crow's pronouncements. Mr. Crow would speak with great authority, and his words would sound grand and important, but when one tried to understand them exactly, they often slipped away like water through a sieve. "He said the watch tells Farmer Green what to do," Frisky repeated, slowly and carefully, as if trying to catch the elusive meaning. "But it does not seem to tell him anything, does it? He just goes about his business, exactly as he always has."

They watched Farmer Green for another hour. He finished mending the fence, then walked slowly towards his little garden patch behind the house. He bent over the soil, pulled out a few weeds, and then straightened up, rubbing his back. All the while, the silver watch remained tucked safely in his pocket. He did not consult it before deciding to pull a weed, nor did he look at it to see if it was time to straighten his back. It was most puzzling.

"I think," Sandy whispered, a new idea forming in his busy little head, "I think we must have misunderstood Mr. Crow entirely. Perhaps the watch does not tell Farmer Green what to do during the day at all. Perhaps it is a special watch for the *night*."

Frisky blinked. "The night?" he echoed. "But what would it tell him to do at night? Farmer Green usually sleeps at night, just as we do. He does not go about chopping wood or mending fences in the dark."

"Ah, but that is just it!" Sandy exclaimed, his tail giving a happy little twitch. "You see, we only observe him during the day. How do we know what the watch tells him to do when the moon is out? Perhaps it tells him to get up and plant a new cabbage patch! Or to check on his cows! Or even to count his chickens, just to make sure they are all there."

Frisky considered this. It did seem rather unlikely. Farmer Green was, to Frisky's certain knowledge, a creature of habit, and his habits mostly involved sleeping soundly in his bed once the sun had gone down. Yet, Sandy's idea did offer a new avenue for investigation, and investigation was what they were about, after all. "It is a thought," Frisky allowed. "But how would we ever know? We cannot very well watch Farmer Green all through the night. Not only would it be very cold, but it would also be very dark, and we would not be able to see the watch even if he did take it out."

"That is true," Sandy admitted, his initial enthusiasm dimming slightly. "And besides, Farmer Green sleeps in his house, and his house has thick walls and tightly closed windows. Even if we stayed awake, we would not be able to see anything at all."

They sat in silence for a few minutes, watching the farmer disappear into his house for what they supposed was his midday meal. The problem of the watch, which had seemed so simple when Mr. Crow first described it, had grown into a knot as tangled as old roots.

"Perhaps," Frisky began again, "perhaps the watch does not tell Farmer Green what to do directly. Perhaps it merely reminds him of the *time* for things. Mr. Crow said it had a 'big hand' and a 'little hand' that pointed to his duties. What if those hands merely point to the general time when a duty ought to be performed, and Farmer Green knows from long practice what that duty is?"

Sandy's brow furrowed again. "But then, why would he need a watch at all? He knows when the sun is high, and when it is low. He knows when it is morning and when it is evening. He does not need a silver watch to tell him that the sun is shining."

"That is true," Frisky conceded. "But perhaps there are some duties that are not tied to the sun. What if he needs to do something at a very specific moment, even if the sun is still high or still low? What if he has to, for instance, water a certain plant exactly when the big hand points to the twelve and the little hand points to the six? And then, if he misses that precise moment, the plant will not grow properly?"

Sandy shivered at the thought. "Oh, that would be terrible! Imagine if all of Farmer Green's crops failed because he did not look at his watch at the exact right moment!" He looked at Frisky with wide eyes. "Do you think that is what Mr. Crow meant? That the watch is a secret, powerful thing that controls all of Farmer Green's farming? That his whole livelihood depends on those two little hands?"

Frisky, who was usually more cautious in his pronouncements, found himself nodding. The idea, though dramatic, had a certain grand logic to it, much like Mr. Crow's own tales. "It might be so, Sandy. Farmer Green is a diligent man, but even the most diligent man might forget a crucial moment without a reminder. And perhaps the watch is just that: a very important reminder."

This new theory brought a fresh wave of concern to Sandy's little heart. If the watch was so important, so crucial to Farmer Green's well-being and the well-being of his farm, then it ought to be treated with utmost respect. And yet, Farmer Green just tucked it away in his pocket, as if it were nothing more than a common pebble.

"He does not seem to appreciate its power," Sandy mused. "He just keeps it hidden away. If I had such a wonderful watch, I would carry it in my paws all the time, just to make sure I did not miss any important duties."

"Perhaps," Frisky suggested, "the watch is not meant to be shown off. Perhaps its power is secret, known only to Farmer Green and to the watch itself. And Mr. Crow, of course," he added as an afterthought.

They watched through the rest of the afternoon. Farmer Green reappeared, fed his animals again, and then spent a long time in his barn. Once or twice, he took out the watch, looked at it, and then tucked it back. Each time, Sandy and Frisky held their breaths, waiting for some sudden, decisive action to follow, some great, watch-commanded deed. But no such deed occurred. Farmer Green merely continued his steady, unhurried pace, as if the watch had told him nothing more than that the sun was still in the sky.

As evening approached, and the shadows grew long across the farmyard, Sandy's patience began to wear thin. "I do not understand it, Frisky," he confessed. "I have watched him all day. I have watched him chop wood, mend fences, pull weeds, feed chickens, and go into his barn. And not once has the watch seemed to tell him to do any of these things. He just does them, as if he knew what to do all along, even without the big hand and the little hand."

Frisky twitched his nose. "It is a mystery, Sandy, a grand mystery. But perhaps we are looking at it all wrong. Mr. Crow said the watch was 'full of clever wheels and springs.' What if those wheels and springs do not tell Farmer Green what to do, but instead tell him *when not to do something*?"

Sandy stared at him. "When *not* to do something? What a strange idea! Why would Farmer Green need a watch to tell him not to do anything?"

"Well," Frisky explained, "perhaps the watch prevents him from doing things too early, or too late. Imagine if Farmer Green were to plant his corn in the middle of winter! Or if he were to harvest his apples before they were ripe! The watch, with its clever hands, might simply tell him, 'No, not yet, Farmer Green,' or 'No, it is too late, Farmer Green.' And so, he waits, or he stops, and that is why his farm always looks so prosperous."

This idea, while equally convoluted, made a certain sense to Sandy. He had often seen Farmer Green waiting for things: for the ground to soften, for the sun to warm, for the fruit to ripen. Perhaps the watch was not a director of actions, but a great preventer of mistakes. "So, the watch helps him *not* to make mistakes," Sandy said, trying out the new theory. "That is almost as important as telling him what to do, isn't it? Perhaps even more important!"

They discussed this new possibility until the last rays of sunlight faded from the sky and the first stars began to twinkle overhead. The air grew chilly, and the forest began to settle down for the night. Their little stomachs, too, began to rumble, reminding them that they had spent the entire day on their watch-watching mission without a single meal.

"We must go home now," Frisky said, shivering slightly. "But we have learned much, Sandy. We have learned that the watch is either a very powerful director of duties, a reminder of specific moments, or a clever preventer of errors."

Sandy nodded, though he felt no closer to understanding the true nature of the silver object. Each theory seemed plausible for a time, only to fall apart under close observation of Farmer Green's unperturbed routine. "Yes," he agreed. "And we have learned that Mr. Crow's knowledge, while vast, is also very, very tricky."

The next morning, Sandy and Frisky were back at their observation post in the oak tree. They had shared their new theories with some of their friends, including Fatty Coon, who merely yawned and said that if a watch did not produce food, it was not worth thinking about. But Jasper Jay, always eager for a bit of news or a good story, had listened with keen interest.

"So, Farmer Green has a magical watch, does he?" Jasper had squawked, his head cocked to one side. "A watch that tells him what to do, or what not to do, or when to do something specific! Well, that is certainly a marvel. But why does he not show it off? If I had such a watch, I would perch it on my head and let everyone see its clever hands moving!"

Sandy had tried to explain that the watch's power might be secret, but Jasper Jay had merely ruffled his feathers and declared that secrets were no fun unless they were shared.

Now, as they watched Farmer Green emerge from his house, yawning and stretching in the morning sun, Sandy had a new idea. "Frisky," he whispered, "do you think the watch changes its instructions for each day? Perhaps yesterday it was telling him to mend fences, and today it will tell him to plow his fields! We must watch carefully today to see if his duties are different."

Frisky, ever the practical one, pointed out that Farmer Green's duties tended to follow a certain cycle, regardless of any watch. "He plows in the spring, and he harvests in the autumn, Sandy. He does not suddenly decide to plant corn in July because a watch tells him to."

"Ah, but that is just it!" Sandy retorted. "What if the watch tells him *when* it is spring, or *when* it is autumn, and then he knows what to do? Perhaps it is not about the chores themselves, but about the *seasons*!"

This was a grand thought, and it kept them busy for a good part of the morning. They watched Farmer Green, who did indeed seem to be engaged in slightly different tasks than the day before. He spent a long time in his garden, planting small seedlings in neat rows. He paused often, straightened up, and occasionally took out his watch, glancing at it before returning to his work.

"See!" Sandy exclaimed triumphantly, nudging Frisky with his elbow. "The watch told him to plant seedlings! Yesterday he was mending fences, and today he is planting! The watch changes its commands from day to day!"

Frisky, however, was not entirely convinced. "But Sandy, it is spring. Farmer Green always plants seedlings in the spring. He has been doing so for as long as I can remember. The watch may have moved its hands, but I am not certain it is the watch that is telling him to plant."

Their debate was interrupted by a familiar, boisterous squawk. "Haw-haw-haw! Still watching that watch, are you, little chipmunks?"

It was Mr. Crow, perched on a branch just above them, his black eyes gleaming with amusement. He had, no doubt, been observing *them* observing Farmer Green.

"Mr. Crow!" Sandy exclaimed. "We have been watching Farmer Green all morning, and all yesterday. And we have many questions about his watch!"

"Questions, indeed!" Mr. Crow cawed, preening a feather on his wing. "I told you all you needed to know. It tells him what to do, with its big hand and its little hand."

"But Mr. Crow," Frisky interjected politely, "we observed Farmer Green taking out his watch many times. And each time, he merely continued with whatever he was doing. It did not seem to tell him to chop wood, or to mend a fence, or to plant seedlings. He simply did those things without its direct command."

Mr. Crow eyed them with a knowing look. "Ah, but you are misunderstanding the subtlety of the watch, my dear squirrels and chipmunks. It does not _shout_ its commands. It _whispers_ them. It reminds him. A clever man like Farmer Green does not need to be told everything loudly, does he? A mere glance, a slight movement of the hands, and he understands. It is a secret language between the farmer and his timepiece."

"A secret language!" Sandy repeated, delighted. This made perfect sense! It explained why they could not see the watch giving direct orders. It was too subtle for their unpracticed eyes and ears.

"Exactly so!" Mr. Crow puffed out his chest. "And furthermore, the watch does not just tell him what to do with his hands and feet, but also with his _mind_. It tells him what to _think_."

Frisky and Sandy exchanged a bewildered glance. "What to _think_?" Sandy asked. "But how can a watch tell a person what to think?"

"Through its hands, of course!" Mr. Crow declared, as if it were the most obvious thing in the world. "When the big hand points to the twelve, and the little hand points to the three, it tells Farmer Green to think about planting beans. And when the big hand points to the six, and the little hand points to the nine, it tells him to think about patching his barn roof. It is a grand mental calendar, a scheduler of thoughts!"

"So, it tells him to *think* about doing things, and then he goes and does them?" Frisky clarified, trying to follow Mr. Crow's elaborate logic.

"Precisely!" Mr. Crow nodded sagely. "It plants the seeds of thought in his mind, and then those thoughts grow into actions. That is why he glances at it so often. He is checking his mental schedule, ensuring his thoughts are in order."

Sandy's head spun. This was by far the most complicated explanation yet. The watch was not just a director of actions, nor a preventer of mistakes, nor a reminder of seasons. It was a director of *thoughts*! A controller of the very inner workings of Farmer Green's mind!

"But Mr. Crow," Sandy asked, "if it tells him what to think, does it also tell him _when_ to think those things? Or does he just think them whenever the hands point?"

"Oh, it is much more complex than that, little chipmunk!" Mr. Crow cawed, relishing the complexity. "The watch, you see, has a rhythm. A secret rhythm of thought. It might tell him to think about beans in the morning, and then about the barn roof in the afternoon, even if the hands point to the same positions. The watch is a very clever fellow indeed. It knows that a man's mind, just like his stomach, needs a varied diet of thoughts."

Frisky Squirrel, who had been listening with his head tilted, seemed to have reached a limit to his understanding. "But Mr. Crow," he said, quite respectfully, "if the watch controls his thoughts, and those thoughts become actions, then why does Farmer Green sometimes just sit and look at the sky, or lean against his hoe, doing nothing at all? Does the watch tell him to think of nothing then?"

Mr. Crow ruffled his feathers again. "Ah, that is the most advanced function of all, young squirrel! Sometimes, the watch tells him to think of _rest_. It tells him that his mind has worked hard enough, and it is time for a quiet moment. It tells him to appreciate the sun, or the clouds, or the song of a bird. It is a very considerate watch, indeed."

Sandy and Frisky looked at each other. This explanation, while wonderfully intricate, seemed to encompass everything and nothing all at once. The watch told Farmer Green what to do, and what not to do, and what to think, and when to think, and even when to think of nothing at all. It was a universal director, an all-knowing guide. It was, in short, a magical object beyond their wildest imaginings.

"So," Sandy said slowly, trying to grasp the full implications, "the watch tells Farmer Green absolutely everything he needs to know, and everything he needs to think, about his entire day, and his entire farm, and even his entire rest?"

"That is the sum of it, my boy!" Mr. Crow declared proudly. "Now, do you still have questions? Or do you see the profound wisdom of my observations?"

Sandy and Frisky had no more questions, not because they understood, but because they felt their heads might burst if they tried to absorb any more of Mr. Crow's profound wisdom. They merely nodded, looking rather dazed.

Mr. Crow, pleased with himself, gave another "Haw-haw-haw!" and flew off, presumably to share his profound insights with some other unsuspecting creature.

"Well," Sandy said, after a long silence, "that explains it all, doesn't it, Frisky?"

Frisky Squirrel shook his head slowly. "I am not so sure, Sandy. It explains so much that it almost explains nothing at all. If the watch tells him to think of everything, and to do everything, and not to do some things, and to rest at other times, then it is merely telling him to be Farmer Green, which he already is, watch or no watch. It is as if the watch is telling him to breathe, which he does without thinking."

"But Mr. Crow said it was a secret language!" Sandy insisted, still clinging to the idea. "And a rhythm of thought! Perhaps we are simply not clever enough to understand such a deep and complicated thing."

"Perhaps," Frisky agreed, though a flicker of doubt remained in his eyes. "But I still wish I could get a closer look at those clever wheels and springs he spoke of. A close look might make all the difference. If only Farmer Green would leave it somewhere accessible."

As if on cue, Farmer Green, who had been planting diligently in his garden, stood up, dusted his hands, and walked towards the farmhouse door. But before he went inside, he did something quite unusual. He reached into his pocket, pulled out the silver watch, looked at it for a moment, and then, instead of putting it back, he placed it carefully on the small wooden ledge by his kitchen door, a ledge where he often left his muddy boots or his gardening gloves. He then entered the house, leaving the watch glinting in the morning sun.

Sandy and Frisky froze. Their little hearts began to thump-thump-thump against their ribs. This was an unprecedented opportunity! The watch, the mysterious, thought-directing, action-commanding, mistake-preventing watch, was right there, sitting on a ledge, unattended.

"He has left it!" Sandy whispered, his voice trembling with excitement. "He has left his magical thought-director right out in the open!"

Frisky's eyes were wide. "It is a chance, Sandy, a wonderful chance! Now we can see the clever wheels and springs, and perhaps even hear the secret whispers that guide Farmer Green's mind!"

But a moment later, a shadow of caution crossed Frisky's face. "But Sandy," he said, "what if it is a trap? What if Farmer Green knows we are curious about his watch, and he has left it there deliberately, to catch us?"

Sandy considered this. Farmer Green, while generally good-natured, was known to set traps for pests in his garden. But a trap that looked like a watch? It seemed rather elaborate, even for Farmer Green. "No, Frisky," Sandy decided. "I do not think so. Farmer Green would not use such a precious thing as a trap. Mr. Crow said it was full of clever parts! He would not risk them in a common trap."

"That is true," Frisky conceded. "And besides, my curiosity is far too great to be held back by such worries. We must go! We must investigate!"

With that, the two little friends scurried down the oak tree, their tails twitching with anticipation. They darted across the open ground of the farmyard, keeping low to avoid detection, though Farmer Green was safely inside his house. They reached the kitchen door in a matter of seconds, their breathing quick and shallow.

The watch sat on the wooden ledge, silent and still. It was smaller than they had imagined, a neat silver circle, with two tiny hands that pointed to small black numbers. It did not seem particularly magical or full of profound wisdom. It was just a small, silver object.

"Well," Sandy whispered, looking closely, "I do not see any clever wheels and springs. Just a smooth silver casing."

Frisky, more adventurous, stretched out a paw and gently poked the watch. It felt cool and hard. "The clever parts must be inside, Sandy. We cannot see them from here. But how do we hear the whispers, or read the secret language?"

Sandy leaned closer, his nose almost touching the smooth surface. He heard a very faint, almost imperceptible sound. "Listen, Frisky! I think I hear it! A tiny little _tick-tock_! It is very, very quiet. Perhaps that is the secret whisper!"

Frisky listened too, straining his ears. "Yes, I hear it! A very small sound, indeed. So quiet, one must be very close to hear it at all. So this _tick-tock_ is the secret language, then? But what does it mean? Does _tick_ mean 'think of beans', and _tock_ mean 'think of the barn roof'?"

They spent several intense minutes listening to the _tick-tock_, trying to discern a pattern, trying to translate the secret language of the watch. But all they heard was the steady, rhythmic sound, unchanging and monotonous. It did not sound like it was whispering profound thoughts or issuing urgent commands. It just sounded like a small, busy thing, going about its own business.

"It is very puzzling," Sandy admitted, finally pulling back. "It just says 'tick-tock, tick-tock.' It does not say 'plant more carrots,' or 'chop more wood.' It just says 'tick-tock.'"

"Perhaps," Frisky suggested, ever ready with a new theory, "the meaning is not in the words, but in the _feeling_ of the sound. Perhaps the _tick-tock_ is a calming sound, a sound that helps Farmer Green to order his thoughts, even if it does not tell him *what* to think."

Just then, a loud, clear sound came from inside the house: "DINNER!" It was Mrs. Green's voice, calling Farmer Green to his meal.

Suddenly, the kitchen door swung open, and Farmer Green himself stepped out. He bent down, picked up the silver watch from the ledge, tucked it into his pocket without a second glance, and then strode off towards the dining room.

Sandy and Frisky, who had frozen solid at his appearance, let out their breaths in unison once he was gone. They had barely managed to flatten themselves against the wall, out of his line of sight.

"He just took it!" Sandy whispered, still a little shaky. "He did not look at it, he did not listen to its _tick-tock_, he did not even seem to acknowledge its profound wisdom! He just picked it up and put it away!"

"It is most perplexing," Frisky agreed. "He acted as if it were a common object, not a magical director of his mind. And Mrs. Green's voice seemed to be the true director of his actions, not the watch at all!"

They scurried back across the yard and up their oak tree, their minds reeling. Mr. Crow's explanations, which had seemed so grand and all-encompassing, now felt as insubstantial as cobwebs. The watch, up close, had revealed none of its promised magic. It had merely _ticked_. And Farmer Green, despite Mr. Crow's elaborate theories, still seemed to be guided by the sound of his wife's voice and the simple needs of his day.

"I think," Sandy said, settling onto a branch and trying to untangle the confusion in his brain, "I think Mr. Crow likes to tell stories more than he likes to tell the plain truth. Or perhaps," he added, with a sigh, "the truth about watches is not nearly as exciting as a good story."

Frisky nodded, already munching on a small bit of bark he had found. "Perhaps, Sandy, the watch is just a watch. And Farmer Green is just Farmer Green. And the world, after all, is just the world, with its meals and its fences and its seedlings, all happening without the need for a magical silver guide."

But even as he said it, a tiny part of him wondered if they had simply failed to understand the watch's true secrets. For sometimes, the most profound magic was hidden in the most ordinary things, and required a very clever mind indeed to uncover it. And neither Sandy nor Frisky were quite sure if their minds were clever enough for such a task. They knew, however, that they would never look at a silver watch, or listen to a boastful crow, in quite the same way again.

The very next morning, the world seemed a little different to Sandy Chipmunk and Frisky Squirrel. The sun still rose with its accustomed cheer, and the leaves still danced upon the branches, but the sharp edge of wonder, which had once accompanied every new human contraption, was now somewhat dulled by a quiet skepticism. They still possessed their boundless curiosity, of course, for an idle mind was an unhappy mind, but they now approached new mysteries with a more cautious spirit, having learned that the most elaborate explanations were not always the truest.

"It is a peculiar thing, this business of understanding," Sandy remarked to Frisky, as they shared a particularly plump acorn on their favorite oak branch. "One thinks one has grasped the very root of a matter, only to find it was but a loose twig, easily snapped."

Frisky, who was always more practical than philosophical, merely nodded, his cheeks already bulging with his share of the nut. "Indeed, Sandy. And some twigs, I have observed, are remarkably sturdy in their appearance, only to crumble to dust when one leans upon them." He paused, swallowing with a faint rustle. "Mr. Crow's wisdom, for instance, often proves to be of such a brittle nature."

They exchanged a glance, a silent agreement passing between them regarding the limitations of their feathered friend's grand pronouncements. It was not that they disliked Mr. Crow, for he was often entertaining and possessed a certain bold spirit, but his theories, especially when applied to the strange habits of Farmer Green, had a way of leading one astray.

Just then, their conversation was interrupted by a most unusual sight. Down in Farmer Green's garden, where rows of young corn stalks now stood tall and green, a new figure had appeared overnight. It stood in the very middle of the field, stiff and unmoving, a strange sentinel watching over the tender plants.

"Look!" Sandy exclaimed, pointing with a small paw. "What in the world is _that_?"

Frisky squinted, his bright eyes trying to make sense of the new arrival. It was taller than Farmer Green himself, clad in an old, faded blue shirt and trousers that flapped gently in the morning breeze. A battered hat, much too large for its head, covered what appeared to be a lumpy sack, fashioned somehow into a face. Its arms, stretched wide upon a stick, seemed to embrace the entire field, or perhaps to ward off some unseen danger.

"It looks like a man," Frisky observed slowly, "but a very peculiar man indeed. He does not move, nor does he seem to breathe. And his face, well, it is not much of a face at all."

Sandy, ever the more adventurous, hopped down a few branches. "Perhaps he is one of those 'watchers' that Mr. Crow spoke of," he suggested, remembering Mr. Crow's elaborate theories about the silver watch. "Perhaps he is watching the corn, to ensure it grows straight and tall, just as the watch watched over Farmer Green's thoughts."

"But if he is watching," Frisky countered, following Sandy, "why does he not blink? Or scratch his ear? Or, at the very least, look about him? He simply stares ahead, as if fixed upon some invisible point."

They debated the matter for a good long while, offering various theories, each more unlikely than the last. Was it a new kind of human sleeping upright? Was it a cleverly disguised tree, planted by Farmer Green to confuse the birds? Or perhaps, and this thought sent a shiver down Sandy's spine, was it a human who had been turned to wood by some mysterious forest magic?

As the sun climbed higher, casting long shadows across Farmer Green's neatly tilled rows, a familiar "caw-caw!" echoed from the distant woods. It was Mr. Crow, making his usual morning rounds, surveying his domain with a keen eye and an even keener sense of his own importance.

"Here comes our learned friend," Frisky whispered, a hint of mischief in his tone. "I wonder what profound wisdom he will impart regarding this new mystery."

Mr. Crow landed on a fence post not far from the garden, his black feathers gleaming in the sun. He puffed out his chest, took a moment to smooth a ruffled feather, and then fixed his beady eyes upon the motionless figure in the field. He stared at it for a long moment, turning his head from side to side, as if absorbing every detail. Sandy and Frisky held their breath, eager to hear his pronouncement.

"Aha!" Mr. Crow finally declared, his voice ringing with self-assurance. "Just as I suspected! Another of Farmer Green's ingenious devices! A truly remarkable specimen, if I do say so myself."

"Do you know what it is, Mr. Crow?" Sandy asked, unable to contain his curiosity.

"Of course I do, young Chipmunk!" Mr. Crow puffed up further, as if Sandy's question were a personal affront to his vast knowledge. "There is very little in Farmer Green's domain, or indeed in the whole of Pleasant Valley, that escapes my discerning eye and superior intellect. This, my dear friends, is what Farmer Green calls his 'Thought-Holder.'"

Sandy and Frisky exchanged bewildered glances. "A Thought-Holder?" Frisky repeated, trying to sound knowledgeable.

"Precisely!" Mr. Crow exclaimed, bobbing his head wisely. "You see, Farmer Green is a man of many thoughts, far too many, in fact, for his single head to contain. He worries about the weather, about the price of corn, about whether his chickens are laying enough eggs, and a hundred other weighty matters. It is a burden, a great burden indeed, to carry so much intellect."

He paused for dramatic effect, scanning the horizon as if searching for other burdened intellectuals.

"So," Mr. Crow continued, "Farmer Green, being a clever fellow, devised this magnificent contraption. He fills it, you see, with all his excess thoughts, those he cannot comfortably hold in his own mind. He pours them into this 'Thought-Holder,' and there they remain, held fast and safe, until he needs them again."

"But... how?" Sandy ventured, utterly perplexed. "How does one pour thoughts into a figure made of old clothes and straw?"

Mr. Crow let out a condescending caw. "Ah, Sandy, you speak as one who has not yet plumbed the depths of human ingenuity! It is not a matter of physical pouring, of course.
