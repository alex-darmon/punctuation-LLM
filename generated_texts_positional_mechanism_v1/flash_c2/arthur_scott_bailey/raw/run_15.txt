He squeezed himself through the narrow opening, his fur brushing against the rough wood of the frame. He dropped lightly onto the dirt floor of the cellar. The air was cool and smelled of damp earth, apples, and potatoes. He scampered over to the bins, his excitement momentarily overcoming his fear. The corn looked plump and delicious. He could almost taste it already. He knew he couldn’t carry whole cobs. He was a chipmunk, after all, not a crow. But he could gather the kernels. He began to nip at the kernels closest to the top, filling his cheek pouches. He worked quickly, his tiny paws deftly separating the golden grains from the cob. In a matter of moments, his pouches were full, bulging outwards on either side of his face. He felt quite proud of his efficiency.

Now came the delicate part: getting out. He scurried back to the window, feeling much heavier with his precious cargo. He poked his head out carefully, checking the outside world again. Still quiet. No sudden movements. He heard another distant caw from Mr. Crow, a long, drawn-out sound that usually meant "all clear."

With a final, exasperated twitch of its antennae, the beetle let go of the paper altogether. Sandy Chipmunk, panting a little from his exertions, held the folded paper triumphantly in his paws. There was a small tear in one corner, where he had gnawed at it, but the letter itself was otherwise unharmed. He smoothed it out carefully, feeling a great sense of relief and accomplishment.

"Indeed?" Mr. Crow replied, cocking his head. "And what do you intend to do with it, pray tell? You still cannot read it, can you? There was his own picture, of course, a little chipmunk with bright eyes and a bushy tail, in the corner. But there were also other pictures, of squirrels and mice and even a rat, just as Mr. Crow had said. He had been so proud to think it was a letter for him, but now a seed of doubt began to sprout in his mind.

Sandy did not know what a bill was, but the way Mr. Crow said it made it sound like something rather unpleasant. He looked at the letter with renewed suspicion. What if it was something bad? He clutched it a little tighter.

"You had best be careful, Sandy," Mr. Crow continued, enjoying Sandy's discomfiture. "If it truly belongs to Farmer Green, and he finds you with it, there is no telling what he might do. Sandy Chipmunk's eyes grew wide. He remembered Farmer Green chasing him and throwing stones. The thought of Mrs. Green, whom he had rarely seen but knew to be a rather formidable woman, sent a shiver down his spine. He quickly tucked the letter under his arm, holding it tightly so that no one else could see it.

"I'd better hide it," he decided. "No one must know I have it."

And so, with a quick glance at Mr. Crow, who merely cawed softly and preened another feather, Sandy scurried down the oak tree. He did not run towards his home, for he knew that Mr. Crow, perched high above, would be able to see exactly where he went. There, among the dry leaves and bits of bark, Sandy carefully placed the folded letter. He covered it with a few more leaves, just for good measure, and then, satisfied that it was quite safe, he hurried away, determined to think about what to do next.

As Sandy scurried through the forest, his mind was abuzz. The excitement of finding a letter had been replaced by a nagging worry. Mr. Crow's words had planted a seed of doubt, and now Sandy was not so sure that owning a letter was such a grand thing after all, especially if it meant being chased by Farmer Green or being scolded by Mrs. Green. He longed to know what the letter said, but he also feared it.

He soon came upon Frisky Squirrel, who was busily burying a cache of acorns near the root of a large maple tree. Frisky Squirrel was a diligent worker, but he also enjoyed a good gossip, and Sandy knew that Frisky had a way of making sense of things, even if his sense was sometimes a bit muddled.

"Hello, Frisky!" Sandy called, trying to sound nonchalant.

Frisky Squirrel paused his digging, his cheeks bulging with acorns. "Why, hello, Sandy! What's the matter? You look as though you've just seen a fox."

"No, nothing like that," Sandy replied, trying to appear calm. "I was just thinking about that mail-box at the cross-roads. And that letter I found."

Frisky Squirrel's eyes lit up. "Oh, yes! The one with the picture of a chipmunk on it! I told you about it, didn't I? The man with the twelve horses and the six haystacks of letters!"

Sandy winced. "Well, not exactly like that, Frisky. The man had only one horse, and only one bag of mail. And he wasn't trying to get rid of the letters, I don't think."

"Oh," said Frisky, looking a little deflated. "Well, that's what I heard, anyway. It came from Fatty Coon, who heard it from somebody else, who heard it from Jasper Jay, who heard it from Mr. Crow. Sandy sighed. He knew how stories grew and changed in Pleasant Valley. "Never mind about that," he said. "The point is, I found a letter, and it had my picture on it. But it also had pictures of other creatures, like squirrels and mice."

Frisky Squirrel paused with an acorn halfway to its hiding place. "Pictures of squirrels, you say?" he asked, his voice suddenly full of interest. "Did it have a picture of *me*?"

Sandy tried to remember. "I'm not sure if it was you specifically, Frisky, but it was a picture of a squirrel. And Mr. Crow said that perhaps it was a bill, and that Farmer Green would be very cross if he found me with it."

Frisky Squirrel dropped the acorn. "A bill!" he exclaimed, his bushy tail twitching with alarm. "What's a bill?"

"I don't know," Sandy admitted. "But Mr. Crow said it was something unpleasant, and that Farmer Green was always getting them."

Frisky Squirrel began to look quite worried. "Unpleasant? And it has pictures of squirrels? Oh, dear, Sandy, do you think it means Farmer Green is going to send a bill to all the squirrels? For eating his corn, perhaps? Or for burying acorns too close to his fences?"

Sandy had not thought of that. He remembered his earlier run-in with Mr. Crow about the corn and the beechnuts. What if the letter was some kind of official complaint? "Oh, my!" he said, "I hadn't thought of that, Frisky. It might be very serious indeed."

Frisky Squirrel paced nervously back and forth, scattering a few more acorns from his cheeks. "We must find out what it says, Sandy! If it's a bill for squirrels, then all the squirrels in Pleasant Valley will need to know. And the mice too! And Rinaldo Rat, if he's in it!"

"But I can't read," Sandy reminded him.

"Neither can I!" Frisky declared. "But we know someone who can. Old Mother Owl! She knows everything, and she can read anything."

Sandy considered this. Mother Owl lived deep in the whispering woods, in a hollow high up in the tallest oak tree. She was very wise, and very old, and sometimes a little bit stern. But she was also fair, and if anyone could tell them what the letter truly said, it would be she.

"But it's hidden," Sandy confessed. "I hid it under the thorn bush, because Mr. Crow said Farmer Green would be cross."

"Nonsense!" Frisky Squirrel said, puffing out his chest. "If it's a bill for all of us, then it's our duty to know what it says. We must fetch it at once, Sandy, and take it to Mother Owl!"

And so, filled with a newfound sense of purpose, Sandy Chipmunk and Frisky Squirrel hurried back to the thorn bush. Sandy carefully uncovered the letter, and then, with Frisky Squirrel chattering excitedly beside him, he clutched it once more and they set off towards the whispering woods.

It was a long journey, for the whispering woods were quite a distance from the cross-roads. They had to cross open fields, where they risked being seen by hawks or Farmer Green's watchful eye, and then they had to make their way through dense thickets and over babbling brooks. All the while, Frisky Squirrel kept asking Sandy if he was sure the picture of the squirrel was not, in fact, a picture of Frisky himself.

"It had a very bushy tail, Sandy," Frisky insisted. "And it looked very much like me when I'm collecting acorns. Did it have a mischievous glint in its eye?"

Sandy shook his head. "I don't remember any mischievous glints, Frisky. It was just a squirrel."

"Well, I'm sure it was me, anyway," Frisky declared, quite satisfied with his own conclusion. "And if it's a bill for me, then I shall certainly argue with Farmer Green about it. I only take a few acorns, and he has so many!"

At last, after much scampering and chattering, they arrived at the foot of Mother Owl's great oak. The tree was ancient and gnarled, its branches spreading wide like welcoming arms. High above, they could just make out the dark opening of Mother Owl's hollow.

"Whoo-hoo!" Frisky Squirrel called up, rather loudly. "Mother Owl! Are you there? We have something very important!"

A moment later, a deep, resonant voice floated down from the hollow. "Who is making such a commotion at my doorstep? Do you not know that a wise old owl needs her rest?"

"It's us, Mother Owl!" Sandy squeaked, feeling a little intimidated by the deep voice. "Sandy Chipmunk and Frisky Squirrel! We have a letter, and we need you to read it for us!"

There was a rustling sound from above, and then Mother Owl's large, round face appeared at the opening of her hollow. Her eyes, like golden lamps, seemed to pierce right through them. She slowly flew down from her perch, landing on a low branch, her soft feathers making no sound at all.

"A letter, you say?" Mother Owl asked, her voice calm but firm. "And why, pray tell, do two such small creatures have a letter, and why can you not read it yourselves?"

Sandy, feeling a little braver now that she was closer, explained the whole story: about finding the mail-box, the man with the one horse, Mr. Crow's teasing, the beetle, and his worry about it being a "bill" from Farmer Green. Frisky Squirrel, of course, interrupted frequently to add his own dramatic flair to the tale, emphasizing the part about the squirrel's picture.

Mother Owl listened patiently, her golden eyes fixed on Sandy. When he had finished, she extended a clawed foot. "Let me see this letter," she said.

Sandy carefully handed her the folded paper. Mother Owl took it gently in her talons and spread it open. She tilted her head, first one way, then the other, her wise eyes scanning the rows of tiny black symbols and the various pictures. Sandy and Frisky watched with bated breath.

Mother Owl did not speak for a long moment. She merely blinked, her eyes moving slowly from one side of the paper to the other. Sandy and Frisky exchanged nervous glances. Had they done something wrong? Was it indeed a terrible bill?

At last, Mother Owl let out a soft hoot, which sounded rather like a chuckle. "A bill, you say? And for all the squirrels and mice in Pleasant Valley?" She let out another soft hoot. "My dear young ones, this is no bill at all. And it does not belong to Farmer Green, either, not in the way you think."

Sandy and Frisky stared, their mouths agape. "It isn't?" Sandy asked. "But Mr. Crow said—"

"Mr. Crow," Mother Owl interrupted, her voice a little sharper, "is often mistaken, and delights in confusing others. This, my dears, is what is called a 'catalogue.'"

"A what?" asked Frisky Squirrel, completely bewildered.

"A catalogue," Mother Owl repeated. "It is a book, or rather, a collection of pictures and words, sent by a store in the village. "This picture, which you thought was a chipmunk, is merely an illustration of a chipmunk sitting on a little birdhouse, which the store sells. Sandy and Frisky peered closer. Indeed, now that Mother Owl pointed it out, the chipmunk in the picture was not just a chipmunk, but a chipmunk looking at a tiny birdhouse. "Oh, my!" Sandy exclaimed. "So it isn't a letter for me at all?"

"Not in the way you imagined, no," Mother Owl said. "It is simply a way for the store to show Farmer Green all the things he might like to purchase. He often orders things from these catalogues, things for his farm, or for his house. Frisky Squirrel's tail drooped. "So it wasn't a bill for me, then? And it wasn't a picture of me after all?"

"No, Frisky," Mother Owl said gently. "It was just a general picture of a squirrel. But you see, it is useful to know what these things are. Farmer Green might decide to buy one of these traps for mice, or perhaps one of these fancy bird feeders. Sandy felt a wave of mixed emotions. He was relieved that it wasn't a bill, and that Farmer Green wouldn't be cross with him specifically for stealing it. But he was also a little disappointed. It wasn't a personal letter at all. It was just a book of pictures.

"So, what should we do with it?" Sandy asked, looking at Mother Owl.

"Well," Mother Owl said, folding the catalogue carefully. "It was meant for Farmer Green. And although you found it in the mail-box, it is not truly yours, since it was addressed to him. Perhaps the best thing to do would be to return it."

Sandy's eyes widened again. "Return it? To Farmer Green?" He remembered the chase, and the stones. "Oh, no, Mother Owl! I don't think I can do that!"

Frisky Squirrel nodded in agreement. "He'll think we're stealing it all over again!"

Mother Owl blinked slowly. "That is true. Farmer Green can be a bit hasty. But perhaps there is another way. We must think carefully." She perched on a lower branch, deep in thought, her head cocked to one side. Sandy and Frisky waited patiently, for they knew Mother Owl's thoughts were always very profound.

After a long moment, Mother Owl spoke. "I know," she said. "We shall leave it somewhere Farmer Green will find it, but not in a place where he will think you have brought it directly to him. We shall leave it on his porch, perhaps. Or near his toolshed."

"But how will we do that without him seeing us?" Sandy asked.

"Ah," Mother Owl said, with a knowing look. "That is where cleverness comes in. You are both small and quick. Frisky Squirrel immediately perked up. "A lookout! Yes! I can climb the big oak by the kitchen window and watch for any signs of movement!"

"Excellent," Mother Owl said. "And Sandy, you shall creep quietly to the porch and leave the catalogue right by the front door. He is sure to find it there in the morning."

Sandy still felt a little nervous about venturing so close to Farmer Green's house, but the idea of being part of a secret mission, and of doing the right thing, appealed to him. "All right, Mother Owl," he said. "We will do it."

So they waited until the sun began to dip below the horizon, painting the sky in shades of orange and purple. Then, quick as a flash, Sandy turned and scampered back across the porch, down the steps, and into the safety of the shadows. He did not stop until he was back at the edge of the woods, where Frisky Squirrel soon joined him.

"You did it, Sandy!" Frisky whispered excitedly. "You were very brave!"

Sandy felt a surge of pride, a different kind of pride than he had felt when he first found the letter. This was the pride of doing something difficult, and doing it well, and doing what was right. "Yes," he said, "I did it."

They watched the farmhouse for a little while longer, until the lights inside were extinguished and the house settled into a deep quiet. Then, satisfied that their mission was complete, Sandy and Frisky made their way back to their respective homes, tired but content.
