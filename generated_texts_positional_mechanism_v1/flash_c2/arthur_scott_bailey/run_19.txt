XII BARNABY'S BEST DAM

Barnaby Beaver hummed to himself as he slapped mud upon a log. He was building his finest dam yet, a magnificent structure that would hold back the clear stream and create a deep pond for his family. But it needed a finishing touch. "It needs something soft," he mused, "something to keep out the chill." He knew just the one to ask.

Soon he found Penelope Partridge pecking at berries. "Good day, Penelope!" Barnaby called, his voice echoing a little. "I need your help for my new dam. Could you gather some soft things for me, to keep the inside cozy?"

Penelope stopped pecking. "Soft things?" she repeated, her head cocked. "Of course, Barnaby. I'll get the very softest things I can find!" And with a flurry of feathers, she flew off.

Barnaby returned to his work, feeling pleased; Penelope was a good helper, if a little scatterbrained. He imagined her returning with bundles of downy moss or fluffy cattail seeds.

Later that afternoon, he heard a great squawking. "Oh, dear! Oh, my!" Penelope cried as she struggled through the undergrowth, dragging behind her a peculiar collection. There was a worn-out mitten, a tattered piece of checkered cloth, a rusty spring from an old mattress, and a crumpled newspaper. "Here they are, Barnaby!" she chirped. "The softest things!"

Barnaby Beaver stared. "Penelope," he said slowly, "what in the world have you brought?"

"Soft things, just as you asked!" Penelope insisted. "This mitten is very soft, even if it has a hole. And this cloth feels quite fluffy. The spring bounces, so it must be soft, too. And the newspaper, why, it crinkles so gently!"

"But I wanted _moss_," Barnaby explained, tapping a paw. "Or _fluff_ from the cattails. Natural soft things!"

"You didn't say so," Penelope replied, ruffling her feathers. "You just said _soft things_. And these are certainly soft. I don't see what the fuss is about. I almost got caught by Farmer Green's dog trying to get that fine mitten!"

"Well, there's no harm done, I suppose," Barnaby sighed. "But I cannot use these for my dam. Please, go back and gather moss. And bring the cattail fluff, if you see any."

"But I'm tired!" Penelope objected. "And my wings ache from carrying these heavy soft things."

Just then, Reginald Rabbit hopped by. He had been listening to the whole conversation, his long ears twitching. "Barnaby Beaver wants _soft things_ for his dam!" he sang out. "And Penelope found him a mitten, a cloth, a spring, and a newspaper! He doesn't like them!"

"Reginald, wait!" Barnaby called, but Reginald was already gone, bouncing away through the trees.

The next day, as Barnaby was still trying to find suitable insulation, he heard chatter from the other animals. "Did you hear about Barnaby's dam?" Mrs. Squirrel chattered to Mr. Groundhog. "He's filling it with _mismatched socks_ and _old frying pans_!"

"No, no!" chuffed Mr. Groundhog. "I heard he's using _broken wagon wheels_ and _feather dusters_!"

Barnaby Beaver slapped his forehead with a paw. "Oh, dear!" he exclaimed. "This will never do!" He stomped over to Penelope, who was once again pecking at berries, quite forgetting her earlier task.

"Penelope," he said sternly, "you must help me clear up this misunderstanding! Reginald Rabbit has been spreading dreadful rumors about my dam!"

"Me?" Penelope squeaked, looking surprised. "But I only gathered what you asked for! Besides, Reginald didn't say anything bad about _my_ soft things."

Barnaby sighed deeply. "No," he said, "he said bad things about _my_ dam, because of what _you_ brought. You must tell everyone that I want _moss_ and _cattail fluff_, and nothing else!"

"But I thought that was understood," Penelope said with a shrug of her wings. "Every one knows that moss and cattail fluff are soft. I merely thought you wanted a change."

"A change!" Barnaby exclaimed. "This is a dam, not a novelty collection!" He was so frustrated that he almost gnawed a hole in his favorite log. "Please, Penelope. Just tell them."

Penelope fluttered her eyelashes. "I'm afraid I'm too confused to explain anything to anyone, Barnaby," she said. "My head is spinning with all these soft things and hard things and dam things."

And Barnaby Beaver, seeing that he would get no help from Penelope, could only shake his head. He spent the rest of the day clearing up the rumors and explaining to every animal he met that his dam would be filled with nothing but the finest, most natural insulation. He was very tired by sunset, and Penelope Partridge, meanwhile, was still wondering if the rusty spring was truly soft or merely _bouncy_.

"Exactly!" Sandy exclaimed. "He thinks he's so important because he gets corn without working. And he thinks my beechnuts are no good. And he said I was stupid!"

"Well, you're not stupid, Sandy," Frisky said, though he sometimes thought Sandy could be a little, shall we say, *single-minded*. "But what about Mr. Crow?"

"I want to send him a message," Sandy explained, puffing out his chest. "A message that will show him what's what! A message about fairness."

Frisky twitched his nose. "A message? But how? We can't write, Sandy. And even if we could, Mr. Crow can't read."

"That's just it!" Sandy cried, his eyes sparkling. "It won't be a letter with words. It will be a letter with *things*. A picture letter, like the one I found in the mail-box."

Frisky Squirrel scratched his ear. "A picture letter with things? What things?"

Sandy then explained his idea, gesturing with his paws. He would gather a small pile of the finest beechnuts he had, the ones he had worked so hard to find and shell. And he would also gather a few kernels of corn, the kind Mr. Crow was so fond of. And he would put them together in the mail-box.

"But what would that mean?" Frisky asked, still a bit puzzled.

"It would mean," Sandy said, drawing closer and lowering his voice conspiratorially, "that *this* is the food we work for, and *this* is the food Mr. Crow expects for nothing. And the difference will be plain for all to see! It will be a lesson in fairness!"

Frisky Squirrel considered this. He knew how annoying Mr. Crow's boasting had become. And the idea of giving Mr. Crow a "lesson" was rather appealing. "Hmm," he said, tapping an acorn thoughtfully. "It's a clever idea, Sandy. But how will Mr. Crow know it's a message for him? And how will he even see it? Farmer Green usually closes the lid."

"That's where you come in, Frisky," Sandy said. "You're much faster than I am. You can get in and out of the mail-box quickly. We can wait for the mail-carrier to come, and when he leaves the lid open, we'll put the message inside. And then, we'll need someone to make sure Mr. Crow actually sees it."

"Aha!" Frisky exclaimed. "Jasper Jay! He loves to spread news, especially news that might make Mr. Crow look foolish."

So it was decided. Sandy and Frisky spent the rest of the afternoon gathering. Sandy meticulously selected his best beechnuts, shining and plump. Frisky, with a little less enthusiasm, found a few stray kernels of corn near the edge of Farmer Green's field, making sure they looked as easy to obtain as possible. They even found a few discarded corn husks, which Sandy thought would add to the effect.

The next morning, Sandy and Frisky hid themselves in the bushes near the cross-roads, waiting patiently. It was a long wait. The sun climbed higher and higher, warming their fur, and Sandy's paws twitched with anticipation.

"Are you sure he's coming today?" Frisky whispered, already getting restless.

"Mr. Crow said he comes every day," Sandy replied. "And Mr. Crow, for all his faults, knows a lot about the mail-carrier."

Just as Frisky was about to complain about the heat, they heard it: the distant clatter of a wagon and the clip-clop of a single horse.

"Here he comes!" Sandy breathed, his heart thumping like a drum.

The mail-carrier pulled up to the oak tree, as he always did. He reached into his bag, placed a few letters and a newspaper into the box, and then, as Sandy had hoped, he left the lid open. With a flick of the reins, the wagon rumbled off down the road.

"Now!" Sandy whispered, giving Frisky a nudge.

Frisky Squirrel, quick as a flash, darted out from the bushes, scampered up the oak tree, and slipped inside the mail-box. Sandy, right behind him, carefully placed his collection of beechnuts, corn kernels, and husks in a neat little display on top of Farmer Green's newspaper. Frisky helped arrange them. They looked rather like a tiny, silent protest.

"Perfect!" Sandy whispered, admiring their handiwork.

They both scurried back down the tree and into the bushes, just as Jasper Jay swooped down from a nearby pine.

"Did you do it?" Jasper squawked, his blue feathers gleaming in the sun.

"It's done," Sandy said, feeling a surge of pride. "Now, Jasper, you must tell Mr. Crow. But don't tell him *what* it is. Just tell him there's a very important message for him in the mail-box. And tell him it's about... about *food*."

Jasper Jay’s eyes gleamed with mischief. "A message about food, you say? From the mail-box? This sounds like just the sort of news Mr. Crow will love to hear. Or perhaps, not love to hear. Consider it done!" And with a caw and a ruffle of feathers, Jasper Jay flew off towards the tallest sycamore where Mr. Crow often perched, preening himself and looking down upon the busy world.

It wasn't long before they heard it: a loud, indignant "Caw-caw!" followed by a very familiar "Haw-haw-haw!" Mr. Crow was laughing, but it sounded a little forced.

Sandy and Frisky waited, their ears perked. They could hear Jasper Jay's excited squawking, and then Mr. Crow's condescending tones.

"A message for *me*?" Mr. Crow's voice drifted through the air. "From the mail-box? Don't be absurd, Jasper. The mail-box is for Farmer Green, and his important letters. Not for me. What nonsense is this?"

"But it's true, Mr. Crow!" Jasper insisted. "Sandy Chipmunk saw it with his own eyes. A special message, just for you, about food!"

This piqued Mr. Crow's interest. Food, after all, was his favorite topic, especially when it involved him not having to work for it. He soared majestically from his sycamore, gliding effortlessly towards the oak at the cross-roads. Sandy and Frisky watched from their hiding place, barely daring to breathe.

Mr. Crow landed on the edge of the mail-box, his sharp black eyes peering inside. He saw Farmer Green's newspaper, and on top of it, the carefully arranged beechnuts, corn kernels, and empty husks.

For a long moment, Mr. Crow was silent. His head cocked first one way, then the other. He poked tentatively at a beechnut with his beak. Then he looked at the corn. He recognized the corn, of course. It was the same kind he enjoyed so much, delivered straight to the barnyard floor by Farmer Green. But the beechnuts… and the husks…

Jasper Jay, unable to contain his curiosity, landed on a branch above the mail-box. "Well, Mr. Crow? What is it? What does the message say?"

Mr. Crow finally let out a loud, theatrical "Caw!" He puffed out his chest. "This, my dear Jasper, is clearly a message for Farmer Green. These are simply samples of his harvest, perhaps. Or perhaps, a reminder for his hired hands about what they are to gather." He tried to sound dismissive, but Sandy, watching from below, could tell there was a flicker of uncertainty in his eyes.

"But Sandy said it was for *you*," Jasper insisted, with a sly gleam in his eye. "About *food*."

Mr. Crow scoffed. "And what does Sandy Chipmunk know about important messages? He was too stupid to understand my simple request for corn! No, this is nothing. Just a few odds and ends. Perhaps Farmer Green means to feed these to his chickens." With a deliberate movement, Mr. Crow nudged the beechnuts and corn with his beak, tumbling them carelessly into a corner of the mail-box. "There. Now it is tidy again. And Farmer Green can find his mail."

Mr. Crow then flew off, trying to look as unconcerned as possible. But Sandy noticed that he did not fly back to his sycamore to boast. Instead, he circled the farmyard a few times, looking rather thoughtful.

Frisky Squirrel turned to Sandy. "He didn't get it," he said, a little disappointed. "He just brushed it aside."

"Oh, he got it," Sandy replied, a small smile playing on his lips. "He just didn't want to admit it. Did you see how he paused? And how he didn't boast when he flew away? He knows. He just doesn't want *you* to know that he knows."

And indeed, Sandy was right. Mr. Crow, for all his bluster, had understood perfectly well. The beechnuts, so painstakingly gathered, represented the hard work of the forest creatures. The corn, so casually displayed, represented his own effortless meals. And the empty husks, they were a silent accusation of his demands and his wastefulness. It made him feel, just for a moment, a prickle of shame, something Mr. Crow rarely experienced.

He flew down to the barnyard, but for once, the sight of the scattered corn did not fill him with joy. He pecked at a kernel half-heartedly. He thought of Sandy Chipmunk, whom he had called stupid. And he thought of the letter with the chipmunk's picture, which he had so readily dismissed. Perhaps, he mused, Sandy Chipmunk was not so stupid after all. And perhaps, just perhaps, his own cleverness was not quite as boundless as he liked to believe.

That evening, the news of Sandy's "mail-box message" spread like wildfire through Pleasant Valley. Jasper Jay, despite Mr. Crow's attempts at denial, made sure to tell everyone exactly what he had seen and what Mr. Crow had said.

"He tried to pretend it was nothing!" Jasper squawked to Mrs. Cottontail, who listened with wide eyes. "He said it was for Farmer Green! But he looked so *uncomfortable*."

"Uncomfortable?" Mrs. Cottontail repeated. "Mr. Crow? I've never known him to be uncomfortable about anything!"

"Well, he was today!" Jasper insisted. "He knows what that message meant. Sandy Chipmunk showed him what was fair and what was not! And he couldn't deny it, not even with all his big talk."

Fatty Coon, who heard the story from Mrs. Cottontail, let out a slow, rumbling chuckle. "So Sandy Chipmunk finally put that old windbag in his place, did he? Good for Sandy! Perhaps now Mr. Crow will learn a little humility."

Even Mrs. Red Squirrel, who was usually too busy chattering about her own affairs, was impressed. "To think," she said to her children, "that little Sandy Chipmunk, whom Mr. Crow called stupid, found a way to speak without words, and yet be understood perfectly. That takes real cleverness, children. Not the kind of cleverness that just takes, but the kind that teaches."

The next morning, Mr. Crow did not appear on his usual sycamore branch. He was nowhere to be seen in the barnyard either. The other animals whispered among themselves.

"Do you think he's angry?" asked Frisky Squirrel, a little worried.

"Or perhaps he's plotting something," suggested a cautious Field Mouse.

But Sandy Chipmunk merely smiled. "He's thinking," he said. "He's thinking about fairness. And that's a good thing, no matter how long it takes him."

It was several days before Mr. Crow was seen again. When he finally reappeared, he flew with less swagger, and his usual loud "Caw-caws" were noticeably softer. He still ate corn from Farmer Green's barnyard, for old habits are hard to break, but he did not boast about it. And when he saw Sandy Chipmunk, instead of sneering or calling him stupid, he simply gave a small, almost imperceptible nod.

Sandy, of course, nodded back. He felt a quiet satisfaction. He hadn't changed Mr. Crow entirely, he knew that. But he had made him think. And sometimes, thinking was the first step towards change.

He also found that his reputation among the other forest creatures had grown. No one called him stupid anymore. They looked at him with new respect, the chipmunk who had used the mail-box to send a message even without writing a single word. And whenever he walked past the oak at the cross-roads, Sandy Chipmunk would pause, glance at the mailbox, and remember the day he had delivered a lesson in fairness. The mail-box, he realized, was not just for letters from faraway places, nor for newspapers, nor even just for Farmer Green. It was for messages, and sometimes, the most important messages were the ones that had no words at all, but spoke directly to the heart. And Sandy Chipmunk, who had once been so worried about being stupid, knew that he had found a way to be very clever indeed.

He never forgot the feeling of triumph and quiet satisfaction, and he always kept the little envelope with the chipmunk's picture, a reminder that even the smallest creatures could have a voice, and that sometimes, a picture was worth more than a thousand boastful words. The corn still appeared in Farmer Green's yard, and Mr. Crow still ate it, but a subtle shift had occurred in the valley. The boasting was less frequent, the murmurs of discontent less common, and a new appreciation for the simple, honest work of gathering beechnuts and acorns had begun to spread, thanks to a small chipmunk and his clever idea. Indeed, the mail-box, which had once been a mere curiosity, had become, for the animals of Pleasant Valley, a symbol of communication beyond words, and a testament to the unexpected wisdom of the most overlooked among them. And from that day forward, whenever an animal had a particularly strong opinion, or felt a deep injustice, they would often look toward the old oak tree, and ponder what kind of 'message' they might leave for others to ponder, knowing that Sandy Chipmunk had shown them the way. The silent lessons, after all, often spoke the loudest.
