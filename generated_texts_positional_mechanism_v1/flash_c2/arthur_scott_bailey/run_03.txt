"Oh, bother!" thought Mr. Crow. "Now I'm truly in for it!" He held himself perfectly still once more, trying to blend in with the shadows, though there were no shadows in the complete darkness. He heard the lid of the mail-box creak open. A sliver of light, bright and sudden, pierced the gloom. Mr. Crow squeezed his eyes shut for a moment, then blinked them open, adjusting to the sudden illumination.

He saw Farmer Green's large hand reach in, rummaging amongst the letters. Mr. Crow, pressed tightly against the side, was almost touched by the farmer's fingers. He could feel the faint warmth radiating from the hand. Farmer Green pulled out a few letters, glancing at them as he did so. One of them, Mr. Crow noticed, was the very one with the chipmunk picture that Sandy had been so proud of. It seemed Farmer Green had found Sandy's treasure. He tucked the letters under his arm, just as Mr. Crow had observed before, and then, without another thought, he let the lid fall back down with a gentle _clunk_.

Mr. Crow was still trapped! Farmer Green had not seen him, which was a relief, but he had also left him just as he was. The darkness returned, not as absolute this time, for a tiny crack in the lid allowed a faint pencil-line of light to seep through. "This is entirely too much!" Mr. Crow declared, his patience worn thin. "I cannot stay in this dusty box all night!" He ruffled his feathers in indignation. He had a reputation to uphold, a winter to plan for, and a comfortable roost to return to.

He began to scheme. He was a clever bird, after all, and even the most difficult situations could often be overcome with a bit of thought. He remembered the faint crack of light. If there was a crack, perhaps he could make it wider. He began to peck, not at the solid wood, but at the very edge of the lid, where it met the side of the box. He found a small splinter, loosened perhaps by years of weather, and began to work at it with his strong, pointed beak. It was slow work, tedious and tiring, but Mr. Crow was determined. Chip by chip, splinter by splinter, he began to widen the gap.

His beak ached, and his neck felt stiff, but at last, after what seemed like an age, he had created a gap wide enough for him to squeeze through. It was not a grand exit, certainly not one befitting the clever Mr. Crow, but it was an exit nonetheless. He pushed his head through first, then his shoulders, and with a final, mighty struggle, he pulled his body free, tumbling ungracefully onto the dusty ground beneath the oak tree. He landed with a soft thump, a little ruffled but otherwise unharmed.

He stood for a moment, catching his breath, and then shook his feathers vigorously, as if to dislodge the indignity of his recent confinement. He cast a dark, scornful look back at the mail-box, now innocently closed upon the trunk of the oak. "Never again!" he vowed to himself. "Never again will I meddle with human contraptions!" He stretched his wings, feeling the evening breeze on his glossy black feathers, and then, with a powerful beat, he launched himself into the air.

He flew straight towards his favorite roost, high in the tallest pine tree. He needed a good rest, and perhaps a good, plump worm to soothe his ruffled nerves. But even as he flew, his clever mind was still turning over the day's events. He still did not understand the letters, not truly, but he had learned one very important thing: human contraptions, no matter how innocent they appeared, could be traps of a different sort.

The next morning, Mr. Crow felt much better after a night's sleep and a breakfast of fat grubs. He was perched on a high branch, enjoying the warmth of the sun, when he saw Sandy Chipmunk scampering along the stone wall, looking rather pleased with himself. Sandy was carrying something small and dark in his mouth, something he seemed to guard with great care.

"Good morning, Sandy Chipmunk!" called Mr. Crow, his voice betraying none of the previous day's adventures. "What treasure have you found so early?"

Sandy paused, his bright eyes darting nervously about, as if expecting Farmer Green to appear at any moment. "It's a letter, Mr. Crow!" he chattered excitedly. "Another one! I went back to the mail-box very early, before the Mail Man or Farmer Green arrived, and there it was! Right inside!"

Mr. Crow narrowed his eyes. "A letter, you say? And does it have your picture on it?"

"Indeed it does!" Sandy replied, puffing out his chest. "Another picture of me! And this one is even larger than the last!"

Mr. Crow hopped along his branch, his curiosity reawakened despite his vow. "Let me see it, Sandy," he commanded. "Perhaps this time we can make sense of these peculiar papers."

Sandy Chipmunk, eager to show off his new possession, carefully laid the envelope on a flat rock. It was indeed a larger envelope than the previous one, and in one corner, quite prominently, was a finely drawn picture of a chipmunk. Mr. Crow leaned closer, tilting his head. He recognized the familiar pattern of black marks on the paper, but his attention was drawn to the picture.

"It is certainly you, Sandy," Mr. Crow conceded. "A very good likeness, too. But what else is there?"

Sandy, remembering his previous disappointment, tore open the envelope with gusto. He pulled out a folded sheet of paper. "More pictures!" he exclaimed. "Look, Mr. Crow! There's one of Frisky Squirrel, and Mrs. Rabbit, and even old Grandfather Tortoise!"

Mr. Crow peered at the paper. It was covered in drawings of various forest animals, all looking quite pleased with themselves. And beneath each picture, just as on the envelope, were those strange black squiggles. "Hmph," Mr. Crow grunted. "A whole collection of pictures, then. But what do they mean, Sandy? Why are they all together?"

Sandy scratched his head with a tiny paw. "I don't know, Mr. Crow. But they are very pretty, aren't they? And they have my picture, so it must be for me!"

Mr. Crow remained silent for a moment, thinking. He remembered his own experience in the mail-box, the boredom and the frustration. He also remembered Farmer Green taking the letters, and Sandy taking the letters. And the Mail Man leaving them. There was a pattern here, a sequence of events.

"Sandy," Mr. Crow began, slowly, "do you suppose that the pictures are for us, the animals? And the black squiggles are for Farmer Green and the humans?"

Sandy Chipmunk blinked. "Why would they want squiggles, Mr. Crow? They are not nearly as nice as pictures!"

"That," said Mr. Crow, tapping a thoughtful claw on the branch, "is the very question. But consider this, Sandy: Farmer Green does not eat the letters, nor does he play with them. He reads them, or so they say. Perhaps these squiggles are like our calls, like your "chip-chip-chip" or my "caw-caw-caw", but written down so that humans can understand them even when we are not there."

Sandy looked bewildered. "Written down? You mean like our tracks in the snow?"

"Something like that," Mr. Crow mused, warming to his theory. "But instead of telling them where we have been, perhaps they tell them what someone wants to say. Like a message, but silent."

"A silent message?" Sandy repeated, his eyes wide. "That sounds very clever, Mr. Crow!"

"It does, doesn't it?" Mr. Crow puffed out his chest a little, pleased that Sandy was finally seeing the cleverness of the idea, even if he himself was still mostly guessing. "And perhaps these pictures, Sandy, are a special kind of silent message, just for us. A way for the humans to show they are thinking of us, even if they cannot speak our language directly."

Sandy Chipmunk looked down at the letter, then up at Mr. Crow, a new understanding dawning in his bright little eyes. "So, this letter, with all the pictures of my friends, is a message from Farmer Green to tell me he is thinking of us?"

"It could be!" Mr. Crow declared, now quite convinced by his own ingenuity. "A very thoughtful gesture, indeed. Though why he would include pictures of so many others, and not just you, I cannot say. Perhaps it is a message for all of us, Sandy, a message of friendship!"

Sandy Chipmunk's face brightened considerably. "A message of friendship! That is much better than a message about corn, Mr. Crow! And much better than beechnuts, too!"

"Indeed," Mr. Crow agreed, though he still privately thought that a good, fat worm was better than any message, silent or otherwise. "But if these are messages of friendship, Sandy, then we should perhaps send one back. It is only polite, after all."

Sandy’s tail twitched with excitement. "Send a message back? But how, Mr. Crow? We cannot write squiggles!"

"No," Mr. Crow admitted, "but we can certainly draw pictures! And if the humans send us pictures, then surely they would appreciate pictures in return."

And so began a new project for Sandy Chipmunk and Mr. Crow. They gathered smooth, flat pieces of bark, and with sharp claws and careful beaks, they began to scratch out their own pictures. Sandy drew a picture of himself, surrounded by a pile of delicious beechnuts. Mr. Crow, after much deliberation, decided to draw a picture of a particularly plump ear of corn, though he made sure to include a small, smiling version of himself next to it, just to indicate who the intended recipient was.

It was hard work, for neither of them had ever attempted anything quite like it before. Sandy's drawing of beechnuts looked suspiciously like lumpy stones, and Mr. Crow's corn resembled a rather large, striped caterpillar. But they were very proud of their efforts.

"Now, Mr. Crow," Sandy asked, holding up his bark-drawing, "how do we send it to Farmer Green? Do we put it in the mail-box?"

Mr. Crow paused. He remembered his recent unpleasant experience. "No, Sandy," he said, with a shake of his head. "I think not. The mail-box is for _human_ messages, and it is a most inhospitable place for a bird of my stature. I believe we should deliver our messages of friendship in a more personal manner. You can leave your picture on Farmer Green's doorstep, perhaps. And I," he added, with a sly glint in his eye, "shall leave mine in his cornfield. A much more direct and impactful method, wouldn't you agree?"

Sandy Chipmunk listened to Mr. Crow, his tail giving an excited little flip. "On Farmer Green's doorstep?" he repeated. "That sounds very brave, Mr. Crow. What if he sees me?"

"Nonsense!" Mr. Crow declared, puffing out his chest. "Farmer Green is a big, slow creature. He spends most of his time either inside his house, or puttering about in his garden. He won't even know you're there. You are a chipmunk, after all, nimble and quick. A mere flicker of brown fur across his path, and then you are gone! No, a doorstep delivery is much more personal than leaving it in that noisy, clanking mail-box." Mr. Crow shuddered at the memory of the mail-box, and the man who drove the one horse. He still couldn't quite believe Sandy's exaggerated tale of twelve horses, but then, chipmunks were known for their flighty imaginations.

Sandy, however, was easily convinced by Mr. Crow’s confident manner. "Very well, Mr. Crow," he said, his tail giving an excited little flip. "A doorstep delivery it shall be! I shall make sure my picture is placed where Farmer Green cannot possibly miss it."

With renewed purpose, Sandy Chipmunk returned to his burrow to prepare his artistic masterpiece. He smoothed the edges of his bark-canvas, inspecting his scratchy rendition of himself surrounded by beechnuts. It was a true labor of love. He even decided to add a few actual, plump beechnuts, polished to a shine, next to the bark drawing. "A little extra touch of friendship," he murmured to himself, "to show I mean well." He imagined Farmer Green finding his gift, his big, human face breaking into a wide, friendly smile. Perhaps Farmer Green would even leave a few extra beechnuts out for him, a silent thank you for the thoughtful gesture.

Meanwhile, Mr. Crow, quite pleased with his own cleverness, soared towards Farmer Green's cornfield. He clutched his bark drawing carefully in his beak. "A direct and impactful method," he cawed to himself, repeating his own words with a sense of self-satisfaction. He intended to place his drawing where it would be most appreciated: right amidst the plumpest, most succulent ears of corn. He knew just the spot, a patch near the edge of the field where the corn grew particularly tall and sweet.

Upon reaching his chosen location, Mr. Crow surveyed the field with a critical eye. The morning sun glinted off the dew-kissed leaves, and the air was rich with the promise of a good harvest. He found a sturdy stalk, heavy with a nearly ripe ear of corn, and with careful precision, he used his sharp

beak to carefully wedge the piece of bark with his self-portrait between the ear of corn and the stalk. He admired his work. "There!" he cawed triumphantly. "A true work of art, placed where it will be most appreciated. Farmer Green will surely understand the depth of my intentions now. This is a far sight better than that silly mail-box business." He paused, preening a bit. He imagined Farmer Green walking through his field, discovering the portrait, and perhaps leaving out a whole basket of corn as a thank you. Mr. Crow chuckled at the thought. He took one last look, then flew off to find a comfortable perch from which to observe the farmer's reaction. He settled high in an old oak tree at the edge of the field, partially hidden by the leaves, and waited.

Meanwhile, Sandy Chipmunk, with his carefully prepared bark-canvas and polished beechnuts, made his way to Farmer Green's house. He crept through the tall grass, his heart thumping with excitement and a little apprehension. The house seemed enormous to him, a great looming structure of wood and stone. He reached the porch and paused, looking around cautiously. No sign of Farmer Green. He scampered up the steps, his tiny claws making no sound on the painted wood. He found a good spot right by the front door, where a large, flat stone served as a doorstep. With meticulous care, he laid down his bark drawing. He then arranged the three shiny beechnuts beside it, like precious jewels. "There," he whispered to himself, taking

a deep breath of satisfaction. This was far better than any mail-box adventure, he thought. This was a direct offering, a true token of good will, he felt sure. Farmer Green could not possibly misunderstand such a thoughtful gesture. He would be pleased, indeed, and perhaps, Sandy hoped, he would place a grand heap of corn on the doorstep as a silent message of thanks. But Sandy did not linger. He knew that humans, especially farmers, were often busy people, and he did not wish to startle Farmer Green. So, with one last glance at his carefully arranged gift, Sandy Chipmunk scampered quietly off the porch and disappeared into the tall grass, his little heart full of hope and cheerful expectation. He found a hiding place behind a great lilac bush, a good distance from the house, and settled down to wait for the farmer’s reaction. He imagined the scene many times over in his mind, picturing Farmer Green’s delight.

Meanwhile, Mr. Crow, perched high in the old oak tree, kept his keen eyes fixed upon the cornfield. He had chosen his perch well, for it gave him an excellent view of the rows of green stalks, and he felt a thrill of anticipation run through his black feathers. He knew that Farmer Green made it a habit to inspect his fields first thing in the morning, especially this time of year when the corn was ripening so handsomely. Mr. Crow smoothed his feathers, quite certain that his artistic masterpiece, wedged so cunningly between the stalk and the ear, would be discovered soon enough. He felt a secret satisfaction, a sense of having outsmarted everyone, especially that simple-minded chipmunk. Sandy Chipmunk, in Mr. Crow’s estimation, would never think of such a sophisticated plan. He would merely leave some common nuts, no doubt, which Farmer Green would probably sweep away with a broom. But a portrait, a work of art, placed with such precision in the very heart of the farmer’s most prized crop, that was a different matter entirely. That, Mr. Crow felt sure, would command respect.

It was not long before Farmer Green emerged from his house. He stretched his great arms above his head, yawned a wide yawn, and then started down the porch steps. Sandy Chipmunk, hidden behind the lilac bush, held his breath. Farmer Green reached the bottom step, and then he paused. His eyes, though still a little sleepy, fell upon the small bark drawing and the three shiny beechnuts. Farmer Green blinked. He bent down slowly, a puzzled expression on his face. He picked up one of the beechnuts. He turned it over in his large fingers. Then he looked at the bark. His brow furrowed. "Well, now," he muttered to himself, "what in the world is this?" He picked up the piece of bark and examined the scratchy image of the chipmunk. He even held it at arm's length, then brought it close again, as if trying to decipher a secret code. "Looks like a chipmunk," he said, quite plainly. "And beechnuts." He chuckled softly, a low rumble that made Sandy Chipmunk’s whiskers twitch with excitement. Farmer Green did not seem angry, not at all. He seemed... amused.

He placed the bark drawing and the beechnuts back on the doorstep, not quite where Sandy had left them, but near enough. Then he walked off the porch and headed towards the barn, still chuckling a little. Sandy Chipmunk watched him go, feeling a warmth spread through his little chest. He had made Farmer Green laugh! That was a good sign, he felt sure. Farmer Green had not swept the gift away, nor had he shown any anger. No, indeed. He had picked up the beechnuts, and he had chuckled. Sandy interpreted this as a positive response, a silent acknowledgment of his good intentions. He waited patiently, expecting that perhaps when Farmer Green returned from the barn, he would bring a bushel of corn or some other delectable treat.

A little later, Farmer Green finished his chores in the barn and then, as Mr. Crow had predicted, he walked out towards his cornfield. Mr. Crow, from his high perch, watched Farmer Green’s every move. Farmer Green strode purposefully down the rows, his large boots rustling the dry leaves at the base of the stalks. He paused here and there, inspecting an ear, pulling back the husk to check for plump kernels. Mr. Crow’s heart beat a little faster when Farmer Green drew near to the very stalk where his own artistic offering had been so cleverly placed. Farmer Green stopped. He looked at the ear of corn. He reached out his hand, and then he noticed the piece of bark wedged there. He pulled it out.

Mr. Crow strained his neck, watching intently. Farmer Green looked at the bark drawing. He squinted a bit. He turned it over. Mr. Crow felt a surge of pride. This was it, he thought. This was the moment of recognition. Farmer Green would surely be impressed by the artistry, the cleverness of the placement, and above all, by the thoughtful gesture. Mr. Crow imagined Farmer Green looking up, perhaps searching for the brilliant artist who had given him such a gift. He puffed out his chest feathers, ready to accept the farmer’s silent gratitude.

But Farmer Green did not look up. He frowned. He looked at the cornstalk, and then at the bark drawing again. "Now, who did this?" he mumbled, his voice carrying faintly on the breeze. He looked around the field, a hint of annoyance creeping into his expression. "This corn is nearly ready," he went on, "and someone has been messing with it." He then took the piece of bark and, with a quick snap of his fingers, he broke it in two. Then he tossed both pieces carelessly onto the ground, where they landed amongst the weeds. "Pesky critters," he grumbled, and then he continued his inspection, pulling off a nearly ripe ear of corn and tucking it under his arm. He walked on, seemingly forgetting the incident at once.

Mr. Crow, witnessing this, felt his feathers droop. Broken! His masterpiece, broken and tossed aside as if it were mere trash! And Farmer Green had called him a "pesky critter"! This was not the reaction Mr. Crow had envisioned at all. He had expected admiration, gratitude, and perhaps even a whole new cornfield dedicated solely to his artistic endeavors. Instead, he had received contempt and dismissal. Mr. Crow felt a sour taste in his beak. It was not fair, he felt. It was not fair at all. He had worked so hard, had spent so much thought and effort, and for what? To be called a "pesky critter" and have his art broken into pieces. He cawed loudly, a harsh, indignant sound that echoed through the quiet morning air. He flew down from the oak tree, circling the spot where his broken drawing lay. He picked up one of the pieces in his beak, turning it over mournfully. It was clear that Farmer Green did not appreciate true art.

Meanwhile, Sandy Chipmunk, still hidden behind the lilac bush, continued his patient wait. He saw Farmer Green return from the cornfield with an ear of corn tucked under his arm. Farmer Green walked back to the house, stepped onto the porch, and paused again. He looked at the bark drawing and the beechnuts. This time, he smiled a broader smile. He picked up the bark, then the beechnuts. "Johnnie!" he called out, his voice a little louder than before. "Johnnie, come here a minute!"

Johnnie Green, who had been playing with a ball near the barn, came running. "What is it, Pa?" he asked.

"Look at this," Farmer Green said, holding out the bark drawing and the beechnuts. "Someone left these for me."

Johnnie took the bark. "It's a chipmunk!" he exclaimed. "And look, three beechnuts! That's real nice, Pa. Do you think Sandy did it?"

Farmer Green chuckled. "Could be, son. Could be. That Sandy is a character, he is. Always up to something." Farmer Green then put the bark and the beechnuts on the little shelf near the door, next to a small potted plant. "We'll keep these," he said. "It's a thoughtful gesture, whatever it is."

Sandy Chipmunk, overhearing this, nearly burst with joy. He had been appreciated! Farmer Green liked his gift, and he even thought it was "thoughtful"! And Johnnie Green, too, had understood. Sandy felt a rush of pride, a sense of having done something truly good. He waited a little longer, hoping for the corn, but Farmer Green and Johnnie went inside the house. Sandy decided it was time to go home and celebrate his success. He scampered back to his burrow, feeling like the most accomplished chipmunk in Pleasant Valley. He felt sure that a pile of fresh corn would appear on the doorstep by and by, a silent thank you from the grateful farmer.

As Sandy scurried along, he happened upon Mr. Crow, who was still muttering to himself and kicking at a loose pebble with his claw. Mr. Crow looked quite disgruntled. "What's the matter, Mr. Crow?" Sandy asked, quite cheerfully. "You look as though you've lost your last worm."

Mr. Crow glared at Sandy. "And what makes you so happy, Sandy Chipmunk?" he cawed crossly. "Have you gone and done something foolish again?"

"Not at all!" Sandy replied, puffing out his chest. "I left a gift for Farmer Green, a picture of myself and some beechnuts, right on his doorstep! And he liked it! He chuckled, and then he called Johnnie, and Johnnie said it was nice, and Farmer Green said it was 'thoughtful,' and they put it on a shelf by the door. I expect a great pile of corn will be waiting for me there soon enough!" Sandy could not help but brag a little, for he was truly pleased with himself.

Mr. Crow scoffed. "A pile of corn, indeed! You and your silly little nuts! Farmer Green probably just put your 'gift' aside to throw away later, you simpleton. He called it 'thoughtful' because he felt sorry for you! You think a farmer appreciates a scratchy drawing and three paltry nuts? You have no idea how humans think, Sandy Chipmunk!"

"But what about your gift, Mr. Crow?" Sandy asked. "Did Farmer Green not appreciate your masterpiece?"

Mr. Crow grew even more agitated. "Appreciate it? That ungrateful farmer! He broke it! He broke my beautiful self-portrait into pieces and threw it on the ground! He called me a 'pesky critter'! After all the effort I put in, the artistry, the clever placement! He clearly has no eye for beauty, no understanding of true talent!" Mr. Crow paced back and forth, quite beside himself with indignation. "He is an uncultured brute, that Farmer Green, and I would not work for him even if he begged me with a whole field of corn!"

Sandy blinked. "But I thought you wanted corn, Mr. Crow," he said. "You were going to make me gather it for you."

"Corn is what I want, to be sure," Mr. Crow replied, "but I will not stoop to begging for it, nor will I waste my talents on such an unappreciative soul! This is an insult, Sandy, an outrage! Farmer Green will regret this, indeed he will! He will learn that one does not simply discard the masterpieces of Mr. Crow!"

"But he kept my picture," Sandy pointed out, a little timidly. "And he called it 'thoughtful'."

Mr. Crow gave another harsh caw. "Thoughtful for a chipmunk, perhaps! But a true work of art, a bold statement of identity and expectation, that he simply destroyed! It shows his true colors, Sandy. Mark my words, Farmer Green is a rogue and a scoundrel, and he will never get another gift from me!"

Sandy found himself quite confused. Here he was, feeling proud and hopeful, while Mr. Crow was filled with anger and resentment. Both had left gifts, both had received a reaction, but their interpretations were as different as night and day. Sandy wondered if perhaps Mr. Crow was right, and Farmer Green had merely humored him. But then, why would he put the drawing on the shelf? He had not thrown it away. "Perhaps he liked your drawing too, Mr. Crow," Sandy suggested, "but he just didn't want it in his cornfield."

"Nonsense!" Mr. Crow exclaimed. "A true patron of the arts would have moved it to a place of honor, not broken it! No, Sandy, you are too naive. Farmer Green is a simple farmer, with simple tastes. He cares only for his crops, and he sees all of us as mere pests, interfering with his precious corn. Your little nuts are harmless, so he tolerates them. My artistic statement, however, was seen as an intrusion, a challenge to his authority over his field!"

Mr. Crow, still fuming, decided he would find another way to get his corn. He would not rely on Farmer Green's appreciation, nor would he trust him to understand any subtle messages. He would take what he wanted, in his own time, and by his own methods. That seemed like a much more reliable plan to Mr. Crow, for he had always prided himself on his self-sufficiency. He flapped his wings and flew off, cawing angrily to himself about the injustices of the world and the blindness of humans.

Sandy Chipmunk watched him go, then shook his head. He still felt good about his own gift, in spite of Mr. Crow’s gloomy pronouncements. He decided that he would keep an eye on Farmer Green’s doorstep, just in case that pile of corn should appear. After all, Farmer Green had said his gift was "thoughtful." And Sandy believed that thoughtful gestures deserved thoughtful rewards. He spent the rest of the morning happily tidying his burrow, humming a little tune to himself, every now and then peeking out to see if the farmer’s porch held any new surprises. The day wore on, however, and no pile of corn appeared. Sandy’s cheerful expectation began to wane, slowly, like the fading light of the afternoon sun. He checked again and again, but the doorstep remained empty, save for a few fallen leaves.

By late afternoon, Sandy Chipmunk felt a prickle of disappointment. Perhaps Mr. Crow had been right, after all. Perhaps Farmer Green had merely been polite, or perhaps he had simply forgotten about the little bark drawing and the beechnuts. Sandy still saw his gift on the shelf by the door, a faint flicker of hope, but the absence of a reciprocal gift was a bitter pill to swallow. He had hoped for so much more. He had envisioned a friendship, a mutual understanding, based on the exchange of gifts. But it seemed that humans and chipmunks, despite their good intentions, often spoke different languages, even when they used no words at all.

Just then, Johnnie Green came skipping out of the house. He had a small wooden toy in his hand and was making airplane noises. He paused at the door, noticed the bark drawing and the beechnuts on the shelf. He picked up the beechnuts, turned them over thoughtfully, and then he popped one into his mouth. Sandy Chipmunk, watching from his hiding place, felt a jolt. Johnnie ate one of his gifts! Was that a good sign or a bad one? Johnnie chewed contentedly, then looked at the other two nuts. He then carefully placed them back on the shelf, next to the bark drawing. "Those were good," he murmured to himself, and then he skipped off, continuing his airplane game.

Sandy was even more confused. Johnnie had eaten one, but left the others. What did that mean? He remembered Mr. Crow's words about Farmer Green breaking his artwork, and he thought about his own beechnuts, some of which had now been eaten. It seemed that even when humans received a gift, their reaction was often unpredictable, and rarely what the giver expected. It was a puzzling world, indeed, full of strange customs and even stranger interpretations. Sandy decided he would go and talk to Frisky Squirrel about it, for Frisky often had a sensible outlook on things, though he was prone to exaggerating the details, of course. Perhaps Frisky could shed some light on the perplexing behavior of Farmer Green and his son, Johnnie. He felt that the whole business of giving gifts and receiving them was far more complicated than it seemed, and perhaps, he thought, it was often easier just to gather one's own food, without all the fuss and misunderstanding. That evening, as the sun dipped below the horizon, painting the sky in hues of orange and purple, Sandy Chipmunk gathered a small pile of fresh seeds and ate them quietly, still pondering the mysterious ways of humans and the broken hopes of artistic crows. He decided that tomorrow, he would stick to gathering his own supply of nuts, for that, at least, was a task he understood perfectly well. And he resolved that if he ever decided to leave another gift, it would be a simple, straightforward gift, with no pictures, no art, and no possibility of misunderstanding. Or so he hoped, for he was still a hopeful little chipmunk, even in the face of human inscrutability.

XIII THE NEW GARDEN MYSTERY

Sandy Chipmunk had indeed resolved to stick to his own business. The very next morning, with the first blush of dawn painting the eastern sky, he scurried from his burrow. The air was crisp and cool, hinting at the approaching autumn, and Sandy knew that every moment was precious. He had many stores to fill before the ground froze hard and the snowdrifts piled high. He spent the morning with an industrious zeal that even Frisky Squirrel would have admired, if Frisky had been awake so early. Sandy darted among the oak trees, where the acorns were beginning to ripen, and beneath the hazel bushes, where the nuts were plump and ready. His cheek pouches, designed by nature for just such a purpose, bulged with his treasures, and he made countless trips between the gathering grounds and the deep, dark tunnels of his home. He whistled a little tune to himself as he worked, a cheerful, wordless melody that spoke of satisfaction and a good day's toil. He was a chipmunk of simple pleasures, and the feel of the cool earth beneath his paws, the rustle of leaves, and the promise of a well-stocked larder were joys enough for him.

As the sun climbed higher, casting longer shadows and warming the forest floor, Sandy found himself near the edge of Farmer Green's garden. He had planned to avoid the human dwelling, remembering the peculiar ways of its inhabitants, but a particularly fine cluster of hazelnuts grew along the stone wall that bordered the garden. With a cautious glance towards the farmhouse, Sandy decided that a

few minutes of careful gathering would do no harm. He slipped through a small opening in the old stone wall, his little nose twitching, and darted towards the hazel bushes. The nuts hung in plump clusters, their husks just beginning to split, revealing the sweet kernels within. Sandy’s cheek pouches quickly swelled with his harvest, and he made several hurried trips back and forth, depositing his treasures in a secret storehouse beneath the roots of a giant pine.

As he worked, his quick eyes, ever on the lookout for danger or opportunity, noticed something peculiar in the freshly turned earth of Farmer Green’s garden patch. It was a series of small, neat holes, dug with an uncommon precision that no badger or skunk would ever achieve. And in each hole, Farmer Green had placed a small, dark, roundish object, which he then covered carefully with soil. Sandy paused, a hazelnut halfway to his pouch, and stared. What could these strange things be? They did not look like corn, nor did they resemble any sort of vegetable seed he had ever seen. They were too large for grain, and too small for a potato.

“What in the world is Farmer Green doing?” Sandy wondered to himself. He remembered the farmer’s peculiar ways with the letter box, and the even more peculiar contents of the human letter itself. Humans, he decided, were a very puzzling sort of creature, always doing things that made no sense to a sensible chipmunk.
