XIII MR. CROW'S GREAT IDEA

Mr. Crow, having declared his need for "thinking" and "planning," did not, in fact, sit down to ponder the deeper philosophies of corn harvesting. Instead, he perched himself on the highest branch of a solitary oak at the edge of the cornfield, and promptly fell asleep. He snored loudly, his head tucked beneath his wing, dreaming, no doubt, of vast, magically self-shelling cornfields. The sun began its slow descent, painting the sky with hues of orange and purple, and still Mr. Crow slept. It was the chill of the evening air that at last stirred him, and he awoke with a start, stretching one leg and then the other, and ruffling his feathers with an air of profound importance.

"Ah," he croaked, as if emerging from a deep intellectual trance. "The solution is clear. My previous methodology was, shall we say, _crude_. Unworthy of a mind such as mine."

He looked down at the discarded twig with the U-shaped hook. It lay forlornly at the base of the cornstalk, a testament to his earlier, rather vigorous, but utterly fruitless, efforts. He felt a vague sense of annoyance that it had not magically transformed itself into a corn-gathering marvel during his nap.

"No," he declared to the twilight, "brute force is for the simple-minded. One must employ _strategy_. One must employ _intelligence_."

And with that pronouncement, Mr. Crow flew off, not towards the cornfield,

but rather away from it, towards the north, towards a place where he might find some unsuspecting creature to assist in his "intelligent" plan.

XIV MR. CROW'S CLEVER PLAN

Mr. Crow flew not to the cornfield, for that was a place of toil, but towards the deep woods, where he knew Fatty Coon made his home. Fatty Coon, though a pleasant enough fellow, was known for his love of a good meal and his even greater love for avoiding unnecessary exertion. He was also, in Mr. Crow's estimation, somewhat easily persuaded, especially when the persuasion involved promises of food. Mr. Crow believed that, with the proper application of his own keen intellect, Fatty Coon could be turned into a most excellent, if unwitting, partner in his new corn-gathering enterprise.

Mr. Crow perched on a branch above Fatty Coon's hollow log, clearing his throat with a loud "Caw! Caw!" to announce his presence. He fluffed his feathers, preparing himself for a grand pronouncement.

A sleepy grumble issued from the log. "Who's there, disturbing a body's afternoon nap?" Fatty Coon's voice was thick with slumber, his words slow and muffled.

"It is I, Mr. Crow, your esteemed acquaintance and, indeed, your benefactor!" Mr. Crow announced, puffing out his chest with an air of profound self-importance. "I have come with tidings of great consequence, Fatty, tidings that concern your very livelihood, and indeed, your winter stores!"

Slowly, and with a great deal of sighing, Fatty Coon hauled his stout body out of the hollow log. His eyes, though still heavy with sleep, narrowed suspiciously as he regarded Mr. Crow. "Winter stores, you say, Mr. Crow?" he rumbled, stretching one paw languidly. "I thought you were one who preferred to flit south when the first frost bit the nose. And as for my livelihood, I manage well enough, thank you, on what the forest provides."

Mr. Crow ruffled his feathers with an air of dignified superiority. "Ah, Fatty, but you speak of *ordinary* provision, of *common* fare. I speak of abundance, of ease, of a winter larder so plump with the choicest morsels that you shall scarce believe your good fortune." He paused, allowing his words to hang in the air, hoping they might prove as tempting as a freshly caught fish.

Fatty Coon yawned, revealing a set of rather large, dull teeth. "Morsels, you say? And what, pray tell, is the nature of these marvelous morsels? And what, pray tell again, is the catch, Mr. Crow? Fatty Coon merely grunted, a low, rumbling sound that seemed to vibrate through the very log he had just vacated. "Those 'morsels,' as you call them, Mr. Crow, were often little more than the husks of sunflower seeds you had already picked clean. Fatty Coon’s ears, which had been drooping somewhat, twitched with a flicker of interest. "Farmer Green's cornfield, you say? Ah, that is indeed a place of bounty. But also a place of peril, Mr. Crow. And of much hard work, as I recall from those foolish expeditions of my youth." He shook his large head, the movement causing his jowls to wobble. "No, no, I am past such strenuous endeavors. I am not suggesting that *we*, with our superior intellects and dignified bearings, should stoop to such common labor. Perish the thought! No, my dear Fatty, I have conceived of a plan so brilliant, so utterly ingenious, that the corn shall practically leap from the stalks into our waiting paws and beaks, with scarcely a drop of sweat upon your brow, or a single strain to my wing."

Fatty Coon looked at Mr. Crow with a long, unblinking stare. "And what, pray tell, is this wondrous plan, Mr. Crow? For I have heard tales of your 'ingenious plans' before, and they usually involve a great deal of exertion for others, and a great deal of waiting around for yourself."

"Ah, but this is different, Fatty, entirely different!" Mr. Crow exclaimed, his voice rising with theatrical fervor. "This is a plan born of true wisdom, of an understanding of the very nature of things. You see, there are creatures in this forest who are, shall we say, more suited to manual labor than ourselves. Creatures who, with but a little persuasion, and perhaps a touch of guidance from superior minds, can be induced to perform such tasks for the common good."

Fatty Coon scratched behind one ear with a thick paw. "Are you speaking of that little Sandy Chipmunk again? For he seems to be under the impression that he works for himself, and that his efforts are for his own larder, not for the 'common good' of a certain black-feathered gentleman with a fondness for corn."

Mr. Crow emitted a sharp caw of indignation. "Sandy Chipmunk is but a small cog in a much larger, more magnificent machine, my dear Fatty! He is easily swayed, easily misguided, and possessed of a singular enthusiasm for collecting. All he requires is a clear directive, and perhaps a notion that he is working for his own benefit, which, in a roundabout way, he will be, for he shall receive a share of the gleanings, of course. A small share, mind you, just enough to keep his little paws busy and his spirits high." Mr. Crow chuckled, a dry, raspy sound. "And that, my dear Fatty, is where you come in."

Fatty Coon’s eyes widened slightly. "Me? And what precisely is my role in this grand, corn-gathering, non-exerting scheme of yours, Mr. Crow? For if it involves any significant movement of my person, you may as well save your breath."

"Your role, my stout friend, is one of strategic oversight, of logistical management, and of, dare I say, moral support!" Mr. Crow declared, puffing out his chest so mightily that a feather came loose and drifted lazily to the ground. "You, with your impressive bulk and your naturally intimidating presence, shall be the overseer of the operation. You shall position yourself at the edge of the cornfield, or perhaps even within it, where your very appearance shall inspire diligence in our little laborers. And you, my dear Fatty, shall ensure that the corn, once gathered, is transported safely to our shared larder."

Fatty Coon pondered this. "A shared larder, you say? And what, pray tell, would be my share?"

"A most generous share, Fatty, a truly substantial portion!" Mr. Crow assured him, though he had not yet fully decided how much would constitute 'substantial' from his own perspective. "Let us say, for argument's sake, a full third of all that is gathered, to be deposited directly into your hollow log, without you having to lift so much as a claw to gather it yourself. You merely need to ensure its safe passage, and to, well, supervise. And perhaps, on occasion, remind our industrious little friend of the importance of his task."

Fatty Coon's eyes narrowed thoughtfully. A full third, without lifting a claw? That sounded almost too good to be true. He was a creature of comfort and convenience, and the thought of a winter store filled to bursting with sweet, golden corn, acquired through no effort of his own, was a most alluring prospect. "And what of the danger, Mr. Crow? Farmer Green is not known for his generosity, especially when it comes to his corn. And his dogs are swift."

"Ah, the dogs!" Mr. Crow waved a dismissive wing. "Mere trifles, my dear Fatty. You, with your formidable scent, shall deter them. And should one approach too closely, a well-timed snarl, a flash of those impressive teeth of yours, shall send them yelping back to the farmhouse. As for Farmer Green himself, he is a creature of predictable habits. We shall work when he is absent, and remain hidden when he is near. This, Fatty, is a matter of *strategy*, of *intelligence*, not of brute force. And in intelligence, as you know, I am unparalleled."

Fatty Coon rubbed his chin with a thoughtful paw. "A third, without labor, and merely to supervise and snarl at dogs. And you, Mr. Crow, what will be your portion, and your effort?"

Mr. Crow drew himself up to his full height. "My effort, my dear Fatty, shall be the greatest of all! For I am the architect of this grand design, the orchestrator of this bountiful harvest! My contribution is one of pure intellect, of strategic genius! I shall survey the field, I shall identify the ripest ears, I shall direct our little laborer with my keen observations, and I shall, of course, provide the necessary encouragement. My share, therefore, shall be an equally generous third, for without my brain, there would be no corn for any of us!"

Fatty Coon let out another low rumble, a sound that might have been a chuckle, or perhaps merely a deep exhalation. "And the remaining third, Mr. Crow? Who, pray tell, is to receive that?"

Mr. Crow blinked. He had, in his grand calculations, quite forgotten the third party involved in the actual labor. "Ah, the remaining third! That, my dear Fatty, shall be for our industrious little friend, Sandy Chipmunk. It is ample reward for his efforts, and will keep him happily engaged, believing himself to be acquiring his own winter stores. Thus, everyone benefits, and all is accomplished with the utmost efficiency and the least possible exertion for us, the masterminds of the operation!" Mr. Crow paused, waiting for Fatty Coon's reaction. He felt he had presented an unassailable argument, a masterpiece of persuasion.

Fatty Coon slowly nodded his head. The thought of a full third of Farmer Green's corn, delivered to his doorstep, as it were, with only the minimal inconvenience of scaring a dog or two and grunting at a chipmunk, was indeed tempting. His natural indolence warred with his appetite, and for once, the appetite seemed to have found an ally in Mr. Crow's cunning plan. "Very well, Mr. Crow," he rumbled. "I shall agree to this, your 'clever plan.' But mark my words, if my share is not delivered as promised, or if it involves more work than you have so artfully described, then this partnership shall be dissolved, and you shall have to find another, less discerning, partner for your schemes."

Mr. Crow hopped excitedly on his branch. "Excellent, excellent, my dear Fatty! You shall not regret it, I assure you! Tomorrow, then, we put our ingenious plan into motion! I shall go and 'enlist' our little chipmunk, and you shall prepare your larder for the bounty to come!" And with a triumphant "Caw! Caw!" Mr. Crow soared into the air, heading back towards the familiar oak tree near the cross-roads, his mind already spinning with the delightful prospect of corn without labor.

Fatty Coon watched him go, a faint, almost imperceptible smile playing on his large, furry face. He was not entirely convinced by Mr. Crow's grand pronouncements, for he knew the Crow's nature well. But a third of the corn for merely sitting and supervising? It was a gamble, perhaps, but one he was willing to take, especially if it meant a winter of full belly and undisturbed slumber. He hauled his body back into his hollow log, already dreaming of golden kernels.

XV MR. CROW ENLISTS SANDY CHIPMUNK

Mr. Crow returned to the great oak near Farmer Green's house, where he knew Sandy Chipmunk frequently scurried. He perched on a high branch, meticulously preening his feathers and clearing his throat with a loud "Caw!" He waited patiently, for he knew that patience, though not his strongest virtue, was sometimes necessary for the successful execution of a truly clever plan.

Presently, Sandy Chipmunk came bounding along, his cheeks already bulging with a collection of acorns he had just unearthed. He chanced to look up, and seeing Mr. Crow, he stopped, a wary expression on his small face. "Good day, Mr. Crow," he chirped, though there was a distinct lack of enthusiasm in his voice. "I trust you are well."

"Never better, my little friend, never better!" Mr. Crow boomed, in a voice that seemed overly cheerful. "And you, Sandy, you seem to be as industrious as ever. Always collecting, always planning for the future. A most commendable trait, indeed, for a creature of your size."

Sandy Chipmunk puffed out his cheeks a bit further. "I am merely going about my business, Mr. Crow. The winter, as you know, is not far off, and a chipmunk must be prepared."

"Precisely, precisely!" Mr. Crow exclaimed. "And that, my dear Sandy, is why I have sought you out. For I, with my vast experience and my intimate knowledge of the changing seasons, have come to offer you an opportunity, an unparalleled chance to secure your winter stores with an ease and an abundance you have never before imagined!"

Sandy Chipmunk looked up, his bright little eyes now holding a flicker of suspicion. He remembered their last encounter over the beechnuts, and how Mr. Crow had so readily accepted his labor only to declare it unsuitable later. "An opportunity, Mr. Crow? And what sort of opportunity might that be? For my time is valuable, and I have many acorns yet to hide."

"Time, Sandy, is precisely what this opportunity shall save you!" Mr. Crow declared grandly. "I speak of Farmer Green's cornfield! A veritable ocean of golden kernels, ripe for the taking! And I, in my generosity, am willing to share this knowledge, and indeed, to oversee the operation, ensuring your safety and maximizing your efficiency!"

Sandy Chipmunk shifted nervously from foot to foot. "Farmer Green's cornfield? But that is a dangerous place, Mr. Crow! And the ears are so large, and so high on the stalks. It is a great deal of work for a small chipmunk, and the risk is considerable."

"Nonsense, my little friend, sheer nonsense!" Mr. Crow scoffed. "With my guidance, the risk is negligible! And as for the work, ah, that is where my clever plan comes into play. You see, Sandy, you shall not be alone in this endeavor. I have, through my ingenious foresight, secured the assistance of a most formidable ally!"

Sandy Chipmunk tilted his head. "An ally? Who might that be, Mr. Crow?" He hoped it might be Frisky Squirrel, for Frisky was quick and strong.

"None other than the venerable Fatty Coon!" Mr. Crow announced, as if presenting a rare jewel. "Fatty Coon, with his imposing stature and his formidable presence, shall act as our watchful guardian, our noble overseer! He shall deter any prying eyes, discourage any canine interference, and ensure that our corn-gathering operation proceeds without a hitch! He shall be our strategic anchor, while you, my little friend, with your boundless energy and your remarkable agility, shall be the hands, or rather, the paws, that bring in the bounty!"

Sandy Chipmunk's eyes grew wide. "Fatty Coon? But he is so... so very large, and so very lazy! And he usually sleeps through most of the day!"

"Ah, but he is a creature of cunning, Sandy, and of surprising swiftness when properly motivated!" Mr. Crow insisted, ignoring the clear evidence to the contrary. "And he is *motivated* by the prospect of a full larder, just as you are. He will be watching, Sandy, always watching, ensuring your safety and the smooth flow of corn. And for your efforts, my dear chipmunk, you shall receive a most handsome reward!"

"A handsome reward?" Sandy repeated, his suspicion still outweighing his interest. He remembered the last "reward" had been a share of his own beechnuts.

"Indeed!" Mr. Crow declared. "A generous portion of the gathered corn, enough to fill your larder to overflowing, and to ensure you a most comfortable winter! Think of it, Sandy, a lifetime supply of golden kernels, secured with the combined intelligence of Mr. Crow, the watchful guardianship of Fatty Coon, and your own undeniable industry! It is a foolproof plan, a symphony of cooperation, a triumph of organized effort!"

Sandy Chipmunk still looked doubtful. "But... but if Mr. Crow is providing the intelligence, and Fatty Coon the guardianship, and I the industry, then what about the sharing, Mr. Crow? Last time, there was some disagreement about the shares."

Mr. Crow bristled slightly. "A mere miscommunication, my dear Sandy, a trifle! This time, it shall be perfectly clear! For your tireless efforts, you shall receive a full third of all the corn you gather! A magnificent third, Sandy, a truly prodigious amount for your own larder! And for my brilliant planning, and Fatty Coon's invaluable oversight, we shall each take an equal third. Is that not fair, Sandy? Is that not a perfectly equitable arrangement for all involved?"

Sandy Chipmunk did some quick mental calculations. A third of a whole cornfield, even if it was only a third of what *he* gathered, seemed like an immense quantity. And with Fatty Coon scaring off the dogs, and Mr. Crow pointing out the best ears, perhaps it would not be so much work after all. The prospect of a truly overflowing winter store, without having to brave the dangers of the forest for every single acorn, was undeniably appealing to his little chipmunk heart. "A third, you say, Mr. Crow? And Fatty Coon will truly be there, watching, and making sure the dogs don't come near?"

"Most assuredly, Sandy, most assuredly!" Mr. Crow puffed himself up. "Fatty Coon will be there, a steadfast sentinel, a bulwark against all danger! And I, from my perch, shall guide your every move, pointing out the ripest ears, and warning you of any approaching peril. It is a grand alliance, Sandy, a partnership destined for success!"

Sandy Chipmunk finally seemed convinced, or at least, sufficiently tempted. The allure of easily acquired bounty, coupled with the promise of protection, proved too strong to resist. He had, after all, once worked for Mr. Crow gathering beechnuts, and while that had ended badly, perhaps this time, with Fatty Coon involved, it would be different. "Very well, Mr. Crow," he chirped. "I shall help you gather corn. But I do hope that this time, you truly mean what you say, and that my third is a true third."

"My dear Sandy, you wound me!" Mr. Crow exclaimed, though his eyes gleamed with satisfaction. "Of course I mean what I say! Honesty, integrity, and fair dealing are the cornerstones of my philosophy! Tomorrow, then, at first light, we shall commence our grand operation! Be ready, Sandy, for a harvest such as you have never seen!" And with another triumphant "Caw!" Mr. Crow flew off, heading towards the cornfield to perform his "strategic survey," which mostly involved finding a comfortable branch from which to observe.

Sandy Chipmunk, his head now filled with visions of golden corn, scurried off to his burrow, trying to plan out how he would store such an immense quantity. He felt a thrill of excitement, mixed with a lingering unease, for Mr. Crow's promises always seemed to have a hidden claw. But a third of the corn! It was a thought almost too wonderful to ponder.

XVI THE CORNFIELD CONSPIRACY

The next morning, as the sun began to peek over the distant hills, painting the sky with soft hues of pink and gold, Mr. Crow was already perched on the tallest oak tree overlooking Farmer Green's cornfield. He adjusted his position, settling into a comfortable spot from which he could survey the entire expanse of tall, rustling stalks. He felt a thrill of self-importance, for he was the mastermind, the silent conductor of this grand enterprise.

Presently, Fatty Coon ambled into view, moving with his characteristic slow, ponderous gait. He yawned widely, stretching his massive paws and blinking in the morning light. He glanced up at Mr. Crow with a questioning look, then lumbered towards the edge of the cornfield, finding a thick patch of weeds where he could settle down with a good view, and, more importantly, a good opportunity for a morning nap should the need arise.

"Ah, Fatty, my dear fellow, punctual as ever!" Mr. Crow cawed softly, so as not to alert Farmer Green's sleeping dogs. "A magnificent day for a harvest, would you not agree? The air is crisp, the sun is rising, and the corn, ah, the corn is just waiting to be gathered!"

Fatty Coon merely grunted in response, his eyes already beginning to glaze over. He yawned again, exposing his impressive array of teeth. He was not much of a morning person, and the prospect of merely supervising was the only thing that had drawn him out of his warm log so early.

Then, a flurry of movement, and Sandy Chipmunk arrived, darting across the open ground with nervous energy. He stopped at the edge of the cornfield, peering cautiously at the tall stalks, then up at Mr. Crow, and finally, with a gulp, at the enormous, sleepy form of Fatty Coon.

"Good morning, Mr. Crow! Good morning, Mr. Coon!" Sandy chirped, his voice a little shaky. He felt a flutter of fear in his stomach, for the cornfield looked even more vast and imposing in the pale morning light than it had in his imagination.

"Good morning, Sandy, my industrious little friend!" Mr. Crow replied, his voice full of jovial encouragement. "The stage is set, the players are assembled, and the bounty awaits! Now, Sandy, listen closely to my instructions. You see that row, the third one from the fence? Those ears, my boy, are particularly plump and golden. Begin there. And remember, be swift, be silent, and above all, be diligent!"

Sandy Chipmunk nodded, his small heart thumping. He took a deep breath, and with a quick dart, disappeared into the rustling depths of the cornfield. The tall stalks closed around him, like a golden wall, and soon only the faint rustle of leaves marked his progress.

Mr. Crow watched with satisfaction. His plan was unfolding perfectly. Fatty Coon, though half-asleep, was a visible presence. Sandy Chipmunk was hard at work. And he, Mr. Crow, was the brilliant mind behind it all, exerting not a single feather, yet destined for a third of the glorious harvest.

Hours passed. The sun climbed higher, warming the earth and making the cornfield shimmer. Mr. Crow occasionally let out a soft caw of encouragement to Sandy, or a slightly louder one to ensure Fatty Coon remained at least partially awake. Fatty Coon, for his part, managed to keep one eye open, occasionally twitching a paw or letting out a low growl when a distant sound suggested a potential threat. But mostly, he simply rested, enjoying the warmth and the peace, knowing that his winter larder was being filled by others.

Sandy Chipmunk worked tirelessly. He scrambled up the stalks, tugged at the large ears, gnawed them free, and then, with immense effort, dragged them down to the base of the stalk. From there, he would gnaw off the kernels, stuffing them into his cheek pouches until they bulged impossibly, and then race back to his hidden burrow, a considerable distance away, to deposit his precious cargo. Then he would return, panting, to begin the laborious process anew. He knew he was working hard, perhaps harder than he had ever worked before, but the thought of his "one-third" of the bounty, and Mr. Crow's assurances of immense quantities, spurred him on.

By midday, Sandy Chipmunk was utterly exhausted. His paws ached, his teeth were sore, and his little heart felt as though it might burst. He had made countless trips, and his burrow, though not yet "overflowing," held a respectable pile of golden kernels. He emerged from the cornfield, looking up at Mr. Crow, his tiny body trembling with weariness.

"Mr. Crow," he panted, "I have gathered a great many kernels. My cheek pouches are quite raw. Perhaps I should rest for a spell?"

Mr. Crow looked down from his perch, his eyes scanning the field. He saw a rather small pile of husks at the base of several stalks, and a few dropped kernels. "Rest, Sandy? Nonsense, my dear fellow! The sun is high, and the day is long! We must make hay, or rather, gather corn, while the sun shines! Think of your magnificent third, Sandy! Think of the winter, so cold and bleak, and the warmth of a full larder! Return to your labors, my little friend, with renewed vigor!"

Sandy Chipmunk sighed. He glanced at Fatty Coon, who seemed to be in a particularly deep state of slumber, snoring faintly amidst the weeds. He longed for such an easy life. But Mr. Crow's voice, though outwardly encouraging, held an undeniable edge of command. And the thought of that immense third, which Mr. Crow had promised, kept him going. With a heavy heart and aching paws, he turned and plunged back into the rustling stalks.

A short while later, Jasper Jay, known for his curious nature and his love of a good bit of gossip, flew by. He spotted Mr. Crow perched regally, and Fatty Coon snoozing contentedly, and the tell-tale rustling in Farmer Green's cornfield. Being a bird who understood how to put two and two together, Jasper Jay quickly surmised that Mr. Crow was, as usual, up to some sort of scheme that involved someone else doing all the work.

Cheerful Robin, though a generally good-natured fellow, was not one to give up a prize easily, especially when it came to nest-building. He had worked hard to gather this unusual material. He imagined Professor Hoot to be a very stern and serious bird, perhaps even a little frightening, with his enormous eyes and quiet demeanor. They walked for a considerable time, passing through sunny glades and shadowy thickets, until the sound of running water grew louder, and they came upon a winding brook. XIII A PECULIAR FEEDER

The days grew shorter and the air took on a decided chill, which was Nature's way of reminding all the forest-folk that winter was not so very far away. Sandy Chipmunk, feeling quite pleased with his recent learning, knew that contentment, however pleasant, could not fill an empty stomach come January. So, with Frisky Squirrel often at his side, he set about his tasks with renewed vigor, gathering all sorts of nuts and seeds, and storing them away in his snug burrow beneath the old oak. He even paused sometimes to inspect the 'Winter Mix' flyer, still tucked safely in his pouch,

when a new and most peculiar object caught his sharp eyes one brisk morning. He had been scampering along the stone wall that bordered Farmer Green’s garden, intent upon a particularly plump acorn which had dropped from an oak limb, when he saw it. There, hanging from a low branch of a young apple tree, was a small, gaily painted box. It was not like the mail-box, for it had no lid that closed tightly, but rather several openings around its base, and a small perch beneath each one.

Sandy Chipmunk paused, the acorn forgotten for a moment. He peered at the strange contraption with a mixture of curiosity and caution. His recent adventure with the mail-box had taught him that human objects, however innocent they might appear, often held surprises. And sometimes, those surprises were not at all pleasant. He remembered Farmer Green’s stones.

“Now, what in the world is that?” Sandy murmured to himself, twitching his nose. It smelled of wood, paint, and, strangely enough, of seeds. Very good seeds, too, if his nose did not deceive him.

He circled the apple tree at a safe distance, keeping a watchful eye on Farmer Green’s house. No one was about. Still, he hesitated. Was it a new kind of trap? Perhaps Farmer Green, vexed by the incident with the letter, had decided to set a more cunning snare for the forest-folk. The openings were just large enough for a chipmunk, or perhaps a bird, to squeeze through.

He thought of the 'Winter Mix' flyer, which still nestled in his cheek pouch. He had quite forgotten about it for a while, so engrossed had he been in his acorn-gathering. He pulled it out, flattened it against the stone wall, and studied the picture. Lo and behold! On one corner of the flyer, there was a small drawing of a box, very much like the one hanging in the apple tree, though not quite so gaily painted. And beneath it, small letters which Sandy still could not read, but which he now associated with a general sense of abundance and perhaps, misunderstanding.

“Hmm,” Sandy hummed, a new thought stirring in his clever little head. “Could this be what the flyer was talking about? This ‘Winter Mix’?”

He sat for a long time, watching the peculiar box, his mind working busily. He saw a small Chickadee flit to the apple tree, alight on one of the perches, and peck eagerly at something inside the box. The Chickadee seemed quite content, and flew off without so much as a nervous peep. A moment later, a pair of hungry Sparrows arrived, darting in and out of the openings, carrying away tiny morsels. They too, showed no signs of alarm.

Sandy’s courage grew. If it were a trap, surely the clever Chickadee and the wary Sparrows would have sensed it. Besides, they had flown off so quickly, taking their bounty with them. It was as if Farmer Green, instead of setting a trap, had actually left food out for them. This was a concept almost too remarkable for Sandy to grasp entirely, given his previous experience with human intentions.

He decided to tell Frisky Squirrel. Frisky was always ready for an adventure, and though sometimes a little rash, he was a good companion. Sandy scampered away from the apple tree, making his way through the fallen leaves and undergrowth, until he found Frisky high up in a hickory tree, busily burying nuts.

“Frisky, oh, Frisky!” Sandy called, panting slightly from his hurried journey. “You’ll never guess what I’ve found!”

Frisky Squirrel peered down from his perch, his tail twitching. “Found what, Sandy? Another picture of a chipmunk on a piece of paper?” he teased, remembering their last adventure.

Sandy puffed out his chest. “No, indeed! Something far more interesting. Something that holds food. Right in Farmer Green’s yard!”

Frisky dropped the nut he was holding, and it bounced off a branch with a dull thud. “Food? In Farmer Green’s yard? Is it a new patch of sunflowers? Or perhaps a forgotten pumpkin?”

“Neither!” Sandy exclaimed. “It’s a box! A peculiar box, hanging from an apple tree. And birds are eating from it, as bold as you please!”

Frisky Squirrel was intrigued. “A box, you say? And not a trap?”

“I don’t believe so,” Sandy replied, trying to sound as confident as possible. “I saw Chickadees and Sparrows going in and out, and they seemed quite happy. It smelled of seeds, Frisky, good seeds, I tell you!”

Frisky, with his usual impetuousness, immediately declared, “Well, what are we waiting for? Let’s go investigate!” And without another word, he leaped from the hickory tree, landing lightly on a lower branch, and began to follow Sandy towards Farmer Green’s yard.

As they approached the apple tree, Sandy pointed with his nose. “There it is, Frisky. What do you make of it?”

Frisky Squirrel’s eyes widened. “My goodness, Sandy! It looks like a tiny house for birds! And you say there’s food inside?”

“That’s what I saw, and that’s what I smelled,” Sandy confirmed. He pulled out his 'Winter Mix' flyer again. “And I believe it has something to do with this piece of paper. See the picture? It’s very much alike.”

Frisky peered at the flyer, then at the feeder. “I daresay you’re right, Sandy. But how do we get to it? It’s hanging from a branch, and those openings look a bit small for me to squeeze through easily.”

Indeed, the feeder was designed for smaller birds, with narrow perches and openings. A chipmunk or a squirrel would have to work a little harder.

Just then, a flash of blue and a raucous squawk announced the arrival of Jasper Jay. He landed on a nearby branch, eyeing the feeder with suspicious intensity.

“Well, well, what have we here?” Jasper Jay demanded, his head cocked. “A new contraption of Farmer Green’s? Is it a trap, Sandy Chipmunk? You know all about traps, don’t you, after your little mail-box escapade?” He chuckled, a harsh, grating sound.

Sandy bristled. “It is no trap, Jasper Jay! It is a box of food, put out by Farmer Green for the birds, I imagine. And perhaps, for others who are clever enough to get to it.” He winked at Frisky.

Jasper Jay preened his blue feathers. “Food for the birds, you say? Why, then, it must be for me! I am a bird, after all, and a very handsome one at that. Farmer Green knows a good bird when he sees one. And I daresay, he knows that a clever bird like myself needs a good meal to keep his wits sharp.”

He flew over to the feeder, alighted on a perch, and peered inside. “Why, it’s full of seeds! Plump sunflower seeds, cracked corn, and all sorts of delicious morsels!” He began to gobble them down greedily, making loud pecking noises.

Frisky Squirrel looked at Sandy. “He’s certainly not shy, is he?”

Sandy shook his head. “Jasper Jay is never shy when there is food to be had, especially if he thinks it is meant specifically for him.”

They watched Jasper Jay for a few more minutes, as he ate his fill. When he finally flew off, chattering about his good fortune, Sandy and Frisky decided it was their turn.

“Now, how to get to it,” Frisky mused, eyeing the feeder carefully. “It’s hanging quite freely, and those openings are a bit tricky.”

Sandy, ever the planner, had an idea. “If we climb the apple tree to the branch just above it, perhaps we can drop down onto the top of the feeder. Then, we can reach down into the openings.”

Frisky agreed. They both scampered up the rough bark of the apple tree, their sharp claws finding purchase easily. Sandy, being smaller, found it a bit easier to maneuver. He reached the branch just above the feeder, and with a small leap, landed on the flat top of the box.

“Aha!” he exclaimed, his eyes shining. “It’s quite sturdy!”

Frisky Squirrel followed, though he wobbled a bit more when he landed. “Careful, Sandy! Don’t knock it down!”

They peered into the openings. The feeder was indeed full of an assortment of seeds and grains. Sunflower seeds, millet, and even some small pieces of dried fruit were visible. It was a veritable feast.

Sandy dipped his tiny paw in, scooping out a few sunflower seeds. He quickly transferred them to his cheek pouches. Frisky, being larger, had a bit more difficulty, but managed to extract a good handful of the mix.

They munched happily, enjoying their unexpected bounty. “This is much better than digging for buried nuts on a cold morning,” Frisky declared, his mouth full.

“Indeed,” Sandy agreed. “But we must be careful not to take too much. It is meant for the birds, after all, and we wouldn’t want to be selfish.” This was a new thought for Sandy, who usually thought only of gathering as much as possible for himself. His recent lessons, however, had made him a little more considerate.

Suddenly, a shadow fell over them, and a harsh, familiar "Caw!" shattered the peaceful scene. Mr. Crow landed heavily on a higher branch, his beady eyes fixed on the feeder.

“Well, well, if it isn’t Sandy Chipmunk and Frisky Squirrel, raiding the bird feeder!” Mr. Crow boomed, his voice full of mock indignation. “I knew that peculiar box would cause trouble. Farmer Green put that out for the *birds*, you know. Not for thieving chipmunks and squirrels!”

Sandy looked up, a sunflower seed still clutched in his paw. “We are not thieving, Mr. Crow. We are merely partaking of the abundance. And besides, I saw Jasper Jay eating from it, and he is a bird.”

“Jasper Jay is a noisy ruffian!” Mr. Crow retorted, ruffling his black feathers. “He has no manners, and he takes more than his share. But that is no excuse for you two ground-dwellers to invade what is rightfully ours!”

Frisky Squirrel puffed up his chest. “But the flyer, Mr. Crow! Sandy’s flyer shows a picture of this very box, and it says ‘Winter Mix’! It didn’t say ‘Winter Mix for Birds Only’!”

Mr. Crow scoffed. “Flyer? What nonsense are you talking about now? Those human papers are nothing but trouble. You ought to know that by now, Sandy. Remember the letter?” He gave a knowing look. “No, this is clearly a bird feeder. And I, Mr. Crow, am a very important bird. Therefore, its contents are primarily for me.”

He stretched his long neck and tried to reach into one of the openings, but his beak was too large and clumsy for the small holes. He tried another, and then another, growing more and more frustrated.

“Confound these small openings!” he grumbled. “They are made for those tiny, insignificant birds! How is a self-respecting crow supposed to get a proper meal from this contraption?”

Sandy and Frisky watched him, trying to suppress their giggles. Mr. Crow’s attempts were quite comical, as he hopped from perch to perch, his large body ill-suited for the delicate feeder.

“Perhaps it was not meant for you, Mr. Crow,” Sandy suggested politely. “Perhaps it is meant for the smaller birds, and they are kind enough to share a little with us.”

This remark only angered Mr. Crow further. “Share? Share with chipmunks? Nonsense! When food is put out, it is for the strongest and the cleverest to take!”

He then proceeded to try a different tactic. He flew up, landing on the very top of the feeder, just as Sandy and Frisky had done. But Mr. Crow was much heavier. The feeder swayed precariously under his weight.

“Oh dear!” Sandy squeaked, holding on tightly.

“Look out!” Frisky cried.

Mr. Crow, however, was not paying attention to their warnings. He was too busy trying to use his beak to pry open the top or somehow enlarge the holes. He pushed and he pulled, his powerful wings flapping for balance. And then, with a groan of stressed wood, one of the small perches gave way. The feeder tilted violently, and a stream of seeds poured out onto the ground below.

“Mr. Crow!” Sandy exclaimed, horrified.

Frisky Squirrel let out a surprised squeal as he nearly lost his footing. Mr. Crow, startled by the sudden cascade of food, lost his balance completely and tumbled off the feeder, landing with an undignified thump on the soft grass below.

He quickly recovered, shaking his head and looking quite disheveled. But his eyes immediately lit upon the spilled seeds on the ground.

“Aha!” he cawed triumphantly. “I knew there was a better way! Now the food is accessible to all!” He began to peck at the scattered seeds with renewed vigor.

Sandy and Frisky, still perched precariously on the damaged feeder, looked at each other.

“He didn’t mean to do that, did he?” Frisky whispered.

“I don’t think so,” Sandy replied, a small smile playing on his lips. “But now, it is indeed accessible to all.”

They carefully climbed down from the feeder, which was now hanging crookedly, and joined Mr. Crow and a growing number of smaller birds who had quickly arrived to partake of the unexpected feast. Even a few field mice, drawn by the scent of the seeds, darted out from the hedgerow.

Farmer Green, meanwhile, had heard the commotion. He came out of his house, carrying a small bag. When he saw his bird feeder hanging at an odd angle, and the ground covered in seeds, he sighed.

“Oh, those crows!” he grumbled good-naturedly. “Always too big for their britches, and too clumsy for my feeders!” He then bent down and began to scoop the seeds back into the feeder, careful to patch up the broken perch as best he could.

The forest-folk scattered, watching from a safe distance. Mr. Crow, feeling a bit sheepish, flew back up to his higher branch.

“He’s putting it back!” Sandy whispered to Frisky. “And he’s fixing it!”

Frisky nodded. “He doesn’t seem angry, does he?”

After Farmer Green had finished, he stood back, surveyed his work, and then looked towards the hiding chipmunks. He smiled, a slow, kind smile. “Don’t you worry, little fellows,” he said, though he knew they wouldn’t understand his words. “There’s enough for everyone, if you just take your turn.”

Then he went back into his house.

Sandy Chipmunk looked at Frisky Squirrel. “Did you hear that, Frisky? He said there’s enough for everyone!”

“He sounded quite jolly, too,” Frisky added. “Not like he was angry at all, even though Mr. Crow broke his feeder.”

Sandy felt a warmth spread through him. It was a new feeling, one that came from understanding. Farmer Green, the same Farmer Green who had chased him with stones for taking a letter, was also capable of great kindness, putting out food for the forest-folk and even fixing what had been broken.

“You know, Frisky,” Sandy said thoughtfully, “Professor Hoot once told me that not everything is as it seems. And that sometimes, what looks like a gift for one, might be a gift for many.”

Frisky nodded, already eyeing the mended feeder. “So, we can eat from it again?”

“I believe so,” Sandy replied. “But perhaps we should be more polite about it. And perhaps, we should let the small birds have their turn first. After all, it is called a ‘bird feeder’.”

That day, Sandy and Frisky learned a valuable lesson about the peculiar generosity of Farmer Green, and the true meaning of sharing. They still gathered their own nuts and seeds, for a chipmunk’s burrow could never be too full. But they also learned that sometimes, a little shared bounty, and a bit of politeness, made the world a much friendlier place.

The days continued to shorten, and the mornings grew frosty, painting the leaves with crisp white edges. The Peculiar Feeder, as Sandy and Frisky had begun to call it, became a regular stop on their foraging rounds. They learned to wait patiently for the smaller birds to take their fill, and even Mr. Crow, having learned that brute force was not the best approach, would often wait for a spill or for Farmer Green to replenish the supply. Though he still grumbled about the small openings, he was less prone to breaking the feeder, understanding perhaps that a steady supply was better than a single, destructive feast.

XIV A SNOWY SURPRISE

One morning, Sandy Chipmunk awoke to an unfamiliar quiet. The world outside his burrow was hushed, muffled, and transformed. He poked his nose out and blinked. Everything was white. A thick blanket of snow covered the ground, the trees, and even the roof of Farmer Green’s house. Winter had truly arrived.

“My goodness,” Sandy whispered to himself, his breath clouding in the cold air. “It’s even colder than I expected.”

He had stored away a respectable amount of food, his cheek pouches having worked diligently for weeks. But the sight of the deep snow, covering every last acorn and seed, made him shiver. It was a stark reminder of how difficult life could become when Mother Nature decided to cover the larder.

He ventured out cautiously, making little tracks in the pristine snow. His first thought was of the Peculiar Feeder. Would Farmer Green still fill it, even with all this snow? He hoped so, for the smaller birds would surely be struggling. And, if truth be told, a few sunflower seeds would make a most welcome addition to his own breakfast.

As he neared the apple tree, he saw that the feeder was indeed still hanging. And to his delight, it was full! Farmer Green had braved the snow to ensure his feathered friends would not go hungry.

Sandy scampered up the tree, making his way to the feeder. He noticed something else, too. The snow had piled up on the perches, making it a bit harder for the birds to land, but also forming little ramps and mounds that made it easier for a small chipmunk to reach the openings. He chuckled to himself. Sometimes, Nature had a funny way of helping.

He ate a few seeds, grateful for the easy meal, and then quickly filled his pouches before heading back to his burrow. The journey through the snow was slow and tiring. He realized that foraging for buried nuts would be nearly impossible now. His foresight in gathering so much food seemed even wiser than before.

Throughout the day, other forest-folk braved the snow. Frisky Squirrel, looking rather fluffy with his winter coat, arrived at the feeder, complaining about the difficulty of finding his buried treasures.

“This snow is much too deep, Sandy!” Frisky chattered, shaking his head. “I can barely remember where I put half my nuts!”

Sandy nodded sympathetically. “It is indeed a challenge, Frisky. But Farmer Green has been kind. The feeder is full.”

Mr. Crow, however, was having a much harder time. His large body left deep impressions in the snow, and his usual scavenging spots were completely covered. He flew to the feeder, complaining loudly.

“This is preposterous!” he croaked, shaking snow from his feathers. “The ground is frozen solid! Where am I to find a decent meal? These tiny seeds are barely enough to keep a flea alive!”

He tried to land on the feeder, but the snow-covered perches made him slip, and he nearly tumbled off again. This time, however, he caught himself, though his dignity suffered greatly.

Sandy, from the safety of a nearby branch, offered a suggestion. “Perhaps you could try pecking at the snow on the ground, Mr. Crow. Sometimes, Farmer Green spills a little when he fills the feeder.”

Mr. Crow scoffed. “Peck at snow? What a ridiculous idea! My beak is for digging out juicy grubs, not for sifting through frozen flakes!” But, seeing no other option, he reluctantly tried, grumbling all the while. To his surprise, he did manage to uncover a few scattered seeds that had been buried by the light snowfall.

“Hmph,” he muttered, gobbling the seeds. “A lucky coincidence, that’s all it is.” But Sandy noticed that Mr. Crow stayed on the ground, sifting through the snow, for quite some time after that.

The heavy snow brought new challenges, and new lessons. Sandy learned that while his own preparations were vital, sometimes, unexpected help came from the most unlikely sources. He also observed how the snow, while making things difficult for some, could inadvertently create opportunities for others.

A few days later, the snow began to melt slightly, creating icy patches and slush. The temperature remained low, however, and Sandy knew that the heart of winter was yet to come. He spent much of his time snug in his burrow, munching on his stored provisions, but still made regular trips to the Peculiar Feeder, especially on the colder days.

One afternoon, as he was returning from the feeder with his pouches bulging, he heard a faint cry. It sounded small, and very distressed. He paused, twitching his ears. The sound came from behind the thick oak tree near the crossroads.

Curiosity, that powerful force that often led Sandy into adventures, overcame his desire to return to his warm burrow. He cautiously made his way towards the sound, peering through the bare branches of the oak.

There, half-buried in a drift of snow, was a tiny, shivering bird. It was a Wren, its feathers fluffed up to twice their size, its tiny body trembling. It seemed to be trapped, its wing tangled in a thorny bramble that had been covered by the snow.

Sandy’s heart went out to the little creature. He knew how unforgiving winter could be, and how quickly a small bird could succumb to the cold. He looked around. No other animal was in sight. No Mr. Crow, no Jasper Jay, no Frisky Squirrel. He was alone.

He approached the Wren slowly, not wanting to frighten it. The Wren, seeing him, chirped a little louder, its eyes wide with fear and pain.

“Don’t worry, little one,” Sandy murmured, his voice soft. “I’ll help you.”

He carefully nudged the snow away from the bramble, using his paws and nose. The thorns were sharp, and he had to be very careful not to prick himself, or to hurt the Wren further. It was a delicate task, requiring patience and a steady paw.

After several minutes of painstaking effort, he managed to free the Wren’s wing. The little bird chirped weakly, flexing its wing gingerly. It seemed uninjured, though very cold and weak.

“Can you fly?” Sandy asked, stepping back a little.

The Wren tested its wing again, then with a small burst of effort, managed to flutter into the lower branches of the oak tree. It perched there, shivering, but safe.

“Thank you, Sandy Chipmunk,” the Wren chirped, its voice tiny but full of gratitude. “You have saved my life.”

Sandy felt a blush spread across his face, hidden by his fur. “It was nothing, little one. Just be careful.”

He watched as the Wren slowly gathered its strength, fluffed its feathers, and then, with a final grateful chirp, flew off towards the Peculiar Feeder. Sandy knew it would find sustenance there, thanks to Farmer Green.

As Sandy made his way back to his burrow, he felt a different kind of warmth than the one that came from a full stomach or a snug bed. It was the warmth of having helped another creature, especially one so small and helpless. He realized that the lessons from the mail-box, and the bird feeder, had not just been about understanding human ways or securing food for himself. They had also been about kindness, and recognizing that even a small chipmunk could make a difference in the harsh world of winter. He felt quite content, indeed, and knew that this quiet satisfaction was perhaps the best 'Winter Mix' of all.when a new and most peculiar object caught his sharp eyes one brisk morning. He had been scampering along the stone wall that bordered Farmer Green’s garden, intent upon a particularly plump acorn which had dropped from an oak limb, when he saw it. There, hanging from a low branch of a young apple tree, was a small, gaily painted box. It was not like the mail-box, for it had no lid that closed tightly, but rather several openings around its base, and a small perch beneath each one.

Sandy Chipmunk paused, the acorn forgotten for a moment. He peered at the strange contraption with a mixture of curiosity and caution. His recent adventure with the mail-box had taught him that human objects, however innocent they might appear, often held surprises. And sometimes, those surprises were not at all pleasant. He remembered Farmer Green’s stones, and the quick sting they delivered if one was not nimble enough.

“Now, what in the world is that?” Sandy murmured to himself, twitching his nose. It smelled of wood, paint, and, strangely enough, of seeds. Very good seeds, too, if his nose did not deceive him. A most inviting aroma, indeed.

He circled the apple tree at a safe distance, keeping a watchful eye on Farmer Green’s house. No one was about. Still, he hesitated. Was it a new kind of trap? Perhaps Farmer Green, vexed by the incident with the letter, had decided to set a more cunning snare for the forest-folk. The openings were just large enough for a chipmunk, or perhaps a bird, to squeeze through. It did look rather innocent, but then, so did the mail-box at first glance.

He thought of the 'Winter Mix' flyer, which still nestled in his cheek pouch. He had quite forgotten about it for a while, so engrossed had he been in his acorn-gathering and burrow-tidying. He pulled it out, flattened it against the stone wall, and studied the picture. Lo and behold! On one corner of the flyer, there was a small drawing of a box, very much like the one hanging in the apple tree, though not quite so gaily painted. And beneath it, small letters which Sandy still could not read, but which he now associated with a general sense of abundance and perhaps, misunderstanding.

“Hmm,” Sandy hummed, a new thought stirring in his clever little head. “Could this be what the flyer was talking about? This ‘Winter Mix’?” He tapped the paper with a tiny paw. It seemed to make sense, in a way that many human things did not.

He sat for a long time, watching the peculiar box, his mind working busily. He saw a small Chickadee flit to the apple tree, alight on one of the perches, and peck eagerly at something inside the box. The Chickadee seemed quite content, and flew off without so much as a nervous peep. A moment later, a pair of hungry Sparrows arrived, darting in and out of the openings, carrying away tiny morsels. They too, showed no signs of alarm, merely a bustling eagerness.

Sandy’s courage grew. If it were a trap, surely the clever Chickadee and the wary Sparrows would have sensed it. Besides, they had flown off so quickly, taking their bounty with them. It was as if Farmer Green, instead of setting a trap, had actually left food out for them. This was a concept almost too remarkable for Sandy to grasp entirely, given his previous experience with human intentions. Farmer Green was, after all, a being of unpredictable moods.

He decided to tell Frisky Squirrel. Frisky was always ready for an adventure, and though sometimes a little rash, he was a good companion, always willing to try a new trick. Sandy scampered away from the apple tree, making his way through the fallen leaves and undergrowth, until he found Frisky high up in a hickory tree, busily burying nuts with a vigor that suggested he meant to hide them from the entire forest.

“Frisky, oh, Frisky!” Sandy called, panting slightly from his hurried journey. “You’ll never guess what I’ve found!”

Frisky Squirrel peered down from his perch, his tail twitching like a small banner. “Found what, Sandy? Another picture of a chipmunk on a piece of paper?” he teased, remembering their last adventure and its surprising turn.

Sandy puffed out his chest, a little annoyed by the light-hearted jest. “No, indeed! Something far more interesting. Something that holds food. Right in Farmer Green’s yard, and not in his cornfield either!”

Frisky dropped the nut he was holding, and it bounced off a branch with a dull thud before rolling out of sight. “Food? In Farmer Green’s yard? Is it a new patch of sunflowers? Or perhaps a forgotten pumpkin, ripe for the nibbling?”

“Neither!” Sandy exclaimed, quite out of breath with excitement. “It’s a box! A peculiar box, hanging from an apple tree. And birds are eating from it, as bold as you please, as though it were their own!”

Frisky Squirrel was immediately intrigued. His eyes gleamed with curiosity. “A box, you say? And not a trap? You are certain, Sandy?”

“I don’t believe so,” Sandy replied, trying to sound as confident as possible. “I saw Chickadees and Sparrows going in and out, and they seemed quite happy. It smelled of seeds, Frisky, good seeds, I tell you, better than any beechnut Mr. Crow ever refused!”

Frisky, with his usual impetuousness, immediately declared, “Well, what are we waiting for? Let’s go investigate, and perhaps taste some of these wonderful seeds!” And without another word, he leaped from the hickory tree, landing lightly on a lower branch, and began to follow Sandy towards Farmer Green’s yard, his tail a-quiver with anticipation.

As they approached the apple tree, Sandy pointed with his nose, which was still twitching with the alluring scent. “There it is, Frisky. What do you make of it?”
