XVII THE MUDDY CROSSING

Young Percival the piglet was feeling particularly bold one sunny afternoon. His nose, always eager for new scents, had caught the sweet aroma of wild strawberries growing on the far bank of Goose Creek. The creek, usually a mere trickle, was swollen and muddy after the morning’s rain. Percival trotted to the edge, his little hooves sinking slightly into the soft earth. “Oh, my!” he grunted to himself. “What a feast awaits me over there!”

He took a tentative step down the bank, testing the dark, glistening mud with one pink foot. It squished invitingly. “I shall cross this very moment!” he declared, puffing out his chest.

From beneath a large oak tree, Old Shep the sheep watched with mild amusement. He had seen many a young creature make similar pronouncements. “Are you quite sure, Percival?” Shep called out, his voice a gentle bleat. “That mud looks rather deep to me.”

Percival pulled his foot from the mire with a soft sucking sound. “Nonsense, Shep!” he cried, a little impatiently. “It’s just a bit of spring mud. I’m quite agile, you know. I’ll simply leap across the narrowest part.”

“Leaping is one thing; but a soft landing can sometimes be more troublesome than a hard one,” Shep replied, slowly rising to his feet. “I remember a time, oh, many years ago, when I was but a lamb like yourself. I saw some particularly tender clover growing on a little island in the middle of the Duck Pond.”

Percival’s ears twitched. He loved stories, especially from Old Shep. “What happened?” he asked, momentarily forgetting his strawberry quest.

“Well,” Shep continued, walking slowly towards the creek bank, “I thought nothing of it. I sprang onto what I thought was firm ground. But the bank was made of the silkiest, stickiest black mud you ever did see. My legs went right out from under me. I floundered and struggled, and with each kick I only sank deeper.”

Percival gasped. “Did Farmer Giles have to pull you out?”

“He did indeed!” Shep nodded solemnly. “He pulled me out by my wool, I was quite a sight. Covered head to hoof in mud. My mother said I looked like a mud pie come to life.” Shep paused, gazing thoughtfully at the creek. “It took me a whole week to feel clean again. And that particular patch of clover, as it turned out, was not nearly so delicious as it had seemed from a distance.”

Percival looked from the muddy creek to Old Shep, then back to the creek. The juicy red berries still beckoned, but the image of Shep, covered in sticky black mud, suddenly made the adventure less appealing. “So it’s not always best to rush into things, is it?” he mused.

“Seldom is haste rewarded when it comes to mud, my boy,” Shep agreed. “There are other strawberries, Percival. And the creek will likely be calmer and clearer by tomorrow. Sometimes, waiting is the best way to get where you want to go.”

Percival sighed, a little wistfully. “I suppose you’re right, Shep.” He turned away from the creek. “Perhaps,” he said, trotting alongside the old sheep, “we can find some nice, dry clover in this pasture instead.”

Shep smiled. “That sounds like a most excellent plan, Percival.” He knew that young Percival had learned a valuable lesson without ever having to get his hooves muddy.

The scrubby white mare, whose name, it turned out, was Daisy, tossed her head. A small, rusty horseshoe, which she had been wearing for a very long time indeed, clattered against the wall of her stall. "Impertinence!" she snorted, her voice like dry leaves skittering across a stony path. "Why, you little whippersnapper! You ought to be grateful for a bit of plain speaking. Someone needs to tell you youngsters the truth. This isn't a nursery. And if you weren't quite so young, you'd know that a respectable mare doesn't speak to a mere colt without a proper introduction."

Ebenezer, who had been patiently listening to this exchange, now stepped forward a little. His large, calm eyes regarded Daisy with a gentle but firm expression. "Now, Daisy," he said, his voice a low rumble, "there is no need for such sharpness. The young one is merely a trifle uneasy, as any creature might be on his first visit to the smithy. And he is not a colt. He is a pony, and his name is Twinkleheels."

Daisy looked Twinkleheels up and down with an exaggerated air of disdain. "A pony, is it?" she muttered. "Well, he looks small enough to be a badger. And he certainly whinnies like a frightened squirrel. Still, a pony or a colt, it matters little. He ought to have a bit more gumption about him."

Twinkleheels felt his cheeks grow hot, if horses could be said to have cheeks that reddened. He wanted to retort again, but Ebenezer gave him a soft, reassuring nudge with his muzzle. "Pay her no mind, Twinkleheels," the old horse whispered. "She has always been a trifle sharp-tongued. It is her way. She means no harm, truly. She just forgets what it was like to be new to things."

Just then, Farmer Green called out, "All right, Ebenezer, old boy! Your turn first, I think. You're the experienced one."

Ebenezer moved forward with a calm, unhurried gait, stepping into the main work area of the smithy. The blacksmith, a large, kindly-looking man with strong arms and a leather apron, greeted him with a friendly pat. "Hello, Ebenezer! Ready for your new boots?"

"As always, Master Brown," Ebenezer replied, his voice a soft whicker. He positioned himself perfectly, lifting his foreleg slightly as if in anticipation.

Twinkleheels watched, wide-eyed, from his position just inside the door. He was grateful that Farmer Green had tied his halter to a stout ring on the wall, for his legs felt a sudden urge to carry him far, far away from all this noise and heat. The forge glowed, the anvil clanged, and the strange, sharp smell of burnt hoof and hot metal filled the air. He saw Master Brown take Ebenezer's hoof, clean it carefully with a small, curved knife, and then, to Twinkleheels' horror, begin to pare away parts of the horny material.

"Does that hurt him, Ebenezer?" Twinkleheels cried out, unable to contain his alarm.

Ebenezer let out a soft chuckle. "Not at all, little friend," he said, without even turning his head. He seemed entirely at ease. "My hoof is like your fingernail, or perhaps more like a very thick toenail. It grows continually. And just as you trim your own nails when they become too long, so too must my hooves be trimmed. It is simply the smith shaping the hoof, so the new shoe will fit snugly and properly."

Twinkleheels watched, fascinated despite his fear, as Master Brown worked with quick, practiced movements. The rasp, a rough metal file, made a peculiar scraping sound as it smoothed the edges of Ebenezer's hoof. Then, Master Brown went to the forge, where a new horseshoe, glowing a faint cherry red, lay waiting. He picked it up with tongs, and the heat radiating from it was palpable even where Twinkleheels stood.

"Watch closely now, Twinkleheels," Ebenezer advised. "This is an important part."

Master Brown placed the hot shoe against Ebenezer's hoof. There was a sudden, pungent smell, and a thin wisp of smoke curled upwards. Twinkleheels gasped. "He's burning him!" he exclaimed, pulling hard against his halter rope. "Oh, Ebenezer, he's burning your foot!"

"Calm yourself, my dear fellow!" Ebenezer said, his voice perfectly steady. "He is not burning me. Not in the way you mean. The hoof, remember, is dead tissue. It has no feeling. The smith simply touches the hot shoe to the hoof for a very brief moment. This leaves a faint mark, which shows him exactly where the shoe needs to be adjusted. It is like a tailor marking a piece of cloth before he begins to sew. It ensures a perfect fit."

The mare, Daisy, snorted again. "Foolishness! A hot shoe on a hoof is a hot shoe on a hoof. It always makes the air smell like breakfast. Some colts never learn to simply stand still and mind their own business."

Johnnie Green, who had been standing quietly beside his father, gently stroked Twinkleheels' neck. "It's all right, Twinkleheels," he said, his voice soft and reassuring. "Ebenezer knows what he's talking about. Master Brown is very careful."

Twinkleheels took a deep, shuddering breath. He watched as Master Brown took the shoe back to the anvil, hammering it with precise blows until it was just the right shape. The clanging, which had seemed so terrifying before, now took on a rhythm, a sort of song of creation. The smith would hammer, then plunge the shoe into the tub of water, and a cloud of steam would rise, accompanied by that strange hiss that had startled Twinkleheels earlier.

"You see," Ebenezer explained patiently, "the water cools the shoe very quickly. It hardens the metal, too, making it strong enough to protect my hoof from the hard roads."

Finally, Master Brown held up the perfectly shaped shoe. He then took Ebenezer's hoof again, and Twinkleheels saw him pick up a handful of nails. They were slender, sharp, and looked very formidable indeed.

"Now, Twinkleheels," Ebenezer said, "this is the part you asked about earlier. The nails. Watch carefully. You will see that the smith does not drive them into the sensitive part of my foot. He drives them into the outer wall of the hoof, which, as I told you, has no feeling. They will hold the shoe firmly in place."

Twinkleheels squinted, trying to see every detail. Master Brown placed the shoe on Ebenezer's hoof and, with a small hammer, began to tap the nails through. Tap, tap, tap. Twinkleheels could hear the nails going in, but Ebenezer did not flinch, did not even twitch an ear. Then, to Twinkleheels' surprise, the points of the nails emerged from the outside of Ebenezer's hoof, a little way up from the sole.

"He does not drive them through the flesh, you see," Ebenezer said, seeing Twinkleheels' amazed expression. "The nails go through the hard, insentient horn. Then the smith will clip off the points and bend them over, clinching them tightly against the hoof. This is what keeps the shoe from falling off."

And so Master Brown did. He clipped the points, then, with a special tool, bent them over and smoothed them down, making them secure and safe. When he was finished, Ebenezer's hoof looked strong and well-protected, the shiny new shoe gleaming in the dim light of the smithy.

"There you are, old fellow!" Master Brown said, patting Ebenezer's leg. "Good as new!"

Ebenezer took a few steps, testing his new shoe. He flexed his hoof, and a sound that Twinkleheels had never heard before, a crisp, clean clop, clop, echoed on the earthen floor. "Ah, that feels splendid!" Ebenezer declared, clearly pleased. "Thank you, Master Brown. These are a fine fit indeed."

Farmer Green then led Ebenezer to a corner of the smithy, where he could wait for Twinkleheels. Now it was Twinkleheels' turn. His heart began to beat a little faster, a quick, nervous tattoo against his ribs.

Johnnie Green untied Twinkleheels' halter. "Come on, boy," he said, his hand firm on the rope. "It's your turn now. Don't worry, it doesn't hurt. You saw Ebenezer."

Twinkleheels hesitated, his eyes darting towards the glowing forge, the clanging anvil, and the very large, very strong Master Brown. He felt a shiver run through his small frame. "But I am not Ebenezer," he whickered softly. "He is big and brave. And he has done this many times."

"And you will do it many times too, after today," Ebenezer called out encouragingly from his corner. "Every journey begins with a first step, Twinkleheels. And this, my friend, is a very important step indeed

"Every journey begins with a first step, Twinkleheels. And this, my friend, is a very important step indeed."

Despite Ebenezer’s kind words, Twinkleheels stood frozen. His small heart thumped a nervous jig within his ribs. He cast a quick, desperate glance towards Ebenezer, who offered a reassuring nod. Then his gaze flickered back to Master Brown, whose large, calloused hands seemed capable of both great gentleness and formidable strength. The smithy, which had only moments before seemed less terrifying, now loomed large and menacing once more. The air, thick with the scent of burnt horn and coal smoke, prickled at his nostrils. The forge still glowed, a fiery eye watching him from the wall. And the anvil, upon which Master Brown now rested a newly forged, unshaped piece of iron, stood ready to unleash its fearsome clangour. Twinkleheels wanted to bolt. He wanted to whinny for his mother. He wanted to be back in the familiar, soft green pasture, where the worst thing that could happen was a mischievous fly or a sudden summer shower.

Johnnie Green, sensing Twinkleheels' hesitation, gave the halter rope a gentle tug. "Come on, Twinkleheels," he said softly, his voice kind and calm. "You can do it. Master Brown is very careful. He won't hurt you." Farmer Green stepped forward, too, placing a large, comforting hand on Twinkleheels' shoulder. "That's right, little fellow," he said. "It's just like Ebenezer told you. No pain, just a little trim and a shiny new pair of shoes." Twinkleheels took a small, uncertain step forward, then another. He shuffled his small hooves across the rough earth floor, his ears swiveling nervously to catch every sound. He saw the scrubby white mare still being worked on. She lifted her head and snorted, giving him a look that seemed to say, "Well, it's about time you got over yourself." Twinkleheels pretended not to notice her, focusing instead on the sturdy wooden block that Master Brown was now placing near him, clearly intended for his small foot.

Master Brown, with a patience that surprised Twinkleheels, knelt down. He did not speak, but his movements were slow and deliberate. He picked up a curious tool, shaped like a long, curved knife. "Now, little fellow," he rumbled, his voice surprisingly soft for such a big man, "let's see those pretty feet." Twinkleheels flinched as the blacksmith's hand reached towards his foreleg. He pulled his leg back instinctively, shaking his head. "Whoa there, boy," Johnnie said, stroking his neck. "It's alright. Just let him lift your foot." Ebenezer whickered from his corner. "Just relax, Twinkleheels. Let him do his work. It feels strange at first, but it is necessary." Taking a deep breath that was more of a shiver, Twinkleheels tried to obey. He watched as Master Brown gently took hold of his front left leg, bending it at the knee. He could not imagine how he was supposed to keep his balance on three legs, but the blacksmith supported his weight with ease.

The sensation of his hoof being lifted was peculiar, almost ticklish. Master Brown held it firmly between his knees, and Twinkleheels felt a slight trembling in his leg. Then, the blacksmith began to work. He used the curved knife, called a hoof knife, to pare away small slivers of the outer hoof wall. It felt like someone was trimming his fingernails, only on a much grander scale. He could smell the dry, slightly pungent scent of the trimmed horn as it fell to the floor. Then came a tool called a nipper, which clipped away larger pieces of his hoof, shaping it carefully. Twinkleheels could hear the snipping sounds, crisp and clear, but he felt no pain, only the pressure and the strange, new sensation of his hoof being expertly shaped. It was exactly as Ebenezer had described. The outer part of his hoof, the horn, truly had no feeling at all. He started to feel a tiny bit braver.

Next, Master Brown took a large, rough file, or rasp, and began to smooth the edges of his hoof. It made a grating sound that made Twinkleheels wiggle his ear, but the sensation was almost pleasant, like a thorough grooming. He could feel the vibrations running up his leg. When the smith finished, Twinkleheels’ hoof looked cleaner and tidier than it ever had before. It was perfectly round and smooth. "There now," Master Brown murmured, as if to himself. "A very fine little hoof indeed." He then took a small, cold piece of iron and held it against Twinkleheels' hoof, tracing the outline. This was to determine the correct size for his new shoe. Twinkleheels stood patiently, much to his own surprise. He was beginning to understand that this was not a terrible ordeal, but a careful, necessary process.

Master Brown then released Twinkleheels’ foot and walked back to the forge. He took the piece of iron he had selected and plunged it into the glowing coals. The iron hissed and smoked, and soon began to turn a cherry red, then orange, then a brilliant, incandescent yellow. Twinkleheels watched, fascinated despite himself. He had seen fire, of course, in the warm hearth of Farmer Green's kitchen or in the crackling bonfire on chilly autumn evenings. But he had never seen iron heated to such an intense glow. Master Brown pulled the glowing iron from the fire with a pair of long tongs. The heat radiated from it, making Twinkleheels take a step back. Then, with powerful, rhythmic strokes, the smith began to hammer the iron on the anvil. CLANG! CLANG! CLANG! The sound was deafening, echoing off the wooden walls of the smithy. Twinkleheels jumped at the first strike, snorting and trying to pull away. Johnnie held his halter firmly. "It's alright, Twinkleheels," he said, patting his neck. "That's just the sound of the shoe being made."

Ebenezer, from his corner, neighed encouragement. "Don't be afraid, little one. He is shaping your shoe. Soon you will have strong new protection for your feet." Twinkleheels trembled less with each successive clang, though the sound was still startling. He watched, wide-eyed, as the rough piece of iron slowly began to take on the familiar, curved shape of a horseshoe under Master Brown's skilled hands. The smith would hammer, then plunge the iron back into the coals, then hammer again. Each time the iron came out, it was closer to the shape of a perfectly formed shoe. Finally, when the shoe was nearly complete, Master Brown brought it, still glowing hot, back to Twinkleheels. He held it up to Twinkleheels' prepared hoof, not quite touching it. A thin wisp of smoke curled upwards, and Twinkleheels could feel the warmth against his sole. He could even smell the faint scent of something like toast. This was the 'burning on,' as Ebenezer had called it, to ensure a perfect fit.

The shoe cooled quickly, turning from fiery orange to dull grey. Master Brown plunged it into the tub of water, and Twinkleheels heard the familiar sizzle and saw the cloud of steam. The little white mare snorted again, shaking her head. "Such a fuss over a simple shoeing," she mumbled, but her tone was less sharp now, more resigned. Twinkleheels ignored her, his attention fixed on Master Brown. The smith now took the cooled shoe, which had been perfectly molded to the unique contours of Twinkleheels’ hoof, and picked up the nails. They were exactly like the ones Twinkleheels had seen earlier, small and sharp. A knot of apprehension tightened in Twinkleheels’ stomach. This was the part he had dreaded most. He closed his eyes for a moment, remembering Ebenezer’s words. *He drives them into the outer wall of the hoof, which has no feeling.*

Master Brown, with the same gentle precision, lifted Twinkleheels' hoof again. He carefully positioned the new shoe. Then, with a small, light hammer, he began to tap the nails through. Tap, tap, tap. The sound was soft, almost delicate, compared to the earlier clanging. Twinkleheels held his breath. He braced himself for a sharp stab, a sudden pain, but there was nothing. Only the gentle pressure of the hammer, the faint vibration as the nails passed through the horn. He dared to open his eyes. He saw the nails appearing, just as they had on Ebenezer’s hoof, a little way up from the sole on the outer wall. Not a drop of blood, not a flicker of discomfort. Twinkleheels felt a wave of relief wash over him, so profound that his knees almost buckled. He was doing it. He was actually doing it, and it didn't hurt at all!

One by one, the nails went in, until the shoe was firmly attached. Then Master Brown took his nippers and clipped off the protruding points of the nails. Finally, with a special tool, he bent them over, pressing them tightly against the hoof wall. This was called 'clinching,' and it made the shoe truly secure. He rasped over the clinched nails, smoothing them down so there were no sharp edges. When he was finished, Twinkleheels’ front left hoof felt strangely heavier, yet also incredibly solid. The new shoe, still a dull grey but beginning to shine, looked strong and purposeful.

"There's one," Master Brown said, patting Twinkleheels' leg. "Now for the others." Twinkleheels felt a surge of pride. He had faced his fear, and he had conquered it. He didn't even mind when Master Brown lifted his other feet. Each time, the process was the same. The trimming, the shaping, the heating of the iron, the hammering, the fitting, and finally, the nailing. With each new shoe, Twinkleheels grew more confident. By the time the fourth shoe was being applied, he stood almost calmly, watching the proceedings with an intelligent, interested gaze. He was a veteran now, a creature of experience, no longer the timid colt who had shied at the threshold.

When all four shoes were on, Master Brown set Twinkleheels’ last hoof down. "There you are, little fellow," he said, wiping his hands on a leather apron. "Ready to face the world." Twinkleheels took a tentative step. CLOP! The sound was crisp and clear, echoing in the smithy. He took another step. CLOP! CLOP! It was a marvelous sound, unlike anything he had ever made before. It felt as if he had been given a new set of feet, strong and resilient. He lifted his head, tossing his mane slightly, and looked at Ebenezer, his eyes shining. "Oh, Ebenezer!" he whinnied, a joyous sound. "It feels wonderful! It truly doesn't hurt a bit!"

Ebenezer chuckled, a deep, rumbling sound. "I told you so, didn't I, Twinkleheels? Now you are truly a horse, ready for any road." The scrubby white mare, who had been watching his transformation with a sort of grudging respect, gave a small nod. "Well," she said, her voice less harsh now, "he does look rather smart, I suppose. Not quite so much like a little child anymore." Twinkleheels felt a warm glow of satisfaction spread through him. Even the stern old mare approved!

Farmer Green paid Master Brown, while Johnnie Green unhitched Ebenezer from the buggy. Then he brought Twinkleheels’ halter. "Ready to go home, brave boy?" he asked, stroking Twinkleheels' nose. Twinkleheels whickered his agreement, his hooves making a confident clatter on the floor as he turned. Johnnie led him out of the smithy, Ebenezer following close behind, and Farmer Green brought up the rear. The sunlight, streaming into the dusty building, seemed even brighter as they emerged.

Outside, Twinkleheels felt a new spring in his step. The road, which had been a rough track before, now felt solid and firm beneath his shod hooves. The metallic clop, clop, clop was a constant, satisfying rhythm accompanying his every movement. He trotted proudly behind the buggy, Ebenezer pulling it steadily, his own new shoes making their own reassuring sound. Twinkleheels almost felt as if he could keep up with Bright and Broad now, even in a deep snowdrift. He glanced at Ebenezer, whose pace was, as always, unhurried and steady. "It feels so different!" Twinkleheels exclaimed, as they passed a particularly stony patch of road. "I used to feel every single pebble. Now it's just smooth and solid!"

"That is the wonder of a good pair of shoes, my friend," Ebenezer replied, his voice calm and wise. "They protect your feet from the harshness of the ground, and they give you confidence. You will find you can go many places now, places you might have shied away from before." Twinkleheels considered this. It was true. The road ahead, stretching out towards home, no longer looked daunting. Instead, it seemed to invite him to explore, to travel, to put his new shoes to the test. He imagined himself galloping across the fields, no longer worrying about sharp stones or rough ground. He thought of helping Farmer Green in the fields, perhaps even pulling a small cart, just like Ebenezer.

The journey back to the farm seemed to pass much more quickly than the journey to the smithy. Perhaps it was because Twinkleheels was no longer filled with dread, but with a newfound pride and a sense of accomplishment. The familiar sights of the countryside, the budding trees, the distant farmhouse, and the rolling hills, all seemed to take on a new clarity, as if his new shoes had also sharpened his perception. As they neared the farm, he spotted old dog Spot, lying near the gate, his tail thumping a slow welcome.

"Did you get your new shoes?" Spot barked, wagging his tail more enthusiastically now. "I heard a lot of clanging all the way over here!" "I did!" Twinkleheels whinnied, lifting a foot slightly to display his gleaming shoe. "And it didn't hurt at all! It feels wonderful! I can clop-clop now, just like Ebenezer!" Spot barked approvingly. "Good for you, Twinkleheels! Now you're a proper grown-up horse." That made Twinkleheels feel even taller.

Soon, they were back in the barn. Johnnie unhitched Ebenezer and led Twinkleheels back to his stall. As Twinkleheels stepped onto the soft straw, his hooves made a muted clunk, clunk, clunk. The sound was still there, but softer, absorbed by the bedding. "Well, little adventurer," Ebenezer said, as he settled into his own stall, shaking his head slightly. "You had quite an experience today, didn't you?"

"I did!" Twinkleheels exclaimed. "And it was not nearly as frightening as I thought it would be. All those noises, the fire, the hammering... I was so silly to be afraid." He pawed at the ground, making a satisfying clunk. "I feel so strong now, Ebenezer. And so ready to go anywhere!" "That is the mark of true courage, Twinkleheels," Ebenezer said wisely. "Not to be unafraid, but to face your fears despite them. And you did that today, very bravely indeed."

Twinkleheels felt a warm flush spread through him. Praise from Ebenezer, the wise old horse, was indeed high praise. He looked down at his shining new shoes, still feeling the novel sensation of their solid presence. He thought of the mare at the smithy, her initial scorn, and her eventual grudging approval. He thought of Farmer Green's kind words, and Johnnie's steady hand. And he thought of the wide, open road, and the many adventures that now awaited him, all because of these four small, strong pieces of iron. He was no longer just Twinkleheels, the small pony. He was Twinkleheels, the pony with shoes, ready for whatever journey lay ahead. He was ready to step high, indeed. He could hardly wait for tomorrow, and the chance to test his new capabilities. He would show them all. He would show the other ponies, and even Bright and Broad, that he, Twinkleheels, was now a horse to be reckoned with. The thought made him whinny softly, a sound of contented anticipation, as he settled down for a well-earned rest. He knew, deep in his heart, that this was only the beginning of many new discoveries and many new journeys for him and his fine new shoes.

The first rays of dawn, filtered through the dusty window of the barn, found Twinkleheels already awake. He had scarcely slept a wink, so filled was he with the excitement of his new shoes and the promise of a day to come. He stood for a long moment, simply feeling the firm, cool iron against his hooves. It was a novel sensation, a comforting weight that made him feel grounded and, somehow, taller. He took a tentative step, then another, listening to the soft clunk, clunk, clunk his new shoes made against the wooden floor of his stall. It was a different sound from the quiet patter of his unshod hooves, a sound that spoke of purpose and readiness.

Old Ebenezer, stirred by the faint sounds, lifted his head from his manger. "Awake so early, young one?" he murmured, his voice a low rumble. "One would think you were off to the village to deliver the mail, rather than waiting for Johnnie Green to fetch the feed."

"I am awake, Ebenezer," Twinkleheels replied, a youthful eagerness in his tone. "I can hardly wait for the day to begin. I want to try out my new shoes. I want to walk, and trot, and perhaps even gallop a little. I want to feel what it is like to have the road beneath me, knowing I am protected."

Ebenezer chuckled softly, a sound like dry leaves rustling. "Ah, youth and new shoes! A potent combination. Remember, Twinkleheels, it is not just the shoes that make the journey, but the steady head and the willing heart that guide them."

"I know," Twinkleheels said, though he was not entirely certain he did know. His heart was so full of gladness that he felt he could conquer any road, any path, any hill, simply because he now had shoes. He imagined the other ponies, and even Bright and Broad, looking at him with new respect. He would be able to go where they went, perhaps even better. The thought sent a thrill through him, making his ears twitch with anticipation.

Soon, Johnnie Green arrived, whistling a cheerful tune. He brought the morning hay and fresh water. As he went about his chores, he paused by Twinkleheels' stall. "Well, Twinkleheels, how do those new shoes feel this morning?" he asked, running a hand down the pony's sleek neck.

Twinkleheels nudged Johnnie’s hand, a silent affirmation. He couldn't speak in human words, of course, but his bright eyes and eager stance conveyed his message clearly. Johnnie seemed to understand.

"He's quite pleased with himself, isn't he, Father?" Johnnie called out to Farmer Green, who was just entering the barn with a milk pail in hand.

Farmer Green smiled. "He certainly is. A good pair of shoes can do wonders for one's spirits, and one's footing, too. We'll have to see if they make him any less prone to sticking in snow drifts," he added with a twinkle in his eye, remembering yesterday's incident.

Twinkleheels dipped his head slightly, as if in agreement. He certainly hoped his new shoes would prevent such embarrassments in the future. He felt much more capable now, more ready to face the world with his confident clunk, clunk.

After breakfast, Farmer Green came back to the barn. "Johnnie," he said, "I need you to take a basket of fresh eggs and some butter over to Mrs. Peterson's house. Her cow is ailing, and she'll be needing these supplies. The lane to her house can be a bit muddy this time of year, especially after the snow melt, so take Twinkleheels. His new shoes will serve him well on that path."

Twinkleheels' ears perked up. A trip! And a real task! This was exactly what he had been hoping for. He would show everyone what a fine, capable pony he was, now that he was properly shod. His chest swelled with importance.

Johnnie, too, seemed pleased. "All right, Father," he said, and went to fetch Twinkleheels' saddle and bridle. He hummed softly as he worked, a reflection of his own contentment.

As Johnnie led him out of the barn, Twinkleheels trotted with a lively step, his new shoes making a pleasing rhythm on the hard packed earth of the farmyard. Old Ebenezer whinnied a farewell from his stall, his voice carrying a note of seasoned advice. "Be careful on the muddy paths, young friend!" he called out. "And remember what I told you. Shoes are an aid, not a substitute for good sense. Pay attention to the ground beneath you."

"I will, Ebenezer!" Twinkleheels called back, feeling a surge of confidence. He was ready for anything, his spirit buoyed by the crisp morning air and the prospect of adventure.
