He fumbled blindly with his outstretched hands, feeling the cold, smooth plaster of the wall, then the textured surface of wallpaper, seeking a doorframe, a window casing, anything that might signify a way out.

The air in the bed-chamber was close and warm, thick with the faint, cloying scent of lavender and dust, a perfume of disuse, of forgotten things. The darkness here was not merely the absence of light; it was a physical weight, pressing against his eyes, muffling his movements, and transforming the familiar contours of what he knew to be a gentleman’s bed-chamber into a labyrinth of unyielding shadows and treacherous angles.

He knew this flat, or at least its general arrangement, from long weeks of patient, meticulous observation, from stolen glances through windows veiled by lace and dusk, from casual conversations with the building’s charwoman, a garrulous soul eager to share the peccadilloes and peculiarities of her tenants for the price of a gin and an attentive ear. He knew where the tall wardrobe stood, a silent sentinel against the far wall, and the dressing-table with its silver-backed brushes and crystal decanters. But knowing and seeing were two profoundly different matters when plunged into this absolute, Stygian blackness. A flicker, brighter this time, from the study. A match had caught, then held. Not the steady, unwavering glow of a lamp, but a small, tentative beacon, dancing erratically, revealing for the briefest instant the angular shadows of the desk, the gleam of brass, the ghostly pallor of the clock’s face. He could hear, too, the muttered imprecations of Maitland, low and gruff, the sound of a man thwarted but not yet defeated. Then, a deeper glow, a more resolute light, swelling into being. The brass student lamp. Maitland had managed it. The green shade, no doubt, would cast a concentrated pool of illumination upon the desk, leaving the rest of the study in a relative, if less profound, shadow. Yet even that partial light was a mortal danger to Elias, for it would surely spill into the doorway, revealing the bed-chamber’s portal, and perhaps, the figure of its unwelcome occupant.

He had no time to waste, no moments to squander in philosophical introspection or self-pity. His mission, so carefully planned, so boldly executed, depended upon his swift, silent disappearance. He was a shadow, a whisper, a thing that should not be, and his only hope lay in maintaining that ethereal, intangible quality. His hands moved with renewed purpose, feeling along the velvet drapes. They were indeed the window hangings, heavy, dust-laden, smelling faintly of old lace and mothballs. He could feel the cold glass of the windowpane beneath them, a blessed indication of an external boundary. It was a poor hiding place, a desperate one, chosen in haste and fear, but it was all that was immediately available. His heart pounded a furious rhythm against his ribs, threatening to burst from its cage. Every muscle in his body was tensed, ready for flight, ready for confrontation, yet paradoxically held in a rigid stasis, praying for invisibility.

From the study, a grunt of satisfaction. A heavy sigh, the sound of a large man settling himself. Then the distinct creak of a chair, one of the heavy leather-cushioned easy chairs, no doubt. Elias strained his ears, picturing the scene, every detail gleaned from his patient surveillance now flashing before his mind’s eye. Maitland would be at the desk, perhaps surveying the room with an air of proprietary annoyance, his temper still simmering from his nocturnal fumbling. Would he notice anything amiss? The disturbed lamp, the slightly askew books, the general sense of disorder that always accompanied the work of an amateur such as Elias? He doubted it. Maitland was a man of routine, a creature of habit, but not, Elias suspected, a man of keen observation when roused from his comfortable complacency. His mind was likely still fuming over the inconvenience, not actively seeking evidence of an intrusion. This was Elias’s slender advantage, the narrow margin of error upon which his very life now depended.

A low growl, a sound of profound irritation, emanated from the study, followed by a heavy thump. Elias flinched, his body recoiling instinctively, though he knew the sound was not directed at him, not yet. It was the sound of Maitland discovering the arrangement on his desk, the inverted bowl, the superimposed book, the silk glove draped with such artistic flourish. A moment of silence then, thick and pregnant with unspoken questions. Elias imagined Maitland’s thick brows furrowing, his jowly face a mask of puzzled indignation. What would he make of such an odd tableau? A child’s prank? The absent-mindedness of a servant? Or, a more sinister interpretation, the calling card of a trespasser? The latter thought sent a fresh wave of icy fear through Elias, chilling him to the bone. He had underestimated the female, her whimsical nature, her peculiar brand of artistry even in the midst of her work. Her intention, he knew, had been to draw attention, to signal, to leave a message for Maitland, but in doing so, she had unwittingly raised the alarm for any other intruders, himself included. He cursed her silently, fervently, beneath his breath, though he knew the fault was not hers, but his own for being caught in such a perilous confluence of events.

He heard the distinct sound of the bowl being lifted, then the clink of metal against wood as the cigarette case and canvas bag were discovered. A sharp intake of breath from Maitland, a sound of genuine surprise, followed by a low, thoughtful hum. The jewels. They had found the jewels. Elias’s body sagged infinitesimally against the wall, a fractional release of tension, a desperate sliver of hope. So, that was her game. The return of stolen property. A dangerous, audacious maneuver, one worthy of grudging admiration, even from a rival in this nocturnal game of chance. But her audacity had complicated his own delicate mission immeasurably. Now Maitland was fully alert, his senses heightened, his mind no longer simply irritated but actively engaged in deciphering the meaning of the intrusion.

The next sound was the rustle of paper, followed by another low murmur. Maitland, no doubt, was examining the contents of the canvas bag, perhaps counting, verifying. Elias could picture the scene in vivid detail, Maitland’s heavy fingers fumbling with the precious stones, his eyes, now adjusted to the lamplight, gleaming with a mixture of relief and indignation. This was his chance, perhaps his only chance. While Maitland was distracted by the returned jewels, Elias needed to move. He needed to find his target, complete his task, and disappear into the night as silently as he had arrived. But where was his target? The object of his quest was not jewels, but information, a specific ledger, a small, leather-bound volume known only to a select few, said to contain details that would ruin Maitland utterly. It was his reason for being here, his singular purpose, a mission that had weighed upon him for months, driving him to this desperate act.

He shifted minutely, testing the security of his hiding place, gauging the sounds from the study. Maitland was still absorbed, muttering to himself, the clinking of the jewels a regular, almost hypnotic sound. He was no longer a crab-like sidle, but a ghost, a whisper of motion, every fiber of his being focused on silence, on stealth. He felt his way around the four-poster bed, his hands brushing against the heavy velvet hangings, the smooth, cool wood of the bedposts. He kept his head low, his body bent, a hunted creature seeking refuge. The room, though dark, now offered a slight advantage. The dense hangings, the heavy furniture, the very lack of clear lines, provided cover. He could use the bulk of the bed, the shadow cast by the wardrobe, to obscure his movements.

His fingers, still outstretched, found the solid, cold surface of the wardrobe door. A moment of hesitation. Could he open it, slip inside, without making a sound? The risk was immense, the reward considerable. From within the wardrobe, he might have a clearer view, a more secure hiding place, and perhaps, a path to the inner sanctum of Maitland’s private papers, which he knew, from his intelligence, were kept not in the study desk, but in a hidden compartment within the wardrobe itself. It was a heavy piece, of dark, polished wood, the kind that creaked and groaned with age, the kind that might betray him with a single misplaced sound. The darkness within was absolute, a darkness that pressed upon his eyeballs, a tangible weight that threatened to crush him. He was encased, entombed, a prisoner in a wooden box, yet paradoxically, he felt a fleeting sense of security. He was hidden, for the moment, from Maitland’s gaze.

He pressed himself against the back wall of the wardrobe, his hand once more questing, searching for the hidden compartment. His information had been precise, painstakingly acquired. Time was a luxury he could ill afford, yet he dared not rush, for a hasty movement, a fumbled touch, could trigger a creak, a snap, a sound that would carry through the thin wood of the wardrobe and into the study, alerting Maitland to his presence. He imagined the old man, his face flushed with anger, his eyes keen with suspicion, striding into the bed-chamber, flinging open the wardrobe door to expose him, like a trapped rat, to the pitiless glare of the lamplight. Had he noticed something amiss? The footsteps moved closer to the doorway, hesitated, then seemed to turn away. A sigh of profound relief escaped Elias, a breath he hadn’t realized he had been holding. This was the weapon he needed, the instrument of justice he had vowed to wield against the man who had brought ruin to his family, who had orchestrated the downfall of his father, stripping him of his fortune, his reputation, and ultimately, his will to live. The distinct Irish brogue, which the woman had heard, suddenly made sense. There was more than one intruder, or perhaps, the woman had been right, and Maitland had brought company. The line of light under the door. The voices, excitedly keyed. Maitland and his company, whoever they were, had clearly settled in for a long discussion, perhaps a celebration of the jewels’ return, a debriefing of the night’s events. He was acutely aware of his own rapid breathing, the frantic drumming of his heart, sounds that seemed amplified in the suffocating silence of the wardrobe. He pressed his ear against the thin wooden back panel, trying to decipher the conversation, to extract any scrap of information that might aid his escape.

The Irish voice, loud and expansive, dominated the exchange. “And the lass, ye say she escaped? After all that excitement?”

“Ah, but ye didn’t catch her, then,” the Irishman observed, a hint of amusement in his tone. “A shame, that. I’d have liked to meet the woman who could outwit old Maitland.”
A sharp, almost resentful retort from Maitland, punctuated by the clinking of ice in a glass.

Elias closed his eyes, his mind racing. So, the woman had indeed escaped. Or at least, Maitland thought she had. This was good news for her, but offered no immediate relief for him. He was still in the lion’s den, and the lions were now gathered, vigilant and alert. He wondered about the “clear enunciation” the woman had heard, the second voice. The current conversation seemed to be dominated by the Irishman and Maitland. Where was the third man? Or was it just two? His intelligence had suggested Maitland rarely entertained at home, especially at such an hour. This confluence of events was entirely unexpected, a brutal twist of fate.

He leaned against the wardrobe wall, trying to conserve his energy, to control the trembling in his limbs. His fingers instinctively sought the ledger in his pocket, reassuring himself of its presence, of the success he had thus far achieved. He had risked everything for this, for this single, small book. The memory of his father’s defeated face, the hollow eyes, the broken spirit, fueled his resolve, steeling his determination against the rising tide of fear. He was not merely a thief; he was an avenger, a messenger of retribution. He would not fail. He could not.

The conversation in the study drifted, now touching on matters of finance, of shipping routes, of political machinations in the city council. Maitland, in his cups, seemed to grow more expansive, his voice less guarded, his pronouncements more boastful. The Irishman, whose name Elias gathered was O’Connell, listened with a flattering attentiveness, interjecting with timely agreements and appreciative murmurs. This was the true Maitland, the ruthless financier, the puppeteer of men’s fates, the architect of his father’s ruin. Listening to him now, his voice thick with self-satisfaction, Elias felt a cold anger coalesce within him, hardening his resolve, sharpening his focus.

Hours seemed to stretch into an eternity. The clock on the mantelpiece in the study, its pallid face now clearly visible from the bed-chamber doorway, chimed the quarter hour, then the half, then the full hour. Midnight had long since passed. The early hours of morning were creeping upon them, bringing with them the faint, distant sounds of the city beginning to stir, a slow, grudging awakening. The voices in the study, though still present, had grown softer, more slurred. O’Connell’s booming laughter had subsided into a contented chuckle, Maitland’s gruff pronouncements into a repetitive drone. They were growing sleepy, their vigilance dulled by drink and the late hour. This was his opportunity.

He needed to get out, to slip through the bed-chamber and the hall, and through the outer door. He knew the building’s layout, the winding staircase, the quiet fire-escape at the back, the very one the woman had sought. He had considered it an escape route, but now, with the jewels returned and Maitland alert, he wondered if it was still viable. He had seen the fire-escape from his own observations, noted its rusted rungs, its precarious angle. It would be a dangerous descent, but preferable to being caught.

He listened intently, straining to catch any subtle change in the rhythm of the conversation, any indication that one of them might be rising, moving towards the bed-chamber, or perhaps, towards the main exit. There was a prolonged silence, broken only by the heavy breathing of Maitland, and a low, almost tuneless whistle from O’Connell. Elias held his breath, his heart a frantic drum against his ribs. Was it over? Had they finally succumbed to sleep?

Then, the unmistakable creak of a chair. A heavy sigh. A shuffling of feet. Maitland. He was moving. Elias pressed himself further into the wardrobe, trying to become one with the wood, with the shadows. He heard the footsteps approaching the bed-chamber doorway, hesitant, uncertain, then receding. Maitland was merely stretching, perhaps, or going to fetch another drink. The sounds continued, a series of fumbled movements, a low curse. He was still in the study.

Elias had to decide. Wait indefinitely, risking discovery should either man decide to use the bed-chamber for any reason, or make his move now, while they were groggy, their senses dulled by drink. The latter seemed the lesser of two evils. He eased the wardrobe door open, again, with the agonizing slowness he had mastered, creating a sliver of an opening, just enough for him to peer out. The bed-chamber was still bathed in oppressive gloom, but a faint, diffuse light now spilled from the study, painting the doorway in a soft, hazy glow. He could see the threshold, the edge of the study carpet, the very rim of the lamplight’s reach.

He slipped out of the wardrobe, a shadow detaching itself from a deeper shadow, and moved like liquid silence across the thick carpet. He navigated the four-poster bed, using its massive bulk as cover, his eyes fixed on the doorway, on the sliver of light that promised danger and freedom in equal measure. He reached the edge of the bed, crouching low, peering around the heavy velvet hangings.

Maitland was slumped in one of the easy chairs, his head tilted back, his mouth slightly agape, a half-empty glass resting precariously on his chest. He was not asleep, not quite, but he was certainly in a state of profound drowsiness, his eyes half-closed, his breathing heavy and regular. O’Connell, meanwhile, was at the towering mahogany bookcase, his back to Elias, his fingers running along the spines of the books, seemingly engrossed in a silent perusal, or perhaps merely awaiting Maitland’s full awakening. The lamplight cast his figure in long, distorted shadows against the far wall, a looming, silent sentinel.

This was it. His chance. He had to cross the expanse of the study, a journey of mere feet, but one fraught with peril. He could not risk disturbing O’Connell, whose back might be turned, but whose senses, dulled as they might be by drink, were still those of an Irishman, famously sharp and quick to anger. And Maitland, though nearly insensible, was a heavy man, a presence that could awaken with a sudden jolt, a loud snore, a dropped glass.

Elias took a deep breath, steeling his nerves. He moved. With an almost superhuman lightness, he launched himself from the cover of the bed-chamber, a dark blur against the diffused light. His feet made no sound on the rug, his body a silent projectile aimed at the hall. He kept his head low, his gaze fixed straight ahead, avoiding even a glance at the two men, lest his gaze be caught, lest his very presence be felt. He was halfway across the study when it happened. A small, almost imperceptible sound, a slight shift in weight, as O’Connell, still at the bookcase, turned his head, a slow, deliberate movement, his eyes, bleary but still capable of perception, sweeping across the room.

Elias froze, his body locked in a rictus of terror, caught in the middle of a stride, one foot poised in mid-air. He was exposed, silhouetted against the dim glow, a stark figure of illicit purpose. Time seemed to stretch, to warp, each second

 a monstrous, leaden thing, burdened with the weight of utter, absolute discovery. Elias hung there, a creature of the dark suddenly caught in a shaft of unwelcome illumination, his heart a frantic drum against his ribs, his lungs aching for a breath he dared not take. O'Connell's head, an indistinct blur of shadow against the deeper gloom of the bookcase, remained tilted; his eyes, though bleared by the evening's copious libations, were undeniably fixed upon the space where Elias hovered, a silent, immobile statue of fear. There was no immediate outcry, no sudden, drunken lunge, only that agonizing, unblinking stare, a gaze that seemed to pierce the very core of Elias's being, stripping him bare of all pretense and purpose. For an eternity, or what felt like one, the two men held this terrifying tableau, the intruder suspended between flight and capture, the discoverer suspended between comprehension and the fog of alcohol. Maitland, blessedly, remained undisturbed, a heavy, resonant snore issuing softly from his half-open mouth, a testament to the powerful draughts of whiskey he had consumed. It was the only sound in the suffocating stillness, a counterpoint to the thunder of Elias's own pulse, a cruel reminder of the oblivion he so desperately craved for himself and the very men who now threatened his liberty, perhaps his life.

The world contracted to this single, unbearable moment, yet within its compressed duration, Elias's mind raced with a terrible, crystalline clarity. O'Connell had seen him; there was no denying the directness,

his mind, of the accusation that hung unspoken in that unwavering gaze.

O’Connell’s eyes, though swollen and bloodshot, held a spark of comprehension, a flicker of something beyond mere inebriation; it was the slow, dawning recognition of an inexplicable, alarming presence, a shadow where there should be none, a figure alien to the familiar contours of Maitland’s study. He could not move, dared not breathe, lest the smallest sound, the faintest tremor, shatter the fragile membrane of uncertainty that still, impossibly, shielded him from outright confrontation. The air grew thick, charged with an electric tension that made the hairs prickle on his neck; the silence, save for Maitland’s rhythmic snore, was a suffocating shroud, pressing down upon Elias’s chest with an unbearable weight. Then, with a suddenness that jolted Elias’s overwrought nerves, O’Connell’s head, which had been fixed in that petrifying stare, began to droop. His eyelids, heavy with drink and weariness, fought a losing battle against the encroaching tide of slumber, flickering once, twice, before surrendering to the irresistible pull of unconsciousness. The spark of recognition died, extinguished by the potent alchemy of whiskey and the late hour. His head lolled against the high back of the leather-cushioned easy chair, his chin sinking to his chest, and a soft, guttural sigh escaped his lips, dissolving into the renewed, unchallenged dominance of Maitland’s stentorian snores. O’Connell had lost the fight; the momentary perception had been but a brief, alcohol-induced hallucination, a fleeting phantom conjured by a mind teetering on the precipice of oblivion. Elias watched, mesmerized by the slow-motion surrender, his heart still hammering against his ribs like a trapped bird, his breath still caught in his throat, a suffocating knot of pure terror. It was not belief, but a desperate, almost insane hope, that held him in place, unable to trust the evidence of his own eyes, unable to credit the staggering, improbable stroke of luck that had just, in that very instant, snatched him from the gaping maw of discovery.

Slowly, painfully, with an almost imperceptible shift of weight, Elias lowered his suspended foot to the thick pile of the rug. The silence, so recently menacing, now seemed fragile, pregnant with the possibility of renewed peril. Each movement was deliberate, measured, a ballet of stealth born of years of clandestine practice, though never, he conceded, under such acutely paralyzing circumstances. He felt the cold sweat trickling down his spine, a testament to the ordeal, and the trembling in his limbs was not easily subdued, a persistent echo of the fear that had so recently gripped him. He had been seen, beyond doubt, but mercifully, incomprehensibly, not truly perceived. It was a reprieve, a stay of execution granted by the very vice that had led Maitland and O’Connell to this state of somnolence, a testament to the unpredictable whims of fortune. He had been presented with a second chance, a fragile window of opportunity, which must be seized with unhesitating, unthinking resolve. Hesitation now would be an act of profound, unforgivable folly.

His destination, the ornate mahogany desk, loomed through the gloom, a dark, imposing rectangle where the lamp’s illumination had recently fallen. It was there, or so his intelligence indicated, that Maitland kept certain documents, papers which held the key to Elias’s own present predicament, a web of unfortunate circumstances from which he desperately sought extrication. The jewels, mentioned so obliquely in the fragmented intelligence he possessed, were of secondary, if not tertiary, concern; his true quarry lay within the desk, locked away, he presumed, behind a series of intricate mechanisms designed to foil all but the most determined, or the most authorized, of hands.

The study, which had seemed so innocuously inviting to the former visitor, now presented itself to Elias as a treacherous labyrinth of potential hazards, each piece of furniture a silent conspirator against his purpose. The divans, the heavy leather-cushioned easy chairs, the tall clock with its pallid staring face (whose insistent tick now seemed to mock the agonizing slowness of time’s passage), the small tables and tabourettes, the towering, old-fashioned mahogany book-case, from which O’Connell had so nearly plucked him from the shadows, and the useless, ornamental, beautiful Chippendale escritoire, lurking in its corner,  all seemed imbued with a sinister, watchful sentience. The very air, still faintly redolent of cigar smoke and stale whiskey, seemed to vibrate with untold dangers. The scent of the extinguished student lamp, a faint reek of hot metal and spent oil, hung stubbornly in the air, a potential betrayer.

He moved, again, with a fluidity that belied his inner turmoil, his body seeming to glide rather than walk, his movements an exquisite study in controlled velocity. He was a phantom, an intruder utterly without substance, existing only in the brief, trembling interval between one shadow and the next. The journey across the study, though only a matter of feet, felt like a monumental traversal, a perilous crossing of an uncharted sea, each step a gamble, each breath a prayer. He reached the desk, finally, his fingers brushing against the cold, polished surface, a tactile affirmation of his arrival, a small victory in a night fraught with peril.

The brass student lamp, its green shade now dark and silent, stood like a blind Cyclops, its single eye extinguished, a monument to the girl’s hasty departure. Elias’s memory, trained to absorb and retain every detail of his surroundings, immediately registered the odd arrangement of objects where the lamplight had once been: an inverted bowl, a book superimposed upon it, and a silk glove draped artfully across the book. It was an arrangement too deliberate, too calculatedly peculiar to be accidental. A ripple of professional curiosity, cold and detached, momentarily superseded the fear that had been his constant companion. Such an idiosyncratic tableau spoke of a prior presence, another intruder, perhaps, whose purpose was as clandestine, as illicit, as his own. The thought, while unsettling, also offered a strange kind of reassurance; he was not alone in this nocturnal theatre of shadows and secrets. There was another player, whose unseen movements might either complicate or simplify his own desperate mission.

The girl, then, had not been seeking to *steal* but to *hide* or *return* them, a thought that added another layer of intrigue to the already complex tapestry of the night. He noted their position, mentally cataloguing them for later consideration, but his primary objective remained the desk itself.

This was an astonishing stroke of luck, an act of sheer, unlooked-for beneficence from the capricious hand of fate. The girl, he presumed, had dropped them, or perhaps Maitland himself in his drunken stupor. It mattered not how they had come to be there; their presence was a godsend, an answer to an unspoken prayer. To fumble with picks, to risk the minute, tell-tale sounds of their operation, was a precarious gamble he had been prepared to take, but this, this was certainty. This was the swift, silent, elegant solution. His heart, which had just begun to settle, now thumped with a renewed, excited rhythm.

Yet, even as relief washed over him, a peculiar hesitation, a fleeting shadow of discomfort, flickered across his mind. There was a certain dishonor, a lack of professional elegance, in using the very tools of the intended victim against him. His own skeleton keys, honed by years of practice, were an extension of his skill, a testament to his unique abilities. To use another’s keys, to simply unlock a door, felt almost… vulgar. It was a fleeting, illogical qualm, quickly dismissed by the harsh realities of his situation. He was not here for honor, nor for the aesthetics of his craft; he was here for survival, for necessity, for the ruthless pursuit of his objective. All’s fair in war, indeed, and this was a war, waged against time, against discovery, against the unforgiving circumstances that had driven him to this clandestine foray. He tightened his grip on the silver ring, the cold metal a stark, unapologetic reminder of his purpose.

He began his systematic exploration of the desk. The drawers, one by one, yielded to the eager keys, their mechanisms responding with satisfying clicks that were, miraculously, almost inaudible in the suffocating stillness of the room. He worked with a swift, methodical efficiency, his senses acutely attuned to every rustle of his clothing, every faint creak of the old furniture, every breath of the two slumbering men who lay but a few feet away. His eyes, accustomed to the low light, swiftly scanned the contents of each drawer, discerning the familiar clutter of a professional man’s private workspace: ledgers, correspondence, an occasional photograph, a discarded pen, a half-empty bottle of ink. Nothing of immediate value, nothing that hinted at the papers he sought. The methodical sifting through Maitland’s personal effects felt, in its own way, a violation, a trespass beyond the mere physical entry, but he hardened his heart against the fleeting pang of conscience. He had to be thorough; no stone could remain unturned, no corner unexamined. His very future depended upon this night’s desperate endeavor.

He worked swiftly, yet without haste, a master of controlled urgency. He encountered no false bottoms, no hidden compartments of the obvious variety, only the straightforward, if voluminous, contents of a man’s private papers. Each drawer, once examined, was meticulously closed, the keys withdrawn, the lock re-engaged, leaving no trace of his intrusion save for the subtle disturbance of the papers themselves, a disorder he quickly rectified, striving for an illusion of untouched repose.

This was the target, the one he had been told to find, the repository of the information he so desperately required. The complexity of the situation, the tangled web of betrayals and countermeasures, presented itself to Elias with a sudden, jarring clarity. He had been sent by a third party, a powerful individual with an interest in Maitland’s affairs, who clearly knew of the jewels and their history, but whose instructions had been deliberately ambiguous, allowing Elias to interpret and act as circumstances dictated. No one, not even Maitland himself, would suspect its secret had been disturbed.

His next task was his exit, a journey across the same treacherous landscape he had so stealthily traversed moments before. He needed to retrace his steps, to slip back into the bed-chamber, and thence to the outer world, without disturbing the delicate balance of slumber and silence. He felt a fleeting, almost theatrical sense of completion, of a scene perfectly played.
