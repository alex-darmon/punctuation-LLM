The last carriage had long since rattled away down the cobbled lane, its sound dying into the damp, chill quiet that always preceded the dawn, and Miss Evelyn Thorne, poised in the deep shadow of the old elm, knew her moment was at hand. She had waited for this hush, for the absolute stillness of the house, every light extinguished save for a faint, solitary glow from the staff’s quarters at the rear, a glow that would soon vanish with the last weary turn of the night watchman’s key. She navigated it all by instinct, a memory of the house’s layout gleaned from months of surreptitious observation, her heart thrumming a low, insistent rhythm against her ribs. Her destination was the study, Mr. Finch’s private domain, a sanctum rarely disturbed by daylight, much less by a visitor of her clandestine intent. Up the back stairs she glided, each step measured, each creak of the old wood anticipated and avoided, her gloved hand brushing lightly against the cold banister, her senses acutely aware of every rustle, every distant sigh of the wind.

The study door, a heavy expanse of dark oak, stood ajar, as she had hoped, a sliver of deeper darkness against the faintly illuminated hall. Her objective was the small, intricately carved rosewood box, often kept locked in the central drawer, containing, she was almost certain, the very item that had brought her to this desperate venture: her grandmother’s locket, a piece of family history unjustly held.

A tremor of hesitation passed through her, a fleeting thought of the impropriety, the sheer audacity of her presence here, a trespasser in the quiet heart of another’s home.
