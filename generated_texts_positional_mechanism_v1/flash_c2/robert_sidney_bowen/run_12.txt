"So we have to move," Dawson concluded, trying to push himself into a standing position, but falling back with a sharp cry of pain from his shoulder.

He looked around again. The landscape was desolate, rocky, with sparse, scrubby bushes. There were no obvious caves or overhangs nearby, nothing that screamed ‘shelter’. The ground was uneven, rising gently in some places, falling away in others. The only prominent feature was the twisted metal carcass of their plane.

"I need to check the tail section," Welsh decided, trying to stand again, but wincing. "See if the emergency kit survived. Or a radio. Though I doubt it." He began to hobble slowly back towards the wreckage, leaving Dawson alone with the unconscious Freddy.

They scoured the immediate area, moving cautiously and painfully, collecting what little dry scrub and twigs they could find. Welsh looked at the flickering flame, his face etched with fatigue and pain, but his eyes holding a steely resolve. "No, Dawson, the mission is far from over. The enemy may think they've scored a major victory, but they’ve only bought us time, time to turn this disaster into a triumph."

"How, sir?" Dawson asked, genuinely perplexed by the colonel’s apparent optimism in the face of such overwhelming odds.

"By getting this briefcase and ourselves to Casablanca," Welsh stated, his gaze meeting Dawson’s directly, "we can verify that the President’s *real* flight was uncompromised. The fake information, destroyed in the minds of the enemy, and our survival, the ultimate proof that the original plan was a decoy. They will think they intercepted and destroyed the President’s plane. They won’t be looking for another, real one. It’s a desperate gamble, but it's the only one we have."

Dawson slowly nodded. He began to understand the audacious scope of the colonel’s thinking. The very crash, the very disaster that had nearly claimed their lives, could be twisted into a strategic advantage, a brilliant deception. It was a risky, high-stakes game of bluff, but it might just work.

"But with Freddy out of commission, and your injuries, sir, how do we make it to Casablanca?" Dawson asked, voicing the practical, crushing reality of their situation. "It must be hundreds of miles. And through hostile territory."

He watched Freddy, checked his pulse periodically, and tried to keep him as warm as possible. He listened to the strange sounds of the desert night, the rustle of wind, the distant cries, every sound amplified by the silence, every shadow a potential threat. Colonel Welsh, though injured and weary, remained alert, occasionally stirring to throw another twig onto their tiny fire or to scan the horizon. He also seemed to find some small comfort in the feel of the briefcase, occasionally running his hand over its worn leather surface.

As the first faint blush of dawn began to paint the eastern sky, turning the inky blackness into shades of deep violet and then soft grey, Dawson felt a fresh wave of determination. The crash was horrific, their situation dire, but they were alive. And the mission, though irrevocably altered, was not lost. They still had a fighting chance. He looked at the wreckage of the B-25, a grim monument to their near demise, then at the vast, unforgiving landscape stretching out before them. It was a daunting prospect, a journey into the unknown, but he had faced daunting prospects before. He looked at Freddy, still unconscious but breathing, and then at Colonel Welsh, a man of unwavering resolve despite his broken body. They were a team, a very broken team, but a team nonetheless. And they had a mission.

"Right, sir," Dawson said quietly as the sun began its slow ascent, casting long, stark shadows across the rugged terrain. "What’s our first move?"

Welsh carefully pushed himself up, leaning heavily on his good leg. He surveyed the land, his eyes narrowing. "First," he said, his voice raspy but clear, "we get a good look at our surroundings. We need to find water, and we need to choose a direction. We head west, towards the coast. Casablanca is our objective. It’s going to be a long walk, Dawson, with a lot of unknowns. Dawson understood. This was not merely a physical journey; it was a test of endurance, of will, of every fiber of their being. They were no longer just pilots, no longer just intelligence officers. They were survivors, on a desperate race against time and unseen enemies, carrying the weight of the war on their injured shoulders. He looked at the vast, desolate landscape, then back at Freddy, then at the resolute face of Colonel Welsh. He knew, with a certainty born of grim experience, that this was going to be the fight of their lives. And he was ready. He had to be.

"Let’s do it, sir," Dawson affirmed, pushing himself up, ignoring the searing pain in his shoulder, his eyes fixed on the horizon, on the faint promise of Casablanca, hundreds of miles away, beyond the unforgiving mountains and the endless desert. He knew the odds were against them. He knew the enemy would be searching. Freddy drank it slowly, reverently, his eyes closed in ecstasy. Then Dawson took his turn, the few drops a blessing, coating his parched throat with blessed moisture. It tasted of earth and minerals, but it was the most delicious thing he had ever consumed. Colonel Welsh took his share last, a grim satisfaction on his face.

“Not much,” he said, looking at the dwindling pool, “but it’s something. Enough to keep us going for a few more hours. We’ll rest here for the day. And pray for more.”

The sun began to crest the horizon again, casting long, golden shadows across the wadi. They found a more sheltered spot, deeper in the gully, where the shadows would last longer. They huddled together, three injured men, parched and exhausted, but alive.

“You know, sir,” Freddy said, breaking the silence, his voice a little stronger after the water, “when you said you had confidence in our making the complete flight to Natal, and then later that fate could always throw a wrench in the works… well, I guess this is what you meant, isn’t it?”

Colonel Welsh looked at him, a weary smile touching his lips. “Precisely, Freddy. A very large, very inconvenient wrench. But we survived it. And we’ll survive this too.” He patted his tattered uniform. “No brief-case, no codes, no duplicate orders. Just us. And the mission, in its own way, is still alive. We have to reach Casablanca. We have to report what happened. We have to ensure that the enemy doesn’t connect us to the President’s trip.”

“And if they do?” Dawson asked, the question hanging heavy in the air.

The Colonel’s eyes hardened. “Then we die fighting. We tell them nothing. But that is the last resort. Our primary objective is to reach friendly lines. And to do that, we keep moving.”

He closed his eyes, leaning his head back against the cool rock. “It’s a long walk, gentlemen. The longest walk of our lives. But every step we take is a step towards victory. Every hour we survive out here is an hour stolen from the enemy. We are the loose ends they cannot tie up. We are the unknown factor. And that, in its own way, is a weapon.”

Dawson looked out at the vast, silent desert, now bathed in the harsh light of a new day. The heat was already beginning to build. His body ached, his shoulder throbbed, and his throat was starting to feel dry again. But a strange sense of resolve, cold and clear, settled over him. They were alone, exposed, vulnerable. But they were not broken. Not yet.

“Yes, sir,” Dawson said, his voice surprisingly firm. “We keep moving.”

He knew the odds were against them, stacked impossibly high. But he also knew, with a certainty born of the raw, brutal fact of their survival, that they would fight for every single mile, for every single drop of water, for every single breath. They had a mission, even if it had changed. They had a President to protect, a war to win. And for now, that was enough. He closed his eyes, allowing himself a few hours of uneasy rest, knowing that when the sun dipped below the horizon again, the long, grueling walk would resume, taking them deeper into the heart of the unforgiving desert, and closer, they hoped, to the distant, elusive promise of Casablanca. The weight of the world, or at least a significant part of the Allied war effort, rested on the shoulders of three injured men, lost and alone in the vast, hostile wilderness. And they would not, could not, fail.
