Perhaps they could still make a difference.

He closed his eyes for a moment, trying to find some semblance of peace. The distant sounds of war were a constant reminder of their perilous situation, but for now, they were safe, hidden in the heart of the Belgian woods. He knew this was just a temporary respite, a brief pause in a relentless journey. They still had a long way to go, through enemy territory, with an invaluable secret tucked into his jacket. The fate of many, perhaps even the course of the war, might well depend on two exhausted young men, an old delivery van, and a determined Belgian patriot. He clutched the Browning, a silent vow passing from his mind to his tired muscles. He would not fail. He could not. The mission was too important, the stakes too high. He would push on, no matter what came next.

The hours they spent in the cabin passed in a tense quiet. Freddy, after a short, fitful sleep, woke feeling slightly refreshed, though still deeply tired. Pierre remained at his post by the window, his senses acutely tuned to the sounds of the forest. He reported no immediate danger, but the drone of German aircraft overhead was a constant, unsettling reminder of their vulnerability. As the sun began its slow descent, painting the sky with streaks of orange and purple, Pierre decided it was time to move again. The cover of dusk would be their best protection.

They slipped out of the cabin, moving cautiously through the darkening woods, back to the van. The air was colder now, carrying with it the damp scent of coming night. Pierre started the engine, its rumble sounding dangerously loud in the growing silence. This time, Dave and Freddy rode in the front with him, crammed onto the single bench seat. It was less concealed, but it offered a better view and the possibility of communication.

Pierre drove slowly, keeping to the narrow, winding back roads, avoiding any hint of a main thoroughfare. He was a master of his craft, navigating the treacherous terrain with an almost supernatural intuition. He pointed out landmarks in the fading light, small churches, ancient farmhouses, gnarled trees, all familiar to him, all silent witnesses to the unfolding tragedy of their country. They passed through deserted hamlets, their windows dark, their doors shut tight, giving off a ghostly aura. The only sounds were the van’s laboring engine and the ever-present, distant growl of the war.

Suddenly, Pierre pointed to a faint glow on the horizon. Ghent, he murmured, his voice tight. We are almost there. But the city is in chaos. He explained that many refugees were trying to flee west, towards the coast, and German forces were tightening their grip. Getting through would be incredibly difficult.

As they approached the outskirts of the city, the scene grew increasingly grim. Rows of abandoned cars lined the roads, their tires slashed, their engines cold. Piles of discarded belongings lay scattered in the ditches, a testament to hurried, desperate departures. And then, they saw the refugees. Thousands of them, a slow, shuffling stream of humanity, moving along the roadside. Old men and women, young mothers clutching infants, children with wide, frightened eyes, all carrying meager bundles, their faces etched with despair.

Pierre slowed the van, merging into the stream of people. The sight was heartbreaking. Dave felt a wave of profound sadness wash over him, a deep empathy for these innocent lives caught in the maelstrom of war. Freddy, usually so quick with a quip, was silent, his gaze fixed on the endless procession of suffering. He reached out and gently touched the hand of a small girl who looked no older than five, her face streaked with dirt and tears. She clung to her mother, her eyes lifeless.

This is what they are fighting for, Dave thought, his resolve hardening. This is why the dispatch must get through. This suffering had to stop.

The van inched forward. The air grew thick with the smell of woodsmoke and unwashed bodies. They could hear the distant wail of sirens, the occasional burst of machine-gun fire, and the constant, unnerving rumble of heavy vehicles. Pierre kept his eyes peeled, constantly scanning the crowd, looking for anything suspicious. He whispered instructions to them, telling them to keep their heads down, to avoid drawing attention.

They reached a makeshift roadblock, manned by a handful of exhausted Belgian gendarmes. Their uniforms were rumpled, their faces grim. They were trying to direct the flow of people, but it was a losing battle. The sheer volume of refugees was overwhelming. Pierre pulled the van to a stop, rolling down his window. A gendarme, his eyes bloodshot, peered inside. Where are you going? he demanded, his voice hoarse.

Pierre explained that he was trying to get supplies to a hospital further in the city. He spoke quickly, confidently, his story well rehearsed. Dave and Freddy remained silent, their faces obscured by the shadows, trying to look as inconspicuous as possible. The gendarme hesitated, then waved them through. Go quickly, he said, it is not safe. The Germans are everywhere.

They moved deeper into the city, the streets becoming a maze of debris and abandoned vehicles. Buildings bore the scars of recent shelling, their windows shattered, their facades pockmarked. They passed through a deserted market square, the stalls overturned, goods strewn across the cobblestones. It was a scene of utter desolation.

Suddenly, Pierre swerved sharply, slamming on the brakes. A German armored car, its machine gun traversing, was blocking the main road ahead. German soldiers were fanned out, questioning refugees, their rifles at the ready. This is it, Pierre whispered, his hands tightening on the steering wheel. We cannot go this way. He quickly reversed the van, turning down a narrow alleyway. The sounds of shouts and a few warning shots echoed behind them. They had been spotted.

Pierre drove like a demon, expertly weaving through the narrow, twisting backstreets, his knowledge of Ghent proving invaluable. The van bumped and scraped against walls, its tires squealing, but he pressed on, determined to evade their pursuers. Dave gripped the Browning, his eyes scanning for any sign of a clear path. Freddy was braced against the dashboard, his face pale but resolute.

They burst out of the alley into another, slightly wider street, only to be met by a dead end. A wall of rubble, too high to climb, blocked their path. Damn it! Pierre cried, slamming his fist on the steering wheel. He began to turn the van around, but it was too late. The ominous rumble of the armored car grew louder, and then it appeared at the entrance to the street, its machine gun swiveling towards them.

We're trapped! Freddy yelled, his voice filled with desperation. Dave knew they had only one option left. He unlatched the passenger door. Get out, he commanded. Run! Don't look back! He shoved Freddy out of the van, then followed himself, the Browning held high. Pierre was already out, drawing his Luger.

Bullets immediately began to chew at the brickwork around them, sending chips of stone flying. The armored car’s machine gun chattered, a terrifying sound. Dave grabbed Freddy's arm. Follow me! he yelled, pulling him towards a small gap in the rubble-strewn wall. It looked like an entrance to a courtyard. They scrambled through, the sounds of gunfire and shouting erupting behind them. Pierre followed, providing covering fire with his Luger, buying them precious seconds.

They found themselves in a dark, narrow courtyard, surrounded by tall, dilapidated buildings. A series of fire escapes zigzagged up the walls, offering a precarious route upwards. This way! Dave shouted, pointing to the nearest fire escape. He pushed Freddy ahead of him, then began to climb, the cold metal rattling under his weight. Pierre, agile despite his size, quickly followed.

They scrambled up the fire escape, bullets ricocheting off the metal, narrowly missing them. Dave could hear the Germans entering the courtyard below, their shouts echoing in the confined space. They reached the roof, gasping for breath, their muscles aching. The city stretched out before them, a panorama of desolation under the night sky, punctuated by the glow of distant fires and the flash of artillery.

Across the rooftop, Dave saw another fire escape leading down to a different street. This is our chance! he gasped. We cross to that street. And we lose them in the alleys. He looked at Pierre, whose face was grim but determined. They nodded at each other, a silent agreement passing between them. The mission was not over. Not yet. They still had a long way to go, and the dangers were far from past. The dispatch, the precious secret, remained safe for now. But the night was long, and Ghent was a city under siege.

Dave wasted no time. He scrambled across the uneven, gravel-strewn rooftop, his feet crunching on loose tiles and debris. Freddy followed close behind, his breath ragged, his young face streaked with dirt and fear. Pierre, with surprising agility for a man of his build, brought up the rear, his Luger still clutched in his hand, his eyes constantly scanning their back trail. The night air was thick with the acrid smell of smoke and burning buildings. Distant artillery thudded, a dull, relentless heartbeat against the chaos. Closer, the sharp crack of rifle fire and the insistent chatter of the armored car's machine gun reminded them of the peril they had just escaped and the enemy still close at their heels.

They moved from one building to another, sometimes leaping across narrow gaps between rooftops, sometimes clambering over low parapet walls. The city below was a labyrinth of dark alleys and bombed-out structures, illuminated occasionally by the red glow of a burning warehouse or the harsh sweep of a searchlight beam. Dave kept his senses sharp, listening for any sign of pursuit from the street below or the rooftops above. He knew the Germans would not give up easily. They understood the importance of the dispatch, even if they did not know its full contents. This was not merely a chase for two downed pilots and their helper, this was a hunt for vital intelligence, and the hunters would be relentless.

Freddy stumbled on a loose piece of masonry, letting out a sharp gasp, but Dave caught his arm, steadying him. "Keep moving," he hissed, his voice low and urgent. "Don't look down. Just keep your eyes on the next step." Freddy nodded, his jaw tight, and pushed himself forward. They reached a wider thoroughfare, its buildings taller and more imposing. The fire escape Dave had spotted was still there, a dark, skeletal ladder against the grimy brickwork. It seemed a long way off, and the gap between their current roof and the next was wider than any they had yet crossed.

"This is it," Dave muttered, assessing the jump. It was perhaps ten feet, a distance that felt like a chasm under pressure. He took a running start and launched himself across, landing with a jarring thud on the opposite roof. He rolled, absorbing the impact, and quickly got to his feet, turning to face Freddy and Pierre. "Your turn, Freddy," he called, extending a hand. Freddy hesitated for a split second, his eyes wide, then he took a deep breath and leaped. He landed awkwardly, stumbling forward, but Dave's strong grip kept him from falling. Pierre, with a grunt of effort, followed, landing with surprising grace for his size.

They made their way to the fire escape, which hung precariously from the side of a five-story building. The metal was cold and slick with grime under their hands. As they began their descent, the faint sounds of shouting drifted up from the street below, closer now. German voices. Dave peered down through the gaps in the ladder. He could just make out the figures of soldiers, their helmets glinting in the pale light of a distant streetlamp, moving cautiously along the street they had just abandoned. They were fanning out, covering both sides.

"Faster," Dave urged, his voice barely a whisper. "They're searching."
They clattered down the rungs, each step echoing in the stillness of the street below them, a street that thankfully seemed deserted for the moment. Their boots hit the pavement with soft thuds, and they quickly ducked into the shadow of an arched entryway. Dave pressed his back against the cold stone, his Browning ready. He could hear the heavy bootfalls of the German patrol, growing louder, moving in their general direction.

"Which way, Pierre?" Dave asked, his gaze fixed on the corner of the building.
Pierre, his face a mask of grim concentration, pointed down a narrow, unlit alley that ran between two tall, leaning houses. "That way," he whispered. "It leads to the canal. We can lose them there, perhaps find a boat."
"A boat?" Freddy asked, his voice incredulous. "In the middle of the night?"
"Any port in a storm, Freddy," Dave said, already moving into the alley. "Right now, a boat is better than a bullet."

The alley was a choked passage, reeking of damp earth and refuse. Piles of broken furniture and discarded crates lined the walls, making their progress slow and difficult. They could hear the Germans closer now, their calls echoing. Dave pushed a loose barrel aside, creating a small gap, and gestured for Freddy to go first. Freddy squeezed through, then Dave, then Pierre, who paused for a moment to subtly re-arrange the barrel, hoping to slow down any immediate pursuit.

They emerged from the alley onto a wider, but still deserted, street. The air here was cooler, carrying the faint, earthy scent of water. Before them, a dark expanse of canal stretched out, its surface reflecting the shattered moon and the glow of distant fires like a cracked mirror. On the far bank, the silhouette of buildings rose up, indistinct in the gloom. No boats were immediately visible, only the occasional moored barge, hulking shadows against the sky.

"Stay low," Dave ordered, scanning both banks of the canal. The silence here was unnerving, broken only by the distant sounds of war and the gentle lapping of water against the embankment. "We need to find something, anything, that floats."
They crept along the canal path, keeping to the deeper shadows cast by the buildings. Dave’s hope was fading with each passing minute. The canal was wide, too wide to swim, and the barges looked heavy and unyielding, secured with thick ropes. Even if they could get onto one, starting its engine, if it even had one, would be impossible without drawing attention.

Then, Pierre stopped abruptly, holding up a hand. He pointed to a small, rickety wooden jetty ahead. Tied to it was a flat-bottomed punt, designed for navigating the shallow waters of the canal. It looked old and well-used, but it was a boat, and it was free.

"Thank God," Freddy breathed, a sliver of hope in his voice.
"Quiet," Dave warned, his eyes darting around. "This is too easy. It might be a trap."
They approached the punt cautiously. It was indeed an old fishing boat, with a single oar resting inside. No engine, just manual power. Better than nothing. Dave quickly untied the mooring rope, his fingers working swiftly in the dark. He gestured for Freddy to get in, then Pierre, and finally he slipped in himself, pushing off from the jetty with the oar.

The punt glided silently across the dark water, the only sound the soft splash of the oar as Dave dipped it into the canal. He wasn't an expert oarsman, but he had rowed boats as a boy, and the muscle memory came back. He pulled with steady, rhythmic strokes, aiming for the opposite bank, for the deeper shadows where they might find cover. The cold spray from the oar splattered his face, a refreshing contrast to the adrenaline-fueled heat that had been burning through him.

They were perhaps halfway across when a sudden, blinding light cut through the darkness from the bank they had just left. A powerful searchlight beam swept across the canal, momentarily catching the punt in its harsh glare.
"They saw us!" Freddy yelled, ducking low.
A volley of rifle fire erupted from the bank, bullets spitting into the water around them, creating small geysers. One struck the wooden side of the punt with a sickening thud, a splinter flying past Dave's ear.
"Row, Dave, row!" Pierre urged, drawing his Luger, although he knew it was useless against the distance and the numbers.

Dave put every ounce of his remaining strength into the oar, pulling with desperate energy. He zig-zagged the punt slightly, making them a harder target. More shots rang out, closer this time, and another bullet ripped through the air just above their heads. The fear was a cold knot in Dave's stomach, but he refused to yield to it. He could not, not with Freddy and Pierre depending on him. The other bank seemed to recede, despite his efforts. It was tantalizingly close, yet impossibly far under the hail of gunfire.

Just when he thought they couldn't take another hit, they reached the opposite bank. Dave scrambled out, dragging the punt up onto the muddy shore, not even bothering to tie it. Freddy and Pierre were right behind him. They plunged into a dense thicket of bushes, the sounds of gunfire fading slightly as the foliage absorbed some of the noise. Dave pushed through the undergrowth, branches slapping against his face and tearing at his clothes. He could hear Freddy gasping behind him, and Pierre’s heavy breathing.

They ran until their lungs burned and their legs ached, until the sounds of the pursuit were swallowed by the general cacophony of the distant fighting. Finally, Dave collapsed against the trunk of a large, ancient tree, gasping for air. Freddy crumpled beside him, coughing violently. Pierre, though clearly winded, remained standing, his eyes still watchful, his Luger held loosely in his hand.

"Are you alright, Freddy?" Dave managed, his voice hoarse.
Freddy nodded, still trying to catch his breath. "Just a bit… winded. Thought we were goners back there."
"Almost," Dave admitted, wiping a hand across his sweaty brow. He looked at Pierre. "Where are we now?"
Pierre took a moment to compose himself. "We are on the outskirts of Ghent, near the forest of Wachtebeke. Not ideal, but better than being caught in the city. The main German forces would be moving through the city center, not here. We need to find a way south, towards the French border."

"South," Dave repeated, trying to picture a map in his head. "How far is that?"
"Many kilometers," Pierre said, shaking his head grimly. "And it will be crawling with Germans. We must be very careful. This dispatch you carry, it must be very important."
Dave patted the inside of his jacket, where the dispatch was secured in a waterproof pouch. "It's vital, Pierre. It concerns troop movements, counter-offensives. It could turn the tide of this invasion, if it reaches the right hands."
Pierre nodded, his expression softening slightly. "Then we will get it there. We will manage."

They rested for a short while, allowing their racing hearts to slow and their weary muscles to recover. The forest was dark and quiet, a welcome contrast to the urban battlefield they had just traversed. The air was cool and smelled of damp earth and pine needles. After a few minutes, Dave pushed himself to his feet. "We can't stay here," he said. "They'll send patrols. We need to put more distance between us and Ghent before dawn."

They moved deeper into the forest, using the compass Dave carried to maintain a southerly direction. The going was tough in the dark, the ground uneven, laced with roots and fallen branches. They stumbled frequently, their exhaustion weighing heavily on them. Freddy was clearly struggling, his young body unaccustomed to such sustained physical exertion and stress. Dave kept an encouraging hand on his shoulder, urging him forward.

As the first faint streaks of grey began to appear on the eastern horizon, painting the sky with the promise of a new day, they found a small, abandoned hunting shack. It was dilapidated, with a collapsed roof and no door, but it offered some shelter from the elements and a place to rest their aching bodies.

"This will have to do," Dave said, peering inside. "We'll stay here until nightfall. It's too risky to move in daylight."
They settled down on the dirt floor, huddling together for warmth. Dave pulled out his emergency rations, two chocolate bars and a small flask of water. He divided the chocolate equally. It was a meager meal, but it provided a much-needed burst of energy.
"Dave," Freddy said, his voice small, "what happens if we don't make it? If they catch us?"
Dave looked at the young man, his eyes full of a weary compassion. "We will make it, Freddy," he said, his voice firm, projecting a confidence he didn't entirely feel. "We have to. And if the worst happens, you destroy that dispatch. Don't let it fall into their hands. Ever."
Freddy nodded, his eyes wide. He understood the gravity of the instruction.

Dave dozed fitfully, his hand still on the Browning, every rustle of leaves, every distant sound, jolting him awake. Pierre, ever watchful, took the first watch, sitting by the open doorway, his Luger across his lap, his gaze sweeping the forest around them.

The day passed slowly, a tense vigil. They heard the distant rumble of convoys on what must have been a main road, and once, the unmistakable roar of German fighter planes flying overhead, their engines a menacing drone. Each sound sent a fresh wave of apprehension through them. They were deep in enemy territory, hunted, isolated.

As dusk began to fall, painting the forest in hues of purple and deep blue, Pierre signaled that it was time to move. They were stiff and sore, but the urgency of their mission drove them forward. They skirted the main roads, sticking to the deeper parts of the woods, navigating by the stars and Dave's compass. The forest was eerily quiet at night, the only sounds their own footsteps and the occasional hoot of an owl.

Freddy, remarkably, seemed to find a renewed burst of energy, perhaps fueled by the desire to prove himself, or simply by the sheer terror of their situation. Pierre, the oldest, seemed to draw on an inexhaustible reserve of grim determination.

It seemed a long way off, and the gap between their current roof and the next was wider than any they had yet crossed.

"This is it," Dave muttered, assessing the jump. It was perhaps ten feet, a distance that felt like a chasm under pressure. He took a running start and launched himself across, landing with a jarring thud on the opposite roof. He rolled, absorbing the impact, and quickly got to his feet, turning to face Freddy and Pierre. "Your turn, Freddy," he called, extending a hand. Freddy hesitated for a split second, his eyes wide, then he took a deep breath and leaped. He landed awkwardly, stumbling forward, but Dave's strong grip kept him from falling. Pierre, with a grunt of effort, followed, landing with surprising grace for his size.

They made their way to the fire escape, which hung precariously from the side of a five-story building. The metal was cold and slick with grime under their hands. As they began their descent, the faint sounds of shouting drifted up from the street below, closer now. German voices. Dave peered down through the gaps in the ladder. He could just make out the figures of soldiers, their helmets glinting in the pale light of a distant streetlamp, moving cautiously along the street they had just abandoned. They were fanning out, covering both sides.

"Faster," Dave urged, his voice barely a whisper. "They're searching."
They clattered down the rungs, each step echoing in the stillness of the street below them, a street that thankfully seemed deserted for the moment. Their boots hit the pavement with soft thuds, and they quickly ducked into the shadow of an arched entryway. Dave pressed his back against the cold stone, his Browning ready. He could hear the heavy bootfalls of the German patrol, growing louder, moving in their general direction.

"Which way, Pierre?" Dave asked, his gaze fixed on the corner of the building.
Pierre, his face a mask of grim concentration, pointed down a narrow, unlit alley that ran between two tall, leaning houses. "That way," he whispered. "It leads to the canal. We can lose them there, perhaps find a boat."
"A boat?" Freddy asked, his voice incredulous. "In the middle of the night?"
"Any port in a storm, Freddy," Dave said, already moving into the alley. "Right now, a boat is better than a bullet."

The alley was a choked passage, reeking of damp earth and refuse. Piles of broken furniture and discarded crates lined the walls, making their progress slow and difficult. They could hear the Germans closer now, their calls echoing. Dave pushed a loose barrel aside, creating a small gap, and gestured for Freddy to go first. Freddy squeezed through, then Dave, then Pierre, who paused for a moment to subtly re-arrange the barrel, hoping to slow down any immediate pursuit.

They emerged from the alley onto a wider, but still deserted, street. The air here was cooler, carrying the faint, earthy scent of water. Before them, a dark expanse of canal stretched out, its surface reflecting the shattered moon and the glow of distant fires like a cracked mirror. On the far bank, the silhouette of buildings rose up, indistinct in the gloom. No boats were immediately visible, only the occasional moored barge, hulking shadows against the sky.

"Stay low," Dave ordered, scanning both banks of the canal. The silence here was unnerving, broken only by the distant sounds of war and the gentle lapping of water against the embankment. "We need to find something, anything, that floats."
They crept along the canal path, keeping to the deeper shadows cast by the buildings. Dave’s hope was fading with each passing minute. The canal was wide, too wide to swim, and the barges looked heavy and unyielding, secured with thick ropes. Even if they could get onto one, starting its engine, if it even had one, would be impossible without drawing attention.

Then, Pierre stopped abruptly, holding up a hand. He pointed to a small, rickety wooden jetty ahead. Tied to it was a flat-bottomed punt, designed for navigating the shallow waters of the canal. It looked old and well-used, but it was a boat, and it was free.

"Thank God," Freddy breathed, a sliver of hope in his voice.
"Quiet," Dave warned, his eyes darting around. "This is too easy. It might be a trap."
They approached the punt cautiously. It was indeed an old fishing boat, with a single oar resting inside. No engine, just manual power. Better than nothing. Dave quickly untied the mooring rope, his fingers working swiftly in the dark. He gestured for Freddy to get in, then Pierre, and finally he slipped in himself, pushing off from the jetty with the oar.

The punt glided silently across the dark water, the only sound the soft splash of the oar as Dave dipped it into the canal. He wasn't an expert oarsman, but he had rowed boats as a boy, and the muscle memory came back. He pulled with steady, rhythmic strokes, aiming for the opposite bank, for the deeper shadows where they might find cover. The cold spray from the oar splattered his face, a refreshing contrast to the adrenaline-fueled heat that had been burning through him.

They were perhaps halfway across when a sudden, blinding light cut through the darkness from the bank they had just left. A powerful searchlight beam swept across the canal, momentarily catching the punt in its harsh glare.
"They saw us!" Freddy yelled, ducking low.
A volley of rifle fire erupted from the bank, bullets spitting into the water around them, creating small geysers. One struck the wooden side of the punt with a sickening thud, a splinter flying past Dave's ear.
"Row, Dave, row!" Pierre urged, drawing his Luger, although he knew it was useless against the distance and the numbers.

Dave put every ounce of his remaining strength into the oar, pulling with desperate energy. He zig-zagged the punt slightly, making them a harder target. More shots rang out, closer this time, and another bullet ripped through the air just above their heads. The fear was a cold knot in Dave's stomach, but he refused to yield to it. He could not, not with Freddy and Pierre depending on him. The other bank seemed to recede, despite his efforts. It was tantalizingly close, yet impossibly far under the hail of gunfire.

Just when he thought they couldn't take another hit, they reached the opposite bank. Dave scrambled out, dragging the punt up onto the muddy shore, not even bothering to tie it. Freddy and Pierre were right behind him. They plunged into a dense thicket of bushes, the sounds of gunfire fading slightly as the foliage absorbed some of the noise. Dave pushed through the undergrowth, branches slapping against his face and tearing at his clothes. He could hear Freddy gasping behind him, and Pierre’s heavy breathing.

They ran until their lungs burned and their legs ached, until the sounds of the pursuit were swallowed by the general cacophony of the distant fighting. Finally, Dave collapsed against the trunk of a large, ancient tree, gasping for air. Freddy crumpled beside him, coughing violently. Pierre, though clearly winded, remained standing, his eyes still watchful, his Luger held loosely in his hand.

"Are you alright, Freddy?" Dave managed, his voice hoarse.
Freddy nodded, still trying to catch his breath. "Just a bit… winded. Thought we were goners back there."
"Almost," Dave admitted, wiping a hand across his sweaty brow. He looked at Pierre. "Where are we now?"
Pierre took a moment to compose himself. "We are on the outskirts of Ghent, near the forest of Wachtebeke. Not ideal, but better than

than being trapped in the open with German bullets snapping at our heels." Pierre finished, his breath coming in ragged gasps. He wiped a hand across his forehead, pushing back a lock of dark hair. "We are safe for a moment here. This forest is dense. German patrols will not find us easily, not in the dark."

Dave nodded, trying to steady his own breathing. The adrenaline was slowly receding, leaving him feeling hollowed out and weary. His body ached from the impact of the crash, the squirming in the mud, the sprint through the undergrowth, and the desperate rowing. He glanced at Freddy, who was still hunched over, his face pale in the dim light filtering through the canopy. "Freddy, you alright?"

"Just... just a little shaken," Freddy mumbled, pushing himself upright with a groan. "That was too close for comfort, Dave. Much too close."

"Tell me about it," Dave sighed. He looked at Pierre. "What's the plan now, Pierre? Where do we go from here? And how far are we from... well, anywhere safe?"

Pierre considered for a moment, his eyes scanning the surrounding trees as if seeking guidance from the ancient trunks. "Ghent is not far, perhaps five kilometers to the east. But Ghent is crawling with Boche. Our path lies west, towards the coast. Dunkirk, perhaps, if the British still hold it. Or further south, towards what remains of the French lines. But that is a long journey, across open country, through towns and villages that will

will be swarming with their soldiers, or worse, with civilians who might mistake us for Germans as those Belgians had done. We need to be careful, very careful, for our lives depend on it, and the information we carry for the Allies. He paused, his gaze fixing on Dave with an intensity that cut through Dave’s fatigue. "Do you still have the documents, American?"

Dave patted the inside of his flight jacket, feeling the thin packet secured there. "Still here, Pierre. Safe and sound, I hope."

"Good," Pierre affirmed with a sharp nod. "They are our only currency in this desperate land. Without them, we are merely fugitives. With them, we are a priority. But first, we must survive. This forest, while offering cover, offers little in the way of sustenance. We have no food, no water, and only the clothes on our backs. We cannot stay here indefinitely, for the Boche will sweep these woods. They are methodical, and they are everywhere."

Freddy, who had been listening intently, pushed himself to his feet, wincing slightly. "So, we walk? West, you said? How far is Dunkirk, really? And how do we even know if it's still held?" His voice, though weary, held a note of youthful impatience.

Pierre sighed, running a hand through his hair again. "Many kilometers, my young English friend. Too many to walk quickly, especially with no supplies. As for whether Dunkirk holds, that is a question no man on this side of the front lines can

answer with certainty, my friend, not here in the belly of the beast." Pierre sighed again, the sound lost in the rustling leaves above them. "What we do know is this, we are behind enemy lines, far behind them. We have no food, no water, and the Boche are everywhere. Dunkirk may be a fortress or it may be a tomb. We cannot know. But we must try to reach it, or any point on the coast where the British still hold. That is our best, perhaps our only, chance to deliver your precious information."

Dave rubbed his tired eyes, feeling the grit of dirt and the stiffness of dried sweat on his skin. He hadn't felt this utterly drained since he'd run the two mile obstacle course in flight school after a full night of simulated combat. This, he thought with a grim twist of his lips, was far worse. There was no hot shower and warm meal waiting at the end of this run. "So, we walk west," Dave said, trying to inject some resolve into his voice. "Tonight. Under cover of darkness. But what about food and water? We can't walk for days without anything."

Pierre looked at him, his face a mask of weary pragmatism. "You are right, American. We cannot. But we cannot hunt or light a fire. That would be suicide. For now, we must trust in stealth and luck. We will move west, yes. But carefully. We will stick to the deepest parts of the woods as long as we can. Perhaps we will stumble upon a deserted farmhouse, or a hidden stream. We must hope for small mercies."

Freddy, who had been listening intently, finally spoke up, his voice still a little hoarse. "But what if we run into Germans? Or those Belgians again, who might not be so understanding this time?"

Pierre's eyes, even in the gloom, seemed to harden. "We avoid them. At all costs. If we cannot avoid them, we run. If we cannot run, we fight. But we do not give up. Not now. Not with what you carry, American. That information is more important than our lives, do you understand?" He paused, letting his words sink in. "We will move slowly, quietly. We will listen to every sound. We will watch every shadow. And we will pray, if you are a praying man, that dawn brings us some small advantage, a way forward."

Dave nodded, a heavy feeling settling in his gut. He was a praying man, sometimes, when things got really bad. And things, he thought, were about as bad as they could get. "Alright, Pierre. Lead the way. But keep us clear of any open fields if you can. I've had enough of running across open ground for one night."

Pierre gave a curt nod, then began to move, melting into the deeper shadows of the forest with a quiet grace that spoke of long familiarity with such terrain. Dave and Freddy followed, their own movements far clumsier, each snap of a twig, each rustle of leaves sounding like a gunshot in the oppressive silence of the night. The air was cool and damp, carrying the scent of rich earth and decaying leaves. Above them, a sliver of moon occasionally broke through the thick canopy, casting dancing, ghostly shadows that played tricks on their tired eyes.

They walked for what felt like an eternity, each step a struggle against exhaustion and the oppressive darkness. Dave’s legs ached, his lungs burned, and the gnawing emptiness in his stomach was beginning to be an insistent torment. He kept a hand on the inside of his flight jacket, reassuring himself that the documents were still there, still safe. That small, thin packet of papers represented the last flickering hope of countless lives, and its weight felt immense, almost unbearable.

Freddy, despite his younger age, seemed to be suffering more visibly. He stumbled often, catching himself on low-hanging branches, and his breathing was shallow and ragged. Dave occasionally reached out a hand, steadying him, offering a quiet word of encouragement. "Keep going, kid. Not much further, I hope."

Freddy would only grunt in response, his eyes fixed on Pierre's retreating back. Pierre, for his part, moved like a phantom, never seeming to tire, his head constantly swiveling, his ears pricked for any sound that might betray their presence or warn them of danger. He was a man utterly transformed by the war, a silent, deadly guide through a hostile landscape.

After what must have been several hours, Pierre suddenly halted, holding up a hand. Dave and Freddy stopped dead, their hearts pounding in their chests. The air seemed to vibrate with an unseen tension. Pierre slowly lowered himself to a crouch, peering through a tangle of undergrowth. Dave crept up beside him, Freddy close behind, and followed his gaze.

Through a small break in the trees, a faint, flickering light could be seen. It was distant, perhaps a kilometer or so, but unmistakable. It was the glow of a fire.

"A village," Pierre whispered, his voice barely audible. "Or a farm. I do not know if it is occupied by Boche, or by our own people, or if it is deserted."

"What do we do?" Freddy whispered back, his voice tight with a mixture of fear and hope. "Do we try to find food? Water?"

Dave considered it, his mind racing. The risk was enormous. A German patrol, a suspicious farmer, anything could give them away. But the hunger and thirst were becoming unbearable, a dull ache that threatened to overwhelm all other thoughts. "We need to know more," Dave decided. "Can we get closer, Pierre? Scout it out?"

Pierre nodded grimly. "We proceed with extreme caution. One mistake, and it is over for us. Stay low, move slowly. No sound, understand? If I signal you to stop, you stop. If I signal you to hide, you hide. Our lives depend on it."

They resumed their movement, but now with an even greater degree of stealth. Each footfall was placed with painstaking care, each branch carefully parted. The light grew a little brighter, resolving itself into the unmistakable silhouette of a small cluster of buildings. A farmhouse, a barn, perhaps a few sheds. And the fire, now clearly visible, was burning in what looked like the farmhouse's hearth, its glow spilling faintly through a window.

As they crept closer, a new sound reached them, carried on the night air. Voices. German voices.

Dave felt a cold dread settle over him. His blood ran cold. The low guttural tones were unmistakable. The farm was occupied. And by the enemy.

Pierre froze, pressing himself against the damp earth. Dave and Freddy immediately followed suit, their bodies rigid with fear. They lay there for what seemed like an eternity, listening to the indistinct murmur of voices, the occasional clatter of something metallic. The smell of woodsmoke was strong now, mingled with a faint aroma that made Dave's stomach clench painfully. Bread. Freshly baked bread. The cruelty of it was almost unbearable.

After a long, agonizing wait, Pierre slowly, carefully, began to backtrack, motioning for them to follow. They retreated deep into the woods, the German voices fading behind them. When they were a safe distance away, Pierre finally stopped, leaning against a thick tree trunk, his face grim.

"It is Boche," he confirmed, his voice low and bitter. "A small patrol, perhaps. They are holed up for the night. We cannot approach it. It is too risky."

"But... but the bread," Freddy whispered, his voice barely a breath, sounding utterly defeated. "I could smell it, Pierre. I'm so hungry."

Dave put a hand on Freddy's shoulder, giving it a reassuring squeeze. He understood completely. He could almost taste the crusty bread himself, the thought of it a torment. "We'll find something, Freddy. We have to. But not there."

"He is right," Pierre said, his gaze sweeping the darkened forest. "We push on. The deeper into the forest we go, the safer we will be from immediate patrols. Perhaps we can find a stream for water at least. Water is more immediate than food. You can last longer without food than without water."

They continued their arduous journey, the forest now feeling less like a sanctuary and more like a vast, indifferent maze. The hunger in Dave's stomach was a dull, constant throb, but the thirst was slowly becoming paramount. His mouth was dry, his throat raw. He licked his lips, but it did no good. He could feel Freddy’s fatigue in the way he walked, the way his breath hitched, the way he occasionally stumbled.

Then, just as despair was beginning to set in, they heard it. The faint, unmistakable gurgle of running water. Pierre stopped dead, his head cocked, listening. A moment later, he gave a small, almost imperceptible nod and began to move again, this time with a renewed sense of purpose.

They followed the sound, pushing through a particularly dense thicket, until they emerged into a small clearing. In the center, a narrow stream, clear and sparkling under the faint moonlight, tumbled over rocks.

"Thank God," Dave breathed, feeling a surge of relief so intense it almost brought tears to his eyes.

They knelt by the stream, cupping their hands and drinking deeply, the cold water a blessed relief to their parched throats. They drank until their stomachs ached, then splashed water on their faces, washing away some of the grime and sweat. For a few moments, the world seemed a little less bleak.

"We rest here for a short time," Pierre decided, his voice softer now, less strained. "We can replenish our canteens, if you have them. Then, we continue west. We must put as much distance between us and that Boche patrol as possible before dawn."

Dave and Freddy didn't argue. They sat by the stream, leaning against the cool, damp earth, allowing a moment of respite to wash over them. The sound of the running water was a calming balm, masking the distant rumble of artillery that was now a constant, ominous presence in the eastern sky.

"It sounds like it's getting closer," Freddy observed, his voice hushed.

Pierre nodded grimly. "The front is collapsing. They are pushing hard. That is why we must move quickly. Every hour we waste here, the greater the chance we become trapped, or worse." He stood up, stretching his tired limbs. "Come. The moon will soon begin to set. We must use the remaining hours of darkness to our advantage."

They refilled their makeshift canteens, using small empty bottles Dave found in his jacket pocket, and then rose, their bodies protesting with every movement. The brief rest had offered some small renewal of strength, but the hunger remained, a dull, persistent ache that would not be ignored.

They continued their westward trek, now with the added burden of knowing that the German forces were advancing rapidly. The distant rumble of artillery fire, which had been a low throb for hours, seemed to gain intensity, sending shivers down Dave's spine. It was a constant reminder of the chaos and destruction that engulfed the land around them. They were moving, yes, but into what? Another trap? Another field of battle?

The forest eventually began to thin as the first gray streaks of dawn began to paint the eastern sky. Pierre, ever watchful, signaled them to stop in a dense copse of trees just as the light grew strong enough to reveal their surroundings.

"We cannot risk moving in daylight in the open," Pierre explained, his voice low and urgent. "Not with the Boche patrols. We will find a place to hide for the day, and continue our journey tonight."

They spent the next few hours searching for a suitable hiding place, eventually settling on a deep, overgrown ditch, partially covered by fallen branches and thick undergrowth. It was cramped, damp, and uncomfortable, but it offered excellent concealment. They huddled together, sharing the meager space, listening to the sounds of the waking world around them. Birds chirped, insects buzzed, and in the distance, the faint sound of vehicle engines, German engines, occasionally broke the natural symphony of the forest.

The long day passed slowly, each minute stretching into an eternity. The lack of food was becoming a serious problem. Dave’s head ached, a dull, throbbing pain behind his eyes, and his stomach growled in protest. Freddy was quiet, almost withdrawn, his face pale and drawn. He huddled into himself, trying to conserve energy. Pierre, despite his apparent resilience, also showed signs of strain, his eyes constantly scanning the meager visible world outside their hiding place, his jaw set in a grim line.

"We cannot continue like this," Dave whispered to Pierre late in the afternoon, his voice hoarse. "We need food. We need to find help. If we keep going like this, we'll be too weak to do anything."

Pierre nodded slowly, his eyes thoughtful. "I agree, American. But where can we find it without risking everything? Every village, every road, every farm could be occupied. We are behind enemy lines. There are no friendly faces, not for us. Not yet."

"What about a trapper's cabin?" Freddy suggested, his voice surprising them both with its sudden clarity. "Or a hunter's lodge? My uncle had one in Scotland, deep in the woods. Sometimes they have emergency rations, or a well. Even a dried fish or some stale bread would be a feast now."

Pierre's eyes brightened fractionally. "A good idea, my young friend. It is a long shot, but possible. There are many such cabins in these woods, places where a man might go to escape for a time, or to hunt game. They are usually well hidden, off the beaten path. If we could find one, it might offer some small salvation."

"It's worth a try," Dave said, a flicker of hope stirring within him. "We can't just lie here and starve."

As dusk began to settle, painting the sky in hues of orange and purple, they carefully emerged from their hiding place. The air was cooler now, and a light mist was beginning to rise from the damp ground. Pierre took the lead again, but this time, his movements were less direct, more exploratory. He was searching, not just for concealment, but for any sign of human habitation, however temporary or abandoned.

They moved slower now, their hunger and fatigue taking a heavy toll. Dave felt a growing sense of desperation, a dull ache that seemed to pervade his entire being. He thought of the juicy steak he had been looking forward to, the hot coffee, the soft bed that awaited him back home. It felt like a lifetime ago.

After another hour of painstaking search, just as the last vestiges of twilight faded into utter darkness, Pierre suddenly stopped. He pointed, his finger trembling slightly, towards a faint, almost invisible track winding deeper into the woods, barely discernible from the surrounding undergrowth.

"Look," he whispered, his voice tinged with a new excitement. "There. A path. Rarely used, but it is there. It could lead to something."

They followed the faint path, their hopes rekindled. It was rough and overgrown, clearly not maintained, but it was a path nonetheless, a sign of human presence in this wilderness. The path twisted and turned, leading them deeper and deeper into the quiet heart of the forest.

After what felt like another mile, they saw it. A small, ramshackle cabin, half-hidden by overgrown bushes and trees. It was dark, silent, and appeared utterly deserted. A wave of relief, so powerful it almost buckled Dave's knees, washed over him.

"Be careful," Pierre cautioned, his voice low. "It could be a trap. Or occupied by vagrants, or worse."

They approached the cabin with extreme caution, moving like ghosts in the night. Pierre circled the small structure, examining the single window, the wooden door, the surrounding area for any signs of recent activity. The window was dark and grimy, the door slightly ajar, hanging crookedly on its hinges.

"It looks abandoned," Pierre finally announced, his voice still cautious but with a hint of satisfaction. "Long abandoned, perhaps. But we must check."

He pushed the door open slowly, a long, drawn-out creak shattering the silence of the night. Dave and Freddy stood ready, their hearts pounding. The inside was a black void, smelling of damp earth, decaying wood, and something vaguely animalistic.

Pierre stepped inside, feeling his way, and after a moment, they heard a muffled grunt. Then a small scratch, and the flickering light of a match.

"Come," Pierre called out softly. "It is empty. But there is a fireplace."

Dave and Freddy stumbled inside, their eyes blinking against the sudden, welcome light of the match in Pierre's hand. It was a single, small room, cluttered with cobwebs and dust. A rickety table and two chairs stood in the center, and a simple cot against one wall. A stone fireplace dominated the far wall. It was bare, but functional.

"Nothing here," Dave said, his voice flat with disappointment as he scanned the empty shelves. "No food, no supplies."

"Wait," Pierre said, his match burning low. He moved towards a corner, kneeling down. "Sometimes there are places. Hidden caches." He felt around, his fingers exploring the rough planks of the floor. Suddenly, he grunted with effort. There was a faint creaking sound, and a section of the floorboards lifted.

Beneath, a small, shallow cavity was revealed. And inside, wrapped in oilcloth, were a few items. Pierre carefully pulled them out. A small, rusted tin of biscuits. A half-empty bottle of some kind of liquor. And, most importantly, a small, leather-bound pouch.

"Biscuits!" Freddy exclaimed, his eyes wide with disbelief and joy. "Oh, thank God! Actual food!"

Pierre broke open the tin, and the smell of stale, but still edible, biscuits filled the air. They devoured them greedily, the dry, hard wafers a feast for their starving bodies. They washed them down with sips of the liquor, which was harsh and burning, but offered a temporary warmth and dulling of their gnawing hunger.

Then Pierre opened the leather pouch. Inside were a few coins, some dried herbs, and, to their utter astonishment, a small, battered map.

"A map!" Dave whispered, his heart thumping. "Is it current?"

Pierre held it carefully, his eyes tracing the faded lines. "It is old, but it shows the area. And look here." He pointed a trembling finger to a small, almost invisible symbol, a tiny cross drawn in faded ink. "A resistance safe house. Not far from here. Marked as active within the last year."

Dave felt a surge of hope, stronger than any he had felt since the crash. "A safe house? Real allies? People who can help us?"

"Perhaps," Pierre cautioned, his face still wary. "Or perhaps it is compromised. Or abandoned. But it is our best chance. If it is active, they will have food, information, and a way to get us closer to the coast. It is risky, but we have no other choice."

"Then we go there," Freddy declared, his voice filled with newfound determination. The biscuits and liquor had clearly given him a much-needed boost. "When? Now?"

"No," Pierre said, shaking his head. "Not now. We will rest here until an hour before dawn. We cannot risk traveling to a potential safe house in full darkness. We need enough light to observe, to be cautious. We will leave then. We will use the map. It is a long walk, even on foot. Perhaps fifteen, twenty kilometers. But it is a path, and it has a destination. And it is west."

They settled down in the dusty cabin, the meager fire in the hearth offering some warmth and a flickering light. The biscuits had dulled their hunger, and the liquor had numbed some of their aches. For the first time since their crash, Dave felt a fragile sense of security, a small island of peace in a sea of chaos. He leaned back against the rough wooden wall, listening to the crackle of the fire, the distant rumble of the guns, and the quiet breathing of Freddy and Pierre. He clutched the map close to him. It was a lifeline. A tangible, if ancient, hope.

Sleep, when it finally came, was fitful and filled with nightmares of crashing planes and angry soldiers. But for a few hours, they rested, and that was all that mattered.

They rose an hour before dawn, the forest still cloaked in a thick, chilly mist. The cabin felt even colder without the fire, but their spirits were somewhat renewed. Pierre carefully extinguished the last embers in the hearth, making sure there was no trace of their presence. The map, now carefully folded, was tucked into Pierre’s jacket pocket.

"Are we ready?" Dave asked, his voice low.

Pierre nodded. "As ready as we can be, American. Remember, extreme caution. If this safe house is indeed active, they will be as wary of strangers as we are. We must approach with care, signal our intentions clearly, and be prepared for anything. They will likely be hardened men, perhaps suspicious of anyone who simply walks in from the wilderness."

They stepped out of the cabin, leaving it as they had found it, a forgotten relic of another time. The mist hung heavy in the trees, obscuring visibility, but also offering them excellent cover. Pierre led the way, relying on the old map and his innate sense of direction, moving through the silent, ghostly forest.

The journey was long and arduous. The terrain was uneven, marked by hidden roots and slippery patches of mud. The mist made it difficult to see, blurring the edges of the trees and turning the forest into a shadowy, dreamlike landscape. Dave's legs ached, his muscles screamed in protest, and the limited rations from the cabin were long since a distant memory, leaving him feeling hollowed out once more.

Freddy, despite his previous burst of youthful energy, was clearly struggling again. He stumbled frequently, his face a pale mask of exhaustion. Dave put a supportive arm around his shoulder when he could, offering quiet words of encouragement. "Almost there, Freddy. Just keep putting one foot in front of the other."

The rumble of artillery fire seemed to grow steadily louder, a constant, menacing backdrop to their desperate journey. It was no longer just a distant throb; it was a deep, resonant growl that made the very ground beneath their feet tremble periodically. The war was closing in on them from the east. The pressure to reach the coast, to deliver their vital information, became even more intense.

As the morning wore on, the mist began to burn off, revealing a sky that was already beginning to cloud over. The air grew heavy, promising rain. Pierre, who had been consulting the map repeatedly, suddenly stopped. He pointed to a faint clearing ahead, obscured by a dense thicket of blackberry bushes.

"It should be there," Pierre whispered, his voice tight with anticipation. "The map marks it as a small, abandoned charcoal burner's hut. The safe house is said to be connected, perhaps a cellar, or an underground bunker."

They approached the clearing with extreme caution, their hearts thumping. Dave felt a knot of anxiety tighten in his stomach. This was it. The moment of truth. Would they find allies, or another trap?

Pushing through the thorny bushes, they emerged into a small, circular clearing. In the center, half-collapsed and clearly long disused, stood the remains of a charcoal burner's hut, its timber walls blackened and rotting. It looked utterly derelict, a place of ghosts.

"There is no one here," Freddy said, his voice laced with disappointment. "It's abandoned. Just like the cabin."

"No," Pierre corrected, his eyes narrowed, scanning the clearing intently. "Look closer. The path to the hut is overgrown, but there are signs. Faint, but they are there. And look at the ground." He pointed to a patch of disturbed earth near the hut's entrance, partially covered by leaves. "Footprints. Not fresh, but not ancient. Someone has been here recently."

He moved towards the hut, his movements slow and deliberate. He circled the structure, his hand brushing against the decaying timber. Then, he stopped at one side, near a particularly dense patch of ivy and brambles. He pushed aside the vegetation, revealing a crudely fashioned wooden door, almost indistinguishable from the hut's structure itself, but undeniably man-made, and recently at that.

"Here," Pierre breathed, his voice a low whisper. "This is it." He pulled out a small, worn piece of cloth from his pocket, tied it to a broken branch nearby, and then carefully, rhythmically, knocked three times on the hidden door. A pause. Then two more knocks. Then a final, single knock. It was a pre-arranged signal, a code.

The silence that followed was agonizing. Dave held his breath, his hand instinctively going to the small, worn pistol he had taken from the downed Messerschmitt pilot’s body. He was ready for anything.

After what felt like an eternity, a small, square peephole in the door slid open, revealing a single, wary eye peering out at them. The eye was dark, suspicious, and showed no hint of welcome.

Pierre stood still, his hands open and visible, a gesture of peace. "Friends," he said, his voice clear and firm, using a dialect of French that Dave didn’t quite understand. "We come seeking assistance. We are not German. We are escaping from the east. We carry vital information for the Allies."

The eye remained, unblinking, unmoving. The silence stretched again, thick with tension. Dave could feel Freddy trembling beside him.

Then, slowly, the peephole slid shut. They heard the faint clatter of bolts being withdrawn, a heavy wooden bar being lifted. The door creaked open, revealing a gaunt, heavily armed man with a grizzled beard and eyes that held the hard glint of one who had seen too much war. He was dressed in a patched and faded civilian coat, but the rifle he held was clean and menacing.

He looked at Pierre, then at Dave and Freddy, his gaze lingering on their disheveled appearances, their exhausted faces. "Who are you?" he demanded, his voice rough, suspicion thick in every syllable. "And what information do you carry that makes you so important?"

Pierre stepped forward, his resolve unwavering. "My name is Pierre. This is Dave, an American pilot, and Freddy, an English officer. We were shot down. We have escaped from behind German lines. We carry aerial reconnaissance photographs and documents that detail the enemy's deployment and strategic intentions along the Albert Canal and further south. Information that the Allied High Command must have, immediately."

The grizzled man's eyes widened almost imperceptibly at the mention of the Albert Canal. His gaze shifted to Dave, then to Freddy, a flicker of something new, a glimmer of respect, replacing some of the hard suspicion. He still held his rifle leveled, but the barrel was no longer quite so aimed at their chests.

"Aerial photographs, you say?" the man growled, still cautious. "And you have them now?"

Dave reached inside his flight jacket, slowly, carefully, pulling out the oilcloth-wrapped packet. He held it out, not to the man, but to Pierre, as a gesture of trust. Pierre took it, then presented it to the armed man.

The man took the packet, his fingers surprisingly gentle as he unwrapped the oilcloth. He peered at the folded maps and photos, his eyes, despite their weariness, quickly discerning the military significance of the detailed images. A low whistle escaped his lips.

"Come," he said, his voice losing some of its harshness, a grudging acceptance replacing the earlier hostility. "Come inside. But no tricks, or you will regret it. We have little to spare, but we will see what can be done. If this is true, then you are indeed important men. More important than you know, perhaps. The situation is... dire. Very dire indeed."

Dave felt a wave of profound relief wash over him, so potent it almost made him dizzy. They were in. They had made it. For now, they were safe. He exchanged a quick, weary glance with Freddy, a shared understanding passing between them. This was not the end of their ordeal, not by a long shot. But it was a beginning, a first step towards getting the vital information to those who could use it. The struggle was far from over, but at least now, they had a fighting chance.

I would feel deeply indebted to you two, if only I could feel anything at all anymore. But come. This is no place for a conversation of such gravity. We will speak further inside."

The Lieutenant's words hung in the air, a grim echo of the corporal's earlier despair. Dave and Freddy followed the Belgian soldiers, a silent procession through the deepening twilight. The sounds of distant shelling were no longer just distant, they were closer now, a rhythmic thudding that vibrated through the earth beneath their feet. They left the temporary camp with its hastily dug emplacements and continued deeper into the woods, the trees growing denser and darker around them, swallowing the last vestiges of daylight. The Lieutenant led them along a barely visible path, twisting and turning, until they reached a small, dilapidated farmhouse, almost entirely hidden by a thick curtain of ivy and overgrown bushes. A faint glow of lamplight seeped from a single window.

"Here," the Lieutenant said, his voice low. "This is one of our forward outposts. We use it for planning and to receive what little intelligence we gather. It is not much, but it is safe, for now."

He pushed open a heavy, creaking wooden door and ushered them inside. The room was small and sparsely furnished, dominated by a rough-hewn table in the center, littered with crumpled maps, half-empty bottles, and a few crusts of bread. A single oil lamp cast flickering shadows across the faces of three other men, older, tougher looking than the soldiers they had met outside. They sat huddled around the table, their eyes, weary and bloodshot, immediately turning towards the newcomers. One of them, a man with a thick, graying mustache and a missing front tooth, rose slowly, his hand resting instinctively on the holstered pistol at his hip.

"Lieutenant," he rumbled, his voice like gravel. "Who are these young men? And why are they here?"

The Lieutenant stepped forward, his own weariness momentarily forgotten. "These are Captain Dave, an American, and Lieutenant Freddy, an Englishman. They were shot down behind enemy lines, but they have escaped, and they bring with them information, vital information, I believe, that could change everything." He gestured towards Dave's flight jacket. "The documents you carry, Captain. Please."

Dave, still feeling the lingering effects of the crash and the constant tension, fumbled inside his jacket and produced the oilcloth-wrapped packet. He handed it to the Lieutenant, who in turn passed it to the man with the mustache. The man, whom the Lieutenant introduced as Sergeant Major Dubois, carefully unwrapped the oilcloth, his eyes scanning the contents with an intense, almost desperate eagerness. Freddy moved closer to the table, his earlier bravado replaced by a quiet, watchful demeanor. He glanced at Dave, then at the maps, a familiar sense of dread beginning to coil in his stomach.

Dubois spread the aerial photographs and intelligence reports across the table, smoothing out the crinkled edges with calloused fingers. The other two men leaned in, their faces grim, illuminated by the lamp's weak light. The silence in the room was absolute, broken only by the crackle of the lamp and the distant, persistent thrum of German artillery. As Dubois’s eyes moved across the detailed images, a low growl escaped his throat, a sound of bitter recognition. He pointed a thick finger at one photograph, a close-up of German panzer divisions forming up near a small village.

"Mon Dieu," he breathed, the words barely audible. "It is worse than we thought. Much, much worse. Look at this concentration of armor. And here, these new bridges they have laid across the canal, not just one or two, but many, ready for a full-scale assault. Our intelligence reports spoke of a probing attack, perhaps a limited advance, but this... this is an invasion, a tidal wave."

He looked up at Dave and Freddy, his face etched with a despair that went beyond mere fatigue. "You say you were prisoners in Germany, that you saw these plans, these deployments? This is not just a tactical map; this is their grand strategy, their push to overwhelm us completely."

"Yes, Sergeant Major," Dave said, his voice hoarse from disuse. "We saw them. They are moving with incredible speed and force. They aim to break through and sweep towards the coast, cutting off the British and French armies before they can properly deploy."

Freddy stepped forward, his eyes burning with renewed urgency. "We believe they intend to capture the Channel ports, trapping the Allied forces against the sea. This information, if it reaches the Allied High Command, could allow them to anticipate the German moves, to redeploy their forces, to reinforce the critical sectors."

Dubois slammed his fist lightly on the table, a sound of frustration. "If it reaches them! We are cut off, young men. Completely cut off. Our communications are down, our lines are collapsing, and the sky is black with their damn planes. How do you propose we get this information to them? It would be a suicide mission to try and break through to our own lines, let alone to French or British headquarters." He swept his hand across the table, indicating the maps. "This information, vital as it is, may as well be written on the moon for all the good it will do us here."

The Lieutenant, who had been listening intently, now spoke up. "Sergeant Major, with all due respect, we cannot simply give up. This is our last hope, perhaps. If what they say is true, if this is truly the blueprint for the entire German offensive, then we must make every effort to get it to our commanders. To the British, to the French. They must know." He turned to Dave. "Captain, you are a pilot. Do you think there is any possibility of getting a plane, perhaps a reconnaissance plane, to fly this out?"

Dave shook his head, a grim certainty in his eyes. "Not from here, Lieutenant. Our plane was destroyed. And even if we found one, the skies are crawling with Messerschmitts. It would be suicide, as the Sergeant Major said. A one-way ticket for whoever tried it."

"Then what?" Dubois demanded, his frustration bubbling to the surface. "We fight, yes. We die, yes. But we die knowing that the crucial information that could have saved tens of thousands of lives, that could have turned the tide, rots here in a forgotten farmhouse in the middle of nowhere."

Freddy suddenly straightened, his young face set with determination. "There must be a way. There is always a way. We have come too far, escaped too much, seen too much, to let it end here. We must get this to them, no matter the risk. We are not just fighting for Belgium, Sergeant Major. We are fighting for Europe, for everything."

The older men looked at the young Englishman, a flicker of grudging respect in their tired eyes. His passion was a stark contrast to their own bone-deep weariness.

"He speaks with fire, this one," one of the other men murmured, "But fire alone will not open a path through the Boche lines."

"No," Dave agreed, "but intelligence and a clear head might. We need a plan. A detailed plan. What do you know of the lines to the west? Where are the gaps, the weak points? Where are the British and French deploying their main forces? We need to aim for a specific target, not just a general direction."

Dubois ran a hand over his grizzled chin, his eyes still fixed on the maps. "The British Expeditionary Force is supposed to be moving into Belgium, along the Dyle Line. The French are to their south. But with this speed of German advance, who knows where they truly are now? Our intelligence is almost non-existent beyond our immediate sector. We are a tiny island in a German sea."

"But there must be pockets of resistance, other communication lines, even unofficial ones," Freddy insisted. "Small groups of scouts, runners, anyone who can get through. We need to find one of those paths, however dangerous."

The Lieutenant looked at Dubois, a question in his eyes. The Sergeant Major sighed, a heavy, world-weary sound. "There is one path, perhaps. A route through the Ardennes, south of the main German thrust. It is treacherous, thinly held by our own scattered remnants, and even thinner by the French cavalry, if they are still there. The Germans consider the Ardennes impassable for their main armor, so it is less heavily patrolled. But it is a long way. And there are still German patrols, reconnaissance units, and always the risk of being caught. It is a gamble, a very long shot."

"A gamble we must take," Dave said, his mind already racing, weighing the risks against the immense importance of their mission. "Show us the way, Sergeant Major. Give us whatever you can spare in the way of provisions, a compass, any local maps you might have. We will go."

The Lieutenant nodded. "Sergeant Major, fetch what you can. And Lieutenant Dubois, you know the Ardennes better than any man here. Can you spare one of your men, a guide, someone who knows the hidden paths?"

Dubois looked at his remaining men, then back at Dave and Freddy. "It will be a man's life, Lieutenant. A young man's life."

"Perhaps not," Dave countered, "if we are careful, if we are smart. This information, if it gets through, could save many more lives than the one we risk."

Dubois hesitated, then nodded slowly. "Very well. I will give you Marcel. He is young, but he is agile, and he knows every rock and tree in that accursed forest. He is also a crack shot. He will not speak much, but he will not fail you." He turned to one of the other men. "Go, fetch Marcel. Tell him he has a mission of the utmost importance."

The man rose and disappeared through a back door, a new sense of purpose in his stride. Dubois then turned back to the table, his earlier despair giving way to a grim determination. He picked up a stubby pencil and began marking a new route on one of the crumpled maps.

"Here," he said, tapping a point deep within the Ardennes. "You will aim for Dinant. If the French hold Dinant, you have a chance. From there, you might find a way to their headquarters. But the journey to Dinant itself will be fraught with peril. You will move only at night, you will avoid all roads, and you will trust no one you do not already know. We have reports of German infiltrators, even some who wear Belgian uniforms. The enemy is everywhere."

He outlined a winding, difficult path, crisscrossing rivers and climbing hills. "It will take you at least three days, perhaps more, depending on what you encounter. Three days and nights of constant movement, with little rest. Are you up to it?"

Dave looked at Freddy, whose face, though pale with exhaustion, was alight with resolve. "We are up to it, Sergeant Major," Dave said firmly. "We have to be."

As Dubois continued to brief them on the details of the route, Marcel, a lean young man with sharp, intelligent eyes, entered the room. He carried a rifle and a small haversack. He looked at Dave and Freddy with a calm, assessing gaze.

"Marcel," Dubois introduced him, "these are the men you will guide. They carry intelligence that must reach the Allied High Command. Your mission is to get them to Dinant, or as close as you can manage safely. Their survival, and the information they carry, is paramount."

Marcel merely nodded, his gaze unwavering. "Oui, Sergeant Major. I understand."

The next hour was spent in preparation. Dubois and the Lieutenant scrounged up what they could: a small quantity of dried bread and some hard cheese, two canteens of water, a more reliable compass, and a detailed map of the Ardennes region, albeit an old one. They also provided them with extra ammunition for Dave's and Freddy's pistols, and a small, wickedly sharp hunting knife for each of them.

"You will need to travel light," the Lieutenant instructed, "and move fast. Every extra gram will weigh you down."

Dave wrapped the vital documents securely in the oilcloth again, then tucked the packet deep inside his flight jacket, close to his body. Freddy, ever practical, checked his own pistol, its familiar weight a small comfort. The weight of the mission, however, was immense.

As they made their final preparations, the sounds of battle outside seemed to grow louder, more insistent. The thud of artillery was now accompanied by the sharper crackle of machine-gun fire, closer than before. The German tidal wave was indeed crashing down, closing in on them even as they spoke.

"It is time," Dubois said, his face grim. "Go quickly. May God be with you."

With a final, desperate handshake, Dave and Freddy, accompanied by Marcel, slipped out the back door of the farmhouse, swallowed by the inky blackness of the Ardennes night. The air was cool and damp, carrying the scent of wet earth and distant smoke. Marcel moved like a shadow, his footsteps almost silent, leading them deeper into the maze of trees. Dave and Freddy followed close behind, their senses on high alert, every rustle, every snap of a twig, magnified in the oppressive darkness.

They walked for hours, their pace relentless, the only sounds the crunch of leaves underfoot and their own strained breathing. Marcel led them through thickets of thorny bushes, across shallow streams, and up steep, muddy inclines. The exhaustion that had been a dull ache now became a searing pain in Dave's muscles, but he pushed it down, focusing on the mission. Freddy, despite his youth, matched their pace, his eyes constantly scanning the shadows around them. The forest was alive with unseen things, and every rustle could be a patrol, every glimmer of light a German camp.

Around midnight, Marcel signaled for them to stop. He pointed silently to a faint glow in the distance, a dull orange smudge against the horizon. "German patrols," he whispered, his voice barely audible. "They are everywhere. We must go around, a long detour."

They veered sharply to the south, adding precious miles to their already arduous journey. The detour took them through a particularly dense part of the forest, where the trees grew so close together that no moonlight could penetrate. Dave stumbled repeatedly, catching himself on low-hanging branches, his hands scraped and bruised. Freddy, too, was struggling, but he never complained, his teeth gritted in silent determination.

Just before dawn, Marcel found a small, hidden hollow, shielded by a cluster of ancient oak trees. "We will rest here for a few hours," he whispered. "It is not safe to move in daylight. The Germans have air reconnaissance, and their ground patrols are more active when they can see."

They collapsed onto the damp earth, their bodies screaming in protest. Dave pulled out the dried bread and cheese, and they shared it in silence, washing it down with meager sips of water. The food tasted like ash in Dave's mouth, but it was sustenance, and he knew they needed every calorie. As he ate, he could hear the distant rumble of German trucks, the faint barking of dogs, and the ever-present thud of artillery. The war was never far away.

He looked at Freddy, who was already struggling to stay awake, his head drooping onto his chest. "Get some sleep, kid," Dave murmured. "We'll need all our strength for tonight."

Freddy nodded, managing a weak smile. "You too, Dave. Don't worry. We'll make it. We have to."

Dave tried to sleep, but his mind refused to shut down. He replayed the images of the German maps, the overwhelming force marshaled for the invasion. He thought of the desperate faces of the Belgian soldiers, their worn uniforms, their shattered hopes. And he thought of the implications, the sheer scale of the disaster that would unfold if this information did not reach the Allied High Command in time. He clenched his fists, the resolve hardening within him. They *would* make it.

Hours later, Marcel gently nudged them awake. The sun was setting, painting the sky in fiery hues that ironically mirrored the destruction below. The sounds of battle seemed to have intensified, a constant roar that filled the air.

"It is time to move," Marcel said, his voice flat. "The Boche are pushing hard. We must hurry."

They rose, their muscles stiff and aching, but their resolve undimmed. The second night was even harder than the first. They encountered more German patrols, forcing them into longer, more circuitous detours. At one point, they had to hide in a ditch for nearly an hour as a column of German infantry marched past, their heavy boots crunching on the path just yards away. Dave could almost feel their presence, the grim efficiency of the enemy, their seemingly endless numbers.

Freddy, despite his fatigue, remained alert. He pointed out a faint shimmer of light through the trees. "Flicker of a cigarette, Dave. Over there."

Marcel nodded, drawing his rifle silently to his shoulder. They skirted the area widely, moving even more cautiously. Dave felt a surge of pride in the young Englishman. Freddy was transforming before his very eyes, growing from a somewhat impetuous youth into a hardened, resourceful soldier.

They passed through several deserted villages, the houses standing like hollowed-out eyesores, their windows shattered, doors hanging open. The silence in these places was eerie, more terrifying than the sounds of battle, a testament to the sudden, brutal flight of their inhabitants. In one village, they found a small, abandoned general store. Marcel, after a careful reconnaissance, slipped inside and returned a few minutes later with a handful of chocolate bars and some more dried fruit, a welcome boost to their dwindling provisions.

"A small victory," Marcel said, a rare, fleeting smile touching his lips. "But we must not linger."

As they pressed on, the terrain became more rugged, a series of steep hills and narrow ravines. The air grew colder, and a fine, persistent drizzle began to fall, turning the already treacherous paths into slippery mud slides. Dave slipped and fell twice, his hands sinking into the mire, but he scrambled back up, his teeth gritted, refusing to let the elements defeat him. Freddy was likewise covered in mud, his uniform torn in several places, but his pace remained steady.

Towards the end of the second night, they heard the unmistakable roar of German tanks. The sound was much closer now, a terrifying rumble that vibrated through their very bones. Marcel brought them to an abrupt halt, pushing them down behind a thick bush.

"Panzers," he whispered, his face grim in the faint pre-dawn light. "They are establishing a new line, pushing even further west than we thought. They are moving fast. Dinant... it may already be compromised."

Dave's heart sank. Dinant was their target, their lifeline. If the Germans had already reached it, their mission would become infinitely more difficult, perhaps impossible. The crucial documents would be useless if there was no one left to receive them, no functional Allied command structure to respond.

They watched in tense silence as the massive, angular shapes of the German tanks, followed by troop carriers, rumbled through a clearing about half a mile away. The sheer destructive power they represented was overwhelming, a stark reminder of the German superiority in arms and numbers. Dave felt a cold knot of fear tighten in his stomach, but he forced it down. Despair was a luxury they could not afford.

When the column had passed, leaving behind a lingering smell of diesel and exhaust, Marcel turned to them, his face serious. "We must change our plan. Dinant may be gone. We must aim further south, or try to skirt their advance. We need to find a way to cross the Meuse River, and then try to link up with any remaining French units. It is a much longer, more dangerous journey."

"But is there any other way?" Freddy asked, his voice betraying a hint of desperation.

Marcel shook his head. "None that I know. The main British and French forces are being encircled. Our only chance is to go wide, to try and get around this new German thrust."

"Then we go wide," Dave said, his voice firm, unwavering. "Lead the way, Marcel. Just get us to someone, anyone, who can get this information to the High Command."

They pressed on, pushing their exhausted bodies beyond what Dave thought possible. The third day dawned, bringing with it a temporary lull in the drizzle, but no respite from the gnawing hunger and fatigue. They moved cautiously, constantly alert for patrols, their senses heightened by the sheer danger of their situation. The landscape had changed now, becoming more open in places, with rolling hills and scattered farms, making concealment more difficult.

In the late afternoon, as they cautiously approached a small rise, Marcel suddenly froze. He held up a hand, signaling them to stop and crouch low. Dave and Freddy immediately dropped, their hearts hammering in their chests. Marcel pointed towards a farmhouse nestled in the valley below, a thin wisp of smoke rising from its chimney.

"German outpost," he whispered. "I see a sentry. And there are vehicles. Too many. We cannot pass this way."

"Then we go around, again," Freddy said, his voice tight.

"No," Marcel said, his eyes scanning the horizon. "We are too exposed here. We need to get across this open ground quickly. There is a small bridge over the Meuse about five kilometers from here, but it will be heavily guarded now. This is a choke point."

Dave looked at the farmhouse, then at the open fields stretching before them. The sun was beginning to set, casting long, deceptive shadows. If they waited until dark, they risked being spotted by German night patrols. If they tried to cross now, they risked being caught in the open.

"We need a distraction," Dave said, his mind working furiously. "Something to draw their attention away from the bridge, or from our crossing point."

Marcel shook his head. "We have nothing, Captain. Only our rifles and our resolve."

"And the invaluable information we carry," Freddy added. "We cannot let them capture us. Not now."

Dave looked at the farmhouse again, a desperate idea forming in his mind. "How about that farmhouse, Marcel? Is it possible for one of us to create a diversion there? Perhaps draw a patrol away, or cause enough noise to make them send a detail to investigate, while the others slip past?"

Marcel considered it, his brow furrowed in thought. "It is a desperate plan, Captain. Whoever goes would almost certainly be captured, or worse. The Germans are ruthless with those who resist them."

"I will go," Freddy said immediately, his voice ringing with conviction. "I am smaller, faster. I can get in and out quickly, create a commotion, and draw their fire. You and Marcel can use the opportunity to get across the river and continue with the documents."

"No, Freddy," Dave countered, a cold dread washing over him. "That's too risky. You're too valuable. Your knowledge of their plans, your understanding of the documents, your language skills... if anything happened to you, it would be a huge loss."

"And what about you, Dave?" Freddy retorted, a defiant spark in his eyes. "You're the pilot, the one who saw it all firsthand. You're just as valuable."

"We need both of us to reach the High Command," Dave said, "but if one of us has to risk capture, it should be the one less likely to talk, or who knows less of the crucial details. Besides, you're better at sneaking around than I am, and faster. You've proven it many times already."

"Then let me try," Marcel interjected, his voice quiet but firm. "It is my country, my duty. I know these lands, these people. I can make a better diversion, perhaps even escape in the confusion. You two must get through. The documents are everything."

Dave looked at Marcel, then at Freddy. Both were offering to sacrifice themselves for the mission, for the greater good. It was a terrible choice, a burden no leader should have to bear. But time was running out. The sun was dipping lower, and the German advance was relentless.

"Alright, Marcel," Dave said, his voice heavy. "What's your plan? How will you do it?"

Marcel nodded, his face grimly resolute. "There is a small barn behind the farmhouse, filled with hay. If I can get to it, I can set it ablaze. The smoke and the fire would surely draw their attention, force them to divert men to extinguish it. It would create enough chaos for you two to make a dash for the bridge, or find another crossing point."

"But it's heavily guarded," Freddy argued. "They'll shoot you on sight."

"I am fast," Marcel replied, a flicker of defiance in his eyes. "And I know the cover, the blind spots. I will draw their fire, yes, but I will try to lead them away, into the woods. Give you a better chance to escape."

Dave looked at the determined young Belgian, a knot of admiration and fear in his throat. He knew Marcel was right. It was their best, perhaps only, chance. "How long will you need? And when should we make our move?"

"As soon as the barn begins to burn," Marcel said. "Do not wait. Go. Do not look back. Your mission is paramount. I will try to meet you on the other side, but do not expect me. Just go." He handed Dave his extra pistol, a small German P-38 he had apparently taken from a fallen enemy. "You will need this more than I. I have my rifle and my knife."

Dave took the pistol, the cold metal a stark contrast to the warmth of Marcel's hand. "Thank you, Marcel," he said, his voice thick with emotion. "May God be with you."

Marcel merely nodded, a faint, sad smile touching his lips. He slipped off his haversack, leaving it with them, a gesture that spoke volumes. Then, with a final, almost imperceptible nod, he melted into the lengthening shadows, moving swiftly and silently towards the farmhouse.

Dave and Freddy watched him go, their hearts pounding. The silence was deafening, broken only by the distant sounds of war. Every minute stretched into an eternity. Dave gripped the P-38, its weight a new, unwelcome presence in his hand. He glanced at Freddy, whose young face was drawn with worry and anticipation.

"He's a brave man," Freddy whispered, his voice barely audible.

"The bravest," Dave agreed, his eyes fixed on the farmhouse. "Now, we hold our breath, and we get ready to run."

They waited, crouched low in the fading light, their muscles tense, their senses stretched to their breaking point. The air grew colder, and the last streaks of orange vanished from the sky, plunging the valley into a murky twilight. The sounds from the farmhouse were faint: a low murmur of voices, the occasional clink of metal.

Then, a sudden, sharp crackle. A small, orange glow appeared behind the farmhouse, quickly growing, licking at the dry hay in the barn. Within seconds, a column of thick, black smoke began to billow upwards, illuminated by the rapidly spreading flames. The fire caught quickly, a roaring inferno against the darkening sky.

Immediately, shouts erupted from the farmhouse. Figures burst out, silhouetted against the blaze, shouting orders in German. The unmistakable crack of rifle fire followed, aimed towards the source of the commotion, towards where Marcel had disappeared.

"Now!" Dave yelled, his voice hoarse. "Run, Freddy, run!"

They scrambled to their feet, their exhaustion momentarily forgotten, fueled by adrenaline and a desperate sense of urgency. They plunged down the slope, dodging trees and bushes, their feet slipping in the mud. The sounds of the fire and the German shouts and gunfire grew louder behind them, a terrifying symphony of destruction. They ran with everything they had, driven by the knowledge that Marcel was buying them precious time, time they could not waste.

They reached the valley floor, still running, their lungs burning, their legs aching. The Meuse River was a dark, ribboning presence in the gloom. They could just make out the silhouette of a small, narrow bridge in the distance, a fragile lifeline across the swirling waters.

"Over there!" Freddy gasped, pointing towards the bridge. "We have to get to it!"

But even as he spoke, a new sound cut through the chaos: the rattle of a machine gun, closer than before, spraying bullets towards the area where they had just emerged from the woods. German voices, angry and sharp, carried on the wind.

"They've spotted us!" Dave yelled, pulling Freddy down into a shallow ditch. Bullets whizzed overhead, kicking up mud just inches from their hiding place.

"They must have patrols covering the river already!" Freddy cried, his face pale.

Dave peered over the edge of the ditch. The machine gun was positioned on the far bank of the Meuse, its tracer fire illuminating the darkness in brief, terrifying arcs. There was no way to cross the bridge without being cut down. Marcel's sacrifice might have drawn some attention, but not enough to clear their path entirely.

"We can't cross the bridge," Dave said, his mind racing. "It's a death trap. We have to find another way. We have to swim."

Freddy's eyes widened. "Swim? In this river? It's freezing, Dave, and the current is strong. We'll be swept away, or drowned."

"We have no choice, Freddy," Dave insisted, pulling the documents from inside his jacket and checking the oilcloth wrapping. "This is our only option. The documents are waterproof, and we'll have to tie them securely to us. Can you swim?"

Freddy nodded, though reluctantly. "Yes, but not in these conditions. And the cold... it will kill us."

"The Germans will kill us faster if we stay here," Dave countered, his voice grim. "We have to try. The river might offer some cover, and the current might even help us get further downstream, away from their patrols."

They stripped off their heavy boots and jackets, bundling the documents and their pistols inside the oilcloth bundle, then tying it securely to Dave's chest. Dave checked the knot twice, three times, making sure it would hold. Freddy likewise secured his smaller bundle. The cold air immediately bit into their skin.

"Stay together," Dave instructed, his voice low. "Don't fight the current too much. Let it carry you, but try to angle towards the far bank. We need to get out of this open area as fast as possible."

With one last glance back at the burning barn, a testament to Marcel's courage and sacrifice, they plunged into the icy waters of the Meuse. The shock of the cold was immediate and brutal, stealing their breath, making their muscles seize up. Dave gasped, fighting to keep his head above the swirling water, his limbs numb. Freddy was beside him, struggling just as much, his face a mask of pain.
