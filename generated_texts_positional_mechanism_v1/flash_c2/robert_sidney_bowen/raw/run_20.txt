"Starboard engine sputtered, sir!" Harrison shouted, his voice tight with alarm. "Trying to clear it! Fuel feed, I think! It's recovering, sir, it's recovering!"

The engines roared back to full power, but the incident had been a stark reminder of their vulnerability. A sudden chill gripped Dawson. Was it the damaged fuselage affecting something else? Or was it just another cruel twist of fate, as the colonel had mentioned earlier?

"Anything on radar, Harrison?" Welsh asked.

"Negative, sir," the pilot replied, a note of frustrated urgency in his voice. "Still nothing. He's either gone, or he’s playing a very clever game."

"He's playing a clever game," Welsh murmured, almost to himself. "He knows what he's looking for. He won't give up easily."

As if on cue, a sudden, bright flash erupted far behind them, a momentary star in the inky blackness. It was followed by a faint, distant rumble, too faint to be a direct hit, but too distinct to be ignored.

"What was that?" Farmer gasped, pointing a trembling finger.

"Looks like an explosion, sir!" the tail gunner’s voice reported over the intercom, a new edge of excitement in his tone. "Far astern, very faint, but I think I saw a flash! Maybe the Hun bought it after all?"

"Don't count your chickens, son," Colonel Welsh advised, though his eyes narrowed in thought. "He might have jettisoned something, or it could be debris from an earlier engagement. Keep scanning. Keep alert."

The momentary surge of hope that had risen in Dawson’s chest deflated. It was true. Hope was a dangerous thing in this game. He forced himself to take a deep breath, trying to calm the frantic beating of his heart. The B-25 continued its relentless eastward flight, a lonely metal bird carving its way through the night, a silent prayer whispered in its wake.

Minutes crawled by, each one laden with suspense. The B-25 held its course, maintaining altitude, its engines a steady drone that somehow managed to be both comforting and terrifying. The cold knot in Dawson's stomach persisted, a constant reminder of the fuel seeping away, droplet by droplet, into the vast emptiness below.

"Colonel," Harrison's voice came through, calmer now, though still with an underlying tension. "I'm not picking up any more sputtering from the starboard engine. Looks like it cleared. But about that fuel leak. I've run some calculations. If this leak continues at the current rate, and we maintain this speed and altitude, we'll be very tight for Casablanca. We might even be short by a considerable margin."

"How considerable, Harrison?" Welsh asked, his voice flat.

"Fifty to seventy-five miles, sir," Harrison admitted, and the number hit Dawson like a physical blow. Seventy-five miles over open ocean was an eternity, a death sentence.

"Options?"

"We could try to reduce speed and altitude, sir, but that makes us an easier target if he's still out there. Or we could try to put down at some unscheduled point, but there's nothing out here for hundreds of miles, except the sea."

"And the leak cannot be stopped or even slowed?"

"Not from inside, sir. Not without someone going out onto the wing, and that's suicide at this speed and altitude, especially at night."

Colonel Welsh was silent for a long moment, his gaze once again fixed on the impenetrable darkness beyond the compartment window. Dawson watched him, mesmerized by the man's unflappable demeanor. He was the Chief of all U. S. Intelligence, a man accustomed to making life and death decisions, but this felt different. This was not a chess game on a map. This was real.

"No," Welsh finally said, his voice firm. "We maintain speed and altitude. We cannot give that Messerschmitt any advantage if it decides to return. And we cannot risk landing anywhere but Casablanca. That is the plan, and we stick to it as long as humanly possible."

"But, sir," Dawson began, unable to hold back, "if we're short by seventy-five miles, what then?"

Welsh turned his gaze upon Dawson, his eyes serious but not unkind. "Then, Dawson, we will have done our duty. But we are not there yet. We still have options, however slim they may be. And we will explore every single one of them. Harrison, push the engines for maximum efficiency, but do not sacrifice power. We need every ounce of thrust we can get, and every drop of fuel we can save."

"Understood, sir," Harrison acknowledged.

The next hour was a brutal test of nerves. The air in the compartment grew heavy with unspoken fears, with the lingering scent of combat, and with the faint, almost imperceptible smell of aviation fuel that now seemed to permeate everything. Dawson kept checking his watch, as if by willing the hands to move faster, he could somehow shorten the distance to Casablanca. Farmer, next to him, had closed his eyes, his breathing shallow, his face still pale. Even Colonel Welsh, though outwardly composed, seemed to have retreated into himself, his jaw set in a grim line.

Then, just as the tension was becoming unbearable, a new voice broke the silence. "Radar contact, sir!" It was the radio operator, his voice sharp with renewed urgency. "Bearing one-eight-zero, closing fast! Looks like another fighter!"

Dawson's heart leaped into his throat. Farmer’s eyes snapped open. Colonel Welsh leaned forward, his hands clenching into fists.

"Confirmation, Harrison?" Welsh demanded.

"Confirming, sir!" Harrison’s voice was grim. "He's coming up from below and behind, same approach as the last one! He must have been waiting, or he’s part of a two-pronged attack!"

"Damn them," Welsh muttered, his voice barely audible. "They know. They absolutely know this plane is important."

"Tail gunner, report!" Harrison barked into his intercom.

"He's there, sir!" the tail gunner replied, his voice already strained. "Looks like a Messerschmitt! Coming in fast! He's not opening fire yet, just closing the gap!"

"Prepare for evasive action, Harrison!" Welsh ordered. "Pilot, what's our current status?"

"Engines running strong, sir, despite the earlier sputter. Rudder is still a bit stiff but responsive. But the fuel leak is definitely continuing. We're losing it at a steady rate."

"Then we fight him off and conserve as much as we can!" Welsh commanded. "Tail gunner, give him everything you’ve got the moment he's in range! Harrison, keep him guessing. Don't let him get a lock!"

The B-25 lurched violently as Harrison threw it into a sharp, banking turn. Dawson was thrown against the bulkhead, his head snapping back. Farmer cried out, clutching the edge of his seat with white knuckles. Outside, the world spun in a dizzying blur of blackness and fleeting stars.

"He's on our six, sir!" the tail gunner yelled, his voice thick with exertion. "He's getting ready to fire!"

Suddenly, the staccato bark of the .50 caliber machine guns erupted from the tail turret, a furious, desperate burst of lead into the darkness behind them. The Messerschmitt, a dark silhouette against the faint glow of the distant horizon, was illuminated for a brief, terrifying second by the tracer fire.

"He's closing! He's too close!"

Then, a series of sharp, metallic impacts ripped through the fuselage. The B-25 shuddered again, more violently this time. A shower of sparks flew from somewhere near the rear of the compartment. The smell of burning insulation and scorching metal joined the lingering scent of cordite.

"We're hit again, sir!" Harrison reported, his voice tight. "Damage report coming in! Rudder control is sloppier now! I'm fighting it!"

"Tail gunner, status!" Welsh bellowed.

"He's breaking off, sir!" the gunner shouted back, his voice ragged with relief and exertion. "I got some hits on him, I think! He's pulling away, climbing hard!"

"Good man!" Welsh said, a note of satisfaction in his voice. "Harrison, what's our damage?"

"Rear fuselage took another beating, sir," Harrison replied. "The rudder cables might be partially severed, it's very mushy now. And the fuel leak, sir... it's worse. Much worse. The impact must have widened the rupture."

A cold dread seeped into Dawson’s bones. "Worse?" he echoed, his voice barely a whisper.

Colonel Welsh ignored him, his face a mask of grim determination. "Harrison, what are our chances now?"

"With the rudder compromised and the increased fuel leak, sir, I don't see us making Casablanca. Not at this rate. We're losing fuel too fast. We'll be lucky to stay airborne for another hour, maybe ninety minutes at best."

The words hung in the air, a death knell. Ninety minutes. Over the vast, indifferent ocean. Dawson felt a wave of nausea wash over him. Farmer had buried his face in his hands, his shoulders shaking.

"Is there any way to reduce drag, Harrison?" Welsh asked, his voice still unnervingly calm. "Anything to conserve fuel, even marginally?"

"We could jettison unnecessary equipment, sir," Harrison offered, "but we don't have much superfluous weight. We're already running fairly light for a bomber of this class on a long-range mission. And the rudder problem is the critical one. Without proper control, we're flying a crippled bird. Even if we had the fuel, landing would be extremely hazardous."

"What about a dive, sir?" Dawson blurted out, a desperate thought sparking in his mind. "A sharp, rapid descent. Would that put out any fires, or perhaps reduce the leak temporarily due to pressure changes?"

Colonel Welsh looked at him, a flicker of something unreadable in his eyes. "A risky maneuver, Dawson. It could tear us apart if the fuselage is too weak. And it might make the rudder problem worse. Harrison, your professional opinion?"

"It's a gamble, sir," the pilot conceded. "But it might jar something, or at least change the pressure dynamics. It’s certainly not standard procedure for a damaged aircraft. It would put immense stress on the airframe. But it's an option. A last resort option."

"And if we don't try something, Harrison, what then?" Welsh asked.

"Then we run out of fuel, sir, and we ditch in the Atlantic," Harrison stated flatly. "And a B-25 doesn't float for long."

The stark reality of their situation settled upon them like a shroud. Ditching in the freezing North Atlantic at night was a sentence of death, swift and unforgiving.

"Alright, Harrison," Colonel Welsh said, his voice ringing with authority. "Prepare for a controlled dive. Make it as shallow as possible to start, gradually increasing the angle if the airframe holds. We're not trying to break records, we're trying to save this aircraft and this mission."

"Understood, sir," Harrison responded, a hint of steel in his voice. "Strap in everyone! This is going to be rough!"

Dawson fumbled with his safety straps, pulling them tight. Farmer, his face white as a sheet, did the same. The cabin lights flickered, a testament to the stresses already on the aircraft's systems. The B-25 leveled out for a moment, then Harrison pushed the nose down.

The plane immediately pitched forward, the engines screaming as they fought against the increasing speed. The world outside became a terrifying blur, the blackness rushing up at them with alarming speed. The pressure on Dawson’s body increased, pushing him down into his seat. The smell of burning grew stronger, a acrid, metallic odor that stung his nostrils.

"Rudder's still unresponsive, sir!" Harrison grunted, his voice strained. "And the leak... it seems to be getting worse with the G-forces!"

A cold wave of despair washed over Dawson. It wasn’t working. They were only hastening their end. He glanced at Colonel Welsh, expecting to see defeat in his eyes, but found only grim resolve.

Suddenly, a loud, wrenching metallic shriek ripped through the aircraft. It was followed by a series of bangs and grinding noises that made Dawson’s teeth ache. The B-25 bucked violently, throwing them all against their restraints.

"What was that?" Farmer shrieked, his voice barely audible over the roar of the dive.

"Something just ripped free from the tail section, sir!" the tail gunner reported, his voice filled with terror. "Part of the fuselage! Oh my God, we're tearing apart!"

"Pull up, Harrison!" Welsh roared, his voice cutting through the chaos. "Pull up! Full power!"

Harrison, despite the failing controls, wrestled with the yoke. The engines screamed anew, straining to claw the B-25 back from its uncontrolled descent. Slowly, agonizingly slowly, the nose began to come up. The G-forces decreased, and Dawson felt a momentary reprieve from the crushing pressure.

The immediate danger of the dive was past, but they were still plummeting, though at a shallower angle now. The roar of the wind rushing through the torn fuselage was deafening. The compartment was now filled with a swirling mist of vaporized fuel and fine metal dust.

"Damage report, Harrison!" Welsh demanded, his voice hoarse.

"Rear fuselage is severely compromised, sir!" Harrison gasped, clearly fighting for control. "I’ve got almost no rudder authority left! We're wallowing! We’re losing altitude rapidly, and the fuel leak is now a torrent, sir! We're losing fuel by the gallon!"

"What about the engines?"

"They're still pushing, sir, but we're too heavy with the drag. I can’t maintain altitude for long!"

Dawson felt a chilling certainty. This was it. The end of the line. All their efforts, all their courage, had come to this. He looked at Farmer, whose face was pale and streaked with dust, his eyes wide with fear.

Colonel Welsh was looking out the window, his eyes scanning the horizon, not for an enemy, but for salvation. "Is there any land?" he asked, his voice low but steady. "Anywhere near?"

"Negative, sir," Harrison replied, his voice barely a whisper now. "We're over open ocean. Nothing but water for hundreds of miles."

A profound silence fell, broken only by the shriek of the wind through the damaged fuselage and the increasingly strained roar of the engines. They were falling, slowly but inexorably, towards the dark, hungry waves below.

"Colonel," Dawson said, his voice surprisingly calm despite the turmoil within him. "What about the President? Did we... did we draw them away?"

Welsh turned, a flicker of something akin to pride in his weary eyes. "We did our job, Dawson. This plane has drawn their attention. If they believed the President was aboard, then our mission has succeeded, whatever happens to us."

"But if we don't make it," Farmer began, his voice trembling, "they'll know the President wasn't on board after all. They'll know it was a trick."

"No," Welsh said, shaking his head. "They will simply believe we were shot down, that their efforts were successful. They will believe they destroyed the President's plane. And that belief will buy the real mission the time it needs. Our sacrifice, if it comes to that, will not be in vain."

Dawson looked at the stern, unyielding face of the Intelligence Chief, and a strange sense of peace settled over him. They were going to die, but they would die having completed their mission. It was a grim comfort, but a comfort nonetheless.

"Colonel," Harrison's voice broke through, sharper now, more urgent. "I'm detecting something on the radar. Very faint. Bearing zero-nine-zero. It's... it's a ship, sir! And it's big!"

Everyone in the compartment stiffened. A ship? Out here?

"Confirmation, Harrison!" Welsh ordered, his voice suddenly infused with new energy.

"Affirmative, sir!" Harrison almost shouted. "It's moving. A large vessel, definitely! And we're going to pass right over it if we continue our current trajectory and descent rate!"

A carrier. The word held a glimmer of hope so fragile it felt dangerous to even acknowledge.

"Harrison," Welsh commanded, "can you nurse us to that ship? Can you get us low enough to signal them, or even attempt a ditching near them?"

"I can try, sir," Harrison said, his voice filled with renewed determination. "It's a long shot, with the rudder control almost gone and the fuel vanishing, but we've got a chance. A very small chance."

The crippled B-25 groaned and shuddered, a dying beast fighting its last fight against the unyielding forces of gravity and the vast, dark ocean below. Harrison, hunched over the controls, wrestled with the unresponsive rudder, his face a mask of intense concentration, illuminated faintly by the instrument lights. The roar of the straining engines, punctuated by an intermittent cough and sputter, filled the compartment, making conversation almost impossible without shouting. Every vibration that racked the airframe seemed to rattle the very bones of Dawson and Farmer, who braced themselves, strapped tightly into their seats, their eyes fixed on the determined back of the pilot.
