The journey was a slow, jarring ordeal. The truck bounced and swayed over cratered roads and muddy tracks, sending them lurching against each other and the hard sides of the vehicle. The air was frigid, cutting through their thin flying suits, and the constant roar of the engine made conversation impossible. The landscape they passed through was a desolate panorama of war. Farmhouses stood as burnt-out husks, their roofs caved in, their windows shattered. Fields were churned into muddy quagmires, scarred by tank tracks and shell craters. Here and there, the grim silhouettes of downed planes, both Allied and German, lay twisted and broken, stark monuments to the aerial battles that raged overhead.

They saw no civilians, only the occasional small groups of Belgian soldiers, hunkered down in defensive positions along the few remaining intact roads, their faces hollow with fatigue and fear. They exchanged quick, somber nods with the men in the truck, a silent acknowledgment of their shared plight. The sky, when they could see it, was a canvas for the war, crisscrossed by contrails and punctuated by the distant, silent puffs of anti-aircraft fire. The relentless drone of German planes, often invisible above the cloud cover, was a constant, unnerving presence.

After what felt like an eternity, the truck ground to a halt at a crossroads that was little more than a muddy junction of two broken roads. A solitary signpost, riddled with bullet holes, pointed vaguely in three directions, its lettering unreadable. The driver cut the engine, and the sudden silence, broken only by the distant artillery and the wind whistling through the wreckage, was almost deafening.

"Menin," one of the Belgian soldiers in the back grunted, gesturing vaguely to the right. "That way. Good luck." He offered no further explanation or instruction. The other soldiers likewise remained silent, their eyes fixed on the horizon. It was clear their escort's responsibilities ended here.

Dave and Freddy climbed out, their limbs stiff and aching. The cold air bit at their exposed faces. They stood for a moment, unsure of which way to go, the vague instructions offering little comfort. The landscape was utterly deserted, a bleak expanse of shattered trees and churned earth.

"Well, Freddy," Dave said, rubbing his arms against the cold. "Looks like we're on our own again. Any bright ideas?"

Freddy squinted at the bullet-pocked signpost, then scanned the horizon. "Menin is usually west of here, isn't it?" he mused.
