Refugees, a heartbreaking stream of old men, women, and children, clogged the roadsides, pushing prams and pulling carts laden with their meager possessions, their eyes wide with fear and uncertainty. The jeep had to swerve around a broken-down truck, then slow to a crawl behind a troop transport, only to pick up speed again as the road cleared. The distant thud of artillery, once a faint tremor, was now a persistent, ominous rhythm that seemed to beat in time with Dave's pounding head.

Dave felt the constant jarring of the vehicle deep in his bones. His head throbbed, a dull ache that mirrored the weariness settling in his soul. Freddy, next to him, was surprisingly quiet, his young face grim, eyes scanning the devastated landscape as if trying to absorb every detail of the unfolding tragedy. Sergeant Leclerc drove with a focused intensity, his jaw set, occasionally glancing into the rearview mirror at his two unusual passengers. He offered them a canteen of water, which they gratefully accepted, the cool liquid a small comfort in the dry, dusty air, though it did little to quench the thirst of their apprehension.

The jeep eventually turned off the main road, entering a small, unassuming town that seemed to have been spared the worst of the fighting, though sandbags were piled high against every public building and soldiers were visible on every corner. They pulled up in front of a grand, pre-war hotel, its windows now taped crisscross to prevent shattering, its lobby transformed into a frenetic command center. Orderlies rushed in and out, telephones rang incessantly, and the air buzzed with a low, anxious hum of activity, a palpable tension.

Sergeant Leclerc led them through a maze of hastily erected partitions and temporary desks, past officers hunched over maps, their faces pale under the harsh electric lights. The building smelled of stale coffee, cigarette smoke, and the metallic tang of fear, a smell Dave was beginning to associate permanently with war. They were directed to a smaller room on the second floor, guarded by two grim-faced military policemen.

Inside, the room was surprisingly sparse. A large, worn map of Belgium and northern France covered one wall, dotted with pins and penciled lines. Around a plain wooden table sat three officers, their uniforms impeccably pressed despite the chaos outside. One, a tall man with a neatly trimmed grey mustache and piercing blue eyes, wore the insignia of a General. The other two, a Colonel and a Major, looked equally serious, their expressions tight with anticipation and dread.

Sergeant Leclerc saluted smartly. "General Dubois, Colonel Mercier, Major Van Der Velde, I have brought the two gentlemen from the front."

Dave felt a sudden dryness in his throat. He looked at Freddy, whose face, though still smudged with dirt, had taken on an air of serious purpose. Freddy cleared his throat, a small, almost imperceptible sound in the quiet room.

"General, officers," Freddy began, his voice clear and surprisingly steady for his age, "my friend Dave and I were shot down three days ago, not far from the German lines, after a reconnaissance flight. We were captured by German forces, Messerschmitt pilots primarily, and taken to a temporary field headquarters. It was there that we managed to escape. But before our escape, we saw something, General, something we believe is of utmost importance."

He paused, letting his words sink in. General Dubois merely nodded, his expression unchanging, a silent invitation to continue. Colonel Mercier shifted, his gaze now fully engaged. Major Van Der Velde’s pen hovered over his notebook.

"This headquarters," Freddy went on, "was located in what seemed to be a requisitioned farm house. Inside, there was a map room, and in that room, there was a large operational map. Dave nodded, adding his weight to Freddy’s words. "Red pins for panzer divisions, sir. And blue for infantry. There were lines, too, thick arrows drawn in crayon, showing directions of advance. And dates, specific dates written next to them."

The officers exchanged a look, a flicker of something in their eyes, perhaps skepticism, perhaps a dawning apprehension. General Dubois leaned forward, resting his forearms on the table. "You expect us to believe that you, two captured airmen, simply walked into a German operational headquarters, memorized their entire battle plan, and then escaped without a scratch?" Enough to understand their immediate intentions, enough to see where they intend to strike next, and in what strength. We saw it because the German officers, in their arrogance, perhaps believed us to be harmless prisoners, or simply didn't care. And we didn't escape without a scratch, sir. We were shot at, we barely made it. We crashed an aircraft in the process."

If accurate, their current defensive strategy, carefully planned and executed, would be outflanked, their forces cut off, and their ability to link up with the arriving British and French armies severely jeopardized.

Dave slumped onto his own cot, rubbing his face. "They have to believe us, Freddy. There's too much at stake. And 'stop them'… that's a big question. They're outnumbered, outgunned, outmaneuvered. Dave added, "And the infantry, sir, they were following closely, to consolidate the breakthrough and widen the breach. They are attempting to create a massive pocket."

The three Belgian officers bent over the map, their heads almost touching, their expressions grim. General Dubois’s finger hovered over the area Freddy had indicated, his lips moving silently as he mentally reviewed the deployment of his own forces. The implications of what Freddy and Dave described were devastating. If accurate, their current defensive strategy, meticulously planned and executed, would be outflanked, their forces cut off, and their ability to link up with the arriving British and French armies severely jeopardized.

"This is… critical," General Dubois finally pronounced, his voice flat with the weight of the revelation. "If this intelligence proves accurate, and I pray it does not, we have very little time to adjust our defenses. We possess precious few reserves to redeploy to this sector on such short notice." He straightened up, rubbing his temples with a hand that trembled slightly. "Colonel Mercier, commence drawing up contingency plans for a redeployment of the 14th Infantry Division. It is our sole mobile reserve capable of reaching that sector in time to offer any meaningful resistance. Major Van Der Velde, prepare a comprehensive report based on this information immediately. It must be communicated to the British and French commands without delay."

"General," Colonel Mercier articulated, his voice low, "the 14th is already overstretched. Relocating them will weaken other vital sectors. And we still lack the heavy

guns to equip them properly for such a counter-thrust."

General Dubois slapped the map with the flat of his hand. The sound was sharp in the tense silence of the small, stuffy room. "We lack everything, Colonel. We lack time, we lack men, we lack tanks, we lack air cover. But we cannot allow them to outflank us here. Not if we have even a whisper of a chance to prevent it. We must buy time, Mercier. Time for the British and French to establish their positions, time for our own forces to regroup. If this gap opens, it is not merely a breach, it is a collapse of the entire front in the north. The entire strategy will unravel. We will be fighting isolated pockets, cut off from all support." He turned to Major Van Der Velde. "Major, how quickly can this information reach the Allied High Command? What is our fastest secure channel?"

Major Van Der Velde ran a hand through his thinning hair. "By radio, General. Encrypted, of course. But the lines are already congested with reports of engagements and desperate requests for air support. It will take time to ensure it gets through to the proper channels and is fully processed. Perhaps an hour, possibly more, before they can act upon it."

"An hour we may not have," General Dubois muttered, staring at the map with eyes that seemed to see not just the lines and symbols but the actual landscape, the advancing German hordes. "Colonel Mercier, begin the preparations for the 14th Division's movement immediately. Alert their commanding officer. They are to move with all possible speed, using every available transport, to establish a delaying action in sector Gamma-7, here," he jabbed a finger at the map point Freddy had indicated. "They are to prepare defensive positions and hold until relieved, or until they can no longer hold. Make it clear this is a do-or-die mission. Their primary objective is to slow the enemy's advance, to buy us hours, even minutes, if that is all they can manage."

Colonel Mercier saluted, his face pale but resolute. "Understood, General. I will convey the orders personally. But General, their equipment… their morale… it has been a brutal seventy-two hours."

"I know, Colonel," Dubois said, his voice softening slightly, tinged with a weariness that belied his stern demeanor. "I know. But we have no other choice. It is their country, their homes they defend. They will understand. They are Belgians."

Mercier nodded once, sharply, and turned to leave the room. He walked with a quick, determined stride, the urgency of his mission etched into every line of his lean frame. As the door swung shut behind him, a heavy silence descended upon the room, broken only by the distant rumble of artillery fire, a constant, menacing reminder of the war that raged just beyond the walls of their temporary headquarters.

General Dubois looked at Dave and Freddy. His expression was still grim, but there was a flicker of something else in his eyes now, a deep, if reluctant, respect. "Gentlemen," he said, his voice gravely serious, "you have rendered an invaluable service to Belgium, and indeed, to the Allied cause. This information, if acted upon swiftly and decisively, might yet avert a disaster of unimaginable proportions. We owe you a debt we may never be able to repay." He hesitated, looking them over. "You have been through much. You are exhausted, I can see it. What are your intentions now? Do you wish to be escorted to a field hospital, perhaps some rest and a hot meal? Or do you have further duties to attend to?"

Dave glanced at Freddy. The young Englishman looked utterly spent, his face still smudged with dirt and the lingering yellow ooze, his uniform torn and stained. Freddy, however, met the General's gaze with surprising steadiness. "General," Freddy began, his voice a little hoarse, "we only want to help. If there's anything else we can do, anything at all, we are at your disposal. We have no other duties, sir. Our mission was to deliver this intelligence."

Dave nodded in agreement. "He's right, General. We didn't come all this way just to sit on our hands. If there's a way we can contribute, even if it's just running messages or helping with logistics, we're ready. We've seen what the Nazis are capable of. We want to fight them any way we can."

General Dubois looked from one to the other, a small, almost imperceptible smile touching the corners of his mouth. "Brave words, young men. But you are not soldiers of the Belgian Army, nor are you officially attached to any Allied unit at this moment. Your primary value, for now, lies in the information you possess. It is crucial that you remain safe and available for further questioning by higher commands, should they require it. For now, however, I believe a period of rest would be most beneficial. Major Van Der Velde will arrange for your accommodation and ensure you are comfortable. We will then arrange for your transport to British Headquarters as soon as communications are established and secure passage can be guaranteed. They will undoubtedly wish to debrief you thoroughly."

Major Van Der Velde, who had been listening intently, stepped forward. "General, perhaps they could be debriefed here, more extensively, while we await confirmation from the British. Their detailed recollections might provide further nuances that could be critical for Colonel Mercier's planning."

Dubois considered this, tapping his fingers on the map. "A good point, Major. Very well. Arrange for a secure room, food, and then conduct a more thorough debriefing. Record everything. Every detail, no matter how small, could be important. But ensure they get some rest first. They look as if they've been through hell, and indeed, they have."

"Yes, General," Major Van Der Velde replied. He turned to Dave and Freddy. "Gentlemen, if you will follow me. We have some basic facilities here, nothing luxurious, I am afraid, but you will be safe and warm."

Dave and Freddy exchanged a look of weary relief. The thought of a warm place, free from constant danger, and perhaps even a real bed, was almost overwhelming. They had been running on pure adrenaline and sheer will for days, perhaps weeks.

"Thank you, Major," Dave said, his voice a little rough with emotion. Freddy just nodded, too tired for further words.

Major Van Der Velde led them out of the map room and down a dimly lit corridor. The building, Dave realized, was an old, sturdy stone structure, possibly a municipal building or a school, hastily converted into a military headquarters. The air was thick with the smell of stale cigarette smoke, disinfectant, and the faint, acrid scent of gunpowder that seemed to cling to everything in this war-torn land. They passed several other officers, their faces etched with the same grim determination and exhaustion Dave had seen in Dubois and Mercier. Telephones rang incessantly, clattering typewriters added a frantic rhythm to the cacophony, and hushed conversations filled the spaces between. Everyone moved with a sense of urgent purpose. It was a beehive of desperate activity.

They were ushered into a small, sparsely furnished room. It contained two cots with thin blankets, a rickety wooden table, and two chairs. On the table sat a pitcher of water and two tin cups. It was simple, but to Dave and Freddy, it looked like paradise.

"It is not much, but it is private," Major Van Der Velde said. "I will have someone bring you some food and fresh water. There are washbasins down the hall if you wish to clean up. Try to rest. I will return in an hour or so to begin our more detailed discussion."

With a final nod, the Major departed, leaving them alone. Dave looked at Freddy, who had already sunk onto one of the cots, his eyes closed.

"Freddy, you alright?" Dave asked, his own voice heavy with fatigue.

Freddy opened his eyes, a faint smile on his mud-streaked face. "Right as rain, Dave. Just… tired. So very tired. But we did it. They believed us."

"Yeah," Dave said, slumping onto the other cot. "They believed us. And now they're going to try and stop them. I just hope it's not too late. That General looked like he had the weight of the world on his shoulders."

"He does," Freddy said quietly. "All of them do. They're outnumbered, outgunned. It's a miracle they're holding on at all." He paused, then sighed deeply. "Still, it's a good feeling, isn't it? To have actually done something useful."

"It is," Dave admitted, closing his eyes. The tension that had been a constant companion for days, weeks even, began to ease its grip, leaving him feeling utterly drained. He could still feel the vibrations of the distant artillery, a low thrumming against the soles of his feet and in his chest, but here, in this small room, it seemed less immediate, less threatening. For the first time in what felt like an eternity, he felt a faint flicker of hope. They had been through hell, had faced death countless times, but they had made it. They had delivered their message. Now, it was up to the soldiers, up to these brave, weary Belgians, to turn the tide.

A young private soon arrived with a tray of food: thick slices of dark bread, some hard cheese, and a mug of lukewarm coffee. It wasn't a feast, but it was nourishing, and they ate in silence, devouring every crumb. The coffee, though poor, was a welcome jolt of warmth. After eating, Dave managed to clean some of the grime from his face and hands in the communal washroom, feeling marginally human again. Freddy, however, was already fast asleep on his cot, his breathing deep and even, clearly having completely succumbed to exhaustion. Dave decided not to disturb him. He lay back on his own cot, pulled the thin blanket over him, and despite the uncomfortable mattress and the distant sounds of war, sleep claimed him quickly, a heavy, dreamless oblivion.

It felt like only minutes later when he was gently shaken awake. Major Van Der Velde stood over him, a small lamp in his hand casting long shadows across the room. Freddy was already stirring, rubbing his eyes.

"Gentlemen, I apologize for disturbing your rest," the Major said softly. "But General Dubois has received a preliminary report from the front. The situation is developing rapidly. We need to go over your memories in more detail now. Every small piece of information could be crucial."

Dave pushed himself up, rubbing the sleep from his eyes. "No problem, Major. We're ready." He glanced at Freddy, who was already sitting up, looking a little more refreshed.

"Good," the Major said. "Follow me."

They were led back to a slightly larger office, this one equipped with a proper map table and several chairs. Another junior officer, a Captain, was already present, notepad and pencil ready. Major Van Der Velde spread out a fresh map, identical to the one in General Dubois's office.

"Right," the Major began, his tone businesslike. "Let's start from the beginning. When you were prisoners, how much access did you have to the German officers and their discussions? How much did you overhear directly, and how much did you piece together?"

For the next two hours, Dave and Freddy recounted their experiences in excruciating detail. They described the layout of the prison, the types of aircraft they saw, the markings on the vehicles, the accents of the officers, snippets of conversation they overheard in German, the general atmosphere of hurried preparation and triumphant arrogance among their captors. Freddy, with his command of German and his sharp memory for maps and symbols, was particularly valuable. He described the unit insignia he recognized, the rank distinctions of the officers, the types of armaments they discussed. Dave filled in the gaps, adding details about the mood of the German troops, the movement of supplies, the sheer scale of the preparations he witnessed.

They meticulously pointed out locations on the map, recalling specific road junctions, river crossings, and villages mentioned in the German officers' conversations or indicated on the map they had seen. Freddy even drew rough sketches of the insignia he remembered, and the Captain meticulously copied them down.

"You mentioned two panzer divisions in this sector," Major Van Der Velde said, tapping the map. "Can you recall any numbers, any specific unit designations?"

Freddy furrowed his brow, concentrating. "I… I think one of them was the 7th Panzer. I heard an officer mention 'Rommel' and 'our lightning advance.' And the other, I believe, was the 5th Panzer. They were discussing logistical coordination between the two. The third one, further south… I couldn't make out the number clearly, but it was another armored division, definitely. They were talking about a pincer movement, cutting off the Belgian forces from the French and British to the south."

Dave chimed in. "They were very confident, sir. Overly confident, I'd say. They believed their speed would be their greatest weapon. They were talking about being in Brussels within forty-eight to seventy-two hours of the breakthrough."

The Major and the Captain exchanged grave glances. "This 'Rommel' you mentioned," the Major said, "that is highly significant. He is known for his aggressive, rapid advances. If he is leading a panzer division in that sector, it means they intend to move with extreme speed. This corroborates the urgency of your intelligence." He made a note. "Any other names? Any other specific unit types? Artillery regiments, engineers?"

They racked their brains, pulling every last detail from their exhausted minds. Freddy remembered hearing talk of bridging units being prepared, of special detachments for clearing obstacles. Dave recalled seeing heavy artillery pieces being moved towards the front lines, disguised under camouflage netting.

Finally, after what felt like an eternity, Major Van Der Velde leaned back, a weary but satisfied expression on his face. "Gentlemen, this is exceptionally thorough. Your memories are remarkably clear under the circumstances. This additional detail will be invaluable for our defensive planning. General Dubois will be very pleased. He will pass this on to Allied High Command immediately."

"Has there been any news from the front?" Dave asked, the concern evident in his voice.

The Major's face clouded slightly. "The situation is… difficult. The fighting has intensified across the entire front. Our forces are holding, but under immense pressure. There are reports of heavy Stuka attacks and relentless infantry assaults. And yes, in the sector you indicated, there are preliminary reports of increased German activity, reconnaissance elements pushing forward. Nothing conclusive yet regarding a major armored thrust, but the signs are certainly there." He paused. "Your information has given us a precious head start, however small. We have moved what little reserves we have. Now we can only wait and see if it is enough."

He stood up. "For now, you have done all you can here. I will arrange for you to be taken to a more secure location further behind our lines. It is safer there, and you will be able to get some proper rest. General Dubois believes it is too risky for you to remain at this forward headquarters should the front lines shift unexpectedly."

Dave and Freddy nodded. The thought of being away from the immediate sound of the guns was appealing, though they both felt a pang of guilt leaving the Belgians to face the onslaught.

"Thank you, Major," Freddy said. "We appreciate your… trust in us."

"Your bravery and resourcefulness speak for themselves, young man," the Major replied with a rare, genuine smile. "You faced great danger to bring us this message. Belgium will not forget it."

Within the hour, a staff car, a sturdy but old Opel painted drab olive green, pulled up to the rear entrance of the headquarters. A young Belgian driver, barely more than a boy, saluted them smartly. Major Van Der Velde walked them out.

"This driver will take you to a town called Wavre," the Major explained. "There is a small field hospital there, and a temporary billet for liaison officers. You will be safe there. I have already sent word ahead. Our British allies are also establishing a forward command post there, so you will be able to make contact with them directly. Inform them of your mission, and they will know what to do."

"Thank you again, Major," Dave said, extending his hand. The Major shook it firmly.

"Good luck, gentlemen. And thank you, for everything."

Dave and Freddy climbed into the back of the car. The driver engaged the gears with a grind and they pulled away, leaving the beleaguered headquarters behind. As they drove, the sounds of battle, though still distant, seemed to grow in intensity, a deep, continuous roar that vibrated through the ground and the very air. The road was filled with military traffic heading in the opposite direction: trucks laden with supplies, troop carriers packed with grim-faced soldiers, ambulances speeding back from the front. They passed long columns of infantry, marching stolidly towards the sound of the guns, their faces mud-streaked, their eyes weary but determined. It was a sobering sight, a stark reminder of the immense human cost of the conflict.

Freddy leaned his head against the window, watching the landscape roll by. The fields were green, dotted with small farmhouses, but everywhere there were signs of war: hastily dug trenches, abandoned farm equipment, the occasional plume of smoke rising on the horizon. The sky, once a brilliant blue, was now hazed with the dust and smoke of battle.

"I hope they make it, Dave," Freddy said quietly, his voice barely audible above the rumble of the car. "I truly hope they make it in time."

Dave said nothing. He looked out at the passing fields, at the resolute faces of the Belgian soldiers marching to their fate. He thought of General Dubois, Colonel Mercier, and Major Van Der Velde, burdened with impossible decisions, fighting with courage against overwhelming odds. He felt a profound sense of helplessness, a gnawing anxiety that despite all they had done, it might not be enough. The German war machine was a terrifying thing, a juggernaut of steel and fire, and the Belgians, brave as they were, were little more than a picket fence against a tidal wave. But they had tried. They had done their part. Now, the fate of Belgium, and perhaps Europe, rested on the strength and speed of those who would face the onslaught. The car sped on, carrying them away from the immediate front, but not away from the war, which was everywhere, a suffocating presence that permeated every breath.
