The frantic rush of the wind softened to a steady, whispering sigh, and the world below, though still an incomprehensible void, no longer seemed to rush up to meet him with such malevolent intent. He was suspended, an almost ethereal presence, between the heavens and the unseen earth.

He spared a quick glance over his shoulder, twisting his body in the harness, and saw Farmer, a small, dark shape a hundred yards or so behind him, his own chute a dark flower against the distant stars. Freddy, bless his youthful exuberance, had followed the drill to the letter, his descent as disciplined and precise as Dawson’s own. The sight brought a flicker of reassurance, a small warmth in the profound chill of the night. They were together, at least for now, two tiny specks in the immensity, bound by the fragile threads of their parachutes and the weight of their shared mission.

Below them, the land began to resolve itself, though still cloaked in a shroud of impenetrable darkness. He could discern the faint, irregular outlines of what appeared to be rugged terrain, perhaps hills or mountains, their jagged peaks swallowed by the night. There was no sign of human habitation, no telltale glow of distant lights, no hint of roads or settlements. It was an alien landscape, silent and foreboding, a canvas of primal wilderness. The air grew steadily colder as he descended, the biting chill seeping into his bones, reminding him that whatever warmth the B-25 had offered was now a distant memory. He wondered about the plane, about Colonel Welsh. Had they continued their decoy flight, drawing the enemy’s attention to a ghost, to an empty shell? Or had they, too, veered off course, perhaps to return to Casablanca with the duplicates of the President’s *actual* flight plan? The colonel’s silence on his own fate, his grim resolve, suggested a deep, unsettling ambiguity, a sacrifice that might yet be required. Dawson pushed the thought away; it was not for him to know, not yet, and certainly not during a jump. Focus, he told himself, focus on the landing, focus on the ground, on survival.

The ground rushed up quickly then, faster than he anticipated in the deceptive darkness. He braced himself, bending his knees, tucking his chin, remembering the countless drills, the hard-won lessons learned on dusty practice fields. The impact was jarring, a sharp, unforgiving blow that knocked the wind from his lungs and sent a jolt of pain up his legs. He hit hard, rolling awkwardly, the heavy pack on his back twisting him, sending him tumbling for several yards before he finally came to a stop, entangled in the lines of his chute. For a moment, he lay there, winded and disoriented, the scent of damp earth and crushed vegetation filling his nostrils. The silence, after the roar of the plane and the rush of the wind, was profound, almost deafening, broken only by the frantic pounding of his own heart.

Slowly, carefully, he untangled himself, his muscles aching, his body protesting the sudden violence of the landing. He checked himself for injuries, feeling for any breaks or sprains, but found only the dull ache of bruised flesh and the stiffness born of cold and shock. He pulled the silk canopy towards him, collapsing it quickly, rolling it into a compact bundle, his movements precise and efficient, born of years of training. The parachute was a tell-tale sign, a white flag in the night, and it had to be secured, hidden, or destroyed as quickly as possible. He would bury it later, when he had a moment of respite, when the immediate danger had passed.

He rose to his feet, his senses immediately alert, straining against the oppressive darkness. The air was still and heavy, carrying the faint, earthy smell of pine and damp soil. The faint glow of the stars offered only the barest outline of the landscape, turning everything into shifting, indistinct shadows. He pulled a small, military-issue flashlight from a pouch on his belt, its beam a meager, controlled pinprick of light that he used sparingly, sweeping it across the immediate vicinity. He was in a clearing, surrounded by a dense stand of trees, their dark silhouettes looming like silent sentinels against the slightly less black sky. The ground beneath his feet was uneven, a mix of soft earth, rocks, and tangled undergrowth. This was no manicured field, no prepared drop zone. This was a jump into the wild.

"Dawson?" a whispered voice, barely audible, cut through the oppressive silence.

He froze, his hand instinctively going to the pistol at his hip, his heart giving an extra thump. Then, recognizing Farmer's voice, he relaxed slightly, though his guard remained up. "Here, Freddy," he whispered back, his voice low and tight. "You alright?"

"Aye, sir, bit bruised but in one piece," Farmer replied, his voice closer now, emerging from the shadows of the trees to Dawson’s left. "That was quite the tumble. Where are we, do you reckon?"

"No idea, not yet anyway," Dawson said, his gaze sweeping the tree line. "Colonel Welsh said Grid Reference Alpha-Four. We'd best move quickly and find it. Don't want to be caught out here in the open come dawn." He pulled out his sealed map, the carefully folded paper crinkling softly in the stillness. He remembered Welsh's instructions: "Destroy them once memorized." The colonel had been deadly serious. No evidence, no trace.

Using the faint glow of his map light, shielded beneath his jacket, Dawson began to orient himself, comparing the topography he could discern with the contours of the map. It was a painstaking process, requiring intense concentration, his mind sifting through elevation lines and symbols in the near-total darkness. The map showed a rugged, forested area, crisscrossed by small ravines and an occasional, unnamed stream. Alpha-Four, he noted, was a natural depression, a kind of shallow bowl nestled between three low hills, approximately a mile to the north-northwest of their presumed drop point. It would provide cover, a defensible position, and a measure of concealment from prying eyes.

"We need to move north-northwest," Dawson whispered to Farmer, pointing a gloved finger in the general direction. "About a mile. We'll stick to the tree line as much as possible. Keep your eyes peeled, your ears open. Any sign of trouble, we melt into the shadows, understand?"

"Understood, sir," Farmer replied, already adjusting the straps of his own pack, his young face etched with a newfound seriousness. The bravado, the casual banter of moments ago, had vanished, replaced by the grim reality of their situation.

They began to move, each step a deliberate, cautious act. The forest floor was a treacherous carpet of fallen leaves, unseen roots, and loose rocks, making silent progress a challenge. Dawson led, his instincts honed by years of navigating hostile territory, his senses stretched to their limits. Every snapping twig, every rustle of leaves, every distant hoot of an owl, was a potential threat, a sign of an unseen enemy. He moved like a ghost, his body low, his rifle held ready, his eyes constantly scanning the shifting patterns of light and shadow. Farmer followed, his movements less seasoned but equally determined, his youthful energy tempered by an awareness of the immense stakes.

As they pushed deeper into the woods, the air grew even colder, the damp chill seeping into their clothing, making their teeth chatter despite their efforts to remain silent. The moon, a thin crescent, offered little illumination, and the stars seemed to blink in and out of existence through the dense canopy above. Dawson glanced at his watch, its luminous dial a tiny, forbidden beacon in the overwhelming darkness. Hours had passed since their jump, though it felt like mere minutes. Time, in this silent, menacing world, seemed to warp and stretch.

After what felt like an eternity, but was probably closer to an hour and a half, Dawson recognized the subtle shift in the terrain, the gentle slope that indicated the beginning of a depression. He held up a hand, bringing Farmer to a halt. They were close. He knelt, pulling out his map once more, double-checking their position. This was it, Grid Reference Alpha-Four. The bowl-shaped depression, shrouded by a dense thicket of undergrowth, offered precisely the kind of concealment Colonel Welsh had specified.

"This is it," Dawson whispered, his voice hoarse from disuse. "Let's get in there, get some cover, and then we'll figure out what we're supposed to be observing."

They eased into the thicket, the thorny branches scratching at their uniforms, the damp leaves brushing against their faces. Inside the depression, it was even darker, the canopy overhead blocking out whatever meager starlight had penetrated the forest. Dawson found a relatively flat spot, protected on three sides by dense bushes, and settled down, motioning for Farmer to do the same a few feet away.

"First things first," Dawson whispered, pulling out his map again. "Memorize it, then destroy it. We can't risk it falling into enemy hands." He spent several minutes poring over the details, etching every contour, every symbol, every potential landmark into his mind. The map revealed a scattering of small villages within a radius of a few miles, connected by rough tracks, and, more significantly, a larger, more prominent feature to the east: a railway line, running north-south, and a cluster of buildings marked with a symbol that suggested a military installation or a logistics hub. This, he suspected, was their ultimate area of interest.

When he was satisfied, he struck a match, the sudden flare of light a shocking contrast to the profound darkness. He held the map over a small, cleared patch of earth, watching as the flames consumed the paper, turning it into curling ash. Farmer followed suit, his own map burning away the last tangible link to their mission's specifics.

"Now what, sir?" Farmer asked, his voice low, a mix of exhaustion and expectation. "The colonel just said 'observe.' Observe what, exactly?"

Dawson leaned back against a tree trunk, the rough bark a cold comfort against his back. The adrenaline of the jump and the trek through the forest was beginning to recede, leaving him feeling the chill and the bone-deep weariness. He pulled a canteen from his pack, taking a long, slow swallow of water. "That's the million-dollar question, Freddy," he said, his voice almost a sigh. "Welsh didn't elaborate beyond that. His main concern, it seemed, was that we survived the jump and rendezvoused. The fact that he stayed with the plane, well, that tells me something important."

Farmer frowned in the darkness. "What's that, sir?"

"It means that B-25, with him on it, is the real decoy," Dawson explained, his voice grim. "The one they want the enemy to find. He's either going to ditch it, or he's going to let it be intercepted, hoping they believe the President was aboard. He took that chance, Freddy, so we could get here, unnoticed, and do whatever it is we're meant to do. He made the ultimate sacrifice, or at least he was prepared to, to ensure the President's safety." The thought was a chilling one, a stark reminder of the brutal realities of their intelligence work. Colonel Welsh, the urbane, slightly cynical intelligence chief, might well be dead or captured by now. The idea made Dawson shiver, a reaction not entirely due to the cold.

"Good God," Farmer breathed, the weight of the revelation settling on him. "So we're on our own, then? No resupply, no extraction plans if things go sour?"

"That's usually how these things work when you're dropped behind enemy lines without a specific extraction plan discussed

behind enemy lines without a specific extraction plan discussed, Dawson finished for him, his voice flat with the stark reality of it all. "We have our emergency rations, our sidearms, and our wits. That's about it. And a vague directive to 'observe'."

Farmer remained silent for a moment, the weight of their predicament settling heavily upon him. The cold night air seemed to bite deeper now, the rustling leaves of the forest taking on a more sinister whisper. "So, no one is coming to get us, sir?" he finally managed, his youthful voice betraying a hint of trepidation.

"Not unless we make it back to Allied lines on our own two feet, Freddy," Dawson confirmed, his own weariness a dull ache in his bones. "Or unless we accomplish something so spectacular that they send a full-scale invasion to retrieve us, which, given the current state of things, is highly unlikely. We are, for all intents and purposes, ghosts. Unacknowledged, untraceable, and utterly alone." He took another slow swallow from his canteen, the metallic taste of the water doing little to quench the thirst that seemed to have taken root deep within him. "But don't let that rattle you, son. It simply means our margin for error is precisely zero. Every decision, every move, has to be the right one. And that's why we're going to take our time."

He leaned forward, lowering his voice even further, though there was no one else for miles to hear them. "We need to establish a routine. For now, we rest. We'll alternate watches. You take the first two hours, I'll take the next three. We conserve our rations, and we move only under the cover of darkness until we have a clearer picture of what we're looking for. The map showed a railway line, didn't it? And what looked like a military installation. Those are likely our primary points of interest. But we don't rush in. We observe. We gather information. We become part of the forest itself."

Farmer nodded, the movement barely perceptible in the profound darkness. He understood the gravity of Dawson's words. Their training, which had seemed so theoretical back in the relative safety of England, was now brutally practical. This was not a drill; this was the real thing, with stakes beyond anything he had ever imagined. The President's life, the war effort, the very fate of the free world rested, in some small, inexplicable way, on two agents dropped blindly into enemy territory with little more than their courage and a cryptic instruction.

Dawson then began to detail the watch schedule, the procedures for signaling any perceived threat, and the absolute necessity of maintaining silence. He emphasized the importance of blending in, of moving like shadows, of leaving no trace. He spoke of the particular smells of the forest at night, the sounds that were natural versus those that were man-made, the subtle shifts in the wind that could betray an approaching presence. He was drawing on years of experience, a lifetime of honed instincts, and Farmer listened intently, soaking in every word like a sponge.

After their quiet conference, Farmer took up his position, his back against a thick oak, his eyes straining into the inky blackness. Dawson settled down, trying to find a comfortable position on the cold, damp earth, but sleep was a distant luxury. His mind raced, replaying the last few days, Colonel Welsh's grim determination, the sealed envelopes, the decoy mission. The sheer audacity of it all was staggering. Welsh had bet his life, and the lives of his crew, on the effectiveness of that elaborate deception. Dawson hoped, with a fervent intensity that bordered on prayer, that the gamble had paid off. He hoped the President was, at this very moment, safely en route to Casablanca, or perhaps already there, oblivious to the sacrificial efforts made on his behalf.

But hope, Dawson knew, was a dangerous commodity in their line of work. Practicality, vigilance, and ruthlessness were far more reliable allies. He closed his eyes, not to sleep, but to visualize the map again, reconstructing every detail in his mind. The railway line ran roughly north to south, a potential conduit for supplies or troops. The military installation, marked with a stylized tank symbol, was a few miles to the east of their current position. He considered the terrain, the likely patrol routes, the potential for local resistance or, conversely, local collaboration with the enemy.

The next few hours passed in a slow, agonizing crawl. Farmer's watch was uneventful, marked only by the nocturnal sounds of the forest: the hoot of an owl, the rustle of small animals in the undergrowth, the distant, almost imperceptible murmur of a breeze through the treetops. When Dawson nudged him awake, he was shivering slightly, more from the pervasive cold than from fear, though a quiet dread had settled in his stomach.

"My turn now, Freddy," Dawson whispered. "Try to get some rest. We'll need it."

Farmer nodded, grateful for the reprieve, and soon, Dawson could hear his slow, even breathing, a small comfort in the vast, hostile darkness. Dawson himself remained alert, his senses heightened, every fiber of his being tuned to the subtle symphony of the night. He thought about his past missions, the close calls, the losses, the weight of responsibility. He thought about the men he had known who had not made it back, their faces flickering through his memory like dim, fading photographs. And he thought about Welsh, a man he had both respected and, at times, found utterly exasperating, now likely a martyr. The war demanded such sacrifices, but it never made them easy.

As dawn approached, a faint, almost imperceptible grayness began to dilute the inky blackness of the sky. The forest slowly started to reveal its form, trees emerging from the gloom like ancient sentinels. Dawson woke Farmer with a gentle touch.

"Time to move," he murmured. "We'll travel east, towards that railway line. But slowly. Cautiously. We eat a small portion of rations now, and then nothing until dusk, unless absolutely necessary. No fires. No unnecessary noise. Understand?"

"Understood, sir," Farmer replied, his voice still thick with sleep but resolute.

They ate their hardtack and dried beef in silence, washing it down with another meager ration of water. The food was bland and unsatisfying, but it provided the necessary sustenance. Then, with Dawson taking the lead, they began their trek.

The forest floor was a treacherous carpet of damp leaves, tangled roots, and unseen obstacles. Every step had to be carefully placed, every rustle of leaves a potential betrayer of their presence. Dawson moved with the fluid grace of a hunter, his eyes constantly scanning, his ears alert to every distant sound. Farmer, though younger and less experienced, quickly adapted, mirroring Dawson's movements, learning to blend into the natural environment.

They moved for several hours, making slow but steady progress. The terrain gradually began to change, the dense forest giving way to sparser woodlands, and then, faintly, the unmistakable scent of coal smoke reached them. They were getting closer to civilization, or at least, to human activity. Dawson held up a hand, bringing Farmer to an immediate halt.

"Smell that, Freddy?" he whispered. "Coal smoke. Means trains. Means industry. Means Germans."

Farmer nodded, his grip tightening on his rifle. The reality of their situation was becoming more palpable with every step.

They continued with even greater caution, eventually reaching the edge of the forest. Below them, stretching across a broad valley, was the railway line, two gleaming steel ribbons cutting through the landscape. A small, nondescript station building stood a few hundred yards away, and beyond it, a cluster of warehouses and what looked like a small marshalling yard. Further still, in the distance, Dawson could make out the faint outlines of larger buildings, their shapes indistinct in the early morning haze. This, he surmised, was the military installation marked on their destroyed map.

Dawson pulled out his binoculars, small, powerful lenses that brought the distant scene into sharper focus. He systematically scanned the area, noting the presence of a few uniformed figures moving around the station, and what appeared to be a small patrol near the entrance to the marshalling yard. There were no obvious signs of heavy military presence, no tanks or artillery pieces, but the overall impression was one of ordered, efficient activity.

"Looks like a fairly busy line," Dawson observed, lowering the binoculars. "And that installation beyond it appears to be some kind of depot or supply hub. Not a major frontline base, but certainly important. This could be what Welsh wanted us to 'observe'."

"What kind of observations, sir?" Farmer asked, peering over Dawson's shoulder, though his naked eyes could discern little detail.

"Anything out of the ordinary," Dawson explained. "Unusual troop movements, new equipment, changes in the flow of traffic. Anything that might indicate a larger strategic plan. Or, given the nature of our initial mission, anything that might relate to the President's actual movements, or perhaps, a counter-operation being planned by the Germans." He paused, his gaze fixed on the distant buildings. "But most importantly, Freddy, we're looking for information that can only be gathered on the ground. Intelligence that won't show up in aerial reconnaissance photos. The mood of the local population, the effectiveness of propaganda, the morale of the enemy troops. Subtle things. The kinds of things that can turn the tide of a battle, or even a war."

They spent the remainder of the day concealed in the dense undergrowth on the hillside, observing the railway line and the distant installation. The activity was steady: freight trains, usually pulled by powerful steam locomotives, chugged along the tracks, carrying unknown cargo. Passenger trains, smaller and faster, occasionally whisked by. The uniformed figures at the station seemed to be railway personnel, not soldiers, though their uniforms were vaguely similar in cut and color. The patrol near the marshalling yard was small, perhaps only two or three men, and they seemed more concerned with idly smoking and chatting than with serious security.

As the day wore on, Dawson began to piece together a rudimentary understanding of the local rhythms. The trains ran on a fairly predictable schedule. The patrols seemed lax. The overall impression was not one of high alert, but rather of routine, almost mundane wartime operations. This was a good sign, implying that their presence had not yet been detected, and that the German high command had not yet identified this specific area as a critical target.

But the lack of immediate excitement was also, paradoxically, a source of unease for Dawson. If this was just a normal railway line, a normal depot, then what exactly were they supposed to be observing? Colonel Welsh's words still echoed in his mind: "What I mean is, one of the envelopes might have been opened, the contents read, and then the envelopes resealed." Had the Germans learned anything about the President's *real* itinerary? Was this location somehow connected to a potential German counter-strike or assassination plot that the decoy operation was trying to flush out?

He needed more. He needed to get closer.

As dusk began to paint the sky in shades of orange and purple, casting long, distorting shadows across the valley, Dawson made his decision.

"We move down tonight," he told Farmer, his voice low and firm. "We'll get closer to that marshalling yard. See what kind of cargo is being moved. See if we can spot anything unusual. We need to be within earshot, within clearer sight, without being seen ourselves."

Farmer simply nodded, his eyes reflecting the dying light. He was tired, hungry, and undoubtedly apprehensive, but his resolve remained unshaken. He trusted Dawson, and he understood the necessity of their mission.

They waited until the last sliver of daylight had vanished, and the moon, a sliver of silver itself, was barely visible through the clouds. Then, moving with excruciating slowness and stealth, they descended the hillside. The forest provided excellent cover, its shadows deep and welcoming. The air grew colder, and a damp mist began to rise from the lowlands.

Reaching the edge of the flat ground near the tracks was an exercise in patience. They navigated thorny bushes, crossed small, muddy streams, and always kept a watchful eye on the distant station lights and the occasional beam of a flashlight from a patrolling guard. Finally, they found themselves in a line of scraggly trees and tall weeds, perhaps fifty yards from the nearest track.

From this vantage point, the marshalling yard was much clearer. They could see the outlines of freight cars, some open, some sealed. They could hear the creak and groan of steel, the hiss of steam from distant locomotives, the shouted commands of railway workers. The sounds carried clearly on the still night air.

Dawson brought his binoculars up again, systematically scanning each freight car, each stack of crates, each shadow. He was looking for anything distinctive, anything that didn't fit the expected pattern of a routine supply line. He looked for German military insignia, for unusual markings, for any sign that something more than ordinary goods was being transported.

Hours passed. The night deepened. The cold became a gnawing companion. Farmer shivered occasionally, but remained focused, his rifle cradled in his arms, his eyes darting from shadow to shadow.

Suddenly, a shift in activity drew Dawson's attention. A shunting engine, a small, powerful locomotive, began to move a line of boxcars. As they passed under a flickering overhead lamp, Dawson focused his binoculars. The boxcars were standard, unremarkable, but then he noticed something else. On one of the cars, obscured by shadow, he saw a barely visible stencil. It was small, discreet, but unmistakable: the familiar symbol of the German SS.

His breath caught in his throat. SS. This was no ordinary cargo.

Farmer squinted, struggling to make it out in the dim light. "I think so, sir. It's very faint."

The shunting engine continued its work, moving the line of cars deeper into the marshalling yard, eventually bringing them to a stop alongside a long, low warehouse. Immediately, a small detail of SS guards, perhaps six or eight men, emerged from the shadows of the building. "This is it, Freddy," Dawson murmured, his heart beginning to pound with a mixture of excitement and apprehension. "This is what we're here for. We need to know what's in those cars."

The SS guards took up positions around the boxcars. Then, a large, canvas-covered truck, also bearing the SS insignia, backed up to the side of the warehouse. A ramp was extended from the truck, and then, slowly, carefully, a series of large, heavily crated objects began to be unloaded.

Dawson strained his eyes, adjusting the focus of his binoculars. The crates were rectangular, approximately ten feet long, four feet wide, and perhaps three feet high. They were solid wood, reinforced with metal bands, and stenciled with cryptic numbers and a single, stylized eagle symbol. The way the SS guards handled them, with extreme care and constant vigilance, suggested immense value, or perhaps immense danger.

"They're moving something big, Freddy," Dawson reported, his voice tight. "Very big. And very well protected. Those crates... they're almost certainly containing some kind of military hardware. But what?"

He watched as the crates were moved into the warehouse. The operation was methodical, silent, and swift. Within an hour, all the crates had been transferred, and the SS guards had disappeared back into the building. Finally, with the first hint of false dawn, Dawson decided it was time to withdraw.

"We've seen enough for tonight," he whispered to Farmer. "We'll find a new hiding place before full daylight. A bit further away, higher up the hillside. We need to digest this. SS guards, cryptic crates. This is significant, Freddy. This is definitely significant."

"We need to get inside that warehouse," Dawson declared, his voice firm, though still a whisper. "Or at least, get a closer look at those crates. We need to identify that eagle symbol, if possible. Look for weak points, patrol schedules, any potential entryways. We'll study their movements until we know them better than they know themselves."

He folded his notepad and carefully tucked it away. "For now, we rest. We'll conserve our strength. We'll need every ounce of it. This isn't just about observation anymore, Freddy. I have a feeling this is about preventing another terrible blow to the United Nations."

The next day passed in a similar pattern of observation, but from a greater distance. They focused on the overall activity around the depot, trying to discern patterns, looking for any other SS movements, or any signs of increased security. They saw none. The regularity of the freight trains continued, the railway workers went about their duties, and the small, lax patrols near the marshalling yard maintained their casual demeanor. It was as if the SS presence and the mysterious crates were an entirely separate, discreet operation, hidden within the mundane backdrop of wartime logistics. This only heightened Dawson's suspicion.

As night fell again, bringing with it a biting wind and the threat of rain, Dawson and Farmer prepared for their closer reconnaissance. They adjusted their gear, checked their weapons, and shared a last, meager ration of food. Dawson reviewed the plan in meticulous detail, outlining their approach, their observation points, their escape routes, and the precise moment of withdrawal.

"Remember, Freddy," Dawson concluded, "we are ghosts. We are unseen. We are unheard. We get in, we gather what we can, and we get out. No heroics. No direct engagement unless our lives absolutely depend on it. Our primary goal is information. Not a firefight."

Farmer nodded, his face etched with a mixture of determination and healthy fear. He understood. This was the moment.

They moved like wraiths through the forest, the wind now rustling through the bare branches of the trees, providing natural cover for any slight sounds they might make. The lights of the marshalling yard seemed closer now, and the rhythmic clang of shunting cars carried on the wind. They approached from a different angle this time, aiming for a cluster of abandoned freight cars on a seldom-used siding that would offer better concealment closer to the warehouse.

The air grew colder, and a fine, persistent drizzle began to fall, turning the ground to slick mud. This was both a hindrance and a blessing; it made movement more difficult, but it also helped to mask any sounds and discouraged enemy patrols.

They reached the abandoned cars and cautiously made their way along the siding, using the massive steel structures as cover. The warehouse loomed before them, a long, low concrete building with only a few small, grimy windows high up on its walls. There was a single main entrance, a large roll-up door, which appeared to be sealed. A smaller, personnel door was visible near one corner, and this was their target.

Dawson deployed his binoculars, scanning the area around the warehouse with renewed intensity. The SS guards from the previous night were not immediately visible. There was no outward sign of increased security. The rain was coming down harder now, drumming softly on the metal roofs of the freight cars and the warehouse.

"No visible guards, sir," Farmer whispered, his breath misting in the cold air.

"That doesn't mean they're not there, Freddy," Dawson cautioned. "They'll be inside, or under cover. This isn't the sort of operation they'd leave unguarded. But it might mean their security is designed to be inconspicuous. Which is fine by us."

The hum of machinery died. The rhythmic clanking stopped. The projection of London on the wall vanished. The launch countdown clock, which had been ticking down relentlessly, froze at fifty-two seconds.

A stunned silence fell over the bunker, broken only by the panting of Dawson and Farmer, and the terrified whimpers of the German technicians.

“You… you stopped it,” Farmer whispered, his voice full of awe and disbelief.

Dawson, breathing heavily, leaned against the console, the axe still clutched in his hand. “Yes,” he managed to say, his voice hoarse. “For now.”

But the silence did not last. The heavy steel door to the bunker suddenly crashed inwards, flung open by the combined force of several German guards. They burst in, their submachine guns raised, their faces contorted with rage.

“_Hände hoch_! Hands up, you swine!” one of them roared, his voice echoing in the now dimly lit bunker.

Dawson and Farmer found themselves facing down a dozen armed men, their Luger and pistol suddenly seeming like mere toys against the overwhelming firepower.

Dawson slowly raised his hands, the axe still in one. “It’s over,” he said, his voice quiet, devoid of defiance, but with an underlying current of grim satisfaction. “The missile is stopped. You can’t launch it now.”

The lead guard, a stern-faced SS officer, snarled, “You will regret this, _Amerikaner_! This is a declaration of war unlike any other!”

“The war is already declared, Oberst,” Dawson replied calmly. “And we just prevented you from committing a crime against humanity that would have made all other acts of barbarism pale in comparison.”

The SS officer gestured to his men. “Take them. And ensure they do not leave this base alive. But first, find out what they did! Can this abomination be undone?” He turned to the whimpering technicians. “You! Report! Is the missile still salvageable?”

One of the technicians, still trembling, looked at the ruined cables. “Oberst, the main power lines… they are severed. It will take days, perhaps weeks, to repair the damage. The launch system is completely offline. And the payload… it was armed. Cutting the power… it may have destabilized it. It is highly volatile now.”

The SS officer’s face went white. “Volatile? What do you mean, volatile?”

Dawson smiled grimly, a thin, tired smile. “I mean, Oberst, that your biological weapon is now a very large, very unstable bomb. And I suggest you evacuate this area immediately, before it decides to go off on its own schedule. Or rather, lack thereof.”

The SS officer stared at Dawson, his eyes wide with dawning horror, then back at the damaged console, then at the trembling technician. The realization hit him with the force of a physical blow. Their ultimate weapon, the instrument of their vengeance, had been turned into a ticking time bomb. A biological ticking time bomb that, if it detonated here, would not only destroy the base but potentially unleash its deadly payload upon the surrounding German countryside. The ultimate irony.

Dawson and Farmer were roughly seized, their weapons confiscated. But as they were marched out of the bunker, their hands bound, Dawson met Farmer’s gaze. A silent message passed between them. They might be captured, they might be facing certain death, but they had bought time. They had stopped the unthinkable. And in the grim calculus of war, sometimes, that was enough.

As they were pushed roughly into the cold night air, the siren began to wail again, a different sound this time, one of frantic urgency, of dire warning. Searchlights swept the base, not for intruders, but for fleeing personnel. The SS officer, his face pale and his voice tight with barely contained panic, was screaming orders to his men, ordering an immediate evacuation of the launch site. The monstrous missile, now a silent, inert harbinger of self-destruction, stood against the dawn sky, a monument to a terror averted, for now. And for Dawson and Farmer, a new, even more uncertain chapter of their ordeal had just begun. Their mission, to prevent that missile from ever being launched, was accomplished. Their next mission, to survive, was now the paramount, terrifying challenge.

They were dragged across the gravel towards a waiting truck, the shouts and chaos of the frantic evacuation swirling around them. The German soldiers, previously focused on their capture, were now visibly nervous, glancing over their shoulders at the colossal missile, their movements jerky and hurried. Fear, a cold and pervasive fear, had seeped into the German ranks. The possibility of the weapon detonating, of its deadly contents being released haphazardly on their own soil, was a terror they had not anticipated.

“They’re genuinely terrified, sir,” Farmer whispered, trying to keep his voice steady despite the rough treatment. A guard pushed him forward roughly.

Dawson grunted in agreement, his eyes scanning their surroundings. The truck they were being shoved towards was a canvas-covered transport, not an enclosed prisoner vehicle. This meant a possible escape opportunity, however slim. He felt the cold steel of the rope binding his wrists, testing its tautness. Not as secure as handcuffs, but still tight.

“They’re moving us because they don’t want us here when it blows,” Dawson murmured to Farmer. “That’s our chance. They’re disorganized, scared. Keep your eyes open.”

The truck rumbled to life, its diesel engine coughing loudly. They were tossed into the back, landing unceremoniously on the hard wooden floor alongside two other German soldiers who were clearly meant to guard them. These men, however, were more interested in peering nervously through the gaps in the canvas, their faces etched with anxiety, than in strictly observing their prisoners.

The truck lurched forward, accelerating rapidly, leaving the missile site behind in a cloud of dust and the still-wailing sirens. They sped along a winding dirt road, the darkness of the pre-dawn forest pressing in on either side.

“Where do you think they’re taking us, sir?” Farmer asked, his voice low.

“Away from the blast radius, for certain,” Dawson replied, trying to discreetly work at his bonds. The rough rope chafed his skin, but a tiny thread had loosened. “They’ll want to interrogate us. Find out how we knew, who sent us, if there are more of us. Standard procedure for high-value prisoners.”

He glanced at the two German guards. One, a young man, was practically glued to the canvas, nervously watching the retreating base. The other, older, was fiddling with his rifle, his brow furrowed. They were both armed with Karabiner 98k rifles, standard issue. Not ideal for close-quarters fighting within the confines of a truck, but still deadly.

Dawson continued to test his bonds, the small thread gradually giving way. He could feel the circulation returning to his hands, a painful tingling sensation. He needed a distraction.

“What about the Colonel, sir?” Farmer asked, perhaps sensing Dawson’s need for an opening. “And the President’s trip? Will they know what happened here?”

“They won’t know about this immediately,” Dawson said, loud enough for the guards to hear. He wanted them to think they were just typical Allied spies, not the men who had just disarmed the ultimate weapon. “Our mission was to observe and report. We’ve been behind enemy lines for weeks, gathering intelligence. We stumbled onto this base by accident, and got a bit too curious.” He glanced at the older guard, who was now listening, a flicker of suspicion in his eyes.

“Accident, you say?” the guard grunted, his voice gruff. “You two look too well-equipped for an accident.”

“We’re air aces, out of our element,” Farmer chimed in, playing along. “Our plane went down, and we’ve been trying to make our way back to Allied lines. We scavenged what we could. We didn’t know what this place was. Just looked like a military installation.”

The older guard snorted, clearly unconvinced. “Air aces, eh? So why were you crawling around a top-secret missile launch site? And how did you get inside the control bunker?”

“We’re resourceful,” Dawson said, still working at his ropes. He felt the knot give slightly more. “And frankly, your security isn’t as good as you think it is. We got lucky.”

Just then, a massive, earth-shaking *CRUMP!* echoed through the forest. The ground beneath the truck shuddered violently, and the sound of a distant, concussive wave ripped through the air, followed by a faint orange glow that briefly illuminated the western horizon through the gaps in the canvas.

The missile. It had blown.

The guards cried out, startled, one of them dropping his rifle with a clatter. The truck swerved violently as the driver, equally shaken, fought for control.

“Now, Freddy!” Dawson yelled, his hands suddenly free. He lunged for the dropped rifle, snatching it up before the younger guard could react. With a swift movement, he brought the butt of the rifle down on the older guard’s head. The man slumped instantly.

Farmer, galvanised by the sudden turn of events, kicked out with his bound feet, catching the remaining conscious guard in the knee. The guard cried out, doubling over, and Farmer, with a surge of adrenaline, headbutted him. The guard stumbled back against the side of the truck, momentarily dazed.

Dawson, now armed, didn’t hesitate. He thrust the rifle at Farmer. “Take this! Cut your ropes!” He grabbed the fallen guard’s pistol, a Walther P38, from his holster.
