Louisa gave a shuddering sigh. “But what am I to do, Elinor? How am I to face the world again? How am I ever to believe in anyone, after such a betrayal?”

“You must rest now, my dearest. And in time, you will find your strength again. This wound is deep, but it will heal. And you will learn to trust again, when the right person comes along. Elinor remained with Louisa for some time, speaking in soft, comforting tones, until at last, exhausted by her grief and the day’s trying events, Louisa drifted into an uneasy sleep. Her own heart was heavy, not only for Louisa’s sorrow, but for the implications of this event on their quiet lives, and the inevitable return of Mrs. Jennings’s well-meant but relentless matchmaking efforts regarding Colonel Brandon. She rose at last, with a silent prayer for her sister’s peace, and left the room, leaving the door ajar, so that she might hear if Louisa stirred.

Mrs. Jennings, ever the gracious hostess, kept a keen eye on the proceedings, offering advice and encouragement, and occasionally a sly jest, her mind still clearly occupied with the advantageous prospects of Colonel Brandon.

Elinor played mechanically, her thoughts constantly straying to Marianne’s solitary suffering upstairs. Each boisterous laugh from Sir John, each thoughtless comment from Mrs. Palmer, each pronouncement from Mrs. Jennings, seemed to underscore the profound isolation of Marianne’s grief. How few, she reflected, truly understood the depth of such feeling, or respected the quiet dignity of a wounded spirit. The world, in its general course, seemed to demand a quick recovery, a swift transition from sorrow to cheerfulness, as if the heart were a machine to be easily mended and set back in motion. But Marianne’s heart was not of such robust and insensible material.

As the evening wore on, and the stock of pleasantries and anecdotes was gradually exhausted, the conversation, despite Elinor’s best efforts, occasionally drifted back to topics dangerously close to the forbidden. Mrs. Palmer, with all the artlessness of a child, would lament Mr. Willoughby’s defection, or praise Miss Grey’s fortune, always with a fresh giggle and a quick apology to Elinor, as if the latter were the only person whose feelings might be ruffled. Sir John, less prone to direct allusions, would nevertheless speak of the follies of young men, or the wisdom of securing a good establishment, often with a pointed glance at Elinor, as if to imply a general lesson to be learned.

Mrs. Jennings, for her part, was a curious mixture of caution and candour. She would frequently catch herself, after a particularly pointed remark about the virtues of an older, steadier suitor, and cast a quick glance at Elinor, as if remembering her promise. Yet, her natural disposition, which favoured plain speaking and direct pronouncements, would inevitably prevail, and she would soon be back on the subject of Delaford’s charms, or Colonel Brandon’s undeniable worth, albeit in a slightly less direct manner.

Elinor endured it all with a patient resignation that belied her inner turmoil. She felt, at times, like a sentinel, constantly on guard, deflecting every casual dart of conversation that might penetrate Marianne’s fragile peace. The evening, which to the others was a pleasant diversion, a social obligation cheerfully discharged, was to her a prolonged exercise in emotional vigilance. She longed for its close, for the quiet solitude of her own thoughts, where she might at last shed the mantle of polite composure and allow herself the luxury of unburdened anxiety.

Finally, the hour grew late, and Sir John, with a yawn that stretched his jovial face, declared it was time to depart. Mrs. Palmer, still in high spirits, though perhaps a little drowsy, gathered her reticule and shawl, reiterating her apologies to Elinor for any inadvertent offence, and expressing her sincere hope that Marianne would soon be quite well.

Goodbye, Elinor, she said, hugging her with a warmth that was both genuine and overwhelming. Do tell Marianne I hope she feels better soon. And do tell her that I think Colonel Brandon is quite the nicest man in the world, and I am sure she would be so happy with him! Oh, dear, I mean, I am sure she will be very happy, whatever happens.

Elinor murmured a polite farewell, and watched as the cheerful party, accompanied by Mrs. Jennings’s final hearty goodnights, made their way out into the night. When the door finally closed, and the silence of the house settled around her, she felt a profound sense of relief.

Mrs. Jennings returned to the drawing-room, her face still flushed with hospitality. Well, Elinor, she said, sinking into an armchair with a sigh of satisfaction, that was a pleasant evening, was it not? They are such good creatures, Sir John and Charlotte, always so ready to cheer one up. And I think I managed to give Charlotte a hint, after all, though she is a little scatter-brained, to be sure. She will not mean to hurt Marianne’s feelings, I am sure of it. And now, my dear, what about a little glass of this excellent Constantia for yourself? You look quite worn out. It has been a trying day for us all. But we must hope for the best, must we not? And I am sure, when Marianne wakes in the morning, she will be feeling much better. A good night’s sleep works wonders. And then, perhaps, we can begin to talk a little more sensibly about the future. Colonel Brandon is not a man to be trifled with, you know. He will make his intentions clear, I am sure of it, and then all will be well.

Elinor, weary but resolute, managed a faint smile. Thank you, Ma’am, she said, but I believe I shall follow Marianne’s example and retire for the night. I confess myself quite fatigued. And indeed, I feel that quiet and solitude are what we both require most at this moment.

Mrs. Jennings, though perhaps a little disappointed to be left alone with her Constantia, accepted Elinor’s decision with good grace. Of course, my dear, of course. Go to bed, and get some rest. And let us hope that tomorrow brings brighter prospects for us all. Especially for our dear Marianne.

Elinor bid her goodnight, and made her way upstairs. The house was utterly silent, save for the faint creaking of the old timbers. She paused for a moment outside Marianne’s door, listening intently. There was no sound, no whisper, no sigh. Only the deep, pervasive quiet of sleep, or perhaps, she feared, of profound exhaustion. She offered a silent prayer for her sister’s repose, and then continued to her own room. The events of the day, so emotionally charged, so full of painful revelations and thoughtless observations, revolved in her mind with a relentless persistence. She reflected upon Willoughby’s perfidy, upon Marianne’s shattered illusions, and upon the curious, often contradictory, workings of human nature as evinced by Mrs. Jennings and her family. The path ahead for Marianne seemed fraught with difficulty, and Elinor felt a weight of responsibility, a solemn duty to protect her sister’s sensitive heart from further wounds, and to guide her towards a future, however uncertain, that might yet hold a measure of peace and happiness. But for tonight, she could do no more. She undressed slowly, her movements quiet and deliberate, and at last lay down in the darkness, seeking the elusive comfort of sleep, hoping that the quiet hours of the night would indeed bring a measure of solace to them both. Her last conscious thought was of Marianne, alone in her room, and the long, arduous journey that lay before them. The world of society, with its rules and expectations, its kindnesses and its cruelties, seemed a distant, intricate web, and all she truly desired was a haven where such delicate hearts might find true and lasting serenity.

Sleep, however, did not come readily to Elinor. Her mind, perpetually vigilant, was too much occupied with the sorrows of the day, and the probable anguish of the morrow, to yield itself to immediate repose. She lay for a long time, tracing the patterns of moonlight upon the ceiling, her thoughts traversing the same weary ground. Willoughby, handsome and captivating, yet so unprincipled; Marianne, open, ardent, and now so cruelly wounded; Mrs. Jennings, kind, though so often indiscreet, whose very benevolence frequently inflicted new distresses upon those she meant to serve. To navigate this intricate web, to protect Marianne without isolating her, to soothe without indulging, to hope without deluding, seemed a task almost beyond her strength. Yet, her strength, though tried, had never failed her, and it was upon this quiet reservoir of fortitude that Elinor knew she must now draw more deeply than ever. At length, nature asserted its claims, and she fell into a restless slumber, filled with fragmented images of carriages dashing along turnpike roads, and the sound of bells ringing, not for joy, but for some forgotten sorrow.

When she awoke, the room was filled with the soft, diffused light of a grey morning. There was a sense of stillness, a quiet expectation, that seemed to hang in the air. She rose with a heaviness in her limbs, but with a renewed sense of purpose in her heart. Before attending to her own needs, she stole to Marianne’s door and listened. No sound broke the silence, which, this time, seemed less like exhaustion and more like the profound quiet of true sleep, or perhaps, a desperate, willed oblivion. She hoped earnestly for the former, for she knew that a mind so violently agitated required the balm of unconsciousness to restore any measure of its former peace.

Descending to the breakfast-parlour, she found Mrs. Jennings already seated, her spirits apparently undiminished by the previous day’s revelations. She greeted Elinor with a cheerful, indeed almost boisterous, good morning. “Well, my dear, and how is our poor invalid this morning? Has she tasted the Constantia? It would have done her a world of good, I am certain.” Elinor, pouring herself a cup of tea, replied with as much composure as she could muster, “I believe, Ma’am, she was still sleeping when I looked in, and I thought it best not to disturb her. She needs quiet more than anything, I imagine.” “Quiet, aye, quiet is all very well,” Mrs. Jennings declared, helping herself to a substantial slice of ham, “but a little company is better than a deal of quiet when one is out of sorts. Depend upon it, Elinor, my dear, she will not be truly mended until she is out and about, and seeing a few friendly faces. Why, my poor Mr. Jennings, when he had his worst fit of the gout, swore that a good laugh with a friend did more for his constitution than all the doctor’s nasty draughts put together. And he was always so jolly, bless his heart.”

Elinor listened with a patient smile, offering only a gentle nod in response. She knew it was useless to argue against Mrs. Jennings’s well-established theories of health and happiness. Her kindness was real, but her perception of others’ needs was often lamentably superficial, and her remedies, though offered with the best intentions, were frequently ill-suited to the delicate sensibilities of Marianne. She considered how she might best manage the coming day, and whether she should prevent Mrs. Jennings from attempting to cheer Marianne too vigorously. The very thought of Marianne being exposed to any further well-meant, but tactless, observations, made Elinor’s heart ache.

“But now, my dear,” Mrs. Jennings continued, interrupting Elinor’s silent planning, “we must think of what will amuse her today. I sent word last night, you know, to Sir John and Mrs. Palmer, that we would be happy to see them. They will be here for luncheon, I daresay, and that will do Marianne good, depend upon it. Charlotte is always so lively, and Sir John, he has such a fund of good humour.” Elinor’s heart sank. She had hoped to have a day of quietude, a day for Marianne to begin the slow, arduous process of healing in privacy. The thought of Mrs. Palmer’s unthinking effusiveness, coupled with Sir John’s hearty, well-intentioned, but often clumsy, jocularity, filled her with a profound sense of dread. She had expressly cautioned Mrs. Jennings against any mention of Mr. Willoughby, or the painful events which had so recently transpired, but she knew that to control the flow of Mrs. Palmer’s conversation was a task beyond even the most resolute of wills.

“Indeed, Ma’am,” Elinor began, choosing her words carefully, “I fear Marianne is still too unwell to receive company. Perhaps if we could prevail upon Sir John and Mrs. Palmer to postpone their visit, or at least to understand that Marianne is in no state for lively conversation.” Mrs. Jennings waved a dismissive hand. “Nonsense, child! It will do her the world of good. Charlotte will tell her all the London gossip, and make her laugh, depend upon it. And Sir John, he will be sure to find some new joke or other. A little laughter, Elinor, is the best medicine for a broken heart, next to a good husband, of course, and we are working on that, are

working on that, are we not, Elinor? Colonel Brandon, to be sure, will make her a very comfortable establishment, and a better match for her, depend upon it, than that flighty Willoughby ever could have been. He is a little older, I grant you, but what of that? A steady man, with a good income, and not a bit dissipated, no curricles and hunters for him, I warrant you. He will be quite the pattern of a husband, quite so.”

Elinor suppressed a sigh, her eyes fixed for a moment on the delicate pattern of the china cup before her. “Indeed, Ma’am,” she began, choosing her words with careful precision, “Colonel Brandon’s many good qualities are beyond dispute, and I have always held him in the highest regard. But I confess, my thoughts just now are entirely with Marianne’s present suffering, which is, I assure you, profound, and not to be alleviated by the prospect of future happiness, however secure, nor by the most well-meaning company, if that company should inadvertently remind her of what she has so recently lost.” She paused, hoping her earnestness might penetrate Mrs. Jennings’s habitual cheerfulness, and lend weight to her plea.

Mrs. Jennings, however, seemed to have taken only the general sense of Elinor’s words, which she immediately refashioned to suit her own theories. “Lost, my dear! Nonsense! What has she lost but a scoundrel who was not worth her little finger? And as for suffering, bless her heart, she will get over it! Young people always do. Why, when my poor sister Anne was crossed in love, she thought the world was at an end, and threatened to take to her bed for a twelvemonth. But then, you see, a charming young ensign came along, and before six weeks were out, she was as merry as a grig and dancing at every assembly. Time, Elinor, time and good company, are the only doctors for these complaints. And as for Colonel Brandon, why, the very sight of him, with his steady, kind eyes, will do her more good than all the broths and cordials in the county. You will see, my dear, you will see. Charlotte and Sir John will set her up in spirits, and then the Colonel, when he comes tonight, will complete the cure. Depend upon it, I know these things.”

Elinor could not help a small smile, recognising the futility of further argument. To reason with Mrs. Jennings was like attempting to stem a torrent with a sieve. Her good nature was so boundless, her conviction so unshakeable, and her understanding of nuanced distress so entirely absent, that any attempt to explain Marianne’s delicate state of mind would be utterly lost upon her. She could only hope to manage the forthcoming visit as best she might, and shield Marianne from its worst excesses.

“I only beg of you, Ma’am,” Elinor ventured, a slight tremor in her voice betraying her anxiety, “that if Sir John and Mrs. Palmer do come, you might perhaps give them a hint, as you so kindly offered before, not to speak of Mr. Willoughby, nor indeed of any matter that might lead Marianne to reflect upon her present unhappiness. Their kindness, I am sure, would lead them to respect such a request, if it were put to them.”

“Lord, yes, my dear, to be sure I shall!” cried Mrs. Jennings, clapping her hands together with a vigour that made the spoons rattle in their dishes. “I told you I would, did I not? And I am as good as my word. I shall pull Charlotte’s sleeve, and give Sir John a meaningful look, and they will understand directly. Not a word about Willoughby, not a syllable. Indeed, as I told you before, the less said the better, and the sooner forgot. It is quite settled, quite settled. Now, what shall we have for luncheon? I daresay Sir John will be hungry after his drive. Some of that nice cold tongue, perhaps, and a pigeon pie, and certainly some of Mrs. Smith’s excellent cheesecakes. Nothing like a good meal to put one in heart, is there?”

Elinor nodded faintly, her mind already racing with the grim possibilities of the day. Mrs. Jennings’s promise, though earnestly given, offered little comfort. She had witnessed Mrs. Palmer’s conversational habits on too many occasions to place much faith in her ability to curb her tongue, however strong the hint. Mrs. Palmer, with the best intentions in the world, often possessed a singular talent for saying precisely the wrong thing, and her vivacity, combined with a profound lack of reflection, made her impervious to subtle signals. Sir John, though generally more perceptive, was also given to an exuberance that might easily carry him beyond the bounds of caution. Elinor foresaw a day of constant vigilance, a series of desperate manoeuvres to steer conversation away from dangerous ground, and a profound weariness of spirit that would likely exceed Marianne’s own, for Marianne, in her deep sorrow, would at least have the mercy of being absorbed in her own pain, while Elinor would be acutely sensitive to every misguided word and glance.

The morning passed slowly, each minute bringing with it a fresh wave of dread for Elinor. Marianne, as Elinor had predicted, remained in her room. Elinor had gone to her shortly after leaving Mrs. Jennings, finding her still tearful and withdrawn, but willing to listen to Elinor’s gentle persuasion to rest. She had taken a little tea and toast, more for Elinor’s sake than her own appetite, and had then turned her face to the wall, requesting only to be left in peace. Elinor had therefore spent the morning in the drawing-room with Mrs. Jennings, attempting to read, though her mind continually wandered to the scene that awaited them. Mrs. Jennings, quite undisturbed by the impending social challenge, had cheerfully busied herself with various domestic arrangements, occasionally interjecting remarks about the suitability of certain dishes for luncheon, or speculating on the exact hour of Sir John and Mrs. Palmer’s arrival.

“I daresay they will be here by one at the latest,” Mrs. Jennings announced, looking out of the window for the tenth time, “Sir John is always so punctual when there is a good luncheon to be had. And Charlotte, she does love a drive in the country air. It puts colour in her cheeks, you know. Ah, there they are! I see the carriage turning in at the gate! Now, Elinor, my dear, do put on a cheerful face for them. It will do you good, too, to see a few happy people about.”

Elinor rose from her chair, her heart sinking, but forcing a polite smile to her lips. She arranged her features into an expression of composed welcome, preparing herself for the ordeal. As the carriage drew up to the door, and the hearty voices of Sir John and the lighter tones of Mrs. Palmer could be heard in the hall, Elinor felt a renewed sense of protectiveness for Marianne. She must, at all costs, prevent any further wound to her sister’s already lacerated heart.

The drawing-room door opened, and in they burst. Sir John, large and boisterous, immediately enveloped Mrs. Jennings in a bear hug, while Mrs. Palmer, a vision of fashionable vivacity, though heavily pregnant, greeted Elinor with a series of quick, bird-like kisses on the cheek.

“My dearest Mrs. Jennings, how delightful to see you!” cried Mrs. Palmer, her voice bright and unmodulated, “And Elinor, how very well you are looking! Though I declare, you have been quite a stranger lately. We have been quite lost without you at Barton Park. And how is dear Marianne? I was so sorry to hear of her indisposition. Is she not coming down to greet us?”

Elinor’s carefully constructed composure threatened to crumble at this direct assault. Mrs. Jennings, however, stepped in with admirable alacrity, and a rather unsubtle tug at Mrs. Palmer’s sleeve. “Ah, Charlotte, my dear, she is a little under the weather, you know, a slight chill, nothing serious, but she is best left to her rest, quite so. We must not disturb her just yet. You will see her later, I daresay. But come, sit down, sit down! Sir John, my dear man, how are you? You look as robust as ever!”

Sir John, having released Mrs. Jennings, turned his attention to Elinor, his face beaming. “Elinor, my dear, a pleasure, a very great pleasure! And how is my little Marianne? I heard she had taken to her bed. Is it true, eh? A touch of the vapours, perhaps? No doubt some sentimental nonsense, eh? Young girls, young girls! Always getting into a muddle over something or other, are they not? But she’ll soon be right as rain, depend upon it! Nothing a good gallop across the downs can’t cure, eh?” He winked conspiratorially at Elinor, quite unaware of the exquisite pain his words inflicted.

Elinor managed a weak smile. “Thank you, Sir John, Marianne is a little unwell, but we hope she will be quite herself again very soon. She is resting quietly at the moment.”

Mrs. Palmer, meanwhile, had settled herself on a sofa, fanning herself vigorously with a large feather fan. “Oh, dear, a chill, you say? How very unfortunate! I was so looking forward to hearing all the news from her. You know, Elinor, I heard the most extraordinary thing about Mr. Willoughby! You will never believe it! Mama told me, and she heard it from Mrs. Watson, who is quite a friend of the Middletons, so it must be true, you know. They say he is to be married, quite soon, to a Miss Grey, a lady with a fortune of fifty thousand pounds! Can you imagine! Fifty thousand pounds! I declare, I nearly fainted when Mama told me. And to think, after all the attention he paid dear Marianne! It is quite the most shocking thing, is it not?”

Elinor felt a cold dread settle in her stomach. She glanced at Mrs. Jennings, who, despite her earlier promises, seemed momentarily paralysed, her mouth slightly agape. Sir John, however, quite oblivious to the thunderbolt that had just struck the room, merely chuckled. “Ha, ha, Charlotte, my girl, always one for a bit of gossip, are we not? Fifty thousand pounds, you say? Well, well, a pretty sum indeed! The young rogue knows how to feather his nest, eh? Though I must say, I always thought he had an eye for our Marianne. A finer girl you won’t find anywhere, no, not anywhere!”

“Sir John, Mrs. Palmer,” Elinor interposed, her voice firm, though she felt a tremor in her hands, “I must beg of you, if you please, to refrain from discussing such matters. Marianne, as you know, is in a delicate state, and any reference to Mr. Willoughby, or indeed to anything that might distress her, is quite out of the question.” She met Mrs. Palmer’s gaze with a directness that, for a moment, seemed to penetrate even that lady’s blithe insensibility.

Mrs. Palmer blinked. “Oh, dear, I am so very sorry, Elinor! I quite forgot! Mama did tell me not to mention it, but it quite slipped my mind! My memory is so bad these days, you know, with the baby coming. And it was such a surprising piece of news! But indeed, I shall be quite silent on the matter, quite silent. I would not distress dear Marianne for the world, no, not for the world. Mama says she is quite broken-hearted, and indeed, I am so very sorry for her. Is she looking very pale, Elinor? I do hope she will not pine away. My cousin, Miss Jenkins, pined away quite dreadfully when she was disappointed in love, and it was quite melancholy to see her.”

Elinor clenched her hands in her lap. It was useless. Mrs. Palmer’s apologies were as thoughtless as her initial blunders, each word a further prick to Elinor’s already frayed nerves. Mrs. Jennings, having recovered her power of speech, now added her own frantic efforts to redirect the conversation.

“Yes, yes, Charlotte, my dear, quite so, quite so. We must not speak of such things. No, indeed. But come, the luncheon will be ready directly. Sir John, I daresay you are quite famished after your drive. We have a most excellent cold tongue, and some pigeon pie, and your favourite cheesecakes, Charlotte. Come, let us go into the dining-room. A good meal will set us all to rights.”

And so, with a flurry of motion and a babble of voices, they moved into the dining-room. Elinor followed, feeling as though she were walking through a waking nightmare. She caught Mrs. Jennings’s eye, a look of profound apology and helplessness passing between them, though Mrs. Jennings quickly recovered her usual sanguine expression.

The luncheon proved to be a prolonged agony for Elinor. Mrs. Palmer, though ostensibly chastened, seemed unable to resist allusions that, to Elinor’s sensitive ear, continually skirted the forbidden topic. She spoke of London, of assemblies, of various gentlemen of fortune, of happy engagements, and once, with particular heedlessness, of a recent elopement that had scandalised society, only to hastily add, “Not that I approve, of course! But one does hear such things, does one not? And it does make one think of poor, dear Marianne, and her sad fate.” Elinor had to exert every ounce of her self-control to maintain a calm demeanour, all the while attempting to introduce safe topics of conversation, such as the weather, the local crops, or Mrs. Jennings’s garden.

Sir John, for his part, was in high spirits, regaling them with anecdotes of his hunting exploits and local gossip. He was genuinely fond of Marianne and Elinor, and his boisterous attempts to cheer Elinor were well-meant, though they often felt to her like a further burden. “Come, Elinor, my dear,” he exclaimed at one point, pushing a dish of tarts towards her, “you are looking quite down in the mouth. What you need is a good laugh! I heard the funniest joke the other day from Parson Davies, quite capital, about a farmer and his pigs, you know. It would make even Marianne smile, I warrant you!”

Elinor forced herself to listen, to laugh faintly at the appropriate moments, and to engage in polite conversation, all the while feeling a profound weariness settling upon her. The thought of Marianne upstairs, alone in her sorrow, while these well-meaning, but utterly insensitive, people chattered below, was almost unbearable. She imagined her sister, perhaps hearing the muffled sounds of laughter and conversation, and feeling only more isolated in her grief.

At last, the interminable luncheon drew to a close. Mrs. Palmer declared herself quite fatigued and longed for a little rest before tea. Sir John, having eaten his fill, announced his intention to take a turn about the garden with Mrs. Jennings, to inspect her improvements. This presented Elinor with a brief window of escape.

“If you will excuse me, Ma’am,” she said to Mrs. Jennings, “I think I shall go and check on Marianne. Perhaps she might like a cup of tea, or feel up to a quiet conversation.”

Mrs. Jennings, momentarily distracted by Sir John’s proposal to examine a new rose bush, waved a dismissive hand. “Yes, yes, my dear, do go! And tell her that Charlotte is feeling quite a bit better after her luncheon, and will look in on her later. We must get her spirits up, you know! A little company and a good joke, that is the thing!”

Elinor made her escape, feeling a profound sense of relief as she ascended the stairs. The silence of the upper landing was a balm to her overtaxed nerves. She paused outside Marianne’s door, taking a deep breath, steeling herself for the quiet grief she knew she would find within.

She found Marianne still lying on the bed, her face turned away from the window, her hair spread dishevelled on the pillow. The curtains were drawn, casting the room into a soft, melancholy dimness. Elinor approached the bed gently.

“Marianne, my love,” she said softly, sitting on the edge of the bed and stroking her sister’s hair, “how do you feel? Would you like some tea? The others have just finished luncheon, and Mrs. Palmer is resting, and Sir John is in the garden with Mrs. Jennings.”

Marianne stirred, and slowly turned her head. Her eyes were still red and swollen, and her face was pale, almost translucent. “Elinor,” she whispered, her voice hoarse, “have they gone? Are they all gone?”

“Not yet, my dearest,” Elinor replied, trying to keep her voice steady, “Mrs. Palmer will rest for a while, and Sir John and Mrs. Jennings are outside. But they will be leaving later this afternoon, I am sure. You do not have to see them, if you do not wish it.”

A fresh wave of tears welled in Marianne’s eyes. “Oh, Elinor,” she sobbed, burying her face in the pillow again, “I heard them. I heard their voices, their laughter. It sounds so cruel, so heartless, when I feel as I do. And I heard Mrs. Palmer. She spoke of him, Elinor, she spoke of him and of his marriage. How could she, Elinor? How could anyone be so thoughtless?”

Elinor wrapped her arm around her sister, pulling her gently into an embrace. “Hush, my love, hush. Do not distress yourself further. Mrs. Palmer is quite thoughtless, it is true, but she means no harm. She does not understand, she cannot understand the depth of your feeling. Mrs. Jennings did try to caution her, but her discretion is not always to be relied upon. Try not to think of it, try not to let their words wound you. They are not worth it.”

“Not worth it!” Marianne cried, pulling away and looking at Elinor with agonised eyes. “Is nothing worth it, Elinor? Is my suffering nothing? Is his faithlessness nothing? Does the world go on unheeding, unfeeling, while my heart breaks?”

“No, my dearest, your suffering is not nothing,” Elinor said, holding Marianne’s hands tightly, “It is everything to me, and it is a pain that few, I hope, will ever know. But you must not let the unkindness or the thoughtlessness of others add to it. You must try to rise above it, for your own sake. They do not mean to hurt you, Marianne, however clumsy their words may be.”

“I wish they would all go away,” Marianne murmured, her voice muffled, “I wish to be alone with you, Elinor. Only with you. The world is too much for me now.”

“And you shall be, my love,” Elinor promised, gently smoothing Marianne’s hair, “You shall be. I will stay here with you. And when they come up for tea, I will tell them you are still too unwell to receive visitors. You must try to rest, Marianne. Try to sleep. Sleep is the kindest friend to sorrow.”

Marianne seemed to take some small comfort from Elinor’s presence and her words. She closed her eyes again, and Elinor remained by her side, holding her hand, listening to the muffled sounds of life downstairs, and wishing fervently that the day, with all its trials, would soon come to an end. She felt a profound and aching love for her sister, a desire to absorb all of Marianne’s pain into her own, to shield her completely from a world that seemed so often to misunderstand and wound.

After a long silence, broken only by Marianne’s quiet sniffling, Elinor heard a light tap at the door. She rose softly, crossing to open it a crack, and found Mrs. Palmer, her face flushed from her nap, peering in.

“Oh, Elinor,” she whispered, “is dear Marianne awake? I felt so much better after my little rest, and I thought I would just pop in and see if she felt like a little chat. Mama said she was not to be disturbed, but I thought a quiet word might do her good, you know. I promise I will not mention anything of that shocking Willoughby affair again, no, not a word. I am quite chastened, quite so.”

Elinor managed a tight smile. “Thank you, Mrs. Palmer, for your kind thought. But Marianne is still sleeping, I believe, and she is truly very unwell. I think it best that she continue to rest undisturbed. Perhaps another time, when she is more recovered.”

Mrs. Palmer’s face fell. “Oh, dear. Still sleeping? How very unfortunate. I had quite set my heart on cheering her up. Mama says a little cheerfulness is the best thing, you know, for low spirits. But indeed, if she is sleeping, I must not wake her. You will tell her I called, will you not? And tell her I am so very sorry for her sadness, and that I hope she will be quite well very soon. We must all be very kind to her now, must we not? For it is a very great disappointment, is it not, to be thrown over for fifty thousand pounds?”

Elinor quickly interjected, before Mrs. Palmer could proceed further down that dangerous path. “Indeed, Mrs. Palmer, your kindness is greatly appreciated. But if you would not mind, I truly believe quiet is what Marianne requires above all else at present. She has been much tried.”

“Yes, yes, of course,” Mrs. Palmer said, though her expression was still one of disappointment. “Quiet. I understand. Well, I shall go downstairs and have my tea. Sir John will be wanting his, I daresay. And perhaps a little something sweet, for I am quite peckish again. Do tell Marianne I was thinking of her, won’t you?”

With another forced smile, Elinor closed the door, leaning against it for a moment, letting out a long, weary breath. It was a constant battle, a continuous effort to deflect and protect, and she felt utterly exhausted by it. As she made her way upstairs, the fragrance of the roses seemed to mock her. How little such superficial tokens could assuage a heart so deeply bruised. “They are merely a token of their kind intentions, dearest,” Elinor said, sitting on the edge of the bed and gently stroking her sister’s hair. “Colonel Brandon is here. He expressed his deepest sympathy for your distress.”

“A reproach? How can you say so, Marianne? He is kindness itself, and truly concerned for your welfare.”

“He knows,” Marianne murmured, her voice filled with a desperate pain. “He knows all of it. His eyes are full of pity, and I cannot bear pity, Elinor. I cannot bear to be an object of contemplation, a spectacle of sorrow for the world to observe.”

Elinor sighed, understanding the raw sensitivity that underlay Marianne’s words. Her sister, in her profound grief, felt herself exposed, her private agony laid bare for public consumption. “No one regards you as a spectacle, my dearest. They are merely concerned for you. And Colonel Brandon, above all, is a gentleman of the utmost discretion. He would never presume to judge you.”

“They pity me for having been abandoned, for being made a fool of,” Marianne continued, her voice growing stronger with a surge of bitterness. “They see my desolation and they shake their heads. And he, who has so long wished for what Willoughby has cast aside, he must surely feel a grim satisfaction. I cannot face his quiet triumph, Elinor. Not now.”

Elinor knew it was useless to argue with Marianne in her present state of mind. Her grief had twisted her perceptions, making her interpret even the most genuine kindness as a form of condemnation. “There is no triumph in his heart, Marianne, only sympathy. But if you do not wish to see him, or anyone else, then you shall not. I will tell Mrs. Jennings that you are too unwell to join us this evening.”

Marianne finally turned, her face pale and tear-stained, her eyes red-rimmed. “Thank you, Elinor. You are ever my guardian angel. But what shall I do with myself? The hours stretch endlessly before me, and every moment is a fresh torment.”

“You must try to rest, my love. Perhaps a little sleep will bring some respite.” Elinor smoothed the rumpled covers, her heart aching for her sister’s suffering.

“Sleep will not come,” Marianne said, shaking her head. “My mind races, Elinor, a thousand images, a thousand words, they chase each other through my thoughts, giving me no peace. Oh, Elinor, how could he have done it? How could he have been so false?”

Elinor sat in silence for a few moments, offering the only comfort she could: her quiet, unwavering presence. She knew that no words of hers could bridge the chasm of Marianne’s despair, but perhaps her steadfast affection might provide a small anchor in the storm. She thought of Mrs. Jennings’s well-meaning but utterly ineffective advice, of Mrs. Palmer’s unintentional cruelty, and she knew that only she could truly understand the depth of Marianne’s wound.

“He is not worthy of your tears, Marianne,” Elinor said at last, her voice soft but resolute. “He has proven himself to be a man of weak principles, and you, my dearest sister, are far too good, too noble, too generous for such a one. Though it seems impossible now, believe me, time will heal this wound. You will find happiness again, with one who truly deserves your affection.”

Marianne merely closed her eyes, a fresh tear tracing a path down her pale cheek. “I shall never love again, Elinor. My heart is broken, shattered beyond repair. He was my life, my soul. There can be no other.”

Elinor said nothing more, knowing that such pronouncements, though heartfelt, were the natural expressions of a soul in the throes of first grief. She remained by Marianne’s side, holding her hand, until the soft murmur of voices from below stairs indicated that the garden party was over, and the company was returning to the drawing-room. She then gently disengaged her hand.

“I must go down, Marianne. But I will return to you often. Do try to rest, my dear.”

Marianne nodded, her eyes still closed. Elinor placed a kiss on her forehead and left the room, leaving the roses, a symbol of fleeting beauty, beside her heartbroken sister. She paused for a moment outside the door, composing herself before descending the stairs. The evening, she knew, would be long and arduous, but she would face it with all the strength she possessed, for Marianne’s sake.

Downstairs, the drawing-room was now bustling with the additional company of two neighbours, Mr. and Mrs. Pratt, a quiet, unassuming couple, and two elderly spinsters, the Miss Steeles, who were distant relations of Mrs. Jennings. Mrs. Jennings was in her element, directing everyone to seats, arranging card tables, and ensuring that all were supplied with tea, coffee, or a glass of port.

“Ah, Elinor, there you are! How is our dear invalid? Still moping, eh? Well, never mind, we shall have a merry evening here, and that will do her more good than all the broths in the world!” Mrs. Jennings declared, oblivious to the fact that Marianne would not be joining them. “Colonel, you must play whist with Sir John and Mrs. Pratt! And Mr. Pratt, you can partner me against the Miss Steeles! And Elinor, my dear, you can sit with Charlotte and listen to the gossip, for that is always entertaining, is it not?” She beamed, pleased with her arrangements.

Elinor offered a polite smile. “I am afraid Marianne is still quite unwell, Ma’am. I think it best if she remain undisturbed tonight.”

“Still unwell? Oh, the poor dear! But a good game of cards would surely cheer her! Or a little music, perhaps? The Miss Steeles are quite excellent on the spinet!”

“No, indeed, Ma’am. Quiet is truly what she needs. I will remain here and keep her company when I can, but for now, she is determined to rest.”

“Well, if she must, she must,” Mrs. Jennings conceded, though with evident reluctance. “But it is a shame, a great shame, for we shall be so merry! Never mind, Elinor, you must make up for her absence! Come, sit by Charlotte. She has been telling me all about the new fashions in London, quite delightful, indeed!”

Elinor found herself seated next to Mrs. Palmer, who immediately launched into a detailed description of a ball she had attended, replete with observations on the dresses, the supper, and the various flirtations observed. Mrs. Palmer, in her characteristic manner, spoke without much thought or consideration, her words flowing freely and happily, much like a sparkling, but rather shallow, stream.

“And you know, Elinor, I saw Mr. Willoughby there! He looked quite dashing, though a little pale, I thought. But he was with Miss Grey, and she is quite plain, indeed she is. But then, she has such a fortune, so it does not signify, does it? And he was quite attentive to her, though I did see him glance at the door once or twice, as if he were looking for someone else. And Lady Middleton told me that he is quite devoted to her now, and they are to be married very soon, perhaps next month! So it is quite settled, and poor Marianne will have to find someone else, indeed she will. Though Colonel Brandon is very fond of her, is he not? And he is very kind, and very sensible, and has two thousand a year, which is quite comfortable, indeed it is. And Delaford is a lovely place, Aunt Jennings says, with a mulberry tree and a dove-cote! Quite delightful, indeed!”

Elinor listened, her heart sinking with each fresh pronouncement. Mrs. Palmer’s conversational style was a relentless tide, sweeping aside all attempts at decorum or discretion. Colonel Brandon, at the whist table, seemed to have heard Mrs. Palmer’s innocent, yet devastating, remarks, for he faltered in his play, and Sir John gave a hearty laugh, saying, “Colonel, your mind is wandering! Are you thinking of Delaford, or of a certain young lady, eh?”

Colonel Brandon merely cleared his throat and resumed his game, though a faint flush remained on his cheeks. Elinor, feeling a profound weariness, excused herself on the pretext of checking on Marianne. She needed to escape the suffocating atmosphere of forced cheerfulness and unintended cruelty. Upstairs, she found Marianne still lying on the bed, her face now buried in the pillow. The roses lay untouched on the table, their freshness a silent testament to the contrast between the world of ordinary life and the private agony of a broken heart. Elinor sat beside her sister, in the quiet darkness of the room, and simply held her hand, offering a silent, steadfast companionship in the face of such profound sorrow.made her impervious to any instruction, however clearly or urgently conveyed, that required the slightest degree of mental exertion or self-restraint. It was not ill-will, Elinor knew, that prompted Mrs. Palmer’s indiscretions; it was simply an incapacity to comprehend that words could wound, or that silence might sometimes be a greater kindness than the most effusive expression of sympathy. She spoke from the heart, and that heart, though warm and generous, was alas, governed by no very keen understanding. She could only steel herself for the inevitable onslaught, praying that Marianne’s seclusion would protect her from the worst excesses of well-meaning but thoughtless chatter.

Elinor retreated to the window, feigning an interest in the damp garden without, whilst Mrs. Jennings bustled about the room, giving orders to the servants regarding the arrangement of the table and the placement of the dishes. The very air seemed to vibrate with Mrs. Jennings’s well-intentioned but overwhelming energy, an energy which now, more than ever, grated upon Elinor’s nerves. Her sister was alone, wrestling with a grief which Elinor herself could barely fathom, whilst she, Elinor, was compelled to prepare for a performance of forced gaiety and strained composure. It was a cruel irony, and one which pressed heavily upon her spirit. She longed for the quiet solitude of their cottage at Barton, or even for the bustling but impersonal streets of London, where anonymity might afford a refuge from such suffocating kindness.

Just as the clock upon the mantelpiece chimed the quarter past one, indicating that the appointed hour was near, the sound of carriage wheels upon the gravel drive announced the arrival of the visitors. Mrs. Jennings gave a triumphant little clap and hurried to the hall, her voice already raised in a joyful welcome. Elinor braced herself, adjusting her shawl and attempting to compose her features into an expression of serene welcome. It was a familiar role, that of the calm and collected elder sister, ever ready to soothe or support, and she drew upon it now with a weariness that belied her outward composure.

The drawing-room door was thrown open, and in they came: Sir John Middleton, a hearty, florid man, whose good humour seemed to expand to fill any available space, and his wife, Mrs. Palmer, a vision of youthful vivacity in a gown of bright muslin, her cheeks flushed with the keen air of the drive and her eyes sparkling with an almost childlike glee.
