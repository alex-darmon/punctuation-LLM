Mrs. Alveston was delighted with the new tapestry. "So very kind of Mr. Denby," she exclaimed, "to procure such an exquisite piece from London. It perfectly adorns this wall, does it not, Miss Fenton? And the colours, so very cheerful."
Miss Fenton, who had been contemplating the rather vibrant hues with a polite but measured gaze, replied, "Indeed, Mrs. Alveston. It quite brightens the room." Her thoughts, however, strayed to Mr. Denby's known penchant for extravagance.
Just then, Mr. Robert Langley entered, having been detained by a stray dog in the lane. "My apologies, ladies," he began, with a bow that conveyed more amusement than contrition, "but a most urgent matter of canine rescue demanded my immediate attention."
Miss Eleanor Vance, perched on a settee like a bird, chirped, "Oh, Mr. Langley, you are always so gallant! I daresay the poor creature was quite lost."

"Oh, Mr. Davies," called Mrs. Alveston, leaning slightly into the open window, "I was merely thinking of your wonderful rose bushes, and your kind promise of cuttings! Any chance you might have them today?"

Mr. Davies, however, seemed determined to escape. "Another time, perhaps, Mrs. Alveston. I truly must press on. My compliments to all within." And with a nod, he spurred his horse and was off.
Mrs. Alveston sighed as Miss Vance closed the window. "Such a good man, Mr. Davies, but always in such a hurry. He never can bear to stand still for long. "I have no knowledge, sir, to form such an opinion," she retorted, looking pointedly at her needlework.

Miss Fenton's hand paused in its motion. "Sir, I believe you are venturing into territory best left unexplored."

Mrs. Alveston, meanwhile, completely oblivious, had settled back with a contented sigh. "Such a cheerful morning! I do so enjoy a good call. And to think of Mr. Davies, so thoughtful about the roses! It quite warms one's heart, does it not? I always say, there is nothing like kindness in this world."

She had, as Mrs. Weston knew, a decided opinion upon the attachment existing between Mr. Frank Churchill and Miss Fairfax, an opinion she had long cherished, and one which Mr. Churchill’s very recent conversation had seemed only to confirm. Frank Churchill patted her arm playfully. “My dear Miss Smith, that is precisely my intention. To make everyone feel a little foolish, except for myself, of course. Emma, though amused by his ready wit, could not quite dismiss the unsettling nature of the morning’s events. Mr. Knightley’s earnestness for Miss Fairfax, and Mr. Churchill’s almost defiant playfulness, presented two narratives that her mind struggled to reconcile. If Mr. Churchill was indeed attached to Miss Fairfax, as she firmly believed, then Mr. Knightley’s intense concern might indeed be purely of a benevolent, perhaps even a fraternal, kind. Yet, there was something in his tone, a certain possessiveness in his inquiry, that belied such a simple explanation. As they drew nearer to Hartfield, the crisp morning air gave way to the familiar warmth of their own gates. And Miss Bates, poor woman, was, I dare say, quite delighted to have her little drawing-room the scene of so much animated discussion.”

“Indeed,” said Emma, with a slight sigh, “Miss Bates is always delighted by any event that furnishes her with new material for conversation, however insignificant or however perplexing it may prove to others. But I confess, Mrs. Weston, that this morning’s occurrences have left me with a rather uncomfortable sense of unresolved questions. Mr. Knightley’s interest in Miss Fairfax, and Mr. Churchill’s equally pronounced, if more subtly expressed, interest—they seem to demand a clarity which they are at present far from possessing.”

Upon their arrival at Hartfield, they found the parlour warm and inviting. Mr. Woodhouse, observing Emma’s quiet reflection, ventured, “My dear, you seem a little fatigued. “Indeed, Papa,” Emma replied, rousing herself with a smile, “I believe I am merely pondering the various currents of opinion that flowed through Miss Bates’s room this morning. Later in the day, after Mr. Churchill had retired to the library, and Harriet to her drawing, Emma found herself alone with her thoughts, which perpetually returned to the strange conjunction of circumstances. She replayed Mr. Knightley’s resolute inquiries, his firm cutting short of Miss Bates, his almost public declaration of Miss Fairfax’s dancing merits. It was true that he had included Mrs. Weston in his compliment, but the initial, emphatic mention of Miss Fairfax had been too marked to be easily dismissed. Then she recalled Frank Churchill’s quick, almost defiant, look at her when he spoke of the waltz, and his subsequent pronouncements upon the new music and the thoughtfulness of Colonel Campbell, all clearly directed at Miss Fairfax, and intended, Emma felt certain, to signal their secret understanding.

“He knew Miss Fairfax could have no music here,” Frank had said, “I honour that part of the attention particularly; it shews it to have been so thoroughly from the heart. Nothing hastily done; nothing incomplete. True affection only could have prompted it.” These words, spoken with such emphasis, and followed by the sight of Jane’s “secret delight” beneath a deep blush, were to Emma irrefutable evidence. Yet, how to reconcile this with Mr. Knightley’s extraordinary, uncharacteristic display?

Could it be that Mr. Knightley, with his customary prudence and his deep affection for the Woodhouse family, was merely attempting to divert attention from what he perceived as a burgeoning, and perhaps imprudent, attachment between Frank Churchill and Jane Fairfax? This thought, once entertained, seemed to offer a momentary, if somewhat strained, resolution. Yes, Mr. Knightley, ever the guardian of propriety, might have sought to counteract Mr. Churchill’s too-obvious gallantry by drawing Jane Fairfax into a more general, and therefore less suspicious, commendation. He might have wanted to protect her, to throw a veil of general regard over what he suspected was a particular, and therefore potentially compromising, attention from a young man without fixed prospects. This explanation, while not entirely satisfying, at least preserved Emma’s primary conviction and Mr. Knightley’s integrity. He was not, could not be, romantically interested in Jane Fairfax. Such an idea was too disruptive to her established order of things, too jarring to her sense of what was proper and fitting.

Yet, a stubborn little doubt persisted, a tiny speck in the otherwise clear lens of her judgment. Emma leaned back, closing her eyes for a moment. Her active intellect, usually so confident in its conclusions, found itself turning in circles. It was rare for her to be so thoroughly perplexed. It was all a tangle, and Emma, for perhaps the first time in a long while, wished for a clear and unambiguous sign. Frank Churchill patted her arm playfully. “My dear Miss Smith, that is precisely my intention. To make everyone feel a little foolish, except for myself, of course. Emma, though amused by his ready wit, could not quite dismiss the unsettling nature of the morning’s events. Mr. Knightley’s earnestness for Miss Fairfax, and Mr. Churchill’s almost defiant playfulness, presented two narratives that her mind struggled to reconcile. If Mr. Churchill was indeed attached to Miss Fairfax, as she firmly believed, then Mr. Knightley’s intense concern might indeed be purely of a benevolent, perhaps even a fraternal, kind. She called to mind Frank Churchill’s pointed remarks, his persistent efforts to link Mr. Knightley with Jane, and even his odd insistence that *Robin Adair* was “his” favourite tune. Could Frank, with his characteristic mischief, have been hinting at a truth he knew, rather than merely indulging in sport? It was possible. Frank, though often thoughtless, was not without a certain shrewdness, and Emma had sometimes suspected him of playing a deeper game than he let appear. If he truly knew of an attachment between Mr. Knightley and Jane, then his behaviour became, if not less reprehensible, at least more understandable in its motivation. He would be using Emma’s blindness, perhaps even her own feelings, as a foil for his own private amusement. The thought brought a fresh wave of indignation.

Emma found herself increasingly disturbed by these conjectures, and the peaceful tenor of her days at Hartfield was perceptibly broken. The mornings, once devoted to cheerful activity or agreeable idleness, now often found her lost in thought, dissecting every past conversation, every observed gesture, in a fruitless effort to piece together the truth. Her solicitude for her father, usually her primary concern, felt at times like a welcome distraction from her own perplexing emotions. She would sit with him, reading or playing backgammon, whilst her mind, unbidden, would wander to the cottage, to the possible secret, to Mr. Knightley. She felt a vague and unfamiliar restlessness, a sense that something vital had shifted in her world, and she knew not how to restore its former equilibrium.

The truth, when it began to dawn upon her, was not something she sought with eagerness, but rather something that forced itself upon her consciousness with the slow, inexorable power of a rising tide. She had always regarded Mr. Knightley with the highest esteem, an admiration founded upon his solid worth, his unerring judgment, and his constant kindness. He was a friend, a mentor, an elder brother; a fixture in her life, as certain and as unchangeable as Hartfield itself. The idea of him as a lover, a suitor, for *anyone*, had been an amusing abstraction, certainly not a matter to stir her own bosom with such disquiet. But now, as she observed his perceived attentions to Jane, as she allowed her mind to entertain the possibility of their union, a different feeling, one she had hitherto resolutely ignored or misidentified, began to assert itself.

It was not mere pique, she now reluctantly admitted, nor disappointment that her own discernment had been so faulty. It was a sensation far more profound, a keen, almost physical ache at the thought of losing him from her immediate, exclusive sphere of influence. To think of him as belonging to another, as sharing his thoughts, his affections, his very life with someone else, was a prospect that filled her with a strange, suffocating dread. She had always imagined him at Hartfield, part of her domestic circle, an unfailing source of counsel and companionship. To picture him, instead, at Donwell, with Jane Fairfax as his mistress, presiding over his home, receiving his guests, sharing his confidences, was an image that wounded her more deeply than she

had ever allowed herself to imagine before. She had always prided herself on her perception, her discernment of character, her sound judgment in matters of affection, especially where others were concerned, yet now, she saw herself to have been most deplorably mistaken, most lamentably blind, not only to the sentiments of those around her, but to the deepest impulses of her own heart. The very idea of Mr. Knightley, her Mr. Knightley, attached to Miss Fairfax, so proper, so elegant, so universally admired, yet to Emma so cold, so reserved, so little interesting, caused her a shock of dismay that was as immediate as it was profound. It was not merely the loss of a companion, a mentor, an elder brother that she now contemplated with such apprehension, it was the chilling realisation that her own happiness, an happiness which she had always considered secure and independent of such contingencies, was, in fact, inextricably bound to his presence, to his undivided regard, to the comforting certainty of his belonging to her, and to her alone, in that peculiar, intimate way which had always been the foundation of their long and cherished friendship. She recollected, with a pang of bitter self-reproach, the easy confidence with which she had discussed his various supposed attachments, and with what utter indifference she had once imagined him married to some other woman, providing only that she might still enjoy his society. How strange, how incredible, how lamentable now appeared that former complacency, that heedless security of heart. She felt a mortifying blush rise to her cheeks, though she was quite alone, at the remembrance of her past presumption, her unfailing readiness to meddle in the affairs of others, whilst remaining so utterly, so ridiculously ignorant of her own. She had played the matchmaker with such a spirit of superiority, so convinced of her own infallible judgment, so blind to the delicate threads of affection that she herself had carelessly, if not culpably, entangled.

Her most immediate, most overwhelming source of misery, however, was the reflection upon Harriet. Poor, deluded Harriet, whom she had so carefully, so assiduously, led astray from the suitable and deserving Mr. Martin, whom she had flattered with such false hopes, whom she had encouraged, nay, almost commanded, to fix her affections upon a gentleman so far removed from her sphere, so entirely out of her reach, and now, as it appeared, so completely preoccupied with another. Emma shuddered to think of the anguish that awaited her young friend, an anguish of which Emma herself was the primary, the unwitting, yet undoubtedly the most responsible, cause. To have wounded so innocent a heart, to have built up such castles in the air, only to see them inevitably, cruelly, dashed to pieces, was a source of torment more sharp, more truly punishing, than any mere personal disappointment could inflict. Her previous self-satisfaction in the notion of Harriet’s elevated prospects, her conviction that Mr. Knightley, despite his present apparent coolness, must eventually discern Harriet’s sweet temper and yielding disposition, now appeared to her as the most egregious folly, a vanity of the most contemptible sort. She had, in her boundless self-approbation, mistaken her own power, her own judgment, her own perception. She had presumed to understand Mr. Knightley’s heart better than he did himself, and had been content to weave a web of delusion around Harriet, believing herself capable of directing fate itself. The consequences of this arrogance were now beginning to unfold before her, and they presented a picture of such distress, such unavoidable suffering for another, that Emma could scarcely bear to contemplate it. She saw herself, not as the clever, benevolent patroness she had always aspired to be, but as a vain, meddling, and ultimately harmful influence, whose actions had brought nothing but pain and confusion.

The days that followed the visit to Mrs. Bates’s cottage were marked by a disquietude at Hartfield such as Emma had never before experienced. The familiar rooms, the routine of her duties, the pleasant companionship of her father, all seemed imbued with a new, unwelcome solemnity. Her cheerful spirits, once so resilient, were now frequently overcast, her thoughts perpetually drawn to the unfolding drama of her own heart and the entangled affections she had so heedlessly manipulated. She found herself scrutinising every past conversation with Mr. Knightley, every look, every nuance of his manner, searching for evidence that might support or refute the terrifying conjecture that had taken root in her mind. His frequent visits to Hartfield, his constant concern for her father, his patient counsel, his mild reproofs, all of which she had once accepted as the natural, unquestioning expressions of their long-standing, unique friendship, now seemed susceptible of a different, more tender interpretation. And yet, almost immediately, her mind would recoil from such a comforting thought, for then she would remember his directness with Miss

Fairfax, that pointed, almost demanding interest in her welfare, his public, unsolicited defence of her against the general merriment, his very readiness to hear every detail concerning her health and comfort, even to the exclusion of all Miss Bates’s effusions upon the carriage and the late party. Was this not the solicitude of a heart already engaged, or at the very least, strongly predisposed? What other motive could explain such an abrupt interruption, such a determined redirection of conversation, but a deeply felt concern, a desire to protect and to honour? Emma felt a cold dread creep through her, dispelling the brief, intoxicating warmth of imagining herself the object of his tender regard. No, it was not for her; it was for Miss Fairfax, the reserved, the accomplished, the universally admired Miss Fairfax, that his attention had been so singularly directed.

And with this conviction, her thoughts returned with renewed and sharpened agony to Harriet. To have so cruelly misguided a creature so innocent, so trusting, whose very simplicity had been, in Emma’s estimation, her greatest charm; to have built for her, with such heedless presumption, a future predicated upon a fancy, a mere conjecture, which now proved to be so utterly, so painfully, unfounded. The image of Harriet’s sweet, open face, beaming with the hopeful anticipation Emma herself had fostered, rose before her, and Emma felt a pang of remorse so acute it might almost be termed physical. How could she have been so blind, so vain, so wickedly confident in her own powers of discernment? She had seen Mr. Knightley’s kindness, his paternal regard for Harriet, as the incipient stages of a tender attachment; she had interpreted his benevolent interest in her welfare, his gentle suggestions for her improvement, as the subtle promptings of a love which would, in time, declare itself. But now, seen through this new, chilling lens of possibility, all those signs appeared simply as the actions of a truly good man, a man whose benevolence extended to all around him, and whose regard for Harriet was no more, and no less, than a kind protector’s interest in a deserving young woman. The peculiar intensity, the almost proprietary air, which had so misled her in his address to Harriet, was now revealed as nothing more than the careful concern of a truly good man for any young woman in his immediate circle. His gentle remonstrances, his patient instructions, his evident desire for her improvement, all those small kindnesses which Emma, in her egregious self-deception, had construed as the tender intimations of a lover, were in reality but the consistent emanations of his benevolent spirit, extending to all who were, however distantly, connected with his regard. He had wished Harriet well, he had wished her to be sensible and well-conducted, for the simple reason that he wished well to all deserving creatures, and that his principles would not suffer him to observe imperfection without a quiet effort to amend it. It was a universal philanthropy, as warm as it was judicious, and utterly distinct from the selective, passionate attachment she had so carelessly imputed to him. How blind had she been, how monstrously arrogant, to interpret such broad, disinterested goodness as the peculiar inclination of a man already secretly devoted to one particular object. Her vanity, her presumptuousness, had painted the canvas of truth with colours of her own imagining, creating a portrait so utterly divorced from reality as to now appear grotesque in its distortion.

And Harriet. Poor, confiding Harriet. Emma’s heart sank with a weight she had never before experienced, a crushing oppression of guilt and shame. She had taken a simple, innocent heart, one susceptible to easy impressions and grateful for every kindness, and filled it

and filled it with notions as delusive as they were dangerous, poisoning, as it were, the very spring of its natural content with the artificial sweetness of an imagined preference. This was the crushing truth, now revealed to her with the force of an unanswerable demonstration, a revelation that stripped away every comforting layer of her self-approbation and left her exposed to the keenest torment of a self-reproach hitherto unknown. The lightness of heart, which had been her constant companion, and the easy confidence in her own judgment, which had guided her through so many ventures of social management, fled from her utterly, leaving only a desolate sense of wrong-doing and an overwhelming dread of its inevitable consequences. What she had done, in the full bloom of her presumptuous vanity, was to take a creature whose happiness lay in the simple enjoyments of a modest life, in the gratitude for genuine kindness, and in the affection of those who truly esteemed her, and to elevate her into an imaginary sphere where a connection with Mr. Knightley, a gentleman of such superior understanding and unquestioned consequence, appeared not merely possible, but almost a settled expectation. The picture she had drawn for Harriet, with such careless vivacity, of a future brightened by a union with one so worthy, now seemed to her a cruel and elaborate deceit, a snare laid by her own thoughtless hand for the innocent foot of her unsuspecting friend.

Her internal agony, though entirely private, was so intense as to make the external world seem dim and distant. She scarcely heard the gentle pleasantries exchanged by Mrs. Weston and her companion, as they walked from Miss Bates’s door; she moved as if in a dream, her eyes fixed upon the path, yet seeing nothing but the unfolding panorama of her own fatal errors. To have been so mistaken, so thoroughly, so egregiously mistaken, was a humiliation she had never before conceived. To have believed herself possessed of a sagacity beyond her years, a penetration into the depths of human character and intention, only to find herself duped by her own ardent wishes and an almost childish preference for romantic complexity over plain, unadorned truth, was a blow to her pride from which she felt it would take her a very long time to recover. And then, there was Harriet; poor, dear Harriet, whose simple heart, so susceptible to affection, so trustful of her friend’s superior intellect, had imbibed every flattering suggestion with innocent faith. How was she to undo such mischief? How was she to retract a hope so carefully nurtured, a prospect so glowingly depicted, without inflicting a wound too deep for her gentle spirit to bear? The task appeared insurmountable, and the thought of Harriet’s disappointment, her sorrow, her possible resentment, caused Emma’s heart to ache with a dull, persistent pain.

They had now reached the entrance to Hartfield. Mrs. Weston, observing Emma’s unusual quietness, and perhaps discerning a deeper agitation than mere fatigue or the effect of the morning’s varied excitements, addressed her with a tender solicitude that roused Emma from her painful reverie. “My dear Emma,” she began, “you are very thoughtful this morning. Has anything happened to displease you, or are you merely weary from the liveliness of Miss Bates’s company, which I confess, can sometimes be a little overwhelming?” Emma started, forcing a smile that felt unnatural upon her lips. “No, no, dear Mrs. Weston, not at all displeased. Only a little fatigued, as you say, and perhaps a little contemplative. The morning has been, as you rightly observe, full of varied excitements.” She cast a quick, involuntary glance towards the other young lady accompanying them, who was indeed Harriet Smith, and caught her looking back with an expression of quiet content, a smile still lingering from the pleasantries of the morning visit, a smile which, to Emma’s now heightened sensibility, seemed to bear the distinct imprint of hopeful anticipation. The sight was a fresh stab to Emma’s conscience, reinforcing the urgency and the difficulty of her impending confession.

“Well, you must rest now,” said Mrs. Weston, with a kind pressure of her hand. “I am glad we were able to walk together, if only for this short distance. Mr. Weston will be waiting, I daresay.” And with a parting word of affection, Mrs. Weston and her companion took their leave, leaving Emma and Harriet to pass through the gates of Hartfield.

The quiet approach to the house, usually a source of tranquil satisfaction, now felt charged with an unbearable weight. Emma sought desperately for a means to delay, to postpone the inevitable conversation that now seemed to loom before her like a dark and unyielding wall. She spoke of the beauty of the gardens, the progress of the spring blossoms, anything to fill the silence, to avert the moment when she must begin to unravel the complicated web she had so meticulously, so foolishly, woven. Harriet, however, seemed perfectly content to stroll by her side, occasionally assenting to Emma’s observations with her usual sweet simplicity, her mind evidently still dwelling on the agreeable impressions of the morning, perhaps even, Emma shuddered to think, on some imagined consequence arising from Mr. Knightley’s brief presence.

Upon entering the house, Emma, with a hasty word about some trifling domestic matter, suggested they retire to her dressing-room, a place where, as she reasoned with herself, they might speak with the utmost privacy, undisturbed by Mr. Woodhouse’s possible interruptions. Her heart pounded with an agitation that belied the calm she strove to project, and as she closed the door behind them, she felt a profound sense of isolation, of being alone with the magnitude of her error and the delicate, dangerous task of its rectification.

She sank into a chair, motioning for Harriet to take another opposite her, and for a moment, neither spoke. Emma gazed at Harriet’s innocent face, the open countenance, the guileless eyes, and felt an overwhelming surge of tenderness mingled with the bitterest regret. How could she have abused such trust? How could she have played so wantonly with the tenderest feelings of so amiable a creature? The words she had planned, the careful circumlocutions, the gentle hints, all evaporated in the face of Harriet’s unblemished faith. She knew she must be direct, yet gentle; honest, yet profoundly kind.

“Harriet,” she began, her voice a little unsteady despite her efforts, “we have been very good friends for some time now, have we not?” Harriet looked up, surprised by the solemnity of Emma’s tone, and answered with immediate warmth, “Oh, yes, dear Miss Woodhouse, the best of friends. You have been so good to me, so very kind, and taught me so much.”

“And in our friendship,” Emma continued, forcing herself to meet Harriet’s gaze, “there have been many conversations, many confidences exchanged. Sometimes, Harriet, perhaps, I have been too free in my suggestions, too quick to draw conclusions, and perhaps, too confident in my own understanding of circumstances which were, in truth, less clear than I imagined them.”

Harriet listened with an expression of increasing perplexity, her brow faintly furrowed. “I do not quite understand, Miss Woodhouse,” she said softly.

“I speak, my dear Harriet,” Emma rejoined, taking a deep breath, “of Mr. Knightley. You know, we have often discussed him, and his character, and—and my hopes for your future happiness.” The last words were almost a whisper, laden with a weight of meaning that Harriet could not yet comprehend.

“Oh, yes,” Harriet replied, a faint colour rising in her cheeks, her eyes brightening with that hopeful anticipation Emma now dreaded. “You have been so good, Miss Woodhouse, to think of me in such a way, to believe me worthy of such a gentleman.”

The very simplicity of this response was a fresh torment to Emma. How deeply had she instilled this belief? How painful would be its extirpation? “Harriet,” Emma said, her voice now gaining a firmer, though still gentle, timbre, “it has been my deepest pleasure to be your friend, and to wish you every felicity. But in my desire for your happiness, and in my admiration for Mr. Knightley’s many excellent qualities, I fear I may have

allowed my own sanguine hopes, and perhaps a degree of injudicious enthusiasm, to misinterpret the expressions of an excellent man, and, in so doing, to foster in your mind expectations which, alas, had no just foundation in reality.”

Harriet’s expression, which had been gradually deepening in its perplexity, now shifted to one of innocent dismay. “No foundation, Miss Woodhouse? “Indeed, Harriet,” Emma replied, her voice soft with self-reproach, “I did say those things. And Mr. Knightley is all that is good, and all that is kind, and he does admire you, as all who know you must. His interest in your welfare, his desire for your happiness, is unquestionable; it is a part of his generous and benevolent nature. But it is here, my dear friend, that my error lay. I mistook a general benevolence, a considerate kindness, such as he would shew to any young woman he esteemed, for a partiality of a deeper and more particular nature. I persuaded myself, and I fear I went far to persuade you, that his attentions, however unassuming, were those of a lover, when in truth, they were merely those of a friend, or perhaps, of a kind and protective elder brother.”

Harriet shook her head, a faint, incredulous smile touching her lips, as if Emma were relating some amusing but utterly improbable story. “An elder brother, Miss Woodhouse? “I remember every instance, Harriet,” Emma assured her, her own memory painfully vivid with each recalled detail, each whisper of encouragement she had offered. “And in each of those instances, I interpreted his actions through the distorting lens of my own misguided hopes. Harriet’s smile faded entirely, replaced by a look of growing consternation. Emma felt a blush of shame rising to her cheeks. She had indeed said all those things, and much more. I confounded kindness with courtship, and a general good opinion with a particular affection. I allowed my own fanciful notions to take hold, and then, with a most regrettable rashness, imposed them upon your innocent and trusting mind. The tears, which had been gathering in Harriet’s eyes, now overflowed, tracing paths down her flushed cheeks. “Then he does not care for me at all in that way?” she whispered, the question a desolate plea. “He does not wish to marry me?”

“Precisely so, my dear,” Emma confirmed, relief mingling with her ongoing contrition. “Because he is good, and because he is a gentleman in the truest sense of the word. He would not wish to offend, nor to neglect, nor to cause discomfiture to any person, least of all to one whom he respected and regarded as a young lady of estimable character, and a friend to those whom he himself held in high esteem. Harriet’s lip trembled. “And I,” she said, her voice small and broken, “I was so happy. I thought I would be Mrs. Knightley, and live at Donwell. Emma allowed her to weep, offering no immediate words, but stroking her hair gently, a gesture of profound sympathy. This was the painful, necessary consequence of her folly. She had to let Harriet feel the disappointment fully, to mourn the loss of a future that had never existed outside of Emma’s own faulty construction.

After a considerable time, when Harriet’s sobs had subsided into choked sniffles, Emma spoke again, her voice low and soothing. “My dear Harriet, I know that at this moment, nothing I can say will wholly alleviate your distress. The shock of disappointment is a bitter thing, and I, who caused it, feel its bitterness doubly for your sake. But I hope, in time, you will forgive me, and understand that my intentions, though woefully misguided, were never malicious. I truly believed, in my blindness, that I was guiding you towards happiness.”

Harriet lifted her head, her eyes still swimming in tears. “I know you wished me well, Miss Woodhouse. You have always been so kind. But oh, it hurts so very much. I had thought he was so perfect.”

“He is perfect, Harriet, in his own sphere, and his perfections are indeed many,” Emma agreed. “But it was not his destiny to be your partner, nor, indeed, was it ever his inclination. Time, my dear, will prove the truth of my words. And time, I trust, will also heal this wound, and reveal to you other sources of happiness, perhaps even a truer and more fitting attachment, than the one I so erroneously held before your gaze.”

Emma carefully avoided any mention of Mr. Martin, knowing that such a suggestion, coming so immediately upon the heels of this painful disclosure, would be entirely ill-timed and unwelcome. But she hoped that with the cessation of her own unpardonable meddling, Harriet’s thoughts might, in their natural course, return to the simple, straightforward affections of the worthy farmer, which, Emma now saw with perfect clarity, were far more suited to her amiable disposition than the complex demands of a union with a man of Mr. Knightley’s intellectual calibre and commanding presence.

“I do not know if I can ever feel happy again,” Harriet murmured, dabbing at her eyes with a small handkerchief. “It seems as if all the joy has gone out of everything.”

“That is the natural consequence of such a sudden reversal of hopes, my dear,” Emma comforted, “but it is a feeling that will pass. The human heart, Harriet, is wonderfully resilient, and its capacity for hope and joy is not easily extinguished. You are young, and possessed of a sweet and amiable disposition, and many will esteem you. This particular chapter of expectation has closed, but many others lie before you, full of promise and true felicity. And I, who have occasioned this sorrow, will do all in my power to assist you in finding that truer path.”

Harriet looked at her, a glimmer of her usual trusting nature appearing through her tears. “You will still be my friend, Miss Woodhouse?”

“Always, Harriet, always,” Emma said with genuine warmth, squeezing her hands again. “And I hope that one day, you will be able to forgive my unpardonable officiousness, and remember only my sincere, if misguided, wish for your well-being.”

“I do not think I can ever be angry with you, Miss Woodhouse,” Harriet replied, a fresh tear escaping. “You have always meant to be good to me. It is just that… I did so very much want to be Mrs. Knightley.”

The reiteration of this lost dream was a fresh pang for Emma, reminding her of the depth of the illusion she had created. It would be a long process, she knew, to fully dismantle it. She must now ensure that no further false hopes were ever allowed to take root. She owed Harriet that much, and more.

They remained for some time longer in the dressing-room, Emma continuing to offer gentle consolation, and Harriet, slowly, haltingly, beginning to accept the difficult truth. The initial shock began to subside, giving way to a quieter, though no less profound, sadness. Emma observed, with a mixture of relief and renewed self-reproach, that Harriet’s nature, while capable of deep feeling, was not one to dwell excessively on philosophical distress or extended melancholy. Her pain was genuine, but her mind, less accustomed to complex reflection, might in time find it easier to relinquish a long-cherished fantasy than Emma had initially feared.

At last, judging the immediate crisis to have passed, and Harriet’s composure to be sufficiently restored for them to re-enter the general company, Emma suggested they return to the drawing-room. Harriet, though still subdued, assented quietly, drying her eyes and attempting to smooth her hair. As they descended the stairs, Emma felt a sense of immense weight having been lifted from her own conscience, though replaced by a heavy sadness for Harriet’s disappointment. She had performed her duty, however painfully, and now, at least, the truth, however unpalatable, was known. The path forward for Harriet would be difficult, but at least it would be a path free of Emma’s egregious errors of judgment.

Upon rejoining the others in the drawing-room, Emma found the scene much as she had left it. Mr. Woodhouse, still in his chair by the fire, was conversing with Mrs. Weston, whose placid smile and gentle voice suggested a peaceful interval. Mr. Frank Churchill, true to his lively and unceasing flow of spirits, had evidently resumed his playful persecution of Jane Fairfax, who, still at the pianoforte, was enduring his banter with her customary quiet dignity, though a faint flush on her cheeks indicated a degree of vexation.

Miss Bates, ever effusive, seized upon their re-entry with characteristic eagerness. “Ah, here are our dear Miss Woodhouse and dear Harriet, returned to us! We were beginning to wonder if you had quite forgotten us, or if Emma had carried you off for a delightful little chat of her own. So much to talk about, I am sure. So many interesting things happening! Mr. Frank Churchill has been so entertaining, has he not, Jane? He has been teasing our dear Jane about her new music, and about Mr. Knightley too, you know! Oh, Mr. Frank Churchill, you are quite incorrigible!”

Emma cast a quick, involuntary glance at Mr. Frank Churchill. His eyes, meeting hers for a fleeting moment, held a spark of amusement, but also a hint of something deeper, something that suggested a shared understanding of the subtle currents of the room. He gave a slight, almost imperceptible nod, as if acknowledging her recent absence, and perhaps, her intention.

“Indeed, Miss Bates,” Mr. Frank Churchill replied, with an air of mock solemnity, “I fear I am. But how can one resist the temptation to enliven such a delightful party? Especially when Miss Fairfax provides such admirable material for a little raillery. And as for Mr. Knightley, I merely observed, with the utmost respect for his superior judgment, that he seemed particularly interested in Miss Fairfax’s welfare this morning. A very proper concern, one would think, for a gentleman of such established character. Though Miss Bates, with her usual discernment, appeared to find something rather more profound in his enquiries.” He concluded with a disarming smile, which Miss Bates, ever susceptible to flattery, took as a compliment.

Emma saw Jane Fairfax colour slightly, but she offered no comment, merely turning a page of her music. Harriet, meanwhile, had found a seat near Mrs. Weston, and was sitting quietly, her eyes cast down, evidently still feeling the weight of her recent disappointment. Emma felt a fresh wave of pity for her.

Mr. Woodhouse, stirring from his gentle discourse with Mrs. Weston, noticed Harriet’s subdued manner. “My dear Harriet, are you quite well? You look a little pale. Perhaps the air in Miss Woodhouse’s room was a trifle close. Or perhaps the walk this morning was too much for you?”

Mrs. Weston, ever perceptive, gave Emma a brief, questioning glance, which Emma answered with a slight, almost imperceptible shake of her head, indicating that all was not entirely well, but that the matter was private.

“I am quite well, sir, I thank you,” Harriet replied, her voice soft. “Only a little tired, perhaps.”

“Ah, yes, tiredness,” Mr. Woodhouse concurred, with a sympathetic sigh. “It is a very common complaint. I often find myself a little fatigued after the slightest exertion. Mrs. Weston, do you not think that Harriet would benefit from a glass of our excellent sherry? Or perhaps a little arrowroot?”

“Indeed, Mr. Woodhouse, I am sure Harriet will be quite revived by a moment’s rest,” Mrs. Weston replied, ever diplomatic. “And perhaps a little conversation will be of comfort.”

Emma, seating herself next to Mrs. Weston, felt an irresistible urge to share some part of her recent tribulation, though she knew she could not divulge the whole of it in the present company. “I confess, Mrs. Weston,” she whispered, leaning slightly towards her former governess, “I have just emerged from a most difficult, though necessary, conversation. It has been a morning of considerable correction for me, and I fear, of some pain for poor Harriet.”

Mrs. Weston’s kind eyes expressed immediate concern. “Indeed, my dear? I had observed that you were closeted for some time. I trust nothing untoward has occurred? No new distress for Harriet?”

“No new distress, Mrs. Weston, but rather the painful and unavoidable undoing of an old one, of my own making, I must confess, with profound shame. I have been forced to acknowledge, both to myself and to Harriet, the full extent of my foolish misjudgment concerning Mr. Knightley. I fear I led her most sadly astray.”

Mrs. Weston’s face registered a gentle surprise, then a dawning understanding. “Ah, my dear Emma,” she said softly, “I had sometimes feared that your lively imagination, combined with your generous heart, might lead you to form hopes for Harriet that were perhaps… not entirely consonant with Mr. Knightley’s disposition. He is, as you know, a man of very steady affections, and not easily swayed by novel attractions, however amiable.”

“You saw it, then?” Emma asked, a fresh wave of mortification sweeping over her. “You saw my egregious error, even when I, in my blindness, was congratulating myself upon my own sagacity?”

“My dear, I merely had a general apprehension,” Mrs. Weston demurred gently. “It was not for me to interfere, or to offer unsolicited counsel, when your mind seemed so firmly fixed upon your course. But I confess, I had sometimes thought that Mr. Knightley’s manner towards Harriet, though always kind, lacked that particular ardour, that distinctive animation, which indicates a serious attachment. His interest, it seemed to me, was rather more that of an old friend, or a benevolent protector, than of a lover.”

“And you were entirely right, Mrs. Weston, as I now see with dreadful clarity,” Emma sighed, leaning back in her chair, feeling utterly spent. “I have been forced, by circumstances I cannot yet fully explain, to confront the absolute truth of the matter, and to convey it, as gently as I could, to poor Harriet. It has been a most humbling experience, and I am quite resolved to put an end to all future match-making. My talents in that sphere, it is now perfectly evident, are worse than useless; they are positively dangerous.”

Mrs. Weston smiled faintly. “I believe, my dear Emma, that you possess many admirable talents, and a heart full of the kindest intentions. It is only that in matters of courtship, the human heart can be a very complicated subject, and often defies the most careful calculations of reason. But I am very glad, for all parties concerned, that this misunderstanding, however painful, has at last been cleared.”

Emma looked towards Harriet again, who was now engaged in a quiet conversation with Mr. Woodhouse about the relative merits of different kinds of gruel. She still looked pale and subdued, but her tears had ceased, and she seemed to be finding some small comfort in the gentle commonplace of the discourse. Emma felt a pang of guilt, but also a measure of relief. It was done. The truth, however hard, had been spoken, and the path, however uncertain, was now open for Harriet to find her own, more suitable way.

Mr. Frank Churchill, having momentarily ceased his playful banter with Jane Fairfax, now turned his attention towards Emma and Mrs. Weston. “May I enquire, ladies, if all is well? You appear to be engaged in a discourse of considerable gravity. Has Mr. Knightley perhaps sent a message from Kingston, bearing tidings of some remarkable event? Or perhaps, Miss Woodhouse, you are devising some new charitable undertaking for the village?” His tone was light, but his eyes, when they met Emma’s, held a curious mixture of shrewdness and unspoken meaning.

Emma, caught by surprise, felt a blush rise. “No, Mr. Frank Churchill,” she replied, attempting a casual air. “Nothing so grand, I assure you. Merely a little private discussion between friends, as often happens when ladies are gathered. We were perhaps discussing the complexities of human nature, a subject which, I believe, offers endless scope for contemplation.”

Mr. Frank Churchill’s smile widened. “Ah, human nature! A most profound topic, indeed. And one, I daresay, Miss Woodhouse, in which you have always shewn a peculiar interest, and, if I may be so bold, a remarkable capacity for insight. Though I have sometimes thought,” he added, glancing playfully towards Jane Fairfax, “that even the keenest observer may occasionally find themselves confounded by the subtle intricacies of the heart, particularly when those hearts are possessed of unusual reserve, or prone to unexpected attachments.”

Jane Fairfax, who had been listening quietly, now raised her eyes, meeting Mr. Frank Churchill’s gaze with a steady, unblinking look, which seemed to hold a silent, but pointed, rebuke. The implication of his words, though veiled, was clear enough to Emma: he was hinting at her past failures in discerning others’ affections, and perhaps, with a deeper significance, at her blindness to the true state of affairs between himself and Jane.

Emma felt a fresh wave of discomfort. The conversation with Harriet had cleared one path, but it seemed to have opened another, equally intricate, and perhaps even more perplexing. The awareness of Jane Fairfax’s secret engagement to Mr. Frank Churchill, now so undeniable, added another layer of complication to her recent enlightenment. She had been wrong about Harriet and Mr. Knightley, but she had been equally, if not more, wrong about Jane and Mr. Frank Churchill. Her powers of observation, upon which she had prided herself, seemed to have utterly deserted her this past year.

“Indeed, Mr. Frank Churchill,” Emma rejoined, forcing a smile, “you speak with a wisdom that does you credit. The heart, as you observe, is a strange and often contradictory thing, and its movements are not always to be readily discerned by outward appearances. One may be mistaken, profoundly mistaken, even in the clearest cases, and find one’s most cherished theories overthrown by an unexpected turn of events.” She directed her gaze pointedly towards him, hoping he would understand the sincerity of her confession, and perhaps, take it as an olive branch, a signal of her newly acquired humility.

Mr. Frank Churchill met her gaze, and for a moment, the playful, bantering expression left his face, replaced by a look of serious understanding. There was a brief, silent communication between them, an acknowledgement of shared secrets and dawning truths. Then, with a slight, almost imperceptible nod, he turned his attention back to Jane Fairfax, and with a renewed lightness in his tone, began to speak of some new piece of music he had discovered.

Emma watched them, a profound sense of introspection settling upon her. She had been so busy arranging the lives of others, so confident in her own superior judgment, that she had completely overlooked the genuine attachments and concealed realities that lay beneath the surface of Highbury society. She had been the busy, officious fool, creating confusion where none existed, and blind to the real affections that flourished quietly around her.

Her mind returned to Mr. Knightley. The true nature of his affections, so recently and astonishingly revealed to her, filled her with a mixture of profound relief and overwhelming gratitude. She, Emma Woodhouse, who had thought herself so clever, so immune to the common vulnerabilities of the heart, had discovered, through a painful process of self-correction, that her own happiness, her own peace of mind, lay inextricably bound with the good opinion, the steady affection, and the upright character of the very man she had so foolishly tried to keep at a distance, and to whom she had so wrongly assigned another’s hand.

The conversation in the room flowed around her, Miss Bates detailing another anecdote about the weather, Mr. Woodhouse enquiring if any draught might be felt from the window, and Mr. Frank Churchill playfully urging Jane Fairfax to play a more lively tune. Emma, though participating in the general politeness of the moment, found her thoughts continually returning to the momentous confessions of the morning. She had confessed her error to Harriet; she had, by implication, acknowledged her new understanding to Mr. Frank Churchill and, by extension, to Jane Fairfax. But the most significant truth, the truth of her own heart, and Mr. Knightley’s, remained unspoken in the public sphere.

She knew now, with an undeniable certainty, that she loved Mr. Knightley. She had always valued him, esteemed him, relied upon him, but the full, tender, overwhelming force of love had only just been revealed to her, like a sudden, glorious sunrise after a long night of confusion. And the thought that he loved her in return, that his affections, steady and true, had been fixed upon her all this time, was a source of such deep, quiet joy as she had never before experienced.

But there were still many obstacles, many delicate considerations. Harriet’s disappointment was but the first. Her father, Mr. Woodhouse, with his settled habits and deep-seated fears of change, presented another. How was she to approach such a subject, such a profound alteration in their household arrangements, with a man so dedicated to the constancy of his home and his daughter’s presence within it? The prospect, though sweetened by the knowledge of Mr. Knightley’s affection, was daunting.

She glanced at the clock on the mantelpiece. The morning was rapidly advancing towards noon. Mrs. Weston would soon be departing with Mr. Frank Churchill. And then, there would be only Mr. Woodhouse, Harriet, and herself. The immediate, pressing duty of caring for Harriet’s wounded spirits remained. But beyond that, lay the future, a future now irrevocably altered by the momentous discoveries of this eventful day. Emma, despite the weariness of spirit occasioned by her self-reproach, felt a stir of hope, a quiet anticipation. The world, which had seemed so clear and manageable through the lens of her own self-confidence, had revealed itself to be far more intricate, more surprising, and ultimately, more genuinely promising, now that she had learned to see it with humility, and with a heart finally open to its true and tender affections. She had made a terrible mistake, but in correcting it, she had, perhaps, finally found her own true path to happiness. Her long, solitary journey of self-discovery, prompted by her own follies, seemed at last to be drawing to a close, and a new, more discerning chapter of her life, one guided by genuine affection and clear-sighted judgment, was about to begin. She resolved, with a new and earnest conviction, never again to meddle in the affections of others, but to concentrate her efforts on cultivating her own heart, and ensuring the happiness of those truly entrusted to her care.
