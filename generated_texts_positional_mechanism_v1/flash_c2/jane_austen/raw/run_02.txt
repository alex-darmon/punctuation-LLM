If there is a secret, it is ill-managed; and if there is none, then his manner towards Miss Fairfax, and indeed towards myself, is certainly most ambiguous, and scarcely to be commended.”

They had now reached the gates of Hartfield, and Frank Churchill, turning back to them with an engaging smile, prevented any further serious discussion. “Here we are then, at the very seat of elegance and good taste! I feel as if I have been on a grand tour, such has been the variety of my morning’s entertainment. To begin with rustic simplicity, and end with the refinements of Hartfield, is a journey indeed. Miss Woodhouse, I trust you have recovered from the ardour of our previous night’s exertions, and are ready for another evening of similar delights?”

Emma smiled, regaining her composure under the direct compliment. “I am always ready for suitable company, Mr. Churchill. But as for ‘ardour,’ I believe that was more distinctly felt by yourself than by your partners. You were indefatigable.”

“And you were, as ever, inimitable,” he replied, with a bow that held precisely the right measure of sincerity and playful flattery. “But I confess, even for an inimitable partner, a few hours’ rest are welcome. I look forward to the next occasion. And now, Mrs. Weston, we must take our leave. My father will be expecting us.”

He took Mrs. Weston’s hand, then Harriet’s, offering a word of warm gratitude to each, before turning to Emma once more. “Miss Woodhouse, a thousand thanks for this delightful morning. Your good sense and lively spirit are a perpetual charm, and your kindness to Miss Fairfax is truly admirable. I shall hope to see you very soon, perhaps even later today, should the Fates be propitious.” With another elegant bow, he and Mrs. Weston proceeded towards their carriage, which was waiting a little further up the lane.

Emma watched them go, a complex mixture of feelings in her breast. His parting words, particularly the reference to her “kindness to Miss Fairfax,” had seemed to carry a double meaning, almost a warning, or perhaps even a subtle taunt. It had both gratified and annoyed her. Why praise her kindness to Jane, if not to suggest that Jane was indeed the object of his own tender feelings? And yet, his entire demeanour had been so engaging, so directed towards herself, that she could not entirely shake off the pleasurable sensation of being his chosen favourite.

Harriet, meanwhile, was already full of chatter about the day. “Oh, Miss Woodhouse, was not Mr. Frank Churchill in such spirits! He is so very amusing. And he did seem to admire your dancing so much, did he not? I heard him tell Mrs. Weston that he thought you quite the best dancer in the county.”

Emma turned to her, a little impatiently. “He is a man of general compliments, Harriet. You must not attach too much importance to every word he utters. As for the dancing, we both performed as well as most, and no more.” She then guided Harriet into the house, her own mind still wrestling with the morning’s observations. The evidence against her theory was, undeniably, accumulating; but her own deep-seated reluctance to admit herself mistaken, combined with Frank Churchill’s continued engaging manner towards her, served to sustain her initial conviction. She decided, with a renewed determination, that she would continue to observe, but that she would not be easily swayed by mere appearances, nor by the perhaps overly eager interpretations of others, however well-meaning.

The day passed, as such days often do, in a somewhat languid sequence of domestic duties and desultory thoughts. Emma found herself repeatedly replaying the scenes at Miss Bates’s, examining each speech and glance for a definitive meaning, and invariably returning to her original position of scepticism. It was all too uncertain, too contradictory, for her to settle on any firm conclusion. She had, she thought, good reason to be cautious. The idea of a secret engagement was so repugnant to her sense of propriety, so fraught with difficulty and potential embarrassment for all involved, that she felt a natural disinclination to credit it. And as for Mr. Frank Churchill, she could not, she truly could not, conceive of him as a young man capable of such deliberate deception or such complicated motives as would be required for such a clandestine affair. He was open, lively, and sincere; and if his sincerity led him to admire Jane Fairfax, then why all the mystery? No, it made no sense. Her steady scepticism, though momentarily ruffled, remained steadfast at the end of the day.

The following morning brought no new light, but a fresh perspective, as Emma received a letter from her father’s sister, Mrs. John Knightley, who resided in London. It was a chatty epistle, full of news of the children and the usual complaints about the difficulties of finding reliable servants, but towards its end, a paragraph caught Emma’s eye with a particular fascination.

“And we had quite a little surprise last week, my dear Emma,” wrote Mrs. John Knightley. “Our friends the Dixons, with whom you know we are so intimate, dined with us, and who do you think was also there? None other than Miss Fairfax’s cousin, Mrs. Churchill’s nephew, Mr. Frank Churchill himself! He was in town for a few days, ostensibly on business, but in truth, I believe, merely to amuse himself before returning to Enscombe. He is a very agreeable young man, though perhaps a little too fond of London diversions for my taste. But what struck me particularly was his manner towards Mrs. Dixon. So very attentive, so very obliging! One would have thought, from his behaviour, that she was the most charming woman in the world, and he quite devoted to her every word. He inquired with such solicitude after her health, complimented her gown, and spoke of her accomplishments with such warmth, that it was quite remarked upon by everyone present. Indeed, my dear, if she were not so happily married to Mr. Dixon, and had he not been so very engaged with us for the dinner, I do believe some would have supposed him quite smitten. It quite put me in mind of the old days, when young men would court with such open ardour. But perhaps it is merely his natural vivacity, which, I must say, is quite captivating. He asked very particularly after Miss Fairfax, and seemed quite eager to hear of her progress on the pianoforte, which he himself, it seems, selected for her. He spoke of her as a young lady of the highest merit, and seemed to think her situation at Highbury rather solitary. I thought it all rather remarkable, considering the family connection, but then, young men of fashion have their own ways, do they not? I felt I must tell you, knowing your interest in Highbury goings-on, and especially as you are now so acquainted with him yourself.”

Emma read and re-read the paragraph, a growing conviction settling upon her mind with the force of irrefutable logic. So, Mr. Frank Churchill’s attentions to Mrs. Dixon, which had once been the subject of conjecture and rumour at Highbury, had been of a similar nature to those he now paid to Jane Fairfax! “So very attentive, so very obliging!” “One would have thought… he quite devoted to her every word.” “He inquired with such solicitude… complimented her… spoke of her accomplishments with such warmth.” It was precisely the pattern of behaviour she had witnessed, and been so puzzled by, at Miss Bates’s. His praise of Colonel Campbell’s “true affection,” his pointed remarks about the music, his observation of Jane’s playing “Robin Adair,” all fell into place. This was not the behaviour of a secretly engaged lover, whose every action, however disguised, would still hold a deeper, more singular meaning. No, this was the behaviour of a man of general gallantry, of a universal charmer, who paid the same exaggerated attentions to every woman who momentarily caught his fancy.

A great weight lifted from Emma’s spirits. Her “steady scepticism” was not only justified, it was triumphantly confirmed. She had been right all along. Frank Churchill was merely polite, perhaps a little flirtatious by nature, but certainly not secretly engaged to Jane Fairfax. His particularity towards Jane was no more than his particularity towards Mrs. Dixon, a natural outflow of his lively spirits and agreeable manners. And as for Jane’s “smile of secret delight,” Emma now understood it to be merely the gratification of any young woman, receiving such charming, if ultimately meaningless, attentions from so eligible a young man. It was, after all, a compliment to be singled out, even if only for an hour. The idea of “reprehensible feelings” melted away; it was all merely feminine vanity, perfectly understandable.

Relief washed over her, mingled with a quiet sense of intellectual victory. She had not been misled. Her judgment, so often confident, had been, in this instance, perfectly correct. Mr. Knightley, with his characteristic bluntness, and Mrs. Weston, with her maternal partiality, had both been mistaken, misled by Frank Churchill’s very captivating demeanour. Emma felt a surge of fresh goodwill towards Mr. Frank Churchill. He was not, after all, a complex, deceptive character, but merely a very charming, very amiable young man, perhaps a little too fond of social admiration, but entirely without ulterior motives in his public conduct. And if his amiable nature led him to be particularly engaging with her, Emma Woodhouse, then that was merely a natural and delightful consequence of their mutual understanding and agreeable intercourse. Her mind was quite settled. All was clear. She felt almost impatient for his next visit, eager to observe his easy, charming ways with this new, clarified understanding. She might even, she thought, gently lead Mrs. Weston to see the error of her ways, and perhaps, with a subtle word or two, open Mr. Knightley’s eyes to the simple truth of Frank Churchill’s universally agreeable disposition. The day, which had begun with a certain lingering disquiet, now promised to be one of clarity and contentment. She refolded her aunt’s letter, pleased by the unexpected intelligence, and sat down to her pianoforte, with a lighter heart than she had felt for many hours. She would play something cheerful, something quite unlike “Robin Adair,” for the air was too much associated with the absurd conjectures she had so happily dismissed.

Emma’s spirits, thus lightened, continued in a state of agreeable composure throughout the remainder of the day. She performed her domestic duties, received a call from Mrs. Goddard, and even engaged in a somewhat lengthy discussion with her father upon the relative merits of boiled fowl and roasted chicken for supper, with an equanimity and cheerfulness which had been quite wanting during the morning’s disquiet. The recollection of Mr. Frank Churchill’s words, and the sudden illumination they had brought, returned to her mind with renewed force; and every time the image of Jane Fairfax’s blush and fleeting smile recurred, it was instantly disarmed of its mystery, transformed from a sign of culpable understanding into the mere natural efflorescence of gratified vanity, a feeling so universally shared amongst her sex that Emma could not but pardon it, and indeed, almost sympathise. The truth, when once perceived, appeared so strikingly obvious that she could only marvel at her own former obtuseness, and the unaccountable blindness of others.

It was not merely that the burden of a secret engagement, an entanglement of such grave import, had been lifted from her imagination; it was the triumph of her judgment, the vindication of her discerning eye, which lent such peculiar zest to her new tranquillity. Others, even those of unquestionable good sense, had been led astray. Mrs. Weston, with her habitual kindness and partiality, had been deceived by the superficial ardour of a young man, interpreting his easy compliment into a profound affection. Mr. Knightley, though seldom wrong in matters of genuine import, had allowed his constitutional bluntness, and perhaps a certain lack of imaginative sympathy, to mislead him into supposing an attachment where none truly existed beyond the bounds of common civility. But Emma, keen-sighted and sagacious, had seen through the superficial gloss, had penetrated the seeming mystery, and had emerged victorious. It was a gratifying reflection, and one which she hugged to herself with increasing satisfaction.

The following morning brought with it a further confirmation, though one not immediately connected with the subject of her recent meditations. A letter arrived from London, addressed to Emma, but containing a postscript from Isabella, her dear sister, which incidentally mentioned an evening spent in the company of the Churchills. “Mr. Frank Churchill was particularly agreeable,” Isabella wrote, in her hurried, somewhat indistinct hand, “and paid us all the most charming compliments. He even ventured to say that my youngest child, little Henry, bore a striking resemblance to me in my youth, a most kind observation, though I am sure he was merely being polite, for I cannot imagine it to be true. He danced several dances with me, though I protested my age, and paid such attention to Papa, complimenting his excellent health and spirits, that Papa was quite delighted. He is indeed a most amiable young man, and I can well understand why Mrs. Weston speaks of him with such warmth.”

Emma smiled as she read this passage. Here was the exact illustration of her newfound understanding. If Mr. Frank Churchill could pay such compliments to a married woman, and dance with her despite her protestations of age, and flatter her father with such amiable assiduity, then his attentions to Jane Fairfax, however pointed they might have seemed, were but a part of his universal system of pleasing. The compliment to little Henry, too, was a stroke of genius, calculated to win the mother’s heart, just as his observations on Jane’s playing “Robin Adair” were designed to engage a young lady’s fancy. He was a master of the art of pleasing, a connoisseur of agreeable discourse, and his skill was applied with charming indiscrimination to all whom he encountered. It was not love; it was merely a highly developed social grace. The more Emma contemplated this, the more lucid and coherent the whole picture became. Her mind, once so clouded by suspicion and perplexity, was now a model of clarity. She felt a lively eagerness to communicate her discovery, to disabuse her friends of their mistaken notions, and to shine forth as the sole possessor of the true interpretation.

Her first opportunity presented itself that very afternoon, when Mrs. Weston called at Hartfield. Mrs. Weston, whose kind heart was ever solicitous for the happiness of others, still wore an air of quiet concern when the name of Mr. Frank Churchill was introduced. “I own,” she began, after Emma had dexterously steered the conversation in that direction, “that I am sometimes quite perplexed by him. He is so delightful, so engaging, so full of spirits, and yet there are moments, Emma, when I cannot but think he acts with a certain imprudence. His attentions to Miss Fairfax, though perhaps not intended to mean so much

, yet do unfortunately give rise to observations, and to hopes, which, I own, make me a little uneasy. She is a girl of such quiet reserve, and has so little to look forward to of advantage in her future, that one cannot but be anxious for her peace of mind. Mr. Churchill’s gaiety, his undeniable charm, and the very openness of his manner, though so pleasing in themselves, are, I fear, too likely to mislead a young woman whose heart, I cannot but suspect, is not entirely disengaged.” Mrs. Weston concluded with a sigh, her benevolent brow clouded by a very tender concern.

Emma, however, felt no answering cloud darken her own countenance. On the contrary, she wore an air of quiet triumph, a modest yet assured satisfaction in her newly acquired insight. “My dear Mrs. Weston,” she began, leaning slightly forward, her voice imbued with the pleasure of elucidation, “I believe I can set your anxieties quite at rest, or at least alleviate them considerably. I have, within the last twenty-four hours, received a communication, a mere passing observation in a letter, which has thrown the most complete and satisfactory light upon this very subject, and which, to my own entire conviction, explains Mr. Churchill’s conduct in a manner most consistent with his amiable character, and perfectly exonerates him from any imputation of serious attachment to Miss Fairfax, or of deliberately misleading her.”

Mrs. Weston regarded her with an expectant, if somewhat perplexed, expression. “Indeed, Emma? A communication? From whom, pray? And what can it contain that should

possess that power?” Mrs. Weston’s gaze, though affectionate, held a touch of incredulity, a gentle challenge to Emma’s assertion of such profound illumination. Emma, however, was in no wise daunted; her conviction grew only stronger under the mild pressure of interrogation.

“It was from your own husband, my dear Mrs. Weston,” Emma replied, lowering her voice slightly, as if imparting a confidence of the highest order, “in a letter to me concerning some trifling household matter; but within it was a passage, so very slight, so apparently inconsequential in its immediate context, yet to my mind, so profoundly illuminating, that it has altered my entire perception of Mr. Churchill’s recent behaviour, and, I trust, will do the same for yours. Indeed, I may venture to say it unravels the whole mystery.”

Mrs. Weston leant forward, her maternal solicitude for Frank now mingled with a lively curiosity. “From Mr. Weston? And to you? Pray, Emma, what could he have written that bears so heavily on this subject, and yet appeared so trifling?”

“He mentioned, quite casually, that Frank, in a recent conversation with him, had expressed a very decided opinion on the subject of – well, of discernment, I believe it was, or perhaps it was quickness of apprehension, or intellectual compatibility; no, it was discerning character, and he added, almost as an afterthought, that Frank had said he found in *my* observations a degree of penetration and judgment that he rarely encountered. Mr. Weston set it down as a mere remark, a compliment, perhaps, paid in passing, but oh, Mrs. Weston, how much more it means when one considers the young man who uttered it, and the peculiar circumstances of his acquaintance here.”

Emma paused, allowing her words to sink in, her eyes fixed upon Mrs. Weston’s face, eager to discern the dawning of similar enlightenment. Mrs. Weston, however, still appeared thoughtful, her brow a little furrowed, as if weighing the various implications.

“A compliment of penetration and judgment,” she repeated slowly. “It is certainly a very high compliment, Emma, and one, I assure you, you richly deserve. Frank has always possessed a discerning eye for merit, and I am not surprised that he should recognise yours. But to deduce from this single observation, however flattering, such a complete exoneration of his conduct towards Miss Fairfax, and a re-direction of his more serious inclinations – it seems a large step, my dear, from so slight a foundation.”

“Ah, but you misunderstand me,” Emma cried, with a gesture of confident correction. “It is not the foundation itself, but the entire edifice that has been built upon it, which now presents itself in an entirely new light. Consider, my dear Mrs. Weston, the nature of Mr. Churchill. He is a young man of spirit, of vivacity, of a naturally agreeable disposition. He wishes to please, and to be pleased. But where does he look for true companionship, for that intellectual sympathy which alone can satisfy a mind like his? Is it in the quiet, reserved Miss Fairfax, whose accomplishments, though elegant, are more of the heart and the hand than of the quick wit and discerning intellect? No, Mrs. Weston, I have long felt that his apparent attentions to Miss Fairfax were but a veil, a natural consequence of his general desire to be universally agreeable, or perhaps, and this is the thought which has so recently illuminated my understanding, a deliberate distraction. A young man of his connections, constrained by circumstances, unable to declare openly where his heart truly tends, may well seek to divert attention, to mislead the less discerning, while his true object of admiration remains, as it were, concealed in plain sight.”

Emma leant back, having delivered this triumphant exposition, a glow of intellectual satisfaction upon her features. She felt that the case was unanswerably made, the tangled threads unwound, the true pattern at last revealed.

Mrs. Weston, though she listened with attention and an evident desire to be convinced, did not immediately surrender. “It is a very ingenious theory, Emma, and I acknowledge its cleverness. I understand your reasoning, that a young man, forced to conceal his genuine sentiments, might adopt such a strategy. But Frank has always been so open, so unreserved, so unlike a man of stratagem. And his admiration for Miss Fairfax’s musical talents, for her gentle manners, her quiet strength – I have always believed that to be sincere. He enjoys music, and Miss Fairfax plays with an uncommon sensibility. His praise for her playing, his request for certain tunes, his interest in her instrument – these seem to me quite natural and unfeigned expressions of appreciation for an elegant young woman’s accomplishments. And you yourself, Emma, have observed his delight in society, his universal desire to please. Why should his attentions to Miss Fairfax be singled out as a deliberate blind, when they might as easily be attributed to his general good nature, and his genuine pleasure in her society, albeit a pleasure not necessarily of a deeper, matrimonial kind?”

“Precisely!” exclaimed Emma, seizing upon Mrs. Weston’s last phrase with an almost theatrical flourish. “Not of a deeper, matrimonial kind! That is the very point. His attentions to her are of a lighter, less significant nature. He dances with her because she dances well; he converses with her because she is agreeable; he praises her music because he enjoys it. All this is true, and all this is perfectly consistent with his character. But a man’s true admiration, his deepest esteem, is not always found in these superficial courtesies. It is found in a recognition of mental superiority, of intellectual accord. And if he finds that in *my* observations, as Mr. Weston so casually reported, then surely, my dear Mrs. Weston, it is to me that his more serious thoughts incline. The attentions to Miss Fairfax become, in this light, not merely general politeness, but a convenient diversion, a well-placed smoke screen, behind which he may observe, admire, and perhaps even woo, without exciting undue suspicion in quarters where suspicion might be inconvenient. Indeed, it explains perfectly that playful, teasing manner he adopts with her, which you yourself have found a little imprudent. It is the manner of one who knows he means nothing serious, and therefore can afford to be carelessly charming, knowing that any deeper interpretation on her part would be a misreading of his true intentions.”

Mrs. Weston sighed, a little wistfully. “I confess, Emma, that I wish it were so. For your happiness, and for Frank’s, I should be delighted to believe it. And indeed, there is a certain plausibility in your argument, particularly when one considers the constraints Mr. Churchill is under. He is not entirely his own master, and prudence might well dictate a degree of dissimulation. But poor Miss Fairfax – if she were to mistake these playful attentions for something more profound, something which they are not intended to be, her situation would be truly pitiable. Her quietness, her very reserve, makes her affections appear the more deeply seated. I have sometimes feared, Emma, that her heart was already engaged before Mr. Churchill’s arrival, but if not, then she is precisely the sort of sensitive, retiring young woman most susceptible to such a charming, spirited attack upon her affections.”

“And it is precisely for this reason,” Emma rejoined, with an air of one who held the key to all dilemmas, “that Mr. Churchill would choose her as his ostensible object. Who would suspect a true attachment there? Her quietness, her lack of fortune, her very situation as an orphan dependent upon the kindness of others – all these circumstances conspire to make any serious attachment between her and a young man of Mr. Churchill’s prospects seem highly improbable. It is the perfect cover, Mrs. Weston! No one would seriously imagine him to be truly enamoured of Miss Fairfax. And thus, while all eyes are upon them, he is free to observe, to converse, to enjoy the society of one who *does* engage his intellect, without exciting comment. It is a scheme, I grant you, of some subtlety, but Mr. Churchill is a young man of considerable parts, and not without a touch of finesse. I have observed him closely, Mrs. Weston, and I confess that his attentions to myself, when I now reflect upon them, have been of a very different character from those he bestows upon Miss Fairfax. There is a certain earnestness, a desire for approval, a seeking of my opinion, which speaks of a deeper respect, a more genuine admiration than any of his gallantries towards Miss Fairfax.”

Emma was now fully convinced, her reasoning having looped back upon itself to reinforce her initial premise. The more she spoke, the more solid her conviction became. Mrs. Weston, observing Emma’s radiant confidence, and wishing her well, found her own doubts gradually softening. She loved Frank as a son, and she loved Emma as a daughter; the idea of their mutual attachment, if indeed it existed, was infinitely pleasing.

“You make a very persuasive case, Emma,” Mrs. Weston said at last, a faint smile playing on her lips. “And I must own, your interpretation certainly paints Frank’s character in a more prudent, if somewhat more artful, light than I had previously seen. If he truly holds you in such high esteem, and if his attentions to Miss Fairfax are indeed a mere feint, then I can only hope that his genuine regard for you will soon be able to declare itself without impediment. It would be a match, my dear Emma, that would give your father and me the sincerest satisfaction. And as for Miss Fairfax, if her heart is indeed as free as we must hope, then a temporary misapprehension, however disquieting, would cause no lasting injury. Perhaps, after all, she is not so easily misled. She has a strong understanding, and a very proper sense of her own situation.”

“Precisely!” Emma repeated, delighted to have secured at least a partial convert. “She is not a foolish girl, Mrs. Weston. And if she has been for a moment flattered, or led to imagine more than exists, her good sense will soon correct the error. Indeed, I fancy I caught a glimpse of that very good sense at work the other day, when Mr. Churchill was being particularly pointed about the piano and the music. She coloured, yes, but there was a flicker of something else, a secret smile, as if she were not entirely displeased by his attentions, but also not entirely taken in by them. As if she understood the game, or at least suspected its nature, and found a certain amusement in it. It was a fleeting impression, but it struck me then, and now, in light of this new understanding, it seems to confirm my idea.”

Emma was here referring to an observation she had made earlier that very morning, which, at the time, she had interpreted as evidence of Jane’s reprehensible feelings, but which now, with the convenient adaptability of her imagination, served to support her new theory.

“Well,” said Mrs. Weston, rising to take her leave, a hopeful rather than entirely convinced expression on her kind face, “I shall certainly view Mr. Churchill’s actions with new eyes, Emma, and I shall endeavour to discover whether your very ingenious solution holds true. I sincerely hope it does, for all our sakes. I confess, it would relieve my mind greatly to think that his gaiety and attentions were guided by such a clever, if slightly circuitous, purpose.”

With this qualified assent, Mrs. Weston departed, leaving Emma in a state of profound satisfaction. She had not only unravelled a complex mystery, but she had also convinced Mrs. Weston, at least in part, of her superior discernment. The world, which had seemed so perplexing only a day ago, was now laid out with admirable clarity. Frank Churchill was not a mere thoughtless flirt; he was a discreet admirer, constrained by circumstances, using admirable subtlety to pursue the object of his true affections. And that object, beyond any reasonable doubt, was Emma Woodhouse.

The next few days passed in a delightful state of anticipation for Emma. She scrutinised every action of Mr. Frank Churchill with renewed interest, each gesture, each word, each glance now filtered through the lens of her brilliant hypothesis. She found confirmation everywhere. His lively animation in her presence, his ready wit in conversation with her, his eagerness to consult her opinion on trifling matters, his very absence, when he was called away to Enscombe, all served as incontrovertible proof of his serious regard. His being obliged to return to his aunt, at such a juncture, was a clear demonstration of the obstacles that prevented him from openly declaring his attachment. She pitied him, of course, for the trials he endured, but her pity was sweetened by the knowledge that she was the inspiring cause of his dissimulation.

Her self-satisfaction was complete. She felt herself to be in a position of intellectual superiority, privy to secrets that eluded the less discerning. She was pleased with Frank, pleased with herself, and even a little pleased with the unfortunate Miss Fairfax, whose role as the unwitting decoy was now seen as a necessary, if somewhat inconvenient, part of a larger, more interesting design.

The days passed, and still Mr. Frank Churchill remained at Enscombe, detained by the capricious humours of his aunt. Emma found this absence, though regrettable for the immediate pleasures of society, rather flattering in its implications. It signified, to her mind, the strength of the chains that bound him, and consequently, the depth of the affection that would one day break them. She wrote to Harriet Smith, describing her newfound clarity and subtly hinting at the true state of affairs, though without revealing the full extent of her conviction, for a certain delicacy was still required. Harriet, of course, replied with effusions of delight, interpreting Emma’s hints with even greater enthusiasm than Emma herself intended, and confirming Emma’s belief that her friend would entirely approve of such a match.

One afternoon, a week or so after her enlightening conversation with Mrs. Weston, Emma was walking in the shrubbery at Hartfield, contemplating the agreeable prospects before her, when she encountered Mr. Knightley. He had called to inquire after her father, and finding her out, had come to seek her in her usual haunts. His presence, though always welcome, sometimes jarred slightly with her present train of thought, for Mr. Knightley, she knew, held a less romantic, less imaginative view of human nature than she did, and was not one to be easily convinced by subtle interpretations.

“Good afternoon, Emma,” he said, with his usual straightforward manner. “I trust your father is well? I called at the house and was told you were enjoying the fine weather.”

“Perfectly well, thank you, Mr. Knightley,” Emma replied, colouring slightly, for she had been deep in reverie about Mr. Churchill. “And indeed, the weather is delightful. It puts one in mind of agreeable things, and agreeable people.”

Mr. Knightley looked at her with an amused expression. “Indeed? And what agreeable person has so recently occupied your thoughts, that your countenance should carry such an air of placid satisfaction?”

Emma laughed, a little self-consciously. “Oh, merely the general agreeableness of life, Mr. Knightley. And perhaps, a certain satisfaction in having, as I believe, solved a little puzzle.”

“A puzzle?” he inquired, raising an eyebrow. “You are still applying your ingenious faculties to the affairs of your neighbours, I presume? I trust it is not another case of mistaken affections, or misdirected advice.” There was a hint of dry humour in his tone, which Emma both appreciated and sometimes resented.

“Not precisely, Mr. Knightley,” she said, choosing her words carefully, for she was not yet prepared to share her full discovery with him. “It is more a clarification, a new understanding of certain behaviours that had previously appeared rather ambiguous. I find it very satisfying to comprehend the true motives behind actions, and to see consistency where before there was only perplexity.”

“And you have achieved this clarity with respect to whom, may I ask?” he pursued, his gaze steady and knowing. “I confess, Emma, I am sometimes surprised by the ease with which you find conviction, and the readiness with which you rearrange facts to suit your preferred conclusions. It is a talent, I suppose, though not always one that leads to truth.”

Emma felt a slight sting at his implied criticism, but she quickly recovered. “You are always so severe, Mr. Knightley! I assure you, my conclusions are based on very sound evidence, and a careful consideration of character. And in this instance, I believe I have done justice to the character in question, by attributing to him a degree of prudence and discretion that had previously escaped me.”

“Prudence and discretion,” Mr. Knightley repeated, a thoughtful expression on his face. “You speak, I presume, of young Mr. Churchill. I have not seen him since his departure for Enscombe. Has his prudence or discretion been more clearly evinced in his absence, than in his presence?”

Emma hesitated for a moment. “His absence, Mr. Knightley, is itself a testament to the constraints under which he labours. And his behaviour before he left, when viewed with a proper understanding of those constraints, appears, to my mind, far more consistent and indeed, commendable, than it did before.”

Mr. Knightley walked on in silence for a few paces, his hands clasped behind his back, his brow furrowed in thought. Emma watched him, a little apprehensively, wondering if he would challenge her, or if he might, perchance, arrive at a similar conclusion through his own more rigorous process of deduction.

“I confess, Emma,” he said at length, turning to her with a serious expression, “I have never been able to view Mr. Frank Churchill’s conduct with quite the same degree of admiration that some of his friends seem to bestow upon it. He is a young man of pleasing manners, certainly, and great vivacity. But there is a levity about him, a want of serious purpose, that I find rather disconcerting. His attentions to Miss Fairfax, for instance, struck me as being rather more thoughtless than discreet. They were calculated to give rise to hopes, and to observations, which, in her situation, could only be productive of uneasiness, if not actual pain. To call that discretion, or prudence, seems to me a misapplication of terms.”

Emma felt a rush of indignation. “You misjudge him, Mr. Knightley! You do not understand the complexities of his situation. He is not free to act as he might wish, and therefore, he must act as he can. Emma, though secretly pleased by his readiness to share such domestic absurdities, and by the particular turn of his eye towards her, which seemed to suggest a shared, privileged understanding, could not quite relinquish her sense of caution. Emma hesitated, considering how much to reveal, and how much to hold back, for she delighted in such verbal fencing with Frank Churchill. “Both, I believe, Mr. Churchill,” she answered, with a graceful inclination of her head. “It is, as an instrument, of excellent tone and quality; but its significance to us, and particularly to Miss Fairfax, lies far more in the thoughtful benevolence of its donor. As she spoke, she observed him closely, searching for any flicker of emotion, any betraying sign that might confirm her suspicions. Frank Churchill’s eyes brightened, and he turned to Mrs. Weston with an eager smile. “My dear Mrs. Weston, you anticipate my very thoughts! I had indeed been contemplating such a visit. Emma felt a blush rise to her cheeks at his direct address, and at the flattering terms he employed. She considered for a moment the implications of such a visit. It would afford her an excellent opportunity to observe Frank Churchill and Jane Fairfax together, to gauge the extent of their interaction, and to confirm or refute her growing conviction regarding their secret engagement. The temptation to play the detective, to unravel the threads of what she perceived as a hidden romance, was too strong to resist.

“I should be very happy to accompany you, Mr. Churchill,” she replied, with a composure that belied her inward flutter. “Indeed, it would be a pleasure to hear Miss Fairfax perform upon an instrument that has occasioned so much agreeable speculation. “Excellent, excellent!” cried Frank Churchill, clapping his hands together with a boyish enthusiasm that was highly engaging. “Then the matter is settled. My dear Mr. Woodhouse, you will excuse our desertion of you for an hour or so? We shall return, refreshed by melody and conversation, and doubtless with a thousand new anecdotes to entertain you.”

Mr. Woodhouse, though loath to see any of his company depart, was too well-bred to object. “Oh, certainly, certainly, my dear. A very kind thought. But do take care, you know. The air can be quite sharp, even in the morning. And Mrs. Bates’s house, I believe, is not quite so sheltered as Hartfield. Do not stay too long, my dear Emma, and wrap up well.”

Mrs. Weston reassured him with a gentle smile, promising to ensure Emma’s comfort, and soon the small party, consisting of Frank Churchill, Emma, and Mrs. Weston, was making its way down the winding lane towards Vicarage Lane. The morning air was indeed crisp, but bright with sunshine, and Frank Churchill’s spirits seemed to rise with every step. He pointed out various landmarks, recounted amusing anecdotes from his childhood in Highbury, and conversed with Emma on a variety of subjects, from the beauty of the passing landscape to the latest London fashions, all with a lively charm that made the walk seem far shorter than it was. Mrs. Weston, walking slightly ahead, occasionally turned to smile at their animated conversation, pleased by the easy companionship between her stepson and her dearest friend.

Mrs. Bates, a placid, elderly woman, rose to greet them with a gentle smile, her spectacles perched precariously on the end of her nose. The small drawing-room, though exceedingly neat, was crowded with furniture and mementos, and the air was thick with the scent of dried lavender and the comforting warmth of a modest fire. Miss Fairfax, seated at the pianoforte, rose gracefully upon their entrance, her composure, as always, remarkable, though a faint blush tinged her pale cheeks as she met Frank Churchill’s eye. Mrs. Bates, overwhelmed by his chivalry, could only murmur her thanks, and Miss Bates, after a moment’s hesitation, eagerly pressed the broken spectacles into his hand. “Oh, Mr. Churchill, you are too good! Frank Churchill, with an air of concentrated expertise, settled himself at a small table by the window, drawing forth a penknife and a piece of fine wire from his pocket. He spoke of the intricacies of such small mechanisms, of the patience required, of the satisfaction of restoring what was broken. He had, as Emma observed, a singular talent for making any occupation, however trivial, seem interesting and agreeable.

As he laboured, he continued his light banter, his eyes occasionally flicking towards Jane Fairfax, who sat quietly, her hands clasped in her lap, her gaze fixed, with an apparent outward composure, on some distant point. Emma allowed herself a small, secret smile. She certainly had enjoyed dancing with Mr. Frank Churchill. He was handsome, lively, and his attention was flattering. She did not wish for any serious attachment to Mr. Frank Churchill, though she enjoyed his company, and the general approbation he commanded.

They walked on for a few minutes in silence, the quietude broken only by the chirping of birds and the rustle of leaves. Emma’s thoughts, however, were far from silent. The image of Jane Fairfax’s flushed cheek and that fleeting, triumphant smile returned to her. It was so unlike the usual composure, the almost chilling perfection, of Miss Fairfax. Emma had always admired, yet secretly resented, Jane’s superior accomplishments and quiet dignity. To discover a hidden imperfection, a concealed indiscretion, in Jane Fairfax, was almost a gratification. “This amiable, upright, perfect Jane Fairfax was apparently cherishing very reprehensible feelings.” The narrator’s words echoed in her mind, though she knew not their origin, nor the exact nature of the reprehensible feelings. Was it merely a secret engagement, or something more scandalous?

“Emma,” said Mrs. Weston at length, her voice softer, a note of genuine concern now present, “what did you truly think of Mr. Knightley’s manner towards Miss Fairfax? Was there not something in it, a solicitude beyond mere friendship, an anxiety that bespoke a deeper interest?”

Emma stopped, turning to face Mrs. Weston, her expression serious. “Mrs. Weston, you know my opinions of Mr. Knightley. He is a man of the highest principle, deeply sensible, and benevolent in the extreme. He is, in short, a man one could always depend upon. But to imagine him in love with Miss Fairfax, or indeed with any woman, is to misunderstand the nature of his character entirely. He is a bachelor by inclination, a man who values his independence and his habits above all else. His concern for Jane Fairfax is, I believe, precisely what it appears to be: the honourable concern of a well-meaning patron and neighbour for a deserving but unprotected young woman.”

“But Emma, a man of such high principle, might he not be precisely the man to be captivated by such merit as Miss Fairfax undoubtedly possesses? Her quiet elegance, her musical talents, her gentle demeanour—surely these qualities must appeal to such a man as Mr. Knightley, who values solid worth above superficial charms?” Mrs. Weston persisted, ever hopeful, ever ready to find a good match for all her friends.

Emma sighed. “My dear Mrs. Weston, you are determined to see what you wish to see. Jane Fairfax is indeed accomplished, and her merits are considerable. But Mr. Knightley is not a man to be swayed by mere accomplishments, however brilliant. He sees deeper. He sees, I venture to say, a certain reserve in Miss Fairfax, a guardedness that would hardly invite the unreserved affection of such a man as Mr. Knightley. He values openness, truth, and a cheerful disposition. Miss Fairfax, for all her virtues, is not cheerful, and she is certainly not open. She is a woman, I suspect, with secrets, and secrets, Mrs. Weston, are rarely conducive to a harmonious domestic partnership, especially with a man of Mr. Knightley’s straightforward nature.”

Harriet, who had listened with an air of bewildered earnestness to this exchange, ventured timidly, “But secrets are sometimes very interesting, Emma, are they not? Like in a book, when you do not know what will happen next. And Miss Fairfax looked very pretty when she blushed.”

Emma smiled at Harriet’s innocent romanticism, but shook her head. “In books, perhaps, Harriet. In real life, secrets are often productive of much unhappiness and confusion. And I, for one, would much rather see all things plain and open. There is too much of mystery surrounding Miss Fairfax, and too much of calculated charm in Mr. Frank Churchill, for my complete satisfaction.”

“Now, Emma, you are being unjust to Mr. Churchill again,” Mrs. Weston chided gently. “He is a young man of spirit, and naturally wishes to please. His vivacity, his lightness of heart, are surely not faults. And his attention to you, my dear, was quite particular. I believe he regards you with a very marked admiration.”

Emma allowed a faint blush to rise to her own cheeks. “His attention to me is mere gallantry, Mrs. Weston, such as he would pay to any young woman he considered agreeable. He is, as you say, lively, and he wishes to amuse himself. And I confess, I am perfectly willing to be amused. But it extends no further than that, I assure you. My regard for him is equally light, and equally free from any serious intent.” This was perhaps a greater untruth than her previous statement, for Emma had, in truth, found herself increasingly interested in Mr. Frank Churchill, and his presence had certainly enlivened the usually placid tenor of her days. Yet, the seeds of suspicion, once sown by Mr. Knightley, and nourished by her own keen observation, continued to sprout.

They had by this time reached the entrance to Hartfield. The familiar gates stood open, inviting them into the well-tended grounds. Mr. Woodhouse, Emma knew, would be fretting over her prolonged absence, and she was eager to reassure him of her safe return.

“Well, we are home,” she said, turning to Mrs. Weston. “Thank you for your company. I trust our walk has not tired you.”

“Not in the least, my dear,” Mrs. Weston replied, adjusting her bonnet. “It has been a very agreeable walk, and a very interesting conversation. Though I fear we remain quite at odds regarding the true state of affairs between our two young friends.” She cast a lingering look back towards the village, as if expecting further revelations to emerge from the cluster of houses. “I confess, Emma, I cannot help but believe that there is more between Mr. Churchill and Miss Fairfax than you allow. His very determined teasing, her blushes, and then his swift attention to you, almost as if to distract from some deeper feeling. It is all very diverting, as Harriet says, but also very perplexing.”

Emma merely smiled. “Time, my dear Mrs. Weston, will undoubtedly reveal all. And until then, I shall continue in my steady scepticism. For my part, I find it all too convenient that Mr. Churchill should appear, so very charming, so very attentive, just as Miss Fairfax acquires a pianoforte, a gift, as it happens, from a relative who has perhaps, after all, only general directions to give, and orders indefinite as to time. It is all too neat, too perfectly arranged, for my plain understanding.”

With these words, they parted, Mrs. Weston continuing her way towards Randalls, and Emma and Harriet entering the gates of Hartfield. Mr. Woodhouse, as expected, was waiting anxiously at the drawing-room window, and hastened to meet them with a thousand gentle remonstrances about the dangers of the morning air.

“My dearest Emma, and Harriet too! You have been out so long, and the air, though I confess it is milder now, was quite brisk earlier. I was so afraid you might catch a chill. And you, Harriet, you must be careful not to exert yourself too much. And have you eaten anything since breakfast? I have had Mrs. Bates send up some fresh gruel, and a little wine whey, just in case.”

Emma affectionately calmed her father’s fears, assuring him that they were both perfectly well, and indeed, much invigorated by their excursion. She recounted the morning’s visit to Miss Bates, describing the arrival of the pianoforte, and the lively company they had found there. She painted a picture as cheerful as she could, omitting, however, the more pointed aspects of Frank Churchill’s conversation, and the underlying tension she had perceived.

“And Mr. Frank Churchill was there, papa, and Mrs. Weston,” she concluded. “He is a very spirited young man, and played some delightful tunes on the new instrument.”

Mr. Woodhouse, ever polite, murmured his satisfaction, though his primary concern remained the health and comfort of those around him. “Ah, yes, a pianoforte. A very noisy instrument, I believe, though I am sure Miss Fairfax plays it with great delicacy. But it must be very trying for the nerves, all that noise. I trust she does not play it too much, for her own sake, and for poor Miss Bates’s. Noise is so very bad for the constitution.”

Emma suppressed a smile. Her father’s gentle anxieties were a constant, and she loved him all the more for them. Later that day, as the afternoon drew on, and the quiet comfort of Hartfield settled around her, Emma found her thoughts returning, with an almost insistent regularity, to the morning’s events. The contrast between Mr. Knightley’s blunt concern and Mr. Frank Churchill’s polished, yet barbed, gallantry was striking. Mr. Knightley, she knew, spoke from a place of genuine, albeit sometimes ungracious, goodwill. Mr. Churchill spoke from, what motive? Amusement? A desire to exert his charm? Or, as Emma increasingly suspected, a carefully orchestrated plan, a hidden agenda that involved Miss Fairfax in a way no one else yet comprehended.

The conversation with Mrs. Weston had done little to dislodge Emma’s conviction. Indeed, Mrs. Weston’s very eagerness to see a match between Emma and Frank Churchill, or between Mr. Knightley and Jane Fairfax, only served to strengthen Emma’s determination to look beneath the surface. Mrs. Weston, for all her kindness, was too ready to take things at face value, too susceptible to agreeable appearances, and too prone to benevolent constructions. Emma, by contrast, delighted in penetrating disguises, in uncovering the hidden springs of human action.

She recalled the entire scene at Miss Bates’s: Frank Churchill’s shaking his head with a smile, looking as if he had “very little doubt and very little mercy”; his precise questions about Colonel Campbell’s commission; the mention of the Irish melodies sent with the instrument; and his emphatic praise of Colonel Campbell’s “thoughtful” attention, implying a depth of feeling “true affection only could have prompted it.” It was all too pointed, too knowing. And then, her own whisper to him, “You speak too plain. She must understand you.” And his confident reply, “I hope she does. I would have her understand me. I am not in the least ashamed of my meaning.”

This last exchange was particularly damning. It confirmed Emma’s suspicion that Frank Churchill was deliberately communicating a message to Jane Fairfax, a message that he *wished* her to understand, and one that he was not “ashamed” of. What could that message be, if not a secret understanding, a clandestine engagement perhaps, or some other arrangement that required such careful, yet public, hints? And Jane Fairfax’s reaction, that momentary “smile of secret delight” amidst the blush of consciousness, had been the key. It was a smile of recognition, of shared knowledge, not of mere embarrassment or mild amusement.

Emma sat by the fire, a book unread in her lap, her gaze fixed on the dancing flames. She felt a thrill of intellectual satisfaction, the pleasure of the detective on the verge of a discovery. She had long been convinced that there was something amiss in Jane Fairfax’s situation, something inconsistent with her outward presentation of dignified submission to a difficult fate. This morning’s events had provided the missing pieces, or at least, the strong indications of where to look for them.

The visit of Frank Churchill to Highbury, the prolonged delay in the arrival of the pianoforte, its mysterious origin, the careful secrecy maintained by Miss Fairfax, her constant efforts to avoid allusions to her past or her future; all these fragments of information now began to coalesce into a coherent, if still shadowy, picture.

“If they are engaged,” Emma mused to herself, “why such secrecy? Why the elaborate charade of the instrument from Colonel Campbell? Why the flirtation with me, and the apparent indifference towards Jane? Unless,” a new thought struck her, “it is a forced engagement, a youthful indiscretion, which Mr. Churchill’s family, particularly his formidable aunt, Mrs. Churchill, would never countenance. Then the secrecy would be paramount, and his flirtation with another, a necessary diversion.” This seemed a very plausible explanation, and one that accounted for all the apparent contradictions. It also added a touch of drama, of hidden suffering and desperate measures, that appealed to Emma’s romantic imagination.

She felt a fresh surge of interest, almost of eagerness, in the prospect of discovering the truth. It would be a triumph of her own discernment, a validation of her often-questioned judgment. She thought of Mr. Knightley, who had so often criticised her tendency to form theories and to meddle in the affairs of others. How gratified she would be to prove him wrong, to show him that her observations, though sometimes playful, were founded on a deeper understanding of human nature than his own staid common sense allowed.

The afternoon passed, and the tea hour arrived, bringing with it the comfortable predictability of Mr. Woodhouse’s routine. Harriet joined them, still bright-eyed from her morning’s outing, and quite ready to discuss the smallest details of Miss Bates’s parlour, or the patterns of the wallpaper. Emma, however, while participating in the conversation, found her mind continually returning to the intricate puzzle of Frank Churchill and Jane Fairfax.

Later in the evening, as she sat sketching in her own room, a plan began to form. She would observe them both more closely, particularly Mr. Churchill. His next visit to Highbury, which was expected soon, would be her opportunity. She would lead him on, subtly encourage his flirtations, not out of any personal interest, but to test his resolve, to see if his attention strayed too far from Jane, or if his occasional pointed remarks continued. She would watch Jane Fairfax’s reactions, looking for any further hints of that “secret delight,” any further evidence of a shared understanding.

Emma knew that this undertaking would require considerable skill and discretion. She would have to appear entirely natural, entirely unsuspicious, lest she betray her own hand, or alarm the parties involved. And she would have to be careful not to wound Harriet, who, Emma knew, was beginning to entertain a rather high opinion of Mr. Frank Churchill herself. Emma did not for a moment believe that Harriet stood any real chance with such a man, but she also did not wish to carelessly inflict pain upon her friend.

No, her object was not mischief, but truth. It was a pursuit of knowledge, a desire to understand the hidden mechanisms of the human heart, and to bring light into the shadowy corners of Highbury’s social landscape. It was, she persuaded herself, a purely intellectual exercise, and one that promised to be most diverting. And if, in the process, she could also demonstrate her superior discernment to Mr. Knightley, that would be an added, though entirely secondary, satisfaction.

Her sketch, a fanciful rendering of a hidden garden with a secret gate, grew beneath her hand, reflecting the workings of her own mind. The gate, she decided, would lead to a hidden path, and at the end of that path, the truth would be waiting. She smiled, a little triumphantly. The game was afoot, and Emma Woodhouse, the cleverest girl in the world, was quite ready to play.

The following day, a small incident occurred which further cemented Emma’s conviction, though it seemed, on the surface, entirely trivial. A parcel arrived at Hartfield for Emma, containing a new set of ribbons she had ordered from London. As she opened it, Mr. Woodhouse, observing her with his usual anxious solicitude, remarked, “My dear, I do hope those ribbons are not too bright. Bright colours are so very trying for the eyes, and I have heard that they can give one a headache. And you know, the poor Miss Bates has been complaining of her eyes since the new pianoforte arrived. All that music, you know, can be very stimulating, and not always in a good way.”

Emma patiently assured her father that the ribbons were of a subdued hue, and would not harm anyone’s eyes. But her mind had already made a leap. “Poor Miss Bates complaining of her eyes since the new pianoforte arrived.” It was a small, almost throwaway remark from her father, yet it struck Emma as significant. Miss Bates, in her boundless garrulity, had made no mention of failing eyesight, or of any discomfort from the pianoforte. In fact, she had only expressed unreserved delight in the instrument. This seemed a small, but telling, inconsistency.

Emma now recalled Mr. Frank Churchill’s deliberate repair of Mrs. Bates’s spectacles that very morning, and his playful comment: “I wish I could conjecture how soon I shall make this rivet quite firm. What nonsense one talks, Miss Woodhouse, when hard at work, if one talks at all;—your real workmen, I suppose, hold their tongues; but we gentlemen labourers if we get hold of a word—Miss Fairfax said something about conjecturing. There, it is done. I have the pleasure, madam, (to Mrs. Bates,) of restoring your spectacles, healed for the present.”

Was it possible, Emma wondered, that his repair of Mrs. Bates’s spectacles, ostensibly a kind act, had also served a deeper purpose? Perhaps to engage Mrs. Bates in conversation, to distract her attention, or to ensure that her vision was indeed adequate for something specific, something connected to Jane Fairfax? It was a wild speculation, perhaps, but Emma’s mind, once set on a course of detection, was prone to such imaginative leaps.

She dismissed the thought of the spectacles for a moment, and returned to her original, stronger intuition. The pianoforte itself was the key. Its arrival, its ostensible giver, its attendant music. Everything pointed to a carefully constructed illusion.

Emma found herself quite regretting Mr. Knightley’s early departure for Kingston. Had he stayed, had he continued his inquiries, she might have found an unexpected ally in her quest for the truth. He, at least, was not blinded by charm or by sentimental fancies. He saw things as they were, or at least, as he believed them to be. His directness, his sometimes uncomfortable honesty, might have served as a valuable counterpoint to her own more speculative approach. But he was gone, and she was left to her own devices, and to the less discerning company of Harriet and her father.

She thought of the apple incident, which had so distressed Miss Bates. Mr. Knightley, sending his entire store of apples to them, and then denying himself. It was so like him, so characteristic of his quiet, unostentatious benevolence. He was a man who did good without seeking praise, who gave generously without thought of return. Such a contrast to the calculated generosity and public display that seemed to characterise Mr. Frank Churchill.

The thought made Emma pause. Was she being unfair to Mr. Churchill? Was her prejudice against him, born of Mr. Knightley’s early reservations, now colouring her judgment too deeply? She prided herself on her impartiality, on her ability to see both sides of a question. Yet, with Frank Churchill, she found herself constantly leaning towards suspicion.

No, she resolved, it was not prejudice. It was observation, plain and simple. The man’s actions, his words, his very presence, suggested a complexity that his superficial charm sought to conceal. And Jane Fairfax’s reactions, those subtle, fleeting glimpses of a hidden life, were too consistent to be accidental.

Emma felt a renewed determination. She would not allow herself to be swayed by external appearances, however agreeable. She would look, she would listen, and she would uncover the truth. Highbury, with its seemingly placid surface, held a secret, and Emma Woodhouse was resolved to reveal it. This quiet, comfortable existence had suddenly acquired an intriguing edge, a thrilling undercurrent of mystery and romance. And she, Emma, was at the very heart of it.
