"My dear Mrs. Gable," said Miss Eleanor Vance, with a faint but polite smile, "it is quite delightful to see you, though I confess I had not expected the honour so early in the day." Mrs. Gable, a lady of some years and considerable volubility, settled herself upon the sofa with a sigh of agreeable exertion. "Nonsense, Eleanor, nonsense. One must take one's opportunities as they arise, especially when the world is quite bursting with novelty. And is it not a novelty, the arrival of the Melbournes, I ask you? Such a family, such an establishment, all quite unprecedented in our little borough, I assure you."

"Indeed," Mr. Edmund Hartley remarked, adjusting his cuff with a languid air, "the town has scarcely known such an upheaval since the Great Frost of '08, when the turnip crop failed entirely. One hopes the Melbournes bring more agreeable consequences." He glanced at Miss Vance, a flicker of amusement in his eye, which she pretended not to notice. "Oh, Mr. Hartley, you are always so droll!" Mrs. Gable exclaimed, quite missing the subtle irony. "But serious matters are now at hand. One hears such a variety of tales. Are they truly of the Lincolnshire Melbournes, that grand branch, or merely some distant connection, hoping to bask in reflected glory?"

"One might suppose," Miss Eleanor ventured, with a measured tone, "that their pedigree, however distinguished or obscure, would matter less than their character. Though, I confess, it is a point of some general interest." She knew that such a sentiment, however rational, would scarcely deter Mrs. Gable from her present line of inquiry.

"Ah, but character, my dear, often reveals itself in such origins. A true Melbourne, you understand, carries a certain air, an undeniable consequence. And yet, one hears whispers. Whispers, I say! Of a certain indiscretion regarding their former residence. Nothing substantial, mind you, nothing one could truly lay hold of, but a certain reticence when the subject of their departure is raised." Mrs. Gable leaned forward, her voice dropping to a theatrical whisper, though it was still perfectly audible throughout the room. "And then there is the daughter, Miss Clarissa. So strikingly beautiful, they say, but with an air of melancholy, a certain pensiveness that does not quite accord with her youthful bloom."

Mr. Hartley, who had been idly turning the pages of a book, now closed it with a snap that made Mrs. Gable start. "Melancholy, Mrs. Gable? Or perhaps merely sensible reserve? The world, after all, is not perpetually a stage for exuberant joy, particularly for those of discerning taste. One might rather commend her for not being perpetually agog at the trivialities of provincial life." He smiled at Eleanor, a smile that suggested he included the present conversation among those trivialities. Eleanor, though she wished him not quite so pointed in his remarks, could not but feel a flicker of agreement.

"Oh, but Mr. Hartley, you quite misunderstand! I would not for the world suggest that our town is trivial. Indeed, it offers a most delightful array of amusements and society, if one knows where to look. And as for Miss Clarissa, I only meant that such beauty, combined with such pensiveness, sets one to wondering. Is it ill-health? Is it a disappointment of the heart? One cannot but speculate."

"Indeed, speculation is a pastime that requires no external stimulus, Mrs. Gable," Mr. Hartley rejoined, rising and strolling to the window. "It flourishes best in the absence of fact, a veritable hothouse flower of the imagination. And for a young lady, such as Miss Clarissa, to inspire such a bounty of conjecture before she has even had a chance to unfold her fan, is truly a testament to her, or perhaps to our collective, mental agility." He paused, turning back into the room. "Perhaps she has merely read too many novels. A common affliction, I am told, among young ladies of a certain sensibility and leisure."

Eleanor, who had been following this exchange with an inner sense of amusement tinged with discomfort, felt compelled to interject. "Mr. Hartley, you are too severe. Mrs. Gable's interest, I am certain, springs from a most benevolent curiosity. We all, I think, are naturally inclined to form some impression of our new neighbours, and without direct acquaintance, what else have we to guide us but report and surmise?" She directed a conciliatory smile towards Mrs. Gable, who appeared somewhat flustered by Mr. Hartley's wit.

"Precisely, Eleanor, precisely! You understand me perfectly. It is merely a natural interest, a desire to welcome them properly, to ensure they feel quite at home. One would not wish to be remiss. And if there are delicate matters, one would wish to be aware, so as to avoid any unfortunate missteps in conversation, would one not?" Mrs. Gable beamed, regaining her composure and her conversational momentum. "For instance, I hear the gentleman, Mr. Melbourne, is excessively fond of his library. A scholar, perhaps? Or merely a man of quiet habits? One does hope he will participate in our local assemblies. The last ball, you know, quite lacked for dancing partners of proper cultivation."

Mr. Hartley coughed, a sound that might have been interpreted as an attempt to suppress a laugh. "Perhaps, Mrs. Gable, Mr. Melbourne's preference for the company of books is a sign of his profound wisdom, or perhaps merely a disinclination for the rigours of the country dance. One cannot say until one sees him attempt a quadrille with sufficient energy. Though, I must admit, a man who prefers the silent counsel of Seneca to the effervescent prattle of the ballroom is, in my estimation, not entirely without merit."

"Oh, you men are all the same, so full of your books and your serious thoughts!" Mrs. Gable waved a dismissive hand, though her smile indicated she took no real offence. "But a little gaiety, a little society, never did anyone harm. And Miss Clarissa, she will need society, surely. Such a delicate looking creature. One only hopes she will find companions, suitable companions; perhaps Miss Amelia Finch, though Amelia is so excessively lively, one fears she might quite overwhelm a pensive spirit; or Miss Caroline Plum, who is perhaps a trifle too reserved herself, to be of much animation."

Eleanor sighed inwardly. The matchmaking attempts were beginning already. "I trust, Mrs. Gable, that Miss Clarissa Melbourne, being a young lady of discernible qualities, will find her own appropriate sphere, and form her own friendships, without too much solicitude on our part. Though I appreciate your kind concern for her well-being."

Mr. Hartley, catching Eleanor's eye, offered a subtle wink. "Indeed, Miss Vance speaks with her usual sagacity. The natural order of things, Mrs. Gable, often surprises us by unfolding quite successfully without our earnest interference. Though, I confess, the spectacle of that interference can often provide a charming diversion for those of us who are mere observers."

"Oh, you will have your joke, Mr. Hartley! But I am merely thinking of what is proper, what is kind. And as for diversions, I trust the Melbournes themselves will prove diversion enough for the entire county for many months to come. One hopes their arrival will not be a disappointment." Mrs. Gable settled back again, seemingly content that she had discharged her duty of information and speculation.

Just as Eleanor was about to offer some polite platitude about the uncertain nature of all new acquaintances, the sound of carriage wheels approaching the drive drew their collective attention. "Oh, dear me," cried Mrs. Gable, rising with surprising alacrity, "can it be? The Melbournes themselves! To call so soon? One must compose oneself; one must appear perfectly at ease, yet suitably welcoming. Oh, Eleanor, do my ribbons look quite right?" She fussed with her cap, while Eleanor, with a half-smile, turned towards the window, ready to greet their much-discussed neighbours. Mr. Hartley, a look of distinct amusement now playing upon his features, merely leant against the mantelpiece, preparing himself for the unfolding drama. It promised to be a most interesting afternoon.

The sound of carriage wheels, distinct and growing louder, sent a small ripple of agitated expectation through the drawing-room. Mrs. Gable, whose concern for appearances had suddenly reached a fever pitch, gave a final, frantic pat to her cap, a gesture which, to Eleanor’s discerning eye, served only to dislodge it further from its intended position. “Oh, dear, they are here,” she whispered, as if the volume of her voice might somehow hasten or delay the moment. “Eleanor, do tell me truthfully, is this particular shade of lavender quite too daring for a first reception? One wishes to appear welcoming, yet not ostentatious, not in the least.” Eleanor, who had already satisfied herself that her own quiet muslin gown was perfectly suitable for any eventuality, offered a reassuring smile. “It is very becoming, Mrs. Gable, and surely no one will be attending to such trifles when they are making their first acquaintance with your generous spirit.” This mild flattery, though perhaps not entirely believed, served its immediate purpose; Mrs. Gable smoothed her skirt with a sigh that indicated a partial restoration of her composure. Mr. Hartley, from his leaning posture against the mantel, merely raised an eyebrow, a silent commentary upon the vagaries of feminine anxieties, which Eleanor found herself almost entirely sharing, though she took pains to conceal it. The arrival of new neighbours, particularly those of the Melbournes’ reputed fortune and mysterious past, was an event of no small consequence in their quiet corner of the country, an opportunity for both social re-ordering and endless speculation.

A bustle in the hall, the quiet murmur of servants, and then the opening of the drawing-room door, announced the arrival itself. A tall, grave-looking gentleman entered first, his countenance perhaps a little too serious for the occasion, yet possessing an undeniable air of command. He bowed with a dignified grace, his dark eyes surveying the room with a calm, almost appraising gaze. This, Eleanor immediately concluded, must be Mr. Melbourne, a man whose presence seemed to command respect without seeking it. Close behind him, and appearing by contrast almost ethereal, was his daughter, Miss Clarissa Melbourne. The description of her as a “delicate looking creature” was not, Eleanor realised, an exaggeration. She was indeed slight, with an almost translucent pallor, and hair the colour of pale moonlight, gathered rather simply at the nape of her neck. Her eyes, of a soft, hazel hue, seemed to hold a certain shyness, or perhaps a thoughtful reserve, as they glanced briefly around the room before settling, almost instinctively, upon her father. She wore a dress of plain grey silk, elegant in its simplicity, but lending her an air of quiet demureness that quite contrasted with the lively colours and confident expressions often seen in younger ladies of fashion.

Mrs. Gable, recovering her voice with a little gasp, advanced upon them with an enthusiasm that threatened to overwhelm. “Mr. Melbourne, Miss Clarissa Melbourne, what an unexpected pleasure, though one we had ardently hoped for. Welcome, welcome to our humble abode, and indeed, to our little corner of Gloucestershire. I am Mrs. Gable, your most devoted well-wisher, and this,” she gestured broadly, first towards Eleanor, then, with a hint of indulgent amusement, towards Mr. Hartley, “are my dear friends, Miss Eleanor Vance, who has been anticipating your arrival with the keenest interest, and Mr. Hartley, a gentleman of much wit and wisdom, who will no doubt be an invaluable guide to all our local peculiarities.”

Mr. Melbourne offered another polite, if somewhat stiff, bow. “Mrs. Gable, we are most obliged by your prompt and cordial welcome. My daughter, Clarissa.” Clarissa, thus introduced, offered a small, graceful curtsy, her gaze still somewhat averted, though a faint, almost imperceptible blush touched her pale cheeks. Eleanor stepped forward with her customary composure. “It is a true pleasure to make your acquaintance, Mr. Melbourne, Miss Melbourne. We are delighted to have you amongst us.” Her voice, calm and clear, seemed to possess a quality of sincerity that perhaps cut through Mrs. Gable’s effusions, for Clarissa’s eyes met hers for a fleeting moment, and in that brief exchange, Eleanor fancied she detected a spark of quiet intelligence, a question, perhaps, or a plea for understanding.

Mr. Hartley, stepping forward from the mantelpiece with an easy, unhurried grace, offered his hand to Mr. Melbourne. “A pleasure, sir. Hartley is my name. I trust your journey was not overly fatiguing, and that the promise of the Gloucestershire landscape has not been entirely diminished by the reality.” Mr. Melbourne’s lips, which had until then maintained a rather rigid line, softened into what might almost be called a smile. “Not at all, Mr. Hartley. The landscape, if anything, exceeds expectation, and our journey, though long, was rendered agreeable by the contemplation of a new beginning. We are most eager to establish ourselves at Melbourne Place.”

The company soon settled, with Mrs. Gable ensuring that Mr. Melbourne occupied the most comfortable armchair, while Clarissa was guided to a settee beside Eleanor. The conversational currents, initiated by Mrs. Gable’s ceaseless flow of questions and observations, began to swirl. “And what are your first impressions of Melbourne Place, Mr. Melbourne? One hears it has been much improved by the late owner, though I always found the east wing a trifle drafty, a persistent problem, you know, even with the new glazing. And the gardens, oh, the gardens are a marvel, though perhaps a little wild for some tastes. Are you a devotee of formal parterres, or do you prefer the romantic wilderness, like so many young ladies nowadays?”

Mr. Melbourne, with a patience that Eleanor admired, responded to these inquiries with a measured politeness. “The house appears well-maintained, Mrs. Gable, and promises to be a most agreeable residence. As for the gardens, we have not yet had the opportunity for a thorough inspection, but I confess I find a certain charm in the less formal aspects of nature. My daughter, I believe, shares this predilection.” He glanced at Clarissa, who offered a small, almost hesitant nod.

“Oh, yes, indeed,” Mrs. Gable seized upon this slender thread of connection, “Miss Clarissa, one can quite imagine you delighting in a ramble through untamed groves, perhaps with a volume of poetry to hand. Are you fond of poetry, my dear? One finds it so often a mark of a truly sensitive soul. And do you draw, or play any instrument? We have a little society for amateur musicians, though perhaps not quite grand enough for one accustomed to the diversions of Dublin.”

Clarissa, thus directly addressed and perhaps a little overwhelmed, responded in a voice that was soft, almost breathy, yet perfectly distinct. “I am fond of poetry, Mrs. Gable, and indeed, of reading generally. And I do play the pianoforte, though I confess I am but an indifferent performer.”

“Indifferent, my dear child,” cried Mrs. Gable, with a wave of her hand that nearly dislodged her cap again, “I am sure that is mere modesty. One has heard such delightful accounts of your accomplishments. And the pianoforte, what a charming instrument. We have no professional musicians in our little society, you understand, but one does so appreciate a melodious evening. Perhaps you would be so kind as to grace us with your talents one day soon?”

Eleanor, sensing Clarissa’s slight discomfort under this barrage of enthusiastic inquiry, intervened gently. “Mrs. Gable, I am sure Miss Melbourne will be delighted to contribute to our local entertainments once she has had time to settle into her new surroundings. The process of moving, particularly from such a distance, must be quite exhaustive.”

“Indeed, Miss Vance,” Mr. Melbourne seconded the sentiment, his voice carrying a note of quiet gratitude. “Clarissa has, I confess, been a little fatigued by the journey and the subsequent exertions of settling in. She has not, as yet, had much opportunity to explore her new home, let alone her new county.”

Mr. Hartley, who had been observing the exchange with his characteristic twinkle of amusement, now spoke. “A most understandable state of affairs, sir. It is always a surprise to find how much energy the mere act of relocating consumes. One expects, perhaps, that the change of scenery alone will prove restorative, but alas, the packing and unpacking, the re-ordering of one’s earthly possessions, these are exertions which even the most robust constitutions must acknowledge. I trust, however, that your books, Mr. Melbourne, have arrived safely and are now arranged to your satisfaction? A gentleman’s library, after all, is the true heart of his home.”

Mr. Melbourne’s serious countenance brightened considerably at this. “They have, Mr. Hartley

, by some marvel of careful packing and conscientious transport, arrived almost entirely unscathed. One or two spines have suffered the indignity of a slight abrasion, and a volume of classical essays, a particular favourite, has a water-stain upon its marbled cover, a casualty I fear of a sudden shower during its passage through the Bristol Channel; but these are trifles, mere superficial blemishes upon the enduring wisdom they contain. My relief, I assure you, was profound when the final crate was opened and its precious contents revealed themselves to be, for the most part, in excellent preservation. Mrs. Gable, who had listened to this discourse upon books with a polite but visibly diminishing interest, seized upon the mention of Clarissa. “Ah, Miss Clarissa, how delightful to hear of your scholarly pursuits. One imagines a charming picture indeed: daughter and father, surrounded by learned tomes, engaged in such an intellectual endeavour. It is precisely what one would expect from a gentleman of Mr. Melbourne’s evident erudition. And so, your mornings have been devoted to this, have they? Well, one understands perfectly. The library is, as Mr. Hartley so aptly puts it, the heart of the home. But one must not neglect the more social aspects of life, must one? Especially for a young lady, newly arrived in our district. There are calls to be made, and calls to be received. One must become acquainted with one’s neighbours, and allow one’s neighbours to become acquainted with oneself. They are, on the whole, a very hospitable

hospitable set of people, and will, I am sure, be quite delighted to make your acquaintance.” Mr. Melbourne, who possessed a disposition less inclined to the easy flow of social intercourse than to the steady application of the mind, received this assurance with a degree of polite abstraction. He bowed slightly, and observed, in a tone that suggested a profound, if unspoken, reservation, “I trust we shall endeavour to be so. Clarissa, I am sure, will profit from the society of ladies of refinement; a benefit which, I confess, I have for some years past felt her to be somewhat deprived of.” He glanced at his daughter, who, though she maintained a composed demeanour, revealed by a slight quickening of her breath and a more intent focus upon the pattern of the carpet, an awareness of the implications of her father’s words. Mr. Hartley, ever the connoisseur of human nature, perceived these subtle indications with an almost scientific exactness, and smiled. “Indeed, Mr. Melbourne, a lively circle of acquaintance is an essential ingredient in the formation of a young lady’s character. Highbury, though a small community, boasts an excellent selection of such society. There are, for instance, the Woodhouses, at Hartfield, whose hospitality is renowned, and whose daughter, Miss Woodhouse, is a young lady of considerable vivacity and accomplishments. Then there are the Westons, at Randalls, a most amiable couple, Mrs. Weston having been formerly Miss Taylor, governess to Miss Woodhouse, and a lady of the most estimable character. And of course, one must not forget Mr. Knightley, of Donwell Abbey, a gentleman of solid judgment and consequence, whose family has been established here for generations.”

Mrs. Gable, not to be outdone in the recitation of local excellencies, interrupted with a flutter of her fan. “Oh, indeed, Mr. Hartley is quite right. The Westons are charm itself, and Mr. Knightley, though perhaps a little too serious for some tastes, is a most upright man. But we must not overlook our own dear Miss Bates, and her sweet mother. Though their circumstances are, alas, not what they once were, their hearts are as warm as any in Highbury, and their company, if one has a taste for innocent prattle, is quite diverting. And then there is the dear new pianoforte! You must hear it, Miss Clarissa; it is the most magnificent instrument, and Miss Fairfax plays upon it divinely. She is such a sweet girl, Miss Fairfax, and so accomplished, though one does sometimes wish she were a little more open with her feelings.” Here Mrs. Gable paused, perhaps remembering a certain delicate situation, and turned her attention back to Clarissa with renewed vigour. “But it is you, my dear Miss Clarissa, whom we are all so eager to receive into our little circle. I am sure you will find companions amongst us. There is Miss Woodhouse, as Mr. Hartley mentioned, and her dear friend Miss Smith, a most agreeable young lady, though perhaps a little too easily led, if one may say so. I shall be making a call at Hartfield myself tomorrow, and if you would do me the very great honour of accompanying me, Miss Clarissa, I should be quite delighted to introduce you. It would be an excellent beginning, a most proper introduction to the first family of Highbury.”

Clarissa, thus directly addressed and presented with a distinct invitation, felt a blush rise to her cheeks. She had anticipated, with a mixture of eagerness and trepidation, the moment when the abstract concept of ‘society’ would resolve itself into concrete plans. Her father, occupied as he was with his books and his contemplations, had given little thought to the practicalities of social establishment, leaving her, as she well knew, to navigate these often intricate passages herself. She had, however, a firm grasp of propriety, and understood that Mrs. Gable’s offer was both kind and necessary. “You are most obliging, Mrs. Gable,” she replied, her voice soft but clear, “and I should be very happy indeed to avail myself of your kindness. I confess I am quite ignorant of the customs of Highbury, and should feel much more at ease with such a guide as yourself.”

Mr. Melbourne, emerging from his mental retreat, gave a nod of approval. “Indeed, Mrs. Gable, your consideration is most welcome. Clarissa has, I believe, a sufficient grounding in general civility, but the nuances of a new locale are best imparted by one already conversant with them. I trust her conduct will be such as to reflect credit upon her instructors.” Mrs. Gable swelled with satisfaction. “Oh, I am sure of it, Mr. Melbourne. Miss Clarissa has all the air of a young lady who has been perfectly brought up. It will be a distinct pleasure to present her. We shall call at half past two, then, tomorrow. Will that suit you, my dear?” Clarissa assented, and the arrangements for her formal introduction to Highbury society were thus settled with a swiftness that left her both gratified and slightly daunted.

The remainder of the morning passed in more desultory conversation, turning upon the merits of various local tradesmen, the excellence of the Highbury air, and

the various methods of preserving fruit. Mrs. Gable, with the kindest intentions, left no corner of Highbury unilluminated by her discourse, touching upon the rector’s admirable sermons, the surprising rise in the price of tea, and the lamentable lack of refinement occasionally observed amongst the newly enriched. Clarissa listened with polite attention, gathering what intelligence she could from the stream of words, and occasionally contributing a brief, well-chosen remark that served to assure Mrs. Gable of her continued interest without distracting her from her flow. Mr. Melbourne, having found his way back into a particularly abstruse volume of classical philosophy, remained happily oblivious to the finer points of Highbury gossip, his occasional murmurs serving only as an amiable bass note to the lighter strains of Mrs. Gable’s conversation.

When Mrs. Gable at last took her leave, promising a punctual arrival on the morrow, Clarissa found herself alone with her father, and a quiet descended upon the small parlour that seemed almost profound after the recent animation. She sat for a moment contemplating the prospect before her, feeling a familiar mixture of hope and resignation. Highbury was to be her home, or at least the scene of her present existence, and it was incumbent upon her to make the best of it. Her father, learned and kind though he was, possessed a singular incapacity for practical affairs, and had, by an unfortunate series of choices, allowed their fortunes to dwindle to a point where a strict economy was not merely advisable but absolutely necessary. It was this necessity, combined with a vague but earnest wish for her daughter’s happiness, that had prompted him to accept a distant relation’s offer of this small, rent-free dwelling in Highbury; a village which, he had been assured, boasted both an agreeable climate and a library of some repute.

Clarissa, though sensible of their reduced circumstances, bore them with a quiet dignity that was, to those who observed it, both admirable and a little sad. She was not one to repine; indeed, her mind was naturally inclined towards optimism, albeit tempered by a clear-sighted understanding of the world’s realities. She was possessed of a gentle countenance, with eyes that were rather thoughtful than sparkling, and a figure slight but graceful. Her manners were composed, her voice soft, and her education, though less brilliant than Emma Woodhouse’s, was solid and extensive, having been conducted under her father’s tutelage. She was, in short, a young woman who, given the right circumstances, might easily have shone in any society, but who, through a combination of reserve and a certain absence of opportunity, had hitherto remained largely unremarked.

The morrow arrived, bright and promising, as Highbury days often did, carrying with it that particular sense of expectation that precedes a significant social event, however small. Clarissa dressed with care, choosing a simple muslin gown of a delicate shade, conscious that her wardrobe, though neat, was not extensive enough to admit of much variety, and that simplicity, well-chosen, was her surest path to propriety. She felt a tremor of nervousness, for this visit to Hartfield was to be her formal introduction to the very pinnacle of Highbury society, a society which, she apprehended, might prove as intricate in its regulations as any she had hitherto encountered, if not more so. She had, of course, heard much of Miss Woodhouse, of her beauty, her wit, and her acknowledged pre-eminence, and she felt a natural curiosity, tinged with a slight apprehension, as to how she would be received. It was a truth universally acknowledged in circles such as hers, that the first impression was often the most lasting, and Clarissa, for all her quiet strength, was not immune to the desire for a favourable reception.

Precisely at half past two, Mrs. Gable’s modest phaeton drew up before their door. Clarissa, having assured her father that she would return before tea, joined her guide, and they set off for Hartfield. Mrs. Gable, arrayed in a bonnet of rather more plumes than was perhaps strictly necessary, seemed in the highest spirits, discoursing animatedly on the agreeable weather, the superiority of her ponies, and the delightful opportunity that now presented itself for Miss Clarissa to form an acquaintance with such a charming young lady as Miss Woodhouse. “You will find her quite delightful, my dear,” she reiterated, “so lively, so accomplished, and possessed of such an excellent heart. Though, of course, she has her little foibles, as we all do, and sometimes, one feels, she might be a little less, well, a little less active in her benevolent schemes. But her intentions are always the best, always the best.” Clarissa listened, making mental notes on the complexities of Highbury character, as interpreted by Mrs. Gable. It seemed that even the first family was not without its gentle criticisms, a detail which, rather than dismaying her, suggested a comfortable familiarity in the village’s social observations.

The drive to Hartfield was not long, and soon they were approaching the elegant gates and the well-maintained grounds which bespoke the residence of taste and consequence. Clarissa could not but admire the cheerful aspect of the house, surrounded by its lawns and shrubberies, and felt a momentary lifting of her spirits. This, she thought, was indeed a pleasant setting, and she hoped that the inhabitants within would prove equally agreeable.

They were ushered into the drawing-room, a spacious apartment bright with natural light, where they found Miss Woodhouse, surrounded by all the comforts and elegancies of a cultivated home. She was, indeed, as Mrs. Gable had described, a young woman of striking beauty, her complexion fresh, her figure graceful, and her eyes, though perhaps a little too discerning, certainly possessed of considerable intelligence and animation. With her was Miss Harriet Smith, a pretty, innocent-looking girl, whose gentle manners and sweet smile made her an agreeable contrast to Emma’s more spirited air. Mr. Woodhouse, kind and solicitous, sat by the fire, a figure of amiable domesticity, whose presence seemed to infuse the room with an atmosphere of gentle, if somewhat delicate, repose.

Emma rose to greet them with a welcoming smile, her manners polished and her voice modulated to an agreeable pitch. “Mrs. Gable, how very good of you to call,” she said, extending a hand. “And this, I presume, is Miss Melbourne? We have been quite looking forward to making your acquaintance.” Her gaze, as she spoke, rested upon Clarissa with a frankness that was at once engaging and a little challenging, as if she were taking the measure of her new visitor with an almost professional eye.

Clarissa, feeling the full weight of this scrutiny, made her curtsy with an accustomed grace. “Miss Woodhouse, I am much obliged to you for your kind reception,” she replied, her voice steady. “I confess myself quite new to Highbury, and am most happy to be introduced to its excellent society.”

Mrs. Gable, ever eager to facilitate, interjected, “Indeed, Miss Woodhouse, I have brought our dear Miss Clarissa here expressly for that purpose. She is, as you can see, a truly charming young lady, and I have no doubt you will find her a most agreeable addition to your circle. Her father, Mr. Melbourne, is a gentleman of the most profound learning, quite devoted to his studies, and Miss Clarissa herself, I can assure you, is possessed of a most excellent education.”

Emma’s smile remained, a little more guarded perhaps. She was accustomed to new faces in Highbury, and to Mrs. Gable’s enthusiastic, if sometimes indiscriminate, recommendations. Her first impression of Clarissa was a favourable one, though she noted a certain quietness, a reserve that might, in Emma’s estimation, be either the mark of true refinement or merely a lack of conversational vivacity. Clarissa’s gown, though simple, was of good quality, and her composure was unexceptionable. Her eyes, Emma decided, held a glimmer of intelligence that promised more than a mere placid sweetness. “Pray, sit down, Miss Melbourne,” said Emma, indicating a chair near her own. “Harriet, my dear, come and join us. I am sure you and Miss Melbourne will find much to converse upon.”

Harriet, with her usual docility, moved to a seat beside Clarissa, offering a shy but genuinely friendly smile. “I am so pleased to meet you, Miss Melbourne,” she said. “We heard you had arrived, and I quite hoped you would come to Hartfield.”

Clarissa returned her smile. “And I, Miss Smith, am equally happy to be here. Highbury seems a most agreeable place.”

The conversation then flowed, directed mostly by Emma, who, with her characteristic ease, drew Clarissa into a discussion of her journey, her impressions of the village, and her father’s literary pursuits. Emma displayed a polite interest in Mr. Melbourne’s scholarly life, though Clarissa suspected that Emma’s true curiosity lay more in assessing the intellectual capabilities of his daughter. Clarissa answered frankly, speaking of their quiet life, her love of reading, and her father’s profound, if somewhat abstract, studies. She found Emma’s manner engaging, though beneath the polished surface, she detected a sharpness of observation, a quickness of judgment, that suggested a mind not easily deceived, and perhaps, not easily swayed.

Mr. Woodhouse, in his turn, offered Clarissa a dish of his excellent gruel, and then, satisfied with her polite refusal, reverted to a gentle conversation with Mrs. Gable about the dangers of damp air and the virtues of his own physician, Mr. Perry, whose skill he held in the highest esteem.

Emma, meanwhile, continued her discreet assessment of Clarissa. She found her remarkably self-possessed for a young lady so recently introduced into a new society. There was no awkwardness, no forced animation, but a quiet dignity that was, Emma grudgingly admitted, rather appealing. Clarissa spoke well, without affectation, and her opinions, though softly expressed, seemed to be her own, a quality Emma respected, though she rarely allowed it to go unchallenged in those she wished to cultivate.

“You mentioned your love of reading, Miss Melbourne,” Emma said, after a pause. “Are you fond of poetry, or do you prefer novels?” Emma herself held a somewhat ambivalent view of novels, finding them often a little too fanciful, though she acknowledged their occasional power to amuse.

“I confess I have a particular fondness for poetry,” Clarissa replied. “My father has instilled in me a deep appreciation for the classical authors, and I find a singular beauty in the elegance of their language and the timelessness of their sentiments. But I am not averse to a well-constructed novel, particularly those which offer a keen observation of human nature.”

Emma nodded slowly. This was a sensible answer, neither too pedantic nor too frivolous. It avoided the common pitfall of declaring an indiscriminate love for all novels, a habit that often betrayed a lack of true critical discernment. “Indeed,” said Emma, “a novel that genuinely observes human nature is a rare and valuable thing. Do you find our society here in Highbury offers much scope for such observation?” There was a playful, almost mischievous, glint in Emma’s eye as she posed this question, clearly inviting Clarissa’s fresh perspective.

Clarissa smiled, a genuine, unforced smile that softened her features considerably. “I have but just arrived, Miss Woodhouse, and my observations are as yet very superficial. But I have already perceived a certain, vivacity, in Highbury that promises much entertainment for the observant eye.” She glanced subtly towards Mrs. Gable, who was at that moment expounding with great earnestness on the merits of a new recipe for currant jelly, oblivious to the nuanced nature of Clarissa’s remark.

Emma almost laughed. This new acquaintance, she decided, was not without her own quiet wit, a quality Emma valued highly. Perhaps, she thought, this Miss Melbourne might prove a more interesting acquisition than she had at first anticipated. She was clearly not a simpleton, nor a mere echo, and that, in Emma’s world, was a distinct advantage.

“Vivacity, indeed,” Emma echoed, her smile widening. “Highbury does, I believe, possess its share of it. We have our little events, our little dramas, and our little characters, each providing endless material for contemplation.” She paused, then, as if a new thought had struck her, turned to Mrs. Gable. “Mrs. Gable, you were speaking the other day of Miss Fairfax’s new pianoforte. Has she quite accustomed herself to it yet? I believe Mr. Frank Churchill was quite struck by its tone.”

Mrs. Gable, momentarily diverted from currant jelly, launched into a fresh account. “Oh, the pianoforte! It is quite magnificent, Miss Woodhouse, quite magnificent. And Miss Fairfax plays upon it divinely, as I am sure you have heard. Such a blessing it is for her, to have such an instrument. Colonel Campbell is such a thoughtful man, is he not? Always so considerate of Jane. And Mr. Frank Churchill, yes, he was very much struck. He declared it to be the finest he had ever heard, save perhaps one in London. He quite insisted on her playing some of the waltzes from the ball, you know, he quite wished to relive them. Such a lively young man, Mr. Frank Churchill, so full of spirits.”

Emma listened with a carefully neutral expression, though her mind was actively re-engaging with the complexities of the Frank Churchill and Jane Fairfax situation. She had, in the wake of Mr. Knightley’s pointed remarks and Jane’s subtle blush, found herself reconsidering her previous convictions. Could there indeed be something more between them? The memory of Jane’s secret smile, glimpsed through her consciousness of a deeper blush, still lingered, a tantalising piece of evidence. She glanced at Clarissa, wondering what a fresh, unbiassed eye might make of such a tangled web.

Clarissa, observing Emma’s subtle shift of attention, and hearing the familiar name of Miss Fairfax, felt a stir of her own curiosity. She had heard Miss Fairfax mentioned with a peculiar frequency since her arrival, always with a certain deference, a certain admiration, often mixed with an unspoken something else. It was clear Miss Fairfax was a figure of some significance in Highbury.

“Miss Fairfax is, I understand, a young lady of considerable accomplishments,” Clarissa ventured, addressing Emma. “I have heard her praised for her musical talents.”

Emma turned her full attention back to Clarissa, a slight lift in her eyebrow. “Indeed, Miss Melbourne, Miss Fairfax is considered quite the paragon of accomplishment here in Highbury. She is highly cultivated, quite elegant, and universally admired for her modesty and quiet demeanour. She is, as Mrs. Gable says, a divine musician. She has quite eclipsed us all in that regard.” There was a subtle irony in Emma’s tone, a hint that perhaps such perfection might, in some ways, be a little too perfect.

Mrs. Gable, missing the nuance, beamed. “Oh, quite so, Miss Woodhouse. Quite so. Jane is truly a credit to her guardians, and to Highbury. She quite inspires us all. Though, as I often say, one does sometimes wish she were a little more communicative, a little more, open,  with her feelings. But perhaps that is merely her quiet nature, which some, of course, find very charming.”

Emma suppressed a faint sigh. Mrs. Gable, for all her good intentions, had a habit of circling around a point without ever quite seizing it, a quality that could be both frustrating and, at times, inadvertently revealing. She considered the situation again. Her initial belief had been that Frank Churchill was merely flirting, and that Jane Fairfax, though perhaps flattered, was too sensible, and too much attached to Colonel Campbell’s family, to engage in anything serious. But Mr. Knightley’s directness, and Jane’s own tell-tale signs, had given her pause. And then there was Mr. Frank Churchill himself, whose charm was undeniable, and whose motives, Emma was beginning to suspect, were rather more complex than a simple desire for harmless amusement.

“You will, of course, meet Miss Fairfax soon, Miss Melbourne,” Emma said, returning to Clarissa. “She is staying with her aunt, Miss Bates, just a short walk from here. I am sure you will find her a very agreeable acquaintance, though perhaps a little reserved for one of your frank and open nature.” Emma had a tendency to categorise people rather quickly, and she was already fitting Clarissa into a mental box labelled ‘sensible, perhaps a little too quiet, but with a surprising vein of dry wit.’

Clarissa merely smiled, accepting the assessment without challenge. She had no particular reason to dispute Emma’s perception of her character, and in truth, she did possess a certain frankness of spirit, though it was often masked by her natural reserve.

The conversation continued in this vein for some time, encompassing a variety of local topics, from the recent charitable donations to the poor, in which Emma had naturally taken a leading role, to the forthcoming assembly, which promised to be a particularly lively affair. Emma found herself increasingly engaged by Clarissa’s quiet intelligence. Clarissa did not seek to dominate the conversation, nor did she merely agree for the sake of amiability. She listened, she reflected, and when she spoke, her observations, though unassuming, often possessed a clarity and good sense that distinguished them from the more voluble pronouncements of Mrs. Gable, or even the gentle platitudes of Harriet.

Emma, for all her considerable merits, possessed a natural inclination to be the foremost figure in any company, and she had grown accustomed to the easy deference of most of her acquaintance. Clarissa, however, did not defer, not in any overt or challenging way, but simply occupied her own space with an unassuming confidence that was, to Emma’s perceptive eye, subtly different from the usual Highbury standard. This piqued Emma’s interest. She liked to understand people, to unravel the threads of their character, and Clarissa Melbourne presented a new and rather intriguing challenge.

As the afternoon drew on, Mr. Woodhouse became increasingly anxious about the declining light and the chill in the air, expressing his concern for Mrs. Gable’s ponies and his guests’ delicate constitutions. “You must not stay too long, my dears,” he fretted, “the evenings grow quite sharp, and a cold can be a very serious matter. Mr. Perry always says that prevention is better than cure.”

Mrs. Gable, ever mindful of propriety and her own good health, agreed. “Indeed, Mr. Woodhouse, you are quite right. We must take our leave. But it has been a most delightful afternoon. And Miss Clarissa has been quite charmed, I assure you.” She rose, gathering her reticule and shawl with an air of bustling satisfaction.

Clarissa offered her thanks to Emma and Mr. Woodhouse with a sincerity that was evident. “I am very much obliged to you, Miss Woodhouse, for your hospitality, and to you, Mr. Woodhouse, for your kindness. I have enjoyed my visit exceedingly.”

“We have been very happy to have you, Miss Melbourne,” Emma replied, her smile now warmer, more genuine. “I trust this will be only the first of many such visits. You must come again very soon, and we shall, perhaps, find an opportunity to walk in the shrubbery, or to discuss those poets you so admire.”

Harriet, too, expressed her pleasure. “I do hope you will come again, Miss Melbourne. I should like very much to hear more of your travels.”

As they made their farewells and were shown to the door, Emma found herself unexpectedly pleased with the new acquaintance. Clarissa Melbourne was clearly not one to be easily dismissed. She possessed an air of quiet intelligence, a pleasing reserve, and a certain self-possession that spoke of good breeding and a cultivated mind. Emma, whose benevolent schemes often revolved around the improvement of others, found herself wondering if Clarissa might not, in fact, be one of those rare individuals who had little need of her shaping hand, a thought that was both novel and, rather surprisingly, refreshing.

“Well, Miss Woodhouse,” Mrs. Gable announced as she settled herself into her phaeton, Clarissa by her side, “what did you think of our dear Miss Clarissa? Is she not quite charming? I told you she would make a delightful addition to Highbury society.”

Emma, standing on the steps of Hartfield, watched them drive away, a thoughtful expression on her face. “She is indeed very agreeable, Mrs. Gable,” she called after them. “A most interesting young lady, I think.”

As the phaeton disappeared down the drive, Emma turned and re-entered the house, her mind already busily engaged in analysing the new arrival. Clarissa Melbourne was certainly not beautiful in the dazzling sense, nor possessed of the ready wit that sparkled in conversation, but there was something about her quiet composure, her intelligent eyes, and her unassuming manner that held a distinct appeal. She was, Emma thought, a young woman of substance, a quality that Emma, despite her youthful vivacity, unconsciously valued above mere superficial charm.

She found Harriet still lingering by the window, her sweet face wearing a rather pensive expression. “Well, Harriet, what do you think of Miss Melbourne?” Emma asked, moving to join her. “Do you not find her agreeable?”

Harriet turned, a slight blush on her cheeks. “Oh, yes, Miss Woodhouse, very agreeable indeed. She seems so quiet, and yet so, sensible. I found her very pleasant.”

“Sensible she certainly is,” Emma agreed. “And quiet, too. Perhaps a little too quiet for your taste, my dear? You, who prefer a more lively companion?” Emma was ever careful to ensure that Harriet’s affections remained firmly anchored to herself, and while she welcomed new additions to their circle, she did so with an eye to maintaining her own pre-eminence.

“Oh, no, Miss Woodhouse,” Harriet quickly assured her, “I like her very much. And she is so gentle. I hope we shall see her often.”

Emma smiled, a little reassured. “I believe we shall, Harriet. She seems a young lady of good understanding, and her father, though a scholar, sounds like a very worthy gentleman. They will be a valuable addition to Highbury, I think. And perhaps,” she added, a speculative gleam in her eye, “perhaps she might prove to be a sensible friend to you, Harriet. One who might offer you some useful counsel upon occasion.” Emma, ever the matchmaker and improver, was already contemplating Clarissa’s potential role in her grand designs, a fresh piece on the chessboard of Highbury society. She was particularly keen to ensure that Harriet, having so narrowly escaped the misguided match with Mr. Elton, should now be guided towards more suitable connections. Clarissa, with her air of quiet good sense, might prove a valuable ally in this ongoing project.

Later that evening, after Mr. Woodhouse had retired and Emma was left alone with her thoughts, she found her mind returning again and again to Clarissa Melbourne. There was something about the young woman that resisted easy classification, something that made Emma pause and consider, a rare occurrence when Emma was usually so quick to form a definitive judgment. Clarissa was not like Jane Fairfax, whose polished perfection Emma sometimes found almost irritatingly flawless, nor was she like Harriet, whose pliable nature invited Emma’s benevolent manipulation. Clarissa seemed to possess an inner steadfastness, a quiet strength that made her, in Emma’s estimation, both formidable and interesting.

Emma found herself comparing Clarissa to Jane Fairfax. Jane, with her universally acknowledged accomplishments and her somewhat guarded manner, was a figure of distant admiration. Clarissa, on the other hand, had a warmth, a gentle openness that, though subtly expressed, invited a more personal connection. Emma, ever keen to discern the true character beneath the social veneer, felt that Clarissa offered a more genuine glimpse of her true self, even in a first interview, than Jane Fairfax did after months of acquaintance. This, Emma admitted to herself, was a point in Clarissa’s favour. Jane, for all her merits, sometimes gave the impression of having something to conceal, a suspicion Emma had found herself entertaining with increasing frequency since Mr. Knightley’s direct interrogations and Frank Churchill’s flirtatious pronouncements.

The thought of Frank Churchill and Jane Fairfax brought a familiar knot of intrigue to Emma’s mind. She had been quite certain, only a short time ago, that there was nothing serious between them, convinced that Frank’s attentions were merely the light-hearted play of a young man of fashion, and that Jane’s reserve was a sign of her attachment elsewhere. But the more she observed, the more she listened, the less clear the situation seemed. Mr. Knightley’s earnestness, Jane’s blushes, and even Frank’s own pointed remarks about the pianoforte, all seemed to suggest a deeper current beneath the surface of their interactions. Emma, who prided herself on her discernment, found this ambiguity quite vexing. She wished to know the truth, to unravel the mystery, and she found herself wondering if Clarissa, with her fresh and perhaps less biased perspective, might eventually prove a useful sounding board in her attempts to understand the nuances of Highbury’s various attachments.

Emma, lying awake in her bed, found her thoughts still circling around the day’s events. The visit of Mrs. Gable and Miss Melbourne had been, on the whole, a success. Clarissa was a promising addition to their social circle, and Emma felt a pleasing sense of anticipation at the prospect of further acquaintance. Highbury, with its gentle rhythms and familiar faces, sometimes threatened to become a little too predictable, a little too enclosed. A new face, a fresh mind, always brought with it a renewed sense of interest, a new canvas upon which Emma might, in her own benevolent way, attempt to paint a picture of improved happiness and well-ordered society. She resolved to call upon Miss Melbourne very soon, to deepen the acquaintance, and to discover more of that quiet intelligence which had so pleasantly surprised her. She might even, she thought with a small smile, take Harriet along, for such a sensible companion would surely be an excellent influence upon her impressionable young friend. With these agreeable thoughts, Emma at last drifted off to sleep, already planning the future interactions of her Highbury world.the various methods of preserving fruit. Mrs. Gable, with the kindest intentions, left no corner of Highbury unilluminated by her discourse, touching upon the rector’s admirable sermons, the surprising rise in the price of tea, and the lamentable lack of refinement occasionally observed amongst the newly enriched. Clarissa listened with polite attention, gathering what intelligence she could from the stream of words, and occasionally contributing a brief, well-chosen remark that served to assure Mrs. Gable of her continued interest without distracting her from her flow. Mr. Melbourne, having found his way back into a particularly abstruse volume of classical philosophy, remained happily oblivious to the finer points of Highbury gossip, his occasional murmurs serving only as an amiable bass note to the lighter strains of Mrs. Gable’s conversation.

When Mrs. Gable at last took her leave, promising a punctual arrival on the morrow, Clarissa found herself alone with her father, and a quiet descended upon the small parlour that seemed almost profound after the recent animation. She sat for a moment contemplating the prospect before her, feeling a familiar mixture of hope and resignation. Highbury was to be her home, or at least the scene of her present existence, and it was incumbent upon her to make the best of it. Her father, learned and kind though he was, possessed a singular incapacity for practical affairs, and had, by an unfortunate series of choices, allowed their fortunes to dwindle to a point where a strict economy was not merely advisable but absolutely necessary. It was this necessity, combined with a vague but earnest wish for her daughter’s happiness, that had prompted him to accept a distant relation’s offer of this small, rent-free dwelling in Highbury; a village which, he had been assured, boasted both an agreeable climate and a library of some repute.

Clarissa, though sensible of their reduced circumstances, bore them with a quiet dignity that was, to those who observed it, both admirable and a little sad. She was not one to repine; indeed, her mind was naturally inclined towards optimism, albeit tempered by a clear-sighted understanding of the world’s realities. She was possessed of a gentle countenance, with eyes that were rather thoughtful than sparkling, and a figure slight but graceful. Her manners were composed, her voice soft, and her education, though less brilliant than Emma Woodhouse’s, was solid and extensive, having been conducted under her father’s tutelage. She was, in short, a young woman who, given the right circumstances, might easily have shone in any society, but who, through a combination of reserve and a certain absence of opportunity, had hitherto remained largely unremarked.

The morrow arrived, bright and promising, as Highbury days often did, carrying with it that particular sense of expectation that precedes a significant social event, however small. Clarissa dressed with care, choosing a simple muslin gown of a delicate shade, conscious that her wardrobe, though neat, was not extensive enough to admit of much variety, and that simplicity, well-chosen, was her surest path to propriety. She felt a tremor of nervousness, for this visit to Hartfield was to be her formal introduction to the very pinnacle of Highbury society, a society which, she apprehended, might prove as intricate in its regulations as any she had hitherto encountered, if not more so. She had, of course, heard much of Miss Woodhouse, of her beauty, her wit, and her acknowledged pre-eminence, and she felt a natural curiosity, tinged with a slight apprehension, as to how she would be received. It was a truth universally acknowledged in circles such as hers, that the first impression was often the most lasting, and Clarissa, for all her quiet strength, was not immune to the desire for a favourable reception.

Precisely at half past two, Mrs. Gable’s modest phaeton drew up before their door. Clarissa, having assured her father that she would return before tea, joined her guide, and they set off for Hartfield. Mrs. Gable, arrayed in a bonnet of rather more plumes than was perhaps strictly necessary, seemed in the highest spirits, discoursing animatedly on the agreeable weather, the superiority of her ponies, and the delightful opportunity that now presented itself for Miss Clarissa to form an acquaintance with such a charming young lady as Miss Woodhouse. “You will find her quite delightful, my dear,” she reiterated, “so lively, so accomplished, and possessed of such an excellent heart. Though, of course, she has her little foibles, as we all do, and sometimes, one feels, she might be a little less, well, a little less active in her benevolent schemes. But her intentions are always the best, always the best.” Clarissa listened, making mental notes on the complexities of Highbury character, as interpreted by Mrs. Gable. It seemed that even the first family was not without its gentle criticisms, a detail which, rather than dismaying her, suggested a comfortable familiarity in the village’s social observations.

The drive to Hartfield was not long, and soon they were approaching the elegant gates and the well-maintained grounds which bespoke the residence of taste and consequence. Clarissa could not but admire the cheerful aspect of the house, surrounded by its lawns and shrubberies, and felt a momentary lifting of her spirits. This, she thought, was indeed a pleasant setting, and she hoped that the inhabitants within would prove equally agreeable.

They were ushered into the drawing-room, a spacious apartment bright with natural light, where they found Miss Woodhouse, surrounded by all the comforts and elegancies of a cultivated home. She was, indeed, as Mrs. Gable had described, a young

lady of a remarkably pleasing aspect, though Clarissa, accustomed to a more reserved style of beauty, perceived a vivacity in her eye and a directness in her address that, while undeniably engaging, suggested a character less easily fathomed than might at first appear. Miss Woodhouse, seated at a small writing table near a window, was gracefully concluding what seemed to be a series of notes or invitations, her head slightly bent, revealing the elegant curve of her neck and the becoming simplicity of her coiffure. A delicate smile played upon her lips as she looked up, her countenance lighting with a warmth that bespoke both good breeding and a genuine pleasure in company.

Mrs. Gable, ever eager to facilitate an agreeable interchange, stepped forward with an air of bustling importance. “My dear Miss Woodhouse,” she began, her voice a little louder than strictly necessary, “here is Miss Clarissa Howard, of whom I have already spoken so highly. She has, you see, yielded to my entreaties and consented to make her first bow to Hartfield. Miss Howard, this is our charming Miss Woodhouse, the very soul and ornament of Highbury.”

Emma rose with an easy grace, extending a hand to Clarissa with a gesture that was at once welcoming and assured. “I am delighted, Miss Howard,” she said, her voice possessing a melodious quality that perfectly matched the brightness of her eyes, “to make your acquaintance. Mrs. Gable has, indeed, spoken of you, and with such warmth that I have been quite eager for this moment. I trust your journey from London was not too tedious, and that you find our country air agreeable?”

Clarissa, a little overwhelmed by the directness of this address and the undeniable presence of Miss Woodhouse, curtsied with a quiet dignity. “Thank you, Miss Woodhouse,” she replied, her voice soft but clear, “the journey was perfectly pleasant, and Highbury, from what I have seen, appears a most charming village.” She met Emma’s gaze with a composure that belied the slight tremor in her hands.

Mrs. Gable, beaming with satisfaction at this auspicious beginning, then took it upon herself to explain Clarissa’s circumstances with a detail that caused Clarissa some slight embarrassment. “Miss Howard has come to assist her father, Mr. Howard, you know, Miss Woodhouse, in his literary pursuits. A gentleman of the most profound learning, though, alas, his health, as you might imagine, does not always permit him the full vigour necessary for the ceaseless demands of authorship. And Miss Clarissa, such a dutiful daughter, has forsaken the gaieties of the metropolis for the quietude of our provincial life. A true paragon, I assure you.”

Emma listened with an attentive politeness that did credit to her breeding, though Clarissa could discern a flicker of something indefinable in her eyes, a kind of quick assessment that suggested a mind constantly at work, categorising and drawing conclusions. “Indeed,” Emma responded, turning her gaze from Mrs. Gable to Clarissa once more, “that is a most commendable sacrifice, Miss Howard, though I trust you will not find Highbury entirely devoid of amusement. We endeavour, as best we can, to provide some diversion, even for those accustomed to a wider sphere. You are, I collect, quite devoted to literature yourself?”

Clarissa felt a sudden warmth in her cheeks. It was a subject upon which she could, when encouraged, discourse with some enthusiasm. “I confess, Miss Woodhouse,” she answered, “that I find great solace and instruction in books. My father has instilled in me a deep appreciation for them, and my own leisure hours are often spent among their pages.”

“A most admirable pursuit,” Emma declared, with an air that suggested she, too, held literature in the highest esteem, though her own practice of it, Clarissa suspected, might be of a more selective and perhaps less arduous nature. “We are fortunate in Highbury to have a small circulating library, though I fear it cannot boast the extensive collections of London. However, for serious study, I imagine your father’s own library must be quite sufficient.” Her tone was amiable, yet Clarissa felt a subtle probing in the question, as if Emma were attempting to gauge the precise social and intellectual standing of her new acquaintance.

Mrs. Gable, ever ready to supplement, interjected, “Oh, Mr. Howard’s library is quite formidable, Miss Woodhouse, quite formidable! Rows and rows of learned tomes, all bound in the most respectable leather. A scholar’s paradise, I assure you. And Miss Clarissa, I dare say, has perused them all.”

Clarissa offered a modest smile. “Many of them, certainly. But my father’s interests are somewhat specific, and I confess I am often drawn to works of imagination more than of pure scholarship.”

Emma’s eyes brightened. “Works of imagination? Ah, I understand perfectly. Novels, perhaps? I own I have a particular fondness for a good novel myself, though one must, of course, be discerning. There are so many frivolous productions. But a well-written novel, with genuine sentiment and a moral purpose, can be a most delightful companion.” She paused, her gaze resting on Clarissa with an expression that seemed to invite further confidence. “Have you, for instance, read anything by Miss Edgeworth, or perhaps the late Mrs. Radcliffe? Their narratives are, I find, often most instructive.”

Clarissa considered her reply. She had indeed read both authors, and with pleasure, but she also possessed a taste for works of a perhaps more daring or unconventional nature, which she knew might not be met with universal approbation. “I have read some of Miss Edgeworth’s works, and found them quite illuminating as to human character,” she said, choosing her words carefully, “and Mrs. Radcliffe’s Romances have certainly the power to transport one. But I confess, I sometimes find myself drawn to narratives that explore the deeper intricacies of the heart, even when

 they venture into regions which some might deem less immediately conducive to the usual course of polite instruction.” Clarissa concluded, a faint, almost imperceptible tremor in her voice, as if she had ventured upon a topic of slight impropriety.

Emma’s quick intelligence, ever alert to the nuances of conversation, registered this subtle declaration with a spark of interest. She perceived that Miss Howard was not merely a passive recipient of her father’s erudition, nor one content to confine her intellectual curiosity within the strictures commonly prescribed for young ladies. This hint of independent thought, of a spirit not entirely moulded by convention, was both stimulating and, to Emma’s taste, a little alarming. She found herself at once desirous of plumbing these deeper intricacies of the heart Miss Howard spoke of, and slightly apprehensive of what discoveries might be made. “Indeed,” Emma rejoined, her tone carefully modulated to convey neither censure nor excessive encouragement, “the heart, as we all know, is a most complex organ, and its intricacies have furnished subject matter for authors since time immemorial. It is, perhaps, natural that one should feel a particular attraction to such studies. Yet, it must also be acknowledged that discretion is a quality no less valuable than discernment, particularly in the selection of one’s literary companions.” She paused, allowing her words to linger, a gentle admonition veiled within a philosophical observation. “For there are, I regret to say, many narratives which, while purporting to explore the heart, do so with an indiscretion that borders upon the dangerous, leading the unsuspecting reader into a labyrinth of sentiment without the guiding thread of moral principle.”

Mrs. Gable, whose comprehension of such finely spun distinctions was, perhaps, less acute, beamed with an air of general agreement. “Oh, quite so, Miss Woodhouse, quite so! My dear Clarissa is the very soul of discretion, I assure you. Her father, Mr. Howard, is most particular as to the books permitted within his library, and though he has, as you rightly observed, a most formidable collection, he exercises the greatest care in their arrangement. He has indeed a cabinet, Miss Woodhouse, a locked cabinet, for certain older volumes which, while valuable for their antiquity, are deemed unsuitable for youthful perusal. But Clarissa, you understand, has never so much as glanced in that direction. Her taste is of the purest kind, I do assure you. She loves nothing better than a good sermon, or a pious poem, or a treatise on botany, which, as I often tell her, is a most wholesome pursuit for a young lady, being so much concerned with the beauties of nature.” Mrs. Gable concluded with a nod of self-satisfaction, convinced she had thus vindicated her charge’s intellectual integrity.

Clarissa, who had listened to her companion’s somewhat misplaced defence with a slight flush mounting to her cheeks, now spoke with a quiet dignity. “Mrs. Gable is very kind to speak so highly of my preferences, Miss Woodhouse, and it is true that I hold in due reverence all that is wholesome and morally upright. But I must confess, the human heart, with its capacity for both great virtue and great error, its struggles and its triumphs, its peculiar contradictions, has always appeared to me a subject worthy of profound contemplation, and not to be dismissed lightly, even when the portrayal might be considered by some to transgress the strict boundaries of conventional propriety. It is in the exploration of these very boundaries, indeed, that one sometimes comes to a deeper understanding of human nature itself, and of the forces that shape our moral decisions.” Her gaze was steady, her expression earnest, suggesting a mind that did not merely echo received opinion, but sought to grapple with truths for herself.

Emma, far from being offended by this quiet assertion of intellectual independence, found herself increasingly intrigued. There was a sincerity in Miss Howard’s manner, a thoughtful depth that went beyond the superficial accomplishments of many young women of her acquaintance. She saw in Clarissa not merely a new face to adorn Highbury society, but a mind of some cultivation, one that might prove a stimulating companion, perhaps even a tractable pupil, for Emma, as all her friends knew, possessed an insatiable desire to manage and refine those around her. This young lady, so reserved yet so earnest, presented a novel challenge. “You speak with considerable conviction, Miss Howard,” Emma observed, a faint smile playing on her lips, “and I confess, your perspective is one that deserves consideration. There is, undoubtedly, a point beyond which caution becomes timidity, and a too rigorous adherence to prescription may stifle true understanding. But tell me, for I am most interested to hear your sentiments on such a matter, what narratives, then, have afforded you these deeper insights into the intricacies of the heart, even those which might be deemed, as you say, unconventional?”

Before Clarissa could formulate a reply, Emma, with a sudden recollection of the hour and the amenities of hospitality, cast a glance towards the pianoforte, which, since the departure of Mr. Frank Churchill, had stood silent and neglected. “But I fear we have fallen into too serious a discourse for an occasion such as this. We must not allow ourselves to become so engrossed in matters of literary philosophy as to neglect the lighter pleasures of social intercourse. Perhaps, Miss Howard, as you have expressed a fondness for music, you might favour us with a piece? I believe I observed some new compositions among the selection Mr. Churchill brought for Miss Fairfax; perhaps there is something there that might suit your inclination, or you might prefer to play one of your own favourites. Music, after all, is another language of the heart, and one less prone, perhaps, to philosophical disputation.”

Clarissa, though momentarily startled by the abrupt change of subject, assented with a graceful inclination of her head. “I should be happy to play, Miss Woodhouse, if my humble abilities might afford any pleasure.” She rose from her seat, her movements unhurried and composed, and walked towards the instrument. As she approached, her eye fell upon the very music Mr. Frank Churchill had been so pointedly displaying earlier. A fleeting, almost imperceptible shadow crossed her features, as if some unspoken thought had been stirred, but it quickly vanished, and she seated herself with quiet dignity. She chose a piece not from the new selection, but a simple air, a Scottish melody of a plaintive yet beautiful cast, which she played with a delicate touch and a refined sensibility that bespoke considerable practice and a natural gift for expression.

Emma watched her closely, assessing every nuance of her playing. The melody, though simple, was rendered with such feeling, such a subtle variation of emphasis, that it captivated the ear and appealed directly to the emotions. It was not the showy, brilliant execution of a fashionable amateur, designed to dazzle and impress, but the heartfelt interpretation of one who truly felt and understood the music. Emma, herself no mean performer, recognised the genuine artistry in Clarissa’s playing. There was a purity of tone, a gentle melancholy in her interpretation that resonated deeply. She thought of Miss Fairfax’s more brilliant, perhaps more technically perfect, playing, and found herself making comparisons. Miss Fairfax’s music was often a display; Miss Howard’s was an invitation.

Mrs. Gable, meanwhile, clapped her hands together softly as Clarissa concluded the air. “Oh, my dear Clarissa, that was quite delightful! Such feeling, such expression! You always play with such heart, my love. Her voice, delicate and clear, suited his preference for all that was soft and unassuming, and her playing, though never brilliant, possessed a correctness and a sensibility that brought an agreeable harmony to the drawing-room. Emma, observing her father’s contented placidity during these musical hours, felt a quiet satisfaction in her own powers of arrangement. She had secured a genuine comfort for him, and in doing so, had deepened her acquaintance with a young woman whose modest virtues, she was now more convinced than ever, would prove a valuable addition to her own intimate circle, and perhaps, eventually, to that of another, more permanently established household, should the opportunity arise. For Emma, with her natural propensity for planning and a benevolent desire to improve the circumstances of those around her, had already begun to turn over in her mind various pleasant possibilities concerning Miss Howard’s future, possibilities which, though yet vague, were nonetheless very agreeable to contemplate.

It was on one such evening, some weeks later, when Miss Howard was lending her gentle strains to a favourite Scottish melody, that Mr. Frank Churchill and Miss Fairfax, with Mrs. Weston, made an unexpected call at Hartfield. Their arrival, though always welcome, brought with it a different atmosphere, a slight quickening of the social pulse, which, while Emma found it exhilarating, was not always so agreeable to her father. Mr. Woodhouse, however, was in a particularly serene frame of mind, having just heard a rendering of ‘My Nanie’s Awa’ that had, he declared, quite transported him to the pleasantest of scenes, and he received his new guests with an unusual degree of composure. Emma gave Harriet a swift, sharp glance. “Indeed, Harriet? You have heard him say this? Well, it is certainly true that Miss Fairfax has had a less fortunate start in life than many, and Mr. Knightley is known for his compassion. Yet, I confess, his behaviour this morning, and Mr. Churchill’s rather transparent allusions, have left me feeling quite bewildered. One moment I am convinced that the instrument is a clandestine gift from a secret admirer, the next I am almost persuaded it is merely an innocent token of affection from a distant relation. And then Mr. Knightley intervenes with his pointed questions, and the whole matter becomes yet more obscure.”

They had now reached the steps of Hartfield. As they entered the house, the familiar quietude of her home seemed to invite Emma to a more deliberate consideration of the morning’s events. Mrs. Weston, with a gentle apology about leaving her to attend to domestic matters at Randalls, took her leave soon after, promising to see Emma again in the evening. Harriet, after expressing her gratitude for the visit, retired to her room, leaving Emma to her own thoughts.

Emma walked into the drawing-room, her mind still actively engaged with the bewildering circumstances surrounding Jane Fairfax. She sat down, not at her pianoforte, for music seemed too light a distraction for her present train of thought, but near the window, looking out upon the well-tended gardens of Hartfield. The morning, so full of conversation and company, had left her with a peculiar sense of intellectual disquiet.

“What a tangled web,” she mused to herself, “do these human affections weave! And how readily do I find myself drawn into their intricacies, despite my firmest resolutions to remain a mere observer. Mr. Churchill, with his persuasive wit, seems determined to implicate me in his ingenious schemes of speculation, and I, regrettably, find myself too often diverted by his engaging manner to maintain a proper distance. He makes a game of everything, and I, it seems, am rather too willing a playmate.”

Her thoughts returned to Jane Fairfax. That quiet, reserved, almost melancholic young woman, so admired for her accomplishments, so pitied for her lack of fortune. Could she truly be involved in a secret engagement? And if so, with whom? Mr. Churchill had certainly implied it was with himself, or at least, he had played upon that idea with such skill as to make it seem highly probable. His knowing smiles, his pointed remarks about the musical instrument, his very persecution of her; it all contributed to a picture of clandestine courtship. Yet, Emma had always held a high opinion of Jane Fairfax’s principles. Deception seemed so foreign to her character, so utterly inconsistent with the gentle dignity she usually exhibited.

“And yet,” Emma reasoned, remembering the glimpse of "secret delight" she had caught on Jane’s face, “what if there is a truth to it, after all? A blush can signify many things; embarrassment, surprise, but also a hidden pleasure. And that smile… it was certainly not the smile of an innocent bystander. If Mr. Churchill’s hints are indeed a deliberate attempt to expose a secret engagement between himself and Jane, then the whole affair takes on a different hue. It becomes a matter of considerable indiscretion, and a challenge to the very reputation of the otherwise irreproachable Miss Fairfax. But why would he wish to expose it, if it were true? Unless it is a mere game to him, a light-hearted diversion, without any real consequences, which, considering his character, is not at all impossible. He enjoys an intellectual chase, and what finer prey than a well-guarded secret?”

Then, another possibility presented itself to Emma’s mind, one which had been briefly touched upon, and perhaps deliberately obscured by Mr. Churchill’s machinations. What if the instrument, and all its attendant mystery, had nothing whatsoever to do with Mr. Churchill, and everything to do with the absent Colonel Campbell? He was, after all, Jane’s guardian, and a man of considerable wealth and affection for her. To send such a gift, even a lavish one, would be entirely consistent with his character and his generosity towards his ward. Yet, if that were the case, why such secrecy? Why the apparent discomfort of Miss Fairfax? And why Mr. Churchill’s deliberate fostering of misapprehension?

“Unless,” Emma speculated, a new line of thought opening before her, “unless Mr. Churchill himself is not so innocent in this matter as he would have me believe. Perhaps his very insistence on a secret engagement between himself and Jane is but a smokescreen, a clever redirection of attention, intended to conceal an entirely different truth. But what truth?” She pondered this for some minutes, her brow furrowed in concentration.

And then there was Mr. Knightley. His unexpected intervention, his unusual directness, his singular focus on Jane Fairfax. He had asked Miss Bates how her niece was, insisted on an answer, and seemed entirely uninterested in Miss Bates’s usual garrulous digressions. Was this merely the benevolent concern of a kind-hearted neighbour, or did it signify something more profound? Mr. Knightley, Emma knew, was not one to indulge in idle curiosity, or to pry into the affairs of others without good reason. His interest, therefore, must have some foundation.

“Could Mr. Knightley,” Emma wondered, the idea striking her with a sudden, almost startling force, “could he possibly entertain an affection for Miss Fairfax himself? It is a thought that has never occurred to me before, so accustomed am I to regarding him as entirely removed from such romantic considerations, at least where the young women of Highbury are concerned. He is so much older, so much more settled. And yet, his concern this morning was undeniably marked. But no,” she shook her head, dismissing the notion almost as quickly as it had arisen, “that is too fanciful. Mr. Knightley is my brother-in-law, my family friend, my staunchest critic, but never, surely, a suitor for the elegant Miss Fairfax. Besides, she is so unlike myself, in her quietness, her reserved nature. He could not possibly find such a contrast agreeable. He often finds *my* vivacity excessive.”

Yet, the image of Mr. Knightley’s earnest, almost commanding expression as he demanded news of Jane Fairfax lingered in her mind. And the contrast between his straightforward, no-nonsense manner, and Mr. Churchill’s charming, evasive banter, was never more apparent than at this moment of reflection. Mr. Churchill played with appearances; Mr. Knightley dealt in facts, in realities, however uncomfortable. He had a way of cutting through the trivialities, of getting to the heart of the matter, even when the matter itself seemed intended for concealment.

“He is going to Kingston,” Emma recalled Miss Bates’s breathless pronouncement. “Can I do anything for you?” And then his subsequent refusal to be drawn into any further conversation, his polite but firm disengagement from Miss Bates’s attempts to delay him. It was characteristic of him, to be purposeful, to be direct.

Emma felt a little flush of annoyance, not at Mr. Knightley, but at the general confusion of the situation. She prided herself on her discernment, on her ability to see into the motives and feelings of those around her. Yet, in this instance, her usual clarity of vision seemed to be entirely obscured. She had allowed herself to be amused, perhaps even swayed, by Mr. Churchill’s artful suggestions, and to dismiss, with her “steady scepticism,” the more obvious clues.

“Perhaps,” she thought, her lips curving into a wry smile, “my esteemed companion, Mr. Churchill, is quite correct, and I do possess an unfortunate aptitude for navigating the currents of human feeling. But it seems, in this instance, that I have merely allowed myself to be tossed about by the cross-currents, rather than to steer with any definite purpose.”

The truth, as it slowly began to dawn upon Emma, was that she had been too much captivated by Mr. Churchill’s charm, too much flattered by his attentions, to examine his conduct with the rigorous impartiality she usually brought to bear upon the affairs of others. She had enjoyed the intellectual game, the feeling of being in an exclusive confidence, and had allowed this enjoyment to blind her to the deeper implications of his behaviour.

The words she had exchanged with him, in their little conspiratorial whispers regarding Jane Fairfax’s supposed reprehensible feelings, now returned to her with a distinct resonance. “I hope she does. I would have her understand me. I am not in the least ashamed of my meaning.” His bold declaration, his apparent desire for Jane Fairfax to grasp the full import of his playful accusations, suddenly seemed less like the confident jest of a suitor and more like the deliberate public taunt of a man who held a secret, and wished to see its object squirm. And if that were the case, what a cruel game it was!

Emma rose from her seat and walked to the window again, gazing out at the quiet garden. The air was still, the shadows lengthening slightly as the morning wore on. She felt a growing sense of discomfort, a prickle of conscience. She had allowed herself to be drawn into a scheme of amusement, the true nature of which she was only now beginning to suspect. And in doing so, she had, perhaps, been less than entirely fair to Jane Fairfax.

“This amiable, upright, perfect Jane Fairfax was apparently cherishing very reprehensible feelings,” she repeated in her mind, the phrase recalling her earlier internal judgment. But what if those feelings, whatever their nature, were being cruelly exposed, or even falsely attributed, by Mr. Churchill for his own amusement? It was a possibility that Emma, in her rush to find amusement and exercise her discernment, had perhaps too readily dismissed.

Her scepticism, so steady and unyielding where her own romantic future was concerned, had proved surprisingly pliable in the face of Mr. Churchill’s captivating influence. She had been flattered by his intelligence, amused by his wit, and so, perhaps, had allowed herself to be led astray in her judgment of another. The idea that she, Emma Woodhouse, who prided herself on her keen observation and moral rectitude, could have been so easily misled, so readily complicit in what might be a very unkind deception, was profoundly unsettling.

“I must be more careful,” she resolved, her chin held high, as if in silent defiance of her own previous errors. “I must not allow Mr. Churchill’s agreeable conversation to obscure my judgment. There is a mystery here, and it is one which deserves to be unravelled with a clear head, and without the distracting influence of flattering words.”

The thought of Mr. Knightley, with his steadfast integrity and his blunt honesty, returned to her, a welcome anchor in the swirling currents of her confused thoughts. He would never play such games; he would never allow himself to be amused at the expense of another’s feelings, or reputation. His directness, which sometimes irritated her, now seemed a beacon of truth. He valued sincerity above all else, and for all his admonitions, his heart was always in the right place.

Emma knew she would have to observe both Mr. Churchill and Jane Fairfax with a renewed and more critical eye. The game, if it was a game, had just become far more serious, and Emma, the self-proclaimed passenger on the river of discourse, felt an unexpected, and unwelcome, responsibility to understand its true destination. The morning’s events, far from resolving anything, had merely deepened the enigma, and left Emma with a disquieting sense that there were truths yet hidden, and motives yet unexamined, beneath the surface of Highbury’s polite society.her own sparkling with a conscious amusement that she did not care to conceal, even while she maintained a delicate air of diffidence. She knew his compliments were framed with an artful purpose, yet she found herself unable to resist the charm of such polished address; it was a species of intellectual flattery which, however dangerous, was almost irresistibly pleasing.

“A passenger, you call yourself, Miss Woodhouse?” Mr. Churchill rejoined, with a smile that deepened the pleasant lines about his mouth, and a glance that spoke volumes of his own understanding. “I fear I must differ from such a modest estimation of your capacities. Passengers, in my humble experience, are content to observe the scenery, to remark upon the speed or the smoothness of the journey, but rarely do they possess such a comprehensive map of the river’s winding course, or such an intimate knowledge of the very vessel itself. Indeed, I have observed that you possess a singular ability to discern every shifting current, every submerged obstacle, and every distant port of call, long before the most experienced pilot might consider them. Your skill is not merely to adapt, but to foresee; your wit not merely responsive, but acutely perceptive. If there is a steering hand upon this river of discourse, I would venture to say it belongs, at least in part, to you, and that my humble efforts are but to navigate in its wake, hoping to keep pace with your more accomplished direction.”

Emma laughed, a clear, silvery sound that expressed her unaffected delight in such refined compliment, even as she felt a slight flutter of apprehension. He saw too much, indeed; he had pierced through her modest protestations with a facility that was almost alarming. It was a delightful game, this verbal sparring, yet she wondered, even as she enjoyed it, to what end he played it so constantly.

“You are determined, I perceive, Mr. Churchill,” she said, recovering her composure with an effort, “to credit me with powers I assure you I do not possess, and to elevate the simplest observation into a profound insight. I apprehend that it is merely your own generosity of spirit, or perhaps a natural inclination to amuse yourself at my expense, that leads you to such extravagant pronouncements. I am a simple inhabitant of Highbury, accustomed to the plain realities of its domestic sphere, and possessed of no greater wisdom than any other young woman of tolerable understanding and moderate experience. My remarks are the common coin of conversation, not the rare treasures of profound thought.”

“Indeed, Miss Woodhouse,” he responded, his tone still light but with a definite edge of persistence, “I should like to know upon what principle you operate when you claim such commonality for your observations. For I must confess, the common coin I usually encounter bears little resemblance to the finely minted gold of your wit. If your thoughts are merely common, then the intellectual currency of Highbury must be richer than any I have yet discovered, and I confess myself eager to understand its source. Perhaps it is the very plainness of your realities that sharpens your perception, or the moderate extent of your experience that allows for such profound conclusions regarding the greater world. Whatever the spring from which your understanding flows, it is a potent draught, and one that is most singularly refreshing.”

Mrs. Weston, who had been walking a little ahead with Harriet, now turned her head, a gentle smile upon her face, and waited for them to draw nearer. “You two are in very earnest conversation,” she observed, her voice soft and maternal. “I hope Mr. Churchill is not overwhelming you, my dear Emma, with his eloquent arguments. “On the contrary, dear Mrs. Weston,” Emma replied, “Mr. Churchill is merely exercising his admirable powers of persuasion in an attempt to convince me of an intellectual merit which I am quite sure I do not possess. It is a very pleasant, though quite unsuccessful, endeavour on his part.”

“And Miss Woodhouse, Mrs. Weston,” Frank added, with a deferential bow in the lady’s direction, “is exercising her equally admirable powers of modesty, in an attempt to deny a merit which she most certainly possesses in abundance. It is a very charming, though equally unsuccessful, endeavour on hers. I confess, however, that her resistance only serves to heighten the pleasure of the pursuit.”
