"Aye, aye, go to her, my dear," Mrs. Jennings said, waving her hand dismissively, her mouth full of cake. "But do not let her mope. Tell her to think of Delaford, and the mulberry tree! Elinor made her escape, the half-finished glass of Constantia still in her hand. The rich, sweet aroma of the wine seemed to mock the bitterness of Marianne’s sorrow. As she ascended the stairs, the sound of Mrs. Jennings’s cheerful humming, punctuated by the occasional crunch of an almond cake, drifted up from the drawing-room. It was a sound that, while intended to convey comfort and good cheer, only served to highlight the profound solitude and unspoken grief that now enveloped Marianne’s world.

She found Marianne exactly as she had left her, still leaning against the small grate, her face hidden from view, her posture speaking volumes of her utter despair. The fire, which had been but a few smouldering embers, had now completely died, leaving the room in a chill gloom, illuminated only by the faint light from the landing. Elinor’s heart ached with a familiar pang of sympathy, mixed with a frustrated helplessness. She placed the glass of wine on the small table beside the bed, knowing full well it would remain untouched.

"Marianne," she said softly, sitting beside her sister and gently placing a hand on her shoulder. Marianne did not stir. "Marianne, dear sister, you must not lie here in this cold room. The fire is out, and it grows very late. You must come to bed, and try to rest. You will make yourself ill otherwise, and that would only add to our troubles."

A low, muffled sound escaped Marianne, a sound that tore at Elinor's heart, though it was not a word. It was a sound of absolute, unmitigated wretchedness.

"The Constantia is here, if you should feel the need for it," Elinor continued, her voice gentle but firm. "Mrs. Jennings sent it, hoping it would bring you some comfort. But if you cannot drink it, at least let me help you to bed. You are shivering, Marianne. Come, let me tuck you in, and you will be warmer there."

Slowly, painfully, Marianne unfolded herself from her crumpled position. Her face, when she at last turned it towards Elinor, was swollen and stained with tears, her eyes red and dulled by weeping. The sight was more eloquent than any outburst of grief. Elinor felt a wave of profound love and pity for her sister, and longed for some power to alleviate her suffering.

"Leave me, Elinor," Marianne whispered, her voice hoarse and broken, barely audible. "I wish only to be left alone. I cannot bear to speak, or to be spoken to. I cannot bear the light, or the sound of human voices. Let me suffer in silence, and in darkness. It is all I am fit for now."

"No, Marianne," Elinor replied, her voice unwavering. "I will not leave you to suffer alone, not when you are in such distress. To leave you now would be to abandon you to your own sorrows, and that I cannot, and will not, do. You are my sister, and I love you. And you must not allow this sorrow to consume you entirely. Come, dear girl, to bed. Sleep, if you can, is the best remedy for a wounded spirit. And if you cannot sleep, then at least rest, and find some momentary oblivion from your thoughts."

With a gentle persuasion that allowed no further argument, Elinor helped Marianne to undress and settle into bed. Her sister offered no resistance, her body limp and unresponsive, moving as if without will of its own. Once Marianne was tucked beneath the covers, Elinor sat beside her, stroking her hair, and murmuring soft words of comfort. The silence that followed was broken only by Marianne’s quiet, shuddering breaths.

She knew that any direct attempt to persuade Marianne to join the company downstairs would be met with an equally resolute refusal, and would only serve to heighten her distress. Marianne’s grief, in its present intensity, was a force that must be allowed to spend itself, rather than be fought against.

Elinor sat down quietly on a low stool near the fireplace, allowing her presence to be a comforting, yet unobtrusive, anchor in Marianne’s tempestuous sea of sorrow. She looked at her sister, so utterly lost in her grief, and felt a profound understanding of the isolation that intense suffering could impose. Marianne, in her romantic fervor, had given her whole heart, and its shattering now left her utterly bereft, unable to comprehend a world that continued without the object of her affections.

Elinor’s own mind, though not prone to such dramatic extremities, was far from tranquil. The scene with Mrs. Jennings had left her emotionally drained, her nerves jangling from the effort of maintaining composure and delicacy amidst such boisterous insensitivity. She thought of Willoughby, his charm, his captivating manner, and the cruel deceit hidden beneath that polished exterior. How could he have been so false? And how could Marianne, with her unerring instinct for truth and beauty, have been so completely misled? It was a perplexing and painful enigma.

Then her thoughts turned to Colonel Brandon. Mrs. Jennings’s persistent advocacy for him, however crude, had a practical foundation. He was, as she had justly observed, a man of character and fortune, and undeniably devoted to Marianne. But for Marianne, in her current state, to even contemplate such a union was an impossibility. Her heart, she felt sure, would remain forever bound to Willoughby, however unworthy he had proved himself to be. Yet, Elinor could not deny the stark reality of their situation. They were not wealthy; Marianne, with her scant fortune, had little prospect of making an advantageous match without the aid of some established attachment. The world, particularly their small corner of society, would not permit her to indulge in perpetual mourning. She would eventually be expected to re-enter society, to find a place for herself, and in the prevailing customs, that place was almost inevitably within the bounds of matrimony.

Elinor felt a deep weariness creep over her, a quiet ache in her own heart that was born not of romantic disappointment, but of the heavy burden of responsibility. She was the elder sister, the sensible one, and it fell to her to navigate these treacherous waters, to protect Marianne from further harm, and to guide her towards some semblance of future happiness, however distant and improbable it might seem at this moment.

After a long period of shared silence, broken only by the faint crackle of the fire and the occasional shuddering breath from Marianne, Elinor felt a renewed urge to persuade her sister to seek rest. The chill of the room was deepening, and Marianne’s thin dress offered little protection.

“Marianne,” Elinor said softly again, “you are growing cold. And you have had no food, nor any refreshment all evening. I must insist that you go to bed. Let me help you.”

Marianne stirred, as if roused from a deep slumber, and slowly, reluctantly, pushed herself up from her kneeling position. Her movements were slow, almost mechanical, as if her limbs were heavy with sorrow. “There is no need, Elinor. I can manage.”

But her voice was still weak, and her step unsteady. Elinor rose quickly and put an arm around her sister’s waist, providing much-needed support. She guided Marianne towards the bed, where she had earlier ensured the sheets were turned back and fresh pillows arranged.

“Lie down, my love,” Elinor urged, her voice full of solicitude. “And try to rest. Perhaps sleep will bring you some temporary oblivion from your thoughts.”

Marianne sank onto the bed, her body seeming to collapse with the effort, and turned her face into the pillow, her form shaking with renewed, though silent, sobs. Elinor watched her for a moment, her heart heavy, then gently pulled the covers over her. She knew that to speak further would be useless. Marianne was beyond the reach of words, lost in the private world of her own despair.

Elinor then walked over to the table by the window, where the glass of Constantia still stood, untouched. She picked it up, contemplating its dark contents for a moment. It was a potent cordial, certainly, and might indeed have brought a fleeting warmth to Marianne’s chilled frame, or a momentary dulling of her senses. But Elinor knew that such artificial comforts would ultimately do more harm than good, masking the pain rather than allowing it to run its natural course. It was not a potion for a bruised heart, but for a physical ailment, and in Marianne’s case, the illness was of the spirit.

She carried the glass out of the room, closing the door softly behind her. Descending the stairs, she encountered Mrs. Jennings in the hall, who was bustling about, overseeing the arrangement of candles and the preparation of refreshments for the expected company.

“Well, Elinor, my dear! Did she drink it?” Mrs. Jennings inquired eagerly, her face alight with expectation. “Did the Constantia do her good? I told you it would. There is nothing like a good drop of wine to set things right. And will she come down? The Steeles are just arrived, and Sir John will be here any moment. We are just about to begin a game of Commerce.”

Elinor managed a faint, regretful smile. “I regret to say, ma’am, that Marianne was too unwell to touch it. She is now in bed, endeavouring to get some rest. Her spirits are too low, and her constitution too delicate, to bear any company this evening.”

Mrs. Jennings’s cheerful expression clouded slightly, though her natural optimism quickly reasserted itself. “Ah, well, I daresay she knows what is best for herself, poor dear. But it is a pity, a great pity. A little bit of company would have done her a world of good. But never mind, never mind. She will be better tomorrow, I daresay. And then we shall have a good long talk about Colonel Brandon. He is sure to be here by then, I know it. And perhaps when she is feeling a little stronger, she will be more amenable to reason. But come, Elinor, you must not stand there looking so grave. Come and join us. Miss Steele and Miss Nancy are in the drawing-room, and they are quite eager to hear all about town. And Mrs. Palmer will be here shortly, I expect, with her good nature and her silly husband. We shall have quite a merry time, despite all. And you must play Commerce with us, Elinor. You are so clever at cards, and we shall need someone to keep an eye on poor Nancy, who always gets so confused with the rules.”

Elinor, though her own heart was heavy, knew that she could not refuse to join the company. It would be both rude and conspicuous, and would only invite further questions about Marianne’s condition, which she was anxious to avoid. Moreover, she recognised that Mrs. Jennings, in her own way, was attempting to offer comfort and to restore a sense of normalcy, however superficial, to the disturbed atmosphere of the house. So, with a silent sigh, she composed herself, summoned a semblance of cheerfulness, and followed Mrs. Jennings into the lively, brightly lit drawing-room, leaving her own quiet anxieties, and Marianne’s profound sorrow, behind the closed door of her sister’s room.

The drawing-room was indeed beginning to fill with company. The two Miss Steeles, Lucy and Nancy, were already ensconced on the sofa, their heads close together, engaged in earnest, whispered conversation, which immediately ceased upon Elinor’s entry. Lucy, the elder, a pretty, but somewhat artful young woman, whose eyes were quick to observe and whose tongue was equally quick to relate, offered Elinor a smile that seemed calculated in its warmth. Nancy, her younger sister, a giggling, foolish creature, merely simpered behind her fan.

“Oh, Miss Dashwood, how delightful to see you!” exclaimed Lucy Steele, her voice carefully modulated to convey an impression of genteel enthusiasm. “We were just saying how much we hoped you would be joining us this evening. It would have been such a dreadful thing for you to have been left alone, you know, with your sister so poorly. Mrs. Jennings has just been telling us all about it, such a sad, sad affair! But you must not grieve too much, my dear Miss Dashwood, for depend upon it, everything happens for the best, as mama always says. And I am sure your sister will soon recover her spirits, and find someone far more worthy of her affections. Indeed, I have no doubt of it.”

Elinor felt a familiar prickle of irritation at Lucy’s thinly veiled curiosity and her easy pronouncements upon Marianne’s private suffering. Lucy Steele was a young woman of determined ambition, whose seemingly artless chatter often concealed a shrewd calculation of advantage. Elinor knew that any detail, however slight, concerning Willoughby’s defection, or Marianne’s subsequent distress, would be eagerly seized upon and dissected by the Miss Steeles, and then, no doubt, disseminated with cheerful alacrity throughout their acquaintance.

“Thank you, Miss Steele,” Elinor replied, striving for an impassive neutrality that would discourage further probing. “Marianne, I am glad to say, is now resting, and we hope that a good night’s sleep will do much to restore her. Her illness, as you know, has been somewhat lingering, and requires careful attention.” She chose to emphasise the physical aspects of Marianne’s malady, hoping to deflect attention from the more sensitive emotional causes.

“Ah, yes, lingering illness, so very trying!” chimed in Nancy Steele, with a nod of her empty head. “I remember when our cousin Martha had such a lingering illness, and she always said a good strong cup of tea, with plenty of sugar, was the only thing to set her right. her ardent imagination, considered it to be. Her sister, with her deep and tender affections, her susceptible heart, and her romantic notions, could not but feel the present disappointment as a calamity beyond mere pecuniary inconvenience or a passing slight. She closed the door behind her with a sigh of relief, the noise of their cheerful optimism somewhat muffled by the thick oak.

She hesitated for a moment, steeling herself, before gently tapping on the door.

So utterly insensible to true feeling?”

Elinor entered the room, placing the glass carefully on a small table by the window. She went to her sister, gently laying a hand on her arm. “They mean well, Marianne,” she said, her voice soft and soothing. “Indeed, they do. Their concern, however expressed, is genuine. “Happy?” Marianne swung round, her eyes flashing with a sudden, almost fierce energy. “How can I ever be happy again? My happiness, my very existence, was bound up in him! How can I ever believe in goodness, when such deceit can hide beneath so fair an exterior?”

Elinor listened with a heavy heart. She knew that any attempt to reason with Marianne in her present state would be futile. Her grief was too raw, her emotions too overwhelming. She could only offer solace through her presence, and perhaps, through gentle distraction. “I know, my dearest sister,” she murmured, drawing Marianne closer and stroking her dishevelled hair. “It is a cruel blow, a most bitter disappointment. Marianne pulled away, her eyes fixed on Elinor’s. “Time? What comfort is there in time, when each moment stretches before me like an eternity of anguish? I

will not, I cannot, accept such cold comfort! My heart is broken, Elinor, irrevocably broken! You speak of time, but what is time to one who has lost all hope, all prospect of joy? Do you imagine that I shall one day wake and find myself miraculously healed, my affections transferred, my principles forgotten? No, Elinor, my love for him was a part of my very being, and now that part is torn away. How can I live without it? How can I pretend that this wound will close, leaving but a faint scar, when it has pierced my very soul?" Marianne’s voice, though still hoarse, had gained a desperate strength, and she paced the small room, wringing her hands anew, her eyes fixed on some distant, sorrowful point.

Elinor watched her, her own heart aching with a sympathy so profound it was almost a physical pain. She knew the futility of argument; Marianne, in her present state, was beyond the reach of reason or conventional consolation. Her distress was too absolute, her perception of the world too entirely coloured by her suffering to admit of any mitigating thought. Yet, Elinor could not remain silent, could not simply observe her sister’s anguish without attempting, however vainly, to offer some fragment of support.

"My dearest Marianne," she said, her voice low and steady, a gentle counterpoint to her sister's agitation, "I do not speak of forgetting, nor of transferring affections, which I know to be as impossible for you as it would be for me. I speak only of the gradual, perhaps imperceptible, lessening of this acute pain. The heart, though wounded, possesses a remarkable capacity for healing, for finding some measure of peace, even after the most grievous of losses. It is not to say that you will cease to remember, or to feel the injustice of what has passed, but that the intensity of these feelings will, in time, become less overwhelming. Marianne stopped her pacing abruptly, turning to Elinor with a look of profound exasperation. "Life? What current is there for me now but this tide of despair? You speak of healing, Elinor, but what solace is there in a life lived without the one whom your soul acknowledges as its other half? You, with your composure, your admirable fortitude, you cannot comprehend this agony. Elinor sighed inwardly. To argue further, she knew, would be not only fruitless but perhaps even unkind, given Mrs. Jennings’ unfeigned desire to be of service. Elinor, receiving the glass from Mrs. Jennings, could only assent with a forced smile to the final sentiments, though her whole soul revolted from the mercenary tenor of the preceding observations. She knew it was useless to argue with Mrs. Jennings upon such points, where her conviction was so deeply rooted and her opinions so firmly established by what she considered the unerring wisdom of the world. She therefore withdrew, holding the glass with care, and made her way once more to Marianne’s chamber, her heart heavy with the fresh burden of Mrs. Jennings’s well-meant, though ill-judged, counsel. Marianne slowly raised her head, her eyes, red and swollen from weeping, turning towards Elinor with a look of pained enquiry. She spoke not a word, but her gaze was eloquent with suffering, a silent reproach to any intrusion upon her misery.

“No? Oh, the poor dear! Well, no matter, no matter. Sleep is the best physician, as they say. But now, you must come and join us! Sir John and Mrs. Palmer are here, and young Mr. Harris from the Grange, a very amiable young man, though perhaps a little shy. We are going to have a little music, and then a game of speculation. It will put some colour in your cheeks, my dear, and take your mind off things. A little diversion is always the best cure for the mopes, is it not?”

Elinor, though she would infinitely have preferred to retire to her own room and seek the quiet solitude that her own agitated mind so greatly craved, knew that to refuse Mrs. Jennings in her present mood would be not only ungracious but also largely ineffectual. She therefore submitted herself to the cheerful tyranny of her hostess, allowing herself to be led forward and introduced to Mr. Harris, a diffident young man who bowed with an awkward grace and seemed scarcely capable of uttering a coherent sentence.

Sir John, upon seeing Elinor, immediately clapped his hands together with a booming laugh. “Aha! Here is our sensible Elinor! Come, my dear, sit down! We were just speaking of Colonel Brandon’s new hunter; a magnificent animal, upon my word! A perfect match for him. Though I daresay he would prefer a certain other mare, if he could but have her, eh, Colonel?” He nudged the Colonel jocularly, his eyes twinkling with mischievous good humour, entirely oblivious to the impropriety of his remark.

Colonel Brandon, however, merely coloured slightly and returned Sir John’s jest with a quiet, dignified smile, offering no further comment. Elinor felt a wave of acute embarrassment wash over her. She glanced quickly at Mrs. Palmer, hoping for some acknowledgement of Sir John’s indelicate hint, but Mrs. Palmer was, as usual, engrossed in a discussion with her husband about some trivial domestic matter, her face alight with an almost childish glee.

Mrs. Jennings, however, seized upon Sir John’s remark with renewed vigour. “Ah, yes, the Colonel’s hunter! A splendid creature indeed. But Sir John is quite right, Colonel, is he not? A fine horse is one thing, but a fine wife is quite another! And you, my dear Colonel, deserve the very best. And I, for one, have every confidence that you shall have her, aye, that you shall! Mark my words, Elinor, mark my words. All will come right in the end, as I always say. Providence has a way of sorting these things out.”

Elinor inwardly groaned. Here it was again, the relentless, tactless pursuit of her sister’s prospects, disguised as benevolent prophecy. She cast a pleading look at Mrs. Jennings, hoping to convey, by the very intensity of her gaze, the urgent necessity of discretion, but her hostess, unfortunately, was not one to be swayed by subtle intimations. Mrs. Jennings was entirely convinced of the wisdom and appropriateness of her own pronouncements, and saw no reason to temper them for the sake of delicate feelings she did not fully comprehend.

“Indeed, Ma’am,” Elinor managed to interject, striving to keep her voice even, “I believe such matters are best left to providence, as you say, and require no further discussion from us at present. My sister is very unwell, as you know, and any mention of these recent unfortunate events can only serve to exacerbate her distress.”

“Oh, my dear, of course, of course!” Mrs. Jennings exclaimed, though her tone suggested a mere polite acknowledgement rather than genuine conviction. “Not a word, not a syllable, to Marianne herself, of course! That would be cruel indeed. You saw how I behaved at dinner, did you not? Not a hint, not a glance! And Sir John and my daughters are just the same, very thoughtful and considerate, especially when I give them a hint, as I certainly will tomorrow. But amongst ourselves, my dear, what harm can there be in a little friendly speculation? It is only natural, is it not, to wish the best for those we care about? And I do care for your sister, Elinor, truly I do. She is a sweet girl, a very sweet girl, and deserves a good husband and a comfortable home. And Colonel Brandon, with his two thousand a year, and his delightful Delaford, would make her an excellent match, an excellent match indeed! Much better than that wild Willoughby, who would have spent her money, and broken her heart all over again, depend upon it!”

Elinor could only sigh, her patience wearing thin. She knew it was useless to protest further. Mrs. Jennings, in her zeal to be of service, and in her boundless good humour, was entirely incorrigible. She could only hope that Marianne, safely tucked away in her room, would remain oblivious to this fresh torrent of well-meant but utterly inappropriate conjecture.

The evening wore on, a tedious progression of forced smiles, vapid conversation, and increasingly uncomfortable allusions. Mrs. Palmer, with her customary effervescence, enquired after Marianne, expressing her regret at her absence, though her tone suggested more of a childish curiosity than genuine sympathy. “Poor Miss Marianne!” she chirped, her eyes wide with a mixture of concern and excitement. “Is she very unhappy? Mama says she is quite broken-hearted! And all because of that horrid Mr. Willoughby! I never did like him, you know, Elinor. Not one bit! He always looked so proud, and so very pleased with himself. But Mama says he has behaved abominably, and that she is well rid of him. And that Colonel Brandon will marry her instead! Is that true, Elinor? Will she marry Colonel Brandon?”

Elinor, her composure severely tested, managed a faint, strained smile. “My sister is indeed very unhappy, Mrs. Palmer, as you may well imagine. And as for the rest, I assure you, it is far too early to speculate upon such matters. She is in no state to consider anything of the kind.”

“Oh, but Mama says it is all settled!” Mrs. Palmer insisted, quite undeterred, “She says the Colonel is quite devoted to her, and will be so happy now that Mr. Willoughby is gone! And Delaford is such a pretty place, is it not? So much nicer than Barton Park. Though Mama says it is a shame about the little love-child, but that it can be put out to nurse, and then no one will ever know!”

Elinor felt a flush of indignant colour rise to her cheeks. The casual cruelty of Mrs. Palmer’s remarks, delivered with such artless innocence, was almost more unbearable than Mrs. Jennings’s more direct, though equally insensitive, pronouncements. That Marianne’s deepest sorrow, her most private grief, should be thus paraded and dissected, its every detail reduced to common gossip and mercenary speculation, filled Elinor with a sense of profound outrage. She cast a sharp, warning glance at Mrs. Jennings, who, to her credit, at last seemed to apprehend the depth of Elinor’s distress, and intervened with a hasty, though not entirely successful, attempt to divert her daughter’s attention.

“Charlotte, my love, do not chatter so!” Mrs. Jennings exclaimed, with a slightly flustered air. “Elinor is quite right; it is far too early to speak of such things. Come now, let us have a game of cards! Who is for speculation? Colonel Brandon, will you join us?”

Colonel Brandon, who had listened to this exchange with his usual quiet gravity, now raised his head, his eyes meeting Elinor’s with an expression of profound understanding and sympathy. There was no hint of reproach in his gaze, but only a deep, unspoken regret for the pain inflicted upon her and her sister. He rose from his seat, and with a slight bow, indicated his willingness to join the card-party. His silent gesture, his quiet dignity, and the genuine compassion in his eyes, provided Elinor with a small, unexpected measure of comfort in the midst of the evening’s ordeal.

As the card game commenced, Elinor found herself seated opposite Colonel Brandon. Throughout the game, which was played with more noise and laughter from Sir John and Mrs. Palmer than actual skill, Colonel Brandon occasionally addressed a few polite, unobtrusive remarks to Elinor, enquiries after her mother’s health, or observations upon the weather, all calculated to convey a quiet friendliness, and a respectful distance from the more personal and painful subjects that had dominated the earlier conversation. He seemed to understand, with a sensitivity that was rare amongst their company, Elinor’s weariness and her desire for a temporary respite from emotional turmoil.

At length, the evening began to draw to a close. Sir John and Mrs. Palmer, after another round of boisterous laughter and farewells, at last took their leave, their carriages rattling away into the night. Mr. Harris, having said scarcely a dozen words throughout the entire evening, bowed himself out with awkward haste. Only Colonel Brandon remained for a few moments, offering a final, gentle word to Elinor.

“I sincerely hope, Miss Dashwood,” he said, his voice low and earnest, “that your sister may find some repose. And that you, too, may find the strength you need in these trying times. Be assured, my good wishes are entirely with you both.”

With another slight bow, he, too, departed, leaving Elinor and Mrs. Jennings alone in the now quiet drawing-room. Elinor felt a profound sense of exhaustion settle upon her. The emotional strain of the day, the constant effort to maintain her composure, and the ceaseless battery of Mrs. Jennings’s well-intentioned but tactless remarks, had taken their toll.

“Well, my dear,” Mrs. Jennings said, stifling a yawn, “that was a very pleasant evening, was it not? A little company does one good, I always say. And Colonel Brandon was quite taken with you, Elinor, I could tell! He has such a serious air, but he has a very warm heart beneath it, depend upon it. And he is quite, quite rich, you know. It would be a very good match for someone, a very good match indeed.”

Elinor merely nodded, too weary to offer any further protest or explanation. Her only desire was to escape to the quiet sanctuary of her own room, to reflect upon the day’s events, and to gather her strength for the morrow, which she knew would bring its own fresh challenges. She bid Mrs. Jennings goodnight, offering a polite excuse about her own weariness, and at last retired, leaving her hostess to her comfortable speculations, and the quiet satisfaction of a day well spent in the service of matchmaking.

As she undressed, her thoughts inevitably returned to Marianne. She pictured her sister, lost in a troubled sleep, haunted by the spectres of past happiness and present betrayal. How long, she wondered, would it take for that sensitive heart to heal? How long before the bitter pangs of disappointment gave way to a more tranquil sorrow, and then, perhaps, to a gentle resignation? Elinor knew that her own path, too, was far from easy. To bear her own unacknowledged pain with fortitude, to suppress her own tender feelings, and to remain the steadfast support for her sister, would require a strength she sometimes feared she scarcely possessed. Yet, for Marianne’s sake, she would find it. She would be the anchor, the calm amidst the storm, the steady hand that guided her sister through the tempest of her grief. And with this quiet resolution, she at last sought her own troubled slumber.

The first rays of the morning sun, creeping cautiously through the drawing-room curtains, found Elinor Dashwood already awake, though not yet risen. Sleep had not brought the profound balm she had hoped for; rather, it had been a restless, fitful state, a mere veil drawn thinly over the anxieties that beset her. Her mind, even in slumber, had been haunted by Marianne’s piteous face, and the echoes of Mrs. Jennings’s indiscreet pronouncements. The prospect of the coming day, with its certain trials and perhaps renewed grief for her sister, weighed heavily upon her spirits. She lay for a time, tracing the patterns of light on the ceiling, endeavoring to arrange her thoughts, to gather that composure which, though often strained, was her most reliable defence against the world’s inclemencies. She considered Marianne, so utterly crushed by a blow which, to Elinor’s more regulated mind, had appeared almost inevitable in its probability, however cruel in its execution. Willoughby, with all his superficial charm, his engaging manners, and his apparent ardour, had never quite inspired in Elinor the same unreserved trust that he had so readily commanded from her sister. There had always been a certain wildness in his eye, a careless disregard for prudence in his expressions, which had whispered to Elinor of an instability beneath the polished surface. Now, that whisper had become a clamour, a loud and undeniable truth which had shattered Marianne’s world.

Her own heart, though bruised by a sorrow equally profound, and perhaps even more perplexing in its nature, found itself obliged to set aside its

heart’s natural bent, for the immediate and pressing demand of her sister’s sorrow. She had to be strong, to be practical, to be the steady compass in a storm that threatened to engulf them both. Her own disappointment, the quiet anguish of a love which had, from the very first, been fraught with such peculiar difficulties, must for the present be held in strict subjection. It was a suffering of a different cast indeed; less tumultuous, perhaps, in its outward manifestations, but none the less deeply felt for its silent endurance. Marianne’s sorrow was like a raging torrent, overflowing its banks and sweeping all before it; Elinor’s, a deep and hidden spring, whose waters, though calmly flowing, were yet profoundly stirred.

She rose at last, with the quiet dignity that marked all her movements, and began the business of dressing. The pale light of the morning did little to alleviate the gloom within the room, or within her spirit. As she fastened a simple gown, her thoughts, ever practical, turned to the immediate future. What was to be done? How were they to retrieve Marianne from this abyss of despair? And how, above all, were they to return to Barton Cottage, to the familiar comforts of home, and away from the well-meaning but often exacerbating attentions of Mrs. Jennings? The very thought of her lively hostess, with her incessant gossip and her unflagging determination to see good in every misfortune, was enough to make Elinor sigh. Yet, she knew, Mrs. Jennings’s heart was truly kind, and her intentions always benevolent, however clumsily expressed.

Having at length completed her toilet, Elinor went softly to Marianne’s door. She hesitated for a moment, steeling herself for the scene she expected to find within. A low sob, barely audible, reached her ear, confirming her fears. She entered, and found Marianne still in bed, her face buried in the pillow, her slender frame shaking with suppressed emotion. The curtains were drawn, and the room was almost dark, a perfect emblem of her sister’s clouded spirit.

“Marianne, my love,” Elinor said softly, approaching the bed. “Are you awake? It is past eight o’clock. We should endeavour to rise.”

Marianne stirred, but did not lift her head. Her voice, when it came, was muffled and thick with tears. “Leave me, Elinor. I cannot. I cannot bear the light, or the sound of voices, or the sight of any human face.”

“But you must, my dear sister,” Elinor replied, sitting gently on the edge of the bed and stroking Marianne’s hair. “To lie here thus will only prolong your suffering. Come, let me draw the curtains. The sun is shining, and a fine day promises to improve your spirits.”

With a swift, yet gentle movement, Elinor pulled back the heavy draperies, flooding the room with light. Marianne winced, pulling the bedclothes higher, but Elinor persisted. “Come, Marianne. Let us try to be brave. You cannot always live in darkness. The world continues, and we must meet it.”

“The world!” Marianne exclaimed, suddenly sitting up, her eyes, though red and swollen, flashing with an almost feverish intensity. “What is the world to me, now that he has proved false? A desolate wilderness, Elinor, a barren waste where every prospect is blasted, every hope extinguished!” She wrung her hands, her gaze fixed on some distant, unseen misery. “Oh, Elinor, how could I have been so deceived? How could I have given my whole heart, my very soul, to one so unworthy?”

Elinor listened patiently, her heart aching for her sister’s raw pain, yet knowing that such effusions, unchecked, could only deepen the wound. “You were deceived, my love, as many are, by outward appearances and persuasive words. But the fault is not yours for having loved sincerely. It is his for having feigned such affection. Let us not dwell on the past, Marianne, but look to the future, however bleak it may seem for the moment. Time, and your own strong spirit, will yet heal this, I am sure.”

“Never!” cried Marianne, throwing herself back onto the pillow. “Never, never! My heart is broken, Elinor, irrevocably broken. I shall never know happiness again. I shall never love again!”

Elinor sighed. “Do not say so, Marianne. You are young, and though this wound is deep, it is not mortal. You will recover, and you will find that joy and affection are still to be found in the world. But for now, let us try to find a little composure. Mrs. Jennings will soon be sending for us, and it will not do to present ourselves in such a state.”

The mention of Mrs. Jennings had a predictable effect. Marianne shuddered. “Oh, that woman! Her incessant chatter, her boisterous good humour, her eternal prying! How can I endure it, Elinor? How can I sit at table, and pretend to partake of food, when my very soul recoils from the thought?”

“You must try, my dear,” Elinor urged, helping her sister to sit up again. “For our sake, for mother’s sake. And for the sake of appearances, which, however little they may signify to your heart, yet signify a great deal to the world. Come, let me help you to dress. A little fresh air, a little simple sustenance, may do you more good than you imagine.”

With a heavy, resigned sigh, Marianne at last allowed herself to be persuaded. Elinor, with her customary gentleness and efficiency, helped her sister to dress, choosing the simplest of gowns, and trying to impart some semblance of order to her dishevelled hair. Marianne moved like an automaton, her eyes vacant, her lips trembling, but she offered no further resistance. When they were both ready, Elinor led her, as one would a child, down to the breakfast parlour.

As they entered, Mrs. Jennings, already seated at the table and presiding over a bountiful breakfast, looked up with a bright, if somewhat pitying, smile. “Ah, my dears, there you are! I was just about to send up for you. Come, sit down, Marianne, my poor child. You look quite peaked. Here, have some coffee, and a slice of this excellent ham. Nothing like a good breakfast to set one up for the day, you know.”

Marianne merely shook her head, sinking into a chair with a visible effort. Elinor, taking her usual place, poured herself some tea and forced a polite smile. “Good morning, Mrs. Jennings. We are a little late, I am afraid.”

“Not a bit of it, my dear, not a bit of it!” exclaimed Mrs. Jennings, bustling about with the teapot. “I quite understand. After such a shocking blow, one cannot expect to be quite oneself. Lord! when I think of it, I quite wonder how you manage to put one foot before the other, Marianne. But you must eat, my love, you must eat. Starving oneself is no remedy for a broken heart, you know. My poor husband always used to say, a good glass of port and a hearty meal would mend any ill, physical or spiritual. And he was a very wise man, though I say it myself.”

Elinor saw Marianne’s face pale further, and quickly interjected, “Mrs. Jennings is quite right, Marianne. A little something to eat will strengthen you. Try a piece of toast.”

Marianne mechanically took the toast offered by Elinor, but merely broke it into crumbs on her plate, her gaze lost in thought. Mrs. Jennings, however, was not to be deterred from her cheerful ministrations.

“Now, about Willoughby,” she began, in a lowered, though perfectly audible, voice, leaning across the table towards Elinor. “I have been thinking, my dear, it is truly a fortunate escape, when one comes to consider it. For though he is a handsome rogue, and has a way with him, there was always something a little bit… you know… about him. A certain wildness. My poor husband, Lord rest his soul, used to say, handsome is as handsome does, and a fine face often hides a hollow heart. And this Miss Grey, fifty thousand pounds, you say? Well, there is no denying that is a pretty sum. It would buy a good many horses and curricles, I dare say, and set his affairs to rights without more ado. Though I own, I still think it a monstrous pity for poor Marianne.”

Elinor, dreading Marianne’s reaction, glanced quickly at her sister. Marianne, however, seemed to be in a stupor, her attention not fully roused by Mrs. Jennings’s pronouncements. “Indeed, ma’am,” Elinor replied, trying to steer the conversation away from Willoughby’s financial motivations, “it is a sad affair for my sister. But I trust she will soon rally her spirits.”

“Oh, she will, she will!” cried Mrs. Jennings, with robust optimism. “Youth is very resilient, you know. And when there is Colonel Brandon to step in! Ah, now, there’s a man for you! Steady, sensible, and with a comfortable fortune, not to mention Delaford, a most delightful place, as I told you yesterday. And only a small encumbrance, which, as I said, can easily be managed. Depend upon it, Elinor, my dear, it will all turn out for the best. Providence moves in mysterious ways, and often a disappointment is but a blessing in disguise. I am sure Marianne will come to see it in time.”

Elinor could only offer a noncommittal murmur. The idea of immediately replacing Willoughby with Colonel Brandon, however kind and estimable the latter might be, was repugnant to her sense of propriety and to her understanding of Marianne’s feelings. She knew that such a suggestion, if truly heard, would only cause her sister fresh pain and indignation.

The morning passed slowly. Elinor attempted to engage Marianne in some gentle occupation, some reading or needlework, but her sister was incapable of sustained attention. She wandered from room to room, sometimes pausing at a window, sometimes sinking into a chair, her eyes vacant, her hands idle. Elinor watched her with a heavy heart, her own plans for the day set aside for the constant vigilance her sister required. Mrs. Jennings, meanwhile, continued her cheerful round of domestic duties and social speculations. She received a caller, a Mrs. Palmer, her daughter, who, with her usual effervescence, added to the clamour and further tested Marianne’s already strained nerves.

“Oh, Mrs. Jennings, what a shocking thing this is about Miss Marianne and Mr. Willoughby!” exclaimed Mrs. Palmer, as soon as she had seated herself, quite out of breath from her morning’s exertions and eager to share in the latest piece of news. “Sir John was quite upset when he heard it, and I am sure I quite agree. Such a handsome young man, and so very attentive! And to throw her over for a fortune, oh, it is quite despicable, is it not? Though I must say, Miss Grey is very rich, they say, and money is a great thing, to be sure. But poor Miss Marianne, she looked quite heartbroken yesterday. I saw her when she came back, and really, I scarcely knew her. So pale! And not a word did she say. I was quite distressed for her, upon my word.”

Elinor tried to signal to Mrs. Palmer with a look, a subtle shake of the head, but Mrs. Palmer, with her characteristic lack of perception, either did not notice or chose to ignore the warning. Marianne, who had been sitting by the window, a book lying neglected in her lap, suddenly rose, her face flushed with a renewed storm of emotion.

“Mrs. Palmer,” she said, her voice trembling with indignation, “I must beg of you, if you have any consideration for my feelings, not to speak of that subject. It is insupportable.”

Mrs. Palmer, startled by Marianne’s vehemence, looked genuinely bewildered. “Oh, dear me, Miss Marianne, I am so very sorry! I meant no harm, upon my honour. I was only saying how very sorry I was for you. Indeed, I was quite shocked myself. But I quite understand, to be sure. It is a very disagreeable subject. And you are quite right, one ought not to speak of disagreeable things.”

Mrs. Jennings, seeing the rising colour in Marianne’s cheeks, quickly intervened. “There, there, my dear, Mrs. Palmer meant well, I am sure. But it is best forgotten, as I always say. Come, Charlotte, tell us, have you heard anything new of the Dashwoods at Norland? Is the baby thriving?”

The conversation, thus redirected, soon flowed into other channels of local gossip, but Marianne’s brief flare of anger had drained her of what little energy she possessed. She retreated from the drawing-room, excusing herself on the plea of a headache, and Elinor, after a few more minutes of strained civility, followed her, offering a quiet apology to Mrs. Jennings for her sister’s abrupt departure.

Elinor found Marianne lying on her bed, her eyes closed, a faint tremor still running through her frame. “I cannot endure it, Elinor,” she whispered, as her sister sat beside her. “Their pity, their coarse observations, their utter lack of understanding! Do they imagine that I can find comfort in their empty words, or that my pain can be discussed like a common piece of market news? It is an outrage to every tender feeling.”

“I know, my love, I know,” Elinor soothed, stroking her forehead. “But they mean well, however clumsy their expressions. You must try to abstract yourself from their remarks, and remember that their kindness, however imperfect, is genuine.”

“Kindness!” Marianne scoffed, opening her eyes, which were now swimming with fresh tears. “What kindness is it to remind me, at every turn, of my humiliation, of his perfidy? Oh, Elinor, I wish we could leave this place! I wish we could go home, back to Barton, where I could be alone with my sorrow, far from these prying eyes and insensitive tongues.”

Elinor herself wished for nothing more than to return to the quiet sanctuary of Barton Cottage. The noise and ceaseless company of Mrs. Jennings’s London house, however well-intentioned, were indeed a trial to her own composure, and an insurmountable obstacle to Marianne’s recovery. But they could not leave immediately. Their mother was expected to arrive in London in a few days, and it would be imprudent, if not impossible, to depart before her arrival.

“We shall return home very soon, Marianne, I promise you,” Elinor said, trying to infuse her voice with a conviction she did not entirely feel. “As soon as Mama arrives, we shall make our arrangements. But for these few days, you must try to bear with it. Perhaps a quiet walk in the park later, when the air is fresh, might do you good? We can avoid the more frequented paths.”

Marianne seemed to consider this for a moment. “Perhaps,” she said, her voice softer. “Perhaps that would be tolerable. At least the trees and the sky do not speak of Willoughby.”

Elinor stayed with Marianne for a considerable time, reading aloud from a volume of poetry that she knew her sister particularly loved, choosing passages that were not too melancholy, but rather spoke of nature’s enduring beauty and resilience. Marianne listened, sometimes with a quiet sigh, sometimes with a faint stirring of interest, and Elinor felt a flicker of hope that the deepest clouds of her grief might yet be beginning to dissipate.

Later that afternoon, as they sat in the drawing-room, a servant announced the arrival of Colonel Brandon. Elinor felt a pang of apprehension, knowing that his visit, however kind, would undoubtedly reawaken Mrs. Jennings’s matchmaking zeal. Colonel Brandon entered, his grave, yet benevolent, countenance expressing a deep concern as his eyes fell upon Marianne, who, though composed by Elinor’s efforts, still bore the undeniable marks of recent suffering.

He bowed to the ladies, addressing Elinor first. “Miss Dashwood, I trust Miss Marianne is tolerably well this afternoon? I confess, I was much concerned to hear of her distress.”

Elinor returned his greeting with genuine warmth. “Thank you, Colonel Brandon. Marianne is much as you see her, still feeling the effects of her recent disappointment, but endeavouring to rally her spirits.”

Mrs. Jennings, however, could not allow such an opportunity to pass without intervention. “Tolerably well, Colonel? Why, she is quite heartbroken, poor dear! But we are doing our best to cheer her up. And I have just been telling Elinor that it will all turn out for the best, you know. Providence, and all that. And a certain gentleman, not a hundred miles from this very room, who I am sure would make a much better husband than a certain other, who shall be nameless.” She winked broadly at the Colonel, who, though he maintained his composure, gave a faint blush.

Marianne, who had been sitting quietly, looked up sharply at Mrs. Jennings’s words, her eyes flashing once more. “Mrs. Jennings, I beg of you!”

Colonel Brandon, seeing her distress, immediately intervened with a quiet dignity. “Mrs. Jennings, perhaps it would be best to speak of other matters. Miss Marianne, I sincerely hope that time and new surroundings will bring you peace. I have some books here, which I believe you might find of interest. A new edition of Cowper’s poems, and some volumes of travels in the East. They are not, perhaps, as lively as some, but I trust they may provide some gentle diversion.” He presented a small parcel of books, neatly bound, to Marianne.

Marianne, touched by his quiet kindness and the understanding that prompted his offering, took the books with a trembling hand. “Thank you, Colonel Brandon,” she murmured, her voice soft with gratitude. “You are always so thoughtful.”

Elinor felt a surge of relief and appreciation for the Colonel’s delicate handling of the situation. He had managed to soothe Marianne’s ruffled sensibilities without recourse to effusive sympathy, and had subtly rebuked Mrs. Jennings’s tactlessness.

The conversation then turned to more general topics, to the weather, to the latest political news, and to the theatre, in which Colonel Brandon showed a moderate but informed interest. Marianne remained largely silent, occasionally glancing at the books in her lap, but the immediate tension had dissipated. Elinor observed the Colonel with renewed respect. His quiet, unassuming nature, his consistent goodness, and his deep, though often unspoken, capacity for feeling, presented a stark contrast to the superficial charm and volatile affections of Willoughby. She could not, for a moment, entertain the thought of him as a suitor for Marianne, knowing her sister’s romantic inclinations, but she could not help but admire his character profoundly.

As the afternoon drew to a close, Colonel Brandon took his leave, expressing his hope that the ladies would soon find themselves in better spirits. Mrs. Jennings, however, could not contain her excitement once he had departed.

“There, Elinor, did I not tell you? A perfect gentleman! And so considerate! Bringing books for poor Marianne. Lord! he is quite smitten, I am sure of it. Did you see how he looked at her? And that blush, when I hinted at a certain thing, oh, he could not hide it! Mark my words, my dear, it will not be long now. This Willoughby affair is but a hiccup in the road, a mere detour. Marianne will be Lady Brandon before Midsummer, I warrant you. And Delaford! Such a place! And two thousand a year! Why, it is enough to make any girl forget a rogue like Willoughby, upon my word it is.”

Elinor could only shake her head, a weary smile touching her lips. “Indeed, Mrs. Jennings, he is a most estimable man. But Marianne’s heart is not yet ready to consider such matters, I assure you.”

“Nonsense!” cried Mrs. Jennings, with a cheerful wave of her hand. “Hearts mend, my dear, hearts mend! And a good house, a good income, and a good husband are wonderful poultices for a wounded spirit. You will see. I shall write to him tomorrow, and tell him to be sure to call again very soon. And perhaps an evening at the theatre? That might distract Marianne. A little gaiety, a little company. We cannot have her moping forever, can we?”

Elinor dreaded the prospect of the theatre, knowing Marianne’s present aversion to public amusements, but she chose not to argue the point, knowing that Mrs. Jennings’s enthusiasms were not easily checked. She merely hoped that her mother’s arrival would provide a natural and timely escape from Mrs. Jennings’s well-meaning but often overwhelming machinations.

The next morning brought a letter from their mother. It was written in her usual affectionate style, expressing her deep concern for Marianne, and her eagerness to be with her daughters. She announced her intention to arrive in London the following day, accompanied by Sir John and Mrs. Palmer, who were to join them at Mrs. Jennings’s house for a short visit before their collective return to Barton.

Elinor felt a mixture of relief and dismay. Relief that their departure was now firmly fixed, dismay at the prospect of the additional company, especially Sir John, whose boisterous good humour and lack of discretion were almost as trying as Mrs. Jennings’s own. She knew that the subject of Willoughby, however painful, would undoubtedly be revived and discussed with a fresh vigour by the newly arrived party.

She broke the news to Marianne with as much gentleness as she could muster. She spent the rest of the day preparing for her mother’s arrival, trying to anticipate every potential difficulty and to smooth the path for Marianne as much as possible. She spoke privately to Mrs. Jennings, requesting her to impress upon Sir John and Mrs. Palmer the absolute necessity of discretion concerning Willoughby, a request which Mrs. Jennings promised to honour, though Elinor had little faith in its ultimate efficacy.

The following day, the house was alive with the bustle of preparation. Elinor felt a weariness settle upon her, knowing the emotional demands that the approaching reunion would place upon her. She had little time for her own private reflections, her own suppressed sorrows, as she moved through the day, a constant mediator, a gentle guide, endeavouring to manage the household, to soothe her sister, and to anticipate the inevitable arrival of their various well-wishers and sympathizers. She felt herself stretched to her limit, her composure, usually so steadfast, beginning to fray at the edges. Yet, she persevered, driven by her deep love for Marianne, and by that strong sense of duty which had always been the guiding principle of her life. Mrs. Dashwood, though visibly distressed by Marianne’s appearance, seemed less inclined to dwell on the specific details of the disappointment, preferring instead to offer general comfort and support. Elinor managed a faint smile. “I am well enough, Mama. My only concern has been for Marianne.” She wished to speak of her own quiet endurance, her own private battle, but knew that the present moment was not the time. Her mother’s attention, quite rightly, was fixed on Marianne.

The first few days after Mrs. Dashwood’s arrival were a whirl of constrained social activity. Mrs. Jennings, delighted to have a full house, arranged various outings and entertainments, hoping to draw Marianne out of her despondency. Elinor watched her sister with a mixture of admiration and anxiety. Marianne’s resilience, however fragile, was beginning to show, but Elinor knew that the wound was still raw, and susceptible to fresh pain. She also found herself increasingly aware of her own unresolved feelings. His engagement to Lucy Steele, a fact she had known for some time, was a constant, dull throb beneath the surface of her composure. That Lucy could speak so glibly of Edward’s deep devotion, of shared domestic dreams, when Elinor herself had witnessed his profound unhappiness, his struggles with honesty and honour, made the entire scene grotesquely false. Yet, she was bound by her promise to Edward, by her own sense of propriety, and by the strictures of society, to maintain her silence, to offer no sign of the storm raging within.

“It is a noble sentiment, Miss Steele, to place affection above all worldly considerations,” Elinor responded, forcing a small, tight smile. “And I sincerely hope that your mutual attachment will indeed overcome all family opposition. Lucy, however, was impervious to such subtleties. She seemed to relish Elinor’s controlled demeanor, taking it as a sign of her own undeniable success. “Oh, it will, Miss Dashwood, it will! For Edward is quite unshakeable when his mind is made up. And he has had to be so very firm, you know, with his mother. She has been quite, quite dreadful about it all, I assure you. Threatening to disinherit him, and all manner of unpleasant things. But he always says, ‘Lucy, my love, a man’s word is his bond, and I would sooner starve than break faith with you.’ Is he not quite the soul of chivalry? And he has even shown me a letter, such a harsh letter, from his mother, detailing all her objections. But he would not yield! He told her, in his reply, that his happiness was tied to mine, and that no fortune could compensate for a broken heart. Such a dear, brave fellow!”

Elinor felt a profound dizziness, a sensation that the floor beneath her feet might give way. A letter? He had shown Lucy a letter from his mother, confirming the extent of the familial opposition? This detail, more than any other, seemed to lend a dreadful authenticity to Lucy’s claims. It painted a picture of Edward not merely resigned to his fate, but actively defending his choice, standing firm against his family for Lucy’s sake. The image she had held of him, as a man trapped by an early, ill-judged promise, began to crack and splinter. Could it be that he truly loved this woman, loved her enough to defy his mother, to sacrifice his worldly prospects for her? The idea was a cruel twist, one that struck at the very core of Elinor’s understanding of him. If he indeed felt such profound affection, such unwavering loyalty, for Miss Steele, then Elinor’s own hopes had been built upon a delusion, a misunderstanding of his true feelings. The pain of this realization was sharp and immediate, a physical ache in her chest.

She struggled for a moment to regain her breath, to compose her features, to prevent any outward manifestation of the turmoil within. “Indeed,” she murmured, the single word feeling like a great effort. “It is… it is a testament to his character, certainly, to stand by his principles in the face of such adversity. One can only hope that Mrs. Ferrars, in time, will come to recognise the strength of such a conviction.” She avoided looking directly at Lucy, instead fixing her gaze on a distant pattern in the carpet, allowing her mind a moment to regain some equilibrium. The implications of Lucy’s words were too vast, too devastating, to be fully grasped in an instant.

Lucy, however, was far from finished with her tortuous disclosures. She leaned forward slightly, her voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper, as if sharing a profound secret, though every word was clearly intended to be heard and understood by Elinor. “And you know, Miss Dashwood, he has often spoken of you, too. And of your dear sister, Miss Marianne. He said she is quite a charming creature, and he would often speak of her music. He told me, once, that he found your family so very different from his own, so much more… comfortable. He always felt so at ease in your society, he said. And he quite admires your good sense, Miss Dashwood. He said you were always so very steady, and so very discerning. He values your judgment, I assure you, above almost any other person’s. He told me that when he is in a quandary, he often thinks, ‘Now, what would Miss Dashwood advise? She has such a clear head, and such a sound understanding.’ Is that not a compliment, now?”

This was a new turn, and in some ways, almost as painful as the direct assertions of his love for Lucy. To be told that Edward valued her understanding, her judgment, her steady sense, but that his heart belonged elsewhere, was a refinement of cruelty. It underscored the truth that she was seen as a friend, a confidante perhaps, but never as the object of his deepest affections. It was a clear delineation of their places in his regard: Lucy, the beloved, for whom he would sacrifice all; Elinor, the sensible acquaintance, whose advice he might hypothetically seek. The hollowness of the compliment echoed in her ears.

“I am grateful for Mr. Ferrars’s good opinion, Miss Steele,” Elinor said, her voice now colder, sharper, than she intended, though she quickly softened it with an effort. “And I am pleased to know that he found some measure of comfort in our society, during what must have been a trying period for him.” She paused, struggling for a polite closing to this conversation, which was now becoming intolerable. “But I confess, Miss Steele, that these are delicate matters, and perhaps best discussed with the principal parties involved, rather than with others who can have no true understanding of their complexities.” It was a direct, yet still polite, attempt to close the subject, to reclaim some small measure of privacy from Lucy’s prying, triumphant gaze.

Lucy, however, was not so easily deterred. She took Elinor’s hint not as a signal for retreat, but as an opportunity for further probing, perhaps even an invitation to elicit a confession. “Oh, but Miss Dashwood, I am sure you understand perfectly well! For you are so very sensible, as Edward himself says. And you have known him so long, have you not? Longer than I, at least. And you must have observed his character, and his feelings, very closely. I daresay you even guessed our little secret before I finally decided to confide in you, did you not? For you are so very clever, and so very quick to perceive things.” Her smile widened, betraying a definite malicious pleasure. She was not merely informing Elinor; she was forcing Elinor to acknowledge, implicitly, her knowledge and her silent complicity in the whole painful affair.

Elinor felt her composure, so carefully maintained, begin to fray at the edges. The audacity, the deliberate attempt to implicate her, to draw her into Lucy’s web of self-congratulation and thinly veiled cruelty, was almost too much. She could not, she would not, be dragged down to Lucy’s level. She took a slow, deliberate breath, steadying herself.
