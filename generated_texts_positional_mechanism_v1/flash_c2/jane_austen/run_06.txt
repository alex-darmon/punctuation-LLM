The news of the Thorndykes’ arrival at Ashworth Park had, for Mrs. Martha Pendelton, quite superseded the usual gossip regarding the curate’s new hat or the deplorable state of Farmer Giles’s fences. Her niece, Eleanor Annesley, who had been residing with her for a fortnight, found herself daily subjected to a fresh flood of conjectures and observations concerning the new family. This particular morning, over an unusually weak cup of tea, the subject was renewed with even greater vigour.

“Indeed, it is quite the talk of the town, my dear Eleanor,” Mrs. Pendelton began, stirring her tea with an almost violent energy, “this arrival at Ashworth Park! Mr. Thorndyke, a bachelor of comfortable fortune, they say, and his sister, Miss Eliza; what an acquisition for the neighbourhood, to be sure! You know, it has been quite dull since the Cranstons left. Their balls, though well-intentioned, were always so very stifled, were they not? But the Thorndykes! They speak of town breeding and country wealth. Oh, I trust we shall soon find them agreeable company.”

“I am sure we shall, Aunt,” Eleanor replied, her gaze fixed upon a stubborn tea-leaf that clung to the rim of her cup. “Such an estate as Ashworth Park requires a master of some consequence, and it is always a pleasure to welcome new faces.”

“Consequence, my dear, is hardly the word! They say his fortune is quite prodigious, well above thirty thousand pounds, and entirely unencumbered. A man of such means, and yet he seems in no particular hurry to present himself to the neighbourhood. It has been a full three weeks since their chaise and four rattled through the village, and still not a card has been left. It shows, I think, a certain reserve, perhaps even a degree of indifference. Not that I blame him, of course; he doubtless has a great deal to attend to after such a move, though one might imagine the servants would see to the trifles. But his sister, Miss Eliza, I hear she is quite elegant, dressed in the very latest fashion from Bond Street. Mrs. Davison, who caught a glimpse of her through the carriage window, swore the lace on her bonnet alone must have cost a king’s ransom. Though, between you and me, Eleanor, I do believe Mrs. Davison rather exaggerates everything; her own new gown, she insisted, was ‘the height of Parisian chic,’ and it looked to me nothing more than a somewhat faded sarcenet. But, as for Miss Thorndyke, her reputation for beauty is, it seems, rather less certain than her reputation for finery.”

“I have heard no reports regarding Miss Thorndyke’s appearance, Aunt,” Eleanor offered, “only that she is esteemed quite accomplished.”

“Accomplished! Well, I should hope so, with all that money. Anyone can be accomplished with the best masters in London, can they not? I hear she plays the pianoforte with considerable flourish, and sketches rather delicately. But amiable? That is the question, my dear, is it not? For a young lady, amiability and sweetness of temper are worth more than all the Italian songs in the world. And from what Mrs. Jenkins, the Rector’s wife, let slip, it seems Miss Thorndyke is accustomed to a great deal of admiration, and perhaps a touch too much flattery. Her previous neighbourhood, it appears, was quite at her beck and call; and we all know how that can spoil a young woman, rendering her quite unable to tolerate any contrary opinion or honest criticism. Lord, but it must be a trial for her brother, if she truly is so! A handsome fortune, you see, does not always guarantee domestic harmony.”

Eleanor merely nodded, a slight frown creasing her brow. She knew from experience that Mrs. Jenkins’s ‘slips’ were often deliberate and rarely softened by charity.

“However,” Mrs. Pendelton continued, oblivious to Eleanor’s private reservations, “she would, of course, make a most suitable match for young Mr. Harrison. His estate, though not vast, is respectable, and his manners are quite unexceptionable. Perhaps if we were to arrange a small dinner, when the Thorndykes condescend to call, we might invite him; it would be a delightful surprise, would it not? Two young people, thrown together by fate and a careful seating arrangement. Lord, I love to see a match well made!”

“Mr. Harrison, Aunt, is a very agreeable young man, certainly,” Eleanor began, carefully choosing her words, “but I believe his affections are already rather engaged with Miss Sophia Finch. I saw them last week at the assembly, and their conversation seemed quite particular.”

Mrs. Pendelton waved a dismissive hand. “Nonsense, my dear! A mere childish fancy. Miss Sophia is well enough, but her connections are hardly the first in the county, and her fortune, I believe, rather scanty. No, no, Mr. Harrison is a sensible boy, he will know where his true advantage lies. Besides, a new face, a fresh opportunity, sometimes quite sets a young man’s head right. I only wish we had known of Mr. Thorndyke’s arrival sooner, or I might have invited his family to our own little gathering last Tuesday. Such a missed chance! But we shall be amongst the first to call, I promise you, and then we shall see the true measure of them all. For nothing, my dear, reveals character quite so thoroughly as a polite morning visit.”

Eleanor, knowing further argument would be fruitless, merely offered a quiet, “Indeed, Aunt.” She reflected, not for the first time, on the curious human inclination to judge and arrange, even before the individuals concerned had exchanged a single word. A quiet morning, she thought, might be a most welcome diversion, if only it were to be had.

“A quiet morning, she thought, might be a most welcome diversion, if only it were to be had.”

“A quiet morning, indeed, Eleanor,” Mrs. Pendelton pronounced, her voice cutting through the very notion of repose, “is precisely what we shall not have. For we must make haste to pay our respects to these new neighbours, lest we be thought negligent or, what is worse, entirely unfashionable. Depend upon it, the sooner we call, the greater our credit will be. One cannot afford to lag behind in these matters, for the world, my dear, is ever observant, and quick to judge. I have already dispatched a note to Mrs. Hopkins, enquiring after the most propitious hour for our visit. She, being acquainted with the Thorndykes’ footman, will no doubt have procured the necessary intelligence by noon. And I have instructed Cook to prepare some of her finest shortbread; a small token, you understand, but one that speaks of thoughtful hospitality. For though they are wealthy, and undoubtedly accustomed to every luxury, a homemade delicacy, presented with a proper card, is always appreciated, and serves to distinguish us from those who merely send a servant with a basket of purchased trifles. It is in these small particulars, Eleanor, that one’s true breeding is revealed, and I should like the Thorndykes to perceive at once that we are not of the common sort, who live without taste or discrimination. Indeed, I should not be surprised if Miss Thorndyke herself plays the pianoforte, for they say she is quite accomplished, and a little exchange of musical preferences can often pave the way for a more intimate acquaintance. I trust your voice is in good order, my dear? It would be quite charming, would it not, if you were to offer a small ballad, should the occasion present itself? Nothing too elaborate, of course, just a simple air to demonstrate your own genteel accomplishments, without appearing to compete with the young lady of the house. For that, you see, would never do. One must always be conscious of one’s place, and of the delicate balance required in polite society.”

Eleanor suppressed a sigh, and merely replied, “My voice, Aunt, is as it usually is, and I shall endeavour to be as agreeable as possible, should the opportunity arise for musical entertainment. Though I confess, I had not anticipated such an expectation for a first call.”

“Anticipated!” exclaimed Mrs. Pendelton, tapping her fan against her wrist. “My dear girl, one must always anticipate every possible contingency when embarking upon a social enterprise of this magnitude. This is not merely a visit, you understand, but an establishment of relations, a subtle declaration of our standing in the county. And besides, it is an excellent opportunity to observe the young lady firsthand. One hears so much, but seeing, as they say, is believing. And I have a particular interest, as you know, in seeing her properly settled, and in determining whether she possesses the qualities necessary to make a suitable companion, and indeed, a future mistress of a respectable establishment. For a great fortune can oft obscure a multitude of deficiencies, and it is the part of those with discerning eyes to look beyond the glittering surface. Though, of course, I have no doubt she is everything one could wish for, and quite amiable besides. For what young lady, possessing fifty thousand pounds, would dare to be otherwise?”

The appointed hour, communicated with much ceremony by Mrs. Hopkins’s messenger, was set for a little past three o’clock. Mrs. Pendelton, dressed in her finest silk gown and a bonnet adorned with fresh ribbons, oversaw Eleanor’s toilette with an eagle eye, ensuring every detail, from the placement of a lace handkerchief to the selection of the most appropriate gloves, was to her satisfaction. “One cannot be too careful,” she reiterated, surveying Eleanor as if she were a prized exhibit. “First impressions, my dear, are lasting, and we must present a united front of elegance and good breeding. Though your simple muslin, with its modest trimmings, is perfectly suitable, and indeed, quite becoming; it speaks of an unpretentious grace, which can be very appealing to those weary of ostentation. And your quiet demeanor, Eleanor, though sometimes a trifle too reserved for my taste, will on this occasion be quite advantageous. For Miss Thorndyke, being so much admired and accustomed to flattery, will perhaps find your gentle presence a refreshing contrast. A little competition, even if unspoken, is never desirable amongst ladies.”

Their carriage, a rather ancient but well-maintained phaeton, made its stately way along the winding country lanes to Thorndyke Hall. It was a substantial residence, Eleanor observed, a red-brick edifice of Georgian design, standing proudly amidst extensive grounds. The approach was grand, a long gravel drive bordered by venerable oaks, leading to a sweeping forecourt. Servants, in liveries of dark blue, moved with an air of quiet efficiency, indicating a household of considerable order and means.

They were ushered into a drawing-room of impressive proportions, richly furnished with velvet and polished mahogany, the walls adorned with large, ornate paintings. A fire crackled cheerfully in a marble fireplace, casting a warm glow upon the heavy draperies. Seated near the fire, engaged in conversation, were an elderly gentleman, whom Eleanor presumed to be Mr. Thorndyke, and a young lady of perhaps one-and-twenty, who must be his daughter.

Mr. Thorndyke was a man of dignified appearance, with silver hair and a rather formal, though not unkind, countenance. His daughter, Miss Thorndyke, was indeed a striking figure. She possessed a profusion of dark, glossy curls, artfully arranged, and a pair of remarkably bright, intelligent eyes. Her gown, of a fashionable deep crimson, was cut with an elegance that bespoke a London dressmaker, and its rich fabric shimmered in the firelight. As they rose to greet their visitors, Eleanor noted a certain self-possession in her bearing, a confidence that bordered, perhaps, on hauteur.

“Mrs. Pendelton, I believe, and Miss Eleanor Dashwood,” announced the butler.

Mr. Thorndyke advanced with a polite bow. “Welcome, ladies, welcome. We are quite delighted to receive you. It is a pleasure to make the acquaintance of our neighbours.” His voice was deep and resonant, his manner impeccably courteous.

Mrs. Pendelton, in her most gracious tone, launched into a cascade of apologies for their delay in calling, assurances of their delight at the Thorndykes’ arrival, and praises for the beauty of Thorndyke Hall and its surroundings. “Indeed, Mr. Thorndyke, the prospect from your windows is quite enchanting, and the improvements you have made are truly admirable. One can see at once that you possess the finest taste.”

Miss Thorndyke, meanwhile, offered Eleanor a cool, appraising glance, a slight, almost imperceptible lift of her eyebrow, before offering a polite but somewhat distant smile. “It is a pleasure to meet you, Miss Dashwood,” she said, her voice clear and well-modulated, though lacking the warmth Eleanor had come to associate with truly amiable women.

The conversation, initially steered by Mrs. Pendelton’s effusive pronouncements, soon settled into a more general exchange. Mr. Thorndyke spoke of his plans for the estate, the quality of the local hunting, and his desire to establish himself comfortably in the neighbourhood. Miss Thorndyke, when addressed, answered with an air of perfect composure, her responses invariably intelligent and articulate, though she rarely initiated a topic herself, preferring to listen with an air of polite reserve.

Eleanor, following her aunt’s earlier instructions, maintained a quiet demeanour, observing her hostess with keen interest. Miss Thorndyke’s beauty was undeniable, a classical regularity of features complemented by a fashionable elegance. Yet, Eleanor perceived an underlying coolness, a certain detachment, which contrasted sharply with the easy vivacity often found in young ladies of her age. When Mrs. Pendelton delicately enquired after her musical accomplishments, Miss Thorndyke replied, with a faint smile, “Oh, I play a little, madam, as most young ladies do. But I confess my true passion lies in the cultivation of my garden, and in the study of botany.”

This unexpected revelation seemed to momentarily discomfit Mrs. Pendelton, who had clearly anticipated a more conventional display of feminine pursuits. “Botany!” she exclaimed, recovering herself with an effort. “How very admirable, Miss Thorndyke. Such a delightful and intellectual pursuit. One so rarely encounters such dedication to the sciences amongst young ladies. Though, of course, the proper ordering of a garden is itself a most artistic endeavour.”

Mr. Thorndyke beamed with paternal pride. “Indeed, Mrs. Pendelton, my daughter possesses a most inquiring mind. She has quite transformed a portion of the conservatory with her exotic specimens. Her passion for the natural world is quite remarkable.”

Eleanor, whose own interests, though less exotic, inclined towards the quiet observation of nature, found this a more promising subject. “Do you find, Miss Thorndyke,” she ventured, “that the soil here is conducive to such varied cultivation? I have often thought our local earth to be rather heavy for certain delicate species.”

Miss Thorndyke turned her gaze upon Eleanor, and for the first time, a flicker of genuine interest seemed to animate her features. “Ah, Miss Dashwood, you have precisely identified the challenge. The clay content can be quite troublesome. However, with careful amendment and judicious selection, one can overcome many obstacles. I have brought with me a collection of rare bulbs from the Continent, which I am eager to test in this new environment. Perhaps, if you have an interest, you might one day care to observe my progress?”

Eleanor, genuinely pleased, replied, “I should be delighted, Miss Thorndyke. I confess my own knowledge of botany is rudimentary, but I have a great admiration for those who possess such skill and dedication.”

A brief, comfortable silence followed this exchange, a moment of shared understanding that momentarily dissolved the stiffness of their initial acquaintance. Mrs. Pendelton, however, ever watchful for any deviation from her carefully constructed social script, soon interjected. “Such a delightful coincidence, Miss Thorndyke, that you should mention the Continent. My dear Eleanor, too, has travelled, though not, alas, to such an extent as yourself. She spent a most agreeable season in Bath, and her cousin, Lady Middleton, resides not far from London, which provides her with occasional opportunities for fashionable society.”

Eleanor felt a familiar flush of embarrassment at her aunt’s clumsy attempts to elevate her standing. Miss Thorndyke, however, merely nodded politely, her expression once again resuming its customary reserve. The conversation drifted back to more general topics; the local gentry, the quality of the roads, and the anticipated events of the coming season. Mrs. Pendelton, with a keen eye on the clock, eventually made the signal for their departure, but not before issuing a warm invitation for the Thorndykes to dine with them the following week.

“We should be honoured, Mr. Thorndyke, if you and your charming daughter would condescend to join us for dinner next Tuesday. It would be a small party, merely our own family and perhaps one or two other intimate friends. Nothing too formal, you understand, merely an opportunity for us to become better acquainted.”

Mr. Thorndyke, after a quick glance at his daughter, accepted with appropriate courtesy. “We should be delighted, Mrs. Pendelton. My daughter and I are eager to cultivate agreeable society in our new abode.”

As they drove away, Mrs. Pendelton’s tongue was loosed, and a torrent of observations and pronouncements poured forth. “Well, Eleanor, what did you think? She is certainly handsome, in a severe sort of way, is she not? And her gown, quite the very latest fashion, I collect. But a trifle cold, perhaps? Not quite the amiable creature one might have hoped for, though her interest in botany is certainly peculiar, and one might even say, somewhat unfeminine. Still, an inquiring mind is never to be despised, and perhaps it merely signifies a desire for intellectual improvement. And her fortune, my dear, will certainly compensate for any slight coolness of manner. For as I always say, money warms the heart, if not immediately, then certainly in time. And Mr. Thorndyke, he is a most agreeable gentleman, quite the picture of an old-fashioned country squire, but with the polish of London. He will be a valuable addition to our social circle. But what did you think of *her*, Eleanor? Did you discern any particular quality that might recommend her to a gentleman of discernment? I confess, I found her quite proper, but perhaps a little too much so.”

Eleanor chose her words carefully. “She is certainly beautiful, Aunt, and possessed of considerable intelligence. Her interest in botany, I found, was rather engaging, and her conversation, when it turned to that subject, became quite animated. I should not describe her as amiable, perhaps, in the way one might describe a Miss Sophia Finch, but she is certainly a lady of discernment and cultivation.”

Mrs. Pendelton waved her hand dismissively. “Sophia Finch! Now, Eleanor, do not begin with that again. Miss Finch is a sweet enough girl, I grant you, but quite without fortune or connections. And Mr. Harrison, as I have told you, is a sensible young man who will know where his true advantage lies. Miss Thorndyke’s fifty thousand pounds, combined with her fine connections, for her mother was, I believe, a cousin to Lord Ashworth, will quite outweigh any trifling fondness he may entertain for a girl like Sophia. Indeed, I have already decided that Mr. Harrison shall be among our intimate friends next Tuesday. It will be a most delightful surprise for them both, for I shall arrange their seating at the table with the utmost care, placing them in close proximity, so that conversation may flow freely, and perhaps, a spark of reciprocal admiration may be kindled. For a well-orchestrated dinner party, my dear, can often do more for a young couple than months of casual acquaintance. And besides, if Miss Thorndyke is, as you suggest, somewhat reserved, a lively young man like Mr. Harrison, with his excellent spirits and engaging conversation, might well be the very tonic she requires. For even the most botanical of young ladies, Eleanor, can appreciate a gentleman’s attentions, especially when those attentions are backed by the approval of society and the promise of a comfortable establishment. And I have every confidence that Mr. Harrison, once apprised of her true worth, will not hesitate to pursue such a felicitous alliance.”

Eleanor sighed inwardly. The path of matchmaking, once embarked upon by her aunt, was rarely deviated from, no matter how clear the obstacles or how fervent the objections. She foresaw an evening of carefully arranged glances, forced conversations, and Mrs. Pendelton’s not-so-subtle machinations. Her heart, however, felt a pang of sympathy for Miss Sophia Finch, whose quiet affection for Mr. Harrison would likely be trampled underfoot by the grand design of her aunt’s ambition. And for Mr. Harrison himself, who, though a young man of good sense and agreeable disposition, might find himself inadvertently caught in a snare of expectations from which escape would be difficult.

The following week, the Pendelton household was abuzz with preparations for the dinner party. Mrs. Pendelton, in a frenzy of activity, personally supervised every detail, from the selection of the menu to the polishing of the silver. “One cannot be too careful, Eleanor,” she declared, for the tenth time, “when entertaining such guests. The Thorndykes, you understand, are accustomed to the very best, and we must not fall short of their expectations. Cook has surpassed herself with the roasted fowl and the almond tart, and I have instructed John to procure the finest claret from Mr. Dobson’s cellar. Everything must be perfect, absolutely perfect.”

Eleanor, though she assisted diligently, found herself increasingly apprehensive. She had managed, with some difficulty, to persuade her aunt against insisting on a musical performance from herself, arguing that it might appear overly solicitous and detract from Miss Thorndyke’s own accomplishments. This small victory, however, was overshadowed by the looming presence of Mr. Harrison, whom Mrs. Pendelton had indeed invited, despite Eleanor’s gentle remonstrances regarding Miss Finch.

“Nonsense, Eleanor,” her aunt had retorted, “a young man must be allowed to weigh his options. And besides, Miss Finch’s family are not invited, so there will be no awkwardness. Mr. Harrison, I assure you, is quite sensible enough to see which side his bread is buttered, and Miss Thorndyke represents a very generously buttered loaf indeed.”

On the evening of the dinner, the drawing-room glittered with candlelight, and a cheerful fire blazed, dispelling the chill of the autumn evening. Mrs. Pendelton, radiant in a gown of shimmering grey, greeted their guests with a flurry of welcomes. Mr. Thorndyke arrived with his daughter, Miss Thorndyke, who, though elegant in a gown of dark blue, still retained that cool composure Eleanor had observed during their first visit. She acknowledged Eleanor with a polite nod, and then, her gaze sweeping across the room, settled briefly upon the figure of Mr. Harrison, who stood conversing amiably with a distant cousin of Mrs. Pendelton’s.

Mr. Harrison, a handsome young man with a frank, open countenance and a ready smile, was indeed an engaging presence. He possessed a vivacity that seemed to animate any company he joined, and his conversation, though not profound, was always pleasant and often amusing. He approached Eleanor with genuine warmth. “Miss Dashwood, a pleasure, as always. Your aunt has assembled a most agreeable company this evening.” His eyes, however, held a trace of concern, and Eleanor suspected he was already aware of Mrs. Pendelton’s intentions.

The dinner itself proceeded with the usual blend of polite conversation, shared anecdotes, and the occasional burst of laughter from Mr. Harrison, who exerted himself to be agreeable to everyone. Mrs. Pendelton, with a triumphant air, had indeed seated Miss Thorndyke and Mr. Harrison side by side, ensuring that their elbows almost touched, and that their conversation was necessarily intertwined. Eleanor, seated opposite, observed the pair with a mixture of curiosity and dismay.

Miss Thorndyke maintained her composed demeanour, responding to Mr. Harrison’s overtures with courteous but somewhat formal replies. He, for his part, attempted various topics, from the merits of the local hunting to the latest London fashions, but found little enthusiastic response. When he mentioned a recent assembly, and the charm of Miss Sophia Finch’s dancing, Miss Thorndyke merely offered a slight, knowing smile, but said nothing.

It was when Mr. Harrison, in a moment of perhaps desperate inspiration, ventured to ask about her botanical pursuits, that a change occurred. “Miss Thorndyke,” he began, “I understand you possess a remarkable collection of exotic plants. My sister, who has a small conservatory of her own, would be quite fascinated to hear of your methods.”

Miss Thorndyke’s eyes brightened, and a faint flush touched her cheeks. “Indeed, Mr. Harrison? It is a subject I find endlessly absorbing. The challenge of persuading a delicate species to thrive in a foreign clime, the subtle adjustments of soil and light, the delicate balance of moisture and air, these are matters of profound interest. I have recently succeeded in propagating a rare orchid from the West Indies, a specimen renowned for its peculiar fragrance and the remarkable resilience of its roots.”

Mr. Harrison, though clearly not possessing a deep understanding of horticulture, listened with commendable patience and even managed a few intelligent questions. Eleanor observed that Miss Thorndyke, when speaking of her passion, lost some of her aloofness. Her gestures became more animated, her voice acquired a softer inflection, and her eyes, usually so composed, sparkled with genuine enthusiasm. Mrs. Pendelton, witnessing this unexpected animation, exchanged a knowing glance with Eleanor, a silent message that seemed to say, “Ah, you see! She is not so cold after all! And Mr. Harrison, he plays his part admirably.”

The evening progressed, and though Miss Thorndyke did not, as Mrs. Pendelton had hoped, burst forth into a torrent of girlish gaiety, she certainly appeared more engaged than Eleanor had initially anticipated. Mr. Harrison, for his part, was unfailingly attentive, and while Eleanor suspected his attentions were largely a matter of politeness and a wish to avoid offending his hostess, he played his role well enough to satisfy Mrs. Pendelton’s expectations.

After the ladies had retired to the drawing-room, leaving the gentlemen to their port, Mrs. Pendelton cornered Eleanor. “Well, my dear! Did you not observe the transformation? When she spoke of her plants, she was quite a different creature, was she not? A little animation, a little warmth. And Mr. Harrison, he handled her admirably. Such a charming young man! He has a way of drawing people out, which is a most valuable quality. I have every confidence that this will progress precisely as I had hoped. Indeed, I overheard Mr. Thorndyke praising his vivacity to our cousin, Mr. Davies, and remarking upon his pleasant countenance. And you know, Eleanor, a father’s approval, especially a wealthy father’s approval, is half the battle won.”

Eleanor merely offered a noncommittal smile. She had indeed observed the change in Miss Thorndyke, but she had also noted the distinct lack of any romantic spark. Miss Thorndyke’s enthusiasm was for her plants, not for Mr. Harrison. And Mr. Harrison’s politeness, though commendable, did not signify affection. She harboured a suspicion that he was, in fact, rather relieved when the conversation moved away from horticulture.

When the gentlemen eventually rejoined them, Mr. Harrison made a point of seeking out Eleanor. “Miss Dashwood,” he said, his voice lowered slightly, “I found Miss Thorndyke’s devotion to her botanical pursuits quite remarkable. Such single-mindedness is truly something to behold.” There was a subtle note of irony in his tone that did not escape Eleanor’s notice. “Indeed,” she replied, “she is clearly very passionate about her garden.” He smiled, a genuine, rather boyish smile, and then, lowering his voice further, added, “I confess, Miss Dashwood, I found myself longing for a simple daisy, or perhaps a buttercup, after such a prolonged disquisition on rare orchids. One appreciates complexity, of course, but sometimes, a little simplicity is a most welcome relief.”

Eleanor could not help but return his smile. “I quite understand, Mr. Harrison. Sometimes, the most beautiful things are those that do not require an entire conservatory for their cultivation.”

Their shared amusement was brief, for Mrs. Pendelton, ever vigilant, soon bustled over, her eyes sparkling with proprietary satisfaction. “Mr. Harrison, my dear boy, I trust you found Miss Thorndyke’s conversation engaging? She is a most intelligent young lady, is she not? And so very dedicated to her studies. A true model of female accomplishment.”

Mr. Harrison, straightening his posture, adopted a more formal tone. “Indeed, Mrs. Pendelton. Miss Thorndyke’s knowledge of botany is truly impressive. I confess, I learned a great deal this evening.”

Mrs. Pendelton beamed. “Excellent, excellent! For a young lady of her fortune, such intellectual pursuits are quite the crowning glory. It shows a depth of character, a mind not given over to mere frivolity. And I have no doubt, Mr. Harrison, that you, with your own excellent sense and agreeable disposition, will find much to admire in her. Indeed, I have already been contemplating a small expedition to Thorndyke Hall, perhaps next week, to admire her conservatory. Miss Thorndyke did mention the possibility to Eleanor, did she not, my dear?” She turned to Eleanor, expecting confirmation.

Eleanor, caught off guard, could only say, “Miss Thorndyke did mention that one might care to observe her progress, Aunt.”

“Precisely!” cried Mrs. Pendelton. “And what a perfect opportunity for you all to become better acquainted in a less formal setting. A little stroll through the conservatory, perhaps followed by tea, would be quite delightful. I shall arrange it with Mr. Thorndyke before they depart. For one must seize these opportunities, Eleanor, when they present themselves. They do not come every day, and a little push, you know, can often be all that is required to set matters on their proper course.”

Mr. Harrison, though maintaining his polite smile, directed a quick, almost imperceptible glance of resignation towards Eleanor. Eleanor, reading his expression with perfect clarity, felt a fresh wave of sympathy. She knew that her aunt’s “little push” was likely to become a rather forceful shove, and that the quiet affections of Mr. Harrison for Miss Sophia Finch would face an increasingly formidable opposition. The prospect of a quiet morning, it seemed, was as distant as ever, replaced by a series of meticulously planned social manoeuvres, all designed to bring about an alliance that Eleanor suspected, with a sinking heart, would bring little happiness to any of the parties involved. She reflected, not for the first time, on the curious paradox of human endeavour; how, in the pursuit of what was deemed advantageous, the simplest truths and affections were often sacrificed, leaving only a trail of polite discomfort and unspoken regrets. For what was the purpose of wealth and standing, she wondered, if it could not secure the freedom to choose one’s own path, or the quiet contentment that sprang from genuine attachment? And as she observed her aunt, flushed with the triumph of her perceived success, Eleanor could not help but think that, in the intricate dance of society, sometimes the most well-intentioned steps led only to a tangled web of unhappiness. The evening, though declared a success by Mrs. Pendelton, concluded with a lingering sense of disquiet in Eleanor’s heart, a silent premonition of complications yet to unfold, of affections misplaced, and of expectations that would, in all likelihood, remain unfulfilled. The Thorndykes departed with promises of their visit the following week, and Mr. Harrison, with a final, almost imperceptible shake of his head towards Eleanor, took his leave, leaving Mrs. Pendelton to revel in the glow of her perceived triumph, and Eleanor to contemplate the delicate, often cruel, machinations of the social world.“A quiet morning, she thought, might be a most welcome diversion, if only it were to be had.”

“A quiet morning, indeed, Eleanor,” Mrs. Pendelton pronounced, her voice cutting through the very notion of repose, “is precisely what we shall not have. For we must make haste to pay our respects to these new neighbours, lest we be thought negligent or, what is worse, entirely unfashionable. Depend upon it, the sooner we call, the greater our credit will be. One cannot afford to lag behind in these matters, for the world, my dear, is ever observant, and quick to judge. I have already dispatched a note to Mrs. Hopkins, enquiring after the most propitious hour for our visit. She, being acquainted with the Thorndykes’ footman, will no doubt have procured the necessary intelligence by noon. And I have instructed Cook to prepare some of her finest shortbread; a small token, you understand, but one that speaks of thoughtful hospitality. For though they are wealthy, and undoubtedly accustomed to every luxury, a homemade delicacy, presented with a proper card, is always appreciated, and serves to distinguish us from those who merely send a servant with a basket of purchased trifles. It is in these small particulars, Eleanor, that one’s true breeding is revealed, and I should like the Thorndykes to perceive at once that we are not of the common sort, who live without taste or discrimination. Indeed, I should not be surprised if Miss Thorndyke herself plays the pianoforte, for they say she is quite accomplished, and a little exchange of musical preferences can often pave the way for a more intimate acquaintance. I trust your voice is in good order, my dear? It would be quite charming, would it not, if you were to offer a small ballad, should the occasion present itself? Nothing too elaborate, of course, just a simple air to demonstrate your own genteel accomplishments, without appearing to compete with the young lady of the house. For that, you see, would never do. One must always be conscious of one’s place, and of the delicate balance required in polite society.”

Eleanor suppressed a sigh, and merely replied, “My voice, Aunt, is as it usually is, and I shall endeavour to be as agreeable as possible, should the opportunity arise for musical entertainment. Though I confess, I had not anticipated such an expectation for a first call.”

“Anticipated!” exclaimed Mrs. Pendelton, tapping her fan against her wrist. “My dear girl, one must always anticipate every possible contingency when embarking upon a social enterprise of this magnitude. This is not merely a visit, you understand, but an establishment of relations, a subtle declaration of our standing in the county. And besides, it is an excellent opportunity to observe the young lady firsthand. One hears so much, but seeing, as they say, is believing. And I have a particular interest, as you know, in seeing her properly settled, and in determining whether she possesses the qualities necessary to make a suitable companion, and indeed, a future mistress of a respectable establishment. For a great fortune can oft obscure a multitude of deficiencies, and it is the part of those with discerning eyes to look beyond the glittering surface. Though, of course, I have no doubt she is everything one could wish for, and quite amiable besides. For what young lady, possessing fifty thousand pounds, would dare to be otherwise?”

The appointed hour, communicated with much ceremony by Mrs. Hopkins’s messenger, was set for a little past three o’clock. Mrs. Pendelton, dressed in her finest silk gown and a bonnet adorned with fresh ribbons, oversaw Eleanor’s toilette with an eagle eye, ensuring every detail, from the placement of a lace handkerchief to the selection of the most appropriate gloves, was to her satisfaction. “One cannot be too careful,” she reiterated, surveying Eleanor as if she were a prized exhibit. “First impressions, my dear, are lasting, and we must present a united front of elegance and good breeding. Though your simple muslin, with its modest trimmings, is perfectly suitable, and indeed, quite becoming; it speaks of an unpretentious grace, which can be very appealing to those weary of ostentation. And your quiet demeanor, Eleanor, though sometimes a trifle too reserved for my taste, will on this occasion be quite advantageous. For Miss Thorndyke, being so much admired and accustomed to flattery, will perhaps find your gentle presence a refreshing contrast. A little competition, even if unspoken, is never desirable amongst ladies.”

The carriage, hired for the occasion and polished to a gleam that reflected the autumn sunshine with admirable fidelity, drew up to Mrs. Pendelton’s door precisely at a quarter past two. Eleanor, having submitted to her aunt’s final adjustments to her bonnet strings and a stern lecture on the perils of a too-casual demeanour, descended the shallow steps with a sense of resigned formality. Her aunt, rustling in silk and exuding an air of triumphant expectation, followed, her hand resting momentarily on Eleanor’s arm as if to impart a final, silent instruction. The short drive was filled with a medley of Mrs. Pendelton’s anxieties and predictions. “Remember, Eleanor, an erect posture, and a smile that is amiable without being too familiar. One must always maintain a certain reserve in initial introductions. And do listen attentively when Miss Thorndyke speaks; nothing is more flattering than a genuinely engaged listener, provided, of course, that your engagement does not lead you to interrupt her. And should she touch upon any topic requiring a modest display of your own intellect, do not be afraid to offer a gentle observation; but let it be brief, my dear, and deferential. We are not there to impress with a parade of learning, but to establish a connection based on mutual respect and, dare I say, a congenial compatibility. Though as to the latter, one can never be entirely certain until a more prolonged acquaintance has been formed.”

Eleanor assented to these injunctions with a quiet dignity, her gaze fixed upon the passing hedgerows. Her mind, however,

was not so easily confined as her person within the polished confines of the carriage, but ranged freely over the circumstances of her present undertaking, weighing the probable against the possible, and endeavouring to prepare itself for an interview which, though merely a formal call of civility, held in the eyes of Mrs. Pendelton a significance far exceeding its stated purpose. Eleanor felt a quiet apprehension, not so much for herself, for she had long since learned the art of polite dissimulation and could present a composed countenance even when her thoughts were in utter disarray, but rather for the delicate balance of her aunt’s expectations, which seemed to rest upon her slender shoulders with an almost crushing weight. She was, as ever, the vessel of Mrs. Pendelton’s social aspirations, the quiet instrument by which her aunt sought to navigate the intricate currents of provincial gentility.

Miss Thorndyke, of Thorndyke Hall, was a figure of no small consequence in their part of the county, a lady of independent fortune and formidable reputation, whose acquaintance was assiduously sought by many, and sparingly granted by herself. Her wealth, considerable enough to render her movements quite independent of common opinion, was matched by an intellect which, though perhaps not profound, was certainly sharp, and a manner which, if not always genial, was invariably assured. To gain an invitation to Thorndyke Hall was deemed an honour, to be received by Miss Thorndyke herself a mark of distinction, and to be admitted to her more intimate circle, a triumph worthy of considerable boast. It was this triumph, or at least its preliminary skirmish, that Mrs. Pendelton so eagerly anticipated.

Eleanor, contemplating these matters, found herself reflecting on the curious disparity between her aunt’s fervent hopes and her own more modest inclinations. For her, the prospect of an afternoon spent in the company of a celebrated wit and arbiter of taste offered little beyond the necessity of maintaining an agreeable demeanour, an exercise of polite endurance rather than genuine pleasure. She valued quietude and sincerity above all else, preferring the unpretentious comfort of a solitary walk in the fields to the most brilliant of social assemblies. Yet, she understood Mrs. Pendelton’s motives, for her aunt, with all her foibles and anxieties, was fundamentally kind, and her efforts, however misdirected, were always intended for Eleanor’s advancement, or at least, for her establishment in a world that prized connection and consequence above mere contentment.

The carriage, having traversed the dusty lanes and passed through the wrought-iron gates, now proceeded along a winding drive, bordered by ancient oaks and neatly trimmed shrubberies, which spoke of long-established wealth and diligent stewardship. The house itself soon came

into view, a substantial edifice of mellowed stone, whose ancient proportions spoke of generations of undisturbed prosperity. It was not a modern structure, nor did it boast the fantastical Gothic flourishes occasionally admired in more whimsical tastes, but rather stood firm in the respectable dignity of Queen Anne architecture, with numerous well-proportioned windows gazing out from beneath a broad, sheltering roof. Climbers, carefully trained, softened its edges, and a broad gravel sweep, impeccably raked, led to a heavy oak door, flanked by ancient urns filled with evergreens. One felt, upon approaching such a residence, that the very air was imbued with a sense of order and permanence, a fitting abode for a lady of Miss Thorndyke’s acknowledged consequence.

As the carriage drew to a halt, a liveried servant appeared with commendable promptness, his demeanour a perfect balance of deference and efficiency. He opened the door for Mrs. Pendelton with a bow that, though slight, was perfectly executed, and then extended the same courtesy to Eleanor, whose more active descent required rather less assistance. Mrs. Pendelton, ever solicitous of her attire, adjusted her bonnet and smoothed the folds of her silk gown, casting a quick, appraising glance at the façade of the house, as if seeking confirmation of its legendary grandeur. Eleanor, meanwhile, observed the quiet precision of the scene, the well-tended gardens, the discreetly placed statuary, and the overall impression of a domestic establishment run with an almost military exactitude. There was, she thought, a certain cold perfection to it all, lacking the warmer, more haphazard charm of Barton Cottage, yet undeniably impressive in its own right.

A large marble bust, severe in its Roman dignity, stood upon a pedestal in one corner, observing the comings and goings with an unchanging, lofty gaze. The floor, of polished oak, reflected the subdued light filtering through a tall, narrow window of stained glass, depicting some ancient hero in battle, his noble countenance now softened by centuries of dust. Eleanor, though accustomed to the more modest elegance of her own home, could not but feel the oppressive weight of such opulence, a demonstration of wealth so assured of its own superiority that it disdained all efforts at frivolous adornment. Every object seemed to declare its ancient lineage and undisputed value, from the heavy Persian rug that covered the centre of the floor to the portraits of stern-faced ancestors that hung, seemingly immovable, from the walls, their eyes following one’s every step with a silent, ancestral judgment. Mrs. Pendelton, however, found herself quite revived by the atmosphere, her previous anxieties now giving way to a hopeful admiration. She straightened her posture, adjusted her shawl once more, and gave Eleanor a small, encouraging smile, a silent exhortation to be properly impressed.

A stout, elderly woman, dressed with a neatness that bespoke her position as housekeeper, emerged from a side passage, her silver hair pulled back tightly beneath a starched cap. Her face, though unsmiling, bore an expression of quiet competence, and she moved with a deliberation that suggested a deep familiarity with every inch of the house and its intricate routines. “Good afternoon, Mrs. Pendelton, Miss Eleanor,” she announced, her voice low and even, betraying no hint of curiosity or surprise, as if the arrival of visitors were but another predictable event in a day of precisely orchestrated duties. “Miss Thorndyke expects you. If you will be so good as to follow me, I shall show you to the small drawing-room. She will join you directly.” Mrs. Pendelton, her voice a little higher than usual, thanked the housekeeper profusely, and, with a quick glance at Eleanor to ensure she was following with appropriate

compliance. The housekeeper, a woman whose every movement seemed designed to avoid disturbing the venerable dust of ages, glided silently before them, her back straight and her starched cap a beacon of domestic rectitude. They followed her down a short, carpeted passage, lined with dark, somewhat forbidding portraits of persons whose lineage was no doubt impeccable, but whose countenances promised little in the way of mirth or conversational vivacity. At length, she paused before a door, of unassuming appearance but of solid oak, and after a gentle knock that seemed almost an impertinence in the hushed grandeur of the house, she opened it.

The room into which they were ushered, though designated “small,” was of a size that would have commanded admiration in many a less distinguished abode. It was a chamber of subdued elegance, where comfort, though clearly present, was kept firmly in its proper place, subservient to taste and propriety. The light, entering through tall, narrow windows draped in heavy damask, fell upon furniture of a dark and lustrous polish, upholstered in a brocade of faded green and gold, which, though perhaps a trifle sombre to a youthful eye, spoke unmistakably of enduring quality and substantial cost. Upon a small table of highly-figured rosewood stood a Sevres vase, of exquisite fragility, filled with a modest arrangement of winter roses, whose scent, faint and delicate, was the only overt sign of a living presence in the otherwise perfectly ordered space. The mantelpiece, of white marble, was adorned with a clock of Parisian workmanship, flanked by two bronze statuettes representing classical figures, their cold, impassive beauty reflecting the quietude of the room. A large, intricately woven Persian carpet, its patterns softened by the passage of time, covered the centre of the floor, absorbing any sound that might dare to break the prevailing calm.

Seated by the fireplace, though no fire burned in the grate, was Miss Thorndyke herself. She was a lady of an age that no one ventured to guess with any precision, though it was generally understood to be beyond sixty. Her figure, though spare, was remarkably erect, and she possessed a dignity of mien that commanded immediate, if perhaps reluctant, respect. Her gown, of a rich, dark silk, was entirely unadorned save for a lace cap of the finest quality which framed a countenance of considerable character, though certainly not of beauty. Her eyes, of a light grey, were remarkably keen, and seemed to miss nothing of their surroundings, nor of the persons who entered them. She held a book, bound in calfskin, which lay open upon her lap, though she now looked up from it, her expression one of polite, almost detached, expectation.

“Good afternoon, Mrs. Pendelton, Miss Eleanor,” she said, her voice clear and precise, entirely devoid of any warmth or effusiveness, yet not unkind. There was in its tone an expectation of formality, a quiet demand for proper behaviour that was at once disarming and a little intimidating. “You are punctual, which is a virtue I find increasingly rare in these lax days.” She offered a slight, almost imperceptible nod towards two straight-backed chairs positioned opposite her own, indicating that they might be seated.

Mrs. Pendelton, whose breath seemed to have been entirely suspended from the moment they crossed the threshold, now released it in a little gasp of nervous pleasure. “Oh, Miss Thorndyke, it is such an honour, such a very great honour, to be received by you in your most excellent home! We were quite certain we should be here precisely at the hour, for punctuality, you know, is a principle I endeavour always to impress upon my dear Eleanor, as indeed upon all young ladies under my charge. One cannot be too careful, can one, in matters of decorum and proper timing, especially when one is seeking to make a good impression.” She settled herself upon the edge of the offered chair, her shawl once more adjusted, her hands clasped tightly in her lap, and cast a quick, reassuring smile at Eleanor, who, feeling the sudden weight of Miss Thorndyke’s keen gaze upon her, found herself wishing for a moment of solitude in which to collect her thoughts.

Eleanor, her cheeks faintly flushed from the combination of the long walk and the sudden intensity of the occasion, offered a polite curtsy, her voice, when she spoke, perhaps a little softer than she might have wished. “Good afternoon, Miss Thorndyke. Thank you for receiving us.” She then seated herself with a grace that, though perhaps less practised than her companion’s, was certainly more natural, and folded her hands, endeavouring to appear as composed as her aunt, who, she knew, was relying upon her to make a favourable impression.

Miss Thorndyke’s eyes, after a brief but thorough inspection of Eleanor’s person, returned to Mrs. Pendelton. “I trust your journey was not too fatiguing, Mrs. Pendelton? The roads, I am told, are rather indifferent at this season.”

“Oh, not at all, not in the least, Miss Thorndyke! We quite enjoyed the invigorating air, did we not, Eleanor? And the prospect of visiting so renowned an establishment, so celebrated an individual, as yourself, would quite dispel any fatigue, however considerable! Indeed, one would not wish to complain of such trifles when so much of consequence is at stake, as it were. I have been telling Eleanor, you know, that a little exertion is good for the constitution, and that young ladies, especially those with some pretensions to accomplishment, must not be afraid of a healthy walk, even to a distance such as this. It builds character, does it not? And character, I always say, is worth far more than beauty, though Eleanor, I must confess, possesses a very fair share of both.” Mrs. Pendelton concluded with a small, self-satisfied nod, quite pleased with her own eloquence and the artful compliment she had woven into her discourse.

Miss Thorndyke’s expression remained unchanged, though a very slight, almost imperceptible, flicker in her eyes might have suggested that she found Mrs. Pendelton’s garrulousness somewhat trying. “Indeed,” she replied, her voice as level as before, “character is of paramount importance. But tell me, Mrs. Pendelton, to what particular ‘consequence’ do you refer? I confess, my memory, though generally reliable, does not immediately furnish me with the precise nature of our intended conversation.”

Mrs. Pendelton, slightly taken aback by this direct, almost blunt, inquiry, stammered for a moment, her carefully rehearsed opening somewhat disrupted. “Oh, why, Miss Thorndyke, I had hoped that is to say, I had presumed upon your well-known kindness, your admirable discernment, and your generous patronage of young talent, to introduce to your notice my dear niece, Eleanor. You see, she has a very decided bent for the arts, particularly in drawing and watercolour, and her accomplishments, though I say it myself, who perhaps should not, are quite beyond the ordinary. Her landscapes, why, they quite capture the very spirit of the countryside, and her portraits, though she has only attempted a few, bear such a striking resemblance to the originals, that one might almost converse with them! And, as you know, Miss Thorndyke, in this world, a young lady, however accomplished, requires a little assistance, a little guidance, a little push, as it were, to make her way, to gain the notice of those who can truly appreciate her gifts and perhaps, who knows?, even advance her prospects.” She leaned forward conspiratorially, as if imparting a secret of profound significance, and finished with a hopeful, almost pleading, look.

Eleanor felt a mortifying blush steal over her face. Her aunt’s open appeal for patronage, while perhaps understandable given their circumstances, struck her as indelicate, almost demeaning. She knew Mrs. Pendelton meant well, but such overt solicitations always caused her a profound sense of discomfort. She wished the floor might open and swallow her whole, or at least that she might become invisible for a few moments, until the embarrassing importuning had passed.

Miss Thorndyke, however, merely observed Mrs. Pendelton with the same quiet scrutiny she had previously applied to Eleanor. “Indeed,” she said again, drawing out the word, “I am aware that young ladies, particularly those without the advantage of a substantial fortune, often require assistance. And I am, as you say, generally inclined to encourage talent, when it is truly present. But drawing and watercolour, Mrs. Pendelton, while certainly refined accomplishments, are not, in themselves, a direct path to, shall we say, financial independence. They are graces, certainly; but are they also a means?”

“Oh, but Miss Thorndyke, with your influence, your connections, your discerning eye! Why, a word from you, a recommendation, a mention of Eleanor’s remarkable abilities in the proper quarters, and she might well find commissions, or perhaps even a situation as a governess where such accomplishments are highly valued! Imagine, to be employed in a household where her artistic sensibilities would be appreciated! It would be such an advantage, such a lift for her, and for our family, I assure you, who have had our share of misfortunes, as you may perhaps have heard.” Mrs. Pendelton’s voice, now tinged with a delicate self-pity, was carefully modulated to elicit sympathy.

Miss Thorndyke raised a finely arched eyebrow, a gesture that, while subtle, spoke volumes. “Misfortunes, Mrs. Pendelton, are a common lot, and do not, in themselves, constitute a claim to patronage. As for governessing, while it provides a livelihood, it is rarely, I think, an avenue for artistic distinction. It requires, primarily, a sound moral character, a thorough education in the usual branches of learning, and a firm, yet gentle, hand with children. Are these attributes Miss Eleanor also possesses?” Her gaze, sharp and unwavering, now returned to Eleanor, seeming to pierce through her carefully constructed composure.

Eleanor, recovering from her initial embarrassment, met Miss Thorndyke’s gaze with as much steadiness as she could muster. “I have endeavoured, Miss Thorndyke,” she began, her voice gaining a little more strength, “to apply myself diligently to my studies. I am proficient in French and Italian, have a passable understanding of history and geography, and have, through my aunt’s kindness, received instruction in music. As for a firm hand with children, I have had some experience with my younger cousins, and I believe I can be both patient and resolute.” She omitted to mention that her patience was often severely tried by the boisterous spirits of Master Thomas and the demanding whims of little Miss Lydia.

A faint, almost imperceptible smile touched Miss Thorndyke’s lips, a fleeting expression that softened the austerity of her features for a moment. “A thoroughly practical education, it seems. And what of your drawings, Miss Eleanor? Are they merely a pastime, or do you dedicate serious effort to their improvement?”

“I do, Miss Thorndyke,” Eleanor replied, feeling a surge of genuine conviction. “I find great solace and satisfaction in them. I study nature, the changing light, the forms of trees and hills, and I endeavour to reproduce what I see with as much fidelity as my hand allows. It is not merely a pastime; it is a serious pursuit, one that I hope to refine and deepen with every effort.”

“A laudable sentiment,” Miss Thorndyke observed, her gaze still fixed on Eleanor. “Too many young ladies, I find, treat accomplishments merely as a means to display their superficial graces, without any true appreciation for the discipline or intellectual engagement required. They learn a few airs on the pianoforte, dabble in watercolours, and then believe themselves masters of the arts. Such frivolity, Miss Eleanor, is not only unproductive; it is, in my estimation, a form of intellectual dishonesty.” She paused, allowing her words to sink in, and Eleanor could not but feel that this stern judgment was directed not only at a general trend, but perhaps also, subtly, at her own presentation.

Mrs. Pendelton, however, ever eager to interject and ensure her niece was viewed in the best possible light, seized upon the opportunity. “Oh, but Miss Thorndyke, Eleanor is quite the opposite! She is diligent, truly diligent! Why, she will sit for hours, sketching by the window, or copying from the masters, with such dedication as would put many a professional to shame. She is not one of those flighty, superficial girls, not at all! She takes her art very seriously, and her spirit, I assure you, is of the finest quality, quite unblemished by any sort of idle fancy or worldly vanity.”

Miss Thorndyke raised a hand, a small, dignified gesture that effectively halted Mrs. Pendelton’s flow. “I am quite certain of Miss Eleanor’s virtues, Mrs. Pendelton, as you so readily assert them. But we must address the practicalities. You wish for my patronage. What precisely do you envision this patronage to entail?”

Eleanor braced herself for her aunt’s next flight of hopeful fancy. She had, in truth, very little expectation that this visit would yield any tangible result, and the thought of asking directly for financial assistance or social preferment filled her with an overwhelming sense of shame.

Mrs. Pendelton, however, seemed entirely immune to such delicate sensibilities. “Why, Miss Thorndyke, anything that your kind heart and remarkable influence might suggest! Perhaps a letter of introduction to some esteemed family in London, where Eleanor’s talents might be seen and appreciated by those of taste and distinction. Or perhaps, if it is not too great a presumption, a small commission for one of her landscapes, to begin her portfolio, as it were. Or even, and this would be the most fortunate outcome, should you ever hear of a situation, a governess position in a refined household, where a young lady of cultivation and artistic sensibility might find a congenial home and a respectable income, why, then, that would be truly beyond our wildest hopes!” Her voice rose slightly with the enthusiasm of her own suggestions, and she clasped her hands even tighter, her eyes shining with an almost childlike hope.

Miss Thorndyke listened with an air of impenetrable calm, her light grey eyes betraying no emotion. When Mrs. Pendelton had quite finished, and a brief silence had settled upon the room, broken only by the faint ticking of the Parisian clock on the mantel, Miss Thorndyke finally spoke. “I see. You wish me, in essence, to act as a sponsor, a referrer, and a provider of opportunities. This is a considerable request, Mrs. Pendelton, and not one to be made lightly, nor granted without due consideration.” She paused, allowing the weight of her words to settle upon them. “It is my custom, when considering such requests, to observe the individual in question for some time. I do not lend my name, nor my influence, to persons of whom I am not entirely convinced. Reputation, Mrs. Pendelton, is a fragile thing, easily tarnished by injudicious associations.”

Eleanor felt a fresh wave of mortification. The implication was clear: her own character, and perhaps even her aunt’s, was now under scrutiny, and her future prospects might well depend upon the precise nature of Miss Thorndyke’s judgment. She could not, for the life of her, imagine what sort of “observation” Miss Thorndyke intended to undertake, nor for how long. The thought of being subtly assessed, like a candidate for a position or a piece of merchandise, was deeply unsettling.

Mrs. Pendelton, however, seemed to interpret Miss Thorndyke’s words as a hopeful sign, a conditional acceptance rather than a veiled rejection. Her face lit up with a renewed, if still nervous, enthusiasm. “Oh, of course, Miss Thorndyke, of course! We would not wish it otherwise! You are entirely right, reputation is everything, and to have your esteemed name associated with Eleanor, it would be a treasure beyond measure! We entirely submit to your wisdom and your judgment. Any period of observation you deem necessary, Miss Thorndyke, we would happily acquiesce to. Indeed, Eleanor is always at your disposal, should you wish to inspect her drawings further, or to converse with her upon any subject. She is a very sensible girl, I assure you, and quite well-read.”

“Sensibility and good sense are indeed valuable attributes,” Miss Thorndyke conceded, with a slight inclination of her head. “I shall consider the matter. It was a subtle signal, Eleanor realised, that the formal interview was, for the moment, concluded, and a more general social exchange might now ensue.

Mrs. Pendelton, always eager to share, and perhaps to demonstrate her own access to the local intelligence network, immediately brightened further. “Oh, Miss Thorndyke, indeed it is true, alas! A dreadful business, quite dreadful! Poor Mr. Ainsworth is quite beside himself, I hear. It is a testament, I always say, to the dangers of novel-reading, for young ladies get such romantic notions into their heads, that they imagine love alone is sufficient for a comfortable establishment. And what is more, Miss Thorndyke, they say she left a note, a most impertinent note, declaring her undying devotion and her refusal to be swayed by mere worldly considerations! As if worldly considerations were not the very bedrock of a respectable marriage!” Mrs. Pendelton shook her head in a gesture of profound disapproval, her earlier anxieties temporarily forgotten in the thrill of recounting scandal.

“Indeed,” Miss Thorndyke remarked, her lips pursed, “a lamentable illustration of the folly of youth, and the inadequate supervision of parents. One hopes, for the sake of the unfortunate girl, that her penurious curate possesses at least a modicum of good sense and industry, though such qualities are rarely, I find, the companions of romantic imprudence.” She paused, a thoughtful expression now upon her face. “And what of the Thorntons? Are they still intent upon their proposed grand tour of the continent? I confess, I find such extensive travel, in these uncertain times, to be a rather extravagant and perhaps even dangerous undertaking, particularly for a family of their moderate means.”

“Oh, the Thorntons, Miss Thorndyke!” Mrs. Pendelton exclaimed, leaning forward once more, her voice dropping to a confidential whisper. “They are quite determined, entirely so! Mrs. Thornton, you know, has always had aspirations beyond her station, and she believes this tour will quite make her daughters, give them an air of fashionable consequence that their father’s rather plodding legal practice could never afford. But between ourselves, Miss Thorndyke, it is whispered that the family finances are not quite as robust as they appear. Mr. Thornton, they say, has been rather too speculative in certain ventures, and the talk is that they are sailing rather close to the wind, as it were. This tour, some suggest, is a desperate attempt to secure advantageous marriages for the girls, before their true circumstances become widely known. Imagine, a grand tour to conceal a precarious fortune! It is quite the most audacious scheme!”

Eleanor, who had been listening to this exchange with a mixture of amusement and dismay, could not help but observe the stark contrast between her aunt’s fervent recounting of gossip and Miss Thorndyke’s measured, almost analytical, absorption of the same. Her aunt spoke with a passionate, if often misguided, conviction, while Miss Thorndyke’s interest, though keen, remained detached, an intellectual exercise in understanding the social fabric. It was clear that Miss Thorndyke valued information, particularly that which shed light upon character or financial standing, but she processed it with the dispassionate rigour of a scholar examining a specimen.

“A desperate measure, indeed,” Miss Thorndyke mused, stroking her chin thoughtfully. “One can only hope that their efforts are not entirely in vain, though I have always found that subterfuge, however cleverly executed, rarely yields lasting happiness or genuine esteem. Character, Mrs. Pendelton, will always out, as will the true state of one’s purse. It is a lesson that many seem reluctant to learn.” She then turned her gaze back to Eleanor, and a very slight, almost imperceptible, softening of her features occurred. “Miss Eleanor, you have been rather quiet during our discussion of these various misfortunes and follies. Do you share your aunt’s rather pronounced views on the perils of imprudent marriages and extravagant travels?”

Eleanor, surprised by the direct address, found herself momentarily flustered. She hesitated for a moment, wishing to be truthful without appearing either too opinionated or too timid. “I confess, Miss Thorndyke,” she began, choosing her words carefully, “that while I understand the wisdom of considering worldly circumstances in marriage, and the prudence of living within one’s means, I cannot but feel a degree of sympathy for those who, perhaps misguidedly, act upon the dictates of their hearts. Though I agree that a solid foundation of sense and reason is essential for any lasting happiness.” She glanced briefly at her aunt, who was now nodding vigorously in approval of Eleanor’s measured response.

Miss Thorndyke regarded her for a long moment, a faint, almost approving, gleam in her keen grey eyes. “An admirably balanced perspective, Miss Eleanor. It is a rare quality in youth, this capacity to appreciate both the dictates of the heart and the exigencies of the world. Too often, one finds either a headstrong romanticism that disregards all prudence, or a cold calculation that sacrifices all genuine affection. The true path, as in all things, lies in a judicious tempering of both.” She then reached for a small silver bell on the table beside her and gave it a single, delicate ring. Almost immediately, the housekeeper reappeared in the doorway, a silent, attentive figure.

“Mrs. Hopkins,” Miss Thorndyke instructed, “please bring tea and some of the smaller cakes. Our visitors have, I believe, earned a refreshment.” There was a subtle emphasis on the word “earned,” which Eleanor did not miss, and which seemed to imply that their conversation, though largely social, had also been a form of assessment, a test which she, and perhaps even her aunt, had, to some extent, passed.

Mrs. Pendelton, her face now beaming with unconcealed triumph, leaned towards Eleanor and whispered, “Tea, my dear! It is a very good sign, a very good sign indeed! She means to observe you, just as I hoped! Now, remember to converse gracefully, and do not be afraid to share your opinions, but always with proper deference, of course. This is precisely the opportunity we needed!”

Eleanor, though still somewhat apprehensive about the duration and nature of Miss Thorndyke’s “observation,” could not deny that the invitation to tea felt like a small victory. I will, of course, do my best to entertain your guests.”

“Oh, you are a good girl, Elinor, a very good girl, and I am sure you will do very well. But we shall see, we shall see. Perhaps a little rest will revive her, and she will come down later. For it is a sad thing to be alone, especially when one is unhappy. And I shall send her up a nice bowl of warm gruel, or some panada, just before bed. It will comfort her more than anything. And I shall tell the servants to be very quiet, so as not to disturb her rest. There, now, is there anything else I can do? For I must go and tell the cook about the supper, and see that the card-tables are ready for our friends. Such a fuss as it all is, but it is pleasant to have a little company. And it will take your mind off things too, Elinor, which is always a good thing. For too much thinking, my dear, is quite bad for the complexion, and makes one quite sallow. Now, do make yourself comfortable, and do not fret so. All will be well, you may depend upon it. The world does not end because a silly young man breaks his word. There are plenty more where he came from, and better ones too, I warrant you. For Willoughby, for all his fine looks, was but a shallow fellow, and not worthy of a good girl like Marianne. And now, thanks be to goodness, she is well rid of him.”

With this final, rather robust summation of the situation, Mrs. Jennings at last rose from her chair, adjusted her cap, and bustled out of the room, leaving Elinor in a state of mingled relief and despair. Relief that the conversation, with its endless flow of unsuitable remedies and inappropriate advice, was for the moment suspended; despair at the utter inability of her hostess to grasp the true nature or depth of Marianne’s suffering, and the fresh anxieties that her well-meaning interferences would undoubtedly create. Elinor leant back in her chair, closing her eyes for a moment, wishing for nothing so much as to be transported back to the quiet simplicity of Barton Cottage, where at least her grief could be indulged without the constant intrusion of bustling, insensitive kindness. She knew, however, that there was no escaping the present circumstances, and that her duty now lay in guarding Marianne from any further assault upon her fragile composure. She would remain, as she had promised, to greet the guests, and to ensure that the evening passed with as little further distress to her sister as possible. The task seemed arduous, for she felt utterly weary, both in body and in spirit, but she knew that Marianne’s welfare depended upon her strength and vigilance. She opened her eyes, and looked around the drawing-room, a place now associated, for her, with so much painful discourse, and prepared herself for the evening’s further trials. She heard the distant sounds of Mrs. Jennings giving orders to the servants, her voice cheerful and resounding, and Elinor knew, with a certainty that chilled her, that the quiet of the house was at an end, and that the coming hours would bring renewed challenges to her fortitude, and a fresh round of social demands that she was ill-equipped to meet, yet dared not shirk, for Marianne’s sake.

  Hardly had Mrs. Jennings’s cheerful, albeit rather loud, exhortations to the domestic staff faded into the distant regions of the house, than the first arrivals began to announce themselves with a brisk rap upon the street door, and the cheerful, if somewhat jarring, clamour of voices in the hall. Elinor, though still oppressed by the weight of her anxieties, and wishing herself a thousand miles from all society, straightened her cap, smoothed the folds of her gown, and assumed that placid demeanour which her good sense, and long practice in managing the turbulent emotions of her family, had rendered habitual. She knew that any display of distress on her own part could only add to the awkwardness of the evening, and perhaps even provoke a fresh torrent of misplaced sympathy from her hostess, or worse, a pointed inquiry from some less benevolent acquaintance.

The drawing-room door opened, and Sir John Middleton, a stout, jovial man with a perpetually ruddy face, burst in, followed by his amiable, though rather foolish, daughter, Mrs. Palmer, and her husband, Mr. Palmer, a gentleman whose habitually bored expression seemed proof against all human gaiety or distress.

“Aha, Elinor, my dear girl!” cried Sir John, advancing with outstretched hand, his voice booming through the room, “Alone, are we? And where is the fair Marianne? Not still moping, I hope, after that rascal Willoughby? Tut, tut, these young girls, they take everything so to heart! Mrs. Jennings, where are you, my good lady? Your room is quite deserted, quite deserted! And Palmer, you dog, come and pay your respects, though I vow you look as if you’d rather be in your coffin than in company!”

Mrs. Palmer, meanwhile, had flown to Elinor, her bright blue eyes sparkling with an almost alarming cheerfulness. “Oh, Miss Dashwood, how delightful to see you! We came as quickly as we could, for Mama said we must not be late, and we have had the most diverting drive! Mr. Palmer hardly spoke a word, which is always so amusing, is it not? And what a fine house this is! So much larger than Barton Park, though I do think the carpets are a little faded in the hall. But one cannot have everything, can one? And where is dear Marianne? I long to hear all the news, for Mama told us a little, but she was in such a hurry, and said we should hear more tonight.”

Elinor forced a smile. “How do you do, Mrs. Palmer? Sir John, it is good to see you. Marianne, I regret to say, is not quite herself this evening, and has retired to her room. She is a little unwell, and I have persuaded her to seek an early rest.” She glanced at Mr. Palmer, who stood by the fireplace, regarding the company with an air of profound indifference, and made a slight inclination of her head, which he acknowledged with a barely perceptible nod.

“Unwell, is she?” exclaimed Sir John, his brows furrowing for a moment, before his natural exuberance reasserted itself. “Ah, well, a good night’s sleep will set her right! A strong constitution, Marianne has! Not like poor Lady Middleton, who catches cold if a fly looks at her. But we shall see her tomorrow, no doubt. And no need to fret, Elinor, for all will be well, I promise you! There are plenty of good fellows about, and a little disappointment never hurt anyone, eh? It makes them value the real thing all the more when it comes!” He then turned his attention to the door, from which Mrs. Jennings now emerged, her face beaming.

“Ah, here you are, Sir John! And Charlotte, my dear girl! And Mr. Palmer! So glad to see you all! We feared you might be late. But come, come, sit down, make yourselves comfortable. Elinor, my love, have you offered them anything? A little sherry, perhaps? Or a glass of punch? There is excellent punch tonight, made with my own recipe. Sir John, you must try it!”

The drawing-room, which a moment before had felt oppressively quiet to Elinor, was now filled with a cacophony of sound. Sir John’s hearty laugh, Mrs. Palmer’s ceaseless chatter, Mrs. Jennings’s effusive welcomes, and even Mr. Palmer’s occasional, almost inaudible grunt, all combined to create an atmosphere of boisterous conviviality that seemed utterly at odds with the silent sorrow weighing upon Elinor’s heart. She watched as Mrs. Jennings settled her guests, pressing refreshments upon them, and directing the conversation with her usual blunt energy.

“So, Charlotte, my dear,” Mrs. Jennings began, turning to her daughter, “I have been telling Elinor all about it, what a terrible thing it is, to be sure, this business with Mr. Willoughby. But then, as I told her, it is all for the best, for he was a shocking bad match, with all his debts and dashing about! And Elinor, you know, is quite an angel about it, though I daresay she is as vexed as anyone. But it’s not Elinor, is it, my love? It’s poor Marianne, who has taken it so to heart. She is upstairs, unwell, Sir John, as Elinor said, but nothing a good night’s rest and a little Constantia wine won’t cure, I hope! And Elinor, I have sent for some warm gruel for her, and she is to have whatever she fancies for supper, no matter what the cook says!”

Elinor felt a fresh wave of mortification wash over her. To have Marianne’s deepest private grief thus publicly aired, however kindly meant, was an agony. She wished the floor would open and swallow her. Mrs. Palmer, ever eager for a fresh topic, clapped her hands together. “Oh, Mama, how shocking! So it is true, then, about Mr. Willoughby? I told Mr. Palmer, did I not, that something was amiss? For he was always so very attentive to Marianne, and such a handsome young man, though Mr. Palmer says he has no patience for handsome young men who are forever bothering young ladies. But tell me, Miss Dashwood, is it all quite over? And is he truly going to marry Miss Grey? The rich one, Mama mentioned?”

Mr. Palmer, surprisingly, spoke. “Indeed, ma’am, if a man is to marry, he might as well marry money. Saves a deal of trouble later on. A woman with fifty thousand pounds, that’s a sensible choice. Nothing to complain of there.” He then resumed his customary silence, as if exhausted by this brief burst of opinion.

Elinor, struggling to maintain her composure, replied, “Indeed, Mrs. Palmer, the engagement between Mr. Willoughby and Miss Grey is, I believe, quite settled. And as for Marianne, her indisposition is, as I said, merely a consequence of a slight chill. She has been very much fatigued lately, and requires quiet.”

Sir John, ever the well-meaning blunderer, chimed in, “Ah, a chill, is it? Well, keep her warm, Elinor! And what a pity! For I always thought Willoughby a capital fellow, for all his wild ways. Such a shot, and such a seat on a horse! But money, money, that’s the thing, eh, Mrs. Jennings? A man must live, and if he hasn’t a farthing, why, he must find one that has! But don’t you worry, Elinor, we’ll find a better one for Marianne yet! Colonel Brandon, now, he’s a solid fellow! And rich as Croesus, they say. I was just telling Lady Middleton the other day, that Brandon has always had an eye for Marianne. A little old, perhaps, but a good man, a very good man!”

Elinor felt her cheeks burn. Mrs. Jennings, however, nodded vigorously. “That’s just what I say, Sir John! A true saying, ‘an ill wind that blows no one good,’ for it will be all the better for Colonel Brandon! He will have her at last, aye, that he will! And a much better match, too, a very good match! Two thousand a year, without debt or drawback, except, you know, the little one, but that does not signify. And Delaford is such a nice place, a truly comfortable place, full of comforts, and so close to the church too, not like Barton, where one has to travel miles for everything! I shall spirit him up as soon as he arrives!”

Elinor closed her eyes for a fleeting moment, wishing she possessed the power to strike Mrs. Jennings temporarily speechless. The egregious mention of the "little one," however casually inserted, was a fresh indignity, a public acknowledgment of a secret she had hoped would remain buried, at least within these walls. That Mrs. Jennings, in her blithe enthusiasm for a new match, could so carelessly reveal such a delicate circumstance, was almost beyond belief. Elinor opened her eyes, and met Mrs. Palmer’s gaze, which, for once, was not entirely cheerful, but rather held a flicker of dawning comprehension. The moment, however, passed quickly, as Mrs. Palmer’s attention was drawn to a rather splendid new ribbon on Mrs. Jennings’s cap.

The evening continued in this vein, a bewildering tapestry of earnest concern, boisterous good humour, and oblivious insensitivity. More guests arrived: a quiet, elderly couple, the Gilberts, who spoke little but listened attentively; and two young officers from the nearby barracks, Captains Davies and Perrott, who, though handsome and eager, seemed primarily interested in discussing their recent hunting exploits and the merits of various cavalry charges. Elinor, ever the dutiful hostess, moved amongst them, answering polite inquiries about Marianne with a carefully constructed vagueness, and deflecting the more pointed questions with gentle firmness. She played a hand of whist, though her mind was far from the cards, and forced herself to join in the general conversation, offering polite remarks and feigning interest in subjects that, in her present state of mind, seemed utterly trivial.

She noticed, with a pang of weary anticipation, the arrival of Colonel Brandon. He entered the room quietly, almost unnoticed amidst the general chatter, and made his way directly to Elinor. His dark eyes, though usually reserved, held a look of profound concern as he bowed to her. “Miss Dashwood,” he said, his voice low and kind, “I am sorry to hear of Miss Marianne’s indisposition. I trust it is nothing serious?”

Elinor appreciated his quiet thoughtfulness. “Thank you, Colonel Brandon. She has been unwell, but I hope a night’s rest will be of benefit. She has been much fatigued, and her spirits have been a little low.”

Before the Colonel could reply, Mrs. Jennings, ever vigilant, descended upon them, her face alight with purpose. “Ah, Colonel! Just the man I wanted to see! Come, come, sit down, you look quite out of breath! Have some punch, it’s excellent tonight! And tell me, have you heard the news? No? Well, it’s quite shocking, quite shocking, but, as I tell Elinor, it’s all for the best! That rogue Willoughby, he has quite thrown over poor Marianne for a rich girl! Fifty thousand pounds, Colonel, think of it! But never mind, never mind, for it is all for the best, I assure you! For Marianne, I mean, of course. For now she is free, and there is a certain gentleman, a very worthy gentleman, who I am sure would make her a much better husband, and who has been quite smitten with her for a long time, has he not, Sir John?”

Sir John, who had overheard this, nodded enthusiastically. “Indeed, Colonel, indeed! A fine girl, Marianne! And a fine fellow, you are! The very man for her, I always say! Delaford, a capital place! And two thousand a year, what more could a man ask for? Come, Colonel, you must not let this opportunity slip by! Strike while the iron is hot, eh?”

Colonel Brandon’s face, which had at first registered a look of pained embarrassment, now settled into a dignified resignation. He glanced at Elinor, a silent apology in his eyes, before turning back to Mrs. Jennings and Sir John. “Indeed, Mrs. Jennings, Sir John, I am very sensible of your good wishes. But I confess, my thoughts are at present entirely with Miss Marianne’s recovery, and not at all upon any such matters as you propose. She has suffered a profound disappointment, and her feelings must, I imagine, be too tender to admit of any immediate consideration of such a nature.” His tone, though gentle, carried an undeniable note of finality, which, for a moment, even managed to silence Mrs. Jennings.

Elinor felt a surge of gratitude towards him. He understood, at least, the delicacy of the situation, and had spared her the task of having to rebuke her hostess directly. She saw Mrs. Jennings exchange a knowing look with Sir John, a look that suggested they believed the Colonel merely needed a little more encouragement, or perhaps a stronger glass of punch. Elinor sighed inwardly. The battle, she knew, was far from over.

The evening wore on, a succession of polite conversations, ill-timed jokes, and, for Elinor, a constant battle to conceal her true feelings. She found herself responding to questions almost mechanically, her mind often wandering to Marianne upstairs, imagining her silent vigil by the fading fire, her tears, her despair. The contrast between the festive chatter below and the profound suffering above was a constant ache in Elinor’s breast. She longed for the guests to depart, for the quiet of the house to return, for the simple, unburdened presence of her sister, even in her grief, to be the only demand upon her.

As the hour grew late, and the guests began to take their leave, Elinor felt a profound sense of relief. Sir John, after shaking her hand with boisterous good wishes for Marianne’s speedy recovery, promised to call again in the morning, with an armful of game, no doubt, for her invalid sister. Mrs. Palmer, after expressing her fervent hope that Marianne would be quite herself again very soon, declared that she must hear all the details directly from Mama, for it was such a romantic tragedy, was it not? Mr. Palmer, with his usual brevity, merely grunted his goodbyes.

At last, the drawing-room was empty, save for Elinor and Mrs. Jennings. The remnants of the evening lay scattered about: empty glasses, discarded cards, and the faint, lingering scent of punch and extinguished candles. Mrs. Jennings, far from seeming tired, appeared invigorated by the social exchange. She rubbed her hands together, a look of satisfied contentment on her face.

“Well, Elinor, my dear,” she exclaimed, “that was a very pleasant evening, was it not? Such good company! And Sir John was in capital spirits! And Charlotte looked quite charming, though I do wish Mr. Palmer would try to be a little more agreeable. But then, men are all alike, are they not? And Colonel Brandon, he is a good man, a very good man, though a little slow to come to the point. But we shall see, we shall see! I have a plan, Elinor, a capital plan! We shall have a little excursion, just the four of us, to some charming spot, and leave those young people to themselves! A little fresh air, and a little quiet conversation, that is what is needed to bring two hearts together, is it not?”

Elinor, utterly exhausted, could only manage a weak smile. “Indeed, ma’am, I daresay that would be very agreeable for them.” She wondered, with a sinking heart, what new torment Mrs. Jennings’s “capital plan” would inflict upon Marianne, and upon herself. The thought of a forced excursion, designed to throw Marianne into the company of a man she respected but did not love, and to whom she would certainly feel no immediate attraction in her present state, filled Elinor with dread.

“But first,” Mrs. Jennings continued, bustling about the room, gathering up stray cards, “I must just check on your poor sister. Did she take the gruel, do you think? And the Constantia? It is a wonderful restorative, you know. My poor husband always swore by it for his gout. I shall just pop up and see. And then, Elinor, my dear, you must get some rest yourself. You look quite pale, quite pale, and too much fretting is very bad for the complexion. Tomorrow is another day, and we shall put a brave face on it all, shall we not? For, as I always say, there is no use crying over spilt milk, especially when there is good cream to be had elsewhere!” With this final, rather confounding piece of wisdom, Mrs. Jennings, still radiating energy, began to make her way towards the door, chattering cheerfully about the benefits of early rising and the delights of a good breakfast.

Elinor watched her go, a profound weariness settling over her. The silence that now descended upon the drawing-room was not the peaceful quiet she had longed for, but a heavy, echoing void, filled with the ghosts of the evening’s conversations. She sank onto a sofa, her head resting against the cool velvet, and closed her eyes. Her limbs ached, her head throbbed, and her heart felt heavy with an almost unbearable burden of sorrow and anxiety. She thought of Marianne, alone in her room, struggling with a grief so profound that it rendered her insensible to all comfort. She thought of Willoughby, his duplicity, his cruelty, the ease with which he had shattered her sister’s happiness. And she thought of Colonel Brandon, his quiet dignity, his unspoken understanding, his unwavering loyalty.

The evening had offered no respite, no moment of peace. Instead, it had piled fresh anxieties upon her, fresh indignities for Marianne. The prospect of Mrs. Jennings’s matchmaking schemes, however well-intentioned, filled her with a new kind of dread. How was she to protect Marianne from such relentless, unthinking interference? How was she to ensure that her sister’s fragile recovery was not further imperilled by the very people who claimed to wish her well?

She knew that she must remain strong, that she must continue to be Marianne’s shield against the world’s insensitivity, her anchor in a sea of despair. But for a fleeting moment, in the quiet solitude of the now-empty room, Elinor allowed herself to feel the full weight of her own exhaustion, the profound loneliness of her position, and the crushing burden of a responsibility she felt herself barely equipped to bear. She longed for her mother’s calm counsel, for a moment of quiet understanding, for anything that might ease the relentless pressure upon her mind. But she was alone, and the night, she knew, would bring little sleep, only a continuation of the painful thoughts that circled endlessly in her weary brain. She would wait for Mrs. Jennings’s return, and then she would steel herself for another day, another round of social performance, another battle to be fought for Marianne’s peace, a peace that seemed, at this moment, an utterly unattainable dream. The house was quiet now, truly quiet, but the quiet brought no comfort, only the stark reality of the pain that resided within its walls, and within Elinor’s own heavy heart.

“Dear Ma’am,” Elinor responded, her voice tinged with a weariness she could scarcely conceal, “this kindness, I assure you, is quite unnecessary. Marianne, I dare say, will not leave her room again this evening. I shall persuade her if I can to go early to bed, for I am sure she wants rest.” “Aye, I believe that will be best for her. Let her name her own supper, and go to bed. Lord! no wonder she has been looking so bad and so cast down this last week or two, for this matter I suppose has been hanging over her head as long as that. And so the letter that came today finished it! Poor soul! I am sure if I had had a notion of it, I would not have joked her about it for all my money. But then you know, how should I guess such a thing? I made sure of its being nothing but a common love letter, and you know young people like to be laughed at about them. Lord! how concerned Sir John and my daughters will be when they hear it! If I had my senses about me I might have called in Conduit Street in my way home, and told them of it. But I shall see them tomorrow.” “It would be unnecessary I am sure, for you to caution Mrs. Palmer and Sir John against ever naming Mr. Willoughby, or making the slightest allusion to what has passed, before my sister. Their own good-nature must point out to them the real cruelty of appearing to know any thing about it when she is present; and the less

much that may ever be said to myself on the subject, the more my feelings will be spared, as you my dear madam will easily believe.” “Oh! Lord! yes, that I do indeed. It must be terrible for you to hear it talked of; and as for your sister, I am sure I would not mention a word about it to her for the world. You saw I did not all dinner time. No more would Sir John, nor my daughters, for they are all very thoughtful and considerate; especially if I give them a hint, as I certainly will. For my part, I think the less that is said about such things, the better, the sooner ’tis blown over and forgot. And what good does talking ever do you know?” “In this affair it can only do harm; more so perhaps than in many cases of a similar kind, for it has been attended by circumstances which, for the sake of every one concerned in it, make it unfit to become the public conversation. I must do _this_ justice to Mr. Willoughby, he has broken no positive engagement with my sister.” “Law, my dear! Don’t pretend to defend him. No positive engagement indeed! after taking her all over Allenham House, and fixing on the very rooms they were to live in hereafter!” Elinor, for her sister’s sake, could not press the subject farther, and she hoped it was not required of her for Willoughby’s; since, though Marianne might lose much, he could gain very little by the enforcement of the real truth. After a short silence on both sides, Mrs. Jennings, with all her natural hilarity, burst forth again. “Well, my dear, ’tis a true saying about an ill-wind, for it will be all the better for Colonel Brandon. He will have her at last; aye, that he will. Mind me, now, if they an’t married by Mid-summer. Lord! how he’ll chuckle over this news! I hope he will come tonight. It will be all to one a better match for your sister. Two thousand a year without debt or drawback, except the little love-child, indeed; aye, I had forgot her; but she may be ’prenticed out at a small cost, and then what does it signify? Delaford is a nice place, I can tell you; exactly what I call a nice old fashioned place, full of comforts and conveniences; quite shut in with great garden walls that are covered with the best fruit-trees in the country; and such a mulberry tree in one corner! Lord! how Charlotte and I did stuff the only time we were there! Then, there is a dove-cote, some delightful stew-ponds, and a very pretty canal; and every thing, in short, that one could wish for; and, moreover, it is close to the church, and only a quarter of a mile from the turnpike-road, so ’tis never dull, for if you only go and sit up in an old yew arbour behind the house, you may see all the carriages that pass along. Oh! ’tis a nice place! A butcher hard by in the village, and the parsonage-house within a stone’s throw. To my fancy, a thousand times prettier than Barton Park, where they are forced to send three miles for their meat, and have not a neighbour nearer than your mother. Well, I shall spirit up the Colonel as soon as I can. One shoulder of mutton, you know, drives another down. If we _can_ but put Willoughby out of her head!” “Ay, if we can do _that_, Ma’am,” said Elinor, “we shall do very well with or without Colonel Brandon.” And then rising, she went away to join Marianne, whom she found, as she expected, in her own room, leaning, in silent misery, over the small remains of a fire, which, till Elinor’s entrance, had been her only light. “You had better leave me,” was all the notice that her sister received from her. “I will leave you,” said Elinor, “if you will go to bed.” But this, from the momentary perverseness of impatient suffering, she at first refused to do. Her sister’s earnest, though gentle persuasion, however, soon softened her to compliance, and Elinor saw her lay her aching head on the pillow, and as she hoped, in a way to get some quiet rest before she left her. In the drawing-room, whither she then repaired, she was soon joined by Mrs. Jennings, with a wine-glass, full of something, in her hand. “My dear,” said she, entering, “I have just recollected that I have some of the finest old Constantia wine in the house that ever was tasted, so I have brought a glass of it for your sister. My poor husband! how fond he was of it! Whenever he had a touch of his old colicky gout, he said it did him more good than any thing else in the world. Do take it to your sister.” “Dear Ma’am,”

“Dear Ma’am,” Elinor began, taking the glass from Mrs. Jennings’s hand, “you are too kind; but Marianne, I assure you, will not require it. Indeed, she is already settled for the night, and I hope will soon be asleep. She needs nothing so much as quiet repose.”

Mrs. Jennings, however, looked disappointed. “Nonsense, my dear, nonsense! Quiet repose indeed! What is quiet repose to a poor creature with a broken heart? A good glass of Constantia is worth all the quiet in the world, I can tell you. It warms the blood, lifts the spirits, and sends away all those doleful fancies. My poor dear husband, Lord rest his soul, knew the value of it. Why, once, when he was in such a state, quite past all hope with the rheumatism, and as cross as two sticks into the bargain, I gave him a glass of this very Constantia, and in half an hour, he was singing an old sea-shanty, as hearty as a lark! And this is much the same, you know, only in the heart rather than the bones. It is a sovereign remedy, I assure you, for all ills, mental or physical. You must take it to her, Elinor. She will thank you for it in the morning.”

Elinor held the glass, its rich, dark liquid gleaming in the faint light of the drawing-room, feeling a profound sense of futility. How was she to make Mrs. Jennings understand the nature of Marianne’s suffering, a pain too deep for the palliative of even the finest Constantia? To offer wine to Marianne now would be like offering a toy to a child who had lost its parent, a gesture well-meant but entirely inadequate, indeed, almost insulting in its cheerful optimism. Marianne needed silence, time, and the slow, arduous process of healing, not artificial spirits.

“Indeed, Ma’am,” Elinor rejoined, choosing her words with care, “I appreciate your thoughtful consideration, and I am sure it is excellent wine, but Marianne is quite delicate at present, and I fear any stimulant might only agitate her. Her constitution is not so robust as to bear much excitement, even of a pleasing sort. Rest, and the absence of all disturbance, is truly what she most requires. I have encouraged her to seek sleep, and I would not wish to interrupt her if she has at last found some peace.”

Mrs. Jennings pursed her lips, but a smile soon broke through. “Well, well, if you say so, my dear. You know your sister best, no doubt. But I must say, it seems a sad waste of good Constantia. Such a pity to let it stand. Perhaps, then, you would take a glass yourself? You look quite worn out, my poor child, and no wonder, with all this trouble on your shoulders. A little something to keep up your strength, eh?” And she held out the bottle, ready to pour another glass.

“No, thank you, Ma’am,” Elinor replied, placing the first glass gently on a small table near her. “I too feel the need for quiet. I intend to retire early myself. But I must again beg of you, Mrs. Jennings, to impress upon your daughters, and upon Sir John, the absolute necessity of avoiding any mention of Mr. Willoughby, or indeed, of any circumstances connected with his late behaviour, in Marianne’s hearing. Even a hint, a glance, a half-whispered word, might undo all our efforts to restore her to any degree of composure. Her sensibilities are such that she feels every slight with an intensity that strangers can hardly comprehend.”

Mrs. Jennings waved her hand dismissively. “Oh, Lord! don’t vex yourself about that, my dear. I told you, I shall give them a proper hint, and you know they are very good children, in their way. Charlotte, though she has a tongue that runs away with her sometimes, is not malicious, bless her heart, and Anne, she always follows her mother’s lead. Sir John, why, he is the best creature in the world, though a little boisterous, I grant you. He means no harm. I shall just say, ‘Now, children, no talking of Mr. Willoughby before poor Miss Marianne, for she is feeling quite poorly, and we don’t want to put her in a flutter.’ That will be quite enough, depend upon it. They are not fools, you know. They can take a word to the wise.”

Elinor sighed inwardly, knowing that Mrs. Jennings’s interpretation of a “proper hint” might be rather different from her own. Sir John’s good-nature, she knew, was a robust and unthinking quality, and Mrs. Palmer’s a careless one, little guided by reflection. To depend on their discretion, however prompted, seemed a dangerous gamble. Yet, what more could she do? She could only reiterate her plea and hope that some part of it might impress upon their volatile understandings.

“I am obliged to you, Ma’am,” Elinor said, trying to infuse a note of finality into her voice, “for any assistance you can render in this matter. It is a subject of the utmost delicacy.”

“Delicacy, aye,” Mrs. Jennings repeated, nodding sagely, “and a great pity, too, for such a handsome young man to turn out so ill. But you see, Elinor, it all comes back to the money, doesn’t it? Elinor could say no more. To argue further would be fruitless; Mrs. Jennings’s mind was not open to argument on such matters. Colonel Brandon, however, answered with his usual quiet politeness, and the conversation turned, for a short while, to more general topics, such as the weather, the state of the roads, and the latest news from London.

Presently, tea was brought in, and with it came the usual cheerful bustle that always accompanied Mrs. Jennings’s evenings. Cards were proposed, and Mrs. Palmer, with her customary enthusiasm, declared for whist, though Elinor gently reminded her that Marianne particularly disliked it.

“Oh, indeed, so she does!” cried Mrs. Jennings. “Well, then, we must have a round game, to be sure. Something lively! Pope Joan, or Speculation. Those are always so diverting. Sir John, you must join us! And Colonel, you too. It will cheer us all up, and keep our minds from gloomy thoughts.”

Elinor knew that her own participation in such a game was expected, and she resigned herself to it, though her thoughts were constantly with Marianne, whose suffering she felt so keenly that all attempts at social amusement seemed a heartless mockery. She found herself playing mechanically, her eyes often straying to the clock, longing for the hour when she might decently retire and return to her sister’s side.

As the evening wore on, Mrs. Jennings, despite Elinor’s attempts to steer the conversation, frequently reverted to the subject of Marianne and Willoughby. Her observations, though intended to be comforting, were often painfully inappropriate, and Elinor found herself constantly on edge, dreading what fresh indiscretion might escape her well-meaning but tactless hostess. Sir John, too, with a boisterous sympathy, occasionally added his own comments, which, though less detailed than Mrs. Jennings’s, were equally public and therefore equally distressing to Elinor. Mrs. Palmer, incapable of retaining a serious thought for any length of time, oscillated between profound declarations of pity for Marianne and animated chatter about the fashions and frivolities of the day.

Colonel Brandon alone maintained a quiet reserve, his infrequent remarks marked by a gentle seriousness that conveyed far more genuine concern than the more elaborate expressions of the others. Elinor noticed that he frequently looked towards the door that led upstairs, and though he said little, she sensed in him a deep, unspoken solicitude for Marianne’s welfare. It was a comfort to her, amidst the general din of unthinking cheerfulness, to feel that there was at least one person present who truly understood the gravity of the situation and whose sympathy was untainted by gossip or self-interest.

At length, as the evening drew to a close, and the guests began to depart, Elinor found herself alone with Mrs. Jennings once more.

“Well, my dear,” said Mrs. Jennings, with a yawn, “that was a pleasant little evening, was it not? I hope it will have helped to keep your spirits up, though I know you must be quite tired out with all this trouble. But now, you must go up and see Marianne. Has she woken yet? I hope she will take her supper. I told Cook to keep it warm for her. A good meal will do her more good than anything, depend upon it.”

“I will go to her immediately, Ma’am,” said Elinor, seizing the opportunity to escape from further conversation. “I have no doubt she is still asleep, and I shall endeavour not to disturb her. But I will certainly see that she has whatever she may require.”

“That’s a good girl,” said Mrs. Jennings. “And do tell her that I shall come up first thing in the morning to see her myself. And I daresay Colonel Brandon will be here too. He asked me particularly to send him word of her, and I told him he might just as well come himself in the morning. He’s a good soul, though a little grave for a young man. But then, he’s not so very young either, is he? Getting on a bit, I suppose. But a very good heart, and such a fine fortune, Elinor! Two thousand a year, without debt or drawback, as I was telling you. And Delaford, quite a desirable place. It would be a fine match for Marianne, a very fine match indeed. Only think of it, Elinor! No more dashing about with curricles and hunters, but a settled life, a comfortable home, and a husband who dotes on her. It is precisely what she needs, poor child, after all this sorrow. And the sooner she forgets that rascal Willoughby, the better. Out of sight, out of mind, you know. That’s my philosophy, and a very good one it is, too.”

Elinor, though inwardly weary of this persistent theme, forced herself to smile and nod. She knew that any attempt to argue with Mrs. Jennings on such a point would be utterly fruitless. Mrs. Jennings had formed her own conclusions, and nothing could sway her from them. Her mind, once fixed upon a particular course of action or a particular interpretation of events, pursued it with a singular pertinacity that was impervious to logic or remonstrance. To her, Marianne’s recovery was inextricably linked with her marriage to Colonel Brandon, and she saw no impropriety in openly planning such a match, even while the wound inflicted by another still bled so freely.

Making her escape at last, Elinor ascended the stairs once more, her heart heavy with fatigue and renewed anxiety. She paused outside Marianne’s door, listening intently. All was silent within, a silence that, though indicative of rest, was yet permeated by a profound melancholy. She entered cautiously, carrying a small nightlight which cast a soft, flickering glow around the room.

Marianne was indeed asleep, her face turned away, as before, towards the wall. Her breathing was regular, but shallow, and her brow, even in sleep, was contracted in a faint frown, as if her dreams were still haunted by the spectres of her waking sorrow. Elinor approached the bed, and after adjusting the coverings, she sat down in the same chair she had occupied earlier. She looked at her sister, her beautiful, passionate Marianne, lying there so utterly subdued, so utterly broken. The contrast between her former vivacity and her present prostration was heart-rending.

Elinor remained by her side for a long time, watching over her, her own mind a prey to a thousand uneasy reflections. She thought of their mother, who, though a woman of strong affections, possessed a composure that had rarely been shaken by the trials of life. She thought of Margaret, their younger sister, whose innocent vivacity would, for a time, at least, be shielded from the harsh realities that had so cruelly awakened Marianne. And she thought of herself, of her own struggles to maintain a semblance of cheerfulness, to uphold the dignity of their family, and to offer whatever solace she could, even when her own heart felt bruised and wounded.

The task of comforting Marianne, of drawing her back from the abyss of despair, seemed an immense, almost impossible undertaking. Her grief was so profound, so absolute, that it seemed to defy all ordinary methods of consolation. It was not merely a disappointment, but a destruction of her entire world, a shattering of her most cherished ideals. Elinor knew that time, though a slow healer, was the only true remedy, but the prospect of the intervening weeks and months, filled with Marianne’s suffering, and the constant need for Elinor’s vigilance and fortitude, stretched before her like an arduous and desolate path.

As the night deepened, and the house fell into a profound stillness, Elinor continued her silent vigil. The Constantia, still untouched, gleamed darkly on the table, a forgotten emblem of Mrs. Jennings’s well-intentioned, but ultimately ineffectual, attempts at comfort. Elinor knew that the wine, however fine, could not mend a broken heart, nor could the noisy solicitude of friends, however genuinely felt, restore a spirit so utterly crushed. It was a private sorrow, one that Marianne herself must eventually confront and, with time, slowly overcome. Elinor’s role, she knew, was simply to be present, to offer the quiet, unwavering support of sisterly affection, and to protect Marianne as best she could from the intrusions of a world that, however well-meaning, seemed incapable of understanding the depth of her sensitive nature.

She was startled from her reverie by a faint moan from Marianne. Her sister stirred, and slowly opened her eyes. They were heavy with sleep, but even in their dimness, Elinor could perceive the returning shadow of her grief. Marianne’s gaze met Elinor’s, and for a moment, a flicker of awareness passed across her features.

“Elinor,” she whispered, her voice still weak, “you are still here.”

“Yes, my love,” replied Elinor, leaning closer. “I have been here all this while. How do you feel?”

Marianne sighed, a long, weary sound that seemed to carry the weight of her entire being. “As if I have awoken to a dream, more dreadful than any nightmare. It is true, then. All of it. He is gone. He loves another. I am abandoned.” Her voice broke on the last word, and a fresh wave of tears began to stream down her pale cheeks.

Elinor reached out and gently stroked her hair. “It is true, my dearest, that you have suffered a terrible disappointment. But you are not abandoned. You have me, and Mama, and Margaret, and many who truly love you. We will help you through this, Marianne. You are not alone.”

Marianne shook her head, her face buried in her hands. “You do not understand, Elinor. You cannot. To lose him, to have him prove so false, so cruel! It is not merely disappointment; it is the utter annihilation of every hope, every prospect of happiness. I have lived only for him. Now, there is nothing. Nothing left for me in this world.”

“That is not true, Marianne,” said Elinor, with a firmness she scarcely felt. “Life is not so easily extinguished. There is much yet to live for, many duties to perform, many affections to cherish. Your happiness does not, cannot, depend solely upon one individual, however dear he may have been. You must believe that. You must strive to believe it, for your own sake.”

Marianne made no reply, but her sobs, though quieter, continued to shake her fragile frame. Elinor knew that arguments, however rational, were useless at this moment. Marianne’s grief was too raw, too overwhelming, to be touched by reason. She could only offer the silent comfort of her presence, the gentle caress of her hand, and the unwavering assurance of her love.

She remained there, seated by her sister’s bedside, through the remainder of the night, watching the slow progress of the hours, and listening to the mournful sounds of Marianne’s sorrow. The night was long and arduous, a testament to the profound suffering that had fallen upon their quiet household. As the first faint streaks of dawn began to appear in the sky, painting the windows with a pale, ethereal light, Elinor felt a weariness settle upon her that was deeper than mere physical exhaustion. It was the weariness of the spirit, the burden of shared sorrow, and the silent, ceaseless effort of maintaining composure in the face of so much distress. She knew that the coming days would be no less challenging, and that her strength, both mental and physical, would be tried to its utmost limits. But as she looked at her sister, still lost in the depths of her grief, Elinor knew that she could not, and would not, falter. Her duty, her love, and her sense of responsibility would sustain her, however long and difficult the path ahead might prove to be. Elinor waited, her heart aching for her sister, whose silent anguish seemed almost a tangible presence in the dimly lit chamber. At length, a low, stifled sob escaped Marianne, and she shifted slightly, turning her face yet further into the pillow, as if to bury her sorrow from all observation, even from the sympathetic gaze of her sister.

“Marianne, my dearest,” Elinor resumed, her voice softer still, “you must not utterly give way to this despair. It is indeed a heavy blow, a cruel disappointment, but time, my love, and reason, will soften its sharpness. Mrs. Jennings, though her methods may sometimes appear a little blunt, has a truly kind heart, and means only to alleviate your suffering. She hopes this wine might give you some strength.”

A faint, almost inaudible murmur was Marianne’s only reply, a sound so expressive of utter wretchedness that Elinor felt her own fortitude begin to waver. To press the matter further, to insist upon a restorative which was clearly repugnant to her sister’s present frame of mind, seemed a pointless cruelty. She rose, and drawing the curtain slightly to admit a sliver of the fading daylight, sat down once more, contenting herself with merely being present, a silent, watchful guardian to a grief so profound it seemed to defy all ordinary consolation. The stillness of the room, broken only by Marianne’s occasional, involuntary sigh, was oppressive. Elinor’s thoughts, however, were far from tranquil. She revolved in her mind the entire melancholy history, recalling Willoughby’s engaging manners, his seemingly sincere professions, the ardour of Marianne’s attachment, and the shocking abruptness of his desertion. It was a tale of such unfeeling profligacy on his part, and such unmerited suffering on hers, that Elinor found it difficult to reconcile with any notion of justice or consistent human nature. That a young man, so favoured by fortune and possessing such accomplishments, should descend to such deliberate cruelty, filled her with a mixture of indignation and bewildered sorrow.

After a considerable silence, Marianne spoke, her voice muffled by the pillow, yet carrying a tone of such deep, unutterable pain that Elinor started. “Leave me, Elinor,” she whispered, “I beg you, leave me. I am not fit for human society. My heart is broken; my life is ruined. Do not stay to witness a spectacle so piteous, so contemptible.”

“Contemptible, Marianne!” exclaimed Elinor, her gentle patience giving way to a more energetic sentiment. “Never call your honest, though unhappily placed, affections contemptible. Your error lay in the excess of your feelings, not in their nature. To suffer deeply is not to be contemptible, my love, but to possess a sensibility which, though at present a source of acute pain, is yet a noble and generous quality. I will not leave you. I have promised myself, and I have promised Mrs. Jennings, that I would endeavour to soothe you, and I will not forsake you in your hour of greatest need.”

Marianne made no further protest, and Elinor, observing that her sister had at last closed her eyes, and that the tremor which had agitated her earlier seemed to have subsided into a more profound, though still sorrowful, stillness, hoped that sleep might at last bring some brief respite. She remained by the bedside, her hand resting lightly on Marianne’s forehead, where she could feel the feverish heat of her distress. The wine remained untouched upon the table, a testament to the inefficacy of material comforts against a wounded spirit.

When, after some time, Elinor perceived that Marianne was indeed sleeping, or at least in a state of semi-consciousness that approached it, she carefully withdrew from the room. Her return to the drawing-room was met with an immediate, eager inquiry from Mrs. Jennings.

“Well, my dear,” cried that lady, bustling forward, “how is she? Did she take the Constantia? Did it do her good? I warrant it did! Nothing like a good glass of wine to set one up, particularly when one has had a shock.”

“She has fallen asleep, Ma’am,” replied Elinor, striving to infuse her tone with as much cheerfulness as she could command, “and I trust that rest will prove more efficacious than any wine at present. She did not take the Constantia, but I left it by her bedside, should she awake and feel inclined for it.”

“Asleep!” exclaimed Mrs. Jennings, her face falling slightly, though quickly recovering its usual good humour. “Well, that is something, to be sure. But I hope she will wake up for her supper. I have sent for a nice little chicken, and some broth. She must eat, you know, Elinor, or she will be quite pulled down. And what did she say? Did she seem more herself?”

“She spoke very little, Ma’am,” said Elinor, “and her spirits are naturally very low. It will require time, and much quiet, to restore her to any degree of cheerfulness.”

“Ah, quiet!” cried Mrs. Jennings, with a little laugh. “Well, we can’t have too much of that now, can we? But a few friends will soon be here, and that will make a little change for her, I hope. Sir John and my Charlotte will be here, and Colonel Brandon, I trust. He is such a good creature, and will be so concerned. I must tell him all about it directly. He’ll be quite shocked, poor man, but I dare say he won’t be surprised. He always said Willoughby was a sad dog, a regular roué, though he never said so to her face, of course. But he had his suspicions, I’ll be bound. And now, as I was saying, it will be all for the best, for Colonel Brandon, you know. He’ll have her yet! And a thousand times better match, I say, than that heartless fellow, Willoughby. Two thousand a year, and Delaford, a charming place indeed, as I was telling you before. And the little love-child, well, that’s neither here nor there, when all’s said and done. She can be put out to nurse, or sent away to school, and then what does it signify? Nothing at all, my dear, nothing at all.”

Elinor listened to this voluble speech with a forced smile, her mind recoiling from the indelicacy of such open speculation upon her sister’s future, and particularly upon a possibility so premature and, at present, so utterly repugnant to Marianne’s feelings. She could not, however, interrupt Mrs. Jennings’s flow of cheerful, if somewhat unfeeling, observations. It was her way, and to argue with her upon such a point would only create vexation without achieving any useful purpose. She could only hope that when the company arrived, Mrs. Jennings’s natural volubility might be somewhat tempered by the presence of others, or at least that her remarks might be directed away from the more sensitive aspects of the recent disaster.

Presently, a carriage was heard drawing up to the door, and in a few moments, Sir John Middleton and Mrs. Palmer made their boisterous entrance, followed by the more sedate figure of Colonel Brandon. Mrs. Palmer, with her customary heedless good nature, rushed in, exclaiming, “Oh, Mrs. Jennings! Oh, Elinor! Have you heard? Is it true? About Miss Marianne? My sister told me, but I would not believe it! Such a dreadful thing! And Mr. Willoughby too! I always thought him such a charming young man, though mamma said he had a wild eye. But then, mamma always thinks everyone has a wild eye if they laugh a little too much, you know. Is she very ill? Is she going to die?”

Elinor, accustomed to Mrs. Palmer’s exaggerated expressions and singular want of tact, endeavoured to reassure her. “No, Mrs. Palmer, she is not dying, though she is naturally in a state of great distress. She is resting in her room, and I trust that she will be much better after a night’s sleep.”

“Resting!” cried Sir John, who had been listening with an air of profound, though fleeting, concern. “Poor girl! I told you, Nancy, she would take it hard. But it’s no use fretting. She’s better rid of him, I say. A rascal! A confounded rascal! And to think I brought him to Barton! I shall never forgive myself. But never mind, there are other fish in the sea, as the saying goes. And a better catch too, eh, Jennings?” He winked broadly at Mrs. Jennings, who, in her turn, gave him a significant nod and a knowing smile.
