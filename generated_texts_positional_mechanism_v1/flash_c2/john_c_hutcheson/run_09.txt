Fail in any of these, and your command will be nothing but a floating quarrel, a source of endless trouble."

He spoke also of Macdougall, though not in the accusatory tone he had employed on deck. Rather, he spoke with a sigh, as if discussing a difficult but unavoidable fact of life at sea. "Mr. Macdougall is a capable seaman in many respects, Mr. Leigh," he began, swirling the port in his glass, "strong in the hand and not without a certain rugged competence. But he is a man of limited horizons, set in his ways, and deeply resentful of any challenge to his authority, perceived or otherwise. He sees the world, I fear, through a very narrow lens, and has little patience for innovation or for those who do not fit neatly into his established view of things. His resentment of your presence, particularly your youth and obvious aptitude, is, regrettably, inevitable. It is a challenge you must face, not with open defiance, for that merely feeds his rancour, but with quiet competence and unyielding adherence to duty. Let your work speak for itself, and let his venom fall on deaf ears. Do not give him cause to fault your actions, and he will eventually be forced to respect your position, if not your person."

His words confirmed my own assessment, and I vowed to follow his counsel to the letter. We conversed thus for nearly an hour, discussing everything from celestial navigation to the proper storage of provisions, and the captain even shared a few amusing anecdotes from his youth, which made me laugh heartily. It was a singular privilege, and one I knew few apprentices, particularly on their first voyage, would ever experience. Finally, as the ship's bell chimed for seven bells, indicating half past three, Captain Billings rose from the table. "Well, Mr. Leigh," he said, with a final, encouraging smile, "I trust you found your dinner agreeable. Now, I must attend to certain matters of the ship's log, and you, no doubt, have duties awaiting you. Remember my words, and hold fast to your principles. Good night, and good watch." I thanked him profusely for his kindness and hospitality, feeling a surge of loyalty and admiration for this remarkable man, and departed the cabin, the rich scent of port and polished mahogany lingering in my nostrils.

The contrast between the captain's private world and the raw reality of the deck was striking. The cool sea air, the creak of the timbers, the distant shouts of the watch, all served to remind me that I was on a ship at sea, not in a gentleman's drawing-room. As I made my way back to the apprentice's cabin, the light was fading from the western sky, painting the clouds in hues of orange and purple. Jorrocks was still there, now engaged in mending a tear in his canvas sea-bag, his fingers deft and surprisingly nimble for a man of his bulk. He looked up expectantly as I entered. "Well, my lad?" he inquired, a knowing look on his face, "how went the grand dinner with the skipper? Did he fill your head with tales of derring-do, and your belly with fine grog?"

I recounted the evening's events to him, omitting none of the details, save perhaps the steward’s obsequiousness, which seemed too minor a point to dwell upon. Jorrocks listened with rapt attention, nodding occasionally, a thoughtful expression on his usually cheerful countenance. When I finished, he let out a low whistle. "Well, I'll be blowed!" he exclaimed, setting aside his needle and thread. "The old man's taken a shine to you, that's plain as the nose on your face. And he's right about that Macdougall, too. He's a sour old codger, always has been, and he won't change his spots for no man. But you've got the skipper's ear now, and that's a powerful thing, Mister Leigh, a powerful thing indeed. Just mind you don't let it go to your head, and remember what he said about working hard and being fair. That's the secret to keeping the respect of the hands, even if you can't get it from the likes o' Macdougall."

His advice, though less eloquent than the captain's, was equally profound in its own way, echoing the same essential truths. I felt a renewed sense of purpose, a determination to prove worthy of the captain's trust and to meet Macdougall’s malice with unassailable competence. The following days, indeed, proved the wisdom of both men's counsel. Macdougall's animosity, though simmering beneath the surface, became palpable, a constant, oppressive presence. He rarely addressed me directly, preferring to communicate through Ohlsen, the second mate, or by barking orders that were clearly intended for me at some unfortunate ordinary seaman standing nearby, all the while casting venomous glances in my direction. When he did speak to me, his tone was invariably sarcastic, his words laced with thinly veiled contempt, often accompanied by a sneering smile that sent a shiver of distaste down my spine.

"Well, Mister Officer," he would drawl, his Scottish burr thick with scorn, "are ye quite sure ye can manage to coil that rope without getting your delicate hands soiled? Perhaps ye'd prefer the steward to fetch ye a pair o' kid gloves?" Or, "Mind ye don't scuff the quarterdeck with your fancy boots, Mister Leigh; the captain wouldnae be pleased if his protégé were to mar his pristine planking." Each barb was delivered just loudly enough for those nearby to hear, often eliciting a nervous glance or a suppressed chuckle from some of the less sympathetic members of the crew, particularly from the steward, who seemed to revel in Macdougall's antagonism towards me.

I, however, remembered the captain's advice and Jorrocks's admonitions. I met Macdougall's provocations with a quiet resolve, focusing on my duties with meticulous care and refusing to rise to his bait. I coiled ropes with extra precision, learned the intricacies of every knot, and applied myself diligently to the myriad tasks that fell to a third officer. I made it a point to lend a hand wherever needed, even on tasks that might typically be considered below my rank, recalling Jorrocks's earlier words about avoiding a "dog's berth." I spent hours in the chartroom, poring over the captain's navigation books, practicing my calculations, and familiarizing myself with the ship's logs, ensuring my own daily reckoning was impeccably kept, as the captain had desired. I even made an effort to engage with the crew, following Jorrocks's advice to be friendly with Pat Doolan, the cook, who indeed provided me with many a welcome pannikin of hot coffee during my cold morning watches, his hearty jokes always lifting my spirits. I observed Haxell, the carpenter, at his quiet, steady work, and learned from Sails, the sail-maker, the art of mending canvas, always with an eye to the practical knowledge that would make me a more capable officer.

This unwavering dedication to my duties, and my refusal to engage in open conflict, seemed only to fuel Macdougall's resentment, for he found himself with little genuine fault to find, though he often invented minor transgressions or exaggerated trivial mistakes. He would loom over me as I took my sights, watching my every movement with a hawk-like intensity, hoping to catch me in an error. But with the captain's instruction and my own earnest application, my work remained sound. Each day, when Captain Billings would inspect my logbook and compare my reckoning with Macdougall's, he would nod approvingly at my figures, sometimes even offering a word of praise, which, I knew, only served to deepen the first mate’s already festering animosity.

One particularly blustery afternoon, as the Esmeralda pitched and rolled through a choppy sea, Macdougall, who was in charge of the deck, decided to inspect the stowage of some spare spars in the waist of the ship. He was in an especially foul mood, his face, usually ruddy, now a dark purplish hue with ill temper. I was overseeing a group of seamen who were re-securing a boat on its davits, a task that required careful coordination in the rising swell. Macdougall approached, his heavy boots stamping on the wet planks, and without a word of warning, kicked at a rope that had been temporarily coiled near my feet, sending it sprawling across the deck. "What's this slovenly work, Mister Leigh?" he bellowed, his voice raw with fury, "Are ye so blind ye cannae see a tripping hazard when it's under your nose? Do ye think this is a pleasure yacht for gentlemen to loll about in, with their fancy navigation tricks? This is a ship, sir, and sloppiness will not be tolerated!"

The suddenness of his outburst, and the sheer vehemence of his tone, caught me off guard. My face flushed with indignation, and for a moment, I was tempted to retort with equal sharpness, to remind him of the captain's favour and my diligence. But then I recalled Captain Billings's advice: "not with open defiance, for that merely feeds his rancour." I took a deep breath, and though my voice trembled slightly, I replied, "My apologies, Mr. Macdougall. The rope was temporarily placed there while we secured the boat. It will be properly coiled immediately." I bent down, my hands moving swiftly, though my heart was pounding with suppressed anger. The seamen around us exchanged nervous glances, and I could feel their eyes on me, watching how I would handle the mate's bullying.

Macdougall, clearly disappointed by my lack of defiance, snorted derisively. "Aye, see that ye do, Mister Leigh," he sneered, "for the next time I find such negligence, I shall see to it that ye learn a lesson ye won't soon forget. There'll be no fancy dining for a man who cannae keep a clean deck!" He stalked off then, muttering imprecations under his breath, leaving me to contend with the aftermath of his vitriol. As soon as he was out of earshot, one of the older seamen, a grizzled fellow named O'Malley, who had been quietly observing, leaned closer to me. "Don't you mind him, Mister Leigh," he said, his voice low and sympathetic, "he's naught but a bully, and he's only doing it because the skipper's taken a fancy to you. He doesn't like anyone better than himself, especially a young gentleman like you who knows his sums better than he does."

O'Malley's words, though simple, were a profound comfort, reinforcing my determination to endure Macdougall's petty persecutions with stoicism and diligence. It was then I realized that the captain's favour, while making me a target for the first mate, had also, perhaps unintentionally, earned me the quiet respect and sympathy of many of the crew, those who valued fairness and competence over bluster and malice. This realization, more than any amount of praise, solidified my resolve. I would not allow Macdougall to break me, nor would I stoop to his level. I would prove my worth, not by winning arguments or engaging in petty squabbles, but by demonstrating, day in and day out, that I was a capable and dedicated officer, one fit to command, regardless of the first mate’s sour disposition. The Esmeralda, a little world within herself, was a crucible in which character was forged, and I knew that my time aboard her, under the watchful eyes of Captain Billings and the malevolent glare of Macdougall, would either make me or break me as a sailor and a man. And I was determined, with every fiber of my being, that it would make me. The voyage had only just begun, and the currents of human conflict, as treacherous as any ocean swell, were already running strong. I squared my shoulders, adjusted my cap, and set about ensuring the boat was indeed secured, and the offending rope coiled with exemplary neatness, all the while bracing myself for whatever fresh malice the morrow might bring. The sea, I was learning, was not just about winds and tides; it was also about navigating the complex and often turbulent waters of human nature.

The sun, though still bright, was beginning its slow descent towards the western horizon, casting long, wavering shadows across the deck as the Esmeralda steadily cut through the placid waters. The offending rope, now coiled with a precision that would have satisfied even the most fastidious boatswain, lay neatly beside the launch, a silent testament to my resolve and to Macdougall's petty tyranny. O'Malley, having delivered his brief but potent counsel, had quietly returned to his duties, leaving me to my thoughts. His words had been a balm, a confirmation that my instincts were not entirely mistaken; the contempt I felt for Macdougall’s methods was not merely the arrogance of youth, but a righteous indignation shared, however silently, by others on board. This realization, coupled with the captain's staunch backing, fortified me against the tide of the first mate's ill-will, transforming what might have been a debilitating humiliation into a renewed determination to excel.

I spent the remainder of the watch attending to my duties with a meticulousness that, I hoped, would leave no crack for Macdougall’s malice to seep through. I supervised the clearing of the deck, ensuring every loose rope was secured, every tool stowed, and every surface wiped clean of sea-salt and grime. My instructions were firm but fair, mirroring, I trusted, the captain's own bearing, and I found that the men responded with a quiet efficiency, seemingly appreciative of a command that was both clear and consistent. Ohlsen, the second mate, a man whose easy-going nature Jorrocks had rightly praised, appeared on deck to relieve me, his usual pleasant smile gracing his sun-burnt face. "All shipshape, Mister Leigh?" he inquired, with a genial nod. "Aye, sir," I replied, "all secured and in order." He glanced towards the perfectly coiled rope by the launch, and a faint, almost imperceptible smirk touched his lips, indicating, I thought, that he too had witnessed or been informed of the earlier exchange with Macdougall. He offered no comment, however, simply taking up his position, his hands clasped behind his back, as I made my respectful departure.

My hunger, which had been momentarily eclipsed by the tension of the confrontation, now asserted itself with undeniable force. It was six bells, and the captain had, with unprecedented kindness, invited me to dine in the cabin. The thought of this privilege, of sitting at the captain's table rather than partaking of the "second table" with the second mate and other junior officers, brought a quiet satisfaction, even as I recognized it as another wedge driven between Macdougall and myself. As I descended the companionway, the rich aroma of Pat Doolan’s cooking, a potent mix of stewed meat and boiling potatoes, wafted up from the galley, promising a hearty repast. Doolan, as Jorrocks had so shrewdly observed, was indeed a treasure, his culinary skills a welcome antidote to the otherwise monotonous fare of a long voyage.

Upon entering the captain's cabin, I found Captain Billings already seated, his formidable presence softened by an air of hospitable expectation. The table, though small, was laid with a crisp white cloth and gleaming silverware, a stark contrast to the rougher settings of the junior officers’ mess. "Come in, Mister Leigh, come in," the captain boomed, his voice devoid of its usual parade-ground sharpness. "Take your seat, my lad. Doolan has outdone himself tonight, I warrant." I saluted and took the chair opposite him, feeling a surge of youthful excitement at this rare honour. The meal commenced, a comfortable silence punctuated by the clinking of cutlery and the gentle creak of the ship. Captain Billings, between mouthfuls of succulent beef, spoke of various nautical matters, testing my knowledge of navigation and seamanship, and listening with genuine interest to my answers. He inquired about my studies, my aspirations, and even my family, treating me not merely as a subordinate but as a young man of potential, worthy of his personal attention.

This interaction, so different from the brusque commands and professional distance I had become accustomed to, further cemented my admiration for Captain Billings. He was, I realized, not just a skilled mariner, but a man of considerable wisdom and an astute judge of character, qualities that only magnified Macdougall’s petty resentments. The meal was concluded with a glass of good sherry, which the captain insisted I partake of, and a brief discussion of the next day's course. As I rose to leave, feeling both physically and mentally replenished, Captain Billings held up a hand. "Mister Leigh," he said, his blue eyes twinkling, "I trust you find your quarters comfortable, and that your general treatment aboard the Esmeralda is satisfactory?" The question was posed with a directness that left no room for prevarication. I hesitated, for a moment considering whether to voice my grievances against Macdougall, but decided against it. To complain would be to descend to the first mate's level, to acknowledge that his malice had truly affected me. "Entirely satisfactory, Captain," I replied, perhaps a little too quickly, "I am most grateful for your kindness, sir, and for this excellent meal." The captain nodded, a knowing smile playing on his lips, indicating, I believed, that he was perfectly aware of the undercurrents of animosity aboard his ship, and that my stoicism was not lost upon him.

I retired to my berth, the small cabin I shared with Ohlsen, the second mate, with a sense of quiet triumph. Macdougall’s barbs, though sharp, had failed to penetrate my newfound armour of self-possession. The next few days settled into a routine that, while busy, was not without its moments of quiet satisfaction. My responsibilities grew, with Captain Billings entrusting me with increasingly complex navigational calculations and a greater share of watch-keeping duties. This, of course, did not escape Macdougall’s notice, nor did it temper his animosity. Indeed, his resentment seemed to fester, erupting in small, almost imperceptible ways, designed to undermine my authority or to catch me in a perceived error. He would assign me tasks that were beneath my station, such as inspecting the bilges for leaks, or supervising the scrubbing of the main deck, duties typically delegated to an ordinary seaman rather than a third officer. Yet, I performed them all without complaint, with a thoroughness that often left him without any legitimate ground for fault-finding.

One morning, during my watch, a particularly stubborn knot had formed in a main sheet, making it difficult to adjust the sail. I was in the process of untangling it, my fingers working deftly, when Macdougall, who had been pacing the poop deck with his customary scowl, descended to the waist of the ship. "Hoot, mon," he sneered, his voice dripping with sarcasm, "can ye no' even manage a simple knot, Mister Leigh? Perhaps ye should stick to yer fancy sums and leave the honest work to proper sailors!" He stood over me, his shadow falling across my work, his eyes glinting with malicious amusement. I looked up, meeting his gaze directly. "The knot is a particularly tight one, Mr. Macdougall," I said, my voice even, "but it will yield." I returned to my task, ignoring his presence, concentrating on the intricate intertwining of the ropes. After a moment, he let out a frustrated grunt and stalked off, clearly annoyed that his baiting had not provoked the desired reaction. The men working nearby, who had paused momentarily, resumed their labour, a few of them exchanging knowing glances, a silent acknowledgement of the ongoing skirmish between the first mate and myself.

Jorrocks, ever the astute observer of human nature, approached me later that day, during the dinner hour in the mess. "You handled him well, Mister Leigh," he said, in a low voice, as he passed me a pannikin of fresh water. "He's like a wee terrier, that one; the more ye kick at him, the more he barks. Best to just let him yap and carry on with yer business." I smiled, grateful for his counsel and his unwavering support. "I try, Jorrocks," I replied, "but his constant antagonism is trying." "Aye, it would be," he conceded, "but mind ye, lad, the captain sees it, and so do the men. They know who's the true officer and who's just a blusterin' windbag." His words, once again, provided a much-needed boost to my morale, reinforcing the idea that my steadfastness was being recognized, even if silently.

The weather, which had been fair for the initial days of our voyage, began to show signs of a change. The sky, which had been a clear, unblemished blue, gradually began to gather grey clouds on the horizon, moving in from the north-west. The wind, hitherto a steady, gentle breeze, became more fitful, gusting with increasing frequency and strength, causing the Esmeralda to roll and pitch with a more pronounced motion. The barometer, consulted religiously by Captain Billings, showed a steady decline. "We're in for a blow, Mister Leigh," the captain informed me one afternoon, as I reported for my navigational duties. "A proper Nor'wester, by the look of it. See that everything is well secured on deck, and that the hatches are battened down tight. I want no surprises when the squall hits." I acknowledged his order, my professional instincts sharpening at the prospect of rough weather. This was the true test of a sailor, not petty squabbles, but the elemental power of the sea.

I spent the next few hours meticulously supervising the preparations for the approaching storm. I ensured that all loose gear was lashed down, that the boats were doubly secured, and that the spare canvas was stowed away in the sail locker. Haxell, the carpenter, a man of few words but immense practical skill, worked diligently alongside his men, reinforcing the main hatch covers. Sails, the sail-maker, was overseeing the re-stowing of some of the lighter sails, his cheery disposition momentarily subdued by the serious work at hand. Even Pat Doolan, the cook, could be seen securing his galley pots and pans, preparing for the inevitable lurching and rolling of the vessel. It was during this period of intense activity that I truly felt the camaraderie of the crew, a shared sense of purpose binding us together against the looming threat of the storm. Differences and personal animosities seemed to recede, replaced by a collective determination to ensure the safety of the ship and all on board.

Macdougall, naturally, was also engaged in supervising the preparations, though his presence seemed to generate more tension than cooperation. He barked orders, often contradicting my own instructions, or those of Ohlsen, causing momentary confusion among the men. His

officiousness, indeed, often served only to confound the less experienced hands and even to frustrate the efforts of the more seasoned sailors, who, accustomed to the clear, concise commands of Ohlsen or the Captain, found themselves frequently at a loss as to whose instruction they ought rightly to obey. I, for my part, endeavoured to maintain a calm exterior, issuing my own orders with a quiet authority that I hoped would cut through the noise and confusion which Mr. Macdougall seemed so adept at generating. It was not a time for open defiance, I knew, but rather for a steady resolve and a quiet demonstration of sound seamanship, qualities which, I believed, were more likely to inspire confidence in the men than any amount of bluster or unnecessary shouting. Ohlsen, the second mate, a man of quiet competence, seemed to share my sentiments, and I noticed him often correcting Macdougall's more egregious errors of judgment with a casual phrase or a deft redirection of the men's efforts, doing so in such a way as to avoid direct confrontation, which would, in Macdougall's present temper, undoubtedly have led to a most unpleasant scene. This mutual understanding, unspoken though it was, created a small eddy of efficiency amidst the general turbulence of Macdougall's supervision, allowing the crucial preparations to proceed with something approaching order.

The barometer continued its relentless fall, and by late afternoon, the heavens themselves seemed to open, unleashing a deluge of rain that sheeted across the deck, turning the very air into a grey, swirling mist. The wind, no longer merely fitful, howled through the rigging with the voice of a banshee, tearing at the canvas and whipping the spray high above the mastheads. The _Esmeralda_, a staunch vessel though she was, began to labour heavily, her bows plunging deep into the oncoming seas, only to rise again with a shuddering groan, shedding tons of green water from her foredeck. I was on the first mate's watch, a duty which, under normal circumstances, I would have shared with Mr. Macdougall; but, with the increasing violence of the gale, Captain Billings had deemed it prudent for all available officers to be on deck, and so we stood, a grim quartet, on the weather-beaten poop, clinging to the stanchions as the ship bucked and plunged like a wild horse. The spray, stinging with salt and cold, lashed our faces, and the very air was filled with the roar of the wind and the thunder of the breaking waves. It was a magnificent, terrifying spectacle, one that filled my young heart with both awe and a profound sense of my own smallness in the face of such raw, untamed power. Yet, strangely, there was no fear, only a heightened awareness, a sharpening of all my senses as I concentrated on the ship's movements and the ceaseless demands of the sea.

As night fell, bringing with it a deeper intensity to the storm, the situation grew increasingly perilous. The wind velocity, by my estimation, must have been nearing hurricane force, and the seas had risen to monstrous proportions, great walls of water that seemed to tower above our mastheads before crashing down upon us with bone-jarring force. The _Esmeralda_ groaned and shuddered, her timbers protesting against the immense strains, and more than once I felt a sickening lurch as she seemed to hang suspended on the crest of a wave, only to plunge into the trough with a violent slap that sent shivers through her entire frame. We had by this time reduced sail to a mere scrap of storm-trysail, just enough to keep her head to the wind and prevent her from broaching, but even this small area of canvas was under immense pressure, and I watched it keenly, knowing that a single flaw could mean disaster. Captain Billings, a man utterly transformed by the storm, stood firmly planted by the wheel, his eyes scanning the tumultuous horizon with an intensity that seemed to pierce the very darkness, his every command sharp and decisive, cutting through the din with an unshakeable authority that gave courage to all who heard him. Ohlsen, ever steady, was close at hand, assisting the helmsman, while Macdougall, his face a pale, strained mask in the flickering light of the binnacle lamp, seemed merely to cling to the mizzenmast, shouting occasionally, but offering little in the way of coherent direction or practical assistance, a stark contrast to his earlier blustering.

Suddenly, with a report like a cannon, a section of the main topsail, which had been furled but not quite perfectly secured, ripped itself free from its gaskets, flogging wildly in the hurricane, a danger to both mast and men. The sound of the tearing canvas, sharp and violent, momentarily cut through the roar of the gale, and all eyes turned upwards in alarm. "Cut it away, you lubbers, cut it away!" shrieked Macdougall, his voice thin and high pitched above the roar of the wind, pointing frantically at the thrashing canvas, a course of action which, I knew instantly, would leave us short of essential sail once the storm abated, to say nothing of the immense danger involved in sending men aloft to hack at a flailing sail in such a tempest. Before the men could obey his reckless command, I instinctively sprang forward, seizing Jorrocks, who was nearest to me, by the arm. "No, Jorrocks, not the knife!" I shouted, my voice straining against the gale, "Stand by the halyards, and let us try to haul it down, foot by foot, before it shakes itself to ribbons! It's our only chance to save the canvas, and to avoid sending a man up to a certain death!" My voice, though young, carried an urgency that seemed to command attention, and Jorrocks, a man of true seamanship, immediately understood my intention, his eyes showing a spark of recognition and approval. He nodded vigorously, despite the wind and spray, and with a swift movement, he began to rally a few of the more experienced hands, calling out his own commands to stand by the sheets and halyards.

Captain Billings, who had been observing the scene with a keen, appraising eye, now turned to Macdougall, his face grim. "Mr. Macdougall," he said, his voice strangely calm amidst the chaos, but with an underlying steel that brooked no argument, "a ship's sail is not to be sacrificed so readily, particularly not a main topsail. Mister Leigh's quick thinking may yet save us a valuable piece of canvas, and perhaps a man or two besides, a mercy for which we shall all be grateful." The implied rebuke was clear, though delivered without any raising of voice, and Macdougall seemed to shrink back into himself, his jaw clamped shut, though his eyes still darted about with a wild, almost frightened expression. Under my direction, and with Jorrocks's experienced assistance, the men worked with a desperate efficiency, battling the wind to gain control of the flailing canvas. It was a perilous and arduous task, every inch gained costing immense effort, but slowly, painstakingly, we managed to master the rebellious sail, hauling it down inch by inch, until it lay, a crumpled but salvageable heap, upon the deck. We secured it as best we could, lashing it tightly to the boom, and though it was torn and battered, it was not beyond repair, a testament to the combined efforts and, I venture to say, my timely intervention. Jorrocks clapped me on the shoulder, a rare gesture of approval from the gruff boatswain, and his weathered face, though streaked with salt and rain, bore a look of genuine admiration.

The rest of the night was a relentless trial, a test of endurance for both the _Esmeralda_ and her crew. The wind never slackened, and the seas continued their furious assault, but with the main topsail secured and the ship riding more steadily, a sense of grim determination settled over us. I took my turn at the wheel for a spell, the heavy spokes fighting against my grip as the rudder wrestled with the immense power of the stern-quartering seas, a task requiring every ounce of strength and concentration, for a moment's inattention could have brought the ship broadside to the waves, with potentially catastrophic consequences. Captain Billings watched me closely, not with suspicion, but with a quiet approval that spurred me on, his occasional brief instructions serving only to refine my technique rather than to correct any fundamental error. I felt a surge of pride and competence as I wrestled with the elements, the true challenge of the sea proving far more rewarding than the petty struggles of personality I had hitherto endured. Macdougall, on the other hand, remained largely inactive, huddled against the companionway, his initial frantic energy having given way to a sullen, almost paralyzed state, offering only an occasional mumbled comment that was lost in the wind, a figure of increasing irrelevance in the midst of the storm's fury.

As dawn approached, a faint, grey light began to pierce the gloom, revealing a scene of battered grandeur. The sky, though still overcast, held a promise of clearing, and the wind, while still strong, seemed to have lost some of its malevolent edge. The seas, though still running high, no longer broke over the decks with such ferocity, and the _Esmeralda_, though weary and salt-encrusted, seemed to breathe a sigh of relief, rising and falling with a more regular, albeit still powerful, rhythm. Captain Billings, his face haggard with exhaustion but his eyes still bright with command, gave orders for the weary men to begin assessing the damage and making temporary repairs. "Mister Leigh," he said to me, his voice hoarse from shouting over the gale, "you handled yourself well tonight, very well indeed. Your presence of mind saved us a good sail and, more importantly, prevented undue risk to the men. I shall remember it." His words, delivered in the presence of Ohlsen and even Macdougall, were a balm to my weary spirit, far more potent than any formal commendation, and I felt a blush of pride steal over my exhausted features. Macdougall, I noticed, merely grunted, his gaze fixed on the deck, unwilling or unable to meet the captain's eye, his discomfiture a tangible presence in the strained atmosphere.

The days that followed were spent in the arduous task of repair and recovery. Haxell, the carpenter, and his men were busy shoring up weakened bulkheads and replacing splintered timbers, while Sails, the sail-maker, began the painstaking work of mending the torn main topsail, his cheery disposition gradually returning as the sun made its welcome reappearance. Even Pat Doolan, the cook, though his galley had been a scene of chaos during the height of the storm, managed to produce surprisingly nourishing meals, his cheerful banter doing much to lift the spirits of the weary crew. I found myself frequently on deck, supervising the work, or aloft, assisting the men in checking the rigging for damage, my hands growing calloused and my muscles aching from the unaccustomed labour, but my heart filled with a new sense of purpose and belonging. The captain continued to rely heavily on my assistance, often consulting me on matters of navigation and seamanship, much to the ill-concealed annoyance of Mr. Macdougall, who saw each such instance as a further encroachment upon his rightful authority. Indeed, his resentment simmered palpably beneath the surface, often manifesting itself in snappish remarks directed at me or in deliberate attempts to contradict my instructions to the men, actions which, more often than not, only served to highlight his own petty nature and my growing competence.

One afternoon, as I was assisting Haxell with the repair of a particularly troublesome piece of rail, a task which required no small degree of ingenuity, Mr. Macdougall approached, a sneer twisting his features. "Still playing at the carpenter, Mister Leigh?" he drawled, his voice thick with sarcasm. "I thought you were rated as a third officer, not a common shipwright's mate. Perhaps the captain has mistaken your talents, eh?" Haxell, a quiet man, merely paused in his work, casting a brief, disapproving glance at the first mate, but saying nothing. As I turned to leave, he added, almost as an afterthought, "And Mister Leigh, should you find occasion to dine with me upon our arrival, I would be most pleased to recount to you, over a good glass of sherry, some of my own youthful adventures in these very waters." This invitation, extended with such grace and genuine warmth, was the crowning reward for my journey, not merely a professional advancement, but an acceptance into the venerable fraternity of those who truly understood and served the sea. I left his cabin with a lightness in my step and a sense of profound contentment, ready to meet the challenges of landfall and the promise of a future shaped by the boundless horizon.

The following day, as we sailed into the clearer, warmer waters that heralded our approach to the South American coast, the air filled with the scent of distant land and the cries of sea birds, I found myself on watch with Mr. Ohlsen. We were discussing the peculiarities of the Pacific currents when Mr. Macdougall approached, his face a mask of barely concealed fury. He had evidently heard word of my impending promotion, the ship’s gossip telegraph having worked with its usual uncanny speed. "So, Mister Leigh," he sneered, coming to a halt directly before me, his hands clasped behind his back, "the captain's pet is to be given a uniform with more braid, is he? An acting second mate, no less. I suppose you think yourself quite the hero now, don't you? A mere lad, barely out of swaddling clothes, to leap over the heads of seasoned men." His voice was low, trembling with suppressed rage, but it carried a chilling edge of malice.

I met his gaze, my heart beating a steady rhythm against my ribs, determined not to flinch. "I merely strive to do my duty, Mister Macdougall," I replied, my voice calm, "and to earn the captain's confidence, as I believe any officer should."

He scoffed, a harsh, guttural sound. "Duty, you say? Confidence? It's favouritism, pure and simple, and an insult to every man who's earned his stripes fairly. But mark my words, Mister Leigh, the favour of a captain can be as fleeting as the wind. And a man who rises too fast often finds himself falling even faster. The sea has a way of humbling those who grow too proud, and there are many who will remember this, many who will see you for what you are, a pampered upstart who knows nothing of real command." He paused, his eyes burning into mine, then added, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper that was far more menacing than any shout, "And when the captain's not looking, there are ways of reminding such upstarts of their true place, ways that don't always appear in the log-book."

The veiled threat hung heavy in the air between us, a stark declaration of his undying animosity. Mr. Ohlsen, who had been quietly observing the exchange, shifted uncomfortably, his easy-going nature clearly discomfited by the palpable tension. For a moment, I considered responding in kind, letting my own indignation burst forth, but Captain Billings's words, about not allowing bitterness to poison my spirit, echoed in my mind. I took a deep breath, steeling myself. "I shall continue to do my duty, Mister Macdougall," I said, my voice firm and clear, betraying none of the inner turmoil he sought to ignite, "and I shall face whatever challenges the sea, or indeed, my fellow men, may present, with

...the steadfast purpose that Captain Billings had so recently instilled in me, even if it meant weathering the tempests of human malice as well as those of the elements. Macdougall merely grunted, a sound of contempt that seemed to rattle in his throat, and without another word, or so much as a backward glance, he turned on his heel and strode away towards the foremast, his stiff, angular figure disappearing amongst the coiled ropes and spare spars. Mr. Ohlsen let out a long, slow exhalation, as if he had been holding his breath throughout the entire exchange, and wiped a hand across his brow, though the air was mild and carried no oppressive heat. "Whew, that was a close one, Mister Leigh," he murmured, his round, usually jovial face now etched with a look of genuine concern; "he's a proper Tartar, that one, when his temper gets the better of him, and it fair does seem to get the better of him when the Captain shows favour to any but his own sort, which means just about every man aboard, I reckon."

I nodded, my gaze still fixed on the spot where Macdougall had stood, a prickle of unease lingering on my skin despite my outward composure. The threat had been thinly veiled, a serpent's hiss in the guise of advice, and I understood then that my path aboard the Esmeralda, already fraught with the usual perils of the sea, would now be doubly so, shadowed by the persistent animosity of the first mate. But a sense of resolve, stubborn and deep-seated

, had begun to crystallise within me, hardening my spirit against the ill-will which I now knew would dog my steps aboard the Esmeralda. If Mr Macdougall thought to break my spirit with his snarling threats and dark insinuations, he would find himself sorely mistaken, for I was a lad of good British stock, bred to face adversity with a stiff upper lip, and not one to be cowed by the petty tyranny of any man, be he first mate or admiral. My father, a man of unyielding principle and quiet courage, had often impressed upon me the folly of succumbing to fear, and the enduring strength to be found in upholding one’s honour, even when confronted by those who would seek to diminish it. It was this ingrained lesson, more than any other, which now stiffened my spine and lent an unshakeable determination to my gaze as I met Ohlsen’s concerned blue eyes.

"He's a difficult man, Mister Ohlsen," I observed, my voice carefully modulated to convey a calm I did not entirely feel, "and one, it seems, who holds a particular grudge against those who rise by merit rather than by favour, though I confess I cannot see how my own humble efforts could possibly be construed as a slight upon his position, which I have always held to be one of considerable responsibility and respect."

Ohlsen scratched at his sandy beard, his gaze momentarily lost somewhere beyond the ship's rail, at the shimmering expanse of the Atlantic, now placid and deceptively calm, much like the surface of a pond after a stone has disturbed

its smooth surface and then settled again, leaving only ripples to fade into nothingness.

"Aye, a stone it is, Mister Leigh," Ohlsen replied, withdrawing his gaze from the horizon and settling it upon my face, a faint, knowing smile playing about his lips, "and Macdougall, he's got a whole quarry of 'em, all ready to cast into the waters should he take a mind to it, which, by the look o' things, he's already done with you, my lad. You've got the Captain's eye, which is a blessing and a curse in equal measure aboard this vessel, for while it will protect you from the roughest edges of the first mate's ill-humour, it will also serve to sharpen the very blade he's honing for you. He's a man, Macdougall, who begrudges every speck of light that falls not upon himself, and a young man with a clear head for navigation, such as yourself, he sees as a threat to his own standing, a standing that, between you and me and the mainmast, is not as firm as he would have us believe. Many's the time I've seen him fume and fret over a reckoning that wouldn't come right, and more than once the skipper's had to quietly set him straight, though never a word said in public, mind you, for discipline must be upheld. But you, Mister Leigh, with your Sumner's methods and your calculations that hit the mark, you are a living reproach to his old ways, and a thorn in his side, as sure as the north star is fixed in the heavens. Just you wait, he'll try to find fault where there is none, or he'll set you to some task he thinks you'll bungle, just to prove his point. But don't you fret overmuch, for the skipper, he knows a good hand when he sees one, and your logbook, filled with accurate figures, will speak for itself louder than any of Macdougall's grumblings."

I listened intently to Mr. Ohlsen’s words, turning them over in my mind, for there was much truth in what he said, and the second mate, for all his easy-going manner, was a man of shrewd observation and long experience at sea. His counsel, though offered with a shrug and a sigh, was more valuable than gold, for it confirmed my own growing suspicions as to the true nature of the first mate’s enmity. It was not merely a matter of personal pique, but a deeper current of professional jealousy, a fear that his own inadequacies might be exposed by the very competence he sought to undermine. Such men, I knew, were often their own worst enemies, but they could also be dangerous adversaries to those who stood in their way. My resolution, which had been simmering beneath the surface, now boiled over into a firm conviction: I would meet Macdougall’s malice with unyielding diligence and unassailable competence, proving him wrong not with defiance, but with sheer, undeniable merit. The Esmeralda, for me, was not merely a ship bound for Callao, but a school of character, where I was destined to learn lessons far more profound than the mere art of navigation, lessons in human nature and the steadfast endurance of the spirit.

"I thank you for your frankness, Mr. Ohlsen," I said, meeting his gaze with one of quiet determination, "and I shall endeavour to bear your words in mind. Rest assured, I am not one to shirk my duties, nor to allow the sour humours of another man to deter me from performing them to the best of my ability. If Mr. Macdougall seeks to find fault, he will find none in my work, and if he seeks to humble me, he will find that I am made of sterner stuff than he perhaps imagines."

Ohlsen chuckled, a low, rumbling sound in his chest. "That's the spirit, my lad! Keep a clear head and a steady hand, and you'll do well enough. Now, I believe six bells are nigh, and the Captain will be expecting you. Don't keep him waiting, for that's one thing he doesn't cotton to, not from anyone, first mate or third officer alike."

With a final nod, I took my leave of Mr. Ohlsen, my heart braced for the evening ahead. The thought of dining in the Captain's cabin, a privilege usually reserved for the highest ranks and most seasoned mariners, filled me with a mixture of anticipation and a keen awareness of the envy it would undoubtedly provoke in Macdougall. As I made my way aft towards the Captain's quarters, the memory of the first mate's "turkey's egg" face and his thinly veiled insult about "prentice lads" brought a fresh surge of resolve. I would not allow his petty resentments to diminish the honour of this invitation.

The Captain's cabin, when I entered, was a haven of order and a surprising degree of comfort, a stark contrast to the utilitarian spaces elsewhere on the ship. A brass lamp swung gently from the ceiling, casting a warm, steady glow over a polished mahogany table, upon which a clean white cloth had been laid, along with silverware and a bottle of claret. Captain Billings, seated at the head of the table, looked up from a chart he was examining, his blue eyes, which had flashed so fiercely earlier, now held a kindly, welcoming expression. He was a man, I observed, who could command respect without needing to resort to bluster, and whose authority seemed to spring from an innate sense of justice and competence, rather than from mere rank.

"Ah, Mister Leigh, come in, come in!" he boomed, gesturing to the seat opposite him. "Just finishing a quick look at our course for the morrow. Make yourself comfortable, my boy. No need for ceremony here, though I confess I do appreciate a bit of order at the dinner table after a long day at sea. Steward!" he called, and the flabby Welshman, who had apparently been lurking just outside the door, entered with surprising alacrity, though his gaze, I noticed, flickered towards me with an unmistakable air of disapproval before he quickly lowered his eyes. "Bring Mister Leigh a glass of wine, and let's have dinner served."

The steward, with a visible effort to conceal his displeasure, poured a glass of the ruby-red claret for me, his hand shaking ever so slightly, a tremor I attributed to his alliance with Macdougall and his evident dislike of myself. I merely offered a polite "Thank you," choosing not to acknowledge his barely concealed animosity, for to do so would only give it power. As the steward retreated, Captain Billings leaned back in his chair, a contented sigh escaping his lips.

"It is not often, Mister Leigh," the Captain began, a genial smile on his face, "that I have the pleasure of such agreeable company at my table, particularly from the younger generation of officers. Too many of them these days, it seems, are more concerned with their own advancement than with the noble art of seamanship, or the proper conduct required of an officer and a gentleman. But you, my lad, you show a promising aptitude, both in your calculations and in your bearing. I had hoped to have a quiet chat with you about your ambitions, and perhaps impart a few notions of my own, gleaned from some forty years upon the fickle bosom of the sea."

Dinner was served with a promptness that suggested the cook, Pat Doolan, had been well-briefed, and the fare, though simple, was hearty and well-prepared: a succulent roast of beef, with boiled potatoes and greens, followed by a plum duff, all of which I consumed with an appetite sharpened by the sea air and the day’s events. Throughout the meal, Captain Billings engaged me in conversation, asking about my studies, my aspirations, and my experiences before joining the Esmeralda. I found myself speaking with an ease and frankness that surprised me, drawn out by the Captain's genuine interest and his unpretentious manner. He spoke of his own early days at sea, of navigating by the stars in a time before chronometers were common, and of the many ports he had visited, spinning tales of distant lands and strange peoples that painted vivid pictures in my mind. He impressed upon me the paramount importance of good navigation, of knowing one's position not only in fair weather, but in the foulest gales, when a single error could spell disaster.

"There are those, Mister Leigh," he said, spearing a piece of beef with his fork, "who rely too heavily on the instruments, and too little on their own observations and common sense. A good seaman, my boy, he uses everything at his disposal, but he trusts his own eyes and his own judgement above all else. The charts, the sextant, the chronometer, these are but tools, and like all tools, they are only as good as the hand that wields them. And as for your modern methods, Sumner's and the like, they are valuable indeed for shortening the labour, but they must be grounded in a thorough understanding of the principles, else they are but so much conjurer's trickery. You seem to have that understanding, and that, I assure you, is a rare commodity amongst some of your older shipmates, whose brains, it seems, have become rather set in their ways, like a rusted old anchor chain." He winked, and though he did not mention Macdougall by name, the implication was clear enough.

I confessed that I found immense satisfaction in the precision of navigation, in the certainty that one could, by careful observation and calculation, pinpoint one's position on the vast, featureless ocean. "It is like solving a puzzle, sir," I ventured, "a puzzle where the pieces are the sun, the stars, and the rolling waves, and the solution is our very safety."

"Precisely so, my boy, precisely so!" the Captain exclaimed, slapping the table lightly in his enthusiasm. "A puzzle, indeed, and a most vital one. You have a good head on your shoulders, Mister Leigh, a very good head. And I shall expect you to put it to good use. I mean to have you keep a daily log of the ship's reckoning, independent of Mr. Macdougall's, and we shall compare them, from time to time, for the sake of good order and to ensure we are always on the straightest path. It will be good practice for you, and a valuable check for the ship, for two heads, even if one be young and one be old, are often better than one, particularly when they are both sharp."

The Captain's words, though intended as encouragement for me, were also a clear sign that my presence was indeed unsettling to the first mate, for this direct comparison of logs was an unprecedented measure, a subtle but unmistakable vote of no confidence in Macdougall's navigational prowess. I could well imagine the resentment it would stir in his breast, a resentment that would, I knew, be directed squarely at me. But I merely bowed my head respectfully. "I shall do my utmost, sir, to justify your confidence."

As the dinner concluded and the Captain poured us both a small glass of port, he spoke more generally of life at sea, of the camaraderie and the hardships, of the sudden storms that could turn a calm sea into a boiling cauldron, and of the enduring spirit of men who faced such challenges. He offered me advice on how to handle the inevitable conflicts that arose amongst a ship's company, stressing the importance of fairness and firmness, but also of understanding and a touch of humour. "A good officer, Mister Leigh," he concluded, "is not just a master of his craft, but a leader of men, and to lead men, you must first understand them, their strengths and their weaknesses, their hopes and their fears. It is a lesson that takes a lifetime to learn, and often, one learns it best in the crucible of a long voyage."

By the time I left the Captain’s cabin, feeling invigorated and affirmed, the ship’s bell had struck eight, signalling the end of Ohlsen’s watch and the beginning of the first mate’s. The moon, a sliver of silver, was beginning to rise, casting a pale sheen on the dark waters. As I stepped onto the main deck, I saw Macdougall standing near the binnacle, his figure silhouetted against the faint glow of the compass light, his shoulders hunched, and his posture rigid. He turned slowly as he heard my footsteps, his eyes, like flint chips, catching the moonlight and glittering with cold fire. There was no need for words; his gaze alone spoke volumes of the fury that simmered within him, a fury exacerbated, I was certain, by the very sight of me emerging from the Captain’s hallowed domain.

"Enjoy your fine repast, Mister Leigh?" he rasped, his voice low and laced with sarcasm, "I trust the Captain's company was to your liking, and that you found the conversation edifying for one of your tender years. It's not every 'prentice lad gets to sup with the skipper, you know, not unless he's got a particularly silver tongue, or a remarkably pliable back, if you catch my meaning."

I met his venomous gaze with a steady, unblinking stare. "I found the Captain's company most agreeable, Mr. Macdougall, and his counsel invaluable. And as for my back, sir, it is as straight as any man's, and quite incapable of bending to flattery or to the unfair insinuations of another." I paused, letting my words hang in the cool night air. "I believe I am on the Captain's watch for the remainder of the night, sir, and I shall endeavour to ensure that all is in good order."

Macdougall’s jaw worked silently for a moment, his anger struggling for expression, but my calm demeanour and the directness of my retort seemed to momentarily disarm him. He merely grunted again, a sound of frustrated rage, and turned his back on me, his broad shoulders seeming to stiffen even further. "See that you do, Mister Leigh," he muttered, his voice barely audible above the gentle slap of waves against the hull, "for I shall be watching, and I assure you, I have a keen eye for any shortcomings, particularly in those who presume to outshine their betters."

I simply inclined my head slightly, though he could not see it, and proceeded to my duties, my heart firm, my resolution unshaken. The night passed without incident, though I was keenly aware of Macdougall's presence, a dark, brooding shadow on the quarterdeck. I checked the trim of the sails, the set of the rigging, the steering, and the lookout, ensuring that every detail was in perfect order, leaving no room for a whispered complaint or a pointed criticism. I made my hourly rounds with meticulous care, the ship’s rhythmic creaks and groans becoming a familiar symphony, the stars above a comforting, unchanging map.

The days that followed fell into a steady rhythm, each bringing its own challenges and confirmations of the complex web of relationships aboard the Esmeralda. True to Captain Billings's word, I began keeping my own detailed navigational log, meticulously recording our daily runs, the observed positions, the calculated reckonings, and the various celestial observations. This task, which I performed with great enjoyment and precision, further cemented my reputation amongst the other officers as a diligent and capable young man, and, naturally, further inflamed Macdougall’s already considerable ire. He would often cast sneering glances at my sextant when I brought it on deck, or make disparaging remarks about "book learning" versus "true seamanship," always loud enough for me to hear, but never directly addressing me, as if by avoiding a direct confrontation he could diminish the sting of his insults.

Jorrocks, my staunch ally, continued to be a fount of good-natured advice and support. We often shared our pannikins of coffee in the early mornings, when the air was crisp and the ship still slumbered, and he would regale me with tales of his seafaring life, often weaving in subtle observations about the current state of affairs aboard the Esmeralda. "He's a proper old grumbler, that Macdougall," Jorrocks confided one morning, with a sly wink, "but don't you let him get under your skin, Mister Leigh. The Captain, he knows what's what, and he sees through all that bluster. And if you ask me, the mate's just jealous, pure and simple. Can't stand the thought of a young 'un coming up and showing him a thing or two. But you keep on with your sums and your stars, my lad, for they're worth more than all his huffing and puffing when it comes to finding our way across these wide waters."

Even Pat Doolan, the cook, contributed to my sense of belonging, and his promised pannikins of hot coffee became a small but significant luxury. He was a cheerful, robust Irishman, with a twinkle in his eye and a ready jest for every occasion, and his galley, though small and perpetually warm, was often a hub of good humour. He had a knack for sensing a man’s mood, and on days when Macdougall’s dark looks were particularly pronounced, Pat would slip me an extra biscuit or a larger portion of stew, accompanied by a conspiratorial grin and a whispered "Keep your chin up, Mister Leigh, there's always a fair wind after the storm, even aboard this old tub." His simple acts of kindness, though minor in themselves, spoke volumes of the prevailing sentiment amongst the crew, who, it seemed, had little love for their first mate and quietly approved of anyone who could stand up to his bullying.

The other crew members, Haxell the carpenter and Sails the sail-maker, also proved to be as Jorrocks had described them: steady, capable men. Haxell, a man of few words, would merely nod a greeting, his eyes calm and observant, while Sails, with his hearty singing, added a much-needed leavening to the sometimes tense atmosphere. Even Mr. Ohlsen, in his watches, continued to be a source of quiet reassurance, often engaging me in discussions about navigation or seamanship, his questions always framed to draw out my knowledge rather than to test it, and his occasional advice proving invaluable in practical matters. He was a master of the understated comment, and his dry wit often served to diffuse situations that might otherwise have festered.

One afternoon, a few days out, as the Esmeralda was making steady progress under a fair breeze, Captain Billings called for me to join him on the poop deck. Macdougall was also present, ostensibly supervising the trim of the fore-and-aft sails, though his attention, I felt, was largely directed towards our conversation. The Captain had a small, well-worn telescope in his hand, and he gestured towards the distant horizon.

"Mister Leigh," he began, his voice clear and resonant, "I believe we are approaching an area where the currents can be somewhat tricky, and while the charts are clear enough, there is no substitute for an extra pair of keen eyes. I have a notion that there might be some tell-tale signs of a shoal or a submerged reef hereabouts, marked on older charts but sometimes omitted from the newer ones for reasons best known to the cartographers themselves. I would like you to keep a particularly sharp lookout over the coming watches, and to take frequent soundings, particularly during your own watch. See what you can find, and report back to me directly."

This was a clear assignment of responsibility, bypassing Macdougall entirely, and a direct challenge to my observational skills. The first mate, who had been listening with an air of barely concealed impatience, bristled visibly. "With all due respect, Captain," he interjected, his voice tight, "I am perfectly capable of supervising the lookout and the soundings. It seems an unnecessary distraction for Mister Leigh, who has his own duties to attend to, and who, with all due deference, lacks the seasoned eye for such subtle dangers."

Captain Billings turned slowly, his blue eyes hardening slightly as he regarded Macdougall. "Indeed, Mr. Macdougall, I have no doubt as to your capabilities. But I believe in fostering the skills of all my officers, and Mister Leigh, as he is to be an officer of the watch, needs to develop that 'seasoned eye' you speak of. Furthermore, a fresh pair of eyes, unburdened by preconceptions, can sometimes spot what an accustomed gaze might overlook. Do you disagree with the wisdom of a second opinion, sir, particularly in matters of the ship's safety?"

Macdougall's face flushed, and he seemed to struggle for a reply, but none came. He merely stiffened further, his lips pressed into a thin, angry line. "As you command, Captain," he finally bit out, his words clipped and resentful.

"Good," the Captain said, turning back to me, his expression softening once more. "Now, Mister Leigh, I want you to be diligent. Not alarmist, mind you, but diligent. Observe the colour of the water, the behaviour of the birds, any unusual ripples or breaks in the surface. All these can be signs. And log your soundings with precision. Take your time, and do it right."

"Aye aye, Captain," I replied, feeling a surge of pride at the trust he placed in me, and an even greater determination to prove worthy of it.

For the next few days, I threw myself into this task with renewed vigour. During my watches, I rarely left the rail, my gaze sweeping the horizon, my ears attuned to the cry of the leadsman taking soundings every half-hour. I carefully noted every change in the depth, every slight variation in the water's hue, every suspicious ripple. Macdougall, true to his word, watched me like a hawk, his eyes never leaving me for long, as if he expected me to make some egregious error that would confirm his gloomy predictions. But I was meticulous, double-checking every reading, comparing them with the charts, and making careful entries in my log.

The weather remained fair, and the sea relatively calm, making the task of observing subtle changes all the more challenging. It was during the forenoon watch, with Mr. Ohlsen in charge, that I thought I saw something. A peculiar, faint discoloration of the water, a subtle greenish tinge that seemed to pulse beneath the surface, just ahead of our bow, where the chart indicated open sea. It was easily dismissed as a trick of the light, or a passing patch of algae, but something about its consistent nature, and its position, troubled me. I called for a sounding, and the leadsman, with his practiced swing, cast the lead line.

"By the mark, twenty-five!" he cried out, his voice echoing across the deck.

Twenty-five fathoms. That was shallower than the chart indicated for this position, which showed closer to forty. My heart gave a little thump. I immediately called for another sounding, and then another, each confirming the shallower depth, and suggesting a gradual rise in the seabed. I consulted the chart again, cross-referencing my position with my recent sight. There was no marked shoal, but the consistent, if slight, discrepancy between the chart and the soundings, coupled with the faint discoloration, sent a prickle of unease down my spine.

"Mister Ohlsen!" I called, my voice betraying a hint of urgency I tried to suppress. "I believe we are in shallower water than indicated, and I've observed a possible discoloration ahead. Might I request a slight alteration of course, to port, if you please, to give us a wider berth?"

Ohlsen, ever calm, walked over to me, peering intently at the water where I pointed, and then at my logbook. He pursed his lips, his brow furrowed in thought. Macdougall, who had been on the quarterdeck overseeing some maintenance, was instantly alert, his eyes narrowed, fixed on our exchange. He sauntered over, his movements deliberately slow and mocking.

"What is it, now, Mister Leigh?" he sneered, "Found a sea-serpent, have we? Or perhaps a lost continent? Your eyes, it seems, are seeing things that aren't there, or perhaps you're simply seeing ghosts from your textbooks. The charts, I believe, indicate clear sailing."

"The soundings, Mr. Macdougall," I replied, my voice steady despite the rising tension, "indicate twenty-five fathoms, sir, where the chart shows forty. And there is a discoloration of the water that I believe warrants caution. A slight change of course would cost us nothing, and might save us a good deal of trouble."

Macdougall scoffed. "A mere five fathoms, my lad, is no cause for alarm. The bottom rises and falls, as even a landsman knows. And as for your 'discoloration,' it's likely nothing but a shadow or a cloud reflection. Are you suggesting we change course for every fanciful notion that swims into your head? Next you'll be wanting to heave-to for a school of dolphins."

Ohlsen, however, seemed to weigh my words more carefully. He was a man who understood the value of a cautious approach, and the dangers of complacency. He looked from the water to Macdougall, and then back to me, his blue eyes thoughtful. "Mister Macdougall," he said, his voice even, "it is indeed a prudent measure to err on the side of caution where the safety of the vessel is concerned. A ship's bottom is a valuable commodity, and one not easily replaced. And Mister Leigh's observations, while perhaps subtle, are consistent. I believe a minor alteration of course, as he suggests, would be no great hardship."

Before Macdougall could further protest, Captain Billings, as if sensing the impending argument, emerged from his cabin once more, perhaps drawn by the raised voices, or merely by a habit of his watch. He walked to the rail, his gaze sweeping the sea, his expression unreadable.

"What is the matter here?" he asked, his voice calm, but with an underlying note of authority that instantly quelled any further dissent.

Mr. Ohlsen quickly explained the situation, detailing my observations and the discrepancy in the soundings. Macdougall, looking distinctly uncomfortable, attempted to downplay the matter, calling it a "trifling deviation" and "the over-anxious imaginings of an inexperienced eye."

"Inexperienced or not, Mr. Macdougall," Captain Billings stated, his eyes now fixed on the first mate, "Mister Leigh has made an observation, and it is corroborated by the lead. The sea, sir, is not always as obliging as our charts would have us believe, and it is the mark of a good seaman to heed every sign, however faint. Mister Leigh," he continued, turning to me, his face showing a rare flash of approval, "I commend your vigilance. Mister Ohlsen, lay a course five degrees to port, if you please, and continue to take soundings at frequent intervals. And keep me informed, both of you."

"Aye aye, Captain!" Ohlsen and I responded in unison, a sense of quiet triumph swelling within me. Macdougall, meanwhile, stood rigid, his face a mask of barely suppressed fury, his eyes darting between me and the Captain, as if he could not quite believe what he was witnessing. The Captain's swift and decisive vindication of my judgment, and his implicit rebuke of Macdougall's dismissal, was a clear signal to all aboard that my role on the Esmeralda, far from being a mere formality, was one of real and growing importance. The small, almost imperceptible shift of the ship’s bow as it answered the helm, was, for me, a silent victory, a testament to the fact that competence, even in the face of resentment, would ultimately carry the day.

The slight alteration of course proved to be a wise decision. Over the next hour, as we continued to take soundings, the depth gradually decreased even further, reaching as shallow as twenty fathoms, before slowly deepening again as we cleared the suspected area. The discoloration, too, became more pronounced before fading back into the deep blue of the open ocean. There was no dramatic grounding, no sudden crunch, for which I was profoundly grateful, as I had no wish for such a disaster, but the evidence was clear: had we continued on our original course, we might not have struck a charted reef, but we would certainly have entered an unexpectedly shallow bank, a situation fraught with peril, especially under less favourable conditions.

Captain Billings, having observed the sequence of soundings and the gradual deepening of the water, returned to the poop deck with a satisfied expression. He paused next to me, placing a hand on my shoulder. "Well done, Mister Leigh," he said, his voice low but firm, "well done indeed. Your 'inexperienced eye,' it seems, is a remarkably sharp one. It is precisely such vigilance that keeps a ship safe and her crew out of harm's way. I shall make a note of this in the log."

The Captain's words, and the simple gesture of his hand on my shoulder, were worth more to me than any praise or promotion, for they signified a trust earned and a duty well performed. Macdougall, who had witnessed the entire exchange, looked as if he had swallowed a bitter draught, his face a thundercloud of resentment. His eyes, fixed on me, now held a new and dangerous glint, a cold, calculating malice that went beyond mere professional jealousy. I knew then that the battle between us, far from being over, had only just begun, and that every success of mine would only serve to deepen the first mate’s animosity. But I also knew, with a quiet certainty that settled deep in my bones, that I

had an ally in Captain Billings, and that for all the bluster and malice of Mr Macdougall, I would not be easily daunted. My resolve was hardening, my spirit becoming less pliant with every fresh display of the first mate's ill-will. I had come aboard the *Esmeralda* an apprentice, keen to learn and eager to prove my mettle, but also perhaps a shade too proud and green for the harsh realities of the sea. Now, however, the very venom of Macdougall's displeasure seemed to temper me, shaping me into something more robust, more determined to succeed not merely for my own advancement, but to demonstrate the sheer injustice of his unwarranted enmity.

The days that followed were not without their trials, for Mr Macdougall, having been publicly, if subtly, rebuked, seemed to find new avenues for his petty tyrannies. He would assign me the most disagreeable tasks, those that were traditionally the purview of the lowest ratings, or invent entirely new duties of a singularly unpleasant nature. One morning, for instance, I found myself supervising the scraping of rust from the anchor chains, a task usually delegated to ordinary seamen, and one which, in the humid, salt-laden air, promised a liberal coating of red dust and grime. Later that day, he ordered me to check and re-stow all the ship’s spare ropes in the forepeak, a dark, cramped, and often damp space, ensuring each coil was perfectly formed, a chore that took me hours by the dim light of a lantern, while the rest of the crew enjoyed a brief spell of leisure. He would find fault with my reports, however meticulously prepared, or dismiss my suggestions with a disdainful sniff, even when they were clearly sound and practical. Yet, through it all, I maintained a scrupulous diligence, performing every task with the utmost care, if only to deny him the satisfaction of a legitimate complaint. I would bite back the retort that sprang to my lips, remembering Jorrocks’s earlier counsel about ‘purteinding’ to humour such a man, though I found myself entirely incapable of doing so with Macdougall, for my nature rebelled against anything that savoured of sycophancy or false humility; instead, I simply carried out his orders with a quiet, efficient resolve that, I suspected, annoyed him even more than open defiance.

Captain Billings, though seemingly immersed in his own duties, possessed an uncanny knack for discerning the undercurrents of discontent aboard his ship. He would often pass by when I was

engaged in some particularly thankless task, his sharp blue eyes taking in my efforts with an almost imperceptible nod, or a brief, reassuring glance. One blustery afternoon, for instance, when Macdougall had set me to cleaning out the scuppers, a vile, greasy job that no officer, not even a third officer, should ever be put to, especially with the ship rolling heavily and the seas breaking over the rail, I felt a shadow fall across me. Looking up from the foul-smelling gunk, I saw the Captain himself, standing at the weather rail, surveying the horizon with his usual calm authority. He did not speak directly to me then, but as he turned to walk away, he paused, and his gaze, though fleeting, conveyed a silent message of understanding, almost of complicity, which warmed me more than any spoken word. Moments such as these were rare, yet they served as anchors to my resolve, reminding me that I was not entirely without powerful friends in this floating microcosm of hierarchies and resentments. They fuelled my determination to perform even the most ignoble chores with a thoroughness that would defy Macdougall’s scrutiny, to leave no crevice for his acidic fault-finding.

My meticulousness, I soon discovered, was a double-edged sword. While it prevented legitimate complaint, it also seemed to inflame the first mate’s already simmering ire. He appeared to interpret my diligent execution of his petty commands not as obedience, but as a deliberate, silent challenge, a mocking display of competence that highlighted his own lack of imagination and grace. The more perfectly I scraped rust, or coiled rope, or polished brass, the more sullen grew his demeanour, the more pointed his sneers when he believed himself out of the Captain’s earshot. He found endless opportunities to make my life difficult, and I, in turn, found endless ways to thwart his intentions by the sheer force of uncomplaining efficiency.

One evening, as the tropical dusk settled over the ocean, painting the western sky in hues of orange and purple, I was meticulously checking the ship’s lamps before they were lit, a duty that fell to the third officer. I was ensuring that each wick was trimmed just so, and that the oil reservoirs were sufficiently filled, when Macdougall approached me in the gloom of the starboard side, his figure silhouetted against the last vestiges of twilight. "Mister Leigh," he began, his voice a low growl, "I’ve been thinking about your navigation. The Captain, he seems mighty impressed with your calculations, aye? Thinks you'll be teaching us all before we reach Callao." There was a venomous edge to his feigned pleasantness that sent a shiver down my spine, for I knew what such insidious praise foreboded. "Indeed, sir," I replied, keeping my tone level, "I strive to do my best."

"Aye, well, striving's one thing, Mister Leigh, but actual ability is another," he scoffed, stepping closer, his breath smelling faintly of cheap tobacco. "The Captain's a kindly soul, and perhaps a wee bit soft in his old age, to be so taken by a lad fresh from the shore. Now, I’ve been thinking, with all this talk of new methods, and shortening calculations, perhaps you’d care to demonstrate your prowess. Tomorrow morning, before sun-up, I want you to take a set of sights, and work out our position using that fancy Sumner method you were so keen to mention. And I want the full reckoning, mind you, with all the usual checks, and no errors. We'll compare it with my own, for the Captain’s benefit, of course." He concluded with a smirk, his eyes glinting in the faint light reflecting from the distant stars.

I knew at once this was a trap. The Sumner method, though indeed a more modern and efficient way of fixing a ship’s position, was still relatively new to me, and while I understood its principles, I had not yet attained the automatic, intuitive grasp that comes only with long practice. Moreover, taking sights accurately in the pre-dawn gloom, with the ship’s motion, was a challenge even for an experienced navigator. Macdougall was undoubtedly counting on me to stumble, to provide him with concrete evidence of my supposed inadequacy, which he could then present to Captain Billings.

"Very good, sir," I said, a slight tremor in my voice that I hoped he did not detect. "I shall endeavour to do so."
"See that you do, Mister Leigh," he retorted, his voice laced with malicious satisfaction, "for mistakes at sea can cost more than pride, they can cost lives. And I'd not like to see the Captain disappointed in his new protégé." He then turned on his heel and disappeared into the shadows, leaving me alone with the silent, rolling ocean and the daunting prospect of the coming dawn.

I spent a restless night, my mind racing through the complex tables and formulae of the Sumner method. I reviewed my notes, mentally rehearsing the steps, the required entries into the nautical almanac, the careful plotting of the lines of position. Jorrocks, observing my agitation during the middle watch, inquired as to the cause. When I explained Macdougall’s challenge, the old boatswain merely nodded sagely. "Aye, I thought as much, Mister Leigh. He's a

He’s a proper old sea-dog, that Macdougall, with a temper like a squall coming up from astern, and a mind full of more twists and turns than a hawser in a gale! He don't like any man to show him up, least of all a young gentleman like yourself, with your books and your new-fangled ways. I took Jorrocks's words to heart, for indeed, the Captain’s good opinion was the only true currency in this small, floating world, and I resolved to apply myself with every fibre of my being to the task at hand. The night passed slowly, every creak and groan of the ship seeming to magnify my unease, though I tried, with what little experience I possessed, to reassure myself that I was capable of the challenge. I had studied navigation diligently, had devoured every treatise and manual I could lay my hands upon, and had spent countless hours practicing the manipulation of the sextant, both on shore and, when opportunity allowed, at sea, under the watchful, albeit sometimes impatient, eye of Captain Billings himself. Yet, the thought of Macdougall’s sneering face, of his triumphant ‘hoot, mon’ should I fail, clung to me like a damp fog. The Sumner method, with its reliance on precise timing and multiple observations to derive intersecting lines of position, demanded not only theoretical understanding but also a cool head and an unfaltering hand, especially when dealing with the ship's perpetual motion, a motion which, even in the calmest of seas, was never truly absent.

I roused myself from my bunk a good hour before the first hint of dawn, determined to be prepared. The fo’c’s’le was still wrapped in the deep slumber of the middle watch, the air thick with the mixed aromas of tar, stale tobacco, and unwashed canvas. I dressed quietly, pulling on my heavy coat against the pre-dawn chill that seeped into every corner of the *Esmeralda*. My sextant, carefully wrapped in its green baize cloth, felt heavy and cold in my hands as I made my way silently up the companionway and onto the main deck. The stars, countless and brilliant in the clear maritime sky, seemed to mock my apprehension, their steady twinkle a stark contrast to the turbulent calculations churning within my mind. A sliver of pale light, like a faint promise, was just beginning to appear on the eastern horizon, painting the sky with the barest hint of grey.

The deck was still deserted, save for Ohlsen, the second mate, who was pacing the weather side of the quarterdeck, a solitary figure against the vastness of the ocean. He nodded curtly as I approached, his gruff demeanor softened, perhaps, by the early hour or by a latent sympathy for my predicament. He was indeed, as Jorrocks had observed, an easy-going chap, though he carried the weight of many years at sea in his weathered face. "Early bird, Mister Leigh," he grunted, his voice a low rumble. "Macdougall will be out directly, waiting for you to stumble." He did not offer further advice, but his simple acknowledgement of the situation, and the slight, almost imperceptible, tilt of his head towards the eastern sky, conveyed a quiet understanding that was, in its own way, reassuring. I found the spot Jorrocks had recommended, forward of the mainmast, securing myself near the fife-rail, my eyes scanning the eastern horizon, patiently awaiting the precise moment of nautical twilight, when the stars would still be visible, yet the horizon clearly defined.

The minutes dragged on, each second an eternity, as the grey light slowly intensified, pushing back the velvet blackness of the night. I checked my chronometer, a magnificent instrument which Captain Billings had entrusted to my care, ensuring its accuracy against the ship's master chronometer each day. The precise time was paramount for the Sumner calculation, for even a few seconds' error could throw the line of position many miles off true. The *Esmeralda* rose and fell with a gentle, rhythmic motion, a deep sigh in the vast expanse of the ocean, and I concentrated on maintaining my balance, my body swaying with the ship, yet keeping my eye steady on the horizon. Then, just as the first faint blush of rose appeared above the distant rim of the world, I saw him. Macdougall emerged from the shadows of the break of the poop, his lean, angular figure silhouetted against the ever-brightening sky. He did not approach immediately, but rather stationed himself by the mizzenmast, a watchful, almost predatory, presence, his arms crossed over his chest, his gaze fixed upon me, an unmistakable smirk playing about the corners of his mouth. I could feel his eyes upon me, like a physical weight, but I forced myself to ignore him, channeling my focus entirely on the delicate instruments in my hand and the shifting light on the horizon.

"Ready, Mister Leigh?" came his voice, sharp and laced with its customary challenge, cutting through the stillness of the dawn. "The first stars will be fading soon, and the horizon will not be clear for long."

"Quite ready, Mister Macdougall," I replied, my voice, to my own surprise, steady and clear. I had rehearsed this moment countless times in my mind, anticipating his every provocation. Jorrocks's advice, to meet his jibes with a cool demeanor, was proving invaluable. I raised the sextant to my eye, the small telescope a window onto the immense world. The stars were indeed beginning to dim, their brilliance fading before the encroaching day, but still clearly discernible. I quickly selected several prominent stars, those whose declinations and sidereal hour angles were easily found in the Nautical Almanac, making my first observations. The technique, as Jorrocks had taught, involved swaying with the ship, catching the star at the precise moment it dipped to the horizon during the swing, then immediately reading the angle. It was a dance of timing and precision, requiring an almost instinctive coordination of eye and hand. I took three sights for each star, noting the time of each observation to the second, calling them out to Ohlsen, who stood nearby, a half-eaten biscuit in his hand, ready to record the data. His presence, silent and neutral, was a small comfort.

Macdougall remained where he was, his figure a dark sentinel against the growing light, undoubtedly scrutinizing my every movement, hoping to catch a flicker of hesitation, a sign of error. I felt the prickle of his gaze, but I refused to be rattled. Each observation was made with deliberate care, my breath held, my body moving as one with the ship, until the star, now a pale ghost in the morning sky, touched the nascent horizon. The ship’s gentle pitch and roll presented a constant challenge, forcing me to anticipate and adjust, but the lessons learned during my numerous practice sessions now came to my aid, guiding my hands with a precision I had not thought myself capable of. "Satisfied, Mister Leigh?" Macdougall’s voice was again a sneer, as I lowered the instrument, my hands still tingling from the intense concentration. "Or do you need to take another half-dozen to be sure? There are other duties aboard a ship, you know, besides stargazing."

"I have sufficient data, Mister Macdougall," I responded calmly, turning to Ohlsen, who was diligently inscribing the final entry in his small notebook. "If you would be so kind as to give me the chronometer readings, Mister Ohlsen."

Ohlsen, without a word, handed me the slip of paper bearing the precise times of my observations. I took it, nodded my thanks, and then, ignoring Macdougall’s continued scrutiny, made my way to the chartroom, a small, cramped cabin adjacent to the Captain’s, where charts, instruments, and the precious Nautical Almanac were kept under lock and key. This was to be the true test, the moment where theoretical knowledge met practical application, and where Macdougall hoped to see me flounder. The air in the chartroom was close, smelling of aged paper and beeswax, and the small lamp, now lit against the still-dim interior, cast a warm, yellow glow upon the chart table. I spread out my observation forms, carefully transcribed Ohlsen's notes, and retrieved the Nautical Almanac, my heart beginning to pound a little faster now that the physical exertion of taking the sights was over, and the mental gymnastics were about to begin.

The Sumner method, for all its elegance, demanded meticulous attention to detail. First, the Greenwich Mean Time of each observation had to be converted to Greenwich Hour Angle, and then the star's declination for that specific time had to be extracted from the almanac. These were straightforward, if tedious, steps. The real work began with the calculation of the 'assumed position,' a point chosen near the estimated position of the ship, from which to begin the geometric calculations. From this assumed position, one calculated the theoretical altitude and azimuth of each observed star. The difference between the observed altitude and the calculated altitude, known as the 'intercept,' gave the distance of the ship's actual position from the assumed position along a line perpendicular to the star's azimuth. Two or more such lines, plotted on a chart, would intersect at the ship’s true position. It was a beautiful, logical system, but prone to errors if any step, however small, was performed incorrectly.

I immersed myself in the figures, my mind a whirlwind of addition, subtraction, and interpolation. The pencil flew across the paper, filling columns with figures, consulting logarithmic tables for the necessary functions. I double-checked each entry, each calculation, aware that Macdougall would undoubtedly demand to see my working, searching for any slip. My brow furrowed in concentration, I worked steadily, the rhythm of the ship’s gentle rolling a subconscious companion to my mental exertions. The first line of position emerged, a clear bearing and an intercept distance. I laid it out on the chart. Then the second, and finally the third. They converged, not perfectly at a single point, for no observation or calculation in the real world is ever truly flawless, but within a small, tight triangle of uncertainty, a "cocked hat," as navigators called it, its centre representing the most probable position of the *Esmeralda*.

I breathed a sigh of relief, a deep, silent exhalation that seemed to carry with it all the pent-up tension of the last few hours. The position was good, well within acceptable navigational limits, and, more importantly, it confirmed our dead reckoning, suggesting that the current, about which I had some lingering uncertainty, was not as strong as I had feared. "The calculated position agrees with our dead reckoning within a mile and a half, Mister Macdougall," I stated, my voice firm, "which, given the ship’s motion and the conditions of observation, is quite satisfactory, and well within the accepted standards for celestial navigation. My sextant, carefully wrapped in its green baize cloth, felt heavy and cold in my hands as I made my way silently up the companionway and onto the main deck. The stars, countless and brilliant in the clear maritime sky, seemed to mock my apprehension, their steady twinkle a stark contrast to the turbulent calculations churning within my mind. A sliver of pale light, like a faint promise, was just beginning to appear on the eastern horizon, painting the sky with the barest hint of grey.

The deck was still deserted, save for Ohlsen, the second mate, who was pacing the weather side of the quarterdeck, a solitary figure against the vastness of the ocean. He nodded curtly as I approached, his gruff demeanor softened, perhaps, by the early hour or by a latent sympathy for my predicament. He was indeed, as Jorrocks had observed, an easy-going chap, though he carried the weight of many years at sea in his weathered face. "Early bird, Mister Leigh," he grunted, his voice a low rumble. "Macdougall will be out directly, waiting for you to stumble." He did not offer further advice, but his simple acknowledgement of the situation, and the slight, almost imperceptible, tilt of his head towards the eastern sky, conveyed a quiet understanding that was, in its own way, reassuring. I found the spot Jorrocks had recommended, forward of the mainmast, securing myself near the fife-rail, my eyes scanning the eastern horizon, patiently awaiting the precise moment of nautical twilight, when the stars would still be visible, yet the horizon clearly defined.

The minutes dragged on, each second an eternity, as the grey light slowly intensified, pushing back the velvet blackness of the night. I checked my chronometer, a magnificent instrument which Captain Billings had entrusted to my care, ensuring its accuracy against the ship's master chronometer each day. The precise time was paramount for the Sumner calculation, for even a few seconds' error could throw the line of position many miles off true. The *Esmeralda* rose and fell with a gentle, rhythmic motion, a deep sigh in the vast expanse of the ocean, and I concentrated on maintaining my balance, my body swaying with the ship, yet keeping my eye steady on the horizon. Then, just as the first faint blush of rose appeared above the distant rim of the world, I saw him. Macdougall emerged from the shadows of the break of the poop, his lean, angular figure silhouetted against the ever-brightening sky. He did not approach immediately, but rather stationed himself by the mizzenmast, a watchful, almost predatory, presence, his arms crossed over his chest, his gaze fixed upon me, an unmistakable smirk playing about the corners of his mouth. I could feel his eyes upon me, like a physical weight, but I forced myself to ignore him, channeling my focus entirely on the delicate instruments in my hand and the shifting light on the horizon.

"Ready, Mister Leigh?" came his voice, sharp and laced with its customary challenge, cutting through the stillness of the dawn. "The first stars will be fading soon, and the horizon will not be clear for long."

"Quite ready, Mister Macdougall," I replied, my voice, to my own surprise, steady and clear. I had rehearsed this moment countless times in my mind, anticipating his every provocation. Jorrocks's advice, to meet his jibes with a cool demeanor, was proving invaluable. I raised the sextant to my eye, the small telescope a window onto the immense world. The stars were indeed beginning to dim, their brilliance fading before the encroaching day, but still clearly discernible. I quickly selected several prominent stars, those whose declinations and sidereal hour angles were easily found in the Nautical Almanac, making my first observations. The technique, as Jorrocks had taught, involved swaying with the ship, catching the star at the precise moment it dipped to the horizon during the swing, then immediately reading the angle. It was a dance of timing and precision, requiring an almost instinctive coordination of eye and hand. I took three sights for each star, noting the time of each observation to the second, calling them out to Ohlsen, who stood nearby, a half-eaten biscuit in his hand, ready to record the data. His presence, silent and neutral, was a small comfort.

Macdougall remained where he was, his figure a dark sentinel against the growing light, undoubtedly scrutinizing my every movement, hoping to catch a flicker of hesitation, a sign of error. I felt the prickle of his gaze, but I refused to be rattled. Each observation was made with deliberate care, my breath held, my body moving as one with the ship, until the star, now a pale ghost in the morning sky, touched the nascent horizon. The ship’s gentle pitch and roll presented a constant challenge, forcing me to anticipate and adjust, but the lessons learned during my numerous practice sessions now came to my aid, guiding my hands with a precision I had not thought myself capable of. I captured Polaris, its steady light a beacon in the northern sky, then Vega, its azure brilliance still piercing the encroaching dawn, and finally, Arcturus, a fiery orange jewel, before the light became too strong to discern them reliably.

"Satisfied, Mister Leigh?" Macdougall’s voice was again a sneer, as I lowered the instrument, my hands still tingling from the intense concentration. "Or do you need to take another half-dozen to be sure? There are other duties aboard a ship, you know, besides stargazing."

"I have sufficient data, Mister Macdougall," I replied calmly, turning to Ohlsen, who was diligently inscribing the final entry in his small notebook. "If you would be so kind as to give me the chronometer readings, Mister Ohlsen."

Ohlsen, without a word, handed me the slip of paper bearing the precise times of my observations. I took it, nodded my thanks, and then, ignoring Macdougall’s continued scrutiny, made my way to the chartroom, a small, cramped cabin adjacent to the Captain’s, where charts, instruments, and the precious Nautical Almanac were kept under lock and key. This was to be the true test, the moment where theoretical knowledge met practical application, and where Macdougall hoped to see me flounder. The air in the chartroom was close, smelling of aged paper and beeswax, and the small lamp, now lit against the still-dim interior, cast a warm, yellow glow upon the chart table. I spread out my observation forms, carefully transcribed Ohlsen's notes, and retrieved the Nautical Almanac, my heart beginning to pound a little faster now that the physical exertion of taking the sights was over, and the mental gymnastics were about to begin.
