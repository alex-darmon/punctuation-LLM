"You must have mistaken me for some other person," Clara stammered, backing a step away from the hotel owner. "Who do you think I am?" Now get out and don't let me catch you in the hotel ever again." Observing the antique key in Clara's hand he reached out and jerked it from her. Clara felt a hot flush creep up her neck. A "gossip sheet"! The _City Chronicle_ was an institution, a respected voice, even if it did sometimes dabble in the juicier stories of the town's elite. Her uncle, Arthur Monroe, was a journalist of integrity, and she, Clara, was his keenest apprentice. She swallowed the angry retort that sprang to her lips, realizing that arguing with Mr. Thorne in his current state of fury would be utterly futile. His face was still a mottled red, his eyes blazing with a hatred that seemed to extend beyond just her, to encompass her whole family, and indeed, her entire profession.

This was mostly true, as Arthur Monroe had merely encouraged her journalistic instincts, never giving her specific instructions for _this_ investigation. He had, however, often told her that a reporter’s best tool was her own ingenuity, and sometimes, a little well-placed subterfuge. But explaining the nuances of investigative journalism to a man whose mind was clearly made up was like trying to stop a runaway train with a silk handkerchief.

Mr. Thorne merely snorted, a derisive sound that dismissed her words as easily as he had dismissed her presence. "Underhanded methods? You call sneaking around my hotel, prying into private affairs, anything but underhanded? You call that antique key a legitimate means of entry? I

Get out before I call the authorities and have you arrested for trespassing and attempted breaking and entering!"

Clara stood her ground for another moment, her chin held high, despite the tremor in her knees. She had lost her primary means of entry, and with it, her immediate objective. For now, discretion was the better part of valor. She would retreat, but she would not surrender.

"You'll regret this, Mr. Thorne," she said, her voice low but firm, refusing to let her anger show. "The truth always comes out, one way or another."

He merely laughed, a harsh, humorless sound. "Get out!" he repeated, pointing a trembling finger toward the main lobby. "And don't let me catch you on my property again, or I assure you, your 'gossip sheet' won't be the only thing getting headlines."

The old custodian, who had been an impassive observer until now, seemed to shrink further into his seat, avoiding eye contact. Then, with as much dignity as she could muster, she turned and marched down the narrow hall, her footsteps echoing sharply on the polished floor.

As she passed through the side door and re-entered the bustling lobby, Clara felt a surge of indignation mix with a grim determination. Her plan had been meticulous, her access almost secured, and now it was all ruined by one irate hotel owner who clearly had something significant to hide. The antique key, which had been entrusted to her with such solemnity, was now in the hands of her adversary. A broken promise, indeed.

She spotted the main entrance and headed directly for it, her mind racing. She could not afford to be seen loitering. Every employee in the hotel, from the desk clerk to the bellhops, now seemed to eye her with suspicion. She imagined Mr. Thorne was already making calls,

making calls, alerting every staff member to her supposed nefarious intentions. Her jaw tightened. Let him call. She had been accused of worse, and her father, the editor-in-chief of the *Monitor*, always said that if you weren't making enemies, you weren't digging deep enough. The *Monitor*, though not quite as "yellow" as the *Riverview Star* Mr. Thorne seemed to be thinking of, certainly wasn't afraid to print an uncomfortable truth. She smiled grimly. Perhaps Mr. Thorne was worried about a truth of his own.

She pushed through the revolving door and stepped out into the crisp mountain air. The late afternoon sun cast long shadows across the snow-covered grounds, and the distant peaks shimmered under a pale blue sky. It was a beautiful day, a stark contrast to the boiling fury that simmered within her. She pulled her wool coat tighter, not against the cold, but against the sting of Mr. Thorne’s words and the frustration of her thwarted mission.

Her first instinct was to find a quiet corner, a place where she could think without the watchful eyes of hotel staff or the biting winds of accusation. The small diner across the street, its windows steaming invitingly, seemed like the perfect refuge. She crossed the icy pavement, her boots crunching on patches of hard snow, her mind already replaying the confrontation, sifting through Mr. Thorne’s rant for any inadvertent clues. His accusation about her "yellow-bellied uncle" had stung, but it also confirmed his belief that she was working for a newspaper, and a sensational one at that. He clearly connected her to a past scandal or rival publication, but it wasn't the *Monitor* he was speaking of directly. Still, the implication remained; he thought she was there to dig up dirt, and he was not entirely wrong.

Inside the diner, the smell of fresh coffee and griddle grease was a welcome distraction. She slid into a booth by the window, ordered a hot chocolate, and pulled out a small notebook and pencil from her purse. Her hand trembled slightly as she opened the cover to a blank page. The antique key was gone, her primary access route into the elusive suite irrevocably lost. This was a significant setback, but Clara Parker was not one to be easily deterred. Her father had taught her that persistence was a reporter’s most valuable asset, even more so than a sharp wit or a keen eye.

She tapped her pencil against the paper. The "broken promise," she thought, that was the key. Not the physical key now in Mr. Thorne's possession, but the underlying reason *why* she had been entrusted with it. A faint memory, like a half-forgotten dream, flickered at the edge of her consciousness. The person who had given her the key had been shrouded in shadows, their voice hushed, their instructions vague yet urgent. "Find the truth, Clara," they had whispered, "for the sake of the promise. For the sake of what was lost."

Lost. What was lost? The key, a delicate thing of ornate brass, had been pressed into her hand only days ago, accompanied by a brittle, yellowed letter. She had committed the letter's contents to memory, then, perhaps foolishly, destroyed it, fearing it might fall into the wrong hands. It had spoken of a forgotten legacy, a hidden injustice, and a room, specifically *the* room, on the second floor of the Fergus Hotel, sealed away for decades. The letter had hinted at a previous attempt to retrieve something vital, an attempt that had failed, resulting in the "broken promise." The key, it had implied, was the *second* chance.

Clara took a long sip of her hot chocolate, the warmth spreading through her. She needed to approach this differently. Brute force or direct confrontation were clearly out. Mr. Thorne was too well-prepared, too paranoid. She needed subtlety, an indirect route.

She looked out the window at the Fergus Hotel. Its grand facade, usually so welcoming, now seemed to frown at her, its many windows like watchful eyes. The second floor, her target, was directly above the main lobby, its windows dark and uninviting. She wondered if the suite had even been used since the incident that had necessitated its sealing, or if it had simply gathered dust for years, a silent mausoleum of secrets.

She closed her eyes, trying to visualize the layout of the hotel, recalling what she had seen during her brief, illicit entry. The narrow hall, the guard, the Green Door. It was a well-guarded secret. But every fortress, no matter how imposing, had its vulnerabilities. There had to be another way in, or at least another way to learn what Mr. Thorne was so desperately trying to hide.

Her gaze drifted past the hotel to the surrounding buildings. The hotel grounds sprawled, encompassing several smaller cottages and service buildings. There was a delivery entrance she had noted earlier, a staff entrance, and a number of fire escapes. None of them, however, seemed to lead directly to the second floor, much less to a specific, sealed-off suite. This required more than just general access; it required precision.

Suddenly, a thought struck her. The old custodian who had been sitting near the suite door. He had been an impassive observer, yes, but he had also shrunk away, avoiding eye contact. He had been there before her, and he remained there after Mr. Thorne had sent her packing. Custodians, she knew, were often the silent keepers of a building's history, privy to its hidden nooks and crannies, its forgotten tales. They saw everything, heard snippets of conversations, and often developed a deep, almost familial, connection to the old bricks and mortar. Could he be a source? He might know something about the suite, or about the "broken promise" hinted at in the letter.

It was a long shot, but it was a lead, a thread she could pull. The trick would be to approach him without drawing Mr. Thorne's attention, and without revealing her true intentions. He would be wary, perhaps even fearful, of repercussions.

Clara finished her hot chocolate, the warmth having now imbued her with a renewed sense of purpose. She paid her bill and stepped back out into the brisk air, pulling her hat low over her eyes. She sat, feeling the cold seep through her coat, but her mind was sharp, focused. Clara rose and walked back towards the main street, her gaze now fixed on the local businesses. A general store, a post office, and a small, unassuming newspaper office. The *Pine Top Echo*. Clara’s heart pounded. The Green Room. The very name from the original passage about Penny Parker. It was confirmation, a direct link. "The Green Room mystery," she echoed softly, trying to sound intrigued, not overly excited. "What exactly happened?"

This was it. The hidden injustice, the forgotten legacy. Eleanor Vance. The lost jewels. The second floor suite. It all clicked into place. Another reporter. The parallelism was striking. This must have been the incident Penny Parker was involved in, or a similar character from the original series. It confirmed that the secret was indeed very old and very well guarded.

She walked back towards the hotel, her mind formulating a plan. Silas would be retiring soon, or perhaps taking a break. She would stake out the employee entrance again, but this time, she wouldn’t just observe. She would wait.

As darkness began to fall, painting the sky in hues of deep purple and orange, Clara settled back on the cold bench behind the pine trees. After what felt like an eternity, a side door opened, and a figure, slow and stooped, emerged. It was Silas Hemlock. He was carrying a worn metal lunch pail and a canvas bag, his shoulders hunched against the cold. This was her chance. Clara stood up, brushing snow from her coat, and walked purposefully, but not hurriedly, towards him. She knew a direct approach might startle him, but a hesitant one might allow him to escape before she could speak.

Silas stopped, his back to her, and slowly turned. His face was a roadmap of wrinkles, his eyes faded but still alert, peering at her through wire-rimmed spectacles. He clutched his lunch pail tighter. "Yes?" he asked, his voice raspy with age.

"My name is Clara Parker," she said, approaching him with an open, friendly demeanor. "I'm a writer, and I was hoping you might spare a few moments of your time. I'm researching the history of the Fergus Hotel, and Mrs. Gable at the *Echo* suggested you would be the best person to speak to."

Silas regarded her skeptically, his gaze lingering for a moment on her face. He had seen her in the hall earlier, standing with that angry Mr. Thorne. His eyes, though old, missed little. "I understand that," Clara replied, her smile unwavering. "My interest is purely historical, Mr. Hemlock. I'm trying to capture the spirit of the old Fergus, the stories that might otherwise be forgotten. You've seen so much history unfold within those walls."

He hesitated, then slowly relaxed his grip on the lunch pail. "History, eh? There's plenty of that, good and bad." He gestured vaguely towards his truck. "I'm just heading home. It's been a long day."

"I wouldn't keep you long," Clara pressed gently. "Perhaps you could tell me about the building's original design, or how the seasons changed here over the years? Mrs. Gable mentioned you started as a bellhop." She deliberately avoided any mention of the Green Room or Eleanor Vance.

Silas seemed to soften at the mention of his early days. "Aye, a bellhop. Used to carry bags for the finest folks in the country. This place was a jewel, it was. Before... well, before things changed." He looked back at the hotel, a wistful, almost sorrowful, expression on his face.

"Things change everywhere, Mr. Hemlock," Clara agreed. "But the memories, those remain. Could I perhaps buy you a cup of coffee at the diner across the street? It's warm in there."

He considered her proposition, his gaze distant. "Coffee sounds good," he finally said, a faint smile touching his lips. "But I'll pay for my own. Can't be beholden, you know."

"Of course," Clara said, relieved. This was a small victory. Getting Silas to talk would be a delicate dance, but she felt a surge of hope. He held the missing pieces of the puzzle, she was sure of it.

They walked across the street to the diner, the snow crunching under their feet. Inside, the warmth was a comfort, and the scent of coffee was stronger. They sat in a quiet booth in the back, away from the prying eyes of the hotel. Clara ordered another hot chocolate, and Silas asked for black coffee.

"So, the Fergus," Clara began, after the waitress had taken their orders. "What was it like in its heyday?"

Silas began to talk, slowly at first, his voice a low rumble, telling tales of grand balls, famous guests, and the bustling excitement of the early resort days. Clara listened patiently, interjecting with small, encouraging questions, letting him build a rapport, letting him trust her. She steered the conversation subtly, guiding it towards the older parts of the hotel, the less-used wings, the "forgotten" spaces.

Finally, after nearly half an hour, she felt the time was right. "Mrs. Gable mentioned something about a... a 'Green Room mystery' from long ago," Clara said, her voice dropping to a near whisper. "She said you were a young man then. Did you ever hear anything about that?"

Silas froze, his hand, midway to his coffee cup, stopping in mid-air. His eyes, which had been warm with nostalgia, suddenly clouded with apprehension. "That's old history, miss," he said, his voice flat. "Better left buried."

"Perhaps," Clara conceded. "But sometimes, old history holds lessons for the present. Sometimes, there are truths that deserve to be unearthed, not just for the sake of history, but for justice." She looked him directly in the eye, her own gaze unwavering. "Justice for Eleanor Vance, Mr. Hemlock. And for the 'broken promise' that followed her disappearance."

Silas gasped, a sharp intake of breath. He set his coffee cup down with a clatter. His face, already pale, seemed to drain of all color. "How do you know about that?" he whispered, his eyes wide with fear. "Nobody's spoken that name in fifty years. Nobody."

"The truth has a way of lingering, Mr. Hemlock," Clara said, keeping her voice steady and calm, despite the thrill of her discovery. "I was given an old key, and a letter. A letter that spoke of a promise made, and a promise broken. It spoke of injustice. And it led me to the Fergus Hotel, and to you."

He slumped back against the booth, looking suddenly much older, much frailer. "The key," he murmured. "The old master key. It was never meant to be used again. Not after..." He trailed off, looking over his shoulder as if expecting Mr. Thorne to appear from the kitchen.

It comes from that direction, the second floor, where the Green Room is. It’s not loud enough to wake anyone up in their rooms, but if you’re down here, or near the service lift, you can hear it. It always stops by dawn. Before the morning shift starts."

"Before dawn," Penny repeated thoughtfully. "And these men, the ones you don't recognize, do they ever stay overnight, or do they just deliver and leave?"

"They usually leave, Miss Parker," Henry replied, gaining a little more confidence now that he was speaking about concrete observations rather than his own fears. "They unload, go up with the crates, and then they're gone within the hour. But sometimes, just sometimes, one of them might stay. I think one of them is called George, but I'm not sure. He's a really quiet fellow. Big, with a scar on his cheek. He always wears a dark coat, even indoors."

"Old George," Penny murmured, remembering Francine's casual remark. "And the dark cars? Do these men use dark cars for their deliveries?"

"Yes, Miss Parker," Henry confirmed, his eyes widening slightly at Penny's accuracy. "Always dark sedans, never taxis or anything. They pull up to the loading dock, just a quick in and out. They never leave the engines running, either, so they don't draw attention."

This was a goldmine of information, far more than Penny had hoped for. The pieces were beginning to fit together, forming a disturbing picture. "Henry, this is all incredibly helpful. But I need one more thing. Is there a way into that service corridor on the second floor without going through the main second-floor landing? A back stairwell, perhaps, or another hidden passage?"

Henry hesitated again, looking around anxiously. "There's an old service staircase, Miss Parker. It's mostly used by the kitchen staff for emergencies, or to get to the employee locker rooms on the mezzanine. But it connects to the second floor, yes. It's usually locked on the second floor landing, but sometimes, if the kitchen is busy and someone forgets, it might be left ajar. It’s behind the linen closet, past the old boiler room." He pointed vaguely down the corridor. "It's dark, though, and quite dusty."

"Thank you, Henry," Penny said, a genuine smile gracing her lips. "You've been wonderful. Now, go on about your duties. And remember, not a word of this to anyone. Not a single word."

Henry nodded vigorously, relief washing over his face. "I won't, Miss Parker. I promise." He retrieved his dropped cigarette, stubbed it out with his foot, and hurried away.

Penny watched him go, then turned her attention back to the dim, service corridor. The old service staircase. That was her next target. It wouldn't be easy, but nothing about this investigation ever was. She needed to observe. She needed to see what was in those canvas-covered crates, what made that thumping sound, and what Harvey Maxwell was so desperate to hide. Her father’s "yellow" paper would soon have a story so solid, so undeniable, that no one would dare question its veracity.

She located the linen closet Henry had mentioned, a heavy wooden door that looked like it hadn't been opened in years. It was unlocked, however, and inside, tucked away behind stacks of crisp sheets, was another, narrower door. This one, a dark steel door with a rusty handle, clearly marked the entrance to the service staircase. It was surprisingly quiet as she pushed it open, revealing a steep, winding flight of concrete steps, plunging into darkness below and ascending into shadows above. The air here was even colder, thick with the scent of dust and damp concrete.

Penny decided to ascend. She didn’t know if the door to the second floor would be unlocked, but she had to try. Each step creaked beneath her weight, echoing eerily in the confined space. She climbed slowly, cautiously, her hand tracing the rough concrete wall for balance. The darkness was absolute in places, alleviated only by faint shafts of light filtering through grimy, high-set windows in the stairwell walls.

As she reached what she judged to be the second floor landing, she pressed her ear against the steel door. No sounds. Carefully, she tried the handle. It turned! A small victory, but a significant one. She opened the door just a crack, peering into the service corridor Henry had described. It was deserted, lit by a single bare bulb hanging from the ceiling, casting long, distorted shadows. The corridor was much narrower than the main hotel corridors, less grand, more purely functional. And at the far end, partially obscured by some discarded crates, she could make out the outlines of what had to be the hidden service lift.

She slipped through the door, closing it silently behind her. Her heart pounded a quick, excited rhythm against her ribs. She was on the right track. This was the backstage area, the hidden pathway to the Green Room. She moved stealthily, her gaze sweeping the corridor, searching for any sign of the "unmarked door of the wing" she had briefly glimpsed earlier, the one guarded by the taciturn man. She found it after a few tense moments, a plain, unadorned door blending almost perfectly with the cream-colored wall. And indeed, the guard was gone. Maxwell’s outburst earlier had likely changed the patrol schedule, at least temporarily.

Penny approached the door, her hand hovering over the cold metal handle. Her original plan had been to observe, to gather more facts before confronting anything directly. But the guard was absent, and the door was here, tantalizingly close. This might be her only chance to get a look inside, to see what Maxwell was so desperate to hide. She remembered the thrill of her past investigations, the blend of fear and exhilaration that always accompanied the cusp of discovery. She took a deep breath, her journalist’s instincts overriding her natural caution.

She pushed the door open.

It led not directly into the Green Room, but into another short, unlit passage, even more utilitarian than the last. At the end of it, stood the Green Door itself, heavy and imposing, with no handle on the outside, only a small keyhole. It was the same door, Penny realized, that the guard had been protecting. And it was locked, of course. Frustration flared within her. So close, and yet so far.

She examined the door, running her fingers over its smooth, cold surface. There were no windows, no obvious cracks, no way to peek inside. But as she leaned closer, she caught a faint, almost imperceptible hum, a low vibration that seemed to emanate from within the heavy door. And beneath that hum, even fainter, she thought she heard it again: a soft, rhythmic *thump-thump-thump*. It was distant, muffled, but unmistakably present. The "thumping sound" Henry had mentioned, the one that stopped before dawn. It was coming from inside the Green Room.

What could be making such a sound? A printing press? A piece of heavy machinery? A clandestine operation of some kind? Her mind raced, sifting through possibilities. Whatever it was, it was large, and it was active. And it was exactly what Maxwell did not want anyone to discover.

Just as Penny was about to retreat, a new sound reached her ears, a sharp, angry voice echoing from further down the service corridor, growing louder with each passing second. It was Maxwell, and he sounded furious.

"I told you to make sure that door was secured! This hotel is not some public thoroughfare for nosey reporters!"

Penny froze, her blood turning to ice. He must have found the service staircase door ajar, or perhaps he was just making rounds, a sudden fit of paranoia striking him. She had mere seconds. Retreating back through the unlit passage and the service staircase was too risky; she might run directly into him.

She glanced frantically around the small, utilitarian passage. To her right was the Green Door. To her left, a blank wall. Above her head, a small, grimy vent. Not wide enough for escape. She was trapped.

The voice was closer now, accompanied by heavy, impatient footsteps. "She's been poking around all day, sniffing where she doesn't belong. If she gets in here, Johnson, you'll be out of a job!"

Penny’s gaze swept desperately around, and then she saw it, almost invisible in the dim light. Tucked into a narrow recess in the wall, half-hidden by a bulging pipe, was a small, maintenance panel, secured by a simple latch. It was the size of a small cupboard door, meant to access plumbing or electrical wiring. Without a second thought, Penny unlatched it and squeezed herself into the cramped, dark space, pulling the panel shut just as Maxwell’s furious face appeared at the end of the passage.

He strode directly to the Green Door, his eyes blazing, and examined its surface. "See, Johnson? It's locked. Good. Now, what was that noise I heard from the second floor service stairwell? Are you certain no one has been up there?"

A new, calmer voice replied, one Penny recognized as the guard, Johnson. "No one has passed my post, Mr. Maxwell. I've been here the whole time, sir."

"Then who was it that left the service door to the second floor ajar?" Maxwell snapped, his voice sharp with suspicion. "I just closed it myself!"

Penny held her breath, pressing herself against the cold pipes, the metallic tang of rust filling her nostrils. Her heart hammered against her ribs, a frantic drumbeat in the confined space. This was far too close.

"Perhaps a kitchen hand, sir?" Johnson suggested. "Sometimes they forget, coming up for supplies."

"Forget?" Maxwell scoffed. "Or perhaps a certain young reporter with too much audacity for her own good is trying to sneak her way in! I warned her, Johnson. I warned her never to set foot in this hotel again. If I find her, I'll have her arrested for trespassing."

Penny bit back a gasp. Arrested for trespassing. That would be a black mark on her record, and more importantly, it would jeopardize her entire investigation. She had to be more careful, much more careful. This was no longer just a game of wits; it was a serious threat.

She heard their footsteps retreat down the corridor, Maxwell’s voice fading into an indignant rumble. Penny waited, counting slowly to one hundred, then another hundred, just to be sure. The air in the recess was stale, and her limbs were beginning to cramp. Finally, with immense caution, she pushed open the panel and eased herself out, stretching her stiff muscles. The corridor was empty.

The close call had unnerved her, but it had also solidified her resolve. Maxwell was terrified of what she might uncover. That meant she was very, very close. She slipped back through the plain door, into the main service corridor, and then made her way back down the creaking service staircase, her earlier exhilaration now tempered with a renewed sense of danger. She needed a new plan, one that didn't involve getting caught.

As Penny descended, her mind whirred, piecing together the bits of information. Canvas-covered crates, a thumping sound, late-night deliveries, dark cars, the hidden service lift, "Old George," and Maxwell's almost frantic desire to keep the Green Room secret. It all pointed to something clandestine, something that couldn't bear the light of day. Could it be a counterfeiting operation? A secret laboratory? A gambling ring with a highly sophisticated setup? The thumping suggested machinery.

Reaching the ground floor, she pushed open the steel door, checked the linen closet for any signs of her passage, and then quietly made her way out of the service entrance. The cold night air was a welcome relief, clearing her head. She decided to circle the hotel's exterior, particularly around the loading dock area, to see if she could find any recent tracks from the dark cars Henry had mentioned.

The moon, a thin crescent in the inky sky, offered little illumination, but the snow-covered ground reflected what light there was. As she approached the loading dock, she saw them: fresh tire tracks, distinct and deep, leading up to the dock and then turning sharply back towards the main road. They were different from the usual delivery truck tracks, narrower, more car-like. Henry had been right. The dark cars were indeed here, and recently.

Penny followed the tracks away from the hotel, a fresh burst of energy invigorating her. The tracks led to a secluded area behind a stand of pines, where she found something else. A discarded cigarette butt, still faintly warm, its filter stained with lipstick. Not a man's cigarette, then. And nearby, a faint scent of expensive perfume. Penny bent down, examining the area. There were faint footprints in the snow, smaller than a man's, indicating a woman had been here recently, waiting.

Could it be Francine? The thought struck Penny with the force of a small avalanche. Francine had also been investigating. Had she been waiting here for the dark cars? For "Old George"? Was she perhaps in league with them, or simply another independent investigator trying to scoop Penny?

The idea that Francine might be involved in something illicit, or at least withholding information, was unsettling. Penny had dismissed her earlier as a rival, but now she wondered if Francine was more deeply entwined in the mystery than she let on. "I've not only been looking for something, I've found it!" Francine had said. Was she referring to the deliveries, or something else entirely? And "It may have before long," when Penny mentioned the lack of news value. Was Francine waiting for a bigger reveal, a climax to the secret operation?

Penny decided that she needed to keep a closer eye on Francine Sellberg. The reporter's motives, once seemingly straightforward competition, now appeared far more complex.

Returning to the Downey Lodge, Penny found Mrs. Downey bustling about the lobby, directing some late-arriving guests. "Penny, dear! You're just in time. We're having a small gathering in the lounge for the first arrivals. Come, join us."

Penny, ever gracious, smiled. "Thank you, Mrs. Downey, I'd love to. But first, I think I'll just slip into my room and freshen up." She needed a moment to herself, to process all the new information, to formulate her next move. The lure of the Green Room was stronger than ever, and she knew now that direct confrontation was not the answer, not yet. Observation, stealth, and a keen eye for detail were her best weapons against Harvey Maxwell and his shadowy operations.

In the quiet solitude of her room, Penny spread out a small map of the Fergus Hotel she had managed to acquire earlier. She marked the service staircase, the loading dock, the service lift, and the location of the Green Room. She noted the timing of the deliveries, the thumping sound, and the presence of the dark cars and the mysterious George. She also added Francine's potential involvement to her mental list of variables. The picture was still hazy, but the outlines were becoming clearer.

She knew what she had to do next. She would have to return to the Fergus Hotel, under the cloak of night, to observe the loading dock and the service lift area. She needed to witness one of these late-night deliveries for herself. She needed to see what was in those canvas-covered crates, to find out who was involved, and to understand the nature of the "thumping" operation. It was risky, incredibly so, but the story was too big, too important, to ignore.

Penny Parker, daughter of the man they called a "yellow journalist," was about to prove them all wrong. She would uncover the truth, whatever it might be, and she would do it with undeniable, irrefutable facts. The Green Room held a secret, a dark secret that Harvey Maxwell was guarding with furious zeal, and Penny was determined to bring it into the light. She had no green ticket now, but she had something far more valuable: a burgeoning understanding of Maxwell’s game, and an unshakeable resolve to win it. She would wait until the entire hotel was asleep, until the last whisper of conversation had died down, and then, she would make her move. The mountain held its secrets, but Penny was ready to scale it, inch by perilous inch, until she reached the summit of truth. The night was young, and for Penny Parker, the real adventure was just beginning. She would be ready.

CHAPTER 15 _A MIDNIGHT WATCH_

The moon was high and full, casting an ethereal glow over the snow-covered peaks and the quiet, slumbering valley as Penny Parker slipped silently from the Downey Lodge. The air was frigid, biting at her exposed skin, but Penny barely noticed. Her mind was entirely focused on the mission ahead. She was dressed warmly, in dark, practical clothing that would allow her to blend into the shadows, and her soft-soled boots made almost no sound on the packed snow. She carried a small backpack containing a flashlight, a notepad, a pencil, and a pair of binoculars, her essential tools for any clandestine investigation. She also had a small, reliable camera, though she knew using it might be risky.

Her plan was simple, if audacious. She would approach the Fergus Hotel from the rear, using the same service routes she had reconnoitered earlier, and position herself somewhere near the loading dock, or perhaps the service corridor, where she could observe any late-night activity. Henry’s information about the post-midnight deliveries had given her a crucial window of opportunity. She knew she might be waiting for hours, but she was prepared for that. Patience, she had learned, was often a detective's most valuable asset.

The ski trail, which had been bustling with activity earlier in the day, was now deserted. The only sounds were the soft crunch of her boots on the snow and the distant hoot of an owl. The Fergus Hotel, when she finally reached its darkened form, looked imposing, almost menacing, under the moonlight. Only a few lights glowed faintly in some of the upper windows, suggesting a handful of guests or staff still awake, but the service entrance area was plunged into deep shadow.

Penny moved cautiously, using the cover of the pines and the hotel's own outbuildings to conceal her approach. She found a suitable vantage point behind a stack of discarded lumber near the loading dock, affording her a clear view of the service entrance, the dock itself, and a stretch of the access road. It was cold, brutally cold, and the wind, though slight, seemed to find every gap in her clothing. She huddled deeper into her jacket, pulling her woolen hat down low over her ears.

The hours dragged by. The cold seeped into her bones, making her teeth chatter despite her best efforts to control it. She checked her watch frequently. Midnight came and went. Then one o'clock. Two o'clock. Nothing. Only the steady, monotonous drone of the hotel’s unseen machinery and the occasional creak of settling timbers. Doubt began to gnaw at her. Had Henry been mistaken? Had Maxwell tightened his security after her earlier encounters, putting a halt to the deliveries? Or perhaps the operation was not daily, but intermittent. She reminded herself of her father’s adage: "A reporter waits, and waits, and then, they pounce." She settled in, determined to outlast the night.

Just as the first faint hint of pre-dawn gray began to soften the eastern sky, a new sound cut through the silence. The low, distant rumble of an engine. Penny’s senses immediately sharpened. She reached for her binoculars, training them on the access road.

A moment later, two dark sedans, just as Henry had described, appeared around the bend in the road. Their headlights were dimmed, almost to pinpricks, and they moved without haste, deliberately, as if trying to avoid drawing any attention. They pulled up to the loading dock, their engines cutting out simultaneously, leaving a sudden, profound quiet.

Penny held her breath, her heart thumping in her chest. This was it.

Four men emerged from the cars, large, burly figures in dark overcoats. Two from each car. They moved with a practiced efficiency, opening the trunks and the back doors of the sedans. Penny adjusted her binoculars, straining to see what they were unloading.

Sure enough, there they were: canvas-covered crates. Large, rectangular objects, too heavy for one man to carry alone. The men worked quickly, grunting with effort, as they carefully maneuvered the crates onto the loading dock. Penny counted three crates from the first car, and two from the second. Five crates in all.

As they unloaded the last crate, one of the men, taller and broader than the others, turned his head slightly, and the moonlight caught a distinctive scar tracing a line down his cheek. "Old George," Penny thought, a thrill of recognition mixed with apprehension. He was real, and he was here.

George barked a low command to the others, his voice a gravelly murmur that barely reached Penny’s hiding spot. The men then went to the service entrance door. It opened from the inside, revealing another figure, shrouded in shadow, who stepped out and surveyed the scene. It was Harvey Maxwell. His posture was stiff, authoritative, even in the dead of night.

Maxwell exchanged a few hushed words with George, then gestured impatiently towards the crates. The men began to move the crates through the service entrance, disappearing one by one into the hotel’s hidden passages. Penny noted their route. They were indeed using the service lift.

This was her moment. She waited until the last of the men had vanished inside, then she cautiously emerged from her hiding place. She couldn't risk following them directly, not with Maxwell and George supervising. But she needed to get closer, to try and catch a glimpse of what was inside those crates, or at least hear the tell-tale thumping again.

She circled around to a less visible part of the hotel's service area, looking for another way to approach the second-floor service corridor. She remembered Henry mentioning a fire escape on the side of the building, rarely used, but potentially accessible. It led to the mezzanine level, which was connected to the staff stairwell she had used earlier.

Locating the rusty iron ladder, Penny tested its stability. It groaned slightly under her weight, but held firm. She began her ascent, her movements slow and deliberate, each rung cold beneath her gloved hands. The climb was strenuous, but the adrenaline pumping through her veins kept her going. She was a silent shadow, scaling the side of the hotel, an unexpected climber in the pre-dawn stillness.

She reached the mezzanine level, where a small, grated window offered a view into a dimly lit storage room. The window was locked, but a tiny pane of glass was missing from the corner, perhaps damaged long ago and never repaired. It wasn't large enough to climb through, but it was enough for her to get her ear close, to listen.

From somewhere deep within the hotel, from above her, she could now clearly hear the distinct *thump-thump-thump* sound. It was stronger now, closer, a regular, powerful rhythm that vibrated faintly through the very structure of the building. It was indeed a machine, large and industrial, working steadily in the early hours of the morning. And it was coming from the Green Room.

What sort of machine made such a sound? A printing press, perhaps, churning out counterfeit currency? Or something more sinister? The thought sent a shiver down Penny’s spine, unrelated to the cold. She closed her eyes, trying to visualize the process, to understand the operation. The canvas covers, the careful handling, the late-night hours, Maxwell’s paranoia, it all pointed to something highly illegal.

She heard voices then, muffled but urgent, from just beyond the wall of the storage room. It was Maxwell, his tone sharper now, more agitated. "Are the new ones in place? We can't afford any delays. Not with the inspection coming."

"They're secured, Mr. Maxwell," George’s gruff voice replied. "All five. And the old ones are ready for removal after the shift."

"Good," Maxwell said, a note of satisfaction in his voice. "Now, get back to your posts. And be absolutely certain no one sees anything. Especially that meddling reporter. She was poking around the service area earlier. I want her gone from Pine Top by sunrise, or else."

Penny pressed herself against the cold wall, her heart seizing. "Inspection." What inspection? And "old ones" and "new ones." Were they replacing something, upgrading some illegal machinery? The words were vague enough to be tantalizing, specific enough to be chilling. Maxwell clearly had more than just a passing interest in this operation. He was directly involved, overseeing it, almost managing it.

And his renewed threat to have her gone by sunrise was a serious one. He meant business. Penny knew she couldn't risk another direct encounter. She had to retreat, to consolidate this new information, and devise a strategy that would expose Maxwell without putting herself in too much danger. She had the proof of the deliveries, the confirmation of George and Maxwell’s involvement, and the definite sound of the mysterious machinery. It was enough to build a case, to start asking questions in the right places.

Carefully, silently, Penny descended the fire escape, her mind racing with a whirlwind of thoughts and deductions. The thumping grew fainter as she moved away, but its rhythmic cadence continued to echo in her ears. She had a story, a big story, a real scoop that would shake the foundations of the Fergus Hotel and expose Harvey Maxwell for the criminal he clearly was. And her father, Anthony Parker, would be proud of the meticulous, factual reporting, far from any "yellow journalism."

spur to her already racing thoughts. The eastern sky, now streaked with vibrant coral and soft violet, promised a clear, albeit cold, day. She had perhaps an hour or two at most before his threat became a reality, before she was forcibly removed from Pine Top.

She reached the Downey Lodge and let herself in quietly, making her way up to her room. The lodge was still mostly asleep, save for the faint clatter from the kitchen where breakfast preparations were no doubt underway. In her room, she shivered, not just from the cold outside, but from the sudden, sharp realization of her precarious position. Maxwell was not a man to make idle threats. He was powerful, connected, and clearly engaged in something illicit enough to make him ruthless.

Her mind replayed the scene in the service corridor: the rhythmic thrumming, the muffled voices, Maxwell’s agitated tone, George’s gruff replies, the words “inspection,” “new ones,” “old ones.” What could it all mean? The Green Room was clearly a front for something far more sinister than a gambling den. The heavy machinery, the careful handling of “new ones,” the disposal of “old ones,” the tight security, Maxwell’s absolute control over every aspect, his fury at any trespasser. It painted a picture of a sophisticated, high-stakes operation. Counterfeiting still seemed a strong possibility, though the specific mention of “inspection” and "old ones" and "new ones" suggested a cycle of production or replacement.
