“Francine Sellberg,” Mr. Gregory murmured, a thoughtful expression on his face. “Yes, I am familiar with her work. Ambitious young woman, always keen for a scoop. Her involvement certainly lends credence to the notion that something significant is afoot. A Green Room, guarded, on the second floor of a hotel owned by Maxwell… the pieces are certainly intriguing.” He returned the ticket to Penny. “And what is your initial impression of this hotel, Miss Parker? Beyond its charming exterior, that is.”
“It feels… secretive,” Penny replied, choosing her words carefully. “There are many guests who seem to be merely enjoying the winter sports, but there’s an undercurrent. A sense of hushed activity. And Maxwell himself… he seems quite ruthless when it comes to protecting his interests.”
“Ruthless, you say. That aligns with some of the preliminary information I gathered before my arrival. There are whispers, Miss Parker, of certain financial irregularities concerning the Pine Top development. Rumors of land acquisitions that were perhaps not entirely above board, and of investors who have seen their fortunes inexplicably dwindle.”
Penny’s eyes widened. “So it’s not just about some secret meeting room, then. This is about larger schemes.”
“It very well could be. The Green Room, whatever its true function, may be merely one piece of a much larger puzzle. My contact, a Mr. Finch, who was to meet me here, has unfortunately not arrived. I fear he may have encountered some unforeseen difficulty. His last message was rather cryptic, suggesting that ‘the game was afoot, and the players were not all known.’”
“A game,” Penny mused. “And I’ve already been thrown out for trying to join. What do you propose we do now, Mr. Gregory?”
“Patience, Miss Parker, is often a virtue in such endeavors. However, I sense in you a rather less patient nature, much like your father. Perhaps that is why he sent you. You have the advantage of youth and a certain… unexpectedness. Maxwell, I imagine, would not anticipate you returning after such a clear dismissal.”
“He certainly wouldn’t,” Penny agreed with a grin. “But I’m not one to be easily deterred. My father raised me to believe that a locked door is just a challenge, not a permanent barrier.”
“Indeed. And you possess a key of sorts, do you not?” Mr. Gregory gestured subtly towards the green ticket in her hand. “Even if its use has been… temporarily suspended. Perhaps there is another way to approach this guarded room. A different angle.”
“I thought about that. Maxwell only saw me enter the hotel once before I was thrown out. He doesn’t know I was there the previous night, nor that I found the ticket then. He might be looking for me, but he won’t be expecting me to come back through the front door, certainly not so soon.”
“Precisely. He has underestimated you, Miss Parker. A common failing of those who rely on brute force over intellect. My initial plan was to use my credentials as a respected historian to gain access, perhaps under the pretense of researching the hotel’s archives. But with Maxwell’s obvious hostility towards anyone probing into his affairs, that approach might now be too direct.”
“And my direct approach was met with an equally direct rejection,” Penny added. “So, we need something subtle. Something that doesn’t scream ‘investigation’.”
“Perhaps we could combine our resources. You have the ticket, a tangible link to this Green Room. I have a certain network of contacts, and some experience in discreet inquiries. Do you have any ideas, Miss Parker, on how one might observe a guarded room from a distance, or perhaps find a way to circumvent a guard, without causing a further scene?”
Penny tapped her chin thoughtfully. “Well, the second floor isn’t entirely without other rooms. There are guest rooms, and I assume service areas. If I could get to that floor undetected, I might be able to observe the Green Room from a different vantage point. But getting past the lobby and then the elevator boy will be tricky, especially with Maxwell on high alert.”
“Perhaps the service entrance?” Mr. Gregory suggested. “Most large establishments have them, for staff, deliveries, and the like. They are often less closely monitored than the main guest thoroughfares.”
“That’s an excellent idea, Mr. Gregory! I saw a staff entrance near the kitchen when I was making my way to the dining room last night. If I could get a staff uniform, or at least something that makes me look like I belong there…”
“A uniform might be difficult to procure without raising suspicion. However, a plausible reason for being in the service area would suffice. Are you, for instance, skilled in any practical arts that might require you to access such areas? Perhaps a theatrical set designer, checking on props for a performance? Or a caterer inspecting the kitchens?”
Penny chuckled. “I can barely cook toast without setting off an alarm, Mr. Gregory. But I am fairly adept at convincing people I am where I’m supposed to be. And I know a little about hotel operations, thanks to my father’s keen interest in local businesses. I could pretend to be looking for a lost item, perhaps an earring or a glove, which supposedly fell into a laundry chute or was misplaced by a maid.”
“A lost item. Ingenious, Miss Parker. It’s mundane enough not to attract undue attention, and it provides a legitimate reason to be in a service area, even if you do not have a uniform. And it allows for a degree of controlled wandering.”
“But before I go wandering, Mr. Gregory, we need to know more about the Green Room itself. What exactly might be happening inside? And why is Maxwell so

protective of it?"

Mr. Gregory leaned forward in his chair, his scholarly gaze fixed on Penny. "That is the question, isn't it, Miss Parker? A man of Maxwell's standing, in a hotel of this reputation, does not usually resort to such heavy-handed tactics unless he has something truly significant to hide. The Green Room, by its very name, suggests a certain exclusivity, perhaps even a covert function. In the history of grand hotels, such rooms have often served as private gaming parlors, clandestine meeting places, or even as secure locations for illicit transactions."

"Gaming, you mean gambling?" Penny asked, her eyes widening slightly. "Like a casino?"

"Precisely," he confirmed. "Though often on a much smaller, more private scale. High stakes games, accessible only to a select few. It could also be a place where confidential business deals are struck, or where sensitive information is exchanged. The fact that it is guarded and requires a special admission card speaks volumes. Maxwell's fury suggests that its contents are not merely valuable, but potentially compromising."

"And the card," Penny mused, pulling the small green rectangle from her pocket. She studied its plain, unadorned surface. "It has no writing, no number, just a smooth green surface. It doesn't look like a key card or a membership pass."

"Which only adds to its mystique," Mr. Gregory agreed. "It is a symbol, a token of entry, rather than a functional tool. This indicates a high degree of secrecy and a system based on personal

personal knowledge and trust," Mr. Gregory finished, leaning back thoughtfully. "It suggests that whatever is conducted within those walls is of such sensitive nature that only those personally vouched for, perhaps by Maxwell himself, are permitted entry. Your card, then, is not merely a pass, but a testament to someone's mistaken trust in you."

Penny nodded, a determined glint in her eyes. "And I intend to exploit that mistake to its fullest, Mr. Gregory. My 'lost item' story will get me into the service areas, and once I am there, I will keep my eyes and ears open. I need to find out what is behind that door, and why Harvey Maxwell is so desperate to keep it secret."

"A commendable resolve, Miss Parker," Mr. Gregory said, a faint smile playing on his lips. "But remember, discretion is often the better part of valor. A good journalist knows when to retreat and when to press on. Be observant, but do not be reckless. Maxwell is a powerful man, and his anger, as you have already witnessed, is not to be taken lightly."

"I will be careful," Penny promised. "But I also know that a good story does not wait for caution. Thank you for your help, Mr. Gregory. I will let you know what I discover."

With a quick, confident nod, Penny rose from her chair. She adjusted the small satchel on her shoulder and walked briskly out of the room, her mind already buzzing with possibilities. The hotel lobby, usually a bustling hub of activity, seemed to hold a different meaning now. Every bellhop, every maid, every guest carried a potential piece of the puzzle. She noted the discreet service doors near the back of the dining room, mentally mapping her approach.

First, she needed an appropriate "lost item." Something small, easily overlooked, but distinctive enough to warrant a prolonged search. She decided on a small, antique silver locket, one her grandmother had given her years ago. It had a delicate, filigree design and a tiny clasp. It was the perfect prop for her ruse.

She made her way to a secluded corner of the lobby, pretending to look for something on the floor. After a moment, she approached a passing maid, a young woman with a tired but pleasant face.

"Excuse me," Penny began, her voice edged with just the right amount of distress. "I seem to have lost something terribly important. It's a small silver locket, very old, with a unique pattern. It might have slipped off my neck near the dining room entrance, or perhaps even further back, toward the kitchen service area. It belonged to my grandmother, and it means the world to me."

The maid's face softened with sympathy. "Oh, dear, that is a shame. I haven't seen anything like that, but I can ask around. Have you checked with the lost and found?"

"Yes, immediately," Penny replied, shaking her head sadly. "Nothing. I was hoping perhaps it might have fallen into an overlooked corner, or perhaps even near one of the laundry chutes. It's so small." She gave a hopeful, pleading look. "I don't suppose I could just... have a quick look myself in the service areas? I know it's a long shot, but I'd recognize it instantly."

The maid hesitated. "Well, usually guests aren't allowed back there, Miss. It's quite busy, and there are many dangers, moving carts and hot equipment."

"I understand completely," Penny said, injecting genuine concern into her tone. "I promise to be utterly discreet and stay out of everyone's way. I just... I can't bear the thought of it being gone forever. My grandmother would be heartbroken." She lowered her gaze, letting a hint of a tear well up in her eye.

The maid, clearly moved by Penny's performance, finally relented. "All right, Miss. But you must be very careful. And if anyone asks, you are looking for your grandmother's locket. I'll just tell Mrs. O'Malley that I'm showing you the lost and found procedures." She led Penny toward a plain, unmarked door near the back of the dining room. "It's through here. The laundry chutes are in the basement, but sometimes things fall into the service passages on the main floor. Just be quick, please."

"Thank you, oh, thank you!" Penny exclaimed, her face brightening with feigned relief. "You're a lifesaver!"

The maid opened the door, revealing a stark, utilitarian corridor. The air immediately grew warmer, filled with the mingled smells of cooking food, detergents, and steam. Unlike the plush, well-lit guest areas, this hallway was painted a drab institutional green, with exposed pipes running along the ceiling. Bare light bulbs cast harsh shadows, and the floor was scuffed and worn. Carts laden with linens and cleaning supplies lined one wall, and the distant clatter of dishes and murmur of voices indicated a bustling kitchen nearby.

Penny stepped inside, feeling a thrill of adventure mixed with a healthy dose of apprehension. This was the hotel's hidden underbelly, the place where the illusion of luxury was maintained. She was behind the curtain now, and she intended to see everything.

"I'll start here," Penny said to the maid, gesturing vaguely down the corridor. "You go about your duties. I'll be fine."

The maid gave her a final, warning glance. "Don't wander too far, Miss. And if you see Mr. Maxwell, just say you got lost looking for the ladies' room."

"Understood!" Penny chirped, watching the maid depart. As soon as she was alone, Penny's demeanor changed. The distressed young woman vanished, replaced by the sharp-eyed reporter. She moved slowly, deliberately, her gaze sweeping over every detail. The walls were scuffed, and here and there, she saw faint marks where objects had been moved or stored. The doors along the corridor were mostly fire exits or utility closets, but one bore a small, hand-lettered sign: "Laundry Pick-Up."

She approached it, pushing open the heavy door to reveal a narrow shaft leading down into darkness. She peered into it, pretending to search, but her real attention was on the surrounding area. There were faint smudges of what looked like grease on the floor, leading away from the chute toward another corridor she hadn't noticed before, one that seemed to curve around the main kitchen area.

Penny decided to follow the grease marks. They led her past roaring ovens and steaming dishwashers, where cooks and kitchen staff worked in a frenzy of activity. No one paid her much mind; they were too busy. Her "lost locket" story, if she needed it, would still work. She just had to look lost and a little desperate.

The curving corridor eventually opened into a quieter, dimly lit section. Here, the air was cooler, and the smells of the kitchen faded. This part of the hotel seemed less trafficked, almost forgotten. There were fewer doors, and those she saw were solid, unmarked. She noted a small service elevator, its brass buttons tarnished with age.

Her heart began to beat a little faster. This felt like a place where secrets might be kept. She walked softly, her rubber-soled ski boots making almost no sound on the concrete floor. She ran her hand along the wall, feeling for any hidden latches or panels. Nothing.

Then, she noticed something peculiar. The paint on one section of the wall, about twenty feet ahead, seemed newer, a slightly different shade of green than the rest. It was subtle, almost imperceptible, but Penny's reporter's eye for detail immediately picked up on it. She approached it cautiously.

The newer paint covered a seam, almost perfectly flush with the wall. It wasn't a standard doorframe. This was a cleverly disguised entrance. She pressed her hand against the wall. It felt solid, but there was a faint give, a slight springiness that suggested it was not entirely fixed.

Penny's mind raced. Could this be it? A secret passage? She remembered Mr. Gregory's words about the Green Room's exclusivity, its need for secrecy. A hidden entrance would certainly fit the bill.

She scanned the floor around the disguised entrance. There were no tracks, no scuffs, nothing to suggest frequent use. But then, she noticed a tiny, almost invisible scratch on the floor directly in front of the seam, as if something heavy had been dragged across it, perhaps a long time ago.

She decided to try the most obvious method of entry. She pushed, pulled, and pressed various sections of the disguised wall, but nothing happened. It remained stubbornly closed. Disappointment began to creep in, but Penny refused to be deterred. There had to be a trick.

She remembered the guard, the one who sat outside the Green Door on the second floor. He wasn't guarding *this* door, if this was indeed another entrance. But it hinted at a system. A key, a button, a lever.

She knelt, examining the base of the wall, then looked up, following the seam all the way to the ceiling. Still nothing. Her fingers probed the edges of the newly painted section. She felt a slight indentation, not a button, but a very narrow groove, about an inch long, near the floor. It was almost perfectly concealed by a build-up of dust and grime.

Penny pulled a small hairpin from her hair and carefully inserted it into the groove. It was a tight fit. She wiggled it gently. There was a faint click, so soft she almost missed it. Then, with a slow, almost imperceptible sigh, the section of the wall she had been examining began to retract inward, then slide silently to one side, revealing a dark, narrow passage beyond.

A gust of stale, cool air wafted out. Penny's heart pounded with excitement. She had found it. Or at least, an entrance to it.

She peered into the darkness. She could make out a short, winding staircase leading down. There was no sound from within. She hesitated only for a moment. Curiosity, and the thrill of the chase, propelled her forward.

"Here goes nothing," she whispered to herself.

She slipped through the opening, careful not to let the disguised door swing shut behind her. She wanted an easy exit. She pulled out a small, battery-operated flashlight from her satchel. Its beam cut through the gloom, illuminating dusty steps and cobwebs. The passage was unpleasantly damp, and the air was thick with the smell of old concrete and something else, something vaguely chemical.

She descended slowly, her hand trailing along the rough stone wall. The passage twisted several times, each turn increasing her sense of being deep within the hotel's bowels. After what felt like a hundred steps, but was probably only fifty, the staircase ended abruptly in another short, level corridor.

This corridor was wider and seemed more regularly maintained, though still devoid of the hotel's usual luxury. The walls were unpainted concrete, and bare bulbs in wire cages provided sparse illumination at long intervals. Penny noticed a series of identical, heavy wooden doors lining one side of the corridor. Each door was plain, with no handles, only a small, circular keyhole.

This was clearly a different part of the hotel, a truly private section. She approached the first door, testing its solidity. It was thick, reinforced, and clearly designed to keep sound in, or out. She tried the keyhole. It was small, a pin tumbler lock, not the type that could be picked with a hairpin.

She moved to the next door, then the next. All were identical. What were these rooms? Storage? Cells? A shiver ran down Penny's spine despite herself. This was more mysterious, and potentially more dangerous, than a simple gambling den.

She continued down the corridor, her flashlight beam sweeping ahead. There were about ten such doors. At the very end of the corridor, she saw a larger, double door, also plain wood, but with a more ornate, heavy brass handle. This, she felt, was her true destination. The Green Room.

She tried the handle. It was locked. Disappointment flared, but then she remembered her green card. The admission card. It was for the Green Room, after all. But this door had no card reader, just the solid brass handle and a traditional lock, though she saw no keyhole.

Penny pulled out her green card. It was smooth, unadorned. She ran her fingers along the edges, then pressed it against the door, wondering if there was some hidden sensor. Nothing. She tried the handle again. Still locked.

Then, she noticed a very fine line, almost invisible, running vertically down the center of the double door. It was a seam, like the one she had found on the secret entrance wall. This door wasn't just a door; it was two panels that met perfectly. And below the brass handle, almost integrated into the wood, was a small, almost imperceptible indentation, perfectly sized for her green card.

With a surge of anticipation, Penny slid the green card into the slot. It fit snugly. There was a soft, almost inaudible click, and then, as if by magic, the heavy brass handle rotated a quarter turn, signaling that the lock had disengaged.

Penny removed her card and slowly, cautiously, pushed the door open.

Beyond lay a room that was anything but green. It was luxuriously appointed, far more so than even the main hotel lobby. Deep, rich mahogany paneling covered the walls, and the floor was laid with a thick, Persian carpet in muted tones of crimson and gold. A large, crystal chandelier hung from the ceiling, casting a warm, inviting glow, even though only a few of its bulbs were lit. In the center of the room stood a massive, highly polished table, surrounded by twelve ornate, high-backed chairs upholstered in dark leather. A thick layer of dust, however, covered every surface, suggesting that the room had not been used in some time, or at least, not recently.

There were no windows. The room felt sealed, insulated from the outside world. On the far wall, a large, ornate fireplace, cold and empty, dominated the space. Above it hung a tapestry depicting a hunting scene.

This was not a gambling parlor. It looked more like a very exclusive boardroom, or perhaps a private dining hall. But the dust, the stillness, the almost eerie silence, indicated a pause in its activity.

Penny stepped inside, her flashlight beam dancing across the opulent furnishings. She ran her hand over the table, leaving a streak in the dust. On the table, she noticed several small, velvet-lined boxes. She opened one carefully. Inside, nestled on the velvet, were several thick, highly polished poker chips, but they weren't ordinary chips. They were heavy, made of some unknown metal, and etched with elaborate designs, including what looked like a stylized 'M'. Maxwell?

She opened another box. More chips. She checked a third. This one contained a small stack of crisp, neatly folded banknotes, clearly foreign currency. This wasn't just gambling; this was high-stakes, international gambling, or perhaps some sort of currency exchange. The chips weren't for a casual game; they were more like tokens of value, almost like currency themselves.

Penny's gaze shifted to the chairs. On the armrest of one, almost hidden by the dust, she saw a small, silver cufflink. She picked it up. It was engraved with a single, elegant initial: 'A'. Could it be from Anthony Parker, her father? No, that was too much of a coincidence. Or was it?

She heard a faint sound from the corridor outside, a distant creak. Penny froze, her heart leaping into her throat. She had been too engrossed in her discovery. Someone was coming.

She glanced around wildly. There was nowhere to hide in this room. The only way out was the door she had come through, but if someone was approaching from that direction, she would be trapped. She considered the fireplace, but it was far too small.

The sound grew louder, a shuffling, then a distinct cough. It was coming from the corridor leading to the main service area, not the one with the private rooms. This meant she had a moment.

Penny quickly slid the foreign banknotes back into their box. She snatched up one of the heavy poker chips, the one with the 'M' etching, and slipped it into her pocket. She replaced the cufflink on the chair arm, trying to make it look undisturbed.

She didn't dare close the main door, fearing the sound might alert whoever was approaching. Instead, she backed silently toward the secret passage she had used to enter this underground lair. The disguised door was still slightly ajar, a sliver of darkness beckoning.

She slipped through the opening, pulling the hidden door silently shut behind her. She didn't try to latch it; she just ensured it was closed flush with the wall. The sound of shuffling and coughing was now directly in the outer corridor, just around the bend from where the hidden entrance was located.

Penny pressed herself against the cold stone wall of the narrow passage, holding her breath. She heard footsteps, then a grumbling voice.

"Blast these new delivery schedules," the voice muttered. "Always changing things. And this damn locket business. Who loses a locket in the service area, I ask you?"

It was a man's voice, gruff and annoyed. Penny recognized it faintly. It sounded like the head of housekeeping, a stern-faced man she had seen giving orders to the maids earlier. He was complaining about her "lost locket."

She heard him walk right past the hidden door, his footsteps receding down the main service corridor. He was heading toward the laundry area, presumably to check on the maid's report of Penny's search.

Penny let out a slow, shaky breath she hadn't realized she was holding. That had been too close. She waited another minute, ensuring he was truly gone, before moving.

She retraced her steps up the winding staircase, pushing open the hidden panel carefully. The main service corridor was now empty. She quickly and quietly made her way back to the maid, who was just emerging from the "Laundry Pick-Up" room, looking somewhat exasperated.

"Any luck, Miss?" the maid asked, trying to sound hopeful.

Penny shook her head sadly, putting on her best dejected face. "No, I'm afraid not. I looked everywhere you suggested, and then some. It's truly gone. My grandmother will be so disappointed." She managed a small, brave smile. "But I do appreciate your kindness in letting me look. It truly means a lot."

The maid patted her arm sympathetically. "I'm so sorry, Miss. I'll make sure to put out the word to all the staff. If it turns up, we'll let you know right away."

"Thank you," Penny said, her voice still tinged with sorrow. "I suppose I'll just have to hope."

She left the service area, re-entering the plush comfort of the main hotel. The contrast was startling. The scents of polish and fresh flowers replaced the steam and detergent. The soft lighting seemed to soothe her frayed nerves.

Penny made her way back to Mr. Gregory's quiet study, her mind a whirl of images: the dusty, opulent Green Room, the velvet boxes, the foreign currency, the heavy 'M' etched chip. And the hidden passage. She pulled the chip from her pocket, examining it under the light. It felt cool and heavy in her palm.

Mr. Gregory looked up as she entered, a question in his eyes.

"Well, Miss Parker?" he inquired, his voice calm. "Did you find your grandmother's locket?"

Penny placed the heavy, metal chip on his desk. It clinked softly against the polished wood. "No, Mr. Gregory," she said, a triumphant gleam in her eyes. "But I think I found something even more interesting."

Mr. Gregory picked up the chip, turning it over in his fingers, his brow furrowing as he studied the 'M' engraving. "This is not an ordinary gambling token, Miss Parker. This has the weight and feel of something much more significant. And the material... it appears to be a rare alloy, perhaps even gold plated. Where did you find this?"

Penny then recounted her entire adventure: the disguised entrance, the winding staircase, the hidden corridor, and finally, the opulent, dusty Green Room. She described the other chips, the foreign currency, and the distinct lack of activity.

Mr. Gregory listened intently, his expression growing more serious with each detail. "A secret underground complex," he murmured, almost to himself. "And a room designed for high-level meetings, not simple recreation. This confirms my suspicions. Maxwell is involved in something far beyond mere hotel management."

"But what, Mr. Gregory?" Penny pressed. "The foreign currency, the special chips... is it still gambling? Or something else entirely?"

"It could be a form of private exchange, a black market of sorts, where illegal or sensitive transactions are made," Mr. Gregory mused. "The chips could represent a form of internal currency, used to mask the true value of the dealings. The 'M' could very well stand for Maxwell, or it could be a code for something else. And the fact that the room was dusty suggests that, while it has been used, it may not be in constant operation. Perhaps it is reserved for specific, infrequent events."

"Events of what nature?" Penny asked, her mind racing. "Could it be related to this 'big story' Francine Sellberg is after? She said it would discredit Harvey Maxwell."

"Indeed," Mr. Gregory said, his gaze fixed on the chip. "If these transactions are illicit, or if they involve powerful figures in a compromising way, that would certainly be a story with tremendous news value, as Francine put it. And it would certainly discredit Mr. Maxwell. He could be acting as a facilitator, providing a secure, untraceable venue for such dealings."

"So, the Green Room is a place where powerful people conduct shady business?" Penny clarified, a thrill of understanding passing through her.

"Precisely, Miss Parker," Mr. Gregory confirmed. "And your admission card, which Maxwell mistakenly allowed you to keep, gives you access. The question now is, what is the next step? How do we find out *what* kind of business, and *who* is involved?"

Penny thought for a moment, her fingers unconsciously tracing the outline of the green card in her pocket. "The room was dusty, Mr. Gregory. It hasn't been used recently, but it was clearly set up for something important. I think we need to find out when the next 'event' is scheduled. That's when we'll find out who is really behind all of this."

"A sound plan," Mr. Gregory agreed. "But how do we ascertain such information without arousing suspicion? Maxwell will be doubly vigilant now that he knows you are investigating him."

"He doesn't know about the secret entrance," Penny reminded him, a small smile playing on her lips. "And he doesn't know I have this." She patted her pocket where the chip lay. "I think the next step is to observe, and perhaps, to listen. The hotel staff, even the lower-level ones, often know more than they let on. They hear things, see things. If something big is coming up, there will be subtle signs, preparations. And I'm quite good at being observant, Mr. Gregory."

"I have no doubt of that, Miss Parker," he said, a genuine admiration in his eyes. "But we must be wary. If Maxwell discovers you poking around his private affairs, especially in that secret chamber, the consequences could be severe. This is no longer a simple game of cat and mouse. You are delving into a very dangerous world, one where the stakes are far higher than a lost locket."

"I understand the risks, Mr. Gregory," Penny said, her voice firm. "But I'm a reporter, and my father taught me that some stories are worth taking risks for. This one, I have a feeling, is one of them. And besides," she added, a mischievous glint in her eyes, "I've always been rather good at getting out of tight spots."

She left Mr. Gregory's study feeling a renewed sense of purpose. The 'M' chip felt heavy in her pocket, a tangible piece of the puzzle. The Green Room, far from being just a myth, was a reality, a hidden heart beating beneath the polished facade of the Fergus Hotel. And Penny Parker was determined to expose its secrets, no matter what danger lurked in the shadows. The broken rod that her father's newspaper represented, the "yellow" sheet Maxwell had so scornfully mentioned, now seemed to have a very real target, and Penny was ready to cast her line.

As she walked through the lobby, she spotted Francine Sellberg sitting in one of the plush armchairs, engrossed in a newspaper. Francine looked up, her eyes narrowing as she saw Penny. A hard smile played on her lips, but Penny merely returned a knowing glance. She knew Francine was still looking for her big story. Little did Francine know, Penny was already deep within its hidden depths. The chase was on, and Penny was one step ahead.

The evening descended on Pine Top. Penny, after a simple dinner in her room, spent hours poring over her notes, sketching out the layout of the hidden passages, and speculating on the nature of the 'M' chip. She also wrote a detailed letter to her father, carefully omitting the more dangerous aspects of her investigation, but hinting at the magnitude of the story she was uncovering. She mentioned the hotel's peculiar atmosphere, the strange behavior of Maxwell, and her growing suspicion of illicit activities. She trusted her father to read between the lines.

The next morning, Penny rose early, refreshed and determined. Her plan for the day was twofold: first, to subtly gather intelligence from the hotel staff about any upcoming events or unusual preparations; second, to pay another visit to Sara Jasko, hoping to glean some local gossip or insight into the hotel's less savory patrons, information a local like Sara might have heard.

She descended to the bustling breakfast room, choosing a table near the kitchen entrance, where she could observe the staff. She feigned interest in her toast and coffee, but her eyes and ears were working overtime. She watched the maids, the bellhops, and the kitchen staff go about their duties, listening to snippets of conversation.

Most of it was mundane chatter about weather, tips, or complaints about demanding guests. However, she did overhear two maids discussing a large "delivery" expected later in the week, something heavy and needing careful handling.

"They're bringing it through the service entrance again, I heard," one maid whispered to the other. "Another one of Mr. Maxwell's special orders, no doubt. Always so secretive."

"Aye," the other replied, shaking her head. "And they're setting up for a big party, too, aren't they? In the main ballroom. That'll be a lot of extra work."

Penny's ears perked up. A "delivery" through the service entrance, coinciding with a "big party" in the ballroom? It could be entirely unrelated, but the timing was suspicious. Maxwell's "special orders" and secrecy dovetailed perfectly with her discovery of the Green Room. Perhaps the party was a cover, or a distraction, for another illicit meeting or transaction in the hidden chamber.

Armed with this new piece of information, Penny finished her breakfast and decided to make her way to the Jasko cabin. The skiing weather was indeed glorious, a brilliant sun shining down on the snow-covered slopes. She strapped on her skis, the cold mountain air invigorating her.

The trip down the mountain was swift. She approached the Jasko cabin, recalling her last visit and Sara's headstrong nature. Penny wondered if Sara had managed to "slip away" from her grandfather.
