"There it is again!" Clara whispered, nudging Daisy with her elbow. "He's definitely doing it on purpose." Daisy peered through the binoculars, her brow furrowed in concentration. "I've always suspected Mr. Finch of being a little too quiet, a little too observant." "Oh, Clara, you have such an imagination," Daisy chuckled. "First the old woman who only buys tinned sardines was a jewel thief, and now Mr. Finch is a spy." "But would a spy necessarily be so obvious?" "Quiet!" Clara warned, pulling Daisy behind a cluster of sea oats. "He's turning the light off!"

The lighthouse beam vanished, plunging the shoreline into near darkness. A few moments later, a small, hunched figure emerged from the lighthouse door, a lantern swinging at his side. Mr. Finch began his slow descent down the winding path. He seemed to stop occasionally, scanning the horizon, then looking toward the town. "See?" Clara whispered when the old man was out of sight. "He's checking to see if anyone noticed. He knows what he's doing." "Or he's just an old man walking home in the dark," Daisy replied, though a hint of unease crept into her voice. "What are we waiting for?" Clara demanded, leaping to her feet. "Let's go investigate!"

From a distance Clara and Daisy had observed the lighthouse keenly. But as they approached the base of the tower, the dim light from the moon made everything look unfamiliar. They could not agree on the exact spot where Mr. Finch might have been signaling from or what he might have dropped. "He was definitely on that platform, I think," Clara said, peering up. "He might have left something there." "You're wasting time climbing those slippery steps," Daisy insisted. "I'll look around the base here. Maybe he dropped a message capsule, or a map." Selecting a patch of overgrown weeds near the lighthouse door, she began to poke around with a stick. The mysterious evidence proved elusive. Scarcely had the girls started work than a piercing foghorn blasted from the distant harbor. "Oh, it's getting foggy!" Daisy exclaimed, turning startled eyes toward the swirling mist. The fog rolled in faster and faster. Faced with a choice of abandoning the search or being completely disoriented, the girls decided to make a dash for Uncle Ben's cottage. As they darted through the gate, they were surprised to see Uncle Ben sitting on the porch swing. A pair of headphones lay on his lap. Whether he had been listening to the radio or their own antics they had no way of knowing. "Uncle Ben, have you seen Mr. Finch?" Clara asked, shaking the mist from her hair. "Indeed, I don't keep track of the old grumbler's wanderings," Uncle Ben replied, his eyes twinkling. "By the way, did you find what you were searching for near the lighthouse?" The question caught Clara off guard. She stammered a few words which only caused their uncle to smile in a knowing, amused way. "I don't mind telling you what I was listening to," he resumed. "It may save you a little trouble. I was just tuning into the old shortwave broadcasts." "Shortwave broadcasts!" "Yes, you know my hobby. There's been some unusual activity on an old military frequency, one that hasn't been used since the war." "Oh," Clara murmured, completely deflated. "I've been watching you girls through my window," Uncle Ben went on. "It really was amusing." "I can imagine," Clara agreed grimly. "Oh, well, I'm glad to provide a little amusement for this sleepy little town." She and Daisy retreated until they were screened from their uncle by a large oak tree. "I guess he scored on you that time, Clara," Daisy commented. "So we wasted our time looking for spy gadgets!" "You needn't rub it in." "But it's all so silly. Why don't we just trust Mr. Finch?" "I'll leave that job up to you. Furthermore, how do I know Uncle Ben was telling the truth? Maybe he just handed us that story so we wouldn't go asking questions about Mr. Finch's signals!" "That's so!" Daisy acknowledged. "Uncle Ben isn't the type to be listening to old wartime broadcasts for no reason." "If Mr. Finch was just fixing his lamp, he would have done it much faster," Clara reasoned. "Instead, he took his time. Then he flashed it in that particular pattern, as if by pre-arrangement." "You think he may have been sending a message, and Uncle Ben knows something about it?" "That's my theory, Lou. Oh, I wish this fog would lift." Restlessly Clara walked to the edge of the porch. The fog showed signs of thinning. And as she watched, a Coast Guard jeep drew up in front of the cottage. Lieutenant Miller leaped out. "Evening, Ben!" he greeted. They seem to originate from the old bunker near the lighthouse, and they've been accompanied by some rather peculiar light patterns from the tower itself."

Lieutenant Miller took the cassette and turned it over in his hand, a thoughtful expression on his face. He was a man of medium height with broad shoulders and a no-nonsense air that suggested long hours spent patrolling the sometimes treacherous coastline. “Peculiar light patterns, you say?” he mused, looking toward the distant, fog-shrouded lighthouse. “That adds a new dimension, Ben. I’d almost dismissed your earlier calls as simply an old radio enthusiast getting excited over a little static.”

Clara and Daisy, who had been hovering just out of earshot, now edged closer, their curiosity overcoming their earlier deflation. “We saw the lights too, Lieutenant!” Daisy piped up, her eyes bright with renewed excitement. “Mr. Finch was fiddling with his lamp, and he made it flash in a really strange way.”

Lieutenant Miller raised an eyebrow, his gaze shifting from Uncle Ben to the two girls. “Mr. Finch, you say? The lighthouse keeper?”

“Yes, sir,” Clara confirmed, stepping forward. “We thought he was fixing it at first, but then Daisy said it looked like he was sending a message. And then Uncle Ben found these signals.” She gestured toward the cassette player in Miller’s hand.

Miller rubbed his chin. “Interesting. Very interesting indeed. Ben, have you had a chance to run these recordings through your analyser yet?”

“Indeed, it does,” Miller replied, a slight smile playing on his lips. “And unfortunately, it’s not entirely fiction. We believe whoever is broadcasting is doing more than just rattling static. “But if the signals are coming from the bunker,” Clara began, her mind racing, “and Mr. Finch was flashing lights from the lighthouse… could he be involved?”

Lieutenant Miller’s expression became more serious. “It’s a possibility we can’t ignore, Clara. The lighthouse offers an excellent vantage point, and the old bunker is secluded. “He’s always seemed a bit grumpy, but not like an enemy agent,” Daisy chimed in, recalling their recent encounter with the lighthouse keeper.

“Appearances can be deceiving, girls,” Uncle Ben interjected. “That’s what makes a good spy, isn’t it? Blending in, seeming harmless.”

“Exactly,” Miller agreed. “Now, Ben, thank you for these recordings. They are very important. And girls, your observations about Mr. Finch’s lights are equally valuable. Have you noticed anything else unusual around the lighthouse or the bunker lately? Any strange visitors, boats, anything at all?”

Clara and Daisy looked at each other, trying to remember every detail of their recent escapades. “Well,” Clara began slowly, “we did see him fixing his lamp, and then he kind of disappeared for a while. And later, when we went back, his tools were still out. It was a bit odd for him to leave them.”

“And there was a strange old fishing boat anchored quite close to the shore a few days ago,” Daisy added, remembering. “It looked a bit out of place for this time of year.”

“A fishing boat, you say?” Miller’s eyes narrowed. “Did you get a look at its markings, or who was on board?”

“No, not really, Lieutenant,” Clara admitted. “It was pretty far out, and we were more interested in the crabs we were trying to catch.”

Miller nodded. “Well, every little detail helps. For now, I need you two to keep everything you’ve told me, and everything you might observe, strictly confidential. This is a sensitive investigation, and loose lips could put lives in danger.”

“We understand, Lieutenant,” Clara said, feeling a thrill of importance. “We won’t tell a soul.”

“Good,” Miller replied. “I’m going to take these recordings back to headquarters immediately. I’ll need to confer with Army intelligence. Ben, I might be back to pick your brain on some of these frequencies later.”

Clara, however, was still deep in thought. “But it’s all so confusing, Daisy. First we thought Mr. Finch was hiding something, then Uncle Ben said he was just listening to radio, and now Lieutenant Miller says it’s all about enemy agents and prisoner escapes. Who are we supposed to believe?”

The next morning, Clara and Daisy couldn’t shake the feeling of unease that had settled over them. us pinpoint the exact location of illegal radio transmissions," Lieutenant Miller finished, his gaze sweeping the horizon toward the lighthouse. "And this is a very sensitive operation, girls. Not something to be discussed with anyone, not even your uncle Ben. Do you understand?"

Agent Sterling, I think we should modify our reconnaissance plan. The walk to the lighthouse was longer than they remembered, perhaps because of the growing tension. Every shadow seemed to hold a secret, every distant sound felt like a warning. They kept their eyes peeled, scanning the dense foliage alongside the path and the rocky slopes above them.

As they finally reached the base of the lighthouse, its beam was already cutting through the gathering gloom, a comforting, rhythmic pulse in the vast expanse of the ocean. The lighthouse keeper’s cottage, nestled beside the towering structure, had a light glowing warmly in one of its windows.

“Good, Mr. Henderson is home,” Daisy whispered, a measure of relief in her voice. “At least we won’t be walking into an empty building.”

“Clara, Daisy, my dears!” he exclaimed, his voice a little gruff from age but full of warmth. “What brings you out here at this hour? Did you come to watch the sunset?”

“Yes, Mr. Henderson,” Clara replied, smiling sweetly. “And we thought we’d say hello. Everything alright tonight?”

“Right as rain, my dears,” he chuckled. “Just making myself a pot of tea. Come in, come in. It’s getting a bit chilly out there.”

They stepped inside the small, cozy cottage. The air was thick with the scent of old wood, beeswax, and brewing tea. A large, well-fed cat lay curled on a cushion by the fireplace, purring contentedly.

Mr. Henderson’s brow furrowed slightly. “Up to the top? Well, I suppose so, but be quick about it. I’ll be closing up the lamp room soon for my nightly checks.”

They hurried through the cottage and into the base of the lighthouse. The spiral staircase, worn smooth by generations of footsteps, seemed to stretch endlessly upwards into the darkness, illuminated only by the occasional window slits and the faint light filtering down from the lamp room above.

As they ascended, they kept their senses alert. They listened for any unusual sounds, sniffed for any strange odors. The air grew cooler, and the wind outside seemed louder as they neared the top. There was nothing overtly suspicious, no signs of forced entry or recent disturbance. The lighthouse felt as familiar and unchanging as always.

They finally reached the lamp room, a circular chamber dominated by the massive Fresnel lens, which rotated slowly, casting its powerful beam out to sea. The panoramic view was breathtaking, even in the fading light.

“It’s beautiful, isn’t it?” Daisy murmured, looking out at the endless expanse of ocean meeting the darkening sky.

“It is,” Clara agreed, but her eyes were not on the view. She was systematically scanning the small room, her gaze sweeping over the polished brass, the sturdy iron railings, the nooks and crannies where supplies were stored.

Suddenly, her eyes caught on something. Tucked away on a high shelf, partially obscured by a coil of rope and an old navigation chart, was a small, dark object. It seemed out of place. Mr. Henderson was meticulous, and rarely left anything out of its proper place.

“What’s that?” Clara whispered, pointing towards the object.

Daisy followed her gaze. It looked like a small, old-fashioned camera, or perhaps a pair of binoculars, but it was wrapped in a piece of dark cloth.

“I don’t think that belongs to Mr. Henderson,” Daisy whispered back. “He only uses his spyglass, and that’s always in its stand.”

Clara reached up, carefully dislodging the object. As she unwrapped the cloth, a small, compact radio receiver was revealed. It was a shortwave receiver, much smaller than the one they had seen in Jerry Livingston’s monitoring truck, but clearly designed for picking up distant broadcasts. And beside it, also wrapped in the cloth, was a folded piece of paper.

“Oh, my,” Clara breathed, her fingers trembling slightly as she unfolded the paper. It was a crude sketch, almost like a child’s drawing, of the lighthouse and the surrounding coastline. But on the sketch, a small ‘X’ was marked on the very top of the lighthouse, right where they were standing. And below it, scribbled in faint pencil, were a series of numbers and letters: “B4,  2300Z,  NW Bearing.”

Daisy gasped. “B4! He was here! He was planning to broadcast from here!”

Clara’s heart pounded. This was it. Concrete evidence. And the time, 2300Z, was for Zulu Time, or Universal Coordinated Time, which meant it was a specific time for a broadcast. They quickly calculated the local time difference in their heads. It was only an hour and a half away.

“We have to tell Lieutenant Miller!” Clara exclaimed, her voice hushed but urgent. “Right now!”

They quickly re-wrapped the receiver and the sketch, placing them back on the shelf exactly as they had found them. They didn't want Mr. Henderson to notice anything was amiss, or for B4, if he returned, to realize his hideout had been compromised.

As they descended the spiral staircase, their earlier caution was replaced by a frantic urgency. They had to get back to the inn, and they had to make that call.

They said a hasty goodnight to Mr. Henderson, offering a quick excuse about needing to get home before it was too dark. He seemed a little perplexed by their sudden rush, but waved them off with a kindly smile.

Once outside, they broke into a run, their feet pounding against the sandy path. The darkness was almost complete now, the lighthouse beam cutting a solitary path across the churning sea. The wind seemed to mock them, pulling at their clothes and whispering ominous warnings.

They finally burst into the lobby of the Crystal Inn, breathless and disheveled. The desk clerk looked up, startled by their sudden appearance.

Clara, ignoring his questioning gaze, rushed to the lobby phone, her fingers fumbling with the slip of paper Agent Thompson had given them. Daisy stood beside her, panting, her eyes darting nervously towards the entrance, as if expecting B4 himself to appear at any moment.

Clara dialed the number, her hand shaking slightly. It rang several times before a gruff voice answered.

“Lieutenant Miller’s line,” the voice said. “Who is this?”

“It’s Clara,” she gasped, trying to catch her breath. “Clara from the beach, Lieutenant. We found something important. At the lighthouse. B4 was there. He was planning to broadcast from there tonight!”

There was a moment of stunned silence on the other end, then the voice, now distinctly Lieutenant Miller’s, became sharper, more alert. “Clara? Are you at the inn? What did you find? Are you safe?”

“We’re safe, Lieutenant,” Daisy interjected, taking the phone. “We found a radio receiver and a map. It says B4 is broadcasting from the lighthouse at 2300Z tonight! That’s in less than two hours!”

The information seemed to electrify Lieutenant Miller. “You’re sure of this? You saw a time and a name?”

“Yes, sir! B4, 2300Z, and NW Bearing,” Clara confirmed, taking the phone back. “It was hidden on a shelf in the lamp room.”

“Good heavens,” Miller muttered, his voice barely audible over the phone. “Girls, you’ve done it again. Stay right there. Do not leave that lobby. Agent Thompson and I are on our way. Do you understand? Do not move from that spot.”

“We understand, Lieutenant,” Clara replied, her voice firm despite the adrenaline coursing through her veins. “We’ll be here.”

She hung up the phone, her hand still trembling. She and Daisy exchanged a look of profound realization. They hadn’t just found a clue; they had pinpointed B4’s next move, potentially preventing an important broadcast and leading the agents directly to him. The game was truly afoot, and they, Clara and Daisy, were at its very heart. They settled back into the armchairs, but there was no pretense of nonchalance now. Trying to leave them behind might waste precious time in an argument.

"All right," he conceded with a sigh, "but you must understand the rules. You will follow my instructions without question, the moment I give them. You will stay behind me, always. If I tell you to hide, you hide. If I tell you to be silent, you do not make a sound. Is that clear?"

"Perfectly clear, Jerry," Penny said, her voice filled with a new seriousness. Louise nodded beside her, her earlier fear giving way to a sense of purpose.

"Good. Now, let's move. We have no time to lose."

Within minutes, the small team was ready. Jerry, Sergeant Davis, Ensign Miller, Penny, and Louise piled into the rugged Army jeep. The engine roared to life, and they sped out of the clearing, leaving the monitoring truck and its silent screens behind. The drive was bumpy and fast, taking them along winding dirt roads that eventually gave way to a rough track leading towards the coast. The rain had entirely ceased, but the sky remained overcast, a heavy grey blanket suppressing the daylight, making the afternoon seem older than it was.

"The promontory where the lighthouse stands is notoriously difficult to access by land," Jerry explained over the rumble of the jeep. "The track we're on will take us as far as it can, then we'll have to proceed on foot. It's a rocky, treacherous climb in places. Be careful where you step."

Penny gazed out the window, her mind racing. The thought of Mr. Thompson and Clara, possibly hurt or captured by enemy agents, filled her with a mixture of dread and resolve. This was far more serious than anything she had imagined. The "Enemy" was no longer a distant concept but a very real threat right here on their own coastline.

As they got closer to the coast, the air grew cooler and salty, carrying the tang of seaweed and damp rock. The sound of crashing waves, muted at first, grew steadily louder, a ceaseless, powerful rhythm against the shore. The jeep finally juddered to a halt at the very end of the track, where the land abruptly rose into a jumble of massive, moss-covered boulders and sheer rock faces.

"This is as far as we go, ladies," Jerry announced, switching off the engine. The sudden silence, broken only by the wind and waves, was unnerving. "From here, it's a climb. Keep your eyes peeled. We don't know what we'll find."

They disembarked, Jerry and his men securing their gear while Penny and Louise quickly scanned their surroundings. The terrain was indeed rugged, a chaotic landscape of jagged rocks interspersed with hardy coastal vegetation clinging to every crevice. The lighthouse, a sturdy, whitewashed tower, was now visible in the distance, perched precariously on the highest point of the promontory, its lantern room a dark, unlit eye against the grey sky. It looked lonely, isolated, and vaguely ominous.

"It looks... quiet," Louise whispered, a tremor in her voice.

"Too quiet," Jerry agreed grimly. "That's what worries me."

They began their ascent, Jerry leading the way, his steps measured and cautious. Sergeant Davis and Ensign Miller followed close behind, their military training evident in their agile movements. Penny and Louise, though not as experienced, tried their best to keep up, their gaze sweeping the rocks and tangled bushes for any sign of disturbance.

The climb was strenuous. Penny felt her breath coming in short gasps, her leg muscles aching with the effort. Louise, though smaller, showed remarkable resilience, her determination matching Penny's. They scrambled over slippery rocks, carefully picking their way around dense thickets of gnarled bushes that seemed to guard the path. The silence was broken only by the crunch of their boots on loose shale and the insistent roar of the sea below.

As they drew nearer to the lighthouse, an eerie feeling intensified. The air seemed heavier, charged with an unspoken tension. Penny noticed a broken branch lying on the ground, seemingly snapped rather than naturally fallen. A little further, a patch of earth looked disturbed, as if something heavy had been dragged across it. She pointed it out to Jerry.

"Good eye, Penny," he commended, bending down to examine the marks. "Looks like fresh tracks, multiple sets. And not just ours. Something was definitely here, recently." His hand instinctively went to the pistol holstered at his hip. "Stay close, both of you. And be absolutely silent from this point on."

They continued their stealthy approach, their senses heightened. The path finally opened up onto a small, level plateau surrounding the lighthouse. The stark white tower rose majestically from the rocks, but its usual welcoming presence was entirely absent. The entrance door, usually secured, appeared to be ajar, a dark, gaping maw in the pristine wall. A large, wooden crate lay overturned near the base, its contents scattered across the ground. Empty ration tins, a few books, and a broken lantern were visible.

"Definitely signs of a struggle," Sergeant Davis muttered, his voice low.

Jerry held up a hand, signaling them to stop. He crouched behind a cluster of large boulders, peering intensely at the lighthouse. "Ensign Miller, cover the rear. Sergeant Davis, with me. Penny, Louise, stay here. Do not move unless I tell you to. Understood?"

"Understood," Penny whispered back, her heart pounding. She clutched Louise's hand, feeling the coldness of her fingers.

Jerry and Sergeant Davis advanced cautiously, their pistols drawn. They moved with a practiced fluidity, keeping low, their eyes constantly scanning the shadows around the lighthouse. They reached the partially open door, and Jerry pushed it wider with his foot, revealing a darkened interior.

A faint clatter from within.

Jerry and Sergeant Davis exchanged a quick glance, then slipped inside, disappearing into the gloom. Penny and Louise held their breath, straining to hear anything from within. Minutes stretched into an eternity. The only sounds were the distant waves and the thumping of their own anxious hearts.

Suddenly, a muffled shout, followed by the unmistakable sound of a struggle. A thud, then another. Penny's blood ran cold. She wanted to rush in, to help, but Jerry's instructions had been absolute. She glanced at Louise, whose face was pale with fear, her eyes wide.

Then, silence again. A heavy, oppressive silence that seemed to press down on them. Had Jerry and the sergeant been overcome? Were they too late?

Penny couldn't stand it any longer. She knew she shouldn't disobey, but the thought of their friends in danger spurred her to action. "We have to do something, Louise," she whispered, tugging at her friend's sleeve. "We can't just wait out here."

"But Jerry said..." Louise began, her voice trembling.

"I know what he said! But if they need help, we're the only ones here." Penny cast a quick look around. Her gaze fell upon a small, narrow window on the side of the lighthouse, partially obscured by an overgrown bush. It was too small for an adult, but perhaps not for them. "Come on. Follow me!"

Carefully, Penny crept around the side of the lighthouse, Louise close on her heels. The window was indeed small, dusty, and high off the ground, but a stack of discarded lobster traps nearby offered a makeshift step. Penny clambered onto the traps, peering through the grimy pane.

The interior was dim, lit only by a sliver of natural light filtering through other windows and the open door. Dust motes danced in the sparse beams. She could make out a circular room, perhaps the main living quarters for the lighthouse keeper. Furniture was overturned, papers scattered everywhere, and a chair lay broken in the middle of the floor. It was clearly the scene of a violent confrontation.

Then she saw them.

Jerry and Sergeant Davis were there, both on their feet, their pistols still drawn, but pointed downwards. They were standing over two figures, who lay still on the floor. One was Mr. Thompson, his usually neat clothes rumpled and torn, a dark bruise forming on his forehead. The other was Clara, her red hair disheveled, a trickle of blood at the corner of her mouth.

"Mr. Thompson! Clara!" Penny cried out, forgetting all about silence. She fumbled with the window latch, trying to open it, but it was stuck fast.

At the sound of her voice, Jerry's head snapped up. His eyes, though still wary, showed a flash of relief. "Penny! Louise! Stay outside!" he commanded, his voice sharp. "It's not safe!"

But Penny was already scrambling down from the lobster traps, ignoring his order. She ran to the open front door, Louise right behind her, and burst into the lighthouse.

"Are they all right, Jerry?" Penny demanded, rushing to Mr. Thompson's side. She knelt, her small hands hovering, unsure how to help.

Jerry quickly holstered his weapon, kneeling beside Thompson himself. "They're alive, but they've taken a beating," he said, checking Thompson's pulse. "Clara too. Looks like they were caught by surprise."

Sergeant Davis, meanwhile, was carefully searching the room, his eyes sharp and methodical. He moved like a shadow, examining every corner, every overturned object.

"Who did this?" Louise asked, her voice barely a whisper, as she looked at Clara's pale face.

"Our friends from the other side, no doubt," Jerry replied, his voice grim. "They must have been watching the lighthouse, waiting for an opportunity. Perhaps the storm gave them the cover they needed to approach undetected." He gently shook Thompson's shoulder. "Thompson? Can you hear me?"

Thompson groaned, his eyelids fluttering. His eyes, when they finally opened, were unfocused and pained. "Jerry... you came... they... they found us..." he mumbled, his voice hoarse.

"Who, Thompson? Who found you?" Jerry pressed, leaning closer.

"The... the Germans... looking for information... the codes..." Thompson coughed, wincing. "They didn't get them... Clara hid them... told her to..." He trailed off, losing consciousness again.

Jerry looked up, his gaze meeting Penny's. "He said 'the Germans'. And they were looking for codes." He turned to Clara, gently checking her for more serious injuries. "Clara, can you hear me?"

Clara slowly stirred, her eyes blinking open. She looked confused for a moment, then recognition dawned. Her eyes darted around the room, then fixed on Jerry. "The book..." she gasped, her voice weak. "The code book... I hid it..."

"Where, Clara? Where did you hide it?" Jerry urged, his voice gentle but firm.

Clara winced, her hand going to her head. "The old man's decoy... the fish bones..."

Penny gasped. "Fish bones! Mrs. Deline's fish bones!"

Jerry's head snapped around, his eyes locking onto Penny. "What are you talking about, Penny? What fish bones?"

"Mrs. Deline! She buried a package of fish bones in the sand dune on Sunset Beach!" Penny exclaimed, the pieces of the puzzle suddenly clicking into place. "She said she was disposing of Jim McCoy's garbage, but we saw her write something on her skirt. She was acting so suspicious!"

Jerry's mind worked quickly, connecting the disparate threads. "Jim McCoy, the lighthouse keeper. Decoy. Fish bones. So the 'garbage' was a cover! Mrs. Deline was a courier, not a spy, but carrying the code book for Thompson!" He stood up, a new urgency in his movements. "Sergeant Davis, check this entire lighthouse. See if you can find anything the enemy might have left behind, or anything missing besides the book. Ensign Miller, stay with Thompson and Clara, administer first aid. Penny, Louise, show me where this Mrs. Deline buried that package!"

"But the Germans might still be nearby!" Louise protested, looking around nervously.

"Then we have to be quick," Jerry declared, his eyes hard. "If they didn't find the code book here, they'll be looking elsewhere. And if Thompson's story is true, they'll likely go back to Sunset Beach, hoping to retrace the 'courier's' steps. We have to beat them to it."

Penny and Louise, their fear now mixed with a renewed sense of purpose, led Jerry out of the lighthouse. The journey back down the rocky promontory was faster, fueled by adrenaline and the pressing need to retrieve the crucial code book. Jerry explained his theory as they hurried along.

"Mrs. Deline must have been an unwitting courier, or perhaps a volunteer in a local network. Thompson, knowing his station was compromised or might soon be, had Clara take the code book, disguised as something innocuous, to a safe drop-off point. Sunset Beach, seemingly unimportant, would have been an ideal location for a hidden exchange, precisely because it's *not* a manufacturing town, yet watched by the Coast Guard. The 'writing on her skirt' could have been a pre-arranged signal or marker for Thompson's contact to find the correct spot, or perhaps even a mnemonic for her own memory."

"But she denied it," Penny mused, remembering Mrs. Deline's knowing smile. "She said it was just fish bones, and she watched us dig, amused."

"A good cover story," Jerry acknowledged. "And her 'amusement' could have been genuine relief that you didn't find the real secret. She might have been testing the waters, seeing if anyone was indeed looking for something. Or perhaps she was simply good at her role. Either way, her actions now make perfect sense in light of Thompson's condition."

The jeep ride back to Sunset Beach was a blur. The sun was beginning to dip below the horizon, casting long, purple shadows across the landscape. The monitoring truck was where they had left it, Ensign Miller standing guard, looking relieved to see them return. Jerry quickly updated his team on the situation, ordering Miller to keep trying to contact headquarters on any available frequency, and to prepare the monitoring equipment for immediate deployment should Thompson's broadcast resume.

"We need to get back to those dunes, and fast," Jerry urged Penny and Louise. "Can you pinpoint the location precisely?"

"We marked it," Penny said confidently. "From a distance, it all looked the same, but we dug right in the area. We can show you."

They drove the jeep as close to the beach as possible, then dashed towards the sand dunes. The wind had picked up, whipping grains of sand across their faces. The familiar landscape of the beach, earlier a place of playful speculation, now felt charged with a new, dangerous energy.

"It was right around here," Penny announced, pointing towards a cluster of dunes not far from where they had encountered Mrs. Deline. "Louise and I were digging in separate mounds. I think it was this one," she indicated a larger dune, "or perhaps this one," she gestured to another nearby.

"We'll need to work quickly and systematically," Jerry instructed, pulling out a small folding shovel from his kit. Sergeant Davis, having rejoined them, also produced a shovel. "We can't afford to miss it. The Germans will surely come looking here once they realize the book isn't at the lighthouse."

They began to dig, the two men working with powerful, rhythmic strokes, while Penny and Louise, using their bare hands and smaller, makeshift tools, sifted through the sand with meticulous care. The cool sand yielded easily, but the sheer volume was daunting. Dune after dune, they excavated, tossing sand aside, their eyes scanning for anything out of place.

The light was fading rapidly, the sky darkening to a deep indigo. A sliver of moon began to peek through the clouds. Time was running out. Penny felt a growing sense of despair. What if the Germans had already been here? What if Mrs. Deline had moved it? Or what if, in their frantic digging, they had simply missed it the first time?

"Wait!" Louise suddenly cried out, her voice sharp with excitement. She was digging furiously in a smaller dune, one that Penny had dismissed earlier. "I feel something! Something hard!"

Jerry and Sergeant Davis immediately converged on Louise's spot, their shovels pausing. Penny scrambled over, her heart pounding. Louise continued to dig, brushing away handfuls of sand, until a corner of a small, rectangular object became visible. It was wrapped in thick oilcloth, bound tightly with twine.

"That's it!" Penny gasped, recognizing the description of the package they had seen Mrs. Deline bury.

Jerry carefully unearthed the package, his fingers deft as he untied the twine. The oilcloth, though damp from the sand, was intact. He unwrapped it, revealing not fish bones, but a sturdy, leather-bound book, no larger than his hand. Its cover was blank, unassuming, but its weight and the seriousness in Jerry's expression spoke volumes.

"The code book," Jerry breathed, his voice filled with relief. He quickly flipped through a few pages. They were filled with dense columns of numbers and seemingly random letters, precisely what Thompson had been broadcasting, and what the enemy desperately wanted.

"We got it!" Penny cheered, unable to contain her excitement. Louise hugged her, laughing with relief.

"You certainly did, girls," Jerry said, a rare, genuine smile spreading across his face. "This was excellent work. You've potentially saved lives, and certainly protected invaluable intelligence." He carefully re-wrapped the book and secured it in an inner pocket of his jacket. "Now, we need to get out of here. And quickly. The Germans will be hot on our heels once they realize they've been outsmarted."

As if on cue, the distant sound of an engine reached their ears, growing steadily louder. It was not the familiar rumble of their jeep.

"That's not Ensign Miller," Sergeant Davis stated grimly, his hand going for his pistol again. "Sounds like a heavier vehicle. And it's coming fast."

"They must have doubled back from the lighthouse," Jerry deduced, his eyes scanning the road leading to the beach. "Or they had a separate party sent directly here. They're not fools." He pointed towards a cluster of dense beach grass and low-lying scrub near the edge of the dunes. "Hide, both of you! Stay absolutely still and out of sight. Do not move until I tell you to. Sergeant, with me. We need to create a diversion, give the girls a chance to slip away and warn Ensign Miller."

"No, Jerry!" Penny protested, but he was already pushing her and Louise towards the cover.

"You have the most important job now, Penny! Get to the jeep, tell Ensign Miller to get out of here with the monitoring equipment and call for reinforcements! This code book is too important to risk!" Jerry commanded, his voice firm, leaving no room for argument. "Go!"

Before Penny or Louise could object further, Jerry and Sergeant Davis melted into the shadows of the dunes, moving towards the incoming sound of the vehicle. Penny knew they were trying to draw the enemy away, giving her and Louise a chance to escape. The sound of the approaching engine grew deafening. Headlights, powerful and blinding, suddenly swept over the beach, illuminating the sand and dunes with stark, artificial light.

Penny grabbed Louise's hand, pulling her deeper into the dense foliage. They crouched low, their hearts pounding in their chests, the sand cool against their faces. The enemy vehicle, a dark, heavy truck, rumbled to a halt right where they had been digging, its engine idling loudly. Dark figures, heavily armed, leaped from the back, their voices harsh and guttural as they began to search the dunes, their flashlights cutting through the darkness like predatory eyes.

Penny strained her ears, trying to hear Jerry and Sergeant Davis. There was no sound of gunfire, no shouts. Had they managed to evade detection? Or were they in trouble? The thought sent a fresh wave of fear through her. She knew she had to obey Jerry's last order. The code book was safe, but the battle for it was far from over. With a surge of renewed determination, Penny began to crawl, pulling Louise along, deeper into the darkness, away from the enemy, towards the safety of their waiting jeep. They had to warn Ensign Miller. They had to get help. The fate of Thompson, Clara, and perhaps even the war effort itself, now rested heavily on their young shoulders. The quiet beach had turned into a dangerous battlefield, and Penny and Louise, far from being mere spectators, were now central players in a game with very real, very perilous stakes.
