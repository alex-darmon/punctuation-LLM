They positioned themselves behind a stack of overflowing refuse bins, the stench surprisingly muted by the freezing air. Chloe shivered, partly from the cold, partly from the anticipation.

The silence was profound, broken only by the gentle patter of snowflakes and the occasional distant hoot of an owl. Chloe watched the service door, her eyes straining in the gloom. Every shadow seemed to shift, every gust of wind seemed to carry a whispered secret.

Then, precisely at twelve thirty, the service door creaked open. A small, stooped figure emerged, pulling the door shut quietly behind him. It was the night porter, a thin, gaunt man with tired eyes and a face etched with worry. He wore a threadbare uniform, and clutched a cigarette in his gloved hand. He shuffled over to a sheltered alcove, his back to them, and lit the cigarette, the flare of the match briefly illuminating his drawn features.

Chloe glanced at Brenda, who gave a slight nod, a signal to wait. The night porter took a long, slow drag from his cigarette, exhaling a plume of smoke that drifted upwards into the snowy night. He seemed lost in thought, his shoulders slumped as if under a great burden.

This was their moment. This was the vulnerable, human element in Brenda’s chilling tale. Chloe felt a surge of adrenaline, her heart pounding a silent rhythm against her ribs. She was no longer just Elias Maxwell’s daughter, no longer a reporter trying to make a name for herself. She was on the cusp of something far greater, something that promised to expose a darkness lurking beneath the polished surface of the Grand Hotel.

Brenda moved first, a silent shadow detaching itself from the bins. Chloe followed, her footsteps barely disturbing the fresh snow. They approached the night porter from either side, cutting off his escape.

The man sensed their presence before they spoke. He stiffened, dropping his cigarette, and slowly turned, his eyes wide with a mixture of fear and surprise.

"Good evening," Brenda said, her voice low and even, devoid of any aggression. "We need to talk."

The night porter’s eyes darted between Brenda and Chloe, his face paling even further in the dim light. He swallowed hard, his throat bobbing nervously. "I, I have nothing to say," he stammered, his voice a reedy whisper. "Please, leave me alone."

"We know about the changes in the register," Chloe said, stepping forward slightly, her voice quiet but firm. "We know about the parcels. And we know about the guests who vanish."

The night porter’s eyes flickered with a raw, undeniable terror. He looked as if he might bolt, but Brenda had subtly positioned herself to block his path.

"Who are you?" he croaked, his gaze fixed on Chloe, as if sensing a flicker of youth and perhaps a sliver of mercy.

"We are reporters," Chloe replied. "And we are investigating the strange occurrences here at the Grand Hotel. We believe you know something about them. Something important."

"I know nothing," the man insisted, though his voice trembled. He wrung his gloved hands together, his gaze constantly darting, as if seeking an invisible escape route.

"We think otherwise," Brenda stated, her tone now sharper, more authoritative. "And we are not leaving until we get some answers. We can do this the easy way, Mr.—"

"Jenkins," the porter supplied automatically, then seemed to regret it instantly.

"—Mr. Jenkins," Brenda continued smoothly. "Or we can do it the hard way. We can involve the authorities. And I assure you, Mr. Jenkins, when the authorities start asking questions, they tend to look very closely at the people who were covering things up."

Jenkins visibly flinched at the mention of the authorities. He cast a desperate look back at the hotel door, as if hoping for a miraculous rescue. But the door remained stubbornly shut, silent and unyielding.

"They will kill me," Jenkins whispered, his voice barely audible over the soft hiss of the snow. "If I talk, they will kill me. And my family."

Chloe and Brenda exchanged a look. This was more serious, and more terrifying, than they had initially imagined. The night porter was not just complicit; he was genuinely afraid for his life. This was not merely about a "big story" anymore. This was about saving lives, perhaps even their own.

"Who are 'they,' Mr. Jenkins?" Chloe asked, her heart pounding. "Who are you so afraid of?"

The night porter’s eyes were filled with a profound, bone-deep terror. He hesitated, then seemed to make a desperate decision. He took a quick, shaky breath, and began to speak. "It is not Mr. Thorne," he rasped, his voice still barely above a whisper. "He is just a puppet. The real power, it comes from the other side. From the Crimson Room."

Chloe felt a chill that had nothing to do with the winter air. The Crimson Room, the mysterious parcels, the vanishing guests. It was all connected, just as Brenda had suspected. And the night porter, Mr. Jenkins, was about to pull back the veil on a secret far darker than any they had imagined.Brenda let her sentence hang, her eyes narrowing as she studied Chloe’s face. "They look away when you ask about the strange incidents. They pretend not to hear, or they suddenly remember an urgent task elsewhere. It is a conspiracy of silence, Chloe, deeper than anything your father Elias has ever uncovered."

Chloe folded her arms across her chest, a thoughtful frown creasing her brow. "A conspiracy of silence? That sounds rather dramatic, even for you, Brenda. Are you implying that Mr. Thorne is running some sort of secret society out of his hotel, where guests disappear and the staff are sworn to secrecy?"

Brenda allowed a faint, almost imperceptible smirk to play on her lips. "I am implying nothing, Chloe. I am merely reporting my observations. The facts are clear: people arrive, they stay for a time, and then they are gone. No check-outs, no forwarding addresses, just an empty room and a freshly made bed. And the names on the register are invariably false, as I said."

"How can you be certain the names are false?" Chloe challenged, her reporter’s instincts fully engaged now. She might be skeptical, but the idea of a genuine mystery stirred something within her.

"Because," Brenda said, stepping closer and lowering her voice conspiratorially, "I have seen the night porter changing the registers. Not adding new names, mind you, but carefully altering existing ones, sometimes even tearing out pages. He performs these little acts of forgery in the dead of night, usually after one of these 'unusual guests' has, shall we say, completed their stay."

Chloe felt a shiver of genuine unease, despite her outward composure. The image of a furtive night porter, tearing pages from a hotel register, was far more concrete and unsettling than vague rumors of malpractice. "You have seen this yourself?" she asked, her voice hushed.

"With my own eyes, Chloe," Brenda confirmed, nodding slowly. "I have spent many sleepless nights observing the service entrance, the back corridors, and yes, even the manager’s office. There is a small, rarely used office at the end of a forgotten hallway on the ground floor, just off the old laundry room. That is where the porter does his work. And it is also where some of these mysterious parcels are stored, before they are either delivered to the disappearing guests or, more curiously, retrieved

"Retrieved?" Chloe echoed, the word hanging heavy in the dim light of the corridor. The atmosphere, already thick with unspoken anxieties, now seemed to press in on her, cold and tangible. Brenda's quiet words, devoid of her usual theatrical flourish, held a chilling authenticity that pierced Chloe's journalistic skepticism. The image of the furtive night porter, tearing pages, altering records, now combined with the unsettling notion of parcels that were not merely delivered, but sometimes, mysteriously, *retrieved*.

"Yes, retrieved," Brenda confirmed, her voice a low murmur that barely carried above the distant creaks and groans of the old hotel. "And always by the same individual, a man I have only seen in shadow, whose face is perpetually obscured by the brim of a wide-brimmed hat. He arrives late, always after midnight, and never uses the main entrance. He comes and goes through a service door at the very back, almost hidden by the old boiler room. He never speaks, merely presents a small, inscribed card to the porter, who then silently hands over a parcel. These parcels, Chloe, are always wrapped in a particular kind of dark, oiled paper, and they are always bound with crimson twine."

Chloe felt a jolt of recognition, a prickling sensation along her scalp. Crimson twine. It was a detail too specific to be coincidence. "The Crimson Room," she breathed, the name now taking on a sinister resonance. "He mentioned the Crimson Room. The night porter, Jenkins. He said Mr. Thorne was just a puppet, and the real power came from the other side, from the Crimson Room."

Brenda nodded slowly, her gaze unwavering. "Indeed. And I believe the parcels are directly connected to it. What they contain, I cannot say. But I have observed the weight of them. They are not light. They are not empty. And the manner in which they are handled, with such extreme care, suggests something of considerable value, or perhaps, of considerable danger."

"So, what do you propose, Brenda?" Chloe asked, her mind racing, piecing together the fragments of information. "We cannot simply confront Mr. Thorne. If he is merely a puppet, as Jenkins implied, then he will either deny everything or be too frightened to speak."

"Confrontation is hardly our style, is it, Chloe?" Brenda replied, a faint, sardonic smile playing on her lips. "No, we must follow the trail, just as we always do. We must find this office you spoke of, the one where the porter performs his clandestine acts. And we must see what secrets it holds."

"It will be risky," Chloe cautioned, though a thrill of anticipation, a familiar and potent surge of adrenaline, coursed through her veins. This was the kind of story her father, Elias, would have relished, a true journalistic treasure hunt. "If we are caught, Mr. Thorne will have every right to dismiss us, or worse."

"And if we are not caught, Chloe," Brenda countered, her eyes gleaming with an adventurous spark, "then we might uncover something that will shake this town to its foundations. We might even save lives, if Jenkins's words hold any truth. 'Vanishing guests' is not a phrase to be taken lightly, my dear."

Chloe considered this. Brenda was right. The stakes were high, but the potential reward, both professionally and ethically, was immense. "Very well," she said, squaring her shoulders. "Where is this office? And how do we gain access without alerting anyone?"

Brenda's smile widened. "I have already ascertained its location, as I mentioned, off the old laundry room on the ground floor. As for access, the laundry room is generally locked at this hour, but there is a small, rarely used service entrance accessible from the rear alley. It is usually left unlatched, a relic of a time when staff members would slip in and out unseen. A simple push, and we should be inside."

"And the porter?" Chloe asked, a flicker of concern in her eyes. "When does he typically perform his duties there?"

"He has a precise schedule," Brenda informed her, lowering her voice further. "He makes his rounds, checking each floor, from eleven o'clock until midnight. Then, he retreats to the main reception desk until one. It is between midnight and one that he goes to his office, after ensuring the building is quiet. We have perhaps thirty minutes to make our move, before he settles in."

"Thirty minutes," Chloe repeated, a sense of urgency now propelling her forward. "Then we must be quick. Show me the way, Brenda."

Silently, Brenda led Chloe down the dimly lit corridor, their footsteps muffled by the thick carpet. They descended a flight of service stairs, rarely used by guests, which spiraled downwards into the cooler, more utilitarian depths of the hotel. The air grew heavier, smelling of disinfectant, stale linen, and the faint, earthy scent of old stone. The soft hum of distant machinery, probably the hotel's heating system, vibrated through the floor.

They emerged into a narrow, unlit passage, where bare light bulbs hung at infrequent intervals, casting long, distorted shadows. On their left, a series of heavy wooden doors led, presumably, to storage rooms and maintenance closets. On their right, the wall was lined with stacks of folded white sheets and towels, emitting a faint, clean smell.

"The laundry room is just beyond this turn," Brenda whispered, pointing ahead. "And the service entrance, our entry point, is at the very end of the alley outside."

They navigated the passage with practiced stealth, Brenda moving with an almost preternatural grace, Chloe following closely, her senses heightened. The silence of the service areas was profound, punctuated only by their soft breathing and the distant creaking of the old building.

Rounding a corner, they found themselves at the entrance to the main laundry room. It was a cavernous space, filled with hulking industrial washing machines and dryers, all silent now, their

massive drums like slumbering giants. The air, though cleaner than the passage, still held the ghosts of detergent and steam. A single, bare bulb near the entrance cast a feeble glow, leaving most of the vast room in deep shadow. Chloe felt a shiver run down her spine, a mixture of cold air and growing apprehension. This was it, the point of no return.

Brenda moved with the confidence of a seasoned scout, her eyes darting through the dimness, assessing every corner, every potential hiding spot or sound. She led Chloe along the perimeter of the room, past towering stacks of laundry carts, past industrial presses that looked capable of flattening a small car. Their footsteps were barely audible on the cold concrete floor, a soft scuffing sound that seemed to echo disproportionately in the silence. Chloe’s heart hammered against her ribs, a frantic drumbeat that threatened to betray their presence. She strained her ears, listening for any sign of movement, any cough or creak that might signal an unexpected occupant. The hotel, usually a hive of activity, felt eerily deserted in these hidden depths.

“The alley entrance is behind that wall,” Brenda whispered, pointing to a section of brick near a loading dock. “It’s obscured by these waste bins. Follow me closely.”

They threaded their way between two enormous dryers, their metallic surfaces cool to the touch. The path was narrow, barely wide enough for one person, and Chloe had to squeeze past a leaning stack of empty laundry baskets. The air grew cooler here, hinting at an opening to the outside world. Brenda reached the wall and peered around it, her head cocked, listening intently. Chloe held her breath, watching Brenda’s silhouette, her posture tense and alert. After a moment, Brenda signaled all clear.

With extreme caution, they slipped around the wall. Before them was a heavy, unmarked metal door, recessed slightly into the brickwork. It looked old and neglected, its paint peeling in places, streaked with grime. A large, rusted padlock hung loosely from a latch, but it was not secured. It was exactly as Brenda had described.

“Unlatched,” Brenda breathed, a note of triumph in her voice. “Just as I thought. He’s meticulous with the front, but forgets the back.”

Chloe felt a surge of relief, quickly followed by a new wave of anxiety. Getting in was one thing, but what would they find? And would they have enough time? She glanced at her watch; it was already twelve-ten. Their window was shrinking.

Brenda pushed the door gently. It groaned on rusty hinges, a sound that seemed deafening in the stillness of the night. They both froze, listening. Nothing. The alley outside was dark and narrow, flanked by the high brick walls of the hotel and what appeared to be the back of a row of shops. A single streetlamp at the far end cast long, distorted shadows, but the area directly outside the door was swallowed by darkness. The air was crisp, carrying the faint scent of pine from the surrounding mountains, a stark contrast to the musty interior of the hotel.

Brenda slipped through the opening first, melting into the shadows. Chloe followed, her eyes scanning the alley for any sign of life. Empty crates and refuse bins lined the walls, silent sentinels. The door swung shut behind them with a soft click, and they were truly outside the hotel’s main structure, in a forgotten corner of its grounds.

“Jenkins’s office is just across this alley, in that annex building,” Brenda murmured, pointing to a low, squat structure attached to the hotel’s main body. It looked like an afterthought, a functional addition without any aesthetic consideration. A single, grimy window, dark as pitch, faced them. “The entrance is around the side, facing away from the street.”

They moved quickly and silently across the narrow alley, their shoes crunching softly on loose gravel. The annex building was even less inviting up close, its brickwork stained and its corrugated iron roof rusted. Reaching the corner, Brenda paused again, peering around it. She gestured for Chloe to stay put, her hand held up. Chloe flattened herself against the cold brick, her breath fogging in the night air. The suspense was almost unbearable.

After another moment, Brenda signaled her forward. Around the corner was another door, similar in style to the one they had just used, but slightly smaller and painted a dull, peeling green. It, too, had a tarnished brass doorknob and no visible lock. Brenda tried the knob, turning it slowly. It resisted for a moment, then clicked softly. The door swung inward with barely a whisper.

They exchanged a quick, meaningful glance. This was almost too easy. A trap, perhaps? Chloe felt a prickle of unease, but Brenda’s resolute expression seemed to push aside her doubts. With a determined nod, Brenda slipped inside.

Chloe followed, her senses on high alert. The interior was dark, smelling faintly of old paper and dust. Brenda fumbled for a moment along the wall, and then a bare bulb hanging from the ceiling flickered to life, bathing the small room in a stark, yellowish light. It was a utilitarian antechamber, sparsely furnished with a rickety wooden chair and a stack of old ledgers. Another door, this one of solid oak, faced them.

“This must be it,” Brenda whispered, her voice tight with anticipation. She approached the oak door, her hand outstretched. She didn’t try the knob immediately, instead she ran her fingers along the doorframe, examining the lock. “Standard tumbler lock,” she breathed. “No deadbolt. Jenkins truly believes this area is secure because it’s off the beaten path.”

She produced a small, slender metal tool from her pocket, one that looked remarkably like a hairpin, though it was sturdier. Chloe watched, fascinated, as Brenda inserted the tool into the keyhole. There was a faint click, then another, almost imperceptible. In a matter of seconds, the lock yielded. Brenda was not only a daring reporter, but a surprisingly resourceful one.

“Where did you learn that?” Chloe asked, her voice barely above a whisper, a genuine note of surprise in her tone.

Brenda gave a small, wry smile. “A misspent youth, my dear. And a knack for observation. Now, let’s see what Mr. Jenkins has been hiding.”

She pushed the door open slowly, revealing a small, cramped office. It was exactly what Chloe had pictured: neat, orderly, and thoroughly impersonal. A large, wooden desk dominated the center of the room, piled with neatly stacked papers and ledgers. A single, unlit lamp with a green shade stood on one corner. Filing cabinets lined one wall, and a large, outdated map of the region, marked with various pins, hung on another. The air was stale, thick with the scent of old paper and tobacco, though there was no ashtray in sight. Jenkins was clearly meticulous about his office, even if he smoked.

Brenda stepped inside, her eyes immediately sweeping across the desk. Chloe followed, her heart pounding. The clock on the wall read twelve-fifteen. They had less than forty-five minutes.

“We need to be quick, and we need to be thorough,” Brenda instructed, her voice low and urgent. “You take the filing cabinets. Look for anything out of the ordinary: guest registers with missing pages, strange correspondence, financial records that don’t add up. I’ll start with the desk.”

They began their search with a practiced efficiency that came from years of journalistic investigation. Chloe approached the filing cabinets. The metal was cold beneath her fingers. She tried the first drawer; it was unlocked. Inside, files were neatly arranged, labeled alphabetically. She pulled out a few, flipping through them quickly. Most seemed to be standard hotel administration: supply orders, staff schedules, maintenance requests. Nothing immediately jumped out.

Meanwhile, Brenda was meticulously going through the drawers of the desk. She opened each one with care, examining its contents.

Brenda pulled out the shallow top drawer. It contained a neatly arranged collection of pens, pencils, paperclips, and a small, leather-bound notebook. She flipped through the notebook quickly. It was a personal diary, filled with terse, almost cryptic entries about daily appointments and observations. Nothing overtly suspicious. She placed it carefully back and moved to the next drawer down. This one was deeper, filled with various office supplies: staplers, blank envelopes, a roll of stamps. She ran her hand along the bottom, feeling for anything unusual. Nothing.

Chloe, meanwhile, was methodically working her way through the filing cabinets. The alphabetical order helped, but the sheer volume of files was daunting. She found folders labeled "Guest Correspondence," "Maintenance Reports," "Staff Payroll," and "Supply Orders." Each was as innocuous as the last. Mr. Jenkins, it seemed, was either incredibly meticulous in his legitimate business, or incredibly good at hiding his illicit activities. She pulled out a folder labeled "Pine Top Lodge Investments." This seemed a little out of place, as they were investigating the Fergus Hotel, but Jenkins managed properties in the area. She opened it. Inside were receipts for renovations, financial statements, and a few letters from a bank. It was all legitimate, boring business.

Brenda moved to the bottom drawer of the desk. It was deeper still, and as she tugged it open, it let out a faint groan. Inside were more ledger books, thicker than the ones in the antechamber. These looked like older records, perhaps from previous years. She pulled one out, its cover faded green, and began to leaf through it. The entries were all neat, precise, columns of figures and dates. She noticed a page was missing near the end. Not torn out, but simply gone, the binding showing a clear gap. She frowned. A missing page in a financial ledger was always suspicious. She continued to examine the binding of the other ledgers, but found no other irregularities.

"Anything, Chloe?" Brenda asked, without looking up. Her voice was strained with the effort of concentration.

"Nothing but a mountain of mundane paperwork, Brenda," Chloe replied, sounding a little exasperated. "Jenkins is either impeccably clean or he's hidden everything perfectly. I found some old investment papers for the Pine Top Lodge, but they seem to be in order."

Brenda closed the green ledger, her gaze still fixed on the missing page. "I have a missing page here, in an old ledger. It doesn't look torn out, just… absent. Like it was never bound in." She pushed the ledger back in and ran her hand along the back of the drawer, under where the ledger had sat. Her fingers brushed against something hard and metallic. She gripped it and pulled.

Chloe immediately stopped her search of the filing cabinets and came over to the desk. "A hidden key? That's promising. Do you think it opens one of these filing cabinets?"

Brenda tried the key in the lock of the top desk drawer. It didn't fit. She tried the others, then the small, ornate lock on the leather-bound notebook. Still no match. "No, this key is for something specific. Something Jenkins wants kept very secure, but not in plain sight." She looked around the office, her eyes sweeping over the filing cabinets, the map on the wall, the unlit lamp.

"Maybe it's for a safe," Chloe suggested, her gaze following Brenda's. "But I don't see one."

"Neither do I," Brenda said, her brow furrowed in thought. "Jenkins is too careful for an obvious safe. This office itself is his safe. But where?" She tapped her chin thoughtfully, then her eyes landed on the large, outdated map of the region hanging on the wall. It was a heavy, framed map. She walked over to it, inspecting the frame. It seemed solid, permanently affixed. But then she noticed the pins. Dozens of small, colored pins dotted the map, marking various locations in the Pine Top area.

"Look at these pins," Brenda said, pointing. "Most of them mark various hotel properties or local landmarks. But there are a few that seem to be randomly placed, in unpopulated areas." She touched a red pin near the edge of a dense forest section. "This one, for instance. Nothing here, according to the map."

Chloe peered closer. "You think the key has something to do with the map?"

Brenda’s eyes narrowed. She pressed gently on the map itself, then on the frame. It was firm. She looked behind the frame, but it was flush against the wall. Then she noticed it. A small, almost invisible seam in the wooden paneling directly beneath the map. It was expertly hidden, disguised to look like a natural grain in the wood.

Inside the compartment sat a single, plain wooden box. It was perhaps twelve inches long, six inches wide, and four inches deep. Unvarnished, unremarkable, but clearly the object of secrecy.

"Aha!" Brenda exclaimed softly, her face alight with triumph. She reached in and carefully extracted the box. It had a small, brass lock set into the front. Brenda inserted the key she had found. It slid in smoothly. With a gentle turn, the lock clicked open.

It was not a mere ornament, Chloe observed. It was a weapon, crafted with a sinister artistry.

"There's an inscription here," Brenda said, her voice barely a whisper, tracing a finger along the hilt. " 'Veritas Vos Liberabit.' Latin. 'The truth shall set you free.' What a strange motto for such an item." She placed the dagger back in the box, her gaze then falling upon the locket.

It was heavier than it looked, wrought of what appeared to be solid gold. The stylized pine tree on its face was intricately detailed, each needle precisely etched. Brenda's thumb sought a clasp, found it, and pressed gently. The locket sprang open with a soft click. Inside, on one side, was a tiny, faded photograph of a young woman with kind eyes and a solemn expression. On the other side, tucked beneath a fragile piece of glass, was a small, tightly curled lock of dark hair.

"A keepsake," Chloe breathed, leaning closer. "Someone clearly cherished this. Do you think it belonged to Jenkins, or H.M., or someone else entirely?"

Brenda shook her head, her attention already drifting back to the letters. "These are critical, Chloe. This is the heart of it." She picked up the stack again, her brow furrowed in concentration. "These aren't just general complaints. These are meticulously documented, every detail spelled out. Dates, amounts, names, properties. Jenkins was a fastidious record-keeper, even in his villainy."

Chloe peered over her shoulder as Brenda continued to read. "Here. H.M. is certainly Harvey Maxwell. The letters often refer to him as 'Maxwell,' or 'H.M., the hotelier.' They discuss the 'Pine Top Development Project,' a front for the dam. Jenkins was the local man, handling the land acquisitions. Maxwell, the financier and strategist, provided the capital and the insider information."

"Harvey Maxwell!" Chloe exclaimed, the name echoing with new dread. "Penny is at his hotel right now. This is more dangerous than we thought."

"Much more," Brenda agreed grimly. "Listen to this. 'The acquisition of the Ferguson parcel must be finalized by month's end. Old Man Ferguson is proving troublesome. He suspects our intentions. We cannot afford loose ends.' And then further down, 'Regarding the Ferguson problem, H.

M., has promised to 'deal with' the old fool. He will see to it that Ferguson's stubbornness is neutralized, one way or another. The land will be ours.' What on earth does 'neutralized' mean in this context?" Brenda's voice was sharp with alarm.

"It sounds like a threat, Brenda," Chloe replied, her face pale. "A very serious threat. And if Old Man Ferguson was an obstacle to this dam project, and Maxwell was involved in ensuring his land was acquired, what happened to him?"

Brenda’s eyes scanned the next page. "Here's another one, dated two weeks after the previous reference to Ferguson. 'The Ferguson parcel has been secured. Our friend proved persuasive. The old man accepted a generous offer, though not without protest. He has since decided to take an extended holiday, far from Pine Top's winter winds. His sudden change of heart is most opportune. The final plans for the spillway can now proceed without further local interference.' A 'sudden change of heart'? That hardly sounds legitimate. It sounds like he was forced out, or worse."

Chloe shivered. "And Penny is in the middle of all of this, inside Maxwell's hotel, trying to expose him. We have to warn her. She doesn't know the full extent of this man's ruthlessness."

Brenda nodded, her gaze still fixed on the letters. "These are not just plans for a dam, Chloe. This is a carefully orchestrated land grab, a conspiracy to defraud, and possibly even something far more sinister. Jenkins meticulously recorded every questionable transaction, every bribe, every threat. He kept a precise account of funds diverted from the 'Pine Top Development Project' for Maxwell’s personal enrichment and to silence opposition. He even recorded details about a 'special fund' for 'unforeseen contingencies,' which I suspect refers to things like Ferguson's 'extended holiday.'"

"But why would Jenkins keep such detailed records of his own crimes?" Chloe mused, perplexed. "It makes no sense."

"Blackmail," Brenda stated without looking up. "He was holding these over Maxwell’s head. Perhaps he foresaw a time when Maxwell would betray him, or he simply wanted leverage to ensure his own share of the spoils. Whatever his motive, these documents are a goldmine. They are the proof we need to bring Maxwell down, and to clear Penny’s father’s name."

"Then we need to get them to the authorities immediately," Chloe urged, her voice trembling slightly. "But first, Penny. We must reach her. She has just had a confrontation with Maxwell himself, and she doesn't have the protection of these documents yet."

Brenda looked up from the papers, a determined glint in her eyes. "You're right. Maxwell knows Penny's identity now. She's no longer just a curious guest. She's Anthony Parker's daughter, a direct threat. He won't hesitate to take drastic measures. We need to get these letters to her, or at least the critical information within them, and quickly. She's probably still on the second floor, near the Green Room."

At the Fergus Hotel, Penny stood motionless in the narrow corridor, her heart pounding against her ribs. Harvey Maxwell’s words echoed in her ears, his face a mask of furious contempt. "You're the daughter of Anthony Parker who runs the yellowest paper in Riverview! I know why he sent you here. Now get out and don't let me catch you in the hotel ever again." The green ticket, her precious admittance card, was gone, snatched from her grasp with a vicious jerk.

Her initial shock gave way to a surge of indignation. How dare he speak to her like that? And how dare he malign her father’s newspaper? The _Riverview Star_ might be small, but it was honest, a beacon of truth in a town often shrouded in shadows. This was not just about the dam project now; it was personal.

Maxwell’s reference to her father, and his swift move to confiscate her ticket, confirmed everything. He knew. He knew who she was, and he knew what she was after. This wasn't some minor local scandal; this was something big, something he was desperate to keep hidden. Her presence on the second floor, her interest in the Green Room, must have been relayed to him by Francine, or perhaps even the guard.

A cold dread seeped into her cheerful demeanor. She was in a hostile environment, alone, and without her key. But fear was quickly replaced by a stubborn resolve. Giving up was not an option. Not when her father’s reputation, and possibly the welfare of Pine Top, hung in the balance.

She took a deep breath, trying to calm her racing pulse. Her first objective: regain entry to the Green Room, or at least discover its secrets. Maxwell had seized her ticket, thinking it would deter her. He clearly underestimated Penny Parker.

She glanced down the corridor. The guard, who had previously been at his post, was nowhere in sight. Maxwell must have sent him away, perhaps to ensure Penny had no further means of gaining access. The door to the Green Room, previously a promise, now loomed like a mocking challenge.

Turning on her heel, Penny walked back towards the elevator landing. She needed a plan, and she needed information. The second floor, she reasoned, was primarily offices and perhaps private suites. The Green Room, whatever it was, was certainly not a guest room.

As she reached the landing, the elevator chimed, and its doors slid open. Francine Sellberg stepped out, a triumphant smirk playing on her lips. "Well, well, Penny Parker. Still moseying around, I see. Or perhaps you've been, shall we say, _escorted_ out of your little investigation?" Her eyes dropped to Penny's empty hand, where the green ticket had been. "Lost something, dear?"

Penny's jaw tightened. So, Francine had indeed seen the confrontation, or at least the aftermath. "My, you're observant, Francine," Penny retorted, trying to keep her voice light, though her anger simmered beneath the surface. "Did Mr. Maxwell offer you a reward for your diligent reporting?"

Francine’s smirk widened. "I simply saw an opportunity, Penny. And now it seems your little 'admittance card' has vanished. Quite a shame, isn't it? Without it, you're just another curious hotel guest, and a rather unwelcome one at that, according to Mr. Maxwell."

"He seems to have a high opinion of himself, doesn't he?" Penny said, trying to gauge Francine’s true intentions. Was she gloating, or was there an underlying motive? "He mentioned my father, Francine. He called the _Riverview Star_ a 'yellow sheet.' I don't suppose he offered you an exclusive for the _Daily Chronicle_ based on that bit of slander, did he?"

Francine’s expression shifted, a flicker of professional indignation crossing her face. "He had no right to insult your father, Penny. That much I agree with. But my loyalties are to the _Chronicle_, and to breaking this story. And it seems I'm still several steps ahead of you."

"Are you?" Penny challenged, a dangerous glint entering her eyes. "Because it seems to me you’ve merely succeeded in having Mr. Maxwell identify his primary opponent. He knows who I am, and what I’m doing. He just doesn’t know what I _know_. Or what I _will_ know. And he certainly doesn’t know that you were also poking around, only to be outmaneuvered by a simple green ticket. A ticket which, incidentally, he took from me, giving me more evidence of his guilt."

Francine’s eyes narrowed. "Evidence of his guilt? What are you talking about? He simply removed a trespasser."

"A trespasser who happened to have a specific card, for a specific room, which he clearly wants to keep secret," Penny countered, stepping closer. "Tell me, Francine, have you found anything inside the Green Room? Have you even found a way in?"

Francine hesitated, a brief moment of uncertainty clouding her confidence. "I… I was still working on that. But now, with your little stunt, the whole floor will be on high alert. You’ve complicated everything."

"Or clarified it," Penny said, a sudden thought striking her. "He thinks he's shut me down. He thinks I'll leave. That means he might relax his guard, just a little. And you, Francine, you’re still here, still looking for your big scoop. We’re in the same boat, whether you like it or not. The Green Room holds the answers. We both know it."

"So, what's your brilliant plan now, Miss Parker, without your magical ticket?" Francine sneered, though her curiosity was piqued.

"My plan is to get in there," Penny stated, her gaze unwavering. "And I don't need a ticket if I know what to look for. Maxwell is clearly hiding something. Something connected to this dam project, and to my father's investigations. And something, perhaps, to Mr. Ferguson's sudden 'holiday'."

Francine scoffed. "Ferguson? What does that old recluse have to do with anything? He sold his land years ago. He was just a grumpy old man who didn't want to sell his property."

"Or was he pressured into it?" Penny asked pointedly. "Coerced? Intimidated? My father was looking into unusual land acquisitions around Pine Top. Parcels that changed hands under suspicious circumstances. Ferguson's land was one of them."

Francine was silent, a thoughtful expression on her face. This was new information, and it clearly resonated with her reporter's instincts. "Where did you hear that?"

"My father's files," Penny replied simply. "He believed there was a powerful figure behind these transactions, pulling the strings. Someone who was benefiting immensely from the dam project, far beyond legitimate means. Someone like Harvey Maxwell, perhaps?"

"You think Maxwell forced Ferguson to sell?" Francine murmured, rubbing her chin. "That's a serious accusation, Penny. Do you have any proof?"

"Not yet," Penny admitted. "But I believe the Green Room holds it. Or at least leads to it. Maxwell's reaction confirms it. He wouldn't have been so enraged if there was nothing to hide."

"And how do you propose we get into a locked room, guarded by who knows how many of Maxwell's henchmen, without a key, and with Maxwell himself breathing down our necks?" Francine challenged, a hint of grudging respect in her voice.

"You said it yourself, Francine. Maxwell thinks he’s dealt with me. He thinks I’m gone. That element of surprise is our only advantage now. As for getting in, I remember something about the layout of this hotel from when I was last here with Mrs. Downey. This wing is older, isn't it? It has service passages, perhaps, or maintenance entrances. Every large hotel has them."

Francine’s eyes widened slightly. "Service passages? That's actually… a good thought. This section was added much later, but designed to connect seamlessly. There are old blueprints in the hotel manager's office, I saw them once."

"Exactly!" Penny exclaimed, a spark of hope igniting. "We don't need a key if we can find another way in. We need to be bold, Francine. We need to act quickly, before Maxwell realizes I haven't left, and before he puts tighter security on this room."

"And if we find anything?" Francine asked, her competitive nature reasserting itself. "Who gets the scoop?"

Penny looked at her squarely. "Right now, Francine, the truth is more important than who gets the headline. If we find something truly incriminating, something that exposes Maxwell's corruption, it's a story big enough for both our papers. We can share the credit, or at least break it simultaneously. My father always taught me that sometimes, cooperation is the fastest way to get to the bottom of things. Especially when you're up against someone as powerful as Maxwell."

Francine hesitated for a long moment, weighing her options. Her ambition warred with her journalistic integrity, and for once, the latter seemed to be winning. The potential for a truly monumental exposé, shared or not, was too tempting to resist. "Alright, Penny," she said, a grudging sigh escaping her lips. "I'll help you. But if this turns out to be a wild goose chase, or if we get caught, I’m blaming you."

"Fair enough," Penny grinned, a genuine smile breaking through her apprehension. "Now, where would these service passages be?"

"There's a utility closet near the end of this corridor, towards the back of the building," Francine replied, her mind already working. "It connects to the main service shaft. From there, if we're lucky, there might be an access panel into the Green Room's wall. Or at least into an adjacent room that might offer a way in."

"Then let's go," Penny urged, her earlier gloom completely dispelled by the thrill of the chase and the unexpected alliance.

Meanwhile, back at the Jasko cabin, Brenda and Chloe were in a frenzy. Brenda had just found a critical letter. "Chloe, listen to this! This is from Jenkins to Maxwell, dated just a few days ago: 'The Green Room at the Fergus Hotel now holds all the original documents, including the land deeds, the falsified environmental reports, and the complete financial ledgers. I have secured them in the wall safe behind the portrait. The key is in the usual place. My conscience, however, can no longer bear the weight of these deceptions. I have made copies of everything. If anything happens to me, or if you attempt to renege on our agreement, these copies will be mailed to Anthony Parker at the _Riverview Star_. Consider this my final warning, H.M. You underestimate me at your peril.'"

Chloe gasped, her hand flying to her mouth. "The Green Room! And the wall safe! Brenda, this is it! This is the smoking gun! But if Jenkins sent this a few days ago, then Maxwell knows about the copies. He knows about Penny's father. That's why he was so furious at Penny!"

"Precisely," Brenda said, her voice taut with urgency. "And Penny is there, at the Fergus Hotel, right now. Maxwell knows she’s Anthony Parker’s daughter. He knows Jenkins threatened to send the copies to her father. He will undoubtedly suspect that Penny is there to retrieve those documents from the Green Room. He won't just throw her out; he'll try to stop her, perhaps permanently."

"We have to get to her, Brenda!" Chloe cried, already gathering her things. "She's in grave danger!"

"Yes, but how?" Brenda ran a hand through her hair, her mind racing. "The hotel is likely heavily guarded now. And Maxwell will be watching for anyone associated with Penny or her father. We can't just walk in the front door."

"We need a distraction," Chloe suggested, her eyes darting around the cabin. "Something to draw attention away from Penny, and away from us when we try to enter."

"A distraction, yes," Brenda mused, looking at the stack of incriminating letters. "And these letters, Chloe, they contain everything. If we can't get them to Penny directly, we need to get them into the right hands. The police, the state prosecutor. Someone who can act quickly and decisively."

"But who can we trust?" Chloe asked, her brow furrowed. "Maxwell is powerful, Brenda. He might have people everywhere, even in the local authorities."

"There's an old friend of my father's, a detective named Harding," Brenda said, a flicker of hope in her eyes. "He's retired now, but he was always incorruptible, a man of fierce integrity. He lives just outside Pine Top, near the old saw mill. If anyone can be trusted with this, it’s him. But we have to move quickly. Penny is walking into a trap."

"Then let's go!" Chloe urged, already pulling on her coat. "You can explain on the way. We need to ski down to Pine Top and get to Harding. And pray we aren't too late for Penny."

With renewed urgency, Brenda gathered the letters, securing them carefully inside her coat. The ornate dagger and the locket, silent witnesses to generations of secrets, were placed back in the wooden box. The snow crunched under their boots as they burst out of the Jasko cabin, their skis carving swift tracks down the mountainside. The wind howled around them, a chilling premonition of the danger that awaited Penny.

Back at the Fergus Hotel, Penny and Francine moved stealthily down the second-floor corridor. The utility closet, a nondescript metal door at the very end, seemed innocuous enough. Francine produced a small set of lock picks from her coat pocket, a reporter’s essential tool. "Been meaning to return these to the city editor," she whispered, her fingers deftly working the lock. With a soft click, the door swung open.

Inside, the closet was dark and filled with dusty mops, buckets, and cleaning supplies. A narrow ladder led up into a small, grimy opening in the ceiling. "The main service shaft is up there," Francine explained, pointing with a gloved finger. "It runs vertically through the building, with access points on each floor."

Penny peered up, a shiver running down her spine. "Looks like a job for a chimney sweep. Think we can fit?"

"We'll have to," Francine replied grimly. "Unless you have a better idea." She climbed the ladder first, her movements surprisingly agile for someone in a smart suit. Penny followed, her heart pounding. The air in the shaft was stale and musty, filled with the faint smell of old metal and dust. A single bare bulb illuminated the cramped space, casting long, dancing shadows.

They clambered upwards, carefully avoiding exposed wires and pipes. Francine, who seemed to have a surprisingly detailed knowledge of the hotel’s inner workings, pointed to a bolted metal panel about ten feet up. "That should be it," she whispered. "That's likely the access into the Green Room, or at least a ventilation shaft that leads there."

Reaching the panel was a precarious task, requiring them to brace themselves against the narrow walls of the shaft. Francine fumbled with a wrench she had brought, her brow furrowed in concentration. The bolts were old and rusted, resisting her efforts. Each groan of metal seemed amplified in the confined space, making Penny wince, fearing they would alert someone.

After several agonizing minutes, one bolt finally gave way with a screech. Then another, and another. With a final heave, Francine managed to pry open the heavy metal panel, revealing a dark, cavernous space beyond. A faint hum of machinery could be heard, along with a distinct, unsettling silence.

"We're in," Francine breathed, her voice a mixture of triumph and trepidation. "I think this leads to a storage area directly behind the Green Room, or perhaps the room itself. It's too dark to tell."

"We need a light," Penny whispered, fumbling in her pockets. She produced a small, pen-sized flashlight. Its beam cut through the darkness, revealing a short, narrow passage, culminating in another wooden door. It looked like a hidden entrance, precisely as Penny had hoped.

They carefully squeezed through the opening, landing softly on the dusty floor of what appeared to be a cramped, unused closet. The air here was cooler, and the faint scent of old paper lingered. Penny directed her flashlight beam around the small room. It was indeed a closet, filled with forgotten filing cabinets and boxes, all covered in a thick layer of dust.

On one wall, however, there was a section that looked different. The wood paneling was newer, more polished than the surrounding, aged planks. Penny ran her hand over it, feeling a slight ridge. "This is it," she whispered, her voice filled with excitement. "A hidden door. Or a false wall. Jenkins mentioned a wall safe."

Francine’s eyes gleamed in the flashlight beam. "Good work, Parker. Now, how do we open it?"

They examined the paneling closely, searching for a latch, a button, anything that would indicate a release mechanism. After a moment, Penny noticed a small, almost invisible seam in the wood, coinciding with a barely perceptible groove in the decorative molding above. She pressed firmly on the molding, then ran her fingers along the seam. Nothing happened.

"Wait," Francine said, pointing. "Look at this. There's a slight discoloration in the wood grain here, like it's been pressed repeatedly." She indicated a specific spot on the panel. Penny pressed there, then simultaneously pushed along the molding.

With a soft, almost inaudible click, a section of the wall paneling silently swung inward, revealing a small, dimly lit room. It was exactly as Penny had imagined, and yet far more menacing.

This was the Green Room.

It was not a lavish office, but rather a functional, almost stark chamber. The walls were painted a dull, sickly green, and a single, heavy oak table dominated the center of the room, surrounded by several high-backed chairs. Maps, large and detailed, were tacked to one wall, depicting the Pine Top area with various plots of land highlighted in different colors. Geological surveys lay scattered on the table, along with engineering schematics that clearly outlined the construction of a large dam.

And on the opposite wall, behind a rather gaudy portrait of a stern-faced man, a large, dark rectangular outline was visible where the painting had been hastily removed. It revealed a recessed wall safe, its heavy steel door gleaming in the flashlight beam.

"There it is," Penny breathed, her voice barely a whisper. "The wall safe. Jenkins' records."

"And the key," Francine added, her eyes wide with a mixture of fear and exhilaration. "Jenkins said the key was 'in the usual place.' What does that mean?"

They moved cautiously into the Green Room, their flashlights dancing across the incriminating evidence. Penny’s gaze fell upon the table. Amongst the documents, a small, silver key lay almost hidden beneath a stack of blueprints. It was old, intricately designed, and looked like something from a bygone era.

"The key," Penny whispered, reaching for it. As her fingers closed around the cold metal, a sudden, sharp sound echoed from the corridor outside the Green Room. Footsteps. Heavy, hurried footsteps. And then, a familiar voice, thick with menace.

"I knew she wouldn't leave! You fools, search every inch of this floor! Find Penny Parker! And if she's found anything, make sure she never speaks of it!"

It was Harvey Maxwell. His rage, previously contained, had now boiled over. He had realized Penny hadn't left. He was coming for her.

Penny and Francine exchanged a terrified glance. They were trapped. The hidden panel was open, exposing their entry point. The main door of the Green Room was still locked, and Maxwell was just outside.

"What do we do?" Francine whispered, her face pale.

"We get those documents," Penny said, her voice firm despite the tremor in her hands. She inserted the key into the safe’s lock, turning it with a decisive click. The heavy door swung open, revealing stacks of thick ledgers and tightly bound bundles of papers. This was it. The proof.

But as Penny reached inside, the main door to the Green Room burst open with a crash, splintering the frame. Harvey Maxwell stood there, his face contorted with fury, two burly men with grim expressions behind him. His eyes fell upon Penny, reaching into the safe, and Francine, standing beside her, flashlight still clutched in her hand.

"Parker!" Maxwell roared, his voice echoing through the room like a clap of thunder. "You insolent little spy! You've gone too far this time!" He lunged forward, his hands outstretched, his intent clear.

Penny had only a split second to react. She snatched a thick ledger from the safe, its pages heavy with incriminating evidence, just as Maxwell’s hands closed around her arm. Francine, quick-witted despite her fear, swung her flashlight, aiming for the nearest henchman. The beam temporarily blinded him, giving Penny a precious moment.

"Run, Francine!" Penny yelled, struggling against Maxwell’s powerful grip. "Get out of here! Tell my father what we found!"

Francine hesitated for a fraction of a second, then, seeing the futility of fighting two large men, darted back towards the hidden panel, squeezing through the narrow opening just as one of Maxwell’s men grabbed at her coat. The panel swung shut behind her with a soft thud, momentarily concealing her escape route.

Maxwell, enraged by Francine’s evasion, tightened his grip on Penny. "You're not going anywhere, Miss Parker! Not with my property!" He twisted her arm, forcing the ledger from her grasp. It fell to the floor with a heavy thud, its pages splaying open, revealing meticulous handwritten accounts of fraud and conspiracy.

Penny cried out, more in frustration than pain. She glared at Maxwell, her chin held high despite the terror clawing at her. "You won't get away with this, Maxwell! The truth will come out! My father will expose you!"

Maxwell laughed, a harsh, humorless sound. "Your father is an old fool, just like Ferguson! And you, my dear, are even more foolish for following in his footsteps. No one will believe a word you say. And no one will ever find out what is in these books." He gestured to his men. "Get her out of here. And make sure she disappears where no one will ever think to look."

The two men advanced, their faces grim. Penny knew she was in serious trouble. Her quick escape with Francine, her alliance, had been a momentary reprieve, but now she was truly alone, trapped. As the men seized her, one on each arm, a sudden crashing sound erupted from outside the Green Room. It sounded like heavy boots running down the corridor, followed by shouts.

Maxwell frowned, his head tilting to listen. "What was that? Who's out there?"

Just then, the outer door of the Green Room, still hanging precariously on its broken hinges, was suddenly flung wide open again. Standing framed in the doorway, their faces grim and determined, were Brenda and Chloe. And behind them, a stern-faced, burly man in a tweed jacket, holding a police whistle to his lips. He blew a long, piercing blast that echoed through the hotel.

"Harvey Maxwell!" Brenda's voice rang out, clear and authoritative. "We have evidence of your crimes! The entire Pine Top Development Project is a sham, a front for illegal land grabs and fraud!"

Maxwell's eyes widened in disbelief and then hardened into a furious glare. He recognized Brenda from her association with Mrs. Downey. He had underestimated the network of young women around Pine Top.

"Detective Harding!" the man in tweed announced, stepping forward, his eyes fixed on Maxwell. "You are under arrest for conspiracy, fraud, and the unlawful coercion of local landowners! And, I believe, for the assault and kidnapping of Miss Penny Parker!"

The hotel's quiet, deceptive facade had shattered. The Green Room, once a bastion of secret corruption, was now exposed, its secrets spilling out into the cold mountain air. Penny, still held by Maxwell's men, looked at Brenda and Chloe, a wave of relief washing over her. They had come. They had actually come.

Maxwell, seeing the jig was up, snarled, "You meddling fools! You'll never prove a thing! I have friends everywhere!" But his words lacked their former conviction. His empire of lies was crumbling around him.

Detective Harding, his gaze unwavering, signaled to two uniformed officers who had appeared behind him. "Take them, boys. And secure this room. Every document, every map, every ledger. It's all evidence."

As the officers moved in, Penny was released from the henchmen's grasp. She stumbled forward, catching herself on the table, her eyes scanning the faces of her friends. Brenda and Chloe rushed to her side, their relief palpable.

"Penny, are you alright?" Brenda asked, her hand on Penny's shoulder.

"I am now," Penny replied, a shaky laugh escaping her. "Just in time, I'd say. You girls certainly know how to make an entrance. And Detective Harding, thank goodness for you!"

"We came as fast as we could," Chloe added, her eyes wide with the excitement of the dramatic rescue. "Brenda found the rest of Jenkins' letters. They detailed everything, including the Green Room, the safe, and Maxwell's plan to silence anyone who got in his way."

Detective Harding nodded gravely. "Jenkins contacted me a few days ago, sending me a coded message about his 'insurance policy.' He was scared, but he also wanted to clear his conscience. He mentioned the possibility of the evidence being at the Fergus Hotel. When Brenda showed up with the full set of letters and the details, it all clicked into place. We owe you girls a great deal."

Penny looked at the open safe, then back at the bewildered face of Harvey Maxwell, now being led away by the officers. The ledger she had dropped lay open on the floor, a testament to his corruption. "So, the truth did come out after all," she murmured, a sense of triumph mixed with exhaustion washing over her. "And my father's name will be cleared."

"Indeed it will, Penny," Detective Harding confirmed, picking up the fallen ledger. "This is a meticulously documented case of fraud and deceit. Thanks to you and your friends, Harvey Maxwell's little 'development project' has just been completely derailed."

Outside, the snow continued to fall, blanketing Pine Top in a pristine white. But the shadows that had once clung to the mountains and the valley below were finally beginning to lift. The secret of the Green Room was out, and with it, the promise of justice. Penny, leaning against the doorway, watched as Maxwell was led away. Her adventure was far from over, but this chapter, at least, had reached a satisfying, if dramatic, conclusion. She glanced at Brenda and Chloe, a deep sense of gratitude in her heart. This was indeed going to be her lucky day, a day of truth and triumph. She felt it in her bones.

The cold mountain air rushed into the Green Room as Detective Harding's men secured the scene. Maxwell, his face a mask of impotent fury, was led away, his protests fading into the distant murmur of the hotel corridor. Penny watched him go, a shiver running through her, not from cold, but from the sudden release of tension. The air, which had felt thick with deceit and danger only moments before, now seemed to breathe a little easier. Brenda and Chloe, still holding onto her, were murmuring words of comfort, their presence a warm, steady anchor.

"Penny, you were so brave," Brenda whispered, her eyes wide with admiration.

"I don't know about brave," Penny admitted, a weak smile touching her lips. "More like stubborn, perhaps. And a little foolhardy. If you two hadn't arrived…" She shuddered at the thought of what might have happened.

Chloe patted her arm. "But we did arrive. Just in the nick of time, thanks to those letters. Mr. Jenkins truly wanted to do the right thing in the end."

Detective Harding, having given instructions to his officers, turned back to the girls, a look of profound respect on his face. "Indeed, he did. His conscience finally got the better of him. And you three, particularly you, Miss Parker, saw that justice was served. This ledger," he said, indicating the open book still lying on the polished floor, "is a goldmine. It details every fraudulent transaction, every bribe, every land deal Maxwell engineered under the guise of the 'Pine Top Development Project.' The man was a snake, through and through."

Penny bent down and carefully picked up the ledger, its leather cover feeling unexpectedly heavy in her hands. She flipped through a few pages, the columns of figures and terse notes painting a stark picture of Maxwell’s avarice. *So much deceit, all for money and power,* she thought, a sense of grim satisfaction settling over her. "And my father, Detective? His name will be cleared, won't it? Maxwell accused him of publishing 'yellow journalism' and hinted that my father supported him."

A wave of profound relief washed over Penny. This, more than anything, was what she had sought. Francine huffed, but for once, she offered no snappy retort. The news, the sight of the police, and Penny's calm confidence seemed to have disarmed her. "I'll see you around, Penny Parker," she muttered, stepping out of the elevator and heading towards the now-empty Green Room corridor, no doubt hoping to glean some last-minute details.

Penny, Brenda, and Chloe stepped into the elevator. "She's probably going to try to piece together her own version," Chloe giggled. "But it won't be as good as yours."

"Let her try," Penny said, feeling a lightness she hadn't experienced in days. "The facts are the facts. And those facts will speak for themselves."

As the elevator descended, Penny glanced at her companions. "I must say, I'm starving. I don't think I've had a proper meal since I arrived at Pine Top. What do you say to an enormous breakfast at the lodge?"

"Sounds wonderful," Brenda agreed. "Mrs. Downey will be beside herself when she hears everything. She's been so worried."

They emerged into the bustling lobby, which was now far more active than the upper floors. The desk clerk looked up, his expression one of surprise as he recognized Penny, Brenda, and Chloe. His gaze flickered towards the elevator, as if expecting to see the police.

"Everything alright, Miss Parker?" he inquired, a hint of trepidation in his voice.

They walked out into the bright, crisp morning. The snow was still falling, but now it felt clean and refreshing, not oppressive. The air sparkled with a new promise. They passed Harvey Maxwell's red sleigh, still parked prominently, but now it looked forlorn, a symbol of a dream that had turned into a nightmare. Penny noticed a small, crumpled green ticket lying in the snow near where she had first been accosted by Maxwell. It was her admittance card, which he had so angrily snatched from her. She bent down and picked it up, a wry smile touching her lips. *No need for this now,* she thought. *The Green Room has yielded all its secrets.*

The walk back to the Downey Lodge was invigorated by their success. The path, though still snow-covered, seemed less daunting. Between bites of Mrs. Downey's delicious buckwheat pancakes, the three friends recounted the entire saga, from Penny's initial suspicions about Maxwell, to her discovery of the green ticket, her encounter with Francine, and the dramatic confrontation in the Green Room. Mrs. Downey listened, her eyes growing wider with each detail, occasionally gasping or clucking her tongue in disapproval.

"That Harvey Maxwell! I always knew he was too slick for his own good," she declared, pouring more syrup onto Penny's plate. "Trying to swindle honest folks out of their land, creating a false boom. "Detective Harding says my father's name will be completely cleared," Penny reiterated, a warm feeling spreading through her. "The _Riverview Star_ will run the full story, exposing Maxwell's scheme. It's a huge victory for honest journalism."

Penny exchanged an amused glance with Brenda and Chloe. "Something like that, Sara. More like a hidden truth, actually. We've just unraveled a massive fraud involving Harvey Maxwell and his 'Pine Top Development Project.'"

Sara's eyes, usually alight with fun, widened in genuine surprise. "Maxwell? The man who owns the fancy hotel? The one who wanted to buy up all the land around here? What about him?" Her usual boisterousness was replaced by a rare moment of earnest curiosity.

Penny, still a little giddy from the morning's events, decided to give Sara the condensed version. "He was using false promises and rigged land deals to try and take over Pine Top. "That's right, Sara," Penny confirmed, a sense of quiet satisfaction settling over her. "The 'Pine Top Development Project' was all a sham, designed to line Maxwell's pockets, not to benefit the town. He won't be building anything now, except perhaps a very uncomfortable cell."

Brenda and Chloe nodded in agreement, still munching on their pancakes. The morning's drama had left them with ravenous appetites.

Sara just laughed, patting Penny on the shoulder. "You were great, Penny! See? I told you it would be fun. Now, who's next for a ride with the best bob-sledder in Pine Top?"
