My problem is keeping you two in one piece until the boss arrives.” He settled back into his armchair, the shotgun still resting casually across his lap, its barrel pointed directly at them. The leaner man, meanwhile, began to pace restlessly, occasionally glancing at the telephone as if considering it a threat.

The night stretched before them, long and silent save for the occasional creaks of the old house. Clara’s mind raced. She replayed every moment of their journey, every detail of the greenhouse, searching for an oversight, a forgotten possibility. The orchid, the very reason for their desperate flight, was now more vulnerable than ever. Finch’s men had no incentive to believe them, or to act, only to follow orders.

Clara remembered the small, almost imperceptible tremor she had felt in the greenhouse after Finch left, a fluctuation in the carefully maintained environment. It was so slight she had dismissed it at first, attributing it to her imagination or the general disturbance. But if it was a deliberate act, a sabotage, then the orchid was on a ticking clock.

She glanced at Leo. He looked defeated, his shoulders slumped. The situation was indeed grim. Two armed guards, in a dark, unfamiliar house. Escape seemed impossible.

Yet, Clara refused to give in to despair. She was Penny Parker’s daughter, after all, and Penny Parker never gave up. She surveyed the room again, her eyes piercing the gloom, searching for anything, any detail, that could be turned to their advantage. The tall bookshelves, the heavy curtains, the massive mahogany desk. Everything was solid, unyielding.

Her gaze fell on the fireplace, a grand structure of carved marble. On the mantelpiece, above a cold, empty grate, sat an assortment of decorative objects: small statues, a heavy brass clock, and a large, intricately worked iron poker and shovel set. The poker, in particular, looked substantial, heavy. A potential weapon.

But how to get to it? Frank was directly opposite, his shotgun a constant, deadly presence. The leaner man, restless, continued his pacing, sometimes disappearing into the deeper shadows of the room, only to reappear a moment later.

Clara began to talk, her voice calm and even. “Mr. Finch is making a terrible mistake,” she said, directing her words at Frank. “He values that orchid above almost anything else. If it perishes due to his negligence, he will be furious. And who do you think he will blame?”

Frank grunted, his eyes narrowing. “He’ll blame whoever tampered with it, if that’s what happened.”

“But you were in charge of securing the estate,” Clara pressed, her voice gaining a slight edge. “You were supposed to protect his ‘prize possession.’ If it’s damaged on your watch, even if by someone else, he’ll hold you accountable. He’s not a man who accepts excuses, is he?”

This seemed to strike a nerve. Frank shifted in his seat, his grip on the shotgun tightening. He glanced nervously at the lean man, who had stopped pacing and was now watching their exchange intently.

“We didn’t know about any tampering,” Frank mumbled. “He just said keep an eye on the place. Not to touch anything.”

“Precisely,” Clara said. “But ‘keeping an eye’ means noticing if something is wrong. If the conditions in the greenhouse are no longer stable, that’s a direct threat to the orchid. And you, as his security, should have been the first to realize it. Or at least to allow someone who _does_ understand to inform him.”

The leaner man scoffed. “She’s trying to play mind games, Frank. Don’t listen to her.”

“She’s not wrong though, about Finch,” Frank said, his brow furrowed in thought. “He’s a stickler for details. And for blame.”

Clara saw a tiny crack in their resolve, a sliver of doubt. She decided to push it. “You have a choice, Frank. You can either follow your orders blindly, risking Mr. Finch’s fury when his prize is lost, or you can take a small risk now and allow us to make that call. If we’re wrong, no harm done. If we’re right, you’ve saved his most valuable possession. And perhaps saved yourselves a lot of trouble.”

The room was silent again, thick with tension. Frank stared at the telephone, then at Clara, then back at the phone. He was clearly torn between his orders and the very real threat of Finch’s wrath.

The leaner man, however, seemed less swayed. “He’s testing us, Frank. Don’t be a fool. He’ll be back soon enough, and then we’ll tell him what happened. He’ll understand we were just following orders.”

“Will he?” Clara’s voice was sharp. “Or will he ask why you let someone else’s sabotage destroy his prize? Why you didn’t take action when you had the chance?”

Frank’s face was a mask of indecision. He gnawed on his lower lip. The shotgun remained pointed at them, but his posture had relaxed fractionally.

Suddenly, Leo coughed, a loud, theatrical sound that startled both guards. As their attention shifted to him for a split second, Clara sprang into action. She was off the armchair, a blur of motion. In two swift strides, she reached the fireplace, her hand grasping the heavy iron poker. With a grunt, she swung it in a wide arc, aiming for the lean man who was closest to her.

He yelped in surprise as the heavy metal thudded against his arm, making him drop his weapon, a small handgun, with a clatter onto the wooden floor. He stumbled back, clutching his injured arm.

Frank, recovering from his momentary surprise, raised his shotgun, but Clara was already moving again. She didn’t stop to pick up the dropped handgun. Her target was the telephone. She reached the desk in another leap, her hand closing over the receiver.

“No!” Frank roared, swinging the shotgun towards her.

But before he could fire, Leo, who had also moved, tackled him from the side. It wasn’t an elegant move, more of a desperate lunge, but it caught Frank off guard. The burly man stumbled, his shotgun discharging with a thunderous roar that echoed deafeningly through the silent house. A shower of plaster erupted from the ceiling as the shot went harmlessly upwards. Frank and Leo crashed to the floor, struggling.

Clara, ignoring the chaos, brought the receiver to her ear. No dial tone. Dead. She slammed it back down in frustration. Finch had anticipated this. The phone lines were cut.

“It’s dead!” she cried out to Leo.

Leo grunted as Frank landed a heavy blow to his side. Frank was a powerful man, even without the advantage of his shotgun, which had skittered across the floor in the struggle.

The leaner man, still dazed, was slowly bending to retrieve his handgun. Clara knew they only had seconds. She glanced wildly around the room, her eyes darting from one object to another. The large, heavy brass clock on the mantelpiece. It was directly above the struggling men.

Without a second thought, Clara seized the clock. It was heavier than she anticipated, but she lifted it with a surge of adrenaline.

“Leo, move!” she yelled.

Leo, seeing her intention, rolled violently, pulling away from Frank just as Clara brought the heavy clock down with all her might. It struck Frank squarely on the head. He let out a surprised gasp, his eyes rolled back, and he went limp, collapsing onto the carpet.

The leaner man, handgun now back in his grasp, looked up, startled, as Clara turned her attention to him. He raised the weapon, but before he could aim, Clara, still holding the brass clock, threw it with surprising force. The clock spun through the air, catching him on the wrist of his gun hand. He shrieked in pain, the handgun flying from his grasp once more, skidding under a heavy armchair.

He backed away, clutching his injured wrist, his face pale with pain and fear. “Stay back!” he whimpered.

Clara advanced, the iron poker back in her hand, its tip scraping lightly on the floor. Her breath came in ragged gasps, but her eyes were firm, determined. The leaner man, seeing the ferocity in her gaze, decided he wanted no more of this. He turned and bolted, disappearing through the study door and down the hallway, his footsteps echoing rapidly until they faded into the distance.

Clara stood panting, the poker still clutched in her hand, looking at the unconscious Frank and the fleeing leaner man. The silence of the house returned, but this time it was a different silence, one of victory, however temporary.

Leo slowly got to his feet, rubbing his side. “Are you alright?” he asked, his voice strained. “That was… quite a performance, Clara. I didn’t know you had it in you.”

Clara dropped the poker with a clatter. “I didn’t know either,” she admitted, a tremor running through her. The adrenaline was beginning to recede, leaving her feeling weak. “The phone is dead. Finch cut the lines.”

“Of course he did,” Leo said, retrieving the shotgun that had slid under the desk. He checked it, found it empty, and set it aside. “He’s more thorough than I gave him credit for. What now?”

Clara looked around the study. The room, once a trap, now offered a moment of respite. But time was still working against them, against the orchid.

“We can’t call for help from here,” Clara stated, her mind already moving to the next problem. “We need to get out of this house and find a way to get word to the Botanical Gardens. But we can’t risk running into Finch or his men again. We need to leave the estate entirely.”

“Leave the estate?” Leo repeated, concern in his voice. “But how? The main gates are certainly guarded. And the perimeter is extensive.”

“We’ll have to find another way,” Clara said, her chin lifting. “A way he won’t expect. There must be a service exit, or a smaller, less obvious gate. This estate is old, it must have many hidden nooks and crannies.”

She walked to the large bay window that overlooked the front grounds. The darkness outside was complete now, punctuated only by the distant gleam of streetlights on a road far beyond the estate’s boundaries. Escape seemed daunting, but not impossible. The sound of the alarm, the cut phone lines, indicated Finch was serious. They had to be just as serious.

“We need to be swift,” Clara declared, turning back to Leo. “We don’t know when Finch will return. Or when that other man will gather his courage, or more of Finch’s men, and come back. We have to go now.”

Leo nodded, his weariness momentarily forgotten, replaced by a fresh surge of resolve. “Lead the way, Clara. You seem to have a knack for finding the impossible solutions.”

Clara allowed herself a faint smile. She wasn’t sure about impossible solutions, but she knew one thing: she wasn’t giving up until that orchid was safe, and Finch was brought to justice. The night was far from over, and the path ahead was still fraught with danger, but they had a mission, and a renewed determination.

Clara nodded, her mind already racing through the blueprints she had studied of the old manor. Finch, in his misguided enthusiasm, had been eager to show her all the "improvements" he had made to the estate, including the rarely used service tunnels beneath the west wing. "The service entrance," she announced, "near the old greenhouse. It's rarely monitored, even by Finch."

"The greenhouse!" Leo exclaimed, a sudden look of horror on his face. "Clara, the orchid! We left it in the research wing. We can't leave without it. All of this, the danger, the risk, it's all for nothing if we don't secure the *Fleur de Nuit*."

Clara pressed her lips together. He was right. In the chaos, with Frank's sudden attack and Finch's men breaking in, the orchid had been momentarily forgotten. "Of course, Leo. We must get it. But first, silence the alarm. The constant wail is a beacon." She moved to the desk, her eyes scanning the array of buttons and monitors. Finch was a connoisseur of security gadgets, and she remembered him boasting about a central control panel. Her fingers danced over the smooth surfaces, recalling the specific combination he had once idly shown her during a tour. After a moment of concentrated effort, a soft click resonated through the room, and the piercing siren outside abruptly ceased. The sudden quiet was almost more unnerving than the alarm itself.

"Good work, Clara," Leo whispered, peering cautiously through a gap in the heavy drapes. "The grounds still seem clear for now. But they won't be for long."

"No," Clara agreed. "Finch will have noticed the alarm's cessation. He'll be back, and with more men, I imagine. We need to move." She retrieved the poker, its weight a comforting, if crude, weapon. "The research wing is through the main hall. It will be risky."

"Then we will be swift," Leo said, his grip firm on the inert shotgun. He handed it to Clara. "Take this. It might offer more psychological deterrence than a poker."

Clara took the shotgun, its cold metal heavy in her hands. She had never fired a weapon in her life. "I hope it doesn't come to that," she murmured, but she held it firmly. They exchanged a quick glance, a silent pact of determination passing between them. With Frank still sprawled on the Persian rug and the other intruder long gone, the study offered no further refuge. Clara led the way, stepping carefully over the unconscious man.

They crept from the study, her ears straining for any sound beyond their own hushed footsteps. The grand hallway, usually a lively corridor of art and light, was now a cavern of shadows. Moonlight streamed through the tall arched windows, casting long, eerie patterns on the polished marble floor. Every creak of the old house, every rustle of unseen fabric, sent a jolt of adrenaline through her.

The research wing was at the far end of the west corridor, a wing Finch had added specifically to house his more precious specimens and, of course, the *Fleur de Nuit*. They moved along the corridor like ghosts, hugging the walls, their breath held tight in their chests. The faint smell of chlorine from the indoor pool drifted from a side passage, a stark reminder of Finch's extravagant lifestyle.

As they neared the entrance to the research wing, a low murmur reached their ears. Clara froze, holding up a hand to stop Leo. She peered around the corner, her heart pounding a frantic rhythm against her ribs. Two men, dark figures in the dim light, were standing guard outside the heavy oak doors of the wing. They were talking in low tones, their voices indistinguishable, but their presence was undeniable.



    Leo, his breath barely audible, tugged gently on Clara's sleeve. He gestured towards a less illuminated section of the hallway, a small alcove usually housing an antique suit of armor. They retreated silently into its shadows, the shotgun feeling heavier in Clara's trembling hands. From their vantage point, they could observe the guards more closely. One was a burly man, his neck thick, his posture slouched against the wall. The other was leaner, more alert, constantly scanning the corridor. Both wore Finch's distinctive dark blue uniform, though their expressions were weary, their eyes betraying hours of restless waiting. They clutched what appeared to be automatic rifles, sleek and menacing in the dim light.

    "Two of them," Leo whispered, his voice a mere exhalation. "And armed. What now, Clara? That shotgun is only good for one shot, if that, and I doubt either of us are marksmen."

    Clara gripped the cold metal of the shotgun, its weight a stark reminder of their precarious position. "No, Leo, you are right. A direct confrontation is out of the question. Not with Frank still unconscious and Finch likely on his way back. We need a diversion." Her mind raced, searching for an overlooked detail, an exploitable weakness in Finch's elaborate security. She remembered Finch's obsession with a specific breed of rare tropical fish he kept in a massive, custom-built aquarium at the end of the east corridor, just past the library. He considered them priceless, a testament to his unique taste and vast resources. The aquarium had its own intricate life support system, a delicate balance of temperature and filtration.

    "Finch's fish," she murmured, a flicker of an idea sparking in her eyes. "The aquarium in the east corridor. He guards those fish almost as fiercely as his *Fleur de Nuit*. If anything were to disturb their delicate environment, he would be beside himself."

    Leo’s brow furrowed. "Disturb them how? And how would that help us get past these two?" He glanced pointedly at the guards, who had now shifted position, their conversation growing slightly louder, though still indistinct.

    "The filtration system," Clara explained, her voice gaining a quiet intensity. "It's a complex network of pumps and pipes. I remember Finch explaining it to me once, boasting about its advanced design. If we could disable it, even for a few moments, it would set off a specific alarm in the main control room. Not a general security breach, but a 'critical systems failure' alert. Finch would be notified instantly. He would panic. He would dispatch all available personnel to secure his precious fish, leaving other areas vulnerable."

    "And the research wing?" Leo asked, catching her drift. "Would it be abandoned?"

    "Temporarily, yes. At least long enough for us to make our move," Clara confirmed. "But it would require speed. Finch would send his most trusted men to deal with the fish, and they would rush there without question. It is a risk, but it is our best option."

    "How do we disable it?" Leo inquired, ever practical.

    Clara bit her lip. "I saw a maintenance panel near the aquarium. A small one, with several large circuit breakers. If we could trip one of them, it might disrupt the flow, perhaps trigger the alarm." She knew it was a long shot, a desperate gamble based on a casual observation from weeks ago. But they had few alternatives. "I will need to get there quickly, without being seen."

    "And I will create the distraction here," Leo stated, his resolve firm despite his unarmed state. "These two need to be occupied. Can you get past them unnoticed to reach the east corridor?"

    Clara shook her head. "No. The main hall is too open. They would see me. We need a more direct diversion, something that draws their attention away from this spot entirely. Perhaps," she pondered, "the alarm we just silenced. They are likely aware it went off and was then disarmed. They might be expecting something to happen at the main entrance, or in the study again."

    An idea formed, chilling in its audacity. "Frank," Clara said, her eyes fixed on the distant entrance to the study. "He is still unconscious. He could be our unwitting diversion."

    Leo's eyes widened in understanding. "You mean, we move him? To draw them away?"

    "Not move him," Clara clarified, her voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. "But make it appear as though he is waking up, making noise, perhaps attempting an escape. The alarm was from the study, they know someone was there. If they think he is stirring, they will investigate. One of them, at least."

    "And the other?"

    "The other will be left alone, for a moment. That is our window. You, Leo, will deal with him. He will be distracted, caught off guard. And then we move, swiftly." Clara knew the plan was incredibly dangerous. Leo, unarmed, against a man with an automatic rifle. But the shotgun she held felt like a useless burden. If Leo could disarm one, they might stand a chance against the other, or at least gain enough time to get to the east corridor.

    Leo nodded slowly, his gaze hardening. "I understand. But what about you? How will you help?"

    "I will be with you. The shotgun might be inert, but it looks formidable enough. It might buy us the precious seconds you need." She looked at him, her determination shining through her apprehension. "Are you with me, Leo?"

    "Always, Clara," he replied, a grim smile touching his lips. "Always."

    They moved with the stealth of jungle cats, creeping back towards the study, their shadows stretching long and distorted behind them. The two guards at the research wing remained oblivious, their low conversation continuing. As they approached the study door, Clara peered inside. Frank lay motionless on the rug, a dark stain spreading slowly from beneath his head. Clara winced, hoping the blow had not been fatal. It was not part of their plan for anyone to die.

    "Okay," Clara whispered to Leo. "You go to the main entrance of the study. I will go to the window. When I give the signal, make some noise. Something to sound like Frank struggling or stumbling."

    Leo nodded, melting away into the shadows towards the study's ornate double doors. Clara, meanwhile, cautiously approached the tall, leaded-glass window of the study. She remembered earlier that one of the panes had been slightly ajar, rattling in the wind. Carefully, she pushed it open a fraction more, just enough to allow sound to escape. Then, with a calculated motion, she reached inside, knocking over a heavy bronze statue that stood on a side table. It crashed to the floor with a resounding clang, followed by a muffled thud.

    The effect was immediate. From their position by the research wing, the guards startled. The leaner one, the more alert of the two, snapped his head towards the study. "What was that?" he barked, his rifle rising instinctively.

    "Sounded like something in the study," the burly guard grunted, but he did not move immediately. He was more hesitant.

    "Stay here," the leaner guard commanded, already moving with surprising speed towards the study. "I'll check it out. Cover me."

    This was it. Clara's heart hammered against her ribs. As the leaner guard reached the study doors, Leo, positioned just out of sight, let out a convincing groan, followed by a series of rustling sounds, as if a large man was trying to get to his feet.

    "Hey!" the guard shouted, pushing open one of the study doors with his foot, his rifle aimed low. He stepped inside, disappearing from view for a fleeting moment.

    This was their chance. "Now, Leo!" Clara breathed, and then, as if by instinct, she burst from the shadows where she had been waiting, the shotgun held high.

    The burly guard, left alone and still facing the research wing, spun around at Clara's sudden appearance. His eyes widened in alarm as he saw the shotgun, gleaming ominously in the moonlight. Before he could fully raise his own rifle, Leo sprang from the other side of the alcove, a blur of motion. He launched himself forward, a silent, desperate attack. His unarmed body collided with the guard, knocking the man off balance. The guard's rifle clattered to the marble floor as they both went down in a heap.

    Clara rushed forward, her mind screaming for speed. Leo, despite his lack of training, was surprisingly effective. He had pinned the guard, his hands expertly going for the man's throat, cutting off his air before he could raise an alarm. The guard struggled, a gurgling sound escaping his lips, but Leo held fast.

    "The door, Clara! Get the door!" Leo strained, his face red with effort.

    Clara did not hesitate. The heavy oak doors of the research wing were right there. She noticed a keypad lock on the wall beside them, and a keycard reader. But she also saw a thick, old-fashioned bolt, secured with a pad of dust. It looked unused, a relic from an earlier, less technologically advanced security system. With a desperate heave, she slid the bolt back. It groaned in protest, but it moved. The doors were no longer fully secured by the bolt. But the keypad was still active.

    "The code, Clara! Do you know it?" Leo gasped, the guard beneath him starting to lose consciousness.

    Clara racked her brain. Finch often used simple, memorable codes for non-critical access points, sometimes based on significant dates. His birthdate? His mother's? No, too obvious. Then, she remembered a casual remark Finch had made about the "true birth" of his greatest achievement. The *Fleur de Nuit*. He had mentioned the exact date he first acquired the seed, a momentous occasion for him. She typed it in, a sequence of eight numbers. The keypad flashed green. With a soft click, the electronic lock disengaged.

    "Yes!" Clara exclaimed, a wave of relief washing over her. She pulled the heavy doors open, revealing a short, brightly lit corridor beyond.

    "Get in! I will be right behind you!" Leo instructed, giving the unconscious guard one last, firm punch to ensure he stayed down. He then snatched up the discarded rifle, its weight reassuring in his hands, before scrambling through the doors.

    Clara pulled the doors shut behind them, fumbling for the old bolt. With a grunt, she shoved it back into place. It would not hold for long if someone tried to force their way in, but it might buy them a few more minutes. They were in.

    The corridor they found themselves in was starkly different from the opulence of the main hall. White walls, stainless steel fixtures, and a distinct, almost sterile odor. It felt like a hospital wing, or a high-tech laboratory. Fluorescent lights hummed softly overhead, casting a harsh, shadowless glow. Ahead, the corridor branched off into several smaller rooms, each with a reinforced door and a small observation window.

    "Which way?" Leo asked, his voice low, the rifle now held ready.

    Clara scanned the signs above the doors. "Biological Samples," "Specimen Storage," "Controlled Environments." Her eyes finally settled on a sign above the largest door at the very end of the corridor: "Tropical Flora Research - Level 4." Finch had told her the *Fleur de Nuit* required very specific conditions, replicating its natural, humid, tropical habitat. Level 4 sounded like the most advanced and carefully controlled environment.

    "That one," she said, pointing. "Tropical Flora Research."

    They moved quickly, their footsteps echoing slightly on the polished floor. Clara still carried the inert shotgun, though she felt more confident now that Leo was armed. As they neared the door, a faint whirring sound became audible, the soft hum of environmental controls working constantly. Another keypad and keycard reader stood beside this door.

    "Finch will have higher security for his prize," Clara observed, already anticipating the next obstacle. "This code will likely be different."

    She tried the previous code, but the keypad remained stubbornly red. She thought of other significant dates, other personal details Finch might have used. His favorite classic car's model year? The date he first made his fortune? Nothing came to mind.

    "He boasted about the uniqueness of the *Fleur de Nuit*," Clara muttered, pressing her forehead against the cool metal of the door. "He called it his 'masterpiece,' his 'singular achievement.' Something personal, something exclusive to this room."

    Leo checked his watch. "We don't have much time, Clara. The other guard will be rousing, or the leaner one will return from the study to find his partner gone. Finch will be here soon."

    Clara closed her eyes, trying to visualize Finch, trying to think like him. What would be the ultimate symbol of his prized possession? The *Fleur de Nuit* itself. But how would that translate into a code? Not its name, that was too obvious. Its scientific classification? *Nocturne Mirabilis*? Too long, and often changing in the scientific world.

    Then it hit her. Finch had a peculiar habit, a vanity, really. He would commission artists to paint hyper-realistic portraits of his most valuable acquisitions. He had a series of these paintings, each one numbered and dated. He had once shown her a photograph of the initial sketch for the *Fleur de Nuit* painting, a simple, elegant line drawing. At the bottom, meticulously inscribed by Finch himself, was its designated collection number. It was a nine-digit number, unique to the orchid within his private catalog.

    "The collection number!" Clara exclaimed, her fingers flying over the keypad. She entered the nine digits from memory. There was a moment of agonizing suspense, a soft beep, and then the familiar green light. The lock disengaged with a solid clunk.

    They pulled open the heavy door. Inside, the air was warm and humid, thick with the scent of rich earth and exotic blossoms. They had entered a large, glass-enclosed space, a veritable jungle under glass. Mist gently drifted from hidden nozzles, creating a soft, ethereal atmosphere. Rows of tropical plants, many of them rare and beautiful, thrived in the carefully controlled environment. Banks of specialized grow-lights bathed the plants in a soft, purplish glow.

    "Incredible," Leo breathed, momentarily forgetting their mission in awe of the spectacle.

    "Find the *Fleur de Nuit*," Clara urged, her eyes already darting around the room, searching for the unique orchid. She knew its description well: a large, dark, almost black blossom, with petals that shimmered with an iridescent blue in certain lights, said to bloom only once every five years and only at night.

    They moved through the aisles of plants, the air growing warmer and more humid with each step. Other rare orchids, some with vibrant colors, others with delicate, intricate patterns, lined the shelves. But none matched the description of the *Fleur de Nuit*.

    "There!" Clara pointed, her voice hushed with excitement. At the very center of the greenhouse, standing on a raised pedestal under its own dedicated, pulsing light, was a single, magnificent plant. Its leaves were a deep, glossy green, almost black, and from its stalk emerged a bloom unlike anything Clara had ever seen. It was indeed a deep, velvety black, but as she drew closer, she could discern the faintest hint of blue, shimmering like distant galaxies within its petals. The bloom was larger than she had imagined, almost the size of a human hand, and it exuded a faint, intoxicating perfume, a mix of musk and sweet spices.

    The *Fleur de Nuit*.

    "We need to get it out," Leo said, his voice low and urgent.

    Clara approached the pedestal. The orchid was planted in a large, heavy ceramic pot, resting on a specialized turntable that rotated slowly, allowing the light to hit every part of the plant. A small, clear glass dome covered the bloom itself, protecting it.

    "How do we transport it?" Clara questioned, realizing the difficulty. The pot was enormous, too heavy for either of them to carry easily. And the bloom was delicate.

    "Finch is meticulous," Leo observed, examining the base of the pedestal. "There must be a way. He wouldn't just expect someone to rip it out of the ground."

    Clara noticed a small, recessed panel near the base of the pedestal. She pressed it. With a soft hiss, the glass dome lifted away. Beneath it, nestled into the soil around the orchid's base, was a smaller, more portable container. It was a specially designed, self-contained unit, almost like a miniature terrarium, with its own independent climate control and nutrient supply. It was evident Finch had designed it for precisely this purpose: secure transportation.

    "He planned for this," Clara marveled. "He planned for its display, its exhibition. This is how he takes it to shows, to potential buyers."

    Carefully, Clara lifted the self-contained unit. It was heavy, but manageable, perhaps twenty pounds. The *Fleur de Nuit* sat serenely within its protective shell, its dark petals glowing faintly under the internal light of its transport case.

    "We have it," Clara whispered, a thrill of triumph running through her.

    But their moment of victory was short-lived. Just as she secured the container in her arms, a loud, insistent alarm blared through the research wing. Not the quiet, critical systems failure alarm they had planned for, but a full-blown, deafening security breach siren. Red lights flashed overhead, casting an ominous pall over the verdant greenhouse.

    "He's here!" Leo shouted over the din, his eyes wide. "Finch knows we're inside!"

    "The other guard must have recovered, or the first one returned," Clara surmised, her mind racing. "We have to go, now!"

    They turned, prepared to rush back the way they came. But the door to the Tropical Flora Research room, the one they had just entered, slammed shut with a sickening thud. The red lights above it flashed furiously, and a digital display above the keypad now read: "LOCKDOWN INITIATED. UNLOCK SEQUENCE REQUIRED."

    "He's locked us in!" Leo exclaimed, trying to force the door open, but it was unyielding. The reinforced steel held firm.

    Clara's heart sank. Trapped. Finch had anticipated their move, or at least he had set a failsafe. "He must have a master control, a panic button for the entire wing," she deduced, her mind desperately searching for a solution.

    "What's the unlock sequence?" Leo demanded, turning to the keypad.

    "I don't know it," Clara admitted, her voice filled with despair. "He would never have given a master code to anyone. It would be his personal, ultimate sequence."

    The blaring alarm continued, a relentless assault on their ears. Footsteps, many footsteps, could now be heard approaching from beyond the locked door. Shouts echoed, distorted by the steel. Finch's voice, raised in fury, was discernible above the commotion.

Mrs. Downey jotted down two addresses on a small notepad. "Start with Silas. He’s a bit gruff, but he has a soft spot for anyone genuinely interested in the hotel's history, not just its gossip. He might even know something about 'Project Emerald,' if that's what Maxwell is truly calling his venture."

Penny folded the slip of paper carefully and tucked it into her pocket. "Thank you, Mrs. Downey. I don't know how to repay you."

"Just bring justice to this valley, Penny," Mrs. Downey said, her voice turning serious once more. "My father always believed that truth, like water, eventually finds its way to the surface, no matter how many rocks try to block its path. Now, it's late. You should get some rest. A clear mind is your best tool."

Penny agreed. The prospect of sleep was appealing after the day's events, but her mind was already racing, plotting her next moves. She bade Mrs. Downey goodnight and retired to her room, the old maps already forming complex routes in her imagination.

The next morning, the Pine Top air was crisp and invigorating. Penny dressed in practical, dark clothing, suitable for exploring possibly dusty and disused passages. She opted for sturdy boots and a jacket with plenty of pockets. A small flashlight, a notebook, and a pencil completed her toolkit. After a quick breakfast, she set out on her new mission.

Silas lived in a small, weather-beaten cabin nestled at the edge of a dense pine forest, a mile or two from Mrs. Downey's lodge. A wisp of smoke curled lazily from its chimney, suggesting someone was home. Penny approached cautiously, knocking on the worn wooden door.

After a moment, the door creaked open, revealing an elderly man with a gnarled face and sharp, intelligent eyes. His beard was a tangle of grey and white, and he leaned heavily on a polished walking stick.

"Aye?" he grunted, his gaze assessing her. "What do you want, young 'un? Not selling encyclopedias, are ye?"

Penny offered her most disarming smile. "Good morning, sir. My name is Penny Parker. Mrs. Downey suggested I speak with you. She said you know a great deal about the Fergus Hotel."

Silas's eyes narrowed, but the mention of Mrs. Downey seemed to soften his expression slightly. "Agnes, eh? She's a good woman. Come in then, don't just stand there letting the cold in."

The cabin was small but surprisingly neat, filled with the scent of pine and woodsmoke. Tools hung neatly on the walls, and a well-worn armchair sat beside a crackling fireplace. Silas motioned for Penny to sit on a small stool while he settled back into his armchair.

"So, the Fergus Hotel," he mused, stroking his beard. "Known that place since I was knee-high to a grasshopper. Worked there, my father worked there, his father too. Three generations of Jaskos and their kin kept that place running, before the new blood took over and started ruining everything."

"Jasko?" Penny asked, a sudden connection sparking in

her mind. The gruff old man nodded slowly, his eyes twinkling for a fleeting moment. "Aye, Jasko," he affirmed. "That's my name, Silas Jasko. And yes, young Sara, the one who rattles around up in the loft, she's my granddaughter. A wild one, that girl. Just like her mother was, bless her soul." He sighed, a low rumble from deep within his chest. "So you've met Sara, have you? She usually scares off anyone who comes near, unless they've got a sled or a pair of skis."

Penny smiled faintly. "Yes, I met her yesterday. She told me about the Horseshoe Curve." She decided to be direct, to a certain extent. "She also mentioned a great deal about the hotel's history. She said your family has quite a legacy there."

Silas leaned forward, propping his chin on his gnarled hand. "Legacy, you say? More like a curse, sometimes. My grandfather, old Elara Jasko, he built the main wing of that hotel with his own two hands, stone by stone. My father, Tobias, he added the grand ballroom and the south wing. And me, I worked every position from stable boy to head groundskeeper. Knew every creak, every draft, every secret passage in that place. Knew the Downey family, too, from the first generation right up to Agnes. Good folk, most of 'em. Believed in honest work and honest pay."

He paused, a flicker of something dark crossing his face. "But then Maxwell came along. Harvey Maxwell. Full of big talk and city money. Bought out the Downeys, piece by piece, when old Mr. Downey passed on. Said he was going to 'modernize' it, 'bring it into the new age.' What he brought was trouble, I tell you. Nothing but trouble."

Penny saw her opening. "What kind of trouble, Mr. Jasko? Mrs. Downey seems quite concerned about what Mr. Maxwell is doing."

Silas snorted, a sound like dry leaves rustling. "Concerned? She ought to be. That man is a menace. He talks of 'Project Emerald' and 'exclusive clientele' and building a reputation. But I hear things, you know. I live up here, away from the city folk, but the mountain whispers its secrets. Whispers of strange comings and goings, of rooms locked up tight, of people paid to keep quiet." He looked at Penny with a piercing gaze. "You're not one of Maxwell's spies, are you? Come to see if I'm still snooping around?"

"Oh, no, Mr. Jasko," Penny assured him quickly. "Absolutely not. I'm actually trying to find out what Mr. Maxwell is up to. I believe he's involved in something dishonest, perhaps even illegal. That's why Mrs. Downey suggested I speak with you. She said you knew the hotel better than anyone."

A slow smile spread across Silas's face, revealing teeth stained by years of pipe smoking. "Agnes always did have a good head on her shoulders. So, you're a truth-seeker, eh? Just like her father." He rose from his armchair with surprising agility, walked to a sturdy wooden chest in the corner, and rummaged through it. He pulled out a large, worn book bound in thick leather. "This here is the Jasko family journal. My grandfather started it. Every Jasko who worked at the hotel, they added to it. Records of repairs, changes, odd occurrences. And sometimes, secrets."

He placed the heavy book on a small table between them, the leather creaking softly. Penny leaned closer, her heart thrumming with anticipation. This was exactly the kind of lead she needed.

"My grandfather," Silas continued, tapping a weathered finger on the cover, "he was a meticulous man. He knew every stone, every beam. And he had a particular fascination with hidden spaces. Said every great house needed its secrets, for safekeeping, for emergencies. He built a few of his own, in the hotel."

Penny's eyes widened. "Hidden spaces? Like secret rooms?"

"More than just rooms," Silas corrected her. "Passageways, storage nooks. And one special place. A room designed not to be found by just anyone. My grandfather called it the 'Green Room' in his own private notes, after the color of the velvet he used to line the walls. Said it was for 'special guests' or 'delicate matters.' A place where a man could conduct business without prying eyes or ears."

Penny felt a thrill run down her spine. The Green Room. The very place she was trying to investigate. "Did he ever say where this Green Room was located?" she asked, trying to keep her voice even.

Silas chuckled, a dry, raspy sound. "Oh, he was too clever for that. Didn't want just anyone stumbling upon it, even in his own journal. But he left clues, if you knew where to look. Hints about its location relative to other permanent features of the building. And he drew a map, too. A very cryptic map, but a map nonetheless." He opened the journal to a page near the middle, its paper yellowed with age. There, amongst faded script, was a rough sketch. It showed a section of the hotel's floor plan, but with certain areas exaggerated or distorted, and a series of symbols Penny couldn't decipher.

"It looks like a puzzle," Penny observed, tracing a finger over the intricate drawing.

"It is," Silas confirmed. "A puzzle only a Jasko is meant to solve, or someone who truly understands the old ways of the hotel. He didn't want any 'new blood' finding it, you see. Thought they'd misuse it. And I reckon he was right." He paused, his gaze fixed on Penny. "You said you had a 'green ticket' last night, didn't you? The one Maxwell snatched?"

Penny was startled. "How did you know that?"

Silas tapped his temple. "Old man's intuition. And the mountain whispers, remember? Agnes mentioned you were asking about a green card. And Maxwell's reaction? That tells me it's important. It tells me he knows something about that room, even if he doesn't know its full history or how to access it easily."

"I found the ticket," Penny explained. "It was a small green card with some unfamiliar symbols on it, and a number. I think it's an admittance card to the Green Room. It even let me past a guard."

"A guard?" Silas's eyebrows shot up. "So, Maxwell *is* using it. Or trying to. That man has no respect for history, only for profit." He studied the map again, then looked at Penny with a determined glint in his

eyes. "We have to get into that room, Penny. Not just for your story, but for the legacy of this mountain, and for my grandfather's memory."

Penny nodded, her earlier dismay at being ejected from the hotel now replaced by a surge of excitement and purpose. Silas’s conviction was infectious. "But Maxwell took my ticket, and he knows who I am. He told me never to come back."

"A minor setback," Silas dismissed with a wave of his hand. "My grandfather built this hotel, and he knew all its secrets. Maxwell is but a newcomer, a squatter trying to claim what is not his. We have advantages he does not even comprehend." He leaned closer to the journal, his gnarled finger tracing a line on the faded map. "This map, Penny, it is not merely a diagram. It is a story, a riddle woven into the very fabric of the hotel's design. See here," he pointed to a crudely drawn symbol that resembled a pair of crossed skis, "this is not just a ski rack. It represents a particular kind of wood, a specific grain. And this," he moved his finger to a series of dots, "these are not just decorative patterns. They mark the number of steps one must take, or perhaps the number of planks in a floor, from a certain point."

Penny peered over his shoulder, trying to make sense of the intricate sketch. It was far more complex than she had initially realized. The lines were not straight architectural drawings but fluid, almost organic shapes, as if drawn from memory rather than precise measurement. "It looks like a code," she murmured.

"Indeed," Silas agreed, his eyes gleaming. "And my grandfather was fond of codes. He believed that true understanding came from discovery, not from being given answers. The Green Room, he intended it to be a place of sanctuary, a safe haven. But it could also be a place of mischief if found by the wrong hands." He paused, rubbing his chin thoughtfully. "The critical element here is not brute force, but observation. My grandfather always said, 'The mountain hides its treasures from the hurried eye, but reveals them to the patient heart.'"

"So, we need patience," Penny concluded. "And an understanding of his mind."

"Precisely. And I spent many an hour as a boy watching him work, listening to his philosophies. Some of his riddles, I believe I can unravel. Let us begin with the starting point. He always anchored his secrets to something immutable, something that would not change with renovations or time. The main fireplace in the grand hall, for instance, or the old pine tree outside the east wing." Silas squinted at the map, then turned a few pages in the journal. "Ah, here it is! A marginal note." He read aloud, his voice a low rumble, "'From the heart of the hearth, one finds the path to the hidden earth.' The hearth, Penny, that must be the grand fireplace in the lobby. It is the oldest and most central feature of the original structure."

Penny felt a surge of excitement. "That makes sense. It's a landmark everyone knows."

"And from there," Silas continued, tracing a path on the map with his finger, "we follow the 'whispering woods.' The whispering woods, my boyish mind interpreted it as the creaking floorboards, but I believe now it refers to a particular section of paneling, perhaps even a specific type of wood that makes a distinct sound when touched. The map shows a zigzagging line from the hearth, not a straight one. This suggests a deviation, a turn, perhaps through a less obvious passage."

"But how do we get back into the hotel without Maxwell seeing me?" Penny asked, her practical nature reasserting itself. "He made it very clear I am persona non grata."

Silas smiled, a crinkling of the corners of his eyes. "Maxwell knows who you are, yes. But he does not know who *I* am, not really. To him, I am merely an old mountaineer, perhaps a curious local. He would not suspect me of having any grand designs. And as for you, my dear, the mountains offer many guises." He looked her up and down. "You are small, nimble. And you have that delightful knack for observation. A simple change of attire, a different way of carrying yourself, and most people will see what they expect to see, not what is truly there."

"You mean a disguise?" Penny asked, intrigued. "Like in the detective novels?"

"Something of the sort. Nothing so elaborate as a false mustache, perhaps. More of a distraction, a shift in perception. We will need to return to Pine Top first, to gather some things. And perhaps to observe Maxwell's routine. He will be on edge now, knowing you were there, knowing you found the ticket. He will be guarding his secrets more closely."

They spent the rest of the afternoon poring over the journal. Silas explained the symbology of his grandfather's notes, the little sketches that indicated textures, the stylized representations of furniture that hinted at hidden mechanisms. Penny, with her keen eye for detail and her quick mind, absorbed every piece of information. She realized that the Green Room was not simply a door in a wall, but an entire system of concealment, a testament to the old man's ingenuity. The map showed not just a location, but a sequence of actions, a dance one had to perform to reveal the room.

As dusk began to settle over the mountains, painting the snow-covered peaks in hues of pink and orange, Silas closed the journal. "We have enough for now," he declared. "The true test will be on site. The mountain will guide us further, if we are ready to listen."

"What about Sara?" Penny asked, remembering her earlier encounter. "She's cooped up here, and she seemed very eager to help Mrs. Downey."

Silas sighed. "Sara is a good girl, but she has too much of her mother's wild spirit. She chafes under rules. I have tried to instill discipline, but it is like trying to tame the wind. Perhaps this bob-sledding venture will occupy her mind, keep her out of mischief for a while. Tell her she may go, on one condition: she is to cause no trouble, and she is to return before dark. And tell Mrs. Downey I will hold her responsible for Sara's safety."

Penny smiled. "I will. She'll be thrilled."

"Now," Silas said, rising with a slight groan, "we need a plan to get you back into that hotel undetected. And for that, we need to think like ghosts. Quiet, unseen, leaving no trace."

The following morning, after a hurried breakfast at Silas's cabin, Penny made her way to Mrs. Downey's lodge. Sara was already there, bursting with energy, practically bouncing off the walls of the great room. "Penny! You came! Did Grandfather say yes? Can I go?"

"He said yes," Penny confirmed, "on the condition that you cause no trouble and return before dark. And Mrs. Downey is responsible for you."

Sara let out a whoop of delight, throwing her arms around Penny. "Oh, Penny, you're the best! I knew you could do it! Come on, Mrs. Downey is already out at the track. She wants to start testing the course."

As they stepped outside, the air was crisp and invigorating. The sun sparkled on the fresh snow, and the sky was an impossibly clear blue. Mrs. Downey, bundled in a thick coat and scarf, was indeed inspecting the bob-sled run, directing a couple of stable hands who were making final adjustments to the icy banks.

"Good morning, girls!" Mrs. Downey called out, her voice cheerful despite the chill. "Sara, wonderful to have you! We need young blood and steady hands. Penny, are you joining us?"

Penny shook her head. "Not this morning, Mrs. Downey. I have some other business to attend to. But I'll be back later, perhaps." She exchanged a quick, knowing glance with Sara. "Just make sure Sara behaves herself."

Sara grinned.
