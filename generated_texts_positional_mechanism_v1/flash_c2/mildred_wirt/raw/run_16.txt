The whirring and clicking continued for a few more moments, then stopped. A different sound followed, a low, guttural voice, speaking in a foreign tongue. It was quick, clipped, almost like code, delivered in a steady, monotonous rhythm. The language was unfamiliar to both girls, but the tone was urgent, purposeful.

The broadcast lasted for less than a minute. Then, with another series of clicks and whirs, the radio fell silent. The lantern inside the boathouse briefly flared, then dimmed, and then the figure re-emerged from the opening in the wall, closing it as best as possible. The individual once again paused, scanning the dark beach with a meticulous, unsettling thoroughness that made Penny and Louise press themselves even further into their rocky refuge.

Satisfied, the hooded figure turned and began to walk briskly back the way it had come, towards the lighthouse. The lantern bobbed rhythmically, its light gradually shrinking into the distance, until it vanished entirely behind the rocky outcrops.

A long moment of stunned silence passed between the girls. The cold had returned, but now it was accompanied by a shiver that had nothing to do with the temperature.

“He spoke in German, didn’t he?” Louise finally whispered, her voice trembling slightly. “It sounded like German.”

Penny, still breathing heavily, slowly lowered the binoculars. “I think so. I couldn’t understand a word, but it wasn’t English. Or French. Or Spanish.” She paused, her mind racing. “This is it, Lou. This is the ‘Voice from the Cave.’ We found him.”

“Or at least, we found someone connected to him,” Louise corrected, ever the pragmatist. “We couldn’t see his face. We don’t know who he was. And we don’t know what he said.”

“But we know where he broadcast from!” Penny countered, a surge of triumph overcoming her fear. “And we heard the radio! We have to tell Jerry, right now!” She fumbled for the two-way radio in the canvas bag.

“Wait,” Louise urged, putting a restraining hand on Penny’s arm. “Jerry said only to use it for an emergency. This isn’t an emergency yet. The figure is gone. And if we report now, Mike will still be another hour and a half away. We can tell him everything when he picks us up. Besides, what if the figure hears us? We don’t know how far he’s gone. Or if he might come back.”

Penny hesitated, considering. Louise had a point. The eerie silence of the night made any sound seem magnified. To use the radio now might be a risk. “All right,” she conceded, though reluctantly. “But we have to remember every single detail. The sounds, the timing, everything.”

They remained in their hiding spot, their senses on high alert. The adrenaline of the encounter slowly began to recede, leaving them both feeling profoundly exhausted and chilled to the bone. The remaining time until 4:30 AM seemed to stretch out interminably. They kept their eyes fixed on the empty boathouse, half expecting the hooded figure to return, or another to appear. But the beach remained deserted, bathed only in the pale, indifferent light of the fading stars.

Finally, just as the sky in the east began to show the faintest hint of grey, a pair of headlights appeared in the distance, tracing a familiar path along the coastal road. Mike was punctual, as expected.

“That’s our ride,” Louise whispered, a wave of immense relief washing over her.

Carefully, silently, they emerged from their hiding place, their joints stiff from the cold and the prolonged stillness. They made their way back to the road, keeping an eye on the boathouse until the last possible moment. Mike’s car was already waiting at the designated spot. They slipped into the back seat, collapsing onto the worn upholstery, suddenly too tired to speak.

Mike merely grunted an acknowledgement, and the car pulled away, leaving the silent, mysterious beach behind. As the lights of Sunset Beach drew closer, Penny felt a powerful urge to tell Mike everything, to relive the excitement of their discovery. But she remembered Jerry’s instructions, and Louise’s caution. No talking unless absolutely necessary. Their report would wait for Jerry Livingston himself.

Back at the Crystal Inn, the sky was now a pale, pearly grey, promising the imminent arrival of dawn. They crept back into their room, shedding their sandy, damp clothes. Neither of them felt like sleeping, despite their exhaustion. The image of the hooded figure, the faint whirring of the radio, and the guttural foreign words were burned into their minds.

“We really did it, Lou,” Penny whispered, curling up on her bed, her voice hoarse with fatigue and lingering excitement. “We really found something.”

Louise sat on the edge of her own bed, rubbing her arms. “Yes, we did. But now what? What does Jerry do with that information? What if that person comes back tonight?”

Penny closed her eyes, a shiver running down her spine. “I don’t know. But I bet Jerry Livingston will. And I bet we haven’t heard the last of the ‘Voice from the Cave.’” The chapter, it seemed, had only just begun.

The first rays of morning light, thin and hesitant, crept through the window of their room at the Crystal Inn, painting the rumpled bedspreads in a pale, indifferent glow. Penny lay awake, staring at the ceiling, her mind a whirlwind of images and sounds. The dark form against the boathouse, the almost inaudible hum of machinery, the harsh, foreign syllables whispering on the wind. Louise, beside her, stirred restlessly, a soft groan escaping her lips.

"Are you awake, Lou?" Penny whispered, her voice still raspy from the night air.

Louise opened her eyes, blinking against the encroaching light. "Wide awake," she murmured, pushing herself up to a sitting position. She hugged her knees to her chest, her gaze unfocused. "I keep hearing it, you know. That strange voice."

"Me too," Penny admitted, sitting up as well. "It's like it's burned into my memory. What do you think it means, Lou? What was that man saying?"

"I have no idea," Louise replied, shaking her head slowly. "But whatever it was, it wasn't friendly. And that radio, Penny. It must have been what Jerry was talking about, the 'Voice from the Cave.' Only it wasn't a cave this time, was it? It was a boathouse."

"A boathouse by the sea," Penny mused, rubbing her arms as if to dispel a lingering chill. "And it's so close to Sunset Beach. Right where Dad said the

Coast Guards watch this place closely. Because of its isolation and jagged coastline it's considered a likely spot for surprise night landings by the Enemy."

"And Dad also said they watch for suspicious signals," Louise added, her voice a little stronger now. "That's exactly what we heard, Penny. A signal, only it was a voice."

"A voice that wasn't speaking English," Penny mused, pulling her knees up to her chin. "I wonder if it was German. That's what Jerry said they suspected, German prisoners escaping."

"It sounded guttural, didn't it?" Louise shivered again. "I don't like thinking about it. We should tell Jerry right away. But how?"

Penny frowned, her brow furrowed in thought. "He told us not to talk about it, not to even mention his name. We can't just call him from the hotel desk. And we don't know where the monitoring truck is anymore, exactly."

"We could wait for him," Louise suggested. "He might come looking for us, or maybe we'll see him in the lobby. He often comes and goes."

"But what if he doesn't?" Penny countered, swinging her legs off the bed. "What if that man comes back to the boathouse tonight? Or what if he moves his equipment? We have vital information, Lou. We can't just sit here."

A sudden thought brightened Penny's face. "The newspaper! Remember how Jerry left it for us the other day, with that coded message? Maybe there's a way to send him a message back, a subtle one."

"Penny, you're brilliant!" Louise exclaimed, a flicker of her usual enthusiasm returning. "But what kind of message? We can't write 'found enemy spy radio' on a postcard."

"No, of course not," Penny said, already rummaging through her small suitcase. "But Jerry is clever. He'll understand a hint. If we can get a message to him through the hotel staff, something seemingly innocent, but with a specific detail."

She pulled out a small notepad and a pencil. "What's something we know Jerry likes? Something we could ask about that would be out of the ordinary for us?"

Louise considered this. "He likes those little sugar cookies the hotel bakes. He mentioned them the other day when we were in the dining room."

"Perfect!" Penny scribbled furiously. "I'll write a note to the concierge, asking if Mr. Livingston has left any special instructions for us, perhaps about those sugar cookies. And I'll add a little detail that only he would recognize as important."

"What detail?" Louise leaned over her shoulder, curious.

"The time," Penny declared. "The time we saw him last, and maybe a hint about the location. But not too obvious." She wrote for a moment more, then read it aloud. "Dear Mr. Henderson, Could you please inquire if Mr. Livingston has left any messages for Penny and Louise, perhaps regarding the special sugar cookies we discussed? We were hoping to find him around sunset yesterday evening, near the old boathouse road. Thank you, Penny and Louise."

"Sunset yesterday evening, near the old boathouse road," Louise repeated slowly. "That's good, Penny. It sounds like a casual inquiry, but Jerry will know what 'sunset yesterday evening' refers to, and the boathouse road is specific enough."

"Exactly," Penny said, tearing off the sheet. "Now, to get this to Mr. Henderson without drawing attention."

They dressed quickly, choosing their most innocuous outfits. Descending to the lobby, they found it still largely deserted, save for a sleepy-looking bellboy polishing a brass railing and Mr. Henderson behind the front desk, arranging newspapers.

"Good morning, Mr. Henderson," Penny chirped, approaching the desk with Louise.

"Good morning, young ladies," he replied, adjusting his spectacles. "An early start for you both."

"Yes, we're eager for breakfast," Penny said, handing him the folded note. "And I was hoping you could help us with something, if you don't mind."

Mr. Henderson took the note, his expression kind but businesslike. "Certainly, what can I do?"

"It's just a little inquiry about Mr. Livingston," Penny explained, trying to sound nonchalant. "He mentioned something about those lovely sugar cookies the hotel makes, and we were wondering if he'd left any message for us regarding them. He's such a busy man, you see, we didn't want to disturb him unless it was important."

Mr. Henderson unfolded the note and read it. A slight frown touched his brow as he reached the part about the boathouse road, but it vanished as quickly as it appeared. He was a professional, after all. "I see. Mr. Livingston is not in his room, but I can certainly have a message sent to him. He usually checks in with us for his mail and any urgent communications."

"Oh, thank you, Mr. Henderson," Penny said, relief flooding through her. "That would be wonderful."

As they headed for the dining room, Louise whispered, "Do you think he suspected anything?"

"Mr. Henderson? No, I don't think so," Penny replied, though she wasn't entirely certain. "He's too proper. But Jerry will get the message. I just know it."

Breakfast was a quiet affair. They picked at their toast and eggs, their minds replaying the events of the previous night. The waiting was the hardest part. Every time the dining room door opened, Penny looked up expectantly, hoping to see Jerry Livingston.

An hour later, back in their room, the phone rang. Penny leaped for it. "Hello?"

"Penny? This is Jerry. Mr. Henderson just forwarded a rather interesting note from you. Sugar cookies, eh? And a pleasant evening walk by the boathouse road?" His voice was low, with an underlying current of seriousness that Penny immediately recognized.

"Yes, Mr. Livingston," Penny replied, trying to keep her voice even. "We certainly had an interesting walk. And we have some very important news about those cookies."

"I thought as much," Jerry said. "Meet me in the small reading room on the second floor in ten minutes. Make sure no one follows you. And don't bring anything that might attract attention."

"We understand," Penny confirmed. "Ten minutes."

She hung up the phone, her heart thumping with excitement and a touch of apprehension. "He got the message, Lou! He wants to meet us in the reading room."

"Oh, good," Louise breathed, clasping her hands together. "Now we can finally tell him."

They made their way to the reading room discreetly, glancing over their shoulders a few times. It was a cozy, seldom-used space, filled with dusty books and plush armchairs. Jerry was already there, standing by a window, looking out to sea. He turned as they entered, his expression grim.

"Girls," he said, his voice quiet. "I take it your note wasn't about baked goods."

"No, sir," Penny said, her usual boisterousness subdued by the gravity of the situation. "It was about the Voice from the Cave."

Jerry gestured to two armchairs. "Sit down. Now, tell me everything. From the very beginning."

They recounted their adventure in detail, Penny filling in the dramatic flourishes and Louise providing the clarifying points. They described their decision to investigate the boathouse, the careful trek in the dark, the faint light they saw within, and the distinct hum of machinery.

"And then you heard a voice?" Jerry prompted, leaning forward, his eyes sharp.

"Yes," Louise confirmed. "A man's voice. And it wasn't English. It was guttural, harsh, speaking very rapidly."

"And you're certain it was coming from inside the boathouse?" Jerry pressed.

"Absolutely," Penny declared. "We crept right up to the window. We saw a man, hooded, his back to us, operating some kind of radio equipment. We could hear the static, and then his voice clearly."

"A hooded figure," Jerry mused, rubbing his chin. "Interesting. And you say it sounded like German?"

"We don't know for sure, Mr. Livingston," Louise admitted. "But it sounded foreign, and very unlike French or Spanish, languages we've heard before."

"And you were certain it was a radio broadcast, not just someone talking on a receiver?"

"It sounded like a broadcast," Penny insisted. "It had that peculiar quality, like someone transmitting. And the equipment looked elaborate. There were wires and dials."

Jerry stood up and walked to the window, his back to them. He was silent for a long moment, the only sound the distant cry of gulls.

"This is very important, girls," he finally said, turning around. "More important than you can imagine. You've given us a concrete location. The boathouse. Can you describe it precisely? Its position relative to any landmarks?"

They did their best, drawing on their memories of the shadowy shoreline. "It's fairly isolated," Penny explained, "past the main stretch of beach where people swim. There's a rocky outcrop, and then a small cove. The boathouse is nestled right there, almost hidden by some scrub brush."

"And there's an old, rickety dock next to it," Louise added. "It looked like it hadn't been used in years, but there were fresh tire tracks on the sand leading up to it, Penny noticed."

Jerry's eyes widened slightly at that detail. "Tire tracks. Excellent. This corroborates a few things we've been suspecting. The Coast Guard has reported unusual activity in that general area, small boats coming and going at odd hours, though they haven't been able to pinpoint anything specific."

"So, it really is the Enemy?" Penny asked, her voice hushed.

Jerry nodded slowly. "It certainly sounds like it. A hidden radio station, broadcasting in a foreign language, in an isolated spot known for potential night landings. It fits the profile perfectly. And the 'Voice from the Cave' description was just a cover, a way to throw us off the scent of a more mobile operation."

"What will you do now?" Louise inquired.

"We move quickly," Jerry stated, his professional demeanor fully engaged. "I'll need to relay this information to my superiors immediately. We'll organize a team to investigate the boathouse. A stakeout, perhaps, or a quiet reconnaissance mission first. We can't go storming in without knowing exactly what we're up against. These people are dangerous, girls."

"We know," Penny said, feeling a thrill of danger mixed with pride.

"I need you both to understand something," Jerry continued, his gaze serious. "What you did last night was incredibly brave, and incredibly foolish. You put yourselves in grave danger. If you had been discovered, the consequences could have been dire."

Penny and Louise exchanged a glance. They hadn't really thought of it that way, only as an exciting adventure.

"However," Jerry went on, a small, approving smile touching his lips, "your courage and keen observation have given us a breakthrough, something we've been searching for for weeks. So, thank you. But for now, your part in this is over. You must remain at the hotel. Do not go near that boathouse again. Do not talk about this to anyone, not even your father, Penny. Not a word, not a hint."

"But Mr. Livingston," Penny protested, "we could help! We know the area. We could watch from a distance."

"No, Penny," Jerry said firmly. "That's precisely what you will not do. This is no longer a game. Professionals will handle it from here. Your job is to act completely normal, enjoy your vacation, and pretend none of this ever happened. Can you do that?"

Penny looked disappointed, but Louise, ever the more sensible one, nodded. "Yes, Mr. Livingston. We understand."

"Good," Jerry said, his eyes scanning the room as if checking for listeners. "I must go. I'll be in touch if there are any developments, but don't expect me to be making social calls. And remember, sugar cookies." He gave them a wink, then slipped out of the reading room as quietly as he had entered.

Penny slumped back in her chair. "Our part is over," she grumbled. "That's not fair. We found it."

"He's right, Penny," Louise said, a bit shaken by Jerry's seriousness. "It was dangerous. And he knows what he's doing. We should do as he says."

"But what if they need more help?" Penny persisted, her adventurous spirit refusing to be quelled. "What if the professionals miss something? What if that Mrs. Deline is involved, and they don't even know it?"

Louise sighed. "Penny, don't start with Mrs. Deline again. She was burying fish bones."

"Or so she said," Penny retorted, a familiar glint in her eye. "But her behavior was odd. And she lives here, she sees everything. What if she's a lookout? Or helping them in some way?"

"You just want an excuse to keep investigating," Louise accused gently.

"Maybe I do," Penny admitted. "But think about it, Lou. She was watching us with that spyglass. She knew we were digging. She seemed to know a lot more than she let on. And what about that whole story about Jim McCoy and the lighthouse? What if that was just a ruse?"

The thought lingered in the air between them. Despite Jerry's stern instructions, Penny's imagination was already spinning new theories, connecting threads that might or might not exist. The quiet agreement to stay out of it felt fragile, like a thin sheet of ice over a deep, turbulent current.

The next few days at the Crystal Inn passed slowly for Penny. She tried to follow Jerry's instructions, taking long walks on the beach with Louise, playing checkers in the lounge, and even attempting to read a book. But her mind was constantly wandering back to the boathouse, to the hooded figure, and to the mysterious broadcasts. Every unfamiliar car that drove past the hotel, every hushed conversation she overheard, seemed to hold a secret meaning.

Louise, on the other hand, seemed content to relax, enjoying the sunshine and the simple pleasures of their vacation. She tried to distract Penny, suggesting shell-collecting expeditions and trips to the local ice cream parlor, but Penny’s heart wasn’t in it.

"Don't you wonder what Jerry is doing?" Penny asked one afternoon, as they sat on the veranda, watching the waves roll in. "Don't you want to know if they caught the Voice from the Cave?"

"Of course I do," Louise replied, turning a page in her magazine. "But Jerry told us to stay put, and I think we should. He's probably very busy, and we'd only get in the way."

"But what if we could help without getting in the way?" Penny mused, leaning forward. "What if we could just observe? See if there's any unusual activity around the boathouse, from a distance? We wouldn't go near it, just check if anything has changed."

"Penny, no," Louise said, putting down her magazine. "That's exactly what he told us not to do. He said it was dangerous."

"But it's been three days, Lou," Penny argued. "Three days since we told him. What if they haven't found anything? What if the man moved? We were the ones who found him in the first place."

Louise looked torn. She knew Penny's stubborn streak, and a part of her was also curious. The mystery hadn't entirely faded for her either.

"We could just take a walk down that way," Penny pressed, seeing Louise waver. "Just a long walk, down the beach, past the general area. We wouldn't even look directly at the boathouse. We could just see if there are any new cars, or if anyone looks suspicious."

"And if we saw Mrs. Deline there?" Louise asked, a hint of a smile playing on her lips.

"Then we'd know for sure she's a spy!" Penny declared, unable to resist the familiar thrill of her own theories.

Reluctantly, Louise agreed. "Alright, but we keep our distance. And if we see anything that looks like trouble, we turn right around. No heroics, Penny."

"Agreed," Penny promised, already scrambling to her feet. "Let's go. We'll pretend we're looking for rare seashells."

They set off, walking along the damp sand at the water's edge. The afternoon sun was warm, and the beach was mostly deserted. As they walked further from the hotel, the landscape grew more rugged, with scattered driftwood and larger rocks appearing. Penny kept scanning the horizon, looking for any sign of activity.

"There's the rocky outcrop," Louise pointed out, when they had walked for what seemed like a very long time. "The boathouse should be just around that bend."

Penny slowed her pace, her heart beginning to beat a little faster. She tried to appear casual, but her eyes were darting everywhere. There was no sign of any Army vehicles, no obvious stakeout, nothing to suggest that Jerry's team had been there.

As they rounded the bend, the small cove came into view. The boathouse, a weathered wooden structure, stood exactly as they remembered it, nestled among the scrub brush. The rickety dock stretched out into the calm water. Everything seemed still, almost eerily so.

"It looks... empty," Louise whispered, a note of disappointment in her voice.

"Too empty," Penny corrected, her gaze fixed on the boathouse. "Something's not right. It's too quiet. Even the gulls aren't around."

They continued to approach, slowly, until they were close enough to see the boathouse clearly without getting too near. There were no tire tracks on the sand leading up to it now, none of the fresh ones they had seen before. The windows were dark, and the door was firmly shut.

"Do you think they already raided it?" Louise wondered. "And the man is gone?"

"Or maybe," Penny said, her voice dropping, "he moved his operation. This place looks abandoned now. It almost looks... cleaned up."

As she spoke, Penny noticed something glinting in the sand near the edge of the boathouse. It was small, metallic, half-buried. Curiosity overriding caution, she started to move a little closer, her eyes fixed on the object.

"Penny, don't," Louise warned, grabbing her arm. "Remember what Jerry said."

But Penny was already pulling away, her eyes wide. "Wait, Lou. Look at this." She bent down quickly, brushing sand away from the object. It was a small, ornate button, made of some dull metal, with a peculiar symbol etched into its surface. It looked old, almost antique, and very out of place.

"What is it?" Louise asked, peering over Penny's shoulder.

"I don't know," Penny murmured, holding it up. "It's not a regular button. And it looks like it's from some kind of uniform, but not an American one I've ever seen." She ran her thumb over the symbol, a stylized bird with outstretched wings. "It feels... foreign."

Suddenly, a voice boomed from behind them. "What are you girls doing here?"

Penny and Louise jumped, startled, dropping the button back into the sand. They spun around to see Mrs. Deline standing a few yards away, her face stern, a large canvas bag clutched in her hand. She wore her usual white suit, which seemed to glow in the afternoon sun.

"Mrs. Deline!" Penny exclaimed, trying to appear innocent. "We were just taking a walk. Looking for seashells."

"Seashells in this desolate spot?" Mrs. Deline's eyes narrowed. "I find that hard to believe. And what were you just picking up from the sand, may I ask?"

Penny felt a chill. Had Mrs. Deline seen the button? Had she been watching them again?

"Nothing, Mrs. Deline," Louise said quickly, trying to diffuse the situation. "Just a piece of driftwood."

Mrs. Deline did not look convinced. She glanced from the girls to the boathouse, and then back again, her gaze lingering suspiciously. A subtle, almost imperceptible flicker crossed her face, a look that Penny couldn't quite decipher.

"This is not a safe place for young girls to be wandering," Mrs. Deline stated coldly. "Especially not near that old boathouse. There are dangerous tides here. I suggest you return to the hotel immediately."

She said it with an air of authority that made Penny's rebellious spirit bristle. "We're perfectly safe, thank you," Penny retorted, trying to sound brave.

Mrs. Deline merely gave them a look that implied she knew much more than she was letting on. Without another word, she turned and continued walking along the beach in the opposite direction, towards a more remote part of the coastline.

"Well, that was strange," Louise whispered, watching Mrs. Deline's retreating figure. "She seemed really angry."

"Or worried," Penny corrected, her mind racing. She bent down quickly and snatched the button from the sand, tucking it into her pocket. "Why is she always popping up where she's least expected? And why is she always telling us to go away?"

"Maybe she's just a cranky old woman who doesn't like children," Louise suggested, though even she sounded less convinced.

"Or maybe," Penny said, her voice filled with renewed suspicion, "she has something to hide. And she doesn't want us poking around this boathouse because she knows what's really going on here. This button, Lou. It's a clue. And Mrs. Deline saw us find it. I bet she knows exactly what it is."

The sun began to dip lower, casting long shadows across the beach. The air grew cooler, and a sense of unease settled over them. They knew they should go back to the hotel, as Mrs. Deline had commanded. But Penny held the strange button in her pocket, a small, metallic piece of evidence that seemed to connect all the disparate elements of the mystery: the Voice from the Cave, the boathouse, and now, perhaps, Mrs. Deline herself. The adventure, it seemed, was far from over.

The sun had indeed slipped beneath the horizon by the time Penny and Louise reached the Crystal Inn. A cool breeze, carrying the scent of salt and damp sand, rustled through the palm trees lining the drive. Though they had walked quickly, Penny’s mind was not on her tired feet but on the small, smooth object nestled securely in her pocket. The button. It felt heavier than it looked, a tiny, silent witness to the strange events of the afternoon.

“That was quite an adventure,” Louise remarked, pushing open the heavy glass door of the hotel lobby. She sighed, her shoulders slumping slightly. “And to think we almost got caught by Mrs. Deline. She looked as though she could bite through nails.”

Penny merely grunted in response. Her thoughts were still revolving around Mrs. Deline’s sudden appearance and her stern warning. It was almost as if the woman had been waiting for them, or perhaps, following them. “She didn’t just look angry, Lou, she looked scared. Or, rather, she looked like someone who was trying very hard not to look scared.”

“Why would she be scared?” Louise asked, picking up a hotel brochure from a polished mahogany table. “She’s the one who was telling us what to do.”

“Exactly,” Penny said, pulling the button from her pocket and examining it under the dim lobby light. It was dark, almost black, and the stylized bird with outstretched wings seemed to gleam faintly. “Why did she want us away from that boathouse so badly? And why did she look so disconcert

ed.”

“Disconcerted,” Louise repeated, slowly lowering the brochure. “That’s a rather large word for a simple case of Mrs. Deline being a grump, Penny.”

“It’s not just grumpiness, Lou. Don’t you see? First, she buries what she pretends are fish bones, though I still maintain that was a ruse. Then she appears at the inn with a spyglass, asking if we found what we were searching for, as if to taunt us. And now, she shows up at the boathouse, trying to shoo us away with that same cold, knowing look. She’s too involved, Lou. Too many coincidences.” Penny held the button closer to her eyes, tracing the outline of the bird. “This isn’t just any button, either. Look at this bird. It’s an eagle, I think, but a very particular sort of eagle. Not American. And the metal feels heavy, expensive.”

Louise peered at it, her brows furrowed. “It’s a button, Penny. Perhaps it simply fell off someone’s jacket. The boathouse is old, I imagine people have been in and out of it for years.”

“But it was fresh in the sand, right where Mrs. Deline had been standing,” Penny countered, her voice filled with conviction. “And it wasn’t muddy or corroded from the sea air. It looked as though it had only just fallen.” She snapped the button back into her pocket with a decisive click. “We need to get to our room, Lou. We can’t discuss this properly here in the lobby.”

The girls ascended the wide staircase to their room on the second floor, their footsteps echoing softly on the carpeted treads. Once inside, Penny locked the door with a swift turn of the key, then pulled the button out again, placing it on the small mahogany writing desk. The overhead light, though dim, still cast a faint gleam upon the dark metal.

“Look at it closely, Lou,” Penny urged, pushing a stray strand of hair from her eyes. “Doesn’t it seem… foreign? The way it’s crafted, the detail of the bird. It’s not like anything I’ve ever seen in Dad’s uniform buttons, or anyone else’s for that matter.”

Louise leaned over, her curiosity piqued despite her earlier skepticism. “It does have a certain… fierceness to it,” she admitted, running a finger lightly over the raised surface. “Almost like a crest. But what could it mean? And why would Mrs. Deline be involved with something so… official-looking, if it is official?”

An unknown station, enemy owned and operated, broadcasting propaganda. And the FBI knows the man only as B4, a code number. They suspect persons connected with the station may be aiding German prisoners to escape.”

“Yes, from Canada, he said,” Louise interjected, nodding.

“Exactly! Now, what if Mrs. Deline isn’t just some cranky old widow? What if she’s involved? What if she’s helping B4, or even *is* B4?” Penny’s eyes widened with the daring of her own theory. “She’s always here on Sunset Beach, watching. She has a spyglass. She’s cold, secretive. And she was hiding something in the sand, then later tried to scare us away from the boathouse. The boathouse, Lou! Where this button was found! What if that boathouse is where the ‘Voice from the Cave’ broadcasts from?”

Louise sank onto the edge of one of the beds, pressing a hand to her forehead. “Penny, you’re letting your imagination run wild again. A boathouse, a spy, a secret radio station. It sounds like something out of one of your adventure books.”

“But why not?” Penny retorted, gesturing emphatically with her hands. “Sunset Beach is isolated, jagged coastline, a likely spot for surprise night landings by the Enemy, Dad said so himself. And the Coast Guards watch this place closely. It all fits, Lou! Mrs. Deline could be a contact, or a courier, or even the mastermind behind it all.”

“But this button,” Louise picked it up again, turning it over. “It has no markings, no numbers, nothing to suggest a military uniform, let alone an enemy one. It’s just… a button with a bird.”

Penny snatched it back. “But it’s a specific bird, Lou. Not a common one. And it’s a clue, I feel it. This button is a key to understanding what Mrs. Deline is truly doing here. We need to find out more about it.” She held the button up to the light, her eyes narrowed in thought. “Maybe there’s something on it we’re missing. Something very small.”

She rummaged through her travel bag, pulling out a small magnifying glass she sometimes used for examining insects or interesting rocks. Holding the button carefully, she peered through the lens. The stylized eagle suddenly grew larger, its feathers more distinct, its wings poised as if in flight. And then, she saw it. Etched almost imperceptibly into the very bottom curve of the bird’s chest, so small it was practically invisible to the naked eye, were three tiny letters: “R.V.K.”

“Lou!” Penny exclaimed, her voice a sharp whisper of triumph. “I found something! Look!”

Louise quickly came to her side, leaning in to look through the magnifying glass. “R.V.K.?” she murmured, sounding puzzled. “What does that mean?”

“I don’t know,” Penny admitted, her initial excitement tempered by the new puzzle. “But it’s something! It’s not just a random button. These initials must stand for something, or someone. Maybe it’s a code, or a person’s name, or a specific unit. This confirms it, Lou. This button is important.”

“It still doesn’t prove Mrs. Deline is a spy,” Louise pointed out, though her tone held less conviction than before. “It just means she was near where an important button was lost.”

“But she tried to shoo us away! She saw us poking around. She knows this button is important, I’m sure of it. Or perhaps it’s her button, or belongs to someone she’s in league with.” Penny put the magnifying glass down, a determined glint in her eyes. “We need to go back to that boathouse tonight.”

Louise gasped. “Tonight? Penny, are you serious? It will be dark. And Mrs. Deline told us to stay away.”

“All the more reason to go,” Penny declared. “If she’s trying to keep us away, there must be something she doesn’t want us to see. And if this boathouse is the secret radio station, then it will be active at night, when broadcasts are harder to trace. Jerry said the ‘Voice from the Cave’ is an elusive fellow. He likely broadcasts under the cover of darkness.”

“But what if she sees us?” Louise worried, wringing her hands. “What if she’s waiting for us?”

“Then we’ll be extra careful,” Penny assured her, though a shiver of apprehension ran down her own spine. “We’ll wait until everyone is asleep, then slip out. We can take our flashlights. We’ll just observe, Lou. Just see if there’s any activity around the boathouse. If we see anything suspicious, we’ll come right back.”

Louise hesitated, then sighed. “You’ll never let this go, will you?”

“Not until I get to the bottom of it,” Penny affirmed, her chin set in a stubborn line. “This is bigger than just a hidden package or a cranky old woman, Lou. This could be enemy activity, right here on Sunset Beach! We have to do something.”

Reluctantly, Louise nodded. “All right. But if we get caught, you’re doing all the explaining.”

“Deal,” Penny agreed, a triumphant smile gracing her lips. “Now, let’s get some rest. We have a long night ahead of us.”

The hours dragged by slowly. Penny found it impossible to sleep, her mind buzzing with possibilities and theories. She lay in bed, listening to the gentle lapping of waves against the shore and the distant calls of night birds. Louise, ever the pragmatist, eventually drifted off to sleep, her soft, even breathing filling the room. Finally, the old grandfather clock in the lobby below chimed two o’clock. Penny rose silently, careful not to wake Louise. She dressed quickly in dark clothes, pulled on sturdy shoes, and then gently roused her friend.

“Time to go,” she whispered, shaking Louise’s shoulder.

Louise groaned, rubbing the sleep from her eyes. “Must we?”

“Yes, we must,” Penny insisted, handing her a small flashlight. “Remember, just observing. And quiet as mice.”

They crept out of their room, down the deserted staircase, and through a side exit that led to the beach path, avoiding the main lobby. The night air was cool and damp, carrying the sharp scent of salt and seaweed. A sliver of moon hung in the sky, casting faint, silvery light upon the sand and the restless sea. The path to the boathouse, which had seemed so clear in the daytime, now felt mysterious and a little daunting. Shadows stretched long and distorted from the sparse bushes and rocks.

“Are you sure about this, Penny?” Louise whispered, clutching her flashlight tightly.

“Positive,” Penny replied, trying to sound braver than she felt. She held her own flashlightbeam low, sweeping it back and forth across the path ahead. The boathouse, a dark, squat silhouette against the paler sky, seemed to loom larger with every step. They moved cautiously, their shoes crunching softly on the coarse sand. The sound seemed alarmingly loud in the stillness of the night.

As they drew closer, a faint, rhythmic humming sound became discernible, almost like a low thrumming vibration. It was too soft to be the waves and too consistent to be the wind. Penny exchanged a significant glance with Louise.

“What’s that sound?” Louise whispered, her voice laced with apprehension.

“I don’t know,” Penny admitted, her heart beginning to pound a little faster. “But it’s coming from the boathouse.”

They found cover behind a thick clump of thorny beach roses, their thorny branches providing a natural screen. From their vantage point, they could see the boathouse clearly. A single, dim light flickered from within, casting vague, shifting shadows against the small, grimy windows. The humming sound was definitely emanating from inside.

Penny slowly raised her flashlight, shielding the beam with her hand, and directed it towards the boathouse. The light caught on the weathered wood, revealing its decrepit state. But then, her beam landed on something else. A small, dark rowboat was tied to a rickety pier beside the boathouse. Its oars were neatly stowed, and it looked well-maintained, almost out of place next to the crumbling structure.

“Someone’s here,” Louise breathed, her hand going to Penny’s arm. “Look, the boat.”

Just then, the light inside the boathouse flickered again, then abruptly went out. The humming sound ceased. A moment of absolute silence descended, broken only by the distant murmur of the ocean. Penny and Louise froze, holding their breath, their eyes fixed on the now darkened boathouse.

Then, the boathouse door, which had been slightly ajar, slowly creaked open. A figure emerged, tall and cloaked in shadow, moving with a deliberate, unhurried pace. The figure paused, glancing around, as if making sure no one was near. Penny’s heart leaped into her throat. It was too dark to make out any distinct features, but the silhouette was undeniably that of a woman.

“Mrs. Deline?” Louise whispered, her voice barely audible.

Penny nodded, her gaze glued to the figure. The woman wore what appeared to be a dark coat or cape, which billowed slightly in the faint breeze. She moved towards the rowboat, untying the rope with practiced ease. As she did, the moonlight caught a brief flash of something metallic at her neck, a brooch perhaps, or a pendant. It shimmered for only an instant, then was gone.

The woman stepped into the rowboat, sitting down with a grace that seemed at odds with her generally rigid demeanor. She picked up the oars, and with strong, silent strokes, pushed off from the pier. The boat glided smoothly over the water, heading out towards the open sea, disappearing quickly into the inky blackness beyond the shore.

Penny and Louise remained hidden, watching until the last trace of the boat had vanished. The quiet hum of the night returned, now utterly devoid of any mechanical sound.

“She’s gone,” Penny finally whispered, letting out a long, shaky breath. “She was definitely in there. And she just rowed out to sea.”

“But why?” Louise asked, bewildered. “And what was that humming sound? It sounded like a machine.”

“A radio transmitter, Lou,” Penny asserted, her voice filled with a renewed sense of purpose. “I’m sure of it. That’s why the Coast Guards watch this place so closely. And that’s why Mrs. Deline wants us away from here.” She paused, a new thought sparking in her mind. “And the boat. She didn’t just leave it there. She took it out to sea. Perhaps to meet someone, or to receive something. Or to make a broadcast from the water itself, to make it even harder to trace.”

“But the boat looked so old and small,” Louise objected. “Surely a spy would have something more sophisticated.”

“Sometimes the simplest things are the most effective, Lou,” Penny countered. “Who would suspect an old rowboat? It’s perfect cover.” She reached into her pocket, pulling out the button. “And this. This button has to be hers, or someone she’s working with. R.V.K. What could it mean?”

“We should go back,” Louise urged, shivering slightly, whether from the cold or fear, Penny could not tell. “We’ve seen enough. We need to tell Jerry Livingston.”

“Not yet,” Penny said, her eyes still fixed on the dark expanse of the ocean where Mrs. Deline had vanished. “We need more proof, Lou. We can’t just go to Jerry with a button and a story about a woman rowing out to sea. He’ll think we’re making it all up again, like with the fish bones.”

“But what more can we do?”

“We go into the boathouse,” Penny decided, her voice firm. “Now that she’s gone, it’s our chance. We have to find out what’s inside.”

Louise’s eyes widened in alarm. “Go inside? Penny, that’s too dangerous! What if she comes back? What if there’s someone else there?”

“We’ll be quick,” Penny promised. “We’ll just look for anything that can link Mrs. Deline to the ‘Voice from the Cave.’ Anything that explains this button. And we’ll be out before anyone knows we were even there.”

Against her better judgment, Louise agreed. The allure of the mystery, despite the danger, was proving too strong for even her cautious nature. They approached the boathouse again, their movements even more stealthy than before. The door was still slightly ajar, a dark invitation into the unknown. Penny pushed it open slowly, the hinges groaning softly in the silence. The interior was shrouded in an oppressive darkness, smelling faintly of salt, mildew, and something metallic.

Penny switched on her flashlight, directing its beam into the gloom. The interior was larger than it appeared from the outside. Dusty fishing nets hung from the rafters, and various pieces of old, rusty nautical equipment lay scattered about. In the center, however, was a clear space, where a small wooden table stood. And on the table, still slightly warm to the touch, was a radio set. Not an ordinary household radio, but a complex array of dials, wires, and a large, oddly shaped antenna. It was exactly the kind of equipment Jerry Livingston had described.

“Lou, look!” Penny whispered, her voice trembling with excitement and a touch of fear. “It’s a shortwave radio! And look at that antenna! It’s just like he described, probably rotating, or capable of it. This is it, Lou! This is the ‘Voice from the Cave’s’ hideout!”

Louise stared, her mouth slightly agape. All her skepticism had vanished, replaced by a dawning comprehension and a sense of awe. “You were right, Penny! All along!”

Penny’s beam swept across the table. Beside the radio, there was a small notebook, its pages filled with what appeared to be rows of numbers and letters, written in a precise, almost elegant hand. A codebook. And next to it, a small, half-empty teacup. The liquid inside was still slightly warm. Mrs. Deline had been here very recently.

Her flashlight beam moved further, catching on a small, dark object tucked beneath the table. Penny reached down and picked it up. It was a dark, leather-bound portfolio, surprisingly thin and light. Her fingers fumbled with the clasp.

“What is it?” Louise asked, her voice hushed.

Penny opened it. Inside, nestled securely in a velvet lining, were several more buttons, identical to the one in her pocket, each with the stylized eagle and the tiny R.V.K. initials. But there was also something else. A small, folded piece of parchment, brittle with age. Penny carefully unfolded it. It was a faded map, crudely drawn, of the coastline around Sunset Beach. Several spots were marked with small, circled Xs. And one of the Xs was right where the boathouse stood. Another X was marked further down the coast, near a cluster of jagged rocks.

“These are more of the buttons, Lou,” Penny said, her voice barely a whisper. “And a map. She’s marking places. Places for what?”

Just then, a sudden, sharp creak from the boathouse door behind them made both girls jump. They froze, their hearts pounding in their chests. Had Mrs. Deline returned? Or had someone else been waiting? Penny’s flashlight beam, shaking slightly in her hand, swung around towards the door. The beam caught the outline of a figure, not as tall or graceful as Mrs. Deline, but broad and heavily built, silhouetted against the faint moonlight. A man.

“Well, well,” a deep, gravelly voice rumbled, echoing ominously in the small space. “What have we here? Little snooping girls, are we?”

The man stepped fully into the boathouse, and the beam of Penny’s flashlight revealed a hulking figure, with a weather-beaten face and cold, piercing eyes. He wore a heavy fisherman’s sweater, and in his hand, he held a long, gnarled piece of driftwood, clutched like a weapon. Penny recognized him instantly from their earlier encounter on the beach. It was old Jake Skagway.

“Jake Skagway!” Penny exclaimed, her voice a mixture of surprise and alarm.

Jake Skagway’s lips curled into a sneer. “So, you remember old Jake, do you? Poking around where you don’t belong, just like I warned you. And now you’ve found Mrs. Deline’s little secrets, haven’t you?” He took a step closer, the driftwood raised slightly. “Too bad you won’t be telling anyone about them.”

Louise gasped, fear flashing in her eyes. “What do you want?”

“What I want,” Skagway said, his voice dropping to a menacing growl, “is for you to forget everything you’ve seen tonight. For you to forget the radio. For you to forget the old woman. And most of all, for you to forget about those pretty little buttons.” His eyes darted to the portfolio Penny still clutched. “Give me that. And then you’ll both take a nice little swim out to sea. The current’s strong tonight.”

Penny clutched the portfolio tighter, her mind racing. Jake Skagway was a spy! Or at least, he was working with them. And he intended to silence them permanently. This was far more dangerous than anything Penny had imagined in her wildest theories. They were in real trouble. There was no escape through the door, as Skagway blocked their path. The only other exit was a small, high window overlooking the water, too small to climb through.

“We haven’t told anyone anything,” Penny tried, her voice wavering slightly. “We just stumbled in here.”

“Stumbled, did you?” Skagway chuckled, a chilling sound. “Little girls don’t just stumble into secret radio stations in the middle of the night. Give me the bag, and maybe I’ll reconsider your swim. But no promises.”

He took another step, the driftwood glinting in the faint light. Penny knew they had only one chance. She tightened her grip on the portfolio and, with a sudden, desperate surge of adrenaline, flung it directly at Jake Skagway’s face.

The heavy leather-bound folder caught him squarely on the nose. With a surprised bellow, Skagway stumbled backward, momentarily dropping the driftwood and clapping a hand to his face.

“Run, Lou!” Penny screamed, seizing Louise’s arm.

They darted past the stunned Skagway, pushing the boathouse door wide open and bursting out into the cool night air. The sand was rough beneath their feet as they scrambled away from the boathouse, not looking back. Behind them, they heard Jake Skagway’s furious roars and the sound of his heavy footsteps pounding after them. They ran as fast as they could, their lungs burning, their hearts hammering against their ribs. The path back to the Crystal Inn seemed miles away.

“He’s gaining on us!” Louise panted, glancing over her shoulder. Skagway, despite his bulk, was surprisingly fast. He was closing the distance.

Penny’s mind worked frantically. They couldn’t outrun him all the way back to the hotel. They needed a diversion. Her eyes darted towards the cluster of jagged rocks marked on the faded map, the second X. It was a dangerous area, but perhaps it offered a place to hide, or to gain a crucial advantage.

“The rocks, Lou!” Penny yelled, pulling her friend towards the treacherous, uneven terrain. “Over the rocks!”

They scrambled over the sharp, slippery stones, their movements hindered by the darkness and the uneven surface. Behind them, Jake Skagway cursed loudly as he struggled to follow, his heavier frame less agile on the difficult ground. The sound of his pursuit spurred them on, pushing them to ignore the scrapes and bruises.

They reached a small, sheltered cove, hidden from the main beach by a large, overhanging rock formation. The waves crashed against the shore with a powerful roar, the sound amplified by the enclosed space. It was a dead end, but also a potential hiding place.

“In here!” Penny urged, pulling Louise into a narrow crevice between two immense boulders. It was just wide enough for them to squeeze into, completely hidden from view. They held their breath, listening intently.

They heard Skagway’s heavy breathing as he drew nearer, his footsteps now crunching on the rocks directly outside their hiding spot. “You can’t hide from old Jake forever!” he bellowed, his voice filled with fury. “I know these rocks like the back of my hand! Come out, you little snoopers, and I promise to make it quick!”

He began to search, his heavy boots echoing on the stones, the beam of his flashlight sweeping wildly across the cove. Penny and Louise pressed themselves deeper into the crevice, hardly daring to breathe. The cold, damp rock scraped against their backs, but they ignored the discomfort.

Suddenly, Jake Skagway let out a frustrated grunt. “Blast it all! Where did those two little nuisances go?” His light passed perilously close to their hiding place, just grazing the edge of their rock, but he did not see them. He continued to search for several more agonizing minutes, his curses growing louder, more desperate. Then, to their immense relief, his footsteps began to recede. He was moving back towards the main beach, perhaps thinking they had managed to circle back.

They waited, motionless, for what felt like an eternity, until the last sound of his footsteps had faded. Only then did Penny slowly, cautiously, peer out of their hiding place. The cove was empty. Jake Skagway was gone.

“He’s gone,” Penny whispered, relief washing over her in a tremendous wave. “We’re safe, for now.”

Louise emerged, her face pale, her hair disheveled. “Safe? Penny, he tried to drown us! He knew about the buttons! And the radio! What are we going to do?”

“We’re going straight back to the hotel,” Penny declared, her voice trembling slightly now that the immediate danger had passed. “And this time, we *are* telling Jerry Livingston everything. About Mrs. Deline, about Jake Skagway, about the boathouse, and about the radio.” She still clutched the precious portfolio, its weight now a comforting presence in her hand. “And we have proof, Lou. Real, tangible proof.”

As they made their way back along the treacherous shoreline, carefully navigating the rocks in the pale moonlight, Penny’s mind was already racing ahead. The mystery was no longer a game or a theory. It was real, and it was dangerous. But they had uncovered a vital part of it. The “Voice from the Cave” was operating right here on Sunset Beach, and Mrs. Deline and old Jake Skagway were deeply involved. They had narrowly escaped a terrible fate, but they had also come away with crucial information.

The Crystal Inn, when they finally reached it, seemed a beacon of safety and warmth. They slipped back inside the same side door, heartbeats still thrumming from their narrow escape. The lobby was still deserted, silent except for the tick-tock of the grandfather clock. They ascended the stairs to their room, locking the door securely behind them.

“We should try to reach Jerry immediately,” Louise urged, her voice still shaky. “He needs to know.”

“Yes,” Penny agreed, pulling out the portfolio and placing it on the desk. “But first, we should examine this map again, Lou. The one with the Xs. If Jake Skagway was involved, and Mrs. Deline was rowing out to sea, these other marked spots must be important too. Perhaps they are rendezvous points, or other caches of equipment. Jerry will need all the information we can give him.”

They spread the brittle map out carefully under the lamp. The light revealed another detail Penny had missed in their hurried escape. Beside the X near the jagged rocks where they had just hidden, there was a tiny, faded symbol, almost like a bird’s claw, etched into the parchment. And near the boathouse X, a similar, though slightly different, symbol. The map was more than just locations; it was a cryptic message in itself.

“Look, Lou,” Penny pointed with a trembling finger. “These symbols. They’re like different bird marks. Could they be more code?”

Louise peered closely. “They look like different ways of drawing parts of an eagle, almost. A wing here, a talon there.”

“R.V.K.,” Penny murmured, looking from the map to the button. “A bird’s claw. An eagle. A spy operating from a boathouse. German prisoners. It’s all coming together, Lou. We’ve stumbled onto something much bigger than we ever imagined.”

They decided to wait until morning to contact Jerry Livingston. Their encounter with Jake Skagway had left them shaken, and they needed to organize their thoughts, and the evidence, before presenting it. Sleep, however, seemed an impossible luxury now. The image of Jake Skagway’s menacing face, the sound of his furious roars, and the cold, dark water of the cove remained vivid in their minds. But amidst the fear, there was also a powerful surge of exhilaration. They had faced danger, and they had survived. And they had uncovered a secret that could be of vital importance to the safety of their country. The adventure, Penny realized, was indeed far from over. In fact, it had only just begun.
