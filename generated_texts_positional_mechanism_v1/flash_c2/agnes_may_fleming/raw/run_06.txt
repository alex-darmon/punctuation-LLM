How can I ask her to share a name that is so profoundly disgraced, a life that is so utterly tainted? She deserves better than a man who discovers his entire existence is a sham, a man whose lineage is a bloody stain upon history."

"You must do what is right, Victor," Inez said, her voice gentle yet firm, piercing through his despair. "You must reveal the truth. You must disavow the title, the estates, everything that came to you through Sir Reginald’s monstrous deception. It will be difficult, profoundly so, but it is the only path to honour, the only way to cleanse your conscience and to finally bring peace to the souls of Lady Catherine and Arthur Ainsworth. "Redeemed?" Victor scoffed, a bitter laugh escaping his lips. "How can it be redeemed when its very foundation is built on such unspeakable evil? To disavow the title, you say? To relinquish the estates? To declare myself a fraud? What would become of me? What would become of Lady Helena, who has lived here her entire life? What would become of the servants, of the tenants? To right a wrong that happened nearly a century ago, to a man who is now dead and has no heirs?"

"For justice, Victor," Inez insisted, her voice gaining strength, "and for honour. For the sake of your own soul, and the soul of your mother, who died for this truth. The estates, the lands, they belong to the Catheron family, yes, but not to the line that usurped them. There are distant relations, from the true Catheron branch, who could perhaps be identified, rightful claimants to the ancient lands, if not the direct title, should you choose to relinquish it entirely. But your personal honour, Victor, that is what is at stake. Your integrity, your standing as a man. Can you truly live a life of privilege, knowing it is built upon murder and fraud? Can you marry Edith, knowing the very name you offer her is a lie?"

Lady Helena, who had been listening with rapt attention, now spoke, her voice still trembling but resolute. "Inez is right, Victor. I have lived with this burden, this terrible secret, for too long. The weight of it has crushed my spirit, withered my joy. To live a lie is a slow, agonizing death. Whatever the cost, however terrible the consequences, the truth must prevail. I would rather face poverty and disgrace with a clear conscience than continue to live in this house, haunted by the ghosts of what my father did. We must face it, Victor. Together. And as for Edith," she added, her gaze softening, "if she is truly worthy of you, she will understand. She will stand by you, not for the title or the wealth, but for the man you are, for your courage in facing such a dreadful truth."

Victor looked at his aunt, then at Inez, a profound and terrible struggle unfolding within him. The enormity of the task, the sheer magnitude of the public scandal, the social ruin, the financial catastrophe, all of it threatened to overwhelm him. Yet, beneath the turmoil, a flicker of his mother’s spirit, of his own innate sense of justice, began to assert itself. He thought of Edith, her bright eyes, her clear moral compass, her disdain for superficiality. Could he truly face her, knowing he was upholding such a deceit? Could he look at himself in the mirror, knowing he was perpetuating a murderer’s legacy?

"To-morrow," he said, his voice now firm, though still strained. "You said I would know the truth to-morrow, Inez. And now I do. But the consequences... they are vast. Give me this day. Give me a night to reconcile myself to this, to consider the path forward. I need to think. I need to absorb this, to grapple with this horror. I need to make a plan. A plan for revelation, for restitution, for... for what comes next. But I promise you this," he concluded, looking first at Inez, then at his aunt, his blue eyes now burning with a cold, hard determination, "the truth shall be known. Sir Reginald’s monstrous deception shall not stand. And my mother’s murderer, though long dead, shall be exposed for the villain he truly was."

Inez nodded, a faint, almost imperceptible softening in her veiled features. "That is all I ask, Victor. To know that justice will finally be served. And for your courage, for your willingness to face this truth, you have my deepest respect. We will speak again in the morning, when the immediate shock has, perhaps, somewhat lessened. There are practical matters to discuss, steps to be taken, and further details I can provide to aid you."

Lady Helena, though still pale and shaken, managed a weak but grateful smile at her nephew. The crushing weight of years of silence and guilt, though not entirely lifted, had at least begun to shift, allowing a faint glimmer of hope for redemption to penetrate her long-darkened spirit. "Thank you, Victor," she whispered. "Thank you, my dear boy. Forgive me my weakness. I will be here, ready to support you in whatever way I can."

Victor rose, the leather wallet clutched tightly in his hand, its contents now seared into his mind. He bowed stiffly to Inez, a gesture of respect born of their shared, terrible knowledge, then turned and walked towards the door, his steps heavy with the burden of his newfound reality. His world had not merely shifted; it had imploded, and from its ashes, he now faced the daunting task of rebuilding, not a grand estate, but an honest life. He knew, with an awful certainty, that nothing would ever be the same again. His conversation with Edith, which he had thought would be difficult due to Lady Helena’s objections, now loomed as an insurmountable precipice, a discussion that would define the very future of his existence. He had vowed to not give up Edith for ten thousand family secrets; but this was not merely a secret, it was the obliteration of his entire identity, the very ground upon which he stood. He was no longer Sir Victor Catheron, but a man stripped bare, standing at the precipice of an unknown and perilous future.

CHAPTER XIII. THE NIGHT OF RECKONING.

As he descended the main staircase, the grand hall, once a symbol of his heritage and stability, now seemed to mock him with its false grandeur. The portraits of his ancestors, particularly that of Sir Reginald, seemed to sneer from their canvases, their eyes holding dark, triumphant secrets. He felt a profound alienation, a sense of being a stranger in his own home. He passed Nixon, the butler, who gave a respectful bow, but Victor merely offered a distracted nod. His mind was elsewhere, grappling with the cataclysmic revelations. He needed solitude, a place where he could think, where he could breathe without the oppressive weight of Catheron deceit.

He found himself, almost instinctively, in the library, a room he had always associated with peace and intellectual pursuit. But even here, surrounded by the wisdom of ages, he found no solace. He sank into a deep leather armchair, the leather wallet still in his hand, its presence a physical manifestation of his shattering reality. He stared blankly at the roaring fire, its flames dancing, reflecting the inferno raging within his soul. Sir Reginald, a murderer. His mother, a victim. Lady Helena, a silenced witness, protecting him. And he, Victor, a fraud. The words echoed in his mind, relentless, unforgiving.

He thought of Edith. Her honesty, her directness, her scorn for pretension. How could he possibly tell her this? How could he expect her to understand, let alone accept, a man whose entire life was a lie? His earlier declaration, "I won't give up Edith, for ten thousand family secrets," now seemed naïve, almost childish. This was not merely a secret; this was the complete dismantling of his world. What did he have to offer her now? A disgraced name? A future of uncertainty and public scorn? He, who had always been so certain of his path, so assured in his position, now stood on shifting sands, gazing into an abyss.

A knock at the door, soft but insistent, startled him from his dark reverie. "Sir Victor? Are you in there?" It was Nixon, his voice laced with polite concern. "Miss Darrell has been asking for you, sir. She wishes to know if the excursion to Eastlake Abbey will be entirely cancelled, or merely postponed until tomorrow. And she seemed, if you will forgive my saying so, sir, a trifle worried."

Victor sighed, a deep, weary sound. Edith. He had avoided her, needing time to process the devastating news. But he could not avoid her indefinitely. He had promised to speak with her. He ran a hand through his dishevelled hair. "Tell Miss Darrell I will join her shortly, Nixon. And yes, the excursion is entirely cancelled, for the foreseeable future. My apologies for the inconvenience."

"Very good, Sir Victor," Nixon replied, and Victor heard his retreating footsteps. He stood up, feeling a strange mixture of dread and resolve. He had to tell her, or at least some part of it. He could not, would not, hide this from her. But how much? How to begin to explain such a labyrinthine tale of murder and deceit? He needed clarity, a way to present this cataclysm to the woman he loved, without utterly destroying her faith in him, or in the possibility of their future. He left the library, the heavy oak door closing behind him with a somber thud, as if sealing away the last vestiges of his former life.

He found Edith in the small drawing-room, a cozy chamber with chintz armchairs and a perpetually burning fire, where she often retired with a book. She looked up as he entered, her brown eyes, usually so bright and full of playful mischief, now clouded with a gentle concern. She was standing by the window, gazing out at the twilight descending over the gardens, a book lying forgotten in her hand. "Victor," she said, her voice soft. "I was beginning to worry. Is everything quite well? Lady Helena seemed rather distressed earlier, and you… you look as if you have seen a ghost."

He forced a smile, a grimace that felt alien on his lips. "Edith, my darling. Come, sit down. I have something of great importance to tell you, something that cannot wait. But I must ask for your patience, and your understanding, for it is a tale of a most confounding and terrible nature." He led her to a sofa by the fire, sitting beside her, taking her hands in his. Her touch was warm, reassuring, a fragile anchor in his suddenly turbulent world.

"You look so grave, Victor," she said, her brow furrowing with genuine apprehension. "What can it be? Is it about Lady Helena? Has she finally... finally decided to be utterly impossible regarding our marriage?" A faint, ironic smile touched her lips, but it quickly faded as she saw the profound sorrow etched on his face.

"It is, and it is not, about Lady Helena, my dearest," he began, struggling to find the right words, to navigate the treacherous waters of his newfound reality. "And it is, in a way, about our marriage. But not as you might imagine. This afternoon, Edith, my entire world has been turned upside down. The very foundations upon which I believed my life was built have been revealed to be nothing but illusion, a monstrous deception spanning generations." He looked into her eyes, pleading for her to believe him, to endure the coming storm. "I have discovered a truth so shocking, so utterly horrifying, that it questions not only my right to the title I bear, but the very honour of my family name, and indeed, my personal worthiness to ask for your hand."

Edith listened, her usual playful defiance replaced by a quiet solemnity. She did not interrupt, allowing him to gather his thoughts, her gaze unwavering, intelligent, and deeply empathetic. "Tell me, Victor," she said at last, her voice barely above a whisper. "Tell me everything. Do not spare me, for I am stronger than you may think, and my love for you, whatever this truth may be, is not so easily shaken."

Her words, simple yet profound, gave him a fresh surge of courage. He squeezed her hands. "You remember, Edith, how I mentioned my aunt’s distress, her hinting at a terrible secret, a murderer known? And the murderer, the orchestrator of all this, was none other than my own grandfather, Sir Reginald Catheron."

Edith gasped, a small, choked sound, her hands tightening convulsively in his. Her face went pale, her eyes now wide with shock and utter disbelief. "Your grandfather? Victor, what are you saying? That is... that is impossible! Sir Reginald was revered, a pillar of society!"
