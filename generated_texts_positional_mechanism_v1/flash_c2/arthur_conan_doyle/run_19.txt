“A mix-up?” Holmes prompted, his eyes keen.

“Aye. There was this fellow, a Mr. Henry Baker, a quiet sort, regular at the club, a bit down on his luck lately, but always pays his dues. The landlord hesitated, rubbing his chin. “Not precisely, no. He usually just comes in for his pint, sits in the corner, reads his paper. A quiet man, as I said. “So, this Mr. Baker was not the owner of the blue carbuncle, then?” I asked, feeling a slight disappointment that the mystery was not yet fully resolved in this simpler manner.

“Not directly, I venture to say,” Holmes responded, his eyes now distant, focused on some internal calculation. “It appears Mr. Baker was merely an unfortunate recipient, a pawn in a game he knew nothing of. He received a goose which, by chance, harboured the jewel. The question now becomes, whose goose did Mr. Baker take? And more importantly, whose hat was left behind in the scuffle? For if Mr. Baker’s hat was left, then the scuffle involved another individual, another goose, and perhaps, the true culprit.”

Mr. Windigate, still somewhat bewildered by our line of questioning, offered, “There was another fellow, a bit of a rough sort, caused most of the trouble. A loudmouth, always trying to stir things up. I think he was with a smaller, ferret-faced chap. They were arguing over a goose, I believe. Holmes nodded slowly. “A rough sort, you say? And a ferret-faced companion. Did you happen to catch their names, Mr. Windigate?”

“No, not really. They’re not regulars, just came in for the Christmas rush. The big one, he did shout something about ‘catching up with Ryott,’ or ‘Ryder,’ something like that, as he ran out. But names? No, not really. Just a couple of rogues looking for a free meal, or maybe a bit of a fight.”

Holmes’s eyes narrowed slightly. “Ryder, you say? Most interesting. Well, Mr. Windigate, you have been exceedingly helpful. We are much obliged to you.” He placed a half-crown on the counter.

Mr. Baker’s face brightened considerably. “My own goose! Oh, that would be a true blessing, gentlemen. My wife, you see, was quite put out by the state of the bird I brought home. It was perfectly edible, mind you, but not quite the plump specimen she had anticipated.”

Holmes gave him a faint smile. “I daresay, Mr. Baker, your wife will be more than satisfied with the goose awaiting you. We shall leave you to your studies then. And thank you once more for your assistance.”

The hansom rattled through the bustling streets, its lamps casting fleeting gleams upon the wet cobblestones. Holmes sat opposite me, his gaze distant, his long fingers drumming a silent tune upon his knee. “It is not the lack of immediate victim, Holmes, but the sheer ingenuity of the method, or rather, the labyrinthine path by which the truth must be unearthed,” I replied. “To think that such a trivial incident as the loss of a goose could conceal a matter of such gravity, is, I confess, beyond my usual experience. Holmes leaned back, a faint smile playing upon his lips. “Precisely, Watson. And in that very ignorance lies our work. The name ‘Ryder,’ while not as common as ‘Smith’ or

“Precisely, Watson. A most admirable deduction, though hardly requiring a keen intellect. The obvious steps are often the most overlooked. The Alpha Inn, then, shall be our first port of call. The cabman was instructed, and soon we were rattling eastward, the city’s nocturnal symphony of rumbling wheels and distant cries

The cab jolted onwards, its wheels grinding over the cobbled streets, the sound a constant accompaniment to the silent workings of Holmes’s mind. The gas lamps cast a wan, yellow light upon the grimy shop-fronts and the occasional hurried pedestrian, painting the familiar urban landscape with an air of mystery that seemed to deepen with the lateness of the hour. I observed Holmes from the corner of my eye. His gaze was fixed not upon the passing scene, but inwards, his brow furrowed in that characteristic manner which indicated a profound concentration. He was undoubtedly piecing together the fragmented narrative of the goose, the hat, the diamond, and the elusive Ryder, constructing a coherent picture from the disparate clues that had, by a stroke of fortune, fallen into our possession.

“It is a curious case, Holmes,” I remarked, breaking the silence, for the strain of my own thoughts demanded utterance. “A veritable concatenation of improbable events. Holmes stirred, turning his head slightly, though his eyes remained unfocused, as if still grappling with an internal vision. “Indeed, Watson. The improbable is often the commonplace in the realm of detection. Had the diamond been conveyed by ordinary means, or stolen by a professional thief employing the usual stratagems, our task would be simplified, though perhaps less stimulating. It is the very absurdity of the vehicle that conceals the enormity of the act, and it is precisely this absurdity which has, until now, allowed the perpetrators to remain undetected.”

“And you believe this Ryder to be the chief culprit?” I inquired, my mind still grappling with the implications of the discovery.

My thoughts revolved around the image of the terrified man, fleeing with his feathered prize, pursued by the indignant Peterson. “It must have been a desperate measure,” I concluded. “No man, however artful, would deliberately conceal a valuable gem in such a manner unless under extreme duress. “A sound deduction, Watson,” Holmes conceded. “Though not entirely certain. As he spoke, the cab turned a corner and slowed, pulling up beside a modest, somewhat dingy establishment whose single gas-lamp above the door cast a feeble glow upon its sign: "The Alpha Inn." It was, as Holmes had predicted, a humble place, nestled in a quiet side street. Holmes adjusted his deerstalker, a subtle gesture that always seemed to precede a more active phase of his investigations. “Observe, Watson, the nature of our surroundings. Holmes’s eyes sharpened almost imperceptibly. “Indeed? Could you perhaps describe him for us? And do you recall his name?”

His sleeves were rolled up, revealing muscular forearms, and his movements were those of a man accustomed to hard physical labour. Holmes, with his usual quiet authority, approached the counter, patiently waiting for a lull in the commotion. When Breckinridge finally noticed us, he grunted, wiping his hands on his apron. “What can I do for you, gentlemen? Busy morning, busy morning. Christmas rush, you see.”

“Indeed,” Holmes responded, his voice calm and perfectly modulated amidst the uproar. “We have come upon a small matter, relating to a consignment of geese which you dispatched to the ‘Alpha’ public-house some days ago.”

Breckinridge’s brow furrowed, a flicker of irritation passing across his features. “The ‘Alpha’? Aye, I recall it. Regular customer, Mr. Windigate, the publican. What about it, then? Nothing wrong with the birds, I trust? Best quality, every one of them.”

“On the contrary,” Holmes assured him, “the birds were, as always, of the finest quality. We are, however, interested in a particular circumstance regarding one of those geese. Do you happen to recall the exact nature of that batch, Mr. Breckinridge?”

The poultry dealer scratched his chin, his memory evidently labouring under the weight of countless transactions. “The ‘Alpha’ batch, you say? Well, now, let me see. “Grey ones, you say?” Holmes queried, his eyes narrowing imperceptibly. “And yet, if I am not mistaken, you also deal in white-feathered geese, do you not?”

Breckinridge gave a short, humourless laugh. “White-feathered? Aye, a few. Special order birds, those. Got a batch of six of them in, a week or so back. Finer birds than the grey ones, and dearer, too.”

“And to whom did you sell these white-feathered geese, Mr. Breckinridge?” Holmes pressed, his voice retaining its even tone, but with an underlying current of keen interest.

“Well, now, that’s another story altogether,” Breckinridge grumbled, his irritation returning. “Had a rare old mix-up over those white ones, I did. “Just yonder, sir, by the main archway,” Breckinridge replied, pointing a meaty finger towards a distant corner of the bustling market. “But you’ll not find much there now. He sells out early, Mr. Oakshott does. Good stock, you see.”

I am a poor, honest man, sir, a commissionaire, and never a shadow of suspicion has rested upon my character in all my days!” He wrung his hands, his honest brow furrowed with genuine distress, and I could see that the simple pleasure of his extraordinary discovery had vanished, replaced by the chilling dread of possible implication in a notorious crime.

“Calm yourself, Peterson, calm yourself,” Holmes said soothingly, though his own eyes continued to gleam with a peculiar excitement that belied his outwardly composed demeanour. He placed the brilliant stone upon the mantelpiece, where its blue fire seemed to contend with the grey light of the window, drawing all attention to itself. “There is no need for such perturbation, my good fellow. You are as innocent in this matter as the goose itself, perhaps even more so, for the goose, though unwitting, served as the carrier of this illicit cargo. You have merely stumbled upon a most extraordinary piece of evidence, and far from being implicated, you have, in fact, rendered a service which may yet prove invaluable. Now, tell me precisely, Peterson, what is the value of this carbuncle, so far as you have gleaned it from the public prints?”

Peterson, still somewhat agitated, swallowed hard, struggling to collect his thoughts. “Why, sir, they say it is worth twenty thousand pounds at the very least. Some of the papers put it at fifty thousand. And it was to be made into a new head for the Countess’s tiara, they said. A princely fortune, Mr. Holmes, a princely fortune indeed!”

“Precisely,” Holmes murmured, his gaze fixed upon the jewel. “Twenty thousand pounds or more. A veritable king’s ransom, secreted within the gizzard of a poultry fowl. It is a piquant situation, Watson, is it not?” He turned to me, his lean face alight with the intellectual thrill of the chase. “Here we have, as you observe, the celebrated Blue Carbuncle, whose theft has been the talk of the town for the past three days. It was stolen, as Peterson rightly recalls, from the Hotel Cosmopolitan, where it was lodged in the suite of the Countess of Morcar. The police, with their usual alacrity in seizing upon the most obvious suspect, have arrested a certain John Horner, a plumber, who had been employed to repair a pipe in the Countess’s dressing-room on the very day of the theft. The case, to all appearances, was simple enough. A valuable jewel, a working man alone in the room, a subsequent disappearance of the jewel, and the natural conclusion that the working man was the culprit. Horner was apprehended, and despite his vehement protestations of innocence, the evidence, at least in the eyes of Inspector Bradstreet, seemed unassailable. A previous conviction for robbery, though of a minor sort, sealed his fate, and he now awaits his trial, with every prospect of a lengthy term of penal servitude.”

Holmes paused, allowing the gravity of the situation to sink in. “And yet, Watson, here we have the carbuncle, not in the possession of the supposedly guilty Horner, nor hidden in some obscure corner of his lodgings, but rather ensconced in the crop of a goose, which itself fell into the hands of an honest commissionaire after a street brawl on Christmas morning. Do you not find this somewhat… anachronistic?”

“Indeed, Holmes,” I replied, my mind racing to grasp the implications. “It suggests that Horner is innocent, and that the true thief, or at least the true possessor of the jewel, is still at large.”

“A most logical deduction, Watson,” Holmes conceded with a slight bow of his head. “And one which places us, with this small, dazzling object, at the very heart of a significant criminal mystery. The question now becomes, how did this jewel, purloined from the Countess’s suite, find its way into the gullet of a goose, which was then, by a series of accidents, conveyed into Peterson’s keeping?”

He picked up the battered hat once more, examining it with renewed intensity, as if the answers were etched into its worn felt. “Let us reconstruct the sequence of events. The jewel was stolen three days ago. Today is the twenty-seventh of December. The goose was acquired by Peterson on Christmas morning. The man who dropped it, whose hat we now possess, was an H.B., and the goose was destined for a Mrs. Henry Baker. We must assume, for the moment, that H.B. is the same Henry Baker, the husband of the lady for whom the goose was intended. Now, this man, H.B., was fleeing from a street disturbance. He was evidently a man in a state of some agitation, perhaps even panic, for he dropped not only his hat, but also the goose which he was carrying, and did not pause to retrieve either. A man, as we deduced from the hat, who has fallen upon evil days, whose foresight is diminished, whose habits suggest a moral retrogression, possibly linked to drink. Such a man might well be engaged in some desperate venture, or become an unwitting accomplice in a criminal enterprise.”

“But how could the jewel get into the goose?” I pressed, finding this detail the most baffling of all.

“Ah, Watson, there lies the essence of the problem,” Holmes replied, a flicker of amusement in his keen grey eyes. “It was not, I venture to say, placed there by means of some elaborate surgical procedure, nor was it swallowed by the fowl in the ordinary course of its feeding. No, my dear fellow, the goose was merely a convenient receptacle, a living hiding-place, chosen in a moment of extreme haste and desperation. Imagine the scene: The thief, having secured the carbuncle, finds himself pursued, or fears pursuit. He needs to dispose of the jewel quickly, but not permanently, for he intends to recover it. A passerby, perhaps this very H.B., is carrying a goose, possibly on his way home for Christmas dinner. In a flash of inspiration, or perhaps a moment of drunken desperation, the thief seizes the goose, makes a rapid incision, thrusts the jewel within, and hastily repairs the cut, perhaps with a needle and thread, or even by merely pressing the skin back together, trusting to the confusion of the moment to escape detection. He then abandons the goose, or perhaps, in the hurry and panic, it is merely dropped, and the thief flees. Alternatively, and perhaps more plausibly, H.B. himself is either the thief or has received the jewel from the actual thief, and, fearing detection or desiring to conceal his ill-gotten gains, decides upon this novel method of conveyance. The goose, intended for consumption, would eventually yield up its secret to its owner. But fate intervened, in the form of Peterson’s arrival.”

Peterson, who had listened intently, now interjected, his voice still edged with trepidation. “But if the goose was meant for Mrs. Henry Baker, and this H.B. was carrying it, then he must have been the one who put it there, sir, or at least knew of its presence. He surely would have come back for it!”

“Precisely, Peterson,” Holmes affirmed, picking up the hat again and turning it over in his hands. “And this is where our earlier deductions about the character of the man from his hat become remarkably pertinent. A man of diminished foresight, of weakened nature, perhaps under the influence of drink or other evil influence, might well panic at the appearance of a commissionaire, particularly if he were carrying an item of such illicit provenance. He fled, leaving his hat and the goose behind. Perhaps he feared that the goose, if recovered, would betray his secret. Perhaps he was too drunk or too afraid to return. Or perhaps, and this is a possibility we must not overlook, the goose was not his property at all, but merely something he had temporarily acquired or stolen himself, and the label ‘For Mrs. Henry Baker’ was simply an existing tag upon the bird, giving us a false lead as to the true identity of the immediate possessor.”

“So, what is our next step, Holmes?” I asked, eager to participate in this suddenly invigorated inquiry.

“Our first and most crucial step, Watson, is to trace the provenance of this goose,” Holmes declared, his eyes now sparkling with a cold, clear light of analytical thought. “The label ‘For Mrs. Henry Baker’ is our sole tangible clue. We must ascertain where this goose was purchased, and by whom. Peterson, you are a man of the people, familiar with the byways and markets of this great city. You have, by your honest discovery, placed yourself in the very centre of this affair. I shall entrust you with the initial inquiries.”

Peterson’s chest swelled a little at the compliment, and some of the fear left his face, replaced by a sense of purpose. “Anything you say, Mr. Holmes! I’ll do my best!”

“Excellent, Peterson, excellent. Now, this goose, being a prime specimen, would not likely have been purchased from a common street vendor. It was clearly intended for a substantial Christmas dinner. Such birds usually come from a respectable poulterer. What I require of you is this: take this card,” Holmes scribbled a few words on a small piece of paper, “and make inquiries at all the principal poulterers in the neighbourhood of Tottenham Court Road and the adjacent districts. Ask them if they sold a goose bearing a label ‘For Mrs. Henry Baker,’ and if so, when and to whom. It is a matter of some urgency, as the trail, like the scent of a fox, grows colder with each passing hour. Do not reveal the true nature of your inquiry, of course. Simply state that you are assisting a gentleman who has mislaid some property and wishes to restore it to its rightful owner. You may tell them that the gentleman is much distressed by the oversight.”

“I understand, Mr. Holmes,” Peterson said, his eyes bright with newfound resolve. “I will go at once. I know many of the shopkeepers there, and they are usually good for a bit of information.”

“And one more thing, Peterson,” Holmes added, picking up the blue carbuncle and handing it to the commissionaire. “Take this with you. Do not expose it, of course, but it will serve as a powerful reminder of the importance of your mission. Guard it well, for it is worth more than your annual salary a hundredfold. Place it in your inner pocket, and do not, under any circumstances, allow it to leave your person until you return.”

Peterson took the glittering jewel with a reverence bordering on awe, carefully wrapping it in a handkerchief before placing it deep within his coat. “You may depend upon me, sir,” he said, his voice firm. He then made his exit, leaving Holmes and me alone once more in the gaslit room, the absence of the carbuncle creating a subtle void on the mantelpiece.

“Well, Watson, the game is afoot,” Holmes declared, rubbing his hands together with evident satisfaction. “We have moved from the trivial pursuit of a lost goose to the recovery of a priceless jewel and the unravelling of a most curious criminal entanglement. The official police theory, as so often happens, is demonstrably flawed. Horner, the plumber, is clearly innocent. Our task is now to discover the true culprit, or at least the true possessor of this carbuncle immediately prior to its insertion into the bird, and to clear the name of the unfortunate man unjustly accused.”

“But how do we proceed, Holmes?” I asked. “Even if Peterson discovers the poulterer, it will merely tell us who purchased the goose. It does not necessarily lead us to the thief.”

“True, Watson, true,” Holmes replied, pacing restlessly about the room. “But it is a thread, and even the smallest thread, when held firmly, can lead us through a labyrinth. Let us assume, for a moment, that the goose was indeed purchased by our Mr. Henry Baker. We would then have to trace Mr. Henry Baker. And here, our deductions from his hat may prove invaluable. We know he is a man of a certain age, with grizzled hair, fallen upon hard times, and prone to carelessness, perhaps even drink. The fact that his wife has, by my inference, ceased to love him, suggests a domestic disharmony that might precipitate desperate measures. The lack of gas in his house, inferred from the tallow stains, paints a picture of genteel poverty, or at least a household struggling to maintain appearances. Such a man, perhaps burdened by debt or the pressures of an unhappy home, might be vulnerable to temptation, or easily drawn into a scheme by a more cunning individual.”

He paused, his eyes gleaming. “Consider the sequence, Watson. The theft occurs three days ago. The jewel is then, for reasons we have yet to fathom, secreted within a goose. This goose is then carried by a man, our Mr. Henry Baker, who, in a moment of panic, drops it. Why the goose? Why not simply carry the jewel in a pocket, as a sane man would? It speaks of a desperate need for concealment, or perhaps a pre-arranged plan for its transfer. If it were a pre-arranged plan, then the goose was merely a sophisticated receptacle. If it was an act of desperation, then the situation must have been far more urgent and unforeseen.”

“Could Mr. Henry Baker himself be the thief?” I mused aloud.

“A distinct possibility,” Holmes conceded, “though the profile we have drawn from the hat does not immediately suggest the audacity required for a direct theft from a Countess’s suite at a high-class hotel. It suggests more a man who might be manipulated, or who, in a moment of weakness, accepts a bribe or a piece of stolen property. The moral retrogression, the decline of his fortunes, the probable drinking habit,  all these combine to paint a picture of a man susceptible to temptation and perhaps prone to acts of desperation or carelessness. If he were the direct thief, the act of stuffing the jewel into the goose might have been a spontaneous decision, made in a moment of panic after the theft, to conceal the evidence during his escape, perhaps intending to retrieve it later from the bird.”

Holmes picked up his pipe, filled it with his customary dark tobacco, and lit it, a fragrant plume of smoke curling lazily towards the ceiling. “Or, Watson, it might be that our Mr. Henry Baker is merely an intermediary. The actual thief, a more cunning and professional villain, might have passed the jewel to Baker, knowing him to be a man down on his luck, and instructed him to convey it in this unusual manner, perhaps to a pre-arranged rendezvous, or simply to keep it hidden until the heat of the pursuit had died down. The thief might have underestimated Baker’s capacity for panic, or his tendency to drop things when startled.”

“But if the thief passed it to Baker, why the goose?” I reiterated, still grappling with this bizarre detail.

“Because, my dear Watson, the thief would know that a man carrying a Christmas goose is far less likely to attract suspicion than a man hurrying through the streets with a bulge in his pocket shortly after a major theft,” Holmes explained, settling back into his armchair. “The goose provides an innocent façade, a perfectly ordinary domestic item for a perfectly ordinary Christmas morning. It is a brilliant stroke, if somewhat reckless in its execution. The thief, in his cunning, misjudged the temperament of his courier, or, perhaps, was himself in too great a hurry to ensure a more secure method of concealment. The hurried insertion, perhaps with a clumsy hand, may have left traces that a more thorough examination of the goose might have revealed, had it not been consumed.”

I shuddered slightly at the thought of the splendid bird, now gone, its glorious secret revealed only at the very last moment. “So, our primary objective is to find Mrs. Henry Baker, and through her, Mr. Henry Baker.”

“Precisely, my dear fellow. The name is common, as I remarked earlier, but the specific context of the goose and the hat provides us with a unique identifier. Peterson, bless his simple heart, will make his rounds. Meanwhile, we shall consider other avenues. The Hotel Cosmopolitan, for instance. I should like to refresh my memory on the precise circumstances of Horner’s arrest. Perhaps there are details the papers overlooked, or facts that only an independent inquiry could bring to light. The police report, if accessible, might offer further clues about the actual theft. Did Horner, for example, have any known associates? Was he observed by anyone else in the vicinity of the Countess’s room? These are questions to be addressed.”

Holmes rose and walked to the window, gazing out at the dreary London street. “It is a curious case, Watson, one that marries the commonplace with the extraordinary, the humble fowl with a gem of almost legendary status. And it is further evidence, if any were needed, of the intricate and often absurd ways in which human folly and villainy manifest themselves. To think that a jewel coveted by royalty, whose disappearance has agitated Scotland Yard to its very foundations, should have been discovered in the offal of a Christmas goose! It is a testament to the unpredictable nature of events, and to the endless capacity for surprise that life so frequently offers to those with eyes to see and minds to comprehend.”

He turned, a thoughtful expression on his face. “We must also consider the possibility that Peterson’s discovery, while fortunate for Horner, may place the commissionaire himself in some peril. If the true thief learns that the carbuncle has been found, and by whom, Peterson may become a target. We must ensure his safety.”

“Should we not inform Inspector Bradstreet?” I suggested. “Surely the police would be interested in this development.”

Holmes sighed, a faint cloud of impatience passing over his features. “Bradstreet? My dear Watson, to inform Bradstreet at this stage would be to invite chaos and inefficiency. He has his culprit, he has his theory, and the prospect of dismantling his carefully constructed case for the sake of a goose’s gizzard would, I fear, be met with a mixture of incredulity and hostility. He would demand the jewel, and then proceed with his own, undoubtedly ponderous, investigation, probably concluding that Peterson is somehow complicit, or that the goose is merely a red herring. No, we shall allow Peterson to pursue his inquiries unhindered for the moment. When we have a clearer picture, when we have identified our true quarry, then, and only then, shall we present the facts to the official authorities. For now, we work in the shadows, guided by the cold light of this magnificent gem and the subtle clues left by a battered hat.”

He returned to his chair, picking up the hat once more. “This hat, Watson, speaks volumes, does it not? The signs of recent cutting of the hair, yet the discolouration from neglect. The lingering odour of lime-cream, suggestive of a past attempt at gentility now abandoned. The fluffy brown dust of the house, indicating a sedentary life, yet the heavy perspiration marks, betraying a man out of condition. All these elements combine to form a portrait, albeit a shadowy one, of a man whose life has taken a distinct downturn. A man who might easily be persuaded to undertake a risky venture, or to accept a sudden, ill-gotten gain. The very man, in short, who might have found himself in possession of a stolen jewel and, in a moment of panic or inebriation, chose a goose as his impromptu hiding place.”

“And the fact that it was not brushed for weeks,” I added, recalling his earlier deduction, “and his wife’s supposed lack of affection.”

“Indeed,” Holmes concurred. “A man whose domestic life is in disarray, whose wife has ceased to care for his appearance, is often a man whose moral compass is adrift. He has less to lose, perhaps, and fewer constraints upon his actions. The goose itself was a peace-offering, as we surmised, but it became, instead, a vessel of crime. It is a deeply ironic turn of events, is it not? A Christmas gift, intended to mend domestic strife, becomes the means by which a notorious theft is unwittingly exposed.”

We sat for some time in silence, the only sound the gentle ticking of the clock and the occasional sputter of the fire. My mind was still reeling from the sudden transformation of our quiet Christmas morning into the prelude to a major criminal investigation. The brilliance of Holmes’s deductions, connecting the mundane details of a hat to the profound implications of a jewel theft, was, as always, a source of endless fascination.

“So, we wait for Peterson,” I finally said.

“For Peterson, yes,” Holmes affirmed, rising and stretching his long limbs. “But we do not wait idly, Watson. While Peterson pursues the goose, I shall consult my invaluable indices of crime, particularly those concerning jewel thefts of recent date, to see if there are any other individuals, perhaps known associates of our hypothetically disreputable Mr. Henry Baker, who might have a hand in this. One must always keep all avenues open, and all possibilities in play. The world, my dear Watson, is full of facts. It is the judicious selection and correlation of those facts that constitutes true detective work. And in this instance, we have a most alluring constellation of facts, shining, if I may say so, with the peculiar blue fire of a priceless carbuncle.”

He picked up a well-worn volume from a shelf and settled back into his armchair, his mind already, I knew, racing ahead, sifting through countless details, seeking the one thread that would unravel the entire intricate web of deceit and desperation. I, for my part, sharpened a pencil and prepared my notebook, ready to record the next unfolding chapters of what promised to be one of the most singular adventures in the annals of our professional experience. The Christmas goose, far from being a mere culinary diversion, had, by the merest chance, brought a grand and complex mystery to our very doorstep. And with Holmes at the helm, I knew that the solution, however intricate, would eventually be laid bare.

Holmes, as was his custom when engaged in a problem of particular interest, leaned forward, his elbows upon his knees, and plunged with an almost visible ardour into the voluminous papers and books which lay scattered upon his table. I watched him for some time, captivated by the singular intensity of his gaze, which, like a hawk’s, seemed to penetrate the very essence of the matters before him. The blue stone, resting now upon a square of white velvet on the mantelpiece, pulsed with a cold, internal fire, a silent testament to the startling turn our Christmas morning had taken. It was an object of such profound beauty and value that its presence in our modest Baker Street apartment, discovered within the crop of a common goose, seemed to defy the very laws of probability.

“Ah, Watson,” he remarked, without looking up, his voice a low, resonant murmur, “the threads begin to intertwine, do they not? The commonplace often conceals the most extraordinary. And this, my dear fellow, is certainly an extraordinary instance of the commonplace revealing the truly exceptional.”

He continued to pore over his notes, occasionally tapping a page with a long, lean finger, or making a swift annotation in the margin of some journal. The silence was broken only by the crackling of the fire and the rustle of paper. I knew better than to interrupt him when he was in such a state of concentrated thought. My function, at these times, was to observe, to await his pronouncements, and to record them for posterity.

At length, with a sigh of satisfaction that indicated he had found what he sought, he leaned back in his chair, a thin volume bound in dark leather now open in his hand. He glanced from the book to the glittering gem on the mantelpiece, then to me, a faint smile playing upon his lips.

“The Blue Carbuncle, Watson,” he announced, his voice devoid of triumph, yet imbued with that peculiar ring of certainty that was so characteristic of his revelations. “Indeed, it is none other. Its description here matches our little friend in every particular: ‘a lustrous blue gem of some twenty carats, cut en cabochon, its colour a deep celestial azure, flecked with a singular streak of scarlet like a miniature lightning flash, an inclusion which renders it unique among all known sapphires.’ Here, observe the streak, Watson,” he said, extending the jewel towards me upon the velvet. I took it, and indeed, within its crystalline depths, a faint thread of crimson light seemed to dance and weave, lending the stone a quality almost magical.

“A jewel of such renown,” I ventured, returning it to him, “must surely have a history attached to its loss.”

“A history indeed, and a most recent one,” Holmes replied, settling the gem back upon the mantel. “It vanished but five days ago, Watson, on the twenty-second of December, from the private dressing-room of the Countess of Morcar, at the Hotel Cosmopolitan. The theft was, as you may imagine, a sensation, though suppressed to some extent by the festive season. The newspapers, particularly The Times, carried a prominent report. The police, of course, have been quite baffled. A reward of a thousand pounds was immediately offered for its return.”

“And you have no doubt that this is the very same gem?” I inquired, my mind still grappling with the sheer audacity of its discovery.

“None whatever, my dear Watson. The distinctive streak, as I mentioned, leaves no room for doubt. Furthermore, the Hotel Cosmopolitan is a mere stone’s throw from Covent Garden Market, where our good Peterson purchased the goose. The threads, you see, are beginning to draw tighter.”

“So, the jewel was stolen from the Countess of Morcar,” I mused aloud. “And our friend, Mr. Henry Baker, somehow came into possession of a goose which contained it. But how did the jewel find its way into the goose, Holmes? That is what truly perplexes me.”

“That, Watson, is the very core of the present inquiry, and one which requires a careful reconstruction of events,” he responded, picking up his pipe and slowly filling it from a Persian slipper. “Let us consider the facts. The gem was stolen on the twenty-second. The goose was acquired by Mr. Baker on Christmas Eve. The period between is narrow, and therefore the chain of events must be relatively direct. The thief, whoever he may be, would have been in possession of the carbuncle on the twenty-second. He would then need to dispose of it, or at least conceal it, with some urgency. The police, one assumes, would be making extensive inquiries. To carry such a gem upon one’s person, or to hide it in any conventional manner, would be to invite disaster.”

He puffed thoughtfully upon his pipe for a moment, sending thin coils of blue smoke towards the ceiling. “Consider the ingenuity, Watson. A goose. A seemingly innocuous Christmas bird. What better receptacle for a valuable, easily concealable object? The thief, panicked perhaps, or acting with a cunning born of desperation, places the gem within the bird. Now, the question is, how did this goose, jewel and all, come into Mr. Henry Baker’s hands?”

“You are suggesting, then, that Mr. Baker is the thief?” I proposed, for it seemed the most direct inference.

Holmes smiled faintly, a glint of amusement in his keen eyes. “That is the very inference the casual observer, or indeed the average police inspector, would immediately jump to. But let us not be so hasty. We have already deduced, from his hat, a character somewhat in decline, but not, I think, a professional criminal. His foresight, though diminished, points to a certain past respectability. His attempts to conceal the stains upon his hat, a lingering spark of self-respect. And, importantly, his acquiring of the goose was, as we inferred, a peace-offering to his wife. Does that sound like the action of a hardened jewel thief attempting to fence his prize?”

“No, I confess it does not,” I admitted. “But then, how does he come into possession of the goose?”

“Precisely. Let us consult the newspapers further,” Holmes said, picking up a stack of recent editions. “The theft, according to The Times, occurred around ten in the evening. The countess’s maid, Catherine Cusack, discovered the larceny. A man named James Ryder, a sub-steward at the hotel, was immediately suspected and taken into custody. However, his rooms were searched, and no trace of the carbuncle was found. He was, apparently, released for lack of evidence, though he remains, I have no doubt, the primary suspect in the eyes of the police.”

“So, James Ryder is the thief,” I said, feeling a surge of satisfaction at this emerging clarity. “And he must have placed the jewel in the goose.”

“A most likely scenario,” Holmes agreed. “Now, how did the goose, containing the carbuncle, pass from Ryder to Baker? This requires a link. And the link, Watson, is often found in the most unexpected places. Consider the market. Covent Garden. A bustling place, particularly before Christmas. Geese would be plentiful, sold by various vendors. Ryder, desperate to conceal the gem, places it in a goose. Then, either through accident or design, that particular goose is sold to Baker.”

At this moment, a brisk knock sounded upon our door, and Peterson, the commissionaire, entered, looking somewhat flustered but with a glint of excitement in his eyes.

“Mr. Holmes, sir, I have been making inquiries, as you suggested. About the goose, you know. I went back to the market.”

“Excellent, Peterson, excellent,” Holmes said, waving him to a chair. “Pray sit down and relate your findings. No detail is too small, however insignificant it may seem to your untutored eye.”

Peterson, swelling with the importance of his mission, took a seat. “Well, sir, I went to the greengrocer’s stall where I bought the goose that morning. Old Breckinridge, he’s called. A surly sort, he is, but honest enough. I asked him about the goose, not letting on, you understand, about the… well, about what was inside it. I just said I was looking for a particularly fine bird, much like the one I’d purchased on Christmas Eve. He remembered it right enough, said it was a prime bird, one of his best. He sells a good many geese, you see.”

“And did he remember who he sold it to?” Holmes interjected, his eyes gleaming.

“Ah, that’s where it gets interesting, sir. He had a whole lot of geese, you see, a score or more. But he told me he had two batches. One batch, a dozen of them, came from a woman named Mrs. Oakshott, out by Brixton. The other batch, about another dozen, came from a smaller dealer, a Mr. Windigate, who has a public-house, The Alpha, near the museum.”

“And how did he differentiate them?” Holmes pressed.

“By their leg bands, sir. Mrs. Oakshott’s geese all had blue bands. Mr. Windigate’s had grey ones. The goose I found, the one Mr. Baker dropped, had a blue band, sir.” Peterson paused, looking expectantly at Holmes.

“Remarkable, Peterson, truly remarkable,” Holmes observed, a flicker of satisfaction passing across his face. “You have indeed provided a crucial piece to our puzzle. The goose came from Mrs. Oakshott, via Mr. Breckinridge. Now, Mr. Windigate, of The Alpha public-house, is a matter of less immediate concern. However, I believe we may pay him a visit later, if need be. But for now, Mrs. Oakshott of Brixton is our quarry.”

“Brixton is a fair distance, Holmes,” I remarked. “Do you intend to call upon this Mrs. Oakshott immediately?”

“Not precisely, Watson,” Holmes replied, picking up a pen and making a rapid notation. “But her establishment, a breeding farm, one assumes, is the source of our particular goose. And if our thief, James Ryder, wished to conceal the carbuncle in a goose, he would need to place it in one before it reached the market. It is highly improbable that he would have access to Breckinridge’s stall, or indeed to Breckinridge himself. But he might very well have access to Mrs. Oakshott, or to her geese, at an earlier point in their journey.”

“You think Ryder works for Mrs. Oakshott?” I suggested.

“It is a possibility, Watson. A very strong possibility. Or he knows someone who does. The Hotel Cosmopolitan, where Ryder was employed, is a large establishment, no doubt requiring a constant supply of provisions. It is not beyond the bounds of reason that they might acquire their Christmas poultry directly from a breeder such as Mrs. Oakshott. Or perhaps Ryder simply has a connection to the Brixton area. We must explore all avenues.”

Holmes then consulted his watch. “It is still early, Watson. The day is young, and a fresh trail, especially on a cold morning, is a joy to pursue. Peterson, you have done admirably. Now, if you would be so kind, fetch us a hansom. We shall proceed directly to Brixton.”

In a very short space of time, we were rattling through the frosty streets in a hansom cab, the driver having been given the address of Mrs. Oakshott’s poultry farm, which Holmes had quickly located through his extensive directories. The cold air, though bracing, seemed to invigorate Holmes further. He sat opposite me, his long, thin fingers interlaced, his eyes fixed on some distant point, clearly already planning his approach.

“This case, Watson, possesses a charming symmetry,” Holmes observed, breaking the silence. “The grandest of gems, discovered by the humblest of means. The high and the low, intertwined by a single, desperate act. It is a microcosm of human affairs, is it not?”

“Indeed, Holmes,” I replied, pulling my collar tighter against the chill. “But I confess, my mind still struggles with the intricacies of the thief’s method. How precisely did he introduce the carbuncle into the goose? One imagines a considerable difficulty in such an operation.”

“Ah, Watson, you are thinking perhaps of surgical precision,” Holmes chuckled. “But the desperate mind often seeks the simplest, if most brutal, solution. A goose, once stunned or dispatched, has a surprisingly yielding gullet. A little force, a quick shove, and the deed is done. The primary requirement is not delicacy, but opportunity and secrecy. And a goose, destined for the Christmas table, would scarcely be subjected to an internal examination before its journey to the cooking pot.”

Our journey to Brixton was a somewhat circuitous one, taking us through increasingly less fashionable thoroughfares. At length, the cab turned into a lane that became progressively rural, lined with small cottages and, finally, a large, somewhat dilapidated farmyard. A sign, almost obscured by overgrown ivy, proclaimed: “Oakshott’s Poultry & Game. Supplier to the Finest Establishments.”

Holmes paid the driver, and we dismounted into the muddy yard. The air was filled with the distinct aroma of poultry, and the cackling of geese and clucking of hens provided a raucous accompaniment to our arrival. A stout, red-faced woman, her sleeves rolled up to reveal muscular forearms, emerged from a large shed, wiping her hands on her apron. She surveyed us with an air of suspicion, her eyes sharp and shrewd.

“Good morning, madam,” Holmes said, with his most charming and disarming smile, a rarity he reserved for such occasions. “I believe I have the pleasure of addressing Mrs. Oakshott?”

“That’s me,” she replied gruffly, her hands on her hips. “What can I do for you two gentlemen? Ain’t often I see your sort out this way.”

“We are making some inquiries regarding a matter of considerable importance, Mrs. Oakshott,” Holmes began, his tone grave but respectful. “We understand that you supply geese to various establishments, and we are particularly interested in a batch of birds sold shortly before Christmas, specifically on the twenty-second of December.”

Mrs. Oakshott’s brow furrowed. “The twenty-second, you say? Aye, that was a busy day. I sold a good score of prime birds that day. Some went to Breckinridge at Covent Garden, others direct to hotels and private customers. What’s the trouble with them geese?”

“No trouble with the geese themselves, madam,” Holmes assured her. “But rather with a particular item that appears to have been inadvertently contained within one of them. We are, in short, searching for a very valuable jewel, the Blue Carbuncle, which we believe may have been hidden inside one of your birds.”

Mrs. Oakshott’s eyes widened, and her mouth fell open. “A jewel? In my geese? That’s the most outlandish thing I ever did hear! Are you saying one of my birds is carrying a diamond or something?”

“Precisely, madam. Or rather, a magnificent sapphire, now unfortunately misplaced. We believe it was concealed within one of the geese that left your farm on the twenty-second. Now, I understand that Mr. James Ryder, a sub-steward at the Hotel Cosmopolitan, is a frequent visitor to your establishment, or at least familiar with your stock.”

At the mention of Ryder’s name, Mrs. Oakshott’s face hardened. “Ryder? That good-for-nothing lout! He ain’t no frequent visitor. He used to be a good lad, my sister’s husband’s nephew, he is. But he’s gone to the bad, that one. Always putting on airs, and now he’s mixed up in that countess’s jewel theft, so I hear. But he ain’t been near my geese, not for weeks.”

“Your sister’s husband’s nephew, you say?” Holmes repeated, his voice silky smooth. “A family connection, then. Most interesting. And when did you last see him, Mrs. Oakshott?”

“Not since before Christmas, as I said. He came out here for a bit, a week or so before the theft, asking about geese, if you can believe it. Said he was looking for a prize bird for some big Christmas dinner at the hotel. I told him which ones were ready, the best of the batch. Those were the ones that went to Breckinridge, mind you. But he never bought one himself, just came round sniffing, asking questions. He said he’d arrange for his friend, Mr. Maudsley, the hotel cook, to pick them up.”

“Ah, Mr. Maudsley, the hotel cook,” Holmes murmured, making another mental note. “And so, on the twenty-second, did Mr. Maudsley, or indeed anyone from the Cosmopolitan, collect geese from you?”

“Aye, a big order it was,” Mrs. Oakshott confirmed. “A dozen prime birds, all with blue bands, destined for the Cosmopolitan’s Christmas feast. I sent them off that morning with my boy. They were delivered direct to the hotel kitchen, I’m sure of it.”

“And it was from this batch that you also supplied Mr. Breckinridge?” Holmes asked.

“No, no, those were a separate dozen,” Mrs. Oakshott clarified. “The Cosmopolitan order was a dozen; Breckinridge got a dozen. Two separate batches, both of my best birds, all with blue bands, mind you. All from the same pen. I pride myself on my prime stock.”

Holmes paced slowly for a moment, his gaze sweeping over the bustling farmyard. “So, if Ryder was interested in securing a particular goose, one that would receive special handling, he would have needed to ensure it was one of the geese destined for the Hotel Cosmopolitan, or one of those sent to Breckinridge, who would then sell it on to the general public. And since our Mr. Baker’s goose came from Breckinridge, it seems most likely that Ryder’s chosen bird ended up in Breckinridge’s stock.”

“But why go through all that trouble?” I interjected. “Why not simply take the goose to some secluded spot, put the jewel in it, and then dispose of it or pass it to an accomplice?”

“Because, Watson, the thief requires a plausible explanation for his possession of such a goose. He cannot simply walk about with a jeweled bird. If he were to be searched, a random goose would be far less incriminating than a carbuncle. And if he intended to pass it on, or reclaim it later, having it blend into a large consignment of similar birds would be an ingenious manoeuvre. No one examines the insides of every goose in a batch before it reaches the consumer. The sheer volume makes it impractical. Moreover, the act of concealment would need to be swift and unobserved, perhaps at the farm itself, if he had the opportunity.”

“So, Ryder, knowing the geese were being despatched, could have taken one, performed the act, and then returned it to the consignment, hoping it would be sold to an unsuspecting customer?” I suggested.

“Precisely, Watson. Or, more likely, he obtained the specific goose he had prepared, took it with him, and then, finding himself in a desperate situation, lost it. The question is, which goose did he prepare? And how did Mr. Henry Baker end up with it? Remember, the goose found by Peterson was one of Breckinridge’s, which means it was one of Mrs. Oakshott’s blue-banded birds. So, Ryder must have somehow secured access to that particular batch.”

Mrs. Oakshott, having listened intently, interjected, “I remember now! The day after the Cosmopolitan order went out, a scrawny young fellow, a friend of Ryder’s, came round. I didn’t think nothing of it at the time. Said Ryder had asked him to pick up a goose for his aunt. I was busy, and he seemed in a hurry, so I just pointed him to the pen and told him to take any one he liked from the remaining blue-banded birds. He chose one, a fine fat one, I recall, paid me for it, and off he went. Didn’t give it a second thought.”

Holmes’s eyes lit up. “A scrawny young fellow, a friend of Ryder’s. And he took a blue-banded goose. That, Mrs. Oakshott, is precisely the detail we needed. For the goose that Mr. Baker lost, the one with the card tied to its leg, was a blue-banded goose. It is now clear, therefore, that this ‘friend of Ryder’ was, in all likelihood, Ryder himself, or an accomplice. He selected a goose, stuffed the jewel inside it, paid for it, and departed. His intention, no doubt, was to make good his escape with the carbuncle safely concealed. However, as we know, fate intervened.”

“So, Ryder stole the jewel, hid it in a goose, and then, through some misadventure, lost both the goose and the hat of Mr. Henry Baker during a street brawl,” I summarized, starting to connect the dots more clearly.

“Exactly, Watson. The chain of events, though convoluted, now reveals itself with a certain logical inevitability. Ryder, having secreted the gem, perhaps fearing immediate apprehension, intended to carry the goose away to a safe house. He then encountered Henry Baker, a man whose declining fortunes and propensity for drink we have already deduced. An altercation ensued, perhaps a simple drunken argument, or a jostling in the crowd that escalated. Ryder, in the ensuing confusion and panic at the appearance of Peterson, dropped his incriminating burden and fled, mistaking Baker’s goose for his own, or perhaps simply abandoning his own goose in the desperate scramble to escape. Baker, who was clearly involved in the same mêlée, then inadvertently picked up the jewel-laden bird, believing it to be his. The hat, too, was lost in the scuffle.”

“But the initials ‘H.B.’ on the hat,” I mused, “and the card ‘For Mrs. Henry Baker’ on the goose. That indicates the goose belonged to Baker, not Ryder.”

“Ah, there, Watson, is the subtle twist,” Holmes said, a triumphant gleam in his eyes. “Ryder, in his haste, would not have had time to attach a card to his goose. The card belonged to Mr. Henry Baker’s *own* goose. He had bought a goose, tied his wife’s name to it, and was carrying it home. In the confusion of the fight, Baker drops his own goose and hat. Ryder, abandoning his jewel-laden goose, flees. Peterson arrives. Baker, dazed and disoriented, gathers what he believes are his belongings: his hat, and *a* goose. But in the chaos, he picks up the wrong goose,  the very goose containing the Blue Carbuncle, the one that Ryder had prepared. The card from Baker’s original goose might then have been retrieved by Peterson or even Baker himself and hastily attached to the *new* goose, perhaps out of habit or simple confusion.”

“Or,” I offered, “the card remained with the hat, and Peterson, finding both hat and goose, simply tied the card to the goose, assuming they were a pair.”

“An equally plausible explanation, Watson, and perhaps even more likely, given Peterson’s general lack of deductive flair,” Holmes conceded with a rare smile. “The key is that Baker inadvertently acquired Ryder’s goose, believing it to be his own. Ryder, now bereft of his prize and perhaps fearing a more thorough search without it, has also fled, leaving a clear trail for us to follow.”

Mrs. Oakshott, who had been following this intricate reasoning with wide-eyed fascination, now interjected, “So, you’re saying that young Ryder, my sister’s nephew, is the real thief, and he put the jewel in my goose, and then Mr. Baker, poor man, picked up the wrong bird?”

“That is precisely what we are saying, madam,” Holmes confirmed. “And we have reason to believe that Ryder, or a close associate, may still be in the vicinity, perhaps hoping to reclaim his lost property.”

“He’s a bad egg, that James Ryder,” Mrs. Oakshott muttered, shaking her head. “Always has been. But I never thought he’d stoop to stealing from a countess. Or stuffing my geese with jewels.”

Holmes rose, signalling the conclusion of our visit. “You have been most helpful, Mrs. Oakshott. Your information has, I believe, put us firmly on the trail of the missing carbuncle and its perpetrator.”

As we made our way back through the muddy farmyard, the cackling of the geese seemed almost to mock the gravity of the situation. Holmes’s pace was brisk, his mind already racing ahead to the next stage of the investigation.

“The next step, Watson, is clear,” he declared, as we waited for a passing cab. “We must find James Ryder. And I have a strong suspicion that Mr. Windigate, of The Alpha public-house, may provide us with the necessary address. It is often the case that those with a guilty conscience seek refuge, or at least camaraderie, in familiar haunts. And a public-house, particularly one that receives geese from the same source, would be a most natural place for our Mr. Ryder, or his associates, to frequent.”

“Then we proceed to The Alpha?” I asked.

“Indeed, Watson. The hunt is on, and the scent, though faint, is becoming increasingly distinct. We have moved from a battered hat and a Christmas goose to a priceless gem and a cunning thief. The arc of this little adventure, you must admit, is quite satisfying.”

As we hailed a cab and directed the driver to The Alpha public-house, I could not help but marvel once again at the extraordinary workings of Holmes’s mind. From a few seemingly trivial objects, he had woven a narrative of crime, desperation, and improbable chance, a tapestry of human folly and ingenuity. The Christmas goose, far from being a mere culinary diversion, had, by the merest chance, brought a grand and complex mystery to our very doorstep. And with Holmes at the helm, I knew that the solution, however intricate, would eventually be laid bare. My pen would be busy, I foresaw, for many hours yet.Holmes, as was his custom when engaged in a problem of particular interest, leaned forward, his elbows upon his knees, and plunged with an almost visible ardour into the voluminous papers and books which lay scattered upon his table. I watched him for some time, captivated by the singular intensity of his gaze, which, like a hawk’s, seemed to penetrate the very essence of the matters before him. The blue stone, resting now upon a square of white velvet on the mantelpiece, pulsed with a cold, internal fire, a silent testament to the startling turn our Christmas morning had taken. It was an object of such profound beauty and value that its presence in our modest Baker Street apartment, discovered within the crop of a common goose, seemed to defy the very laws of probability.

“Ah, Watson,” he remarked, without looking up, his voice a low, resonant murmur, “the threads begin to intertwine, do they not? The commonplace often conceals the most extraordinary. And this, my dear fellow, is certainly an extraordinary instance of the commonplace revealing the truly exceptional.”

He continued to pore over his notes, occasionally tapping a page with a long, lean finger, or making a swift annotation in the margin of some journal. The silence was broken only by the crackling of the fire and the rustle of paper. I knew better than to interrupt him when he was in such a state of concentrated thought. My function, at these times, was to observe, to await his pronouncements, and to record them for posterity.

At length, with a sigh of satisfaction that indicated he had found what he sought, he leaned back in his chair, a thin volume bound in dark leather now open in his hand. He glanced from the book to the glittering gem on the mantelpiece, then to me, a faint smile playing upon his lips.

“The Blue Carbuncle, Watson,” he announced, his voice devoid of triumph, yet imbued with that peculiar ring of certainty that was so characteristic of his revelations. “Indeed, it is none other. Its description here matches our little friend in every particular: ‘a lustrous blue gem of some twenty carats, cut en cabochon, its colour a deep celestial azure, flecked with a singular streak of scarlet like a miniature lightning flash, an inclusion which renders it unique among all known sapphires.’ Here, observe the streak, Watson,” he said, extending the jewel towards me upon the velvet. I took it, and indeed, within its crystalline depths, a faint thread of crimson light seemed to dance and weave, lending the stone a quality almost magical.

“A jewel of such renown,” I ventured, returning it to him, “must surely have a history attached to its loss.”

“A history indeed, and a most recent one,” Holmes replied, settling the gem back upon the mantel. “It vanished but five days ago, Watson, on the twenty-second of December, from the private dressing-room of the Countess of Morcar, at the Hotel Cosmopolitan. The theft was, as you may imagine, a sensation, though suppressed to some extent by the festive season. The newspapers, particularly The Times, carried a prominent report. The police, of course, have been quite baffled. A reward of a thousand pounds was immediately offered for its return.”

“And you have no doubt that this is the very same gem?” I inquired, my mind still grappling with the sheer audacity of its discovery.

“None whatever, my dear Watson. The distinctive streak, as I mentioned, leaves no room for doubt. Furthermore, the Hotel Cosmopolitan is a mere stone’s throw from Covent Garden Market, where our good Peterson purchased the goose. The threads, you see, are beginning to draw tighter.”

“So, the jewel was stolen from the Countess of Morcar,” I mused aloud. “And our friend, Mr. Henry Baker, somehow came into possession of a goose which contained it. But how did the jewel find its way into the goose, Holmes? That is what truly perplexes me.”

“That, Watson, is the very core of the present inquiry, and one which requires a careful reconstruction of events,” he responded, picking up his pipe and slowly filling it from a Persian slipper. “Let us consider the facts. The gem was stolen on the twenty-second. The goose was acquired by Mr. Baker on Christmas Eve. The period between is narrow, and therefore the chain of events must be relatively direct. The thief, whoever he may be, would have been in possession of the carbuncle on the twenty-second. He would then need to dispose of it, or at least conceal it, with some urgency. The police, one assumes, would be making extensive inquiries. To carry such a gem upon one’s person, or to hide it in any conventional manner, would be to invite disaster.”
