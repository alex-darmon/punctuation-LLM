But it was a thrilling noise, the sound of a world humming with power. They strolled along Fifth Avenue, and Julia’s eyes widened at the shop windows, each one a curated display of impossible luxury. Gowns of silk and velvet, jewels that glittered with cold fire, furs that looked softer than anything she had ever touched.

"See anything you like?" Jim asked, his voice full of amusement at her rapt expression.

"Everything," she whispered. "It’s like a fairy tale." She stopped before one window, where a single evening gown of deep, shimmering blue was draped over a mannequin. It was the color of the bay at twilight, scattered with tiny, glittering beads like stars.

"That's a nice one," Jim said practically. "Let's go in."

"Oh, no!" Julia said, pulling back. "Jim, we couldn't! It must cost a fortune!"

"What's a fortune for?" he said, laughing. He propelled her gently through the heavy glass door, into a hushed world of plush carpets and deferential quiet. A woman with silver hair and a severe black dress approached them, her eyes gliding over Julia with a swift, appraising glance.

"May I help you?" the woman asked, her voice cool.

"Yes," Jim said, with his easy, confident smile. "My wife would like to try on the gown in the window. The blue one."

The woman's expression thawed almost instantly. "Of course, sir. Madame. This way, please."

In a private fitting room, surrounded by mirrors, Julia felt like an actress in a play. The saleswoman helped her out of her walking suit and into the rustling silk of the gown. It slid over her skin like cool water. When she looked up, she did not recognize the woman in the mirror. This woman was elegant, poised, a creature of grace and light. The dress fit as if it had been made for her, the neckline dipping just so, the beaded stars catching the light with every breath she took.

"Oh, Jim," she breathed, when he was allowed in to see.

His face was all she needed to see. He didn't speak for a moment, just looked at her with an expression of such profound love and admiration that it brought a blush to her cheeks. "Well," he said finally, his voice a little husky. "I guess we'll take it."

"Jim, it's too much," she protested, but her protest was weak. She loved the dress. She loved the way it made her feel.

"Nonsense," he said. "We're on our honeymoon. You're to have everything you want. We'll take it," he said again, to the hovering saleswoman. "And whatever else she needs to go with it. Shoes. A wrap. The whole business."

Julia felt a dizzying wave of happiness. It was not the dress itself, or the money. It was him. It was the sheer, uncomplicated joy he took in giving her things, in making her happy. He was erasing the shadows of her past, not by ignoring them, but by flooding them with so much light they could no longer survive.

They left the shop with a delivery order for the hotel and a Julia who walked a little taller, her spirit clad in shimmering blue silk even if her body was back in its sensible traveling suit.

"Now for my bag," Jim announced, and led her down a side street to a shop that smelled richly of leather and brass polish. While he discussed the finer points of a surgeon's satchel with the proprietor, Julia wandered about, running her hand over the smooth, sturdy hides. It was in here, surrounded by the scent of craftsmanship and purpose, that she felt a new kind of pride. This was Jim's world, a world of skill and dedication, and she was a part of it now, too.

As they emerged back onto the bustling avenue, a man walking briskly in the opposite direction stopped short. "Studdiford? Jim Studdiford, is that you?"

Jim turned, a look of surprise on his face that quickly morphed into a grin. "Arthur! Arthur Vance, you old horse thief! What in the world are you doing here?"

The two men shook hands with vigor. The other man, Arthur, was taller than Jim and impeccably dressed, with a sharp, intelligent face and an air of urbane confidence.

"I'm at Bellevue now," he said. "Finishing my residency. I heard you'd stayed in San Francisco to go in with your father. The golden boy returns to the golden coast, eh?" He said it with a laugh, but Julia detected a faint, wry edge to his tone.

"Something like that," Jim said, his arm tightening around Julia's waist. "Arthur, I want you to meet my wife, Julia. Julia, this is Arthur Vance. We suffered through medical college together."

"His wife!" Arthur exclaimed, his eyes turning to Julia. He took her hand. "Studdiford, you always did have all the luck. A pleasure to meet you, Mrs. Studdiford. I must say, I'm surprised. I thought you'd be married to your surgical kit."

"I almost was," Jim said, smiling down at Julia. "But Julia rescued me."

"I see," Arthur said, his gaze lingering on Julia for a moment. It wasn't an unpleasant look, but it was assessing, the way the saleswoman's had been, and it made her feel suddenly conscious of her plain suit, of her West Coast accent, of the fact that she had never heard of Bellevue. "Well, New York seems to agree with you both. Are you here for long?"

"Just the honeymoon," Jim said. "We're heading back on Thursday."

"A shame. We'll have to get together for a drink before you go. The old crowd would be thrilled to see you. How about tomorrow night? There's a little place we all go, on Thirty-fourth."

"We'd love to," Jim said, before turning to Julia. "Wouldn't we, Ju?"

"Of course," Julia said, finding her voice. "We'd be delighted."

"Excellent! I'll see you there around eight, then," Arthur said. With another tip of his hat to Julia and a clap on Jim's shoulder, he was gone, disappearing back into the river of people.

"What a funny coincidence," Julia remarked, as they started walking again.

"The world's small," Jim said cheerfully. "Arthur's a good fellow. One of the sharpest minds in our class. A bit of a cynic, but his heart's in the right place."

Julia was quiet for a moment. Meeting Arthur Vance had stirred something in her. It was the first time she had seen Jim through someone else's eyes, not just as her Jim, but as "Studdiford," the golden boy, the one who had all the luck. It made her feel, just for a second, like an outsider again. He came from this world of bright, cynical men and famous hospitals, a world she knew nothing about.

"What is it?" Jim asked, sensing her change in mood.

"Nothing," she said quickly. "It's just--he made you sound so successful. So important."

"He was teasing," Jim said, squeezing her arm. "Pay him no mind. I'm just a fellow who knows how to set a bone. And who was lucky enough to get the best girl in the world. Now, have you had enough of this noise? Shall we find a quiet place for lunch?"

He found them a small restaurant tucked away from the main thoroughfare, a place of dark wood and quiet corners. Over plates of cold chicken and salad, he told her stories about medical school, funny, self-deprecating anecdotes about him and Arthur Vance trying to outsmart their professors and failing miserably. He made it sound like a grand adventure, not a prestigious education, and by the time they had finished their coffee, Julia’s brief unease had dissolved completely. He had a gift for it, for making his world her world, for leaving no room for fences or walls between them.

That evening, they went to the theatre. Julia wore a simple dinner dress, not the magnificent blue creation, which was being saved for another night. But even so, as she sat beside Jim in the plush velvet seats of the box he had secured, she felt a sense of glamour and occasion she had only ever read about in books. The theatre was a cavern of gold leaf and red damask, humming with the murmur of hundreds of conversations, the air thick with the scent of perfume and cigar smoke.

Julia watched the people in the orchestra seats below, the women in their jewels and gowns, the men in their stark black and white evening clothes. They were the people from the magazines, the people who lived in the grand houses on the avenues. And here she was, among them. She remembered sitting with her grandmother on their porch, watching the few wealthy families of her town drive by in their carriages, feeling as if they belonged to a different species. Now, she was one of them. Or, at least, she was with one of them.

"A penny for them," Jim whispered, as the orchestra began to tune.

She looked at him, at his handsome, familiar face, so dear to her, and she smiled. "I was just thinking," she said, "that I shall never get used to this. And I hope I never do. I hope I never forget what a miracle it is."

"The only miracle here is you," he said, and he took her gloved hand in his.

The house lights dimmed, the curtain rose, and Julia leaned her head just slightly against his shoulder, her heart so full it ached. The play was a comedy, and the theatre rang with laughter, but Julia was only half aware of it. Her attention was on the solid warmth of her husband beside her, the secure clasp of his hand on hers, the overwhelming, unbelievable fact of her own happiness. The girl from the bad belt had not only pushed into the good; she had found herself in the very heart of it, a place so bright and warm she could hardly believe it was real. And as Jim's thumb traced a slow circle on the back of her hand, she knew that as long as he was there, she would never be an outsider again. She was home.

He drew her closer, his lips finding hers in the fragrant darkness, and for a long moment, the stars, the bay, the whole spinning world fell away, leaving only the two of them in the dim curve of the O’Connell seat, secure in a promise that felt as eternal as the night itself.

The first days of their New York honeymoon passed in a blur of blissful discovery. They were housed in a grand hotel on a wide avenue, a palace of marble and gilt where bellboys seemed to materialise from thin air and the quiet hum of efficiency was a constant, soothing presence. For Julia, who had known only the threadbare austerity of her grandmother’s house and the functional neatness of her own small rooms, it was a world of impossible luxury. She would wake each morning in the enormous, soft bed and lie still for a moment, simply absorbing the reality of it: the heavy silk of the curtains, the distant, polite murmur of the city, and the solid, sleeping warmth of the man beside her.

“Are you awake?” Jim would murmur, his eyes still closed.

“For hours,” she would lie, her voice full of laughter. “I’ve been watching you and plotting my day.”

“And what’s the plot? Are we to be terribly energetic and see a museum, or terribly lazy and order breakfast in our room?”

“Oh, the last one!” Julia would say, propping herself on an elbow to look at him. “It’s the most decadent thing I can imagine. I feel like a scandalous woman in a French novel.”

“You are a scandalous woman,” Jim would agree, pulling her down for a kiss. “You’ve stolen a perfectly respectable surgeon from his practice, and you’re forcing him to lounge about eating toast and marmalade. What will people say?”

“They’ll say he’s the luckiest man in the world,” she’d answer, her happiness so keen it was almost a physical ache.

They spent their days exploring. They walked until their feet were tired, rode on the tops of omnibuses, and gaped at the height of the buildings that seemed to scrape the very clouds. Jim, who had spent part of his youth in the city, was the perfect guide. He knew the quietest paths in the park, the best place for oysters, the most interesting shops. He showed her the city not as a tourist, but as a native, and with every step, Julia felt herself sinking deeper into his world, the boundaries of her old life receding like a distant shore.

One afternoon, as they were strolling down a crowded street, a woman’s voice called out. “Jimmy Studdiford! Is that really you?”

They turned to see a lady of exquisite tailoring and an air of formidable poise bearing down upon them. She was older than Jim, perhaps in her late forties, with a face that was handsome rather than pretty and eyes that took in everything at a glance.

“Mrs. Breckenridge! What a surprise!” Jim said, his face lighting up with genuine pleasure as he took her gloved hand. “What are you doing here? I thought you were in Europe.”

“We returned last month. The Continent is simply dreadful in the spring, full of Americans,” she said, with a dry smile that took any sting from the words. Her sharp eyes moved from Jim to Julia, a flicker of frank curiosity in their depths. “And you have not introduced me to your companion, Jimmy.”

“Of course! My apologies. Mrs. Breckenridge, may I present my wife, Julia. Julia, this is Mrs. Breckenridge, an old friend of my mother’s.”

“Your wife!” The woman’s eyebrows shot up, and she subjected Julia to a new, more thorough appraisal. Julia felt the warmth of a blush rise in her cheeks, suddenly conscious of her simple walking dress, her unadorned hat. “Well, you have been a busy boy, haven’t you? It is a pleasure to meet you, my dear,” she said, her voice a shade cooler now. “Studdiford. That is your family name, then?”

“It is,” Julia said, her own voice sounding small and thin to her ears.

“And you are from…?” Mrs. Breckenridge let the question hang in the air.

“From California,” Jim answered for her, smoothly. “We were just married in San Francisco.”

“How very romantic,” Mrs. Breckenridge commented, though her tone suggested it was anything but. “Well, you must come to dinner. We are having a small gathering on Friday. Just a few people. You will come, won’t you? My husband will be so eager to see you, Jimmy. He still talks about the time your father let you watch that surgery when you were only sixteen.”

“We’d be delighted,” Jim said, beaming. “That’s awfully kind of you.”

“Not at all. We shall expect you at eight. It was so lovely to meet you, Mrs. Studdiford.” And with a final, regal nod, she swept away into the crowd, leaving a trail of expensive perfume in her wake.

“Well! What a stroke of luck!” Jim exclaimed, turning to Julia. “The Breckenridges are the most wonderful people. You’ll adore them.”

“She seemed… very grand,” Julia said, choosing her word carefully.

“Oh, she is. She terrifies half of New York society,” Jim said with a laugh. “But she has a heart of gold. Isn’t it marvellous, running into her like this? Now our honeymoon will have a real New York party.” He squeezed her arm, his face alight with boyish enthusiasm, entirely missing the shadow that had fallen across her own.

For the rest of the afternoon, a small, cold knot of anxiety tightened in Julia’s stomach. The encounter had pricked the beautiful, shimmering bubble of her happiness, reminding her of who she was, and who she was not. She was Julia Page, from the wrong side of the tracks, a girl who had worked for her living. Mrs. Breckenridge and her kind were the women she used to see stepping from carriages, their lives as remote and inaccessible as the moon. And on Friday night, she was expected to sit at their table.

That evening, Jim suggested the theatre again, but Julia pleaded a headache. The truth was, she could not bear the thought of sitting amongst the silks and jewels, feeling like an imposter. While Jim went down to the hotel dining room for a solitary supper, she stood at the window of their suite, looking down at the endless river of lights that was the avenue. For the first time since their wedding, she felt a pang of profound loneliness. Jim’s world, which had seemed so open and welcoming, suddenly felt like a fortress, and she was outside the walls, peering in.

He found her there when he returned. “Head still hurting, darling?” he asked, coming up behind her and wrapping his arms around her waist.

“It’s a little better,” she said, leaning her head back against his shoulder.

“You were very quiet after we met Mrs. Breckenridge,” he observed, his cheek against her hair. “Is everything all right?”

Here was her chance to tell him. To confess her fears, her sense of inadequacy. But the words would not come. How could she explain her terror of a simple dinner party to this man who had been born to it, who moved through the world with such unconscious grace? He would think her foolish. He would try to reassure her, of course, in his kind, loving way, but he would not, could not, understand.

“I’m just tired,” she said. “It has been a whirlwind.”

“Of course you are,” he said, his concern immediate and uncomplicated. “I’m a brute for dragging you all over the city. We’ll have a quiet day tomorrow. Just the two of us.”

He was so good, so dear, that her heart ached with a fresh wave of love and despair. She turned in his arms and kissed him, a kiss that was both a reassurance and a desperate attempt to lose herself in him, to borrow his strength and his easy confidence. “I love you, Jim,” she whispered against his lips.

“I love you, Ju,” he answered, holding her tight. “More than anything.”

The day of the dinner party arrived, a grey, drizzly Friday that seemed to mirror Julia’s mood. Jim, however, was in the highest of spirits. He had bought a new set of evening studs and spent a comical amount of time trying to fix them in his shirt, his hair rumpled, his face screwed up in concentration.

“This is absurd!” he declared. “I can put a man’s shoulder back in its socket, but I can’t manage a simple bit of pearl and platinum.”

Julia, watching him from her dressing table, gave a weak smile. She had spent the better part of the afternoon in a state of quiet panic. The magnificent blue gown, which had seemed so splendid in San Francisco, now looked garish and provincial to her worried eyes. Her hair, which she had tried to arrange in a fashionable new style, refused to cooperate. Every glance in the mirror seemed to show a stranger, a frightened country girl playing dress-up.

“There!” Jim said, finally triumphant. He came over to her, handsome and elegant in his evening clothes. “Now, how are you doing? My goodness, Ju, you look like a queen.” He bent and kissed her bare shoulder. “A very serious, thoughtful queen. Are you nervous?”

“A little,” she confessed, her fingers twisting the sash of her dress.

“Don’t be,” he said, his tone light and reassuring. “They’re just people. And they’re going to be so captivated by you they won’t know what’s hit them. Just be yourself.”

But that was the very thing she was afraid of. Who she was, she felt with a sinking heart, was precisely what was wrong.

The Breckenridge residence was a fortress of brownstone on a street of other, equally imposing fortresses. A severe-looking butler led them through a hall that seemed to be all marble and shadows, into a drawing room that glittered with the light of a hundred candles. The room was already full of people, the women in shimmering gowns, the men in stark black and white. The air hummed with a low, confident murmur of conversation, a sound Julia had only ever heard from a stage.

Mrs. Breckenridge detached herself from a small group and sailed towards them. “Jimmy, you’re here! And Mrs. Studdiford. You look lovely, my dear. That colour is very… vibrant.” She made it sound like a criticism. “Come, I want you to meet everyone.”

What followed was a dizzying, terrifying blur of names and faces. There was a judge, a banker, a famous artist, and their equally formidable wives. They were all polite, all charming, but Julia felt their eyes on her, assessing, cataloguing. They asked her questions, the same questions Mrs. Breckenridge had asked, but couched in smoother, more subtle terms.

“And your family is in California, Mrs. Studdiford?” asked the banker’s wife, a woman with a face like a dried apple.

“I have no family,” Julia said, the words falling like stones into the polite silence. “My grandmother raised me, but she passed away.”

“Ah, I see,” the woman said, her eyes flickering away for a moment. “How… unfortunate.”

At dinner, she was seated between the ancient, deaf judge and the famous artist, a man with a flowing tie and a world-weary air who seemed interested only in the quality of the wine. Across the vast expanse of damask and silver, she could see Jim, seated next to a vivacious young woman with startlingly red hair. He was laughing, talking, completely in his element. He caught her eye once and gave her a brilliant, encouraging smile, but it only made her feel more isolated. He was one of them. She was an alien, a creature from another planet.

The conversation swirled around her, a confusing torrent of references to people she did not know and places she had never been. They talked of summers in Newport, of winters in St. Moritz, of a scandalous affair involving a duke and an actress. Julia sat in silent misery, pushing a piece of fish around her plate, praying for the evening to end.

“And you, Mrs. Studdiford?” The voice of her host, Mr. Breckenridge, boomed from the head of the table. “What are your passions? Do you paint? Play a musical instrument?”

The entire table seemed to fall silent, turning to look at her. Julia’s heart began to pound. She could feel the blood draining from her face.

“No,” she said, her voice barely a whisper. “I’m afraid I don’t.”

“She is a reader,” Jim’s voice cut in, clear and proud from across the table. “She has read more books than anyone I know. And she has the kindest heart in the world. That is her passion.”

A few people smiled politely. The banker’s wife gave a small, condescending titter. The moment passed, the conversation moved on, but Julia felt as if she had been flayed alive. Jim had tried to save her, but his words had only highlighted her deficiencies. She did not paint or play the piano; she was merely… kind. It was the sort of thing one said about a faithful dog.

The cab ride back to the hotel was conducted in near silence. Jim, tired from the social exertion, hummed a tune from the theatre and looked out at the rain-slicked streets. Julia sat rigid in her corner, the vibrant blue of her dress feeling like a shroud. She felt a cold, hard anger solidifying around her heart. It was not anger at the Breckenridges or their snobbish friends, but at Jim. At his blindness, his effortless assumption that his world could be hers without any cost.

Back in their suite, he finally seemed to notice her silence.

“Tired, sweetheart?” he asked, loosening his tie. “It was a bit of a stuffy affair, wasn’t it? But it was good to see them all.”

Julia walked over to the dressing table and began to remove her earrings, her movements stiff and jerky. “They hated me,” she said, her voice flat.

Jim stopped, his tie half undone, and stared at her reflection in the mirror. “What? Ju, don’t be silly. They loved you. Mrs. Breckenridge said you were a breath of fresh air.”

“She said I was ‘vibrant’,” Julia retorted, her voice trembling. “She said it like I was a species of tropical fungus. And that woman, the banker’s wife, she looked at me as if I were something she had found on the bottom of her shoe.”

“Oh, darling, that’s just their way. They’re a bit formal, that’s all. You’re imagining things.”

“Am I?” she cried, spinning around to face him. Her carefully constructed composure had shattered, and all the fear and humiliation of the evening came pouring out. “Did I imagine them asking about my family? Did I imagine them looking right through me when I couldn't talk about their ridiculous European vacations? Did you not see their faces when you said my ‘passion’ was being kind?”

Jim looked utterly bewildered. “But… I was trying to help! I was proud of you. I am proud of you!”

“You were proud of your little charity case!” she said, the words tasting like poison. “The poor girl from the bad belt you’ve polished up and brought to the ball! Don’t you see, Jim? It’s not enough! I can’t be you. I wasn’t born ready-made, with money and a famous father and a path laid out for me in gold. I have to learn things so hard, and I will never, ever learn to be one of those people!”

She was sobbing now, great, tearing sobs that shook her entire body. Jim stood frozen for a moment, his face a mask of shock and pain. Then he crossed the room in two strides and gathered her into his arms, holding her tightly as she wept against his chest. He did not speak, did not offer any easy platitudes. He just held her, his hand stroking her hair, his starched shirtfront growing damp with her tears.

When her sobs finally subsided into shuddering breaths, he led her to a chair and knelt before her, taking her hands in his.

“Julia,” he said, his voice low and ragged. “Look at me.”

She lifted her tear-streaked face. His own was pale, his eyes full of a misery that matched her own.

“You’re right,” he said, and the simple words were more stunning than any argument. “I am an idiot. A blind, lucky fool. I never… I never thought. Things just come my way, I told you that. I never stopped to think what it would be like for you, to be thrown into the middle of it. I saw you, and I fell in love with you, and all I wanted was to have you with me, in my life. I never thought that my life might… hurt you.”

He looked down at their joined hands. “When you said I was proud of my charity case,” he continued, his voice thick, “that was the cruelest thing you could have said. And maybe I deserved it. But it’s not true, Ju. It’s not true. I don’t love you in spite of where you came from. I think… I think I love you because of it. You’ve fought for everything you have. You’ve built yourself, piece by piece, into this incredible, brave, wonderful woman. Those people tonight… they’ve had everything handed to them. They’re like hothouse flowers, pale and brittle. You, you’re like one of those wildflowers that grows on the cliffs by the sea. You’re stronger and more beautiful than all of them put together.”

He looked up at her, his eyes pleading. “I don’t want you to be one of them. God, no. I want you to be you. Just you. And if my world doesn’t have a place for you, then to hell with my world. We’ll make our own.”

A single tear slipped down Julia’s cheek, but this one was not of sorrow. It was for the breathtaking, heart-stopping relief of being seen. Of being truly, completely understood.

“Oh, Jim,” she whispered.

“We’ll leave tomorrow,” he said, a sudden urgency in his voice. “We’ll go somewhere else. Anywhere. We’ll go to the country and walk in the woods. We’ll never go to a dinner party again, I swear it.”

She managed a small, watery laugh. “You can’t do that. You’re a rising young surgeon. You have to go to dinner parties.”

“Then you’ll go with me,” he said fiercely. “And you’ll hold your head up high. And if anyone looks at you the wrong way, I’ll… I’ll challenge them to a duel.”

“You’re ridiculous,” she said, her hand coming up to cup his cheek.

“I am,” he agreed. “Completely ridiculous about you. Please, Ju. Don’t let them spoil us. Don’t let them make you doubt.”

“They didn’t,” she said, her voice gaining strength. “I did. I let the old ghosts in.” She looked at him, at his dear, earnest, worried face, and a great wave of tenderness washed through her. The girl from the bad belt had not just pushed into the good; she had found a partner who was willing to leave the good behind, if that’s what it took.

She stood up, pulling him with her. “I am not going to let you run away from your life, James Studdiford. But,” she added, a spark of her old self returning, “we are going to have a very quiet rest of the honeymoon. No more grand people. And tomorrow, I want to ride the ferry, like any ordinary person.”

“Anything you want,” he said, his relief palpable. He drew her close, burying his face in her hair. “As long as I’m with you.”

“Wherever you go, you go with me,” she reminded him softly, echoing his own words back to him. This time, there was no swift colour rising to her face, only a deep, quiet certainty. The house lights had dimmed on her fairytale, the curtain had fallen, and in the plain, honest light of day, she found that reality was infinitely better. She was not just a guest in his world. They were building their own, and this time, she knew with every fibre of her being that she was home.
