"I wish I could read it," she said, tracing the French title with her finger. "I wish I could speak the language. It sounds so lovely, even when people are just arguing."

"We'll get you a tutor," Jim said at once, as if it were the simplest thing in the world. "You'll be chattering away like a native in six months. You learn things, Ju. You don't just let them sit on the surface."

"I have to," she said, with her familiar, thoughtful seriousness. "Things don't just come to me. I have to dig for them." She looked up at him. "Do you think it's too extravagant? A French tutor?"

"I think it's extravagant not to learn everything you can when you have the chance," he said firmly. "We're a team, remember? My luck, your hard work. We're unstoppable." He tucked the book of poetry under his arm. "My treat," he said, handing the vendor a few coins before she could protest.

They wandered into the winding streets of the Latin Quarter, and Jim, with his schoolboy French, managed to buy them each a crêpe filled with chocolate from a street cart. They ate them standing up, laughing as melted chocolate dripped onto their fingers. Julia felt a lightness in her heart she had never known, a freedom from the constant, low-level hum of worry that had been the soundtrack to her life for so long.

"You know," she said, dabbing at his chin with her napkin, "I don't think I've ever just walked. Without having to be somewhere. Without rushing."

"It's a fine art," Jim said gravely. "The art of the _flâneur_. I think you're a natural."

In the afternoon, the sun finally broke through the clouds, and the wet stones of the street began to gleam. As they passed a row of elegant shops, Jim stopped abruptly in front of a window displaying a single, perfect hat. It was a deep, velvety blue, the colour of a twilight sky, with a soft brim and a simple, elegant ribbon.

"That's your hat," he declared.

"Oh, it's beautiful," Julia breathed. "But, Jim, I have a hat. Two hats!"

"But you don't have a Paris hat," he said, his eyes alight with the particular joy he took in giving things to her. "Come on. Let's just go in and look."

Inside, the shop was quiet and smelled of lavender and felt. A chic, severe-looking woman with her hair in a tight chignon approached them. Jim, undaunted, pointed to the hat in the window. "Pour madame," he said with a confident smile.

Julia felt a blush creep up her neck. The shop was so silent, so luxurious. It felt like a place she was not supposed to be, a place for women like Mrs. Albright, women who had been born to this. The saleswoman brought the hat, handling it with reverence, and held it for Julia to try on. Julia looked at her reflection in the gilded mirror. The hat settled on her hair, framing her face, and the deep blue made her eyes look brighter, her skin fairer. It changed her. She looked... elegant. She looked like she belonged.

"We'll take it," Jim said, already reaching for his wallet.

"Jim, no," Julia whispered, turning to him. "It must cost a fortune. It's too much."

His face fell a little. "Don't you like it? I thought you looked like a queen."

"I love it," she said earnestly. "That's the trouble! It's too lovely. It's an extravagance."

The saleswoman was watching them with a detached sort of interest. Jim took Julia's arm and led her a few steps away. "Listen to me," he said, his voice low and serious. "This isn't about the money. It's the hat we'll take out of a box twenty years from now, and you'll put it on, and we'll remember this afternoon. It's not an extravagance, it's an investment. In us."

She looked at him, at his earnest, pleading face, and the last of her resistance melted away. He wasn't just buying her things; he was building a life with her, a life full of memories. He was taking his good luck and weaving it into the fabric of her own, until she could no longer tell where one began and the other ended.

"All right," she said, a smile trembling on her lips. "All right, Doctor Studdiford. You've convinced me."

His face lit up. "Sure I have!" he said, triumphant. "It's a medical necessity. For the treatment of insufficient Parisian headwear."

She laughed, and the sound was so full of joy that even the severe saleswoman allowed a faint, wintry smile to touch her lips as she boxed up the hat.

They walked out of the shop, Julia carrying the beautiful striped box by its cord. "Thank you, Jim," she said, squeezing his arm. "It's not just the hat."

"I know," he said softly.

That evening, they went to a grand restaurant that Barbara had insisted they visit. It was all polished brass, red velvet, and sparkling crystal. Julia wore a simple silk dress she had made herself, but on her head, she wore her new Paris hat. She felt a little thrill of daring as she walked in, but no one stared, no one seemed to find her out of place. She belonged. With Jim, she belonged everywhere.

They were seated at a small table by the window. Julia looked around at the other diners, the elegant women in their jewels, the distinguished-looking men, all speaking in the low, rapid murmur of French. "It's just like a play," she whispered to Jim.

"To us," she repeated, her eyes shining over the rim of her glass.

The dinner was a long, leisurely affair, with course after course of food so delicious it made Julia close her eyes in pleasure. They talked about everything and nothing. They planned the house they would build one day, a house with a big garden like the Tolands', but with a view of the San Francisco Bay.

"And a big sunny room for you to work in," Julia said, "with lots of windows. For when you're not at the hospital."

"And a room for you," he countered, "with a sewing machine and books clear up to the ceiling. And maybe," he added, his voice dropping a little, "a nursery."

The swift colour came to her face, as it always did. The thought of a child, a child with Jim's kind eyes and rumpled hair, was a happiness so profound it was almost frightening. "Maybe," she said softly.

"I think you'd be a wonderful mother, Ju," he said, his gaze steady and warm. "You've got so much... goodness in you. So much love to give."

"I'd be afraid," she confessed. "Afraid I wouldn't know how. I never had much of a mother myself, to learn from."

"You'd learn," he said with absolute conviction. "You learn everything you set your mind to. You'd read all the books, and you'd talk to all the nurses, and you'd dig down right into the very soul of it. And you'd be the best mother in the whole world."

"You're implying that I'm not a pampered child of luxury," she said, her lips twitching as she threw his own words back at him.

He grinned, catching her hand across the table. "You think I think you're a pampered child of luxury, but I don't! I think you're the strongest person I know. I'm the one who's had it easy. I just came in for an extraordinary line of luck. The best part of it," he finished, his voice growing thick, "was you."

Later, they walked back to their hotel under a sky full of stars. The air was cool and smelled of rain and roasted chestnuts. The city was quieter now, but the lights of the bridges still glittered on the dark, smooth water of the Seine. They didn't talk much, but walked in a comfortable, happy silence, their shoulders brushing.

"Was there ever such a day?" she said, her voice barely a whisper. She rested her head against his shoulder.

"There'll be others," he said, turning to wrap his arms around her. "Thousands of them, I hope." He looked down at her, his face serious in the soft glow from the room behind them. "You're not afraid anymore, are you, Ju?"

"No," she said, looking up at him. "Not of the old things. Not of not belonging. I only have one fear now."

"What's that?" he asked, his brow furrowing with concern.

"That I'll wake up," she murmured. "That I'll wake up and find myself back in my little room on Larkin Street, and this was all just the most wonderful dream."

"Jim!" she cried, laughing and clinging to him.

And as he set her down gently, Julia knew with a certainty that reached into the very soul of her that he was right. This was real. This was her life. And it was just beginning.

The final leg of the journey was by ferry, across the familiar, beloved bay. San Francisco rose from the water like a city in a fairytale, its hills painted gold by the late afternoon sun. Jim looked surprised, but pleased. "Mother did? Well, that's just like her! Always one step ahead."

She had assumed they were going to the Studdiford family home on Nob Hill, but after a few minutes, Dr. Studdiford turned down a quieter, tree-lined street she did not recognize. It was not a palace, not one of the imposing mansions that crowned the hills, but a house of substance and grace, set back from the road behind a neat iron fence and a lawn that was already greening with the promise of spring.

She led them from the bright foyer into a large, formal drawing room. It was exquisitely furnished. The rest of the hour passed in a similar vein. Eleanor Studdiford presided over the tea service, pouring from a heavy silver pot and asking Julia polite, pointed questions about her journey. She was the girl from the wrong side of the tracks, the girl who had pushed her way into the good belt, but here, in this perfect house, under the cool gaze of this perfect woman, she felt as if she were right back on Larkin Street, dreaming of a world that would never truly be hers. Jim, meanwhile, was entirely at ease, chatting with his mother about hospital gossip and mutual acquaintances, happily consuming sandwiches and cake. He was home. This was his world, his language. Julia looked at him, at his dear, happy, unsuspecting face, and felt a wave of love so fierce it was almost painful. He simply didn't see it. He couldn't. He had been born to this, ready-made. The right sort of people. The phrase echoed what Barbara had said, what the whole world of San Francisco society seemed to be whispering. She had been told, in the politest, most brutal way, that her old life, her old self, was an embarrassment to be discarded. She looked up at him, at his dear, rumpled hair, his earnest, loving face, and the last of the fear and despair melted away, replaced by a wave of love so powerful it almost brought her to her knees. The life that was just beginning was hers, after all. She just had to be brave enough to claim it.
