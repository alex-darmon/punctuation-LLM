CHAPTER VI

The days that followed settled into a new and strange routine for Margaret. Outwardly, nothing had changed. She was still the quiet, capable Miss Paget, organizing Mrs. Carr-Boldt's chaotic social life, balancing the cheque-book, reading aloud in the evenings when her employer's eyes were tired. But inwardly, a revolution was taking place. Every spare moment, every ounce of her energy, was now directed toward a single, secret goal.

Her first act of rebellion was small, but significant. She had been in the habit of spending her modest allowance on little things; a new ribbon for her hair, a pretty piece of stationery, the occasional novel. Now, she stopped. Every penny was counted, recorded in a small, private ledger, and tucked away. The small deprivations gave her a curious sense of satisfaction, as if each unbought trifle was a step toward her freedom.

Her second act was to write to the extension division of a university in the city. She requested their catalogue of correspondence courses, and when it arrived, a thin pamphlet full of thrilling possibilities, she spent hours poring over it in the dead of night. Latin? French? Advanced bookkeeping? The world of knowledge seemed to spread out before her, a vast and liberating territory. She chose a course in business administration, a practical choice, but one that felt like a solid, tangible tool. The first set of textbooks arrived in a plain brown wrapper, and she hid them at the bottom of her trunk as if they were jewels.

Her life became a study in contrasts. By day, she was immersed in the frivolous world of the Carr-Boldts. She listened to endless discussions about menus for dinner parties, about the guest list for a charity bazaar, about the appalling modern fashion of young women smoking cigarettes.
"Can you imagine, Peggy?" Mrs. Carr-Boldt would say, fanning herself indignantly. "Lily Allen, smoking in public! Her mother just laughed. I tell you, the world is going to the dogs."
Margaret would murmur a suitable reply, her mind a million miles away, wrestling with theories of capital and labor, of supply and demand. The conversations in the morning room, once a source of quiet pain, now seemed like a strange and distant comedy. The women's concerns were so small, their worldview so narrow, she felt a kind of pity for them. They were prisoners in their own gilded cages, bound by a thousand tiny rules and expectations, while she, in her plain little room with her secret books, was preparing to be free.

John Tenison's name was mentioned often. He was at this dinner, that yachting party. He was seen at the opera with the Vandercourts. Milly Vandercourt, it was universally agreed, was managing him beautifully.
"She's getting him to be much more sociable," Mrs. Watson reported one afternoon over tea. "He always was a bit of a stick-in-the-mud, wasn't he? Always with his nose in a book. Milly has him out and about. It's the best thing in the world for him."
Margaret listened, her pen flying across the page as she took notes for Mrs. Carr-Boldt's memoirs. She felt a pang, sharp and quick as a needle, but it was no longer the deep, tearing agony of before. It was the ache of a scar, a reminder of a wound that was beginning to heal. Her love for him had been a beautiful, impossible dream, a flower that could not grow in the thin soil of her life. Her new life, the one she was building for herself, would be made of sterner stuff; brick and mortar and hard-won knowledge.

One evening, Mrs. Carr-Boldt, feeling unusually energetic, decided to sort through a dusty trunk of old family papers that had been sent down from the attic.
"You can help me, Peggy," she commanded. "I want to find the letters my great-aunt Sophia wrote from Paris in the 'sixties. They're supposed to be quite scandalous."
For hours they worked, surrounded by bundles of yellowed letters tied with faded ribbons. The air grew thick with the scent of old paper and lavender. Margaret, her fingers dusty, her back aching, carefully sorted the papers, her mind on the chapter on corporate law she planned to read that night.
Suddenly, Mrs. Carr-Boldt gave a cry of triumph. "Ah, here we are! No, wait. What's this?" She pulled out a small, leather-bound diary. "Why, this is my grandmother's. I never knew she kept a diary."
She opened it, and began to read aloud, her voice filled with a new and softer interest. The diary was not scandalous or exciting. It was a simple, daily record of a young wife's life in the country a half-century before.

CHAPTER VI

The letter from John arrived on a Tuesday, tucked discreetly between a bill from a Parisian milliner and an invitation to a charity bazaar. “Yes,” Margaret said simply.

CHAPTER VI

The arrival of Dr. Tenison that evening was an exercise in masterful social orchestration, conducted by a woman who had been born to the task. The life that seemed to grow warmer, more rosy colored, was not a fourteen-day revelation. It was here, in this noisy, crowded, love-filled house, and it was just beginning.
