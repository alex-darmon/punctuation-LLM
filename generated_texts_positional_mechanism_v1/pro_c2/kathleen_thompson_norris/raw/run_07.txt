CHAPTER VI

The journey home was a penance. Every click of the train wheels on the track seemed to mock her, hammering out the rhythm of her failure. New York, New York, you are not for you. Eleanor stared out the window at the green fields of Connecticut flying past, but she did not see them. She had run away. There was no other word for it. She, who had prided herself on her courage, had turned tail and fled from a phantom, a whisper of slander, and worse, she had fled from the one person who had offered her genuine kindness.

The thought of her letter was a fresh torment. "I am very busy... returning to my family's home for a while." How hollow and false it had sounded, even as she wrote it. Julian was no fool. He would know it was an evasion, a rebuff. And he would think--what would he think? That she was capricious, ungrateful, a flirt who had tired of the game. Her pride, which she had always considered her finest armor, had become a cage of her own making. It had not protected her; it had isolated her.

She had been gone only a few months, but it seemed a lifetime.

"I'm just readjusting," she said evasively. "It's a different pace of life."

Margaret stared at him, aghast. "I can't just… not go back! They are depending on me. I have a responsibility!"

"Your responsibility is here now," he said, his eyes burning into hers. "With your family. With me. Let them find another girl to sign cheques and placate them. Your life is not with them anymore. It's with me. Marry me, Margaret. Marry me this week, and let me be the one to send the telegram."

The audacity of it, the sheer, breathtaking simplicity of his solution, left her speechless. Her parents, who had been watching from the doorway, looked as stunned as she felt.

"Marry you?" she whispered.

"Yes. Right away. There is nothing to wait for. I love you. You love me. We will live in a small house near the university, and you will wear ruffly things, and we will have a home like this one, filled with children and books instead of things-in-law and nervous collapses. Say yes, Margaret."

All her life, Margaret had been sensible. She had been responsible. She had done the thing that was required of her. Now, for the first time, she was being offered not what was required, but what she desired. The choice was so clear, so stark. On one side was the world of the Carr-Boldts, a world of borrowed luxury and perpetual servitude. On the other was this man, offering her a life that was real, a life that was her own.

She looked from John's intense, waiting face to her mother's. Mrs. Allen's eyes were shining with unshed tears, but her mouth was curved in a smile. "Well, Peggy," she said softly. "It seems the professor does not believe in wasting time."

And in her mother's smile, Margaret saw her answer. She saw her freedom. She turned back to John, and a slow, radiant smile spread across her own face, a smile of such pure, unadulterated joy that it transformed her.

"Yes," she said. "Oh, yes."

The silence that followed her single word was a profound and holy thing, lasting but a moment, yet containing the whole of her past and all the promise of her future. Then the small parlor erupted. John Tenison let out a sound that was half laugh, half whoop of triumph, and gathered her into his arms, lifting her clean off the worn rug. He kissed her not with the gentle reverence of their walk in the woods, but with the joyful, possessive certainty of a man who has just been handed the keys to the kingdom.

Margaret, breathless and laughing and crying all at once, clung to him. Over his shoulder she saw her mother, her face a beautiful ruin of happy tears, being hugged by her father, whose own eyes were suspiciously bright. The worried lines on Mr. Allen's face, etched there by years of quiet struggle, had vanished, replaced by a look of immense, wondering relief.

"John, put me down, you lunatic!" Margaret gasped, her hair coming undone from its neat braids and tumbling over his arm.

"Never," he declared, his voice muffled in her hair. "I shall carry you everywhere. To the church, to the university, to the butcher's. It will be a new local custom." He set her down, but kept her hands firmly in his, his face alight with an almost boyish glee. "Now," he said, turning to her parents with a look of mock seriousness. "Mr. and Mrs. Allen, I know this is sudden, but I am in love with your daughter, and I believe in acting upon the important truths of life. I may not have the fortune of a Carr-Boldt, but I have a decent professorship, a small inheritance from my father, and a profound admiration for families of seven. I promise to make her happy."

Mr. Allen cleared his throat, a gruff, rumbling sound. He was a man of few words, his hands more eloquent than his tongue. He stepped forward and put one of those large, calloused hands on the professor's shoulder. "Professor," he said, and his voice was thick with emotion. "You just did. Seeing my Peggy this happy… well. That's all a father wants."

Mrs. Allen, dabbing her eyes with a corner of her apron, beamed at them all. "Oh, John, we know you will! We've seen how she is with you. It's like the sun has come out after a long winter." She came forward and gave him a quick, maternal kiss on the cheek. "Now, what's all this about this week? You children are mad!" But her protest was without any real force; it was clear she was as swept up in the romance of it as anyone.

"Madness is exactly what's called for," John announced. "The world is too full of sensible, miserable people. We shall be gloriously, wonderfully mad. First thing is first." He turned to Margaret, his eyes dancing. "That telegram. We must send a reply. No, not a reply, a declaration of independence!"

He strode over to the little writing desk in the corner, where Margaret had so often sat addressing envelopes or correcting school papers. He picked up a pen as if it were a sword. Margaret watched him, her heart thumping a wild, joyful rhythm against her ribs. It was happening. It was all happening so fast, a torrent of happiness that threatened to sweep her away entirely.

He found a blank telegraph form in a drawer and began to write, his hand firm and swift. Margaret came to stand beside him, her hand resting on his shoulder, and read as he wrote.

"Mrs. Carr-Boldt," he murmured as he composed, "Care of Crawford Estate, Long Island. Stop. Regret to inform you Margaret Allen will not be returning. Stop. She is otherwise engaged. Stop. Permanently. Stop. Suggest you find a new secretary as she will be busy setting up her own household. Stop. Regards. John Tenison, Professor of History, future husband of the aforementioned."

Margaret gave a little gasp, a mixture of horror and delight. "John, you can't! It's… it's so abrupt! So rude!"

"It is honest," he said, not looking up from his work. "And it is final. Do you want to spend the next week writing long, apologetic letters, giving them an opportunity to argue, to persuade, to make you feel guilty? Or do you want to be free?" He looked up at her then, and his gaze was so direct, so full of understanding, that she felt a fresh wave of love for him. "Let this be the last time you ever have to explain yourself to them, Margaret. Let me cut the cord."

She looked at the words on the flimsy paper. "She is otherwise engaged. Stop. Permanently." It was the most beautiful sentence she had ever read. It was freedom, spelled out in clipped, paid-for words. "Send it," she whispered, a little shakily. "Oh, John, send it."

The next few days were a blur, a whirlwind of happy, disorganized chaos that was the very antithesis of her life with Mrs. Carr-Boldt. John, true to his word, had the telegram sent at once, and then seemed to banish the entire world of wealth and servitude from his mind. His focus was entirely on their immediate future, a future he seemed to be constructing before her very eyes with the force of his will.

"A license," he announced on Monday morning, arriving at the Allen house before the dew was off the grass. "We need a license. And a ring. And a minister. Margaret, have you a preference for any particular denomination, or shall we just find the nearest one with a free afternoon this week?"

"John!" she laughed. "You have to give me a moment to breathe!"

"No time for breathing," he said, pulling her out the door. "Breathing is for after we are married. For now, we plan."

The planning, as it were, was a family affair. The news of Peggy's engagement spread through the crowded little house like wildfire. Her younger siblings, who had always regarded the "professor" with a mixture of awe and suspicion, now mobbed him whenever he appeared. Young Tom, who was fourteen and fascinated by history, cornered him in the garden to ask about the Punic Wars, and John, to Margaret's delight, settled down on the grass and talked for a full hour, drawing maps in the dirt with a stick. Her twin sisters, Mary and Martha, aged sixteen and on the cusp of young womanhood themselves, regarded the whole affair with breathless, romantic sighs, trailing Margaret around the house, asking for every single detail of the proposal.

"And he really tore up the telegram?" Mary whispered, her eyes wide.

"And he just said, 'Marry me'?" Martha added, clutching a dish towel to her chest.

"Yes," Margaret would say, laughing at their vicarious excitement. "He really did."

It was an education for John, this immersion in the noisy, sprawling life of the Allens. Margaret watched him with a tender, anxious heart at first, wondering if the reality of her family, the worn furniture and the mended clothes and the constant, overlapping chatter, would tarnish his romantic "Dayton picture." She need not have worried. He seemed to thrive in the chaos.

On Tuesday evening, Mrs. Allen declared they must have a proper family dinner to celebrate. The dining room, which also served as the main thoroughfare to the kitchen, was barely large enough to contain them all. The table was extended with all its extra leaves, and even then, elbows knocked and chairs scraped together.

"All right, everyone, find a seat!" Mrs. Allen called out, flushed and happy as she brought in a great platter of fried chicken. "John, you sit here, next to Peggy. Father, you're at the head. Tom, stop kicking Martha under the table before I have your father speak to you!"

John took his seat beside Margaret, his eyes twinkling. "This is magnificent," he murmured to her under the cover of the din. "It's like a painting by a Dutch master. A very loud Dutch master."

He was seated across from nine-year-old Susie, the youngest, who stared at him with solemn, unblinking eyes for the first ten minutes of the meal. Finally, she pointed a greasy finger at him.

"You're the professor," she stated.

"I am," John agreed gravely, pausing with a piece of chicken halfway to his mouth.

"Peggy says you know everything about old things."

"Well, not everything," John said modestly. "But quite a lot."

"Do you know why my tooth is wiggly?" Susie demanded, opening her mouth to exhibit the offending incisor.

From the end of the table, Mr. Allen gave a small chuckle. "He's a history professor, Susie, not a dentist."

"Oh," she said, disappointed. Then she brightened. "Is history as old as my tooth?"

John, instead of being dismissive, leaned forward with great seriousness. "That is an excellent question," he said. "History is the story of everything that has ever happened, so it is much, much older than your tooth. But the moment your tooth falls out, that becomes a part of your personal history. A very important event!"

Susie seemed satisfied with this, and the conversation became general again, a cheerful babble of school news and neighborhood gossip. John listened to it all, asking questions, laughing at Mr. Allen's quiet jokes, and seemingly winning over every member of the family, from skeptical Tom to shy little Susie.

Later that evening, after the younger children had been shooed off to bed, he and Margaret sat on the front porch swing, the air sweet with the smell of Mrs. Allen's honeysuckle.

"I was worried," Margaret confessed softly, her head on his shoulder.

"Worried? About what?"

"About this." She gestured vaguely toward the house, from which the sounds of dish-washing and sibling squabbles could still be heard. "That it would be too much. Too crowded, too poor. That your beautiful idea of my family would be ruined by the reality of it."

He was silent for a moment, and her heart gave a little anxious flutter. Then he took her hand and brought it to his lips. "Margaret," he said, his voice low and earnest. "Don't you see? This is the reality I was looking for. When I was with the Carr-Boldts and their circle, I felt like I was in a museum. Everything was beautiful, and polished, and behind glass. No one was allowed to touch anything. It was all about what one had. This," he squeezed her hand, "this is about what one is. This house is full of life. It is not poor; it is rich in the only way that matters."

He pulled a small, worn velvet box from his pocket. "I know it isn't what the Allens or the Carr-Boldts would choose," he said, "but I found it in an old shop in the city, and it seemed to belong to you. It's old, you see."

He opened the box. Inside, nestled on faded silk, was a small, delicate ring. It was not a diamond. It was a single, perfect pearl, set in a filigree of old, soft-hued gold. It was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen.

"Oh, John," she breathed, as he slipped it onto her finger. It fit perfectly.

"Aunt Pam gave me some good advice once," he said, smiling. "She said, 'John, never give a woman a diamond unless she is the sort of woman who counts them. Give a pearl to a woman who will understand it is the one pearl in the world.'" He kissed her finger. "You, my dear Miss Allen, are that woman."

Their wedding day arrived at the end of the week, a Thursday, bright and clear and full of the promise of early autumn. There was no fanfare, no cascade of flowers, no engraved invitations. Margaret wore a simple cream-colored dress, one of the "ruffly things" that John so admired, which she and her mother had stayed up late the night before to finish. She wore her hair not in a complicated up-do, but in the thick, shining braids he loved, woven with a few late-blooming white asters from the garden.

The ceremony was held in the small front parlor, the same room where he had proposed. It was crowded with her family, the scent of lavender and chicken soup and old books filling the air. The minister from the little church down the road spoke of love and companionship, his voice a gentle drone against the backdrop of a happy, humming house.

Margaret was barely aware of the words. Her entire being was focused on John, who stood beside her, his hand holding hers, his face alight with a love so pure and strong it felt like a physical warmth. When he slid the plain gold band onto her finger, next to the treasured pearl, she felt a sense of rightness, of coming home, that was so profound it brought tears to her eyes.

Afterwards, there was a great, lopsided cake baked by Mrs. Allen, and lemonade served in mismatched glasses. Her father made a short, halting speech about his pride in his daughter and his gladness in his new son. Her brothers and sisters presented them with a clumsily wrapped gift: a book of poetry, its cover already smudged with fingerprints, with a painstakingly inscribed card signed by all of them. Margaret hugged each one of them in turn, her heart overflowing. This was her wealth. These noisy, loving people were her treasure.

Two days later, a letter arrived, forwarded from her old address. It bore the crisp, elegant crest of Mrs. Carr-Boldt. Margaret held it in her hand for a long moment, a ghost from another life. She was sitting in the sunlit breakfast nook of the small house John had rented near the university, a house filled with books and the scent of fresh paint.

She slit it open with a butter knife. The handwriting was sharp, angry.

"Margaret," it began, without salutation. "Your telegram was an act of insubordination and ingratitude of the highest order. After all I have done for you, to be abandoned in such a manner is unthinkable. I was forced to cancel my trip to Newport as I could not possibly manage my correspondence alone. Mrs. Watson agrees it is the most selfish thing she has ever heard of. I suppose this professor has filled your head with nonsense about love in a cottage. You will learn, as all foolish girls do, that love does not pay the bills. You have made a grave error, and I assure you, you will not find another position in a respectable house again. Your name will be remembered for this."

It was signed, "H. Carr-Boldt."

Margaret read it through twice. A year ago, a week ago, such a letter would have devastated her. It would have sent her into a spiral of guilt and anxiety. Now, she looked around the cheerful little kitchen. She could hear John in his study down the hall, whistling off-key as he unpacked his books. The pearl on her finger caught the morning light.

She folded the letter, a faint smile playing on her lips. Mrs. Carr-Boldt was right about one thing. Her name would be remembered. But it would be as Mrs. John Tenison. She walked to the small fireplace, struck a match, and held the corner of the heavy linen stationery to the flame. She watched as the angry black script curled, turned brown, and dissolved into gray, weightless ash. And as the last of it floated up the chimney, Margaret felt the last vestige of the paid dependent, the little country school teacher, float away with it, leaving only a happy woman, at home at last, in its place.

"Theoretically," said Mrs. Carr-Boldt, with a little sniff. "In practice, you find that the minute you turn your back, the nurse is giving the baby a pacifier, or letting the toddler eat cake. The conversation, like so many others, had washed over Margaret, leaving only a faint residue of bewilderment. These women, with all their privileges, spoke of children as one might speak of a perilous and expensive hobby, like breeding prize orchids or racing yachts. They saw only the complications, the expense, the endless, fretting details. They never spoke of the joy. They never mentioned a child's hand slipping into one's own, or the sound of laughter in a quiet house.

Now, in her own small house, joy was the very air she breathed. The first few months of her marriage to John Tenison were an idyll of such simple, profound happiness that it sometimes brought a lump to her throat. Life was not, as Mrs. Carr-Boldt had so confidently predicted, a severe and endless struggle. It was a partnership. They were poor, yes, by the standards of Bar Harbor, but they were not poor by the standards of a world that knew real want. John's salary as a professor, which would have seemed a pittance to the Carr-Boldt circle, was more than enough for their modest needs.

Their little rented house, with its slightly crooked porch and its garden full of unruly rose bushes, was a palace to her. She filled it with five-and-dime-store curtains that billowed in the breeze, and cheap, cheerful pottery that she arranged on the old oak sideboard. She learned the art of making a little go a long way, of turning inexpensive cuts of meat into savory stews, of mending John's socks by the fire in the evening while he read aloud to her from his beloved books. This was not the drudgery she had always feared; it was a labor of love, a daily act of creation.

John, for his part, was completely, blissfully happy. He had never known a home like this. His life with Aunt Pam, while full of intellectual vigor, had been an austere one, a life of boarding houses and furnished rooms. To have a home of his own, and to have Margaret in it, was a miracle he never ceased to marvel at. He would often look up from his papers, his face lit with a sort of wondering delight, and simply watch her as she moved about the room, her bright hair catching the light.

"You know," he said one afternoon, finding her in the kitchen humming as she kneaded bread, "I don't think I have ever eaten so well in my entire life. Aunt Pam's idea of a meal was a chop and a boiled potato. She considered anything else a concession to the flesh."

Margaret laughed, dusting flour from her hands. "Well, I hope this is a concession you can grow used to. I thought we might have your friend Professor Albright over for supper next week. Do you think he'd mind pot roast?"

"Mind it? My dear girl, he will probably propose to you on the spot," John said, stealing a piece of raw dough and popping it into his mouth. "But there is one person whose judgment I await with far more trepidation." He grew serious, his eyes twinkling. "Aunt Pam has written. She is coming for a visit."

A little flutter of anxiety went through Margaret. Aunt Pam. The woman who had been his only mother, the woman who detested the usual New York girl. "Oh, John! When?"

"The week after next. She's attending a philosophical conference in Boston and will stop for two days on her way back. Now, don't you look so worried," he said, taking her floury hands in his. "She will adore you. I told you that from the first. You are precisely the sort of woman she admires, sensible and good, with no nonsense about you."

Despite his assurances, Margaret spent the next ten days in a fever of preparation. She scrubbed and polished the little house until it shone. She worried endlessly over menus, rejecting one idea after another as either too plain or too pretentious. She even bought a new dress, a simple thing of dark blue wool, hoping it would make her look mature and capable. Aunt Pam loomed in her imagination as a figure of immense, almost terrifying, intellect and moral rectitude. She was the final test. To win her approval felt, to Margaret, almost as important as winning John's had been.

Miss Pamela Tenison arrived on a Tuesday, on the afternoon train. John went to meet her at the station, and Margaret waited on the porch, her hands cold with nerves. When she saw them walking up the path, she was surprised. She had pictured someone tall and severe, but Aunt Pam was a small, wiry woman, with sharp, bird-like features and eyes of an astonishingly bright, intelligent blue. Her gray hair was pulled back in a tight, uncompromising bun, and she wore a plain, well-worn tweed suit that had clearly been chosen for durability rather than style.

"So," said Aunt Pam, stopping at the foot of the porch steps and fixing Margaret with a look that seemed to take in everything at a single glance. "You are the girl who lured my John away from his books." Her voice was crisp and dry, without a trace of warmth.

"I'm Margaret," she said, managing a smile. "I'm so happy to finally meet you. Please, come in."

Aunt Pam did not smile back. She mounted the steps and entered the house, her keen eyes sweeping over the living room, noting the darned spot on the armchair, the slightly faded rug, the profusion of books. "Hmph. It is clean, I'll grant you that." She ran a gloved finger along a windowsill and inspected it. "Very clean."

Tea was a strained affair. Aunt Pam sat bolt upright in her chair, holding her cup and saucer as if they were scientific instruments. She asked a series of brisk, pointed questions, not about Margaret's family or her feelings, but about household management.

"And what do you pay for butter, may I ask?" she demanded. "The price in the city is an outrage. And your butcher? Does he give you honest weight?"

Margaret, who had dreaded questions about the Carr-Boldts and Bar Harbor, found herself on surprisingly firm ground. She answered with the quiet confidence of a woman who knew her own accounts. "Thirty-two cents a pound, but I get it from a farm just outside of town," she replied. "And Mr. Henderson is very reliable. His wife is in my church guild."

Aunt Pam's expression did not change, but a flicker of something, perhaps respect, showed in her eyes. "I see. And John tells me you have a large family. Seven, is it?"

"Yes," Margaret said, her heart warming at the thought of them. "Four brothers and two sisters."

"And your father is a... a teacher, I believe?"

"A schoolmaster, yes. In a small country school."

"And you find you can manage on John's salary?" The question was direct, almost brutal.

John started to speak, a flush rising on his cheeks, but Margaret answered calmly. "We manage very well," she said simply. "We have everything we need."

Later that evening, after a supper of the very pot roast Margaret had worried over, a meal which Aunt Pam ate with silent, efficient concentration, the older woman made an announcement. "John, I should like to speak with your wife. Alone."

John, looking startled, rose at once. "Of course, Aunt Pam." He gave Margaret a look of encouragement and retreated to his study.

Margaret's heart began to pound again. She sat, her hands clasped in her lap, and waited. Aunt Pam was silent for a long moment, staring into the fire.

"I did not approve of this marriage, you know," she said at last, without looking at Margaret. "I had a different life pictured for John. An academic life. Unfettered. I feared he had been ensnared by a pretty face, a society girl from that frivolous world he foolishly wandered into."

She turned her piercing blue eyes on Margaret. "That woman you worked for, Mrs. Carr-Boldt. I know her type. All vanity and nerves. I assumed you would be a pale imitation. I assumed you would complain about this little house, that you would miss the servants and the motor-cars, that you would run up debts and make my boy's life a misery."

She paused, and the silence in the room was broken only by the crackle of the fire. "I was wrong," she said, the words clipped and precise. "You are not like that at all. You have sense. And you have courage. It takes courage to leave a world of ease for a world of work. And you love him. I see it in your eyes when you look at him."

Tears sprang to Margaret's eyes, but she blinked them back. "I do," she whispered.

"He is a good boy, but he is not a practical man," Aunt Pam went on, her tone softening almost imperceptibly. "He lives in his books. He needs a woman to anchor him to the earth. I see now that you are that woman. You will be good for him." She stood up, her small figure straight and dignified. "You have my blessing, for what it is worth." And with that, she turned and went to her room, leaving Margaret sitting by the fire, overwhelmed with a relief so profound it felt like a benediction.

The next few months passed in a happy, productive blur. Winter settled over the university town, and their little house became a cozy fortress against the snow and wind. Margaret's days fell into a comfortable rhythm: housework in the morning, a walk into town for errands in the afternoon, and long, quiet evenings with John. She joined the church guild, and made friends with other young faculty wives, women who, like her, knew how to count their pennies and turn a worn sheet side to middle.

It was on one of her afternoon walks that the ghost of her past life appeared. She had stopped in front of the town's one fashionable dress shop, not with any intention of buying, but merely to admire a gown of emerald green velvet in the window. It was the sort of thing Mrs. Carr-Boldt would have called a "simple little frock," and it probably cost more than their rent for three months.

As she stood there, a large, gleaming motor-car pulled up to the curb, and from it emerged a figure swathed in furs and feathers. It was Maude Allen. She was louder, richer, and more arrogant than ever. She did not see Margaret at first, but swept into the shop, calling out to the proprietress in her shrill, commanding voice.

"Mrs. Phillips! I need something stunning for the Assembly next week! Something absolutely new!"

Margaret turned to go, a familiar feeling of invisibility and resentment prickling at her. She was just the secretary, the paid dependent, after all. But then she stopped. No, she was not. She was Mrs. John Tenison, a respected professor's wife. She had a home, and a husband who loved her. She had nothing to be ashamed of.

Drawing a deep breath, she followed Maude Allen into the shop. She needed thread, and she was not going to be chased out of a public establishment by the memory of her own servitude. The shop girl, a young woman she knew from the guild, smiled at her. "Good afternoon, Mrs. Tenison. Can I help you?"

At the sound of the name, Maude Allen turned. Her eyes widened in surprise, and then a slow, patronizing smile spread across her face. "Well, I'll be! If it isn't little Peggy Paget. Or, I'm sorry, what was that name? Tenison?" She looked Margaret up and down, taking in the simple wool coat and the sturdy walking shoes. "My dear, what are you doing in this little town? I heard you'd run off with some professor. Is this where he keeps you?"

"My husband has a chair in the Philosophy department at the university," Margaret said, her voice steady and cool. "We live here, yes. It's a very pleasant town."

"Pleasant!" Maude gave a hoot of laughter. "My dear girl, it's the jumping-off place! But I suppose it's what you're used to. Mamma was furious with you, you know. Absolutely livid. Said she'd never met with such ingratitude. We had to get a new girl from a typing school, and she can't spell to save her life."

"I'm sorry to hear that," Margaret said politely. She turned to the shop girl. "I'll have three spools of the white cotton thread, please, Mary."

Maude was not to be ignored. "We're just passing through," she announced to the room at large. "On our way to the Springs for the cure. Daddy's digestion is a wreck again. I told him it was all that champagne at Lily's wedding." She lowered her voice to a conspiratorial whisper. "She married that dreadful boy from Boston, you know, the one with the yacht. They say he drinks like a fish, but his family has tons of money, so I suppose it will all work out."

She chattered on, about parties and jewels and scandals, a torrent of meaningless noise. Margaret paid for her thread, nodded a polite goodbye to Mrs. Phillips, and walked out of the shop, her head held high. She did not look back.

Walking home through the crisp winter air, she felt not anger, not even the old, familiar sting of humiliation. She felt a strange sort of pity. Maude Allen, with all her furs and her money, seemed trapped in a world as small and airless as a jewel box. Her life was a frantic pursuit of the next pleasure, the next sensation, with no room for quiet, or contentment, or love.

When she got home, the house was warm and smelled of the soup she had left simmering on the stove. John was in his study, surrounded by his books, a look of serene concentration on his face. He looked up as she came in and his whole face broke into a smile.

"There you are," he said. "I was just thinking about you."

She went to him and put her arms around his neck, burying her face in his shoulder. She was home. This was her world, her real life.

That evening, as they sat by the fire, she told him about her encounter with Maude Allen. He listened quietly, his hand covering hers.

"And you know," she finished, a note of wonder in her voice, "I wasn't even angry. I just felt... sorry for her."

"That," said John, his eyes full of love and pride, "is because you have something she will never have. It is because you are rich, my darling Margaret, in all the ways that truly matter."

She leaned her head against his shoulder, watching the flames dance in the hearth. The conversation she had overheard so long ago, in the white-and-gray morning room, came back to her. The talk of money and complications, the fear of children, the sterile, calculated approach to life. Mrs. Carr-Boldt had been so certain that love did not pay the bills, that a life like this was an error. But she had been wrong.

Margaret looked around the cozy, book-filled room. She thought of her noisy, loving family, of Aunt Pam's grudging but genuine approval, of the kind new friends she had made. She felt John's hand, warm and strong, holding hers. It was true that love did not pay the bills. But it built a home where a bill was just a piece of paper, a problem to be solved together. It built a life where joy was not a rare and expensive commodity, but the simple, steady pulse of every day. And as she looked into the fire, a new and hopeful thought began to take root in her heart, a quiet, thrilling little secret. Perhaps, before too long, their small, happy house would have the sound of laughter in it.
