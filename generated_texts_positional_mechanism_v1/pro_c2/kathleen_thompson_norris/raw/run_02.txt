Later, at dinner, she was seated between a portly, bald man who talked of nothing but stocks and bonds, and a young, silent man who seemed to be afraid of his food. Across the table, she could see Jim, seated next to a vivacious young woman in a red dress who was clearly the Caroline Cushing whose heart he had supposedly broken. She was laughing up at him, her hand resting on his arm, and for a moment, a sharp, ugly pang of jealousy went through Julia. But Jim, feeling her eyes on him, looked over and gave her a small, secret wink, a quick, almost imperceptible shake of his head, as if to say, "This is all nonsense, isn't it? You and I are the only real people here." And suddenly, Julia was not afraid anymore. She turned to the portly man beside her. "You mentioned the railroad," she said, her voice clear and calm. She talked about San Francisco, about the rebuilding after the fire, about the spirit of the people there. She talked about the hospital, about the courage of the patients, the dedication of the doctors. She did not raise her voice, she did not try to impress. She simply spoke her truth, in her own voice. Slowly, the man beside her put down his fork and began to listen. The silent young man looked up. Even Mrs. Vance, from the head of the table, seemed to be paying attention. It was something quieter, and more important. It was the simple act of being herself.
