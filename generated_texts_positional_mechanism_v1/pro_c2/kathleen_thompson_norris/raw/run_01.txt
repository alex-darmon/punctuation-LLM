"You must find us terribly dull after London, Mr. Davies," Celia remarked, tilting her parasol just so, to cast a becoming shadow across her face. "No theater, no grand balls, nothing but birdsong and endless green."

"On the contrary, Mrs. Ashton," the young architect replied, his gaze drifting from the meticulously manicured rose garden to the young woman seated quietly on the stone bench. "I find it a welcome reprieve. There's an honesty to the country. It doesn't pretend." He turned his full attention to the other woman. "You play beautifully, Miss Vance. That Chopin piece was, well, it was more than just notes."

Eleanor felt a warmth rise in her cheeks that had nothing to do with the afternoon sun. "My father taught me," she said simply. "He believes music should tell a story." It was not necessary to add, "My father, a retired country parson with failing eyes, sits in a worn armchair in a house that smells of old books and damp wool, and finds his only color in the music I play for him. Our life is a world away from this." No, that would be an imposition. She merely smoothed a wrinkle in her plain muslin skirt. No, Mr. Davies would enjoy his stay here at Northwood Manor, he would talk with her of books and music, and then he would return to his world of blueprints and wealthy clients. And in time, perhaps, the ache of this brief, impossible connection would fade.

For one glorious week, Eleanor felt as if she were the heroine of a novel. The days unfolded in a soft, sun-drenched haze. Conversations with Theodore Davies became the focal point of her existence; a shared glance across the dinner table, a brief moment alone in the library, a walk along the gravel paths. Her quiet nature, which the Ashtons and their friends treated with a sort of kindly indifference, seemed to him a thing of great charm. A new confidence settled upon her; she spoke more, she laughed more easily, the reserve in her deep blue eyes melting away to reveal a startling vivacity. She and Mr. Davies moved through the placid routines of the great house like two people who shared a delightful secret, too content in the present to question the future.

Then, inevitably, the spell was broken. A motor-car, impossibly loud and kicking up a cloud of dust, roared up the drive, and from it spilled the Sinclair sisters, Florence and Clara, all shrill laughter and expensive French perfume. They descended upon the quiet garden party like a flock of brilliant, noisy parrots.

"Teddy, you rogue!" cried Florence, tapping his arm with her fan. "Hiding away from us in the wilderness! Mamma said we'd find you here!"

"Don't be horrid, Flo," Clara chirped, linking her arm through his. "He's been bored to tears, I'm sure. We've come to rescue you. We're having a party on the yacht tomorrow, you simply must come!"

"I was just enjoying the, ah, the tranquility," Mr. Davies said, a faint line of annoyance appearing between his brows, but he was already being drawn away into their chattering circle. Eleanor’s last sight of him was as he was maneuvered between Florence’s towering hat of silk roses and Clara’s aggressively fashionable feathered boa. A secret coldness seeped into her heart, and it remained there through the long evening and the sleepless night that followed. For the first time in her life, Eleanor drooped, and for the first time a sharp, painful envy for the things she had always scorned, for money and the careless power it conferred, gnawed at her. With it, she could hold her own against a thousand Florence and Clara Sinclairs. As it was, she was only the governess, a useful and quiet piece of household machinery.

CHAPTER VIII

"Are you quite packed for your little holiday, Eleanor? It must be lovely to get back to your own family," said Mrs. Ashton vaguely. She was reclining on a chaise longue, a silk pillow behind her head, flipping through a glossy magazine. "I do hope the trains won't be dreadfully crowded. It's so unpleasant to travel with the public."

Sunlight fell in buttery slabs across the Persian rug, illuminating dust motes dancing in the still, warm air. The scent of lilies from a vase on the mantelpiece was almost overpowering. Eleanor, folding her last blouse and placing it neatly in her worn leather suitcase, offered a small smile.

"We're quite used to it, Mrs. Ashton," she replied.

"I suppose you are," Mrs. Ashton conceded, without a trace of irony. "It's all a question of what one is accustomed to, isn't it? Celia was just saying yesterday how she couldn't possibly imagine living without a proper ladies' maid. I mean, who would do one's hair? It's a genuine problem!"

"A terrifying thought," came a dry voice from the doorway. It was Mrs. Albright, a neighboring widow with a sharp tongue and an even sharper eye for social folly. "In my mother's day, a girl was fortunate to have a ribbon that wasn't frayed. We've become hopelessly soft, all of us."

"Oh, I don't know about soft," Mrs. Ashton demurred, turning a page. "Life is simply more demanding now. Think of all the engagements one has! The fittings, the teas, the dinners. It's exhausting just to keep up. Our grandmothers had one good black silk and they wore it for ten years."

"And they were probably happier for it," Mrs. Albright sniffed, seating herself in a brocade armchair. "This modern obsession with novelty is a disease. A new gown for every event, a new destination for every holiday. You went to Rome last year, Alice. This year it must be Egypt. Next year, who knows? The moon, I suppose, if someone builds a hotel there."

"Well, one must have experiences," said Mrs. Ashton defensively. "It broadens the mind. We had a simply marvelous guide in Rome, an old professor. He made the whole Forum come alive. Though I confess," she added with a little laugh, "after the third ruin, they all began to look rather the same. Just piles of stone, really."

"Exactly," Mrs. Albright said with satisfaction. "You go to see, but you don't truly look. You collect places like you collect pearls. My husband, God rest his soul, never went farther than Paris, but he could tell you the history of every stone in our little village church. He read. He paid attention. That is what broadens the mind."

"Oh, reading is all very well for a winter evening," Mrs. Ashton said dismissively. "But it isn't the same as truly being there, is it? We're going to Greece next spring. The agency says the new yachts are utterly divine, with real bathtubs. One simply cannot be expected to travel without a proper bath."

Eleanor, having finally secured the last strap on her suitcase, made her quiet farewells, and was presently conveyed to the station in the second motor-car, a vehicle used primarily for errands and staff, but which to her felt like a magnificent chariot departing from a world of enchantment. Mrs. Albright had given her a sharp, not unkindly nod, and Mrs. Ashton had pressed a folded bill into her hand.

"For your mother, dear," she had whispered, with an air of benevolent conspiracy. "Buy the children some sweets. It must be so difficult for her, with so many."

A faint flush of shame and gratitude warred within Eleanor. She murmured her thanks, her eyes on the floor, hating the feeling of being a recipient of charity, yet knowing how very much the ten dollars would mean at home. It would buy shoes for little Tom, or the medicine her mother had been putting off purchasing for her cough. She tucked it deep into her purse, a secret brand of her dependency.

The train was precisely as Mrs. Ashton had predicted: dreadfully crowded. It was a hot, clammy, jostling world of tired mothers with fretful babies, stout men in shirtsleeves reading newspapers, and a general air of weariness and wilted collars. The scent was a mixture of coal smoke, orange peels, and warm humanity. Eleanor found a seat by a window, squeezing her suitcase between her knees and placing her small hatbox on her lap. The plush seats of the Ashton motor, the scent of lilies and leather, seemed a world away, a dream from which she had just been rudely awakened. Greece, she thought with a wry inner smile, with private yachts and real bathtubs. How little they knew of the real world, of this world, which was for millions of people the only one that existed.

She leaned her head against the grimy glass as the train shuddered into motion, watching the manicured green of the countryside give way to the grimier, more crowded outskirts of the city, then to the city itself, a blur of brick and stone. She felt a familiar pang of dislocation, the strange sensation of living two lives, neither of which felt entirely her own. In one, she was a silent, efficient machine, a pair of hands to write cheques and a pleasant voice to answer the telephone. In the other, she was a sister, a daughter, a part of a boisterous, struggling, loving whole.

"I beg your pardon, is this seat taken?"

The voice was masculine, and pleasant. Eleanor started and looked up. A young man was standing in the aisle, gesturing to the small space beside her. He was not handsome in the chiseled, predictable way of the young men who played tennis at the Ashtons' club, but he had a clean, intelligent face, a pair of earnest gray eyes, and a slight smile that crinkled their corners. He was holding a worn copy of a book on architecture and a rather battered-looking portfolio.

"No, not at all," she said, pulling her coat more tightly around her.

He slid into the seat with a grateful sigh, stowing his portfolio carefully beneath his legs. "Thank you. I thought for a moment I was doomed to stand all the way to the city. The weekend exodus."

"Yes," Eleanor agreed. "Everyone is going somewhere."

"And you? Are you going home, or running away from it?" he asked, his tone light.

She was surprised into a genuine smile. "Going home, I think. And you?"

"A bit of both," he admitted. "I'm an architect, or trying to be. I've been upstate looking at a site for a new library. A wonderful old building, a shame to tear it down, but the town council has its heart set on something modern. So I've been taking notes, trying to see if I can't persuade them to incorporate some of the old character. A fool's errand, most likely." He looked at her directly, and she felt he was not just making conversation, but was truly interested in her response.

"It doesn't sound like a fool's errand to me," she said softly. "It sounds like a worthy one. So much is torn down just for the sake of newness." She thought of Mrs. Albright's words about the disease of novelty.

"My thoughts exactly!" he said, his face lighting up. "People forget that a building has a soul, a history. These new glass-and-steel boxes, they have no stories to tell. My name is Arthur Davies, by the way."

"Eleanor Vance," she replied.

They fell into a comfortable conversation. He talked of his work, of the buildings he dreamed of creating, buildings that would serve the people who used them, that would have light and air and beauty without costing a fortune. He spoke not of collecting experiences in Greece, but of a walking trip he had taken through the English countryside, sketching old churches and cottages. Eleanor found herself talking, too, more than she ever did with the Ashtons' circle. She spoke of her love for books, of the little town where she grew up, editing, as Margaret had, the details of her life, but feeling, with him, that the truth would not be a source of shame. A little secret thrill ran through her, a warmth that had nothing to do with the stuffy air of the train car. When he looked at her, his clear gray eyes saw not Mrs. Ashton's secretary, but a young woman with thoughts and opinions of her own.

All too soon, the train was pulling into the vast, echoing cavern of the city station. The spell was broken. They were once again two anonymous people in a churning crowd.

"Well, Miss Vance," he said, retrieving his portfolio as they stood in the aisle. "This has been the most pleasant journey I have had in a very long time."

"For me, too, Mr. Davies."

"Perhaps," he began, then hesitated. "Might I have your address? I should very much like to continue our conversation about books and buildings."

Eleanor's heart gave a little leap, and then fell. Her address. The crowded, noisy house in a street of other crowded, noisy houses. What would he think, this man who dreamed of beautiful, orderly spaces? She thought of Margaret's dilemma, of the professor and Bar Harbor and the Carr-Boldts. But then she looked at his face, at the genuine kindness in his eyes, and a little spark of courage asserted itself. She was not Margaret Paget; she was Eleanor Vance. And this was not a Tenison, accustomed to wealth. This was a man who worked for a living, who carried a battered portfolio and respected old things.

"Of course," she said, and taking the pencil he offered, she wrote her address on a slip of paper from her purse. She handed it to him, her hand trembling slightly.

He smiled as he took it. "Thank you. I will write to you, Eleanor Vance."

And then he was gone, swallowed up by the crowd, leaving Eleanor with a racing heart and the name Arthur Davies echoing in her mind.

CHAPTER VI

The last leg of the journey was on a smaller, local train, and then a short walk from the station. The air here was different, tinged with the smell of coal smoke from the nearby factory and the scent of damp earth from the small, neat vegetable gardens behind each house. The houses on her street stood shoulder to shoulder, each with a tiny patch of lawn and a welcoming front porch. Children's shouts echoed in the late afternoon air. This was home.

She opened the front door and was met with a wall of warm, chaotic life. The scent of stewing beef and onions from the kitchen, the sound of her sister Mary's voice, sharp and commanding, directing the younger ones, the muffled thump of her father's boots as he came down the stairs.

"Ellie's home!" nine-year-old Tom shouted, abandoning his toy soldiers on the floor and launching himself at her legs.

"Careful, you little monster, you'll knock her over!" This was Mary, emerging from the kitchen, a smudge of flour on her cheek. She was two years older than Eleanor, pretty in a tired, capable way, her hair already losing its girlhood softness from being constantly twisted into a practical knot. "Let her get her coat off, at least."

Her mother came next, wiping her hands on her apron. Mrs. Vance was a small, wiry woman, whose face showed the tracks of too many worries and too little sleep, but whose eyes, the same dark blue as Eleanor's, were full of an indomitable love.

"Eleanor, darling. It's so good to have you." She hugged her daughter tightly, a hug that spoke of a week of missing her. "You look thin. Aren't they feeding you?"

"They're feeding me far too much," Eleanor laughed, hugging her back. "Oh, it's good to be home."

The house, after the cavernous, silent spaces of the Ashton residence, seemed impossibly small and full. Every surface was covered with something: a pile of mending on a chair, schoolbooks on the dining table, her father's newspaper, a clutter of mugs. But it was a living clutter, the evidence of a family's full and complicated life. This was the reality that Mrs. Watson, who couldn't stand a "houseful of things-in-law," would never understand. Here, a houseful was the very definition of home.

"Did you have a good week, dear?" her mother asked, herding her toward the kitchen. "How is Mrs. Ashton's digestion?"

"As delicate as ever," Eleanor said, smiling. "And Mrs. Albright came to call. She told Mrs. Ashton that her obsession with novelty was a disease."

Mary snorted. "For once, the rich are talking sense."

Dinner was a noisy, crowded affair around the big oak table. Her father, a foreman at the factory, was quiet, eating with the steady, focused appetite of a man who had earned his meal with a long day of physical labor. He asked Eleanor a few questions about her week, his brow furrowed as he tried to imagine the world she inhabited, a world of ladies who did nothing but consider their travel plans and criticize their finger-nails. The younger children, Tom and six-year-old Clara, bickered over the last piece of bread. Mary recounted the week's domestic crises: the leaky sink, Clara's fever, a dispute with the iceman.

"It's just one thing after another," Mary said, sighing, as she began to clear the plates. "Sometimes I feel like all I do is run in circles, scrubbing and mending and cooking."

"Now, Mary," their mother said gently. "It's a good life. We have each other."

Eleanor, helping to wash the dishes in the steamy little kitchen, felt a pang of guilt. Her life, for all its emotional complexities, was physically easy. She had quiet rooms and meals she did not have to cook. Mary, who was just as bright, just as pretty, was already bound to the wheel of domestic duty. She remembered the ten-dollar bill in her purse. Later, when they were alone, she would press it into her mother's hand, just as Mrs. Ashton had pressed it into hers, and they would plan its careful disbursement.

After the little ones were in bed, the three women sat on the porch in the gathering twilight, enjoying the cool breeze. The sounds of the neighborhood were a comfort: a piano being inexpertly played a few houses down, the murmur of conversation from other porches, the distant rumble of a train.

"They were talking about families today," Eleanor said, her voice musing. "At the Ashtons'. About how people don't have children anymore, how it's all too complicated and expensive." She recounted the conversation about sterilizing and fumigating, about trained nurses who took two baths a day.

Mary gave a short, bitter laugh. "Imagine! I'm lucky if I get one bath a week in peace. Complicated! They don't know the meaning of the word. Try making Father's wages stretch to feed five mouths and see how complicated life gets. Nursery refrigerators! I'd be grateful for an icebox that didn't leak."

"It's a different world, that's all," Mrs. Vance said, her worn hands resting in her lap. She was not a bitter woman. "They do what they know, we do what we must. But I wouldn't trade one of you for all their quiet and their fancy nurses. A house isn't a home without the sound of children, even if it's the sound of them fighting." She looked at Eleanor, her expression soft. "You're happy there, though, Ellie? They're good to you?"

"They're very good to me," Eleanor said, and it was true, in its way. They were kind, if thoughtless. But was she happy? She was… suspended. Waiting. For what, she did not know. Until today.

She thought of Arthur Davies, of his earnest gray eyes and his talk of buildings with souls. She thought of the slip of paper with her address on it, now in his pocket. He had promised to write. A letter, addressed to her, would arrive at this very house. It would be a link between her two worlds, a secret thread of promise that belonged only to her. It would not be a yacht trip with the Carr-Boldts or a luncheon brought down by motor-car. It would be something quieter, something realer.

A smile touched her lips, a secret, hopeful smile that her mother and sister, deep in their own thoughts, did not see. The life of a paid dependent was lonely, and the longing for money and power could be a sharp and bitter thing. But perhaps, just perhaps, there were other things in the world that could hold their own against a hundred Maude and Lily Allens. Perhaps a man who loved old churches and a girl who loved books could build a world of their own, a world with a soul, a place where it was not a question of what one was accustomed to, but of what one chose to build together. For the first time in a long time, Eleanor felt that she was not just waiting, but was on the very brink of living.

Eleanor, signing her own name at the bottom of a list of household expenses, felt a faint blush rise to her cheeks. She was, after all, one of those "silly young things" hired to help manage a life too complicated for its owner. She folded the paper, slipped it into its envelope, and sealed it with a decisive tap. The conversation flowed on, a lazy river of complaint and self-justification, but she did not hear it. She was thinking of the cool evening porch at home, of her mother's quiet wisdom, and of a house that was a home precisely because of the seven souls who made it complicated.

The promised letter did not come the next day, nor the day after. By Wednesday, a small, cold knot of anxiety had formed in Eleanor's chest. Had she imagined the look in his eyes? Had his promise been a mere pleasantry, a polite way to end a conversation? The world of the Carr-Boldts and the Crawfords, which had seemed so hollow and distant on Monday, began to feel solid and inescapable once more. Her beautiful, brief dream of a life with a soul was fading, leaving behind only the familiar ache of her position. She found herself short with one of the maids, and instantly regretted it, the shame burning hotter because she knew its source was not irritation, but a bitter disappointment she could share with no one.

On Thursday morning, a small packet of letters arrived with the early post. Mrs. Carr-Boldt, in a silk wrapper and a foul temper, sifted through them with disdain. "Bills, bills, an invitation from the Mortimers--as if I'd go to their dreadful musicale!--and, what's this? For you, Peggy. From a Mr. Davies. Who on earth is Mr. Davies?"

Eleanor's heart gave a great, painful leap. She reached for the crisp, cream-colored envelope, her hand not quite steady. "He's an architect, Mrs. Carr-Boldt. I met him in the village last week."

"An architect?" Mrs. Watson, who was painting her nails a startling shade of coral, looked up with interest. "Is he any good? George and I have been talking about adding a conservatory. The man we used for the library was a positive robber."

"I really couldn't say," Eleanor murmured, her eyes fixed on the strong, clear handwriting. "We only spoke for a moment." She wanted to flee, to tear it open in private, but Mrs. Carr-Boldt's sharp eyes were on her.

"Well, open it, child, open it! Don't stand there clutching it like a winning lottery ticket. Perhaps he's sending you a bill for his valuable time," she said, with a short, dry laugh.

With burning cheeks, Eleanor slit the envelope. The note was brief, its very simplicity a balm to her frayed nerves. Mr. Davies wrote that he had enjoyed their conversation more than he could say, and that he found himself in the area for a consultation on Saturday. Would she, he wondered, do him the honor of taking tea with him at the Willowbrook Inn at four o'clock?

"Well?" prompted Mrs. Carr-Boldt. "Is it a declaration of love, or a summons to court?"

Eleanor folded the note carefully. "He has invited me to tea on Saturday," she said, her voice betraying nothing of the wild singing in her heart.

"To tea!" Mrs. Crawford echoed, setting down her novel. "How very quaint and proper! An architect. Is he handsome, Peggy?"

"He's a gentleman," Eleanor said simply, and the way she said it put an end, for a moment, to their prying. But as she went to her desk to resume her duties, she heard Mrs. Watson murmur to Mrs. Crawford, "I wonder what his connections are. Davies. It isn't a name one knows."

The small cold knot in Eleanor's chest was replaced by a different kind of anxiety. The joy of the invitation was tangled with the certain knowledge that these women, in their careless way, would dissect and judge and, if they found him wanting, dismiss not only Arthur Davies, but her own foolish heart as well.

Saturday was a day of brilliant sunshine, one of those perfect June days that feels like a gift. Eleanor had been granted the afternoon off with a sort of amused indulgence, as if for a childish escapade. She dressed with exquisite care, choosing a simple dress of pale blue muslin with a white collar and cuffs. It was a frock her mother had made, and it had a sweetness and modesty that her more fashionable Bar Harbor things lacked. She braided her yellow hair and pinned it in a soft coronet, a style that made her look younger, more like the girl who lived in the crowded little house and less like Mrs. Carr-Boldt's polished secretary.

She walked the mile to the Willowbrook Inn, her feet light on the dusty road. The inn was a charming old place, with a wide veranda covered in climbing roses. He was already there, standing at the foot of the steps, and the sight of his tall, rather serious figure sent a fresh wave of shyness through her. He was not handsome in the way of the glossy young men who played tennis at the country club, but his face had a strength and an intelligence that she found infinitely more compelling.

"Miss Vance," he said, his gray eyes lighting up as she approached. "I was afraid you might not come."

"I'm very glad I could, Mr. Davies," she replied, her voice a little breathless.

Their conversation over tea was at first halting, then easy and natural. They did not speak of the wealthy hostesses or the complicated lives of the rich. Instead, he told her of his childhood in a quiet university town, of his passion for the great cathedrals of Europe, of his dream of one day designing a public library, a building for everyone, a place with a soul. He listened with a flattering intensity as she spoke of books, of her love for poetry, of the quiet happiness she found in the turning of a well-wrought phrase.

"It is a rare thing," he said, looking at her across the little table, "to meet someone who understands that beauty is not a luxury, but a necessity."

In that moment, Eleanor felt a sense of rightness, of homecoming, that was entirely new. This was real. This was the world she belonged in. But even as the thought formed, a shadow fell.

"And you?" he asked gently. "You are visiting in the area? You mentioned your work--you are a governess, perhaps?"

The question was innocent, born of her simple dress and her talk of books. And the lie--the lie of omission--was on her lips before she had even considered it. It was so much easier, so much less complicated, than the truth.

"I am a companion," she said, which was not untrue. "To an older lady who is summering here. It is very quiet work." She let him believe her employer was some gentle, elderly widow, not the formidable, restless Mrs. Carr-Boldt. She did not mention the motor-cars, the endless parties, the casual cruelties of the very rich. She painted a life for herself as quiet and simple as her blue muslin dress. It was a betrayal, she knew, but a betrayal of a world she longed to escape. The truth of her own world, the one with Arthur Davies in it, felt more important.

They parted an hour later with a promise to write, and to meet again as soon as he returned to the city. Eleanor walked home in a daze of happiness, the small, cowardly lie a faint and distant cloud in the brilliant sky of her hopes.

For two months, their letters flew back and forth. His were filled with his work, with his thoughts on art and life, with a growing and unmistakable tenderness for her. Hers were a carefully curated version of her existence. She wrote of walks she took, of books she read, of her family at home, but she never wrote of the suffocating luxury that surrounded her, of the daily thousand proofs that she was, in essence, a high-class servant. The omission grew, a silent wall between her real life and the life she shared with him on paper.

The reckoning, when it came, was as swift and brutal as a summer thunderstorm. Mrs. Carr-Boldt, in a fit of architectural enthusiasm, had decided she required a teahouse. Not a simple gazebo, but a proper Japanese teahouse, to be built on a small island in the ornamental lake on her estate.

"Not that dreadful man who did the conservatory, Hattie," Mrs. Watson had insisted. "He has no vision. I heard of a young man, a Mr. Davies, who is doing the most wonderful things for the Astors. Very clever, they say, and not yet spoiled. You should have him look at it."

The name struck Eleanor like a physical blow. She sat frozen at her desk, the pen slipping from her fingers.

"Davies?" Mrs. Carr-Boldt mused. "Peggy, you know a Davies, don't you? Your architect friend?"

"Yes," Eleanor said, her voice a thread. "Yes, Mrs. Carr-Boldt."

"Well, for heaven's sake, child, is it the same one? Does he work for the Astors?"

"I--I believe he has done some work for them," Eleanor stammered, feeling the trap closing around her.

"Excellent! A connection! See, Peggy, you are useful for something besides your pretty penmanship after all," Mrs. Carr-Boldt said, with what she intended as a kindly joke. "Get him on the telephone. Tell him I want to see him tomorrow morning. Ten o'clock. Don't take no for an answer. Tell him it's for Mrs. Hamilton Carr-Boldt."

Eleanor felt sick. There was no escape. She made the call from the telephone in the hall, her back to the open drawing-room door. Arthur's voice, when he answered, was warm with surprise and pleasure, until she delivered her message. There was a pause.

"Mrs. Hamilton Carr-Boldt?" he repeated, his tone suddenly formal, distant. "The lady you work for?"

"Yes," she whispered.

"I see," he said, after another, longer pause. "Very well. Ten o'clock tomorrow." And he hung up.

Eleanor stood with the receiver in her hand, the dial tone buzzing in her ear like a swarm of angry bees. He knew. He had heard the name, and in that instant, her carefully constructed world of quiet companionship and simple living had shattered. He knew she was part of this world of arrogant wealth, a world he had, in his letters, gently disdained.

The next morning was an agony. Eleanor dressed in a severe gray frock, the uniform of a secretary, and tied her hair back in a tight, merciless knot. She felt a hundred years old. When Arthur Davies was announced, she was seated at her desk in the morning room, a ledger open before her, a pen in her hand. Mrs. Carr-Boldt was reclining on a chaise longue, dictating a series of imperious notes.

He was shown in by the butler. He stopped just inside the door, and his eyes found Eleanor immediately. She saw the shock in them, the confusion, and then a dawning, painful comprehension. He took in the magnificent room, the priceless Aubusson carpet, the Fragonards on the wall, Mrs. Carr-Boldt in her elaborate silk gown, and finally, Eleanor, bent over her ledger, a part of the scenery, an accessory to the grandeur. The girl in the simple blue dress at the Willowbrook Inn had vanished. In her place was Miss Vance, the paid dependent.

"Ah, Mr. Davies," said Mrs. Carr-Boldt, extending a hand laden with rings. "So good of you to come on such short notice. You know my secretary, Miss Vance, of course. She's been singing your praises."

The cruelty of it, whether intentional or not, was exquisite. Arthur's face was a pale, polite mask. He bowed slightly to Eleanor, a bow one might give a stranger. "Miss Vance and I have met," he said, his voice devoid of all warmth.

For the next hour, Eleanor sat with her pen poised, taking notes as they discussed the teahouse. She did not dare to look at him. She heard his voice, professional and cool, as he spoke of sight lines and building materials. She heard Mrs. Carr-Boldt's voice, cajoling, commanding, treating him with the mixture of flattery and condescension she reserved for tradesmen, however talented. And through it all, Eleanor's own silence was a screaming confession of her foolish, cowardly deceit. The longing for money and power, the feeling she had thought vanquished, returned with a force that made her physically tremble. If she were her own woman, if she had her own means, she could have met him as an equal. She would not have had to hide. She would not be sitting here now, a prisoner in a gray dress, watching the death of the most precious thing she had ever known.

When the interview was over, Mrs. Carr-Boldt dismissed him with a wave. "Send me the preliminary sketches, Mr. Davies. Peggy will be in touch about your fee."

He bowed, and his eyes met Eleanor's one last time. There was no anger in them now, only a deep, quiet hurt that was far worse. He turned and left without another word.

Eleanor sat at her desk, her head bowed, pretending to make a final note in her ledger. The page was a blur. The beautiful, looping script of her handwriting swam before her eyes, the letters mocking her.

"Well!" said Mrs. Carr-Boldt, with satisfaction. "A very capable young man. A little stiff, perhaps, but he knows his business. It is a good thing you met him, Peggy. You see how these little connections can be useful."

In a moment she was surrounded. Her mother came out, wiping her hands on her apron, her face alight with that weary, all-encompassing love that was the bedrock of Eleanor's world.

"You look tired, dear," Mrs. Paget said, her keen eyes missing nothing. "Did Mrs. Carr-Boldt work you to the bone again?"

"No, Mother, not at all," Eleanor said, her voice a little breathless. She looked from one beloved face to the next: Tom's boisterous, clever face; Grace's steady, practical one; Alice's, still soft with the bloom of girlhood; and little Beth, who had abandoned her scales to wrap her arms around Eleanor's waist.

"I have something to tell you all," she said. Her heart was beating so fast it felt like a trapped bird. She held up her left hand.

For a moment, there was a stunned silence, a rare and precious commodity in the Paget household. The simple gold band, with its single pinprick of light, seemed to gather all the lamplight in the room to itself.

It was Grace who spoke first. "Eleanor! Is that... are you...?"

"He asked me this afternoon," Eleanor said softly. "And I said yes."

The silence was broken by a whoop from Tom. "Dr. Tenison? The professor? Well, I'll be! Good for you, El! I knew he wasn't just coming up here to look at ferns!"

Then the dam of questions broke. Her mother took her hand, turning it over and over, examining the ring with a sort of fearful wonder. "Oh, my dear," she murmured. "My dear girl. Are you sure? Is he a good man?"

"The best man in the world, Mother," Eleanor said, her voice thick with unshed tears.

"But his people," Mrs. Paget fretted, her mind immediately flying to the practical obstacles. "They're so different from us, Eleanor. Will they accept you? And how will you live? A professor's salary... it isn't like the money you're used to seeing every day."

"We will have enough," Eleanor said with a certainty that surprised even herself. "And his people... he has only an aunt, his Aunt Pam. He says she will love me." She did not add that she harbored a deep and secret terror of this very woman, this paragon of old-world virtue who detested the "usual New York girl." What would she think of a middle-class country girl with seven siblings and a house that was never, ever quiet?

"Well," said Mrs. Paget, her practical nature reasserting itself, though her eyes were suspiciously bright. "If you are happy, then we are happy for you. He seemed a very kind gentleman. And now," she added, turning briskly toward the kitchen, "we must have a proper supper to celebrate. Alice, the bread! Grace, lay another place at the table, a proper one. Tom, for heaven's sake, get that smelly net out of the hall!"

The family problem, as Mrs. Crawford would have called it, had been solved with an embrace and the promise of a celebratory meal. Lying in her own small bed that night, the familiar sounds of the house settling around her, Eleanor felt a profound sense of peace. The world of the Carr-Boldts seemed a fiction, a story she had once read. This was reality: the weight of the ring on her finger, and the love that filled her crowded, imperfect home.

Her next task, however, belonged to that other world. On Monday morning, she returned to the great country house on the Hudson, her heart beating a nervous rhythm against her ribs. She found Mrs. Carr-Boldt on the terrace, dictating letters with her usual brisk authority.

"Good morning, Peggy," she said, without looking up from her papers. "There's a note from the household treasurer; it seems the bill for the new delphiniums is simply outrageous. Take a look at it, will you?"

"Yes, Mrs. Carr-Boldt," Eleanor said. She waited a moment, gathering her courage. "There is something I must tell you."

The tone of her voice made Mrs. Carr-Boldt look up. Her sharp, intelligent eyes narrowed slightly. "What is it? Is something wrong at home?"

"No, nothing is wrong. Everything," Eleanor said, a small, involuntary smile touching her lips, "is very right. Dr. Tenison has asked me to marry him. And I have accepted."

For the second time in three days, Eleanor was met with a profound silence. Mrs. Carr-Boldt stared at her as if she had suddenly announced her intention to fly to the moon. Her expression was a mixture of utter astonishment and something else, something Eleanor could not quite decipher; was it disapproval, or merely shock?

"John Tenison?" she said at last, her voice flat. "You and Jack Tenison?"

"Yes."

Mrs. Carr-Boldt leaned back in her wicker chair. She picked up a delicate porcelain paperweight and turned it over in her hands. "Well," she said. "Well. I am... surprised. I had no idea things had progressed so far."

"It was... rather sudden," Eleanor admitted.

"I should say so," Mrs. Carr-Boldt said dryly. She placed the paperweight down with a decisive click. "And what are your plans? When is this to be?"

"We haven't made any definite plans. He must speak with his aunt first."

"Ah, yes. Pamela Tenison." A thin smile played on Mrs. Carr-Boldt's lips. "That will be an interview worth witnessing. Pam Tenison does not suffer fools, or fortune hunters, gladly."

The color drained from Eleanor's face. "I am not a fortune hunter, Mrs. Carr-Boldt."

"Of course you aren't, my dear," she said, her tone softening slightly, though her eyes remained critical. "But you must see how this looks. You are my secretary. He is a professor, a man of good family but no real means. It is a... a romantic notion. But marriage, Peggy, is not a romantic notion. It is a business. It requires capital, and management, and a great deal of work."

It was an echo of the conversation from the morning room, the same weary, cynical wisdom of the wealthy. "I am not afraid of work," Eleanor said quietly.

"No, I know you are not," Mrs. Carr-Boldt conceded. "You are the most competent girl I have ever had. Which is precisely why this is all so... inconvenient." The word hung in the air, cold and selfish. The loss of a good secretary, it was clear, was a more tangible problem to Mrs. Carr-Boldt than the prospect of her secretary's happiness. "However," she went on, waving a dismissive hand, "what's done is done. We shall have to see what Pamela says. If she gives her blessing, then I suppose there is nothing more to be said. If not..." She left the sentence unfinished, a quiet threat. "Now, about these delphiniums. The bill is on my desk."

The interview was over. Eleanor retreated to the morning room, her cheeks burning. The joy of her engagement felt suddenly tarnished, viewed through the cynical lens of her employer. She was not a girl in love; she was an "inconvenience," a "romantic notion." She sat at her desk, the ledger open before her, but the numbers swam. She thought of John, of his dear, earnest face, and the simple faith he had in her, and in his aunt. He did not see the chasm between their worlds; he saw only a bridge his love had built. But Eleanor, standing on her side of the canyon, could feel the ground crumbling beneath her feet.

A few days later, a letter arrived for her, addressed in John's familiar, scholarly hand. His own news was all good. He had written to his Aunt Pam, and her reply had been swift and, in his view, encouraging.

"She is quite intrigued!" he wrote. "She says she remembers you from the station, though only vaguely. She writes, and I quote, 'If she is the girl with the sensible braids and the unfortunate hat, then she is at least not one of these modern flibbertigibbets. Bring her to luncheon on Saturday. We shall see what we shall see.' You see, my darling? I told you she would like you! She already appreciates your sensible braids! Saturday at one o'clock. Do not be frightened of her. She has a bark that is far, far worse than her bite, and a heart of the purest gold."

Eleanor read the letter three times. "Unfortunate hat." The phrase stung, a tiny, sharp dart. Her dashing little hat, the one she had pinned so confidently over her hair, had been catalogued and dismissed. "We shall see what we shall see." It sounded less like an invitation and more like a summons, a subpoena to appear before a judge. A heart of gold she might have, but it was apparently guarded by a dragon of terrifying discernment.

The intervening days were a torment of anxiety. What should she wear? The question, which seemed so trivial, assumed monstrous proportions. Her small wardrobe, which had always seemed perfectly adequate, now looked pitifully insufficient. Her best silk dress seemed too plain; her ruffled blouses, which John had admired, suddenly seemed childish. In the end, with Grace's stern, practical help, she decided on a simple, dark blue dress of good wool crepe. It was neither fashionable nor dowdy; it was merely respectable, and in its very quietness, Eleanor hoped to find a kind of armor.

John met her at the train station in the city, his face aglow with a happiness so pure it almost made her forget her fears. He tucked her arm in his, chattering away about a new manuscript he was reviewing, about a funny remark one of his students had made, about the beauty of the autumn leaves in the park. He seemed utterly oblivious to the fact that they were walking toward her doom.

Aunt Pam's house was on a quiet, tree-lined street on the Upper East Side, a handsome brownstone with gleaming windows and a polished brass knocker. It did not shout its wealth, like the Carr-Boldt mansion; it murmured it, in a voice of quiet, unassailable authority. A silent, uniformed maid opened the door and led them into a hall that was cool and dim, smelling of beeswax and old roses.

And then she was there. Pamela Tenison was a small, upright woman in her late seventies, dressed in a gown of heavy, dove-gray silk. Her white hair was swept up in an elaborate but severe style, and her eyes, the same intelligent blue as her nephew's, were sharp and observant. She did not smile as she extended a hand, its skin as fine and dry as old paper.

"Miss Paget," she said. Her voice was crisp. "So you are the young woman who has turned my nephew's head."

"I'm very pleased to meet you, Mrs. Tenison," Eleanor said, her own voice sounding small and thin in the quiet room.

"It is Miss Tenison," the old woman corrected her gently. "I never married. I found the management of my own affairs quite enough of a complication, without adding a husband to the mix. John, give the girl a glass of sherry. She looks terrified."

John, blushing, did as he was told, while his aunt subjected Eleanor to a scrutiny so intense it was almost physical. She was being weighed, measured, and assessed, and she felt certain she was being found wanting.

Luncheon was announced by the silent maid. They ate in a formal dining room, at a table that gleamed with old silver and fragile china. The conversation was led, or rather, commanded, by Miss Tenison. She quizzed Eleanor about her life, her family, her work, her education. Her questions were not unkind, but they were relentlessly probing.

"Seven of you," she said, raising a delicate eyebrow. "How very... prolific. Your mother must be a remarkably organized woman."

"She is a remarkably busy woman," Eleanor answered honestly. "I don't know that organization has much to do with it. It is more a matter of perpetual motion."

A flicker of something, perhaps amusement, crossed Miss Tenison's face. "And this work you do for Hattie Carr-Boldt. Do you enjoy it? Hattie was always a flighty girl. I cannot imagine she has improved with age and a ridiculous amount of money."

"Mrs. Carr-Boldt is a very energetic employer," Eleanor said, choosing her words with care. "And she has been very kind to me."

"Kindness is the last resort of the idle rich when they cannot think of anything else to do," Miss Tenison stated, as if it were a law of physics. "It requires no effort. Tell me, what do you read, Miss Paget?"

And so it went on, a relentless examination. Eleanor felt as if she were defending a thesis for which she had not prepared. She spoke of her family, of their struggles and their closeness. She spoke of her love for the country, for the simple, difficult life she had always known. She did not try to embellish or excuse. She answered each question with the plain, unvarnished truth, for it was the only thing she had to offer. John, dear, blind John, sat beaming, convinced that the two women he loved most in the world were getting on famously.

After luncheon, Miss Tenison led them into a library, a magnificent room lined from floor to ceiling with books. An old man with a frail body and a face like a wrinkled apple was sitting by the fire, a tartan blanket over his knees.

"This is Mr. Abernathy," Miss Tenison said. "He was my father's valet, and has been with our family for sixty years. He is intolerably deaf and impossibly stubborn, but the house would fall down without him. Abernathy, this is Miss Paget."

The old man nodded vaguely, his eyes dim. As Miss Tenison turned to pour the coffee, his hand, trembling with age, knocked a small silver box from the table beside him. It fell to the floor, spilling its contents: a jumble of old pens, seals, and sealing wax.

"Oh, bother," Miss Tenison said with some asperity. "He does that at least once a day." She made to ring a small bell for the maid.

But Eleanor was already on her knees. "Please, allow me," she said. She quickly and deftly gathered the scattered items. One of the pens, an old fountain pen with a gold nib, had rolled under the old man's chair. As Eleanor reached for it, she saw that the nib was bent, almost at a right angle.

"Oh, what a shame," she said, looking at the fine old instrument.

Mr. Abernathy, who had been watching her with surprising clarity in his faded eyes, spoke in a thin, reedy voice. "My father gave it to me. It... it does not write."

John and his aunt exchanged a look. "He's been complaining about that pen for a month," John whispered to Eleanor. "We've told him it's broken, but he won't let it go."

Eleanor looked at the pen, and then at the old man's disappointed face. On an impulse, she took the pen and, holding it carefully, examined the bent nib. Then, using nothing more than her thumbnails, with a pressure that was both firm and exquisitely gentle, she began to work the delicate metal. She straightened it, minutely adjusting the tines, her brow furrowed in concentration. It was a task that required a steady hand and a patient eye, skills she had learned from years of mending everything from torn lace to broken toys.

After a minute, she rose. She dipped the nib into the inkwell on the library desk, and on a piece of scrap paper, she wrote a single word: "There." The pen moved smoothly, leaving a line of clean, black ink.

She handed the pen and the paper to Mr. Abernathy. The old man took it, his hand shaking. He looked at the word she had written, and then at the pen. A slow, wondrous smile spread across his wrinkled face. He looked up at Eleanor, and for the first time, his eyes were truly focused, and filled with a look of pure, unadulterated gratitude.

Eleanor turned back to find Miss Tenison watching her, her expression unreadable. The old woman said nothing for a long moment. She simply stood, her teacup halfway to her lips, her sharp eyes fixed on Eleanor's face.

Then she set the cup down. "John," she said, her voice softer than Eleanor had yet heard it. "Be a good boy and go talk to Abernathy about his wretched pen. I wish to have a word with your Miss Paget. Alone."

John, delighted, went to the old man's side. Eleanor stood before the fireplace, her hands clasped, waiting for the final verdict.

"That was a foolish thing to do," Miss Tenison said.

"I'm sorry?" Eleanor faltered.

"That pen is a valuable antique. You might have broken it entirely."

"I was very careful," Eleanor said. "And it was already broken. I thought... I only wanted to make him happy."

Miss Tenison was silent for another long moment. She walked to the window and looked out at the quiet street. "My nephew tells me he wishes to marry you," she said, her back still to the room.

"Yes."

"He is a good boy, but he is a fool. He lives in his books. He knows nothing of the world, and less than nothing of women. He believes that love is enough. It is not." She turned to face Eleanor, her eyes piercing. "Do you love him?"

"More than my own life," Eleanor said simply.

"And are you prepared to be poor? Not genteelly poor, like a clergyman's widow. But actually poor. To count pennies, and turn your own collars, and make do? He will never be a rich man. His mind is on other things."

"My mother has nine people to feed on my father's salary from the school," Eleanor said. "I have been turning collars and making do my entire life, Miss Tenison. It holds no terror for me."

A very small, very dry smile touched the old woman's lips. "No," she said, almost to herself. "I don't suppose it does." She came closer and took Eleanor's hand, the one with the simple gold ring. She looked at it, then back at Eleanor's face. "You are not beautiful, not in the way Hattie Carr-Boldt's friends are beautiful. You are not clever, in the way they think they are clever. You are not rich, and your hat is, indeed, unfortunate."

Eleanor felt a fresh wave of despair.

"But," Miss Tenison continued, her grip tightening slightly, "you have good hands. And you have a good heart. You did not ring for a servant to clean up a mess. You knelt and did it yourself. You did not see a broken object; you saw an old man's sorrow, and you fixed it. You, my dear, are not a 'New York girl'. You are something far rarer. You are a lady." She released Eleanor's hand. "John is a fool, but it appears he is a lucky fool. You have my blessing."

Tears, hot and sudden, sprang to Eleanor's eyes. It was not the effusive praise she might have dreamed of, but it was something far better. It was an acceptance earned, a respect granted by a woman whose respect was clearly not easily won.

When John returned to her side, his aunt was calmly discussing the proper temperature at which to serve consommé. But as they prepared to leave, Miss Tenison placed a dry, papery kiss on Eleanor's cheek.

"Come again next week, child," she said. "And do not wear that hat."

Walking back toward the station, with John's arm securely in hers, the city streets seemed transformed. The noise of the traffic was a triumphant symphony, the jostling crowds a company of well-wishers. The chasm between their worlds had not vanished. The future would still hold its struggles, its uncertainties, its endless negotiations between two very different ways of life. But a bridge had been built, not of love's naive fancy, but of sturdier stuff: of character, of kindness, of a simple, honest girl and a mended pen. And as Eleanor looked at the dear, earnest man beside her, she knew, with a certainty that settled deep in her soul, that their bridge would hold.

Margaret, whose name had been so unceremoniously dropped into the conversation, felt a slow flush mount her cheeks. Summer was fading, and with it, her hopes. She had written him, a simple, friendly little note, inquiring after his aunt and mentioning their hurried departure. There had been no answer. "Of course, Peggy's a perfect treasure," Mrs. Carr-Boldt was saying, as if bestowing a medal. "But that's my point. Peggy's one in a million. Most girls today are not willing to be useful. They want to be entertained."

Margaret dipped her pen into the inkwell, her face a mask of pleasant neutrality. She was not a treasure. She was not one in a million. She was a girl whose heart was breaking, slowly and quietly, in a white-and-gray morning room filled with the idle chatter of women for whom life's greatest complication was the hiring of a second nursery maid.

"Speaking of Peggy," said Mrs. Crawford, with a sudden spark of interest, "isn't this the week you go home? To--where is it, dear?--Rockland?"

"Millfield," Margaret corrected softly.

"Millfield! That's it. And your wonderful family of seven. I think it's just fascinating. Are you taking the afternoon train?"

"Yes, the four-o'-clock," Margaret said, her voice betraying none of the wild leap of her heart at the thought of it. Home! The thought was a balm, a sanctuary. The crowded, noisy, shabby house that she had once been so afraid to describe to John Tenison now seemed the only real and desirable place on earth. There, at least, love was not a commodity, and a person's worth was not measured by their ability to hold their own against the Lily Allens of the world.

The journey home was always a strange transition, a shedding of skins. For two hours, as the train rattled through the manicured suburbs and then into the real, untidy countryside, she was still Miss Paget, secretary to the wealthy Mrs. Carr-Boldt. Her suit was smart, her gloves immaculate. But as the familiar hills of her own county began to roll past the window, a subtle change occurred. The rigid set of her shoulders relaxed. She was no longer a paid dependent; she was Margaret, the eldest daughter of the Paget family, going home.

Her brother Tom met her at the station, a lanky, overgrown boy of seventeen with his father's gentle eyes and a shock of unruly brown hair.

"Peggy!" he shouted, his voice cracking with the delightful uncertainty of adolescence. He grabbed her suitcase, nearly overbalancing in his eagerness. "Mother sent me. Did you have a good trip? Is the old dragon in good health?"

"Tommy, don't call her that," Margaret laughed, but the relief of being with him, of hearing his uncomplicated, irreverent nonsense, was so immense that she had to blink back sudden tears. "She's fine. How is everyone? Is Father's cold better?"

"Oh, you know him. He coughs and says it's nothing," Tom said, swinging her bag with perilous energy as they walked. "Mary burned the pot roast last night, so we had bread and milk for supper, and she cried about it for an hour. And little Sue fell out of the apple tree again, but she only got a scratch this time. It's all the same."

All the same. The words were music. The familiar, chaotic, loving litany of her family's life. The walk home was short, under the overarching elms that lined Millfield's quiet streets. Their house, a large, rambling frame building that had seen better days, stood on a corner lot, its paint peeling in places, its wide porch sagging slightly. But the windows shone, and the curtains were crisp, and from the open kitchen door came the unmistakable, delicious scent of her mother's gingerbread.

The moment she stepped inside, she was enveloped. Her mother, her apron dusted with flour and a smudge on her cheek, wrapped her in a hug that smelled of cinnamon and love. "Margaret, dear! You look thin. Aren't they feeding you?"

"I'm fine, Mother," she laughed, clinging to her.

Her father emerged from his study, a book in his hand, his face lighting up with a quiet, scholarly smile. "There's my girl."

Little Sue and nine-year-old Bobby clamored for her attention, pulling at her coat, demanding to know what she had brought them. Mary, her sensitive sixteen-year-old sister, hung back shyly, her eyes full of adoration. In the midst of the happy, noisy confusion, Margaret felt her carefully constructed composure begin to dissolve. This was real. This was where she belonged.

That evening, after the younger children were in bed, the family gathered in the shabby, comfortable living-room. The talk was of small things; the church social, the price of eggs, Tom's difficulties with Latin. Margaret, curled in a worn armchair, felt the tension of the past months seep out of her. She told them amusing, carefully edited stories of her life with Mrs. Carr-Boldt, painting a picture of a world so foreign to them that it might as well have been on another planet. She did not speak of Bar Harbor. She did not speak of John Tenison. That part of her life was a secret garden, now overgrown with weeds, whose gate she could not bear to open.

It was later, when she was helping her mother with the mending in the kitchen, that the question she had been dreading finally came.

"There was a young man, wasn't there, Peggy?" Mrs. Paget asked quietly, not looking up from the sock she was darning. Her mother had a sixth sense, an uncanny ability to know what was in her children's hearts.

Margaret's needle paused. "For a little while, Mother. In the summer."

"What happened?"

"Nothing," Margaret said, and the word was a small, flat stone dropped into a deep well. "He was--from a very different world. His people are very wealthy, very important. I am the family secretary. That's all."

Mrs. Paget set down her darning egg and looked at her daughter, her gaze clear and direct. "And that's the reason it ended? Because you are a secretary?"

"It's a good enough reason, isn't it?" Margaret said, a note of the old bitterness creeping into her voice.

"No," her mother said simply. "It is a very poor reason, if the man was a good man and he loved you." She picked up her work again. "If he let that stand in his way, then he was not the man for you, and you are better off without him, no matter how many books he has written or how grand his aunt is."

The simple, unassailable logic of it was both comforting and devastating. Margaret knew her mother was right. And yet, the ache remained.

The week end passed in a dream of domesticity. She helped Mary with a dress pattern, she drilled Bobby on his multiplication tables, she listened to her father read aloud from Dickens, his voice rumbling peacefully in the quiet house. She felt herself healing, piece by piece.

On Monday morning, laden with a jar of preserves and a bag of gingerbread, she was back at the station with Tom.

"Write soon, Peggy," he said, suddenly awkward again. "It's better when you're home."

"I'll write," she promised.

As she settled into her seat on the train, a man coming down the aisle stopped, his face breaking into a smile of surprised recognition.

"Miss Paget! Well, this is a happy chance. I did not know you lived this way."

Margaret's heart gave a sickening lurch. It was Dr. Tenison. He looked exactly the same; the same eager, intelligent eyes, the same slightly rumpled tweeds, the same air of being absorbed in a world of important thoughts. He was holding a thick, serious-looking book, his finger marking his place.

"Dr. Tenison," she said, her voice sounding faint and strange in her own ears. "I--I was just home for the week end."

"May I?" he asked, indicating the empty seat beside her. She nodded, her mind a complete blank. He sat down, placing his book on his lap. "I was visiting my old mentor from the university. He has a place a few stops down the line. A wonderful man, Professor Albright. He's doing fascinating work on monastic land-grant patterns. But you don't want to hear about that." He turned his full attention to her, and his smile was so open, so genuinely pleased, that her confusion deepened. "You know, I was so disappointed to have missed you," he said. "I called at Mrs. Carr-Boldt's house in New York a few weeks after you left Bar Harbor, but the butler said the family was still away. I have been buried in work for the new term, and I confess I let everything else slide. I did mean to write."

He meant to write. He had called. The world, which had been a dull, leaden gray, suddenly blazed with color. The Allens had not won. There had been no dismissal. There had only been life, and work, and a missed connection.

"We had to leave in a great hurry," she found herself explaining, her voice miraculously steady. "Victoria, Mrs. Carr-Boldt's daughter, was very ill. We were in the hospital for weeks."

"Good heavens, I am so sorry to hear it! Is she well now?"

He had not wanted to dine with them. He had simply been polite. Margaret felt a wave of joy so pure and overwhelming that she felt light-headed.

"My aunt has been asking for you," he went on, his tone earnest again. "She was quite taken with you, you know. She said you had 'sense,' which is the highest praise she bestows on anyone, particularly a young woman. She said you did not seem at all like a New Yorker."

"I'm not," Margaret said, and this time, she smiled. "I am from Millfield. My father is the head of the school there."

"Is he really! A schoolmaster! How splendid. Aunt Pam will be so pleased. She has a great respect for educators. She always says they are the bedrock of the republic."

The train rattled on, but Margaret was no longer aware of it. The chasm between their worlds, which had seemed so impossibly wide, was suddenly shrinking. He did not see her as a secretary, a dependent. He saw her as the daughter of a schoolmaster, a girl with "sense" whom his formidable aunt approved of.

"My family is--large," she said, taking a sudden, brave plunge. "There are seven of us children. Our house is rather crowded and noisy."

"I would love that," Margaret said simply. The words that had been impossible to say just a few weeks ago now came with perfect ease. The ugly things of life, the struggle, the crowded house; they were not things to be ashamed of. They were simply life. They were her life, and she would no longer hide it.

"And you must come and see Aunt Pam again," he was saying eagerly. "Perhaps for tea on Saturday? If you are not too busy? She will want to hear all about Victoria's recovery. And she will be so glad to know that you are not, in fact, a Bar Harborer, but the daughter of a schoolmaster from Millfield."

For the second time in one short summer, Margaret felt that she had stepped into Wonderland. But this time it was not a revelation of rosy colors and fleeting joy. It was something quieter, deeper, and more real. It was the feeling of a door swinging open, not into a fantasy of yachts and motor-cars, but into a future she could actually inhabit. A future where a girl from a crowded house in Millfield and a professor from a quiet house in New York could meet and talk and perhaps, just perhaps, build a bridge between their two worlds.

As the train pulled into the great, echoing cavern of the city station, he helped her with her bag and the jar of preserves.

"Gingerbread?" he asked, peering at her other parcel.

"My mother's," she said.

"Aunt Pam has a theory," he said, with his serious, impersonal air, "that the quality of a nation's home-baked goods is a direct index of its moral fiber." He smiled, a warm, personal smile that was just for her. "I have a feeling she and your mother would get on famously."

And as Margaret walked out into the clamor of the city, she did not feel like a secretary, or a dependent, or a little middle-class country school teacher. She felt like a woman whose future was her own, a woman who was carrying a jar of her mother's preserves and a bag of her mother's gingerbread, and the promise of tea on Saturday. And she knew, with a certainty that settled deep in her soul, that it was more than enough.
