The world had not changed; she was still a secretary. Our purpose. The words echoed in her mind. She sat, placing her notepad and pen on the polished surface of the table. "As you wish, Mrs. Carr-Boldt," she murmured.

The next few days were a master class in Mrs. Carr-Boldt's particular brand of social engineering. Margaret found herself busier than she had ever been. There were endless lists to be compiled, calls to be made, minute arrangements for the luncheon that seemed to multiply with every passing hour. He, for his part, was subjected to a similar, if more luxurious, form of management. A new guest had arrived, a Miss Eleanor Deering, the daughter of an old friend of Mrs. Carr-Boldt's. She was a pretty, placid girl with wide blue eyes and a fortune that was spoken of in hushed, reverent tones. Margaret observed it all from the periphery, a silent, efficient shadow. She saw them on the lawn from the office window, heard their polite laughter from the hall. She belonged in this world of sun-drenched lawns and effortless leisure. Margaret, with her ledgers and her carefully mended blouses, belonged to the world of paid dependency. The old bitterness, the longing for money and power of her own, returned with a vengeance. "I thought I might find you here," he said.

"I was just getting some air," she stammered, feeling foolishly like a truant caught out of bounds.

"I have been attempting to escape for the past hour," he said, a note of dry amusement in his voice. "Miss Deering has been giving me a most exhaustive account of her trip to Italy. She does not understand that the more one is pushed toward a thing, the more one's instinct is to retreat."

Margaret's heart, which had been a leaden weight in her chest, began to beat a little faster.

"So am I," he said. "I am leaving tomorrow, after the luncheon. My house is habitable enough. But I do not wish to leave things as they are. This is not the end of our conversation." He did not move closer, did not attempt any gesture that would compromise her. He simply stood with her in the moonlight, two conspirators who had just signed a secret treaty. The fragrance of the roses no longer seemed mocking. It was the scent of a promise, of a future that had, against all odds, just begun. The power of the Maude and Lily Allens of the world, the power of money and position, seemed to recede, diminished by the simple, quiet force of a man who saw her, the real her, and was not deterred. For the second time, Margaret felt herself on the verge of a wonderland, but this one felt real, its foundations built not on a rosy fantasy, but on the solid ground of mutual respect. And she knew, with a certainty that warmed her to the soul, that she would be strong enough to walk in it.

"Well, I don't know what to make of him," Mrs. Crawford announced, peeling off her gloves. "Perfectly charming, of course, and his family is one of the best. But he made absolutely no effort with Eleanor! Did you notice, Hattie? It was like pulling teeth."

Margaret, sitting on the small folding seat, her back to the driver, stared out at the passing landscape and said nothing. Peculiar. Yes, perhaps it was. But it was also the most wonderful thing that had ever happened to her. She held the secret of it close, a warm, glowing coal in the center of her being.

The Paget house, when she reached it late on Friday, smelled of yeast and roasting chicken and the faint, clean scent of her mother's mending basket. The hall was narrow and dark, the floorboards worn smooth by the passage of countless feet. From the parlor came the sound of her sister Alice practicing a particularly difficult passage on the piano, the notes repeating with dogged persistence.

"Peggy, is that you?" her mother's voice called from the kitchen.

"It's me, Mother!"

She found her mother, her fine, tired face flushed from the heat of the stove, sliding a pan of rolls into the oven. Mrs. Paget wiped a stray wisp of graying hair from her forehead with the back of her wrist and smiled at her eldest daughter.

"Well, you look like you've seen a ghost. Was it a dreadful week?"

"No, not at all," Margaret said, her voice softer than it had been all week. "It was very quiet." She unpinned her hat and laid it on the crowded kitchen table, next to a bowl of beans waiting to be snapped and a history book left open by her brother, Tom. The comfortable, lived-in clutter of it all was a balm to her soul. This was real. This was home.

"Seven of you!" Mrs. Potter had echoed in that cool, white-and-gray room. "How early Victorian!"

Here, the seven were not an abstract concept, but a vibrant, noisy reality. Teddy, at fourteen, clattered down the stairs, demanding to know if supper was ready. Jane, her head full of the novel she was reading, wandered in and had to be reminded to set the table. Her father came in from the garden, his hands earthy, and kissed her cheek, asking if those rich folks were treating her properly.

They sat down to supper, the ten of them, for her two youngest brothers, twins of eight, were home as well. The table was a chaotic symphony of clattering forks, spilled milk, and half a dozen conversations happening at once. Teddy was recounting a baseball game; Alice was complaining about her piano teacher; Tom was arguing about politics with his father; and the twins were giggling over some secret joke.

Her mother, at the head of the table, presided over it all with a tired but serene authority, ladling stew, cutting bread, settling disputes. Margaret looked at her mother's hands, the knuckles swollen slightly with rheumatism, the nails short and clean, hands that had worked and washed and cooked and mended for thirty years. She thought of Mrs. Crawford looking critically at her own perfectly manicured nails, and a wave of fierce, protective love for her family washed over her.

This was what Mr. Forbes had to understand. This was the life she came from. Not a place of exquisite order, but one of severe and endless struggle, yes, but also of love, and noise, and duty, and a deep, unshakable loyalty. Could a man like him, a man of quiet libraries and European travel, ever truly comprehend it? The thought was a small, cold stone in the pit of her stomach. She had told herself she would be strong enough, but now, immersed in the overwhelming reality of her world, a flicker of the old doubt returned.

The week passed in a blur of domesticity. She helped her mother with the mending, she drilled the twins on their spelling, she listened to Alice's dreams of attending the conservatory, and she talked late into the night with her father about the rising cost of everything. She felt herself expanding back into her true shape, the stiff, careful persona of "Miss Paget" falling away to reveal the daughter and sister she was.

She told no one about Mr. Forbes. The secret was too new, too precious. It was a private promise, a seed that needed to be kept in the dark to see if it would sprout.

On Monday morning, she took the train back to New York, back to Mrs. Crawford's townhouse, a world of silent, well-trained servants and days ordered by the telephone and the engagement book. The contrast was more jarring than ever before. The silence of the house seemed oppressive, the luxury felt decadent and thin.

The days that followed were a trial of patience. She went about her duties with her usual efficiency, writing letters, balancing accounts, arranging appointments. But her inner ear was constantly listening for the postman's ring, her heart giving a lurch every time the telephone shrilled. Mrs. Crawford, who was planning a large dinner party, was more demanding than usual, and Margaret was grateful for the distraction. It kept her from simply sitting and watching the clock.

"Peggy, you seem a bit dreamy today," Mrs. Crawford remarked one afternoon, as Margaret made an error in addressing an invitation. "Is everything all right at home?"

"Everything is fine, thank you, Mrs. Crawford," Margaret replied, her cheeks warm. "I was just thinking about the menu."

A week passed. Then ten days. The glowing coal of hope within her began to feel like a dull, gray ash. He had said he would write. Had he changed his mind? Had he returned to his world of wealth and position and decided that the gulf between them was simply too wide? Perhaps he had met another Miss Deering, one with a little more conversation, and had forgotten all about the quiet secretary in the country. The old bitterness, the familiar ache of her position, began to creep back into her heart. She was only a little middle-class country school teacher, after all. What had she been thinking, to imagine anything else?

The thought of Dr. Tenison, of that other brief wonderland, returned to her, a mocking ghost. That had ended in a confusion of straw hats and spangled quills, a public desertion for the loud, arrogant world she despised. Perhaps this was just a quieter version of the same story. A polite dismissal instead of a public slight.

On the twelfth day, a Thursday, she was in the small office off the main hall, trying to make sense of a florist's bill that seemed wildly exorbitant, even for Mrs. Crawford. The house was quiet. Her employer was out for the afternoon. Margaret felt weary and discouraged, the endless columns of figures blurring before her eyes.

The doorbell rang, a sharp, insistent peal. A moment later, the butler appeared at the office door, a silver tray in his hand.

"A letter for you, Miss Paget."

Her heart stopped, then began to beat in a frantic, suffocating rhythm. It was a man's handwriting, strong and black, on heavy cream-colored stationery. There was no return address. Her fingers trembled as she took it.

"Thank you, Fields," she said, her voice a bare whisper.

My dear Miss Paget,

I hope you will forgive the delay in my writing. The business I returned to in New York proved more entangling than I had anticipated. I have thought often of our conversation in the garden, and I find I am even more determined than I was then not to let it be our last.

My house is in order, but I find it excessively quiet. I am no great lover of society, as you may have gathered, but I do not care for a solitary existence either. I would be honored if you would allow me to call upon you.

You need only send a wire to my club, the Century, to let me know if the day is suitable.

Margaret read the letter once, twice, a third time. The words on the page seemed to shimmer. He was not forgetting. He was not dismissing her. He was coming. Not to Bar Harbor, not to a neutral territory brokered by a wealthy patron, but to her home. To Dayton. To the crowded, noisy, loving house that was the center of her universe.

He was not afraid of her reality. He was walking straight toward it.

A great, shuddering breath escaped her. She pressed the letter to her chest, the stiff paper crinkling against her dress. The weariness, the doubt, the bitterness of the last few days dissolved as if they had never been. The future, which had seemed to be closing in on her, a long, gray corridor of servitude, suddenly opened up into a wide, sunlit vista.

She stood up, walked to the window, and looked out at the prim, orderly New York street. It no longer seemed like a prison. It was merely a temporary address. For the first time, the two halves of her life, the world of the Carr-Boldts and the Crawfords, and the world of the Pagets, did not feel like warring territories. A bridge was being built between them, a bridge made of courage and sincerity, and a man named Stephen Forbes was walking across it to meet her. And she, Margaret Paget, would be there to welcome him.

CHAPTER VI

She sent the wire that afternoon, a simple message composed with a beating heart and handed to a uniformed boy with a sense of finality, as if she were posting the banns of her own wedding. "Saturday will be very pleasant. Margaret Paget." It seemed at once too little and too much, a bare thread of a sentence on which to hang such a universe of hope. Returning to the silent, cavernous house of the Carr-Boldts, she felt like a conspirator, a spy carrying joyful secrets through an enemy land. The quiet opulence of the rooms, which had so often oppressed her, now seemed merely theatrical, a painted backdrop against which her own real, vibrant life was about to begin.

The journey home on Friday was a new experience. Usually, the train ride was a slow decompression, a shedding of the skin of Miss Paget, secretary, to become Peggy, daughter and sister. It was a journey from quiet servitude to noisy, demanding love. This time, however, she carried the promise of New York with her, tucked into her handbag in the form of a stiff, cream-colored envelope. She saw the familiar, dusty landscapes of the countryside not as an escape, but as the destination, the very ground where her future would be decided.

Would he find it dreadfully plain? The thought, which had been a constant, nagging ache for years, now presented itself for calm inspection. She pictured the house as he would see it. The paint on the porch railings was certainly peeling; the front gate had a distinct and permanent squeak that no amount of oil ever seemed to conquer; and inside, oh, inside was the glorious, impossible clutter of seven lives lived at full tilt. There were Charlie's engineering textbooks on the sideboard, and Kitty's half-finished sewing project on the best armchair in the parlor. There would be the lingering, delicious scent of her mother's Saturday baking, and the almost certain sound of the younger boys, Ted and Jamie, arguing over a baseball bat in the backyard. It was not the exquisitely ordered existence of a Bar Harbor cottage, that was certain. No, she couldn't pretend it was. But for the first time, she did not entirely wish it were. This was her home. This was the soil from which she had grown. If he was to care for the flower, he must not despise the earth.

"He is a man of business, I suppose," Margaret said, feeling suddenly at a loss. How could she describe him? "He is very kind, and very well-read. He is not like the other people I know there. He is… different."
"Different how?" Mrs. Paget's eyes, the same dark-lashed eyes as Margaret's, were shrewd but gentle.
"He is sincere," Margaret said simply. "He wishes to come to Dayton. To call on me here."
The significance of this was not lost on her mother. To come to Dayton was to come to the Pagets, to see them as they were. A silence fell over the little parlor, a silence filled with the momentousness of the news.
"Well," Mrs. Paget said at last, folding the sock with decisive pats. "If a gentleman wishes to pay his respects to my daughter, he will be made welcome in this house. Kitty, we will use the good tablecloth. And we must have a proper roast for dinner. Margaret, what time is he to arrive?"
"He only mentioned the afternoon," Margaret said, a wave of relief washing over her. "He thought we might go for a drive."
"Then we shall have tea," her mother declared. "With the lemon cakes he'll not get any better in New York, I can promise you that."
And so, the house was thrown into a flurry of happy, anxious preparation. On Saturday morning, Charlie was enlisted to fix the squeaking gate, which he did with a great deal of technical muttering and a very small amount of success. Kitty and Margaret dusted the parlor until every surface gleamed, plumping pillows and arranging fresh flowers from the garden in a vase. The younger boys were given stern injunctions to remain clean, quiet, and as far from the front of the house as possible, a command they received with the solemnity of soldiers being sent on a perilous mission.
Margaret dressed with more care than she ever took for one of Mrs. Carr-Boldt's grand dinners. She chose a simple afternoon dress of blue muslin, with a white collar and cuffs, a dress that spoke of home and summer days. She braided her hair and pinned it in its usual coronet, but her fingers trembled so that she had to do it twice. She looked at herself in the wavy mirror of her little bedroom. She was not the startling beauty she became in New York, surrounded by jewels and silks. Here, she was just Peggy Paget, a girl in a muslin dress, waiting in her crowded, beloved home for a visitor who might change her life forever.

He arrived precisely at two o'clock. Margaret, who had been watching from the parlor window, saw the hired motor-car pull up. Her heart gave a great leap. He was taller than she remembered, and his sober gray suit seemed to make him all the more distinguished against the backdrop of their little street. He stood for a moment, looking at the house, and Margaret held her breath. He did not seem to be judging it; he seemed merely to be looking at it, taking it in. Then he came up the walk, his footsteps firm and confident, ignoring the gate's predictable shriek.
She opened the door before he could knock.
"Mr. Forbes," she said, her voice softer than she intended.
"Miss Paget." He smiled, a slow, genuine smile that reached his serious gray eyes, and all her anxieties vanished. He removed his hat, and she saw him properly for the first time without the formal backdrop of a New York garden. He seemed younger, less formidable. "I hope I am not too early."
"Not at all. You are perfectly on time. Please, come in."
She led him into the parlor, which felt suddenly very small and very full of sunlight. Her mother rose to meet them, her initial nervousness hidden behind a mask of maternal dignity.
"Mother, may I present Mr. Stephen Forbes. Mr. Forbes, my mother, Mrs. Paget."
"It is an honor to meet you, Mrs. Paget," he said, taking her hand. His voice was warm and respectful. "Margaret has spoken of you."
Margaret froze. What had she spoken of? She could not recall mentioning her family to him at all. She must have, in some brief, unremembered exchange.
"Has she now?" Mrs. Paget's smile became a little less formal. "I hope she has been a good girl in that wild city."
"She has been a godsend to Mrs. Carr-Boldt, I am told," he said, "and a very kind friend to me."
At that moment, as if on cue, Kitty entered with a tray, followed by Ted and Jamie, who had evidently escaped their backyard exile and were peering around the door-frame with unabashed curiosity.
"My sister, Kitty," Margaret said, "and my younger brothers, Ted and Jamie."
Mr. Forbes did not seem at all discomposed by the sudden appearance of a small crowd. He nodded gravely to Kitty, and then his serious face broke into another smile as he looked at the boys.
"Engineers, I presume?" he asked, his eyes twinkling.
Ted, the bolder of the two, stepped forward. "Charlie's the engineer. We're going to be baseball players."
"A fine ambition," said Mr. Forbes. "I played a little myself at university. Second base."
This information was a triumph. The boys were won over instantly, and even Mrs. Paget seemed to relax. The tea was a happy, noisy affair. Mr. Forbes listened with grave attention as Mrs. Paget described the challenges of raising seven children, and he complimented her lemon cakes with a sincerity that was unmistakable. He spoke with Charlie, who eventually emerged from upstairs, about the future of steel construction, and he won Kitty's heart by asking her opinion on a new novel that all of New York was discussing.
He did not patronize them. He did not act the great man from the city, bestowing his attention upon the simple country folk. He was simply a gentleman, courteous and interested, who found himself in the parlor of a family he wished to know better. Margaret watched him, a great, swelling pride in her heart. He was not just seeing her family; he was seeing her. He was seeing the life that made her, and he was not afraid.

"The roads are not what you are used to, I'm afraid," Margaret said, as the motor-car rumbled over a familiar patch of uneven ground.
"They are fine roads," he said, "and they lead through beautiful country." He was not looking at the country, however. He was looking at her. "Your family is wonderful, Margaret. May I call you Margaret?"
"Of course," she said, her voice catching a little. "And I have always thought of you as Stephen."
"Good." He settled back against the seat, a comfortable silence falling between them. "I liked your mother very much. She has your eyes, but hers are a little wiser."
"She has had more to see," Margaret said quietly. "Life has not always been easy."
"No," he agreed. "It rarely is, for the people who matter most. You know," he went on, turning to her, "when I spoke of my quiet house, I think I was not being entirely truthful. It is not quiet, it is empty. There is a great deal of difference." He paused. "My father built that house for a large family. He and my mother had only me. They are both gone now, and the rooms echo. A house, I am discovering, is not a home until there is life in it. The sort of life I saw in your parlor this afternoon."
Margaret's heart was beating so fast she could hardly breathe. She looked at her hands, which were twisting a handkerchief in her lap.
"We are very poor, you know," she said, the words rushing out before she could stop them. It was the confession she had longed to make to Professor Tenison, the truth she had hidden behind her dashing hat and storm coat. "We have good blood, as my mother says, but we have nothing else. That house, the noise, the clutter, that is all we have."
"That is not all you have," he said, his voice firm and low. He reached over and gently stilled her twisting hands with his own. His touch was warm and strong. "You have loyalty, and affection, and courage. You have a mother who would defend her children like a lioness, and brothers and a sister who adore you. Do you think those things are so common? I have spent my life among the wealthiest people in America, and I can tell you they are the rarest commodities in the world. Riches are a burden, Margaret. They build walls around people. I have seen it my whole life. The greatest fortune a man can have is a family like yours."
She looked up at him then, and the sincerity in his gray eyes was so profound it made her want to weep. All the shame, all the years of feeling second-rate, of being the poor relation, the paid dependant, all of it melted away under the warmth of his gaze.
He was still holding her hand. "I did not come to Dayton to inspect your family's finances, Margaret. I came to see if the woman I met in a garden was as real and as true as I believed her to be. And she is. Even more so."
They drove on for a long time after that, talking of small things, of books and music and the changing seasons, but beneath the surface of their words ran a deep and powerful current, a silent understanding that something of immense importance had been settled between them. The bridge she had imagined was real. He had walked across it, and he was not going back.

When he brought her home, the sun was setting, casting long, golden shadows across the lawn. The squeaking gate sounded almost cheerful in the evening air. He walked with her to the door.
"I must catch the evening train," he said. "But I hope this will not be my last visit to Dayton."
"I hope so, too," she said, smiling.
He did not try to kiss her. He simply took her hand again, holding it for a moment in both of his. "Thank you for today, Margaret. Thank you for letting me see your home."
"Thank you for coming," she whispered.
She watched from the doorway as he walked back to the car, a tall, straight-backed figure in the fading light. He turned and raised a hand in farewell before he got in, and then the motor-car rumbled away down the street, leaving a cloud of dust hanging in the still air.
She closed the door and leaned against it, her eyes closed. She could still feel the warm, firm pressure of his hand. The house was quiet. In the kitchen, she could hear the clatter of plates as Kitty and her mother prepared supper. She could smell the roast. It was the scent of home. It was the scent of her life. And it was, she thought with a sudden, soaring joy, the scent of her future. He had come. He had seen. And he understood. The world of the Carr-Boldts and the Allens, with its nervous breakdowns and its casual cruelties, seemed a thousand miles away, a dim and distant land to which she might never need to return. For the first time, Margaret Paget was not caught between two worlds. She was standing firmly, joyfully, in one. And she was home.

Her beauty, which had glowed so brightly during their two weeks together, now took on a deeper, more serene quality. There was a confidence in her step, a new directness in her gaze. “You’ve become quite the dreamer, Margaret,” she remarked one morning, her pen scratching decisively across a cheque. “I’ve had to ask you for the blotter twice. Is Bar Harbor finally getting the better of your good sense?”

“I’m sorry, Mrs. Carr-Boldt,” Margaret said, her cheeks flushing. “I was thinking.”

“Thinking,” Mrs. Carr-Boldt repeated, her eyes narrowed. “I should hope so. That is what I pay you for. But your thoughts seem to be taking you to very pleasant places these days. You have a look about you. It’s the look a girl gets when she believes a man is serious.”

Margaret’s heart gave a violent leap. She said nothing, merely arranging the signed cheques into a neat pile.

“It is Doctor Tenison, I presume,” Mrs. Carr-Boldt went on, her voice devoid of expression. She leaned back, tapping the end of her pen against her teeth. “He has been writing to you.” It was not a question.

“Yes,” Margaret said, her voice quiet but firm.

“I see.” A silence descended upon the morning room, a silence more potent than any exclamation. Mrs. Watson and Mrs. Crawford, who had been lazily turning the pages of their magazines, became very still, their ears straining for the drama they sensed was about to unfold.

“Margaret,” Mrs. Carr-Boldt said, and her voice took on the tone of one explaining a simple but vital truth to a backward child. “Come here.”

Margaret rose and stood before the desk.

“I am fond of you, Margaret. You know that. In the five years you have been with me, you have been a comfort and a treasure. You have sense. You have breeding. That is why I feel it is my duty to speak plainly with you, for your own good.”

She paused, as if to let the weight of her impending wisdom settle upon the girl.

“John Tenison is an excellent man. His family is one of the best, if you discount the fact that his great-grandfather was a clergyman. He is a scholar, a gentleman. And he is, in his own way, a man of prospects. He will one day hold a very important chair at the university. He will be a man of influence. Such a man requires a certain type of wife.”

Margaret’s chin lifted. “And what type is that?”

“A wife who is an asset,” Mrs. Carr-Boldt stated flatly. “A woman with connections of her own. A woman with a fortune, however small, to ease the considerable burdens of a professional man’s life. A woman who can run a household with the proper sort of servants, who can entertain foreign dignitaries, who can hold her own with the wives of trustees and deans. A woman, in short, who belongs in his world.”

The unspoken words hung in the air: a woman who is not you.

“I think,” Margaret began, her voice trembling slightly, “that you underestimate Doctor Tenison.”

“On the contrary,” Mrs. Carr-Boldt countered smoothly. “I am thinking of him. And I am thinking of you. Have you considered, for one moment, the reality of such a match? You have nothing, my dear child. Nothing but a pretty face and a head full of romantic notions. Do you fancy he will live with you in some dreadful little walk-up apartment, while you cook his meals and scrub his floors? Do you imagine his Aunt Pamela, who is a dragon of the old school, will welcome a little country school teacher with seven bothers and sisters into the family? They will tear you to pieces, you and your sweet, simple goodness. You will be a millstone around his neck, a constant source of embarrassment. He may think he is in love now, in the romance of a summer flirtation. But in the cold light of a New York winter, he will see his mistake. And you, my dear, will be heartbroken.”

Each word was a carefully aimed dart, designed to find the old, vulnerable places in Margaret’s heart. A year ago, a month ago, they would have found their mark. She would have crumpled, convinced by the terrible, cold logic of the world Mrs. Carr-Boldt represented. But something had changed. She was no longer defending a fantasy. She was defending a truth, a truth that had been tested and proven in the humble parlor of her own home.

“You are mistaken, Mrs. Carr-Boldt,” she said, and her voice was steady now, infused with a calm authority that surprised even herself. The memory of John’s face, alight with sincerity as he spoke of her family, was a shield against the woman’s sharp-edged words. “You speak of money and connections and servants. Doctor Tenison and I have spoken of other things. Of honesty, and family, and mutual respect. The life you describe, the one you believe he must have, is not one he wants. And the woman you believe he needs is not the woman he has chosen.”

Mrs. Crawford let out a tiny, audible gasp. Mrs. Watson’s magazine slipped from her lap to the floor with a soft thud. No one, in their memory, had ever spoken to Harriet Carr-Boldt in such a manner.

Mrs. Carr-Boldt’s face, which had been a mask of condescending pity, hardened into granite. Her eyes were like chips of ice.

“Chosen?” she repeated, her voice dangerously soft. “He has chosen you? Has he asked you to be his wife?”

“That is a matter between Doctor Tenison and myself,” Margaret replied, her heart pounding but her resolve unbroken.

“I see,” Mrs. Carr-Boldt said again. She stood up, a short, powerful figure radiating immense displeasure. “So this is what it has come to. After all I have done for you. After I lifted you out of obscurity and gave you a place in a civilized world. You throw it all back in my face for the sake of a schoolgirl fancy. You are a fool, Margaret. A bigger fool than I ever took you for.”

“Perhaps,” Margaret said. “But I think, for the first time in my life, I am not a coward. I have let you and your world intimidate me for five years. I have been ashamed of who I am, of my family, of the simple, good life my parents gave me. I am not ashamed anymore. “Pardon me, madam,” he said, his voice impassive. “Doctor Tenison is here. He is asking for Miss Paget.”

“I have spoken with my aunt, Mrs. Carr-Boldt,” he said, his voice ringing with a quiet formality. “And I have come to Bar Harbor today for one purpose only. I have come to ask Margaret to do me the honor of becoming my wife.”

Margaret’s hand flew to her mouth, and her eyes filled with sudden, happy tears. It was real. It was all real.

Mrs. Carr-Boldt sank back into her chair as if her legs had given way. The fight had gone out of her. John Tenison paid her no more mind. He looked only at Margaret. “I believe,” he said softly, a gentle humor in his voice, “that you have not yet given me an answer.”

She couldn’t speak. She could only nod, tears tracing paths down her cheeks as a smile of pure, unadulterated joy broke across her face.

They sat opposite each other in the plush seats of the parlor car, the world sliding past the window unnoticed. For a long while they did not speak, content to simply be together, to let the reality of the morning sink in. Margaret studied his face, memorizing the line of his jaw, the way his dark hair fell across his forehead, the earnest light in his eyes. He was real. This was real. The secret heartache she had carried since that day at the station, the gnawing feeling of being only a paid dependant, was gone, washed away by the tide of his love. But as the miles mounted between them and Bar Harbor, another, older anxiety began to stir in its place. The truth she had suppressed, the confession she had rehearsed and discarded a dozen times. “John,” she began, her voice low. He looked up from his book, his full attention on her at once. “There is something I must tell you. Something I should have told you long ago.” His expression did not change. He simply waited, his calm gaze inviting her trust. “When you spoke of Dayton,” she said, her hands twisting in her lap, “and your aunt, and that--that beautiful, orderly life. And you said I would fit in. You were wrong.” She took a deep breath. “You must not confuse my home with any such existence. We are poor people, John. Very poor. My father is a Latin teacher at the high school; his salary is very small. There are seven of us children, and I am the oldest. Our house is small and always crowded, and noisy. My mother--my dear mother--she would not understand one tenth of your aunt’s conversation. Our days are a struggle. Not a graceful, charming struggle, but a severe and endless one with ugly things, with bills and mended clothes and not quite having enough.” The words tumbled out, a torrent of truth she could no longer hold back. “We have good blood, I suppose, but so do thousands of other families. We are not special. We are just a big, loving, chaotic, middle-class family from a little town in Massachusetts. And I am not a Bar Harborer, or a New Yorker. I am just a country school teacher who took a job as a secretary to help her family. And I let you believe--I let everyone believe--I was something I am not.”

She finished, her heart pounding, her eyes fixed on his face, dreading the flicker of disappointment, the shadow of regret. But it did not come. Instead, John Tenison leaned forward, and taking both her hands in his, he held them firmly. “Margaret,” he said, and his voice was filled with a gentle reproof that was more comforting than any protestation. “Did you really think I did not know?” She stared at him, speechless. “My dear girl,” he went on, a smile touching the corners of his mouth. “I am a professor. It is my business to observe. I saw your hands on the very first day we met; they are not the hands of an idle woman. I heard the way you spoke of home, always with love, but never with details. I saw how you managed your money, how a new hat was an event. Lily and Maude Allen would not notice a dozen new hats. Do you think I fell in love with a guest of the Carr-Boldts? I fell in love with you, Margaret. With your courage, and your kindness, and the way your eyes shine when you defend the things you love, like your family.” He squeezed her hands. “I knew you were not one of them. That is precisely why I loved you. I told my aunt you were not the usual New York girl. Do you know what she said? She said, ‘Thank God for that!’” Margaret felt the tight knot of fear in her chest dissolve into a wave of overwhelming relief. The last barrier was down. He knew, and he loved her still. He loved her for it. “Oh, John,” she whispered, her eyes shining with unshed tears. “I do not deserve you.” “That is a matter for debate,” he said gravely, though his eyes were twinkling. “Aunt Pam will be the final judge. And I have a strong suspicion she is already on your side.”

The house was not a mansion, like the Carr-Boldt’s, but a handsome, rambling brick house set far back from a quiet, tree-lined street. It had the settled, comfortable look of a place that had been loved and lived in for generations. Ivy climbed the walls, and the gardens were a riot of late-summer color, beautiful but not obsessively manicured. It was a house with character, with a soul. A maid with a friendly, wrinkled face opened the door and smiled at John with genuine affection. “Dr. John! It’s good to have you home. Your aunt is in the library.” The library was just as Margaret had imagined it would be. Bookshelves stretched to the ceiling on all four walls, filled not with pristine, matching sets, but with books of all shapes and sizes, some ancient and leather-bound, others modern and bright. The furniture was old and comfortable: deep leather armchairs, a sofa piled with cushions, and a great mahogany desk littered with papers and correspondence. A fire crackled in the hearth, taking the slight chill from the autumn air. A woman was standing by the window, looking out at the garden. She turned as they entered, and Margaret found herself face to face with the famous Aunt Pam. She was not a tall woman, but she carried herself with an erect, confident posture that gave her presence. Her hair was snow white, swept up in an old-fashioned but elegant style, and her eyes were the same intelligent, observant gray as her nephew’s. She wore a simple, well-cut dress of dark purple silk, and a string of pearls was her only ornament. She was, Margaret thought, exactly what a professor’s aunt should be. “So,” said Mrs. Pamela Tenison, her gaze sweeping over Margaret from head to toe. It was a sharp, appraising look, but not an unkind one. “You are the girl with the braids.” John stepped forward and kissed her cheek. “Aunt Pam, this is Margaret Paget. Margaret, my aunt, Mrs. Tenison.” “I am so happy to meet you, Mrs. Tenison,” Margaret said, her voice a little breathless. She extended her hand. Aunt Pam took it, her grip surprisingly firm. She did not let go, but held Margaret’s hand for a moment, studying her face. “Paget,” she said, her voice crisp and clear. “There were Pagets in Boston. Related to the Adams family, I believe. Are you one of those?” “No, Mrs. Tenison,” Margaret said simply. “My father’s family is from Vermont. Farmers and teachers, mostly.” “Good,” said Aunt Pam, decisively. “Can’t stand the Boston Adamses. Smug. So, you stood up to Hattie Carr-Boldt.” It wasn’t a question. “I--I suppose I did,” Margaret stammered. “She is a foolish woman,” Mrs. Tenison declared, finally releasing Margaret’s hand and gesturing towards an armchair by the fire. “Sit down, child, you look tired. John, tell Mary to bring tea. Hattie Carr-Boldt has more money than sense, always has. She thinks life is a business transaction. Buys her friends, buys her servants, and thinks she can buy her happiness. And you told her she was wrong.” “I told her I would not trade my family for all the money in the world,” Margaret said quietly. A slow smile spread across Aunt Pam’s face. It transformed her, softening the sharp lines and making her eyes sparkle. She looked at John, who was watching them with an anxious fondness. “Well, John,” she said. “You were right. She’ll do. She has backbone.” She turned back to Margaret. “My nephew tells me you come from a large family. Seven children, is it? Excellent. This house was built for children. Mine are all grown and gone, and the place is too quiet. John has been rattling around his university rooms by himself for far too long.” Margaret felt a blush rise to her cheeks. It was all happening so fast, so easily. This formidable old woman was not discussing whether or not she was a suitable match; she was already arranging the future, a future that included children and this warm, book-filled house. “And I hear,” Aunt Pam continued, her eyes twinkling, “that you wear ruffly things. I approve. A bit of softness is a good thing in a woman. Far better than those spangled quills and calla lilies the Allen girls affect. Ghastly. You have good taste, my dear. And good sense. A rare combination.”

Later that evening, after a simple, delicious dinner served in a dining room that glowed with polished wood and candlelight, Aunt Pam announced that she was retiring early. “You two have things to talk about,” she said, pausing at the door. “Don’t stay up all night. We have a great deal to do tomorrow. We must plan a wedding.” She fixed her gaze on Margaret. “And we must arrange a journey. John tells me your family is in Massachusetts. It is high time we all went to meet them.” She disappeared, leaving a comfortable silence in her wake. John came and sat on the sofa beside Margaret. He took her hand, his thumb tracing gentle circles on her palm. “Well?” he asked softly. “Was it so very terrible?” Margaret leaned her head against his shoulder, a feeling of peace and utter contentment washing over her. The worries and heartaches of the past weeks, the past years, seemed a lifetime away. “She is wonderful, John,” she whispered. “She is everything you said.” “She loves you already,” he said. “I knew she would.” They sat for a long time in the warm firelight, surrounded by the quiet wisdom of a thousand books. The great house was still around them, its history seeming to welcome her into its fold. The life she had dreamed of, the “Wonderland” she had visited for two brief weeks, was no longer a fleeting fantasy. It was solid, it was real, it was here. She was home. “We’ll go to see my mother next week,” she said, thinking of the crowded, noisy little house with a rush of love. She was not ashamed of it now; she was proud. It was a part of her, the part that had given her the strength to win this incredible prize. “Yes,” John agreed. “We will.” He lifted her chin and looked into her eyes, his own full of a deep and steady love. “And then our life will begin.” Margaret smiled, a true, radiant smile that came from the very depths of her happy heart. It had already begun.
