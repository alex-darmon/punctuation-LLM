She escaped to the quiet sanctuary of her own room, a pretty chintz-draped space that looked out over the sparkling fountain. For a long time she stood at the window, not seeing the gardens, but the faces of her family. She was going home. And a homesickness for her own world, a world of real work and real love, swept over her with surprising force, eclipsing for a moment even the bright, impossible dream of John Tenison. Perhaps, she thought, with a sudden lift of her heart, perhaps her own world was Wonderland after all, and this, this sun-drenched, empty house, was only a shadow.

CHAPTER V

The telegram arrived on a Tuesday morning, a hideous yellow blot against the cheerful clutter of the Paget family's breakfast table. It was Tom, her seventeen-year-old brother, who brought it from the station on his way back from an early errand. He handed it to Margaret, for she was the one who had dealings with the world of telegrams. The happy clamor of the younger children, arguing over the last of the oatmeal, died away into a sudden, frightened hush as she tore it open.

FATHER FELL FROM LADDER. UNCONSCIOUS. COME AT ONCE.

The words swam before her eyes. For a disoriented second she was back in the white-and-gray morning room, hearing Mrs. Carr-Boldt's brisk chatter. Then the reality of her own kitchen, with its smell of coffee and fried bacon and the palpable presence of her family's fear, crashed down upon her.

"Mother," she said, and her voice sounded strange and thin. "It's Father. There's been an accident."

The journey home was a nightmare of jarring wheels and smoke-grimed windows, every mile a torment of suspense. She saw nothing of the passing country; she saw only her father's face, his kind, scholarly, slightly stooped figure. He was the quiet center of their boisterous family, the gentle, humorous authority to whom they all turned. The thought of him broken, silenced, was an agony too sharp to be borne.

She found the little house in Dayton in a state of terrified, unnaturally quiet order. Her mother, pale but composed, met her at the door, her face a mask of worn courage. The doctor had been and gone. Her father was in his bed, in the small downstairs room that was usually his study. He had not woken up.

The days that followed were a blur of hushed voices, of tiptoeing on the worn linoleum; a weary round of whispered conferences with the doctor, of boiling cloths, of trying to tempt an appetite that was no longer there. The younger children, wide-eyed and unnaturally good, were sent to play at a neighbor's house. Tom, suddenly a man, ran the endless errands, his boyish face set in grim lines. Margaret and her mother took turns at the bedside, a silent, terrible vigil. Life, which had seemed so rosy-colored only a few weeks before, had narrowed to the four walls of this sickroom, to the sound of labored breathing, to the desperate, unspoken prayer in their hearts.

She sent a telegram to Mrs. Carr-Boldt from the little office at the great country house: FATHER'S CONDITION UNCHANGED. SITUATION GRAVE. PLEASE EXTEND LEAVE. A reply came almost immediately: TAKE ALL TIME NECESSARY. WE ARE MANAGING. DO NOT WORRY ABOUT US. CB. The kindness was a comfort, but it was also a reminder of the vast, impassable chasm between her two lives. Here, in this small, dark house, she was at the center of the storm, a pillar of a desperate family. There, in that bright, airy world, she was a dispensable part, a secretary whose absence could be "managed."

One evening, after a particularly grueling day, she sat down at the crowded kitchen table to write a letter. She had to write to him. It was a compulsion, a need to connect with the one person who represented the life she had briefly touched, the life that now seemed an impossible dream. She had no fine stationery now, only a sheet of lined paper torn from a child's notebook.

"Dear Dr. Tenison," she began, her pen scratching in the quiet room. "I must write to you to explain my silence. I am at home, in Dayton. My father has had a terrible accident, and he is very ill. I do not know when I will be able to return East."

She paused, the words she wanted to write crowding her throat. She wanted to tell him everything. She wanted to describe the smell of sickness and poverty that clung to the house, the constant, gnawing fear of the next bill, the crushing weight of her family's despair. She wanted to tell him that the girl with the braids and the ruffly things he thought would "fit into the Dayton picture" was a fantasy, a character she had played for two happy weeks. The real Margaret Paget was here, in this kitchen, with ink on her fingers and terror in her heart.

But she could not. She could not bridge that gulf with a letter. To do so would be to ask for pity, and her pride, the last unassailable fortress of the poor, recoiled from that. She dipped her pen again.

"I am afraid our plans to visit the museum must be postponed indefinitely. I hope your own autumn begins pleasantly. Sincerely, Margaret Paget."

She sealed the letter and gave it to Tom to post. As she watched him go, a sense of utter finality washed over her. She had closed the door to Wonderland herself. She had sent her regrets to a life she could never lead, and now she was left in the dark, with nothing but the ugly, vital things she had been born to. The longing for money and power was no longer a girlish fancy; it was a desperate prayer. It was the only thing that could save them. It was the only thing that could buy a man's life, and a daughter's future. And she did not have it. She was only a little middle-class country school teacher, after all, and her happy days were over.

A week later, a letter arrived for her. It was an envelope of heavy, cream-colored stock, addressed in a strong, slightly academic hand that made her heart give a painful leap. It was from him. She carried it up to the little attic room she shared with her two younger sisters, and sat on the edge of her bed to read it, her hands trembling.

"My dear Miss Paget," it began. "I was very sorry to hear of your father's accident, and I hope that by now his condition has taken a turn for the better. There is nothing so trying as such a vigil. Please do not trouble yourself for a moment about postponed plans; they will wait. I find myself thinking often of our walks, and the surprising similarity between certain species of Bar Harbor lichen and those I have catalogued in the Adirondacks. I trust the German physician's diagnosis for Victoria was not too severe in the end; Mrs. Carr-Boldt's nerves are hardly a match for a prolonged crisis. If there is anything, anything at all, that a humble professor might do to be of service from this distance, you have only to command me. With sincerest wishes for your father's recovery, I remain, Yours, John Tenison."

She read it three times, her heart hammering against her ribs. It was a kind letter, a gentleman's letter; it was also as remote as a message from another planet. Lichen! He was thinking of lichen, while she was thinking of doctor's bills and the terrible, rattling sound in her father's chest. A wave of shame washed over her, hot and prickling. What had she expected? A declaration of love? A promise to rush to her side? He was exactly what he had always seemed: a kind, intelligent, deeply impersonal man. He would send his sincere wishes, and then he would go back to his books and his specimens, and the Allens would invite him to dine, and he would gratefully accept.

She folded the letter and put it away in the little wooden box where she kept her few treasures. It was a souvenir from Wonderland, nothing more. The real world was here, in the hushed house, and it was growing more grim with every passing day.

Dr. Evans came again that afternoon. He was a good man, an old friend of the family, but his face was grave. He stood with Margaret and her mother in the little parlor, twisting his hat in his hands.

"I'll be plain with you, Mary," he said to Mrs. Paget. "There's pressure on the brain. I've done all I can. He needs a specialist. There's a man in Cincinnati, a Dr. Wainwright, who's doing wonderful things with these sorts of injuries. A surgeon. If anyone can help him, he can."

"A surgeon," Mrs. Paget whispered, her hand flying to her throat. The word itself was terrifying, a harbinger of gleaming knives and unthinkable risks.

"And the cost?" Margaret asked, her voice steady. It was always her, now, who had to ask the practical questions.

Dr. Evans looked away, at the worn spot on the carpet. "He's not a cheap man, Margaret. The consultation, the hospital, the operation itself... you would be fortunate to manage it for three hundred dollars."

Three hundred dollars. It might as well have been three million. A stunned silence fell in the little room. Three hundred dollars was more than half of what her father earned in an entire year. They had a few dollars saved in a tin box on the mantel, for emergencies. This was the ultimate emergency, and it would empty the box and barely make a dent in the sum required.

That night, after her mother had cried herself into an exhausted sleep, Margaret sat alone at the kitchen table. The house was silent save for the ticking of the clock and the faint, labored breathing from the other room. Three hundred dollars. The sum beat a tattoo in her brain. It was the price of a few dresses for Mrs. Carr-Boldt, the cost of a single piece of jewelry for Mrs. Watson, a casual loss at cards for the men who visited the great country house. It was a fortune to them; it was nothing to those others.

The longing for money and power, the desperate prayer, returned with the force of a physical blow. It was no longer a vague wish for a better life; it was a sharp, specific, agonizing need. Pride was a luxury she could no longer afford. The memory of the white-and-gray morning room, of the careless talk of complications, came back to her with bitter clarity. These women, for all their emptiness, had power. They could command surgeons from Cincinnati. They could buy life.

She thought of her letter to Dr. Tenison, of her stiff-necked pride in refusing to ask for anything. What a fool she had been! What did her pride matter, measured against her father's life? She had one last connection to that world of power, one person to whom she was not simply a name on a list of applicants. She was "Peggy dear," the competent, trusted secretary.

To beg, for that is what it was, to lay bare their poverty and their desperation to the very woman whose life was a constant, galling reminder of all they did not have--it was the most bitter pill she could imagine. But she would swallow it. For her father. For her mother's ravaged face. For the frightened children.

She pulled another sheet of paper from the children's tablet and dipped her pen. There was no hesitation this time. The words came starkly, stripped of all pretense.

"Dear Mrs. Carr-Boldt," she wrote. "I am writing to you under the most terrible circumstances. My father's condition has worsened, and his only hope is an operation by a specialist in Cincinnati. The cost is three hundred dollars, a sum which is utterly beyond our means. I know I have no right to ask, but my desperation is great. Would it be possible to arrange for an advance on my future salary in the amount of three hundred dollars? I would, of course, repay it, working for as long as it takes. I am ashamed to have to ask you, but I have no one else to turn to. I await your reply with the greatest anxiety. Yours respectfully, Margaret Paget."

She read it over, and a deep, shuddering sigh escaped her. It was done. The gulf between her two lives had been bridged, not by a letter of friendship, but by a beggar's plea. She had sent her formal good-bye to John Tenison, preserving her dignity. Now, she had cast that dignity aside for a chance at a miracle.

She sealed the envelope and placed it on the mantelpiece for Tom to post first thing in the morning. As she blew out the lamp and felt her way up the dark stairs, she felt a strange, cold calm settle over her. She had done it. She had sold the last of her pride for a chance, and now she could only wait, a paid dependent in fact as well as in name.

Margaret, arranging letters for the afternoon post, heard them with only half an ear. The letter to Mrs. Carr-Boldt sat on the mantel, a small white rectangle of hope and profound humiliation. The waiting began. It was a peculiar, suspended torment, a silence that was louder than any sound. Every footstep on the walk outside, every rattle of the postman's cart down the street, sent a jolt through Margaret's system, a sickening lurch of hope and fear. The hours stretched into days, each one a duplicate of the last. Morning brought the weary chores; boiling water, trying to tempt her father with a spoonful of broth, keeping the younger children quiet, a task that grew harder as their own childish spirits chafed against the oppressive gloom. Afternoons were an agony of listening, of watching the sun creep across the worn linoleum, of straining for a sound that never came.

Her mother moved through the house like a gentle ghost, her hands never idle, her eyes perpetually fixed on the door to the master bedroom. She and Margaret rarely spoke of the letter. The subject was too fraught, too packed with the terrible possibility of failure. She was their conduit, their only bridge to a world of miracles, and the responsibility was crushing her.

"Do you think she's got it yet, Maggie?" Tom asked one evening, his young face uncharacteristically somber as they sat on the porch steps.

"I suppose so, Tom. The post is slow sometimes."

"She'll do it, won't she?" he whispered, not looking at her. "She's rich. Three hundred dollars is probably nothing to her."

"It's a great deal of money to anyone, dear," Margaret said, her voice tight. She could not bring herself to say yes. She could not bear to give him a hope that might be dashed, to see the light in his eyes extinguished by a rich woman's whim. The memory of John Tenison, smiling between the two absurd hats, was a constant, bitter reminder of how easily the wealthy discarded things, and people, that ceased to amuse them. Was she, to Mrs. Carr-Boldt, anything more than an amusing, useful possession? A secretary who was "a perfect treasure" was one thing; a beggar with a sick father was quite another.

On the fourth day, the silence broke. It was late afternoon, a hot, sticky, breathless time of day. The younger children were napping fitfully, and the house was steeped in the unhappy smells of stale air and carbolic soap. Suddenly, from down the street, came a sound so alien, so startlingly out of place, that Margaret's heart stopped in her chest. It was the low, powerful rumble of a motor-car.

The children, roused from their sleep, scrambled to the window. "A car, a car!" shouted little Beth, her face pressed to the glass.

Margaret stood frozen in the middle of the room. A cold dread, far worse than the anxiety of waiting, washed over her. It couldn't be. And yet, she knew, with an unshakeable certainty, who it was. The car, a magnificent, gleaming black affair, purred to a stop directly in front of their small, shabby house. A uniformed chauffeur opened the door, and from the cool, dark interior emerged Mrs. Carr-Boldt.

She was a vision of another universe. In the dusty, sun-baked street, amidst the wilting gardens and peeling paint of the neighboring houses, she seemed impossibly cool and pristine in a traveling dress of pale gray linen, with a matching hat and immaculate white gloves. She looked at the house for a long moment, her expression unreadable, and then started up the cracked paving stone walk.

Margaret's mother gave a small, terrified gasp. "Saints preserve us," she whispered, twisting her apron in her hands. "It's her!"

There was nothing for it. Drawing a deep breath that did little to calm the frantic hammering in her chest, Margaret walked to the door and opened it.

"Mrs. Carr-Boldt," she said. Her voice sounded strange and thin in her own ears.

"Peggy, my dear," said Mrs. Carr-Boldt. Her tone was brisk, entirely devoid of sentiment. "I received your letter. A telegram would have been faster, but no matter. I took the morning train. Now, let us not have any hysterics. The situation is difficult, I can see that."

She swept past Margaret into the narrow hallway, bringing with her a faint, expensive scent of lavender water that seemed to suck all the familiar, homely air from the room. Her sharp eyes missed nothing; the scuffed baseboards, the faded photograph of Margaret's grandparents, the tiny crack in the plaster near the ceiling.

Her mother had shrunk back against the wall, utterly intimidated.

Mrs. Carr-Boldt noticed her at once. "You must be Mrs. Paget," she said, extending a gloved hand in a brief, firm gesture. It was not a handshake so much as a presentation. "Your daughter is a great treasure to me. I would be quite lost without her. We cannot have her efficiency impaired by worry."

The words, meant to be kind, landed like tiny, sharp blows. Margaret felt a hot blush creep up her neck. She was not a daughter whose family was in trouble; she was a valuable employee whose performance was at risk.

"Now," Mrs. Carr-Boldt continued, already moving toward the living room, "where is your husband? I should like to see the patient."

"He's resting," Margaret faltered. "The doctor said he must be kept absolutely quiet."

"I am not the doctor, Peggy, I am the one who is going to pay him," Mrs. Carr-Boldt stated, with irrefutable logic. "A brief inspection will do no harm."

She paused at the threshold of her father's room. He lay frighteningly still under the thin sheet, his face pale and sunken, his breathing shallow. Mrs. Carr-Boldt looked at him for no more than ten seconds, a frown of concentration on her face. "Hmph," she said, turning away. "Yes. I see. The matter is urgent."

She marched into the kitchen as if she owned it, placing her handsome leather handbag on the worn oilcloth of the table where the family ate their meals. She seemed to dwarf the little room, her presence an overwhelming force of wealth and will.

"Now then," she said, clicking open her bag. "Let us be practical. You asked for three hundred dollars. That is quite absurd. You cannot get a first rate man from Cincinnati, pay for transportation, a private room at the hospital, and proper nursing care for three hundred dollars. It is a beginning, perhaps, but it is not a solution. We are not interested in beginnings, Peggy, we are interested in solutions."

She pulled out a thick leather cheque-book and a fountain pen. Margaret and her mother watched, mesmerized, as if it were a magic wand.

"I will make the cheque for six hundred dollars," she announced, her pen scratching decisively across the paper. "That should cover the necessities. It is an advance against your salary, as you suggested. At your current rate, it will take you, let me see, approximately eighteen months to repay. That is agreeable?"

"Yes," Margaret whispered. "Oh, yes. Thank you." The words were wholly inadequate.

"Do not thank me," Mrs. Carr-Boldt said sharply, tearing the cheque from the book with a neat rip. "This is a business arrangement. You are my most trusted associate. Your mind needs to be on my affairs, not on… all this." She made a small, dismissive gesture that took in the entire house, the entire life, that Margaret knew. "Gratitude is an emotion. We are dealing with logistics."

She laid the cheque on the table. It was a long slip of pale green paper, covered in elegant script. It looked like a king's ransom. It was a life.

"Furthermore," Mrs. Carr-Boldt went on, already snapping her bag shut, her mind moving on to the next item on her agenda, "I have taken the liberty of sending a wire to the Good Samaritan Hospital in Cincinnati. They are expecting him. I have also spoken by telephone to Doctor Albright's office. He will see him tomorrow afternoon. The train leaves Dalton at seven fifty-two this evening. My chauffeur will take your father and your mother to the station. I have arranged for a compartment."

Margaret stared at her, speechless. In the space of five minutes, this woman had entered her home, assessed her family's tragedy, and solved it with the casual, terrifying efficiency of a general deploying troops. She had not offered sympathy; she had issued orders. She had not given a gift; she had made an investment.

"But… how?" Margaret finally managed. "How did you do all that?"

"My dear Peggy," Mrs. Carr-Boldt said, a flicker of what might have been amusement in her eyes. "One has connections. One has a telephone. It is not complicated. It is merely a matter of being decisive." She glanced at her wrist-watch. "Now, I must be going. I have an engagement in New York this evening. The chauffeur will return for you at seven thirty. You," she said, her gaze falling upon Mrs. Paget, who still had not moved, "will need to pack a bag. Just the essentials. Peggy will see to the rest."

And with that, she was gone. She swept out of the kitchen, through the hall, and out the front door, leaving a whirlwind of silence in her wake. The low rumble of the motor-car faded down the street, and the little house seemed to settle back into itself, stunned.

For a long moment, no one moved. Then, Mrs. Paget sank into a chair at the table, put her head down on her arms, and began to sob. They were not the silent, weary tears of the past weeks, but great, gulping, shuddering sobs of relief, of shock, of a release so profound it was painful.

Margaret walked over to the table and picked up the cheque. Six Hundred Dollars. The name of the New York bank was engraved at the top. It felt impossibly light in her hand, as if it were made of paper and not of miracles. Her prayer had been answered, but in a way she could never have imagined. She had not been granted a gift from a friend; she had been issued a line of credit from an employer. She had wanted to bridge the gulf between her world and theirs, and she had succeeded. Mrs. Carr-Boldt had reached across, and pulled her father to safety, and in the same motion, had bound Margaret to her service with chains of gratitude and debt, stronger and colder than any steel. The taste of victory was the taste of ashes.

She looked at her weeping mother, and then at the closed door of her father's room. He was saved. That was all that mattered. For now, it had to be.

"Don't cry, Mother," she said, her voice gentle but firm. A new tone had entered it, a note of authority she had not known she possessed. It was the echo of Mrs. Carr-Boldt's voice. "We have a great deal to do. The train leaves in less than three hours."



Her mother raised her head, her face a mess of tears and bewildered hope. She looked at Margaret, not as a daughter, but as a deliverer, a stranger who had suddenly appeared with a plan. It was the same look of helpless trust that Margaret had seen her give to the family doctor, to the minister, to any man who spoke with authority. Now, that look was for her. It was a burden heavier than any she had ever carried.

"But Peggy," her mother whispered, wiping her eyes with the back of her hand. "The house--the boys--"

"James and Rob can manage for a night," Margaret said, her voice crisp. She was already mentally packing, organizing, commanding. "Aunt Eleanor can come over in the morning. Her mother, her face still blotched with weeping, moved with a sort of dazed obedience; she folded Mr. Paget's worn nightshirts and his one good suit as if they were artifacts from another life. The boys, startled out of their own private griefs by this sudden onslaught of feminine efficiency, were clumsy but willing. They stumbled over each other in the narrow hallway, fetching, carrying, asking quiet, anxious questions that Margaret answered in monosyllables.

She herself was a marvel of competence. She went into her father's room, a cool, appraising look in her eyes. "Father," she said gently, "we're going to take you to New York. To a wonderful hospital. Everything is arranged."

He looked at her from his pillow, his eyes fever-bright but lucid. He had heard the murmur of a strange voice, the low thrum of a powerful motor. "Margaret? What--what's happened?"

A flicker of the old Paget pride, the pride of a man who had always paid his own way, flared in his sunken cheeks. "Carr-Boldt? The woman you work for? Margaret, I can't take charity from your employer."

"It's not charity, Father," she said, and the words tasted like metal in her mouth. "It is a loan. We will pay it back. Every cent." She looked him directly in the eye, and in her steady gaze he saw not his young daughter but a woman he did not recognize, a woman who brokered deals with the rich and powerful. He fell back against the pillows, exhausted, defeated.

To Margaret, it felt like the opening of a tomb.

The process of getting her father from his bed to the car was a nightmare of gentle hands and muffled groans, a slow, agonizing procession that stripped the family of its last shred of privacy. The neighbors were treated to the spectacle of Mr. Paget, pale and frail in his overcoat, supported on either side by his two lanky sons, shuffling down the porch steps. They saw Mrs. Paget, her face a mask of anxiety, clutching a small bag. And they saw Margaret, calm and erect, directing the whole sorry operation with the air of a visiting nurse. The ignominy of it burned her cheeks, a hot, shameful blush that had nothing to do with exertion.

Finally, they were all inside. The contrast with their own worn clothes, their own desperate mission, was a physical pain.

They were early, and they stood in a little, forlorn group on the platform, a tiny, shabby island in the river of travelers. Mr. Paget was seated on a bench, his head leaning wearily against a pillar. He was coming down the platform, walking with that easy, long-legged stride she knew so well. He had a book under his arm, and he was looking about him with the same eager, impersonal curiosity with which he looked at everything, at life itself. It was Professor John Tenison. Her heart gave a single, violent lurch, and then seemed to stop altogether. Of all the people in the world, of all the places, of all the times. She had a wild, animal impulse to turn, to hide, to push her family behind a baggage cart and pretend she was not there. But it was too late. He had seen her. His face broke into that wonderful, familiar smile, a smile of pure, uncomplicated delight.

"Miss Paget!" he called out, his voice carrying over the hiss of a nearby engine. "Margaret! What a magnificent coincidence!"

"Professor Tenison," she said. Her voice sounded strange and thin in her own ears.

There was no escape. The worlds she had so carefully kept separate had collided with the force of a railway disaster.

"I had to come home," Margaret said, her chin lifting a fraction of an inch. "My father has been very ill." She turned, a terrible, cold calm settling over her. "May I present my family? Professor, this is my mother, Mrs. Paget. And my brothers, James and Rob. And this is my father."

John Tenison was, before all else, a gentleman. He took off his hat, his expression shifting instantly to one of grave, sincere concern. "Mrs. Paget," he said, bowing slightly. "I am so honored to meet you. Margaret--Miss Paget--has spoken of you."

Mrs. Paget, flustered, could only murmur, "How do you do."

He shook hands with the boys, who looked at him as if he were a creature from another planet. "James, Rob. A pleasure." Then he turned to the man on the bench. "Mr. Paget, sir. I am deeply sorry to hear of your illness. I hope with all my heart for your speedy recovery."

Her father looked up, and for a moment, the fog of pain and fever seemed to lift. "You're the professor," he said, his voice a dry rasp. "The one Peggy talks about."

"I am," John Tenison said, with a gentle smile. "And she is my most prized student, though she doesn't know it."

It was all perfectly correct, perfectly kind, and perfectly awful. He was trying so hard to bridge the chasm, but his every polite phrase only served to make it wider. He could not see that their clothes were cheap, that their hands were rough, that they were consumed by a terror about money and death that was as foreign to him as a problem in Sanskrit. He saw only Margaret's family, and so he was kind to them. It was a kindness that felt, to Margaret, like a final, devastating blow.

"We are taking him to New York," she stated, her voice devoid of all emotion. "To the hospital."

"To New York!" His face brightened with intellectual fervor. "An excellent choice. The medical men there are second to none. Have you a specialist in mind? I know a fellow at Columbia, a Dr. Albright, who has done some truly revolutionary work on systemic infections. I could give him a call, if you like. Write you a letter of introduction."

"Do, Peggy, darling," said Mrs. Crawford, without opening her eyes. "Give our love to the--to the tribe!"

Mrs. Carr-Boldt, however, followed her into the hall. Her eyes were sharp, her manner brusque. "Don't pay any attention to them, Margaret. Rose Crawford has one spoiled little boy who is being raised by a German governess and a psychologist, and she thinks she's a Roman matron. And Evelyn Watson's greatest maternal sacrifice was giving up her morning cocktail for a month. They don't know the first thing about life."

"They know about their life," Margaret said quietly.

"Exactly. And their life is a padded box. You, my dear," Hattie poked a finger into Margaret's arm for emphasis, "you have a great advantage over them. You know what struggle is. You know what things cost. Don't ever forget that. It's your greatest asset."

Margaret looked at her employer, and the chill from the night before returned with a vengeance. An asset. Like her fluent French, or her perfect penmanship, or her knack for soothing difficult tradesmen over the telephone. Her poverty, her crowded home, her mother's sacrifices, all bundled up and labeled "Asset" in Hattie Carr-Boldt's mental ledger.

"I have to meet the professor this afternoon," Margaret said, changing the subject.

"Of course you do! I told him you were going away for the week end, but that he might catch you for an hour. Don't look so funereal about it, girl! He is a magnificent catch. Good family, a fine mind, and not entirely dependent on that university salary, I've made sure of that. His Aunt Pamela has a great deal of money and he's the only heir." Mrs. Carr-Boldt said it as if she were reporting the satisfactory results of a stock market investigation. "Just be your charming self. Let him do the talking. Men of his type love to explain things. And for heaven's sake, don't mention your seven brothers and sisters."

The warning hung in the air between them as the motor-car crunched on the gravel of the drive. Every instinct in Margaret's body screamed in protest. She wanted to turn and say, "I shall tell him everything. I shall tell him we have to plan our meals around the price of potatoes, and that my sister is wearing my old coat, and that my brother wants to go to college but we don't know how we can possibly send him. And if that frightens him off, then he isn't worth having!"

But she did not. She smiled her placid secretary's smile, murmured her thanks, and stepped into the car. She was still a paid dependent, after all. She was a soldier, and Hattie Carr-Boldt was her general. And the battle was not yet won.

He arrived promptly at three, looking just as she remembered him from Bar Harbor, with his eager, slightly abstracted air and his kind eyes. He was not dressed for the country; his city suit looked stiff and formal in the summery, chintz-filled drawing room. Margaret had changed into a simple white dress, one of her own, not a gift from Mrs. Carr-Boldt. It felt like a small, necessary declaration of independence.

"I was afraid I would miss you," he began, taking her hand and holding it for a moment longer than was strictly necessary. "Your employer--Mrs. Carr-Boldt--she's a formidable woman! She gave me a very precise ten minute window in which I might see you before you were whisked away."

"I am going home for two days," Margaret said, pouring him a cup of tea. Her hands were perfectly steady. "To Dayton."

"Ah, Dayton! The picture you fit into," he said, smiling with pleasure at the memory. "With your braids, and your ruffly things. I've told my Aunt Pam all about you. She is most anxious to meet the girl who is not like a New York girl."

Here was her chance. The words Mrs. Carr-Boldt had forbidden her to say rose to her lips. She thought of her mother, at this very moment probably turning the heel of a sock, with the younger children squabbling at her feet. She thought of the scent of laundry and baking bread that filled their small house, a scent so different from the potpourri and beeswax of this one.

"You and your aunt have a very idealized picture of me, I'm afraid, Doctor Tenison," she said, her voice low but clear. "My home is not at all like you imagine. We are not... we do not have a great deal."

He leaned forward, his expression full of a gentle, earnest sympathy that was almost harder to bear than scorn would have been. "Miss Paget, I am a professor. I assure you, I do not measure people by what they have. It is what they are. You have a fineness of spirit, a sort of--of inner light. That is not something money can buy. It is something one is born with. It is what I saw in you the moment we met."

He was sincere; she could not doubt it. But his sincerity was a silken net, woven from his own ideals. He was in love, not with Margaret Paget, the struggling school teacher from a crowded house, but with a heroine he had created, a high-minded, simple-hearted maiden who was a refreshing contrast to the Lily Allens of his world. She was an idea to him, just as she was an asset to Mrs. Carr-Boldt. A wave of profound loneliness washed over her.

She smiled, a little sadly. "You are very kind," she said, and allowed him to steer the conversation toward books, and travel, and his work at the university. When he left, an hour later, he asked permission to write to her. She granted it, feeling as if she were an actress playing a part, a part written for her by two different authors with two entirely different plots in mind.

The journey home was always a strange transition, a passage from one world to another so completely different that it felt like crossing a border into a foreign land. The luxurious motor-car gave way to the dusty, crowded train. The scent of salt air and roses was replaced by the smell of coal smoke and hot upholstery. And then, at the Dayton station, there was her brother Tom, seventeen and already taller than she, his face beaming, his suit a little too tight in the shoulders.

"Peggy!" he shouted, grabbing her bag. "You made it! Ma was afraid the millionaires wouldn't let you go!"

"Don't be silly, Tom," she said, laughing as she hugged him. "How is everyone? How is Ma's lumbago?"

"Same as ever. Charlie cut his knee open again, and Annie's in love with the grocer's boy, and Pa's worried about the tariff. Everything's normal," he said cheerfully. "You look like a queen, Peg. Honest. Like you belong on the cover of a magazine."

Walking beside him down the familiar, tree-lined streets, Margaret felt the tension of her other life begin to melt away. Here, she was not an asset or an ideal. She was Peggy, who had to be careful not to get gravy on her best dress, who knew how to soothe a crying child and how to make a dollar stretch further than seemed possible. Here, the struggle was not for a man's admiration or a place in society, but for things far more elemental: for food, for shoes, for a roof that did not leak.

The house, when they reached it, was a cacophony of life. Her younger sister, Mary, was practicing scales on the old upright piano, stumbling over the same passage again and again. Two small brothers, Charlie and Will, were arguing loudly over a wooden boat in the hall. From the kitchen came the warm, yeasty smell of baking bread and the sound of her mother's voice, humming slightly off-key.

"Margaret!" her mother cried, wiping her floury hands on her apron before enveloping her in a hug that smelled of cinnamon and love. Mrs. Paget was a small, tired-looking woman, her hair streaked with gray, her face a web of fine lines from worry and work. But her eyes, the same dark-lashed eyes as Margaret's, were full of a fierce, unyielding tenderness. "Let me look at you. Oh, you're too thin, child. They don't feed you properly in that grand house. We're having pot roast tonight. Your favorite."

The week end passed in a blur of simple, familiar routines. Margaret helped shell peas for dinner, her manicured nails looking absurdly out of place against the green pods. She read a story to the little ones, their warm bodies pressed close against her. She sat with her father in the evening, listening to his quiet, thoughtful pronouncements on politics and the state of the world. She helped her sister Annie press a dress for her meeting with the grocer's boy, offering advice on how to arrange her hair.

And through it all, she carried the thought of John Tenison like a secret jewel. A letter from him arrived on Saturday morning, a thick, creamy envelope that stood out from the household bills like an orchid in a vegetable patch. She took it up to the small room she shared with Annie, and read it sitting on the edge of her narrow bed. It was a beautiful letter, full of his thoughts on a book he was reading, and a witty description of his Aunt Pam's horror at some new social development. And it was full of her. He wrote of her calmness, her grace, her "still center in a turning world." He was building his fantasy of her, line by line.

That afternoon, as she sat on the porch swing, mending one of Charlie's torn shirts, a thought, audacious and terrifying, took hold of her. It was one thing to tell him about her family; it was another thing entirely to show him. The idea was madness. It would shatter his idealized image to pieces. It would be a disaster. Mrs. Carr-Boldt would call it social suicide. And yet. The thought would not leave her. If he was to care for her, the real her, he had to see this. He had to see the mended shirts, and the crowded rooms, and the mother who hummed off-key. He had to see the soil from which the "inner light" he so admired had actually grown.

Before she could lose her nerve, she went inside to the telephone in the hall, her heart hammering. She placed a long distance call to the hotel in New York where she knew he was staying.

"Doctor Tenison," she said, her voice breathless when he answered. "It's Margaret Paget."

"Miss Paget! What a wonderful surprise! Is everything all right?"

"Everything is fine. I... I had a rather impulsive idea. I know you said your aunt wished to meet me. But I was wondering if, before that, you might like to meet my family." She rushed on before he could answer. "I know it's a long way. But you could take the train tomorrow morning. We could meet you at the station. We have a very simple lunch in the middle of the day. You could see... you could see Dayton."

There was a silence on the other end of the line, a silence in which Margaret could hear the frantic beating of her own heart. She had done it. She had thrown her little bit of Wonderland to the wolves of reality.

Then he spoke, his voice warmer than she had ever heard it. "Miss Paget," he said, and she could hear the smile in his voice. "Margaret. I would be absolutely delighted. There is nothing in the world I would like more."

The next twenty four hours were a controlled panic. Her mother, at first flustered, rose to the occasion with grim determination. The entire house was scrubbed from top to bottom. The best tablecloth, reserved for Christmas and funerals, was brought out and ironed. Tom was dispatched to the butcher for a chicken, an unheard-of extravagance for a Sunday. Annie and Margaret spent hours debating the menu, trying to find a middle ground between what they could afford and what a man like Doctor Tenison might expect.

"He's a professor, Ma, not a king," Margaret said, trying to calm her mother's anxieties. "He'll be happy with whatever we give him."

"He's your young man," Mrs. Paget said, with an air of finality that permitted no argument. "And he is coming to my house. He will have the best we can offer."

When Margaret and Tom met him at the station, she saw her home town through his eyes for the first time. The dusty main street, the slightly shabby storefronts, the farmers in their Sunday best. He, in his light summer suit and straw boater, looked as if he had stepped out of a different century. Yet he showed no sign of condescension, only a keen and friendly interest.

"So this is it," he said, looking around with his eager air. "It's exactly as a proper American town should be."

The walk to the house was the longest of Margaret's life. At the gate, Charlie and Will, scrubbed and slicked down into an unnatural state of cleanliness, stared at him with wide, solemn eyes.

He crouched down to their level. "And who are you two gentlemen?" he asked gravely.

"I'm Charlie. I cut my knee," the boy announced, pointing to the impressive bandage.

"A wound received in battle, I have no doubt," John said, with a smile that instantly won them over.

Lunch was an agony and a triumph. Her father, stiff in his Sunday collar, spoke little, but listened to the professor with deep attention. Her mother, flushed from the heat of the stove, presided over the meal with a quiet dignity that Margaret had never appreciated so fully. The younger children, after their initial shyness, began to chatter. Tom, emboldened, asked the professor about his work, his questions surprisingly intelligent. John Tenison, for his part, was magnificent. He listened to Mr. Paget's views on the government with respect, he complimented Mrs. Paget's roast chicken with what was obviously genuine delight, and he engaged the children in a discussion about their wooden boat with the same earnestness he might have applied to a Platonic dialogue.

He did not once look at Margaret with pity, or with the disillusionment she had so dreaded. Instead, he looked at her with a new sort of wonder.

After the meal, while Mrs. Paget and Annie refused to let her help with the dishes, she and John sat on the worn cushions of the porch swing. The air was sleepy and warm, filled with the drone of bees and the distant shouts of children playing.

"Well," she said, her voice not quite steady. "Now you have seen the Dayton picture in its natural state. Not quite what you imagined, I suspect."

He was quiet for a long moment, pushing the swing gently with his foot. Margaret held her breath.

"No," he said at last, turning to look at her. His kind, scholarly face was serious. "No, it is not what I imagined." He paused. "It is a great deal more."

"More?" she whispered.

"Margaret," he said, taking her hand. His own was warm and firm. "When I met you, I thought you were like a rare, perfect flower I had found growing by chance in a fashionable, but very artificial, garden. I was wrong. You are not a flower. You are a tree. And this," he gestured with his free hand towards the noisy, loving, struggling life of the house, "this is the soil you grew in. It's not poor soil. It's deep. It's real. Don't you see? It explains everything. It explains your strength, your loyalty. Your... your fineness."

Tears pricked at the back of Margaret's eyes, tears of such profound relief that she felt almost weak. He saw. He truly saw. He had looked past the worn carpets and the mended clothes, and he had seen the love, and the strength, and the dignity. He had not been disillusioned; he had been enlightened.

"I was so afraid," she confessed, her voice thick with emotion. "I was afraid you would... despise us."

"Despise you?" He looked genuinely shocked. "My dear girl, I am humbled. My Aunt Pam, for all her goodness, lives a life as rarefied as Mrs. Carr-Boldt's. My whole life has been spent among books and theories. This," he said, his voice full of a quiet reverence, "this is life itself. Unvarnished. And it is a beautiful thing to see."

He raised her hand to his lips and kissed her work-roughened fingers, right there on the front porch of her parents' shabby, crowded, wonderful house. And in that moment, Margaret knew that the power she had craved was not the power that came from money, or a clever gown, or a victory over Maude Allen. It was the power to be herself, and to be loved for it. She had thrown down her cards, revealing her hand not as a play for social advantage, but as a simple, terrifying act of faith. And she had won. Not the battle Mrs. Carr-Boldt was fighting, but her own. And that, she was beginning to understand, was the only victory that truly mattered.
