Mr. Hemlock, my host’s uncle, having drained his claret, proceeded to hold his empty glass up to the candlelight, revolving it slowly between a thick thumb and forefinger, as if it contained some profound secret which he, and he alone, was privileged to decipher. We sat until the ladies withdrew, and then it was settled that upon my return to the city, I should be given a letter of introduction to Genevieve’s cousin; and then I was permitted to bow to her, and to watch her depart, and the room was instantly diminished. This was the man who had threatened to cut out my heart and liver; the man who had been hunted across the marshes and captured in a ditch, fighting like a wild beast with another of his kind.

It was well past nine o’t clock when we finally clattered over the cobblestones of London. The gas-lamps were lit, casting long, dancing shadows on the wet pavement. The disgrace of Trabb’s boy seemed a small and childish thing now, a trivial farce from a life I had long since left behind. I shook my head. “It is worse than that, Herbert. Far worse.” And then, in a low and halting voice, I told him. The man on the coach, my man, would have used such a fabric to wipe the mud from his boots.

“Nevertheless,” I insisted, with a stubbornness that surprised myself. “Something dark. A black, perhaps. Or a very deep grey.”

A shadow passed over Mr. Porson’s face, as if I had spoken a blasphemy. He exchanged a look with Herbert, a look that said, plainly, “Is our young friend unwell? Has he suffered a reverse of fortune? Has he, heaven forfend, been reading poetry?”

Herbert, bless his heart, came to my rescue. “Handel has a contemplative turn of mind, Porson,” he said lightly. “He is a philosopher in the making. He requires a waistcoat that will not distract him from his great thoughts.”

This explanation, improbable as it was, seemed to satisfy the tailor. He retired his idyllic sprigs with a sigh, and produced instead a length of black satin, so deep and lustrous that it seemed to absorb the light. I saw a pale, tense young man, dressed in the clothes of a gentleman, with the haunted eyes of a fugitive. And in that moment, the black satin seemed not merely appropriate, but inevitable. It was mourning cloth, and I was in mourning for the life I thought I had.

We left the shop, my order placed, and walked on through the labyrinth of the city. But the small errand had done nothing to calm my nerves; on the contrary, it had sharpened them. Every face in the passing crowd seemed to hold a potential threat. A burly drayman, wiping his mouth on the back of his hand, became my convict’s confederate. A shabby, furtive man, slipping into a gin-palace, was surely one of his messengers. I began to see the city not as a place of opportunity and adventure, but as a vast, intricate web, and I, a blundering fly, had just become aware of the spider.

“Handel, you are as jumpy as a cat on hot bricks,” Herbert remarked, as I started at the sudden clang of a blacksmith’s hammer from a nearby forge. The sound, so familiar from my childhood, now seemed menacing, a reminder of the world I had left behind, a world that was now, it seemed, reaching out to reclaim me.

“It is nothing,” I said, forcing a smile. “The city is so full of noises.”

But it was not nothing. The encounter on the coach had broken a seal on my past, and now its contents were seeping into my present. I thought of my sister, and of the weapon that had struck her down; the leg-iron, filed off, from a convict’s chain. My convict. I had given him the file. I had been his accomplice. The thought, which had lain dormant for so many years, now rose up with a terrible, accusing force. What if the man on the coach was not the one I had fed, but the other one, the one with the scarred face, his sworn enemy? What if he knew my secret? What if he had come to exact some terrible price for it?

My mind, in its agitation, fixed upon the one person in London who dealt in such secrets, who moved among such men as if they were his natural element. Mr. Jaggers. But the thought of confiding in him was more terrifying than the secret itself. I could sooner have confessed my sins to a marble statue. Then, another name came to me, a name associated not with the grim machinery of the law, but with a kind of eccentric security, a whimsical fortress against the cares of the world. Wemmick.

“Herbert,” I said, stopping so suddenly that a man behind us carrying a ladder had to swerve to avoid me, “I have a mind to walk to Walworth.”

Herbert looked at me in surprise. “To Walworth? To see Wemmick?”

“Yes,” I said, a strange sense of purpose solidifying within me. “I should like to see his castle. I should like to see the Aged Parent.”

Herbert, who had a standing invitation to visit The Castle himself but had never found the occasion, readily assented. The idea of such a bizarre excursion seemed to appeal to his sense of novelty. “A capital idea, Handel! A pilgrimage to the suburban shrine of portable property. We shall see the drawbridge, and the cannon, and all the wonders of which he speaks.”

The decision made, I felt a slight lifting of my spirits. To go to Walworth was to do something, to take some action, however indirect. It felt like a strategic retreat to a fortified position. We took a hackney-coach for part of the way, and then proceeded on foot, as the character of the streets began to change. The grand avenues gave way to meaner, more crowded thoroughfares, the shops grew smaller and more specialized in the sale of life’s grimmer necessities, and the air grew thick with the smells of river mud and coal smoke.

At last, we came to the region of tiny, identical houses, each with its pathetic little patch of garden, each asserting its individuality with a brightly painted door or a fanciful name. And there, amongst them, was Wemmick’s creation, a miniature fortress so absurd and yet so earnest that it was impossible not to be charmed by it. There was the moat, a ditch about four feet wide. There was the drawbridge, a single plank with a chain. There was the flagstaff, with a union jack fluttering bravely. And atop the roof, pointing menacingly at a neighbouring wash-house, was the cannon, the famous “Stinger.”

Wemmick himself was in his garden, tending his lettuces with the same meticulous care he applied to his legal documents. He wore a gardening hat and an old coat, and he looked so utterly different from the dry, guarded man of Little Britain that he might have been his own more cheerful brother.

“Halloa!” he cried, spotting us. “Mr. Pip! And Mr. Pocket! Welcome to the castle. You find me repelling an invasion of slugs. Stand by to be boarded!”

He lowered the plank-drawbridge with a great rattling of its chain, and we crossed the moat and entered his domain. The Aged Parent was seated in his customary chair by the fire, his face a picture of serene deafness, and we were presented to him with the usual ceremony of loud greetings and vigorous nods, which he received with a placid smile. Wemmick, it was clear, was immensely proud of his domestic arrangements, and he showed us around with the enthusiasm of a king displaying his kingdom.

“The secret, gentlemen,” he said, tapping a whitewashed wall, “is defensibility. A man’s home is his castle. My principle entirely. At nine o’clock every night, I fire the Stinger. The neighbours are used to it. It tells them, ‘Wemmick is home. Wemmick is secure.’ It is a very comforting sound.”

He bustled about, making us a drink, a strange concoction of his own devising involving rum and spices, which he served in thick glasses. As he did so, I watched him, my original purpose reasserting itself. Here, in this absurdly theatrical security, was the man who had advised me to get a hold of portable property. Here was a man who understood the value of what could be held, and what could be defended.

We sat, Herbert and Wemmick exchanging pleasantries, the Aged nodding happily at the fire, and I, waiting for an opportunity. At length, Herbert, who had a great interest in all things nautical, was drawn to a framed print of a ship on the wall, and fell into a conversation with Wemmick about tonnage and rigging. This was my chance.

I drew my chair a little closer to Wemmick’s. “Mr. Wemmick,” I began, my voice lower than I intended.

He turned to me, his bright, bird-like eyes fixing on mine. His Walworth face was open and friendly, but I could see the ghost of his Little Britain face behind it, the shrewd, cautious appraiser of men.

“I find myself in a… a predicament,” I said, finding the words difficult to form. “A matter from long ago. Something I had thought buried.”

Wemmick’s expression did not change, but a subtle alteration took place. The friendly host receded, and the man of business looked out. He did not speak, but merely cocked his head, an invitation to continue.

“I met a man,” I said, my heart hammering against my ribs. “A man from that time. From the place where I grew up. He knew me, though I could wish he did not. He seemed to believe he had some… some claim upon me.”

I stopped, unable to go on. I could not bring myself to say the word ‘convict.’ I could not utter the name of the place, ‘the hulks.’ To do so, even here, in this fortress of whimsy, seemed to bring the danger impossibly close.

Wemmick considered my words, his head tilted. He glanced towards the fire, where the Aged was peacefully sleeping, and then back at me. He took a sip of his rum concoction. When he spoke, his voice was quiet and practical, but it was the voice of Walworth, not Little Britain.

“A garden is a fine thing for worries, Pip,” he said, in a tone of utter conviction. “You plant a seed, it grows. You pull a weed, it is gone. Very satisfactory. Now, I have a patch of ground out by the back wall. It is overrun with nettles. A terrible business. They sting, and they spread, and they choke the good plants. What do you do with a nettle, Pip?”

I stared at him, bewildered by this sudden turn to horticulture. “I… I suppose you pull it up,” I stammered.

“Precisely,” said Wemmick, slapping his knee. “You take a firm hold, minding the stingers, and you pull it up by the roots. You don’t trim it. You don’t pretend it’s a flower. You get the whole thing, root and all, and you cast it on the fire. That is my advice.”

He nodded, as if he had solved the most complex of legal cases, and then turned his attention back to Herbert, who was still admiring the ship. “A fine vessel, is she not, Mr. Pocket? Built for the West Indies trade, I believe.”

And that was all. He had given his counsel. It was oblique, it was metaphorical, it was couched in the simple, practical terms of his domestic world. But I understood it perfectly. He had told me to face the problem, to grasp it firmly, and to eradicate it completely. It was sound advice, no doubt. But as I sat there, in that little wooden castle, a new and more terrible fear began to creep over me. What if the nettle was not something I could pull up? What if its roots were so entwined with my own that to pull it up would be to destroy myself?

We left Walworth as the dusk was gathering, after witnessing the firing of the Stinger, a ceremony which involved a great deal of smoke, a satisfying bang, and a look of immense pride on Wemmick’s face. As we walked back towards the more familiar and less fortified parts of London, Herbert was full of the strangeness and the charm of it all. But I was silent. Wemmick’s advice, meant to be empowering, had only served to deepen my sense of dread. He had shown me the path of the prudent man, the man of property. But I was not a prudent man. I was a man with expectations, a man in a story written by someone else, and I had a terrible feeling that I was not the hero of the tale, but merely its central, pitiable object. The night fell, and the gas-lamps of London bloomed one by one in the gloom, each one a small, temporary victory against a darkness that seemed to me to be infinite, and to be closing in.

The cheerfulness of Herbert, which on most occasions served as a kind of salutary tonic to my spirits, had on this evening an effect precisely the opposite, for his light-hearted chatter about the fortifications at Walworth and the singular character of Mr. Wemmick only served to throw the darkness of my own mood into sharper relief.

For Wemmick’s parable of the nettle, so practically and confidently delivered, had taken root in my mind and was flourishing there with a terrifying vigour. To grasp the problem, he had said; to pull it up, root and all. It was the advice of a man who saw the world as a series of manageable propositions. But my own world was not so. My nettle, I now saw with a clarity that was itself a form of agony, was not one problem but two, entwined and inseparable. There was the secret of my benefactor, a thing of dread and danger which I must at all costs conceal; and there was the secret of my own origins, the blacksmith’s forge and the coarse life from which I had been plucked, a thing of shame which I sought with equal desperation to forget. To pull up one was to lay bare the other. To eradicate the danger was to expose the shame; to maintain my gentlemanly station was to live in perpetual fear of the coarse hand that sustained me in it. I felt myself trapped not in a garden, but in a thicket of my own making, where every path led to a thorn.

We had been back in our chambers at Barnard’s Inn for perhaps an hour, and Herbert was making some attempt to render our cheerless apartment more habitable by the application of a feather duster, when a folded paper was thrust under our door with a scraping sound. Herbert, being nearer, retrieved it.

“For you, Pip,” he said, handing it to me. “A lady’s hand, I think, though a very determined one.”

I took it, and my heart gave a sickening lurch, for I did not recognise the script. It was not the elegant, spidery hand of Estella, nor the imperious scrawl of Miss Havisham. The letters were formed with a careful, unpractised deliberation; there were blots and hesitations, as if the writer had paused often to consider the weight of her words, or perhaps merely to re-dip her pen. I broke the seal with a sense of foreboding, and unfolded the sheet. The words within were few, and their message was simple, yet they struck me with the force of a physical blow.

It was from Biddy. She wrote to inform me that Joe Gargery, having some business which necessitated a journey to the metropolis, intended to call upon me on the following morning. He would be at Barnard’s Inn, she wrote, and hoped it would not be an inconvenience. She added that he was in good health, and sent his love. And that was all.

My first sensation was not of pleasure at the prospect of seeing my old, dear friend, but of a sudden, cold apprehension, a feeling so shot through with shame that I could not at first confess it, even to myself. Joe, here? In my rooms, where Herbert and I strove to maintain our precarious footing as gentlemen? Joe, with his honest, rough speech and his Sunday clothes and his profound unease in any setting grander than the kitchen of the Three Jolly Bargemen? The blood rushed to my face, and I crumpled the letter in my hand.

“Bad news, Pip?” Herbert asked, pausing in his labours.

“No, not bad news,” I stammered, attempting a smile that felt like a painful contortion of my features. “It is… it is only that Joe is coming to see me. Joe Gargery, my brother-in-law.”

“Is he?” said Herbert, with his unfailing good nature. “How famous! I have heard so much of him. We shall make him welcome, Pip. We must give him the best breakfast the larder can afford.”

His kindness was like a hot coal upon my head. For while he saw an opportunity for hospitality, I saw only the impending horror of the contrast between Joe’s world and the one I now inhabited. The memory of Trabb’s boy was fresh and raw, a public mockery of my pretensions. Joe’s visit, I felt with a terrible certainty, would be a private one, an intimate exposure of the same fraudulence. He would see my fine clothes, my books, my attempts at elegant living, and I would see in his simple, wondering eyes the reflection of the blacksmith’s boy I once was, and in some essential part of my being, still remained.

“Yes,” I said, my voice sounding strange and distant in my own ears. “Yes, we must make him welcome.”

I spent the ensuing morning in a state of pitiable agitation, which I endeavoured to conceal from Herbert under the guise of preparing for our guest. I rearranged the furniture, as if a different placement of our two chairs might somehow lessen the social gulf between us. I hid a silver-topped cane which I had lately purchased, deeming it an object of insupportable foppery. I even instructed the boy who brought our morning rolls to be particularly quiet, as though a boisterous delivery of bread might offend the delicate sensibilities of a blacksmith. Herbert watched my frantic activities with a gentle, puzzled air, but said nothing, for which I was at once grateful and resentful; grateful for his tact, and resentful of the innocence that prevented him from understanding the true nature of my turmoil.

At the appointed hour, I heard a heavy, deliberate tread upon the stairs. It was a sound I knew as well as my own name; the sound of Joe’s boots. But here, on the worn wooden steps of Barnard’s Inn, it sounded alien and thunderous, an invasion from another world. Each step seemed to shake the flimsy foundations of my new life. I stood by the door, my hand on the latch, my mouth dry.

When I opened it, he was standing on the landing, looking larger and more out of place than I could have possibly imagined. He wore his best suit of black, which was much too tight for him, and which, by its stiff and formal cut, only served to emphasize the immense, artless strength of his frame. He held his hat, a new and funereally black beaver, in both hands, turning it over and over as if it were a strange and hostile creature whose properties he was struggling to understand. His face, when he looked at me, was a study in conflicting emotions; there was affection, and pride, and a profound and bottomless awkwardness.

“Joe,” I said, trying to infuse my voice with a warmth I did not feel. “How good it is to see you. Come in, come in.”

“Pip,” he said, his voice a low rumble. Then, correcting himself with a glance at my attire, he added, “Mr. Pip, Sir. Which you have that growed, and that swelled, and that gentle-folked, as to be sure is a credit to all as ever had a hand in you.”

He stepped into the room as if entering a lion’s den, placing his feet with exaggerated care. He seemed to take up all the air. Herbert, with his customary grace, came forward and shook his hand, saying how glad he was to make his acquaintance at last. Joe, abashed, looked from Herbert’s easy smile to my own strained one, and seemed to shrink a little within his formidable suit.

“Your servant, Sir,” he said to Herbert. “And I am proud to be so.”

The ensuing breakfast was an ordeal of exquisite torture. I had laid out our best china, a decision I now regretted with every fibre of my being. Joe, accustomed to holding his cup in the palm of his hand and taking his bread and butter in mighty bites, was clearly perplexed by the delicate instruments before him. He addressed himself chiefly to Herbert, as if finding it easier to speak to a stranger than to me, with whom he shared so much and yet, at that moment, so little. He answered Herbert’s polite questions about the forge and the village with a constrained formality, peppering his speech with so many “Sirs” that it sounded like a litany.

But the true horror of the occasion was the business of the hat. Having entered with it, Joe appeared to have no idea what to do with it. He would not put it on the floor. He would not put it on a chair. He held it on his lap, where it impeded his attempts to manage his knife and fork. At one point, he tried to lodge it behind him, but it fell to the floor with a hollow boom. He retrieved it with a face of such burning mortification that I felt a stab of pity so sharp it was almost a physical pain. He then placed it upon the mantelpiece, a position from which it twice fell, once into the fender, until Herbert, in an act of supreme mercy, took it from him and placed it upon a side-table, saying, “There, Mr. Gargery, he will be quite safe till you are ready for him.”

Joe looked at the hat, marooned on the far side of the room, as if it were his only friend in the world, and then subsided into a miserable silence, from which even Herbert’s best efforts could not draw him. It was then that he turned to me.

“Pip,” he said, and the reversion to my old name was so sudden that it startled me. “I had a message for you, which it were the main reason for my coming, though I am sure I should have come in any case, to see how you was getting on.”

“A message, Joe?”

“From Miss Havisham, Pip,” he said, lowering his voice as if the name itself were a thing to be handled with reverence. “She sent a man over to the forge. My feelings for Estella, which I considered the highest and most refined part of my nature, were suddenly and horribly entangled with the coarse black suit and the dropped hat and the agonizing shame of Joe’s presence.

“Thank you, Joe,” I managed to say. “That is… that is great news.”

But my voice lacked all conviction. A silence fell, heavier and more profound than any that had come before. Joe looked at the crumbs on his plate, and then at his great, calloused hands, and then, finally, at me. When he spoke again, his voice was different. The forced gentility was gone, replaced by a simple, sorrowful dignity that was more powerful than any accusation.

“Pip, dear old chap,” he began, “life is made of ever so many partings welded together, as I may say, and one man’s a blacksmith, and one’s a whitesmith, and one’s a goldsmith, and one’s a coppersmith. Divisions among such must come, and must be met as they come. If there’s been any fault at all to-day, it’s mine. You and me is not two figures to be together in London; nor yet anywheres else but what is private, and where you’re known and respected, and where what’s well seen in me is not ill seen in you. I’m wrong in these clothes. I’m wrong out of the forge, the kitchen, or off th’ meshes. You won’t find half so much fault in me if you think of me in my forge dress, with my hammer in my hand, or even my pipe. You won’t find half so much fault in me if, supposing as you should ever wish to see me, you come and put your head in at the forge window and see Joe the blacksmith, there, at the old anvil, in the old burnt apron, sticking to the old work. I’m awful dull, but I hope I’ve beat out something nigh the rights of this at last. And so, God bless you, dear old Pip, old chap, God bless you!”

He stood up, retrieved his hat from the side-table without another glance at it, and walked to the door. He did not offer to shake my hand, nor did I offer mine. He simply gave me one last look, a look of unutterable, wounded love, and then he was gone. The sound of his boots descended the stairs, each step slower and heavier than the one before, until they faded into the general noise of the Inn.

I stood frozen for a moment, and then a wave of remorse so powerful that it threatened to drown me crashed over my head. I ran from the room and down the stairs, calling his name. “Joe! Joe!” But the courtyard was empty. I ran out into the street, looking this way and that, but he was lost in the London crowd. He had vanished, taking with him all the simple goodness I had just so cruelly rejected.

I returned to our rooms to find Herbert quietly clearing away the breakfast things. He did not look at me, but said, in a low voice, “A true gentleman, Pip, your brother-in-law. A nobleman of nature.”

I threw myself into a chair and buried my face in my hands. The news for which I had yearned, the summons from Estella, lay like ashes in my mouth. And so the glorious prospect that Miss Havisham had set before me arrived not as a gift, but as a judgement. It had been brought by the hand of the best man I had ever known, and in receiving it, I had revealed myself to be a snob, a coward, and a wretch. The shame of Trabb’s boy was a harmless comedy; this was tragedy. Wemmick’s nettle was not a simple weed to be pulled. It was the very ground upon which I stood, and it was poisoned through and through with my own ingratitude.

Herbert’s words, though meant in kindness, were as so many coals heaped upon my head. I could not speak; I could only nod, and feel the truth of his observation sink into me like a stone dropped into a stagnant well, stirring up the mud and foulness at the bottom. He did not press the point, but with that quiet delicacy that was his nature, began to speak of other things; of his own small progresses in the counting-house, of a new book he was reading, of some amusing folly of his father’s at Hammersmith. He talked, I believe, not for the sake of conversation, but to erect a shelter of commonplace noise around my monumental misery, a kindness for which I was at once profoundly grateful and miserably unworthy. The shame was absolute. It was not merely that I had been cruel to Joe; it was that in his simple presence, my own magnificent aspirations had been revealed for what they were: a heap of gaudy, ill-fitting clothes, which could not for a moment disguise the frightened, ungrateful boy from the forge. I saw him in my mind’s eye: his honest, bewildered face; his great, gentle hands, so clumsy with the knife and fork, so masterful with the hammer; his loyal heart, which I had just broken as carelessly as a boy breaks a toy. And for what? For a phantom, for a dream of being a gentleman, a dream that now seemed to consist chiefly in the liberty to be a greater snob and a more profound fool than Trabb’s boy had ever imagined.

The summons from Miss Havisham, the very paper that Joe had delivered, lay on the table between us. I had looked forward to this moment with a feverish intensity, imagining it as the glorious next chapter in the story of my life; now it looked like the warrant for my own moral execution. Herbert, seeing my eye fall upon it, at last fell silent.

“Well, Pip,” he said, with a gentle smile that had a little pity in it. “A dark cloud has passed, but the sun is commanded to shine, it seems. You must go to her.”

“Yes, Herbert,” I said, my voice sounding hollow and distant in my own ears. “I must go to her.”

“She is to be in London, then? To be near you?” he asked, with a hopeful air, as if this might be the balm for all my recent wounds.

“So it would appear,” I answered, picking up the note and reading it again, though its words were already burned into my memory. Estella was to arrive by the midday coach on the day after tomorrow. I was to meet her, and receive her instructions. Instructions! As if I were a footman, or a paid agent, which, when I considered my mysterious patronage, I supposed I was. And the most galling part of it was that I was entirely happy to be so. I, who had just winced at my connection to an honest blacksmith, was eager to prostrate myself before a girl who treated me with the most consistent and elegant contempt. Such were the contradictions that tore me apart.

The intervening day was a purgatory of my own making. I could not settle to any task. I tried to read, but the words swam before my eyes, and every hero of romance seemed to mock my own unheroic nature. I accompanied Herbert to his counting-house, but the sight of the clerks on their high stools, scratching away with their pens, filled me with a restless dread of a future that seemed at once too grand and too empty. The memory of Joe’s departing back was a spectre that followed me through the bustling streets of Cheapside and haunted my solitary walks along the river. I felt that if I could only have run after him, begged his forgiveness, and returned with him to the forge, I might have been saved; but I knew, with a sickening certainty, that I would not have done so even if the opportunity had been presented again. I was chained to my expectations, and the chain was forged of my own vanity.

On the appointed day, I dressed myself with a care that was agonising. Every coat seemed too plain, every cravat too common. I was in a torment of indecision, pacing my small room while Herbert watched me with an expression of amused sympathy. I was dressing for Estella, but I was also dressing for the ghost of Joe, and the two ideals were so irreconcilably opposed that I felt I might be rent in twain. Should I appear the fine London gentleman, and so confirm Joe’s sad judgement of me? Or should I affect a plainness that Estella would surely despise? In the end, I chose the path of least immediate resistance, which is to say, I chose the path of pleasing Estella. I put on my newest suit, my most lustrous boots, and a hat of such a fashionable gloss that it seemed to accuse the very air of being dusty.

Thus attired, and feeling like a fraud in every particular, I made my way to the coach-office, a place I had not visited since my own momentous arrival in London. It was a cavern of echoing clamour and the pungent smells of horse and leather and damp straw. Porters shouted, wheels rumbled on the cobblestones, and a general air of frantic transition pervaded the place. I found a corner from which I could watch the arrivals platform, and commenced my vigil, my heart beating a tattoo against my ribs that was worthy of a blacksmith’s hammer.

I was early, and had a long time to wait. I watched other coaches arrive, disgorging their passengers: florid-faced farmers blinking in the city gloom; prim-looking governesses shepherding their restless charges; young men in loud check trousers, full of provincial swagger. Each one of them seemed to me a character in a play for which I had no relish, and I looked upon them all with the supercilious eye of a man who is expecting a queen. My feelings for Estella had not been diminished by my shame; rather, they had been sharpened by it. She was the polar opposite of the world I had just rejected, the world of Joe and the forge. She was beauty, and elegance, and cold, glittering triumph. To win her, I felt, would be to justify everything, to silence the accusing voice of my own conscience. She was not a person; she was an absolution.

At last, the coach from our part of the country was called. It thundered into the yard, mud-spattered and travel-worn, the horses steaming in the cool air. The door was swung open, and the passengers began to descend. I scanned them with an eagle eye, my breath caught in my throat. A stout woman with a basket of geese; a sailor with a parrot in a cage; an elderly clergyman who sneezed violently upon hitting the London air. None of them was she. My heart sank. Had she missed the coach? Had Miss Havisham changed her mind?

And then I saw her. She had waited, it seemed, for the commoner sort to disembark first. She appeared in the doorway of the coach, and the grimy, bustling yard seemed to fall silent and hold its breath. She was not so much a passenger as a vision. The journey had not left the slightest trace of weariness upon her. Her dark eyes swept the crowd with an imperious and slightly bored curiosity, as if she were a star that had condescended to fall to earth, and found the ground rather dirtier than she had expected. A porter, struck dumb by her appearance, fumbled with the steps, and she descended with a grace that made the act of stepping onto muddy cobblestones seem like a movement from a celestial ballet.

I started forward, and then stopped, my own fine clothes suddenly feeling like sackcloth. She saw me. The faintest flicker of recognition crossed her features, but it was not a smile. It was the look one might give to a familiar piece of furniture that has been moved to a new and slightly inconvenient location.

“Ah, Pip,” she said, her voice the same clear, cold bell it had always been. “You are here. That is as it should be.”

I stammered something about being glad to see her, about hoping her journey was not too tiresome.

“The journey was what journeys are,” she said, dismissing the topic with a slight wave of her gloved hand. “Tedious. Miss Havisham has made arrangements. A carriage is waiting.”

She did not ask after my health, or my life, or my feelings. She simply stated the facts of our new situation. I was a part of the arrangements, a cog in the machine of her London debut. She gestured towards a street corner, where a smart but plain carriage, much finer than any hackney, was indeed waiting. A footman, seeing her signal, opened the door.

“We are to go to Richmond,” she said, as we walked towards it, my steps feeling clumsy and uncertain next to her fluid and confident stride. “A Mrs. Brandley has taken a house there for me. Miss Havisham considers her a suitable person to introduce me to society. You are to come and see me.”

“To Richmond? Yes, Estella. Whenever you wish,” I said, my heart leaping at the thought, even as my pride winced at the command.

We reached the carriage, and the footman bowed. But as I moved to follow Estella in, she paused and turned to me.

“Not now, Pip,” she said. “I must go with Mrs. Brandley’s woman, who is inside. You are to follow later. Mrs. Brandley will expect you. The address is The Laurels.”

She gave me the direction with the cool precision of a general giving orders to a subordinate. Before I could formulate a reply, a face appeared in the window of the carriage. It was a woman of middle age, with a pinched, severe expression, who looked me up and down as if I were a tradesman with a doubtful bill. This, I presumed, was Mrs. Brandley’s woman, a sort of human watchdog. Estella got into the carriage without a backward glance. The door closed, the footman sprang to his place, and the carriage rolled away, leaving me standing on the pavement, alone once more, with the smells of the coach-yard in my nostrils and the cold echo of her voice in my ears.

I stood there for a long time, the object of some curiosity to the loitering porters. I felt a fool. I had dressed myself up for a queen, and had been dismissed at the palace gate like a beggar. The contrast between the rapturous welcome I had imagined and the curt, business-like transaction that had actually occurred was so stark as to be almost comical. And yet, I was not angry. I was not even truly disappointed. This was Estella, after all. This was the way of things. Her coldness was a part of her beauty, the ice that preserved the perfection of the flower. To have her treat me with warmth and familiarity would have been so out of character as to be alarming. My role was to adore from a distance, to be the moth drawn to her flame, and if I were singed in the process, that was only to be expected.

And so, with a sigh that was one part misery and nine parts hopeless devotion, I set about finding my own way to Richmond. I had never been there, and knew it only as a name, a place of genteel retreat for the wealthy. It seemed a world away from Barnard’s Inn, and an eternity away from the Kentish marshes. I hired a cab, and as it rattled over the bridges and through the increasingly green and pleasant suburbs, I felt as if I were travelling not just through space, but through the strata of society itself. I was leaving the world of Herbert, and the memory of Joe, and entering the world of Estella, the world of The Laurels and Mrs. Brandley.

The Laurels was a handsome white villa, set back from the road behind a screen of immaculate shrubbery, from which it presumably took its name. It had an air of quiet, unostentatious wealth that was far more intimidating than the decaying grandeur of Satis House. A maid with an improbably white cap answered the door and showed me into a drawing-room that was a marvel of polished furniture, pale silks, and watercolour paintings of Swiss lakes. It was a room in which no one had ever had a loud thought, let alone a coarse emotion.

I was kept waiting for what felt like an age, and was just beginning to think I had been forgotten entirely, when the door opened and a lady entered. She was tall and slender, with a faded, aristocratic beauty and an air of gentle, perpetual melancholy. This was not the watchdog from the carriage, but a far more refined and formidable creature.

“Mr. Pip?” she said, her voice a soft, melodious whisper. “I am Mrs. Brandley. Estella has told me about you. It is a pleasure to welcome a friend of our dear Miss Havisham’s ward.”

She was all politeness and grace, but I felt her eyes assessing me with a practiced and unnerving precision. She was, I surmised, a widow with a fine house and a respectable name, but perhaps not the fortune to match. Taking on the ward of the wealthy and eccentric Miss Havisham would be a mutually beneficial arrangement: Estella would gain a chaperone and an entry into society; Mrs. Brandley would gain a beautiful charge and, no doubt, a handsome fee.

Estella then entered the room, and I saw at once that she had already been absorbed into this new world. She had changed her travelling dress for one of silk, and seemed as much a part of the elegant room as Mrs. Brandley herself. She greeted me with the same cool reserve as before, and for the next hour I was made to sit and make conversation, a task for which I was singularly ill-equipped.

The conversation was a delicate and baffling dance. They spoke of people I had never heard of, of balls and assemblies, of visits to be paid and invitations to be sought. It was a language of hints and nuances, in which everything was understood and nothing was stated plainly. I sat clutching my teacup, acutely conscious of my large hands and my provincial accent, feeling like an explorer who has stumbled into a society of beings whose customs are utterly alien. My one attempt to introduce a topic of my own, a mention of Mr. Wopsle’s theatrical endeavours, was met with a blank, polite silence that instantly made me wish the floor would swallow me up.

What I suffered from, in that pristine drawing-room, was an amplified version of what I had suffered in Miss Havisham’s chamber. It was the agonizing incompatibility, not between a cold presence and my feelings, but between an entire cold world and my own clumsy, earnest, and utterly unsuitable self. I was there on sufferance, a strange pet brought in from the country for Estella’s amusement, and I knew it. And Estella, I think, knew that I knew it, and derived a quiet satisfaction from the fact. She would occasionally direct a question to me, or translate some social nicety for my benefit, but she did it with the air of a tutor instructing a particularly slow pupil.

And then I understood the nature of my new existence, the true meaning of Miss Havisham’s command to “Love her, love her, love her!” I was to be Estella’s tame escort, her constant, adoring shadow. I was to be present as other, more suitable men, men who understood the language of the drawing-room and were not afraid of their teacups, admired her and courted her. I was to watch her break their hearts, and to have my own broken anew every single day. This was the high and great emotion to which I had been called. It was not to win the prize, but to be a perpetual, living testament to its value.

When at last I took my leave, promising to call again soon, I felt a sense of exhaustion so profound it was almost a physical pain. As I walked from the quiet, tree-lined street back towards the noise and grime of London, I felt as if I were crossing a border between two warring countries. Behind me was the world of Estella, a world of glittering, heartless perfection. Ahead of me was the world of Barnard’s Inn, of Herbert’s friendship, and of the lingering, shameful memory of Joe’s love. I was a citizen of neither, a spy in both, and the only passport I owned was a wallet full of money I had not earned, and a heart full of a love I was commanded to feel, but which brought me nothing but the most exquisite and unending pain. I had escaped the forge, but I had not found my freedom; I had merely exchanged one kind of fire for another.

The walk from that quiet street of fashionable retirement to the less salubrious precincts of my own Barnard’s Inn was a journey through more than mere miles of paving-stones and gas-lamps. It was a descent; a peeling away, layer by layer, of the fragile skin of gentlemanly pretence I had so recently acquired. The fine coat that had felt so necessary in Richmond now seemed a piece of foolish masquerade in the jostling, indifferent crowds of Cheapside. The polished boots that had trod so carefully on the Aubusson carpets now splashed through puddles of a most dubious and murky origin. With each step, the vision of Estella, resplendent and cold, receded a little, and the gnawing consciousness of myself, the blacksmith’s boy in gentleman’s clothes, grew more pronounced. It was a miserable and perplexing state; for while I felt the shame of my origins more keenly than ever in her presence, I also felt the utter hollowness of my new position more keenly than ever when I was away from her. I was a finely dressed fraud, and the worst of it was that the only person I truly wished to deceive, Estella, saw through the deception with perfect clarity, whilst the one person I should have been most honest with, myself, I was deceiving most completely.

As I was trudging through this mire of self-recrimination, my head downcast against the damp London air, a figure stepped out from a narrow alleyway with such abruptness that I nearly collided with him. I looked up, prepared with an apology, and found myself staring into the dry, wooden face of Mr. Wemmick. He was on his way home from Little Britain, his posture already beginning to lose some of its rigid, office-bound severity, like a clockwork toy beginning to wind down at the end of the day.

“Halloa, Pip!” he said, his mouth not quite relaxing into a post-office smile, but certainly less of a slit than usual. “Lost in contemplation? Or just lost? They are often the same thing in this city.”

“Mr. Wemmick,” I returned, feeling a strange sort of relief at the sight of his familiar, un-judging countenance. “I am on my way back to Barnard’s Inn.”

“Ah, from Richmond, I’ll be bound,” he said, with a sharp, bird-like nod. “I saw the direction in your eye. A valuable client, that one. A very valuable connection.” He seemed to be speaking of Estella’s guardian as one might speak of a particularly lucrative stock, which in a way, I supposed she was. “And did you make a good investment of your time?”

“An investment?” I repeated, confused. “I am not sure I take your meaning.”

“Every social call is an investment, Pip,” he explained, falling into step beside me and lowering his voice, as if imparting a great secret of commerce. “You put in your time, your best waistcoat, your most agreeable conversation. You watch the market. You see what the returns might be. It’s all a matter of assets and liabilities.” He glanced at my face, which must have expressed my utter bewilderment. “Feelings, for instance,” he continued, tapping my arm with a surprisingly hard finger, “are a liability in the field. “But a man must have feelings, Mr. Wemmick,” I protested, my own agonized emotions churning within me.

“Of course, of course,” he said briskly. “But he must know where to keep them. This is London,” he waved a hand at the smoky, clamorous street, “this is business. This is Little Britain. Feelings are for Walworth. You understand? Separate accounts. Never mix the funds.” He looked at me with an expression that was as close to kindly as his features would allow. “You’ve got a look about you, Pip. The look of a man who is mixing his funds. Take my advice: get yourself some portable property to worry about. Something you can touch. Something you can count. It will do you a world of good.” And with another sharp nod, a final, “Walworth!” uttered like a password, he turned off towards his own domestic castle, leaving me to ponder his cryptic financial advice.

His words, far from clarifying anything, only added to my confusion. He had spoken of Estella and my visit as a business transaction, and of my feelings as a poor investment. And yet, was not my entire life now a transaction, funded by a mysterious benefactor for a purpose I did not understand? Was not my love for Estella the very currency I was expected to spend? Wemmick’s philosophy, which seemed to work so well for him, with its clear division between the office and the home, was impossible for me. My office and my home were one and the same, and they were both filled with the agonizing presence of Estella. There was no Walworth for me, no drawbridge to pull up against the world. My heart was my castle, and it was under permanent siege.

When I finally reached the gloomy portal of Barnard’s Inn, I felt as if I had been walking for days. The air in the courtyard was stagnant and smelled of soot and decay, a scent which, until that evening, I had always found oppressive, but which now seemed almost like a welcome, if shabby, embrace. It was real, at least. It was not pretending to be a rose garden.

I climbed the rickety stairs to our chambers, my feet heavy with more than just fatigue. I found Herbert within, seated at our small table under the light of a single candle, surrounded by the papers and ledgers through which he was attempting, with his usual cheerful optimism, to chart a course to prosperity as an insurer of ships. The sight of him, so earnestly absorbed and yet so ready with a welcoming smile, struck a painful chord within me. Here was true friendship, simple and undemanded, and yet I felt I was betraying it with every beat of my Estella-obsessed heart.

“Handel, my dear boy!” he exclaimed, pushing his papers aside. “You have returned! I was beginning to think you had been enchanted and spirited away to a palace of ice. You have the look of one who has seen a Queen, and been found wanting.”

I forced a smile, though it felt like a crack in a mask. “And you, Herbert, have the look of a man who has insured the entire Royal Navy and is now calculating his profits.”

“If only, Handel, if only,” he sighed, but his eyes were fixed on me with a gentle, probing concern. “But never mind my phantom fleets. Tell me of your voyage. Was the shore as welcoming as you had hoped?”

I sank into the other chair, tossing my hat onto a pile of books. “The shore was beautiful, Herbert. Perfectly sculpted. And as hard as marble.” I could not bring myself to recount the humiliating details of the tea party, the feeling of being an exhibit, the cold perfection of Estella’s condescension. To speak of it would be to give it a greater reality, to admit the full depth of my own folly. “She was as lovely as ever,” I said, which was the one unassailable truth I could offer.

“And?” Herbert prompted softly. “Is lovely all?”

“Is it not enough?” I retorted, with more sharpness than I intended. “I am commanded to love her. I am not commanded to find her a chatterbox.”

Herbert was silent for a moment, tracing a line on the table with his finger. “My dear Handel,” he said at last, his voice losing none of its kindness, “I would not for the world speak ill of the lady. But a man may be commanded to besiege a fortress; he cannot be commanded to find the stones of its walls warm and inviting. I only fear that you are laying siege to a place that has no gate, and that you will spend your own warmth trying to thaw its frozen ramparts, until you have no warmth left for yourself.”

His words were so apt, so dreadfully close to the truth I had just been wrestling with, that they felt like a physical blow. I had no answer for him. What could I say? That this was my destiny, decreed by a madwoman in a rotting wedding dress? That my great expectations depended on my willingness to freeze to death at Estella’s feet? I mumbled something about being tired from the journey, and feigned a sudden interest in a letter that was lying on the table, addressed to me in a familiar, careful hand.

It had come by the afternoon post, Herbert informed me, while I was away playing the gentleman. I picked it up. The hand was Biddy’s. A wave of something indescribable washed over me: a mixture of warmth and profound, unshakable guilt. I had not written to her, or to Joe, in weeks. My new life had consumed me, and the thought of their simple, honest world was a constant, low-level reproach that I did my best to ignore. Now, here it was, in my hand, refusing to be ignored any longer.

“I shall just, ah, read this,” I said to Herbert, turning away from his compassionate gaze and moving closer to the candle. My hands trembled slightly as I broke the seal.

The letter was not long, and it was written in Biddy’s clear, unadorned prose, which was as different from the high-flown language of Estella’s circle as a homespun coat is from a silk one.

I hope this letter finds you well and prospering in your new station. We do not hear from you as often as we would like, but I tell Joe that a gentleman in London has many great and important things to occupy his time, and we must not be selfish.

I write to you on a matter that I am not sure is my place to mention, but I have no one else to turn to for an opinion, and I know you are grown so clever. It concerns Mr. Wopsle, who has, as you know, left the church for the stage. He has been performing in a small theatre not far from us, and Joe, in his good nature, went to see him, thinking to give him some encouragement. Pip, it was a disaster. Joe came back quite downcast. He said the people laughed in all the wrong places, and that Mr. Wopsle’s Hamlet was more like a man suffering from a terrible indigestion than a prince in mourning. Joe worries for him, and so do I. He has given up a steady, respectable position for this fancy, and I fear he will come to ruin.

But that is not the chief reason for my writing. My dear Pip, it is Joe I am most concerned about. He is not the same. He works as hard as ever in the forge, but the hammer does not ring with the same heart. I read the letter twice, and then a third time. Each word was a small, sharp pinprick to my conscience. Biddy’s simple, heartfelt concern for Wopsle, for Joe, for me, stood in such stark and terrible contrast to the cold, self-absorbed world I had just left. There, in that glittering drawing-room, no one was concerned for anyone but themselves. Here, in this smudged and simple letter, was a world of care.

The image of Joe, sitting alone in the chimney-corner, his smile not reaching his eyes, rose before me with unbearable clarity. Joe, who had protected me, who had loved me, who had been my truest and best friend. And I, in my new-found gentility, was ashamed of him. The thought came to me, unbidden and vile: what would Estella think if she saw him? What would she think of his rough hands, his misplaced aspirates, his honest, simple soul? The shame I felt then was not for Joe, but for myself. It was a deep, burning shame that scorched away all the fine layers of my gentlemanly pretence and left me raw.

“Bad news, Handel?” Herbert asked, his voice soft in the quiet room.

I could not speak. I folded the letter carefully and placed it in my waistcoat pocket, where it felt as heavy as a stone. I nodded, for my throat was thick with unshed tears. For Joe, and for the boy I once was, who would never have been ashamed of him.

“I am very sorry for it,” Herbert said, with a delicacy that was a balm to my spirit. He asked no more questions, but quietly went about making a pot of tea, as if he understood that what I needed most was not words, but the simple, unassuming comfort of a shared silence. As I sat there, watching the steam rise from my cup, I felt myself to be the poorest man in London. I had a pocketful of money and a heart full of misery. I was caught, not between two countries, but between two versions of myself: the one I had been, and the one I was supposed to be. And in that moment, I hated them both. The love I was commanded to feel was a poison, and the love I had left behind was a wound that would not heal. And I saw, with a clarity that was itself a kind of agony, that my great expectations were nothing but a grand and terrible mistake.

He gestured to a small table upon which were laid out a number of pieces of paper, covered in columns of figures and what appeared to be small, despairing-looking sketches of ships and anchors. It was Herbert’s method of “looking into his affairs,” an exercise which he undertook with great seriousness at regular intervals, and which always concluded with him being more cheerfully and hopelessly in debt than he was before.

“It is the case of the accruing interest, you see,” he explained, running a hand through his hair. “It accrues, Handel. It does so with a persistence that is quite deaf to reason. I have been explaining to it that I have prospects, but it does not seem to listen.”

Ordinarily, I should have entered into the spirit of this little comedy, and we would have spent an agreeable half-hour devising fantastical schemes to placate his creditors, which generally involved discovering a treasure island or inventing a patent sauce that would make us millionaires. He looked at me sharply, his own troubles forgotten in an instant. “You are out of spirits, Handel. Was your journey not a success? Did you not see _her_?”

The question pierced me. A success? I had seen Estella, more beautiful and more unattainable than ever. I had suffered the silent contempt of Jaggers, the public humiliation of Trabb’s boy, and the sharp, unendurable agony of my own conscience. And through it all, I had been commanded to love. A silence fell between us, more uncomfortable than any argument. It was the first time my own misery had ever been so great that it could not make room for Herbert’s; it was a small, selfish act, but it felt like a great betrayal.

Finally, he came and stood beside me. “Handel,” he said, very gently. “I am your friend. If there is a cloud, let me stand under it with you, even if I cannot bid it to disperse.”

His kindness was my undoing. The careful wall I had built around my feelings crumbled, and I found myself telling him, not everything, for I could not speak of Miss Havisham’s command, nor of the true depth of my shameful feelings about Joe; but I told him of my visit to the old town, of the way it had made me feel like a ghost haunting his own past, and of the letter from Biddy. “It is a torment, Herbert,” I burst out. “Here I am, with all these… expectations. I am to be a gentleman. But what sort of gentleman is ashamed of the man who raised him? What sort of gentleman winces at the thought of his own home?” I turned from the window and faced him, my hands clenched. “It is not Joe who is the problem. He is a noble man, Herbert, a king in a blacksmith’s apron. The problem is _me_. This new life, it has poisoned me. It has made me small and mean.”

Your affections are in one place, and your fortune is in another. We talked for a long time then, as the London evening closed in around us, filling the room with shadows. Herbert, with his gentle heart and his chivalrous imagination, proposed a dozen solutions. Perhaps I could educate Joe? We could send him books, and a tutor in the evenings to correct his speech. Or perhaps I could set him up in a new, more genteel business? A respectable ironmonger’s, perhaps, in a clean part of town?

I listened to these well-meaning absurdities with a feeling of profound hopelessness. To imagine Joe, my Joe, with a tutor and a book of grammar was like imagining a lion submitting to a haircut. It was to miss the entire point of him. His goodness was not something that could be refined or improved upon; it was rough, and it was elemental, like the iron he worked. To try and polish it would be to destroy it.

“You do not understand, Herbert,” I said, when he had run out of suggestions. “You cannot. It is not his manners I would change, but my own heart.”

It was then, in the depths of my despair, that a new, and I thought brilliant, idea began to form in my mind. It was an idea born of my new world, an idea of account books and transactions. If I could not give Joe my presence without shame, I could at least give him a portion of my prosperity. My guilt was a debt, and I would pay it, not with love and respect, for those were commodities I found myself bankrupt in, but with cold, hard cash.

The thought excited me. It was a clean solution, a gentleman’s solution. It would lift Joe and Biddy from their daily toil, it would be a magnificent, anonymous gesture, and it would, most importantly, allow me to keep my distance. I could be a benefactor from afar, a remote and mysterious power for good, without ever having to face the humbling reality of the forge.

“I know what I must do,” I said to Herbert, my voice filled with a new and artificial energy. “I will make amends. I will do something for them.”

I did not elaborate, and he, seeing my change of mood, was wise enough not to press me. But the plan had taken root. The next morning, I rose early, my mind abuzz. I would not simply send money, for that might be traced, and would require a letter of explanation that I felt utterly incapable of writing. No, my plan was more subtle, more in keeping with the grand and mysterious nature of my own expectations. I would use an agent.

And who better to act as an agent in a delicate financial matter than Mr. Wemmick? His dual life, his strict separation of business and pleasure, of Little Britain and Walworth, made him the perfect man. He understood the world of portable property, and he would appreciate the need for discretion.

Filled with this new purpose, I set out for Little Britain. It was still early, and the clerks were not yet at their desks. I found Wemmick alone, sharpening a collection of quills with a small, vicious-looking penknife.

“Halloa, Mr. Pip,” he said, without looking up. “You are an early bird. Come for a worm of legal advice?”

He stopped his sharpening and looked at me, his post-office mouth firmly closed. He gestured with his knife towards a chair. “In my official capacity, or…?”

“Oh, entirely unofficial,” I said quickly. “A Walworth matter, if you will.”

A glimmer of interest appeared in his eyes. He put down the knife and the quill, and leaned back in his chair, a signal that he was now in his Walworth frame of mind. “Fire away,” he said.

With a great deal of hesitation and many roundabout phrases, I laid out my plan. I did not mention Joe or Biddy by name, but referred to them as “a certain party,” a “worthy and deserving individual” to whom I owed a great deal. Wemmick listened to my entire convoluted proposal without a single interruption. His face was a perfect mask of professional neutrality. When I had finished, he picked up a new, unsharpened quill and examined it from all angles, as if its structure held the key to my dilemma.

She was more beautiful, more commanding, than I had even remembered. She had changed, had grown from a captivating girl into a woman of such startling loveliness that it took my breath away. She was dressed for travelling, but with an elegance that made the clothes of every other woman in sight look like rags. Her eyes, which held the cool indifference of a winter sky, swept the crowd, and I saw a flicker of something, perhaps impatience, perhaps disdain.

I stepped forward, my hat in my hand, my heart hammering. “Estella,” I said.

Her gaze found me. There was no smile of welcome, no sign of recognition beyond a slight, almost imperceptible nod. “You are Pip?” she asked, her voice as clear and cold as I remembered it. It was not a question so much as a confirmation of a fact.

“Yes,” I said, feeling suddenly clumsy and boyish. “I have come to meet you. Mr. Jaggers sent me.”

“I know,” she said, descending the final step with a grace that seemed effortless. “He wrote to Miss Havisham. I have a great deal of luggage. You will see to it.”

It was not a request, but an order. I, who had been dreaming of a tender reunion, was instantly cast in the role of a courier. I turned to find a porter, flustered and eager to please, and gave him instructions for the mountain of trunks and boxes that were being unloaded from the roof of the coach. When I turned back, Estella was watching me, her expression unreadable.

“We are to go to Richmond,” she stated. “A carriage is waiting, I presume?”

“Yes, I have arranged it,” I said. “It is just outside the yard.”

I offered her my arm. She looked at it for a second, a flicker of hesitation in her eyes, before resting her gloved fingers upon it with a touch so light it was barely there. The simple contact sent a jolt through me. We walked in silence through the bustling yard, a path clearing before us as if by magic. People stared, and I felt a surge of pride, a foolish, heady delight in being seen with her. I was the one chosen to escort this celestial creature.

The carriage I had hired was a handsome one, and I was glad of it. I handed her in, and after a final word with the porter to ensure the luggage would follow, I took my seat opposite her. The door closed, shutting out the noise of the city, and we were enclosed in our own small, quiet world. For a moment, I was overwhelmed with the sheer reality of it. She was here. She was sitting so close to me that I could see the intricate lace at her collar and smell the faint, clean scent of her perfume.

“It has been a long time, Estella,” I began, my voice a little unsteady.

“Has it?” she replied, looking out of the window as the carriage began to move. “I have not counted the days.”

The coldness of her reply was like a splash of icy water. I fell silent, my carefully rehearsed words of welcome dying on my lips. We rode for some time without speaking, the carriage rattling over the cobblestones. I watched her profile, the perfect, classical line of her nose and chin, the elegant curve of her neck. She seemed entirely self-possessed, as cool and distant as a star.

“Miss Havisham is well, I hope?” I ventured at last, seeking a safe topic.

“She is as she always is,” Estella said, without turning her head. “She has sent me to London to be educated. Not in books,” she added, a faint, metallic ring of amusement in her voice, “but in the world. I am to be finished. Like a piece of furniture. And I am to be seen by society.” She turned her eyes upon me then, and they were sharp and knowing. “You are to be my escort, Pip, to the various places I am taken. You are part of the plan. Are you equal to the task?”

“I will do anything you ask,” I said, earnestly.

A small, wintry smile touched her lips. “Miss Havisham has provided me with an allowance,” she went on, in a business-like tone. “And she has instructed me to pay my own expenses, and yours. You are to have no power over me, Pip. I am to have all the power. That is part of the education, you see. For both of us.”

She was to be admired, but not touched; to be accompanied, but not possessed.

“I understand,” I said, though I understood nothing except the pain that was coiling in my gut.

“No, you do not,” she said, with unnerving certainty. “But you will. I am to have suitors, you know. Admirers. Miss Havisham wishes it. She wishes to see their hearts break. “And what of your own heart, Estella?” I asked, the words escaping me before I could stop them.

She laughed, a sound that was beautiful but held no warmth. “You should know the answer to that, Pip. You were there when it was made. I have no heart. Or if I have one, it is not of the kind that breaks.”

We travelled the rest of the way to Richmond in a silence that was heavier and more profound than any I had ever known. My glorious expectations, which had soared so high that morning, lay in ruins around me. The prize I had longed for was in my grasp, and it was made of ice.

The house in Richmond was a large, handsome villa set back from the green, behind a screen of old trees. It belonged to a widow named Mrs. Brandley, a lady who had been a friend of Miss Havisham’s in some distant, forgotten era before the clocks had stopped. Mrs. Brandley and her daughter, a pale and rather plain young woman, received Estella with a flutter of respect and admiration. They were, it seemed, to be her chaperones, to introduce her into the proper circles. I was presented as “a ward of Mr. Jaggers,” a description which placed me in a vague and unsatisfactory position.

I stayed for a strained and formal dinner. Estella was the undisputed centre of the small universe. Mrs. Brandley hung on her every word; the daughter watched her with an expression of open-mouthed awe. I, meanwhile, felt myself to be an awkward and superfluous appendage. Estella treated me with a distant, polite civility that was more galling than open contempt. She directed a question to me now and then, about London or Mr. Jaggers, but her eyes would glaze over before I had finished my reply.

After dinner, at Estella’s cool suggestion, I took my leave. She accompanied me to the door. The hallway was dimly lit by a single lamp, which cast our shadows long upon the wall.

“You will call for me on Thursday,” she said. “We are to attend a ball. Mrs. Brandley has arranged it. Be here at eight.”

“I will, Estella. I will.” I looked at her, standing there in the soft light, so impossibly beautiful, so impossibly remote. All my carefully constructed gentlemanly composure deserted me. “Oh, Estella,” I whispered, “I have thought of nothing but you.”

She did not move, but her expression hardened, the faint softness of the lamplight freezing on her features. “That is your folly, not my fault,” she said, her voice low and sharp. “I have warned you. Remember what I told you. I have no heart.” She opened the door, and the cool night air rushed in. “Good night, Pip.”

I walked away from the house, my head in a spin. The elation of the morning had curdled into a familiar, aching misery. I had been given what I wanted, and it was a form of torture. As I made my way back towards London under a sky full of cold and distant stars, I thought of Joe’s warm and honest hand, and of the money I had sent to keep him at a comfortable distance. I had paid to be free of the blacksmith’s forge, only to find myself bound in chains of a far more exquisite and painful design. The wall I had built was higher than ever, and I now saw, with a clarity that was itself a kind of agony, that I was imprisoned on the wrong side of it.

I found myself studying the deep lines on my guardian’s face, the intimidating jut of his brow, and wondering if he had ever, in the whole of his closely guarded life, felt a pang of the helpless devotion that was then consuming me. The question, so innocently put, was a small, sharp dagger in my soul. I pictured Joe, his honest face smudged with soot, his strong arm swinging the hammer; and then I pictured myself, in my fine clothes, deliberately avoiding him, skulking through back ways to escape the notice of my oldest friends.

Herbert let out a low whistle. He put down his pen and came to stand before the fire, looking at me with a concerned expression. “To Richmond? So, it begins. Handel, my dear friend,” he said, adopting the gentle, advisory tone that I had come to both value and, in my pride, sometimes resent, “you must be careful. You must guard your heart.”

“My heart is already lost, Herbert,” I confessed, the words escaping me in a low rush. “It has been lost for years.”

He laid a hand on my shoulder. “I know it, Pip. But to be lost is one thing; to be broken is another. From what you have told me of this young lady, she is… well, she is prodigiously beautiful, I have no doubt. But is she kind?”

What could I say to that? That her kindness was to warn me of her cruelty? That her only act of mercy was to declare she had no mercy to give? I mumbled something noncommittal and turned the conversation to his own affairs, asking after the progress of his shipping business, a topic which, though it existed entirely on paper, was always sufficient to restore his good humour. He spoke with enthusiasm of potential cargoes and distant ports, of bills of lading and insurance premiums, and as he spoke, I felt a pang of envy for the simple, wholesome nature of his ambitions. He wanted to earn a living, to make his way, to marry his dear Clara and build a life. I, on the other hand, had been given a fortune, and I wanted the moon, a cold and distant sphere that was promised to me and yet withheld, leaving me to circle it in a state of perpetual, maddening orbit.

The days leading up to the ball were a trial. I lived in a state of feverish anticipation, my mind a constant theatre wherein I rehearsed a thousand imaginary conversations with Estella. In some, I was witty and charming, and she was captivated; in others, I was passionate and eloquent, and she was moved to tears. In none of them, I now reflect, was she the Estella I actually knew. I procured for myself the finest possible evening attire, sparing no expense, driven by the foolish notion that if only my coat were splendid enough, my waistcoat sufficiently elegant, she might see the gentleman in me and overlook the blacksmith’s boy. I spent hours before my glass, practising expressions of nonchalant sophistication, only to see the same anxious, unhappy face staring back at me.

On the appointed evening, I presented myself at the villa in Richmond. I was shown into the drawing room, where Mrs. Brandley and her daughter were fluttering about Estella like a pair of drab moths around a magnificent, icy flame. Estella herself was a vision. She was dressed in a gown of shimmering white, and the Havisham jewels, which I had last seen upon her in the dim candlelight of Satis House, now blazed upon her neck and in her hair under the full glare of the gas lamps, scattering rainbows of light with every turn of her head. She acknowledged my arrival with a cool nod.

“You are punctual, Pip,” she said, her voice devoid of any particular warmth. “Mrs. Brandley, this is Mr. Pirrip, of whom I have spoken.”

Mrs. Brandley, a woman who seemed to exist in a permanent state of obsequious nervousness, bestowed upon me a smile that was all bewildered courtesy. We made our way to the ball, a grand affair in a vast, echoing house in a fashionable square. The ballroom was a positive furnace of light and noise, a great churning sea of silks and satins, of powdered shoulders and gleaming jewels, where the music of the orchestra fought a losing battle against the clamour of a hundred conversations. I felt at once out of my depth, a clumsy swimmer cast into a glittering, treacherous ocean.

Estella, however, was in her element. She moved through it all not as a participant, but as a queen reviewing her subjects, her beauty a kind of cold fire that drew all eyes and yet gave no warmth. A circle of admirers formed around her instantly; young men with titled names and easy manners, old men with leering eyes and impressive fortunes. They buzzed about her, vying for a word or a glance, and she dispensed her attention in small, calculated doses, favouring no one, yet keeping them all in a state of hopeful suspense.

My role, I soon discovered, was to be little more than a silent and stationary sentinel. I was her escort, the one who had brought her, and this gave me a certain brief prominence, but once we were there, I was largely forgotten. I stood by the wall, holding her fan and her flowers, watching as she was swept onto the dance floor by one partner after another. With each man who took her hand, a fresh wave of jealousy and resentment washed over me. I saw the way they looked at her, with open, covetous admiration, and I saw the faint, cool smile she gave them in return, the same smile she so rarely gave to me.

It was during a waltz that my suffering reached its zenith. Her partner was a heavy, sallow young man, with a look of dull and brutish obstinacy about him; he had the air of a man who had never been contradicted in his life, and who found the world to be a perfectly simple arrangement of things he wanted and things he had not yet taken. Herbert, who knew everyone, had once pointed him out to me at the theatre and given him the name “the Spider,” on account of his sluggish manner and the unpleasant way he seemed to entrap everything that came within his reach. His name was Bentley Drummle.

To see Estella in the arms of this man, to see her head tilted back as she listened to whatever brutish inanities he was murmuring to her, was a form of torment so refined that I felt Miss Havisham herself must have designed it. He was clumsy, and trod on the hem of her gown; he was graceless, and held her far too closely; he was, in short, everything I believed myself not to be. And yet, she seemed to give him more of her attention than she had given any other. She laughed, a sound so rare from her lips that it was like a shard of glass, at something he said. I felt a hot, sick feeling rise in my throat.

When the dance was over, and she had returned to my side to reclaim her fan, I could not contain myself.

“Estella,” I said, my voice low and shaking with a passion I could not control, “how can you? How can you waste your time with such a creature? A man like that, an oaf, a dolt!”

She turned her gaze upon me, and there was no softness in it, no sympathy, only a kind of detached, clinical curiosity. “A creature?” she repeated, fanning herself slowly. “He is heir to a baronetcy, I am told. And a considerable fortune.”

“What is that to me? To you?” I demanded. “He is my inferior in every way that matters. He has no heart, no mind, no soul!”

“He has a name, and a property,” she said, her voice as cool and precise as an icicle. “You value things differently here, I am learning. Do you not see, you foolish boy?” she leaned a fraction closer, and her voice dropped to a conspiratorial whisper that was more chilling than any shout. “They are all moths, and I am the candle. It matters not which one’s wings are singed. This one,” she indicated Drummle, who was now swaggering towards the refreshment table, “is simply the largest and most stupid moth in the room. Is it not my purpose to attract them?”

“And I, Estella?” I asked, my own voice now a whisper. “Am I a moth as well?”

She looked at me for a long, unblinking moment, and for the first time that evening, a flicker of something unreadable passed through her eyes. “You,” she said, “are a lesson I was taught to learn by heart. Now, be silent. People are watching us.”

She turned away from me then, and smiled at an approaching dowager, leaving me to stand there with her cruel words echoing in the cavern of my chest. I was not a moth to be singed; I was something worse. I was a part of her design, a piece in her game, a lesson learned by rote. The misery of that moment was absolute. I had paid a fortune for this seat at the play, only to discover my role was to be that of the agonized spectator, forever watching the prize I could never possess be fought over by men I despised.

The rest of the evening passed in a blur of music and misery. I performed my duties mechanically, fetching her refreshments, enduring the condescending glances of her many suitors, and waiting for the moment when this exquisite torture would end. The ride back to Richmond was conducted in near-total silence. The carriage was dark, save for the occasional flicker of a passing gaslight, which would for a moment illuminate her perfect, impassive face. She seemed as remote and untouchable as a star, and I, beside her, felt my own worth dwindle to nothing.

At the door of the villa, I took my leave. I did not attempt to take her hand. My gentlemanly composure, so painstakingly constructed, had been shattered into a thousand pieces.

“I will call for you when next you require me, Estella,” I said, my voice flat and hollow.

“There is another ball, next week,” she said, not looking at me, her eyes fixed on the door before her. “Mrs. Brandley will send you a card with the details.”

And that was all. No word of thanks, no acknowledgement of the torment she had put me through. She was a task to be performed, a duty to be fulfilled. She turned and went inside, the door closing behind her with a soft, final click, leaving me alone in the cold, pre-dawn air.

As I began the long, solitary walk back to my own lodgings, I felt a strange and terrible clarity descend upon me. My great expectations, the mysterious gift that had lifted me from the forge and set me upon this path, had not been a promise of happiness with Estella. They had been a sentence. I was condemned to love her, to orbit her, to serve her purpose, which was to break the hearts of men. And I, the blacksmith’s boy, was to be the primary instrument of her education, and its first and most complete victim. The wall I had built around my old life was indeed high, but I saw now it was not to keep the world out; it was to keep me in, a prisoner in a gilded cage of my own foolish hopes, with the ghost of a blacksmith’s forge laughing at me from the other side. The stars above were fading, and the grey light of a new day was beginning to seep into the London sky, a day I had no wish to face.

“All is as it ever is at Satis House, Herbert,” I replied, my voice a dead thing in the chilly air. “And with Estella.”

I felt, in that moment, a profound and terrible isolation, the isolation of a man who has sold his soul for a dream and woken to find the payment was counterfeit.

We sat at breakfast, the two of us, I picking at a piece of toast that might as well have been sawdust, and he eating with the healthy appetite of a man whose conscience and heart were in good working order. It was then that a sharp rap came upon the door, and the boy from the porter’s lodge entered with the morning’s post. He wonders what grand things you are doing, and is prouder of you than I can say in a letter.

I read the letter twice, and then a third time. Each simple, earnest sentence was like a gentle hand turning a knife in a wound I had not known was so deep. The image of Joe, sitting alone by the fire in my old place, his honest face clouded with thought, wondering about my grand life; the mention of our lessons on the marshes, of a time when my world was small and my heart was whole; the sheer, unadorned goodness of Biddy, who asked for nothing and gave everything, it all conspired to overwhelm me. A tide of guilt and shame rose in me, so powerful that it threatened to drown me. There, in that letter, was a love as true and as steadfast as the forge itself, and I had abandoned it. I had traded it for a fistful of glittering jewels in a cold woman’s hair, for the privilege of being tolerated in rooms where my very soul felt unwelcome.

I had built a wall, I had thought, between my new life and my old. But the wall was a fiction. The old life was within me, a part of my very bones, and Biddy’s letter had reminded me of it with a force that was all the more devastating for its gentleness. For a wild, desperate moment, I entertained the notion of packing my trunk, of taking the first coach back to the village, of throwing myself at Joe’s feet and begging his forgiveness. I imagined the feel of his strong, blacksmith’s hand on my shoulder, the kindness in Biddy’s eyes.

But then, as swiftly as it came, the vision vanished, chased away by another. It was the image of Estella, her lip curling with disdain. “And who is this?” I could hear her ask, her voice cool and cutting, as Joe stood awkwardly in my London rooms, his hands stained with soot, his best clothes sitting unnaturally upon him. “Another of your charming country relations?” The thought was so vivid, so mortifying, that I physically recoiled. I could not bear it. I could not subject Joe to that, nor could I subject myself to the shame of it. My snobbery, the very quality I despised in others and had so diligently cultivated in myself, had become my jailor.

“No bad news, I hope, Handel?” Herbert asked gently, peering at me over the top of his newspaper.

“No,” I said, folding the letter with meticulous care, my fingers feeling clumsy and thick. “No, not bad news. Just, news from home.” I tucked the letter into my breast-pocket, where it lay like a warm poultice upon my cold heart, or perhaps a hot coal. I could not decide which. I resolved to answer it, of course; to write a long, kind, and appreciative letter. But not today. Today the task was beyond me. And I knew, with a certainty that was another form of agony, that tomorrow would likely be the same.

In the weeks that followed, I sought to bury the memory of that morning, and the letter, and the ball at Richmond, under a great mound of activity and expense. Herbert and I, having discovered that our joint finances were in a perpetually precarious state, joined a club for young gentlemen of similar disposition called “The Finches of the Grove.” The chief object of this society appeared to be the dining together at great cost, the quarrelling with waiters, and the general accumulation of debt, all under the guise of convivial fellowship. We were prodigiously wasteful. We hired boats and rowed on the river with no particular destination in mind; we attended the theatre, not to watch the play, but to be seen in our box; we purchased waistcoats of such startling colour and intricate design that they might have served as maps of unknown continents.

I threw myself into this life with a kind of desperate fervour. I was determined to be a London gentleman, if it killed me. And all the while, I was waiting. Waiting for the card from Mrs. Brandley, waiting for the summons from Estella, waiting for the next turn of the screw. The card arrived, as she had said it would, a stiff, formal square of pasteboard announcing another ball, at which my attendance was evidently expected. I sent my acceptance, and then spent an absurd sum of money on a new set of evening clothes, as if a finer cut of coat could somehow armor me against the pain that was to come.

My own finances, managed with such a careless hand, were becoming a source of considerable anxiety. The quarterly allowance from Mr. Jaggers, which had at first seemed a king’s ransom, was proving to be woefully inadequate for a Finch of the Grove who was also in orbit around a star like Estella. After a particularly expensive dinner with the Finches, during which the principal topic of conversation was the ordering of a magnificent silver punch-bowl for the club, a purchase to which all members were expected to contribute handsomely, I knew I could put it off no longer. I would have to go to Mr. Jaggers.

The journey to Little Britain was always a sobering one. The cheerful, bustling commerce of the main thoroughfares gave way to a warren of narrow, gloomy courts and alleys, where the very air seemed thick with secrets and sorrows. Mr. Jaggers’s office sat in the heart of this district like a spider in the centre of its web. The windows were so begrimed with the dust of ages that they admitted only a grudging, greyish light, and the atmosphere within was one of perpetual twilight.

As I entered, I saw that Mr. Jaggers was engaged with a client, a pale, twitching creature of a man who looked as though he had not seen daylight in a month. I took my seat on the hard bench reserved for those in waiting, a place I had occupied once before, and tried to appear as if I belonged there. A woman in a tattered shawl wept silently in the corner, clutching a bundle that might have been a baby, or merely rags. The air was heavy with the scent of unwashed bodies, stale law-books, and Mr. Jaggers’s ubiquitous scented soap.

Presently, the twitching man was dismissed, with a curt nod from my guardian, and he scuttled out of the office like a frightened beetle. Mr. Jaggers did not immediately acknowledge me. He stood before the empty fireplace, his hands clasped behind his back, a formidable and imposing figure. He then proceeded to wash his hands in the basin in the corner, a ritual he performed with a thoroughness that was both fascinating and unnerving, as if he were scrubbing away not merely dirt, but the very taint of the people with whom he dealt.

“Well, Pip,” he said, without turning round, his voice echoing slightly in the funereal quiet of the room. “And what brings you here? Is your quarter not yet up?”

I stood and approached his great desk, feeling like a schoolboy called before the headmaster. “No, Mr. Jaggers,” I said, my voice sounding thin and reedy. “But my expenses… that is to say, the requirements of my station…”

He turned then, and fixed me with his penetrating gaze. His thick, dark eyebrows were drawn together, and he bit his forefinger, a habit of his when considering a difficult point. “The requirements of your station,” he repeated slowly, as if the words themselves had a bad taste. “You are finding that being a gentleman is an expensive proposition, are you?”

“It is, sir. More so than I had anticipated.”

“I see.” He sat down in his great chair, which creaked under his weight like a coffin lid, and gestured for me to take the seat opposite. The chair he indicated was lower than his, so that I was forced to look up at him, a position of which I am sure he was well aware. “You have been keeping accounts, I trust? You have a ledger of your expenditures?”

“Well,” I stammered, feeling the heat rise in my cheeks. “Not a formal ledger, perhaps. But I have a general idea…”

“A general idea,” he cut in, his tone laced with a sarcasm so dry it was almost imperceptible. “Wemmick!” he called out, his voice a sharp bark.

The door to the outer office opened, and Wemmick appeared, his face a perfect mask of clerical impassivity. “Yes, Mr. Jaggers?”

“Mr. Pip finds his allowance insufficient. He has a ‘general idea’ of his expenses. What do you make of that, Wemmick?”

Wemmick’s post-office mouth twitched slightly, but his expression did not change. “A general idea, Mr. Jaggers,” he said, as if reporting a fact of law, “is not a receipt. It is not portable property.”

“Precisely,” said Mr. Jaggers. He leaned forward, his elbows on the desk, his great watch-chain glinting in the gloom. “Pip, your benefactor has provided you with a handsome income. An income on which many a man could raise a family and live in comfort. If you find it insufficient, the fault lies not in the sum, but in your management of it. However,” he paused, and I held my breath, “your benefactor’s instructions are that you are to be maintained as a gentleman. And if the society in which you move requires a certain level of expenditure, then that expenditure must be met. Within reason.”

He named a sum, a figure that was a considerable advance on my usual allowance, and my heart gave a leap of relief so profound it was almost painful. “Thank you, Mr. Jaggers,” I breathed. “Thank you.”

“Do not thank me,” he said, waving a hand dismissively. “I am merely an agent. It is not my money. Wemmick, see to it.” He turned his attention to a pile of papers on his desk, and I knew I was dismissed.

I followed Wemmick into the outer office, where he counted out the sum in crisp bank-notes, his movements precise and economical. As he handed them to me, he leaned in a little, and his voice dropped to a conspiratorial whisper. “Starting a collection of portable property, are we, Mr. Pip?” he asked, with a slight, dry wink that was gone as soon as it appeared. “Good principle. But take my advice: a gentleman’s life in London is a fine thing, but a little garden in Walworth, with a pig and a cannon… that’s the real prize. Something tangible.”

I murmured my thanks and fled from that place, clutching the bank-notes in my hand. The air of Little Britain seemed fresher, the sky brighter. I was solvent again. I was a gentleman with prospects. I could face the Finches, I could buy a new waistcoat, I could hire a carriage to the ball. And yet, as I made my way back towards the more cheerful parts of the city, a cold thought intruded upon my relief. The money, which should have felt like a gift, felt instead like a wage. I was being paid to maintain my station, to orbit Estella, to play my part in a drama whose author remained unknown. Wemmick’s words echoed in my ears. He spoke of a garden, a pig, something tangible. I had a pocketful of money, a new set of clothes, and a ticket to a ball. I had my great expectations. But as I walked through the crowded London streets, a wealthy and fashionable young man, I had the distinct and chilling sensation that I possessed nothing at all.

That this sensation, so sharp and unwelcome, should have chosen to accompany me on my return from Little Britain, felt a particular cruelty of Fate. For as I traversed the London streets, my pockets heavy with the rustling assurance of Mr. Jaggers’s bank-notes, my mind was a veritable theatre of grand designs. I saw myself at the Assembly, a figure of easy elegance, catching Estella’s eye across the crowded room; I pictured myself at the Finches of the Grove, that exclusive society of young gentlemen to which Herbert and I had lately been admitted, holding my own in wit and expenditure; I even composed, in the space of a half-dozen streets, a short and moving speech of gratitude to be delivered to my unknown benefactor, should the occasion ever arise. Yet, underneath this fine tapestry of future triumphs, the cold thread of Wemmick’s words, and of my own disquiet, ran insistently. A garden, a pig, something tangible. I had a pocketful of paper, and a headful of dreams, and the whole felt as substantial as a puff of London smoke.

My unease, however, is a creature that has ever thrived in solitude and wilted in society, and thus it was that upon rejoining Herbert at our chambers in Barnard’s Inn, my spirits experienced a considerable revival. He had news; a dinner was to be held by the Finches that very evening at a tavern in Covent Garden, a special occasion to settle some obscure point of honour regarding a horse-race, which naturally required a vast quantity of claret to adjudicate.

“You will come, of course, Pip?” he asked, already laying out his finest cravat. “Start-op will be there, and Drummle, I suppose.”

At the mention of Bentley Drummle, a shadow fell upon my newfound cheerfulness. This was the same Drummle I had known at Mr. Pocket’s, a young man of a sullen and heavy disposition, whom Mr. Jaggers himself had christened “the Spider.” He was possessed of a title, a fortune, and an air of morose superiority that seemed to crush all gaiety from his immediate vicinity. That he was also an admirer of Estella was a fact I knew with a dreadful certainty, and the thought of his brutish presence near her was a torment to me.

“Drummle,” I said, with a poor attempt at indifference. “Must he be everywhere?”

“He is a Finch, Pip, as are we,” said Herbert, with his customary good sense. “And we must take the rough with the smooth. Think of the claret, not the Spider.”

I allowed myself to be persuaded, for the alternative was to remain alone with my thoughts, which were proving to be most uncongenial company. And so it was that we presented ourselves that evening at the appointed tavern, in a private room that was already thick with the fumes of wine and the sound of loud, confident voices. The Finches of the Grove were in session, and their chief business, it seemed, was the expenditure of money and the cultivation of an almost heroic degree of indolence.

The room was a study in expensive carelessness; velvet curtains were drawn against the evening, half-drunken glasses stood upon every available surface, and young men with flushed faces and loosened collars sprawled in their chairs as if the very act of sitting upright was an intolerable exertion. They talked of horses they owned, debts they had accrued, and women they had pursued, all in a jargon so peculiar to their set that I, for all my gentlemanly aspirations, felt like a foreigner listening to a strange and baffling tongue.

Herbert, with his easy grace, was soon engaged in a lively debate with Start-op over the merits of a new theatrical production. I, meanwhile, found myself seated at the lower end of the table, a silent observer, feeling the bank-notes in my pocket as a kind of ticket of admission, a proof that I had paid for my seat, though I was not yet qualified to join the performance.

And then I saw him. Bentley Drummle was holding court at the head of the table, his large, block-like form filling his chair with a sullen magnificence. He was not speaking so much as emitting low, rumbling pronouncements, which his sycophants received with roars of laughter. He drank deeply, his eyes, small and sluggish in his heavy face, roving over the company as if assessing their value. When his gaze fell upon me, there was not the slightest flicker of recognition, only a dull, dismissive contempt, the look a man might give to a piece of furniture that was not to his taste.

I suffered this in silence, taking refuge in my wine and affecting an interest in the pattern of the wallpaper. I told myself that I was above him, that my finer sensibilities and my secret love for Estella elevated me to a sphere he could never enter. Yet, I could not deny the power he wielded in that room. It was the power of absolute, unthinking certainty; the certainty of a man who has never had to doubt his place in the world, who has never had to pretend or to strive. My wealth was a secret, a mystery, a gift from an unknown hand; his was a fact, as solid and immovable as his own brutish person. He was all portable property, and I was a mere expectation.

The dinner wore on, and with it, the consumption of wine grew more and more prodigious. Toasts were made, unintelligible to me, to horses named The Dandy and The Despair, and to persons referred to only by their initials. The air grew hotter, the laughter louder, the faces redder. Through it all, Drummle sat, a bloated monarch, his sullenness deepening with every glass.

It was during a lull in the boisterousness that he chose to speak, his voice cutting through the haze with its thick, deliberate tone. “A toast,” he slurred, raising his glass and fixing his heavy eyes on a point just above my head. “To the brightest star in all of Richmond.”

A murmur of interest went around the table. Richmond was where Estella was staying, under the protection of a lady of fashion who was to introduce her to society. My heart began to beat a terrible, suffocating tattoo against my ribs.

“A star, you say, Drummle?” called out one of the Finches. “And have you claimed this star for your own constellation?”

Drummle smiled, a slow, unpleasant stretching of his lips. It was a smile of ownership. “A man does not boast of such things,” he said, in a tone that was the very essence of a boast. “But let us say that the star does not… object to being observed. By the right sort of telescope.”

He drank, and the others laughed, a knowing, masculine laugh that made me feel sick with rage and impotence. He had not named her, but he did not need to. In that room, among those men, she had been reduced to a prize, a conquest, her name and reputation tossed about like a common bauble. And he, the Spider, had laid his claim.

I do not know what madness seized me then. Perhaps it was the wine, or the oppressive heat of the room, or the accumulated humiliations of the day. But I found myself on my feet, my own glass in my hand, my voice sounding strange and high in my own ears.

“And what of stars,” I said, my voice trembling, “that are so far above, that they mistake the observer for a mere insect, crawling in the dirt below?”

A sudden, complete silence fell upon the room. All eyes turned to me. I saw Herbert’s face, pale and alarmed, signalling for me to sit down. But I could not. I was transfixed by Drummle’s gaze, which had finally settled upon me, no longer dismissive, but filled with a slow, reptilian curiosity.

He set his glass down with a heavy thud. “An insect, you say?” he repeated, his voice dangerously soft. “We have an expert in entomology among us, it appears. Tell me, Mr… er… Pup?” He misstated my name with a deliberate, insulting laziness. “What kind of insect are you?”

“My name is Pip,” I said, though the words felt like dust in my mouth.

“Pip. Of course,” he said, as if remembering a tiresome detail. “The one with the… expectations. From somewhere muddy. Tell us, Pip. Is it true that your guardian is a Newgate lawyer? Does he give you an allowance for your impertinence, as well as your waistcoats?”

The insult was so public, so perfectly calculated to expose my precarious position, that I had no reply. To challenge him would be to risk a brawl, a thing utterly beneath the dignity I was so desperate to maintain. To remain silent was to accept the humiliation. I felt the blood drain from my face. My grand speech to my benefactor, my dreams of easy elegance, all shrivelled in the heat of his contempt. I was a boy in a gentleman’s suit, and he had seen it at a glance.

It was Herbert who saved me. He rose, his manner light but firm, and placed a hand on my shoulder. “Come, Pip,” he said, his voice ringing with a false cheerfulness. “It is late, and you know we have that business with the boat-builder in the morning. Finches! A pleasure, as always. Drummle, your wit is as sharp as ever.”

He steered me from the room, his grip on my arm surprisingly strong. I moved like a sleepwalker, past the blur of curious and mocking faces, out of the heat and noise and into the cool, damp air of the Covent Garden night. We did not speak until we had left the tavern far behind, our footsteps echoing in the empty market square.

“Are you quite mad, Pip?” Herbert asked, his voice a mixture of exasperation and concern. “To challenge Drummle? To what end? He is a block of wood. You cannot carve sense into him, you can only get splinters for your trouble.”

“He spoke of her, Herbert,” I said, my voice hoarse. “He spoke of Estella.”

“He spoke of a star,” Herbert corrected gently. “And he is welcome to it. A star is a cold and distant thing to worship. Drummle is a boor and a fool, and Estella, if she has any sense, will see it. You gain nothing by descending to his level.”

But I had descended. I had tried to play the gentleman’s game and had been swatted aside like a fly. The money in my pocket felt like a fraud. The fine clothes on my back felt like a costume. As we walked back to the gloom of Barnard’s Inn, I felt more keenly than ever that chilling sensation of possessing nothing, least of all the self-possession required of a true gentleman. I had great expectations, but Bentley Drummle had the one thing I lacked: he had the world as it was, and I had only the world as I wished it to be. And that night, the distance between the two seemed impossibly, agonizingly vast.

Back in our chambers, the air of defeat hung about me like a shroud. Herbert, with a kindness that I felt I did not deserve, busied himself making a weak, warm beverage, a task he always undertook in times of crisis, as if a lukewarm drink could solve the problems of the world. I slumped into a chair by the cold hearth, the scene at the tavern replaying in my mind with excruciating clarity: Drummle’s sneer, the laughter of the other Finches, my own feeble, impotent rage.

“It is not just that he is a brute, Herbert,” I said, speaking more to the empty fireplace than to him. “It is that he is a rich brute. His boorishness is seen as a kind of charming eccentricity, a sign of his authenticity. If I were to act as he does, I would be thrown out into the street. He has the right to be odious. My only right is to be pleasing.”

“Then be pleasing to those who matter, Pip,” he replied, handing me a cup. “To me. To your friends. Not to the likes of Drummle. His opinion is worth less than the dregs in this cup.” He took a sip of his own concoction and made a face. “And that, I assure you, is saying a great deal.”

His attempt at levity was a balm, but it could not heal the wound. The night had exposed a fundamental truth about my situation. I was not a gentleman in my own right; I was a gentleman by proxy, funded by a mysterious source for a mysterious purpose. Every pound I spent, every social circle I entered, only served to highlight the hollowness of my position. I was an actor on a stage, and I was beginning to suspect that my part was that of the fool.

We sat in silence for a time, the only sound the distant rumble of a cart on Holborn. It was in this quiet moment that our landlady’s daughter, a small, perpetually startled-looking girl, knocked and entered with a letter.

“For Mr. Pip, sir,” she said, placing it on the table and curtsying her way out of the room as if fleeing a pair of ghosts.

A letter at this hour was an unusual occurrence. I picked it up with a sense of foreboding. The envelope was of a common sort, the direction written in a hand that was at once careful and unpractised, the letters formed with a laborious precision that spoke of a great and unfamiliar effort. I knew the hand at once. It was Biddy’s.

My heart gave a lurch, a complex mixture of affection and shame. I had not written to them, not to Joe, not to Biddy, since my last visit. My life in London, with its frantic pursuit of Estella and social standing, had consumed me entirely, and the forge, and the marshes, and the two people in the world who loved me most unconditionally, had been relegated to a distant, hazy memory, a part of the past I was so eager to escape.

With trembling fingers, I broke the wax seal. Herbert, seeing my face, discreetly turned his attention to a book. The letter was not long. Biddy’s prose, like her person, was direct, and unadorned with any of the flourishes I was now accustomed to.

My Dear Pip,

I hope this letter finds you well, and that you are not finding London too vast or too lonely a place. We think of you often here. News is slight. Mr. Wopsle has gone to London himself, to seek his fortune on the stage. He gave a final reading of Hamlet in the village, which was much admired, though Mrs. Hubble fainted during the ghost scene and had to be carried out. Pumblechook was at the last market day, and told everyone that he was the founder of your fortune, and that you consult him on all your investments. I did not think this sounded quite like you, but I said nothing.

The forge is well. Joe is well. He works as hard as ever, and is a comfort to all. He asked me to write this for him, Pip. He has been taking lessons from me in the evenings, in his reading and his letters. He is a slow scholar, but a willing one, and his greatest ambition is to be able to write to you himself. He says, Pip, that if he were a scholar, he would write to you himself to tell you that his heart is with you, and that he is proud of the gentleman you have become, and that he hopes you will not forget your old friends at the forge. He said to tell you he is, and ever will be, your ever loving and affectionate friend, Joe.

And so I am his hand in this, and I am also,

Your affectionate friend,
Biddy.

I read the letter once, and then again. The words, so simple, so honest, struck me with the force of a physical blow. The scene at the Finches, my humiliation at the hands of Drummle, my anxieties about my allowance, all of it dissolved in a sudden, scalding wave of shame.

Mr. Wopsle’s folly, Pumblechook’s lies, none of it mattered. What mattered were those few, painful sentences. Joe, my Joe, patiently learning his letters in the evening glow of the forge, his great, clumsy hands struggling with a pen, all so that he could bridge the gulf that I had so wilfully created between us. He, who had protected me, who had loved me, who had asked for nothing in return, felt he had to become a “scholar” to be worthy of addressing me.

The letter fell from my hand onto the floor. I put my head in my hands, and for the first time since that day in the churchyard long ago, I wept. Not for my wounded pride, not for my unrequited love for Estella, but for my own monstrous ingratitude. I wept for the goodness I had abandoned, for the simple, true affection I had traded for the cold glitter of society. The tears that had come to my eyes at Joe’s last visit had soon dried; these, I knew, would not.

“Pip? My dear fellow, what is it?” Herbert was beside me, his hand on my shoulder, his face a mask of alarm.

I could not speak. I could only point to the letter on the floor. He picked it up and read it, his expression shifting from confusion to a deep, compassionate understanding. He said nothing, but simply stood by me, a silent, steadfast presence in the wreck of my own making.

I had left Little Britain that afternoon feeling that I possessed nothing. But I had been wrong. I possessed the unwavering love of a simple, honest man, a love I had done everything in my power to spurn. The bank-notes from Jaggers were a wage to keep me in a state of genteel captivity. This letter, this message from a world I had deemed too common for me, was a lifeline, a call back to something real and true. And in that moment, in our humble rooms in Barnard’s Inn, surrounded by the trappings of my new, empty life, I knew that the greatest debt I owed was not to the Finches, nor to my tailor, nor even to my mysterious benefactor, but to the man I had so wilfully and so shamefully determined to forget.
