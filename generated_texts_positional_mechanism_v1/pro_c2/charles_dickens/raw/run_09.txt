Mr. Albright had a habit of removing his spectacles and polishing them with a slow, circular motion on his napkin, as if our very conversation had fogged the lenses with its triviality. He did so now, holding them up to the candlelight, and I was certain he was not inspecting them for smudges, but rather using the brief, shining vacancy of his gaze to appraise me as a particularly dull specimen of insect life. She had a way of turning her head so that the candlelight caught in the little golden clasp of her necklace; and I felt it a personal affront that Mr. Albright’s cynical gaze should fall upon the same sight which, to me, was the very summit of all earthly beauty. What I could not bear, what made my collar feel tight and my hands grow damp, was that my own adoration should be made to exist in such close proximity to his indifference; it was that my holiest of feelings should be compelled to share the air with his cold, dissecting intellect, _that_ was the truly unbearable circumstance.

We parted company at ten o’clock, and it was settled that I should present myself at Mr. Albright’s offices in two days’ time to review the quarterly accounts of my inheritance; and then I was allowed to take Eleanor’s hand for a moment, a moment that felt both an eternity and an instant, and I departed. Then a wave of magnificent hope washed over me, that I, once a humble scrivener’s boy, should be destined for her. Before the house was a tiny moat, perhaps four feet wide, spanned by a very small drawbridge that was, at that moment, pulled up. A placard on the gatepost announced, “THE CASTLE.”

I stood for a moment, utterly charmed and bewildered. It was a perfect folly, a grown man’s plaything, a defiant assertion of whimsy in a world of brick and mortar. I felt a little shy about how to announce my presence, but before I could decide, a window was thrown up, and Wemmick himself appeared.

“Halloa!” he called out. “Mr. Pip! You have found your way!”

At the sight of me, he beamed. It was not the dry, tight smile of Little Britain; it was a broad, genuine grin that transformed his entire face. His post-office mouth was gone, replaced by an expression of pure, unadulterated pleasure.

“I’ll lower the bridge for you!” he shouted, and disappeared from the window.

There was a rattling of chains, and the little drawbridge slowly descended with a groan, coming to rest over the moat with a satisfying clunk. I crossed it, feeling as if I were entering an enchanted kingdom, and Wemmick met me at the door.

“Welcome to the Castle, Mr. Pip,” he said, shaking my hand warmly. “Come in, come in! The Aged is expecting you.”

He led me into a tiny parlour, spotlessly clean and crammed with curiosities. There was a collection of pipes on the wall, a ship in a bottle on the mantelpiece, and several framed prints of famous criminals. In an armchair by the fire sat a very old man, with a placid, cheerful face and a startling resemblance to Wemmick, though all the angles and sharpness of his son’s features had been worn smooth by time. This was the Aged Parent.

“Aged P.,” said Wemmick, speaking very loudly and distinctly into the old man’s ear. “This is Mr. Pip. A friend from London.”

The old man cupped a hand to his ear and smiled benignly. “Pip?” he said, in a faint, reedy voice. “Very good. Any friend of John’s is welcome. We shall have a pleasant afternoon, I am sure.” It was clear he was extremely deaf.

“He enjoys company,” Wemmick confided to me, “but his hearing is, shall we say, not what it once was. He likes it when I nod. It assures him that he is part of the conversation.”

Wemmick then proceeded to nod vigorously at his father, who nodded back with great satisfaction. The entire scene was so peculiar and so good-natured that I felt a lump rise in my throat. It was a world of simple, devoted affection, the likes of which I had not seen since I had left the forge. I thought, with a pang that was half sweet and half bitter, how Joe would have delighted in this scene, in the miniature drawbridge and the deaf old man and the air of contented absurdity.

Presently, another person was added to our party. A lady arrived, whom Wemmick introduced as Miss Skiffins. She was a woman of a certain age, with a figure like a wooden Dutch doll, and she wore a pair of bright green gloves that seemed a little too tight for her. Her face had a curious, pursed quality, not unlike Wemmick’s own business face, as if she too had a private post-office of her own to attend to. It was clear, from the way Wemmick fussed over her and offered her the best chair, that she was a particular friend.

The afternoon passed in a state of tranquil cheerfulness. Wemmick showed me his garden, where he grew salad-stuff in neat little rows, and a small pen where he kept a few chickens. He referred to all of it as “portable property.”

“You see, Mr. Pip,” he explained, pointing to a particularly fine lettuce, “this is real. You can eat it. That’s the beauty of it. In my other life, it’s all paper and promises. But here,” he patted a cabbage with genuine affection, “here, it’s all tangible. Get hold of portable property, that’s my advice.”

We dined on a roasted fowl, no doubt one of the less fortunate residents of the chicken-pen, and the salad from the garden. The Aged Parent ate with quiet enjoyment, with Wemmick occasionally shouting a piece of information into his ear and then nodding encouragingly. After dinner, a great ceremony was made of preparing a rum punch. Miss Skiffins, after much persuasion, removed her green gloves to assist, revealing a pair of red, work-roughened hands. The ceremony of the punch, which involved a great deal of measuring and stirring and tasting, was an operation of such gravity and precision that it took on the air of a sacred rite. As Wemmick stirred the fragrant mixture, he made several attempts to place his arm around Miss Skiffins’s waist. Each time, she would gently but firmly remove it, without the slightest change in her placid expression, as if this were a familiar and accepted part of the ritual.

At last, the punch was ready. Wemmick ladled it into our glasses, and we sat by the fire, sipping the hot, sweet liquid. A profound sense of peace and contentment filled the little room. Was this, then, what it was to be happy? This small, fortified island of domesticity, with its toy cannon and its gentle, deaf old man, and its quiet, unstated affections? It was a world away from the agonizing passions and lofty ambitions that tormented me. It had nothing to do with Estella, or Miss Havisham, or the mysterious benefactor who was shaping my destiny. It was merely life, in a small, secure, and happy form.

As nine o’clock approached, a sense of anticipation grew. Wemmick consulted his pocket-watch. “Time for the Stinger!” he announced. He went to a corner of the room and produced a miniature cannon, no more than a foot and a half long, mounted on a wooden carriage. He and Miss Skiffins carried it out to a small paved area in the garden. He loaded it with a pinch of powder, and then, with a great sense of occasion, lit the fuse. The Aged Parent, who had been brought to the window, watched with an expression of intense delight. There was a fizz, a pause, and then a surprisingly loud BANG! that echoed through the quiet suburban evening, followed by a puff of white smoke.

The Aged Parent clapped his hands feebly. “He loves it,” said Wemmick, looking thoroughly pleased with himself. “Never misses it.”

Soon after this climax, it was time for me to depart. Miss Skiffins was also taking her leave, and Wemmick insisted on escorting us both part of the way. As we walked away from the house, I looked back and saw the little castle, dark now save for the single light in the parlour where the Aged Parent sat waiting. The drawbridge was up. The fortress was secure for the night.

As we drew closer to the more central parts of London, a change came over Wemmick. The cheerful, beaming host of Walworth gradually receded, and the dry, wooden clerk of Little Britain re-emerged. His mouth tightened, his shoulders stiffened, and his conversation turned from garden produce to matters of law. By the time he bid me good-night, he was once again the man of portable property and official business, and Walworth and its gentle absurdities might have been a dream.

I walked the rest of the way to Barnard’s Inn alone, my mind in a turmoil. The five hundred pounds in my pocket felt heavier than before, and the path to becoming a gentleman, I was beginning to suspect, was not a straight road to a shining destination, but a bewildering maze of crooked lanes, some of which, to my great alarm, led backwards. The command to “Love her, love her, love her!” still echoed in my soul, but it was now accompanied by the quiet, contented bang of Wemmick’s evening cannon, a sound which spoke of a different kind of life altogether, one that my great expectations had given me no language to understand.

My perambulations back to my lodgings were of a nature I can scarcely describe, for my mind was not a single entity, but a tumultuous parliament of warring factions. One moment, I was a knight of romance, destined for the hand of a peerless, if somewhat cold, princess; the next, I was a contemptible snob, justly mocked by a tailor’s boy of singular and diabolical genius. Now, I saw the glitter of Estella’s jewels and my heart swelled with an ambition so vast it was painful; then, the phantom report of Wemmick’s nine o’clock gun echoed in my ears, and I was visited by a strange longing for a miniature drawbridge and a deaf parent to nod at. These contradictions did not resolve themselves into any coherent whole, but rather chased one another around the dark chambers of my brain, like frantic and illogical ghosts. It was with a profound sense of relief, therefore, that upon my return to that dislocated sepulchre of a place, Barnard’s Inn, I found a light, however feeble, burning in our window, and knew that Herbert was within.

He was seated at our rickety table, attempting, with the aid of a book and a guttering candle, to improve his knowledge of the shipping trade, a subject upon which his prospects, if not his immediate comforts, depended. He looked up as I entered, his kind and open face breaking into its customary smile.

“My dear Handel!” he exclaimed. “You are returned at last. I was beginning to fear you had been set upon by footpads, or, what is perhaps worse, had fallen into the company of Pumblechook.”

I returned his greeting, and feeling the packet of banknotes in my coat, which seemed to burn with a holy and yet somehow profane fire, I was overcome with the need to unburden myself of my great news. I threw myself into a chair, and before I could even remove my hat, I began a breathless account of my visit. I spoke of Miss Havisham’s strange and feverish manner, of Estella’s beauty, so enhanced by the jewels that she looked like a queen of starlight, and of the agonizing proximity of Mr. Jaggers during our game of whist. Herbert listened with his usual sympathetic attention, nodding and offering a quiet word here and there, never judging, but merely receiving the torrent of my impressions.

When I had at last exhausted this part of my narrative, I paused for dramatic effect, and then, drawing the packet from my pocket, I laid it upon the table between us. It was a thick, crinkling bundle of white paper, held together by a simple string.

“And this, Herbert,” I said, my voice trembling a little with the magnitude of it all, “is a present. From my guardian. He informs me it is my quarterly allowance.”

Herbert’s eyes widened as he looked from the packet to my face. “Handel! What a prodigious sum! Why, it looks like a fortune.”

“It is five hundred pounds,” I declared, and I leaned back in my chair, endeavouring to look as if five hundred pounds were a mere trifle to which I was perfectly accustomed, though in truth my heart was hammering against my ribs like a blacksmith at his forge.

For a moment, Herbert was quite struck dumb. He picked up the packet, weighed it in his hand as if to test the solidity of such a miracle, and then set it down again with a soft whistle. “Five hundred pounds,” he repeated, almost in a whisper. “Well, Handel. Your expectations are very great indeed.”

A silence fell between us, filled by the immense and powerful presence of the money on the table. It seemed to change the very character of our shabby room, making the threadbare carpet look pitiable, the cracked ceiling an insult. I felt a sudden, intoxicating sense of power, a feeling that with this paper talisman, I could conjure up any delight or luxury the world had to offer. My mind, which had so lately been a battlefield, was now flooded with a single, triumphant idea: that of a Gentleman of Fortune.

It was Herbert, as always, who brought us back to earth. His initial astonishment gave way to a look of thoughtful and gentle seriousness. He rested his chin on his hand and gazed at the money, not with the avaricious glee that I felt, but with the cautious eye of a navigator looking at a treacherous reef.

“My dear Handel,” he began, in his quiet, reasonable way, “this is a magnificent thing, a truly magnificent thing. But it is also, if you will permit me to say so, a dangerous one.”

“Dangerous?” I asked, a little offended. “How can it be dangerous? It is the means to everything we desire.”

“Perhaps,” he said. “But I have been thinking, since you have been away. I have been thinking about our affairs. And I have been making a little list.”

He produced a slip of paper from his waistcoat pocket, which he unfolded on the table. It was covered in his neat, careful handwriting. My own scrawling signature appeared here and there, on what looked distressingly like a series of promissory notes.

“You see, Handel,” he continued, pointing to the items with the tip of his finger, “we are not, in the strictest sense of the word, entirely free from pecuniary embarrassment. There is the tailor, who has become somewhat pressing of late. And the bootmaker, whose boy left a note of a rather threatening character only yesterday. Then there is the matter of dinners at the Blue Boar, and our subscription to the library, and that unfortunate business with the hired horse that took a dislike to the milestone.”

He went on down the list, his voice a calm and steady recitation of our follies. Each item was a small monument to my new-found gentility; each debt a testament to my eagerness to live up to my great expectations without the least understanding of how to manage them. As he spoke, the five hundred pounds on the table seemed to shrink, its magnificent aura dimming under the harsh light of reality.

When he had finished, he looked at me, not with accusation, but with a kind of shared complicity. “Now,” he said, “I do not have the complete figures. I propose, Handel, that before we consider what is to be done with this princely sum, we first undertake a comprehensive survey of what we owe. Let us make a complete and honest catalogue of our debts. It will be an exercise in practical finance.”

My first impulse was to resist. This was a tedious and ungentlemanly business, a clerk’s task, wholly at odds with the grand, romantic visions that filled my head. I wanted to talk more of Estella, to dream of the life this money would buy us, a life of fashionable clothes and splendid entertainments, not to grub about in a ledger of petty bills. But there was a quiet authority in Herbert’s manner that I could not defy; and besides, a part of me, the small, frightened part that had been so thoroughly routed by Trabb’s boy, knew that he was right.

And so we began. We procured a larger sheet of paper, an ink-pot, and a new pen. Herbert, with his methodical mind, was to be the chief scribe and accountant. My role was to be that of memory and confession, to dredge up from the depths of my mind all the instances of casual expenditure, of thoughtless extravagance, that had marked my life in London.

It was a strange and melancholy occupation for a young man newly come into a fortune. We sat for hours, the candle burning lower and lower, while the ghosts of our purchases were summoned and set down in black and white. A bill for gloves, another for scented soap. The cost of a dozen dinners, each more elaborate than the last. The price of a silver-topped cane I had thought essential to my station, which I had since lost. My own part in our financial ruin I set down with a queer mixture of shame and a sort of perverse pride; for was not this mountain of debt a testament to my having well and truly begun my education as a gentleman, a curriculum in which the arts of disbursement were taught far more readily than those of economy?

As we worked, we talked. I told him, in more detail, of my day. I described Estella’s coldness, and how it seemed only to increase my adoration.

“It is as if she were a star, Herbert,” I said, pausing in my recollection of a bill for oysters. “So bright and beautiful, and so impossibly distant. To be near her is a kind of exquisite torture.”

Herbert, who was carefully adding a column of figures, looked up with a troubled expression. “A star gives light, Handel, but it gives no warmth. And one can freeze to death, staring at the prettiest star in the sky.” He knew her, of course, far better than I; he was, after all, a Pocket. He had seen her grow up under Miss Havisham’s blighting influence. “Be careful,” he added softly. “Miss Havisham has raised her for a purpose. And I fear it is not a happy one.”

I dismissed his warning with a wave of my hand, as the folly of a man who had never felt the grand passion that consumed me. What did Herbert, with his simple, good-hearted affection for Clara, know of the sublime agony of loving an Estella?

I then recounted my visit to Walworth, describing the little castle, the Aged Parent, the drawbridge, and the firing of the Stinger. Herbert was greatly amused by this, and laughed more heartily than I had heard him laugh in weeks.

“Wemmick!” he cried. “A fortress and a cannon! Who would have thought the dry old stick had such fancies in him? Walworth must be a very different climate from Little Britain.”

“It is a different world,” I said, and a note of the confusion I had felt on my walk home crept back into my voice. “At his office, he is all hard facts and portable property. At his home, he is… well, he is happy. There is no other word for it. They are happy, Herbert. In their ridiculous little wooden castle.”

The thought hung in the air between us. The life of a gentleman, as I was pursuing it, seemed to consist of running up bills we could not pay, in pursuit of a lady who did not love me, all to fulfill the mysterious designs of a benefactor I did not know. Wemmick, meanwhile, had his own small kingdom, his own clear affections, and a cannon to announce the closing of his day. The contrast was unsettling, and I had no framework with which to understand it.

To change the subject from these uncomfortable reflections, I brought up a matter that had been simmering in my mind for some time, an ambition I considered the very pinnacle of gentlemanly life.

“Herbert,” I said, affecting a casual tone. “Bentley Drummle mentioned to me the other day a society he belongs to. A club, of sorts. He called it The Finches of the Grove.”

Herbert stopped writing and laid down his pen. His expression became grave. “I have heard of it, Handel.”

“He said it was for young men of spirit and fortune. They dine together, and… and enjoy themselves. He suggested that I might be eligible for membership.”

“I have no doubt that you would be,” said Herbert slowly. “Drummle is the leading light of the Finches, I believe. And his qualifications for membership appear to be a thick head, a heavy purse, and a brutish disposition.”

“But it is what gentlemen do, is it not?” I pressed. “They belong to clubs.”

“Some gentlemen do, Handel. But the Finches of the Grove have a particular reputation. They are famous for quarrelling, for drinking to excess, and for spending money as if it were water. They call themselves Finches, but I have heard them described more accurately as a flock of expensive and brainless cuckoos. To join them would be to pour this good money,” he gestured to the five hundred pounds, “into a sieve.”

His remonstrance was so gentle, and yet so firm, that I felt a flush of shame. He saw my ambition for what it was: another shallow attempt to purchase a station in life that I had not earned. I was about to protest, to argue that Drummle was my rival for Estella and that I must meet him on his own ground, but just then Herbert finished his calculations.

He wrote a final figure at the bottom of the page, circled it twice, and slid the paper across to me.

“There,” he said. “That is our position. As near as I can make it.”

I looked at the sum. It was a truly astonishing figure, far greater than I could ever have imagined. Our tailors and bootmakers, our dinners and diversions, our thoughtless daily wants had accumulated into a mountain of debt that was both ludicrous and terrifying. I stared at the total, and then at the five hundred pounds, which now seemed a very modest sum indeed, a mere foothill to the Everest of our obligations.

“Good heavens, Herbert,” I whispered, utterly appalled. “Is it possible?”

“It is not only possible, Handel,” he replied, with a faint, weary smile. “It is a fact. Set down in black and white.”

For a long time, I could say nothing. The grandeur of my expectations, the intoxicating power of my new allowance, all of it evaporated in an instant, leaving behind the cold, hard residue of this sordid arithmetic. I was not a Gentleman of Fortune on the brink of a splendid career; I was a young fool, deeply in debt and no more in command of my life than a rudderless boat in a storm.

“What is to be done?” I asked at last, my voice hollow.

“What is to be done,” said Herbert, with a return of his cheerful briskness, “is clear. We shall take this money, this wonderful, timely gift, and we shall pay what we owe. Every last penny. We will go round tomorrow, you and I, and we shall settle our accounts. We will look the tailor in the eye, not as debtors, but as honest men. And then, Handel, we shall be free.”

He took the list of our debts and, with a theatrical flourish, held it in the flame of the candle. It caught, flared up, and in a few moments was reduced to a black, curling ash in the hearth.

“There,” he said. “The past is incinerated. Tomorrow, we begin anew.”

His optimism was so infectious, his goodness so plain, that I felt a great weight lift from my shoulders. The path he proposed was clear, honourable, and right. It lacked the romantic glamour I craved, but it possessed a solidity that my life had been sorely wanting. I agreed to his plan with a gratitude so profound it almost brought tears to my eyes. We would pay our debts. We would live more modestly. We would be, in our own small way, as secure in our lodgings as Wemmick in his castle.

We sat up a little longer, making our plans for the morrow, and for a while, I felt a sense of peace and purpose I had not known since arriving in London. But as I lay in my bed that night, the sensible resolutions of the evening began to fade. The image of Herbert’s calm, determined face was replaced by the memory of Estella’s, beautiful and remote. The honest satisfaction of paying a bill seemed a dull and common thing compared to the glorious, painful hope of winning her love. And the quiet command of my conscience was drowned out once more by the echoing words of Miss Havisham, which seemed to rise from the very depths of my soul, promising a destiny far greater, and far more terrible, than a life of balanced accounts. “Love her, love her, love her!” the voice commanded, and my foolish heart, in despite of all reason, pledged itself again to that ruinous and impossible cause.

The morning came, as mornings have a habit of doing, with an unwelcome practicality. Herbert, whose spirits were ever of a buoyant and cork-like nature, capable of being submerged by a sea of debt one evening only to bob up again with the first light of day, was already dressed and making a great show of counting out bank-notes upon our table. He arranged them in neat little piles, one for each of our creditors, and regarded them with the satisfaction of a general marshalling his troops for a victorious campaign.

“There, Handel,” he declared, as I emerged from my chamber. “Our forces are assembled. Today we rout the enemy, and by dinnertime we shall be masters of our own field once more. Is it not a glorious prospect?”

I assented that it was, though in truth I felt none of his martial enthusiasm. My own battle was an internal one, and the prospect of this expedition, this tour of our follies, filled me with a deep and complicated shame. It was not the paying of money that I dreaded, for I had it to pay; it was the confession of having owed it. It felt like a public declaration of my own mismanagement, a stain upon the very gentility I was so anxious to acquire. To be a gentleman, in my estimation, was to have money so naturally and in such abundance that the sordid business of its exchange never became visible. This, on the contrary, was to make it very visible indeed.

Our first port of call was the establishment of our tailor, a small, bird-like man whose chin was perpetually receding into a collar of startling stiffness. His establishment smelled of hot irons and new cloth; a scent I had once found intoxicating, as it was the very perfume of my transformation, but which now seemed to reek of my imprudence. Mr. Snipwell, upon seeing us enter, had greeted us with a bow that was a masterpiece of subservience, his hands rubbing together as if to polish the very air between us.

“Mr. Pocket, sir! And Mr. Pip! An honour, gentlemen, a very great honour. And what can I have the pleasure of showing you today? A new waistcoat, perhaps? I have a roll of silk from Lyons that would do justice to a prince, a veritable marvel of the loom.”

“Thank you, Mr. Snipwell,” said Herbert, with his easy good-nature, “but we are not here to purchase today. We are here,” and here he produced his first little pile of notes with a flourish, “to settle our account.”

The change in Mr. Snipwell’s demeanour was a study in itself. It was not that he became disrespectful; on the contrary, he remained civil enough. But the effusive warmth vanished as if a tap had been turned off. The rubbing of his hands ceased. His smile became a mere matter of business, a transaction in itself. He took the money, counted it with a swift and practiced eye, and then consulted a large, dusty ledger.

“Very good, Mr. Pocket. Very good, Mr. Pip. All in order.” He made a great scratch through a page in his book, a sound that to my ears was like the grinding of my own teeth. He then wrote out a receipt with a sharp, spiky script, and handed it to Herbert without a word. The audience, it seemed, was over. The man who had been so delighted to see us a moment before was now quite clearly anxious for us to be gone, so that he might attend to customers who represented future profit, rather than those who merely represented past liability.

I felt a hot flush of shame creep up my neck, not at the debt itself, but at the public spectacle of its discharge; it was as if, in settling our accounts, we were paradoxically cheapening ourselves in the eyes of the very man whose opinion we were purchasing. Herbert, however, appeared to notice nothing of this. He pocketed the receipt with an air of triumph and clapped me on the shoulder as we emerged into the street.

“One down!” he cried. “Handel, did you not feel a weight lift? The man can no longer think of us with a frown, but must acknowledge us as men of our word!”

I could not bring myself to explain that my fear was precisely the opposite; that now he had our money, he would likely not think of us at all, or if he did, it would be with the cool indifference of a shopkeeper for a closed account.

We proceeded in this manner through the morning. We visited the boot-maker, a grim and leathery man who took our money with a suspicious grunt, as if he suspected the notes of being forgeries. We visited the hatter, who gave us a curt nod, and the tobacconist, who made a joke about our having “smoked our credit to the end.” At each stop, Herbert’s cheerfulness grew in proportion to my misery. He saw only the restoration of our honour; I saw only the dismantling of my consequence. On the night of my induction, I was determined to prove my credentials beyond all doubt.

The Finches met in a private room at a Covent Garden hotel, a room that reeked of stale wine and expensive cigars. The air was thick with loud talk and louder laughter. It was a company of some twenty young men, all dressed in the height of fashion, all affecting an air of languid boredom that sat oddly with their boisterous behaviour. They were sprawled on velvet chairs, their boots on the tables, and their conversation consisted of little more than gossip about horses, actresses, and the foolishness of their tailors.

And among them, more sullen and more brutish than any other, was Bentley Drummle. He sat apart from the main group, a bottle of port at his elbow, glowering at the company from under his heavy brows. He acknowledged my entrance with a slight, contemptuous curl of his lip, but said nothing.

Remembering Mr. Jaggers’s cynical advice—_Just be more expensive_—I put on an air of supreme indifference. I called the waiter over with a lordly wave.

“Waiter,” I said, my voice carrying across the room, “a round of claret for the Finches. Your best. And make sure it is the ’14.”

This caused something of a stir. Heads turned. The chatter subsided for a moment. To order a single bottle of the ’14 claret was an extravagance; to order a round for the entire club was a declaration. I saw Drummle’s eyes fix on me, a flicker of something—surprise? resentment?—in their muddy depths. The other Finches, however, looked upon me with a new and gratifying respect. I was no longer merely Herbert’s friend; I was a gentleman of substance.

The evening wore on in a haze of drink and forced merriment. We dined prodigiously, and after dinner, we played at cards for stakes that made my blood run cold, though I feigned a careless disregard for the mounting pile of sovereigns on the table. I won a little, lost a great deal more, and paid my debts with a smile, as if the loss of twenty guineas were a matter of complete indifference. All the while, I was conscious of Drummle watching me. He did not speak to me directly, but I felt his hostile gaze upon me, a silent challenge that I was determined to meet.

The true conflict, when it came, was not over cards or wine, but over the one subject that lay like a burning coal between us.

“I was in Richmond yesterday,” Drummle announced suddenly, his thick voice cutting through the general hubbub. He had drunk a great deal, and his face was flushed. “Dined with the old lady. The little queen was there.”

A silence fell upon the group nearest to us. Everyone knew of whom he spoke. He meant Miss Havisham, and he meant Estella. My heart began to beat a furious tattoo against my ribs, but I forced myself to take a slow sip of my wine, my hand as steady as a rock.

“She was in a fine temper,” Drummle continued, looking directly at me now, a sneer playing on his mouth. “Told me I was a brute and a blockhead. She has a charming way about her, has Estella.” He laughed, a short, ugly bark. “She lets me kiss her hand, though. Can’t resist the Spider, you see.”

The room seemed to tilt. The faces of the other Finches became a blur. To hear her name on his lips, to hear him speak of her with this brutish, possessive familiarity, was a torment beyond anything I had ever known. All my fine clothes, my expensive rooms, my five hundred pounds—they were nothing. He had the one thing I could not buy: her tolerance, her intimacy.

I knew I must say something. To remain silent would be to concede defeat. I could not challenge him to a duel; I could not even raise my voice, for that would be to betray how deeply his words had wounded me. I had to meet him on his own ground, the ground of insolent superiority.

I set my glass down with a delicate click. I looked not at him, but at Startop, who was sitting opposite me.

“It is a curious thing about women, Startop,” I said, my voice cool and conversational. “They are often obliged to be civil to all manner of creatures. It is part of their social duty. One might as well boast that the sun shines on one’s dunghill.”

There was a moment of absolute silence. Then, one or two of the Finches snickered. Drummle’s face went from flushed to a dark, mottled purple. He half rose from his chair, his fists clenched.

“What did you say, you cursed upstart?” he growled.

I met his furious gaze without flinching, though inside I was a quaking jelly. I raised an eyebrow, as if in mild surprise. “I was merely making a general observation on the nature of society,” I said placidly. “Did you think I was referring to you?”

For a second, I thought he would strike me. His whole brutish frame quivered with rage. But we were in a room full of gentlemen, however debauched, and there were limits. He glared at me for a long moment, his chest heaving, and then he sank back into his chair with a muttered oath. He picked up his bottle and drank directly from it, his eyes never leaving my face, full of a black and promising hatred.

I had won. I had faced the Spider and made him back down. I had used the weapons of this new world—money and insolence—and I had been victorious. A wave of heady triumph washed over me. The other Finches were looking at me with admiration. I was one of them.

Later that night, as Herbert and I walked home through the dark and silent courts of the Temple, the triumph began to curdle. The cold night air seemed to clear my head, and I saw the evening not as a glorious victory, but as a squalid, drunken brawl fought with words instead of fists.

“You handled him beautifully, Handel,” Herbert said, clapping me on the shoulder. “You put the old Spider right in his place.”

“Did I, Herbert?” I said, my voice sounding hollow in my own ears. “Or did I just become another kind of spider myself?”

He did not understand, and I could not explain. I could not tell him that in vanquishing Drummle, I had become like him. I had been cruel and arrogant and disdainful. I had used my newfound wealth not to elevate myself, but merely to purchase a more effective kind of scorn. I had bought my place at their table, and found that the feast was made of ashes.

Back in our grand new rooms, I stood by the window, looking out at the dark, swirling river below. The five hundred pounds had brought me here, to this fine vantage point, but I had never felt so low. Miss Havisham’s words echoed in my head, but they were twisted now, mingled with the cynical advice of Mr. Jaggers and the ugly sneer of Bentley Drummle. “Love her, love her, love her!” it sounded now like a command to enter a contest for which I was utterly unfit, a race whose only prize was degradation. And as I looked at my reflection in the dark glass, I saw not a gentleman, but only the blacksmith’s boy, dressed in borrowed finery and trying desperately to convince himself that he was not an impostor. The shame of it was a heavier weight than all the gold in my pockets. The shame of it, and the knowledge that, for the love of Estella, I would do it all again tomorrow.

I slept, if it can be called sleep, a restless and unrefreshing slumber, in which the jeering face of Trabb’s boy and the brutish sneer of Bentley Drummle seemed to merge into a single, tormenting phantasm. I was perpetually on a stage, under the glare of a thousand critical eyes, my fine clothes turning to rags and my pockets, which I knew to be full of gold, revealing themselves to be stuffed with nothing but blacksmith’s soot and marsh-weeds. I would start awake with a gasp, the echo of “Don’t know yah!” ringing in my ears, and find myself in the opulent darkness of our chambers, the soft ticking of the mantel-clock a lonely counterpoint to the frantic beating of my own heart. The grand new rooms, which had but yesterday seemed the very pinnacle of my ambitions, now felt like a gilded cage, and the river beyond the window whispered not of commerce and adventure, but of depths and oblivion.

The morning brought with it a grey London drizzle that seemed to seep through the very walls and into my soul, leaving a damp chill upon my spirits that no fire could dispel. Herbert, who possessed a constitution for cheerfulness that was as boundless as it was, to my jaundiced eye, inexplicable, was already up and bustling about, making a great show of preparing our breakfast. He hummed a tune from some popular air, a jaunty and carefree melody that grated on my nerves like a file on a rusty chain.

“A fine morning, Handel, a fine morning!” he declared, placing a plate of toast, which in his enthusiasm he had liberally blackened, upon the table. “A bit of a soft day, as they say, but it will clear the air. And speaking of clearing the air, you did it magnificently last night. Put the Spider back in his web, you did! I have never seen a man so thoroughly discomfited.”

I poked at the charred offering on my plate with a fork, my appetite having long since fled. “Do you think so, Herbert?” I said, my voice low. “I feel as though I was the one discomfited. I feel as though I wallowed in the same mire as he, and merely proved myself the more adept pig.”

Herbert paused, the coffee-pot held aloft, his good-natured face clouded with a moment’s confusion. “My dear Handel, what a notion! You were a gentleman defending his honour. He was a brute, and you treated him with the contempt he deserved. It is the way of the world. One must either be the hammer or the anvil.”

“And which am I, Herbert?” I asked, looking up at him. “For last night I felt I was both at once: hammering him down, whilst being hammered myself into a shape I do not recognise.”

He did not, as I have said before, understand; and I believe it was a testament to his own untroubled goodness that he could not. He clapped me on the shoulder, a gesture of such honest fellowship that it sent a fresh pang of shame through me. “You are thinking too much upon it, Pip. It was a victory, and you must accustom yourself to victory. It is what gentlemen do. Now, what shall we do to celebrate? A stroll to the Exchange? Or perhaps we might call upon Mrs. Pocket, now that you are in such favour there?”

The thought of facing my fellow Finches again, of having to live up to the reputation for cutting insolence I had so unwillingly earned, was more than I could bear. The thought of my own relations, of the very sight of them, was scarcely more appealing. I was saved from the necessity of an answer by the arrival of the post, delivered by a boy whose uncanny resemblance in demeanour to the fiend of my village, Trabb’s boy, caused me a moment of unreasoning panic. He slapped a small handful of letters onto the salver with an air of profound disdain, as if the very act of carrying correspondence for such as us were a personal affront.

Most were for Herbert, business-like missives and, I suspected, one or two notes from his secret Clara. But there was one for me. It was a single, folded sheet, of the finest cream-laid paper, sealed not with a humble wafer but with a proud splotch of green wax, impressed with a star. My heart, which had been a leaden weight in my chest, gave a sudden, violent lurch, as if it would leap from my body. I knew the hand, though I had seen it but seldom. It was the sharp, elegant, and imperious script of Estella.

Herbert, seeing my face, discreetly busied himself with his own letters. I broke the seal with a trembling finger. The scent of the paper, faint and floral, rose up to me, a scent I associated with her and with the strange, decaying grandeur of Satis House. It was like breathing the air of another world, a world of beauty and pain. The note was brief, its contents as cool and direct as its author.

_I am to come to London. I shall be here in two days’ time, and shall reside for a season with Mrs. Brandley at Richmond. You are to come and meet me at the coach, and you are to come and see me. Do not fail._

It was not a request, but a command; not an invitation, but a summons. There was no salutation, no word of affection, only the stark instruction and her name, _Estella_, scrawled at the bottom. And yet, I read it and reread it as if it were the most tender of love poems. She was coming! She was coming to London, and I was to be her appointed guardian, her first point of contact in this great metropolis. The shame of the night, the conflict in my soul, the ashes in my mouth, all were swept away in a single, glorious tide of anticipation. My purpose was renewed. The squalid brawl with Drummle was not a degradation after all; it was a necessary trial, a sharpening of the tools I would need to conquer this world for her.

“Herbert!” I exclaimed, leaping to my feet so suddenly that I knocked over my chair. “Herbert, she is coming!”

“She?” he asked, looking up from a letter that was causing his own brow to furrow. “Not… not Miss Havisham?”

“No, no, a thousand times no!” I cried, laughing with a joy that felt entirely foreign. “Estella! She is to be in London in two days. I am to meet her. Here, read it!”



Herbert took the single sheet from my hand, his good-natured smile still lingering about his lips, but as his eyes scanned the few imperious lines, I saw a change come over him; a shadow, however faint, that passed across the sunny landscape of his face. He read it once, and then again, his brow knitting in that thoughtful way he had when considering a particularly knotty point of commerce or character. He did not immediately share in my exultation, which I found, for a moment, to be a most unaccountable and even injurious thing.
“Well?” I demanded, my voice high with an impatience that bordered on the frantic. “Is it not wonderful, Herbert? She is to be here, in London!”
“It is certainly news, Pip,” he said, folding the letter with a precision that seemed to me entirely unnecessary, and handing it back. He looked not at me, but at the rumpled tablecloth, where the remains of our breakfast lay in a state of disarray that mirrored my own tumultuous spirit. “She is to reside with a Mrs. Brandley at Richmond, you say.”
“Yes! For a season! I am to meet her, Herbert, I am to be her escort. It is all arranged.”
“Mrs. Brandley,” he mused, tapping a finger on the table. “I believe I have heard the name. A widow of some property, I think, with a daughter. They move in society. Yes, a very proper arrangement for a young lady of fortune being introduced.” He at last raised his eyes to mine, and in their honest depths I saw not the boundless joy I expected, but a profound and gentle concern. I felt a great and sudden pity for him; a pity that was instantly consumed by the furnace of my own selfish joy. He could not, of course, understand the height and breadth and depth of my feelings; he, with his simple, requited love for his Clara, could never comprehend the magnificent agony of adoring a star.
“You are very happy, old fellow,” he said, and managed a smile that was nearly his own.
“Happy! Herbert, the word is a pauper! I am transported! The world is made anew!”
“Then I am happy for you,” he said, though the shadow in his eyes remained. “Only, Pip,” he added, his voice lowering, “be careful.”
“Careful?” I echoed, laughing. “Of what should I be careful? My destiny is arriving by the London coach! The time of waiting is at an end!”
“I do not mean to cloud your expectations,” he said earnestly, leaning forward. “God knows I wish you every success. But Richmond is not Satis House. London is not the village. She is to be introduced, as they say; she will be surrounded, admired. There will be others.”
The very thought was a physical blow. The mention of Drummle, of the night before, returned to me with the sour taste of stale wine. But I thrust it away, a dark and bat-like fancy that had no place in the brilliant morning of my hope.
“Let them admire,” I declared, with a magnanimity that cost me a sharp pang. “It is her due. But I, Herbert, I have the right of it. Miss Havisham herself has decreed it. I am to meet her at the coach. I am her oldest friend.”
He saw it was of no use, and with a small sigh, he wisely turned the conversation to the practical. The two days that followed were the longest and most excruciating of my life, a perfect eternity of suspense lived out in sixty-minute hours. I could not read, I could not apply myself to my studies with Mr. Pocket, I could not sit still. My soul was a tuned string, vibrating with a single, incessant note of anticipation. The whole of my allowance for the quarter, and a little more besides which I procured on credit, was expended in a fever of preparation. I repaired to my tailor and commissioned a new coat of the finest blue broadcloth, and a waistcoat of such dazzling silk that looking upon it was like gazing into a kaleidoscope. I purchased new boots that shone with a light of their own, and a new hat, and gloves of a pale lemon kid. I laid out these treasures upon my bed and regarded them not as clothes, but as a suit of armour, a panoply in which I might prove worthy to stand beside her.
Herbert watched these proceedings with a kind of rueful tolerance, making no further attempt to temper my spirits, for which I was grateful. He was the best of friends, and if he could not share my heaven, he would not, at least, drag me from its gates. I walked the streets of London for hours, seeing nothing of the city’s grimy grandeur, but imagining it all through her eyes. Would she find the Strand too noisy, or Cheapside too common? Would the river air at Richmond please her? Every street corner, every shop front, every passing face became a question that related only to her. My mind was a whirlwind of imagined conversations, of greetings and glances, of moments in which she would at last see me, truly see me, for the gentleman I had become.
On the appointed day, I was at the coaching-inn a full hour before the coach from my old neighbourhood was due. The yard was a bedlam of organised chaos, a place of perpetual arrivals and departures. Hostlers shouted, whips cracked, iron-shod wheels ground upon the cobblestones, and the air was thick with the smell of horseflesh, damp straw, and humanity. I paced the grimy purlieus of the yard like a caged animal, my heart hammering against my ribs, my new lemon gloves becoming soiled from my unconscious clasping and unclasping of my hands. Every distant rumble of an approaching vehicle sent a jolt of electricity through my frame. A coach from York arrived, disgorging a stout countryman and his family; another from Bath, from which descended a pair of elderly ladies swathed in shawls. Each was a fresh disappointment.
And then, I heard it; a cry from one of the yard-men, “The Comet! Make way for the Comet!”
It was the name of the coach. It came swaying and rattling through the archway, its four horses steaming, their harness jingling, their flanks flecked with the mud of the road. It was no celestial body, but a lumbering, travel-stained machine, yet to my eyes it was as glorious as any chariot of the gods. I pressed forward, my breath caught in my throat, scanning the windows. The door was opened by a guard, and the passengers began to descend. An old farmer, a woman with a crying baby, a clerkly-looking man with a portfolio. My heart sank into my new, tight boots. Was she not there? Had she changed her mind? Had some accident befallen her? The world began to swim before my eyes in a grey mist of despair.
And then I saw her.
She was the last to alight, and she did so not with the weary clumsiness of her fellow travellers, but with a fluid and unhurried grace, assisted by a sour-faced woman in a drab bonnet who I took to be her maid. For a moment, she stood framed in the doorway of the coach, and the squalid yard with its noise and its dirt simply fell away, ceasing to exist. She was a woman. The girl I had last seen at Satis House had been angular and sharp, a bud tightly closed; this was the bloom, and it was devastating. She was taller, more poised, and dressed in a travelling-pelisse of a soft, dove-grey colour, with a bonnet to match from which a single white feather curved with an insolent elegance. She surveyed the chaotic scene with an expression of faint, regal disdain, her eyes, cool and beautiful as ever, sweeping over the crowd. Then they found me.
There was no start of surprise, no smile of welcome. Her expression did not change by so much as a flicker. She gave a small, almost imperceptible nod, a sign of acknowledgement, as one might nod to a servant who has appeared as summoned. The sour-faced maid was directing a porter to their luggage, a collection of handsome trunks that made my own single portmanteau seem a sad and humble thing. I moved towards her, my feet feeling as if they were made of lead, all my rehearsed speeches, my witty and charming greetings, having flown from my head like startled birds.
“Estella,” I managed to say, my voice sounding strange and thin in my own ears.
“You have come, Pip,” she said. Her voice was the same, that low, clear music that had haunted my dreams, but it held a new note of worldly composure. “That is good. We are to go to Richmond. Have you procured a carriage?”
The question, so practical and so immediate, was like a dash of cold water. A carriage! In my fever of anticipation, I had entirely forgotten such a crucial detail. I felt the blood rush to my face, a hot tide of shame.
“A carriage,” I stammered. “No, I, that is, I will see to it at once.”
Her lips curved in a smile that was not a smile at all; it was a mere lesson in anatomy. “It would be as well,” she said. “Abigail does not care for standing about in public places.” She indicated the maid, who was watching our exchange with the gimlet eyes of a hawk.
I turned and fled, as if from a field of battle, plunging into the throng to find a hackney-coach for hire. I felt a fool, an incompetent, bumbling boy, exposed in the first thirty seconds of our reunion. I found a carriage, a reasonably clean one, and after a hasty and flustered negotiation with its driver, I directed him towards the inn-yard and hurried back, my composure in tatters.
By the time I returned, the trunks had been assembled and Estella was waiting, tapping one foot impatiently upon the flagstones. She said nothing of my failure, which was somehow worse than any reprimand. Under the basilisk stare of Abigail the maid, the porter and the driver manhandled the luggage into place, and I held the door for Estella to enter. As she passed me, the scent of her, that same faint, spectral perfume from her letter, enveloped me, and for a moment my head swam with a dizzying intoxication. She settled herself on the seat, arranging her skirts with a deliberate hand. The maid climbed in after her, and I, feeling very much like an afterthought, took the seat opposite.
The journey to Richmond began in a silence that was, for me, thick with unspoken adoration and mortifying self-reproach. I sat on the edge of my seat, my new hat on my knees, my gloved hands clasped whitely before me. I watched her as she looked out of the window at the passing streets of London. She was taking in the great city, and I was taking in her. The light from the window caught the curve of her cheek, the long line of her throat, the exquisite shell of her ear. I saw now that Miss Havisham’s jewels had been but a gaudy prelude; her own beauty was a gem of a finer and more terrible water.
“London is larger than I had imagined,” she observed at last, to the window-pane more than to me. “And much dirtier.”
“It has its fine parts,” I said eagerly. “The parks are very fine. And the shops in Bond Street. I will take you to see them, if you will allow me.”
“Mrs. Brandley will take me,” she replied, not unkindly, but as a simple statement of fact. “She is to be my guide in all such things. You are to be my escort, Pip, when I have need of one. You will find it useful to make yourself acquainted with the geography of the place, so that you do not get us lost.”
The word ‘useful’ stung me, yet I seized upon it. To be useful to her! It was a post, a function, a reason to be near her. It was more than I had dared to hope for.
“I will,” I promised fervently. “I have a map. I will study it. I will know every street, every square.”
She turned her gaze from the window and looked at me properly for the first time. It was a calm, dispassionate appraisal, the look of a scientist examining a specimen.
“You have been spending your money, Pip,” she said. My heart leaped; she had noticed my new clothes.
“Do you… do you like them?” I asked, my voice trembling.
“They are the clothes of a gentleman,” she allowed. “That is what you are supposed to be, is it not? You must not be disappointing. There will be many eyes upon you, as my escort.”
The pride I had felt in my finery curdled within me. They were not for me, not a mark of my own taste or station; they were a costume, a uniform for the part I was to play in her life. The pleasure of them was gone, and only the anxiety remained.
We passed out of the city’s dense heart, through Knightsbridge and Hammersmith, the buildings growing grander and more separated by gardens. The air grew cleaner. Estella seemed to approve of the change, leaning back against the squabs with a sigh of relief. Abigail the maid had not uttered a single word, but sat as rigid as a wooden doll, her disapproval radiating from her in silent waves.
“Mrs. Brandley is a widow,” Estella informed me, as if continuing a lesson. “She has a daughter who is my own age, but very plain. Miss Havisham has made it clear that I am not to be troubled with a rival in the house. Mrs. Brandley is a distant relation of Miss Havisham’s, on the paternal side. A poor relation, which is why she is so accommodating. She will introduce me to the people I am to know.”
“And shall I know them too?” I asked, a tremor of hope in my voice.
“Some of them, perhaps,” she said, and then turned to look out the window again, as if the subject, and I, were exhausted.
The rest of the journey I spent in a misery of my own making. I loved her, I adored the very air she displaced, but I saw with a terrible clarity the immense gulf that lay between us. She was not a girl to be won by a faithful heart; she was a princess being presented to a kingdom, and I was merely a page, permitted to carry her train. Every word she spoke, every cool glance, reinforced my position. And yet, this torment was a thousand times more precious than the dull peace of a life without her. To be in the same carriage, to be breathing the same air, to be on a mission for her; _that_, as I had once thought in Mr. Jaggers’s presence, was the agonizing, ecstatic circumstance.
At last we came to Richmond, and drew up before a handsome villa of pale brick, withdrawn from the road behind a screen of laurels and a wrought-iron gate. A boy ran to open the gate, and we crunched up a neat gravel drive to a porticoed entrance. The door was opened before I could even descend. A footman stood there, and behind him, in the hall, a tall, thin lady with an elaborately arranged head of hair and a smile that seemed both welcoming and profoundly anxious.
“Estella, my dear child!” this person cried, advancing with her hands outstretched. “Welcome, welcome to our humble home!”
This, I presumed, was Mrs. Brandley. Estella submitted to a light embrace, her own posture remaining perfectly composed.
“You are very good to have me, Mrs. Brandley,” she said.
“It is our honour, our very great honour!” Mrs. Brandley effused. “And this must be Mr. Pip, of whom we have heard!” She turned her smile upon me, though it lost a degree of its wattage in the process. I bowed, feeling clumsy and oversized in the elegant, light-filled hall. Another figure, a pale girl with frightened eyes, hovered in the background; Mrs. Brandley’s daughter, no doubt.
Estella turned to me. The audience was at an end. “Thank you for your service, Pip,” she said, the words as formal as a dismissal. “Mrs. Brandley will let me know when I require you.”
And with that, she turned her back on me and, escorted by the fluttering Mrs. Brandley, ascended a graceful, curving staircase and disappeared from my sight.
I was left in the hall with my hat in my hand. The footman, with a polite but firm inclination of his head, held the door open for me. I walked out into the falling dusk. The carriage I had hired was gone. I was alone, a mile or more from the nearest coach-stand back to London, with the scent of her perfume still in my senses and the chill of her parting words in my heart. I had seen her. She was more beautiful than I had ever dreamed, and more distant than I had ever feared. As I began the long walk back towards the city, a single thought repeated itself in my mind, a new and more painful refrain for my lonely pillow. I was to be of use to her. For now, it would have to be enough.

So I endured, and walking on, soon found myself leaving the town behind. The road stretched before me, a ribbon of lighter grey in the general blackness, and the open country, with its mysterious rustlings and sighs, closed in on either side. My blood, which had been boiling with a helpless rage, began to cool, and the mortification I had suffered at the hands of Trabb’s boy was presently supplanted by a deeper and more abiding ache. I had been ejected from the town, a town which held Joe, and Biddy, and the graves on the marshes, as if I were a foreign body it could no longer tolerate. And for what? For a destiny that had set me apart, that had dressed me in fine clothes only to be mocked, and that had given me the brief, intoxicating poison of Estella’s company only to leave me stranded in the dark, miles from my lodgings, with nothing but the memory of her cool dismissal for company.

The air grew chill; a dampness rose from the river unseen in the gloom to my left, and I thought of the mists on the marshes at home, and of the beacon of the forge-fire. How warm and safe that fire had always seemed, and how immeasurably distant it was now. I was to be of use to her. This was the thought that I turned over and over, as a man might turn a strange coin in his pocket, uncertain of its value. It was not the lover’s part, nor the friend’s; it was the part of an instrument, a tool to be picked up and set down at her convenience. And yet, to be of use to Estella! Was that not, in itself, a kind of intimacy? To be the one she called for, the one entrusted with her person, even if it were only as a liveried guard might be? The thought was a torment, but it was a torment I hugged to myself, for it was all I had of her.

My walk was a long and melancholy pilgrimage. The stars came out, cold and remote as her eyes, and the sounds of the night were my only companions. It must have been past ten o’clock when the first lamps of London began to glimmer in the distance, a sullen red smear against the black sky. The city received me into its smoky embrace, and I navigated the familiar, crooked streets back to Barnard’s Inn. The Inn, which had once seemed to me the very pinnacle of gentlemanly abode, now appeared in its true colours: shabby, down-at-heel, and smelling faintly of decay. My own fire had gone out, and the room was cold. I stood for a moment in the darkness, the elation of the day utterly extinguished, feeling the full weight of my solitude.

Presently, I heard footsteps on the stairs, and a light appeared under my door. It was Herbert, returning from some evening’s occupation, and his cheerful call of, “Handel, are you there?” was the most welcome sound I had heard all day.

I opened the door, and he came in, his candle casting long, dancing shadows on the walls. His hair was in its customary state of amiable disorder, and his face, though tired, wore its usual expression of hopeful kindness.
“My dear Handel!” he exclaimed, seeing my face more clearly in the light. “You look utterly done for! A weary day of it, eh? Was the fair Estella as enchanting as memory painted her?”

“She was more so, Herbert,” I said, sinking into a chair. “More beautiful, and more—more herself.”

“Ah,” he said, with a delicate and comprehending sympathy. He set his candle down and began to busy himself with the fire, coaxing a flame from the dead embers. “And the situation at Richmond? Is it a suitable one? A Mrs. Brandley, I think you said?”

“A very fine house,” I told him, watching the fire catch and begin to lick at the coals. “And Mrs. Brandley and her daughter seem positioned to worship her. She will have everything she requires.”

“Except, perhaps, a warm heart,” Herbert murmured, more to the fire than to me. He then seemed to think this remark uncharitable, and added briskly, “Well! It is a great step for her, and a great thing for you to be her appointed knight-errant in the city. You will be seeing a good deal of her, I expect.”

“I am to be of use to her, when she requires it,” I said, the phrase tasting like ash in my mouth.

Herbert shot me a quick, perceptive glance. He knew me too well to miss the note of despair in my voice. Instead of dwelling on it, however, he changed the subject, as was his gentle way. “Speaking of society, Handel,” he began, straightening up and brushing the coal-dust from his hands, “I have been making some inquiries. If you and I are to take our proper places in the world, we must be seen in the proper places. There is a certain club, a society for young men of means and expectations, called The Finches of the Grove. Startop is a member, and he has proposed to put our names forward.”

“The Finches of the Grove?” I repeated, the name sounding both elegant and vaguely ridiculous.

“Just so,” said Herbert, warming to his theme. “They dine together, they patronise the arts, they are, in short, the very picture of young gentlemen of fashion. It is devilishly expensive, I hear, but we must not be deterred by that. We must invest in our own consequence, you see. It is what is expected of us.”

I looked at Herbert, his face glowing with innocent ambition in the new-born firelight, and I felt a pang of something like pity, for him and for myself. We were like two children playing at being grown-up, spending money we did not possess to keep up an appearance that was, at its heart, a complete fiction. My own expectations were a mystery, and his were founded upon a vague hope of “looking about him” for a capital investment that never seemed to materialise. Yet, I could not deny the appeal of his proposal. To be a Finch of the Grove! It sounded like precisely the sort of thing a man in my position ought to be. Would Estella not be more likely to look with favour on a member of such a distinguished fraternity?

“Very well, Herbert,” I said, my spirits rising a little at the prospect of this new venture. “If Startop will have us, let us be Finches.”

And so it was that within a fortnight, Herbert and I were elected to this august body. We celebrated our admission with a dinner at a hotel, for which I paid from Mr. Jaggers’s liberal allowance, and we felt ourselves to be making prodigious strides in the world. The Finches of the Grove were a collection of some twenty young men, who had no apparent occupation other than to spend money. Their chief purpose, so far as I could discern, was to dine together expensively and quarrel amongst themselves. Their arguments were seldom about anything of substance, but rather concerned points of honour so minute as to be invisible to the naked eye, and they were generally concluded by the throwing of plates and the ordering of more wine.

Amongst this company was one young man whom I took an immediate and profound dislike to; a heavy-limbed young man of a sulky complexion, who was older than the rest of us and carried himself with an air of sullen superiority. He was heir to a baronetcy, it was whispered, and his wealth was immense. His name was Bentley Drummle. He spoke little, and then only to contradict someone, and he had a habit of staring at his own boots as if they were the most interesting things in the room. Herbert, with his knack for nicknames, had dubbed him “the Spider,” on account of a certain sluggish, watchful quality he possessed, as if he were perpetually waiting in a dark corner for a fly to blunder into his web.

My duties as Estella’s escort, in these first few weeks, were not onerous. I was summoned to Richmond on three or four occasions, to accompany her and Mrs. Brandley to a dress-maker’s, or to a concert. On these expeditions, my role was a silent one. I would walk a few paces behind, or sit in a corner of the box, an ornamental guard. Estella was always polite, always distant. She moved through the world as if it were a stage set for her particular drama, and the rest of us were merely players with minor, insignificant roles. Every glimpse of her, however, was enough to fuel my devotion for another week, and I would return to my rooms at Barnard’s Inn feeling that my life, for all its anxieties, had a high and noble purpose.

It was this feeling of purpose that I carried with me to a grand dinner of the Finches of the Grove, held to celebrate nothing in particular. We convened at a tavern in Covent Garden, in a private room that was far too ornate for its own good. The evening began, as it always did, with a great deal of self-congratulatory noise. Toasts were made to the club, to its members, to the wine, to the cook, to anything that came to mind. I drank as much as anyone, feeling that it was my duty as a Finch to do so, and the wine went to my head, filling it with a foolish and buoyant confidence.

As the night wore on, and the table became a wreck of spilt claret and disordered plates, the conversation grew more boastful. Young men bragged of horses they had bought, of debts they had incurred, of hearts they had supposedly broken. Through it all, Bentley Drummle sat in his customary slouch, glowering at the proceedings. At length, he roused himself and, holding his glass unsteadily, proposed a toast of his own.

“A toast,” he said, his voice a low growl that cut through the general din. “To conquest. To taking what you want, because you can.”

There was a roar of approval from the assembled Finches, who found this sentiment much to their liking. Glasses were raised and drained. But I, in my wine-addled state, felt a sudden, chivalrous objection. His words seemed coarse and brutish, an insult to the very idea of love, which was, to my mind, a thing of gentle worship and patient service.

Flushed with wine and idealism, I got to my feet. “I would propose another toast,” I announced, my voice sounding strangely loud in a momentary lull.
Drummle turned his heavy eyes upon me. “And what would that be?”

“To beauty,” I said, thinking, of course, of Estella. “And to the devotion it inspires.”

A few of the more romantically inclined Finches murmured their assent, but most looked merely confused. Herbert gave me a slight, warning shake of his head. Drummle, however, continued to stare at me, a slow, unpleasant smile spreading across his face.

“Beauty?” he sneered. “Beauty is a trinket. Something to be won, and worn, and discarded when one grows tired of it. Like a horse, or a watch.”

“It is not!” I retorted, my blood up. “It is the highest of all things. It is a star to guide a man’s life.” My hand, I am ashamed to say, was trembling with the force of my feeling.

Drummle let out a short, barking laugh. “A star? You are a poet, are you, Pip? Or just a fool?” He looked around the table. “He talks of stars. I talk of property.”

He then made a particular and pointed reference to a lady, a star of the first magnitude, he called her, who was then new in the society of Richmond. He did not name Estella, but there could be no doubt of whom he meant. He spoke of her as a prize to be stalked and captured, using language of such coarse possession that it made me feel physically ill. The other Finches, sensing a confrontation, fell silent, watching us with drunken curiosity.

“You have no right to speak of her in that way,” I said, my voice low and shaking.

“I have every right,” Drummle retorted, his eyes glinting with malice. “The best-looking girl in the room is for the best man. That’s the law of nature, isn’t it?” He drained his glass and slammed it down on the table, shattering its stem. “And I am the best man here.”

What I might have done next, I dare not think. My head was swimming, and a blind rage was upon me, the same helpless fury I had felt before Trabb’s boy. I believe I might have struck him, or committed some other folly that would have led to my utter ruin. But at that moment, Herbert, with a strength I had not known he possessed, took my arm in a firm grip.

“Handel, my dear fellow,” he said, his voice calm and sober in the midst of the chaos. “The room is too hot. Let us take a turn outside for some air.”

He half-pulled, half-led me from the room, leaving Drummle to the loud congratulations of his admirers. We stumbled down the stairs and out into the cool night air of the piazza. The contrast was a shock. My head began to clear, and a wave of profound shame washed over me. I had made a spectacle of myself. I had brawled, or nearly brawled, in a public house, over a lady whose name I had no right to defend, against a man whose brutishness made my own gentlemanly pretensions seem a pathetic joke.

“He is a lout, Handel, a complete brute,” Herbert said, patting my shoulder. “Not worth your breath, and certainly not worth your fist.”

“But what he said, Herbert! The way he spoke of her!”

“I know,” Herbert said softly. “But you cannot champion her cause by descending to his level. That is not the way. You must rise above him. You must prove yourself the true gentleman, not by fighting, but by your conduct.”

His words were wise and true, but they offered little comfort. As we walked home through the dark and silent streets, I felt that my new life was a sham. The Finches of the Grove were not a society of gentlemen, but a pack of arrogant fools, and I was the greatest fool of all for having joined them. My great expectations had led me here, to a state of debt and dissipation, and had placed me in the orbit of a man like Bentley Drummle, who saw the object of my adoration as nothing more than another possession to be acquired. The ignominy of the evening was complete. I went to bed with a throbbing head and a heavy heart, feeling further from Estella, and from the man I hoped to become, than ever before.

The next morning brought with it a splitting headache and a deep sense of self-reproach. I lay in bed, watching the grimy London daylight filter through my window, and I thought that I was a complete failure. My guardian, Mr. Jaggers, had furnished me with the means to be a gentleman, and I was squandering them on absurd dinners and pointless quarrels. I had done nothing to improve my mind, nothing to secure my future; I had merely learned to spend money and to feel ashamed of my origins. Joe’s honest, loving face rose in my memory, and I turned my own to the pillow, as if to hide from his imagined disappointment.

It was in this despondent state that I was found, an hour later, by the serving-boy from the inn, who brought with him a letter. It was a small, crisp note, sealed with a wafer of pale green wax. My heart gave a violent leap, for I recognised the elegant, sloping hand at once. It was from Estella.

With trembling fingers, I broke the seal. The message was brief, and contained no salutation.

_Be at Mrs. Brandley’s to-morrow at two, to escort me to a reception in town. I shall be accompanied by a Miss Fanshawe._
_E._

I read the note three times. My headache vanished. The shame of the previous night evaporated like morning mist. Drummle and the Finches and my own foolishness were all forgotten. I was summoned. I was needed. My purpose was restored; my universe, which had threatened to fly into a thousand sordid pieces, was once more fixed upon its proper star. I was to be of use to her. And as I laid the note upon my pillow, I felt not the torment of the phrase, but only its profound and dizzying privilege.

I found that my hands were shaking, and that my breath was coming in short, uncertain puffs; it was a most ungentlemanly exhibition, and I pressed my face into the cool linen of the pillow, as if to smother the evidence of an agitation that was part joy and part terror. To be summoned! To be the chosen instrument for her convenience, for an afternoon! The thought sent a jolt through me that was more potent than any wine, and I sprang from my bed as if propelled by a spring. All languor, all self-pity, was burned away in an instant, and in its place was a singular, brilliant, and altogether terrifying purpose: I had to be ready.

My entire existence, it seemed, had suddenly narrowed to the space of the next twenty-four hours, and every action I took within them was freighted with a significance that was almost too much to bear. I flew to my wardrobe, pulling out my finest coat, a dark blue superfine wool that had cost me a sum I dared not confess even to Herbert. I laid it out upon a chair, smoothing its lapels with a reverence I might have afforded a sacred relic. My waistcoat, of a cream-coloured silk, was subjected to a similar scrutiny; so too were my trousers, my cravat, my boots. The polish on my boots, I decided with a lurch of panic, was not nearly lustrous enough. It was dull; it was common; it was the polish of a man who did not expect to be escorting Estella.

Herbert, who had come in to find me in a state of frantic indecision between two neckcloths, one of a simple, elegant fold and the other of a more elaborate and fashionable knot, looked on with an expression of gentle amusement.
“Going somewhere, Handel?” he asked, leaning against the doorframe.
“To Mrs. Brandley’s,” I said, my voice tight with concentration as I attempted the more complex knot. “To meet Estella. She requires an escort.”
“Ah,” he said, and the single syllable was filled with a world of understanding, of sympathy, and of something that sounded very much like caution. “Well. The second knot, I think. It has an air of quiet confidence.”
I seized on his advice as a drowning man might seize a spar, my fingers fumbling with the silk. Quiet confidence was precisely the air I wished to have, though I felt it no more than the man in the moon. My insides were a churning sea of inadequacy. What if my gloves were not the right shade of pearl grey? What if my hat had accumulated some invisible speck of London grime? What if, upon seeing me, she should simply change her mind? The horror of that last possibility was so great that I had to sit down for a moment, my half-tied cravat dangling foolishly. Herbert, with his unfailing good nature, simply picked up the discarded cloth, and with a few deft movements, tied it perfectly for me himself.
“There,” he said, stepping back to admire his work. “The very picture of a man about town. Now, try not to look as if you are on your way to your own execution.”

I endeavoured to follow his advice, but as I hired a hackney coach,—an extravagance that made my already precarious finances groan in protest,—and directed the driver to the address in Richmond, I felt that my expression was indeed that of a condemned man, albeit one who marches to the scaffold with a wild and impossible hope beating in his breast.
Mrs. Brandley’s house was exactly as I should have pictured it; a tall, narrow, and impeccably respectable brick edifice, with windows that shone with a kind of cold, exclusionary cleanliness. A parlour-maid with a starched cap and an air of profound suspicion answered my knock, and after taking my card as if it might be contaminated, left me to wait in a dim and chilly hall. The very air in the house seemed different; it was thin and rarefied, scented with beeswax and a faint, floral pot-pourri, a stark contrast to the hearty, if grimy, atmosphere of Barnard’s Inn. I stood with my hat in my hands, my heart thumping a ridiculously loud rhythm against my ribs, and I studied the pattern on the floor as if my life depended on it.

I was presently shown into a drawing-room, a place of oppressive order and muted colours. And there, seated upon a sofa so upright it seemed to forbid any notion of comfort, were three figures. One was a lady of a certain age, with a face that looked as though it had been carefully preserved in vinegar, all sharp angles and pursed lips; this, I surmised, must be Mrs. Brandley. The second, seated a little apart and seeming to blend into the brocade of a nearby curtain, was a young woman of such an unassuming and colourless aspect that she was almost invisible. She had pale hair, pale eyes, and a pale, frightened mouth; she was Miss Fanshawe. And the third, the very centre from which all light and life in the room seemed to emanate, was Estella.
She was not looking at me. She was examining a small enamelled fan, her head tilted at an angle that displayed the perfect curve of her neck. She wore a dress of pale blue, the colour of a winter sky, and there were pearls woven into her dark hair. The sight of her was so overwhelming, so far beyond the paltry imaginings that had sustained me, that I felt a dizzying sense of unreality. I had a sudden, absurd conviction that if I were to reach out and touch her, my hand would pass straight through, for nothing so beautiful could possibly be solid.

“You are punctual, Mr. Pip,” Mrs. Brandley observed, her voice as crisp as a dried leaf. She did not smile.
“I endeavour to be, ma’am,” I stammered, executing a bow that felt at once too deep and not deep enough.
It was only then that Estella looked up. Her eyes, cool and grey, swept over me in a single, comprehensive glance, from my carefully polished boots to my painstakingly tied cravat. It was an inspection, as thorough and impersonal as that of a horse-dealer judging new stock. I felt the blood rush to my face, certain that she had detected some fatal flaw,—a wrinkle in my coat, a hair out of place,—that would instantly disqualify me.
Then, to my infinite relief, a small, almost imperceptible nod. “You will do,” she said, and the words, which from any other woman would have been an insult, were from her a glorious benediction. She rose, and the room seemed to rise with her. Miss Fanshawe gathered a shawl and a reticule, her movements small and bird-like, while Mrs. Brandley issued a series of prim instructions about the lateness of the hour and the untrustworthiness of London society.
“We are to go to Lady Scallop’s,” Estella informed me, as if continuing a conversation we had been having all day. “I shall have need of you to fetch me ices, and to keep tiresome people at bay. Miss Fanshawe, you will remain with Mrs. Brandley.”
A flicker of what might have been either disappointment or relief crossed Miss Fanshawe’s pale face, but she said nothing, merely inclining her head in silent acquiescence. I felt a momentary pang of pity for her, this shadow-creature destined to remain behind while the star went out to shine, but it was instantly consumed by the blazing, selfish joy of my own good fortune. I was not to be left behind. I was to be her chosen attendant.

The coach ride to Lady Scallop’s was a study in exquisite torture. I sat opposite her, the space of a mere few feet seeming both an impossible chasm and a proximity so intense it made it difficult to breathe. She did not speak. She arranged her skirts, settled her hands in her lap, and then turned her face to the window, watching the blur of London’s streets pass by with an air of complete detachment. It was as if I were not there; as if the coachman, or the seat opposite, were as much her companion as I. I, meanwhile, was engaged in a frantic internal monologue, a desperate search for some topic of conversation that would be worthy of her notice. Should I comment on the weather? Too banal. On the architecture of a passing church? Too pedantic. On my undying and eternal love for her? Too terrifying. And so I said nothing, and the silence stretched between us, thick with all the things I longed to say. My own reflection, a pale and anxious ghost, stared back at me from the window-pane, and I saw with a fresh wave of misery that my hair was beginning to lose its careful arrangement.

Lady Scallop’s reception was a nightmare of paradise. The rooms were blazing with chandeliers and crammed with a suffocating mass of humanity, all rustling silk, glittering jewels, and braying laughter. The air was thick with the scent of perfume, hot wax, and crushed hopes. To me, it was a terrifying labyrinth, a sea of unfamiliar faces and unspoken rules. To Estella, it was her natural element.
She entered the room and a hush, a subtle shift in the social atmosphere, seemed to follow her. Heads turned. Conversations faltered. She was instantly surrounded, a queen holding court among a throng of lesser nobles. I, her designated escort, found myself shuffled to the periphery, a forgotten planet in her solar system. My role, as she had so clearly stated, was to be of use. I dutifully fought my way through the throng to procure a dish of lemon ice for her, which she accepted without a word, her attention already claimed by a whiskered old General who was bending low over her hand. I stood by, a sentinel with no one to guard against, and watched.
I watched as she smiled a slow, dazzling smile at one man, and then turned a shoulder of pure, cold marble to another. I watched as she moved through the crowd, a beautiful, deadly ship navigating treacherous waters, leaving a wake of admiration and despair. And I watched as she was approached by a familiar, hulking figure, a man whose presence made the lemon ice in my stomach turn to a ball of lead. It was Bentley Drummle.
He did not swagger this time. His manner was transformed. He approached her not with the bluster of the tavern, but with a kind of heavy, confident possessiveness, as if he were merely claiming that which was already his. He spoke to her in a low voice, and she, who had dismissed a dozen more witty and handsome men with a flicker of her fan, inclined her head to listen. She laughed at something he said; a low, musical laugh that I had never heard before, and that felt like a physical blow.
I felt a hot, primal surge of hatred. The desire to stride across the room, to seize the Spider by his thick neck and hurl him through the nearest window, was almost overwhelming. But I was paralyzed, not only by the fear of making a scene, but by the cold, clear knowledge of my own impotence. I was the blacksmith’s boy, playing at being a gentleman. He was… whatever he was, he was speaking to Estella, and she was listening.

After an eternity,—it may have been ten minutes,—she seemed to remember my existence. She sent Drummle away with a slight inclination of her head, a dismissal so graceful it looked like a favour, and then beckoned to me with a subtle movement of her fan. I was at her side in an instant, my heart hammering with a mixture of hope and dread.
“You are looking very grim, Pip,” she said, her voice cool and amused. “One would think you were not enjoying yourself.”
“I am here to be of service to you,” I said, the words sounding stiff and formal in my own ears.
“And so you have been,” she replied, her eyes scanning the crowd. “You make a very good shield. The way you stand there, so earnestly, it frightens off the dull ones. They see the devotion in your eyes and they think, ah, this one is occupied territory.”
The casual cruelty of it took my breath away. She was not merely observing my adoration; she was cataloguing it, analyzing its practical applications. I was not a suitor; I was a strategic advantage, a scarecrow in a field of desirable crows.
“Is that all I am to you?” I asked, my voice barely a whisper. “A shield? A scarecrow?”
She turned her full attention upon me then, and her gaze was not unkind. It was something far worse: it was pedagogical. It was the look of a teacher explaining a difficult but necessary lesson to a particularly slow student.
“You, and all the others,” she said, her voice dropping to a confidential murmur that was more intoxicating than any shout of love. She made a slight gesture with her fan, a gesture that encompassed the entire glittering, clamouring room. “Do you see that young man with the unfortunate cravat? I have made him believe I am captivated by his knowledge of Roman history. Do you see the old General? He is convinced I admire his military bearing. And Mr. Drummle,”—my name for him, the Spider, rose in my throat, but I choked it back down,—“I have allowed him to think that his brutishness is a sign of strength, and that I find it… compelling.”
She paused, letting her words sink in. “I do this to them all. I deceive and I ensnare. I have been taught to do it, and I am a very good student. It is my purpose. And you, Pip, are part of the lesson. You are my practice.”
The room seemed to tilt on its axis. The chandeliers blurred into smears of painful light. To be her practice. Not even the object of the hunt, but the tool for sharpening the spear.
“Why?” I managed to ask. “Why are you telling me this?”
“Because,” she said, and for a fleeting moment, I saw something in her eyes, a flicker of a caged and lonely thing, “I do not wish to deceive _you_. Not entirely. The others are my game. You… you are something else. A reminder, perhaps, of a time before the lessons began. Now, take me home. The air in here is growing stale.”

The journey back to Richmond was made in an even deeper silence than the one that had preceded it. But this was a different kind of silence. The first had been a void, which my foolish heart had rushed to fill with hopes and fantasies. This new silence was a plenum, a heavy, suffocating weight of knowledge. I knew my place now. I had been told it, with a frankness that was more devastating than any lie.
When the coach drew up before Mrs. Brandley’s immaculate door, she did not wait for me to assist her. She was out of the carriage in a single, fluid motion. She stood for a moment on the pavement, a pale figure in the gaslight, and looked at me where I sat, slumped in the shadows of the coach.
“Thank you for your service, Pip,” she said, the words formal, distant. “You performed your duties well.”
And then she was gone, the heavy front door closing behind her with a sound of utter finality. I was left alone with the smell of horsehair and my own particular agony, the coachman looking down from his perch with an air of bored impatience. I had been dismissed. My purpose was served.
I did not tell him to drive me back to Barnard’s Inn. I paid him what I owed, and more, and when the coach had rumbled away into the darkness, I began to walk. I did not know where I was going. I walked through the quiet, tree-lined streets of Richmond, my gentleman’s boots echoing on the clean pavements. My fine coat felt like a costume, my silk waistcoat a mockery. The man she had pronounced would “do” had been found wanting in some fundamental way he could not even name.

My thoughts, a venomous and disordered swarm, circled back again and again to that one terrible, illuminating phrase: “You are my practice.” All the small kindnesses, all the moments of perceived connection I had hoarded like a miser’s gold, were revealed to be nothing more than practice strokes. She was training for the subjugation of men, and I was her willing, witless sparring partner. And the most agonizing part of it all was the small, treacherous part of my soul that was not angry, but proud. Even in this, I was special. I was not the game; I was the whetstone upon which the blade was sharpened. It was a distinction that offered no comfort, only a more refined and enduring pain.
I thought of Joe, and the forge, and the simple, honest world I had left behind. I had been so ashamed of it, so desperate to escape it. And for what? To become a shield, a scarecrow, a practice dummy in a game I could not hope to win, or even to properly understand. The tears that had sprung to my eyes at the thought of Joe’s goodness a day before were now a hot, searing shame at the back of my throat, but they would not fall. I was, after all, a gentleman now. And gentlemen, I supposed, did not weep on the streets of Richmond, no matter how thoroughly their hearts had been broken. They simply paid for their foolishness, in silence, and walked on into the night.

Mr. Jaggers was inside, reading some papers by the dim light, and he gave me a short, uninterested nod as I took my seat. He made no inquiry as to the cause of my delay, nor did he seem to notice the flush of shame that I am certain still burned upon my cheeks. To him, I was a ward, a pecuniary responsibility, a case; the smaller agonies of my existence were of no more consequence than the dust motes dancing in the single sunbeam that pierced the carriage’s gloom. And for this, in that moment, I was grateful. His cold detachment was a balm after the hot, personal ignominy I had just endured. We rode on towards London in a near-perfect silence, broken only by the rustle of his papers and the steady, monotonous rumble of the wheels. My thoughts, however, were a riot. They fled from the crowing of Trabb’s boy only to collide with the vision of Estella, her hair ablaze with jewels, her beauty a weapon she was only just learning to wield. The two humiliations, one so low and comical, the other so high and tragic, became tangled in my mind. Was I not a fool in both instances? A boy playing at being a gentleman, only to be reminded of his coarse origins by a shop-boy’s mimicry; and a lover playing at romance, only to be told he was a mere instrument for practice. It seemed my whole life was a pretence, a poorly staged play in which I was the only actor who did not know his lines were a jest.

I walked on into the Richmond night, the echo of my own footsteps my only company. The handsome, sleeping houses, with their dark windows and secure gates, seemed to look down upon me with a genteel pity. I was among them, but not of them; a ghost in fine clothes, haunting a world I could not possess. The words, “You are my practice,” played themselves over and over in my mind, not with the deafening repetition of Miss Havisham’s command, but as a low, insistent whisper that dissected my soul. I was a tool. Not even a beloved tool, kept for its own sake, but a practice tool, to be used and perhaps discarded when the true work began. Who, I wondered, was the true work for? Was there some gentleman of high birth and vast fortune for whom she was being perfected? Was I merely the coarse stone on which the blade was first ground, before it was brought to the fine whetstone of London society, and thence to the final strop of a brilliant marriage? The thought was a fresh agony. To be a stepping stone is a poor enough fate; but to be a practice dummy, knocked about for sport and education, was an indignity of a more profound and chilling order.

I must have walked for an hour or more, my course a random and unthinking pattern through the elegant streets, when a damp chill in the air, a precursor to a late autumn fog, finally drove me to seek shelter. I had no destination, no friend to call upon in this strange and beautiful suburb. The idea of presenting myself at Mrs. Brandley’s door again, a spurned satellite returning to its cold star, was unthinkable. My eyes, scanning the street ahead, fell upon the warm, if somewhat greasy, light spilling from the lower windows of a public house. It was not a grand establishment, not a hotel for gentlemen, but a tavern, plain and simple, its sign creaking faintly in the rising wind. The thought of a fire, of a glass of something hot to ward off the cold that had settled deep in my bones, was more than I could resist. With a sense of lowering myself, which was itself a kind of bitter medicine, I pushed open the heavy door and went in.

The place was thick with the acrid smell of stale beer and wet wool, of clay pipes and sawdust. A handful of men, coachmen and gardeners by their look, were huddled near a modest fire, their conversation a low rumble. They looked up as I entered, and I was acutely conscious of my London coat and my polished boots, which seemed to shine with an unnatural lustre in the dim, smoky light. I was an alien presence, and I felt their eyes follow me as I made my way to a small, secluded table in a dark corner, as far from the fire and from them as I could manage. A pot-boy, with a permanently surprised expression and a dirty apron, came to take my order, and I asked for brandy and water, feeling that my choice of drink might at least lend me an air of familiarity with such places, though in truth I felt as exposed as if I stood on a stage.

I sat stirring the hot liquid, letting the steam warm my face, and tried to lose myself in the anonymity of the room. But my thoughts gave me no peace. Estella’s face floated in the steam before me, now smiling, now cold, now looking at me with that calm, dispassionate curiosity with which a scientist might observe a specimen. I saw her place the jewels in her hair, and I saw my guardian, Mr. Jaggers himself, raise his eyebrows at her loveliness. Was it all a performance for him, too? Was he the audience for whom she practiced her arts? The idea was grotesque, yet it had a certain logic. He was the paymaster, the director of the entire strange drama of Satis House. Perhaps he had come not to see me, but to inspect his investment, to see how his ward was progressing in her lessons of breaking hearts. And I, Pip, was the heart provided for the lesson.

My miserable reflections were interrupted by a voice from a nearby table, a voice that was attempting to be refined but was failing utterly, dragged down by drink into a slurry of indistinct consonants. “A gentleman, sir,” the voice declared to its companion, who appeared to be a surly man in a carter’s smock, “is not defined by the contents of his purse, but by the quality of his soul. Pecuniary embarrassment is a temporary state; a vulgar soul is an eternal damnation.”

I could not help but glance over. The speaker was a man of perhaps fifty years, though he might have been younger, for dissipation had aged him prematurely. He wore the sad vestiges of finery; a stained cravat, a waistcoat from which most of the buttons were missing, and a coat whose velvet collar was worn to a greasy sheen. He had the watery eyes and the trembling hands of a man for whom brandy was not a comfort but a necessity. Yet he held his head with a kind of defiant pride, as if addressing a courtroom rather than a drowsing carter in a wayside tavern.

“Take my case,” he continued, gesturing with a shaky hand that slopped some of his drink onto the table. “Born to expectations. A fine estate in the North. Horses. Hounds. A name, sir, a name that meant something. And love! Ah, love.” He sighed, a great, theatrical exhalation of boozy sentiment. “There was a lady, an angel, the daughter of a baronet. We were to be married. But fortune, sir, fortune is a fickle jade. A bad investment. A turn of the cards. The details are dreary and not for such an evening as this. The estate is gone. The name is… in abeyance. And the lady,” he paused, and a tear, or perhaps just a drop of condensed humidity, trickled down his cheek, “the lady married a man who manufactured buttons. Buttons! Can you conceive of it? Her delicate hands, destined for the finest silks, condemned to the company of a man who thinks of nothing but buttons!”

The carter grunted, a sound that conveyed neither sympathy nor interest, and took a long pull from his own mug.

The ruined gentleman seemed not to notice. He was lost in his own phantasmagoria of self-pity. “But do I complain?” he asked, his voice rising. “No. I endure. For I know, here,” he thumped his chest weakly, “that I am still a gentleman. She may be Mrs. Buttons, but I am still who I was. And she knows it. In her heart, she knows she chose the dross and left the gold. She practices her smiles on him, her wifely duties, her household management. But it is a sham. A performance. Her heart, sir, her true heart, was educated by me. I taught her what it was to be loved by a man of feeling, a man of spirit! And that, that is something her button-maker can never give her, and never take from me.”

I felt a cold dread creep over me. The man’s maudlin, drunken rambling was a grotesque parody of my own secret thoughts. His talk of the lady who had left him, of her life being a performance, of his own indelible and superior place in her education of the heart; it was as if he had plucked the very thoughts from my head and twisted them into this pathetic, public spectacle. Was this my future? Was this what awaited the man who was used for “practice”? To sit in a tavern, years hence, boasting to strangers of the part he had played in the schooling of a heart that now belonged to another? The brandy tasted sour in my mouth. The man’s self-deception, so obvious and so pitiful, cast a harsh, unflattering light upon my own. My grand, tragic suffering suddenly seemed small, and common, and deeply, deeply foolish.

I could bear it no longer. I threw some coins on the table, far more than the brandy was worth, and fled from the tavern as if it were on fire. The fog had thickened now, and the world was a grey, swirling blindness. The lamps were halos of indistinct light, and the handsome houses of Richmond had vanished. I was alone in a void, with the ghost of my future self gibbering at my heels. I had to get back to London. I had to get back to my rooms, to the life I had chosen, the life of a gentleman with great expectations.

I stumbled through the fog until I found a main road, and by a stroke of luck, I heard the clatter of hooves and the call of a horn. It was a late mail coach, making its way to the city. I hailed it, my voice sounding thin and strange in the damp air, and a gruff guard, wrapped to the eyeballs in scarves, let me clamber up into an outside seat. The coach was crowded with damp, silent figures, and we huddled together for warmth as we rattled and swayed through the night. The journey was a misery. The fog was wet and chilling, and it seemed to work its way through my fine coat, through my skin, and into my very marrow. I dozed fitfully, my head lolling against the shoulder of a sleeping farmer, and my dreams were a feverish torment. I was at the forge with Joe, the hammer rising and falling, and with every blow, Trabb’s boy shrieked with laughter and Estella’s voice whispered, “Practice, Pip, more practice.” Then I was in the tavern, and the ruined gentleman was me, and I was weeping into my brandy over a lost lady who had married a blacksmith.

It was in the deadest part of the night, when London was a vast, sleeping beast, that the coach finally deposited me near my own part of the town. The streets were deserted, save for a few shuffling watchmen and the occasional stray cat. Barnard’s Inn, when I reached it, looked meaner and more desolate than ever. The old bricks seemed to weep with damp, and the central courtyard was a puddle of black, stagnant water. My own rooms, when I let myself in with a fumbling key, were cold and dark. I struck a light, and the candle flame revealed Herbert, asleep in his chair by the dead fire, a book fallen to the floor beside him. He had waited up for me.

A wave of affection for him, and a simultaneous wave of profound shame, washed over me. He was so good, so honest, so completely without the sort of tortuous, self-inflicted misery in which I was drowning. He stirred as I moved about the room, blinking in the candlelight.

“Pip? Is that you?” he said, his voice thick with sleep. “Good heavens, what time is it? I must have drifted off. Did you have a pleasant evening?”

The question was a simple kindness, but it felt like a dagger. A pleasant evening. I had been scorned by a boy, dismissed by the woman I loved, and confronted with a terrifying vision of my own potential ruin. I could not tell him. I could not expose my foolish, hopeful heart to his clear-sighted sympathy. It would have been a confession of my own utter idiocy. And so I lied. I arranged my features into what I hoped was an expression of gentlemanly fatigue, and summoned a casual tone.

“Tolerable, Herbert, tolerable,” I said, turning away to light another candle, so he could not see my eyes. “Miss Havisham was in a strange mood, as ever. And Estella… Estella was very beautiful.” The words almost choked me. “We played at whist. It was all very quiet. The country air has made me desperately sleepy, that is all.”

“Ah, of course,” he said, yawning and stretching. “Well, I am glad you are back safe. The fog came on something dreadful.” He stood up, patted my shoulder, and shuffled off towards his own small bedroom. “Good night, old boy.”

“Good night, Herbert,” I whispered to his retreating back.

I was alone. I did not go to bed. I stood before the small, warped looking-glass above the mantelpiece and stared at my own reflection. The face that stared back was pale and drawn, the eyes shadowed with a misery that I had just denied. I saw the blacksmith’s boy, trying so desperately to hide inside the gentleman’s coat. I saw the practice dummy, marked and battered from his lesson. The ruined gentleman in the tavern was wrong. A vulgar soul might be an eternal damnation, but a broken heart, I was beginning to suspect, was a far more immediate and painful hell. And yet, as I looked at the pathetic figure in the glass, a new and terrible resolve began to form within me, a resolve born not of hope, but of a desperate, wounded pride. I would not be the man in the tavern. I would not be discarded. If I was her practice, then I would become so expert, so perfect in my role, that she could not bear to move on to another. I would make myself indispensable. I would learn her lessons so well, anticipate her every move, and adore her so completely, that my "practice" would become the performance itself. I would make her love me, not in spite of what I was, but because of it. I blew out the candle and stood for a long time in the darkness, the foolish, impossible plan taking root in the ruins of my heart. I would love her, love her, love her; not as a boy loves a star, but as a craftsman loves his tormenting, exquisite, and ultimately unconquerable art.

That resolution I had formed in the darkness was a cold and intricate machine, and I spent the remainder of the night constructing it in the workshop of my mind. My pride, a poor and shabby tutor who had lately been much abused, began my instruction. To become the master of this new and terrible art, the art of winning Estella, I must first understand its materials. The material was her heart, a thing I conceived of as being like the jewels she wore; beautiful, hard, and utterly indifferent to the warmth of the hand that held it. The tools of my craft, then, must be equally cold and sharp. I must learn the language of indifference. I must study the posture of disdain. I must acquire a gentlemanly cruelty that could meet her own and not be broken by it.

It was a monstrous curriculum of study, and I, the blacksmith’s boy, was its sole and trembling pupil. My love, which had been a simple, if painful, adoration, was to be hammered and reshaped upon this new and terrible anvil. I would burn away the dross of my natural feeling, my earnestness, my unfortunate tendency to care. I would forge myself into a creature of pure surface, a mirror of fashionable listlessness, so that when she looked at me she would see nothing but a pleasing reflection of her own world. It was a kind of suicide of the soul that I contemplated, and I embraced it with the desperate fervour of a man who believes he has no other choice but to perish.

When morning came, a gray and sullen affair that seemed to weep upon the window-panes, I found Herbert already at his breakfast, his face as bright and sanguine as the London weather was grim. His good nature was a constant, gentle reproach to the tangled and secretive edifice of my own ambitions.

“Hallo, Handel,” he said, looking up from a letter. “You look as if you have wrestled with ghosts all night. Still the country air, is it? Or was it the whist? I am told Jaggers is a fearsome opponent, and plays his cards as if they were warrants for a man’s execution.”

“Something of the sort,” I said, attempting a lightness I was a world away from feeling. I poured myself a cup of tea with a hand that I was disturbed to find was not quite steady. “His presence is not conducive to a peaceful night’s rest. I have some small matters to see to in the city today, Herbert; some affairs connected with my allowance that Mr. Jaggers must be consulted upon.”

This was a lie, but it was a necessary one. My true destination was not Mr. Jaggers’s office, that repository of secrets and sorrows, but a far more frivolous, and in its own way, far more dangerous institution. I had heard Herbert speak of it before, a club for young men of means and little occupation, called the Finches of the Grove. It was a place where they met to dine, and drink, and boast, and generally prove their superiority to the working world by the magnificent scale of their idleness. Herbert himself had been a member, but had resigned for reasons of economy. He spoke of it with a kind of fond, retrospective amusement, but I had heard another name mentioned in connection with it, a name that now seemed to my fevered mind to be a signpost on my new and dismal road. Bentley Drummle was a Finch.

Of course he was. Where else would such a creature find its flock? I conceived that to master the art of her heart, I must first apprentice myself to the very school of cynicism and languid cruelty that had shaped it; and Drummle, I felt with a sick certainty, was its most accomplished graduate. He was my new lesson. He was the foul and bitter medicine I must force myself to take.

“Affairs with Jaggers?” said Herbert, looking at me with some concern. “Nothing amiss, I hope? You are not in any difficulty?”

“No, no, a mere formality,” I said, turning away to hide the flush of shame that rose to my cheeks. “A question of investment, that is all. I shall likely be occupied for most of the day.”

“Very well, old boy. As it happens, I have a new prospect myself! A man in the shipping trade, a connection of my father’s, who is looking for a partner with, as he puts it, ‘a modicum of capital and a plenitude of spirit.’ I have the latter in abundance, and I shall endeavour to convince him that what I lack in the former I can make up for in the latter!”

He laughed his easy, hopeful laugh, and I felt a pang of something so like grief that it was a physical ache in my chest. He was building his future, plank by plank, with honest labour and a cheerful heart. I was preparing to demolish my own, and call the ruins a palace fit for a queen.

I left him to his hopeful schemes and made my way, not towards Little Britain, but to a far more polished and less purposeful quarter of the town. The Finches of the Grove had their rooms in a grand building near St. James’s, a place of such imposing and expensive sombreness that I felt my provincial origins clinging to me like mud. I gave my name to a porter who seemed to look right through me, and was directed up a sweeping staircase to a set of double doors from which issued a sound of laughter that was not at all like Herbert’s, but had the brittle, jarring quality of breaking glass.

I pushed open the doors and entered my new schoolroom. The air was thick with the scent of wine and cigars and a kind of expensive, perfumed indolence. A dozen or so young men were scattered about the room in attitudes of contrived nonchalance. They were all dressed in the height of fashion, but with a calculated carelessness, as if their tailors had done their best and they had then done their best to undo it. They were sprawled on velvet sofas, their polished boots resting on priceless tables; they were leaning against the marble mantelpiece, flicking ash into a great porcelain vase. It was a scene of magnificent, wasteful profligacy, and I felt at once a tremor of my old insecurity and a new, cold determination to belong to it.

And then I saw him. He was not in the centre of the room, but he was its centre of gravity. Bentley Drummle was slumped in a large wing-backed chair, a glass of dark liquid in his hand, his heavy-lidded eyes surveying the scene with a look of sullen proprietorship. He was a large, thick-set young man, with a blotchy complexion and a sulky mouth. There was nothing in his appearance to recommend him, save for the undeniable stamp of wealth and the arrogant assurance that came with it. He was, I had heard Herbert say, a baronet in waiting, and he carried the knowledge of it not as a responsibility, but as a weapon. He was known among them as the Spider, a name I thought particularly apt, for he seemed to spin a web of sullenness and ill-temper around him, in which the others, who were mostly younger and more foolish, were pleased to be caught.

No one greeted my entrance. A few heads turned, registered a new face, and turned away again with a disinterest so profound as to be an insult. This, I told myself, was my first lesson: a true gentleman does not notice those who are not worthy of his notice. I moved to an unoccupied corner of the room, affecting an air of contemplative ease, and pretended to study a large, dark painting of a naval battle, while in reality every one of my senses was strained to observe the master at his work.

Drummle did not speak often, but when he did, the others fell silent. His pronouncements were delivered in a thick, grudging voice, and were chiefly concerned with the inferiority of other people’s horses, the poor quality of the wine, and the general imbecility of the world at large. He seemed to be a connoisseur of disappointment. A young man with a foolish, eager face, whom they called Punting, ventured to tell a humorous story, and at its conclusion looked to Drummle for approval.

“I have heard dogs make more amusing sounds when they are ill,” Drummle observed, without a flicker of expression. The laughter that followed was directed not with Punting, but at him, and he subsided into a state of crimson mortification.

Lesson the second, I recorded in the ledger of my mind: wit is for common players; a true man of fashion conveys his superiority through the crushing weight of his displeasure.

I watched for an hour or more, a silent and invisible scholar in this academy of scorn. They spoke of cards, and of debts, and of duels. They spoke of women, but not as I had ever thought of them; they spoke of them as they spoke of horses, assessing their points, their breeding, their market value. A pretty actress was a “likely filly”; a wealthy heiress was “a good property to be settled.” It was all so coarse, so brutal, so utterly devoid of the romantic agony that consumed me, that I felt a wave of revulsion. And yet, this was the world in which Estella moved. This was the language she understood. Was not her own beauty a kind of currency? Had not Miss Havisham put her up for sale, in a manner of speaking, grooming her for the highest bidder?

The thought was a betrayal, and yet it felt like a truth. And with that truth came a fresh wave of cold resolve. If I could not beat these men at their own game, I would surely lose her to one of them. I was about to slip away, my head full of these ugly new theories, when the Spider stirred in his web.

“A toast,” he mumbled, raising his glass so that it slopped a little dark red wine onto his cuff. He did not seem to notice. The other Finches, eager for any directive, raised their own glasses.

“To the fairest,” Drummle said, his voice thick and possessive. “To the star of my acquaintance. To Estella.”

The name fell into the smoky air of the room like a stone into a stagnant pool. Every eye turned, not to Drummle, but to me. My presence, which had been so studiously ignored, was now the most interesting thing in the room. I do not know how they knew; perhaps Drummle had boasted, perhaps gossip, that fleet-footed and malicious messenger, had already connected my name with hers. But they knew. I saw it in their sudden, avid curiosity. I was no longer an invisible scholar; I was a rival claimant, a provincial upstart trespassing on the Spider’s territory.

My heart was beating a wild and panicked tattoo against my ribs. The old Pip, the blacksmith’s boy, would have sprung to his feet, flushed with righteous anger, and challenged him then and there. The old Pip would have made a passionate, foolish speech about love and honour, and been laughed out of the room for his troubles. But I was supposed to be the new Pip, the craftsman of coldness. This was my first test. My tutor, Pride, whispered in my ear.

I did not move. I did not flush. I forced myself to meet Drummle’s heavy, challenging gaze. I raised the glass of wine I had been nursing, a gesture I hoped looked languid and unconcerned.

Drummle’s sullen mouth twisted into a smirk. “You know the lady, I believe, Pip?” he said, drawling my name as if it were a comical absurdity. The Finches tittered.

“One has the honour of her acquaintance,” I said. My voice sounded thin and reedy in my own ears, but I strove to lace it with an ice I did not feel. “She is a ward, as you know, of a relation of my patron.” I framed it this way deliberately, to make it sound like a matter of business, of obligation, not of the heart.

“A very fine piece of property,” said Punting, the foolish one, eager to get back into the Spider’s good graces. “Exquisite confirmation. Pity about the connections, though. One hears they are… peculiar.”

“Her connections are of no consequence,” Drummle growled, silencing Punting with a look. “She is a star. A diamond. And she will be worn by the man who can afford her.” He looked directly at me when he said this, a look of such brutal, proprietary confidence that it was all I could do not to throw my glass in his face.

But the craftsman held me back. The lesson. I must be cold. I must be sharp. I must be cruel. I took a slow sip of my wine, making a show of considering its taste, as I had seen Jaggers do. I set the glass down with a soft click.

“A diamond, as you say,” I observed, pitching my voice to be heard by the whole room. “But then, diamonds are notoriously hard. Brittle, even. One must have a very delicate touch. A clumsy hand, a heavy setting, can cause them to shatter into worthless dust. It takes a true connoisseur, not merely a man with a full purse, to appreciate such a jewel and, more importantly, to be worthy of it.”

There was a moment of absolute silence. I had not shouted. I had not issued a challenge. I had delivered my rejoinder in the cool, appraising tones of a jeweller discussing a stone. I had used their own language of commerce and possession against them. I saw a flicker of confusion in Drummle’s dull eyes. He was a man of fists and fortunes, not of metaphors. He knew he had been insulted, but he could not quite grasp how. He was like a bull that has received a picador’s dart; surprised, wounded, but not yet enraged.

I did not wait for his rage to gather. I had made my point, or rather, my new self had. I gave a slight, almost imperceptible nod to the room at large, a gesture that was meant to say, “This has been moderately amusing, but I have more important things to attend to.” Then I turned, and with a steady, unhurried pace, I walked out of the room, leaving the silence and the cigar smoke and the Spider behind me.

Once out on the street, the cold air hitting my face, the rigid posture I had maintained collapsed. I leaned against a wall, my legs trembling, my heart hammering as if it would burst from my chest. I had done it. I had faced the Spider in his own web and had not been consumed. I had used the tools of my new, ugly craft, and they had, in a fashion, worked. I should have felt victorious.

But what I felt was a profound and bottomless self-disgust. The words I had spoken, so clever and so cold, were a blasphemy against the pure and agonizing love I felt. I had spoken of Estella as a thing, a jewel, a piece of property, just as they did. In my attempt to prove myself worthy of her, I had become just as coarse and transactional as the men I despised. I had won the battle, perhaps, a tiny, insignificant skirmish in a club of fools, but I had betrayed the very essence of the thing I was fighting for.

I began to walk, quickly, blindly, away from the grand houses of St. James’s, towards the grimy, familiar tangle of the city. I thought of Joe, with his simple, honest heart, and how he would never have understood the scene I had just played. I thought of Biddy, with her clear, kind eyes, and knew that she would have seen straight through my pathetic performance to the miserable boy beneath. In seeking to make myself a gentleman fit for Estella, I was making myself a man unfit for the company of anyone who had ever truly loved me. The price of my apprenticeship, I was beginning to see, was not merely the torment of my own heart, but the forfeiture of my own history, my own soul. The craftsman had completed his first piece, and it was a small, sharp, perfectly polished instrument of self-inflicted pain.
