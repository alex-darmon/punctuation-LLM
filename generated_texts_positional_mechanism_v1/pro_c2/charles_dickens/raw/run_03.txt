It was to admit that the quiet afternoon, the talk of Clara, the vision of a simple, unadorned life, was but an interlude, a pleasant fiction read between chapters of a much grander and more terrible book.

“I know, old fellow, I know,” he said, and clapped me on the shoulder, a gesture of such profound and simple friendship that it almost brought the tears to my eyes again. “You will go to the coach. And you will be the perfect gentleman. And I,” he added, with a twinkle trying to break through the clouds of his concern, “shall have a hot supper waiting for your return, at which you may tell me everything, or nothing, precisely as you choose.”

His kindness was a balm and a reproach. It highlighted the chasm between the steady, honourable affection he offered, and the glittering, capricious cruelty I sought. I resolved, in a flash of self-awareness that was as painful as it was fleeting, that I would strive to be more like Herbert; that I would meet Estella with a calm and manly dignity, that I would not let her see the frantic boy who had once cried into his pillow for love of her. I would be a gentleman. The appointed hour found me at the Cross Keys, an inn and coaching-house of some notoriety, a place of such frantic and grimy purpose that it seemed the very antithesis of Estella. The air was thick with the smell of damp straw and horse, of ale and unwashed bodies, all of it mingling with the smoke that belched from a great fireplace and the hissing of the gas-lamps that threw a sallow, theatrical light upon the scene. Porters shouted, ostlers cursed, passengers clamoured for their luggage, and I stood amidst it all, an island of starched linen and morbid anticipation, feeling as out of place as a pearl in an oyster-stew.

I had arrived early, a habit of the anxious, and my punctuality afforded me a long and miserable period of observation. I watched families bidding loud, tearful farewells, and businessmen greeting one another with bluff, hearty cries. I saw a young woman, not much older than I, being handed into a carriage by a beaming young man who was clearly her husband, and the sight of their simple, unselfconscious happiness was a knife in my heart. Every scene of ordinary life I witnessed served only to underscore the extraordinary, the unnatural, nature of my own vigil. I was not here to greet a wife, a sister, or a friend; I was here to receive a star that had fallen to Earth, and my only duty was to ensure that its lustre was not dimmed by any contact with the common mud.

At last, with a great clatter of hooves and a groan of springs, the coach from Richmond rattled into the yard. My heart, which had been capering about in my chest like a lunatic, gave a great lurch and then seemed to stop altogether. The door was opened by a guard, and a stream of passengers began to disembark, a rumpled and weary collection of humanity. I scanned their faces with a feverish intensity, my carefully constructed gentlemanly composure crumbling with every passing second. And then, I saw her.

She was the last to descend. She did not step, but appeared, upon the carriage step, framed for a moment in the doorway like a portrait. A collective murmur went through the portion of the yard that could see her; a silence from the porters, a turning of heads from the other passengers. She was dressed for travel in a deep blue pelisse and a matching bonnet from which a single, proud feather curved. She was paler than I remembered, and the gaslight caught in the jewels at her throat, making them glitter like cold, blue stars. Another woman, older, stout, and possessing an air of severe respectability, descended after her and began to fuss with a collection of bags and wraps.

Estella’s eyes, cool and dismissive, swept over the chaotic scene before her, over the shouting men and the weary travelers, and for a terrifying moment I was certain she would not see me, that I was simply another drab figure in the grimy tapestry of the coaching-inn. Then, her gaze found me, and it paused. There was no recognition in it, no warmth, no sign that she was looking at a person she knew. It was the look of a mistress finding her footman, a confirmation that a necessary object was in its appointed place.

I moved towards her then, my hat in my hand, my heart a leaden weight in my chest. “Estella,” I said, my voice barely a whisper. “I am here.”

“I see you are,” she replied, and her voice was the same as ever, a beautiful, clear music that made no attempt to conceal its utter lack of interest. “You will be so good as to see to our luggage. We are for Mrs. Brandley’s, in Richmond. This is she.” She gestured with a slight inclination of her head towards the older woman, who gave me a sharp, appraising look, as if I were a piece of furniture she was considering whether to purchase.

“Pip,” I said, bowing to the woman, though I felt she was the one who ought to have been bowing to me, the friend of Miss Havisham’s ward. But then, I was not truly a friend, was I? I was an instrument, a convenience.

“The luggage is here, Pip,” Estella said, her use of my name a sharp prod, a reminder of my station. “Mrs. Brandley is fatigued from the journey. You will hire a hackney and direct the driver. A clean one, if such a thing exists in this place.”

And that was it. That was the reunion for which I had dressed in my finest, for which I had abandoned the gentle peace of Herbert’s company. I was an errand-boy. I was a higher class of servant, one who wore a better coat. As I turned to bellow for a porter, a task I had not performed since my first bewildering days in London, I felt a hot flush of shame and fury rise in my throat. The gentlemanly dignity I had so proudly resolved to maintain had been shattered with a dozen careless words.

I did as I was bid. I oversaw the loading of a truly prodigious number of trunks and hat-boxes onto a waiting hackney, which I had selected with as much care as if I were choosing a steed for a king. I gave the driver the address in a voice of command that I hoped conveyed a lofty and careless authority, though I felt it trembled with humiliation. I handed Mrs. Brandley into the cab with a stiff and formal courtesy, and then I turned to Estella.

She stood watching me, a faint, unreadable smile playing on her lips. It was a smile I knew well, a smile that held both mockery and a strange, distant sort of curiosity, as if she found my earnest exertions mildly amusing.

“You have become quite the man of London, Pip,” she said, as I held the door for her.

“I endeavour to be,” I said, the words tasting like ash in my mouth.

“Do you?” she asked, and she placed her gloved hand on the carriage frame to steady herself. For a single, mad instant, her fingers were but an inch from my own. The nearness of her, the scent of her perfume, a faint fragrance of winter flowers, went to my head like wine. All the anger, all the humiliation, was swept away in a fresh tide of my old, hopeless adoration. She was here. She was speaking to me. That was all that mattered.

“I shall call upon you tomorrow,” I said, my voice thick with the emotion I had sworn to conceal.

“Mrs. Brandley receives on Tuesdays,” she said, settling herself into the carriage without a backward glance. “You may call on Tuesday, if you are not otherwise engaged.”

And with a lurch and a rattle, the hackney pulled away, leaving me standing alone in the centre of the courtyard, the smells of horse and smoke once again ascendant. A porter jostled me, and I came back to myself with a start. I was covered in a fine layer of grime, my cravat was askew, and the great hope of my life had just treated me with the careless condescension she might have shown a stable-boy.

I walked back to the Temple in a daze. The resolution to be a dignified gentleman had failed. The hope of a warm reunion had been a fool’s dream. I had been goaded, tested, and found wanting, and yet, and yet… I would be there on Tuesday. I would be there with my heart in my hand, ready for it to be trampled once more.

When I entered our chambers, Herbert looked up from his book with a hopeful expression that died the moment he saw my face.

“Ah,” he said softly. “Not the reunion you had hoped for, then.”

“She was as beautiful as ever,” I said, sinking into a chair and staring into the fire. “And she wishes me to call on Tuesday.”

Herbert said nothing for a long moment. He simply got up, went to the small pantry we kept, and returned with a bottle and two glasses. He poured a generous measure of brandy for each of us and handed me one.

“To Tuesday, then,” he said, with a kindness that felt like a rebuke to my own folly. “May it be a better day than this one.”

I drank the brandy in one gulp, the spirit burning a fiery path down my throat, but it could not touch the cold knot of misery and longing that was lodged in my chest. I had seen Estella, and the sight had undone me. The agonizing circumstance was not merely renewed; it had been reinforced, strengthened, and made the absolute and undeniable centre of my world once more. The quiet garden by the river felt a thousand miles away, and a lifetime ago. The only thing that was real was the prospect of Tuesday, and the torment, and the glory, of seeing her again. It was a pitiful existence, I knew, but it was the only one I seemed capable of wanting. The blacksmith’s boy, who had once been so ashamed of his coarse hands, was now a gentleman ashamed of his coarse heart, a heart that could not, for all its education, learn to stop loving the one who despised it.

And indeed, the intervening days stretched before me like a barren desert, a landscape of intolerable waiting. The hours passed with a leaden slowness, each tick of the clock in our chambers a tiny hammer-blow upon the anvil of my impatience. I tried to read, but the words swam before my eyes, forming themselves into one name, Estella. My entire existence had narrowed to a single point in the future; a Tuesday afternoon in Richmond.

It was on the Sunday evening, a day of particular gloom both in the London weather and in my own soul, that Herbert, in an act of supreme friendly desperation, declared that we must have a distraction.

“This will not do, Pip,” he announced, closing his book with a decisive snap. “You are becoming a veritable hermit, a melancholy ghost haunting the Temple. We shall go out. We shall go to the theatre.”

“The theatre?” I said, without much enthusiasm. “I have no mind for a play, Herbert.”

“Nonsense,” he returned, with a forced brightness. “It is the very thing. It will take you out of yourself. Besides,” he added, with a twinkle in his eye, “it is a special occasion. A cold dread, entirely distinct from my Estella-centric misery, took hold of me. Mr. Wopsle. And so, with the heaviest of hearts, I allowed myself to be led from our chambers and towards the small, rather disreputable theatre where Mr. Wopsle, or rather Mr. Waldengarver, was to wrestle with the slings and arrows of outrageous fortune. The place itself was an assault upon the senses; the air was thick with the smell of stale tobacco, spilled ale, and the mass of unwashed humanity that crowded the pit. Oranges were being sold by loud women with baskets, and the floor was covered in a damp mulch of sawdust and nutshells. We, as gentlemen, took seats in a box, a slight elevation which did little to remove us from the general miasma, but which did, I confess, appeal to that part of me that was so terribly conscious of my station.

The curtain rose on a scene of Elsinore that looked suspiciously like a painted view of Rochester Castle, and after a moment of shuffling and a loud prompter’s cough from the wings, the tragedy began. I will not attempt a full accounting of the performance, for my memory recoils from the task. Suffice it to say that the Ghost of Hamlet’s Father was afflicted with a severe cough, and the Queen of Denmark appeared to have partaken of too much gin before the play had even commenced. He did not so much walk upon the stage as launch himself onto it, striking a pose with one hand upon his hip and the other pointing accusingly at the ceiling. Mr. Wopsle glared at the interruption, his Hamlet momentarily forgotten in favour of the indignant church clerk. I shrank back in my seat, my face burning with a shame so acute it was almost a physical pain. This was the world I had come from. This absurd, bellowing, preposterous man had known me as a boy; he had eaten at my sister’s table; he had witnessed my earliest humiliations. What if someone here knew me? The wildness of my own speech surprised me. I fell silent, breathing heavily, the shame of the theatre curdling into the more familiar, more profound shame of my own origins. Herbert placed a hand on my shoulder.

His loyalty was a balm, but it could not heal the wound. He did not understand. He had been born a gentleman; he had no forge-smoke to wash away. He could watch Mr. Wopsle’s performance and see only a comedy; I watched it and saw a reflection in a distorted mirror.

We returned to the Temple, and the rest of the night passed in a somber quiet. The attempted distraction had failed; worse, it had sharpened my anxieties, giving them a new and horribly public form. The wait for Tuesday now seemed not only a trial of patience, but a race against time, a desperate hope that I could somehow reconstruct my gentlemanly façade, so badly damaged by the vision of Mr. Wopsle’s black-hosed legs, before I had to present myself to her.

When Monday came, a day of drizzling rain that seemed to weep for my predicament, I found I could not settle at all. I was a caged animal. I believe he is descended from a long line of prize-winning oxen.”

My heart gave a sickening lurch. Bentley Drummle. I knew the man. He was one of our set at Mr. Pocket’s, a heavy, sulky, brutish fellow whom we had nicknamed “the Spider” for his disagreeable habit of lurking in corners and his general air of unpleasantness. The thought of him, of his dull, possessive eyes, being near Estella was a torment.

“You find that amusing?” she asked, for some flicker of my inner agony must have shown on my face.

“He is not a man I would have thought you would… encourage,” I stammered.

“Encourage?” She raised her eyebrows. “My dear boy, I encourage them all. That is my purpose. I am a candle, and they are the moths. Is it my fault they wish to burn their wings?”

She smiled then, a smile of pure, cold triumph, and it was the most beautiful and most terrible thing I had ever seen. She had made me a moth, and I was burning, and she was watching the fire with a detached and lovely curiosity.

We went for a walk in the garden, just the two of us. The air was crisp and cold, and the bare branches of the trees made a delicate black lace against the pale sky. For a few moments, with the house out of sight, I allowed myself to pretend. I pretended we were two ordinary young people, walking in a garden, with a future of ordinary happiness before us. But Estella soon dispelled the illusion.

“Miss Havisham writes to me constantly,” she said, her eyes on the gravel path. “She wishes for a full report. She asks after your progress. She asks if you are suffering.”

The blood ran cold in my veins. “And what do you tell her?”

“I tell her the truth,” she said, stopping to look at a small, hardy winter-rose that was blooming against the wall. “I tell her that you are as foolish as ever. I tell her that your love is a burden to me. I tell her that I have no heart to give you, or anyone else.” She plucked the rose from its stem, her fingers careless of the thorns. “It is a fine weapon she has given me, this beauty. She held the rose to her lips, and for one mad moment I thought of Miss Havisham’s words, “Love her, love her, love her!”, and I felt a wild impulse to seize her in my arms, to tell her that I did not care about her coldness, that I would melt it with the force of my devotion, that my heart was big enough for both of us. But I was Pip, the blacksmith’s boy, and she was Estella, and the distance between us was not merely the space on a gravel path, but a chasm dug by years of careful, cruel instruction.

So I said nothing. I merely watched as she idly pulled the petals from the rose, one by one, and let them fall to the ground. They were little drops of blood on the grey gravel.

“They are all the same,” she said, tossing the ruined stem away. “Mr. Drummle, you, all the others. You all want what you cannot have. It is what makes the game so interesting.”

“It is not a game to me, Estella,” I said, my voice low and thick with a pain that I could no longer conceal.

She turned to look at me, and for an instant, a single, fleeting instant, I thought I saw something different in her eyes. Not pity, not warmth, but something akin to a shared secret, a flicker of understanding of the prison we were both in.

“Then you are the greatest fool of all,” she whispered, and the moment was gone.

We returned to the house, and I stayed for tea. The ritual was an agony. I sat with a porcelain cup in my hand that felt as fragile as my hopes, and I listened to Mrs. Brandley talk about a charity bazaar, and I watched Estella move and speak and breathe, a being of unattainable perfection. She spoke to me very little more, but directed her conversation to Mrs. Brandley’s daughter, who blushed and stammered under the force of her attention. Estella, I saw, could be charming when she chose; it was another tool in her arsenal, and the fact that she did not choose to use it on me was another turn of the screw.

My departure was as stilted as my arrival. I shook Mrs. Brandley’s hand. I bowed to her daughter. I stood before Estella, my hat in my hand, my heart in my throat.

“I shall be in London soon,” she said, as if granting a royal audience. “There is to be a ball. Mrs. Brandley will take me. Perhaps I shall see you there. You must learn to dance, if you do not know how already. Mr. Drummle, for all his faults, is a very forceful dancer.”

It was the final, exquisite twist of the knife. She had not only presented me with a rival, but had made my imagined inadequacy the very reason for his success.

I took her hand, which lay in mine for a moment as cool and lifeless as a marble carving, and then I left. I walked blindly away from the neat, white villa, down the respectable road, my mind a turmoil of shame and longing. I did not wait for the coach, but began to walk, walking as if I could outpace my own thoughts, as if the physical exertion could burn away the humiliation.

The lights of London were beginning to prick the gathering dusk as I finally neared the city, a weary and footsore traveller. But my physical exhaustion was nothing to the exhaustion of my spirit. I had gone seeking a sign, a token of her favour; I had received a lesson in my own insignificance. She had spoken of another man; she had looked upon me with an indifference more wounding than any scorn. The victory, if there had been one, was hers; the defeat, as always, was mine. The blacksmith’s boy had put on a gentleman’s coat, but it was a coat of fire, consuming him from within.

When I at last reached our chambers in the Temple, Herbert took one look at my face and wisely asked no questions. He simply put a glass into my hand and steered me towards a chair by the fire. I sat there for a long time, staring into the flames, seeing her face in the shifting coals. Bentley Drummle. The name was a stone in my shoe, a grit in my eye, a heavy, brutish sound that echoed in the empty chambers of my heart. And yet, the fire of my adoration was not quenched. It was, to my shame and my despair, burning brighter than ever, fed by the very coldness that sought to extinguish it. I loved her. God help me, I loved her. And I would learn to dance.

And so it was that on the following afternoon, having fortified ourselves with a modest lunch, we set out for the rooms of Monsieur Anatole, which were situated in a narrow and somewhat down-at-heel street in the vicinity of Soho; a street that seemed to have given up on any pretensions to gentility and was now dedicated to the housing of foreign artists, exiled patriots, and teachers of obscure accomplishments.

The establishment itself was on the first floor, up a flight of stairs that creaked with a profound melancholy, as if they were weary of the endless procession of clumsy feet that trod upon them. The air on the landing was thick with a faint, sweetish smell of wax and perspiration, and from behind a closed door, we could hear the thin, reedy voice of a solitary violin, scraping its way through a tune with a kind of desperate insistence. Herbert knocked. The violin abruptly ceased.

After a moment, the door was opened by a man so thin and tightly wound that he seemed to be composed entirely of sinew and nerve. “Oui?” he said, the word a sharp, interrogative dart.

“Monsieur Anatole?” Herbert inquired, with his best and most pleasant smile.

“I am he,” the man replied, his eyes flicking from Herbert to me, and lingering on my boots with a look of faint disdain. “You have an appointment?”

“We have come,” said Herbert, undeterred, “to place ourselves under your celebrated tutelage. To become, in short, your pupils.”

A flicker of something—was it interest, or merely the calculation of a tradesman?—passed across Monsieur Anatole’s face. He stood back, making a gesture with one long-fingered hand that was both an invitation and a command. “Entrez,” he said. “Enter. Let me see what material the good Lord has given me to mould.”

We stepped into a long, bare room, the floorboards of which were polished to a dangerous sheen. A row of spindly chairs lined one wall, and the opposite wall was almost entirely consumed by a vast, fly-spotted mirror that reflected our awkward figures with pitiless clarity. Monsieur Anatole directed us to the centre of the room, and then circled us as a butcher might circle a prize steer, his head cocked to one side. I felt my hands grow damp, and I was acutely conscious of my own size, of the space I occupied, of the very sound of my breathing. He stopped in front of me.

“You,” he said, pointing a finger at my chest. “You are the one who needs the help, non? The friend, he has the air of one who can at least stand upright without assistance.”

I felt my cheeks flush. “I—yes,” I stammered. “I wish to learn to dance.”

“To learn to dance,” he repeated slowly, as if the concept were a strange and exotic delicacy he was tasting for the first time. “Everyone wishes to ‘learn to dance’. What they mean is, they wish to not make a fool of themselves in front of a pretty girl. It is the same story, a thousand times. Very well. We begin with the foundation. With the posture.”

He approached me, and with two fingers, delivered a surprisingly forceful poke to my sternum. “The chest, it is out! Like a proud pigeon! For the next hour, I was his creature. All the while, the violin lay silent, for we were not, he declared, within a thousand miles of being ready for the complication of music. Herbert, to his credit, sat on one of the spindly chairs, offering quiet encouragement and managing, with heroic effort, not to laugh.

“Non, non, non!” Monsieur Anatole would cry, clapping his hands to his head in a gesture of theatrical despair. “Monsieur Pip! The leg, it does not bend from the middle! It is not a piece of boiled asparagus! It is a column! A pillar of the temple! Think of yourself as a Greek god!”

I tried to think of myself as a Greek god, but I succeeded only in feeling like a particularly clumsy mortal who had wandered into the wrong pantheon. My own limbs, which had served me tolerably well in the coarse pursuits of the forge and the marsh, seemed now to have been replaced by a set of ill-fitting wooden appendages, each with a will of its own, and a will that was in direct and malicious opposition to the dictates of Monsieur Anatole’s sharp commands. The vast, spotted mirror threw back at me an image of concentrated misery and awkwardness; a lanky, red-faced young man whose coat did not seem to hang right and whose feet were implements of chaos. “Attention!” shrieked Monsieur Anatole, and I was jolted back to the grim reality of the dusty room and my own profound incompetence.

When at last the lesson was over, and I had paid Monsieur Anatole a sum that seemed to me quite astronomical for the privilege of being so thoroughly humiliated, we were released back into the grimy London air. Herbert was kind enough to say that he had seen a marked improvement in my posture, but I knew the truth. I walked with my head down, the Frenchman’s criticisms echoing in my ears, a litany of my own failings.

“Pip,” Herbert said gently, as we turned into a broader, more respectable thoroughfare where the gas lamps were just beginning to be lit, casting a greasy yellow glow on the pavement. “It is only the first lesson. Rome was not built in a day, you know. And I daresay Romulus was a clumsy dancer at first.”

But it was no mystery to me. I knew, with a dreadful certainty, exactly what she saw. He was of her world. He was a gentleman born and bred, not a clumsy imitation cobbled together with a benefactor’s money and a French dancing master’s despairing instructions. His arrogance was not the brittle shield of an insecure boy, but the solid, unthinking assumption of a man who had never been told he was anything less than his own estimation of himself. Estella, who had been trained to break hearts, would see in his brutishness a kind of strength, a force to be matched or mastered, a far more interesting challenge than the pathetic adoration of a blacksmith’s boy who trembled when she looked at him.

The knowledge was a cold weight in my stomach, heavier than any of my recent defeats. My humiliation at the hands of Trabb’s boy, my ineptitude in the dancing studio; these were but symptoms of the greater, incurable disease of my own origins. I could put on a gentleman’s coat, I could learn a gentleman’s steps, but the soul of the creature was a thing I could not acquire. I was an actor, playing a part for which I had not the talent, on a stage that was altogether too grand for me.

We walked the rest of the way back to the Temple in silence. The cheerful fire awaited us, and the familiar, comforting clutter of our chambers, but I could find no solace in them. I sat in my chair, not as one resting after a day’s labour, but as one who has received a mortal wound and is merely waiting for the bleeding to stop. Herbert, with his infinite tact, busied himself with making tea, the clatter of the cups and saucers a small, domestic sound in the vast silence of my despair.

He brought me a cup, and as I took it, my hand shook so that the liquid slopped over into the saucer. “Pip,” he said, his voice full of a concern that I felt I did not deserve. “Do not let them win. Do not let Drummle, or Estella’s coldness, or even a tyrannical Frenchman with a moustache, defeat you. You are a better man than he is, a thousand times over.”

I looked up at my friend, at his earnest, kind face, and I felt a surge of something that was half gratitude, half shame. He believed in me, with a simple faith that I could not begin to muster for myself. He saw a gentleman of worth and feeling, where I saw only a fraud.

“It is not about being a better man, Herbert,” I said, my voice hoarse. “It is about being the right man. And I am not he.”

“Then you must become him,” Herbert said, with a surprising firmness. “Or rather, you must show her the man you already are. The battle is not lost, Pip. It has merely begun. Now, drink your tea. We have another lesson tomorrow.”

The music stopped. The purple turban was still. I had not merely failed to dance; I had actively assaulted the very source of the music itself.

I was hauled to my feet by Herbert and a surprisingly strong Monsieur Anatole. Monsieur Alphonse was helped to a chair, where he sat cradling his wounded instrument and muttering what sounded like French curses.

“It is enough,” Monsieur Anatole said, his voice quiet with a terrible finality. He looked at me, not with anger, but with a kind of profound, philosophical pity, as one might look upon a creature born without some vital sense. “For today, it is enough. We can do no more.”

The lesson was over. I paid for the damage to the fiddle, a sum that made me wince, and fled the studio without a backward glance, the silent judgment of the purple turban burning a hole in my back. I could not speak. The shame was a physical thing, a foul taste in my mouth. My grand poem to Estella had ended in a calamitous blot of ink and a broken fiddle. We walked back to the Temple in a miserable silence, the grey London sky a perfect mirror of my soul. I was a fraud, a clumsy imposter, and the universe, in the form of a small, tyrannical Frenchman, had found me out.

Upon our arrival at Garden Court, we found a letter awaiting me on the table. It was not a bill, nor was it a note from a tradesman. It was enclosed in a crisp, business-like envelope, and the hand was the dry, angular script of Mr. Wemmick. A summons to Little Britain was never a thing to be taken lightly; it usually presaged some new direction from my unseen benefactor, some fresh turning of the wheel of my fortune, and my heart gave a little lurch of apprehension. The rest of the day I passed in a state of low-grade misery, punctuated by Herbert’s valiant attempts to cheer me. He proposed we go to the theatre; I pictured myself tripping in the aisle. My every thought was infected with the virus of my own clumsiness, a contagion for which there appeared to be no cure.

The next morning, I made my way to Little Britain with the familiar feeling of a man approaching a dentist. The offices of Mr. Jaggers had their usual air of grim and dusty consequence, the very light that filtered through the grimy windows seeming to be composed of legal precedents and human sorrow. The death-masks on the shelf looked down with their impassive plaster eyes, and the clients waiting on the hard benches looked up with the anxious, hunted expressions of mice in a room full of cats.

I was shown directly into Wemmick’s own small office, a cramped space that was a fortress of legal documents and personal oddities. Wemmick himself was at his desk, polishing one of his rings with a scrap of chamois leather, his mouth set in its customary post-box slit.

“Ah, Mr. Pip,” he said, looking up without a flicker of surprise, as if I had been standing there for an hour. “On the dot. Punctuality is a valuable piece of portable property. Take a seat, if you can find one.”

I cleared a space for myself on a chair already laden with deed-boxes, and waited. Wemmick finished his polishing, held the ring up to the light, and then tucked the chamois away.

“The business is brief,” he said, in his dry, official tone. “I am instructed by Mr. Jaggers to inform you of a new disposition. The quarterly allowance you have been receiving is to be augmented. I stammered my thanks, my mind reeling. This sudden increase in my expectations was so vast, so unexpected, that it felt less like a gift and more like a burden. It was another layer of costume, another heavy piece of scenery in the grand play of my new life.

“That is the official matter,” Wemmick continued, leaning back in his chair and tapping his teeth with a pen. “Now, for a bit of news. Off the record, you understand. A matter of general interest.”

I leaned forward, my senses suddenly sharp. Wemmick’s “off the record” news often held more weight than his official pronouncements.

“A certain party,” he began, glancing at the door as if to ensure it was shut, “a young lady, in fact, has been brought to town. The ward of our mutual acquaintance at Satis House.”

My heart, which had been stumbling over the news of my allowance, now stopped altogether for a moment, before commencing to beat with a painful, frantic rhythm, like the wings of a trapped bird. Estella. She was in London.

“Placed under the care of a lady of society,” Wemmick went on, examining the nib of his pen with great interest. “A Mrs. Brandley. A widow of good standing, though limited means. They are to reside in Richmond. The idea is to introduce the young lady to the world. A finishing, you might say.”

Richmond. The name sounded in my ears like a trumpet call. It was a real place, a place on a map, a place to which a man could travel. It was not a dream-castle in the mists of Kent, but a tangible destination, a suburb of the very city in which I now stood. She was here. She was near. All my vague and hopeless longings, all my agonized striving, suddenly had a focal point.

“Richmond,” I repeated, my voice a stupid croak.

“Just so,” said Wemmick. “A very pleasant spot. Green. Trees. The river. A suitable backdrop for a young lady of property. And for those who might wish to, ah, pay their respects.” He gave me a quick, bird-like glance from under his eyelids, a look that contained not an ounce of sentiment, but a universe of worldly understanding. It was as if he had said, “There is the prize; the race is now open.”

I felt a dizzying mixture of elation and sheer, gut-wrenching terror. My grand ambitions, which had seemed so abstract, so much a thing of the distant future, were now terrifyingly imminent. I was to be launched into society, the same society as Estella, with a gentleman’s income but with the grace of a collapsing scarecrow. The image of the broken fiddle rose before my eyes, a symbol of my utter unreadiness. I was to meet her, to speak with her, perhaps even to dance with her, and I had just yesterday proven myself to be a menace to musical instruments and a disgrace to the human form.

“I see,” I said, struggling to arrange my features into an expression of gentlemanly composure. “Thank you, Wemmick. That is… interesting news.”

“Thought it might be,” he said, already turning his attention to a stack of papers and effectively dismissing me. “Mind how you go, Mr. Pip. The world is full of sharp edges.”

I stumbled out of his office and back into the grim corridor, my mind a whirlwind. Richmond! Estella in Richmond! The clients on the benches seemed to blur before my eyes; the sounds of the street outside were a dull roar. The increase in my fortune, which should have been a cause for celebration, now felt like a terrible sort of joke. I was being given all the tools to win her, except for the one I needed most: the ability to be a man she might not despise.

My feet carried me away from Little Britain, though I knew not where I was going. I walked for hours, through a labyrinth of streets, my thoughts in turmoil. My love for her was a fire in my chest, but my fear of my own inadequacy was a block of ice around it. I saw her face before me, beautiful and scornful. I heard again the cry of “Non, non, non!” and it was no longer the voice of a French dancing-master, but the voice of my own destiny, pronouncing judgment. The battle, I had told myself, had merely begun. But now, with the enemy’s fortress in sight, I felt less like a brave soldier and more like a fool who had been sent to war armed with a broken fiddle. And yet, I would go. I had to go. My fate, it seemed, was to charge headlong towards my own humiliation, all for the love of a star that was, and would likely always be, impossibly far away.

My feet, having no better instruction, had at last carried me back to the familiar soot-stained portal of Barnard’s Inn, where I ascended the rickety stairs with the heavy tread of a man returning from a military defeat. The very air in our chambers, which usually smelt of Herbert’s cheerful disarray and the faint, ever-present hint of London grime, seemed now to be thick with the dust of my recent humiliation. I found Herbert himself engaged in a financial calculation of such labyrinthine complexity, involving the projected profits of an imaginary shipping concern, that he did not at first notice my entrance. He sat with his hair even more whimsically ruffled than usual, looking with a kind of hopeful despair at a long column of figures that refused, I could see, to balance.

“Herbert,” I said, and my voice came out with a hollow sound, as if from the bottom of a well.

He looked up, his pen poised, his expression of commercial anxiety instantly replaced by one of friendly concern. “Pip! My dear fellow! You look as if you have seen a ghost. Or, what is worse, as if you have had dealings with Pumblechook.”

“Worse than Pumblechook, Herbert,” I said, sinking into a chair. “Far worse. I have been in the presence of Trabb’s boy.”

Herbert winced in sympathy, for he had heard the tale of my previous encounter. “Ah. Say no more. But there is a look about you, Pip, that speaks of more than just a miscreant apprentice. You have news.”

And so, I told him. I recounted my interview with Mr. Jaggers, the abrupt dismissal of Orlick, which now seemed a trivial affair from a distant past, and then the stunning intelligence delivered by Wemmick. I spoke of Richmond; I spoke of the house taken for her; I spoke of my increased allowance, which I presented not as a boon but as an instrument of my own impending torture. I spoke, in short, of Estella.

As I spoke her name, Herbert’s face underwent a remarkable transformation. The financial woes of his imaginary shipping empire vanished. His eyes lit up, his posture straightened, and he assumed the air of a general planning a brilliant campaign.

“Pip!” he cried, casting his ledger aside with a magnificent gesture that sent a flurry of papers scattering to the floor. “This is it! This is the moment! Richmond! Why, it is the most capital of situations. Not so far as to be an exile, yet far enough from the smoke and din to be fashionable. A house of her own, so to speak! You will call upon her. You will be seen with her. It is a campaign, Pip, a positive campaign, and we must lay our plans with the utmost strategic care.”

“Plans, Herbert?” I echoed miserably. “What plans can be laid? I am to go to Richmond and show myself for what I am; a clumsy, awkward boy who will only disgrace her. Did I not tell you of the dancing-master? Did I not prove myself unfit for civilised motion? What am I to do when I am there? Stand in a corner and pray she does not notice me? She will notice me only to be ashamed of me.”

“Nonsense,” said Herbert, with a confidence I could only marvel at. “You are not the same boy. You are a gentleman of property. You have merely been neglecting the, ah, the practical application of your station. What you require, Pip, is not less confidence, but more accomplishments. You must acquire the necessary polish.”

“Polish?” I asked, thinking of my coarse hands and heavy boots, which no amount of blacking seemed to render truly elegant.

“Precisely. You must ride, for instance. A gentleman must be seen on horseback. It is an essential component of the fashionable life. To be seen riding in the park, to arrive at a country house on a fine mount; it speaks volumes, Pip, volumes!”

The thought of myself on horseback was almost as terrifying as the thought of myself on the dance floor. I had a dim memory of being placed upon a cart-horse once at the forge, and the world had seemed a perilous and unstable place from that great height. “I cannot ride, Herbert,” I said flatly.

“You will learn!” he declared. “I have some little knowledge of the matter myself. We shall procure you a suitable horse. Nothing too spirited, of course. A steady, reliable, gentlemanly beast. It will be the first step in our Richmond campaign. We shall go tomorrow.”

His enthusiasm was so infectious, so pure and unalloyed by the sort of practical doubts that plagued me, that I found myself, against my better judgment, agreeing. The image he painted, of a dashing and self-assured Pip on a noble steed, was so far removed from my own self-conception that it had the allure of pure fantasy. And so it was settled.

The next day, Herbert, full of the wisdom of a man who had once ridden a pony at his grandfather’s estate, accompanied me to a mews tucked away behind a row of grander houses. It was a place of astonishing noise and odour; a symphony of whinnies and stamping hooves, overlaid with the pungent aroma of hay and manure and wet leather. The man who sold us the horse, a portly individual with a nose so inflamed by drink that it seemed a beacon of warning against his own character, spoke of the animal’s virtues in a language so poetic and profuse that it quite overwhelmed our meagre knowledge of equine matters, leaving us in a state of bewildered admiration before we had even considered the question of price.

“Here he is, gentlemen,” said the dealer, whose name was Mr. Grimmer, leading out a tall, bony, bay-coloured horse. “Noble, I calls him. And noble by nature he is. The spirit of a lion, but the gentleness of a lamb. Why, a child could ride him. Look at that eye. That’s an honest eye, that is. Not a trace of vice in him.”

The horse, Noble, did indeed have a large, liquid brown eye. It looked at me with an expression of profound and philosophical melancholy, as if it were contemplating the sorrows of the world. Its coat shone, its legs seemed sturdy enough, and when Herbert, with a great show of expertise, ran a hand down its flank, it did not so much as flinch.

“He seems a fine animal,” Herbert pronounced, looking at me for confirmation.

I, who knew as much about horses as I did about the Chaldean language, could only nod in mute agreement. The horse seemed impressive. It seemed like the sort of horse a gentleman would own. And so, after a brief and utterly one-sided negotiation in which Mr. Grimmer seemed to be bargaining with himself for our benefit, a significant portion of my new allowance was exchanged for the ownership of Noble, along with a saddle and bridle of dubious vintage.

Our first attempt at what Herbert called “mastering the equestrian arts” took place in a small, dusty park not far from our lodgings. It took the combined efforts of myself, Herbert, and a passing baker’s boy to persuade Noble to allow me onto his back. Once I was seated, the world did indeed feel, as I had remembered, a very high and precarious place.

“Now, Pip,” instructed Herbert, standing back to admire the effect, which was, I felt certain, more comical than impressive. “Hold the reins lightly but firmly. A gentleman’s hands are his primary means of communication with his mount. A gentle pressure on the left rein to turn left, on the right to turn right. A slight pressure with the knees to encourage forward motion.”

I applied a slight pressure with my knees. Nothing happened. Noble stood as still as a statue, his melancholic eye fixed on some distant point, lost, it seemed, in a private reverie.

“A little more pressure, perhaps,” suggested Herbert. “And a confident word of encouragement.”

“Go on, horse,” I said, feeling profoundly foolish. I applied more pressure. I might as well have been squeezing a stone wall. Noble let out a long, weary sigh, a sound that seemed to express his utter contempt for the entire proceeding.

A small crowd of street urchins, who had materialised from nowhere as they always do when a spectacle of potential humiliation is afoot, began to offer their own advice.

“Give ’im a kick, mister!” one shouted.

“’E’s ’avin a kip, ’e is!” yelled another.

Feeling my cheeks burn with a heat that had nothing to do with the morning sun, I gave a more vigorous squeeze with my knees and a desperate tug on the reins. The effect was instantaneous and not at all what I had intended. Noble, roused from his philosophical state, seemed to take personal offence at my clumsy command. His head shot down, his back arched like a drawn bow, and I was launched into the air with a force that was truly astonishing. I landed in a heap on the dusty ground, my hat flying one way and my dignity flying the other, to the immense delight of my small audience.

Noble, for his part, having unburdened himself of his rider, took two or three jaunty steps, shook his head as if to clear it of a bad thought, and then began placidly cropping the thin grass, his moment of rebellion apparently forgotten.

Herbert rushed to my side. “Pip! Are you hurt?”

“Only in my soul, Herbert,” I groaned, dusting myself off.

The scene was repeated, with variations, over the next few days. I learned that Noble had, in fact, two moods: the first was a state of immovable contemplation, and the second was a state of sudden, violent objection to being ridden. I was ejected into bushes; I was deposited unceremoniously into puddles; on one memorable occasion, he simply lay down in the middle of the road and refused to get up, leaving me stranded atop him like a shipwrecked mariner on a rock. Each attempt was a fresh chapter in the book of my own inadequacy, a public performance of my fundamental unsuitability for the role I was trying to play. The campaign for Richmond, it seemed, was beginning with a rout.

It was in the midst of this equestrian misery that the summons finally came. A crisp, cream-coloured envelope was delivered by a messenger, bearing not the spidery script of Miss Havisham, but an elegant, flowing hand that was unknown to me. Inside, a card was enclosed. Mrs. Brandley, it stated, the lady with whom Estella was lodging, requested the pleasure of Mr. Pip’s company for tea on the following Tuesday.

I held the card in my hand, and it felt as heavy as a stone. All my failures, my awkwardness, my clumsy attempts at gentlemanly polish, seemed to culminate in that single piece of pasteboard. The broken fiddle, the mockery of Trabb’s boy, the obstinate refusal of my horse Noble to be anything other than a four-legged instrument of my humiliation; they all rushed back to me. I was not ready. I would never be ready.

“Well, Pip,” said Herbert, who had read the card over my shoulder. “The trumpet sounds. You must answer the call.”

And so, on the appointed Tuesday, I found myself in a hired coach, rattling along the road to Richmond. I had abandoned all thought of arriving on Noble; the risk of being seen walking the last two miles after he had inevitably deposited me in a ditch was too great. I wore my best clothes, my hands were scrubbed clean, my boots were polished to a mirror-like shine. I had rehearsed several neutral topics of conversation with Herbert, involving the weather, the latest poetry, and the undeniable beauty of the Thames valley. Yet beneath this fragile veneer of preparation, my heart was a frantic drum, beating out a rhythm of pure, unadulterated terror.

The house was not at all like Satis House. Where that had been a tomb of decay and darkness, this was a vision of light and air. It was a white villa, surrounded by a lawn of impossible greenness, with roses climbing its walls and windows that glittered in the afternoon sun. It was a place of serene, conventional beauty, and I hated it at once, for it seemed a setting in which my own rough-hewn nature would stand out in even starker relief.

I was shown into a drawing-room that looked out over the river. The air was filled with the scent of flowers and the murmur of polite conversation. And there she was.

She was standing by the window, talking to a stout, florid young man with an air of immense self-satisfaction. She was more beautiful than I had ever seen her, more beautiful than I had even remembered. She had changed; she was no longer the girl I had played with, nor the imperious young woman I had last seen at Miss Havisham’s. She was a lady, poised and finished and breathtakingly remote. The jewels Miss Havisham had once put in her hair now seemed not a fantastic adornment, but her natural right.

She saw me enter, and for a moment her conversation faltered. She gave me a cool, measured look, a look that took in my entire person, from my carefully arranged hair to my polished boots. There was not a flicker of warmth in it, not a hint of shared history, only a calm, dispassionate assessment. Then she turned to the woman beside her, a kindly looking lady in grey silk whom I took to be Mrs. Brandley.

“Mrs. Brandley,” she said, her voice as clear and cold as winter bells. “Here is Mr. Pip.”

Mrs. Brandley greeted me with a gentle kindness that was almost more unnerving than open hostility. She introduced me to the florid young man, whose name was Mr. Flintham and who acknowledged me with a curt nod that was a masterpiece of condescension. And then, I was left standing before Estella.

“You have come, then,” she said. It was not a question.

“I received your invitation,” I said, my rehearsed pleasantries about the weather fleeing from my mind. “Richmond is very beautiful.”

“It is green,” she said, with a slight shrug. “Miss Havisham wished me to come into the world of society. So here I am. And you, it seems, are to be a part of it. How very strange our lives are.”

“Estella,” I began, a thousand foolish, heartfelt things rising to my lips.

“You will find a great many people here you do not know,” she interrupted, her eyes scanning the room as if searching for a more interesting diversion. “I am to be admired, you see. That is my purpose. Mrs. Brandley has a list of suitable young men who are to be given the opportunity to do so. Mr. Flintham is one of them. He has, I am told, a great deal of money and very few ideas, which is considered an excellent combination.”

The cruelty was so matter-of-fact, so utterly devoid of passion, that it left me speechless. She was not trying to wound me; she was merely stating the facts of her existence, facts in which I played a small and insignificant part.

“She wishes you to see me in this new life,” Estella continued, her voice lower, a private communication in a public room. “She wishes you to see what you can never have. Are you not tired of the game, Pip? I am. I have been taught my lesson very well. I have no heart, you know. You should remember that.”

She looked down at my hands, which I had clasped together in my nervousness. “You have been trying to become a gentleman,” she observed, a faint, almost imperceptible smile touching her lips. “Your hands are cleaner. But they are still the hands of a blacksmith’s boy. Just as mine,” and here her eyes grew distant, and for a fleeting instant I saw the shadow of Satis House in them, “are the hands of a creature made of ice. We are what we were made, you and I.”

With that, she turned away from me as smoothly as if we had never spoken, and rejoined Mr. Flintham, leaving me standing alone in the middle of the bright, sunlit room. I felt the eyes of the other guests upon me, a poor riddle, as Mr. Jaggers might have said, that they had found out long ago. The scent of the roses was suffocating. My grand campaign, so bravely planned by Herbert, had ended before the first battle had even been joined; it had ended with a quiet word and a look of dismissal. And I knew, with a certainty that was as cold and hard as the jewels on her breast, that though I would obey Miss Havisham’s mad command and love her, love her, love her, my love was nothing more than another chain she was doomed to wear, and I, the blacksmith’s boy, was the one who had forged it.

And so I went on, tossed between the memory of a boy’s mockery and the torment of a man’s love, until London, with its smoky breath and rumbling voice, swallowed us up once more. The days that followed were of a singular character, a mixture of feverish anticipation and dull, grinding study. I awaited some word, some sign from Richmond, a summons that would draw me back into her orbit. I pictured our next meeting, rehearsing what I would say, how I would conduct myself with a cool dignity that would at last command her respect, if not her affection. Herbert, with his unfailing good nature, abetted me in these fancies. He devised what he called a ‘campaign,’ a series of strategic social manoeuvres by which I was to lay siege to Estella’s heart, a plan that took no account of her repeated declarations that no such fortress existed to be conquered.

“You see, my dear Handel,” he would say, pacing our small room at Barnard’s Inn and sketching fortifications in the air with his finger, “the problem is one of approach. You have hitherto advanced upon a broad front, displaying your affections openly. This is a tactical error. We must employ subtlety, a certain gentlemanly reserve. Let her see you in the company of others; let her observe your wit, your growing consequence in the world. We must make you an object of curiosity!”

That I had no wit to speak of, and no consequence save that which was bought with the mysterious shillings of my benefactor, seemed to him a minor logistical detail. He drew up lists of places we should be seen: the theatre, certain public gardens, a fencing exhibition. He even coached me in the art of the indifferent bow and the casual, world-weary sigh, as if I were burdened by a surfeit of social engagements. I, in my desperation, was a willing pupil, and for a week or two, I threw myself into this new character, a man of fashion and mystery, with all the clumsy ardour of an amateur actor in his first part.

It was in the midst of this grand campaign, on a fine, bright afternoon, that the long-awaited summons arrived. It was not a delicate, perfumed note from Estella, however, but a dry, business-like letter from Mrs. Brandley, the lady with whom Estella was lodging. It stated, with a decided lack of ceremony, that Miss Havisham had expressed a wish for me to call upon Estella at Richmond, and that I was to present myself the following day.

Herbert was ecstatic. “The first shot has been fired!” he declared, clapping me on the shoulder. “She has noted your absence, Handel! She has become curious! The strategy is a triumph!”

I was not so certain. The tone of the letter was cold, an instruction rather than an invitation, and it bore the unmistakable imprint of Miss Havisham’s puppeteering hand. Nevertheless, a wild hope, an unquenchable and foolish hope, surged within me. I spent the remainder of the day in a state of nervous preparation, selecting my finest waistcoat, purchasing a new pair of gloves, and rehearsing yet again those casual, witty remarks that Herbert assured me would prove irresistible.

The next day, I presented myself at the house in Richmond. It was a handsome villa, set back from the road behind a screen of impossibly green trees, with wide windows that shone in the sun and gardens that spilled over with roses. A maid showed me into a bright, airy drawing-room where a number of people were already gathered, their polite chatter mixing with the clink of teacups. For a moment, I was bewildered, having expected a private audience, but then I saw her, and the rest of the room dissolved into a meaningless blur.

She was standing by a window, talking to a stout, red-faced young man whose chief attribute seemed to be a look of immense, bovine self-satisfaction. She was more beautiful than I had ever seen her. It was not the strange, starlit beauty of Satis House, but something new and dazzling, a creature of sunlight and society. She wore a dress of a pale green that seemed to capture the light, and there were no jewels in her hair now, no fantastic adornments, only her own proud loveliness, which was more potent than any gem. I felt my carefully constructed persona, the man of fashion and mystery, crumble into dust. I was once more the coarse, common boy gaping at a star.

She saw me. Her conversation did not falter, but her eyes met mine across the room, and she gave me the smallest, most fractional of nods, a recognition so slight it might have been imagined. I stood, fixed to the spot, my heart hammering against my ribs, waiting for her to come to me, or to beckon me over. She did neither. She turned back to the red-faced young man, whose name I later learned was Flintham, and laughed at something he said.

It was Mrs. Brandley, a thin, anxious-looking woman with the air of a professional chaperone, who eventually rescued me. She fluttered over, introduced herself, and made a great show of finding me a cup of tea, all the while keeping a watchful eye on Estella, as a jeweller might watch his most valuable diamond. It was some minutes before Estella, having at last disengaged herself from Mr. Flintham, glided towards us.

“So you have come, Pip,” she said, her voice as cool and clear as the day. It was the first time she had used my name in what felt like an eternity, and the sound of it on her lips was a shock, both sweet and painful. There was no warmth in it, merely an acknowledgment of a fact. I muttered something in reply, I know not what. I felt my face flush, and was horribly conscious of my new gloves, which suddenly seemed clumsy and ill-fitting.

“Miss Havisham is determined that we shall play out our parts,” she continued, her gaze sweeping over me with a dispassionate appraisal that took in my new waistcoat and my polished boots and found them, I felt, entirely unimpressive. “She wished you to see me like this. In my new element.” She made a small, elegant gesture that encompassed the sunlit room, the polite society, Mr. Flintham staring at her from across the room as if she were a prize mare.

“Estella,” I began, my voice thick with all the things I had rehearsed and all the things I had not, “I had hoped…”

“Hope?” she interrupted, and a flicker of something, not quite amusement, but a kind of weary irony, crossed her face. “That is a word we should leave to others. It has no place in our story. Look at them, Pip.” Her eyes moved over the other young men in the room. “They hope. They hope for a smile, a dance, a word. They hope for my fortune. Mr. Drummle, I believe, is coming later. He hopes most of all. He is quite determined.”

The name fell into the conversation like a stone into a still pond. Bentley Drummle. The Spider. The boorish, sulky brute from my guardian’s house. A cold dread, a feeling of utter futility, washed over me. Of all the men in the world, it was he, the one I most despised, who was to be my chief rival. It was a stroke of cruelty so perfect it could only have been devised by Miss Havisham.

“You are to be part of the collection,” Estella said, her voice dropping to a lower, more confiding tone, the tone she had used once or twice at Satis House, which always drew me in even as it warned me away. “You are to be the one who watches. The one who is reminded of what he cannot have. That is your role. Does it not entertain you?”

Before I could answer, before I could give voice to the protest and the pain that were choking me, she gave me a smile, a brilliant, public smile that was meant for the whole room to see. “You must excuse me,” she said, her voice once again light and social. “Mrs. Brandley has a list of gentlemen I am required to charm. It is a very long list.”

And with that, she turned and left me. She did not glide away this time; she moved with a purpose, a queen returning to her court, and I saw her take the arm of another young man, a handsome, empty-faced fellow in a blue coat, and allow him to lead her towards the French windows that opened onto the garden. I was left standing alone, a cup of cold tea in my hand, the very picture of a poor relation at a wedding feast. The grand campaign was over. My carefully planned siege had been broken not by a great battle, but by a single, effortless sally on her part, which had left my forces scattered and my spirit in full retreat.

I do not know how I got out of that house. I have a vague memory of muttering some excuse to a flustered Mrs. Brandley, of nearly stumbling over a footstool, of escaping into the blessed anonymity of the open air. I did not take a coach back to London. I walked. I walked for miles, blindly, with no destination in mind but to put as much distance as possible between myself and that sunlit room where my heart had been so coolly and thoroughly dissected.

My mind was a torment of warring impulses. I hated her. I hated her coldness, her calculated cruelty, the way she used her beauty as a weapon. I hated Miss Havisham for having forged that weapon, for having twisted a human soul into this terrible, beautiful shape. I hated Bentley Drummle, with his loutish arrogance, and Mr. Flintham, with his bovine stare. I hated myself, for my foolishness, for my persistence, for the blacksmith’s blood that I felt was still on my hands, no matter how much I scrubbed them. And yet, beneath it all, stronger than all of it, was the hopeless, maddening command: “Love her, love her, love her!” I saw her smile, not the cold, public one, but the flicker of weary irony that had crossed her face before, and in my wretchedness, I clung to that one sliver of shared understanding, that one moment in which she had acknowledged the strangeness of our linked fates.

It was late when I finally found my way back to the familiar grime of Barnard’s Inn. The lamps were lit, and the clamour of the city had subsided into a low, persistent hum. I climbed the dark, creaking stairs to our rooms, feeling as if I had aged ten years since morning. A light was showing under our door, and I knew Herbert would be within, waiting for his triumphant general to return with news of victory. The thought of his cheerful, expectant face was almost more than I could bear.

I pushed open the door. He was sitting at our table, a book open before him, though he was not reading. He looked up as I entered, and his face broke into a wide, welcoming smile.

“Handel, my dear boy! You are back! I have been on tenterhooks. Well? How went the assault? Did you deploy the indifferent bow? Did she remark upon it? Tell me everything!”

I threw my hat onto a chair and sank down opposite him, the last of my strength deserting me. I could not meet his eye. I stared at the flickering candle, at the guttering wax that seemed to be weeping for me.

“It is no use, Herbert,” I said, and my voice was a dead, flat thing. “It is all no use.”

His smile faded, replaced by a look of profound concern. He leaned forward, his elbows on the table. “What is it? What happened? Was she not there?”

“She was there,” I said. I proceeded to give him a full and miserable account of the afternoon’s proceedings. I spared him nothing. I recounted her words, her looks, the arrival of Mr. Flintham, the mention of Bentley Drummle, the final, dismissive smile. I described the feeling of being an exhibit in a collection, a piece of the grim game set up for Miss Havisham’s spectral amusement.

When I had finished, there was a long silence, broken only by the sighing of the wind in the chimney. Herbert did not offer any of his usual optimistic pronouncements. He did not suggest a new strategy or a revised plan of campaign. He simply sat there, his good, honest face filled with a sympathy that was more comforting than any words could have been.

“The Spider,” he said at last, his voice low with disgust. “That she should even have to be in the same room with such a creature. It is monstrous.”

“He is to be there often,” I said. “She told me. He is ‘quite determined’.”

“And she, Handel?” Herbert asked gently. “What did you make of her? Truly?”

“She is what she said she is,” I answered, looking up at him at last. “She told me once that she had no heart. I did not believe her. I thought it was a fancy, a thing Miss Havisham had put into her head. But it is true, Herbert. There is nothing there. It is like a beautiful house with all the rooms empty. And I am doomed to stand outside, knocking on a door that will never be answered.” I felt a familiar welling behind my eyes, the ghost of those tears Joe had once brought forth, but they would not fall. I had gone beyond that.

Herbert reached across the table and laid a hand on my arm. “My poor, dear Handel,” he said. “What a cruel fate. To be given a fortune, only to have it tied to such a misery.”

We sat for some time in that gloomy fellowship. Then, with a sigh, Herbert pushed his chair back and went to the little escritoire where we kept our accounts. He brought back a small, dog-eared ledger and opened it on the table between us.

“There is another matter, Handel,” he said, his tone hesitant. “I did not wish to broach it tonight, of all nights, but it cannot be helped. I have been looking at the book.”

I looked down at the columns of figures, the spidery lists of expenditures for dinners, for clothes, for lessons in this and that, for all the gentlemanly accoutrements that had failed so spectacularly to make me a gentleman in her eyes.

“We are getting deeper into the mire,” Herbert said quietly, pointing a finger at the final sum. “My own small prospects are, as you know, nebulous at best, and your allowance, generous as it is, does not seem to stretch as far as it once did. We are spending, Handel. We are spending a prodigious amount.”

I stared at the figures, which seemed to mock me from the page. Each shilling was a testament to my folly, a monument to a hope that had been rotten from the start. The cost of my new gloves, the price of the waistcoat, the fee for the coach I had been too proud and too miserable to take, it was all there, a tally of my failure. The grand, romantic tragedy of my love for Estella was suddenly entangled with the sordid, common comedy of debt.

“It does not matter,” I said, with a recklessness born of despair. “Let us spend. Let us be ruined. What difference does it make?”

“It makes a great deal of difference, my dear fellow,” Herbert replied, with a sad smile. “For we must eat. And even a gentleman in the midst of a tragic passion requires a roof over his head. We must be more careful, Handel. We must pull in.”

I said nothing. I only stared at the ledger, at the cold, hard arithmetic of my life. I was a blacksmith’s boy who had been commanded to love a star, and who, in the process, was squandering a fortune he had not earned, on a life he could not lead, for a prize he could never win. And as I sat there in the dim candlelight of our shabby room, with the murmur of London all around me, I felt a new and terrible kind of chain settle upon me; not the hot chain of my love, but the cold, heavy iron of debt, forged by my own vanity and despair. I was trapped, and I saw no way out.

He spoke as if we were generals planning a campaign, but I felt more like a condemned prisoner cataloguing the instruments of his own execution. Nevertheless, I submitted. We drew our chairs closer to the table, and with that damning ledger as our guide, and with a fresh sheet of paper that seemed far too clean and hopeful for the task at hand, we began our melancholy accounting. We sat late into the night, the candle burning low in its socket, the sounds of London fading to a distant hum. Herbert, with a furrowed brow, would call out an item, “To the tobacconist, one pound, three shillings,” or “For the hire of a horse to Richmond,” and I, with a sinking heart, would add it to the column of our ruin. The sums mounted with a terrifying speed. The cost of my membership at the Finches of the Grove, that society of useless and expensive young men whom I now regarded with a loathing I had previously reserved for Orlick; the price of dinners I could not remember eating, for wines whose flavour was lost on me, for gloves and boots and jewelled pins that were all part of the costume for a part I had failed so miserably to play. It was a long and sorry litany of my vanity.

By the time we were done, and had calculated a final, staggering total at the bottom of the page, the grey light of a new day was creeping through the windows of our chambers in Barnard’s Inn. The figure on the paper seemed to stare up at us, a Cyclopean eye of debt, and I felt a weariness so profound it was as if I had been physically labouring at the forge for a week. Herbert, ever practical, devised a plan of severe and immediate retrenchment. We would dine on the plainest fare; we would dismiss the boy who cleaned our boots; we would cease all entertainments; we would walk everywhere, regardless of the distance or the weather. My own private resolution was a far more bitter one; I would not go near the places where I might see Drummle, I would not torment myself with the society in which Estella now moved. My period of being a young gentleman about town was over.

It was in this new and sober frame of mind that I received, some two days later, a curt and peremptory note, delivered by a messenger whose very knock at the door sent a jolt of alarm through me, for I had begun to suspect a bailiff in every stranger. The note was from Mr. Jaggers’s clerk, and it requested my attendance in Little Britain at eleven o’alock the following morning. There was no reason given, no word of explanation, only the time and the place, written in a spidery, precise hand that was the very picture of inexorable business.

My first thought, a cold and sickly one, was that my benefactor, whomever he might be, had discovered the state of my affairs and was about to cut me off entirely. The very ground, which had already shifted so calamitously beneath my feet, seemed to tremble anew. I showed the note to Herbert, who read it with a thoughtful air.

“It may be nothing, Handel,” he said, though he did not sound convinced. “A matter of papers to be signed. A quarterly review of your progress, perhaps.”

But we both knew my progress had been of a kind not easily reviewed with satisfaction. The journey to Little Britain the next morning was a walk of profound anxiety. I felt as if I were going to a sentencing. The sun was out, and the streets were full of a cheerful, bustling life that seemed to mock my own despondent state. I saw young men of my own age, or what I supposed to be my own station, laughing as they went, their clothes unworn by care, their faces untroubled by the secret arithmetic of debt. I felt a gulf between myself and them, a gulf as wide as the one that separated me from Joe in his Sunday best. I was a fraud, and I fancied that every shopkeeper I passed, every cabman on his box, could see it written on my face.

Mr. Jaggers’s office was as it always was; smelling of soap and secrets, a place where human misery was weighed and measured and filed away in tin boxes. Mr. Wemmick was in his usual place, a sentry at the gate of law, and he greeted me with a nod and a dry, “Morning, Mr. Pip. The principal is expecting you.” He did not invite me to inspect any portable property, nor did he make any mention of Walworth or the Aged Parent; his manner was strictly official, and this, to my overwrought nerves, was a further ill omen.

I was shown into that grim inner sanctum, where Mr. Jaggers sat behind his great desk like a dark judge. He was not writing, but leaning back in his chair, biting his forefinger, a habit which I knew signified a matter of some gravity. He did not ask me to sit down, but let me stand before him, much as a prisoner would stand in the dock.

“Pip,” he began, without any preamble, his voice as hard and unpolished as a piece of granite. “I have been looking into your accounts.”

My heart, which had been beating a frantic rhythm against my ribs, seemed to stop altogether. So, it had come. The discovery and the disgrace. I could say nothing, but could only stare at him, waiting for the blow to fall.

“You are spending a great deal of money,” he continued, his eyes fixed on me with an unnerving intensity. “A very great deal of money. You are living at a rate which, though provided for by your allowance, is nonetheless, shall we say, spirited.”

I managed to find my voice, though it came out as a mere croak. “I am aware of it, sir. I have been… I intend to be more careful.”

“You intend,” he repeated, with a slight, unsmiling curl of his lip. “Intentions are not currency, Pip. I have had men in this room who intended to be virtuous, who intended to be solvent, who intended to be innocent. Their intentions did not prevent them from being transported, or from being hanged.” He paused, and took his finger from his mouth. “Your allowance is five hundred pounds a year. It is a handsome sum. It is the sum stipulated by your benefactor. It will not be increased.”

“I do not ask for it to be, sir,” I stammered, feeling a faint rush of relief that he was not, at least, cutting it off.

“No, you do not ask,” he said. “But you are acquiring expensive habits. You are acquiring expensive friends. You are acquiring debts.” He said this last word with a particular emphasis, as if it were a foul disease. “I am informed by my sources, which are reliable, that you and your companion, Mr. Herbert Pocket, owe a considerable amount to various tradesmen.”

I felt my face burn with shame. That he should know this, that my private, sordid difficulties should be a topic for his “sources,” was a humiliation of a new and terrible kind.

“We have made a plan to repay it, sir,” I said, trying to sound more confident than I felt. “We are economizing.”

“Are you,” he said, in a tone that suggested he gave very little for our chances. He stood up and began to pace the room, his boots creaking on the floorboards, a sound that sent an involuntary shiver through me. “Let me be plain with you, Pip. Your benefactor placed you in my charge. I am responsible for the disbursal of your funds, and for seeing that you are raised as a gentleman with great expectations. I am not responsible for your follies. If you run into debt, you will get yourself out of it. If you are pursued by creditors, you will deal with them yourself. No assistance will be forthcoming from this office, or from the source of your fortune. Is that understood?”

“Yes, sir. Perfectly.”

“Good.” He stopped his pacing and pointed that formidable finger at me. “Now, as to another matter. The reason I sent for you. Your twenty-first birthday is approaching.”

This was so unexpected that for a moment I could not process it. In the whirlwind of my passion for Estella and the sudden panic of my debts, I had entirely forgotten such a simple, chronological fact.

“Upon that day,” Mr. Jaggers continued, “you come of age. And my formal guardianship of you will be at an end. Your allowance of five hundred a year will be secured to you directly, and you will manage it, or mismanage it, as you see fit. My professional duty towards you will be largely discharged.”

There was a finality in his tone that was chilling. It was as if he were washing his hands of me, just as I had feared he would one day wash his hands of Estella. I was to be cast adrift on the sea of my own incompetence, with a handsome income that I had already proved myself incapable of managing.

“Furthermore,” he said, returning to his chair and fixing me with his gaze once more, “it is the wish of your benefactor that on your twenty-first birthday, the identity of that person should be at last revealed to you.”

The room seemed to tilt. For all these years, the identity of my patron had been the great mystery of my life, the sun around which my entire existence had orbited. I had woven so many fantasies around it, with Miss Havisham always at their centre, that the prospect of the actual truth was at once thrilling and terrifying. And it was to come so soon.

“The benefactor,” Mr. Jaggers said, as if reading my thoughts, “will choose the time and place of the revelation. You are to hold yourself in readiness. It may be on the day itself, or it may be some time after. You will be contacted.” He waved his hand, a gesture of dismissal. “That is all, Pip. You may go.”

I stood for a moment, stunned by the weight of the information he had so dispassionately delivered. My debts, my coming of age, and the imminent revelation of my great secret. It was too much to absorb. I turned, and walked from the room in a daze. In the outer office, Wemmick was polishing a large key with a piece of chamois leather. He looked up as I passed, and this time there was a flicker of something unofficial in his eye.

He followed me out onto the landing, closing the door quietly behind him. “A moment, Mr. Pip,” he said, his voice lowered to its Walworth pitch.

“Wemmick,” I said, my head still spinning. “Did you hear?”

“The walls of this establishment have ears, Mr. Pip,” he replied, with a twitch of his post-office mouth. “The principal was very firm with you.”

“He was.”

“Debt, Mr. Pip,” he said, shaking his head slowly. “It’s a bad business. It’s a quicksand. You put one foot in, and before you know it, you’re up to your neck. Get out of it, Mr. Pip. Get any saleable portable property you have, and get out of it.” He glanced around, as if to be sure we were not overheard. “And about this other business. The revelation.”

“Yes?” I asked eagerly.

“Don’t build on it,” he said, his voice barely a whisper. “Don’t expect too much. When a thing has been a mystery for a long time, the truth is often a disappointment. It’s rarely as grand as what a man imagines. Just… be prepared.” He gave a short, sharp nod. “Take a walk, Mr. Pip. A long walk. And think about portable property. Good day.”

He turned and slipped back into the office, leaving me on the dusty landing with his cryptic warning ringing in my ears. A long walk was indeed what I needed. I did not return directly to Barnard’s Inn, but set off with no particular destination in mind, plunging into the labyrinth of the city. The interview with Jaggers had, strangely, had the effect of clearing my head. The vague terror of the unknown had been replaced by the stark reality of my situation. My debt was my own to conquer. My future, after my birthday, would be my own to navigate. And the great mystery, the cornerstone of my entire life, was about to be solved.

Wemmick’s words, “the truth is often a disappointment,” echoed in my mind. Could it be that Miss Havisham was not my benefactor after all? The thought was so deeply contrary to the entire structure of my belief that I could scarcely entertain it. For years, I had seen her as the eccentric fairy godmother, Satis House as the enchanted castle, and Estella as the princess destined for me. If that was not the story, then what was? Who else could it be? The idea that some other, unknown person had been pulling the strings of my life was deeply unsettling. It made my world feel thinner, less real.

But for all the anxiety this new mystery provoked, my encounter with Mr. Jaggers had produced one unexpectedly positive result; it had given me a focus. The vague, romantic despair over Estella, which had threatened to drown me, was now forced to share my attention with the sharp, immediate problem of my debts. There was something to be done, a battle to be fought on a field I could understand, a field of ledgers and bills. It was a lower, more common sort of struggle, but it was a struggle nonetheless, and it was mine.

When I finally returned to our chambers, Herbert looked up with an anxious face. I related to him the entire interview, from Jaggers’s knowledge of our debts to the news of my impending birthday and the promised revelation. He listened with his usual sympathy, but when I was finished, his expression was one of grim determination.

“Well, Handel,” he said, “this changes nothing, and it changes everything. We knew we were in the mire, and now we know for certain that no one will be throwing us a rope. We must climb out ourselves.” He went to the little escritoire and took out the list we had made, the catalogue of our follies. “And as for this other news,” he continued, tapping the paper, “it makes this all the more urgent. You must meet your benefactor, whoever he is, not as a debtor and a wastrel, but as a man of honour who has his affairs in order. We must begin at once.”

And so we did. The grand, melancholy performance of my broken heart was set aside. In its place came a period of the most stringent and uncomfortable economy. We made a comprehensive plan, dividing our debts into categories, and resolving to pay a small amount to each of our creditors every week from my allowance. This required a series of humiliating visits to tradesmen, where I, who had once entered their shops with the careless air of a young prince, now had to explain our situation and beg for their patience. Some were kind, some were suspicious, and some were openly contemptuous, but in the end, they all agreed. My shame was acute, but with each arrangement I made, I felt the cold iron of the chain of debt lighten by some small degree. I was no longer trapped and seeing no way out; I was a prisoner, to be sure, but I was now busily, painfully, filing at the bars. And in this humbling, arduous work, I found a strange and unexpected relief from the greater, more ethereal agony of my love.

And so we did, with a grim assiduity that would have been comical had it not been so profoundly miserable. Our chambers, which had once rung with the boisterous laughter of the Finches and echoed with the clinking of glasses filled with expensive claret, now fell into a monastic quiet. The Startop and Drummle set, with their casual prodigality, soon found our new, abstemious society to be a dead bore, and their visits dwindled and then ceased altogether; a circumstance for which Herbert and I, in our private consultations, professed a mutual and profound relief. We, who had thought nothing of ordering in a feast from the chop-house, now found ourselves hunched over our own little fire, attempting to concoct some semblance of a meal from provisions bought at the cheapest market. Herbert, with his unfailingly sanguine disposition, would approach a piece of mutton as if it were a complex legal case, considering it from all angles before committing it to the pot with an air of hopeful uncertainty. More often than not, the result was a grey and watery stew, which we would eat with a gravity befitting a far grander repast. “A little more salt, I think, Handel,” he would say, tasting it with a judicial frown, “but the fundamental principle of the thing is sound, decidedly sound.”

There was a particular humiliation in this newfound poverty that went beyond the mere absence of luxury. It was the constant, nagging consciousness of it, the way it informed every small decision of the day. A walk down the street became a strategic exercise in avoiding the shops of our most pressing creditors. A new pair of gloves, once an idle purchase, was now an unattainable object of desire. We took to mending our own clothes, Herbert proving surprisingly adept with a needle and thread, a skill he claimed to have picked up from a maiden aunt. I watched him one evening, his handsome face puckered in concentration as he stitched a patch onto the elbow of my coat, and I was struck by a wave of affection for him so strong it was like a physical blow. He was in this mire on my account, his own modest prospects entangled with my ruinous expectations, yet not once did a word of reproach pass his lips. His loyalty was a steady, warm light in the gathering gloom of my circumstances.

To raise a more immediate sum, we resolved to part with some of our superfluous finery. We made a melancholy pile upon our table; a silver-topped cane of mine, a pair of ornate cuff-links Herbert was fond of, a silk waistcoat of a particularly glorious pattern that had been the envy of the Finches. We took them, not to a respectable pawnbroker where our shame might be witnessed by our own class, but to a dingy, furtive little shop in a labyrinth of back streets near the river. The proprietor was a small, wizened man with eyes that seemed to have absorbed all the misery and desperation that had ever passed through his door. He weighed our treasures in his grimy hands, his gaze flitting from the objects to our faces, as if calculating the precise depth of our need. He named a sum that was so insultingly small it was all I could do not to snatch the items back. But Herbert, ever the diplomat, gave a slight, sad nod. “It will have to suffice,” he said, and the transaction was concluded. We walked back to Barnard’s Inn with the few coins clinking in Herbert’s pocket, not speaking a word, the grand architecture of London seeming to mock the smallness of our defeat.

It was during this period of diligent self-denial that I was forced one afternoon into an encounter I would have given a great deal to avoid. I had been dispatched on an errand to the Temple, and as I was crossing a courtyard, I heard a loud, braying laugh that I knew all too well. Lounging on a bench with two other listless young men was Bentley Drummle. He was, if possible, more swollen and brutish than ever, his complexion a mottled purple, his small eyes glittering with a kind of dull, predatory arrogance. He saw me at the same moment I saw him, and a slow, unpleasant smile spread across his face.

“Well, well,” he drawled, not bothering to rise, “if it isn’t the little blacksmith. Come into a fortune, they say. Spending it all on books, are we? You look devilish shabby, Spider.”

His companions snickered. The old fire of humiliation and rage flared up in me. My first instinct was to deliver a sharp retort, to put him in his place with some cutting remark about his own lack of wit or breeding. But then, with a clarity that was itself a product of my recent hardships, I saw the utter futility of it. What could I say that would wound a creature of such impenetrable conceit? To argue with him would be to wrestle with a chimney-sweep; I could not win, and was certain to come away dirtied. I remembered the lesson of Trabb’s boy. So I merely inclined my head, a gesture of the barest civility, and made to walk on.

This, however, did not suit Drummle at all. He had baited his hook and was incensed that the fish refused to bite. He rose from the bench and swaggered into my path.

“Not so fast,” he said, his face unpleasantly close to mine. “I hear you’ve been keeping company with a certain young lady. A diamond of the first water. A star.” He winked at his friends. “Tell me, blacksmith, does she let you fetch and carry for her? Does she let you shine her little shoes?”

The mention of Estella was like a hot poker to my heart. All my hard-won composure vanished. I felt the blood drain from my face, and my hands clenched into fists at my sides. I saw, in that moment, the abject misery of my position. I was a gentleman in name only, a pauper aping the manners of my betters, and this oaf, this “blockhead,” as Jaggers had so aptly named him, had a clearer, more confident claim on the world I so desperately wished to inhabit. He had money, he had a name, and he had the unthinking insolence that so often passes for power.

“That is no concern of yours,” I managed to say, my voice tight and strange in my own ears.

“Oh, but it is,” he sneered, his breath sour with wine. “It’s a great concern to all of us. A great amusement. The little blacksmith, all dressed up and playing at love with the ice-princess. Don’t you know she’s not for the likes of you? She’ll play with you for a bit, maybe, and then she’ll step on you like the bug you are.”

He poked me in the chest with a thick finger. And in that instant, I knew with a dreadful certainty that I was going to strike him. My arm was tensing, my shoulder drawing back, the memory of our long-ago fight in Miss Havisham’s garden surging through me. But before I could commit this final, ruinous folly, a dry voice cut through the air.

“Mr. Drummle. Mr. Pip.”

We both turned. It was Mr. Jaggers, standing not ten feet away, his hands clasped behind his back, his formidable presence radiating a cold authority that instantly quelled the heated atmosphere. Drummle’s hand dropped from my chest, and his swagger deflated, a hint of sullen fear creeping into his eyes.

“Jaggers,” he muttered, with a poor attempt at nonchalance.

“I have business in the Temple,” Mr. Jaggers said, his eyes moving from Drummle to me and back again, taking in the entire scene with one comprehensive glance. “I should not like to be delayed by witnessing a common brawl. Mr. Drummle, I believe your father is a client of mine. He would be most displeased to hear of you causing trouble. Mr. Pip, you and I have an arrangement. Do not jeopardise it.”

His words were not a request but a command. He looked at me, and I felt again that sensation of being an obvious and poor riddle that he had long since solved. He saw my clenched fists, my white face, my trembling rage, and assessed them as dispassionately as he would a legal document. He gave a slight, almost imperceptible nod in the direction I had been heading, a clear dismissal. I took a deep, shuddering breath, my anger turning to a cold, sick shame. Without another look at Drummle, I walked away, my legs unsteady beneath me. I did not look back, but I could feel Mr. Jaggers’s gaze on me until I had turned the corner, and I was as certain as if I had seen it that he then turned that same chilling gaze upon Drummle, saying nothing more, but ensuring by his very presence that the matter was at an end.

That night, as Herbert and I sat by our meagre fire, I recounted the incident to him. He listened with his usual grave sympathy.

“The man is a brute, Handel,” he said at last. “A contemptible brute. But Jaggers was right. To have struck him would have gained you nothing but a black eye and a damaged reputation. You did the harder, braver thing.”

“Did I, Herbert?” I asked, staring into the embers. “It did not feel brave. It felt like cowardice. He spoke of Estella.”

“And what of it?” Herbert replied, with a firmness that surprised me. “His speaking of her does not sully her, any more than a dog’s barking at the moon diminishes its light. Your course is set, Handel. You are paying your debts, you are making yourself a man of substance, not of show. That is your answer to the likes of Drummle. Not your fists.”

His words were a comfort, but the poison of the encounter lingered. Drummle’s taunts had reminded me of the true stakes of my struggle. It was not merely about money; it was about worthiness. All my scraping and saving, all my humiliating economies, were directed towards a single end: to be ready for Estella. The thought that she might arrive in London and find me shabby, indebted, and embroiled in a street brawl with Bentley Drummle was a vision of purest horror. The fear of it lent a new, frantic energy to our efforts.

The weeks that followed were a blur of work and worry. By day, I applied myself to my studies with Herbert, and by evening, we pored over our account books, which we called the “Book of Judgment,” moving small sums from one column to another, celebrating the settling of some tiny debt as if it were a great victory. The relief from the constant, aching thoughts of Estella, which I had felt at the beginning of this enterprise, had evaporated. Now, she was the silent witness to all our privations. Every shilling we saved, every small luxury we forswore, was a sacrifice laid upon her altar. The more I denied myself, the more my imagination feasted upon her. My love, no longer a passive, melancholy state, had become an active, driving force, a relentless taskmaster.

One Saturday, feeling the need for some air that was not thick with the gloom of Barnard’s Inn, I took it into my head to walk to Walworth. I had not seen Wemmick outside of his professional capacity for some time, and I felt a longing for the eccentric warmth of his castle. The walk itself was a balm, and as I approached the familiar little drawbridge, I saw Wemmick himself pottering in his garden, tending to his salad, a miniature flag fluttering proudly from the roof of his house.

He greeted me with a pleasure that was entirely of Walworth and had no taint of Little Britain about it. “Pip! I am glad to see you. You find me enjoying my property. The Aged has been asking after you.”

We went inside, and the Aged Parent, who was toasting a sausage on a very long fork over the fire, beamed at me. “All right, John?” he inquired, his customary greeting.

“All right, sir,” I replied, taking the seat Wemmick offered me.

The atmosphere of the castle was, as always, one of cheerful, self-contained industry. Everything had its place, every object spoke of its owner’s pride. As we sat, and Wemmick mixed a glass of hot rum and water for me, I found myself confiding in him. I did not, of course, speak of Estella, but I told him of my pecuniary difficulties and of the strict plan of economy Herbert and I had undertaken. I expected a word of caution, perhaps, or a dry aphorism about the follies of youth. Instead, Wemmick’s face lit up with genuine enthusiasm.

“Excellent, Pip!” he exclaimed, rapping the table with his knuckles. “A plan! A system! That is the key. Get a plan, and stick to it. That’s how you acquire portable property. You look at your liabilities, you look at your assets, and you make the assets swallow the liabilities. It’s a beautiful thing when it works.” He leaned closer, his expression earnest. “And let me give you a piece of Walworth wisdom. When you pay a man, Pip, pay him yourself. Go to him, look him in the eye, and hand him the money. Don’t send it. Don’t use an agent. The look on a man’s face when he receives his due, and the feeling in your own heart when you give it, that’s part of the profit. That’s a return on your investment.”

He then bustled about, showing me his latest acquisition, a curious new species of fowl he was raising in a pen at the back, which he referred to as his “poultry investment.” The sheer, unadulterated pleasure he took in his small, secure world was profoundly affecting. Here was a man who had drawn a clear line between his work and his life, and who had built his happiness within the walls he had erected for himself. He was not a man of great expectations, but of small, tangible, and deeply cherished realities. I left Walworth that evening feeling strangely fortified, as if some of Wemmick’s practical, defensive spirit had been imparted to me.

It was well that it had, for the trial I had been dreading and longing for was now close at hand. Two days after my visit to Walworth, as Herbert and I were finishing a particularly frugal breakfast of bread and tea, the post arrived. There were two letters; one a receipt from a tailor to whom we had just paid an installment, a small victory in our campaign. The other was addressed to me. The hand was elegant and feminine, and my heart gave a great, painful leap before I had even broken the seal.

It was not from Estella, but from Miss Havisham. Her notes were always brief and imperious. This one contained but two lines.

_Estella is coming to London. You are to meet her at the coach, as was arranged. She will be there to live under the care of a lady in Richmond, and you will be expected to call upon her there._

There was no date, no time, no further information. The note was as cold and sharp as an icicle. I read it, and then read it again, the words seeming to dance before my eyes. Herbert, seeing the look on my face, came and stood behind me, reading it over my shoulder.

“Handel,” he said, his voice soft. “This is it.”

This was it. The words echoed in my mind. For months, my life had been a strange play, with me as the sole actor on a darkened stage, performing for an audience of one who was not there. Now, the house lights were coming up. Estella was coming. The object of all my hopes, all my fears, all my striving, was to be within my reach. A wave of delirious joy washed over me, so potent it made me dizzy. I would see her, I would speak to her, I would be in the same room with her, breathing the same air.

And then, crashing in upon the joy, came the terror. I looked around our poor, bare chambers. I thought of my mended coat, of the coins we had received for my silver-topped cane, of the sneering face of Bentley Drummle. Was I ready? Was this shabby, debt-ridden existence the one I was to present to her? My grand expectations had shrunk to this: a daily struggle to keep my head above water. I, who was to be her destined partner, felt more like a fraud than ever before. The blacksmith’s boy, with his coarse hands and his common ways, was lurking just beneath the thin veneer of the gentleman, and I felt with a sickening certainty that her sharp, beautiful eyes would see him at once.

“When, Herbert, when?” I asked, my voice trembling. “The note does not say when!”

“We will find out,” he said, with his customary calm. “We will make inquiries. And you will be ready, Handel. Look how far we have come. You are not the man you were two months ago. You are a man who is mastering his own affairs.”

I wanted to believe him. I clutched the note in my hand as if it were a holy relic. She was coming. All the world narrowed to that single, electrifying fact. My period of quiet, miserable industry was over. The performance was about to begin. And as I stood there, torn between an ecstasy of hope and an agony of fear, I felt that my entire life had been nothing but a long and winding road leading to the door of that coach, where I would stand waiting, once again, for Estella.

Herbert, whose own affairs were perpetually in a state of hopeful disarray but whose concern for mine was as constant as the rising of the sun, seemed to read the tempest in my soul without the need for any words of mine. He took the note from my trembling hand, folded it with a precision that was quite at odds with his general character, and placed it upon the mantelpiece as if it were a legal document of the utmost gravity.

“Now, Handel,” he began, adopting the air of a seasoned general planning a most delicate campaign, “the first thing is not to be overwhelmed. We are men of the world, or at least we are in the process of becoming so, and we must meet this with strategy. The note is imperious, you say? It is cold? Well, that is the sender’s character, and has no bearing on the recipient’s duty. The duty, in this case, is to be ready.”

“But ready for what, Herbert?” I cried, gesturing helplessly at our surroundings; at the threadbare rug, the chairs that bore the scars of our fencing practice, the general air of genteel poverty that clung to everything like a fine dust. “Ready to present this? To show her that my great expectations have amounted to little more than a room in Barnard’s Inn and a ledger full of debts?”

“You are not to show her the ledger,” said Herbert, with unassailable logic. “As for the rooms, we shall make them presentable. We shall have flowers. The Aged is partial to a bit of greenery, and I daresay a woman of fashion appreciates such things. The main point, the central strategic objective, is to ascertain the day and the hour. Without that intelligence, the entire campaign is a wash.”

His military metaphors, which he produced with an easy fluency that would have done credit to a field marshal, had the effect of putting a little starch back into my own collapsed spirits. It was true; without the particulars of her arrival, my agonies were merely speculative, a wasteful expense of emotional capital. We must have facts. And for facts of a certain, unyielding kind, there was but one place in all of London to go.

“I must go to Mr. Jaggers,” I said, the name leaving a taste like iron in my mouth.

“Precisely,” said Herbert, clapping me on the shoulder. “You will go to Little Britain. You will be cool, you will be businesslike. You are his ward, he manages your affairs; it is a simple matter of requesting a point of information relevant to your arrangements. Nothing more.”

Nothing more. It sounded so simple when Herbert said it. Yet, as I put on my hat and coat, my heart began a low, heavy drumming against my ribs. To approach Mr. Jaggers on any matter was to invite scrutiny of the most piercing and uncomfortable kind; to approach him on a matter touching Estella felt like willingly placing my most vulnerable, tender feelings upon an anvil and handing him the hammer.

The journey to Little Britain did nothing to soothe my nerves. The great, grimy heart of London beat all around me, a monstrous, sprawling organism of which I felt a very small and insignificant part. Every well dressed man I saw seemed to mock my own aspirations, every beautiful carriage that rattled past seemed to be a chariot carrying some more worthy suitor to his triumph. I felt the phantom of Trabb’s boy at my elbow, drawling “Don’t know yah!” to my very soul.

Mr. Jaggers’s office was as I always remembered it: a place where hope went to die. The air was thick with the scent of unswept floors and human misery, and the two plaster casts on the shelf, their necks gruesomely displayed, seemed to wink at me in the gloomy light. In the outer office, a sad collection of clients waited, a sort of human menagerie of desperation, their faces turned towards the inner door like plants towards a grudging sun. I, being on a different footing, was to go straight in, a privilege which, as always, made me feel both important and deeply ashamed.

I found Mr. Jaggers standing before the fire, washing his hands. He did this with a terrifying deliberation, scrubbing at his fingers with a scented soap as if cleansing himself of the moral filth of his profession. He did not look up when I entered, but merely acknowledged my presence with a slight, interrogative noise from his throat.

“Mr. Jaggers, sir,” I began, my voice sounding thin and reedy in the oppressive silence. “I have come on a small matter of business.”

He ceased his scrubbing and began to dry his hands, one finger at a time, on a rough towel, his eyes fixed upon the process as if it were a case of the utmost legal complexity. “Speak,” he said, the word coming out like a shot.

I produced the note from my pocket. My hand was shaking so badly that the paper rattled. “I have received this communication, sir. From Miss Havisham. It concerns Miss Estella’s coming to London.”

He took the note from me, held it at a distance as if it might be contaminated, and read it with a single, sweeping glance of his formidable eyes. Then he handed it back to me.

“Well?” he said. “It seems clear enough. You are to meet her.”

“Yes, sir,” I said, feeling my courage dwindle. “But it does not say when. Or where, precisely. The coaching inns are numerous, and I would not wish to make a mistake.”

Mr. Jaggers fixed me with a look that I can only describe as one of profound and weary contempt, as if my inability to divine the future through sheer force of will was a personal failing of the most irritating kind.

“When you have a specific, answerable question of a business nature, Pip,” he said, biting off each word, “you may bring it to me. This,” and he made a vague gesture that encompassed my question, my anxiety, and my entire romantic predicament, “is not business. This is folly. Folly is not my department. Wemmick may have some information for you. Or he may not. Now, if you will excuse me, I have a man to see about a hanging.”

And with that, he turned his back on me and resumed his position before the fire, leaving me to feel as though I had been summarily dismissed not only from his office, but from the category of rational human beings. I retreated into the outer office, my face burning.

Wemmick was at his desk, his post office mouth set in its customary slit, his whole being radiating an aura of dry, official caution. He was writing in a ledger with a pen that scratched and squeaked like a trapped mouse.

“Mr. Wemmick,” I said, leaning over the tall desk. “A moment, if you please.”

He looked up, his eyes small and bright like beads. “Mr. Pip. To what do we owe the pleasure?”

I explained my predicament in a low voice, conscious of the listening ears of the miserable clients. Wemmick listened, his expression unchanging, occasionally polishing his thumbnail on the lapel of his coat.

When I had finished, he dipped his pen in the ink, examined the nib, and then looked at me. “Richmond,” he said, as if it were a piece of portable property. “A lady. Very good. As for the when and the where, that is information not currently in my possession.” He paused, and his post office mouth twitched almost imperceptibly. “However,” he added, his voice dropping even lower, “I have it on good authority that the Blue Boar coach from your part of the world, the one they call the Comet, has been booked to arrive at the Cross Keys Inn, Wood Street, Cheapside, on Friday next, at three of the clock.”

A wave of relief so profound that it almost buckled my knees washed over me. “Wemmick!” I breathed. “Thank you! How can I ever…”

“No need,” he cut in, his mouth snapping shut. “I have offered no opinion on the matter. I have merely stated a fact regarding coaching schedules. It is a matter of public record. You could have found it out yourself. Good day, Mr. Pip.”

He immediately bent over his ledger again, the scratching of his pen resuming its melancholy song. I understood that the official interview was over, and that any gratitude was best expressed elsewhere, perhaps to a certain little castle in Walworth with a drawbridge and a cannon.

I returned to Barnard’s Inn in a state of high agitation, a strange and volatile mixture of terror and elation. Friday! It was but two days away. Two days to prepare myself, to transform myself from the debt ridden Pip into the gentleman of fortune Estella was expecting.

The next forty eight hours were, I think, among the most wretched of my life. Herbert, with his unfailing good nature, threw himself into the project with gusto. We pooled our remaining shillings and purchased a truly extravagant bouquet of flowers, which gave our chambers the festive air of a funeral parlour. We rearranged the furniture a dozen times, attempting to hide the shabbiest bits, a process which only seemed to highlight their deficiencies. Herbert even proposed we hire a boy for a few hours to answer the door, a plan we abandoned when we realised we could not afford the boy, let alone the door.

For my own part, I was lost in a fever of self scrutiny. I spent hours before our small, warped looking glass, examining my face for flaws, my clothes for signs of wear. I tried to practice expressions of worldly ease and confidence, but my reflection only stared back at me with the terrified eyes of the blacksmith’s boy. My past clung to me like a shroud. I thought of Joe, with his honest, loving heart, and felt a pang of shame so sharp it was like a physical blow. I had abandoned him, I had been ashamed of him, all for this; for the chance to stand before a beautiful, cold woman who would, I was certain, see through my fine coat to the common clay beneath. I repeated her name to myself, Estella, Estella, until it lost all meaning and became just a sound, a tolling bell marking the hours until my judgment.

On Friday morning, I could not eat. The very thought of food was repugnant to me. Herbert, seeing my state, forced a cup of tea upon me and laid out my best clothes as if I were a condemned man preparing for the scaffold. He brushed my coat, straightened my cravat, and offered a stream of cheerful, encouraging chatter that I scarcely heard.

“There now, Handel,” he said, standing back to admire his work. “You look every inch the true gentleman. She will be dazzled.”

I looked in the glass and saw only a pale, haunted face, a fraud in a starched collar.

I set out for Cheapside far too early, unable to bear the confines of our rooms a moment longer. I walked through the teeming streets of the city, a ghost at a feast, seeing nothing, hearing nothing but the frantic thumping of my own heart. The Cross Keys Inn, when I finally found it, was a maelstrom of organised chaos. A sprawling, galleried yard, pungent with the smell of horses and wet straw, thronged with people. Porters shouted, ostlers cursed, passengers bustled to and fro, and the air was filled with the rumble of iron shod wheels on cobblestones and the jangling of harnesses.

I consulted a large, grimy clock tower. It was not yet two. An hour to wait. I found a secluded spot in a corner of the gallery, overlooking the yard, and stood there, my hands clenched in my coat pockets. Every new face that appeared sent a fresh jolt of panic through me. Was that her? No. Was that her? No. The crowd was a shifting sea of strangers, and I was utterly alone in it.

The minutes crawled by, each one a small eternity of suspense. The clock began to strike three. The sound went through me like a series of hammer blows. And then, at the far end of the yard, there was a cry, “Make way for the Comet!” and a great, mud spattered coach, drawn by four steaming horses, came rattling through the archway.

My heart leaped into my throat. The coach lumbered to a halt, and a swarm of ostlers and porters descended upon it. The door was opened. An old woman with a great many parcels got out. A stout farmer. A young man with a foolish face. I scanned the windows, my eyes straining. And then I saw her.

She was not looking out of the window; she was looking at something inside the coach, her profile turned towards me. Even in the gloom of the carriage, I could see that she was more beautiful than I had remembered, if such a thing were possible. The years had not so much changed her as perfected her. The proud, delicate lines of her face, the graceful curve of her neck; it was all as if some master sculptor had taken the girl I knew and refined her into a work of art.

A coachman opened the door and set down the steps. She descended, not with the awkward haste of the other passengers, but with a slow, deliberate grace, as if the dirty, chaotic inn yard were a ballroom floor and all the world her audience. She wore a dark green travelling cloak, the hood of which was thrown back, and her dark hair was gathered neatly at her neck. She stood for a moment, her eyes coolly surveying the scene with an air of complete and utter detachment, a queen in exile.

For a long moment, I could not move. I was rooted to the spot, a creature of stone. She was here. She was real. All my frantic imaginings, all my hopes and fears, coalesced into this single, breathtaking reality. Then, as if from a great distance, I heard Herbert’s voice in my head, “The campaign, Handel! The objective!”

I took a deep breath, straightened my shoulders, and began to make my way down the crowded gallery steps and across the yard towards her. The noise of the inn seemed to fade away. The bustling people became mere shadows. There was only the muddy ground between us, and the beating of my own heart.

She saw me when I was about ten paces away. Her expression did not change. There was no surprise, no pleasure, not even, I thought with a sinking heart, any recognition. She simply watched me approach as she might watch a curious insect.

I stopped before her, removed my hat, and bowed. I had rehearsed this moment a hundred times in my mind, had imagined a dozen clever, charming things to say. They all deserted me now.

“Estella,” I said, and my voice was a hoarse whisper. “It is I. Pip.”

She looked at me, her head tilted slightly, a faint, questioning arch to her perfect eyebrows. And then she smiled. It was not a warm smile; it was a smile of cool appraisal, the kind of smile one might give upon identifying a piece of furniture from one’s childhood home.

“I know,” she said, her voice as clear and cold as winter air. “You have been sent to meet me. How much you have grown.”

It was not the greeting of a destined lover. It was the greeting of a superior to a servant. And as I stood there before her in the noisy, dirty yard of the Cross Keys Inn, with the smell of horses in my nostrils and my heart a leaden weight in my chest, I understood that the performance had indeed begun; but I also understood, with a certainty that was as cold and sharp as an icicle, that I had been cast in a part I did not know how to play, and that the audience for whom I was to perform was utterly, impossibly, unforgiving.
