The final stroke of the bell in the church-tower, announcing the ninth hour, had long since dissolved into the damp evening air. It was a sound that had measured the days of Mr. Ambrose Hettlebridge for forty years; a sound he had come to regard as a personal and rather impertinent commentary on his own slow progress through life. He sat now, not in his usual chair by the window overlooking the solicitor’s office opposite, but in the dim parlour, where the only light was that which fought its way from a single street-lamp through the grimy pane. The ghost he saw was not of the past, but of the future; a vacant chair, a silent room, a life from which the music had been abruptly withdrawn. It was the spectre of his own impending solitude. Can you understand my meaning, Thomas? I fancy not. One must have kept a thing safe for a lifetime to truly comprehend the terror of its release.

His quiet and measured tone did nothing to prevent his nephew’s heart from feeling a sudden chill, as the old man thus dissected the anatomy of his own affection. “In this state of quiet dread, I have often pictured him, your grandfather, not as the stern man of business the world knew, but as he was when he brought you to my door; a babe of no more than two years, wrapped in a shawl that was worth more than all my furniture. ‘Keep her safe, Ambrose,’ he said, ‘and keep her ignorant of the ruin that bears my name.’ His fortune was a wreck, his reputation in tatters. His life was a tempest; but you, my dear Clara, were the single, solitary light from his own private lighthouse.”

“I was that child, my uncle, I was not half so bright, but in my heart that was I.”

“And he charged me to raise you as a simple girl,” said Mr. Hettlebridge, “far from the gilded snares that had undone him. He said the world would try to find his heir, and that they must find nothing but a poor clerk’s niece. He could never come to you himself; I always imagined that he watched from a distance, a sad, unseen guardian. But then, finding blessed relief in his sacrifice, I would fall upon my knees in this very room, and thank him for the trust.”

“I am that child, I hope, my uncle. O my dear, my dear, will you trust me as freely to-morrow when I leave?”

“Clara, I recall these old sorrows only because I have cause to-night to be prouder than any words might tell, and to thank Providence for my great blessing. My dreams, when they were at their most fearful, never conjured a misery so profound as the thought of this parting, nor a joy so complete as the life that I have known with you, and the happiness that awaits you.” He took her hand, his own trembling slightly, and solemnly commended her to the future, and humbly thanked the past for having lent her to him. By-and-bye, they heard a sharp rap at the door.

There was no one bidden to the farewell supper but young Mr. Hayes; there was not even to be a chaperone but the formidable Mrs. Diggle from the floor above. The departure was to make no great stir; they had been able to secure a small but respectable cottage near the sea, where the air was recommended for a young constitution, and they desired nothing more. Mrs. Diggle was very cheerful at the little supper. They were only four at table, and Mr. Hayes made the fourth. She regretted that Clara’s finery was not more ostentatious; was more than half disposed to find fault with the humble little plan that took her away from society; and drank to her future health with gusto.

So, the time came for him to bid Clara good night, and they separated at the foot of the stairs. But, in the stillness of the eleventh hour, Mr. Hettlebridge came downstairs again, and stole into the parlour; not free from a shapeless anxiety, beforehand. All things, however, were in their places; all was quiet; and his own empty armchair stood sentinel in the gloom, a faithful soldier awaiting a commander who would not return. He put his unlit candle on the mantelpiece, crept up to the window, and put his brow to the cold glass. Into his gentle face, the worries of a lifetime had etched their fine map; but, he bore their geography with a resignation so complete, that he held dominion over them even in his solitude. A more affecting countenance in its quiet, loving, and patient struggle with an unseen adversary, was not to be found in all the vast territories of sorrow, that night. He softly laid his hand upon the dusty ledger where he had kept her accounts, and put up a prayer that she might ever be as happy as his love wished her to be, and as her goodness deserved. Then, he withdrew his hand, and brushed a speck of dust from his sleeve, and went away. So, the moon rose, and the shadows of the skeletal winter branches moved upon the floor, as softly as her feet had moved in coming to comfort him.

CHAPTER II. A New Pattern

The morning of the departure was shining with a brittle, wintery brightness, and they were ready outside the closed door of the parlour, where Mr. Hayes was speaking with Clara. They were ready to go to the station; the beautiful young lady, Mr. Hettlebridge, and Mrs. Diggle; to whom the event, through a slow process of accepting what could not be helped, would have been one of sheer vexation, but for the lingering hope that Mr. Hettlebridge might now take his meals with her on a more permanent basis.

“I did not mean it for an expense,” remarked the literal-minded Mr. Hettlebridge, “and therefore how could it be one? Nonsense!”

“Really? Well; but don’t sigh,” said the robust Mrs. Diggle.

“I am not sighing,” said Mr. Hettlebridge; “_you_ are.”

“I, my Ambrose?” (By this time, Mrs. Diggle dared to be familiar with him, on occasion.)

“You were, just now; I heard you do it, and I do not wonder at it. Such a quantity of preserves as you have packed for her is enough to make anyone breathless. “I am highly gratified,” said Mrs. Diggle, “though, upon my word, I had no intention of making those trifling provisions a mirror for your melancholy. Dear me! This is a day that makes a woman consider all she has missed. Dear, dear, dear! To think that there might have been a Mr. Diggle, any time these thirty years almost!”

“Not at all!” From Mr. Hettlebridge.

“You think there never might have been a Mr. Diggle?” asked the woman of that name.

“Pooh!” rejoined Mr. Hettlebridge; “you were a manageress in your cradle.”

“Well!” observed Mrs. Diggle, proudly adjusting her bonnet, “that seems probable, too.”

“And you were cut out for a manageress,” pursued Mr. Hettlebridge, “before you were put in your cradle.”

“Then, I think,” said Mrs. Diggle, “that I was very unfairly treated, and that I ought to have had a say in the matter. Enough! You leave your good uncle, my dear, in hands as capable and as caring as your own; he shall be looked after in every particular; during the next month, while you are settling in by the sea, even his ledgers shall take second place to his dinner (comparatively speaking) before him. And when, at the month’s end, you receive our first letter with news from London, you shall say that we have kept him for you in the best of health and in the happiest of spirits. Now, I hear Somebody’s footstep approaching the door. Let me kiss my dear girl with a sensible woman’s blessing, before Somebody comes to whisk her away.”

For a moment, she held the sweet face from her to look at the familiar expression of gentle resolve, and then laid the girl’s rosy cheek against her own weathered one, with a genuine warmth and affection which, if such things be sensible, were as sensible as the earth itself.

The door of the parlour opened, and she came out with Mr. Hayes. She was so deathly calm; which had not been the case when they went in together; that no sign of her recent tears was visible on her face. She gave her arm to her uncle, and took him down-stairs to the cab which Mr. Hayes had summoned in honour of the day. The rest followed with the luggage, and soon, at a bustling station, where many strange eyes looked on, Clara Hettlebridge and Mr. Hayes were happily on their way. He allowed himself to be led, his movements stiff and automatic. It was only when they were settled in a rattling, musty-smelling hackney cab that Mrs. Diggle observed the true extent of his abstraction. He stared out at the passing tableau of London life; the hurried clerks, the dawdling urchins, the grand carriages and the humble carts; but his eyes, she noted, took nothing in. The house, when they reached it, seemed to have shrunk in their absence. The silence that greeted them as Mrs. Diggle turned the key in the lock was of a different quality to the one they had carried from the station; it was a settled, domestic silence, a silence that had a shape and a history. It was the silence of Clara’s empty chair by the window; the silence of her sewing basket, now tidied away into a cupboard; the silence of the little piano in the corner, its lid firmly closed. “Now then,” said Mrs. Diggle, with a determined bustle, removing her bonnet and tying on a formidable apron. “The fire needs seeing to. And I shall make the tea. A proper pot. No sense in moping.”

She performed these domestic rites with a brisk and noisy efficiency, as though she could physically drive out the quiet with the clatter of a poker and the clink of a teacup. Mr. Hettlebridge had sunk into his usual armchair, the banknotes now lying forgotten on the small table beside him. He had taken up a newspaper, but he was not reading it; he held it as a shield, a flimsy fortification against the encroachments of the present moment.

They took their supper in a similar state of strained normalcy. She spoke of these things as if they were matters of the utmost gravity, and Mr. Hettlebridge nodded at what seemed to be the appropriate intervals, his own contributions to the discourse being no more than a few murmured assents. After the meal, he announced his intention to retire to his study to look over some accounts. This was his custom, his refuge. In the neat columns and indisputable sums of his ledgers, he found a comfort and a certainty that the wider world, with its messy emotions and unpredictable turns, so often denied him.

“A capital idea,” Mrs. Diggle had said, clearing the plates. “Work is the best tonic for a troubled mind.”

Yet, as the evening wore on, a feeling of disquiet settled upon her. The house was too quiet. The scratch of Mr. Hettlebridge’s pen, a sound as familiar and as comforting as the ticking of the grandfather clock in the hall, was absent. There was only the clock, measuring out the minutes of this new, emptier life, and the distant rumble of London.

At half-past ten, her mending basket exhausted and her patience with it, she put aside her work. She banked the fire, locked the front door, and made her way upstairs. The light was still shining from beneath the door of his study. She paused, her hand hovering near the knob, her ear inclined towards the panel of wood. She could hear nothing; not the rustle of paper, not the shifting of a chair, nothing.

This utter stillness was more alarming than any sound could have been. Acting on an impulse she did not pause to question, she turned the handle softly and pushed the door ajar, no more than an inch.

Mr. Hettlebridge was at his desk, as she had known he would be. The oil-lamp cast a warm, steady glow, illuminating his bent head and the papers spread before him. But he was not working. He was not making entries in the current accounts, nor was he drafting a letter of business. His pen lay untouched in its stand. Before him, occupying the centre of the desk where the day-book usually lay, was a much older volume, one she had not seen for nearly twenty years. It was a heavy ledger, bound in dark, cracked leather, its pages yellowed with a stain that was more than age. It was not a book of business; it was a book of ghosts.

His finger, the long, delicate finger of a man accustomed to the fine points of a nib, was tracing lines upon the page. He was not reading, not calculating. He traced the faded ink of names and dates, his lips moving silently, forming words she could not hear. His face, in the lamplight, was a mask of sorrow so profound, so naked, that she felt a trespasser for looking upon it. The calm composure he had maintained all day, the composure he had worn like a well-fitting coat for two decades, had fallen away, and the man beneath was one she barely recognised; a man lost in a wilderness of his own memory.

She saw him pause, his finger resting on a particular entry. He leaned closer, as if to impart a secret to the page, or to receive one from it. His shoulders, usually so straight and correct, were slumped in an attitude of utter defeat. The conversation in the parlour, the one he had shared with Mr. Hayes while she and Clara waited outside, had not been about settlements or futures. She knew that now. It had been about the past. Mr. Hayes, in his happiness, had unknowingly trod upon a grave.

Quietly, so quietly that not even a floorboard dared to creak in protest, Mrs. Diggle drew the door closed again. She leaned against the wall in the darkened hallway, her heart beating a heavy, frightened rhythm against her ribs. She knew, with a certainty that chilled her to the bone, what he was doing. He was not merely remembering; he was rebuilding. In the silent theatre of his mind, he was reconstructing the architecture of a ruin, painstakingly replacing each fallen stone, each shattered beam, of a life that had collapsed long ago. The crisp banknotes on the table downstairs were not a gift; they were a propitiation, an unwitting payment for an old and terrible debt.

She went down the stairs, not to her own room, but back to the parlour. The house was cold now. She took the forgotten banknotes from the table. They were for a considerable sum; enough to signify a great respect, a deep gratitude from the young husband. But to her, in that moment, they felt like the price of a soul. She folded them carefully and placed them in the drawer of the little writing desk, a drawer that had always held Clara’s spare ribbons and sheets of drawing paper. Then, she sat in the chair opposite Mr. Hettlebridge’s, and prepared to wait. She did not know for what, precisely; only that the night was not over, and that her watch had just begun.

He looked at her then, a direct, pleading look. “I do not know if I can learn to swim again.”

Mrs. Diggle rose from her chair. She was a plain woman, a manageress, a woman of accounts and practicalities. She was not equipped for deep waters and talk of tides. “No, Mrs. Diggle,” he whispered, his voice cracking. “I will not.”

They concocted a story, a plausible fiction that the Doctor had been called away on a matter of urgent professional business, a consultation with a former patient in a distant part of the country. They sent this news to the inn in Warwickshire, in a letter penned in Mr. Lorry’s neat, commercial hand; a letter that cost him more effort and anguish than any financial report he had ever compiled. They filled it with cheerful assurances and promises of a swift reunion, every word a lie, every sentence a betrayal of the happy trust reposed in them.

During these nine days, a strange and profound intimacy grew between the two guardians. The formal barriers that had always existed between the man of business and the fierce, protective governess were dissolved by their shared anxiety. They sat together in the evenings in the little parlour, the lamp unlit, the shadows gathering around them. They did not speak much, for there was little to say. The sound of the hammer from the room above was a constant, melancholy commentary on their failure.

“If only she had not gone,” Mr. Lorry would murmur, for the hundredth time.

“Nonsense,” Miss Pross would retort, her voice less sharp than usual. “It was her right. It was her happiness. We could not have kept her. It is not her fault.”

“No,” Mr. Lorry would agree, sighing. “It is not her fault. It is the fault of that old, cruel time. It is a ghost that will not be laid to rest.”

On the fifth day, Mr. Lorry, driven by a need for action, however fruitless, made a bold suggestion. “We must try to speak with him, my Pross. We cannot let this continue. We must attempt to reason with him.”

“Reason?” she said, with a flash of her old asperity. “You cannot reason with a broken mind. You might as well reason with a storm. It will only break itself, or you.”

“Nevertheless,” he insisted, “I must try. I cannot sit here and listen to that sound for another day without making some effort. It is my duty.”

And so, he went upstairs. He knocked upon the locked door. There was no answer. The tapping continued, regular and monotonous. “Manette?” he called, his voice gentle. “My dear friend, it is Lorry. Will you not open the door? Will you not speak with me?”

There was no response. The hammer fell, and fell again. He pleaded, he reasoned, he spoke of Lucie, of her love, of her imminent return. The only reply was the dispassionate, unvarying tap, tap, tap of the shoemaker at his work. Defeated, Mr. Lorry returned to the parlour, his face grey with despair. “You were right,” he said to Miss Pross. “He does not hear me. He is not there. It is only the shoemaker of the North Tower who remains.”

On the morning of the tenth day, a change occurred. Mr. Lorry, who had slept fitfully in an armchair, was awoken by a sudden and profound silence. The house was still. He sat up, his heart pounding. The tapping had stopped.

He looked at Miss Pross, who was standing by the window, her hand pressed to her chest. She had heard it too. They looked at one another for a long, fearful moment. Then, moving as one, they crept up the stairs. They stood outside the Doctor’s door, listening. Not a sound came from within. The silence was more terrifying than the noise had been.

“What has happened?” whispered Mr. Lorry. “Is he…?” He could not finish the sentence.

Miss Pross, her face pale but resolute, tried the handle. The door was still locked. “We must get in,” she said. “We must break it down.”

But at that moment, they heard a faint sound from within; the scraping of a chair, a heavy sigh. Then, a key turned in the lock. The door opened, and Doctor Manette stood before them.

He was dressed in his wedding clothes, though they were now rumpled and travel-stained, as if he had slept in them for nine nights. His face was gaunt and ashen, his white hair dishevelled. But the vacant look was gone from his eyes. They were clear, though filled with a terrible exhaustion and a deep, bottomless shame. He looked at Mr. Lorry, and at Miss Pross, and then at the room behind him, where the shoemaker’s bench stood in the morning light.

“It is over,” he said, his voice weak and hoarse. “The fit has passed.”

He took a step forward, and would have fallen, had not Mr. Lorry and Miss Pross both rushed to support him. They half carried him to his own bedroom and laid him down upon the bed. He closed his eyes, his breathing shallow. “I have been on a long journey,” he whispered. “A long and difficult journey. But I have come back.”

Miss Pross, her practical nature reasserting itself, hurried away to prepare some broth. Mr. Lorry sat by the bedside, holding his friend’s hand, his heart overflowing with a relief so immense it was painful. He did not ask any questions. It was enough that the Doctor had returned to them.

Later that day, when the Doctor had taken some nourishment and slept for a few hours, he opened his eyes and looked at Mr. Lorry. “Lorry,” he said, his voice stronger now. “There is something that must be done. A task for you and for me. While I am strong enough to face it.”

“Anything, my dear friend. Anything at all.”

“The bench,” the Doctor said, his gaze unwavering. “The tools. They must be destroyed. They must be chopped to pieces, burned to ashes, and the ashes buried in the garden. It must be done to-night. While she is away. She must never know of this. She must never know that the ghost of the shoemaker came to her wedding.”

Mr. Lorry’s first instinct was to protest, to argue that such a relic, however terrible, was a part of him. But he looked into the Doctor’s eyes, and saw there a resolution so absolute, a plea so desperate, that he knew there was no other way. This was not a surrender; it was a final, defiant act of liberation.

“It shall be done,” he said, his voice solemn. “To-night. We shall do it together.”

And so, late that night, when the house was dark and the city slept, two old men, the banker and the doctor, stole out into the garden. They carried with them an axe and a saw from the wood-shed. And in the dark shadow of the plane-tree, they performed a strange and terrible ceremony. With grim determination, they hacked and sawed at the shoemaker’s bench, breaking it down into a shapeless pile of wood. They gathered the tools, the leather, the half-finished shoe, and placed them all upon the pyre. They set it alight, and stood back, watching as the flames leaped up, consuming the last vestiges of that long and dreadful captivity. The fire cast their two figures into stark relief; two faithful guardians, burying a secret deep in the earth, so that the future of the one they loved might remain untroubled and bright. The ashes they would bury on the morrow; for now, they watched until the last ember died, and the ghost of the North Tower was, at last, laid to rest.

But the silence that fell upon the house in Soho that afternoon was of a different quality from the one that had preceded the wedding. It was not the quiet of anticipation, but the heavier, more leaden stillness of an accomplished fact, which, in its wake, had dragged up a ghost from the depths of a long-suffering mind. The Doctor had been conveyed to his room; Miss Pross and Mr. Lorry had exchanged a look of such shared, unspoken terror as would bind them together more surely than any vow. For nine days, the spectre of the Bastille had returned to the little home, not in the form of a political threat, but as an intimate, internal foe that took possession of the very man they sought to protect.

For nine days, the shoemaker’s bench was re-established in its old place, and the gentle, learned Doctor was lost to them, replaced by a vacant-eyed man who hammered and stitched, who answered to the name “One Hundred and Five, North Tower,” and who knew nothing of a daughter named Lucie, nor of a wedding that had just taken place. Mr. Lorry, true to his word, let Tellson’s Bank go to the wall; he took up his residence in the house, sleeping on a sofa, a sentinel whose charge had seemingly been reclaimed by the very prison from which he had rescued him so many years before. He and Miss Pross kept their vigil, speaking in low tones, moving about the house with a fearful quiet, their lives revolving around the closed door of the Doctor’s room. They wrote to Lucie, letters of careful fabrication, speaking of a slight indisposition, a cold, a need for rest after the excitements of the day; letters that cost them agonies of guilt to compose.

Miss Pross, whose love was a fierce and sometimes terrifying thing, was the more outwardly distraught of the two. Her gaunt figure seemed to grow yet more angular, her face more severe, but her hands were indefatigable. She prepared the most nourishing broths and jellies, which the shoemaker would sometimes take, and sometimes refuse, without a flicker of recognition. She would sit outside his door for hours, her knitting needles clicking like a frantic, tiny loom, as if she could by sheer force of will weave a tapestry of recovery around him.

Mr. Lorry, a man of business, approached the catastrophe with a kind of desperate practicality. He maintained the fiction for the outside world, spoke to the few callers, and spent his evenings watching the shadow under the locked door, his mind a turmoil of ledgers and losses, the greatest loss being the friend he saw regressing before his very eyes. The happiness of the wedding morning seemed a dream from another lifetime.

It was on the morning of the tenth day that the change came. Mr. Lorry had dozed off in his chair, a book unread upon his lap, when a sound from within the room startled him awake. It was not the rhythmic tap of the hammer, but a silence, a profound and unnerving cessation of the sound that had been the heartbeat of their misery for more than a week. He rose, his own heart hammering in his chest, and crept to the door. He listened. There was nothing. After a moment of terrible indecision, he knocked softly.

There was no answer. He knocked again, more loudly. “Doctor? Manette, my dear friend? Are you well?”

A shuffling sound came from within, and then, a voice; a voice so faint, so rusted with disuse, that it was barely recognisable, yet it was not the voice of the shoemaker. “Lorry? Is that you?”

Tears sprang to Mr. Lorry’s eyes. He fumbled with the key, which Miss Pross had insisted he keep, and opened the door. Doctor Manette was standing in the middle of the room, fully dressed, his white hair dishevelled. The shoemaker’s bench was in its corner, a half-finished shoe upon it, but the Doctor was looking at it as a man looks upon the scene of some dreadful accident from which he has just escaped. His face was pale, his eyes hollowed out by his ordeal, but they were his own eyes, filled with a dawning horror and comprehension. He swayed, and saw Mr. Lorry as if for the first time.

“Lorry,” he said, his voice trembling. “She is married. Lucie is married.”

“Yes, my dear friend,” Mr. Lorry said, his own voice thick with emotion. “Happily married. To Charles.”

“And I… I was not there. I was… here.” His gaze drifted back to the bench, and a shudder wracked his entire body. He looked at his hands, turning them over as if they were not his own. “How long?”

“Nine days,” Mr. Lorry answered softly.

The Doctor closed his eyes, a look of immense pain crossing his features. He did not speak of the relapse again, not in any direct manner. Instead, his mind, with the instinct of a man pulling himself from a great depth, fixed upon the future. It was in this moment of terrible clarity that he declared the bench must be destroyed.

And so it was, that very night, after Miss Pross had been informed, had wept tears of such abundant and grateful relief that she was quite undone, and had then recovered herself to force a large meal upon the Doctor, that the two old friends undertook their grim task.

When the last spark had vanished into the impenetrable blackness of the London night, and the faint, acrid smell of burnt wood and leather was all that remained of the grim spectre, the two men stood for a moment longer in the profound silence, as if listening for an echo that, blessedly, did not come. He was tired, a weariness that went deeper than the physical exertion; it was the fatigue of a general who had, after a long and brutal campaign, finally routed his most tenacious enemy. Doctor Manette leaned upon Mr. Lorry’s arm, and together they turned and walked back towards the house.

Miss Pross was waiting for them in the little parlour, a single candle burning. She had not pryed, had not watched from any window; her delicacy in this was as absolute as her loyalty. She looked from one man to the other, her sharp eyes taking in the soot on their hands and the grim set of their mouths. She asked no questions, but simply said, in a voice that was gruffer than usual to conceal its trembling, “There is hot wine mulled on the hob. You will both drink some before you sleep.”

It was not a suggestion, but a command, and the two men obeyed her as if they were boys. They sat, sipping the warm, spiced liquid, the silence in the room now peaceful, restorative. The ghost had been exorcised not only from the Doctor’s mind, but from the very air of the house itself. Before they retired, the Doctor placed a hand on Mr. Lorry’s shoulder. “Thank you, my friend,” he said, his voice clear and steady. “For everything. We shall not speak of it again.”

“There is nothing to speak of,” Mr. Lorry replied, understanding him perfectly. “It is done.”

The next morning, before the city was properly awake, they met again in the garden. They had left the pile of charred wood and blackened iron to cool in the night. Now, with a spade from the small tool-shed, Mr. Lorry dug a deep hole in the earth at the foot of the great plane-tree, the very tree whose leaves had cast their soft shadows upon the Doctor’s sleeping face on the eve of his daughter’s wedding. Doctor Manette knelt, and with his own hands, he gathered the ashes and the warped nails, the pathetic remnants of his long confinement, and placed them in the hole. He did it with a strange reverence, as if he were burying not an enemy, but a part of himself that had died and, in its dying, had given him back his life. When all was interred, Mr. Lorry shovelled the earth back, and they patted the ground smooth, leaving no sign of the grave that lay beneath. They had buried the shoemaker of the Bastille in a quiet corner of a London garden, so that Lucie Manette’s husband’s home might be free of his shadow forever.

In the days that followed, a remarkable change was wrought in Doctor Manette. It was not merely that he was recovered, but that he seemed possessed of a new and quiet strength, a fortitude that came not from ignoring his past, but from having finally faced and conquered it. The air of dread and avoidance that had so often clouded his features was gone, replaced by a serene, if profoundly sad, composure. He had, as he had told Lucie, held the mastery of the bitter waters of his captivity, and now, having stared into their depths for nine days and returned, he seemed to have gained an even greater command.

Mr. Lorry, extending his leave from Tellson’s, remained a fixture in the little house, a circumstance to which Miss Pross became so accustomed that his presence was as much a part of the furniture as the chairs. Their conversations, held over breakfast or in the evenings while the Doctor read, were a constant source of gentle friction and unspoken affection.

“Really, Mr. Lorry,” Miss Pross declared one afternoon, finding the banker attempting to dust a row of books. “You will do yourself a mischief. A man of your standing has no business meddling with household dust. Leave it be.”

“My dear Pross,” returned Mr. Lorry, peering at a smudge on his finger through his spectacles, “I am merely endeavouring to be useful. I cannot have you and the Doctor’s man doing everything, while I sit about like a… like a decorative object.”

“You are far more use sitting still,” she retorted. “You are a comfort to him, and to me, though I’ve no doubt you don’t mean to be. Your fussing only puts things in a muddle. There is a system to the dust in this house, and you are disrupting it.”

Mr. Lorry, wisely, abandoned the books. He had come to understand that Miss Pross’s sharp-tongued administration of the household was the very expression of her love; to interfere with it was to question her devotion, a crime of which he would never be guilty. Instead, he drew his chair nearer to the fire and watched his friend. The Doctor was not reading, but looking out of the window, a small, peaceful smile on his face. He seemed to be gaining in health and spirit with every passing hour. He took walks with Mr. Lorry in the afternoons, short at first, then longer, venturing into the busier London streets without any sign of the agitation that had once plagued him. He spoke of politics, of the news from France which was growing ever more disquieting, and of matters of finance, asking Mr. Lorry astute questions about the workings of Tellson’s Bank with a clarity of mind that delighted the old clerk.

The fortnight was drawing to its close when a letter arrived, addressed to the Doctor in Lucie’s beautiful, clear hand. It had been forwarded from their intended address in Warwickshire. Mr. Lorry and Miss Pross watched with a shared, secret anxiety as he broke the seal. They had maintained their careful deceit, and now they dreaded the effect that Lucie’s unsuspecting happiness might have upon him.

He read the letter slowly, his expression unreadable. When he had finished, he let the pages rest in his lap and looked up, and his faithful guardians saw, to their immense relief, that his eyes were shining with tears not of sorrow, but of a profound and overwhelming joy.

“She is well,” he said, his voice thick with emotion. “She is happy. Happier than she can say.” He picked up the letter again, and read a passage aloud. “‘My dearest father, the countryside is so beautiful, but I find I see it all through your eyes, and wish you were here to see it with me. Charles is the best and most considerate of husbands, and his every other thought is for you, and for your comfort. He speaks of you so often, and with such reverence, that I feel you are with us on our walks and in our evenings by the fire. We are counting the moments until you join us, and we may be a family all together.’”

He paused, and had to wipe a tear from his cheek before he could continue. “‘I pray for you every night, my dear father, as I have always done. “Never,” Mr. Lorry affirmed, his own eyes suspiciously moist.

Miss Pross, for her part, made a great show of sniffing disdainfully and marching from the room, but Mr. Lorry, who had grown wise in her ways, knew that she had gone to have a good, grateful cry in the pantry, where no one could see her.

The arrival of the letter seemed to be the final seal upon the Doctor’s recovery. It was the proof he needed that his sacrifice had been worthwhile, that the secret they kept was the very foundation upon which his daughter’s new life was being built. From that day forward, he was not only calm, but cheerful, actively participating in the plans for his own journey to join the newlyweds.

The packing of his trunk was an affair of some ceremony. Miss Pross presided, ensuring that his linen was aired, his coats brushed, and his necessities arranged with a mathematical precision. The Doctor himself took an interest, selecting the books he wished to take, and adding a small, wrapped parcel to the contents.

“And what is this, Doctor?” Mr. Lorry enquired, picking up the neatly tied package.

“A small wedding gift for Charles,” the Doctor explained, a fond smile playing on his lips. “Something from my own library. A collection of philosophical treatises. It is a dry subject, perhaps, for a honeymoon, but he is a scholar, and I thought it might please him.”

The gesture was so normal, so touchingly paternal, that it moved Mr. Lorry more than any grand declaration could have done. It was the Doctor of Beauvais, the respected man of learning, who was packing this gift; the shoemaker was well and truly buried under the plane-tree.

On the final evening before the Doctor’s departure, the three of them sat down to supper, just as they had on the night before the wedding. “Indeed,” said Mr. Lorry, beaming. “It has been our privilege, Manette. Our very great privilege. And to see you now, so well, so strong… it is the only repayment we could ever desire. We shall send you to them in the best health and in the happiest frame, just as I promised.”

The nine days that followed were a period of strange and watchful quiet. The Doctor did not relapse; he did not return to his shoemaking. To all outward appearances, he was calm. He read his books, he walked in the garden, he conversed with Mr. Lorry on matters of philosophy and current events. He spoke of Lucie often, picturing her journey, imagining her delight in her new surroundings. And yet, the calm was a brittle one. Mr. Lorry, ever observant, noted that he rarely slept more than an hour or two at a time. Often, in the dead of night, the banker would hear the soft tread of the Doctor’s feet as he paced his room, a caged, restless sound that went on for hours. He would fall silent at the slightest noise from outside his door, as if he feared being discovered in his private struggle.

During the day, he was never idle. He busied himself with his papers, or with tending the plants in the small garden, working with a dogged, feverish energy that seemed designed to leave no room for thought. But sometimes, in the midst of a conversation, or while turning the page of a book, a shadow would pass over his face, the old air of dread and avoidance that Mr. Lorry had seen on the wedding morning. It was as if a cold wind from some forgotten prison-yard had blown across his soul. At such times, he would fall silent, his brow furrowed, his eyes fixed on nothing, and it would take some gentle prompting from Mr. Lorry, or some sharp, practical remark from Miss Pross, to recall him to himself.

Miss Pross, for her part, maintained a ceaseless, vigilant guard. She seemed to require no sleep at all, and was always present, a gaunt and formidable sentinel. Her love for the Doctor, so long secondary to her adoration of Lucie, now came to the fore, expressing itself in a hundred gruff, practical ways. She saw to it that his meals were to his liking, that his favourite chair was always in its proper place, that no unnecessary noise from the street should disturb him. She was a dragon guarding a fragile treasure, and her sharp eyes missed nothing. She and Mr. Lorry exchanged few words on the subject of their shared anxiety, but a perfect understanding existed between them. They were partners in a sacred trust, and their lives revolved around the ticking of the clock, counting down the days until the Doctor should be safely restored to his daughter’s side.

On the afternoon of the ninth day, as Mr. Lorry sat reading by the window and the Doctor wrote letters at his desk, a sound from the street below broke the accustomed quiet. It was not the usual rumble of a passing cart or the cry of a vendor, but a heavy, shambling footstep, accompanied by a low, intermittent grumbling, as of a man in ill-humoured discourse with himself. The sound stopped directly below, and was followed by a loud, impatient rap at the door, a knock that had none of the civility of a social call, but rather the peremptory summons of one demanding entry on business.

Miss Pross, who had been darning stockings with ferocious concentration, looked up, her brow furrowed. “Who in the world is that?” she muttered. “Making a noise fit to wake the dead.”

Before she could rise, the knock came again, louder this time. Mr. Lorry put down his book. “I will see to it, Miss Pross,” he said. He had a premonition, a faint unease that the sound of those footsteps had awoken. He went downstairs and opened the door.

Standing on the step, his shoulders hunched and a familiar piece of straw protruding from the corner of his mouth, was the odd-job-man from Tellson’s Bank, Mr. Jerry Cruncher. His boots were caked with mud, and his fingers, as he knuckled his forehead in a grudging salute, showed their usual coating of mysterious rust.

“A message for you, Mr. Lorry,” he growled, his voice a gravelly rumble. “From T. and Co.”

“For me?” said Mr. Lorry, his unease sharpening. “Here? Why was it not delivered to my rooms at Clerkenwell?”

“Orders was to bring it here, sir,” said Cruncher, shifting his weight from one foot to the other. “Urgent, they said. From the Paris branch. Arrived by the packet this morning.”

Paris. The word fell into the quiet air of the house like a stone into a still pool. Mr. Lorry’s heart gave a lurch. He took the sealed letter that Cruncher held out to him. “Very well, Cruncher. That will be all. You may go.”

But Mr. Cruncher did not go. He lingered on the doorstep, his small, close-set eyes peering past Mr. Lorry into the hallway. “Begging your pardon, Mr. Lorry, sir,” he said, his tone a mixture of surliness and a strange, prying curiosity. “But hoping the Doctor is keeping well? And the young lady, his daughter?”

“They are perfectly well,” said Mr. Lorry, his manner becoming cold and distant. “There is no need for your concern.”

“Glad to hear it, sir. Very glad,” Cruncher mumbled, though his expression was anything but glad. He craned his neck, as if hoping for a glimpse of the Doctor himself. “It being such a miraculous thing, his being… what you might call… Recalled to Life. A man would want to know such a miracle holds up, as it were.”

The phrase, spoken in that place, was a desecration. It was the password of their secret enterprise, the phrase Mr. Lorry himself had carried in his mind on the Dover mail so long ago, and to hear it now on the coarse tongue of this man, with his graveyard aura and his rusty fingers, was deeply offensive. A wave of anger, sharp and protective, surged through the gentle banker.

“That is Tellson’s business, Cruncher, and mine,” he said, his voice dangerously quiet. “Your business was to deliver the letter. You have done so. Good day.”

He made to close the door, but at that moment, Miss Pross’s voice came sharply from the head of the stairs. “Mr. Lorry? Is there a difficulty?”

Cruncher started, and a look of something like fear passed over his face as he looked up and saw her, a rigid figure of indignation, glaring down at him. “No difficulty, ma’am,” he muttered, backing away from the door at last. “Just delivering my message. An honest tradesman, earning a crust.” With a final, resentful tug at his forelock, he turned and shambled away down the street, his grumbling audible until he rounded the corner.

Mr. Lorry closed the door and bolted it, a gesture he rarely made in the daytime. He leaned against it for a moment, the letter clutched in his hand. The peace of the house had been violated. The grimy, menacing world outside, the world of rusty secrets and unquiet graves, had thrust its unwelcome presence into their sanctuary.

“What was it?” Miss Pross demanded, descending the stairs. “What did that dreadful man want?”

“A letter,” said Mr. Lorry, holding it up. “From Paris.”

He broke the seal and read the brief, coded message. His face grew grave as he did so. The letter was from the head clerk of the Paris branch of Tellson’s. It spoke of an unprecedented volume of clients transferring their properties and assets to the London branch. It mentioned “a general and rising agitation” and advised that all correspondence should be treated with the utmost discretion. The letter concluded with a sentence that made Mr. Lorry’s blood run cold: “Our esteemed customers are departing in such numbers, it is as if they flee a coming plague.”

He looked up from the page to see the Doctor standing at the top of the stairs. He had evidently been drawn from his room by the sound of Cruncher’s knock. His face was pale, and his eyes were fixed on the letter in Mr. Lorry’s hand.

“Is it news?” he asked, his voice quiet but strained. “From France?”

For a second, Mr. Lorry was tempted to lie, to invent some trivial banking matter. But he knew it would be useless. The Doctor’s senses, honed by long years of suffering and suspicion, were too acute. He would detect the falsehood, and the evasion would be more damaging than the truth.

“It is, Manette,” he said, folding the letter and putting it in his pocket. “A business communication from our Paris office. There appears to be… some unrest.”

“Unrest,” the Doctor repeated, the word a flat, dead echo. He descended the stairs slowly, his hand trailing on the banister. He did not ask for details. He did not need to. The single word was enough. The shadow that had haunted him for nine days now seemed to take solid form, filling the little hallway. He looked at Mr. Lorry, and in his eyes was the confirmation of the dread that Charles Darnay’s secret, whatever it was, had instilled in him. It was the look of a man who sees the bars of a cage re-forming around him.

“The tenth day,” the Doctor said, almost to himself. “To-morrow he joins her. He will be with her.” The thought seemed to be a kind of anchor, the one solid point in a swirling sea of fear.

“Yes,” said Mr. Lorry, seizing on it. “To-morrow you go to them. You will be with Lucie, and all will be well. This news from Paris is a long way off, Manette. It has nothing to do with you. Nothing to do with us here.”

But he knew as he spoke that it was not true. The footsteps of the coming Revolution, which he had once imagined as a distant, echoing sea, had now reached their very door, in the form of a grumbling messenger with rust on his fingers. The past was not buried under the plane-tree; it was alive and stirring in Paris, and its shadow stretched all the way to their quiet corner in Soho. The fortnight of waiting was over. The time of watching and fearing had begun again.

Mr. Lorry took the liberty of kissing that hand, as he had kissed the owner’s face; and then he gave her into the keeping of her husband. There was a breakfast, of a quiet and domestic character, that followed, at which Mr. Lorry presided with a fatherly grace, and at which Miss Pross, resolutely dry of eye, served as chief sentinel against any intrusion of melancholy. The Doctor was, to all outward appearance, himself again; he spoke of the future, of the home in Wales to which he would travel, and of the happiness that shone in his daughter’s eyes. Yet Mr. Lorry, whose sixty years of Tellson’s had made him a practiced reader of ledgers far less complex than the human countenance, saw behind the Doctor’s composure the faint, ruled lines of a terrible accounting. He saw the effort it cost the man to hold his head high; the minute tightening of the jaw when Charles Darnay spoke; the flicker in the eye that betrayed a mind listening for a sound no one else could hear.

When the time came for the young couple to depart, the Doctor held his daughter in a long embrace, his lips moving silently against her hair. He was blessing her, as he had promised he would. He then turned and shook his son-in-law’s hand, a grip that was firm and steady, yet to Mr. Lorry’s observant eye, conveyed a message of desperate, fragile trust.

“You will guard her, Charles,” he said, his voice low, but clear in the quiet room. “With your life.”

“With my life, sir,” Charles Darnay answered, his face as pale as the Doctor’s had been at the church door.

And so they were gone. The sound of the carriage wheels died away down the street, leaving a silence in the house that was deeper and more unnerving than the nine days of hammering had been. Miss Pross, her duty done, immediately declared her intention of putting the entire house to rights, as if a vigorous campaign against dust could ward off the more intangible spectres that now gathered in the corners. Mr. Lorry, left alone with the Doctor, found himself at a loss for words. The business of the day was concluded; the bill was paid; the entry was made. What remained was the human balance, and it seemed to him a fearful one.

The Doctor had walked to the window, and was staring out at the plane-tree, its leaves stirring in the afternoon breeze. “She is on her way to a new life, Lorry,” he said, without turning. “A life of her own.”

“And a very happy one it will be, Manette,” replied Mr. Lorry, with as much cheer as he could muster. “With a devoted husband, whom she loves dearly.”

A slight shudder passed through the Doctor’s frame, a movement so faint that only a friend’s anxious eye would have marked it. “Yes,” he said. “Whom she loves dearly. It is the nature of a good heart to love. It is its strength, and its greatest vulnerability.” He turned from the window, and his face was calm again, the mask of composure perfectly restored. “You have a journey of your own to prepare for, my friend. You are to join them in a fortnight.”

“We are to join them,” Mr. Lorry corrected him, gently. “You and I, Manette. And Miss Pross. A holiday in Wales. Green hills and fresh air. It will do us all a world of good.”

“Yes,” the Doctor said, the word a mere breath. “A world of good.” He walked towards the door of his own room, his step measured and unnaturally steady. “I find I am a little tired. The day’s excitements. I think I will rest.”

He did not go to his bench. Mr. Lorry listened, his ear sharpened by dread, but heard no sound but the quiet click of the closing door. The silence that followed was absolute. Miss Pross, clattering with plates in the kitchen, was making a sound of determined, defiant life. But Mr. Lorry, standing in the centre of the parlour, felt as though the whole house were holding its breath, waiting. The nine days were over; the relapse was ended. But he had the terrible conviction that it was not a cure, only a reprieve. The secret that Charles Darnay had confessed on his wedding morning had not been buried with the shoemaker’s tools; it had been planted, a seed of dragon’s teeth, and Mr. Lorry feared that the news he now carried in his pocket was the rain that would make it grow.

The days that followed were quiet, but charged with an unspoken tension. Mr. Lorry remained in the house in Soho, a situation quite unprecedented in the annals of Tellson’s Bank, but one which he felt was his undelegable duty. He was a sentry, posted by his own affections. He and Miss Pross formed a tacit alliance, a two-person garrison whose sole purpose was the well-being of the Doctor. They spoke to him with a careful cheerfulness; they engaged him in conversation about the weather, about the price of goods, about Mr. Lorry’s reminiscences of old London; they spoke of anything and everything, except France. That country, and all that pertained to it, was a forbidden territory, a dark continent on their conversational map.

The Doctor, for his part, was a model of placid convalescence. He read his books, he walked in the small garden, he conversed with them at meals. He even, on one occasion, made a quiet joke that sent Miss Pross into a flustered state of gratification. But the watchfulness never left him. He was a man listening. When the post-boy’s horn sounded in the street, his head would lift. When a stranger knocked at the door, a stillness would fall upon him. He was waiting, as he had waited for eighteen years in his prison, for a footstep on the stair, a key in the lock. Mr. Lorry saw it, and Miss Pross, who knew nothing of politics but everything of the human heart, felt it like a change in the air.

“He is not himself, Mr. Lorry,” she said one evening, as they sat alone, the Doctor having retired early to his room. “He is as gentle as a lamb, and as quiet as a mouse, but there is a look in his eye. It is the look he had when you first brought him here. The look of a man who is lost, and is afraid of being found.”

“He has had a great shock, my Pross,” Mr. Lorry said, polishing his spectacles, a gesture of his that often accompanied a troubled mind. “The marriage of his only child, it is a great event. It stirs up the past.”

“It is not the marriage,” she retorted, her knitting needles clicking like tiny, indignant weapons. “My Ladybird’s happiness could bring him nothing but joy. It is something else. It is something to do with that Mr. Darnay. For all his fine manners and his handsome face, I have never fully trusted him. A Frenchman! There is a darkness about him.”

“He is a good man, Pross. He loves Lucie with all his heart.”

“He may love her, but he has brought a shadow with him. I saw the Doctor’s face on the wedding morning. I saw it. It was the face of a man who had seen a ghost. And whatever that ghost was, it has not gone away. It is sitting in that room with him now.”

Mr. Lorry had no answer for this, for it was the very echo of his own fears. The letter from the Paris branch lay in the locked cash-box he kept in his room, a venomous secret. He had received another, two days since, more urgent than the first. It spoke of bread riots, of the sacking of a wallpaper manufactory, of pamphlets distributed in the streets at night, of a name being whispered from group to group in the poorer quarters: Defarge. The name meant nothing to Mr. Lorry, but the tone of the letter was one of rising panic. Tellson’s Paris was no longer merely losing clients; it was becoming a fortress in a hostile city.

On the ninth day after the wedding, Jerry Cruncher arrived with a small, mud-spattered portmanteau and word that the mail-coach for Warwickshire would depart from the city on the morrow. Preparations began in earnest. Miss Pross packed for the Doctor and for herself with formidable efficiency, assembling linen, coats, and small jars of medicaments as if equipping an expedition into a wilderness, which, in her opinion, any place outside of London was. Mr. Lorry attended to his own smaller luggage, his mind a whirl of anxieties. The journey was meant to be a joyful reunion, the happy closing of the circle. Now, it felt like a retreat, a flight.

That evening, as the three of them sat at their last supper before the trip, the Doctor was more animated than he had been all week. He spoke of Warwickshire, of the pastoral scenery, and, above all, of Lucie.

“She writes that they are walking every day,” he said, a genuine smile lighting his face. “She says the country air is a marvel, and that Charles is an excellent guide. She is happy, Lorry. Think of it. After all the shadows, her life is filled with sunshine.”

“As it deserves to be,” said Mr. Lorry, raising his glass. “To Lucie, and her happiness.”

“To Lucie,” the Doctor echoed, his voice full. Miss Pross, for her part, sniffed and muttered something about her Ladybird deserving all the sunshine in the firmament and a good deal more besides.

For a moment, the cloud seemed to lift. They were just a small, affectionate family, on the eve of a holiday. The room was warm, the lamp-light soft, and the terrors of the world seemed far away. But as they were rising from the table, there came a heavy, insistent knocking at the front door.

Every head turned. It was a late hour for a casual caller. Jerry Cruncher had long since departed for his own mysterious lodgings. Miss Pross looked at Mr. Lorry with alarm. The Doctor stood perfectly still, his hand resting on the back of his chair, his face a mask of rigid inquiry.

Mr. Lorry, his heart thudding with a nameless apprehension, went to the door himself. On the step stood a man, cloaked and travel-stained, his face obscured by the brim of his hat.

“Mr. Jarvis Lorry?” the man asked, his English accented.

“I am he,” said Mr. Lorry, his business-like manner a shield against his fear.

The man produced a sealed letter. “From Tellson’s Bank, in Paris. Urgent.”

The word hung in the air of the little hall like a sentence of doom. Mr. Lorry took the letter. His hand was trembling slightly. He could feel the Doctor’s eyes on him from the parlour doorway.

“Thank you. There is no reply,” he said, and closed the door, sliding the bolt with a sharp, definitive crack.

He turned, the letter in his hand. The Doctor had not moved. His face was white, all the brief, happy colour of the evening drained from it.

“News?” the Doctor asked. The single word was not a question, but a statement of dreadful certainty. “From France?”

There was no point in dissembling. The arrival of the messenger, the lateness of the hour, the very air of crisis had betrayed him. “Yes, Manette,” Mr. Lorry said, his own voice sounding thin and distant. He did not open the letter. He did not need to. He knew its contents would only be a darker shade of the same story. A plague of madness, sweeping the city.

The Doctor came slowly out of the parlour. He looked at the sealed letter in Mr. Lorry’s hand as a man might look at a weapon. He did not ask what was in it. His old prison-honed senses, the instincts that could read the meaning in a footfall or a distant cry, told him all he needed to know. The past was not a ghost; it was a living, breathing monster, and it had found his scent.

“To-morrow,” the Doctor said, his voice a low monotone, as if speaking to himself. “We go to her to-morrow. I must be with her.”

“Yes, Manette. To-morrow,” Mr. Lorry affirmed, his heart aching with pity and fear. “Nothing has changed. We will go to Warwickshire. You will be with Lucie.”

The Doctor nodded slowly, his eyes fixed on some point in the middle distance, a point some two hundred miles away, in Paris. “She must be kept safe,” he whispered. “She must never know the darkness. Never.”

He turned and, without another word, walked to his room, not with the measured tread of the past week, but with the shuffling, inward-drawn gait of the shoemaker of the North Tower. Mr. Lorry heard the door close, and then the faint, unmistakable sound of a key turning in the lock.

Miss Pross was at his elbow, her face grim. “What is it? What bad news does that letter bring?”

Mr. Lorry broke the seal. The light from the hall lamp was poor, but the words seemed to leap from the page, written in a frantic, spidery hand. It was not a report; it was a plea. The head clerk of the Paris branch was sending his wife and daughter to England. Other members of the staff were doing likewise. The political clubs had taken over entire districts. An arsenal had been stormed. The letter ended with a sentence that made Mr. Lorry’s blood run cold. “The popular cry is for arms, and they have found a list of the nobility. The name St. Evrémonde is upon it. God help any who bear that name, for the people will not.”

St. Evrémonde. The name Charles Darnay had revealed to the Doctor on his wedding day. The name that had sent him back to his shoemaker’s bench.

“Mr. Lorry!” Miss Pross insisted, her hand gripping his arm. “Tell me!”

He folded the letter and put it deep into his pocket. “It is… it is a confirmation of the unrest, my Pross. Nothing more. Business matters.” He could not tell her the truth. He could not speak the name. The knowledge was a burden he must bear alone, for now. “We must be ready to leave at first light,” he said, his voice all business once more. “The Doctor needs to be with his daughter. That is the most important thing in the world right now.”

But as he ascended the stairs to his own room, the polished wood of the banister cold beneath his hand, he felt the terrible weight of his own words. Keeping the Doctor with his daughter was important. But he now knew, with a chilling certainty, that the true and terrible task would be to keep her husband, Charles St. Evrémonde, from the long, grasping reach of a past that had, at last, bared its teeth. The holiday in Warwickshire was no longer a holiday. It was a council of war, and they were already under siege.



The dawn, when it came, was a reluctant visitor to the little house in Soho; it sent pale, watery feelers of light through the upper windows, as if uncertain of its welcome. Mr. Lorry had been a witness to its entire, timid arrival. He had not slept; or if he had, it was a shallow and unrefreshing state, a mere skim of oblivion over a churning sea of anxieties. The letter from Paris lay not in his pocket, but seared upon his mind; its frantic sentences, its spidery script, and the damning name of St. Evrémonde were characters of fire that had burned through the flimsy fabric of the night. He sat in a chair by his window, fully dressed, having long since abandoned the pretence of rest, and watched the world outside slowly assume its familiar shapes. A cart rumbled by on its way to market. A lone bird began its morning interrogations from the plane-tree. All was ordinary, all was peaceful; and the contrast with the tempest in his own breast, and the silent, locked room down the hall, was a torment.

He was a man of business, and his business was facts and figures, ledgers and balances. Yet here he was, trafficking in secrets that defied calculation, in emotions that could not be entered into any book. His obligation to the late Mr. Manette, an obligation he had thought discharged with the doctor’s miraculous recovery and his daughter’s happiness, had returned with the force of an old debt, long forgotten but now called in with ruinous interest. He thought of Lucie and Charles, so young, so bright, so utterly undeserving of the storm that was gathering. They were in Warwickshire, in a green and pleasant land, under a summer sky; they were living the idyll that he and the Doctor and Miss Pross had so painstakingly built for them. And he, Jarvis Lorry, was the gatekeeper who must now, somehow, prevent the wolves of the past from bursting through and devouring it all.

A creak on the stair announced Miss Pross. She descended not with her usual martial tread, but with a softer, more cautious step, as if the house itself were a patient in a fever. She carried a tray, upon which sat a single cup of tea and a slice of dry toast; sustenance for the long day ahead. Her face, in the thin morning light, was more gaunt than ever, but her eyes, when they met Mr. Lorry’s, were not questioning, but resolved.

“There is no sound from his room,” she said, her voice a low rumble. “I have listened at the door. Not a sound.”

“He has locked it, my Pross,” Mr. Lorry answered, rising. His joints ached with the chill of his vigil.

“I know it. I heard the key turn last night. It was a sound I had prayed to God I should never hear again in this house.” She set the tray down on a small table with a decisive clatter. “We are to leave at first light, you said. The coach is ordered for the hour. Are we to break down the door to fetch him?”

“Patience, my dear lady. Patience is our watchword now.” Mr. Lorry took a step towards the Doctor’s door, his own heart beating a nervous tattoo against his ribs. He felt the heavy responsibility of his knowledge; he alone knew the specific nature of the poison that had re-entered the Doctor’s mind. It was not merely the unrest in Paris; it was the ghost of a name, the spectre of a lineage, a thing so terrible it had shattered the man once and could do so again.

He raised his hand and knocked, softly at first, then with more firmness. “Doctor? Manette? It is Lorry. The morning is here, and we have a journey to make.”

There was a silence that seemed to stretch for an eternity, a void into which Mr. Lorry’s fears rushed, filling it with dreadful possibilities: the vacant chair, the half-finished shoe, the man gone and the prisoner returned. Miss Pross stood behind him, her hands clasped so tightly her knuckles were white, her breath held.

Then, from within the room, came the sound they had both dreaded and prayed for: the turning of the key in the lock. The door opened, not wide, but a few inches, and Doctor Manette stood there.

He was dressed. He was washed and his white hair was combed. But his face was a mask of unnatural composure. The deep lines that captivity had carved were there, but the active, resolute struggle that Lucie had so admired was gone; in its place was a blank, fixed placidity, as if his features had been smoothed by a hand other than his own. His eyes did not quite meet Mr. Lorry’s, but seemed to look through him, towards a destination already settled in his mind.

“I am ready,” he said, his voice flat and devoid of inflection. “We must not keep Lucie waiting.”

It was this single, animating idea that propelled them. The Doctor moved as if guided by this one powerful, internal compass. He ate the toast Miss Pross offered, not as if he were hungry, but as if it were a necessary preparation for his task. He allowed Mr. Lorry to help him with his coat, not with the easy familiarity of their recent life, but with the passive compliance of one who is being conducted from one place to another. Through all the small rituals of departure, he was with them, but not with them. His spirit was already on the road to Warwickshire, a silent, determined outrider, posted to guard his daughter.

The journey in the coach was a strange and silent affair. Miss Pross sat bolt upright, a sentinel of affection, her gaze alternating between the blankly staring Doctor and the passing countryside, which she appeared to hold in deep suspicion. Mr. Lorry, seated opposite, made a few attempts at conversation, remarks on the weather, the state of the roads, the quality of the inns they passed; but his words fell into the carriage’s deep well of silence and were lost. The Doctor sat by the window, his hands resting on his knees, his eyes fixed on the blur of green hedges and ripening fields. He did not speak a word.

For Mr. Lorry, the rhythmic clatter of the horses’ hooves and the rumble of the wheels became a kind of torturous refrain. With every mile that drew them closer to Lucie, the weight of his secret grew heavier. St. Evrémonde. The name echoed in his mind, a name of aristocratic pride and peasant suffering, a name steeped in blood and guilt. He pictured Charles, his honest, handsome face, his gentle manner; a man so utterly different from the cruel marquises who were his forebears. And yet, the blood was the same. The name was the same. And in the fevered mind of revolutionary Paris, such distinctions were worth less than dust. He, Jarvis Lorry, an agent of Tellson’s Bank, was now in the position of shielding a French nobleman, a proscribed St. Evrémonde, from the consequences of a history he had personally renounced. It was a matter of business so far outside the scope of Tellson’s that it verged on the absurd; and yet, it was a matter of life and death.

They arrived late in the afternoon at the little country inn where the young couple had made their nest. It was a picture of English tranquility: a rambling stone building draped in ivy, with roses climbing the porch and the lazy hum of bees in the air. The contrast with the grim news from Paris was so violent, so profound, that for a moment Mr. Lorry felt as if he had stepped into another world, a painted scene from a book of fables.

And then the fable came to life. Lucie, hearing the sound of the coach, came running from the garden gate, her face alight with surprise and joy, her golden hair catching the sun. Charles was a pace behind her, smiling, the very image of a happy bridegroom.

“Father! Mr. Lorry! And my dear Pross! What are you doing here? Is everything well? We did not expect you for a fortnight!”

Lucie’s eyes, those clear blue pools of love and concern, went straight to her father. The radiant smile on her face faltered, clouded by the first shadow of doubt. She saw, with the unerring instinct of the heart, what Mr. Lorry had seen that morning. She saw the careful stillness, the guarded emptiness in his eyes.

“Father?” she said again, her voice softer now, her hand reaching for his. “Are you not well? You are pale from your journey.”

The Doctor took her hand, and a flicker of life, of genuine feeling, returned to his face. He held her hand not as a father greeting his child, but as a drowning man grasping a spar. “I am well, my Lucie. I am well, now that I am with you. I wished… we wished to see you.”

Charles came forward, clapping Mr. Lorry on the shoulder with a cheerful warmth that made the old banker’s heart ache. “This is a splendid surprise! We have been wondering how to fill our days, and now here you are to fill them for us. Come in, come in! You must be parched and weary.”

As they were ushered into the cool, low-ceilinged parlour of the inn, Lucie did not release her father’s hand. She walked beside him, her head tilted, studying his face, her own expression a mixture of love, confusion, and a dawning, familiar fear. She was the golden thread, and she felt the old, dark tangle beginning to pull at the man she had rescued from it.

That evening, a fiction was maintained. A fiction of a pleasant, impromptu visit, of a father who missed his daughter, of old friends wishing to share in her new happiness. They sat for a simple supper in the inn’s private dining room. Charles was in high spirits, recounting their country excursions, teasing Miss Pross about her formidable demeanour frightening the local sheep, and trying to draw his father-in-law into the conversation.

“And you, sir,” he said, raising his glass, “we must show you the old castle ruins tomorrow. The air is sovereign, and the view is one to restore any man’s spirits.”

“Yes,” the Doctor said, his gaze fixed on Lucie. “To-morrow.” He drank, but his eyes never left his daughter’s face. He seemed to be memorising her features, absorbing her presence like a man dying of thirst.

Later, when the ladies had retired, and Miss Pross had been posted like a grenadier in the hallway outside the Doctor’s room (for he had not locked his door, a fact which she and Mr. Lorry regarded as a victory hard-won), Mr. Lorry found himself alone with Charles before the fire. The time had come for him to act, to deploy the caution and strategy that were the hallmarks of Tellson’s, but in a cause far more personal.

“Charles,” he began, stirring the logs with a poker, a man of business preparing his ground. “There is a matter I feel it my duty to speak of.”

“Indeed, Mr. Lorry?” Charles replied, his mood still light. “If it concerns my account, I fear my new station as a husband has made me a profligate.”

“No, not that. It is… it is a matter of prudence, concerning your connections in France.” Mr. Lorry kept his eyes on the fire. It was easier than looking at the young man’s open, unsuspecting face. “The news from the Paris branch is disquieting. The popular temper is much inflamed. There is a great deal of… of unfortunate sentiment directed at those who have left France, particularly those of noble birth.”

He felt Charles stiffen beside him. “I have no connection to France that matters, save what is in the past. I am a citizen of England now, an obscure tutor of the French language.”

“Just so. Obscurity, in these times, is a blessing. And it is a blessing that must be cultivated.” Mr. Lorry put down the poker and turned, his expression grave. “I would counsel you, Charles, as your friend and as a man of business who understands the risks of the world, to be exceedingly careful. Let no correspondence pass between yourself and France. If any should reach you, I would urge you to send it, unopened, to me at Tellson’s. We have… channels for dealing with such things. Let your name, the name you have made for yourself here, be the only one you answer to. The past must be a closed book, its covers sealed.”

Charles looked at him, a frown of puzzlement on his brow. He sensed the gravity in Mr. Lorry’s tone, but could not grasp the shape of the danger it implied. “Is the situation so serious, then?”

“It is volatile,” Mr. Lorry said, choosing his word with care. “And volatile situations require the utmost discretion. This is for Lucie’s sake, as much as your own. Her happiness is tied to your safety. We are all her guardians now.”

The mention of Lucie was the masterstroke. Charles’s expression softened. The vague warning, which might have been dismissed as the over-cautiousness of an old banker, was now framed in the light of his greatest love and responsibility.

“You are right, of course, Mr. Lorry. I shall be as obscure as a mouse in a wainscoting. My only business is here, with my wife.” He smiled, the shadow passing from his face. “And now, I think my wife will be wondering where I have got to. Will you take another glass?”

“No, my boy. No more for me,” said Mr. Lorry, feeling the exhaustion of a hundred years. “I must get what rest I can.”

As he ascended the stairs to his own chamber, he passed the Doctor’s door. It was slightly ajar, and a low light spilled into the hall. Peeking through the crack, he saw a scene that stopped his breath. The Doctor was seated in a chair, fast asleep, his head drooped to one side. And on a low stool at his feet, her head resting on his knees and her hand holding his, was Lucie. She too had fallen asleep in her vigil, the golden thread physically, tenaciously, holding her father to the shores of reason. Mr. Lorry looked at them, the rescued and the rescuer, bound together in sorrow and in love. He saw the sleeping father, whose mind held a secret that could destroy the husband. He saw the sleeping daughter, whose love was the only shield they had. And he knew, with a certainty that was colder and harder than any fact in Tellson’s ledgers, that the council of war in Warwickshire had only just begun. The siege was not a matter of a day or a night; it was the new condition of their lives.

The moment of departure, so long anticipated and, by some, so deeply dreaded, was upon them. The chariot, a handsome if not extravagant vehicle hired by Mr. Lorry with a reverence for the occasion that bordered on the religious, stood waiting. The horses, adorned with white ribbons that fluttered in the gentle breeze, stamped their feet upon the quiet street, their impatience a strange counterpoint to the suspended emotion of the little group at the door.
Lucie turned to her father. The deadly pallor that had so alarmed Mr. Lorry was still there, a ghostly under-painting beneath the Doctor’s effort at a serene smile. He held himself with a rigid straightness, as a man who feared that to relax a single muscle was to collapse entirely.
“Father,” she said, her own voice trembling but a little, “you will be well? You will take care, and think of me, and be happy in my happiness?”
She threw her arms about his neck, and for a moment he was lost in the embrace, his face buried in her bright golden hair. Those who watched, and there were only two who watched with understanding, saw his hands clench at his sides before they rose, slowly, as if lifting an immense weight, to pat her back.
“My darling child,” he said, and his voice was a marvel of control, a thin sheet of ice over a raging sea. “I am more than happy; I am blessed beyond any hope I ever dared to have in my darkest hours. Go with your husband. Be the light of his life, as you have been the resurrection of mine. There is no care I need take, but to count the days until I see you again.”
He kissed her forehead, a solemn, sacramental act, and then gently disengaged himself, turning her towards her husband. Charles Darnay, whose own face was a study in concealed anxiety, took her hand. He met the Doctor’s eyes for a brief, unreadable second, a silent exchange of knowledge and of pain that passed far above Lucie’s innocent head. He bowed, a gesture of profound respect and, perhaps, of profound apology, before leading his bride to the carriage door.
Miss Pross was next. She seized Lucie in a hug so fierce it was more like a capture, her gaunt frame shaking with suppressed sobs that found no outlet in tears. “My Ladybird! My precious! To think of you going off with… with him!” she whispered, a last, defiant shot at the bridegroom. “You write to me, you hear? You tell me everything. If he is not the best of husbands to you, I will come to Warwickshire myself, and I will… I will give him a piece of my mind!”
“I will, I will, my dear Pross,” Lucie laughed, though tears now mingled with her smiles. “And he will be. I know he will.”
Mr. Lorry, ever the master of ceremonies, stepped forward to hand Lucie into the chariot. “Our love and our prayers go with you, my dear,” he said, his voice thick with a genuine, fatherly emotion that decades at Tellson’s had not been able to erode. “May your life be as bright as you have made ours.”
He shut the door with a decisive click. The driver cracked his whip, the wheels grated on the stones, and the chariot moved away, gathering speed as it turned the corner. Lucie’s face, framed in the window, a vision of hopeful, smiling beauty, lingered for a moment and was gone.
For a full minute, the three left behind stood upon the pavement as if frozen. The sound of the departing wheels faded into the general hum of London, leaving a silence that was vast and terrible. The play was over; the audience had departed.
Then, without a word, without a glance at his companions, Doctor Manette turned and walked back into the house. His step was unnervingly steady, his back ramrod straight. The front door, which had stood open, was closed by his hand, the sound of the latch clicking into place as final as a prison gate.
Miss Pross and Mr. Lorry exchanged a look. It was a look of shared, unspoken terror. The mask of cheerful composure had not slipped from the Doctor; it had been shattered, and what they had just witnessed was a man walking away in the myriad, glittering fragments.
“Mr. Lorry,” said Miss Pross, her voice low and harsh, all its matter-of-fact quality sharpened into a weapon. “What was it? What did that man, her husband, say to him in that room?”
“I do not know,” answered Mr. Lorry, his gaze fixed on the closed door, his ruddy complexion faded to a pasty grey. “I only know the effect. We must see to him.”
They entered the house. A strange stillness had fallen. The little supper table, with its three cleared places and the affectionate toast to an absent Charles still hanging in the air, seemed a relic from another age. The sunlight, slanting through the window, illuminated dust motes dancing in a world that had, in a quarter of an hour, grown old and fearful.
They ascended the stairs, their footfalls unnaturally loud in the quiet. Mr. Lorry’s heart, a reliable and business-like organ, was hammering against his ribs in a most disorderly fashion. He approached the Doctor’s chamber. The door was shut. He knocked, softly at first.
“Doctor? Manette, my dear friend? It is Lorry.”
There was no reply. Only a faint, rhythmic sound from within. A soft, persistent tapping.
Tap. Tap. Tap.
The sound struck Mr. Lorry with a physical force, as if the hammer were striking not leather, but his own breast. He knew that sound. He had heard it in his dreams, a dreadful echo from a garret in Paris. He pushed the door open.
The room was in a dim shadow, the curtains partly drawn. And there, in the corner, on the low, rough stool that had not been seen for years, sat the Doctor. His back was to them, his shoulders hunched. Before him stood the old, disused shoemaker’s bench, which had been stored away as a melancholy curiosity. Upon it was a half-finished lady’s shoe. In his hand was the hammer, rising and falling with a dreadful, mechanical regularity upon the sole.
He was utterly absorbed, his whole being contracted into the small, repetitive task. He was no longer Doctor Manette, the respected physician, the resurrected father. He was a haggard, white-haired shoemaker, lost in the phantom gloom of the Bastille.
“Good God,” whispered Mr. Lorry, his hand flying to his mouth.
Miss Pross pushed past him, her face a mask of disbelief and fury. “Doctor!” she cried, her voice sharp enough to cut glass. “What is this folly? Stop that this instant! Your daughter is married! You have a life! Look at me!”
The shoemaker did not look up. He did not seem to hear. His hammer continued its implacable work. Tap. Tap. Tap.
Mr. Lorry gently drew Miss Pross back. “Hush, my dear Pross. Hush. It is the old malady. It has returned. Shouting will not reach him now. He is… he is far away.”
“Away?” she retorted, her voice a choked hiss. “He is here! In this room! Making shoes! On her wedding-day! It is a monstrous crime! That man, Darnay, has done this. He told him something that has broken him again.”
“It is probable, it is more than probable,” said Mr. Lorry, his mind racing, trying to impose the order of business upon this chaos of the soul. “But recrimination is a luxury we cannot afford. The first duty, our only duty, is to Lucie. She must not know of this. Not for a single moment of her honeymoon. We gave our word that her father would be safe and happy in our care.”
He looked from the grim, indomitable face of Miss Pross to the hunched, oblivious figure at the bench. A fortnight. Fourteen days. They had promised to keep him safe for fourteen days. It seemed, at that moment, a task more colossal than balancing the ledgers of all the Tellson’s branches in Christendom.
“She must have her peace,” Miss Pross affirmed, her loyalty to her Ladybird a bedrock in this shifting nightmare. “She has earned it. But what are we to do? We cannot leave him like this.”
“No,” said Mr. Lorry, taking a steadying breath. “We cannot. We will become his keepers. You and I, Pross. We will watch him, by turns. We will speak to him. We will wait. This is a relapse, a terrible one, but we have seen him recover before. We must have faith that the mind, having found its way back once, can find its way back again.”
And so, the strange and secret vigil began. That very day, a message was dispatched to the servants below, explaining that the Doctor was overcome by the emotion of the day and had retired to his room, desiring absolute quiet and to be attended only by Mr. Lorry and Miss Pross. It was a thin story, but in that house of fierce loyalties, it was enough.
The first day passed into night. Mr. Lorry took the first watch. He sat in a chair in the Doctor’s room, the book he had brought to read lying unopened in his lap. The only sound was the dreadful, metronomic tapping of the hammer, a sound that seemed to measure out the slow destruction of a noble mind. Mr. Lorry tried to speak to him, in a calm, reasonable tone.
“My dear Manette,” he would say, “this is an occupation unworthy of you. Let us put these tools away. Let us take a turn about the room and speak of… of accounts. Of the new policies at Tellson’s.”
The shoemaker would sometimes pause, look up with vacant eyes, and murmur a number. “One Hundred and Five.” Then he would shake his head, as if vexed by the interruption, and resume his work.
When Miss Pross came to relieve him in the dead of night, she found Mr. Lorry slumped in his chair, his face looking a hundred years old. She carried a small tray with a cup of hot broth for the patient, and a glass of wine for the banker. The shoemaker ignored the broth. Mr. Lorry drank the wine as if it were medicine.
“Go and rest,” she commanded, her tone leaving no room for argument. “There is nothing to be gained by two of us falling to pieces.”
The second day was as the first. And the third. They established a routine born of desperate necessity. They spoke to each other in whispers in the hallway. They ate their meals separately, one always standing guard. The greatest and most painful task fell to Mr. Lorry on the fourth day: the composition of a letter to Lucie in Warwickshire.
He sat at his little travelling-desk, the paper before him a glaring, accusatory white. How to fill it with cheerful falsehoods? How to paint a picture of domestic tranquillity and paternal contentment, when the reality was a madhouse of two rooms? He dipped his pen, and, with the same meticulous care he would have used to draft a sensitive financial instrument for Tellson’s Bank, he began to lie.
“Your dear father,” he wrote, “is in excellent spirits, though he misses you, as is only natural. He speaks of you constantly. Miss Pross and I are taking the best of care of him, and he spends his days reading and taking gentle walks with me in the garden when the sun is out.”
Every word was a betrayal of the truth, and yet every word was a testament to a deeper loyalty. He read the letter to Miss Pross, who nodded her grim approval. “It will do,” she said. “Her heart must not be broken.”
The days wore on. Five. Six. Seven. The little pile of finished and half-finished shoes in the corner grew. They were all of the same pattern: a young lady’s walking shoe. To Mr. Lorry, it was as if he were manufacturing tangible pieces of his own despair. They tried every expedient they could devise. They moved the bench while he slept, but he awoke in a state of silent, haggard agitation, pacing the room until they restored it to its place. They tried reading to him, passages from the books Lucie loved, but the words washed over him, unheard.
His physical health began to suffer. He ate little, taking only what Miss Pross could coax into him, and his face grew more gaunt, the skin stretched tight over his handsome bones. The spectre of the Bastille was reclaiming him, body and soul.
On the evening of the eighth day, Mr. Lorry felt a despair so profound it threatened to overwhelm him. He was seated on his watch, the candle-flame making the shadows in the room leap and dance. Nine days. They had promised Lucie a fortnight. The ninth day would dawn in a few hours, and their time was running out. Failure loomed, a prospect more terrifying than any financial ruin.
In his desperation, he abandoned the gentle reasoning he had been attempting. He walked over to the bench and stood directly before the Doctor, blocking his light.
The hammer faltered. The shoemaker looked up, his eyes clouded with annoyance.
“Listen to me,” Mr. Lorry said, his voice low and intense, devoid of all its usual fussy gentility. It was the voice of a man of business issuing a final, non-negotiable demand. “Your name is Alexandre Manette. You were a physician in Beauvais. You were a prisoner in the Bastille for eighteen years. But you were released. You were recalled to life.”
The shoemaker stared at him, a flicker of something unreadable in his eyes. He put down his hammer.
Encouraged, Mr. Lorry pressed on, his words coming faster. “You have a daughter. Her name is Lucie. She is the golden thread that pulled you from the abyss. You love her more than your life. You live for her. It was she who rescued you, she who restored you.” He gestured around the quiet house. “This is your home. In Soho. In London. You have been free for years. Years.”
The man at the bench passed a trembling hand over his own brow, a gesture of profound confusion, as if trying to wipe away a fog that clouded his very soul.
“Lucie,” Mr. Lorry said, his voice softening as he spoke the name. It was the key, it had to be the key. “Your Lucie. Your darling. She was married. Eight days ago. To a good man, Charles Darnay. You gave her away yourself. She is on her honeymoon. She will be back. She expects to find her father. Her strong, brilliant father. Not… not this.”
He did not need to gesture at the bench, at the pathetic leather scraps. The word hung in the air, full of sorrow and judgement.
The Doctor’s gaze drifted from Mr. Lorry’s face to his own hands, calloused and stained from the unfamiliar work. He looked at the half-made shoe. He looked around the room, at the familiar furniture, the books, the pictures on the wall, as if seeing them for the first time. His eyes were no longer vacant. They were filled with a dawning, horrified comprehension.
“The wedding,” he whispered, his voice a dry rasp. “Was I… was I there?”
Tears of pure, unadulterated relief sprang to Mr. Lorry’s eyes. He fought them back, knowing the slightest misstep could send the fragile mind tumbling back into darkness.
“You were,” he said, his own voice steady as a rock. “You were the proudest father in all of England. You were magnificent.”
Doctor Manette looked down at the shoemaker’s bench, at the tools laid out so neatly, and a shudder ran through his entire body. “What day is it, Lorry?” he asked, his voice weak but perfectly lucid.
“It is the morning of the ninth day,” Mr. Lorry replied.
“Nine days,” the Doctor murmured, closing his eyes. “Have I been… unwell for nine days?”
“You have been tired,” Mr. Lorry said, choosing his words with infinite care. “The wedding was a great strain. You have needed to rest. Miss Pross and I have been looking after you.”
The Doctor said nothing for a long time. He simply sat, his head in his hands. The shoemaker was gone. Doctor Manette, weary, broken, but himself, had returned. The long siege was over. The city of his mind, so nearly retaken by the enemy, had been held.

The silence that followed was of a different quality than the one that had reigned for the better part of a week; it was not the silence of vacancy, but the silence of a mind returning to a house long shuttered, and finding it in disarray. The shoemaker was gone, but the ghost of his labour remained, a palpable presence in the little garret room. Doctor Manette raised his head from his hands, and the movement was that of a man aged by a decade in the space of a minute. He looked at Mr. Lorry, and in his eyes, where before there had been a terrible blankness, there now resided a profound and weary sorrow.

“Lorry,” he said, and his voice was his own, though thin as thread, “you are a good friend.”

Mr. Lorry, who had been holding his breath as if the slightest exhalation might shatter the fragile recovery, let it out in a long, unsteady sigh. He could not speak; he could only nod, his periwig slightly askew, his kindly face a battlefield of relief and concern.

“How long,” the Doctor asked, his gaze dropping to the leather in his lap, to the hammer and the knife on the bench beside him. He did not need to finish the question.

“To-day is the ninth day,” Mr. Lorry answered, his voice gaining its customary steadiness. He chose, with the instinct of a man of business navigating a delicate transaction, to present the facts as an ordinary matter of account. “You were taken unwell, my dear friend, on the evening of the wedding. A nervous exhaustion, a prostration of the spirits, entirely understandable after such a momentous day. Miss Pross and I have been your attendants. We gave out that you were not to be disturbed, that you required absolute repose.”

A spasm of something like shame crossed the Doctor’s features. He looked at his hands, turning them over. They were not the hands of a learned physician; they were the hard, stained hands of a craftsman. “And I,” he murmured, “I have been… reposing.”

The irony was so bitter, so steeped in the dregs of his long suffering, that Mr. Lorry felt a fresh pang of pity. Before he could frame a reply, the door, which had been left ajar, was pushed fully open, and Miss Pross stood on the threshold. For nine days she had been a dragon at the gate, a sentinel of grim and silent efficiency. Now, seeing the Doctor sitting upright, seeing the light of recognition in his eyes, the rigid fortress of her composure crumbled utterly.

“Oh, my Doctor! My dear Doctor!” she cried, and rushing forward, she fell on her knees beside his chair, not daring to touch him, but weeping with an abandon that shook her gaunt frame. Her hands, dusted with flour from some abandoned kitchen enterprise, were clasped before her as if in prayer. “Oh, thank God, thank God you are yourself again! My darling, my poor darling’s father!”

The Doctor, moved by this unexpected torrent of affection from his formidable housekeeper, reached out a trembling hand and laid it on her head. “My good Pross,” he said softly. “My loyal, good Pross. You have had a trying time of it, I fear.”

“Trying!” sobbed Miss Pross, looking up, her face streaked with tears and a faint bit of flour. “It has been no trial to serve you, Doctor. The trial was to see you… not yourself. To see *that* come back.” She gestured with a kind of horrified reverence towards the shoemaker’s bench. “Now that you are returned, it is as if the sun has come out. It is a blessing, a miracle!”

She rose then, as if embarrassed by her display, and began to bustle about the room, straightening a book here, adjusting a curtain there, her energy a stark contrast to the heavy stillness that had just been broken.

Mr. Lorry saw his moment. He cleared his throat, a sound that brought both the Doctor and Miss Pross to attention. “My dear Manette,” he began, adopting the air of a consulting physician himself, “now that you are, as Miss Pross so rightly says, returned to us, there is a matter of business to be addressed. A small, practical matter.”

The Doctor followed Mr. Lorry’s gaze, and it settled, as it had to, on the bench and the tools. It was the elephant in the room; or rather, it was the spectre at the feast, the skeleton in the closet made real and tangible in wood and steel. He stared at it, his face setting into the guarded, resolute lines that Lucie had so often studied in his sleep. It was an accuser. It was a relic of the Bastille, transported as if by some dark magic into the heart of his peaceful London home.

“Yes,” the Doctor said, his voice low and steady. “Yes, Lorry. There is that.” He stood up, and for a moment he swayed, but he waved away Mr. Lorry’s proffered arm. He walked over to the bench, his step deliberate, as a man might walk towards the grave of an enemy. He picked up the half-finished shoe, a lady’s shoe of a delicate size, and examined it with a strange, clinical detachment. “A relapse,” he said, more to himself than to them. “The shock… the memories stirred by the wedding… Charles’s story…” His voice trailed off. He put the shoe down and looked at his two friends, a question in his eyes. “Lucie. She must never know. She must never know that this… this phantom returned.”

“Never,” said Mr. Lorry with great emphasis. “It would break her heart. The entire purpose of our small conspiracy, my dear friend, has been to shield her. She believes you are recovering from a simple fatigue. She is happy. Her letters, which will be a great tonic for you to read, are full of it.”

“Then,” said the Doctor, turning his gaze back to the bench, his jaw tight, “this must be undone. It must be as if it never was. It cannot remain here. It cannot exist under the same roof as she does.”

Miss Pross, who had been wiping her eyes with the corner of her apron, spoke up with her usual practicality. “Burn it,” she said, her voice resolute. “Chop it to bits and burn it in the kitchen grate.”

“An excellent, if vigorous, proposition, my dear Pross,” said Mr. Lorry, a faint smile touching his lips. “But we are in the middle of a July morning. A great fire and the sound of an axe might occasion more comment than we should desire. This requires a delicate touch. An operation, if you will, to be performed with discretion.”

The three of them stood there, a council of war plotting the annihilation of a piece of furniture. It was the Doctor, his scientific mind reasserting its command, who saw the way forward.

“To-night,” he said. “When the house is asleep. We will do it to-night. We will need,” he paused, and a flicker of the old darkness crossed his face, but he mastered it, “we will need a saw. And a chopper. And a brazier, perhaps, in the yard.”

“I have a chopper,” declared Miss Pross, as if announcing she possessed the crown jewels. “A good, sharp one for the kindling. And the saw from the garden shed.”

“Excellent,” said Mr. Lorry, rubbing his hands together as if closing a deal at Tellson’s. “The plan is formed. We shall execute it with precision. To-night, we shall lay this ghost to rest for good and all. And now, Doctor, I prescribe for you a comfortable chair, a glass of the best claret, and the perusal of your daughter’s first letter, which arrived this very morning.”

The rest of that day passed in a strange state of suspense. The Doctor, though pale and moving with a weariness that went deeper than the bone, was himself. He sat in his usual chair by the window, he spoke of his books, he enquired after Mr. Lorry’s business at Tellson’s, and he read Lucie’s letter over and over, his lips moving silently, a soft light coming into his eyes. But beneath the placid surface of domesticity, the three conspirators were united by their secret purpose. Every glance that passed between them was a reaffirmation of the pact. Every time the Doctor’s gaze drifted towards the closed door of the garret, Mr. Lorry or Miss Pross would create a small diversion, a question about the weather, a comment on a passing carriage, steering him gently away from the precipice.

At last, the house grew quiet. The servants, long accustomed to the strange hours kept by their betters, retired to their beds. The lamps were extinguished, and the great city of London subsided into a low, distant murmur. Shortly after the stroke of two, three shadows convened in the small paved yard behind the kitchen.

Mr. Lorry, feeling more like a housebreaker than a respectable banker, carried a lantern, its yellow light casting enormous, dancing shadows against the brick walls. Miss Pross, her face set with grim determination, carried the chopper in one hand and the garden saw in the other, looking like a figure from a particularly grim fairy tale. And between them came Doctor Manette, his face illuminated by the lantern, pale and resolute as a marble statue. He carried nothing; it was his burden they had come to destroy.

First, they brought down the tools, wrapped in a coarse sack. The Doctor laid them out upon a flat stone, the little hammers and knives and awls gleaming dully in the lantern light. He looked at them for a long, silent moment, then nodded to Mr. Lorry. One by one, they took the tools, and with a heavy blacksmith’s hammer that Miss Pross had also produced, they performed their grim task. Mr. Lorry, with an unaccustomed violence, struck the delicate shoemaker’s hammer, bending its head. Miss Pross took a strange, fierce pleasure in snapping the blades of the knives under her heel. The Doctor himself took the largest hammer and, with two or three powerful, precise blows, shattered the stone lap-iron upon which he had beaten out so many miserable hours. The sound was sharp and final in the stillness of the night.

Then came the bench. It was heavy, and they manoeuvred it down the narrow stairs with difficulty, their grunts and whispers the only sound. In the yard, it looked smaller, more pathetic, a wretched and decrepit thing.

“The saw,” the Doctor said, his voice a command.

Mr. Lorry held the lantern high while the Doctor, with a strength that surprised them both, began to saw through the legs of the bench. The rasp of the teeth on the old, dry wood was a sound that set Mr. Lorry’s teeth on edge, but the Doctor worked with a focused, methodical fury. Sawdust, like the dust of ages, fell to the stones. Miss Pross then took up the chopper, and with swings of surprising force, splintered the sawn-off pieces into smaller and smaller fragments. It was a methodical dismemberment, a careful annihilation. They reduced the solid bench to a heap of kindling, a pile of anonymous wood that held no more story than any other.

Finally, they brought out the small brazier used for heating irons. Into its iron belly they fed the splinters of the bench and the wooden handles of the tools. Mr. Lorry struck a light, and a small flame wavered, then caught. It grew, licking at the dry wood, and soon a healthy fire was blazing, sending sparks up into the dark London sky. They stood and watched, the three of them, their faces glowing in the ruddy light, as the last physical vestiges of the Bastille and the shoemaker of the North Tower were consumed. The fire crackled and spat, and it seemed to them that with the smoke, a long-imprisoned spirit was finally ascending, and dissipating into nothingness.

When the last piece of wood had collapsed into a bed of glowing embers, a deep and profound exhaustion settled upon them. They had not merely destroyed a bench; they had fought a battle with a ghost, and they had won.

“It is done,” the Doctor said, and his voice was full of a peace that had not been there before. He leaned a hand on Mr. Lorry’s shoulder, a gesture of comradeship and gratitude.

They buried the mangled metal tools deep in the earth at the bottom of the little garden, beneath the shade of the plane-tree, and smoothed the soil over them. No cross would mark that grave.

They went back into the silent house, their faces smudged with soot, their clothes smelling of smoke. In the hall, by the light of a single candle, they looked at one another. The respectable banker, the fierce spinster, and the resurrected physician. An unlikely trinity, bound by a secret act of love and destruction.

“Thank you,” said the Doctor, his voice thick with emotion. “Both of you. I do not have the words to thank you.”

“Nonsense,” said Miss Pross, though her voice was not quite steady. “It was no more than spring cleaning.”

Mr. Lorry simply clasped his friend’s hand. “Go and rest now, Manette,” he said gently. “Sleep. The morning is not far off.”

The Doctor ascended the stairs, but this time he walked not to the garret, but to his own chamber, the room that was his, and his alone.

Mr. Lorry and Miss Pross were left in the hall. For a moment, the old bachelor and the old maid stood in an awkward silence, partners in a crime of compassion.

“He will be well now, Mr. Lorry?” she asked, a plea in her voice.

“With God’s help, and ours, my dear Pross,” he replied softly. “He has looked the enemy in the face and struck him down. That is a great victory.” He looked towards the stairs, and his thoughts turned, as they always did, to Lucie. “We have done a good night’s work,” he murmured. “A good night’s work. We have cleared the stage for her return. Let no one ever be the wiser. It shall be our secret, and our trust.”

And as the first hint of grey dawn began to silhouette the chimney-pots of Soho, Mr. Lorry felt a profound sense of rightness. They had committed a pious fraud, a conspiracy of love, and in the dark and grimy yard, by the light of a destructive fire, they had truly, finally, recalled the Doctor to life.

They retired, not to sleep, but to a state of watchful repose, their ears straining against the familiar quiet of the house for any sound that might betray a relapse, a return of the dreadful hammering that had for so long been the metronome of their anxieties. Mr. Lorry, in the small guest chamber, found he could not so much as remove his gaiters; he sat on the edge of his bed, his wig slightly askew, listening. Miss Pross, in her own domain, no doubt did the same, her formidable form perched on a chair, her hearing as acute as a mother cat’s. It was a vigil, not of despair, as so many past vigils had been, but of a fragile, fiercely protected hope.

The sun was well up, and had sent a cheerful shaft of light to dance upon the very spot where Mr. Lorry’s best waistcoat was stretched over his anxious heart, when a sound did finally come. It was not, however, a sound from the garret, for that room was now as empty of purpose as a forgotten tomb. It was the sound of the Doctor’s own chamber door opening, followed by steady footsteps descending the stairs.

Mr. Lorry held his breath. He heard Miss Pross emerge from her room below, a faint rustle of determined calico. He smoothed his wig, composed his features into an expression of gentlemanly nonchalance, and opened his own door.

Doctor Manette stood in the hall below, fully dressed. Not only dressed, but dressed with a neatness and care that had been absent for days. His white hair was brushed, his collar was straight, his face, though pale and marked by the deep channels of his suffering, was placid. He was looking towards the little garden, a faint, unreadable smile upon his lips.

Miss Pross, her night-cap still askew but her purpose as upright as the poker she now wielded against the morning’s chill, met him at the foot of the stairs, her expression a formidable mixture of sleeplessness, suspicion, and a grudging sort of triumph.

“Breakfast is nearly laid, Doctor,” she announced, her voice having the quality of a friendly broadside. “I trust you slept.”

“I did, my dear Pross,” he answered, and his voice was so calm, so devoid of the old undercurrent of dread, that Mr. Lorry felt a distinct pricking behind his eyes. “I slept as I have not slept in almost twenty years. I dreamed of a garden, and of my Lucie as a little girl, planting flowers.”

He turned as Mr. Lorry descended, and the two men looked at one another across the little hall. In the Doctor’s eyes, there was no longer the haunted, hunted look of a man peering from the shadows of a prison, but the clear, direct gaze of a friend. He held out his hand.

“My good friend,” he said, simply. “It is a fine morning.”

Mr. Lorry took the offered hand, and found it steady and warm. “The finest in a great while, Manette,” he replied, his own voice not quite as business-like as he would have wished. “The very finest.”

And so began the fortnight of waiting. The first few days were a study in cautious observation. Mr. Lorry, despite the pressing demands of Tellson’s Bank, found a thousand and one reasons to call at the house in Soho, appearing at odd hours with the pretext of a forgotten paper or a query about some household account. He would find the Doctor not in the garret, never in the garret, but in the main sitting-room, reading, or writing letters, or simply sitting by the window, watching the quiet life of the courtyard unfold. He looked, to all outward appearances, like any other retired gentleman enjoying a peaceful convalescence.

Miss Pross, for her part, maintained her own peculiar brand of surveillance, which consisted of preparing an endless succession of beef-tea, broths, and custards, and delivering them with a gruffness that failed to conceal the trembling hope behind it. She would burst into the room, place the offering before him with a thump, and demand, “You are not thinking, I hope?”

“My dear Pross,” the Doctor would reply, looking up from his book with a gentle smile, “I am thinking that you are the best and kindest of women, and that this broth is, as ever, excellent.”

“Humph,” she would say, and retreat, but not before her sharp eyes had swept the room, taking in his placid face, his steady hands, his very posture, for any sign of the old, dark weather returning to his soul.

One afternoon, a week into Lucie’s absence, Mr. Lorry arrived to find the Doctor in the hall, standing before a dusty, leather-bound trunk that had long sat unused under the stairs. He was holding a small, brass key.

“Lorry, my friend,” he said, his tone one of quiet discovery. “Do you know, I believe I had almost forgotten this.”

Mr. Lorry’s heart gave a leap of alarm. A trunk from the past; what ghosts might it contain? “Indeed, Manette? Some old possession?”

“My instruments,” the Doctor said, and he turned the key in the lock. The lid opened with a protesting creak, releasing the faint, dry scent of aged leather and carbolic. Inside, nestled in worn velvet, lay a collection of surgical tools; scalpels, forceps, and probes, their steel still holding a dull gleam. They were the implements of his former life, the life before the Bastille, the life of a respected and skilful physician.

Mr. Lorry watched his friend’s face with an intensity that was almost painful. The Doctor reached in, his long, delicate fingers hovering over the instruments. For a terrible moment, the banker imagined those fingers closing around a tool with the old, frantic energy of the shoemaker. But they did not. Instead, the Doctor touched a small scalpel with a kind of reverence, the way a musician might touch a long-silent instrument.

“I was a good physician once,” he said softly, more to himself than to Mr. Lorry. “I brought life into the world. I eased pain. It was a useful calling.”

He closed the trunk, locked it, and put the key in his waistcoat pocket. “There are many who suffer in this city, Lorry. The poor, the forgotten. They have no one to turn to.”

A great and profound understanding dawned on Mr. Lorry. It was not enough for the Doctor to be merely idle and at peace; peace, for a man of his nature, must be found in purpose. The ghost of the shoemaker, who toiled for oblivion, was to be vanquished for good by the resurrection of the physician, who worked for life.

“Manette,” Mr. Lorry said, his voice ringing with a sudden, joyful conviction. “If you are thinking what I believe you are thinking, then Tellson’s Bank, sir, will be proud to assist in any way it can. New premises, perhaps? Instruments? We shall be your men of business in this, as in all things!”

The Doctor smiled, a true and luminous smile that seemed to erase the last shadows from his face. “No, no, my dear Lorry. No grand premises. A small room, here perhaps. A sign on the door. For those who cannot afford the great doctors of Harley Street. It is not a business I wish to build, but a life.”

And so, with Miss Pross’s grudging but absolute co-operation, a small, street-level room, formerly used for storage, was cleared and scrubbed and made ready. It was furnished with a simple table, two chairs, and the newly polished medical trunk. A small, neatly painted sign was commissioned, bearing the simple legend, “Doctor Manette. Physician.”

The first patient was a timid washerwoman from a nearby lane, her child’s arm broken in a fall. Miss Pross, who had at first declared the whole enterprise “improper” and likely to “bring all manner of diseases into the house,” found herself acting as a most formidable and unlikely nurse, holding the weeping child’s other hand while the Doctor, with a skill that seemed to flow back into his fingers as if it had never left, set the bone and fashioned a splint. His touch was so gentle, his voice so soothing, that the child’s cries soon subsided into sniffles.

As the woman departed, stammering her tearful thanks and offering a few pennies which the Doctor gently refused, he looked at Mr. Lorry, who had witnessed the entire procedure from the doorway.

“It is a better work than making shoes, is it not?” he asked, and in his eyes there was a light of such profound and quiet happiness that Mr. Lorry knew, with a certainty that settled deep in his soul, that the battle was truly won. The man who had been buried alive for eighteen years was not merely recalled to life; he was recalled to himself.

The days that followed fell into a new, harmonious rhythm. The Doctor would spend his mornings seeing a small but steady stream of the local poor, tending to their coughs and fevers, their aches and injuries, with a serene and patient competence. He spoke little of his past, and never of the prison, but his immense and quiet compassion for all who suffered seemed to spring from a deep and inexhaustible well.

At last, on the thirteenth day of Lucie’s absence, a letter arrived, bearing a Warwickshire postmark. It was addressed to the Doctor, but he insisted on waiting until Mr. Lorry arrived from the bank that evening to open it.

They sat together in the twilight, Miss Pross with her knitting, a silent, watchful guardian, as the Doctor broke the seal. The letter was from Lucie, her handwriting a familiar, elegant script, but with a new and joyful flourish to it. She wrote of their happiness, of the beauty of the countryside, of the kindness of the people they had met. She spoke of Charles with a love that was plain in every line.

“And my dearest father,” she wrote, “Charles sends his most devoted respects, and says that he has never known such contentment as he has found in being your son. We speak of you every hour. We picture you in your chair by the fire, with Mr. Lorry and our dear Pross, and we count the moments until you join us. We shall all be together in Wales, a true family, and I shall be the proudest wife, and the proudest daughter, in all the world.”

The Doctor read the words aloud, his voice clear and steady. When he had finished, he folded the letter carefully and looked at his two companions. His eyes were bright, not with the feverish glint of madness, nor with the wildness of old sorrow, but with the quiet, glistening sheen of tears; the tears of a man whose cup of happiness was filled to the very brim.

“She is happy,” he said, his voice thick with an emotion that was pure joy. “My darling is happy.”

“As she deserves to be,” said Mr. Lorry, blowing his nose with some violence.

“And as you deserve to see her,” added Miss Pross, jabbing a needle into her yarn with unnecessary force to disguise the fact that her own vision had grown suddenly blurry.

The next day, the Doctor’s trunk was packed for his journey to Wales. It was a very different packing from any he had known before. There were no hidden tools, no remnants of a desperate craft. There were only fresh linens, his books, the new suit of clothes Mr. Lorry had insisted upon, and, tucked into a corner, Lucie’s letter.

Mr. Lorry was to accompany him on the first leg of the journey, to see him safely onto the mail coach that would take him west. As they stood on the pavement, the coachman preparing the horses, the banker performed a final, surreptitious inspection of his friend. The Doctor stood tall, his face turned towards the morning sun, a look of peaceful anticipation upon his features. He was not merely a man restored; he was a man made whole.

“You have him in the best health and in the happiest frame,” Mr. Lorry murmured to himself, echoing his own promise to Lucie. A promise kept.

The Doctor turned, seeming to sense his friend’s thoughts. He placed a hand on Mr. Lorry’s shoulder, the very gesture of comradeship he had used on the night of the fire.

“All this, my dear Lorry,” he said, his gaze taking in the quiet house, the little courtyard, the memory of his two faithful friends. “All this, I owe to you. You and Pross. You brought me out of the darkness, not once, but twice.”

“We merely held the light, Manette,” replied Mr. Lorry, his heart swelling. “You found your own way.”

The guard called for the passengers to board. The Doctor clasped Mr. Lorry’s hand one last time, a firm, grateful pressure. Then he climbed into the coach, took his seat by the window, and as the carriage pulled away, he smiled and waved. It was the wave of a man setting out on a joyful holiday, not the gesture of a spectre fleeing a haunted past.

Mr. Lorry stood on the pavement long after the coach had turned the corner and disappeared from view. The morning sun was climbing higher, chasing the last shadows from the streets of Soho. He thought of the shoemaker’s bench, reduced to ash. He thought of the tools, buried deep beneath the plane-tree. He thought of the Doctor, his face bright with hope, travelling towards his daughter. A secret trust had been discharged; a pious fraud had borne the most blessed fruit. With a sigh of profound and utter satisfaction, the old banker turned, and began his walk back towards Tellson’s, a man of business who had, for a brief and glorious fortnight, been engaged in the far more important business of heaven.

They were to travel to Warwickshire for a fortnight, he and his bride, a quiet journey to a country inn where the green lanes of England would be their only society. Then, they were to be joined by the Doctor, and the three would proceed together into the mountains of Wales. The departure left a quietude in its wake, a silence more profound than that which merely follows the cessation of sound. It was the silence of a purpose fulfilled, a great and anxious labour brought to its blessed conclusion. Mr. Lorry, Miss Pross, and the Doctor returned to the house in Soho, which seemed at once larger and more empty than it had an hour before. Yet, the emptiness was not a sorrowful one; it was, rather, like a vessel scoured clean and made ready for a new and peaceful vintage.

In the days that followed, a strange and pleasant calm descended upon the corner house. The fortnight of the Doctor’s solitude before he was to join the happy pair was a period of singular tranquility. Mr. Lorry, true to his word, allowed Tellson’s to go to the wall, comparatively speaking. He made his daily journey to the grim edifice of finance, but his mind, and often his person, was to be found in Soho. He and Miss Pross established a kind of joint guardianship over the Doctor, a solemn and unspoken pact between two of the most un-alike individuals in all of London.

They would sit with him in the evenings, Miss Pross with her knitting, a fortress of clicking needles and fierce affection, and Mr. Lorry with his newspaper, which he would read aloud in a gentle, rumbling monotone. The Doctor himself was a marvel to them. The last vestiges of the prisoner, the haunted, vacant look that could, in moments of stress, pass like a cold wind over his features, had vanished. He read, he walked in the little garden, he spoke of his scientific work in Paris from long ago, and he spoke of Lucie’s future. He was a man not merely resurrected, but reborn into a world he had thought forever lost.

“He is mended, Pross,” Mr. Lorry whispered one evening, as the Doctor sat engrossed in a book, his face placid in the lamplight. “Completely mended. The terrible work is undone.”

“It was never work for a man like him,” Miss Pross declared, her needles stabbing at the air. “Shoemaking! A gentleman and a scholar. It was an insult to his very hands.” She looked at those hands, now lying peacefully on the pages of his book, and a fierce, protective pride shone in her eyes. “They are a doctor’s hands again. As they ought to be.”

So the days passed, and the time for the Doctor’s own journey approached. The plan was that Mr. Lorry would escort him to the posting-inn whence the mail coach to Wales departed, seeing him safely on his way. The evening before, a letter had arrived from Lucie, full of sunshine and contented love, describing the Warwickshire countryside and her great happiness, and filled with impatient anticipation for her father’s arrival. The letter was a cordial to all their hearts.

The next morning, Mr. Lorry arrived in a hackney-coach to find the Doctor’s trunk already standing in the hall. It was a very different packing from the one he had overseen seventeen years before. Then, it had been the meagre possessions of a released captive, a man who was little more than a spectre. Now, it was the luggage of a gentleman travelling to a family holiday; it contained fresh linen, new books, and the handsome suit of clothes that Mr. Lorry himself had insisted was a necessity for the father of Mrs. Darnay.

As they stood on the pavement, the coachman strapping the trunk securely, Miss Pross performed a final, rigorous inspection of the Doctor’s travelling coat, brushing away a speck of dust that no one else could see.

“You will write, Doctor Manette,” she commanded, rather than asked. “You will let us know you have arrived safely and that my Ladybird is well.”

“I will, my dear Pross, my good friend,” he said, taking her hand. He looked from her to Mr. Lorry, his face illuminated by a gratitude so profound it needed no words. “I leave my home in your care. There are no two people on earth I would trust more readily.”

Mr. Lorry cleared his throat with some violence. “It will be here for you, Manette, unchanged. We shall keep the hearth warm.”

The Doctor smiled, a genuine, untroubled smile that reached his eyes and crinkled their corners. He climbed into the coach, and Mr. Lorry seated himself opposite. With a final wave to the indomitable figure of Miss Pross on the doorstep, they rattled away over the cobblestones, leaving the quiet corner of Soho behind them.

The journey to the inn was a cheerful one. The Doctor spoke with animation of the geological formations he hoped to see in the Welsh mountains, of theories he wished to discuss with his son-in-law, of the grandchildren he hoped, in time, to hold on his knee. To Mr. Lorry, listening with an expression of quiet satisfaction, it was as if the seventeen years in the Bastille had been a terrible dream from which the dreamer had, at last, fully and finally awakened.

At the bustling yard of The Golden Cross, they found the mail coach preparing for its departure. Porters hurried with luggage, horses stamped and snorted in the traces, and passengers bustled about, securing their places. Mr. Lorry saw the Doctor comfortably seated, and stood by the window of the coach for a final word.

“All is well, Manette. You are on your way to happiness.”

“A happiness I never dared to imagine,” the Doctor replied, his voice low and earnest. He placed his hand on the window-ledge, near to Mr. Lorry’s. “My dear, dear friend. What I owe you is a debt that can never be repaid.”

“Nonsense,” said Mr. Lorry, though his own eyes were suspiciously bright. “A banker deals in transactions, Manette. This was a human one. It is the only sort that truly pays a dividend. Now, go on with you. Don’t keep your daughter waiting.”

The guard blew his horn, a long, thrilling note that echoed through the yard. The coach gave a lurch and began to move. The Doctor waved, his face bright with anticipation, until the coach swung out of the yard and was lost in the great river of London traffic. Mr. Lorry stood for a moment, a small, neat figure in the bustling chaos, a profound sense of peace settling over him. He had delivered his friend from the living death of the Bastille; he had watched over his recovery; and he had now dispatched him, a whole and happy man, to the arms of his loving daughter. The trust was discharged. The account was closed, and the balance was overwhelmingly in favour of joy.

He turned and began his walk back towards Temple Bar, his step lighter than it had been in years. The air of business, however, began to reassert its claim as he drew nearer to Tellson’s. The world, after all, was not composed entirely of loving reunions and rescued souls. It was also composed of ledgers and accounts, of bills of exchange and securities held in trust. As he entered the dark, respectable gloom of the bank, the fortnight in Soho seemed to recede, taking on the quality of a pleasant but finished story.

He had been at his desk for less than an hour, his mind deep in a particularly tangled matter concerning a shipment of wine from Oporto, when one of the junior clerks approached his desk.

“A person to see you, Mr. Lorry.”

“A person?” Mr. Lorry looked up, his pen hovering. “Have they an appointment? What is the name?”

“He gives no name, sir,” the clerk said, with an air of faint disapproval. “He says only that he is from France, and has a matter of great private importance that concerns a former client of the bank. A very… insistent person, sir.”

Mr. Lorry’s mind, trained by decades of caution, immediately became alert. A Frenchman. A private matter. Insistent. These were not words that, in combination, promised anything good.

“Show him to the small consulting room. I will be there directly.”

The consulting room was a small, panelled box, furnished with two hard chairs and a small table, a place designed for interviews that required more privacy than the public floor of the bank allowed, but which offered no comfort to encourage a lengthy stay. When Mr. Lorry entered, the man was already there, not seated, but standing by the single grimy window that looked out onto a brick wall. He turned as the door closed, and Mr. Lorry felt an immediate and inexplicable sense of disquiet.

The man was of middle height, thin and wiry, with a sallow complexion that spoke of a life lived out of the sun. His clothes were a strange mixture of shabby finery; a worn velvet coat that had once been expensive, a ruffled shirt of questionable cleanliness, and boots that were cracked and down at heel. But it was his face that held Mr. Lorry’s attention. It was a sharp, acquisitive face, with eyes set a little too close together; eyes that darted about, taking in every detail of the room, of Mr. Lorry’s person, with a silent, calculating appraisal. There was a restless energy about him, a coiled watchfulness that was entirely out of place in the staid atmosphere of Tellson’s Bank.

“You wished to see me,” Mr. Lorry began, taking the seat behind the table and gesturing for the man to do the same. “I am Jarvis Lorry.”

The man gave a thin, unsmiling bow, but remained standing. “So I was told. You are the man of business. The man of trust.” He spoke English with a thick, guttural accent, but the words were chosen with deliberate care.

“I am a servant of Tellson’s Bank,” Mr. Lorry corrected him coolly. “What is your business, sir?”

The man’s eyes fixed on Mr. Lorry’s face. “My business is with the past, Monsieur. A past that you helped to, shall we say, disinter.”

A cold dread, sharp and sudden as an icicle to the heart, pierced Mr. Lorry’s composure. He kept his face a mask of polite inquiry, but his hands, under the table, gripped each other tightly.

“I am afraid I do not follow you.”

“Oh, I think you do,” the man said softly. He took a step closer to the table. “I was a turnkey, you see. In a certain prison. A prison of the State, across the water. For many years. I had duties in the North Tower.”

The North Tower. The words fell into the silence of the little room like stones into a deep well. Mr. Lorry’s blood ran cold. He could feel the colour draining from his face, and he fought to maintain his calm, business-like demeanour.

“Many men have had many duties in many prisons,” he said, his voice a little too stiff. “This has nothing to do with Tellson’s Bank.”

“Doesn’t it?” The man’s lips stretched into a semblance of a smile. “I was there for a long time. I remember a certain prisoner. A physician. A learned man. We had a name for him, of course. We did not use his real one. One Hundred and Five, North Tower. I remember him well. And I remember the day he was released. Into the care of a certain English banker.”

The unspoken threat hung in the air, thick and poisonous. This was not a chance encounter. This man had sought him out. He knew.

“What is it you want?” Mr. Lorry asked, his voice now hard and clipped.

The man finally sat down, leaning forward and placing his elbows on the table, his manner shifting from menacing suggestion to sordid negotiation. “France is… unsettled, Monsieur Lorry. A man of my profession is no longer esteemed. The very ground shifts under our feet. Old loyalties are worth nothing. A patriot must look to his own future. I have come to England, like many of my countrymen, seeking a new life.”

“Then I wish you well of it,” Mr. Lorry said, preparing to rise. “But I cannot see how I can be of service.”

“But you can. You see, in my time in that place, I became a keeper of more than just keys. I kept… memorabilia. Written things. A physician, alone for so many years, with his mind still working… he might write things down. A history, perhaps. An accusation. A denunciation of the family that put him there. A very powerful, a very noble family.” The man’s eyes glittered. “Such a document, should it exist, would be of great interest. And of great danger to the descendants of that noble family. Descendants who might now be, shall we say, connected to the physician himself. Through marriage, for instance.”

Mr. Lorry felt as if the floor had fallen away beneath him. The manuscript. The story the Doctor had written and hidden in the chimney. He had believed it destroyed with the bench, reduced to ash. But what if there was another? Or what if this man had found the original, and a copy had been made? The implications were monstrous. The fragile peace, the newfound happiness of Lucie and Charles, rested on a foundation that this creature was threatening to shatter.

“This is a fantasy,” Mr. Lorry said, his voice hoarse. “A despicable attempt to extort money through lies.”

The man, whose name Mr. Lorry had not even thought to ask, merely shrugged. “Believe what you will. But I know what I know. And I think that the good Doctor’s son-in-law, the man who calls himself Charles Darnay, but who is in fact the nephew of the late Marquis St. Evrémonde, would pay a great deal for such a fantasy to remain buried.”

He had said it. The name. The connection. The whole dreadful secret lay bare on the dusty table between them.

“I am a poor man, Monsieur,” the turnkey continued, his voice taking on a whining tone. “A refugee. I require only a small stipend. A sign of goodwill from one who values peace and quiet above all things. Say, a sum to establish myself. And then a modest quarterly payment. For my silence.”

Mr. Lorry rose to his feet, his small frame rigid with a mixture of terror and revulsion. “Get out,” he said, his voice trembling with suppressed rage. “Get out of this bank.”

The man rose too, his smirk returning. “As you wish. I will not trouble you further. Here.” He gave a slow, insolent look around the room. “I find these surroundings… oppressive. I prefer the air of Soho. A quiet, pleasant neighbourhood. I shall take a walk there. Perhaps I will find the air more conducive to a friendly understanding.”

He turned and, without another word, left the room. Mr. Lorry stood frozen, listening to the man’s footsteps fade away down the passage. The fortress he had so carefully built around Doctor Manette’s life had been breached. A spy from the Bastille was loose in London, a carrion-bird drawn to the scent of a buried past, and he was heading for Soho. The business of heaven was over; the business of hell had just begun.

For a space of time that might have been a minute or an age, Mr. Lorry remained as motionless as the ancient furniture that surrounded him. The quiet ticking of the great clock in the hall, a sound so familiar it was usually part of the silence, now seemed to beat a frantic and terrifying tattoo upon his very brain. The words of the man, the spectre from the fallen Bastille, echoed not merely in the dusty air of the chamber but in the very chambers of his heart, each syllable a hammer-blow against the fragile structure of peace he had laboured so long to erect. Soho. He was heading for Soho. The carrion-bird was on the wing, and his destination was the nest Mr. Lorry had sworn to protect with his life.

With a start, as though a physical blow had at last landed, he snatched his hat and cane, his movements jerky and uncertain, the very opposite of the methodical precision that governed his life at Tellson’s. He did not pause to consider a plan; there was no plan to be had. There was only an overwhelming, instinctual urgency to place his own insignificant person between the threat and the threatened, to serve as a shield, however frail, for Lucie and her father. He hurried out into the narrow street, the familiar grey stones of Temple Bar seeming to lean over him with a new and sinister weight. He hailed a hackney-coach with a wildness in his gesture that startled the driver, who was accustomed to the more sedate summons of the men of Tellson’s.

“Where to, guv’nor?” the man asked, peering down from his box.

“Soho! To the corner near the church. Drive, man, drive as if the devil himself were after you!”

The driver, concluding that his elderly fare was either drunk, mad, or a messenger of the most vital importance, shrugged his shoulders and cracked his whip. The coach lurched into the teeming stream of London traffic, and Mr. Lorry was thrown back against the musty squabs. The city passed by the window in a blur of stone and brick, of faces indifferent and faces curious. To Mr. Lorry, it was a meaningless pantomime. His entire consciousness was fixed upon a quiet corner of London, a house of unassuming brick with a plane-tree in its small garden, a house that contained all the treasure he possessed in the world.

What was he to do? Burst in upon their evening’s peace and declare the catastrophe? The very thought was an abomination. To see the colour drain from Lucie’s face, to risk the unseating of her father’s reason, so precariously re-established on the throne of his mind; it was unthinkable. No, he could not tell them. He could not be the bearer of such tidings. But what was the alternative? To allow this vile creature, this Brossard, for Mr. Lorry had mentally assigned him a name as harsh and grating as his voice, to make his approach? To have him knock upon that door, his smirk a harbinger of ruin?

The implications of the man’s knowledge spun in a dizzying vortex. Charles’s true name, his lineage as an Evrémonde, was a secret known to but four souls in England: Charles himself, the Doctor, Lucie, and Mr. Lorry. For it to be spoken by a fifth, a stranger dredged up from the dungeons of Paris, was a calamity beyond calculation. And the manuscript! The Doctor’s harrowing account of his suffering and his denunciation of the very family into which his daughter had married. Mr. Lorry had presided over the burning of the shoemaker’s bench and tools, believing that with the flames he was cauterising the Doctor’s last link to his trauma. He and Miss Pross had buried the remnants in the garden as if laying a ghost to rest. But this man’s claim suggested the ghost had a copy of its own testimony, a testament that could destroy the living.

The coachman drew rein at the designated corner. “Here you are, sir. Soho.”

Mr. Lorry pressed a coin into his hand without counting it and clambered out. The air here was different from the City’s; it was quieter, more domestic. The scent of coal-smoke and evening meals mingled with the faint perfume of the late-blooming flowers in the little gardens. It was the air of home, and it had never seemed so fragile. He kept to the shadows on the opposite side of the street, his heart hammering against his ribs, and looked at the house.

Light shone from the downstairs windows, a warm, inviting glow that spoke of peace and sanctuary. It was a picture of such profound and gentle happiness that it brought an ache to his throat. He could almost hear the quiet conversation, Lucie’s soft voice, the Doctor’s calmer, deeper tones. He imagined them sitting together, perhaps with little Lucie playing at their feet, the past a healed wound, the future a promise of continued blessings. And out here, in the encroaching twilight, a wolf was circling.

He scanned the street, his eyes piercing the gloom. There, half-hidden in the alcove of a neighbouring house, stood a figure. It was the man. He was not looking at the house directly, but stood with a feigned casualness, his posture a study in insolent patience. He was watching. Waiting. Confirming for himself the location of his prey. Their eyes met across the street. The man, Brossard, offered no sign of recognition beyond a slight, slow widening of his thin-lipped smile. It was a look of shared conspiracy and of absolute power. He knew that Mr. Lorry was there, he knew why he was there, and he knew that the banker was helpless. After a moment that stretched into an eternity, the man touched the brim of his shabby hat in a mock salute, turned, and sauntered away into the darkness, his footsteps echoing with a dreadful finality.

Mr. Lorry felt a cold sweat break out upon his forehead. The man was not bluffing. The threat was not some distant possibility; it was here, now, camped on their very doorstep. Action was required, not the frantic, unthinking action of his flight from the bank, but the cold, calculated action of a man of business, even if the business was of the blackest kind. He could not protect them by standing sentinel in the street. He had to engage the enemy, to meet him on a field of battle of his own choosing.

He crossed the street, but instead of going to the front door, he went around to the kitchen entrance, where he was less likely to disturb the tranquil scene in the parlour. The door was opened by Miss Pross, who was in the midst of polishing a silver teapot with a vigour that threatened to rub it into nothingness.

“Mr. Lorry!” she exclaimed, her sharp features expressing a mixture of surprise and suspicion. “What brings you here at this hour? Is anything amiss at Tellson’s? Has the world of business finally collapsed upon its own nonsense?”

“No, my dear Pross, no. All is well,” he lied, forcing a smile that felt like a grimace. “I… I found myself with an unexpected free evening, and I thought a little supper with you all would be a pleasant change.”

Miss Pross eyed him shrewdly. She had a perception, born of her fierce and uncomplicated love, that often saw through more sophisticated deceptions. “You look as if you’ve seen a ghost, Mr. Lorry. Your cravat is askew.”

He hastily adjusted it. “The drive, my dear Pross, the jolting of the coach. A man is not so young as he once was.”

She accepted this, though her gaze lingered on him for a moment longer. “Well, come in out of the night air. There’s cold beef and a pigeon pie. The Doctor and my Ladybird are in the parlour. Don’t you go upsetting them with any of your long faces.”

“I shall be the very soul of cheerfulness,” he promised, his heart as heavy as lead.

He entered the small, comfortable parlour, and the sight that greeted him was the very picture he had imagined from the street. Doctor Manette was reading by the light of a lamp, his face serene and handsome, the lines of suffering softened into a dignified gravity. Lucie was sitting on a low stool at his feet, mending one of little Lucie’s frocks, her golden hair catching the light. She looked up as he entered, her face breaking into that smile which, for Mr. Lorry, was a light that had shone into all the darkest corners of his later life.

“Mr. Lorry! What a wonderful surprise!” she said, rising to take his hands.

“My dear Lucie,” he said, his voice thick with an emotion he struggled to conceal. “My dear Doctor.”

Doctor Manette set down his book and rose to greet him warmly. “Jarvis. We are very glad to see you. You have come to rescue me from these two women, who cosset and spoil me beyond all reason.”

The easy domesticity, the gentle teasing, was almost more than Mr. Lorry could bear. He was an impostor in this temple of peace, a bearer of a secret that would shatter it to atoms. He played his part through the simple supper, speaking of matters at Tellson’s, of the price of coin, of the weather. He complimented Miss Pross on her pie, he allowed the Doctor to pour him a glass of wine, and he listened as Lucie recounted a charmingly incoherent story told to her by her daughter. All the while, his mind was a battlefield. He saw the face of Brossard, he heard his whining, menacing voice, he felt the chill of the Bastille’s shadow falling across the warm and lighted room.

He knew, with a certainty that settled upon him like a shroud, that he could not confide in the Doctor. The risk was too great. The old horror lay too close to the surface, and a shock of this magnitude could send him plunging back into the abyss, searching for a shoemaker’s bench that was no longer there. He could not tell Lucie, for her sake and for her husband’s. There was only one person who had the right to know, and the strength to bear the knowledge: Charles. But Charles was away, visiting a client in the north. He was not expected back for two days. Two days in which the wolf would be at the door. Mr. Lorry would have to act alone.

He took his leave early, pleading the press of morning business. Lucie accompanied him to the door.

“You are troubled, dear Mr. Lorry,” she said softly, her hand on his arm, her blue eyes full of concern. “I have seen it all evening. You are not yourself. Will you not tell me what it is?”

He looked down into her earnest, loving face, and for a terrible moment, he was tempted to unburden himself, to share the dreadful load. But the image of her consequent terror stopped him. He patted her hand, summoning the last of his reserves of deception.

“It is nothing, my dear, but the cares of a foolish old man of business. My thoughts are full of ledgers and accounts. You must not worry for me. All is well. All will be well.”

He kissed her forehead, a paternal blessing that felt like a Judas kiss, and walked away into the night. He did not take a coach. He walked, the steady rhythm of his steps on the pavement a metronome for his racing thoughts. He walked all the way back to the City, to the dark and silent edifice of Tellson’s Bank. He let himself in with his own key, the sound of the heavy bolts sliding home echoing in the tomb-like stillness.

He did not light a lamp in his own office, but sat in the dark, looking out at the sliver of a moon hanging over the London rooftops. A plan, cold and terrible, began to form in his mind. He was a man of business. This was a transaction. An ugly, foul, damnable transaction, but a transaction nonetheless. It required a negotiation, a price, and a payment. He could not allow the blackmailer to set the terms or the location. He would summon the man, Brossard, back to this place, to Tellson’s. He would meet him here, after hours, surrounded by the iron and stone of his own domain. Here, he was not just an old man; he was the representative of an institution, a power.

He would have to pay. He knew that. The man would not be frightened away by mere indignation. He would demand proof, of course. He would not part with a single shilling of the bank’s money, or his own, without absolute certainty. But he had little doubt the proof would be forthcoming. And when it was, he would pay what was necessary to purchase silence, to buy time until Charles returned, to keep the shadow from falling upon the house in Soho.

He dipped a pen in ink, and by the faint moonlight, he wrote a short, curt note on a slip of paper.

*Be at the main entrance to Tellson’s Bank by Temple Bar to-morrow night, when the clock strikes ten. You will be admitted. Come alone.*

He did not sign it. He folded it, and then he sat, waiting for the dawn, a lonely old man plotting a secret and sordid business in the heart of his respectable world. In the morning, he would send for Jerry Cruncher. The "Odd-Job Man," with his proximity to the less salubrious elements of the city, would know how to find a shabby Frenchman, newly arrived from Paris, who had taken an interest in the air of Soho. Jerry, Mr. Lorry reflected with a grim irony, was perhaps the only man on Tellson's staff suited to be an agent in the business of hell.
