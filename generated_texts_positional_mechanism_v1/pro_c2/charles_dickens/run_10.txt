Mr. Ambrose Finn, a man whose heart ticked with the cold precision of an escapement wheel, stood amidst the orderly chorus of his own shop. Here, time was not a river but a series of calculated clicks; a universe of polished brass and gleaming crystal, where every second was captured, counted, and confined behind a glass face. His was a world of quiet diligence, its air smelling faintly of oil and old wood, its only music the gentle, overlapping pulse of a hundred clocks. Beyond his window, however, the city had erupted in a fever, for it was the day of the annual Founder’s Fair, a chaotic and vulgar spectacle that Mr. Finn held in the deepest contempt.

"A tremendous waste of profitable hours!" he muttered, polishing a carriage clock with a soft, grey cloth.

A small bell above the door jingled, not with the timid note of a customer, but with a sudden, impish clatter, as if it had been shaken by a joyful wind. A girl stood there, no older than ten, with eyes the colour of a summer sky and hair like a dandelion puff. She wore a coat of a dozen mismatched patches, and in her hand she held not a purse, but a single, brilliant blue marble. Her shoes were worn, and her face was smudged with city dust, yet she carried herself with the magnificent authority of a visiting queen.

"You have never truly heard a clock tick before!" she announced, her voice as clear as a silver bell.

"I have heard little else my entire life," Mr. Finn answered drily, not looking up.

"Have you never heard one laugh, then; meaning (for all clocks are children) its younger cousins, the watches that sleep in pockets, or its elder sisters, the tower-bells that sing to the whole city?" pursued the child.

"I cannot say that I have," said Finn, a frown creasing his brow. "Clocks do not laugh. Have you no parents to attend to, child?"

"More than a thousand!" she said, with a sweeping gesture that encompassed the street outside. "A boisterous family to keep cheerful!"

Mr. Finn sighed, a sound like a winding spring running down. The girl skipped forward. "Sir," she said, her tone suddenly earnest, "you must see the grandest clock of all. I came here on a mission, and I learnt a secret which is unfolding now. To-day, if you have a moment to spare, let me show it to you."

"Touch my hand!"

Ambrose Finn, against all logic and better judgment, did as he was told. The ticking of the hundred clocks, the scent of oil, the polished brass, the gleaming crystal, all faded. So did the quiet dust motes dancing in the sunbeams, the neat stacks of receipts, the very walls of his shop, and they stood on a high gantry overlooking the ironworks district, where (for the noise was tremendous) the clang of hammers and the hiss of steam made a deafening, but strangely rhythmic and not unmusical thunder.

The foundry roofs looked skeletal and black, their chimneys blacker, breathing out plumes of thick grey smoke against the sky. Below, the ground was a dark tapestry of coal-dust and mud, scarred with the shining tracks of rail-carts that wove and interwove like the threads of some vast, industrial loom. The air was acrid, and the heat rising from the furnaces shimmered in visible waves, distorting the world as if seen through heated glass. There was nothing pretty in the work or the place, and yet was there an energy crackling on the air that the most placid country meadow and the gentlest summer breeze could never hope to kindle.

For, the men who were wrestling with white-hot bars of iron were nimble and full of ferocious grace; calling warnings to one another that sounded like verses of a song, and now and then sharing a swig of cold tea from a tin bottle--a more reviving nectar far than many a costly wine--grinning through the grime if the metal bent true and cursing with gusto if it did not. The foundries! oh, the foundries! with their doors thrown wide; but through those gaps such visions! It was not alone that the great steam-hammers descending upon the anvils made a deafening crash, or that the chains and pulleys groaned and sang so mightily, or that the sparks flew up like swarms of angry, golden bees, or even that the blended smells of hot metal and burning coal were so potent to the throat. Nor was it that the puddles of cooling slag glowed with sullen reds and purples, or that the finished iron plates lay in monumental stacks, waiting for their purpose; but the workers were all so alive and so intent in the fierce promise of their craft, that they moved as one, a single body of sweat and muscle, shouting and heaving and labouring with a spirit that was almost holy; while the Foreman and his apprentices were so brisk and bright that the honest dirt upon their faces might have been a mask of honour, worn for all the world to see, and for the factory daws to admire if they chose.

But soon a whistle shrieked, a piercing note that cut through the din, and away the men came, flooding from the foundry gates in their work-clothes, but with their faces transformed by relief. And at the same time there emerged from a dozen stalls and carts a new population, hawkers and vendors, carrying their wares to the weary workers. The sight of these folk seemed to delight the girl very much, for she stood with Finn beside him at a pie-seller’s stall, and reaching into her pocket, pulled out not a coin, but a pinch of what looked like dust, and sprinkled it upon the pies. "Is there a peculiar quality in what you sprinkle from your pocket?" asked Finn.

"There is. My own."

"Would it apply to any kind of labour on this day?" asked Finn.

"To any honestly done. To the hardest most."

"Why to the hardest most?" asked Finn.

"Because it needs it most."

"Child," said Finn, after a moment’s thought, "I wonder that you, of all the joyful beings in this loud city, should wish to celebrate this filthy, dangerous toil."

"I!" cried the girl.

"You would have them find joy in this furnace, which shortens their lives and fills their lungs with poison," said Finn. "Wouldn't you?"

"I!" cried the girl.

"You seek to glamorise this place on this Festival Day?" said Finn. "When they should be resting."

"I seek!" exclaimed the child. "Forgive me if I am wrong. But was it not you, or men like you, who argued against this very holiday?"

"There are some upon this earth of yours," returned the girl, "who claim to know what is best for all, and who do their deeds of prudence, thrift, ill-will, judgment, and righteousness in the name of Order, who are as strange to us and all our kith and kin as if they had never felt a moment's joy. Its purpose was not to forge a harder world, but to furnish a softer one, to create, for a few shillings, a small, tangible piece of happiness.

From the toy-shop they passed, and came to a public park. It was no manicured garden of a country estate, but a true city park, a great green lung for the metropolis. Its trees were sturdy and soot-stained, their branches etched black against the grey sky, and its lawns were worn in patches to the bare earth by the ceaseless traffic of feet. Yet on this day, it was transformed. The sky, for all its gloom, was pricked with colour, for the boys were flying kites. They were not flimsy paper things, but robust contraptions of canvas and wood, shaped like dragons and birds and great, geometric diamonds, which climbed and bucked on the wind that swept between the tall buildings. Down below, the park was a sea of humanity. Whole families were camped upon blankets, sharing out pies and bottles of ginger beer. Finn, who had only ever seen this land as a potential site for a new warehouse, surveyed the scene with a strange feeling taking root in his chest. Finn peered at the humble dwelling. "How should I? It is one of thousands. A labourer, I suppose. A cog in the machine."

"It is the home of Silas Wright," said the girl. "Your junior clerk."

A woman was at the hearth, basting a small bird that was roasting on a spit. This must be Mrs Wright. It was a place for the forgotten, for those whose memory had faded from the world.

The Spirit moved through the overgrown paths until it stopped before one particular grave. It was a miserable plot, choked with weeds, distinguished from the others only by its stark loneliness. The Phantom stood, silent and immense, and pointed down at the headstone.

Finn approached it with a terrible reluctance. His legs felt like lead, his heart hammered against his ribs like a trapped bird. He knew, with a certainty that was beyond reason, what he was about to see. Yet he had to know. He had to be sure.

"Before I draw nearer to that stone to which you point," he said, his voice a mere husk of its former self, "answer me one question. Are these the shadows of the things that Will be, or are they shadows of things that May be, only?"

The Spirit made no reply. It merely held its position, its finger still fixed upon the grave. The silence was its answer.

Finn crept towards it, trembling, and fell upon his knees. He brushed away the snow and the clinging weeds from the stone, his fingers numb with cold and fear. And then he read the inscription, carved deep into the weather-beaten granite, the name of the man so unloved, unmourned, and plundered in his death.

FINNIGAN HALE.

"No!" he screamed, the sound torn from the very depths of his soul. "No, Spirit! Oh no, no!"

For the first time, the Spirit seemed to alter. The hand that had been pointing trembled slightly, a subtle but undeniable sign.

His own heart laughed: and that was quite enough for him.

He had no further intercourse with Spirits, but lived upon the Total Abstinence Principle, ever afterwards; and it was always said of him, that he knew how to keep Christmas well, if any man alive possessed the knowledge. Bob's voice was tremulous when he told them this, and trembled more when he said that Tiny Tim was growing strong and hearty.

His active little crutch was heard upon the floor, and back came Tiny Tim before another word was spoken, escorted by his brother and sister to his stool before the fire; and while Bob, turning up his cuffs--as if, poor fellow, they were capable of being made more shabby--compounded some hot mixture in a jug with gin and lemons, and stirred it round and round and put it on the hob to simmer; Master Peter, and the two ubiquitous young Cratchits went to fetch the goose, with which they soon returned in high procession.

Such a bustle ensued that you might have thought a goose the rarest of all birds; a feathered phenomenon, to which a black swan was a matter of course--and in truth it was something very like it in that house. Mrs. Cratchit made the gravy (ready beforehand in a little saucepan) hissing hot; Master Peter mashed the potatoes with incredible vigour; Miss Belinda sweetened up the apple-sauce; Martha dusted the hot plates; Bob took Tiny Tim beside him in a tiny corner at the table; the two young Cratchits set chairs for everybody, not forgetting themselves, and mounting guard upon their posts, crammed spoons into their mouths, lest they should shriek for goose before their turn came to be helped.

At last the dishes were set on, and grace was said. It was succeeded by a breathless pause, as Mrs. Cratchit, looking slowly all along the carving-knife, prepared to plunge it in the breast; but when she did, and when the long expected gush of stuffing issued forth, one murmur of delight arose all round the board, and even Tiny Tim, excited by the two young Cratchits, beat on the table with the handle of his knife, and feebly cried Hurrah!

There never was such a goose. Bob said he didn't believe there ever was such a goose cooked. Its tenderness and flavour, size and cheapness, were the themes of universal admiration. Eked out by apple-sauce and mashed potatoes, it was a sufficient dinner for the whole family; indeed, as Mrs. Cratchit said with great delight (surveying one small atom of a bone upon the dish), they hadn't ate it all at last! Yet every one had had enough, and the youngest Cratchits in particular, were steeped in sage and onion to the eyebrows! Suppose it should not be done enough! Suppose it should break in turning out! Suppose somebody should have got over the wall of the back-yard, and stolen it, while they were merry with the goose--a supposition at which the two young Cratchits became livid! All sorts of horrors were supposed.

Hallo! A great deal of steam! The pudding was out of the copper. A smell like a washing-day! That was the cloth. Oh, a wonderful pudding! Bob Cratchit said, and calmly too, that he regarded it as the greatest success achieved by Mrs. Cratchit since their marriage. After it had passed away, they were ten times merrier than before, from the mere relief of Scrooge the Baleful being done with. Bob Cratchit told them how he had a situation in his eye for Master Peter, which would bring in, if obtained, full five-and-sixpence weekly. Also how she had seen a countess and a lord some days before, and how the lord was much about as tall as Peter; at which Peter pulled up his collars so high that you couldn't have seen his head if you had been there. There was nothing of high mark in this. They were not a handsome family; they were not well dressed; their shoes were far from being water-proof; their clothes were scanty; and Peter might have known, and very likely did, the inside of a pawnbroker's. But, they were happy, grateful, pleased with one another, and contented with the time; and when they faded, and looked happier yet in the bright sprinklings of the Spirit's torch at parting, Scrooge had his eye upon them, and especially on Tiny Tim, until the last.

By this time it was getting dark, and snowing pretty heavily; and as Scrooge and the Spirit went along the streets, the brightness of the roaring fires in kitchens, parlours, and all sorts of rooms, was wonderful. Here, the flickering of the blaze showed preparations for a cosy dinner, with hot plates baking through and through before the fire, and deep red curtains, ready to be drawn to shut out cold and darkness. There, all the children of the house were running out into the snow to meet their married sisters, brothers, cousins, uncles, aunts, and be the first to greet them. Here, again, were shadows on the window-blind of guests assembling; and there a group of handsome girls, all hooded and fur-booted, and all chattering at once, tripped lightly off to some near neighbour's house; where, woe upon the single man who saw them enter--artful witches, well they knew it--in a glow!

But, if you had judged from the numbers of people on their way to friendly gatherings, you might have thought that no one was at home to give them welcome when they got there, instead of every house expecting company, and piling up its fires half-chimney high. Blessings on it, how the Ghost exulted! How it bared its breadth of breast, and opened its capacious palm, and floated on, pouring its generous kindness on everything within its reach! The very lamplighter, who ran on before, dotting the dusky street with specks of light, and who was dressed to spend the evening somewhere, laughed out loudly as the Spirit passed, though little dreaming of its company.

To Scrooge's horror, looking back, he saw the last of the land, a frightful range of rocks, behind them; and his ears were deafened by the thunder of water, as it rolled and roared, and raged among the dreadful caverns it had worn, and fiercely tried to undermine the earth.

Built upon a dismal reef of sunken rocks, some league or so from shore, on which the waters chafed and dashed, the wild year through, there stood a solitary lighthouse. Great heaps of sea-weed clung to its base, and storm-birds--born of the wind one might suppose, as sea-weed of the water--rose and fell about it, like the waves they skimmed.

But even here, two men who watched the light had made a fire, that through the loophole in the thick stone wall shed out a ray of brightness on the awful sea. Joining their horny hands over the rough table at which they sat, they wished each other Merry Christmas in their can of grog; and one of them, the elder, too, with his face all damaged and scarred with hard weather, as the figure-head of an old ship might be, struck up a sturdy song that was like a Gale in itself.

Again the Ghost sped on, above the black and heaving sea--on, on--until, being far away, as he told Scrooge, from any shore, they lighted on a ship. It was a great surprise to Scrooge, while listening to the moaning of the wind, and thinking what a solemn thing it was to move on through the lonely darkness over an unknown abyss, whose depths were secrets as profound as Death: it was a great surprise to Scrooge, to hear a hearty laugh. It was a much greater surprise to Scrooge to recognise it as his own nephew's, and to find himself in a bright, dry, gleaming room, with the Spirit standing smiling by his side, and looking at that same nephew with approving affability!

"Ha, ha!" laughed Scrooge's nephew. "Ha, ha, ha!"

If you should happen, by any unlikely chance, to know a man more blest in a laugh than Scrooge's nephew, all I can say is, I should like to know him too. Introduce him to me, and I'll cultivate his acquaintance.

It is a fair, even-handed, noble adjustment of things, that while there is infection in disease and sorrow, there is nothing in the world so irresistibly contagious as laughter and good-humour. When Scrooge's nephew laughed in this way: holding his sides, rolling his head, and twisting his face into the most extravagant contortions: Scrooge's niece, by marriage, laughed as heartily as he. And their assembled friends being not a bit behindhand, roared out lustily.

"He said that Christmas was a humbug, as I live!" cried Scrooge's nephew. "He believed it too!"

"More shame for him, Fred!" said Scrooge's niece, indignantly. Bless those women; they never do anything by halves. They are always in earnest.

She was very pretty: exceedingly pretty. With a dimpled, surprised-looking, capital face; a ripe little mouth, that seemed made to be kissed--as no doubt it was; all kinds of good little dots about her chin, that melted into one another when she laughed; and the sunniest pair of eyes you ever saw in any little creature's head. Scrooge's nephew revelled in another laugh, and as it was impossible to keep the infection off; though the plump sister tried hard to do it with aromatic vinegar; his example was unanimously followed.

"I was only going to say," said Scrooge's nephew, "that the consequence of his taking a dislike to us, and not making merry with us, is, as I think, that he loses some pleasant moments, which could do him no harm. If you should happen, by any unlikely chance, to know a man more blest in a laugh than Scrooge's nephew, all I can say is, I should like to know him too. Introduce him to me, and I'll cultivate his acquaintance.

It is a fair, even-handed, noble adjustment of things, that while there is infection in disease and sorrow, there is nothing in the world so irresistibly contagious as laughter and good-humour. When Scrooge's nephew laughed in this way: holding his sides, rolling his head, and twisting his face into the most extravagant contortions: Scrooge's niece, by marriage, laughed as heartily as he. And their assembled friends being not a bit behindhand, roared out lustily.

"He said that Christmas was a humbug, as I live!" cried Scrooge's nephew. "He believed it too!"

"More shame for him, Fred!" said Scrooge's niece, indignantly. Bless those women; they never do anything by halves. They are always in earnest. She was very pretty: exceedingly pretty. With a dimpled, surprised-looking, capital face; a ripe little mouth, that seemed made to be kissed--as no doubt it was; all kinds of good little dots about her chin, that melted into one another when she laughed; and the sunniest pair of eyes you ever saw in any little creature's head. Altogether she was what you would have called provoking, you know; but satisfactory, too. Oh, perfectly satisfactory.

"He's a comical old fellow," said Scrooge's nephew, "that's the truth: and not so pleasant as he might be. However, his offences carry their own punishment, and I have nothing to say against him."

"I'm sure he is very rich, Fred," hinted Scrooge's niece. "At least you always tell me so."

"What of that, my dear!" said Scrooge's nephew. "His wealth is of no use to him. He don't do any good with it. He don't make himself comfortable with it. He hasn't the satisfaction of thinking--ha, ha, ha!--that he is ever going to make us the richer for it."

"It seems a dreadful waste," observed the niece, thoughtfully arranging the crimson ribbon on her bodice. "To have so much, and to feel so little."

"The feeling is the very thing he has barricaded himself against, my love," returned Fred. "He has built his counting-house not of bricks and mortar, but of figures and ledgers, and has locked the door against any visitor who does not come bearing interest. He considers joy a liability, and affection a bad debt. What a sad accounting!"

Scrooge shifted his feet upon the carpet. He had never considered his life in such terms, as a balance-sheet of the soul, and found, to his dismay, that his nephew's audit rang with a terrible truth. The assets he had so prized, his gold and his guarantees, suddenly seemed liabilities of the most crushing sort, weighing him down, down, down into a debtor's prison of his own making.

The party, however, was not to be diverted by such sombre philosophy. No, not they. For Topper, having been justly vanquished in blind-man's buff, was now called upon to pay his forfeit, which, after much theatrical deliberation, was declared to be this: that he must sing a song of his own composing, on a topic chosen by the company. After a flurry of suggestions, ranging from the price of fish to the Queen's new bonnet, they settled, with great glee, upon "The Woes of a Lonely Bachelor at Christmas."

Topper, who was anything but woeful, and whose intentions towards the plump sister were becoming as transparent as the clearest window-pane, stood up, cleared his throat with the gravity of a man about to address Parliament, and began a doleful ballad. It was all about his empty chair, his single stocking, his solitary goose, and the tragic absence of a gentle hand to stir his pudding. He sang of seeing happy couples walking arm-in-arm, and of turning his face to the wall to weep, at which point he cast such a look of heartbroken despair at the plump sister that she hid her face in her fan, though her shoulders shook with a merriment she could not contain.

And Scrooge, invisible Scrooge, found himself chuckling. A dry, rusty sort of chuckle at first, like a key turning in a lock that had not been used for decades. But as Topper continued his lament, piling absurdity upon absurdity, the chuckle grew. It became a genuine laugh, a quiet one, to be sure, but a laugh nonetheless. He looked at these people, these bright, kind faces, illuminated by the fire and by their own good spirits, and felt a warmth spread through him that had nothing to do with the temperature of the room. It was the warmth of belonging, a feeling so foreign it was almost alarming.

The song ended with a final, booming note of utter misery, and the room erupted in applause. Topper gave a low, mournful bow, and then, his duty done, made a direct line for the seat next to the plump sister, his woes apparently forgotten.

"Spirit," Scrooge whispered, his voice hoarse with disuse and a strange new emotion. "This is... this is a fine thing. A very fine thing."

The Ghost of Christmas Present smiled down at him, his broad face a picture of pure benevolence. His torch seemed to burn a little brighter, casting a warm, golden light upon Scrooge's face. "There is happiness in the world, Ebenezer," the Spirit said, his voice like the gentle rolling of distant, summer thunder. "It is not a commodity to be bought or sold. It is a fire to be shared."

But as he spoke, Scrooge noticed a change come over the Spirit. A faint weariness touched the corners of his sparkling eyes, and the hairs of his great brown beard seemed to have acquired a few more threads of silver, like frost glittering on a winter's branch. The scene before them began to waver, the laughter to fade into an echo.

"My time with you grows short," the Spirit said, his voice losing some of its resonance. "Come. There is more to see."

He gestured, and the bright parlour dissolved like smoke. The warmth vanished, replaced by a biting cold that seemed to creep into Scrooge's very bones. They stood now in a part of the town Scrooge had never trod, though it was but a stone's throw from his own counting-house. It was a warren of alleys, a labyrinth of destitution, where the houses leaned against one another for support, as if they were too tired and broken to stand on their own. The air was thick with the smells of coal-smoke, unwashed bodies, and a general, pervasive dampness.

Yet even here, a flicker of the day's good cheer persisted. Through a grimy window, they saw a family huddled together in a room scarcely large enough for one. A father, his face smudged with soot from some factory or other, was attempting to mend a small wooden horse for his son, a pale, thin boy with eyes too large for his face. The mother was dividing a single, small meat-pie into five portions, an act of culinary magic that seemed to defy the laws of mathematics. There was no fire in the grate, only a few sorry-looking embers, yet they drew close to it as if it were a roaring blaze, sharing its memory of warmth.

The Ghost raised his torch, and for a moment, an unseen incense seemed to fill the room. The father looked up from his work and smiled at his wife. The children, who had been bickering in low, hungry whispers, fell silent and watched their mother with hopeful eyes. The meagre pie, when she served it, seemed to them a feast fit for a king.

"Do you see, Scrooge?" the Spirit murmured, his voice now noticeably weaker, older. "Your nephew spoke of your wealth. This man's wages for a week would not equal what you keep in your pocket for a day. Yet, who is the richer man this Christmas night?"

Scrooge had no answer. He could only watch, his heart a leaden weight in his chest. He saw not poverty, but love. He saw not squalor, but resilience. He saw a wealth he had never known how to count.

They moved on, drifting like a silent fog through the city's hidden veins of sorrow. They paused at the grim archway of a debtor's prison, where a woman and her children waited, hoping for a glimpse of the husband and father locked within. They did not weep, but stood in a silent, patient vigil, their faces upturned to a barred window high above. And from that window, a hand waved, just for a moment, before being pulled away. It was enough. It was a Christmas greeting. It was everything.

They hovered over the murky waters of the Thames, where, on the freezing deck of a coal-barge, two ragged boys, who should have been at home and in their beds, shared a stolen orange, peeling it carefully with frost-bitten fingers and dividing the segments with a solemnity befitting a sacrament. They spoke of the pantomime they had seen advertised, and dreamt of the glories of the clown and the fairy queen, their imaginations a roaring fire against the cold.

With every scene, Scrooge felt a new and unfamiliar pain. It was not the sharp sting of regret, but the dull, aching throb of empathy. These were not shadows; they were people. Their joys were real. Their sorrows were real. And he, in his fortress of gold, had declared them "surplus population." The words, his own words, came back to him now, and he would have given all his fortune to have them unsaid.

He turned to the Spirit, to ask some question, to voice the terrible turmoil in his soul, but he stopped short at the sight of him. The Ghost was terribly changed. He had aged years, no, decades, in the space of mere minutes. His jovial face was lined with care, his sparkling eyes were dim, and his once-brown hair and beard were as white as the snow that now began to fall in thick, silent flakes around them. He stooped, and seemed to have shrunk in his colossal size, leaning heavily upon his torch, which now gave off but a feeble, flickering light.

"Spirit! You are altered!" cried Scrooge in alarm. "What ails you?"

"My life upon this globe is very brief," the Spirit answered, his voice a hollow whisper. "It ends to-night."

"To-night!" exclaimed Scrooge.

"To-night at midnight. Hark! The time is drawing near."

The chimes of a neighbouring church clock rang out the three-quarters. Scrooge listened, and a dreadful new fear, a fear not for himself but for this mighty being, seized him.

"Forgive me if I am not justified in what I ask," said Scrooge, looking intently at the Spirit's robe, "but I see something strange, and not belonging to yourself, protruding from your skirts. Is it a foot or a claw?"

"It might be a claw, for the flesh there is upon it," was the Spirit's sorrowful reply. "Look here."

From the foldings of his robe, he brought two children; wretched, abject, frightful, hideous, miserable. They knelt down at his feet, and clung upon the outside of his garment. They were not lovely children, no, but monstrous and appalling. A boy and a girl. Yellow, meagre, ragged, scowling, wolfish; but prostrate, too, in their humility. Where graceful youth should have filled their features out, and touched them with its freshest tints, a stale and shrivelled hand, like that of age, had pinched, and twisted them, and pulled them into shreds. Where angels might have sat enthroned, devils lurked, and glared out menacingly. No change, no degradation, no perversion of humanity, in any grade, through all the mysteries of wonderful creation, has monsters half so horrible and dread.

Scrooge started back, appalled. Having them shown to him in this way, he tried to say they were fine children, but the words choked themselves, rather than be parties to a lie of such enormous magnitude.

"Spirit! are they yours?" Scrooge could say no more.

"They are Man's," said the Spirit, looking down upon them. "And they cling to me, appealing from their fathers. This boy is Ignorance. This girl is Want. Beware them both, and all of their degree, but most of all beware this boy, for on his brow I see that written which is Doom, unless the writing be erased. Deny it!" cried the Spirit, stretching out his hand towards the city. "Slander those who tell it ye! Admit it for your factious purposes, and make it worse! And bide the end!"

"Have they no refuge or resource?" cried Scrooge.

"Are there no prisons?" said the Spirit, turning on him for the last time with his own words. "

"Are there no workhouses?" The Spirit's voice, no longer booming with mirth but hollowed out by a terrible, righteous anger, struck Scrooge more forcefully than any physical blow. He threw back Scrooge's own words, the very ones he had spoken with such casual cruelty in his counting house, and they returned to him now as poisoned darts, each one finding its mark in his newly awakened conscience. Scrooge recoiled, not from the Spirit, but from the hideous image of himself that the Spirit's words had conjured. He could not answer. He could not even look up from the two wretched children who still clung to the Spirit's robe, their eyes, one dull with ignorance, the other sharp with want, fixed upon him. The bell of the church clock began to toll the hour, each stroke a leaden blow upon the frozen air. It was a funeral knell. The Spirit shuddered with each clang, and his form, already diminished, seemed to flicker and grow more transparent. The light from his torch, which had been a beacon of warmth and plenty, now sputtered like a dying candle, casting long, fearful shadows that danced like spectres. "My time is nearly gone," the Spirit breathed, his voice a rustle of dry leaves. "One more journey, Scrooge. One last sight this night has to show you." Scrooge, his heart a lump of ice in his chest, did not dare refuse. He reached out a trembling hand, but there was no robe to touch. He found himself grasping at empty air, and a cold more profound than any winter's frost passed through him as he realised the Spirit was becoming unsubstantial. He could see the grimy wall of the building behind the Ghost's once-sturdy frame. "Hurry!" the Spirit's voice echoed, seemingly from all around him. "There is little time! Little time!" The world dissolved. The city street, the falling snow, the two monstrous children, all swirled together into a vortex of grey and black. The sounds of the city were gone, replaced by a profound and unnerving silence, a silence broken only by a faint, rhythmic, and utterly soulless clang. It was not the cheerful ring of a blacksmith's hammer, nor the industrious sound of a shipwright's yard; it was a dead sound, a sound of grim necessity and nothing more. They stood before a building. It was not a house, nor a shop, nor a church. It was a great, grim pile of brick, so blackened by soot and time that it seemed to have absorbed all the misery of the world into its very pores. It was a fortress of despair, a monument to Misery, built of grey stone and colder still with human sorrow. High, unfeeling walls surrounded it, topped with jagged shards of broken glass that glittered cruelly in the faint, spectral light that now was all that remained of the Spirit's torch. A single, iron-bound gate stood barred against the world; or, as Scrooge suddenly, dreadfully understood, barred to keep the world within. "Spirit," Scrooge whispered, his voice catching in his throat. "What is this place?" "It is the answer to your question," the Spirit replied, his form now so faint that he was little more than a shimmering outline against the dreadful building. "It is a Workhouse." Scrooge felt a tremor of recognition, a vile and sickening feeling. He had passed such places, of course, many times, but had always looked away, dismissing them as a necessary, if unpleasant, part of the city's machinery, a place for the idle and the profligate. He had never truly seen one before. Now, he saw. He saw it with the Spirit's eyes, and with his own newly opened ones, and the sight was a desolation to his soul. The Spirit raised a hand that was now like smoke, and they passed through the barred gate and the thick stone walls as if they were made of mist. They were in a vast, cold, whitewashed hall. The air was heavy, not with the scent of pine and roasting goose, but with a single, sour odour of boiled cabbage, carbolic soap, and a deep, underlying scent of hopelessness that clung to everything. Long wooden tables and benches were arranged in stark, unforgiving rows. Here sat men, dozens of them, all dressed in the same coarse, grey uniform. They were hunched over bowls of thin, steaming gruel, spooning it into their mouths with a mechanical, joyless motion. They did not speak. They did not look at one another. Their faces were all turned downwards, as if the weight of their failure was a physical burden upon their necks. They were not old men, not all of them. Scrooge saw young men, men in the prime of their lives, their shoulders stooped, their hands, which might have held a craftsman's tool or a lover's hand, lying limp and useless on the table. He saw faces from which all hope had been scrubbed clean away, leaving only the dull resignation of a beast of burden. "These are the men," the Spirit's voice whispered in his ear, a sound as cold as the grave. "The fathers, the sons, the brothers. They are fed. They are housed. Are they not fortunate? Is this not the refuge you would provide?" Scrooge could not speak. His throat was tight with a new and unfamiliar agony. He had thought of the poor as a lazy, undifferentiated mass. Now he saw them as men, and their silent, individual suffering was a torment to him. They passed from the men's hall into another, separated by a barred door and a stern-faced warder who looked as if he had never smiled in his life. Here were the women. The same grey uniforms, the same silent benches, the same bowls of gruel. Mothers sat with a dreadful stillness, their eyes staring into nothing, their hands that had once rocked cradles now folded in their laps, empty. Young women, whose faces should have been bright with the promise of life, were pale and listless, their youth already curdled into a premature, weary age. A low, persistent coughing echoed through the hall, a dry, racking sound that spoke of damp and cold and lungs that were giving up the fight. And then they were in another ward, the most terrible of all. The children. They were not ragged and wolfish like Ignorance and Want; they were clean, horribly clean, and quiet, horribly quiet. They sat in rows on low stools, their little faces pale, their eyes large and unnaturally solemn. A few of the very smallest were crying, a thin, weak, whimpering sound that was almost worse than a full-throated wail, for it was a cry that expected no comfort. They were eating their Christmas dinner. A small tin mug of the same thin gruel, and a single, small piece of bread. Scrooge watched as one little boy, no older than Tiny Tim, finished his portion and, after a long, hesitant moment, held up his empty bowl. He did not speak. He simply held it up, a silent, desperate plea. A stern-faced woman in a black dress snatched the bowl from his hand without a word and set it down with a clatter. The boy flinched, drew his hand back as if struck, and lowered his head, his small shoulders shaking with silent sobs. Scrooge cried out. It was a choked, strangled sound, a sound of pure anguish. He turned to the Ghost, his face wet with tears he did not know he was shedding. "Spirit, no! Not on this day! Not on Christmas Day!" The Ghost of Christmas Present looked down at him, and in his fading eyes, Scrooge saw not anger, but a profound and bottomless sorrow. His light was almost gone. The torch was but a single, wavering spark, and his giant form was a mere shadow. "They have their dinner," the Spirit said, his voice barely a whisper. "The Union provides it. They are spared the indignity of starvation. Is that not what your fellows in Parliament argue? Is this not a kindness?" "A kindness?" Scrooge repeated, horrified. "This is torture! This is a tomb for the living!" The bell began to toll its final strokes before midnight. Eleven. The Spirit swayed on his feet. The two figures of Ignorance and Want, who had been but a memory, reappeared at his dissolving feet, but they were changed. They were no longer children. They had grown vast and monstrous, their shadowy forms filling the cold hall. Ignorance was a brutish giant with a low, sloping brow and eyes that burned with a dull, hateful fire. Want was a skeletal hag, her claws extended, her face a mask of insatiable hunger. They were no longer appealing from their fathers; they were claiming their inheritance. Twelve. The final stroke of midnight. As the sound died away, a great, final shudder went through the Spirit. His light, the last ember in his torch, winked out, plunging the scene into absolute darkness and a cold that seemed to freeze the very marrow in Scrooge's bones. The last thing he saw was the Spirit's face, looking at him with an expression of immense pity, and the last thing he heard was a single, fading, ghostly word that seemed to hang in the blackness forever. "Beware..." Then, nothing. The workhouse, the sorrowful inmates, the monstrous spectres of Ignorance and Want, all vanished. Scrooge was alone. He was kneeling on a cold stone floor in a darkness so complete it felt solid, a darkness that was not merely the absence of light, but a presence in itself. The silence was absolute, a heavy, pressing weight. He was adrift in a sea of black nothingness, the Spirit's final warning echoing in the abyss of his soul. He waited. He did not know for how long he waited. It might have been seconds, it might have been an eternity. He was afraid, more afraid than he had ever been in his life, but beneath the fear was a new and potent feeling: a desperate, yearning hope. A hope that it was not too late. A hope that the lessons he had learned would be allowed to bear fruit. A hope that another Spirit would come. He strained his ears, listening for a footstep, for a whisper, for any sign. And then, he felt it. A change in the quality of the cold. It grew deeper, more profound, a chilling cold that did not come from the weather but from the grave. A creeping dread, formless and silent, began to fill the void. Slowly, out of the deepest heart of the darkness, a new shape began to emerge. It was not illuminated, but rather seemed to be woven from the darkness itself; a tall, shrouded figure, its form and face concealed in the deep gloom of its own sable garment. It was the Phantom. And it was coming for him.

The Phantom moved, a silent, gliding motion that stirred no air and made no sound. It did not walk, but rather proceeded, as a vessel on a dark and tideless sea. He tried to speak, to frame a question, but his tongue cleaved to the roof of his mouth. The fear was different now. The first Spirit had been strange and ethereal; the second, boisterous and grand. This one was simply an absence. It was the void at the end of all things, the final, silent answer to every question life could pose. Its silence was more terrible than any shriek, its stillness more menacing than any threat.

"Ghost of the Future!" he said at last, his voice a dry croak. "I fear you more than any spectre I have seen. It answered not a word; but pointed onward with its hand.

"Lead on!" said Scrooge. "Lead on! The night is waning fast, and it is precious time to me, I know. Lead on, Spirit!"

The Phantom moved away as it had come towards him. Scrooge followed in the shadow of its dress, which seemed to bear him up, he thought, and carry him along. They scarcely seemed to enter the city; for the city rather seemed to spring up about them, and encompass them of its own accord. But there they were, in the heart of it; on 'Change, amongst the merchants; who hurried up and down, and chinked the money in their pockets, and conversed in groups, and looked at their watches, and trifled with their great gold seals; and so forth, as Scrooge had seen them do, full many a time.

The Spirit stopped beside one little knot of business men. Observing that the hand was pointed to them, Scrooge advanced to listen to their talk.

"No," said a great, fat man with a monstrous chin, "I don't know much about it, either way. I only know he's dead."

"When did he die?" inquired another.

"Last night, I believe."

"Why, what was the matter with him?" asked a third, taking a vast quantity of snuff out of a large snuff box. "I thought he'd never die."

"God knows," said the first, with a yawn.

"What has he done with his money?" asked a red-faced gentleman with a pendulous excrescence on the end of his nose, that shook like the gills of a turkey-cock.

"I haven't heard," said the man with the large chin, yawning again. "Left it to his Company, perhaps. He hasn't left it to me. That's all I know."

This pleasantry was received with a general laugh.

"It's likely to be a very cheap funeral," said the same speaker; "for upon my life I don't know of anybody to go to it. Suppose we make up a party and volunteer?"

"I don't mind going if a lunch is provided," observed the gentleman with the excrescence on his nose. "But I must be fed, if I make one."

Another laugh.

"Well, I am the most disinterested among you, after all," said the first speaker, "for I never wear black gloves, and I never eat lunch. But I'll offer to go, if anybody else will. When I come to think of it, I'm not at all sure that I wasn't his most particular friend; for we used to stop and speak whenever we met. Bye, bye!"

Speakers and listeners strolled away, and mixed with other groups. Scrooge knew the men, and looked towards the Spirit for an explanation. He had seen them every day upon the 'Change, and knew them to be men of substance, of good repute; yet their words were like the cawing of carrion crows, picking over the bones of some unlamented beast. The Phantom, silent as the grave to which it was the portal, glided onward to another part of the city.

It led him into a part of the town with which he was well acquainted; a district of ill fame, where the streets were foul and narrow; the shops and houses wretched; the people half-naked, drunken, slipshod, ugly. Alleys and archways, like so many cesspools, disgorged their offences of smell, and dirt, and life, upon the straggling streets; and the whole quarter reeked with crime, with filth, and misery.

Far in this den of infamous resort, there was a low-browed, beetling shop, below a pent-house roof, where iron, old rags, bottles, bones, and greasy offal, were bought. Upon the floor within, were piled up heaps of rusty keys, nails, chains, hinges, files, scales, weights, and refuse iron of all kinds. Secrets that few would like to scrutinise were bred and hidden in mountains of unseemly rags, masses of corrupted fat, and sepulchres of bones. Sitting in among the wares he dealt in, by a charcoal-stove, made of old bricks, was a grey-haired rascal, nearly seventy years of age; who had screened himself from the cold air without, by a frowsy curtain of miscellaneous tatters, hung upon a line; and was smoking his pipe with an air of placid enjoyment.

Scrooge and the Phantom came into the presence of this man, just as a woman with a heavy bundle slunk into the shop. But she had scarcely entered, when another woman, similarly laden, came in too; and she was closely followed by a man in faded black, who was no less startled by the sight of them, than they had been upon seeing each other. After a short period of blank astonishment, in which the old man with the pipe had joined them, they all three burst into a laugh.

"Let the charwoman alone to be the first!" cried she who had entered first. "Let the laundress alone to be the second; and let the undertaker's man alone to be the third. Look here, old Joe, here's a chance! If we haven't all three met here without meaning it!"

"You couldn't have met in a better place," said old Joe, removing his pipe from his mouth. "Come into the parlour. You were made free of it long ago, you know; and the other two an't strangers. Stop till I shut the door of the shop. Ah! How it skreeks! There an't such a rusty bit of metal in the place as its own hinges, I believe; and I'm sure there's no such old bones here, as mine. Ha, ha! We're all suitable to our calling, we're well matched. Come into the parlour. Come into the parlour."

The parlour was the space behind the screen of rags. The old man raked the fire together with an old stair-rod, and having trimmed his smoky lamp (for it was night), with the stem of his pipe, put it in his mouth again.

While he did this, the woman who had already spoken, threw her bundle on the floor, and sat down in a flaunting manner on a stool; crossing her elbows on her knees, and looking with a bold defiance at the other two.

"What odds then! What odds, Mrs Dilber?" said the woman. "Every person has a right to take care of themselves. He always did."

"That's true, indeed!" said the laundress. "No man more so."

"Why then, don't stand staring as if you was afraid, woman; who's the wiser? We're not going to pick holes in each other's coats, I suppose?"

"No, indeed!" said Mrs Dilber and the man together. "We should hope not."

"Very well, then!" cried the charwoman. "That's enough. Who's the worse for the loss of a few things like these? Not a dead man, I suppose."

"No, indeed," said Mrs Dilber, laughing.

"If he wanted to keep 'em after he was dead, a wicked old screw," pursued the woman, "why wasn't he natural in his lifetime? If he had been, he'd have had somebody to look after him when he was struck with Death, instead of lying gasping out his last there, alone by himself."

"It's the truest word that ever was spoke," said Mrs Dilber. "It's a judgment on him."

"I wish it was a little heavier judgment," replied the woman; "and it should have been, you may depend upon it, if I could have laid my hands on anything else. Open that bundle, old Joe, and let me know the value of it. Speak out plain. I'm not afraid to be the first, nor afraid for them to see it. We know pretty well that we were helping ourselves, before we met here, I believe. It's no sin. Open the bundle, Joe."

But the gallantry of her friends would not allow of this; and the man in faded black, mounting the breach first, produced his plunder. It was not extensive. A seal or two, a pencil-case, a pair of sleeve-buttons, and a brooch of no great value, were all. They were severally examined and appraised by old Joe, who chalked the sums he was disposed to give for each, upon the wall, and added them up into a total when he found there was nothing more to come.

"That's your account," said Joe, "and I wouldn't give another sixpence, if I was to be boiled for not doing it. Who's next?"

Mrs Dilber was next. Sheets and towels, a little wearing apparel, two old-fashioned silver teaspoons, a pair of sugar-tongs, and a few boots. Her account was entered on the wall in the same manner.

"I always give too much to ladies. It's a weakness of mine, and that's the way I ruin myself," said old Joe. "That's your account. If you asked me for another penny, and made it an open question, I'd repent of being so liberal and knock off half-a-crown."

"And now undo my bundle, Joe," said the first woman.

Joe went down on his knees for the greater convenience of opening it, and having unfastened a great many knots, dragged out a large and heavy roll of some dark stuff.

"What do you call this?" said Joe. "Bed-curtains!"

"Ah!" returned the woman, laughing and leaning forward on her crossed arms. "Bed-curtains!"

"You don't mean to say you took 'em down, rings and all, with him lying there?" said Joe.

"Yes I do," replied the woman. "Why not?"

"You were born to make your fortune," said Joe, "and you'll certainly do it."

"I certainly shan't hold my hand, when I can get anything in it by reaching it out, for the sake of such a man as He was, I promise you, Joe," returned the woman coolly. "Don't drop that oil upon the blankets, now."

"His blankets?" asked Joe.

"Whose else's do you think?" replied the woman. "He isn't likely to take cold without 'em, I dare say."

"I hope he didn't die of anything catching? Eh?" said old Joe, stopping in his work, and looking up.

"Don't you be afraid of that," returned the woman. "I an't so fond of his company that I'd loiter about him for such things, if he did. Ah! you may look through that shirt till your eyes ache; but you won't find a hole in it, nor a threadbare place. It's the best he had, and a fine one too. They'd have wasted it, if it hadn't been for me."

"What do you call wasting of it?" asked old Joe.

"Putting it on him to be buried in, to be sure," replied the woman with a laugh. "Somebody was fool enough to do it, but I took it off again. If calico an't good enough for such a purpose, it isn't good enough for anything. It's quite as becoming to the body. He can't look uglier than he did in that one."

Scrooge listened to this dialogue in horror. As they sat grouped about their spoil, in the scanty light afforded by the old man's lamp, he viewed them with a detestation and disgust, which could hardly be greater were they obscene demons, marketing the corpse itself.

"Ha, ha!" laughed the same woman, when old Joe, producing a flannel bag with money in it, told out their several gains upon the ground. "This is the end of it, you see! He frightened every one away from him when he was alive, to profit us when he was dead! Ha, ha, ha!"

"Spirit!" said Scrooge, shuddering from head to foot. "I see, I see. The case of this unhappy man might be my own. My life tends that way, now. Merciful Heaven, what is this!"

He recoiled in terror, for the scene had changed, and now he almost touched a bed: a bare, uncurtained bed, on which, beneath a ragged sheet, there lay a something covered up, which, though it was dumb, announced itself in awful language.

The room was very dark, too dark to be observed with any accuracy, though Scrooge glanced about it in obedience to a secret impulse, anxious to know what kind of room it was. A pale light, rising in the outer air, fell straight upon the bed; and on it, plundered and bereft, unwatched, unwept, uncared for, was the body of this man.

Scrooge glanced towards the Phantom. Its steady hand was pointed to the head. The cover was so carelessly adjusted that the slightest raising of it, the motion of a finger upon Scrooge's part, would have disclosed the face. He thought of it, felt how easy it would be to do, and longed to do it; but had no more power to withdraw the veil than to dismiss the spectre at his side.

Oh cold, cold, rigid, dreadful Death, set up thine altar here, and dress it with such terrors as thou hast at thy command: for this is thy dominion! But of the loved, revered, and honoured head, thou canst not turn one hair to thy dread purposes, or make one feature odious. It is not that the hand is heavy and will fall down when released; it is not that the heart and pulse are still; but that the hand was open, generous, and true; the heart brave, warm, and tender; and the pulse a man's. Strike, Shadow, strike! And see his good deeds springing from the wound, to sow the world with life immortal!

No voice pronounced these words in Scrooge's ears, and yet he heard them when he looked upon the bed. He thought, if this man could be raised up now, what would be his foremost thoughts? Avarice, hard-dealing, griping cares? They have brought him to a rich end, truly!

He lay, in the dark empty house, with not a man, a woman, or a child, to say that he was kind to me in this or that, and for the memory of one kind word I will be kind to him. A cat was tearing at the door, and there was a sound of gnawing rats beneath the hearth-stone. What they wanted in the room of death, and why they were so restless and disturbed, Scrooge did not dare to think.

"Spirit!" he said, "this is a fearful place. In leaving it, I shall not leave its lesson, trust me. Let us go!"

Still the Ghost pointed with an unmoved finger to the head.

"I understand you," Scrooge returned, "and I would do it if I could. But I have not the power, Spirit. I have not the power."

Again it seemed to look upon him.

"If there is any person in the town, who feels emotion caused by this man's death," said Scrooge, quite agonised, "show that person to me, Spirit, I beseech you!"

The phantom spread its dark robe before him for a moment, like a wing; and withdrawing it, revealed a room by daylight, where a mother and her children were.

She was expecting some one, and with anxious eagerness; for she walked up and down the room; started at every sound; looked out from the window; glanced at the clock; tried, but in vain, to work with her needle; and could hardly bear the voices of the children in their play.

At length the long-expected knock was heard. She hurried to the door, and met her husband; a man whose face was careworn and depressed, though he was young. There was a remarkable expression in it now; a kind of serious delight of which he felt ashamed, and which he struggled to repress.

He sat down to the dinner that had been hoarding for him by the fire; and when she asked him faintly what news (which was not until after a long silence), he appeared embarrassed how to answer.

"Is it good," she said, "or bad?", to help him.

"Bad," he answered.

"We are quite ruined?"

"No. There is hope yet, Caroline."

"If he relents," she said, amazed, "there is! Nothing is past hope, if such a miracle has happened."

"He is past relenting," said her husband. "He is dead."

She was a mild and patient creature if her face spoke truth; but she was thankful in her soul to hear it, and she said so, with clasped hands. She prayed forgiveness the next moment, and was sorry; but the first was the emotion of her heart.

"What the half-drunken woman whom I told you of last night, said to me, when I tried to see him and obtain a week's delay; and what I thought was a mere excuse to avoid me; turns out to have been quite true. He was not only very ill, but dying, then."

"To whom will our debt be transferred?"

"I don't know. But before that time we shall be ready with the money; and even though we were not, it would be a bad fortune indeed to find so merciless a creditor in his successor. We may sleep to-night with light hearts, Caroline!"

Yes. The emotion was a thankful one, and a happy one. Their breasts were lighter for this man's death. The only emotion that the Ghost could show him, caused by the event, was one of pleasure.

"Let me see some tenderness connected with a death," said Scrooge; "or that dark chamber, Spirit, which we left just now, will be for ever present to me."

The Ghost conducted him through several streets familiar to his feet; and as they went along, Scrooge looked here and there to find himself, but nowhere was he to be seen. They entered poor Bob Cratchit's house; the dwelling he had visited before; and found the mother and the children seated round the fire.

Quiet. Very quiet. The noisy little Cratchits were as still as statues in one corner, and sat looking up at Peter, who had a book before him. The mother and her daughters were engaged in sewing. But surely they were very quiet!

"And he took a child, and set him in the midst of them."

Where had Scrooge heard those words? He had not dreamed them. The boy must have been reading them out, as he and the Spirit crossed the threshold. Why did he not go on?

The mother laid her work upon the table, and put her hand up to her face.

"The colour hurts my eyes," she said.

The colour? Ah, poor Tiny Tim!

"They're better now again," said Cratchit's wife. "It makes them weak by candle-light; and I wouldn't show weak eyes to your father when he comes home, for the world. It must be near his time."

"Past it rather," Peter answered, shutting up his book. "But I think he has walked a little slower than he used, these last few evenings, mother."

They were very quiet again. At last she said, and in a steady, cheerful voice, that only faltered once:

"I have known him walk with, I have known him walk with Tiny Tim upon his shoulder, very fast indeed."

"And so have I," cried Peter. "Often."

"And so have I," exclaimed another. So had all.

"But he was very light to carry," she resumed, intent upon her work, "and his father loved him so, that it was no trouble: no trouble. And there is your father at the door!"

She hurried out to meet him; and little Bob in his comforter, he had need of it, poor fellow, came in. His tea was ready for him on the hob, and they all tried who should help him to it most. Then the two young Cratchits got upon his knees and laid, each child a little cheek, against his face, as if they said, "Don't mind it, father. Don't be grieved!"

Bob was very cheerful with them, and spoke pleasantly to all the family. He looked at the work upon the table, and praised the industry and speed of Mrs Cratchit and the girls. They would be done long before Sunday, he said.

"Sunday! You went to-day, then, Robert?" said his wife.

"Yes, my dear," returned Bob. "I wish you could have gone. It would have done you good to see how green a place it is. But you'll see it often. I promised him that I would walk there on a Sunday. My little, little child!" cried Bob. "My little child!"

He broke down all at once. He couldn't help it. If he could have helped it, he and his child would have been farther apart perhaps than they were.

He left the room, and went up-stairs into the room above, which was lighted cheerfully, and hung with Christmas. There was a chair set close beside the child, and there were signs of some one having been there lately. Poor Bob sat down in it, and when he had thought a little and composed himself, he kissed the little face. He was reconciled to what had happened, and went down again quite happy.

They drew about the fire, and talked; the girls and mother working still. Bob told them of the extraordinary kindness of Mr Scrooge's nephew, whom he had scarcely seen but once, and who, meeting him in the street that day, and seeing that he looked a little, "just a little down you know," said Bob, enquired what had happened to distress him. "On which," said Bob, "for he is the pleasantest-spoken gentleman you ever heard, I told him. 'I am heartily sorry for it, Mr Cratchit,' he said, 'and heartily sorry for your good wife.' By the bye, how he ever knew that, I don't know."

"Knew what, my dear?"

"Why, that you were a good wife," replied Bob.

"Everybody knows that!" said Peter.

"Very well observed, my boy!" cried Bob. "I hope they do. 'Heartily sorry,' he said, 'for your good wife. If I can be of service to you in any way,' he said, giving me his card, 'that's where I live. Pray come to me.' Now, it wasn't," cried Bob, "for the sake of anything he might be able to do for us, so much as for his kind way, that this was quite delightful. It really seemed as if he had known our Tiny Tim, and felt with us."

"I'm sure he's a good soul!" said Mrs Cratchit.

"You would be surer of it, my dear," returned Bob, "if you saw and spoke to him. I shouldn't be at all surprised, mark what I say!, if he got Peter a better situation."

"Only hear that, Peter," said Mrs Cratchit.

"And then," cried one of the girls, "Peter will be keeping company with some one, and setting up for himself."

"Get along with you!" retorted Peter, grinning.

"It's just as likely as not," said Bob, "one of these days; though there's plenty of time for that, my dear. But however and whenever we part from one another, I am sure we shall none of us forget poor Tiny Tim, shall we, or this first parting that there was among us?"

"Never, father!" cried they all.

"And I know," said Bob, "I know, my dears, that when we recollect how patient and how mild he was; although he was a little, little child; we shall not quarrel easily among ourselves, and forget poor Tiny Tim in doing it."

"No, never, father!" they all cried again.

"I am very happy," said little Bob, "I am very happy!"

Mrs Cratchit kissed him, his daughters kissed him, the two young Cratchits kissed him, and Peter and himself shook hands. Spirit of Tiny Tim, thy childish essence was from God!

"Spectre," said Scrooge, "something informs me that our parting is at hand. I know it, but I know not how. Tell me what man that was whom we saw lying dead?"

The Ghost of Christmas Yet To Come conveyed him, as before, though at what rate of speed Scrooge could not tell, into the resorts of business men, but showed him not himself. Indeed, the Spirit did not stay for anything, but went straight on, as to the end just now desired, until besought by Scrooge to tarry for a moment.

"This court," said Scrooge, "through which we hurry now, is where my place of occupation is, and has been for a length of time. I see the house. Let me behold what I shall be

in the days to come.

The Phantom, as before, answered not, but with its shrouded hand, pointed forward into the gloom of the court. The familiar grime-streaked facade of Scrooge's own counting-house loomed before them, a place where he had spent the best part of his days, counting his gains and, as it now seemed, compounding his losses. The sign above the door was altered. SCROOGE & MARLEY was gone, scraped from the wood as if it had never been, and in its place, painted in letters of a stark and unforgiving black, was a new name entirely. Scrooge squinted, a fresh dread icing his veins. He could not make out the particulars, and he felt a strange, unwilling gratitude for the dimness.

"Spirit, I see the place," Scrooge stammered, his voice a dry rasp. "It is as I left it, and yet... it is not. Is there no one there? Have I... have I quit this life of business for one of retirement and peace?"

The Ghost's only reply was to glide through the heavy oak door, beckoning Scrooge to follow. They passed through the wood as if it were but a denser fog, and stood within the office. A fire, larger than any Scrooge had ever permitted, burned in the grate; yet it cast no warmth, only a flickering, anxious light that made the shadows dance like imps of avarice. The clerk's desk, once Bob Cratchit's chilly perch, was occupied by a sallow-faced youth who scribbled furiously in a ledger, his brow furrowed not with cold, but with the intense calculation of one who fears his master's eye.

And in Scrooge's own chair, at Scrooge's own desk, sat a man of perhaps half Scrooge's years, but with a face already twice as pinched. His hair was thin and plastered to his scalp, his eyes were small and perpetually roving, and his lips were a bloodless line, a closed ledger in themselves. He was sorting a pile of deeds and bonds, his fingers moving with a nimble, predatory quickness.

"Well, Wickett?" the man snapped, without looking up. "Is the quarterly accounting complete?"

"Almost, sir," the clerk replied, his pen scratching even faster. "Only the matter of the shipping insurances and the Widnes mortgage to reconcile."

"The Widnes mortgage!" the man scoffed, at last looking up. His eyes were the colour of slate. "The widow has had her indulgence. Is the interest paid?"

"She sent a letter, sir," said the clerk, shrinking in his stool. "Pleading for another week. Her son has the fever, she says, and the apothecary's bills have..."

"Have made her a poor investment," the master of the house finished for him, his voice as sharp as a newly minted shilling. "Sentiment, Wickett, is the rust upon the hinges of commerce. It makes for a great deal of squeaking, but no turning. You will send round the bailiff this afternoon. A single day's indulgence is a crack in the dam of fiscal responsibility, and I will not have my enterprise drowned by a deluge of feminine tears. See to it."

"Yes, sir," the clerk whispered, his face pale.

Scrooge watched, aghast. This was his office, his chair, his very way of business, but distilled to an even colder, more potent essence. Here was a man who had taken the gospel of Gain, which Scrooge himself had so long preached, and had stripped it of any last, lingering vestige of humanity. This creature was not merely a successor; he was an improvement, a dreadful apotheosis of all that Scrooge had been.

"Spirit! I cannot bear this!" Scrooge cried, turning to the Phantom. "This man is a monster! A vulture picking at the bones of the unfortunate! Why do you show me such a heartless tyrant in my own place?"

The Ghost remained silent, but slowly turned its head, directing Scrooge's gaze back to the scene. The master of the house had returned to his papers, but a small, mirthless smile played on his lips.

"Good," he muttered to himself. "With the Widnes property secured, and the foreclosure on the dock-side warehouse, this will be the most profitable quarter since... since the old fool finally dropped off his perch." He chuckled, a dry, rattling sound. "To think he held that mortgage for a year, all for a few whimpered promises. The man had gone soft in his dotage. It took his death to make this business truly efficient."

The blood drained from Scrooge's face. The new name on the sign, the new master in his chair, the chillingly familiar ruthlessness... it all clicked into place with the terrible finality of a coffin lid being nailed shut. This was his legacy. Not to be remembered and reviled, but to be forgotten and surpassed in cruelty. His life's work had merely tilled the soil for a harder, more rapacious weed to grow.

He would have lingered, to protest, to argue, to deny the horrible logic of the scene, but the Spirit was already gliding away, its dark robe sweeping through the wall and out into the street. Scrooge stumbled after it, his mind reeling. The clamour of the city seemed louder now, more frantic, a chaotic symphony played on the strings of human want and desperation.

The Ghost did not pause. It led him through a labyrinth of narrow, ill-lit alleys he had never deigned to tread, places where the air was thick with the fug of cheap tobacco and the sour breath of gin. It stopped at last before a low-ceilinged public-house, from which roared a coarse, brutal kind of laughter. Scrooge hesitated at the threshold, the sounds within grating on his soul, but the Phantom's unyielding presence propelled him forward.

Inside, a knot of men, their faces flushed with drink and shabby triumph, were crowded around a rickety table. They were not the sleek men of the 'Change, but a rougher sort: small-time merchants, struggling artisans, men who had clearly lived their lives on the knife-edge of debt. They raised their pewter tankards high.

"A toast!" bellowed a large man with a face like a boiled ham. "To the late and unlamented Ebenezer Scrooge! May his bones lie as cold as his heart, and his soul find as much interest in the next world as he demanded in this one!"

A roar of approval went up. Another man, thin and weaselly, slammed his tankard down. "Hear, hear! I tell you, lads, when I heard the news, I felt like a man let out of Newgate! For fifteen years, his shadow has been over my door. Every quarter-day, there he was, a frost in summer, demanding his pound of flesh to the very last farthing!"

"He nearly had my printing press for a debt of a few pounds!" a third chimed in. "My little girl was sick, and I fell behind. Do you think he cared? He said her sickness was not a commercially viable reason for delay!"

"Uncharitable wretches!" Scrooge whispered, his hands clenched. "Ungrateful! I gave them time, I extended their credit! I merely expected what was mine!"

Yet a colder voice within him, a voice that had been silent for many years, admitted the justice of their bitter mirth. He had shown them no mercy, and they, in turn, showed him none now. They were celebrating his death as a liberation. The only emotion his passing had inspired was joy.

"He's in the blackest book of all now, I'll wager," said the weasel-faced man with a sly grin. "And the Devil himself is foreclosing on his soul!"

This sally was met with another howl of laughter, so cruel and so genuine that Scrooge felt a pang sharper than any he had ever known. He was not a man remembered; he was a problem solved.

Before the laughter could die down, the scene dissolved. The tavern, with its jeering faces and stale air, melted away, and Scrooge found himself standing in the biting wind before a building so vast and forbidding it seemed to suck the very light from the sky. It was a great brick pile, so devoid of ornament or welcome that it seemed to have been built from solidified gloom. High, barred windows stared out like vacant eyes over a desolate yard. Scrooge knew the place, though he had only ever passed it from the comfort of his carriage. It was the Union workhouse.

"Spirit," he pleaded, his teeth chattering from a cold that was not of the weather, "no. Not here. Any place but this."

The Ghost moved implacably towards the entrance. Within, the misery was palpable, a physical presence in the air. A long, cold refectory was sparsely populated with gaunt figures spooning a thin gruel from tin bowls. The silence was unnerving, broken only by the scrape of metal on metal and the occasional, hacking cough that echoed in the vaulted ceiling. Despair was the very mortar in the bricks; it was the seasoning in the food.

The Ghost directed Scrooge's attention to a corner of the great hall. An overseer, a portly man with a face full of sour authority, stood over a thin, haggard woman who was clutching a small, whimpering child.

"I am asking only for a little more, sir," the woman begged, her voice barely a whisper. "For the boy. He has no strength. The doctor said he needs nourishment."

"The portions are determined by the Board," the overseer stated, his voice devoid of any emotion. "Rules are rules, good woman. We are not here to encourage idleness or to subsidise weakness. If the boy cannot thrive on what is provided, then perhaps he is... superfluous."

Superfluous. A dreadful echo of his own words, spoken so carelessly to the gentlemen in his counting-house, now rang in Scrooge's ears with the force of a funeral bell. "Decrease the surplus population." He had said that. He had believed that. And here, in this dreadful, sterile hall, was the machinery of that philosophy at work. This pale, shivering child was the surplus population. Scrooge felt a wave of nausea so profound he thought his ghostly form might dissipate entirely.

He saw it now. It was not enough to die unloved and be replaced by a greater tyrant. It was not enough to be the subject of gleeful toasts in squalid pubs. The true horror of his life was this: this vast, efficient engine of misery, which he had endorsed with his words, with his taxes, with his cold, unblinking indifference. He was a shareholder in this establishment, a silent partner in the business of breaking spirits and starving children.

"Take me from this place!" he shrieked, clutching at the Spirit's robe. "I cannot! I will not! Spirit, hear me! I am not the man I was. I will be different! Show me no more!"

But the Ghost, for the first time, seemed to respond to his touch. Its silent, inexorable movement ceased. It stood perfectly still, and the dreadful workhouse faded from view, not into another scene, but into a thick, clinging blackness, a void that was colder and more silent than anything that had come before. They were no longer in the city. The air was damp and smelled of earth and decay.

Slowly, the darkness thinned, resolving itself into the shapes of stone. They stood in a churchyard, a place of dreadful neglect. It was a place Scrooge knew, the final resting place for the city's paupers and those with no one left to pay for a more respectable plot. It was a horrid, overgrown place, choked with grass of an unnaturally rank and vigorous growth, as if it were feeding on the despair of those buried beneath. Headstones, what few there were, were lopsided and weather-beaten, their inscriptions scoured away by time and indifference.

The Phantom stood among the graves, and pointed down.

Scrooge's legs would not move. He knew this was the end of their journey. He knew what the Spirit wanted him to see. His gaze followed the direction of the pointing hand. It singled out one grave amongst the tangle of weeds, a miserable plot of earth distinguished from the others only by the fact that it had been recently disturbed. Upon it was a simple, cheap headstone.

"Before I draw nearer to that stone to which you point," said Scrooge, in a voice trembling with a terror that shook his very soul, "answer me one question. Are these the shadows of the things that Will be, or are they shadows of the things that May be, only?"

Still the Ghost pointed downward to the grave by which it stood.

"Men's courses will foreshadow certain ends, to which, if persevered in, they must lead," said Scrooge. "But if the courses be departed from, the ends will change. Say it is so with what you show me!"

The Spirit was immovable as ever.

Scrooge crept towards it, trembling as he went; and following the finger, read upon the stone of the neglected grave his own name.

EBENEZER SCROOGE.

The name was the only thing upon the stone. No date. No epitaph. No word of remembrance. It was the stark, final, unadorned fact of his existence and its end. He had come into the world and had been taken from it, and this forgotten, weed-choked patch of earth was the sum total of his legacy.

"No, no," moaned Scrooge, dropping to his knees upon the cold, damp ground. "No, Spirit! Oh, no, no!"

The finger still pointed from the grave to him, and back again.

"Spirit!" he cried, in a final agony of supplication, clutching at its spectral robe. "Hear me! I am not the man I was. I will not be the man I must have been but for this intercourse. Why show me this, if I am past all hope?"

For the first time the hand appeared to shake.

"Good Spirit," he pursued, as down upon the ground he fell before it, "Your nature intercedes for me, and pities me. Assure me that I yet may change these shadows you have shown me, by an altered life!"

The kind hand trembled.

"I will honour Christmas in my heart, and try to keep it all the year. I will live in the Past, the Present, and the Future. The Spirits of all Three shall strive within me. I will not shut out the lessons that they teach. Oh, tell me I may sponge away the writing on this stone!"

In his agony, he caught the spectral hand. He sought to resolution, to find some substance in the shadowy form, but as he clasped it, he felt it shrink, collapse, and dwindle. The solemn Phantom, with its dreadful lessons and its silent, pointing finger, became smaller and smaller, until it was nothing more than a bedpost.
