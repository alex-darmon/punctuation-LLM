Barnaby Badger grumbled. "There is too much noise in this wood." "But it is very exciting news!" Penelope Rabbit told him, her nose twitching. "Willie Weasel has found a window that grew right out of the ground!" "A window!" Barnaby snorted. "Windows do not grow out of the ground, Penelope. You are a silly rabbit." "No, indeed!" she replied. "It is a tiny, shiny window, and Willie says you can see another world through it. A world that looks just like ours, only everything is backwards!" Barnaby Badger shook his great head. "I never heard such a foolish tale. I want _quiet_. I make it a rule to have a quiet afternoon. A ground-window is what you are talking about?" "You didn't listen," Penelope Rabbit said. "You asked for _news_. And everyone knows there is no more interesting news than a magic window." "That," said Barnaby Badger, "that is where we are not in agreement. I supposed you knew I wanted peace. But there is no great harm done, I expect," he added. "Tomorrow you can go look at your backwards world. But you must do it quietly, and you must not tell anyone else and disturb _my_ part of the forest." "But then nobody else would get to see it," Penelope objected. "But just think of all the quiet you will have," Barnaby reminded her. Penelope Rabbit stamped her foot. "I am afraid you are too grumpy for me to talk to any more," she told Barnaby Badger. "Oh! I did not mean to be so cross," Barnaby hastened to explain. "Then," Penelope said, "then how do I know you mean what you say when you ask me to be quiet?" And Barnaby Badger could find no answer to that. He was disappointed, too. For he was afraid he would never get his nap, after all.

Later that afternoon, the news of the magic window had traveled all the way to the Old Oak where Professor Owl lived. By the time Professor Owl heard the story, it had become quite grand. It went something like this: a diamond as big as a pumpkin had fallen from the sky. And it lay in the North Meadow. And if you looked into it, you could see the future. And if you looked at it for too long, you would be turned into a stone statue.

When Professor Owl heard the news he blinked his big, round eyes very slowly. "Who told you this?" he asked Willie Weasel. (It was Willie who brought the story to the Old Oak.) "You would not think it was so calm a matter if you were turned to stone."

"Stone!" Professor Owl hooted. "That is no magic diamond. That is what is called a _looking-glass_. A human from the big house must have dropped it on a picnic. It only shows you what is right in front of it."

As soon as he heard that, Willie Weasel scurried away to tell everybody about the looking-glass. And at last Penelope Rabbit heard the story. But by the time it reached her ears, after it had been told by one person to another a dozen times, the story was somewhat different from what it had been when Professor Owl first told it to Willie Weasel.

This is what Penelope heard: The thing in the meadow was a looking-glass. It had been left there by a king who had ninety-nine horses. The glass was filled with pictures of all the food in the world. And it could show you a picture of a carrot patch as big as the whole forest. And anyone could have a carrot, if they could figure out how to pull it out of the glass.

It was a flighty sort of bluebird who told the story to Penelope. Of course, after so much telling it had changed a good deal. But Penelope Rabbit did not know that. And she hurried to the North Meadow at once, to find the king's looking-glass.

When she reached the spot, she found the shiny object lying in the clover. Penelope Rabbit was surprised. It was not as big as a pumpkin at all. It was only as big as her two ears put together. And there was no king, nor any of his ninety-nine horses. Penelope Rabbit was somewhat disappointed. But she was glad of one thing: there was a picture of a beautiful rabbit with very long whiskers looking right at her.

"How lovely!" she said to herself. "And there is a giant clover right behind her! I must have it for my dinner."

Now, Penelope had never seen a looking-glass before. And you might think it would do her no good at all to try and take food from a picture. But she was quite sure she could do it. And the reason for that was that the rabbit in the glass seemed to be trying to nibble the clover, too!

That was enough for Penelope. "I'm glad I hurried!" she said to herself. "Here is the biggest clover I have ever seen! And how surprised everyone will be!" So she tried to grab the clover with her teeth. But all she did was bump her nose hard on the glass.

The very first person she surprised was Barnaby Badger himself. He had been woken up by all the chatter. And he had stomped to the meadow to see what all the fuss was about when he saw Penelope Rabbit bumping her nose again and again on the little piece of glass.

Barnaby Badger sighed. And he rolled his eyes. But Penelope Rabbit was so busy that she did not see him.

"I would like to know what she thinks she is doing," Barnaby Badger said, when he told his wife what had happened. "I will have to warn Professor Owl to keep his wise notions to himself, for I cannot have all this foolishness in the meadow."

Barnaby Badger's wife, a gentle soul named Beatrice, patted her husband's paw. "Now, Barnaby," she said soothingly. "You know how these things are. A bit of excitement is like a summer shower; it comes on fast and it is gone just as quickly."

"Humph!" Barnaby Badger grumbled. "This shower is leaving the meadow soaked with foolishness." And with that he turned over and tried to go back to sleep, though it was difficult with all the twittering and chattering that still echoed from the meadow.

XIII JASPER JAY'S DISCOVERY

After Penelope Rabbit hopped away, feeling rather silly and with a very sore nose, the little looking-glass lay quite forgotten in the clover. The sun shone upon it, and the glass shone right back, sending a bright, winking light across the North Meadow.

It was this winking light that caught the eye of Jasper Jay. Now, Jasper was known throughout the Green Forest for two things: his handsome blue coat and his love of knowing everybody's business. He was perched on the top of a tall hickory tree, pretending to be looking for beetles, but he was really watching the goings-on below. He had seen Penelope Rabbit bumping her nose, and he had laughed a harsh _caw-caw_ that made Penelope's ears burn with shame as she fled.

"Silly rabbit!" Jasper squawked to himself. "What could be so interesting in a patch of clover?"

Curiosity, which was even stronger in Jasper than his handsome feathers were blue, made him swoop down from his high perch. He landed with a little hop and strutted toward the shiny object. He cocked his head to one side, then the other. The looking-glass was lying flat, and in its shiny face Jasper Jay saw a perfect, deep blue, with fluffy white clouds drifting slowly across it.

Jasper Jay gasped. He looked up. There was the sky. He looked down. There was the sky again! It was a piece of the sky; a perfect, smooth, unwrinkled piece of the sky, captured and held right there in the grass. He had heard of the sky falling, but he never thought he would be the one to find a piece of it.

"Well, I never!" he cried out loud. "This is the most wonderful thing that has ever happened in Pleasant Valley! And I am the one who found it!"

He hopped around the looking-glass, admiring it from every angle. He pecked at the edge gently with his beak. It was quite solid. He was sure of it. He, Jasper Jay, had discovered a fallen piece of the sky.

He couldn't keep such important news to himself. Not for a minute longer! He had to tell someone. And he knew just who to tell first. He wanted to tell someone who would be very impressed indeed. So he picked up the looking-glass carefully in his beak. It was heavier than he expected, but his pride made him strong. And he flew off toward the great hollow log where Fatty Coon was usually taking his afternoon nap.

"Fatty Coon! Oh, Fatty Coon!" Jasper Jay shouted, his voice muffled by the looking-glass. He dropped it on the ground near the log and hopped up and down with excitement.

A sleepy face appeared in the end of the log. "What is all this racket about?" Fatty Coon grumbled, rubbing his eyes with his paws. "Can't a fellow get any rest? I was dreaming of a chicken coop with no latch on the door."

"Better than chicken coops!" Jasper squawked, puffing out his chest. "I have made a discovery! A great discovery! Look!" He pointed with his wing at the looking-glass.

Fatty Coon blinked. He yawned. "It is a piece of glass, Jasper. Someone has been careless."

"Glass!" Jasper Jay sneered. "That is how much you know, you sleepy-head! That is not glass. That is a piece of the sky!"

Fatty Coon sat up a little straighter. "A piece of the sky?" he repeated, and he waddled out of his log for a closer look. He peered into the glass. He saw the blue and the white clouds, just as Jasper had. Then he saw his own whiskered face looking back at him, right in the middle of the sky. "My, my," he murmured. "I am a handsome fellow, aren't I?"

"Never mind that!" Jasper said impatiently. "Do you see? It is the sky! I found it!"

Fatty Coon was a practical creature. His mind did not often dwell on things like the sky, unless it was about to rain on him. He poked the looking-glass with a curious claw. "If it is a piece of the sky," he said slowly, thinking hard, "then it must have fallen at night, too."

"Of course it did!" Jasper said, though he had not thought of that at all.

"Well," Fatty Coon continued, a new and interesting thought dawning in his mind, "when the sky is out at night, what does it have in it?"

"The moon," Jasper answered promptly.

"And what else?" Fatty prodded.

"Stars," said Jasper.

"Exactly!" Fatty Coon exclaimed. "Stars! And what are stars but little bright, shiny things? Little twinkly things. If this is a piece of the sky, then when it gets dark, it will be full of stars! And those stars must be like little shiny pebbles. Maybe even," he whispered, his eyes growing wide, "maybe even _diamonds_!"

Now Jasper Jay had not thought of this, either. A piece of the sky was a wonderful thing to find. But a piece of the sky full of diamonds! That was a hundred times more wonderful.

"You are right!" Jasper cried. "You are very clever, Fatty! It is full of diamonds! We just cannot see them in the daytime! We must wait until dark!"

"Wait?" Fatty Coon said, and his brow furrowed. "I am not very good at waiting. And what if someone else finds it before dark? What if Rinaldo Rat finds it? He would try to trade the diamonds for a piece of mouldy cheese. No, no. The diamonds are surely in there somewhere. We just have to look hard enough."

So the news traveled. Fatty Coon forgot all about his nap. He waddled off to tell Chatterer the Red Squirrel, because he knew Chatterer could spread news faster than a forest fire.

And Fatty Coon's story was a little different from what Jasper had told him.

"Chatterer!" he called, seeing the red squirrel chasing his own tail on a pine branch. "Stop that foolishness! I have news! Great news! Jasper Jay has found a piece of the sky in the North Meadow. And it is filled with _diamonds_!"

"Diamonds!" Chatterer squeaked, stopping so suddenly he nearly fell off the branch. His tail twitched back and forth. "Real diamonds?"

"As real as my appetite!" Fatty Coon assured him. "Hundreds of them! Maybe thousands! They are hidden inside, and we must all go and get them before Farmer Green finds them!"

That was all Chatterer the Red Squirrel needed to hear. He did not wait for any more details. He did not even say goodbye. He leaped from the branch and went racing through the treetops, a flash of red fur and pure excitement.

"Diamonds!" he chattered to a family of field mice he passed. "A box full of diamonds in the North Meadow! As big as acorns!"

"Diamonds!" he shrieked at a sleepy groundhog. "A whole pile of diamonds! As big as your head! Hurry, hurry!"

And so the news traveled, until at last it reached the sharp ears of old Mr. Crow.

By the time Mr. Crow heard the story, it had grown so much that it was almost bursting. It was Jimmy Skunk who told him, and Jimmy was so frightened and excited that he could hardly speak properly. He had been hiding under a fern when a whole flock of sparrows flew by, all chirping at once.

"Oh, Mr. Crow, sir!" Jimmy panted, running up to the tall pine where Mr. Crow was resting. "The most terrible, wonderful thing has happened!"

"Calm yourself," Mr. Crow said in his cross, gravelly voice. "And do not stand so close. What is it now? Has Farmer Green bought a new scarecrow?"

"Worse! Better! I mean," Jimmy stammered, "the sky has fallen! A big piece of it, right in the North Meadow! And it broke open, and it is filled with diamonds! Millions of them! As big as hen's eggs! And everyone is going to get them and they are going to fight and it will be dreadful!"

Mr. Crow blinked one black, beady eye. "Diamonds," he repeated slowly. He looked down at the trembling skunk. "You are a fool, Jimmy Skunk."

"But everyone is saying it!" Jimmy insisted. "Chatterer the Red Squirrel saw them! And Fatty Coon! It is true!"

Mr. Crow was silent for a long time. Of course, it was nonsense. The sky falling. Diamonds as big as hen's eggs. It was the sort of foolishness that little creatures with brains the size of a berry would believe. It was just like the time Sandy Chipmunk thought a mail-box was a trap, or that a letter with a picture on it was his.

And yet.

A tiny little thought took root in Mr. Crow's greedy heart. What if it were not _entirely_ nonsense? What if some rich person from the village had dropped a jewelry box? And what if these silly animals, seeing something shiny, had made up a story about the sky? The diamonds, however, might be real. And if there were diamonds, they belonged to someone who was smart enough to appreciate them. Someone like himself. Not to be scattered among squirrels and skunks and rabbits.

"Fools," he muttered to himself, but he was already ruffling his feathers. "They would not know a diamond from a dewdrop. If there is anything of value there, they will surely lose it or bury it like a nut and forget where they put it."

He looked down at Jimmy Skunk. "You stay here," he commanded. "I will go and look into this matter. I will put a stop to this foolishness."

But as Mr. Crow launched himself into the air and flew toward the North Meadow, he was not thinking about stopping foolishness at all. He was thinking about how beautiful a large, shiny diamond would look, and how all the other birds would envy him. He would be the king of the Green Forest, and not just in his own mind.

When he arrived at the North Meadow, it was a scene of great confusion. A crowd of little animals had gathered. Chatterer the Red Squirrel was running in circles. Penelope Rabbit was there, peeking from behind a bush, wanting to see the diamonds but afraid to get her nose bumped again. Fatty Coon was trying to push his way to the front, explaining to everyone that it was his idea about the diamonds in the first place.

And in the center of it all, on the patch of clover, lay the small, innocent looking-glass.

Mr. Crow landed with an important flap of his great black wings. "Stand back!" he cawed in his loudest, most commanding voice. "Let me see this great treasure!"

The smaller animals fell silent and moved aside, for they were all a little afraid of Mr. Crow when he used that voice.

Mr. Crow strutted forward. He looked down at the looking-glass. He saw the grass. He saw the sky. He saw a large, black, and very angry-looking crow staring back at him.

He stared for a long moment. He leaned closer. The crow in the glass leaned closer, too, its beak looking just as sharp and cross as his own. There were no diamonds. There were no stars. There was no treasure. It was a piece of glass. A worthless, common piece of glass from the world of Men.

A great and terrible anger welled up inside Mr. Crow. He had been tricked! Not by any clever creature, but by the foolishness of the entire forest! He had allowed his own good sense to be clouded by a silly rumor.

"_Haw-haw-haw!_" he laughed, but it was not a happy laugh. It was a harsh, bitter sound. "Diamonds! You brainless nitwits! You chattering fools! You have been chasing your own tails over a piece of trash!"

He jabbed at the looking-glass with his beak. It flipped over, showing its plain wooden back.

"There are your diamonds!" he sneered at the disappointed crowd. "A piece of wood and glass! Now go home and stop bothering me with your nonsense!"

The animals began to murmur. They felt very foolish. Chatterer's tail drooped. Fatty Coon tried to slink away as if he had never been there at all.

But just then, a soft _whoo-hoo_ echoed from the edge of the woods.

Professor Owl floated down on silent wings, landing on a low branch overlooking the meadow. He had been woken from his daylight sleep by the commotion, and had decided to investigate.

"It seems," Professor Owl said in his deep, calm voice, "that my property has caused quite a stir."

All heads turned to look at the owl.

"Your property, Professor?" asked Penelope Rabbit in a small voice.

"Indeed," said the owl. "I was preening my feathers with it this morning on this very branch. A clumsy move, I must admit, and I dropped it. I had intended to come back for it after my nap."

He looked at the little crowd of embarrassed faces. "I heard a great deal of talk," he went on. "Talk of the sky, and talk of diamonds. It is a curious thing, is it not? This is a _looking-glass_. It does not hold the sky, Jasper Jay; it only shows you what is already there. And it is not filled with diamonds, Fatty Coon; it only shows you what you wish to see in your own mind."

Professor Owl then looked directly at Mr. Crow, who was trying to look as if he had known it was a looking-glass all along.

"And you, Mr. Crow," the owl hooted softly. "I suppose when you looked into it, you saw a great and clever crow who was about to become very rich."

Mr. Crow ruffled his feathers and said nothing, for he knew that the Professor had seen right through him.

With a graceful swoop, Professor Owl dropped to the ground, picked up the looking-glass in his talons, and with a final, wise _whoo-hoo_, flew back to his home in the old oak tree.

The crowd of animals slowly drifted away. They were much quieter now. And for the rest of the day, not a single rumor was heard in the Green Forest. For everyone had been reminded that sometimes, the most foolish thing to see in a looking-glass is your own reflection, believing a silly story.

"Haw-haw!" Mr. Crow laughed loudly from his branch. "That's the end of that! A lot of fuss about nothing at all. Sandy Chipmunk is a very silly fellow indeed." And he flapped his great black wings and flew off to the cornfield, feeling very wise and important. He had put Sandy Chipmunk in his place, and that was always a satisfying feeling.

But the letter did not lie on the ground for long. A small, shy face peeked out from behind a clump of clover. It was Mrs. Meadow Mouse. She had seen the whole affair from a safe distance. She had seen Sandy's pride, and then his anger, and finally his disappointment. She was a quiet soul and did not like arguments, so she had kept herself hidden.

Now that both Sandy and Mr. Crow were gone, her curiosity got the better of her. She crept out, her little whiskers twitching, and scampered over to the piece of paper that Sandy had thrown away. The envelope was torn, but the folded sheet inside had fluttered open.

Mrs. Meadow Mouse was not a great reader, any more than Mr. Crow was. But she was very observant, and she knew a great deal about the small, important things of the world. She looked at the paper. Just as Mr. Crow had said, there were pictures on it. There was a picture of a chipmunk that looked very much like Sandy. And beside it was a picture of a plump, perfect beechnut.

Then she saw another picture. It was a picture of a red squirrel. And beside it was a picture of an ear of yellow corn.

Her heart gave a little jump. She looked closer. And there, near the bottom, was a picture of a little field mouse! It looked just like her own son, Timothy! And right next to the picture of the mouse was a picture of a round, yellow piece of cheese with holes in it.

"Why," she whispered to herself, "this is not a mean letter at all! It is a picture-list!"

She looked at the other pictures. There was the rat that Mr. Crow had mentioned, and next to it was a picture of a bag with strange markings on it; a bag of grain, she supposed. And there was a picture of a rabbit, and next to it, a fine-looking carrot.

Mrs. Meadow Mouse understood at once. It was not a letter filled with pictures of other people. It was a list of good things to eat, with pictures to show who might like them best! It was a wonderful, a beautiful, a most useful paper! Sandy Chipmunk had been right, in a way. The picture of the chipmunk meant that part of it _was_ for him!

She looked around. The wind was beginning to stir the leaves. She could not leave such a valuable thing lying on the ground. Carefully, she took a corner of the paper in her mouth and began to drag it toward her cozy home under the roots of the old willow tree. It was hard work, for the paper was large and she was very small. But she was a determined creature, and at last she had it safely inside her front door.

Meanwhile, Mr. Crow was not content to have his wisdom to himself. He flew straight to the big hickory tree where he knew he would find an audience. And sure enough, there was Jasper Jay, chattering away to Frisky Squirrel.

"Well, well," Mr. Crow announced, landing on a branch above them with a great flapping of his wings. "You will never guess the news I have."

"What is it? What is it?" cried Frisky Squirrel, hopping with excitement.

"It is about Sandy Chipmunk and his famous letter," Mr. Crow said, puffing out his chest. "I have discovered the truth of it."

"Tell us!" demanded Jasper Jay, who loved nothing more than a fresh piece of gossip.

"That letter," Mr. Crow said in a low, important voice, "was full of pictures of other forest-folk. It had a Red-Squirrel boy, and one of the Mouse family, and even Rinaldo Rat!"

"Goodness me!" said Frisky Squirrel. "Why would any one send Sandy a letter with pictures of other people?"

"Ah!" said Mr. Crow, looking very sly. "That is the secret. It was not a friendly letter. It was a list! And I believe it came from Farmer Green himself! It was a list of all the creatures he dislikes! I told Sandy it was a bad business, and he was so frightened he threw it away and ran for his life!"

Jasper Jay's eyes grew wide. "A danger-list! From Farmer Green!"

"Exactly!" Mr. Crow confirmed, though he had just thought of it that very second. "A picture-list of his enemies!"

That was all Jasper Jay needed to hear. "I must warn every one!" he shrieked, and flew off toward the briar-patch where Penelope Rabbit and her family lived.

Frisky Squirrel, who had forgotten all about his earlier part in the mail-box story, scampered down the hickory and raced toward the stone wall. The story was too exciting to keep to himself.

And so the news traveled once more through Pleasant Valley. But this time it was not a funny story about twelve horses and six haystacks of mail. This time it was a frightening story.

When Jasper Jay told Penelope Rabbit, he said that Farmer Green had made a list of all the folk he meant to catch in traps.

When Penelope Rabbit told Jimmy Skunk, she said that Farmer Green had drawn pictures of every one and nailed them to his barn as a warning.

When Jimmy Skunk told Fatty Coon, he said that the mail-carrier was now delivering threat-letters from the farmer, and that any one who received one would soon find his house torn down.

By the time the story reached poor Sandy Chipmunk, who was hiding miserably in a hollow log, it was a terrible tale indeed. It was Frisky Squirrel who found him.

"Sandy! Sandy! You're safe!" Frisky chattered, peering into the log.

"What do you mean?" Sandy asked in a small voice.

"The letter! The letter!" Frisky said breathlessly. "It was a black-list from Farmer Green! Mr. Crow figured it out! It had pictures of every one the farmer is going to hunt this winter! You are the cleverest chipmunk in the woods for throwing it away! You saved yourself from a dreadful fate!"

Sandy Chipmunk's heart went cold. A black-list! So Mr. Crow had been right to be suspicious. And Sandy had held the terrible thing right in his paws! He had even carried it in his mouth! He shivered all over.

"I--I'm glad I threw it away," he stammered. And he burrowed deeper into his log, determined not to come out for a very long time.

All over the Green Forest and through the Pleasant Valley, a hush fell. The cheerful chatter of the squirrels ceased. The rabbits stayed close to their burrows. No one dared to go near Farmer Green's fields. They were all waiting, and they were all afraid.

The only one who was not afraid was Mr. Crow. He perched on the highest branch of a dead chestnut tree, looking down on the quiet valley. He felt very proud. He, Mr. Crow, had saved them all from their foolishness. He was not just old; he was wise. And now every one knew it.

But down in her home under the willow roots, Mrs. Meadow Mouse was growing quite distressed. She could hear the fearful silence outside. Her own children were afraid to go out and play. She heard whispers from passers-by, whispers about a terrible list and Farmer Green's anger. And she knew it was all a dreadful mistake.

For three days she kept to herself, hoping the foolishness would pass. But it only grew worse. Finally, she knew she could not stay silent any longer. It was not her way to make a fuss, but it was also not her way to let a lie cause so much unhappiness.

She took the picture-paper, folded it as neatly as she could, and crept out of her house. She did not go to Jasper Jay or Frisky Squirrel. She knew that would only make matters worse. She went straight to the oldest and most sensible person she knew, besides Professor Owl who was often asleep. She went to the dell behind the big rocks to find Old Man Badger.

Old Man Badger was busy digging. He was a grumpy fellow who did not like to be disturbed.

"What is it? What is it?" he grumbled, poking his black-and-white nose out of his doorway. "Can't a fellow have a moment's peace?"

"I beg your pardon, Mr. Badger," Mrs. Mouse said in her timid voice. "But it is very important. It is about the Great Fear."

"The Great Fear?" Old Man Badger snorted. "You mean the Great Nonsense about Farmer Green's list. I've heard the whispers. A lot of foolishness."

"But it _is_ foolishness, Mr. Badger," she said. "And I have the proof."

She unfolded the paper on a flat stone. Old Man Badger squinted at it. He, too, could not read the strange black squiggles of Men. But like Mrs. Mouse, he had sharp eyes and a great deal of common sense.

"Hmmph," he said, looking at the pictures. "There is a chipmunk. And a nut. There is a squirrel. And corn." He peered closer, his nose almost touching the paper. "And there is a mouse. And cheese. This is not a list of enemies. This is a list of dinners."

"That is what I thought, sir," Mrs. Meadow Mouse squeaked.

"And all this fuss is because that black-feathered bag of wind, Mr. Crow, looked at it and saw only what his own jealous heart told him to see," the badger growled. "He has frightened the entire valley for nothing."

He sat back on his haunches and thought for a moment, scratching his chin with a long claw. "This has gone on long enough," he said finally. "We will have a meeting. A proper meeting. At sunset, in the little meadow. You will bring the paper, Mrs. Mouse. And I will make sure _every one_ is there. Especially Mr. Crow."

Word went out from Old Man Badger that there was to be a gathering. And when Old Man Badger called a meeting, every one came. He was not a gossip like Jasper Jay, nor a show-off like Mr. Crow. He was serious and fair, and the folk of the Green Forest respected him.

As the sun began to set, the little meadow filled with animals. They were all whispering nervously to one another. Sandy Chipmunk came, too, though he stayed near the edge of the crowd, ready to run. And high on a branch overlooking them all sat Mr. Crow, looking smug.

Old Man Badger lumbered into the center of the meadow. Mrs. Meadow Mouse stood beside him, looking very small and very brave.

"There has been a great deal of fear in the valley these past few days," Old Man Badger began in his deep, rumbling voice. "Fear of a list. A danger-list from Farmer Green."

A nervous murmur went through the crowd.

"Mr. Crow," the badger continued, looking up at the branch, "has told every one that he discovered this danger and warned you all."

"That I did," Mr. Crow cawed, fluffing his feathers. "It was a terrible document. Full of threats!"

"Is that so?" said the badger. "Well, Mrs. Meadow Mouse here found that very same document. And she has brought it for us all to see."

Mrs. Mouse carefully unfolded the paper on the flat stone where every one could see it. The animals crept closer. They saw the picture of Sandy Chipmunk. They saw the picture of the Red-Squirrel boy. They saw the picture of Mrs. Mouse's son. The whispers grew louder and more fearful.

"You see!" Mr. Crow shouted from his branch. "It is just as I said! A list of victims!"

"Be quiet, you," Old Man Badger grumbled without looking up. Then he turned to the crowd. "Now, I want you to look closely. Look at what is _beside_ each picture. Mrs. Mouse, will you please explain what you saw?"

In her small, clear voice, Mrs. Meadow Mouse pointed with a tiny paw. "Beside the picture of the chipmunk," she said, "is a picture of a beechnut. Beside the picture of the squirrel is a picture of corn. And beside the picture of the mouse is a picture of cheese."

She looked up at the crowd of faces. "It is not a list of enemies," she said. "It is a picture-list of food. It is to show what good things there are to eat, and who likes to eat them."

A great silence fell over the meadow. Then, Chatterer the Red Squirrel spoke. "You mean... it is like a menu?"

"Precisely," said Old Man Badger. "It is likely from that new store in the village, the one that sells feed and grain. It is an advertisement."

The animals all looked at one another. They felt very, very foolish. They had been hiding and trembling because of an advertisement. Then, all at once, they turned their heads and looked up at Mr. Crow.

Mr. Crow was so surprised that he almost fell off his branch. "A menu?" he squawked. "An advertisement? That is ridiculous! I--I saw what I saw!"

"You saw what your own pride wanted you to see," Old Man Badger said sternly. "You could not stand that Sandy Chipmunk might have something of his own. So you twisted the truth to make him feel small. And then you twisted it again to make yourself seem important. And in doing so, you have made a fool of every one, but most of all, you have made a fool of yourself."

Mr. Crow opened his beak to say something, but no sound came out. He looked down at the dozens of pairs of eyes staring up at him. Jasper Jay gave a small, mocking _caw_. Penelope Rabbit wrinkled her nose at him. Even Frisky Squirrel sat with his paws on his hips, shaking his head.

Sandy Chipmunk stepped forward. "Then... then the letter _was_ for me, in a way," he said. "The picture of the chipmunk was for me to see."

Sandy looked at the paper, and then he looked at Mr. Crow. He wasn't angry anymore. He just felt sad that Mr. Crow had to be so mean. And he felt ashamed that he had listened to him and thrown away something so interesting.

As for Mr. Crow, he could not bear the silence and the staring any longer. With a loud, angry croak that did not sound at all like a laugh, he launched himself into the air and flew away toward the dark woods, his feathers ruffled with shame.

The animals in the meadow began to chatter again, their relief turning into amusement at their own foolishness. They gathered around the picture-paper, pointing and laughing. It was indeed a very fine list of food.

And Sandy Chipmunk learned two things that day. The first was that a picture on a letter might mean it is for you after all. And the second, more important thing, was that it is far better to trust your own eyes than to listen to the jealous croaking of a grumpy old crow.

XIII A GRAND EXPEDITION

For a day or two after the great picture-paper excitement, Sandy Chipmunk was quite the most important person in Pleasant Valley. Every one wanted to hear the story from him. And Sandy was only too happy to tell it, making sure to show how he had been clever enough to notice the picture of a chipmunk on the envelope in the first place.

He did not mention that Mr. Crow had made him throw the letter away. And he did not mention that he, too, had been frightened by Mr. Crow's stories of a terrible hunter. It seemed best to forget those parts.

But while it was pleasant to be a hero, it was even more pleasant to think about that new store in the village. Frisky Squirrel could think of nothing else.

"Just imagine, Sandy!" he chattered, his tail twitching with excitement. "Sacks and sacks of corn! Barrels of nuts! Bins full of sunflower seeds! It must be the most wonderful place in the world."

"It is a long way to the village," Sandy Chipmunk said thoughtfully. He was sitting on his doorstep, nibbling a beechnut and trying to seem very wise and not at all excited. "And the village is full of people. And dogs."

"But think of the _food_!" Frisky insisted. "We wouldn't have to worry about winter at all if we could just get a few pawfuls from that store. The picture-paper said they had everything."

The news of Frisky's plan traveled quickly, as news always did. Soon a small group had gathered at the foot of the great oak where the mail-box was. Penelope Rabbit was there, her nose wrinkling nervously. And Mrs. Meadow Mouse had come with her eldest son, who was very brave for his size.

"It sounds very dangerous," Penelope Rabbit said in a small voice. "My mother always told me to stay away from the village."

"Nonsense!" declared Frisky Squirrel, puffing out his chest. "We are not babies. We shall be very careful. We will creep and crawl and hide. And Sandy will be with us. He has been to the cross-roads and is not afraid."

All eyes turned to Sandy Chipmunk. He had not said he would go. He was still thinking about the dogs. But when he saw them all looking at him, waiting for him to be brave, he found he could not say no.

"Of course I am not afraid," he said, trying to make his voice sound deep and steady. "We will just have to be very clever. We will go early in the morning, before too many people are awake."

Old Man Badger, who had been listening from the entrance to his burrow, shuffled forward. "That is a long walk for short legs," he rumbled. "And the village is no place for folk like us. A good plan is not enough; you must have sharp eyes and even sharper ears. And you must be ready to run."

"We will!" cried Frisky. "We will be as fast as the wind!"

"See that you are," the old badger said. And he turned and went back into his dark home.

So it was decided. The grand expedition to the new feed store would set out at dawn.

And all the while, high up in the top of a lonely pine tree at the edge of the woods, old Mr. Crow sat and watched. He had not spoken to any one since he had flown away in shame. He had heard the chatter and the planning, and his heart was black with bitterness.

"Fools," he muttered to himself, preening a feather that was not out of place. "Utter and complete fools. They believe a picture on a piece of paper, and now they will march straight into danger. They will be caught by cats or chased by dogs or trapped by boys. And it will serve them right."

He watched them scurry home to their beds, full of excitement for the morning.

"They will be sorry," he croaked softly to the setting sun. "They will be sorry they did not listen to Mr. Crow. And I will be there to see it." He sharpened his beak on a pine cone. "Yes, I will be there to say, 'I told you so'."

XIV THE LONG WALK

The sun had not yet climbed over the hills when a small and rather nervous party gathered by the stone wall. There was Sandy Chipmunk, trying to look like a bold leader. There was Frisky Squirrel, who was so excited he could not keep his tail still. And there was Penelope Rabbit, who looked as if she might bolt for her burrow at any moment, and Mrs. Mouse's son, Timothy, who stayed so close to Sandy that he kept tripping over his feet.

"Is every one ready?" Sandy whispered.

Frisky gave a little hop. "Ready and waiting!"

"Let us go, then," Sandy said. "And remember what Old Man Badger told us. Sharp eyes, sharp ears, and be ready to run."

They set off, a tiny parade of adventurers. They kept to the shadows of the stone wall, running from one patch of long grass to the next. The world seemed very big and quiet in the early morning light. A rooster crowed from Farmer Green's barn, and Penelope Rabbit jumped so high she almost landed on Frisky's back.

"It is only a rooster," Sandy said bravely, though his own heart had given a great thump.

High above them, a black shape detached itself from the top of the pine tree and followed, silent as a shadow. Mr. Crow kept a great distance, so they would not see him. He watched them scurry along the wall, and he sneered.

"Like ants going to a picnic," he scoffed to a passing cloud. "A picnic where a great foot will squash them flat."

Their first real trouble came when they reached a wide, muddy puddle that covered the entire path. It was too wide to jump and too deep to wade through.

"Oh, dear!" wailed Penelope Rabbit. "We shall have to go back."

"Never!" declared Frisky. He scrambled up the stone wall to get a better look. "There is no other way. The field on the other side has a dog in it. I can hear him yawning."

Sandy Chipmunk looked at the puddle. Then he looked at the wall. Then he looked at the branch of an old willow tree that hung low over the water. An idea popped into his head.

"Wait here," he said. He scurried up the wall just as Frisky had done. Then, gathering all his courage, he took a running leap from the wall to the willow branch. It bounced and swayed, but it held him. He crept along it until he was over the middle of the puddle.

"Now what are you doing?" called Frisky.

"I am making a bridge!" Sandy called back. He began to chew at a small, leafy twig that hung directly over the path on the other side. He chewed and chewed until, with a snap, the twig fell and landed perfectly across the narrowest part of the puddle. It was a very small bridge, but it was a bridge.

"It is too small!" said Penelope Rabbit.

"It is not too small for me!" squeaked Timothy Mouse. And he scurried across the twig as quick as a wink, his little feet making no sound at all.

Frisky Squirrel, who was a fine jumper, did not need the bridge. He simply took a mighty leap and landed neatly on the other side. But Penelope Rabbit was too timid to jump and too big for the twig.

Sandy thought for a moment. Then he scampered back along the branch and down the wall. He found a long, sturdy piece of bark and, with Frisky's help, they pushed and pulled it until it lay across the puddle. It was wobbly, but it was strong enough. Penelope Rabbit scurried across, her ears laid back.

Mr. Crow, watching from his branch, gave a grudging grunt. "Clever," he admitted to himself. "For a chipmunk." But then he shook his head. "It will not save them. Their luck will run out."

The travelers continued on. The sun was higher now, and the road was no longer empty. A milk wagon rattled past, drawn by a great horse whose hooves sounded like thunder. Our friends flattened themselves in the grass, their hearts pounding, until it was gone.

At last, they came to the edge of the village. The single road became a confusion of roads. There were houses everywhere, and fences, and the air was filled with strange smells and sounds: smoke from chimneys, the barking of a dozen dogs, and the clang-clang-clang of a blacksmith's hammer.

Penelope Rabbit's nose twitched without stopping. "I do not like it," she whispered. "It smells of people."

"But it also smells of... of _grain_!" Frisky said, his own nose held high. "This way! I am sure of it!"

And he led them cautiously around the back of a house, toward a new wooden building that stood on the corner. It had a wide porch and big glass windows. And painted in big black letters above the door were the words: MILLER'S FEED & GRAIN.

They had found it.

XV THE YELLOW BEAST

The feed store was everything the picture-paper had promised, and more. Through the wide open door, they could see sacks piled nearly to the ceiling. There were sacks of corn, sacks of oats, and fat burlap bags that smelled richly of peanuts and sunflower seeds. It was a treasure house. A palace of food.

The little adventurers hid in a thick lilac bush across the street, their eyes wide with wonder.

"Look at it all," Frisky breathed, his voice full of awe. "We could eat for a year and not make a dent in it."

"But how are we to get any?" asked Sandy. He was being the practical one. "There are men going in and out. And the sacks are all sealed."

"There will be spilled grain," Frisky said with great confidence. "There is _always_ spilled grain. We just have to wait for our chance."

But there was another problem. A very large, very yellow problem. On the sunny porch of the store, curled up on a worn-out mat, was a big yellow cat. It was fast asleep, its tail twitching now and then as it dreamed of catching mice.

"Oh, my," whispered Penelope Rabbit, her eyes as round as saucers. "It is a lion."

"It is not a lion," said Sandy, though he had to admit it was the biggest cat he had ever seen. "It is just a cat. But it is still very bad for us."

Just then, a loud, familiar _caw_ echoed from the roof of the blacksmith's shop nearby.

"Danger!" squawked Mr. Crow. He had flown ahead of them and had seen the cat at once. "Danger! A great yellow beast! It will eat you all for breakfast! Turn back, you fools, while you still can!"

Frisky Squirrel rolled his eyes. "Pay him no mind," he chattered to the others. "It is just Mr. Crow, trying to frighten us. He cannot bear that we are having an adventure without him. The cat is asleep! Now is our chance!"

But Sandy Chipmunk hesitated. He remembered what had happened with the picture-paper. He had listened to Mr. Crow and been wrong. But he had also learned that it was best to trust his own eyes. And his own eyes told him that the yellow cat, sleeping or not, was very real.

"Wait, Frisky," he said quietly. "He... he might be right this time."

"Right?" scoffed Frisky. "That old bag of black feathers? He is just jealous!"

"Look for yourself!" Sandy insisted. "Do you want to end up inside that beast?"

As if it had heard them, the yellow cat's ears twitched. One eye opened, a slit of green. It yawned, showing a great many very sharp, white teeth. Then it stood up, stretched its long body, and leaped lightly from the porch. It began to walk slowly, silently, toward the lilac bush where they were hiding.

"He sees us!" squeaked Timothy Mouse.

Penelope Rabbit was frozen with fear. She could not move a muscle.

"I told you! I told you so!" shrieked Mr. Crow from the rooftop. He was hopping from foot to foot in a mixture of terror and triumphant glee. "Fools! Now you will see what happens when you do not listen to your elders and betters!"

The cat was closer now. It crouched low to the ground, its tail lashing back and forth. It was no longer a sleepy, lazy cat. It was a hunter.

Sandy knew they had to do something. The street was too open, and their hiding place was about to be discovered. His mind raced. He looked at the cat, then at the store, then at the street. And then he saw it. A boy was coming out of a nearby house, carrying a pail.

"Frisky!" Sandy whispered urgently. "When I give the signal, you run to that fence post. Timothy, you hide under that loose board by the steps. Penelope, you must run for the woodpile. Do you understand?"

They all nodded, their eyes fixed on the creeping cat.

"What about you?" Frisky asked.

"I have a plan," Sandy said. "Now... get ready... _Now_!"

At his word, Frisky and Timothy darted in opposite directions. The cat was surprised. It did not know who to chase first. It took a step toward Frisky, then turned its head toward Timothy.

That was the moment Sandy had been waiting for. He took a deep breath and ran straight out of the bush, not away from the cat, but directly past its nose and toward the street.

This was a move the cat had not expected at all. A chipmunk running _toward_ danger? It was an insult to all good hunting. With an angry yowl, the cat abandoned all thought of Frisky and Timothy and sprang after Sandy.

Sandy ran as he had never run before. He dashed into the street, right into the path of the boy with the pail.

"Whoa!" the boy shouted, stumbling to avoid stepping on the little chipmunk. As he stumbled, the pail tipped, and a great splash of soapy water flew through the air and landed directly on the yellow cat, which was right behind Sandy.

Just then, their luck turned even better. A farmer came out of the feed store, grumbling to himself. "These new paper sacks are no good," he was saying. He hoisted a huge sack of cracked corn onto his shoulder. And as he did, the corner of the sack tore.

A golden stream of corn kernels poured onto the ground, making a lovely trail from the store's porch all the way to the farmer's wagon.

Quickly, before any one could see them, the little band of adventurers darted out and began to stuff their cheek pouches. It was a silly thing to be proud of, a letter with pictures of other people in it. He tried to put it out of his mind entirely. And helping Frisky Squirrel carry their new-won corn to their storehouses was a fine way to forget.

Each trip they made from the blackberry patch to their respective homes was a happy one. And from his high perch on a dead limb, Mr. Crow watched them. He had already tucked his own small share away in a secret hollow of the oak tree. And a strange feeling it was, to sit and watch others work without wanting to swoop down and take what they had. It was, he decided, a feeling of being very wise and very important. A general, watching his troops.

"That Silas Sow-Thistle won't be back for a week," Frisky Squirrel chattered, his cheeks bulging with kernels. "He will be the muddiest fellow in Pleasant Valley."

"He will," Sandy agreed, picking up another mouthful. "But it was Mr. Crow who sent him there. That was very clever, Mr. Crow."

Mr. Crow puffed out his chest. "Cleverness," he announced to the woods at large, "is a matter of knowing how other people think. And I knew that Silas Sow-Thistle thinks only of his stomach. A simple matter, really." He preened a feather, very pleased with himself.

The afternoon wore on, and the pile of corn behind the blackberry bushes grew smaller and smaller. The work was hard, but with the threat of Silas Sow-Thistle gone, it seemed more like a game. They were just finishing their last trip when a flash of red fur darted through the underbrush.

It was Chatterer, one of the Red-Squirrel boys. He was known for being even more excitable than Frisky Squirrel, and now he looked as if he might pop with excitement. He was holding something in his paws. It was a folded piece of paper, bright with colors.

"Look!" Chatterer squeaked, holding it up for them to see. "Look what I found! It fell from the sky!"

Sandy Chipmunk's heart gave a little jump. He knew that paper. It was the letter he had thrown away.

"It is a treasure map!" Chatterer declared, his nose twitching. "I am sure of it. See here," he said, pointing with a tiny claw at the pictures. "This is a picture of a great feast. There are pumpkins as big as stumps, and apples in a pile as high as a bush, and a prize ribbon! A great, blue prize ribbon!"

Frisky Squirrel scurried closer, his eyes wide. "A feast? Where?"

"I do not know," Chatterer admitted. "The map is a secret code. But it must be nearby! Why else would it have pictures of us on it?" He unfolded the paper more. "See? There is a picture that looks like you, Sandy. And one that looks like me! And there is Danny Meadow Mouse's cousin, and even a picture of a rat who looks like that sly fellow Rinaldo."

Mr. Crow swooped down from his branch and landed silently beside them. He peered at the paper. It was, just as he had remembered, an advertisement of some sort. But he was not about to admit that.

"Let me see that," he said in his most important voice. He took the paper in his beak and spread it on a flat stone. He stared at it for a long, long time. Of course, the strange black squiggles meant nothing to him, but the pictures told a story of their own. A story he could invent as he went along.

"Hmph," he said at last. "It is not a map to a feast. It is an invitation."

"An invitation!" Frisky and Chatterer said at the same time.

"Yes," said Mr. Crow, warming to his subject. "An invitation from Farmer Green himself. He is holding a Grand Fair for all the folk of the woods. And he will give a prize to the best of us."

"A prize?" Sandy asked timidly. He was beginning to think he had thrown away something very important indeed.

"A Grand Prize," Mr. Crow corrected him. He pointed a claw at the picture of a large bag with strange lettering on it. "This, you see, is a picture of a bag of seeds. The prize is a whole bag of seeds, bigger than any of you, to last all winter."

Chatterer the Red Squirrel let out a squeal of pure joy. A whole bag of seeds! He did not wait to hear another word. He snatched the paper back from Mr. Crow.

"I am going to tell everyone!" he cried. "Farmer Green's Grand Fair! With a Grand Prize!" And he zipped away through the trees, a streak of red fur and happy chatter.

Sandy looked at Frisky. Frisky looked at Sandy. Then they both looked at Mr. Crow.

"Is it true, Mr. Crow?" Sandy asked. "Is there really a fair?"

Mr. Crow shrugged his black shoulders. "There are pictures of prizes," he said. "And pictures of folk from the woods. It is a logical conclusion. And besides," he added, "it is a much better story than a simple treasure map." He felt very pleased. He had taken a silly rumor and turned it into a grand and exciting one.

But their talk was cut short by a terrible sound. It was a low, angry rumbling, coming from the direction of the swamp. It sounded like a thunderstorm, but the sky was clear and blue. The rumbling grew louder, and with it came the snapping of twigs and the crashing of bushes.

Sandy and Frisky dove for cover under the roots of the old oak. Mr. Crow flew to his highest branch and peered toward the noise.

A fearsome sight met his eyes.

Lumbering out of the woods was Silas Sow-Thistle. He was no longer just a big, hungry fellow. He was a monster. He was covered from his snout to his tail in thick, black, stinking mud. Cattail reeds were stuck to his back, and green slime dripped from his ears. And his little eyes, peering out from the mask of mud, were burning with rage.

"CHIPMUNK!" he roared, and the ground seemed to shake. "SQUIRREL!"

Sandy trembled under the root. Frisky whimpered beside him.

Silas stomped into the clearing, his great feet squelching with mud and water. He sniffed the air. He smelled corn. He followed the scent to the blackberry patch and saw the few stray kernels left on the ground. The pile was gone.

His roar was even louder this time. "They took it all! After they sent me to the swamp! They tricked me!" He spun around, his eyes scanning the trees. "I will find them! I will find them and I will sit on their houses! I will squash their winter food! I will..."

He stopped, for his eyes had found Mr. Crow, sitting calmly on the high branch.

"You!" Silas bellowed. "You were with them! This was _your_ idea!"

Mr. Crow swallowed. Silas Sow-Thistle looked even bigger up close. And much angrier. For a moment, Mr. Crow thought about flying away. It was the sensible thing to do. It was no concern of his if a chipmunk's house was sat upon. But then he looked down and saw two small, terrified faces peeking out from under the root. He remembered the feeling of being a general. A general did not abandon his troops.

"My dear Silas," Mr. Crow called down, his voice as smooth as he could make it. "You seem to be upset."

"Upset?" Silas roared, kicking a stone so hard it flew into the bushes. "I have spent half the day digging in mud! For nothing! I am tired and I am hungry and I am very, very angry! And I am going to take all of their nuts and corn!"

"Ah," said Mr. Crow. "That is where you are mistaken. You were not digging for nothing. You were practicing."

Silas stopped. "Practicing? Practicing for what?"

"Why, for the Grand Fair, of course," Mr. Crow said. "I am surprised you have not heard. The news is all over the valley."

Just then, as if on cue, they heard Chatterer's excited voice in the distance, telling Peter Rabbit about the fair. Then they heard Peter Rabbit telling Jimmy Skunk. The rumor was traveling fast.

Silas Sow-Thistle's muddy face wrinkled in confusion. "Fair? What fair?"

"Farmer Green's Grand Fair," Mr. Crow explained patiently. "I sent you to the swamp so you could get a head start on the competition. What better way to train for the Strongest Fellow contest than to dig in thick mud?"

Silas blinked. "Strongest Fellow contest?"

"But of course," Mr. Crow went on, feeling his confidence return. "And the prize for that is a whole bushel of apples. But that is nothing. _Nothing_." He leaned forward conspiratorially. "That little bit of corn you wanted? That was just a distraction. Sandy and Frisky were trying to keep you busy with small things, so they could have the _real_ prize for themselves."

Greed began to battle with anger on Silas Sow-Thistle's face. And greed, as it usually did with Silas, was winning. "The real prize?" he grunted.

"The Grand Prize," Mr. Crow said in a hushed, dramatic voice. "A mountain of clover. Sweet, fresh clover, piled as high as your head. Enough to last you ten winters."

Silas Sow-Thistle's jaw dropped. A mountain of clover. He forgot all about the corn. He forgot about being tricked. All he could think of was that beautiful, wonderful prize.

"How?" he demanded. "How do I win this... this mountain?"

"Ah," said Mr. Crow. "It will not be easy. There are many contests. You must prove you are the best of all the forest-folk." He motioned with his wing, and Sandy and Frisky, understanding at last, crept out from under the root. Sandy was holding the folded paper, which Chatterer had dropped in his haste.

"Show him the invitation, Sandy," Mr. Crow commanded.

Sandy timidly held up the paper. Silas squinted at it.

"It is all there, in the pictures," Mr. Crow explained, flying down to a lower branch to point. "Here," he said, tapping the image of a very large pumpkin, "is the Biggest Vegetable contest. You must find the largest pumpkin in Farmer Green's patch."

Silas nodded, his little mind working.

"And here," Mr. Crow said, pointing to a drawing of a leaping rabbit, "is the Fastest Runner contest. You must practice running."

Silas nodded again. He was not a fast runner, but for a mountain of clover, he would try.

"And here," Mr. Crow said, tapping the picture of the prize ribbon, "is the most important contest of all. The Handsomest Fellow contest."

Silas looked down at himself. He was covered in black, smelly mud.

"You see?" said Mr. Crow. "You are a mess. You cannot win the prize for Handsomest Fellow looking like that. You must go to the river and wash. You must make yourself presentable."

Silas Sow-Thistle looked from the muddy mess on his own fur to the beautiful blue ribbon in the picture. He looked at the drawing of the mountain of clover that Mr. Crow had so cleverly invented. He had to win. He simply had to.

"A fair," he mumbled. "A Grand Prize." He looked at Sandy and Frisky, and his eyes were no longer angry; they were suspicious. "You tried to keep it from me," he accused them.

"We were just following orders," Frisky squeaked, glancing up at Mr. Crow.

"The general's orders," Sandy added bravely.

Silas grunted. It made a sort of sense. Of course they would want the prize for themselves. But he, Silas Sow-Thistle, was bigger and stronger. And now he was smarter, for he knew the secret.

"I will win," he declared. "I will find the biggest pumpkin. I will practice my running. And I will go and wash this instant!"

Without another glance at them, he turned and stomped off again, this time in the direction of Swift River. His mind was not on revenge any more. It was full of pumpkins and races and, most of all, a glorious mountain of sweet clover.

When he was gone, the woods seemed very quiet. Sandy and Frisky stared at the spot where the muddy giant had stood. Then they turned and looked up at Mr. Crow.

Mr. Crow was busy preening a feather that was not out of place at all. "Well," he said. "That is that. I believe he will be busy for some time."

"You did it again, Mr. Crow!" Frisky chattered. "You saved us!"

"I merely corrected another rumor," Mr. Crow said, though he could not help but sound pleased. "And I applied a bit of logic. It is a powerful tool."

Sandy Chipmunk looked down at the colorful paper in his paws. The very same letter that Mr. Crow had called foolish and worthless just a short while ago. "This letter," he said slowly. "It is a very useful thing, after all."

"Indeed," said Mr. Crow. "In the right hands--or wings--anything can be useful." He peered at the paper again. "The pictures are quite clear. A pumpkin. A rabbit. Ribbons. It is plainly an advertisement for the county fair that the humans have every autumn. Silas is simply going to be looking in all the wrong places for it."

"But Mr. Crow," Sandy said, a worried wrinkle on his forehead. "What will happen when he finds out there is no Grand Prize of clover? He will be even angrier than before."

Mr. Crow gave a dry caw that was his version of a laugh. "By the time he figures that out," he said, "winter will have come. He will be so busy looking for a non-existent fair that he will forget to be angry. And he will be the cleanest sow-thistle in the valley. We have done him a favor, if you think about it."

Frisky Squirrel giggled. Sandy Chipmunk thought about it, and he had to agree. He looked at the clever, crotchety old bird, who had saved them not once, but twice. He thought of the shared corn, and the shared danger, and the shared victory.

"Mr. Crow," Sandy said. "I think you are not just a general. I think you are a friend."

Mr. Crow stopped preening. He looked at Sandy, and then at Frisky, who was nodding his head so hard his tail bobbed up and down. A friend. It was a very strange word. He had followers, and listeners, and creatures who were afraid of him, but he had never had a friend.

"Hmph," he said gruffly. "Do not be sentimental. It was a matter of convenience." But he did not fly away. He stayed on his branch, watching the two small creatures who were his troops, and his allies, and perhaps, his friends. And for the second time that day, Mr. Crow felt that strange, unfamiliar feeling. And he decided he did not mind it at all. It was almost as good as finding a whole field of corn all for himself. XIII THE GOLDEN WATCHER

The last days of autumn were pleasant in Pleasant Valley. The air was crisp, the leaves were a riot of red and gold, and the forest-folk were busy with their final preparations for winter. Sandy Chipmunk and Frisky Squirrel were partners now in almost everything. And often, perched on a high branch above them, old Mr. Crow would offer his advice; for which he expected, and usually received, a toll of a few corn kernels or a particularly fine acorn.

He still called it a matter of convenience. But Frisky and Sandy knew better. They knew that when Jasper Jay tried to bully Frisky away from a hickory tree, Mr. Crow had only to clear his throat from a neighboring pine to send Jasper packing. And they knew that when Sandy had slipped on a mossy stone and tumbled into a puddle, Mr. Crow had cawed so loudly and continuously that Farmer Green's boy, Johnnie, had come to see what the fuss was about, frightening away a weasel that had been creeping up with a hungry look in his eyes.

"I was merely announcing a change in the weather," Mr. Crow had said gruffly, after the weasel had vanished. "A sudden dampness was in the air."

But Sandy Chipmunk had seen the weasel. And he knew that Mr. Crow was more than a general; he was a friend who was simply too proud to admit it.

One bright afternoon, Frisky Squirrel was making a death-defying leap from the top of a tall maple to the roof of Farmer Green's woodshed. He was a great show-off, was Frisky. He liked to feel the wind in his whiskers and to imagine that all the world was watching him. But on this day, as he landed with a light thump on the shingles, he saw something that made him forget all about showing off.

Perched on the very peak of Farmer Green's newest, tallest shed was a bird. But it was a bird unlike any Frisky had ever seen. It was made of pure, shining gold. It stood on one leg on a little ball, and it held its chest out with a pride that made Mr. Crow himself seem humble. It caught the afternoon sun and blazed with a light that made Frisky squint. It was, he decided, the most magnificent bird in the whole world.

"Good day, sir!" Frisky chattered, for he was a friendly soul.

The golden bird said nothing. It simply stared straight ahead, its painted eye fixed on some point far away.

"Ahem! A fine day for sitting on a roof!" Frisky tried again, creeping a little closer.

Still the golden bird was silent. It was so proud, so aloof, that it would not even turn its head. Frisky was terribly impressed. This was no ordinary bird. This must be a king. A king of all the birds! He had heard stories of such things, but he had never expected to see one in Pleasant Valley.

Frisky was so excited that he quite forgot the nuts he had come to hide in the woodshed eaves. He scampered back down the maple tree and raced through the woods until he found Fatty Coon, who was trying to wash a mushroom in a stream.

"Fatty! Fatty! You'll never guess!" Frisky squeaked, his tail twitching with excitement. "A king has arrived! A golden king of all the birds! He is sitting on Farmer Green's new shed, and he is so grand that he will not speak to common folk like me!"

Fatty Coon, who was not very bright but had a great respect for royalty, dropped his mushroom. It floated away downstream, but he paid it no mind. "A king?" he mumbled. "A golden king?"

"Yes!" said Frisky. "And he just stands there, watching everything."

By the time Fatty Coon told the story to Mrs. Rabbit, it had changed a little. "There is a new guard at the farm," he said solemnly. "A giant golden bird sent by the King of the Birds himself. He stands on the roof and he watches _everything_. He never blinks."

Mrs. Rabbit, who had seven children to worry about, wrung her paws. A guard! That was terrible news. She hurried away and told Peter Rabbit, who was her second cousin. "Peter, you must be careful!" she whispered. "Farmer Green has a new watchman. It's a magical golden bird that stands on the shed, and it sees everything you do, and it never sleeps!"

Peter Rabbit told Jasper Jay. And Jasper Jay, who loved to be the bearer of bad news, flew far and wide, shouting the story from the treetops. By the time the sun began to set, every single creature in the woods and along the river bank had heard the news. And the story they heard was a terrifying one indeed.

It went something like this: Farmer Green had made a terrible bargain with a mountain wizard. And the wizard had given him a fearful creature, the Golden Watcher. It looked like a bird, but it was made of magic metal. It stood on the shed roof and its eyes could see through trees and into burrows. It could hear a whisper from a mile away. And every night, it flew to Farmer Green's window and told him every secret of the forest-folk; who had been in his corn patch, who had nibbled his carrots, and who had dug under his fence.

A great fear fell upon Pleasant Valley. No one dared go near the farm. The squirrels stayed high in the trees, the rabbits huddled in their briar patch, and the chipmunks did not venture far from their stone walls. Winter was just around the corner, and their larders were not yet full. But the risk of being seen by the Golden Watcher was too great.

Finally, Sandy Chipmunk could stand it no longer. He had a family to feed, and his store of beechnuts was not quite enough. "Something must be done," he said to Frisky Squirrel, who was hiding under a large fern and trembling.

"But what can we do?" chattered Frisky. "It can see everything! It probably hears us right now!"

"There is one person who is not afraid of anything," Sandy said, his mind made up. "And he is the only one who can help us. We must go to Mr. Crow."

Finding Mr. Crow was not difficult. He was in his usual spot, on the highest limb of the great dead oak that overlooked the valley. He was preening his feathers and pretending not to notice the hush that had fallen over the woods.

"Mr. Crow!" Sandy called up to him. "Mr. Crow, we are in terrible trouble!"

Mr. Crow looked down his long beak at the two small creatures below. "Trouble?" he croaked. "You are always in trouble. What is it this time? Did you fall in another puddle?"

"It is much worse than that," said Frisky, his voice a squeak. "It is the Golden Watcher!"

"The Golden Watcher?" Mr. Crow repeated, and he ruffled his feathers. The name annoyed him. It sounded far too important.

Sandy quickly told him the whole story; the story of the magical metal bird, the all-seeing eyes, and the nightly reports to Farmer Green.

Mr. Crow listened, and the longer he listened, the angrier he became. But he was not angry because he was frightened. He was angry because he was insulted. A golden king of the birds? A magical watchman? Who did this newcomer think he was? There was only one bird in Pleasant Valley whose opinion mattered, whose eyes saw the farthest, and whose wisdom was supreme. And that bird was Mr. Crow himself. This Golden Watcher was an impostor.

"Bah!" Mr. Crow sneered when Sandy was finished. "A mountain wizard! Magical metal! You will believe anything you hear. You are as bad as Jasper Jay."

"But everyone says so!" Frisky insisted. "Fatty Coon heard it from me, and I saw it with my own eyes!"

"You _saw_ a bird sitting on a roof," Mr. Crow corrected him sternly. "The rest is foolish gossip. There is no bird in this valley more important than I am. And I have certainly not heard of any golden kings."

He stretched his great black wings. "I will look into this matter myself," he announced. "Not for your sakes, mind you. But to put an end to these ridiculous rumors. And to tell this... this _Golden Watcher_... that he is not welcome in my valley."

With a powerful caw, he launched himself into the air and flew straight towards Farmer Green's farm. Sandy and Frisky watched him go, a black speck against the blue sky. They felt a little bit better, but they were still very worried.

Mr. Crow flew with purpose, his anger driving him on. He would find this golden pretender and give him a piece of his mind. He would peck his shiny head and chase him right out of the county. He circled high above the farm, his sharp eyes scanning the rooftops. And then he saw it.

It was just as Frisky had described. On the peak of the new shed, a golden rooster stood gleaming in the sun. It was very shiny, Mr. Crow had to admit. And it stood very still, with an air of great dignity.

"Hah!" cawed Mr. Crow, and he swooped lower. "You there! Golden fellow! I am Mr. Crow! This valley is under my supervision! Who gave you permission to perch here?"

The golden rooster did not answer. It simply stared eastward, paying him no mind at all.

This was an outrage. Mr. Crow was now furious. He landed with a clatter on the shingled roof, just a few feet from the stranger. He puffed out his chest. "I am speaking to you!" he shouted. "Have you no manners? When a gentleman introduces himself, you are expected to reply!"

The golden bird remained silent. Just then, a little puff of wind came up from the west. And a strange thing happened. The golden rooster turned smoothly, silently, until its beak was pointing directly at Mr. Crow.

For a moment, Mr. Crow was taken aback. So! It would fight, would it? "So you are ready to face me now!" he cawed. "Very well! Let us see what you are made of!"

He hopped closer and gave the golden bird a sharp peck on its tail.

_Ting_.

The sound was not the sound of feathers or flesh. It was the sound of metal. And the bird was cold; hard and cold as a stone in winter. Mr. Crow pecked it again, harder this time. _Ting_.

And then Mr. Crow understood. He looked at the arrow-shaped tail. He looked at the letters--N, S, E, W--on the stand below the bird. He looked at the way it had turned into the wind. He threw his head back and the loudest, rustiest _haw-haw-haw_ echoed across the farm.

It was not a king. It was not a magical watcher. It was a _thing_. A human thing, a machine for telling the direction of the wind. A weather-vane! He, Mr. Crow, had been angry at a piece of tin. The whole forest was terrified of a farm implement. It was the funniest thing he had heard in years.

He laughed until his sides hurt. Then he stopped. A sly thought crept into his clever old head. The whole forest was afraid. They had come to _him_ for help. If he flew back and told them the truth right away, the panic would be over. But where was the fun in that? If they were going to believe such a silly story, perhaps they should worry about it for a little while. It would teach them a valuable lesson about believing gossip. And when he _did_ finally save them, they would be even more grateful. His reputation as the wisest bird in the valley would be more secure than ever.

So Mr. Crow did not fly back to the woods and tell everyone the truth. Instead, he flew back to his high perch on the dead oak. He settled his feathers and adopted an expression of great seriousness.

Sandy and Frisky were waiting for him at the bottom of the tree. "What is it, Mr. Crow?" Sandy asked anxiously. "Did you see it? Is the story true?"

Mr. Crow looked down at them with a grave and mysterious air. "The matter," he said slowly, "is more complicated than I had supposed. It requires a great deal of thought. Do not go near the farm. And do not speak to anyone about this. I must have time to think."

And with that, he closed his eyes, leaving Sandy and Frisky more frightened than before.

For three whole days, a strange quiet hung over Pleasant Valley. Mr. Crow sat on his branch, looking wise and saying nothing. The other animals stayed in their homes, their supplies of food dwindling, while their fear grew larger with every passing hour.

But Sandy Chipmunk was a thinker. Mr. Crow had taught him that. And as he sat on his favorite stone wall, which was now as close to the farm as he dared to go, he thought very hard. He watched the Golden Watcher. He had been watching it for three days. And he had noticed some very queer things.

For one thing, it never moved at all unless the wind was blowing. On a still morning, it would point east for hours. Then, when the afternoon breeze came up, it would swing around to face west. It did not seem to be looking _at_ anything. It seemed to be looking _into_ the wind.

For another thing, he had seen Johnnie Green go right up to the shed. He had even thrown a ball against the wall. And the Golden Watcher had done nothing. It had not flown to his father. It had not made a sound. It had simply sat there, gleaming.

"Frisky," Sandy said on the evening of the third day. "I do not think that bird is watching us at all."

"What do you mean?" Frisky whispered. "Mr. Crow said it was complicated."

"I know what he said," Sandy replied. "But I have been using my eyes. A real bird, even a magic one, would move its head to look at things. This one does not. It only moves when the wind tells it to. It is like a big leaf on a stem." He paused, a bold thought forming in his little head. "I do not think it is a king, or a guard. I think it is a bully."

"A bully?" Frisky squeaked.

"Yes," said Sandy. "Just like Mr. Crow. It sits up high and looks important and frightens everyone, but it is possible that it cannot do anything at all. It is possible that Mr. Crow knows this and is letting us all be frightened just so he can feel important."

This was a shocking thought, and Frisky Squirrel's tail bushed out in alarm. To doubt Mr. Crow was a very serious thing to do.

"We must go and ask him," Sandy said with a new firmness in his voice. "But this time, we will not ask him to help us. We will tell him what _we_ have seen."

They found Mr. Crow exactly where they had left him. He opened one eye as they approached.

"We have been thinking, Mr. Crow," Sandy began, trying to sound as respectful as possible.

"Indeed," said Mr. Crow. "That must have been a great strain for you."

"We have been watching the Golden Watcher," Sandy continued, ignoring the jibe. "And we have noticed that it is very obedient to the wind. And that it does not seem to notice when people are near. It does not act like a watcher at all, sir. It acts more like... like a piece of farm equipment."

There was a long silence. Mr. Crow looked down at the tiny chipmunk. He saw no fear in Sandy's eyes, only earnest curiosity. He saw a mind at work. The little fellow had figured it out. Or at least, he had figured out enough to spoil Mr. Crow's fun.

Mr. Crow could have been angry. A week ago, he would have been furious that his authority was being questioned. But now, looking at Sandy, he felt a strange flicker of pride. He had wanted a clever follower; it seemed he had gotten one.

He let out a long sigh, a martyr giving up his secret for the good of the world. "Very well," he said. "You are not as stupid as the others, Sandy Chipmunk. I will admit it. You are correct."

And then, in a loud voice, so that any other animals hiding nearby could hear, Mr. Crow made his grand announcement. "The period of observation is over!" he cawed. "I have completed my strategic analysis of the entity known as the Golden Watcher! It is, as I suspected from the very beginning, a complete and utter fraud!"

He launched himself into the air and flew towards the farm, calling for all to follow him. Timidly at first, then more boldly, the squirrels and rabbits and other creatures emerged from their hiding places and followed the great black bird.

They gathered at a safe distance while Mr. Crow landed once more on the shed roof. "Behold!" he shouted. "Your terrible magical king!" He hopped over and gave the weather-vane a mighty peck. _Ting!_ "It is hollow!" he cried. He pushed against it with his shoulder. It creaked and turned. "It is stupid!" he roared. "It is nothing but a tin rooster on a stick to tell a farmer which way the wind blows! It is a _weather-vane_!"

A great sigh of relief went through the crowd of animals. A weather-vane! They looked at each other, feeling very foolish.

Mr. Crow flew back to his great oak, landing on a lower branch near Sandy and Frisky. He felt very pleased with himself.

"You knew all along," Sandy said. It was not an accusation, just a statement.

"I knew that the truth is a powerful weapon," Mr. Crow said evasively. "And that it is best deployed at the most dramatic moment. It is a matter of tactics."

"And it is also a matter of letting your friends worry for three days," Sandy said quietly.

Mr. Crow had the grace to look uncomfortable. He cleared his throat. "Hmph. The lesson was valuable. Never trust a rumor. Always trust your own eyes. And," he added, looking directly at Sandy, "always trust a crow who is much smarter than you are. You have learned the first two lessons well. The third is the most important."

Frisky Squirrel, who was just happy that he could go back to Farmer Green's garden, giggled. "You are the best general, Mr. Crow!"

"I am," Mr. Crow agreed, preening a feather. He then looked at Sandy Chipmunk, who was still watching him thoughtfully. "You did well, Sandy. You used your head. You are becoming less stupid every day."

For Mr. Crow, this was the highest praise imaginable.

"Thank you, Mr. Crow," Sandy said. "Does this mean I am now smart enough to work for you?"

Mr. Crow considered this. He thought of the beechnuts and the corn, so long ago. "No," he said at last. "You are too smart to work _for_ me. But you might be just smart enough to work _with_ me."

And as the sun set on Pleasant Valley, the forest-folk, free from their fear, scurried about their business. And on the great oak, a large black bird and two small furry creatures sat together, not as a general and his troops, but as partners. And for once, old Mr. Crow did not feel the need to say that it was only a matter of convenience.

"A very good riddance to very bad rubbish!" Mr. Crow cawed after him. He felt quite pleased with himself. It was one thing for Sandy Chipmunk to have a letter, but it was quite another for that letter to be filled with pictures of Rinaldo Rat and the common Mouse family. It was an insult to anyone of importance. And since Sandy Chipmunk was a friend of his--or at least, a sort of assistant--Mr. Crow felt that the insult was aimed partly at him. He was glad the whole affair was over.

But the affair was not over at all.

The letter, with its strange pictures, lay in the deep grass beneath the hickory tree where Sandy had thrown it. And not ten minutes later, along came Mrs. Gopher. She was not walking along, of course, for Mrs. Gopher seldom walked anywhere above ground if she could help it. She was pushing a small mound of fresh earth up from below, making a new doorway for her long, dark tunnels.

When her head popped out into the sunshine, her nose twitched. And her bright, beady eyes saw something new. It was the paper that Sandy had thrown away. It was white and clean, except for a few wrinkles, and it had a most interesting picture of a chipmunk on the part that Sandy had torn open.

"My, my!" Mrs. Gopher said to herself. "This is a fine, clean piece of paper. It would do nicely to line the winter storeroom."

She tugged the paper down into her new doorway and then scurried through her tunnels, pulling the prize behind her. She went this way and that way, through the dark, until she came to a small, round room she had dug only last week. It was here that she planned to store her winter roots.

She was about to flatten the paper against the far wall when she saw the other pictures inside. She held it closer to her face, peering at it in the dim light that filtered down from a hidden air hole.

"Well, for goodness' sake!" she whispered. "If that isn't young Master Mouse, the very son of my dear friend, Mrs. Mouse! What is he doing on this paper?"

She looked again. There he was, as plain as day, holding a sunflower seed. And there was one of the Red-Squirrels. And there, a little farther down, was a picture of a sleek-looking rat.

Mrs. Gopher forgot all about lining her storeroom. Her friend, Mrs. Mouse, lived in the old stone wall not far from her own tunnels. And Mrs. Gopher was quite sure that Mrs. Mouse would want to see this important paper with her son's picture on it. It was clearly a message of some great importance.

So, holding the paper carefully in her mouth, she turned and hurried back through her tunnels until she reached an exit at the bottom of the stone wall. Then she poked her head out and called, in a soft, dusty voice, "Yoo-hoo! Mrs. Mouse! Are you at home?"

Mrs. Mouse poked her head out of a crack between two stones. "I am, Mrs. Gopher," she squeaked. "Is anything the matter?"

"I have something for you," Mrs. Gopher said. "Something I found. It is a very important paper, and I am almost certain it is about your family."

She passed the folded paper up to Mrs. Mouse, who took it with trembling paws. Mrs. Mouse was a good mother, and she was always worried that one of her children might be in some sort of trouble. She unfolded the paper and gasped.

"It is my son!" she cried. "Here is his picture! He is famous! Oh, thank you, thank you, Mrs. Gopher! You cannot know what this means to me!"

Mrs. Gopher beamed. "I thought you would be pleased," she said. "I knew it must be for you as soon as I saw his dear little face."

Mrs. Mouse looked at the paper again, her heart swelling with pride. But then her brow wrinkled. "But what," she said, pointing a tiny claw, "is _he_ doing here? That is one of those rowdy Red-Squirrel boys! And Rinaldo Rat! They have no business being on a paper with my son!"

"It is strange," Mrs. Gopher agreed. "Perhaps it is a list of prize-winners, and your son won the first prize."

"Of course!" Mrs. Mouse exclaimed. "That must be it! My son has won a great prize, and they had to mention the others who did not win. I must go and tell everyone!"

And without so much as another word to Mrs. Gopher, Mrs. Mouse scurried up the stone wall and ran along the top of it, waving the paper for all to see.

The news traveled through Pleasant Valley almost as fast as the last story about the mail-box. But this story was quite different. Mrs. Mouse was telling everyone that her son had won a grand prize and had his picture put in the newspaper. Some people said the prize was a pile of cheese as big as Farmer Green's barn. Others said he had been elected mayor of the corn-crib.

When the Red-Squirrel family heard the news, they were very angry. For they declared the paper was about _their_ son, and that Mrs. Mouse's boy was only mentioned as a runner-up.

Soon, Sandy Chipmunk heard the rumors. At first he paid them no mind. But when Frisky Squirrel told him that the fuss was all over a piece of paper with pictures on it, Sandy's ears pricked up.

"What sort of pictures?" he asked.

"Oh," said Frisky, "a picture of one of the Mouse boys, and a Red-Squirrel, and Rinaldo Rat, I think. Mrs. Mouse says it's a newspaper, and she's been arguing with everybody about it."

Sandy Chipmunk's heart sank. "Frisky," he said in a small voice, "I think that is my letter."

"Your letter?" Frisky cried. "But you threw it away! Mr. Crow said it was rubbish!"

"I know," Sandy said miserably. "But it was mine first. And now I must find out what it truly means. It has caused nothing but trouble. First for me, and now for everyone else."

He made up his mind at once. He would get to the bottom of this mystery, with or without Mr. Crow's help. He scurried off toward the stone wall, a very determined look on his face.

XIII

SOLOMON OWL'S OPINION

Sandy Chipmunk found Mrs. Mouse near her home, surrounded by a small crowd. She was still holding the paper and explaining to anyone who would listen that her son was the most important person on it. A short distance away, two of the Red-Squirrels were chattering angrily that Mrs. Mouse didn't know what she was talking about.

"Mrs. Mouse," Sandy said, pushing his way through the crowd.

"What is it, Sandy Chipmunk?" she asked, a little impatiently. "Can't you see I am busy explaining the news?"

"That paper," Sandy said bravely. "It is the letter that was left for me in the mail-box. I threw it away, and I am very sorry for it. But it belongs to me."

Mrs. Mouse clutched the paper to her chest. "It most certainly does not!" she retorted. "It has my son's picture on it! Finders keepers!"

"But you did not find it," a dusty voice said from the bottom of the wall. It was Mrs. Gopher, who had come to see what all the commotion was about. "_I_ found it. And I only gave it to Mrs. Mouse because I thought it was for her."

This only made things more confusing. Now a rabbit, who had been listening quietly, spoke up. "If it came from the mail-box," he said, "then it really belongs to Farmer Green. We should give it back to him."

At this, everyone began talking at once. It was a terrible squabble.

Suddenly, a great shadow passed over them, and a loud "HAW-HAW-HAW!" echoed from the sky. It was Mr. Crow, circling high above.

"What is all this foolish chattering about?" he called down. "It sounds like a flock of jays arguing over a single berry!"

"It is my letter!" Sandy shouted up at him. "The one you told me to throw away! It is causing a great argument!"

Mr. Crow landed on the topmost branch of a nearby maple tree. He preened a wing feather and looked down his beak at them all. "I told you it was rubbish," he declared. "If you had listened to me, there would be no argument. The problem is that you are all too stupid to follow good advice."

This, of course, did not help at all.

Then Peter Rabbit had an idea. "Let us take the paper to Solomon Owl," he suggested. "He lives in the Deep Woods, and everyone says he is the wisest creature in the valley. They say he can even read the strange marks that men make on signs."

Everyone fell silent. This was a wonderful idea. If anyone could solve the riddle of the paper, it was Solomon Owl.

"Very well," said Mrs. Mouse, though she was reluctant to let the paper out of her sight. "But I will carry it."

"And I will go, too," Sandy announced, "since it was my letter first."

"I shall go as well," said one of the Red-Squirrels, "to make sure my family is properly represented."

And so a little party was formed. As they set off toward the Deep Woods, Mr. Crow scoffed loudly. "A fool's errand!" he shouted. "Going all that way to ask a sleepy owl about a piece of trash!" But as soon as they had entered the woods, he lifted silently from his branch and followed them, flying from tree to tree, careful to stay out of sight. He would not admit it, but he was terribly curious.

The Deep Woods were darker and quieter than the friendly fields of Pleasant Valley. The party of small animals walked close together, until at last they came to a great, ancient hemlock tree. High up in a dark hollow, they could see two enormous yellow eyes blinking slowly.

"Mr. Owl?" Sandy called up nervously. "Mr. Solomon Owl?"

The great head turned, and the eyes looked down. "Hoo, hoo," a deep, soft voice rumbled. "Who is it that disturbs my afternoon nap?"

"It is us, sir," Sandy said. "Sandy Chipmunk, and Mrs. Mouse, and one of the Red-Squirrels. We have a problem that only you can solve."

Slowly, the great owl floated down and landed on a low, mossy limb. He was much bigger up close. He looked at each of them in turn with his solemn, unblinking eyes. From a tree nearby, there was a slight rustle of leaves as Mr. Crow shifted his weight to get a better view. Solomon Owl's head swiveled in that direction for a moment, and then turned back.

"A problem?" he said. "State your case."

Mrs. Mouse held up the paper. "Sir," she began, "this is a newspaper announcing that my son has won a great prize. But these others--"

"It is not!" the Red-Squirrel interrupted. "It is a notice about my brother!"

"And it was my letter first!" Sandy put in.

Solomon Owl held up a wing for silence. "One at a time," he hooted softly. "And let me see the object of this dispute."

Mrs. Mouse nervously passed the paper to Sandy, who scampered up the trunk and gave it to the owl.

Solomon Owl took the paper in his claw. Then, to the great surprise of everyone, he reached into his hollow and brought out a pair of spectacles with cracked lenses, which he carefully placed upon his beak. He had found them near a picnic spot long ago and had been waiting for a chance to use them.

He held the paper up and studied it for a very long time. The little animals on the ground held their breath. Mr. Crow leaned so far forward on his branch that he nearly fell off.

"Hoo," said Solomon Owl at last, clearing his throat. "There is much confusion here."

"Is it a newspaper?" asked Mrs. Mouse.

"It is not a newspaper," the owl said.

"Is it a letter?" asked Sandy.

"It is not a letter in the way you mean," he replied.

"Then what is it?" the Red-Squirrel chattered.

Solomon Owl pointed a wing-tip to the big words at the top. "This paper," he announced gravely, "is what Farmer Green would call a _catalog_."

"A cat-a-log?" they all repeated, looking at one another in confusion.

"A paper for selling things," Solomon Owl explained patiently. "This is from a company that sells seeds to farmers. It is telling Farmer Green that he can buy fine sunflower seeds, and cracked corn, and a special mixture of nuts."

He then pointed to the picture of the chipmunk. "This picture is here to show how much a chipmunk would enjoy those nuts. It is not a picture _of_ you, Sandy. It is a picture of _a_ chipmunk."

Sandy's face fell. So the letter was not for him after all.

"And the same is true for these others," the owl continued, pointing to the other pictures. "This is _a_ mouse enjoying a seed, to sell seeds to the farmer. And this is _a_ squirrel, and _a_ rat. They are not famous. They are not prize-winners. They are merely advertisements."

A deep silence fell over the little group. An advertisement! They all felt very, very foolish.

From the nearby tree, a loud "HAW-HAW! I KNEW IT!" suddenly broke the silence. Mr. Crow flew out from his hiding place and landed beside Solomon Owl. "A catalog! Of course! It was obvious from the start! I was merely testing your intelligence! A simple test, which you all failed!"

Solomon Owl turned his great head and stared at Mr. Crow. He did not say a word. He just stared. Under that silent, knowing gaze, Mr. Crow's blustering seemed to shrink. "Well," he mumbled, "any clever bird could see it."

Sandy Chipmunk looked up at Mr. Crow, who refused to meet his eyes. He understood everything now. Mr. Crow had not been wise; he had been jealous.

Solomon Owl handed the paper back to Sandy. "This belongs to Farmer Green, of course," he said. "But I doubt he will miss one catalog. You may keep it, if you wish."

Sandy took the paper. On the way home, nobody said very much. Mrs. Mouse and the Red-Squirrel scurried off to their homes, looking embarrassed. Mr. Crow flew ahead, muttering to himself about how he had been right all along.

Sandy walked slowly, looking at the catalog in his paws. He felt silly for thinking the picture was of him, but he also felt something else. He felt smarter. He had wanted to know the truth, and he had found it. He had not listened to Mr. Crow's boasting or Mrs. Mouse's pride. He had followed the mystery to its end.

He looked at the picture of the happy chipmunk eating a nut. "An advertisement," he said to himself with a little smile. "Well, he certainly does look happy." And holding the paper that had caused so much trouble, Sandy Chipmunk scurried home, feeling that he had learned a lesson that was worth more than any letter.

tossed the letter away and scampered off.

But he did not scamper far. For just around the bend in the path, where he was quite sure Mr. Crow could not see him, he stopped and picked the paper up again. Though he felt foolish for his mistake, and though Mr. Crow had been very disagreeable about it, Sandy Chipmunk could not quite bring himself to leave the catalog lying on the ground. It was, after all, the most interesting paper he had ever seen.

He took it to his cozy home in the old stone wall and spread it out on the floor. There was the picture of the chipmunk--the one that looked so much like him. And there was the red-squirrel boy, and the mouse, and the rat. Solomon Owl had called them advertisements. Sandy did not know what that word meant, exactly, but he knew it meant the letter was not for him.

Still, he looked at the pictures for a long time. The chipmunk in the picture had a pile of nuts so big that Sandy's eyes grew wide just looking at it. The mouse was nibbling a sunflower seed that was almost as big as its head. And there was a whole page filled with pictures of corn; yellow corn and white corn and corn that was a mixture of both.

Sandy remembered Mr. Crow. "Corn is what I want," he had said.

An idea, a very big idea, began to form in Sandy's little head. Solomon Owl had said the paper was for _selling_ things. What if--what if he could use the paper to _get_ those things? What if he could get a pile of nuts like the one in the picture? What if he could get some of that corn for Mr. Crow? Perhaps then Mr. Crow would stop calling him stupid.

It was a grand idea! But how was one to do it? Sandy puzzled over this for the rest of the day. He turned the catalog over and over. He poked at the pictures. He even nibbled a corner of the paper, but it did not taste at all like beechnuts and he stopped at once.

XIII

A GRAND PLAN

The next morning, Sandy Chipmunk took his catalog and went to find Frisky Squirrel. He found him chattering excitedly on a branch of a maple tree, telling two bluejays all about the adventure with the letter.

"...and it was a catalog!" Frisky was saying. "To sell things! And it had pictures of everybody! And Solomon Owl read it to us!"

When he saw Sandy, he stopped his story and leaped to a lower branch. "There you are, Sandy! I was just telling them! Wasn't it a fine adventure?"

"It was," Sandy agreed. "But I have been thinking, Frisky. If the paper is for selling things, then maybe we can use it to _buy_ things."

Frisky Squirrel tilted his head. "Buy things? What do you mean?"

"I mean," said Sandy, unfolding the catalog on a flat part of the branch, "that we can get these things. Look!" He pointed with his paw to the picture of the happy chipmunk with the mountain of nuts. "Solomon Owl said the paper was to sell nuts to Farmer Green. But why should Farmer Green have them all? We should have some, too!"

Frisky's eyes grew as round as acorns. "You mean... we can just _get_ them? From the paper?" He looked at the catalog as if it were a magical thing. "Is it a wishing-paper, Sandy? Do we just wish for the nuts and they appear?"

"I don't think so," Sandy said thoughtfully. "I think we have to send a message back. We have to tell the paper what we want."

"An order!" Frisky chattered, hopping up and down. "We must place an _order_! I heard Farmer Green's wife say that once! She said, 'I must place an order for some sugar from the village.'"

That settled it. They had to place an order.

"But how do we do that?" Sandy asked.

They thought for a long time. They tried chattering at the paper, telling it very clearly that they wanted the nuts in the picture, and also some of the cracked corn. But the paper just lay there, and no nuts or corn appeared.

"It is not listening," Frisky declared. "We must send our order back to the place it came from. We must put it in the mail-box!"

This seemed like a very sensible plan. But a new problem arose at once.

"An order is a kind of letter, I think," Sandy said. "And letters have writing on them. Neither of us knows how to write."

They were quiet for a moment, feeling very discouraged. Then Frisky Squirrel had another idea. "We do not need to write!" he squeaked. "We can make a _picture_! We can make a picture of what we want!"

Sandy Chipmunk thought that was the cleverest idea he had heard all week. They scurried down the tree at once to begin their work. They found a large, flat hickory leaf that was perfect for their letter. Then they began to gather their art supplies. For the corn, they found little yellow pebbles and stuck them to the leaf with sticky pine sap. For the nuts, they used tiny brown seeds from a weed. They worked all morning, arranging their picture just so.

When it was finished, they stood back to admire it. It looked very fine indeed. There was a handsome pile of yellow "corn" and a neat little heap of brown "nuts."

"Anyone who sees this will know exactly what we want," Sandy said proudly.

Just then, a shadow passed over them. "Haw-haw! What is this foolishness?"

It was Mr. Crow, of course. He perched on a low-hanging pine bough and peered down at their work.

"We are making an order, Mr. Crow," Frisky Squirrel explained importantly. "We are going to send it in the mail-box to get nuts and corn from the catalog."

Mr. Crow let out a great sneer. "An order! You simpletons! You cannot just put a leaf in the mail-box. It will not go anywhere."

"And why not?" Sandy demanded.

"Because," said Mr. Crow, puffing out his chest, "it does not have a _stamp_. I heard Farmer Green complaining about it just yesterday. 'The price of stamps has gone up again,' he said. You cannot send a letter without a stamp."

Sandy and Frisky looked at each other. They had not thought of a stamp.

"What is a stamp?" Sandy asked.

Mr. Crow looked very wise. He had no more idea than they did, but he would never admit it. "A stamp," he said slowly, "is a small, sticky square. And it must be red. Yes, I am quite certain it must be red. You must find a sticky red square and put it on your letter, or the mail-man will just throw it away."

Having delivered this important piece of information, Mr. Crow gave another loud _haw-haw_ and flew away, leaving Sandy and Frisky to stare at their beautiful picture-letter, which was missing the most important part.

"A sticky red square," Frisky murmured. "Where will we ever find such a thing?"

They searched the whole afternoon. They looked under leaves and behind rocks. They looked in the meadow and by the edge of Swift River. They found red berries, but they were round, not square. They found red flowers, but they were not sticky.

Just as they were about to give up, Sandy saw it. Lying on the damp earth was a leaf from a maple tree. The autumn frosts had turned it a brilliant, fiery red. And it was almost a perfect square.

"Look, Frisky!" he cried.

"It is red! And it is a square!" Frisky chattered. "But is it sticky?"

It was not. But Sandy had an idea. He ran back to the pine tree and carefully gathered a drop of sap on a twig. He dabbed it onto the back of the red maple leaf, and then he pressed it firmly onto a corner of their hickory-leaf letter. It stuck fast.

"There!" Sandy said, feeling very proud of himself. "Now we have a stamp."

They were both very excited. They picked up their order, carrying it carefully between them, and hurried to the cross-roads. They scrambled up the oak tree and peeked at the mail-box. The lid was open. This was a bit of a problem. If they put their letter in now, the wind might blow it away.

"We must wait for the mail-man," Sandy whispered. "He will come and put Farmer Green's letters in, and then he will close the lid. We can slip our letter in right before he closes it."

So they hid in a cluster of leaves and waited. It was a long wait. Frisky Squirrel grew very fidgety, and Sandy had to tell him to be quiet more than ten times.

At last, they heard the clop-clop of a horse's hoofs and the rattle of wagon wheels. It was the mail-man. He stopped his horse beneath the tree, just as he did every day. He put some letters and a newspaper into the box. Then, just as he was reaching to close the metal lid, Sandy and Frisky scurried down the trunk. Quick as a flash, they tossed their hickory leaf into the box.

The mail-man did not see them. He saw only a blur of brown and grey fur. He closed the lid with a loud _clang_ and drove away down the road.

Inside the dark box, the letter with its pebble-corn and its seed-nuts and its beautiful red-leaf stamp lay on top of Farmer Green's mail. Sandy and Frisky scurried back to their hiding place, their hearts thumping with excitement.

"He took it!" Frisky whispered. "He took our order!"

"Now we just have to wait," Sandy said. They did not know how long they would have to wait. But they were sure that very soon, a huge pile of nuts and corn would appear for them right at the crossroads.

XIV

A SURPRISING DELIVERY

A whole day went by, and nothing happened. Sandy Chipmunk and Frisky Squirrel visited the cross-roads a dozen times, but the ground beneath the oak tree was bare. There was no pile of nuts and no mountain of corn.

"Perhaps it takes two days," Frisky suggested, though he sounded disappointed.

The next day, they waited again. They watched the mail-man come and go. He opened the box, took something out--it must have been their letter! they thought--and put some new letters in. But when he drove away, there was still nothing for them.

"I am beginning to think Mr. Crow was wrong about the stamp," Sandy said sadly.

"Or perhaps we used the wrong kind of pebbles," Friskey worried.

They were about to go home, feeling very discouraged, when they saw Johnnie Green walking up the road from the farm. He was not carrying anything, and he was whistling a cheerful tune. When he reached the mail-box, he did not open it. Instead, he bent down and looked at the big, flat top of the stone wall next to the tree.

Sandy and Frisky, hidden in the branches above, watched him curiously. What was he doing?

Johnnie Green reached into his coat pocket and pulled something out. It was a handful of something dark and small. He carefully poured it out onto the stone wall, making a neat little pile. Then he looked up into the branches of the oak tree, as if he was looking for someone. He smiled a little, then turned and walked back toward his house.

Sandy and Frisky waited until he was gone. Then, very cautiously, they crept down the tree and onto the stone wall. They could not believe their eyes.

There, right where Johnnie Green had been standing, was a pile of food. There was cracked corn, just like in the catalog. And mixed in with it were beautiful, black-and-white striped sunflower seeds. It was not the mountain of nuts they had ordered, but it was a wonderful feast all the same.

"It worked!" Frisky Squirrel shrieked with delight. "Our order worked! The wishing-paper sent us our food!"

Sandy Chipmunk looked at the pile of seeds and corn. Then he looked down the road where Johnnie Green had disappeared. He was not so sure. It seemed a very great coincidence that the food had appeared right after Johnnie Green had been there.

Just then, Mr. Crow swooped down and landed beside them with a triumphant squawk. "Aha! Just as I expected! My advice is never wrong!"

He strutted over to the pile of food and poked at it with his beak. "You see? The red stamp was the key! It told the mail-man that this was a very important order. I knew it would work all along." He looked at Sandy and Frisky. "Of course, since my advice was what made it happen, I believe I am entitled to a finder's fee. I will just take this corn here."

He bent down to scoop up a beakful of corn. But Sandy Chipmunk darted in front of him.

"No, Mr. Crow," Sandy said firmly.

Mr. Crow stopped and stared at him. "No? What do you mean, _no_? I helped you!"

"I do not think this food came from our order," Sandy said, looking Mr. Crow right in the eye. "I think this food came from Johnnie Green."

"Johnnie Green!" Mr. Crow sneered. "Why would he give you food? Don't be silly. This is from the catalog company. It is a direct result of my expert knowledge of the mail system."

"Then why did Johnnie Green put it here?" Sandy asked.

Mr. Crow did not have an answer for that. "He was... he was just the delivery boy!" he blustered. "Yes, that is it! The company hired him to deliver your order!"

But Sandy Chipmunk shook his head. He was beginning to understand. The day before, when the mail-man had come, he had opened the box and cleaned it out. He must have found their hickory-leaf letter and thought it was just rubbish. Sandy remembered seeing the man toss a few scraps of paper to the ground. Their order must have been one of them.

And he had a very strong suspicion that Johnnie Green had found it. Johnnie had laughed when he heard about Sandy taking the first letter. Perhaps he had found their picture-letter and thought it was funny, but also felt a little bit sorry for them.

"This is a gift, Mr. Crow," Sandy said. "It is not from the catalog. It is from Johnnie Green. And it is for me and Frisky."

Frisky Squirrel, who had been listening with his mouth full of sunflower seeds, nodded in agreement. "Yes! It is from Johnnie Green! We saw him!"

Mr. Crow looked from Sandy to Frisky, and then to the delicious pile of corn. He saw that Sandy was not going to be fooled this time. Sandy was not the same chipmunk who had gathered beechnuts when he was asked for food. He had learned a thing or two.

With a cross mutter about ungrateful chipmunks, Mr. Crow flew up into the oak tree to sulk.

Sandy and Frisky did not mind at all. They sat on the stone wall in the warm afternoon sun and ate until their bellies were round and full. Sandy saved a few of the sunflower seeds, tucking them into his cheek pouches to examine later.

He still had the catalog back in his house. He knew now that it was not a wishing-paper. And he knew that sending a picture-letter with a red-leaf stamp would not magically make food appear. Food came from hard work, like gathering nuts for the winter. Or sometimes, it came from the unexpected kindness of a farmer's boy.

As he scurried home, his cheeks bulging with seeds, Sandy Chipmunk felt very wise indeed. He had not received a letter, and his grand plan had not worked the way he expected. But he had received a very fine meal and a very important lesson. And he decided that was much, much better.
