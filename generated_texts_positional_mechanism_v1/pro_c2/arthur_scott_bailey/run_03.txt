He grew bolder. He swooped down and pecked sharply at the scarecrow's shoulder, tearing a small hole in the flannel shirt. A few bits of straw fell out.

"Aha!" he shrieked. "I have wounded it! Its life-stuff is spilling out!"

The crowd gasped. Even Barnaby Badger leaned forward a little. This was more entertaining than he had expected.

Mr. Crow, puffed up with pride, decided it was time for the final, heroic blow. The monster's power, he reasoned, must be in its head. If he could destroy its head, his victory would be complete. The most obvious part of its head was the old, battered hat perched on top.

"And now," he bellowed for all to hear, "I shall unmask the beast! I will remove its crown of evil!"

He flew high above the scarecrow, then folded his wings for a dramatic dive. He aimed directly for the hat. With his strong feet, he gripped the brim and pulled with all his might. The hat was stuck on tight, but Mr. Crow was determined. He flapped his wings furiously, beating against the air, pulling and tugging.

The crowd watched, breathless. "He is doing it!" squeaked Sandy Chipmunk.

With a final, mighty heave, the hat came loose.

But underneath the hat was something Mr. Crow had not expected at all. For weeks, a colony of yellow-jacket wasps had been building a large, papery nest inside the dark, sheltered space. And they were not at all happy to have their roof torn off.

A low, angry hum filled the air. Then, like a black and yellow cloud, the wasps poured out of the scarecrow's head. And the very first thing they saw was the large, black thief who had destroyed their home.

Mr. Crow had only a single instant to realize his terrible mistake. "Caw?" he said, a small, questioning sound of pure terror.

And then the wasps were upon him.

He shrieked, a sound unlike any he had ever made before. It was not a sound of pride or anger, but of pure, painful panic. He let go of the hat and tried to fly away, but the wasps were everywhere. They clung to his feathers, they buzzed in his ears, they stung his legs and his wings and his beak.

"Help!" he squawked. "They are fighting back! The monster's soul has been released!"

But there was no help to be had. The crowd of onlookers took one look at the angry cloud of wasps and scattered in every direction. Frisky Squirrel ran so fast he dropped the three nuts he had been saving. Fatty Coon woke up completely and lumbered into the thickest part of the woods. Sandy Chipmunk dove into a hole in the stone wall and did not dare to peek out.

Barnaby Badger, from a safe distance, watched Mr. Crow flap frantically across the sky, a small, furious swarm of yellow-jackets chasing him like a comet's tail. The crow was heading south, and he was flying faster than Barnaby had ever seen him fly before.

The old badger let out a low chuckle, deeper and more satisfied than the one he had given for Diggory Mole. Mr. Crow had wanted to give everyone a story to tell, and now he had. It was a much better story than the one about the giant, Barnaby thought.

Later that evening, when the air was cool and the last of the angry wasps had returned to their ruined home, Sandy Chipmunk cautiously crept out from the stone wall. He looked at the quiet cornfield. The scarecrow stood just as it had before, only now it was hatless. It did not look nearly so frightening anymore. It just looked like a pile of old clothes on a stick.

He saw Barnaby Badger sitting near the edge of the woods, looking thoughtful. "Is he gone?" Sandy asked in a small voice.

"Who?" Barnaby rumbled. "The monster?"

"No," said Sandy. "Mr. Crow."

"Oh, he is gone," Barnaby confirmed. "I suspect he will not be stopping until he reaches the warm lands in the south. He seemed to be in a great hurry."

Sandy Chipmunk shook his head. "I suppose," he said, thinking of the beechnuts and the corn, "I suppose those wasps did not agree with him, either."

Barnaby Badger smiled, a rare and wide smile. "No, Sandy," he said. "I do not believe they did."

And as winter began to settle over Pleasant Valley, the story that was told around the nut stores and in the hollow logs was not of a Giant King, or of a pillow, or even of a Field Demon. It was the story of how Mr. Crow, in his great cleverness, had tried to fight a man made of straw and had been chased clear out of the county by a swarm of angry wasps. And everyone agreed, it was the best story about Mr. Crow they had ever heard.

But the letter did not lie on the ground for long. A flash of blue, a noisy flutter of wings, and Jasper Jay landed on the very branch from which the paper had been tossed. "What's this? What's this?" Jasper chattered to himself, for he was a great one for asking questions, even when there was no one but himself to answer. He hopped down to the crumpled paper, his head cocked to one side.

He could not read, any more than Mr. Crow could. But his eyes, which were as sharp as his beak, saw the bright colors and the clear pictures. He saw the chipmunk, and the squirrel, and the mouse. And he saw something else that neither Sandy nor Mr. Crow had paid much attention to. On the paper, behind the pictures of the little folk, were drawings of what looked like enormous, shining seeds. They were golden and plump, and they seemed to glow with a light all their own.

"A-ha!" said Jasper Jay, which was his way of saying he had made a great discovery. He did not see a letter with too many pictures in it. He saw a promise. He saw a treasure map.

He snatched up the paper in his beak and flew straight to the old pine tree where Frisky Squirrel was trying to remember where he had buried a particularly fine hickory nut.

"Big news! Big news!" squawked Jasper, dropping the paper at Frisky's feet.

Frisky Squirrel jumped, nearly losing his balance. "Don't do that, Jasper!" he chattered. "You nearly made me forget my entire winter's supply! What is it now? Has Farmer Green bought another trap?"

"Better than that!" Jasper Jay declared, puffing out his chest. "Or worse, depending on how you see it. It is a secret message. A picture-letter from a Great King, I shouldn't wonder."

Frisky looked at the paper. He saw the familiar face of Sandy Chipmunk. "This is just the letter Sandy was showing off," he said, disappointed. "Mr. Crow told him to throw it away."

"Ah!" Jasper said wisely. "But Mr. Crow did not look closely enough! He is getting old, you know. His eyes are not what they were. Look here!" He pointed his beak at the golden seeds in the drawing. "What do you suppose those are?"

Frisky Squirrel peered at the picture. His eyes grew wide. He had never seen such seeds. They looked tastier than acorns, more satisfying than walnuts, and more sunny than a sunflower seed. "Why," he breathed, "they look like pieces of the sun you could eat!"

"Exactly!" cried Jasper Jay. "It is an invitation! The letter shows all the little people of the wood, which means us. And it shows the food we are invited to eat! It is a royal feast!"

The news was too good for Frisky Squirrel to keep to himself. He left Jasper Jay guarding the "invitation" and scurried off as fast as his legs could carry him. The first person he met was Fatty Coon, who was just waking from a nap in a hollow log.

"Wake up, Fatty! Wake up!" Frisky squeaked, tugging on one of Fatty's ears.

"I am awake," Fatty Coon mumbled, without opening his eyes. "What is the emergency? Is the river on fire?"

"Better!" chirped Frisky. "There is to be a great feast! With golden food! Like pieces of the sun! Jasper Jay has the invitation!"

That made Fatty Coon open one eye. "Golden food?" he said. His voice was suddenly much more alert. "Did you say _golden_ food?"

"As golden as honey, and as big as acorns!" Frisky exaggerated. "Everyone is invited! Sandy Chipmunk, and the squirrel-folk, and even the mice! We are all to go to a great feast!"

And so the story traveled. Fatty Coon told Mrs. Possum, who had nine children and was always interested in a free meal. Mrs. Possum told Barnaby Badger, who just grunted and said he preferred roots. And soon, everyone in Pleasant Valley was chattering about the Great Feast of the Golden Seeds.

The news, as news has a way of doing, finally reached old Mr. Crow. He was perched on a fence post, feeling rather sorry for himself. He had been clever in making Sandy get rid of the letter, but now he was bored. And he was still hungry.

It was Danny Meadow Mouse who brought him the tale. Danny was out of breath and his whiskers twitched with excitement. "Oh, Mr. Crow!" he squeaked. "Have you heard? We are all invited to a grand party! With seeds made of sunshine!"

Mr. Crow blinked. "What foolishness is this?" he croaked. "Who told you such a thing?"

"Everybody knows!" Danny said. "Frisky Squirrel told Fatty Coon, and Fatty Coon told Mrs. Possum, and--oh, it's the biggest news! Jasper Jay has the map!"

_Map?_ Mr. Crow thought. His sharp mind began to work. The letter. The pictures. The golden seeds Jasper had seen. He had been a fool! He had not been jealous of a silly letter; he had been tricked into throwing away a treasure map! His reputation for cleverness was at stake.

"Hah!" he cawed loudly, to cover his surprise. "Of course I have heard! I was the one who figured it out in the first place!"

Danny Meadow Mouse looked puzzled. "But... Sandy said you told him to throw it away."

"A test!" Mr. Crow announced, thinking quickly. "A test of intelligence! I wanted to see who was smart enough to see the letter's true meaning. And it seems Jasper Jay was the one. A pity. I expected more from Sandy Chipmunk. Now, where is this map?"

He did not wait for an answer but launched himself into the air and flew toward the old pine tree. There was Jasper Jay, standing proudly beside the paper, explaining its meaning to a growing crowd of squirrels, chipmunks, and jays.

"Stand aside! Stand aside!" Mr. Crow commanded as he landed. "Official business! This is a matter for a superior intellect."

He pushed Jasper aside and stared down at the paper. It was indeed the very same one. Now, in the light of the new rumor, the pictures looked very different.

"Just as I suspected," he said in a low, important voice. He looked at the picture of the chipmunk. "This is not Sandy. This is a symbol. It means the treasure is hidden near a place where chipmunks gather. The stone wall by the road!"

He then pointed to the picture of the mouse. "And this means we must look low to the ground. Very clever!"

Finally, he tapped the picture of the squirrel. "And this tells us that the prize is guarded by a great tree. The oak at the cross-roads, no doubt! It all fits together. It is a map to Farmer Green's secret store of Golden Seeds!"

A hush fell over the crowd. It sounded so very logical when Mr. Crow explained it.

"But... what do we do?" asked Frisky Squirrel.

"We follow the map, of course!" Mr. Crow declared. "I will lead the expedition. We shall go tonight, when the moon is high. And we shall all be rich in Golden Seeds! But," he added, looking sternly at the crowd, "it will be dangerous. We will need our wits about us. I will require a small, nimble scout. Sandy Chipmunk!"

Sandy, who had been hiding at the back of the crowd, felt a shiver run down his back. He did not want to have anything more to do with Mr. Crow's plans.

"I don't know," he began. "It seems like--"

"Nonsense!" cawed Mr. Crow. "You were the first to hold the map! It is your destiny! Now, all of you, meet at the cross-roads when the old owl hoots for the third time. And be silent!"

With that, Mr. Crow flew off to a high branch to supervise the preparations, which consisted mostly of him telling everyone else to be quiet and listen to him.

Sandy Chipmunk did not want to go. He had had enough of Mr. Crow's schemes. He thought of his own cozy burrow, filled with good, honest beechnuts that he had gathered himself. They were not golden, and they did not look like pieces of the sun, but he knew they were real.

Still, the thought of those seeds was a powerful one. And Frisky Squirrel was so excited that he could barely sit still. "Aren't you coming, Sandy?" Frisky asked. "Just think of it! We'll have food that even Mr. Crow thinks is special!"

Sandy sighed. He was afraid of being called stupid again. And a tiny part of him, a part he did not like to admit was there, was curious. What if, for once, Mr. Crow was right?

And so, when the old owl hooted for the third time that night, a small, nervous chipmunk found himself at the cross-roads, hiding in the shadow of the great oak tree. A strange procession was gathering. There was Frisky Squirrel and two of his cousins. There was Fatty Coon, moving with surprising quickness for once. There were a half-dozen jays, led by Jasper. And overseeing them all from the mail-box was Mr. Crow, looking very much like a general inspecting his troops.

"Is every one here?" Mr. Crow demanded in a harsh whisper.

"We are all here," Jasper Jay reported.

"Good," said Mr. Crow. "Now, listen closely. The map is clear. The treasure is somewhere near here. First, we investigate the stone wall, as indicated by the chipmunk symbol." He looked directly at Sandy. "Sandy, you will be the scout. Creep along the wall and look for any sign of a secret entrance or a hidden container."

Sandy's heart beat like a drum. The stone wall was long, and it was full of dark holes where anything might be hiding. But all eyes were on him. So, with a gulp, he scurried down the tree and began to creep along the top of the wall, his nose twitching, his eyes straining in the moonlight.

He went ten feet. Then twenty. He passed the hole where a garter snake sometimes slept. He passed the loose stone where he had once hidden from Farmer Green. He saw nothing that looked like a secret entrance. He saw nothing but stones and shadows.

"Anything?" hissed Mr. Crow from the tree.

"Nothing yet," Sandy whispered back.

He kept going, feeling more and more foolish. He was just about to tell Mr. Crow that this was a wild goose chase when his nose caught a strange scent. It was not the scent of stone or moss or night-damp earth. It was a dusty, savory smell. It was the smell of seeds.

His curiosity suddenly got the better of him. He followed the scent. It led him not to a secret door, but to a small cloth bag tucked into a crevice between two large stones. It was a small, plain bag, not golden at all. But it was clear that Farmer Green had hidden it there, perhaps while mending the fence.

Sandy sniffed at the bag. He could smell corn and sunflower seeds inside. It was good food, to be sure, but it was not the golden treasure they were looking for. He poked it with his nose. A few seeds spilled out onto the stone.

"I've found something!" he called, his voice a little shaky.

At once, there was a great commotion. "He's found it!" chirped Frisky Squirrel. "The treasure!"

"Silence, you fool!" croaked Mr. Crow. He flew down and landed silently on the wall beside Sandy. Fatty Coon lumbered up, his eyes gleaming.

Mr. Crow peered at the little cloth bag. He nudged it with his beak. He looked at the plain seeds spilled on the stone. His shoulders seemed to slump. This was not a treasure worthy of his great expedition.

"This is not it," he said in a disappointed tone. "This is common chicken feed. The map was more complicated than I thought. The chipmunk was merely the starting point."

Fatty Coon did not seem to care. He began licking up the spilled seeds with his long tongue. "Common or not," he mumbled, "it's very tasty."

"Enough of that!" snapped Mr. Crow. "We are on a mission! Now, the mouse symbol. It means we must look low to the ground. But where? And the squirrel symbol! The great tree!" He looked up at the huge oak they were under. "Of course! The treasure is not _on_ the wall, or _in_ the wall. It is _under_ the tree! The mouse symbol means we must dig!"

This seemed to make sense to everyone. Or at least, no one was brave enough to argue with Mr. Crow.

"Dig!" he commanded. "Everyone, dig!"

And so began the most ridiculous sight Pleasant Valley had ever seen. Frisky Squirrel and his cousins began scratching furiously at the dirt around the base of the oak. Sandy, caught up in the moment, joined them. Even Fatty Coon, after he had licked the last seed from the stone, began to paw half-heartedly at a soft patch of earth.

They dug and they dug. They unearthed roots. They disturbed a family of beetles. They made a great pile of dirt, but they found no golden seeds.

After nearly an hour, Frisky Squirrel sat back on his haunches, panting. "I don't think there's anything here, Mr. Crow," he said, his voice full of doubt.

Mr. Crow was perched on a low branch, watching the work. He was beginning to feel that his authority was slipping. His grand expedition was turning into a failure.

"We have not dug deep enough!" he insisted. "The best treasures are always the deepest!"

But no one had the energy to dig any more. They all just sat and looked at the mess they had made. The moon shone down on their dusty faces. The grand adventure was not so grand anymore. It was just a lot of hard work with nothing to show for it.

It was Sandy Chipmunk who finally looked at the paper again. It had been left on the stone wall, and the wind had turned it over. He crept over to it. There, on the other side, was writing. Sandy could not read it, of course. But below the writing was a picture he had not seen before. It was a picture of a little house with big windows. A shed. And sitting right in front of the shed was a brightly colored bag with the same pictures of the chipmunk, the squirrel, and the mouse.

"Mr. Crow," Sandy said in a small voice.

"What is it now?" Mr. Crow asked crossly. "Have you found a worm? We are not looking for worms."

"No," said Sandy. "It's the map. I think... I think we've been reading it upside down."

He explained what he saw. Mr. Crow flew over at once. He stared at the picture of the shed. It was Farmer Green's potting shed, the one behind the hen-house. And the bag in the picture was unmistakably the treasure they were seeking.

"A-hem!" Mr. Crow cleared his throat. "An excellent observation, Sandy! I was just about to point that out myself. It is a two-part map, you see! Very clever! The first part leads to confusion, to throw off the unworthy. The second part, which only the cleverest scout would find"--he nodded toward Sandy--"reveals the true location."

He had managed, in one breath, to save his own reputation and call Sandy clever at the same time.

"To the potting shed!" he declared. "Our quest is not over! It has just begun!"

The troop, weary but with hope renewed, followed Mr. Crow across the field. They crept through the tall grass, past the sleeping hen-house, and to the dark shape of the potting shed. The air here smelled of dirt and clay pots and something else: the faint, delicious, dusty scent of the Golden Seeds.

The shed door was closed tight. But one of the windows was propped open just a crack, no wider than Sandy's littlest finger.

"There is our entrance," Mr. Crow whispered from the roof. "Sandy, you are the smallest. You must go in and see if you can open the door from the inside."

Sandy looked at the dark, narrow opening. He looked at the expectant faces of Frisky and Fatty. He looked at Mr. Crow, who was already acting as if the treasure was his. He sighed. It seemed it was always his job to do the dangerous part.

Taking a deep breath, he squeezed through the opening and dropped silently onto a wooden bench inside. It was dark, but moonlight streamed through the dusty windows. And there, in the center of the floor, was a magnificent sight. It was a large, brightly colored paper bag. On its side were the pictures from the letter. And from a small tear near the top, a few of the Golden Seeds had spilled out.

They shone in the moonlight, and Sandy had to admit, they did look a little bit like magic. He scurried over to the door. There was a simple latch, a wooden bar that swiveled up and down. It was too heavy for him to lift, but he saw that if he climbed onto it, his weight might be just enough to pull it down.

He scrambled up the door, got his footing on the bar, and leaned with all his might. Slowly, creakily, the bar moved. It swung down and out of its bracket. The door was unlatched.

With a gentle push from Fatty Coon on the outside, the door swung open. The treasure was theirs.

Fatty Coon and Frisky Squirrel stared, their mouths open. Even Mr. Crow, from his perch on the roof, was silent for a moment. The bag of Golden Seeds was even bigger than the stories had said. It was nearly as tall as Fatty Coon himself, and the colors on the paper were as bright as a summer sunrise.

"Well!" Mr. Crow broke the silence, fluffing his chest feathers. "My calculations were, as usual, impeccable. The treasure is secured." He flew down from the roof and landed with a thump on top of the bag, as if it were a throne. "As the leader and chief strategist of this daring expedition, I shall take this prize into my own keeping. It is a great responsibility, but one I am willing to bear for the good of the troop."

Frisky Squirrel looked at Sandy Chipmunk. Sandy looked at Fatty Coon. Fatty, who was usually slow to anger, narrowed his eyes. "Now see here, Mr. Crow," he said, his voice a low rumble. "We all helped. I pushed the door. And Sandy--"

"Sandy squeezed through the window!" Frisky Squirrel chirped, hopping from one foot to the other. "He did the most dangerous part! He unlatched the door! If it wasn't for Sandy, you'd still be on the roof shouting orders that didn't help at all!"

Sandy blushed at the praise but nodded firmly. "Frisky is right," he said, feeling braver than usual. "And you told me I would have to pay Frisky with _my_ share of the corn. It seems only fair that you should pay us all from _your_ share of this treasure."

Mr. Crow drew himself up to his full height. "Nonsense and poppycock! Leadership has its privileges. Do you think it is easy, planning and directing such an operation? It takes a great mind! A mind like mine! While you three were busy with the... the physical aspects of the mission, I was engaged in constant vigilance, watching for Farmer Green!"

"I didn't see you watching for anything," Fatty Coon grumbled. "I saw you preening your feathers on the roof peak."

"A-hem! That was a strategic preen!" Mr. Crow retorted. "It makes me look like an ordinary, uninteresting crow, which is the very best sort of camouflage. You woodland folk do not understand the finer points of espionage."

He pecked at the paper bag possessively. "The treasure is mine. I may, if I am feeling generous, grant you each a small finder's fee. Perhaps one or two seeds apiece."

"One or two seeds!" Sandy exclaimed. He had not gone through all that trouble for two seeds. He thought of the beechnuts he had gathered, and the unfairness of it all made his whiskers twitch. "That is not fair, Mr. Crow, and you know it."

"Fair?" Mr. Crow cawed. "Life is not fair! Now, the subject is closed. Our next problem is to transport my treasure to a place of safety."

But nobody moved to help him. Frisky sat down and began washing his face with his paws. Fatty crossed his arms over his broad chest. And Sandy simply stared at Mr. Crow with disappointed eyes.

Mr. Crow looked from one stubborn face to another. He tried to push the bag himself, but it was far too heavy. It wobbled and then settled firmly back into place. He was strong for a crow, but he was not _that_ strong. He sighed a long, dramatic sigh.

"Oh, very well!" he snapped. "We shall move it together. But we will discuss the final division of the spoils later, when you are all in a more reasonable frame of mind. The leader's share will, of course, be the largest, to compensate for the mental strain."

That seemed to be the best deal they were going to get for now. Fatty Coon gave the bag a mighty shove. It slid a few inches across the dirt floor.

"This is going to take all night," Frisky Squirrel complained.

"We should roll it," Sandy suggested, remembering how he had once seen Johnnie Green roll a barrel across the yard.

"An excellent idea!" Mr. Crow announced at once, as if he had just thought of it. "Just what I was about to propose! Tip it on its side, Fatty! Put your back into it!"

Fatty Coon, with a great grunt, managed to heave the bag over onto its side. It was still heavy, but now it was possible to move it. With Fatty pushing from behind, and Frisky and Sandy guiding it from the sides, the bag of Golden Seeds began to roll slowly out of the potting shed and into the moonlight. Mr. Crow, naturally, flew overhead.

"A little to the left!" he squawked. "No, no, your _other_ left! Watch out for that thistle! Mind the puddle! Faster, faster! Farmer Green will be waking up any minute now!"

They paid him no mind at all. It was hard work. They rolled the heavy bag across the damp grass of the lawn, their paws and feet sinking into the soft earth. They rested behind the hen-house, listening to the gentle clucking of the sleeping chickens. Then they continued on, across the field and toward the edge of the woods.

"Where are we taking it?" Frisky panted, his bushy tail drooping with exhaustion.

"My den is not far," Fatty Coon suggested. "It is big and comfortable."

"No, no, much too obvious!" Mr. Crow called down. "Farmer Green knows where you live, Fatty. He will look there first. We need a secret place. A place nobody would ever think to look."

"Like that big hollow chestnut tree near the brook?" Sandy asked. "The one that was struck by lightning two summers ago? The opening is hidden by blackberry bushes."

"Precisely!" Mr. Crow declared. "The hollow chestnut! My secret storehouse! I have been saving it for just such an occasion. Lead on, Sandy! Your local knowledge is proving moderately useful."

And so they pushed and rolled and guided the heavy bag all the way to the edge of the woods, through a tangle of undergrowth, and to the base of the great, scarred chestnut tree. The entrance was indeed hidden by a thicket of thorny blackberry vines.

"Now what?" Frisky asked, looking at the sharp thorns.

"I will clear a path," Fatty Coon said. And with his strong, clever paws, he began to carefully bend and break the brittle vines, creating a narrow tunnel leading to the dark opening in the tree's trunk.

With one last, mighty effort, they shoved the bag through the opening. It tumbled inside and landed with a soft, rustling thud. They were all panting, their fur damp and covered in bits of grass and dirt. But the treasure was safe.

"A successful mission," Mr. Crow said, landing on a branch above them. "You may all go home and rest. I will stand guard tonight."

Sandy was too tired to argue. He just wanted to curl up in his own cozy bed. He said good-night to Frisky and Fatty and scurried home to his own burrow in the old stone wall.

He did not know that their work had been observed. High in a maple tree on the other side of the field, a pair of sharp eyes had watched the whole strange procession. Jasper Jay had seen the four of them struggling with a huge, colorful bag. He had seen them hide it in the old chestnut tree. And he could not imagine what was so important that it would make a chipmunk, a squirrel, and a coon work together under the orders of old Mr. Crow.

"This is big news," Jasper whispered to himself. "Very big news indeed." And he flew off, not to his nest, but toward the swampy part of the woods where the news would be most welcome.

XIII RUMORS OF GOLD

The next morning, the story was all over Pleasant Valley. But as usual, it had changed a good deal in the telling. Jasper Jay had first told his cousin, who told a field mouse, who told a rabbit, who told another squirrel, who told a woodchuck.

When Jasper first told it, he said, "Sandy Chipmunk and his friends found a huge bag of something and hid it in the hollow chestnut tree."

By the time the field mouse told it, the story was: "Sandy Chipmunk's gang stole a giant sack of seeds from Farmer Green."

By the time the woodchuck heard it, it had become something else entirely. "Did you hear?" the woodchuck said to his neighbor. "Mr. Crow led a raid on Farmer Green's house and they carried off a chest full of gold. They say it shines like the sun, and they have it hidden in the old chestnut tree."

And it was this last story that traveled the farthest and the fastest. For there is nothing that travels quite so fast as a rumor of gold. The news flew from the Pleasant Valley woods, across Swift River, and all the way to the noisy, crowded burrows beneath the town dump. And there, it reached the ears of Rinaldo Rat.

Rinaldo Rat was not like the animals of Pleasant Valley. He was sleek and gray and had cold, beady eyes. And he was not at all interested in nuts or berries. He was interested in things that were shiny, things that were valuable, and things that belonged to somebody else.

"Gold, you say?" he sneered at the breathless messenger, a skinny water-rat with a torn ear. "In the woods? Hidden by a chipmunk and a squirrel and that fat fool of a coon?"

"And a crow!" the water-rat squeaked. "They say a crow is guarding it!"

Rinaldo Rat showed his long yellow teeth in a grin that was not at all pleasant. "A crow," he repeated softly. "Well, well. We will have to go and pay our respects. And see this _gold_. Gather the boys, Snitch. We are taking a trip to the country."

Meanwhile, back at the chestnut tree, Mr. Crow was feeling very pleased with himself. He had slept soundly on a high branch and was now pacing back and forth before the hidden entrance, acting very much like a royal guard.

Sandy and Frisky arrived together, looking well-rested and cheerful. Fatty Coon lumbered up a few moments later, yawning.

"Good morning, troop!" Mr. Crow called out. "I trust you slept well. I, of course, have not closed an eye. The responsibilities of guarding a great treasure are immense."

"Is it time to divide it?" Frisky asked eagerly, his nose twitching.

"All in good time," Mr. Crow said grandly. "First, we must have a proper accounting. A ceremony. I, as leader, will now officially take possession of the treasure."

He hopped down and squeezed through the opening Fatty had made. A moment later he came backing out, dragging a corner of the paper bag with his beak. "It is stuck," he announced. "Fatty, you will have to go in and push it out."

Fatty Coon sighed and squeezed into the hollow tree. With a good deal of grunting from inside, the bag was slowly pushed out into the daylight. It looked even more magnificent than it had in the moonlight.

"Now," Mr. Crow began, puffing out his chest. "Regarding the division. I have decided that a fifty percent share for the leader is appropriate. The remaining fifty percent will be divided among the three of you. That is more than fair."

"Fifty percent!" Sandy cried. "That's half! There are four of us! We should each get one fourth!"

"That's right!" Frisky chirped. "One fourth each! That's what's fair!"

"Mathematics," Mr. Crow said with a wave of his wing, "is not a strong point for you smaller creatures. You do not account for the intellectual labor. The planning! The stress! My share is for services rendered. The matter is not open for debate."

He was about to say more, but he was interrupted by a smooth, cold voice from the bushes.

"I do hope we are not interrupting a private conversation."

They all froze. Out from the woods stepped Rinaldo Rat. And behind him were four other rats, bigger and tougher-looking than Rinaldo himself. They did not look friendly at all. They looked hungry.

"Who are you?" Mr. Crow demanded, trying to sound braver than he felt. "This is a private meeting."

"Oh, we know," Rinaldo said with his unpleasant grin. "We heard you had a little party. And that you found something. Something... golden." His beady eyes fixed on the brightly colored bag. "We have come to admire it. And perhaps... to make you an offer you cannot refuse."

Mr. Crow puffed up his feathers. "I am Mr. Crow, leader of this troop. And you are trespassing! Be on your way at once!"

One of the big rats behind Rinaldo laughed, a harsh, scraping sound. "Listen to the feather-duster," the rat sneered. "Thinks he's a king."

Rinaldo held up a paw for silence. "Now, now, let's be civil. We are all businessmen here. You have something we want. We are prepared to take it. You can give it to us, and we will let you go about your day. Or, you can refuse, and my friends here will have to... persuade you."

Fatty Coon took a step forward, a low growl in his throat. Frisky Squirrel scrambled halfway up the chestnut tree, chattering in fear. Sandy stood his ground, but his heart was beating like a drum. He looked at the five rats, and then at his friends, and then at Mr. Crow, who for the first time seemed to have no idea what to say. The crow's arrogance had vanished, replaced by a look of alarm.

"This," Rinaldo said, stepping toward the bag, "is what we came for." He reached out a dirty paw to touch it.

"Wait!" Sandy Chipmunk squeaked.

Everyone turned to look at him. Even Rinaldo Rat paused, a look of mild surprise on his sharp face.

"You... you can't have it," Sandy said, his voice trembling a little.

Rinaldo laughed. "Oh? And why not, little one?"

"Because," Sandy said, thinking faster than he ever had before. "Because it is cursed!"

"Cursed?" Rinaldo raised an eyebrow. The other rats muttered among themselves.

"Yes," Sandy went on, gaining confidence. "It is Farmer Green's magic seeds. Whoever steals them from the rightful owners... a terrible fate befalls them. That is why we did not take it to our homes. We are performing a ritual to remove the curse. If you take it now... well, I would not want to be in your shoes."

Mr. Crow, seeing a chance, quickly recovered. "A-hem! The chipmunk is correct! A powerful and ancient curse! I was just about to explain it to you. It involves baldness, tail loss, and an uncontrollable urge to sing opera. Very nasty."

Rinaldo Rat stared at them. For a long moment, he seemed to be considering it. Then his lips curled back from his teeth. "Nice try," he said. "Very creative. But I am not a superstitious fool from the woods. I am from the city. We do not believe in curses. We believe in what we can see and what we can take. Now, stand aside."

The rats moved forward. It seemed that all was lost. But Sandy had one more idea. It was a desperate one.

"You do not believe us?" he said quickly. "Then see for yourself! Look at the gold! But do not say I did not warn you!"

He scurried to the bag, and with his sharp teeth, he tore a larger hole in the paper. Then he nudged the bag with his nose. A stream of seeds poured out onto the ground. They were black with white stripes. They shone in the sun, but they were not gold.

Rinaldo Rat stared at the pile of seeds. He bent down and sniffed them. He picked one up and cracked it between his teeth. He spat out the shell.

"This... " he said, his voice dangerously quiet. "This is bird seed."

"Sunflower seeds, to be precise," Mr. Crow added, forgetting to be scared.

"You told everyone you had _gold_!" Rinaldo shrieked, his voice rising to a furious squeak. He whirled on the water-rat who had brought him the news. "You said it was gold!"

"That's what the woodchuck said!" the water-rat whimpered, backing away.

"We never said it was gold!" Frisky chattered from the tree. "We said it was Golden Seeds! It says so on the letter!"

Rinaldo's face was a mask of fury and embarrassment. He had marched his whole gang all the way out here for a bag of bird seed. He kicked at the pile of seeds. "This has been a complete waste of my time," he hissed. He glared at Sandy and his friends. "Do not think I will forget this. The next time I hear a rumor from these woods, I will not be so polite."

And with that, he turned, and he and his gang of tough-looking rats stalked back into the woods and disappeared.

There was a long silence. Then Frisky Squirrel scrambled down the tree. Fatty Coon let out a long breath he had been holding.

Mr. Crow puffed out his chest. "You see?" he announced loudly. "My plan worked to perfection! I knew those city ruffians would have no interest in high-quality agricultural products! I lured them here, and then, by revealing the true nature of the treasure, I sent them away with their tails between their legs! It was a masterpiece of psychological strategy!"

Sandy Chipmunk just looked at the pile of sunflower seeds. He picked one up and nibbled it. It was nutty and delicious. He had never tasted anything quite like it. Frisky tried one too, and his eyes went wide with delight.

"These are wonderful!" he cried.

Fatty Coon wasted no time. He scooped up a handful and began munching happily. "Much better than gold," he declared, his mouth full. "You cannot eat gold."

Mr. Crow watched them, a strange look on his face. He, of course, had claimed he did not like nuts or seeds. But seeing how much the others were enjoying them made him feel left out.

"A-hem," he said. "As part of my inspection of the treasure, it is my duty to... to test its quality." He hopped over and pecked cautiously at a single seed. He cracked it open and swallowed the kernel. He was silent for a moment.

"Well," he said, trying not to sound too pleased. "They are... adequate. Edible. For the good of the troop, to ensure no one gets sick, I suppose I ought to consume my share."

Sandy looked at the giant bag, and then at his friends, and then at Mr. Crow. The danger was past. The treasure was safe. And it was delicious.

"I still think," he said quietly but firmly, "that we should divide it in four equal parts."

Frisky and Fatty stopped eating and looked at Mr. Crow. The crow looked at the three of them. He saw three determined faces. He was outnumbered. And he did want some more of those tasty seeds.

He sighed, a long, suffering sigh. "Oh, very well," he said, as if he were making a great sacrifice. "Since you are all so insistent, and since I am a generous and benevolent leader... we shall divide it into four parts. But my part," he added quickly, "must be from the very top of the bag."

Sandy Chipmunk nodded slowly. "Very well, Mr. Crow," he said. "Your part can come from the very top."

And so the four of them set to work. It was not as simple as it sounded, for the bag of sunflower seeds was very large, and their paws and claws were very small. Frisky Squirrel, who was always in a hurry, suggested they simply tear a hole in the bottom and let the seeds pour out.

"Certainly not!" Mr. Crow declared, horrified. "That would be a most inefficient and messy method. We must be organized. We will use measuring cups."

"Where will we find measuring cups?" Fatty Coon asked, pausing from his nibbling.

Mr. Crow looked stumped for a moment. "We shall use... large leaves!" he announced, as if this had been his plan all along. "One scoop for me, from the top. Then one for Sandy. Then one for Frisky. Then one for Fatty. We will repeat this until the bag is empty."

It seemed a fair system, and so they agreed. Sandy found four broad maple leaves that had curled into perfect little scoops. Mr. Crow watched with a sharp eye as Sandy dipped a leaf into the very top of the bag and handed it to him. The crow took the leaf-full of seeds and hopped away to a private spot to count them.

Then Sandy took a scoop for himself, and gave one each to Frisky and Fatty. The process was slow. After each of them had received ten scoops, Frisky Squirrel's patience wore thin.

"This will take all winter!" he chattered, flicking his tail. "Why do we not just move the whole bag to a safe place and take seeds whenever we like?"

Fatty Coon nodded vigorously, his cheeks bulging. "A fine idea," he mumbled through a mouthful of kernels. "My paws are getting tired."

Sandy Chipmunk had to agree. "He is right, Mr. Crow. We can hide the bag in the old hollow log by the creek. It is dry inside, and no one will think to look there."

Mr. Crow, who had been trying to carry three leaf-scoops at once and had spilled most of them, considered this. He did not like to admit that Frisky's idea was better than his own, but the work was indeed very tedious.

"A-hem," he said, clearing his throat. "That was to be the second phase of my plan, naturally. First, the initial distribution to test for fairness. And now, the strategic relocation of the primary asset. Yes. A very good idea. _My_ very good idea."

None of the others bothered to argue. The task of moving the great bag was a difficult one. Fatty Coon pushed from behind with all his might. Frisky and Sandy pulled from the front, digging their claws into the thick burlap. And Mr. Crow flew overhead, shouting instructions.

"A little to the left, Fatty! More pulling, less chattering, Frisky! Sandy, you must watch for roots! It is a good thing I am here to direct you all, or this would never get done!"

At last, grunting and sweating, they managed to shove the heavy bag deep inside the hollow log. It was a perfect hiding place. They sealed the entrance with a few loose stones and some brush, and then stood back to admire their work. They had a supply of the most delicious food that would last them for a very long time.

What they did not know was that, far away, another resident of Pleasant Valley was also thinking about that bag of seeds.

The next morning, Farmer Green decided it was time to fill his bird feeders for the winter. "The chickadees and juncos will be arriving soon," he said to his son, Johnnie. "I'll just get that new bag of sunflower seeds I bought in the village."

He went out to the shed where he had left the bag. But the corner where he had put it was empty. He looked around. He moved a rake and an old watering can. The bag was gone.

"Now where did I put that thing?" Farmer Green muttered to himself. He searched the whole shed. He looked in the barn. He even looked in the back of his wagon. The bag of sunflower seeds had vanished completely.

"It's been stolen!" he declared, marching back into the house. "Those rascals from the woods have stolen my fifty-pound bag of sunflower seeds!"

"A chipmunk couldn't carry a fifty-pound bag, Pa," Johnnie Green said with a grin.

"It wasn't just one chipmunk!" Farmer Green retorted, his face growing red. "It was a gang of them! The same one that stole my letter, I'll be bound! First my mail, now my bird seed! Well, I will not stand for it. I will show them. I am going to build a contraption that will keep them out of my garden and away from my shed for good!"

Farmer Green stomped out to his workshop. And a little while later, Jasper Jay, who was sitting on the roof of the barn preening his blue feathers, saw a most alarming sight. Farmer Green came out of the workshop carrying long, thin metal pipes and some strange-looking brass objects that glittered in the sun.

"I'll fix those thieving varmints," Jasper heard the farmer grumble as he walked toward the vegetable garden. "This will give them a surprise they will not soon forget."

Jasper Jay did not wait to see what would happen next. Pipes and glittering objects and talk of "fixing varmints" could only mean one thing: new traps! And these looked far more complicated and dangerous than any simple box trap. He leaped into the air and flew as fast as he could toward the woods.

The first person he met was Mrs. Rabbit, who was nibbling clover near her burrow.

"Farmer Green has new traps!" Jasper shrieked, landing on a branch above her head. "I saw them myself! They are made of long metal pipes! He is going to line the entire edge of the woods with them!"

Mrs. Rabbit's nose twitched with fear. "Metal pipes?" she whispered. "Oh, my poor children!" And she scurried down into her burrow to spread the terrifying news.

She told her cousin, Peter, that Farmer Green was laying pipes that would spray hot steam at anyone who came near the farm. Peter Rabbit, who was a great worrier, hurried off and told Bobby Beaver that Farmer Green had invented a new kind of fence that was made of pipes filled with lightning.

By the time the story reached old Mr. Crow, it had grown even more dreadful. It was Fatty Coon who brought him the news, his eyes wide with fright.

"Mr. Crow! Mr. Crow!" he panted, for he had run all the way to the tall pine where the crow liked to sit. "Have you heard? Farmer Green has a new machine! It is made of dozens of pipes, and it shoots fire and boiling water! He is building it all around his property to cook any animal that tries to cross!"

Mr. Crow puffed out his chest. He looked down his long beak at Fatty Coon with an expression of great wisdom. "Fire and boiling water, you say? Hmm. A thermohydraulic perimeter defense. Yes, I have heard of such things. It is a serious development, to be sure."

Soon, the entire forest was in a panic. No one dared go near the edge of the woods. The squirrels stayed high in their trees. The chipmunks kept to their stone walls, far from the farm. And everyone talked of nothing but Farmer Green's terrible new invention.

Sandy Chipmunk was as frightened as anyone else. But he was also a little bit suspicious. The story seemed to get worse every time he heard it. First it was pipes, then steam, then lightning, and now fire. It reminded him of the story about the mail-box, and how it had changed from a simple box into a trap for fish and eels.

That afternoon, Mr. Crow called a great meeting of all the forest-folk. He stood on a high stump and looked down at the anxious faces below him.

"A-hem!" he began loudly. "We are faced with a grave threat! A menace of pipes and fire! But do not despair! For I, your trusted advisor, have devised a plan!"

A hopeful murmur went through the crowd.

"This fiery machine," Mr. Crow continued, "is, of course, powered by water. That is how such things work. Therefore, the solution is simple. We must divert the brook!"

Everyone stared at him.

"Yes!" Mr. Crow declared, warming to his subject. "We will build a dam! We will stop the flow of water to the farm. Without water, the machine will be useless! It is a brilliant, foolproof plan!"

There was a confused silence. Bobby Beaver, whose family had been building dams for generations, scratched his head. "But the brook does not flow to Farmer Green's farm, Mr. Crow," he said timidly. "It flows the other way, toward Swift River."

Mr. Crow glared at him. "Minor details! We will divert the _other_ brook, then! The one that does not exist yet! We will dig a new one!"

Sandy Chipmunk shook his head. This was making no sense at all. He gathered his courage and spoke up. "But, Mr. Crow," he said, his voice small but clear. "How do we know it is really a fire-shooting machine? Has any one actually _seen_ it shoot fire?"

All the animals looked at one another. No one had. They had only heard about it from someone, who had heard it from someone else.

"Are you questioning my strategic analysis?" Mr. Crow demanded, fixing Sandy with a beady eye. "The evidence is clear! Jasper Jay saw the pipes with his own eyes!"

"He saw pipes," Sandy said bravely. "He did not see fire. Perhaps it is not a trap at all. Perhaps it is something else."

"Nonsense!" Mr. Crow sneered. "What else could it be? Are you suggesting Farmer Green is laying pipes for our amusement? I say it is a trap, and I say my plan to build a dam is the only way to save us!"

But Sandy's words had planted a seed of doubt. The animals began to mutter among themselves. A dam seemed like a great deal of work, especially if it was not needed.

"I think," Sandy said, "that before we build any dams, someone should go and look. Someone small and quiet, who can get close without being seen."

All eyes turned to Sandy Chipmunk. He had not meant that _he_ should be the one to go, but now that he had said it, he knew it was up to him. His heart began to beat very fast.

"Oh, very well," Mr. Crow said with a sniff. "If you insist on wasting time with reconnaissance instead of taking immediate, decisive action, then go. But when you are chased by jets of fire, do not come crying to me."

And so, with the worried eyes of the whole forest upon him, Sandy Chipmunk set off toward Farmer Green's garden. He crept from stone wall to stone wall, his tail low to the ground. He darted from the shadow of one bush to the next. The closer he got, the more frightened he became. He could hear the farmer hammering and whistling a cheerful tune, which did not sound like the tune of a man building a terrible machine.

At last, Sandy reached the edge of the vegetable garden. Peeking through a gap in the stone wall, he saw the contraption. It was a network of long, green metal pipes, laid out in neat rows among the cabbages and beans. And every few feet, a little brass nozzle pointed up toward the sky. It did not look very frightening. In fact, it looked rather odd.

Farmer Green was at the far end of the garden, attaching a long, black hose to one of the pipes. Sandy watched, holding his breath. This was the moment. The farmer was going to turn it on. Sandy braced himself, ready to run for his life from the expected blast of fire and steam.

The farmer walked over to a spigot on the side of the house and turned a handle. There was a gurgling sound in the pipes. A little hiss came from the brass nozzles.

And then, all at once, a fine, cool spray of water shot up from every nozzle, falling gently over the entire garden like a soft summer rain.

One of the sprays landed directly on Sandy Chipmunk. It was not hot. It was not steam. It was just cool, clean water. It tickled his nose and soaked his fur, but it did not hurt him at all.

Sandy sat there, dripping and amazed. So this was the terrible, fire-breathing monster! It was a watering machine for the garden! He felt so relieved that he wanted to laugh out loud. Jasper Jay had seen pipes, and his imagination had done the rest.

Sandy turned and scampered back to the woods as fast as his legs could carry him. He burst into the clearing where all the other animals were still waiting nervously.

"It is not a trap!" he shouted. "It is not a machine for shooting fire!"

Everyone gathered around him as he told his story. He described the green pipes and the brass nozzles and the lovely, cool shower of water.

When he had finished, there was a great sigh of relief from the crowd. Mrs. Rabbit began to weep with joy. Frisky Squirrel did a happy flip in the air.

Only Mr. Crow looked displeased. His great plan for building a dam was ruined. "A sprinkler system!" he said, trying to look as if he knew it all along. "Of course! A hydro-percussive dispersal field! I was merely testing you, Sandy. Before ordering the commencement of major earthworks, it is always wise to confirm the exact nature of the threat. You have performed adequately. My plan worked perfectly, you see. It led us to the truth!"

Nobody paid him much attention. They were all too busy celebrating. "Let's have some sunflower seeds!" Frisky cried. "A feast to celebrate not being cooked!"

"An excellent idea!" boomed Fatty Coon.

The four friends hurried to the hollow log. Sandy pulled away the stones and brush they had used to hide the entrance. He peered inside.

And then he gasped.

"What is it?" Frisky asked, scrambling up beside him.

The entrance to the log was clear, but just inside, the ground was littered with empty sunflower seed husks. And the great bag, which had been full and heavy, now looked much, much smaller. It sagged in the middle, as if at least a third of its contents were gone.

"Our seeds!" Sandy whispered in dismay.

Fatty Coon pushed past them and sniffed at the ground. "Rats," he grumbled, his voice low and angry. "I smell rats. Lots of them." He pointed a chubby claw at the soft dirt near the log's entrance. There, pressed clearly into the earth, were dozens of tiny, sharp-toed footprints. They were not the prints of any squirrel, or chipmunk, or coon.

"Rinaldo Rat!" Frisky Squirrel chattered, his tail bristling with fury. "He came back! He said he would not be so polite next time!"

Mr. Crow hopped forward, his black eyes blazing. He had been cheated. While he had been busy saving the entire forest from a non-existent threat, thieves had been plundering his treasure.

"This is an outrage!" he squawked. "A violation! This means war! Rinaldo Rat has made a powerful enemy this day!"

Sandy Chipmunk looked at the half-empty bag, and then at the rat tracks leading away toward the dark woods and the distant city. He remembered Rinaldo's sneering face. He had not come for the seeds because he wanted them. He had come for revenge.

Sandy felt a new kind of determination harden inside him. They had taken his letter. They had taken Farmer Green's seeds. But this time, the rats had stolen from _all_ of them.

"He will not get away with this," Sandy said, his voice quiet but firm. "We are going to get our seeds back."

Mr. Crow puffed out his chest, his feathers rustling like dry leaves. "This is not a mere theft," he declared in his most solemn voice. "This is an act of war. Rinaldo Rat and his brigands have crossed a line. They have initiated hostilities, and they shall be met with the full force of our righteous fury."

Frisky Squirrel, his tail twitching so fast it was a blur, chattered, "Let's go! Let's go right now! We'll run to the city and take our seeds back! We'll bite their tails and pull their whiskers!"

"Hold on, Frisky," Fatty Coon said calmly, placing a heavy paw on his friend's shoulder. "Running in without a thought is what a squirrel might do. But it is not a wise plan. I smelled many rats. We are only four."

"Fatty is right," Sandy Chipmunk agreed, his earlier dismay turning into focused resolve. "Rinaldo was expecting us to be angry. He is probably waiting for us to do something foolish. We cannot just rush in."

"Nonsense!" Mr. Crow squawked, hopping from one foot to the other. "A swift, decisive strike is exactly what is called for! I shall lead the charge myself. We will execute a classic pincer movement. Frisky, you and Sandy will form the southern flank; you will create a diversion. Fatty, you and I shall be the northern flank; we will conduct the main assault from the air and the ground simultaneously. It is a brilliant strategy. I devised it myself, just now."

Sandy Chipmunk looked at Frisky Squirrel, who seemed ready to agree to any plan that involved action. Then he looked at Fatty Coon, who was slowly shaking his big head.

"A pincer movement sounds very grand, Mr. Crow," Sandy said politely. "But we do not know where we are going. And we do not know how many of them there are. Or how they are guarding the seeds."

"Details! Minor details!" Mr. Crow waved a wing dismissively. "The spirit of the attack is what matters!"

"The details are what keep you from getting caught in a trap," Fatty Coon rumbled. "I say we follow the tracks. We move quietly. We find their nest. And _then_ we decide how to get the seeds back."

"Exactly!" Sandy exclaimed. "We must do what you call--what you call _reconnaissance_, Mr. Crow."

Mr. Crow blinked. He straightened his feathers and cleared his throat. "Ah! Yes! Precisely! Reconnaissance. The first phase of my brilliant plan, of course. I was merely testing you to see if you were paying attention. First, reconnaissance to gather intelligence, _then_ the pincer movement. You are learning the art of war from me, Sandy, I can see that."

Frisky Squirrel bounced impatiently. "Spying, then fighting! I like it! Let's go spy!"

And so it was agreed. Fatty Coon, with his keen nose, would lead them on the ground. Mr. Crow would serve as their eye in the sky, flying ahead to scout the path. And Sandy and Frisky would follow close behind, their sharp eyes and ears ready for any sign of danger.

They set off at once. Fatty Coon lowered his nose to the ground and began to follow the scent and the tiny footprints that led away from the hollow log. The trail was easy to follow at first, a faint, musky scent hanging in the air and a messy path of trampled leaves. The trail led them out of the cheerful woods of Pleasant Valley, past the whispering pines, and into a dark, tangled stretch of forest that none of them liked very much. The trees grew thick and close together here, and the sunlight struggled to reach the forest floor.

Mr. Crow, flying high above, circled back. "The trail continues straight ahead!" he called down in a loud whisper. "It leads toward the great stone river where the horseless carriages run! Beyond that lies the city! Proceed with caution!"

They crept onward. The sweet smell of the woods was soon replaced by other, less pleasant smells. There was a sour smell from a stagnant puddle, and a sharp, oily smell from the great stone river, which was really a road. They hid in the bushes and watched with wide eyes as the noisy carriages roared past, faster than any fox or dog they had ever seen.

"How are we to cross that?" Frisky chattered nervously, flattening himself against the ground.

"We wait," Fatty Coon said, his eyes scanning the road. "There will be a gap."

They waited for a long time, until the sun was high in the sky. At last, there was a quiet moment. "Now!" Fatty grunted, and he lumbered across the hot, black surface. Sandy and Frisky darted after him, their little legs pumping as fast as they could go. They felt the heat of the stone through the soles of their feet and were very glad to reach the other side and dive into a patch of scraggly weeds. Mr. Crow simply flew over, landing beside them with a triumphant look, as if he had performed the most difficult part of the crossing.

The world on this side of the road was different. The trees were fewer, and the buildings were bigger. They were in the city now. The air was filled with a constant hum of noise, and the ground was hard and gray. Fatty's nose was not as useful here, for there were a thousand new smells, all jumbled together.

"The trail is lost!" he grumbled, sniffing at a patch of concrete. "I smell oil and garbage and strange city things, but the rat scent is gone."

"This is a job for aerial reconnaissance!" Mr. Crow announced. "Stay here. I shall survey the terrain from above and locate the enemy's fortress!" He leaped into the air and began to circle high over the strange landscape of rooftops and chimneys.

Sandy, Frisky, and Fatty huddled in the shadow of a great brick wall. They waited and waited. Frisky grew tired of waiting and started practicing his tail-flips on a patch of dusty grass. Fatty Coon began to doze, his head nodding. Sandy, however, kept watch, his nose twitching, his eyes scanning every direction.

Finally, a black shape swooped down from the sky. It was Mr. Crow. He landed with a flutter, looking very excited.

"I have found them!" he squawked. "Their lair is exactly where a creature of my intelligence would expect it to be! They are nesting in a great heap of human rubbish! Old wheels, broken boxes, rusty metal--a true rat palace! It is not far from here, by a place where humans leave their garbage in big metal cans."

"Did you see the seeds?" Sandy asked eagerly.

"I did not," Mr. Crow admitted. "Their nest is built under a pile of old boards. But I saw the rats themselves. There are dozens of them, scurrying about. And Rinaldo Rat is there, sitting on a broken chair like a king."

"Dozens," Fatty Coon repeated, opening one eye. "Then a direct assault is out of the question. We would be overwhelmed."

Frisky stopped his flipping. "But we have to get our seeds! We can't just go home!"

They all looked at Sandy Chipmunk. He was nibbling on a claw, thinking hard. He remembered Rinaldo's sneering face and his boastful words. A plan began to form in his mind; a plan that was not about fighting, but about trickery.

"Mr. Crow," Sandy said, "you are very good at telling stories."

Mr. Crow preened. "I am a master orator. My tales are known throughout the valley."

"And your voice," Sandy continued, "it carries a very long way, does it not?"

"Indeed! My pronouncements can be heard from the highest treetop to the lowest burrow!"

"Good," Sandy said. "And Fatty, you know all about the best kinds of food, do you not?"

Fatty Coon's eyes lit up. "I am an expert," he said proudly. "A connoisseur of fine edibles, from the tenderest corn to the sweetest grub."

"Perfect," Sandy said, his plan becoming clearer. "Rats are greedy. Rinaldo is the greediest of all. We cannot fight them for the seeds, so we will make them _give_ the seeds to us."

"Give them?" Frisky squeaked. "Why would they do that?"

"Not give them to _us_," Sandy corrected. "They will leave the seeds behind, because they will be rushing to get something even better. We are going to tell them a story about a treasure greater than a bag of sunflower seeds."

Mr. Crow cocked his head. "A strategic deception! A ruse! I like it! What is this treasure?"

Sandy looked at Fatty Coon. "What is the one thing a city rat would want more than anything?"

Fatty Coon thought for a moment, his brow furrowed. "Our sunflower seeds are good winter food. But a city rat is lazy. He wants food that is easy to get. And he loves rich food. He would love... _cheese_."

"Cheese!" Frisky whispered, his eyes wide.

"Not just any cheese," Fatty elaborated, warming to the subject. "A whole wheel of it. The yellow kind with holes in it. The kind the baker sometimes leaves by his back door."

"A wheel of cheese!" Sandy said. "That is our treasure! Now here is the plan. Mr. Crow, you will fly near the rats' nest. But you must not let them see you watching them. You must pretend you are talking to someone else. Perhaps you could find Jasper Jay."

"Jasper is a gossip," Mr. Crow cawed. "An excellent choice for spreading a rumor! But I do not see him here."

"Then you will have to talk to yourself," Sandy said. "Talk loudly. Complain about how you saw the baker's boy drop a whole wheel of cheese behind the bakery. And how it is too heavy for one crow to carry. And how you are going to go and get all your crow friends to help you move it."

"And where is this bakery?" Mr. Crow asked.

"Fatty will show you," Sandy said. "Find a real bakery, so the smell is right. But you must tell them the cheese is on the _other_ side of the city. Far away from their nest. And far away from us."

Mr. Crow's eyes gleamed. "Ah, I see! A masterful piece of misdirection! I will fly to the rats' lair and perform my part with theatrical genius. They will overhear my 'private' conversation and their greed will do the rest!"

"While they are all gone looking for the cheese," Sandy finished, "we will sneak in and take our seeds back."

Fatty Coon nodded slowly. "It is a clever plan, little one. It might just work."

So Fatty led Mr. Crow on a short trip, pointing out the back of a bakery a few streets away. The smell of fresh bread and cakes was heavy in the air. Mr. Crow sniffed, committed the location to memory, and then flew off toward the junkyard.

Sandy, Frisky, and Fatty crept as close as they dared to the rats' nest. They hid behind a large, rusty metal box and peered through a gap. It was just as Mr. Crow had described. The junkyard was a maze of filth and forgotten things. And in the middle of it all was Rinaldo Rat, lounging on the tattered remains of a cushioned chair. Other rats, sleek and gray and mean-looking, were scurrying around, some sharpening their teeth on old bones, others wrestling in the dust. And there, tucked beneath the platform of the chair, was their bag of sunflower seeds. It looked very small and far away.

Soon, they saw Mr. Crow land on a telephone wire high above the junkyard. He began to preen his feathers, acting as if he had not a care in the world. Then, he began to speak, his voice carrying clearly in the quiet afternoon.

"Unbelievable!" he cawed, as if speaking to another bird just out of sight. "Simply unbelievable! A whole wheel of it! Yellow cheese! Tumbled right off the back of the baker's cart on Elm Street! Too heavy for me alone. What a shame! I shall have to gather the whole flock. We will feast for a week!"

Down in the junkyard, the rats stopped what they were doing. Their heads tilted up. Rinaldo Rat sat forward on his throne, his whiskers twitching.

Mr. Crow continued his performance. "Yes, right behind the old red warehouse on Elm Street. Just lying there. I must hurry before someone else finds it!" With a final, loud caw, he launched himself from the wire and flew off in the direction opposite the bakery.

For a moment, there was silence in the junkyard. Then, a great chattering broke out among the rats. Rinaldo Rat leaped down from his chair.

"Cheese!" he squeaked, his voice sharp with greed. "A whole wheel! That stupid crow has given us a gift! Why settle for seeds when we can have cheese? Everyone, follow me! To Elm Street! Hurry!"

A wave of gray bodies poured out from the junkyard. Rinaldo led the charge, his long tail whipping behind him. Within seconds, the entire rat army was gone, scrambling over the piles of junk and disappearing down the street. The junkyard was suddenly silent and empty.

"It worked!" Frisky whispered, trembling with excitement.

"Now!" Fatty grunted. "Before they realize they have been tricked!"

The three of them scrambled from their hiding place and ran toward the tattered chair. The bag of seeds was there, right where they had seen it. But just as Fatty reached for it, a small, sleepy-looking rat poked its head out from under a rotten floorboard. It had clearly been left behind as a guard and had fallen asleep. It blinked its beady eyes, looking confused.

Frisky froze. Fatty growled low in his throat. But Sandy acted instantly. He grabbed a small, loose bolt from the ground and, with a flick of his paw, tossed it so it landed with a clatter behind a stack of old tires on the far side of the nest.

The guard rat's head snapped toward the sound. He hesitated for a second, then scurried off to investigate the noise, disappearing behind the tires.

"Quickly!" Sandy urged.

Fatty Coon seized the bag in his powerful jaws. It was still heavy, but much lighter than it had been. With Frisky and Sandy pushing from behind, they began to drag the precious sack out of the junkyard. It was hard work. The bag snagged on sharp pieces of metal, and the ground was uneven.

"Hurry!" Frisky chattered. "I think I hear them coming back!"

In the distance, they could hear angry squeaking. The rats had discovered the trick.

"This way!" Fatty grunted, turning toward a high wooden fence. "We cannot go back the way we came!"

They half-dragged, half-carried the bag to the fence. It was too high for Fatty to climb while holding the bag.

"Up!" Fatty ordered. Frisky and Sandy scrambled to the top of the fence in an instant. "Now, pull!"

Fatty heaved the bag upward with his head, and Sandy and Frisky grabbed the burlap with their teeth and paws. They pulled and strained. For a moment, the bag was stuck. But then, with a final mighty shove from Fatty below, it tumbled over the top of the fence. Fatty scrambled up and over right behind it. They dropped down on the other side, into a quiet alleyway, just as the first of the angry rats appeared at the far end of the junkyard.

They did not stop. They dragged the bag down the alley, across another street, and into the cover of a small, forgotten park. They were all panting, their hearts pounding.

Mr. Crow swooped down to land beside them, looking immensely pleased with himself. "My performance was flawless, was it not? The intonation, the dramatic pause--I had them completely convinced! My plan was a spectacular success!"

Sandy, Frisky, and Fatty were too out of breath to argue. They simply nodded.

The journey home was long and slow. They took turns dragging the heavy bag, hiding whenever they heard a noise. By the time they reached the familiar woods of Pleasant Valley, the moon was rising. They were tired, dusty, and sore, but they were triumphant.

They dragged the bag back to the hollow log and pushed it deep inside. Then they collapsed in a heap at the entrance, too weary to even think of eating.

"We did it," Frisky said, his voice a tired whisper.

"We did," Fatty Coon agreed, a rare smile on his face. He looked at Sandy. "That was a good plan, little chipmunk. A very good plan."

Sandy felt a surge of pride that warmed him more than any nut could.

"Of course it was a good plan!" Mr. Crow declared, puffing up his chest. "It was _my_ plan, after all. The strategic use of misinformation is a complex art, one which I have clearly mastered. You all served your roles adequately as part of my grand design."

This time, Sandy and Frisky and Fatty did not even bother to look at one another. They were too happy. They had faced the city, outsmarted a gang of thieves, and brought their treasure home. And tomorrow, they knew, they would all share in a great feast of sunflower seeds.

XIII THE GREAT FEAST

The next morning, the sun shone brightly on Pleasant Valley. And there was a great feeling of excitement in the air. For today was the day of the great sunflower seed feast. The news of the adventure had spread almost as quickly as the story of the mail-box, though this time the tale did not change quite so much in the telling. For Mr. Crow made sure to fly from treetop to treetop, repeating the story himself so that everyone would get the details right.

"It was I who devised the plan," he would announce to anyone who would listen, from Jimmy Skunk to old Grandfather Frog. "A masterful piece of psychological warfare, you understand. I knew the rat folk, being of a low and greedy nature, could be easily distracted by a performance of high drama. Sandy and his friends merely played the parts I assigned to them."

Most of the forest-folk just nodded. They were used to Mr. Crow. But they were all very grateful for the return of the sunflower seeds, for the rats had stolen from nearly everyone.

By midday, a great crowd had gathered near the hollow log. Fatty Coon, with his great strength, had rolled a large, flat stone to the clearing to serve as a table. And upon this stone, Sandy and Frisky piled the magnificent sunflower seeds. They made a mountain of black and white treasure that glittered in the sun.

"My, my!" chattered Mrs. Squirrel, Frisky's mother. "I have not seen so many seeds in one place in all my born days!"

"It is a testament to superior planning," Mr. Crow said, landing on a branch just above the stone. He puffed out his chest and looked down at the crowd. "A leader must not only be wise, but also bold. And as you can all see, your faith in me was not misplaced."

Sandy Chipmunk did not say anything. He was busy helping to make sure even the smallest folk, like Timothy Titmouse and Danny Meadow Mouse, got their fair share. He felt a quiet happiness as he watched his neighbors nibble on the seeds, chattering and laughing. This was much better than having all the beechnuts for himself. It was better because everyone was happy together.

Fatty Coon did not eat much at first. He sat near the edge of the clearing, his broad back against a hickory tree, and watched. He saw Sandy making sure everyone was served. He saw Frisky chasing his cousins in a happy game around the great stone. And he saw Mr. Crow telling the story for what must have been the twentieth time, each time making his own part in it a little bigger. A slow smile spread across Fatty's face. It had been a good adventure.

The feast lasted all afternoon. When at last the sun began to dip below the trees, the forest-folk started to head back to their own homes, their bellies full and their hearts content.

"A great success!" Mr. Crow declared to Sandy, Frisky, and Fatty, who were gathering the remaining seeds to put back in the hollow log. "Just as I predicted. A feast to be remembered for ages. And all thanks to my leadership."

"It was a good feast," Sandy said simply. He was tired, but it was a good kind of tired.

As they worked, a stranger appeared at the edge of the clearing. He was a squirrel, but not a red squirrel like Frisky and his family. He was a large, sleek gray squirrel with a very bushy tail and eyes that seemed to take in everything at once. He was dressed, if a squirrel can be said to be dressed, in a look of supreme confidence.

"Good evening, gentlemen," the stranger said, his voice as smooth as polished stone. "I could not help but overhear tales of a great victory and a subsequent celebration. My travels have brought me to your charming valley, and I find myself intrigued."

Frisky Squirrel stopped, his paws full of seeds, and stared. Sandy felt a prickle of caution. And Fatty Coon, who had been about to push the last of the seeds into the log, stopped and simply watched the newcomer.

But Mr. Crow, who was never one to miss an audience, flew down from his branch. "Intrigued you should be!" he cawed. "You are looking at the architect of that victory! Mr. Crow, at your service. And these are my... assistants." He waved a wing vaguely at the others.

The gray squirrel bowed low, a gesture so grand and sweeping that his tail made a perfect arc over his back. "An honor, sir! A true honor! My name is Slick, and I am a purveyor of progress. But more than that, I am an admirer of greatness. And I can tell, Mr. Crow, that I am in the presence of true greatness now."

Mr. Crow's chest puffed out so far it seemed he might float away. "Slick, you say? A fine name. And you have a keen eye for talent, I must admit. A very keen eye indeed."

XIV A GRAND PROPOSAL

Slick the gray squirrel smiled, a wide smile that did not quite seem to reach his sharp, observant eyes. "I have traveled through many woods and across many fields," he said, his voice flowing like honey. "And I have seen many communities. But never have I heard of a feat so daring, so brilliantly executed, as the one you have just accomplished."

He gestured toward the mountain of seeds. "To reclaim such a treasure from the clutches of those city fiends... it speaks of courage, of strength, and above all, of _vision_." He looked directly at Mr. Crow when he said the last word.

"Vision is my specialty," Mr. Crow agreed readily. "I see the whole board, so to speak. While others are looking at the nuts on the ground, I am charting the course of the clouds."

"Precisely!" Slick exclaimed, clapping his front paws together softly. "And it is that very vision that I have come seeking. For I see a problem in this valley, a problem that only a great leader can solve. And I see a resource"--he nodded at the seeds--"that can be the key to that solution."

Sandy Chipmunk shuffled his feet. He did not like the way this Mr. Slick looked at their seeds. It was not the look of someone who was hungry. It was the look of someone who was counting.

"What problem do you see?" Fatty Coon asked. His voice was a low rumble, and he had not moved from his spot by the hollow log.

Slick turned his smooth smile on Fatty. "Ah, the strong and silent type! A pillar of the community, I have no doubt. The problem, my dear sir, is the future. Winter is a cruel master, is it not? You have this magnificent bounty _now_, but what happens when the snow flies? You will nibble away at it, a seed here, a seed there. It will dwindle. It will be gone. And you will be left as you were before--cold, and waiting for spring."

Frisky Squirrel shivered, even though the evening was mild. "He is right," he chattered. "Winter is very long."

"But what if," Slick continued, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper, "what if this treasure could be used not just for food, but to build something permanent? Something that would benefit _all_ of you for winters to come? What if you could invest these seeds in a project of such magnificence that it would make Pleasant Valley the envy of every forest for miles around?"

Mr. Crow was now leaning forward, his head cocked. "A project, you say? I am fond of grand projects."

"I knew you would be!" Slick said. "I propose the construction of the Pleasant Valley Grand Treetop Hotel! Not just a hole in a tree, mind you. I am talking about a grand, multi-level structure in the Great Oak. Woven vine walls to keep out the wind! Moss-lined floors for comfort! A central storage chamber, insulated with milkweed fluff, that can hold ten times what that simple log can! A place where everyone can gather in the coldest storms, warm and safe and together!"

Frisky's eyes were wide with wonder. "A hotel! In the Great Oak! Could we really?"

"With my expertise and your resources, anything is possible," Slick announced. "I have the plans. I have the architectural knowledge. All that is needed is the capital. The payment for my services and the materials, you see."

"And what payment would that be?" Sandy asked quietly.

Slick turned to Sandy, his smile tightening just a little. "Why, the seeds, of course, my little friend. The entire lot. A small price to pay for a lifetime of security and comfort, would you not agree? Think of it! The Pleasant Valley Grand Treetop Hotel, a monument to the genius of its founder." He winked at Mr. Crow. "And a safe haven for all."

Mr. Crow was speechless for a moment. A monument! To him! He could already see it. "The Crow's Nest" would be the penthouse suite, of course.

"It is a brilliant idea!" Mr. Crow finally cawed. "Stunning in its simplicity and its ambition! It is exactly the sort of idea _I_ would have had, if I had not been so busy with the tactical details of our recent campaign. Young Slick, you have my full endorsement! We shall begin at once!"

"But... all the seeds?" Sandy said, looking at the huge pile they had worked so hard to bring home. "We would have nothing to eat while we waited."

"A minor detail!" Mr. Crow scoffed. "One must make small sacrifices for great progress. We can gather other food. Nuts! Beechnuts, perhaps!" He seemed to have forgotten his earlier complaints on that subject.

Fatty Coon said nothing at all. He just pushed the last of the seeds into the hollow log and then sat down in front of the entrance, blocking it completely. His face was unreadable.

Slick saw the movement. "Of course," he said smoothly, "there is no need to rush such a momentous decision. Think on it. Discuss it. I shall take a room at a nearby branch for the night. We can speak again tomorrow, when you have all had a chance to appreciate the sheer scope of my proposal."

And with another grand bow, Slick the gray squirrel scampered up a nearby tree and vanished into the leaves.

A silence fell over the little clearing. Frisky was practically vibrating with excitement. Mr. Crow was gazing up at the Great Oak, a dreamy look in his eyes.

But Sandy Chipmunk looked at the hollow log, which was now guarded by Fatty Coon. And he felt a deep sense of unease that had nothing to do with the coming winter.

XV DOUBTS AND DIVISIONS

The next day, the Pleasant Valley Grand Treetop Hotel was all anyone could talk about. Slick the gray squirrel was a masterful storyteller, and he spent the morning going from burrow to nest, painting a wonderful picture of the future.

He told the Rabbit family of cozy, underground rooms connected to the Hotel by a special tunnel, safe from weasels. He told the finches of high-perched balconies with excellent views. He told everyone of the Great Feasting Hall, where they could all eat together even in a blizzard. And to everyone, he mentioned Mr. Crow's enthusiastic support.

"As your recognized leader of vision," Slick would say, "Mr. Crow saw the potential immediately. He understands progress."

By noon, most of Pleasant Valley was convinced. The seeds, which had seemed like such a treasure yesterday, now seemed like a simple, plain thing compared to the glorious future that Slick was selling.

Sandy Chipmunk found Frisky Squirrel trying to weave two twigs together.

"What are you doing?" Sandy asked.

"I am practicing!" Frisky said, his nose twitching with effort. "Slick says we will all need to help. He is going to teach us advanced weaving techniques. He says my paws are perfect for detailed work on the window frames! Is it not wonderful, Sandy? Our own hotel!"

Sandy's heart sank. "But Frisky, what about the seeds? We gathered them. They are our food for the winter."

"Slick says we must think bigger than our own stomachs," Frisky replied, quoting the gray squirrel perfectly. "This is for the good of everyone. Besides," he added, "think how famous we will be! The squirrels who helped build the Grand Treetop Hotel!"

Sandy left his friend and went to find Fatty Coon. He found the big raccoon down by the creek, carefully washing a small, shiny stone. Fatty was the only person who had seemed as doubtful as Sandy felt.

"Fatty," Sandy began, "what do you think of this hotel idea?"

Fatty Coon stopped washing the stone and looked at it in his paws. "A hotel is a building," he rumbled. "Buildings need a foundation. A plan drawn in the air and a promise paid for with food is not a foundation."

"That is what I think!" Sandy exclaimed, relieved. "Everyone else is so excited, but it does not feel right. To give away all our food... for a promise?"

"Mr. Crow is a fool for flattery," Fatty said, his voice low. "And the others are fools for a pretty dream. It is easier to believe in a magic hotel than it is to do the hard work of storing nuts for the winter."

"So what do we do?" Sandy asked, his whiskers drooping. "Mr. Crow has already declared that the seeds belong to Slick. He says the 'transfer of assets' will happen at sunset."

Fatty placed the washed stone carefully on the bank. He looked at Sandy, and his dark eyes were serious. "A trickster does not like to be watched," he said. "Slick is smooth on the outside. But a thing that is all smooth on the outside often has something rough to hide. You are small and quick, Sandy. You can go where I cannot. Watch him. See what his paws do when he thinks no one is looking."

That was all Fatty said. He picked up another stone and began to wash it, as if the conversation were over.

Sandy understood. He was not big enough to guard the seeds like Fatty, or loud enough to argue with Mr. Crow. But he was quiet. And he was a very good watcher.

He scurried away from the creek, his mind made up. Mr. Slick talked a good game. But Sandy Chipmunk was going to find out what sort of game the gray squirrel was _really_ playing.

XVI A CHIPMUNK'S INVESTIGATION

Sandy knew he had to be careful. Slick was clever, and his eyes seemed to miss nothing. So Sandy did not follow him directly. Instead, he went to the Great Oak, the proposed site of the magnificent hotel.

He climbed high into its branches, much higher than any squirrel would bother to go for a simple look. He found a spot where a cluster of leaves gave him a perfect, hidden view of the clearing below, where Slick was holding court.

For an hour, Sandy watched. He saw Slick demonstrating a complicated knot to a group of admiring field mice. He saw him pacing out the "foundation" with Mr. Crow, pointing to various branches and speaking of "load-bearing limbs" and "optimal ventilation." It all looked very official. Mr. Crow nodded gravely at everything, occasionally adding a comment like, "Precisely my thought!" or "Yes, yes, the cross-breezes are essential."

But Sandy noticed something. For all his talk of building, Slick did not have any tools. For all his talk of weaving, he had not gathered a single vine. All he did was talk, and point, and collect compliments.

Late in the afternoon, the crowd began to thin out. Slick announced that he needed to "survey the surrounding area for secondary materials." He said it with a grand air, as if he were an explorer off to map the wilderness. Mr. Crow told him it was a fine idea and went off to take a nap.

This was the chance Sandy was waiting for.

Slick scampered away from the Great Oak, but he did not go toward the thick woods where the best vines grew. Nor did he head toward the riverbed where one might find strong reeds. Instead, he made a wide, secretive circle around the clearing and headed back toward the hollow log where the seeds were stored.

Sandy's heart began to beat faster. He scrambled down from his perch, staying on the far side of the tree trunks, and followed. He was so small and brown that he was nearly invisible against the forest floor.

Slick moved differently now that he thought he was alone. His confident, strolling gait was gone. He moved in quick, furtive dashes, stopping often to look over his shoulder. He went straight to the hollow log. Fatty Coon was no longer there, having gone to find his own dinner.

Slick sniffed at the entrance to the log. He put his head inside and looked around. Then he pulled it out and began to examine the ground nearby. He was not looking for "secondary materials." He was inspecting the site of his prize.

Then he did something very curious. He scurried a little way off, to a spot behind a thicket of ferns where no one would normally look. And he began to dig.

It was not the busy, energetic digging of a squirrel burying a single nut for the winter. It was a slow, methodical, determined digging. He used his sharp claws to loosen the earth and his paws to push it away, carefully scattering the loose dirt under the ferns so it would not make a pile.

Sandy watched, hidden behind a toadstool. He did not understand at first. Then, as the hole grew bigger and bigger--much bigger than any squirrel would need for a nest--a cold dread settled in his stomach.

Slick was not making a plan for a hotel. He was making a hole. A hole just big enough, Sandy realized, to hold a very large bag of sunflower seeds. He was not going to build anything. He was going to take the seeds, bury them in his secret spot, and then disappear. And when the forest-folk discovered the seeds were gone, he would be miles away, and they would be left with nothing but a silly dream and an empty winter ahead.

Sandy Chipmunk knew he had to do something. But what could one little chipmunk do against a clever trickster that the whole valley believed in? He needed help. And he knew just who to ask.

XVII THE TRAP IS SET

Sandy did not run to Mr. Crow. He knew the old bird was too proud to admit he had been fooled. And he did not run to Frisky, who was still lost in the dream of the Treetop Hotel. He ran straight back to the creek, his little legs pumping as fast as they could go.

He found Fatty Coon in the same spot, calmly finishing his dinner of fresh-water clams.

"Fatty!" Sandy panted, skidding to a stop. "You were right! He is a trickster! He is a thief!"

In a torrent of chittering words, Sandy explained everything he had seen: Slick's sneaky trip back to the log, the secret hole behind the ferns, the careful way he hid the dirt.

Fatty Coon listened without interrupting. He cracked open one last clam, ate the morsel inside, and then carefully wiped his paws on the damp grass.

"So," Fatty rumbled. "The fox builds a burrow before he steals the chickens."

"What do we do?" Sandy asked. "Sunset is almost here! That is when he is supposed to get the seeds!"

"A thief who comes to take something," Fatty said, standing up, "is often surprised to find something else waiting for him. We will let him have the seeds."

Sandy's jaw dropped. "Let him have them? But--!"

"We will let him take the bag," Fatty continued, a rare gleam of cunning in his eyes. "But the journey to his secret hole will be... difficult. You are a good watcher, Sandy. Now you must be a good worker. And a quiet one. Go to the path between the hollow log and the ferns. The path he will have to drag the sack along. Find every loose root and low vine you can. Do not break them. Just pull them up a little bit, so they make small loops. Do you understand?"

Sandy's mind raced. Loops! Of course! He nodded eagerly.

"I will get the bag ready," Fatty said. And with a speed that was surprising in a creature so large, he lumbered off toward the hollow log.

Sandy hurried to do as he was told. He found the path Slick would have to take. It was narrow and twisted through the undergrowth. Sandy's small size was an advantage here. He scurried along, tugging at the thin, tough roots of the briar bushes and pulling up long runners of wild grapevines, arranging them into little traps across the path. He worked quickly and silently, his earlier fear replaced by a nervous excitement.

Meanwhile, Fatty Coon reached the hollow log. He pulled the heavy sack of sunflower seeds out into the clearing. But he did not leave it there. He dragged it over to a large mud puddle left over from a recent rain. With a grunt, he pushed and rolled the heavy bag through the puddle until the burlap was completely soaked and coated in thick, heavy mud. The bag, which was already heavy, must have weighed twice as much now.

Then, he dragged the dripping, muddy sack back to the front of the hollow log and went to hide himself in a thicket nearby, settling down to wait.

Just as the sun touched the treetops, turning the sky orange and purple, Slick the gray squirrel returned to the clearing. Mr. Crow was there to meet him, looking very important.

"Ah, Slick!" Mr. Crow cawed. "The hour is upon us! The first great step toward our glorious future! As the official leader of this enterprise, I hereby authorize the transfer of the initial investment capital."

"Thank you, Mr. Crow," Slick said with a bow. His eyes were fixed on the big sack. He noticed it was wet and muddy, but he was too full of triumph to care. "Your trust is well-placed. The work will begin tomorrow at dawn."

"Excellent!" said Mr. Crow. "I shall supervise, of course."

Slick grabbed the top of the sack. He gave a mighty heave, expecting to swing it onto his back. But the bag, waterlogged and heavy with mud, barely moved.

"Oof!" Slick grunted, surprised. "This is... heavier than I remembered."

"It is the weight of progress!" Mr. Crow declared grandly from his branch. "And responsibility! Heave-ho, my boy!"

Slick set his jaw. He was strong, and he was not going to be defeated by a simple bag. He wrapped his paws around the burlap, dug his back feet into the ground, and began to drag the sack away from the log, toward the darkening woods. Toward the ferns. And toward the trap Sandy had set.
