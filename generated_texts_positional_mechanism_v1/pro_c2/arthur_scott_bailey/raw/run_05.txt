Pip Sparrow fluttered down beside a freshly dug mound of earth. "What have you found now, Digby Mole?" she chirped.

Digby Mole poked his twitching nose out of the tunnel. "I have found the most wonderful thing!" he announced. "It is a root cellar, all of its own! And it is full of the most delicious, tender worms."

"A root cellar!" Pip Sparrow repeated, her head cocked to one side. "Are you quite certain? Where is it?"

"Right over there, by the old stone wall," Digby said proudly. "A perfect circle in the ground, with a shiny roof. I believe it was dropped from the sky just for me."

"My, my!" Pip Sparrow chirped. And she flew straight away to tell Barnaby Badger, who was grumpily trying to pull a stubborn turnip from Farmer Green's garden.

"Digby Mole has found a house made of silver!" Pip exclaimed. "It fell from the sky, and it is filled with a great feast!"

Barnaby Badger let go of the turnip. "Silver!" he grunted. "That sounds far too fancy for a mole. I expect he does not know what to do with it. _I_ shall go and have a look."

But by the time Barnaby Badger arrived at the stone wall, a small crowd had gathered. For Pip had told Tabitha Rabbit, and Tabitha had told old Mr. Groundhog, and the story had changed just a little bit with each telling.

"I hear it is a silver castle," Tabitha Rabbit whispered to Barnaby.

"And it makes music if you touch it," added Mr. Groundhog.

"And it grants wishes!" squeaked a little field mouse, who was peeking from behind a rock.

Barnaby Badger puffed out his chest. "Stand aside!" he boomed. "A silver castle that grants wishes is no small thing. It must be investigated by someone of importance. Someone brave." He marched right up to the object lying in the grass. It was indeed round, and its top was very shiny.

"It is a forgotten dinner pail," a voice cawed from above. It was wise old Mr. Crow, perched on the stone wall. "A human left it."

"Nonsense!" Barnaby Badger declared. "This is clearly a magical treasure. You are only saying that because you are jealous, Mr. Crow. Now, I shall claim the first wish."

"You did not find it," Digby Mole piped up from his hole. "I found it. It is my worm cellar."

"Worms!" Barnaby scoffed. "Do not be ridiculous. This is a wishing palace. And the first wish will be for a winter's supply of _honeycakes_." He tapped the shiny pail with a claw. Nothing happened.

"You have to tap it _three_ times," the field mouse suggested timidly.

Barnaby Badger rolled his eyes. But he tapped the pail once. Twice. And on the third tap, the lid popped open with a loud _SNAP_! A great cloud of buzzing insects flew out in a fury, for Barnaby had not found a wishing palace, but a nest of very angry hornets.

Barnaby yelped and tumbled backward, swatting at his nose. "They--they were guarding the treasure!" he sputtered, as he ran for the safety of the nearest thicket.

The other animals scattered in a panic. All except for Digby Mole, who was safely underground, and Mr. Crow, who was high up on the wall.

"Well," Mr. Crow said to himself, once the buzzing had faded. "I suppose that is one way to get a pail open." He chuckled a dry, cawing laugh. "But I do not think Barnaby will be wishing for honeycakes again any time soon."

Mr. Crow chuckled a dry, rusty sort of chuckle. "Some people can't take good advice," he said to a passing butterfly. "It's for his own good, you know. He's much too proud for a chipmunk." The bit of paper lay half hidden in the clover, its bright pictures turned up toward the blue sky. It lay there all afternoon. The sun warmed it, and a little green grasshopper hopped on it and then off again, and a field mouse sniffed at one corner of it before deciding it was not good to eat.

Late in the day, when shadows were growing long, Peter Rabbit came hopping down the lane. He was thinking of the delicious young carrots in Farmer Green's garden; and he was wondering if he dared try to snatch just one. His nose twitched as he imagined the sweet, crunchy taste.

It was his twitching nose that first noticed the strange paper. It smelled of something new; it smelled of the ink from the village printing press and faintly of the mail-carrier's leather bag. And it smelled a little bit of Sandy Chipmunk.

"Well, now! What's this?" Peter Rabbit said to himself, for he was a most inquisitive fellow. He nudged the paper with his nose. He saw the pictures, just as Sandy and Mr. Crow had. "It's a list!" he gasped. "Farmer Green has made a list! I heard him talking just last week about pests and nuisances. This must be it! This is a list of all the folk he means to trap!"

The thought was so dreadful that Peter Rabbit quite forgot all about carrots. His only thought was to warn the others. The very first person he must tell was poor Mrs. Mouse, whose son was clearly marked for doom.

But the story, as stories so often do when they are in a hurry, began to change.

When Mrs. Mouse found Mrs. Red-Squirrel, she was in such a state that the words tumbled out all wrong. "Farmer Green has a book!" she cried. "A black book with pictures in it! He's going to catch our sons and put them in a pie!"

Mrs. Red-Squirrel did not wait to hear any more. She chattered and scolded and flew through the treetops, telling everyone she met that Farmer Green had a book of recipes and was planning a great feast.

By the time the news reached old Mr. Groundhog, who was napping in the sun, the story was that Farmer Green had hired a hunter from the village. And the hunter was coming the very next day with a dozen cages and a great big net to catch everyone in Pleasant Valley.

"Even those of us who mind our own business," Mr. Groundhog grumbled. And he went to tell Fatty Coon, who was very clever at opening latches and might know how to open a cage.

Fatty Coon listened to the story with great concern. "A hunter, you say? With a net? This is serious. This sounds like the work of Mr. Crow. He is always flying over the farm; he must have seen something."

And so it was that Jasper Jay, who had heard the story from Fatty Coon, flew straight to the tall pine tree where Mr. Crow was still perched, feeling very satisfied with himself.

"Mr. Crow! Mr. Crow! Terrible news!" Jasper shrieked, landing on a branch just below him.

"Calm yourself, Jasper," Mr. Crow said in a bored voice. "Your screeching is giving me a headache. There is never any news that is as terrible as you make it sound."

"Oh, but this is!" Jasper insisted. "Farmer Green has sent for a hunter! He is going to clear the whole valley! He has a book with all our pictures in it, and he is going to catch us all in a giant net and cook us in a pie for the village festival!"

Mr. Crow blinked. For a moment, he was speechless. "A book? With pictures?" he croaked.

"Yes!" said Jasper. "Peter Rabbit found the first page of it. It has pictures of Timothy Mouse and a Red-Squirrel and that rascal Rinaldo Rat. It's a death list, Mr. Crow! A death list!"

A cold feeling, colder than any winter wind, swept over Mr. Crow. He knew at once what "page" they were talking about. It was the letter. The letter that _he_ had made Sandy Chipmunk throw away. His little bit of jealous mischief had turned into a monster. All of Pleasant Valley was in a panic because of him.

But Mr. Crow was far too proud to admit such a thing. "Nonsense!" he cawed, trying to sound as wise and untroubled as ever. "A complete fabrication. You smaller folk are always getting carried away."

"But Peter Rabbit saw it!" Jasper chirped.

"Peter Rabbit," Mr. Crow said with a great sneer, "would be frightened by his own shadow if it hopped unexpectedly. Now, if you will excuse me, I have important matters to attend to."

But as soon as Jasper Jay had flown away, grumbling about how proud Mr. Crow had become, the old crow's confidence vanished. He was in a terrible fix. If he told the truth, everyone would know he had been mean to Sandy and that he could not read. He would be a laughing-stock. But if he did nothing, the whole forest might tear itself apart with fear.

Meanwhile, the dreadful news had finally found its way to Sandy Chipmunk. He was sitting inside his cozy home, trying to forget about the letter, when Frisky Squirrel burst in without even knocking.

"Sandy! You must hide!" Frisky chattered, his eyes wide with fear. "A hunter is coming! Farmer Green is going to catch us all!"

And when Sandy heard the part about a paper with pictures on it, a horrible, sinking feeling filled his stomach. It was his letter. His beautiful, proud letter was the cause of all this trouble.

"Oh, Frisky! What have I done?" Sandy moaned. "There is no hunter! It's just a paper I had!"

"A paper?" Frisky asked, confused. "But Peter Rabbit said--"

"It doesn't matter what he said!" Sandy cried. "I threw it away! Mr. Crow told me to. Oh, this is all my fault!"

And Sandy Chipmunk, who a few hours before had been the proudest creature in the wood, now felt like the most miserable. He had to fix this. He darted out of his home, leaving a bewildered Frisky Squirrel behind. He had to find that paper and show everyone that it was just a silly advertisement.

He ran to the clump of clover where he had thrown it. But it was not there. Of course it was not there! Peter Rabbit had taken it!

A great council was now gathering in a clearing by the creek. Barnaby Badger was there, looking very important. He had a plan. "We shall build a great wall of mud and sticks!" he was booming. "We will not let this hunter pass!"

"A wall will not stop a net!" shrieked Mrs. Red-Squirrel. "We must attack! We will gather all the hard nuts and drop them on his head!"

Just then, Sandy Chipmunk scurried into the middle of the circle. "Stop!" he squeaked, out of breath. "Everybody stop! It's all a mistake!"

"A mistake?" Barnaby Badger grunted. "It is no mistake that our lives are in danger!"

"But they are not!" Sandy insisted. "The paper you are all talking about--it was mine! It was a letter! And it is harmless!"

"A letter?" said Peter Rabbit, stepping forward. He was still holding the crumpled paper. "Then why does it have a picture of Timothy Mouse on it? Answer that!" He held it up for all to see.

All eyes turned to Sandy. He looked at the paper, and he looked at all the frightened faces around him. He did not know what to say; for he still did not know what the paper truly meant.

"I... I don't know," he stammered.

A great groaning and chattering arose from the crowd. Sandy's confession had only made things worse.

Suddenly, a loud "CAW! CAW! CAW!" silenced everyone. Mr. Crow swooped down from a tree and landed on a large rock. He looked very solemn and very important. He had decided that the only way out was to pretend he was in charge of the solution.

"Friends! Neighbors!" he began in his loudest voice. "There is no need for panic. There has been a... a slight misunderstanding."

He glared at Sandy and Peter. "This paper," he went on, pointing a wing at it, "is indeed a thing of great importance. But it is not a 'death list'."

A sigh of relief went through the crowd.

"It is," Mr. Crow announced, thinking as fast as he could, "a... a list of champions!"

"Champions?" asked Tabitha Rabbit.

"Yes! Champions!" Mr. Crow declared. "Farmer Green is not hunting you. He is... admiring you! This is a list of the finest, most clever creatures in Pleasant Valley. The Red-Squirrel with the fluffiest tail! The mouse with the quickest feet! He has made pictures to honor you!"

This was a much more pleasant story. The animals began to look at one another with pride instead of fear.

"But... why does he want to honor us with a net and a pie?" asked Mr. Groundhog, who was a practical fellow.

Mr. Crow faltered for only a second. "That part of the story," he cawed dismissively, "was a foolish addition by excitable minds. There is no net. There is no pie. There is only... the honor!"

Just at that moment, a new voice was heard. "What in the world is going on here?"

It was Johnnie Green. He had been walking home from the village and had heard the commotion. He saw all the animals gathered, and Peter Rabbit holding a crumpled piece of paper.

The animals froze. They stared at the farmer's boy with wide eyes.

The animals stared, holding their breath. Mr. Crow puffed out his chest, ready to be proven right.

And this little fellow..." he pointed to the chipmunk picture, "won 'Cutest Seed-Stuffer'."

He looked right at Sandy Chipmunk. "My sister wanted to enter you this year," he said with a smile.

A great silence fell over the clearing. The animals looked from the paper to Johnnie Green, and then to each other. They felt very, very foolish. A pet show! All that panic and fear over a pet show.

Slowly, the crowd began to break up. Mrs. Mouse hurried home to tell Timothy he was not going to be in a pie after all. Barnaby Badger stomped off, muttering about the foolishness of pet shows. Peter Rabbit looked at Sandy Chipmunk.

"I am sorry, Sandy," Peter said quietly. "I was so frightened, I did not think."

"And I am sorry, too," Sandy replied. "I was so proud, I did not think either."

They looked at Mr. Crow, who was trying to look as if he had known the truth all along.

"Well," Mr. Crow cawed, clearing his throat. "It is just as I said. A misunderstanding. It is a good thing I was here to keep a cool head."

And he flew off without another word, because he could feel a great many eyes upon him, and for the first time in a long while, he did not feel wise at all. He felt very much as if everyone knew that he, old Mr. Crow, could not tell the difference between a death list and an advertisement for a pet show. And that, he thought, was a most uncomfortable feeling indeed.

XIII A STRANGE NEW STAR

For a few days after the great misunderstanding about the pet show, a quiet fell over Pleasant Valley. The animals went about their business of gathering food for the winter, but they were more thoughtful than before. And when they met one another on the forest paths, they often looked at Mr. Crow with a twinkle in their eyes.

Mr. Crow did not like it at all. He held his head higher than ever and tried to look more wise than he had ever looked before. But he knew that behind his back, Jasper Jay and Frisky Squirrel and perhaps even shy Peter Rabbit were telling the story of the advertisement. He could feel their laughter in the rustle of the leaves.

"A very good morning to you, Mr. Crow," Jasper Jay would call out from a high branch. "Have you received any more important letters?"

"I am far too busy with important thoughts to trouble myself with mail," Mr. Crow would reply stiffly, and he would fly away before Jasper Jay could say another word.

But the teasing wore on him. His reputation, which he valued more than the plumpest corn kernels, had been damaged. He had always been Mr. Crow, the one who knew things. And now, he was Mr. Crow, the one who could not tell a pet show from a pie recipe. It was a most disagreeable state of affairs. He knew he must do something to prove his wisdom once and for all.

His chance came one evening, just as the sun was setting. Frisky Squirrel was sitting high in a hickory tree, trying to knock down one last nut before bedtime. As the sky grew dark, he saw something new. Far away, beyond the woods and in the direction of the village, a faint light began to glow against the twilight. It was not the moon, and it was not a star he had ever seen before. It grew brighter, and then it seemed to move in a slow, grand circle.

"Goodness me!" Frisky chattered to himself. "The moon has a new child! And it is learning to dance!"

He scampered down the tree and raced through the woods until he found Fatty Coon, who was washing his paws in a little brook.

"Fatty!" Frisky squeaked, his tail twitching with excitement. "There is a new moon in the sky, and it is spinning around and around! It is made of bright lights!"

Fatty Coon stopped his washing and looked up. He could not see the new light from down in the hollow. "A spinning moon?" he said doubtfully. "I think you have eaten a bad nut, Frisky. Moons do not spin."

"But it is true!" Frisky insisted. "It is over by the village. It must be a special moon just for the farmers!"

So Fatty Coon told Barnaby Badger that Farmer Green had somehow captured a piece of the moon and was making it spin for his own amusement. And Barnaby Badger told Mrs. Mouse that a whole family of stars had fallen out of the sky and were rolling around on the ground near the village, burning holes in the earth.

By the time the story reached Sandy Chipmunk, it had grown very alarming indeed. What he heard was this: The world was beginning to come apart. A giant, fiery wheel had broken off the sky and was rolling across the country, setting fire to everything in its path. And it was coming straight for Pleasant Valley.

Sandy Chipmunk shivered and hid his face in his paws. He had stored up so many beechnuts, and now a fiery wheel was coming to burn down his cozy home in the old stone wall.

The next morning, the forest was buzzing with fear. The animals gathered in a clearing, all chattering at once. They did not know what to do.

"We must run!" squeaked Timothy Mouse.

"Where can we run?" grumbled Barnaby Badger. "If it is a wheel of fire, it will roll faster than any of us."

It was then that Mr. Crow arrived. He landed on a high limb and looked down at the frightened crowd. Here, he thought, was his opportunity.

"What is all this foolish chattering?" he cawed in his deepest, most important voice.

Jasper Jay flew to a branch near him. "Have you not heard, O wise Mr. Crow?" he asked, and there was a tiny bit of mockery in his voice. "The sky is falling. A great wheel of fire is coming to burn us all. We thought you might have a plan."

Mr. Crow puffed out his chest. "Nonsense," he said. "The sky is not falling. It takes more than a few nervous squirrels to make the sky fall. I shall investigate this matter myself and tell you the truth of it."

And without another word, he spread his black wings and flew high into the air, heading toward the village. The animals watched him go, their fear mixed with a small amount of hope. Perhaps Mr. Crow really did know everything, after all.

Mr. Crow flew for a long time. The strange new thing was farther away than it looked. As he drew closer, he saw that it was on the edge of the village, in a big, open field. And it was not a wheel of fire at all. It was a huge circle made of iron, taller than the tallest trees in Pleasant Valley. It was covered in little chairs and a great many unlit lamps. Men were working all around its base with hammers and wrenches.

Mr. Crow circled high above, careful not to be seen. He had never seen such a thing. What could it be? It was round, like the sun. It was made of metal, which grew hot in the sun. And Farmer Green and the other men were building it. He thought very hard for a full minute. Then, he was sure he had the answer. It was so simple, so obvious, that he nearly cawed with delight.

He flew back to the forest as fast as he could. He landed on the same high branch, looking down at the waiting animals. He cleared his throat.

"I have returned," he announced. "And I have discovered the truth. It is far worse than a simple wheel of fire."

A gasp went through the crowd.

"It is not fire," Mr. Crow continued, letting his words sink in. "It is a machine. A terrible machine. It is a _Sun-Catcher_."

"A Sun-Catcher?" Sandy Chipmunk whispered.

"Precisely," said Mr. Crow, pleased that they were hanging on his every word. "Farmer Green and the other humans are greedy. They are not content with the warmth of summer. They mean to steal the sun itself."

He paused for effect. "This great metal wheel is designed to catch the sun's rays and hold them. They will store up all the heat and light, so that their houses will be warm all winter long. But for us," he said, his voice dropping to a grim tone, "for us, it means there will be no sun. The days will be dark. The winter will be a time of endless ice and snow. Nothing will grow. We will all starve in the cold and the dark."

A terrible silence fell over the clearing. The animals looked at one another with wide, terrified eyes. Mr. Crow's explanation made a dreadful sort of sense. The humans were always doing strange things. Why shouldn't they try to steal the sun?

"What... what can we do?" asked Frisky Squirrel, his voice trembling.

Mr. Crow looked down, a feeling of great importance swelling in his chest. He was their leader again. "We must act," he declared. "We cannot destroy the machine; for it is too big and strong. But we can stop it from working. We must prevent the sun from touching it."

"But how?" asked Barnaby Badger.

"We must cover it," Mr. Crow explained. "We must cover it with something the sun cannot shine through. We will cover it with mud and wet leaves! We will work tonight, under the cover of darkness. We will plaster every inch of that machine, so that when the sun rises, its rays will find no metal to catch. The plan will fail, and Pleasant Valley will be saved. And it will be because of _me_."

This time, no one doubted him. The fear was too great. And the plan, for all its strangeness, was a plan. It was something to do.

XIV THE GREAT SUN-CATCHER FIASCO

That night, a strange army gathered at the edge of the woods. Under the pale light of the real moon, Mr. Crow organized his forces.

"Frisky Squirrel and your cousins," he commanded, "you will climb the highest parts and drop down wet leaves. Fatty Coon and your family, your clever paws are perfect for plastering mud. Barnaby Badger, you will dig up the wettest earth from the stream bank. Peter Rabbit, you and your brothers will carry leaves. Sandy Chipmunk, you will... well, you will also carry leaves."

Sandy Chipmunk did not feel right about the plan. He remembered the letter, and how sure he had been. And he remembered how sure Mr. Crow had been. "Mr. Crow," he began timidly, "are you quite certain? It did not look like a Sun-Catcher to me when I peeked from the hill. It had little chairs on it."

"Chairs!" Mr. Crow scoffed. "Of course it has chairs. That is where the sun-keepers will sit to guard their stolen warmth. Do not be stupid, Sandy. There is important work to do. Now, get to it!"

And so, the work began. It was a long, messy business. Barnaby Badger dug up great clods of dark, gooey mud. Peter Rabbit and Sandy Chipmunk dragged armfuls of damp, rotting leaves from the forest floor. The coons, chattering with glee at the glorious mess, scooped up handfuls of mud and began smearing it all over the lower parts of the giant wheel. They filled the little chairs with mud and leaves. They covered the spokes and the platform.

Frisky Squirrel and his friends scampered up the iron beams, their claws making tiny scratching sounds in the night. They dropped bundles of wet leaves down onto the chairs below, which the coons then stomped into the mud.

Mr. Crow did not do any of the messy work himself, of course. He perched on a nearby fence post, directing the operation like a great general. "More mud on the left side!" he would caw. "Cover that shiny bit! Hurry, before the dawn comes!"

They worked for hours. The animals were covered in mud from their ears to their tails. The beautiful, freshly painted Ferris wheel was a dreadful sight. It looked as if the swamp had tried to swallow it.

At last, as the first hint of gray appeared in the eastern sky, Mr. Crow called a halt.

"It is done!" he proclaimed. "We have saved the sun! Now, we must hide and watch our victory."

The weary, muddy creatures scurried back into the woods and hid behind bushes and trees. They peeked out at their handiwork, feeling both tired and proud. They had faced a great danger and had won.

Not long after, Farmer Green and his son, Johnnie, arrived in their wagon. They were followed by several other men from the village who worked at the fair.

"What in the world--!" Farmer Green exclaimed, stopping dead in his tracks.

The men stared at the Ferris wheel. It was covered in a disgusting coat of mud and decaying leaves. The little chairs, which they had cleaned so carefully the day before, were overflowing with swampy muck.

"Vandals!" one of the men shouted angrily. "Someone has tried to ruin the new ride!"

"Who would do such a thing?" another asked, kicking at a lump of mud.

Johnnie Green, however, was looking not at the wheel, but at the ground. In the soft earth, he saw a great many tiny footprints. He saw the tracks of squirrels and rabbits. He saw the distinctive hand-like print of a raccoon. And he saw the delicate track of a chipmunk.

A slow smile spread across his face. He remembered the panic over the pet show advertisement.

"I do not think it was vandals, Father," he said quietly.

"Then who?" Farmer Green demanded.

"I think," Johnnie said, trying not to laugh, "that our friends from the forest have had another misunderstanding."

From their hiding place, the animals watched. They saw the men look angry. They saw them get out buckets and brushes and hoses. They expected the men to look at the sky, and then at the mud-covered wheel, and then to give up and go home, defeated.

But that is not what happened. The men began to clean the wheel. They scraped off the mud. They washed out the chairs. It took them a very long time, and they were grumbling the whole while.

Mr. Crow was confused. "They do not understand that their plan has failed," he whispered to the others. "Just wait. When they finish, they will see that it is no use."

Finally, the wheel was clean again. The sun was now high in the sky, shining brightly. The animals held their breath. Now the men would see. The Sun-Catcher would not work.

But then, one of the men climbed into a little booth and pulled a large lever. There was a low hum, and then a cheerful tune began to play from a hidden speaker. And then, the great wheel began to turn.

It went around and around, slowly at first, then a little faster. The little chairs swayed gently. And as it turned, the hundreds of lamps all over it suddenly flashed on, twinkling with bright colors even in the daylight.

It was not catching the sun at all. It was making its own light. It was beautiful.

A great, collective gasp came from the bushes. It was not a trap. It was not a weapon. It was... wonderful.

All the animals turned to look at Mr. Crow.

He was staring at the turning wheel, his beak hanging open. All his importance, all his wisdom, had vanished. He looked very small and very silly.

"I--," he began. "I-- that is to say-- it is a trick! They have found a new way to make it work!"

But nobody was listening. They knew the truth now. They had been fooled again. And this time, they had made a very big, very foolish mess for nothing.

Johnnie Green had seen the rustling in the bushes. He walked slowly toward the edge of the woods. "It's all right," he called out softly. "You can come out. You are not in trouble."

Hesitantly, Sandy Chipmunk poked his head out from behind a large fern. He was still covered in a bit of mud.

Johnnie smiled at him. "You have been very busy," he said kindly. "But you do not need to be afraid. This is not a Sun-Catcher."

"It is not?" Sandy asked in a tiny voice.

"No," said Johnnie. "It is called a Ferris wheel. It is just for fun. People sit in the chairs, and it carries them way up into the sky so they can see for miles. It is for the County Fair. It is a ride." He reached into his pocket and pulled out a shelled peanut, which he tossed gently onto the ground in front of Sandy.

Sandy looked at the peanut, then at the wonderful, spinning, musical wheel, and then back at Johnnie Green. He felt very foolish, but he also felt a great sense of relief.

One by one, the other animals came out of hiding. They stood at the edge of the woods, staring in awe at the Ferris wheel.

Then, slowly, they all turned to look at Mr. Crow. He tried to puff out his chest, but he could not.

"Well," he cawed, his voice weak. "It is just as I suspected. A misunderstanding of a most complex sort. It required a bold test to reveal the truth, and my plan worked perfectly. You now know what it is. You are welcome."

Nobody said a word. They just stared. Even Jasper Jay was silent. The silence was far, far worse than any teasing.

Mr. Crow could not bear it. The weight of all those knowing eyes was too much. Without another squawk, he leaped into the air and flew away. He did not fly toward his nest in the tall pine. He flew south, toward the Swift River, and he did not look back. He thought perhaps he would spend the rest of the season very far away, where no one had ever heard of a pet show, or a great and messy plan to save the sun.

Sandy Chipmunk picked up the peanut Johnnie Green had given him. He looked at Frisky Squirrel, whose whiskers were still caked with mud.

"A Ferris wheel," Sandy said, his voice full of wonder.

"It is the most beautiful thing I have ever seen," Frisky replied.

They watched the great wheel turn against the blue sky, its lights twinkling and its music drifting through the trees. And Sandy Chipmunk thought that perhaps it was better not to be the one who always had the answer. It was much more interesting, he decided, to simply watch and wait and see what wonderful things might happen next.

XIII A DELICIOUS MYSTERY

For a time, after the great Ferris wheel misunderstanding, a pleasant quiet settled over the woods near Farmer Green's. Without Mr. Crow to dream up grand plans or to tell everyone what he thought they ought to know, the days fell into a simple rhythm. Sandy Chipmunk gathered nuts. Frisky Squirrel chased his tail. And Jasper Jay, having been thoroughly humbled, spoke only when he was spoken to, and even then he did not say very much.

Some of the animals missed Mr. Crow, for even if he was a great bother, he was never dull. But Sandy Chipmunk was not one of them. He was quite happy to spend his days scampering up and down his favorite hickory tree without a single worry about sun-catchers or stolen letters.

One afternoon, as the sun began to sink low in the sky, Sandy found himself near the edge of the woods that bordered Farmer Green's backyard. He was searching for a particular patch of late-blooming clover when a new smell reached his nose. It was not a smell he knew, but it was the most wonderful smell he had ever encountered. It was warm and sweet and spicy all at once, and it made his whiskers twitch with longing.

Curiosity, which was always Sandy's closest companion, urged him onward. He crept from the shadow of the great oak tree to the stone wall, and from the stone wall to the lilac bush right beneath the kitchen window. He peeked through the leaves, and what he saw made him gasp.

There on the windowsill, cooling in the evening air, sat the source of the wonderful smell. It was a pie. But it was not just any pie. Its crust was a perfect golden-brown, fluted around the edges like the petals of a flower. And through a little design cut in the top, a thick, bubbling, dark red filling peeked out. It was the most beautiful thing to eat that Sandy had ever laid eyes on.

Just then, the kitchen door opened and Mrs. Green came out, wiping her hands on her apron. Johnnie Green was with her.

"There now," said Mrs. Green, looking at the pie with a proud smile. "It's my best one yet. If this doesn't win the prize at the Fair tomorrow, I don't know what will."

"It smells like a winner, Ma," Johnnie said.

Sandy Chipmunk's ears perked up. _Win the prize!_ he thought. He did not understand. He looked at the pie, and then he looked at the place on the shelf where a prize might sit. But there was no prize. There was only the pie.

A sudden, thrilling thought struck him. The pie _was_ the prize! It was not just a pie; it was a _Prize Pie_! A special pie that was itself a reward. He did not know what it was a reward _for_, but it must be something very important indeed.

He did not wait to hear another word. He scrambled down from the lilac bush, his heart thumping with excitement. He had to tell somebody. He had to tell Frisky Squirrel.

"Frisky!" he squeaked, as he scampered up the trunk of a maple tree where his friend was pretending to be a leaf. "Frisky, you will not believe what I have seen!"

"Is it bigger than the Ferris wheel?" Frisky asked, stopping his game at once.

"It is smaller," Sandy admitted, "but oh, it is so much more wonderful! It is a Prize Pie!"

"A Prize Pie!" Frisky Squirrel repeated, his eyes wide. "What is a Prize Pie?"

"I am not quite sure," said Sandy. "But it is the most delicious-smelling thing in the whole world. And Mrs. Green said it was a prize. It is cooling on her windowsill right now!"

The two friends sat on the branch, chattering excitedly. A Prize Pie! The very words were full of magic and mystery. And as the news began its journey from that maple branch, it was destined to become much more magical--and much more troublesome--than either of them could possibly imagine.

XIV THE LEGEND OF THE PIE

The story of the Prize Pie did not stay with Sandy and Frisky for long. Frisky Squirrel, who could never keep a secret for more than a minute, hurried off at once to tell Fatty Coon.

Now, if there was one thing that interested Fatty Coon more than a comfortable nap, it was a good meal. And the idea of a _Prize Pie_ captured his imagination completely.

"A Prize Pie, you say!" he rumbled, waking from a doze in a hollow log. "Is it a prize made of pie? Or a pie that is a prize?"

"Both!" Frisky said with great confidence, though he had no idea at all. "Sandy saw it! It is golden and red and smells like heaven!"

Fatty Coon's mouth began to water. "A pie that is a prize," he murmured to himself. "Such a thing must have special powers. A single bite might keep a fellow full for a whole week! Perhaps a whole month!"

And that is how the first little bit of fancy was added to the story. Fatty Coon did not mean to make things up; he simply believed that anything so wonderful must also be magical. He lumbered off to the bank of the Swift River, where he found Peter Rabbit talking with Jimmy Skunk.

"Have you heard?" Fatty began, his voice low and serious. "Farmer Green's wife has made a magical pie. It is a Prize Pie. They say one bite will keep you full for the entire winter!"

Peter Rabbit's long ears stood straight up. "For the whole winter?" he gasped. Imagine! No more digging for frozen carrots under the snow!

Jimmy Skunk, who was a more practical sort, just sniffed. "I have never heard of such a thing," he said.

But it was too late. The story was loose, and it was running faster than Peter Rabbit himself. Peter told his twenty-seven cousins. One of his cousins told Chatterer the Red Squirrel. And Chatterer, who always liked to be the center of attention, told a whole flock of sparrows, adding a few details of his own.

By the time Jasper Jay heard the news from a sparrow, the story had grown quite grand.

"It is a pie of kings!" the sparrow chirped breathlessly. "Made by the farmer's wife for the King of the County Fair! Whoever eats it becomes the King of the Forest for a whole year! He gets the best nuts, the first pick of berries, and nobody can argue with him about anything!"

Jasper Jay puffed out his chest. King of the Forest! The idea appealed to him very much. For too long he had lived in the shadow of Mr. Crow. With Mr. Crow gone south, here was his chance. If he, Jasper Jay, could get that pie, then everyone would have to respect him. They would have to listen to _him_.

"King of the Forest, you say," Jasper said, trying to look as if he knew it all along. "Yes, yes. I have heard of these royal pies. They are a very ancient tradition. It is a very serious matter."

The story continued to travel, each teller adding a new and more wonderful layer. It was no longer just a pie that kept you full for the winter, or a pie that made you a king. By the time the news circled all the way back to Sandy Chipmunk, it had become a legend.

It was Fatty Coon who told him the latest version, his eyes gleaming with excitement and greed.

"Sandy! It is even better than we thought!" he panted, having run all the way from the river bank. "It is the Great Pie of Plenty! Not only will it make you King of the Forest and keep you fed for a whole year, but it will also grant you three wishes! And the first wish of every creature who has ever eaten from a Pie of Plenty has always been for more pie!"

Sandy Chipmunk stared at him, his head spinning. Three wishes! King of the Forest! It all seemed a little too much. He remembered the simple, beautiful pie on the windowsill. Could it really do all those things?

"Are you sure, Fatty?" Sandy asked nervously.

"Of course I am sure!" Fatty Coon boomed. "Jasper Jay explained it all! It is a very ancient tradition!"

And so it was that the idea of the Prize Pie took firm root in the minds of the creatures of Pleasant Valley. It was a magical thing, a source of power and plenty, an answer to every problem. And every single animal, from the smallest mouse to the biggest raccoon, now wanted that pie more than anything else in the world.

XV A VERY BAD PLAN

The desire for the Great Pie of Plenty burned like a fever in the woods. Little groups of animals gathered everywhere, whispering and plotting. The squirrels argued with the chipmunks over who had seen it first. The rabbits declared that their great speed made them the most worthy. And Fatty Coon simply sat by himself, dreaming of endless meals and licking his lips.

It was clear that something had to be done. If every animal tried to get the pie for himself, there would be nothing but chaos, and no one would get to be king or have any wishes at all.

Jasper Jay saw his opportunity. "A meeting!" he shrieked from the highest branch of the tallest pine tree. "There must be a meeting! I have a plan!"

And so, that evening, the animals gathered in the moonlit clearing where they had so often listened to Mr. Crow. But this felt different. Without Mr. Crow's booming voice and scornful gaze, the meeting was disorderly. Everyone chattered at once.

"The pie belongs to the swift!" cried Frisky Squirrel.

"It belongs to the hungry!" bellowed Fatty Coon.

"It belongs to whoever is cleverest!" squeaked a little field mouse, before being shushed by his mother.

"Quiet! Quiet!" squawked Jasper Jay, flapping his wings for attention. "I, Jasper Jay, have thought this all through. We cannot all go at once. It would attract too much attention. We must work together!"

This seemed sensible enough, and the animals quieted down to listen.

"Mrs. Green is taking the pie to the County Fair tomorrow," Jasper announced. "It will be on a table in a big tent, with many other lesser pies. Our task is to get _our_ pie--the Great Pie of Plenty--away from the fair and back to the woods, where we can share it fairly."

A murmur of agreement went through the crowd. "But how?" called Sandy Chipmunk from the edge of the crowd. He felt a familiar sense of dread. This was beginning to feel very much like the plan to catch the sun.

"With a brilliant plan, of course!" Jasper preened. "My plan! It has three parts. Part One: The Distraction. Part Two: The Lookout. Part Three: The Retrieval."

He paused for dramatic effect.

"For Part One," he continued, "I will need a creature who is loud and annoying. Chatterer the Red Squirrel, you will do nicely. You will go to the other side of the fair and start a loud argument with a scarecrow. This will draw the attention of the humans."

Chatterer puffed his chest out, pleased with the important-sounding job.

"For Part Two, the Lookout," Jasper went on, "we need someone with sharp eyes who can sit very still. Peter Rabbit, you will hide beneath the pie tent and stamp your foot three times if a human is about to look at the pie table."

Peter Rabbit nodded solemnly, his ears twitching with the weight of his responsibility.

"And finally," Jasper said, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper, "Part Three: The Retrieval. This is the most important job. It requires speed, stealth, and strength. A team of two. They must sneak under the tent, grab the pie, and run for the woods. For this task, I have chosen... Frisky Squirrel and Sandy Chipmunk!"

Sandy's heart jumped into his throat. "Me?" he squeaked. "But... but the pie will be heavy! And it might be hot!"

"Nonsense!" said Jasper dismissively. "You are small and quick, Sandy. You can get into tight places. And Frisky is fast enough to outrun a farmer's dog. You will push it off the table, and Fatty Coon will be waiting just outside the tent to carry it the rest of the way."

Fatty Coon, who had been assigned the part he wanted most, grinned a wide, happy grin.

The plan seemed perfect to everyone except Sandy. He remembered his promise to himself, to stay out of trouble and simply watch the world. But now the whole forest was looking at him. Frisky was bouncing up and down with excitement. Fatty Coon was patting his stomach in anticipation. How could he say no? If he did, everyone would say he was a coward. They would say he did not want them to have three wishes and be full all winter.

"All right," he said in a very small voice. "I will do it."

A cheer went up from the crowd. Jasper Jay fluffed his feathers, looking very pleased with himself. He was the leader now. He had made a plan, and everyone had agreed to it. Surely, nothing could possibly go wrong.

XVI THE GREAT PIE HEIST

The next day was the day of the County Fair. The air buzzed not just with bees, but with the sounds of faraway music and the excited chatter of humans. From the edge of the woods, the fair looked like a terrifying monster of colorful tents and spinning rides.

The brave little company of pie thieves gathered for a final briefing from their leader, Jasper Jay.

"Is everyone ready?" he squawked. "Remember the plan. Chatterer, go make a scene. Peter, get to your post. Sandy, Frisky, wait for Peter's signal. Fatty, be ready to run. Now go!"

The animals scattered to their positions. Sandy's paws were damp with nervousness. He and Frisky crept through the tall grass, their hearts pounding. The smell of the fair was overwhelming--a strange mix of popcorn, dust, and farm animals. It made Sandy's nose feel dizzy.

They found the pie tent easily. It was a huge, striped tent filled with long tables. And on those tables sat dozens upon dozens of pies. Pies with lattice tops, pies with crumb toppings, pies with meringue as white as snow.

"Oh, my," whispered Frisky. "How will we ever find the right one?"

"It has a flower cut in the crust," Sandy whispered back, remembering what he had seen. "And the filling is dark red."

They peeked under the edge of the tent. There, on a table in the very center, marked with a little card that said "Mrs. Green," sat the Great Pie of Plenty. It was even more beautiful than Sandy remembered.

They slipped under the tent flap and hid beneath a long tablecloth, their noses twitching. They could hear the low murmur of human voices and the scuffling of feet. They found Peter Rabbit already in his hiding spot. He gave them a nervous wink.

Suddenly, from the far side of the fairgrounds, a terrible chattering and scolding began. It sounded as if a dozen red squirrels were fighting over a single acorn.

"That is Chatterer," Frisky whispered. "The plan is working!"

Sure enough, several humans who had been strolling near the pie tent turned and walked toward the sound of the commotion. The tent was nearly empty.

"Now is our chance," Sandy said, his voice trembling.

They scurried from under the table and climbed up a tablecloth onto the bench, and then onto the tabletop itself. The pie was huge! It was much bigger up close than it had looked from the ground.

"We cannot carry it," Frisky panted. "It is too heavy."

"Jasper said to push it," Sandy replied. "Fatty is waiting outside."

They put their shoulders to the pie plate. It was warm. They pushed with all their might. It slid an inch. They pushed again. It slid another inch, closer and closer to the edge of the table.

Just then, Peter Rabbit stamped his foot. Once. Twice. Three times. _Thump! Thump! Thump!_

A large woman with a big hat was walking back toward the table.

"Hurry!" squeaked Sandy.

With one last desperate shove, they pushed the pie over the edge.

But things did not happen as the plan said they should. The pie did not simply fall to the ground for Fatty Coon to catch. Instead, it landed on the trailing edge of the long tablecloth. And as it slid down the white cloth, it pulled the cloth with it.

There was a sound like thunder as a dozen other pies--apple, cherry, blueberry, and lemon meringue--were pulled off the table after it. They crashed to the ground in a glorious, sticky, fruity explosion.

Worse yet, Fatty Coon, who had been waiting just outside the tent, had become impatient. Hearing the crash, and smelling the sudden, overwhelming scent of warm pie, he lost all control. He burst through the tent flap with a happy roar, expecting to find his prize.

Instead, he slipped on a puddle of lemon meringue, skated through a patch of blueberry, and landed face-first in what was left of Mrs. Green's prize-winning blackberry pie.

The chaos was complete. The woman with the hat screamed. People came running. Peter Rabbit bolted from under the table. Chatterer's argument with the scarecrow was forgotten.

Sandy and Frisky, splattered with cherry filling, scrambled down from the ruined table and fled for their lives, leaving a trail of tiny, sticky footprints behind them. The Great Pie Heist had ended in a great, sticky, terrible mess.

XVII A FAMILIAR LAUGH

Dripping with fruit filling and shame, the failed pie thieves gathered one by one in the deep woods. Sandy and Frisky were covered in red. Fatty Coon was a miserable mess of purple and black. Peter Rabbit was trembling so hard he could barely stand, and Jasper Jay was nowhere to be seen.

They huddled together under a fern, a sad and sticky group. There was no Great Pie of Plenty. There would be no kings, no wishes, no winter-long fullness. There was only the bitter taste of failure and spilled blueberry.

"It is all Jasper's fault," Chatterer the Red Squirrel chattered angrily, having finally arrived. "A terrible plan! Arguing with a scarecrow! I have never been so embarrassed in my life!"

"He told us to push it," Frisky said, trying to lick a glob of cherry from his whiskers. "He did not say the other pies would fall too."

"And he made me the getaway driver!" Fatty Coon wailed, his voice thick with pie and sorrow. "But there was nothing to get away _with_!"

They sat in miserable silence. The grand plan, which had seemed so brilliant in the moonlight, looked foolish and childish in the afternoon sun. They had caused a terrible mess and had nothing to show for it but stained fur.

Suddenly, from the branches of the tall pine tree above them, came a sound that made them all freeze.

It was a loud, mocking laugh. A `_haw-haw-haw_` that was both dreaded and deeply familiar.

Slowly, they all looked up. And there, perched on a branch and looking down at them with a scornful eye, was old Mr. Crow. He was back.

"Well, well, well," he cawed, fluttering down to a lower limb. "What have we here? It looks like a party for clowns. Did you decide to paint yourselves for the fair?"

No one said a word. They just stared, their sticky faces a mixture of shock and guilt.

"I have been watching you from the border of the woods," Mr. Crow continued, enjoying himself immensely. "The Great Pie Heist, I believe it was called? Led by that military genius, Jasper Jay?" He scanned the crowd. "Ah, I see the brave general has already fled the battlefield."

He hopped along the branch, peering down at Fatty Coon. "Trying to eat a whole table at once, were you, Fatty? A bold strategy. How did that work out for you?"

Fatty Coon just whimpered.

Mr. Crow turned his sharp gaze on Sandy Chipmunk. "And you, Sandy. I thought you had learned your lesson about grand plans. Yet here you are, covered in what I presume was supposed to be a 'Prize Pie'."

Sandy hung his head. He had no answer.

"Do you fools have any idea what you have done?" Mr. Crow demanded, his voice suddenly sharp. "You have ruined the County Fair pie contest. But more importantly, you have once again shown that you cannot think for yourselves for five minutes without a proper leader."

He puffed out his chest, his authority returning to him with every word.

"There is no such thing as a 'Great Pie of Plenty'," he declared. "There are no pies that make you king, or grant you wishes. A 'prize pie' is a pie that _wins_ a prize. A prize like a blue ribbon! The _honor_ is the prize, you numbskulls! The honor of having baked the best pie!"

The animals looked at each other. A blue ribbon. It was so simple, and so much less exciting than what they had imagined. They felt more foolish than ever.

"You see," Mr. Crow cawed, settling his feathers. "This is what happens when I go away. Chaos. Anarchy. Sticky fur. You need me to interpret the ways of the human world for you."

Just then, a small figure appeared at the edge of the clearing. It was Johnnie Green, carrying a bucket. He looked sad. He did not see the animals hiding in the shadows. He walked to a log, sat down, and sighed. He began scraping the ruined contents of the pie tent--crusts, fillings, and all--from a dusty sheet into his bucket.

Mr. Crow watched him for a moment. Then he looked at the dejected animals.

"Well," he said, in a slightly softer tone. "It seems your foolishness may have a silver lining after all. Or a blackberry lining, in this case."

When Johnnie Green had finished his sad task and walked away, Mr. Crow nodded toward the log. "Go on," he said. "It appears your feast is waiting. A bit messy, perhaps, but a feast nonetheless."

Hesitantly at first, and then with more courage, the animals crept out. On the log, Johnnie had spilled a great deal of the pie scraps. There was a little of everything.

They ate in silence, not with the joy of victors, but with the quiet thankfulness of creatures who had been given a lucky break.

Sandy Chipmunk nibbled a piece of buttery crust. It was delicious. But as he ate, he looked up at Mr. Crow, who was watching them with his head cocked. It was good to have the old rascal back, Sandy thought. It was true that he was bossy and arrogant. But it was also true that life in the woods was much more interesting--and much less likely to end in a sticky disaster--when Mr. Crow was around to explain things. Even if his explanations were not always quite right either.

For a few days after the adventure with the letter, the woods were very quiet. Sandy Chipmunk kept mostly to himself, busily storing the last of the autumn nuts in his burrow. He was still quite cross with Mr. Crow for tricking him into throwing away his special letter, even if it had pictures of other people in it. The letter had been _his_, and that was what mattered.

But it is very hard for a chipmunk to stay cross for long, especially when the sun is shining and there is a whole world to explore. So, one bright afternoon, Sandy decided he had stored enough nuts for one day. He scampered up the trunk of the very tallest hickory tree he could find, just to see what he could see.

From his high perch, the whole of Pleasant Valley spread out below him. He could see the woods, the winding ribbon of Swift River, and the neat squares of Farmer Green's fields. It was while he was gazing at the big meadow behind the barn that he saw it.

A flash of brilliant color soared high in the air. It was red and blue and yellow, like a flower that had learned to fly. It dipped and swerved and danced against the blue sky, with a long, fluttering tail trailing behind it. Sandy Chipmunk blinked his eyes. He had never seen such a bird before. It was not a bird, he decided. It was too flat, and it moved too strangely. And what was more, it seemed to be held by a very, very long, thin string that stretched all the way down to the ground. And at the end of the string was Johnnie Green.

Sandy's heart beat fast with excitement. Johnnie Green had captured a piece of the rainbow! Or perhaps it was a special kind of star that could come out in the daytime. Whatever it was, it was the most wonderful thing Sandy had ever seen.

He scrambled down the tree so fast he almost tumbled head over heels. He had to tell somebody! The very first person he met was Frisky Squirrel, who was trying to balance a large acorn on a fence post.

"Frisky! Frisky! You'll never guess what I saw!" Sandy chattered, quite out of breath. "Johnnie Green has a flying flower! It's tied to a string, and it dances in the sky!"

Frisky Squirrel dropped his acorn in surprise. "A flying flower?" he repeated. "Are you sure it wasn't a big butterfly?"

"Oh, it was much bigger than any butterfly!" Sandy insisted. "And it was made of paper, I think! And it had a long tail made of little bows!"

Frisky's eyes grew wide. A flying paper flower with a tail of bows! That was certainly something to talk about. He forgot all about his acorn and scurried away to find his cousin. "Did you hear?" he said to his cousin. "Johnnie Green has caught a magic creature made of paper! It flies without flapping its wings, and it's tied down so it can't get back to the sky-country it came from!"

His cousin told Fatty Coon. And Fatty Coon, who was always a little bit lazy and did not like to move about too much, told Mrs. Rabbit without even getting up. "There is a great monster in the sky above Farmer Green's meadow," he said with a yawn. "It is red and blue and has a tail a mile long. Johnnie Green is holding it with a magic rope, but if it breaks free, it will surely eat us all!"

Mrs. Rabbit was terribly frightened. She gathered her twenty children and hopped as fast as she could to warn Jasper Jay. "Jasper! Jasper!" she cried. "A terrible dragon is attacking the valley! It breathes fire of red and yellow, and its tail is whipping the clouds to pieces! Farmer Green's boy is trying to fight it, but he is sure to be eaten! We must all hide!"

Of course, Jasper Jay was delighted to hear such exciting news. He flew straight to the old pine tree where Mr. Crow was taking a nap.

"Mr. Crow! Wake up!" Jasper shrieked. "The world is ending! A sky-serpent is breathing fire on Farmer Green's meadow!"

Mr. Crow opened one eye. "A what?" he croaked irritably.

"A sky-serpent!" Jasper repeated, hopping from foot to foot. "Fatty Coon saw it! It is held by a single thread of magic, and Johnnie Green is going to be its supper!"

Mr. Crow sat up and shook his feathers. He was very annoyed to be woken up for such a foolish story. "Sky-serpent," he sneered. "Jasper Jay, you have more feathers than brains. There is no such thing."

"But everybody is saying so!" Jasper insisted.

"Then everybody is a fool," Mr. Crow stated flatly. "I suppose I must go and see this great 'monster' for myself. It is plain that you cannot be left alone for a single afternoon without inventing some nonsense."

With a great flapping of his black wings, Mr. Crow launched himself into the air and flew toward Farmer Green's meadow. The other animals, who had heard Jasper's shouting, peeked out from their hiding places to watch.

Mr. Crow circled high above the meadow. He saw Johnnie Green, and he saw the colorful object dancing on the end of its string. He watched for a long moment, his head cocked to one side. Then he flew back to the woods and landed on a high branch, looking very important.

All the little folk gathered below him. "Well?" squeaked Timmy Field Mouse. "Is it a dragon?"

Mr. Crow puffed out his chest. "Dragon! Nonsense!" he cawed loudly, so everyone could hear. "What you see is a highly scientific instrument. It is called an _Aerodynamic Weather-Leaf_."

The animals all looked at one another, very impressed. Aerodynamic Weather-Leaf. It sounded very grand and important.

"What is it for?" asked Sandy Chipmunk, who had pushed his way to the front.

"It is for," Mr. Crow announced, "testing the air. Humans cannot fly, you see. So they send up these Weather-Leaves to find out what the wind is doing. The long tail measures the wind's speed, and the colors tell them if the air is 'healthy' or 'unhealthy'."

"Oh!" said all the animals together.

"It is a very delicate and expensive piece of equipment," Mr. Crow continued, enjoying the attention. "And you are all to stay away from it. If you were to disturb it, you might break the wind, so to speak. And that would be a catastrophe." He glared particularly at Sandy Chipmunk. "No one is to go near it. Is that understood? It is a matter of _science_."

For the rest of the afternoon, the animals kept a respectful distance, watching the wonderful Weather-Leaf. But Sandy Chipmunk could not get it out of his head. He did not believe it was just for "testing the air." It looked too happy for that. It looked like it was playing.

Later, the wind began to grow stronger. The Weather-Leaf pulled and tugged, and suddenly, with a great swoop, it dove straight into the branches of a tall maple tree at the edge of the woods and did not come out.

Johnnie Green pulled on the string, but the Weather-Leaf was stuck fast. He pulled and pulled, but it would not move. At last, looking very sad, he dropped the string and ran back toward the farmhouse, no doubt to get help.

The woods grew quiet. The Weather-Leaf was trapped in the tree.

"Now is our chance!" whispered Frisky Squirrel in Sandy's ear.

"But Mr. Crow said we must not go near it," Sandy replied, though he wanted to very much.

"Mr. Crow is not here," Frisky pointed out. "And besides, the poor thing is caught. It is our duty to help it. What if it is frightened?"

Frisky's argument was all the encouragement Sandy needed. Helping a trapped creature was a noble thing to do! Mr. Crow could not be angry about that. Together, the two friends scurried across the meadow to the tall maple tree.

They climbed up, branch by branch, until they were right beside the trapped Weather-Leaf. It was even more beautiful up close. It was made of thin paper stretched over a frame of sticks, and the tail of little cloth bows hung down through the leaves below them.

"It is not a creature at all!" Sandy exclaimed in a hushed voice, touching the paper skin. "It is a thing! Just a thing made of paper and sticks!"

"Mr. Crow was right," Frisky said, looking a little disappointed. "It is a science instrument." He scurried out onto the branch where the Weather-Leaf was tangled. "But it is still stuck. We should free it. Then the wind can have it back."

"The string is what is holding it," Sandy observed. "Johnnie Green tied it up so it could not fly away forever. If I chew through the string, it will be free."

"That is a fine idea!" Frisky chirped. "I will help untangle its tail."

While Frisky scampered down to bat at the cloth bows, Sandy set his sharp little teeth to work on the strong cord. It was tougher than a hickory nut shell, but Sandy was determined. He was going to set the beautiful thing free. He gnawed and gnawed, and the strands began to snap.

_Twang!_

With a sudden lurch, the string broke. At that very same moment, a powerful gust of wind roared through the treetop. It caught the Weather-Leaf and ripped it from the branches with a loud _WHOOSH_.

"I did it!" Sandy cried triumphantly. "It's free!"

But his triumph turned to terror. For Frisky Squirrel, in his efforts to untangle the tail, had gotten himself tangled up instead. One of the cloth bows had wrapped itself tight around his leg!

When the Weather-Leaf shot into the sky, it took Frisky Squirrel with it.

"Help!" shrieked Frisky, his little voice faint and high.

Sandy watched in horror as his best friend, clinging desperately to the long tail, was swept up and away, sailing over the tops of the trees and growing smaller and smaller against the vast blue sky.

For a moment, Sandy Chipmunk could not move. He was frozen to the branch. He had freed the Weather-Leaf, but he had sent his friend flying away with it. What had he done?

Panic seized him. He scrambled down the tree, his claws slipping on the bark. He ran through the woods, crying out Frisky's name, but there was no answer. There was only the rustle of the leaves and the sound of his own terrified heart.

There was only one person who could help. Swallowing his pride and his fear, Sandy ran as fast as his legs could carry him toward the old pine tree where he had last seen Mr. Crow.

He found the old crow preening his feathers, looking very satisfied with his day's work of explaining science to the ignorant.

"Mr. Crow! Mr. Crow!" Sandy sobbed, skidding to a halt at the bottom of the tree.

Mr. Crow looked down his beak. "What is it now, Sandy Chipmunk? Has another sky-serpent appeared?"

"It's worse! Much worse!" Sandy wailed. "It's Frisky! He's gone!"

"Gone?" Mr. Crow repeated. "Gone where? Did he fall in the river?"

"No! He flew away!" Sandy cried.

Mr. Crow stared. "Flew away? Sandy, squirrels do not fly. That is a biological fact. You chipmunks do not fly either, though you seem to spend a great deal of time falling out of trees."

"But he did!" Sandy insisted. "On the Weather-Leaf! I chewed the string to set it free, and the tail was caught on his leg, and the wind took him away!"

The smug look vanished from Mr. Crow's face. His feathers seemed to droop. He stared at Sandy, and his black eyes were hard as stones. "You... did... _what_?" he said in a low, dangerous voice.

Sandy trembled and told him the whole story. He told him how they had climbed the tree and how he had gnawed the string. When he had finished, there was a long, dreadful silence.

"Of all the brainless, idiotic, feather-headed numbskulls!" Mr. Crow finally exploded, his voice like thunder. "I told you! I _warned_ you! I said it was a delicate instrument! I told you not to meddle! And what do you do? You climb up and _chew_ on it! And now you have mailed Frisky Squirrel to the moon!"

Tears rolled down Sandy's nose. "I was only trying to help!" he whimpered.

"You were only trying to cause trouble, as usual!" Mr. Crow snapped. But then he seemed to sag a little. He looked out across the treetops in the direction the Weather-Leaf had gone. He let out a long, weary caw. "There is no time for scolding now," he said grimly. "That foolish squirrel could be anywhere."

He turned his sharp gaze back to Sandy. "Which way did it go? Point!"

Sandy pointed a trembling paw. "That way! Over the big oaks!"

"Stay here," Mr. Crow commanded. "And do not touch anything else!"

With a powerful beat of his wings, he rose into the air and became a black speck against the sky. Sandy huddled at the foot of the pine tree, feeling smaller and more miserable than he ever had in his life. He had never seen Mr. Crow so angry. But he had also never seen him fly with such purpose.

The minutes crawled by like caterpillars. The sun began to sink lower, painting the sky in shades of orange and pink. Sandy shivered, thinking of Frisky, all alone, tangled in that terrible Weather-Leaf, somewhere in the deep, dark woods. It was all his fault.

Just when he was about to give up all hope, he heard a loud _CAW! CAW!_ from far away. He looked up and saw Mr. Crow circling in the distance. Then the crow flew back toward him, landing heavily on a branch above his head.

"Well," Mr. Crow said, breathing hard. "I have found him."

Sandy jumped to his feet. "Is he all right? Is he hurt?"

"He is more tangled than a ball of yarn after a kitten has played with it, but he appears to be in one piece," Mr. Crow reported. "The Weather-Leaf, as I predicted, was not built for cross-country travel. It has crashed into the top of the Great Pine on Whisper Ridge."

"The Great Pine!" Sandy gasped. That was the tallest tree in the whole forest, miles away.

"Yes," said Mr. Crow. "And you, Sandy Chipmunk, are going to come with me and help get him down. It is the least you can do, since this is entirely your fault." He did not wait for a reply but launched himself into the air again. "Follow me! And try to keep up!"

Sandy did not need to be told twice. He ran as he had never run before, scrambling over logs and under ferns, his eyes fixed on the black shape of Mr. Crow flying ahead, guiding him through the deepening twilight. It was a long and tiring journey, but Sandy did not think of his aching legs. He thought only of Frisky.

At last they reached Whisper Ridge. The Great Pine stood like a giant's spear against the darkening sky. And there, caught in its highest branches, was a flash of red and yellow. It was the Weather-Leaf, torn and tattered.

"Frisky!" Sandy called.

A weak little voice answered from high above. "Sandy? Is that you?"

"I'm here!" Sandy shouted back. "Mr. Crow found you!"

It took a long time, but with Mr. Crow directing from a nearby limb and Sandy calling encouragement from below, Frisky finally managed to untangle his leg and climb slowly and shakily down the enormous tree.

When he reached the ground, he looked very small and frightened. He ran straight to Sandy, and the two friends hugged each other tightly.

"I thought I would never see you again!" Frisky chattered, his teeth still clicking with fright. "It was awful! The wind just threw me about! And the ground was so far away!"

"I am so sorry, Frisky," Sandy said, his voice thick with guilt. "It is all my fault."

Mr. Crow flew down and landed on a low stump, folding his wings with an air of great authority. He looked at the two of them.

"Well," he said, his voice no longer angry, but very, very tired. "I hope you have both learned a valuable lesson."

Sandy and Frisky hung their heads. "Yes, Mr. Crow," they mumbled.

"The lesson," Mr. Crow declared, "is that human science is not a toy. And when someone older and wiser tells you to leave something alone, you would be wise to listen."

"But Frisky," Sandy said, turning to his friend. "What was it like? Up there?"

Frisky's eyes, which had been full of fear, suddenly got a little sparkle in them. "Oh, Sandy," he said. "It was terrible. But for just a minute, before I got too scared, it was... it was wonderful. I could see everything! I could see the whole world! I was flying, Sandy! I was really flying!"

Mr. Crow snorted. "You were not flying. You were falling in a very uncontrolled manner. There is a difference."

But Sandy Chipmunk saw the look in Frisky's eyes. And as they all started the long walk home under the stars, with Mr. Crow complaining about the foolishness of youth every step of the way, Sandy thought that perhaps, just perhaps, it had been worth it. He had almost lost his best friend, and he had made a terrible mistake. But Frisky Squirrel was the first and only squirrel in Pleasant Valley who had ever danced with the wind. And that, Sandy decided, was a story they would tell for a very long time.

Frisky Squirrel stared at the paper. An advertisement! All that trouble and shame, for an advertisement! He felt a giggle bubble up inside him.

"So Sandy was not wrong to think the picture was for him," Frisky said. "But Mr. Crow was very wrong to make him feel ashamed."

"Pride is a tricky thing," Old Man Owl said wisely. "And jealousy is its troublesome cousin. They can make a mountain out of a molehill, or a scolding letter out of a seed advertisement. You would do well to take this back to your friend Sandy. It will ease his mind."

"Oh, I will!" said Frisky. "Thank you, Mr. Owl! Thank you!"

And he snatched up the paper and raced away, leaving Old Man Owl to settle back into his log for the rest of his daytime nap.

Frisky found Sandy moping by the edge of a brook, staring sadly into the water.

"Sandy!" Frisky called. "I have news! Wonderful news!"

Sandy Chipmunk did not even look up. "If it is about that letter, I do not want to hear it," he said glumly. "Mr. Crow was right. I was a fool."

"No, he was not right!" Frisky chattered, spreading the paper out on the ground. "He was wrong! Completely and utterly wrong! Old Man Owl told me so!"

And then Frisky explained all that the wise owl had told him. He pointed to the pictures and told Sandy about the 'Forest Friends Winter Mix' and how it was not a letter at all.

As Sandy listened, his drooping ears began to perk up. His long face began to brighten. He looked at the picture of the handsome chipmunk, and then at the picture of the squirrel, and he understood. It was not a letter filled with other people. It was a page showing happy friends!

A little smile appeared on his face. Then it grew into a grin. "An advertisement!" he exclaimed. "For seeds!"

"Yes!" Frisky squeaked. "Mr. Crow was just jealous because he did not get one!"

Suddenly, all the unhappiness that had been sitting like a stone in Sandy's chest vanished. He felt so light and happy he thought he might float away. He began to laugh. And Frisky, seeing his friend so happy, began to laugh too. They giggled and chattered until they were both rolling on the grass.

"Well!" Sandy said, when he finally caught his breath. "Is that not the most ridiculous thing you have ever heard?"

"Mr. Crow will feel very silly when he finds out," Frisky said, wiping a tear of laughter from his eye.

"Perhaps we should not tell him," Sandy said, feeling generous in his relief. "He might be embarrassed."

But they both knew that news in Pleasant Valley traveled faster than a hawk diving for its dinner. And they knew it would not be long before Mr. Crow heard the real story of Sandy Chipmunk's "letter."

XIII A STRANGE NEW FLOWER

For a few days, all was peaceful in Pleasant Valley. Sandy Chipmunk was busy gathering the last of the beechnuts for his winter store. He worked so hard that he barely had time to think about mail-boxes or advertisements. And whenever he saw Mr. Crow, the old bird would simply give a loud _harrumph_ and fly in the other direction, which suited Sandy just fine.

One bright, crisp morning, Sandy was scurrying along the top of the stone wall that ran between Farmer Green's fields. The air was cool and smelled of frost and fallen leaves. He was on his way to a special hickory tree he knew, when he saw something that made him stop in his tracks.

There, in the middle of the pasture, not far from the wall, was a strange new flower. It was bigger than any flower Sandy had ever seen. It was bright, bright red, and it stood on a single, thin, green stem. But the oddest thing about it was its shape. It did not have soft petals like a daisy or a buttercup. Instead, it was made of many little spinning wings. And as the wind blew across the field, the little wings whirled around and around with a soft, whizzing sound.

Sandy stared, his whiskers twitching with curiosity. He had never seen such a thing. Was it good to eat? Was it dangerous? He was a cautious chipmunk, and he remembered what

his mother had told him about the pretty red berries that looked so tasty but were really very bad for a chipmunk's stomach. No, it was better to be safe than sorry.

He scurried along the wall until he was out of sight of the strange red flower. But he could not get it out of his mind. Its bright color and whizzing sound seemed to follow him all the way to the hickory tree. He gathered a few nuts, his cheeks puffed out full, but his heart was not in his work. He had to tell someone.

The first person he looked for was Frisky Squirrel. He found him chasing his tail in a circle at the foot of a big maple tree.

"Frisky!" Sandy chattered, after he had emptied the nuts from his cheeks into a little hole. "You will not believe what I have seen!"

Frisky Squirrel stopped so suddenly he tumbled over himself. "Is it a new kind of nut?" he asked eagerly. "Is it bigger than a walnut? Is it sweeter than a pecan?"

"It is not a nut at all," Sandy said, his eyes wide. "It is a flower. A giant red flower in Farmer Green's pasture. And it spins!"

"Spins!" Frisky repeated, his own eyes growing as round as acorns. "A spinning flower? What do you mean, it spins?"

"It has little wings instead of petals," Sandy explained. "And when the wind blows, they all whirl around. It makes a sound like a tiny bee, _whizz-whizz-whizz_."

Frisky Squirrel's tail twitched with excitement. "A magic flower!" he declared. "It must be a magic flower! Perhaps if you touch it, it grants wishes! Did you wish for anything, Sandy?"

"I did not go near it," Sandy confessed. "I was afraid it might be a trap. It looked too strange to be friendly."

"Nonsense!" said Frisky. "A flower cannot be a trap. We must go and see it at once! And I will wish for a tree that grows nothing but hazelnuts all year round!"

And so, Frisky Squirrel scampered off towards the pasture, with Sandy following close behind, feeling both nervous and excited.

They were not the only ones who saw the strange flower that day. Fatty Coon, who had been taking a late morning nap in a hollow log, poked his head out and saw the flash of red. He blinked his sleepy eyes.

"Now what has Farmer Green planted?" he mumbled to himself. "It looks like a big piece of rock candy on a stick. But I do not suppose it is." Fatty Coon was very wise about things that could be eaten, and he knew that things that looked like candy in Farmer Green's fields were usually not candy at all. Still, he waddled a little closer, his black nose twitching, sniffing the air for any hint of a sugary smell.

The news, as news does in Pleasant Valley, began to travel. Frisky Squirrel, in his excitement, told Chatterer the Red Squirrel that there was a magic wishing-flower in the field that could make hazelnuts grow on pine trees. Chatterer, who was always looking for an argument, told Mrs. Mouse that Farmer Green had invented a new kind of scarecrow, one that spun around to frighten squirrels. And Mrs. Mouse told her twenty-two children that there was a dangerous spinning thing in the field and they must not go near it, which of course made them all want to see it more than anything else in the world.

By midday, there was a small crowd of onlookers peeking from behind the stone wall and from the branches of the nearby trees. And it was not long before all the chattering and whispering reached the ears of old Mr. Crow, who was sitting on the highest branch of a dead chestnut tree, pretending he was not interested in the affairs of smaller folk.

He had been feeling quite grumpy ever since the matter of the "letter." Everyone now knew it had been an advertisement for seeds, and though no one said anything to his face, he could feel them smiling behind their paws and wings. He felt his reputation for wisdom had been slightly damaged.

"What is all this commotion?" he called down in his loudest, most important-sounding voice.

Jasper Jay, who loved to be the bearer of news, flew up to Mr. Crow's branch at once. "Oh, Mr. Crow!" he squawked. "It is the most terrible thing! Farmer Green has put a spinning monster in the pasture! It has red wings, and it hums, and Frisky Squirrel says it will turn us all into pumpkins if we get too close!"

Mr. Crow looked down at the chattering crowd and then at the little red object spinning cheerfully in the breeze. He had seen Johnnie Green playing with just such a thing yesterday. But he was not about to say so. Here was a perfect opportunity to show everyone how much he knew.

He gave a great _haw-haw_ that made Jasper Jay jump. "A monster!" he sneered. "Pumpkins! You are all as foolish as a flock of sheep. Do you not know what that is?"

All the little animals fell silent and looked up at Mr. Crow. Even Sandy and Frisky stopped whispering.

"That," Mr. Crow announced, puffing out his chest, "is a highly scientific instrument. It is what is known as a _Farmer's Farsight_."

There were murmurs of awe from the crowd below. "A Farmer's Farsight," they repeated to one another. It sounded very important indeed.

"What does it do?" asked Sandy Chipmunk, who was always polite, even when he was doubtful.

"Do?" Mr. Crow said crossly, as if it were a stupid question. "It does a great many things. It measures the turning of the world. And it listens to the wind to find out if it is coming from the north or the south. And by the speed of its spinning, Farmer Green can tell if the winter will be long and hard, or short and mild."

"Oh!" said Frisky Squirrel, completely forgetting his magic wishing-flower. "It is spinning very fast right now! Does that mean the winter will be very, very hard?"

"Naturally," Mr. Crow said with great authority. "It is spinning with what we scientists call 'maximum velocity'. That indicates a winter of unprecedented severity. There will be snow so deep it will cover the tallest trees!"

A wave of fear went through the little crowd. Mrs. Mouse gathered her children closer. Fatty Coon began to worry if he had stored enough acorns. Sandy Chipmunk looked at the little spinning flower and felt a shiver that had nothing to do with the cool autumn breeze.

"Furthermore," Mr. Crow went on, enjoying his position as the center of attention, "when it stops spinning, that is the most important signal of all. A complete stop means that a great change is coming. Farmer Green is no doubt watching it this very moment from his window."

For the rest of the day, the Farmer's Farsight was the only thing anyone in Pleasant Valley could talk about. The animals took turns watching it from the safety of the wall.

"It is slowing down!" Chatterer the Red Squirrel would cry. "The great change is coming!"

But then a little gust of wind would come along, and the red wings would whir faster than ever.

"The change has been postponed!" Frisky would announce with relief. "The world will keep turning for now!"

Sandy Chipmunk watched with the others. But he was a practical fellow. And he noticed something that no one else seemed to mention. He noticed that when he stood behind the big oak tree where no wind could touch him, he felt no breeze at all, and the Farmer's Farsight seemed to spin more slowly. But when he scurried along the top of the wall, he could feel the wind on his whiskers, and at the very same moment, the red wings would begin to spin faster.

"That is queer," he said to himself. "It seems to spin only when the wind blows." He looked up at Mr. Crow, who was still perched on his high branch like a king on a throne. Sandy was going to ask him about it. But then he remembered the corn and the beechnuts, and the letter, and he decided to keep his thoughts to himself.

The sun began to sink lower in the sky, painting the clouds orange and purple. The wind died down, as it often did at the end of the day. And in the quiet, still air of the evening, the Farmer's Farsight stopped spinning altogether. It stood perfectly still on its thin green stem.

A hush fell over the crowd.

"It has stopped," whispered Frisky Squirrel, his voice trembling. "Mr. Crow was right! The great change is upon us!"

"The end of the world!" wailed one of Mrs. Mouse's children, and then they all started to cry.

Mr. Crow cleared his throat. "Ahem! As I predicted," he said, though he was beginning to feel a little nervous himself. He had not expected it to stop so completely. "This is a sign of great importance. A very great importance indeed."

Just then, a sound came from the direction of the farmhouse. It was the sound of a screen door banging shut. And then a boy's voice was heard, calling out, "I'm going to get my pinwheel, Ma! The wind's gone!"

It was Johnnie Green. He came running across the yard and into the pasture. He did not seem to notice the dozens of little eyes watching him from the wall. He ran right up to the Farmer's Farsight.

The animals held their breath. What would he do? Was he going to read the message of the great change?

Johnnie Green reached down, and with a quick pull, he plucked the strange red flower right out of the ground. He held it up to his mouth. And he blew.

_Whirrrrrrrrr!_

The red wings spun faster than they had all day, making a loud, satisfying zzzzing sound. Johnnie laughed with delight. He ran across the pasture, holding the Farmer's Farsight in his hand, and the red wings spun and spun in the air he made by running.

Sandy Chipmunk stared. His mouth fell open. He looked at Frisky Squirrel, whose mouth was also open. He looked at Fatty Coon, who was peeking over the wall with a look of pure confusion on his round face.

The great scientific instrument... the Farmer's Farsight... the predictor of winter... was a toy. It was just a silly spinning thing that a boy played with. It was a _blow-flower_.

Sandy looked up to the high branch where Mr. Crow had been sitting. But the branch was empty. The old crow had seen Johnnie Green coming, and he had vanished without a sound.

A little giggle started in Sandy's chest. He tried to hold it back, but he could not. He thought of the deep snow that was supposed to cover the trees. He thought of the great change and the end of the world. And he let out a happy chatter of laughter.

Frisky Squirrel looked at Sandy, then at Johnnie Green running with the pinwheel, and then back at Sandy. A slow smile spread across his face. And then he began to laugh, too. Soon, all the little animals along the wall were chattering and squeaking and laughing. The scare was over. The monster was just a plaything.

"A Farmer's Farsight!" Frisky giggled, rolling on his back. "Oh, my! Mr. Crow will never live this down!"

"I almost feel sorry for him," Sandy said, wiping a tear of laughter from his eye. But he did not feel very sorry at all. He felt light and free. He felt as if a great, heavy winter had been lifted from his shoulders.

Later, as the last light of day faded, a loud, grumpy voice was heard from the top of the dead chestnut tree.

"Ahem!" the voice of Mr. Crow boomed, making them all jump. "I can see that the experiment was a complete success!"

Sandy and Frisky looked at each other.

"Experiment?" Sandy called up.

"Of course!" Mr. Crow said, though he was careful to stay high up in the tree. "Did you really think I believed that was a scientific instrument? Goodness me, no! I was conducting a psychological test. A test of your gullibility. To see if you would believe such a preposterous story. I must say, you all failed miserably. You should learn to think for yourselves, instead of believing every old crow that comes along."

And with a final, booming _haw-haw_ that did not sound very merry at all, he launched himself from the branch and flew away towards the dark woods, leaving the animals of Pleasant Valley to shake their heads in wonder.

"Well," said Sandy Chipmunk to Frisky Squirrel, "I suppose, in a way, he is right."

"He is?" Frisky asked, surprised.

"Yes," Sandy said thoughtfully. "I am never, ever going to believe another word that old crow says."

And he scampered back to the hickory tree to finish his work, feeling very wise indeed. The air was cool, the world was not ending, and there were still plenty of good nuts to gather before the real winter came.

Sandy Chipmunk worked from sunup to sundown, and his storehouse under the roots of the big hickory tree was growing fuller by the day. He had been right about Mr. Crow, and he felt a new sort of wisdom inside him. It was a quiet, comfortable feeling, much like a full belly on a cold day. XIII THE TALKING VINE One morning, while exploring along the stone wall that marked the edge of Farmer Green's meadow, Sandy Chipmunk came upon a most peculiar sight. A long, thin, gray vine was stretched between two of the fence posts. It was a very straight vine, not like a wild grapevine at all, and it hummed with a low thrumming sound when the wind blew. And at each end of the vine, dangling just above the ground, was a shiny cup. Sandy stopped a good ways off and looked at it. He twitched his nose. It did not smell like a trap. It did not smell like food. It did not smell like anything at all, except for the faint scent of the metal that made the shiny cups. He remembered the mail-box, and how he had been afraid of it at first. And he remembered the Farmer's Farsight, which had scared nearly everyone in Pleasant Valley. "I am too wise to be frightened by a vine and two cups," he said to himself. But he was also too wise, he thought, to go poking at it. So he sat on the top of the stone wall for a long time, just watching. After a while, Frisky Squirrel came leaping along the top of the wall. When he saw Sandy sitting so still, he stopped. "What is it?" Frisky whispered, his eyes wide. "Is it another monster?" "It is not a monster," Sandy said, with an air of great authority. "It is a vine. With cups." Frisky crept closer and peered at the strange new thing. "It looks like a spider-web for a giant," he chattered. "Maybe it's to catch Mr. Crow!" The thought of this made Frisky Squirrel giggle, and he had to cover his mouth with his paws. "It is not a spider-web," Sandy said patiently. "It is just a thing. I am waiting to see what it is _for_." They did not have to wait long. Soon, Johnnie Green came running out of the farmhouse. And with him was another boy from the village. They ran right up to the strange vine. Johnnie Green picked up one of the shiny cups, and the other boy picked up the other. Then, they ran in opposite directions, until the vine was stretched tight between them. Johnnie put the cup to his mouth and said something into it. The other boy held his cup to his ear and laughed. The animals were too far away to hear any words, but they could see it all quite clearly. "Why," Sandy Chipmunk exclaimed, "he is catching his voice in that cup!" "And the other boy is drinking it!" Frisky Squirrel added, his eyes like little black beads. They watched for a long time, as the two boys took turns catching their voices and listening to them. It was the most wonderful and mysterious thing they had ever seen. Soon, Johnnie and his friend ran off towards the barn, leaving the vine and the cups dangling on the fence posts. "I must tell Fatty Coon about this," Frisky said, all excitement. "A voice-catcher! Imagine that! Farmer Green must be the cleverest man in the world." And with a flick of his tail, he was gone, leaping from stone to stone. Sandy stayed behind. He was thinking. He did not believe the cup could really _catch_ a voice. That seemed silly. But something was happening. And he wanted to understand it. Just as he was about to creep closer for a better look, a dark shadow fell over him. A loud, grating voice boomed from the air. "_Ahem!_" Sandy jumped and looked up. There, on the highest branch of a nearby maple tree, sat old Mr. Crow. He was pretending to clean a feather in his wing, but Sandy could see he was watching the whole time. "Well, well," Mr. Crow said, looking down his beak. "Staring at things you do not understand, I see. As usual." Sandy Chipmunk bristled. "I understand it perfectly well," he said, though it was not entirely true. "It is a toy. For boys." "A toy!" Mr. Crow sneered, and he let out a short, sharp _haw_. "A toy! You poor, simple-minded little creature. Do you have sawdust for brains? That is no toy. That is one of the most dangerous devices in the human world." He flew down to a lower branch, so he could speak in a more serious and frightening tone. "That," he said, leaning forward, "is a _Voice-Snatcher_." "A Voice-Snatcher?" Sandy repeated, in spite of himself. He felt a little thrill of fear run down his back. "Precisely," said Mr. Crow, puffing out his chest. "Farmer Green is a very cunning man. He sets it up, and when some unsuspecting creature--like a bird with a pretty song, or a chipmunk with a cheerful chatter--comes by and makes a noise, the Voice-Snatcher sucks the sound right out of you. It stores the sounds in those cups. Then, at the end of the week, he takes the cups to the village and sells your voices. That is why you hear so little singing in the village. All the voices are locked up in boxes on shelves." The story was so terrible and so detailed that for a moment, Sandy almost believed it. He thought of his own cheerful chatter. Would he want to live in a world where he could not make a sound? But then he remembered. He remembered the pinwheel. He remembered his vow. "That is not true," Sandy said, and his voice was surprisingly steady. "I saw the boys playing with it. They were laughing. They were putting their own voices into it." Mr. Crow looked furious. It was one thing to be wrong; it was another thing entirely to be doubted by a chipmunk. "Playing!" he scoffed. "They were practicing! To make sure it was working! You have seen nothing, and you understand less. I, who have flown over this valley for many, many winters, know these things. It is my duty to warn the foolish and the ignorant." By this time, Frisky Squirrel had returned, and with him were Fatty Coon and Peter Rabbit and several others. They had heard Mr. Crow's booming voice and had come to see what the matter was. "What is it, Mr. Crow?" Peter Rabbit asked timidly. "What new danger is there?" Mr. Crow ignored Sandy and addressed the growing crowd. "I have just been explaining to our--ahem--_stupid_ little friend here," he said, with a nasty look at Sandy, "that this new device is a Voice-Snatcher. It will steal your very breath if you are not careful." A murmur of fear went through the little crowd of animals. Fatty Coon's eyes grew wide, and he took a step back from the fence. Frisky Squirrel, who had been so bold a moment before, now hid behind a large stone. But Sandy Chipmunk stood his ground. "He is making it up," Sandy said loudly, so they all could hear. "It's just another one of his stories. Like the Farmer's Farsight that was going to end the world." Mr. Crow turned a terrible shade of purple, which is very difficult for a black crow to do. He hopped up and down on his branch with rage. "Making it up!" he squawked. "Making it up! I, who have saved you all from countless dangers with my superior intellect! How dare you! This is not a story! It is--it is--science!" He stopped, searching for a more impressive word. "It is not a Voice-Snatcher," he said, changing his story in an instant. "I was using a simple word for your simple minds. Its real name is a _Tele-thoughtic Conductor_." The animals all stared blankly. "It does not steal your voice," Mr. Crow explained, calming down now that he had their attention again. "It does something far more amazing. It carries your _thoughts_. You put your head in the cup at one end, and you think a thought. And your thought travels along the vine--the conductor--and whoever is at the other end receives your thought directly in his brain. It is a way of speaking without making a sound." This new story was even more amazing than the first. A Tele-thoughtic Conductor! To send thoughts! "Prove it," said Sandy Chipmunk. The words were out of his mouth before he could stop them. Mr. Crow smiled a slow, cruel smile. "An excellent idea," he said. "A perfect scientific demonstration. You, little doubter, shall be the one to send the thought. And I, with my highly developed mind, shall be the one to receive it." Sandy's heart began to beat a little faster. This was not what he had intended at all. But all the other animals were looking at him. He could not back down now. "Very well," he said. "Go to the other end." Mr. Crow chuckled. "This will be most instructive," he said. He flew to the far fence post and picked up the shiny cup in his beak. He held it carefully over his head, so that the opening was near where his ear would be, if he had an ear on the outside of his head. "Now!" he called. "Think of something! Think hard! Send me a thought!" Sandy looked at the other cup, which dangled near the stone wall. He was very nervous. What if Mr. Crow was right? What if this thing really did send thoughts? He crept forward and cautiously poked his head into the cool, metal cup. It smelled of tin and of Johnnie Green's hands. "Think of something everyone knows!" Frisky Squirrel whispered excitedly. "Think of an acorn!" Sandy thought that was a good idea. It was simple. He closed his eyes. He pictured the biggest, fattest acorn he had found all week. He pictured its smooth cap and its perfect, brown shell. He thought about it as hard as he could. For a full minute, there was silence in the meadow. All the animals held their breath. Then, from the far end of the vine, Mr. Crow shouted. "_Aha!_" he boomed. "The thought has arrived! It traveled the conductor perfectly! You are thinking," he declared, "of an _acorn_!" A great gasp went up from the crowd. Frisky Squirrel did a little somersault of joy. Fatty Coon clapped his paws together. It was true! It worked! Mr. Crow was the wisest bird in the whole world! Sandy pulled his head out of the cup. He was confused. How could Mr. Crow have known? It was just a lucky guess. It had to be. An acorn was what almost any one of them would have thought of. "It was a lucky guess," Sandy said, but his voice was small. The other animals shushed him. "Don't be a sore loser, Sandy," said Peter Rabbit. "Mr. Crow was right." Mr. Crow strutted back and forth along his fence post. "A lucky guess?" he cawed. "There is no luck in science! Only facts! The Tele-thoughtic Conductor works. I have proven it. Are there any _more_ doubts?" He looked directly at Sandy. Sandy knew he was being made a fool of. But he also knew, deep in his little chipmunk heart, that something was not right. "Yes," he said. "I have another doubt. Let us try it again." Mr. Crow sighed a long, dramatic sigh. "Very well," he said. "If we must waste the day proving simple facts to simpletons. But this time, you must think of something very, very clear. Send me another thought." Sandy nodded. He went back to the cup. But this time, he was not going to think of something obvious. He was not going to think of a nut, or a berry, or anything from the forest at all. He closed his eyes. He thought very, very hard. He thought about the letter he had once taken from the mail-box. He thought about the strange paper inside it. And he thought of the pictures on that paper--the picture of the Red-Squirrel boy, and the son of Mrs. Mouse, and the one that looked like Rinaldo Rat. It was a very complicated thought. It was a secret thought that only he and Mr. Crow shared. And Mr. Crow had been very angry about it. Surely, if this machine worked, Mr. Crow would receive this memory. Sandy concentrated until his head hurt. Then he pulled his head out of the cup and waited. At the other end, Mr. Crow was making a great show of receiving the thought. He staggered a little on the post. He put a wing to his forehead. "My, my," he said. "That is a powerful one! It is coming through... yes... yes, I have it!" He paused for dramatic effect. "You are thinking," he announced to the silent audience, "of a big, round, yellow... _pumpkin_!" For a second, no one moved. A pumpkin? Then Frisky Squirrel looked towards Farmer Green's garden, where a whole field of big, round, yellow pumpkins was growing. "Of course!" Frisky chattered. "A pumpkin! What a good thought, Sandy!" But Sandy Chipmunk was not looking at the pumpkins. He was looking at Mr. Crow. And he was smiling. "No, Mr. Crow," Sandy said, and his voice was clear and strong and happy. "That is not what I was thinking of at all." Mr. Crow froze. "What?" he squawked. "Of course it was! I received it! A pumpkin! Very large!" "No," Sandy said again. "I was thinking of the letter I got from the mail-box. The one with my picture on it. And the pictures of the other little animals inside. The one you told me to throw away because you were jealous." A terrible silence fell upon the fence post. Mr. Crow's beak opened and closed, but no sound came out. He looked at Sandy. He looked at the other animals, who were now looking at him with suspicion. He had been caught. Just at that moment, Farmer Green came walking out of the barn. He saw the string and the cans on his fence. "Ah," he said to himself with a smile. "Johnnie left his telephone out again." He walked over to the fence post where Sandy had been. He picked up the shiny cup and held it to his ear. Then he called out, in a joking voice, "Hello? Hello? Is anybody there?" From the other end of the meadow, Johnnie Green, who had just come out of the house, heard his father. He ran to the other fence post and picked up the other cup. He put it to his mouth. All eyes turned to the maple tree. But the high branch where Mr. Crow had been sitting was empty. He had vanished, as silent as a shadow, as soon as he heard Farmer Green's voice. There was a moment of silence. And then Frisky Squirrel started to giggle. "A pumpkin!" he squeaked, rolling over on the wall. "He said you were thinking of a pumpkin!" Soon Fatty Coon was chuckling, and Peter Rabbit was thumping his foot in amusement. Sandy Chipmunk felt a warm, happy feeling spread through his chest. He had done it. He had trusted himself, and he had been right. He had faced down Mr. Crow, and he had won. A loud, indignant squawk came from the very top of the tallest tree in the woods. "IT WAS A TEST!" the voice of Mr. Crow boomed across the valley. "A test of logic! I put forth two incorrect theories--the Voice-Snatcher and the Tele-thoughtic Conductor--to see if any of you had the intelligence to arrive at the correct solution! Only Sandy showed any promise! The rest of you are a profound disappointment! A profound disappointment!" And with a final, angry _haw-haw-haw!_ that echoed over the fields, he was truly gone. "Well," said Frisky Squirrel, wiping a tear of laughter from his eye. "I suppose, in a way, he is right." "He is?" Sandy asked, surprised. "Yes," said Frisky. "I am profoundly disappointed... that I ever believed him!" Sandy Chipmunk chattered with laughter. He felt very wise indeed. The autumn sun was warm on his back, his storehouse was nearly full, and he had learned the most important lesson of all: that the loudest voice in the forest is not always the one that tells the truth. And with a light heart, he scampered back to the hickory tree, ready to finish his work for the winter.
