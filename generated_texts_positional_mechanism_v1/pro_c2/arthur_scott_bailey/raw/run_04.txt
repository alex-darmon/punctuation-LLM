I
THE RED TREASURE

Pip was a very young field mouse, and he believed the whole world was made just for him. So when he found the strange, wonderful object lying in the garden, he felt sure it was a special gift. It was round and fat at the top, a brilliant red, with a cheerful yellow stripe that wound down to a tiny metal point. It was bigger than the largest strawberry and much prettier.
"What a prize!" Pip squeaked to a ladybug climbing a blade of grass. "I'll have this in my pantry before the sun is high. The other mice will wish they had my luck!"
He pushed his nose against his treasure. It did not move. He set his shoulder to it and heaved with all his might. The great red thing tipped, wobbled dizzily, and then settled right back where it had been.
"Humph!" Pip grunted, shaking his head. "You are a stubborn one." He backed away to get a running start and charged forward, bumping his head soundly against the smooth, painted wood.
The treasure wobbled again, but this time it began to spin in a wild circle, making a soft whirring sound.
"Goodness me!" Pip cried, jumping back in alarm. "It's alive! It's an angry beetle!"
A low chuckle rumbled from a nearby clump of hosta leaves. "It is no more alive than that watering can, little fellow."
Pip peered into the cool shadows and saw the broad, striped face of Barnaby the badger. Barnaby was very old; he had seen many seasons come and go in the garden.
"It moved all by itself!" Pip insisted, his voice trembling a little. "I saw it! And it hummed at me!"
"You are the one who made it move," Barnaby said patiently. "It is a human child's plaything. A spinning top."
"A plaything?" Pip repeated, his nose wrinkling with disbelief. "It looks like a new kind of apple. A much sweeter kind, I'm sure."
"It would give you a terrible toothache," the old badger declared. "And a tummy full of splinters, besides. That shiny red coat is only paint. It is nothing but wood underneath."
"I think," Pip said loftily, "that you are mistaken. You are too slow to find such treasures, so you do not believe they exist. I am quick and clever. I will have this prize in my nest soon enough!"
Barnaby the badger shook his great head slowly. "Some things are not what they seem, Pip. Be careful you do not waste your whole day on a painted block of wood." And with that, he turned and ambled away toward his cozy burrow under the big oak tree.

II
A WASTED DAY

"He is old and overly cautious," Pip muttered to himself. He was determined to prove the badger wrong. He pushed the top again. It spun away and bumped into a stone. He nudged it from another side. It whirred and danced sideways, landing in a patch of soft moss. For the rest of the morning, Pip chased the red treasure all over the garden bed. He was growing very tired, and his nose was sore from bumping it.
"This is the most difficult fruit I have ever tried to gather!" he panted.
A little chickadee with a black cap landed on a tomato stake above him. "Chee-dee-dee! What a game you are playing!" the bird chirped. "What are you doing, Pip?"
"I am trying to take this fine fruit home," Pip said, trying to sound as if he were having no trouble at all. "It is very fine, but very tricky."
The chickadee tilted its head. "Fruit?" it peeped. "Why, that is not fruit! That is little Willow's whirly-top! She dropped it yesterday and was ever so sad. It is no good for eating!"
Pip's whiskers drooped. He looked at the spinning top, then at the chickadee, and suddenly he felt very foolish. "Oh!" he said in a small voice. "A whirly-top."
He sat down on a clod of dirt and thought for a moment. Barnaby had been right all along. And Pip had been quite rude to him. With a sigh, he turned and trotted back across the garden until he came to the big oak tree. Barnaby was sitting at the entrance to his burrow, cleaning his paws.
"You were right, Barnaby," Pip said quietly, looking at the ground. "It was not a treasure. It was just a silly toy."
The old badger looked down at the little mouse and his stern face softened into a smile. "Ah!" he said gently. "But you have found a different sort of treasure to-day, have you not?"
Pip looked up, confused. "What do you mean?"
"A bit of wisdom," Barnaby explained. "And that is worth more than all the painted toys in the world. Now, run along home. The sun is getting low, and there are plenty of good, honest seeds waiting for you by the stone wall."
Pip felt a great deal better. "Thank you, Barnaby!" he squeaked. And he scampered off, leaving the red top for little Willow to find. He had wasted most of his day, but he did not mind. As Barnaby had said, he was going home a little bit wiser than he had been when he left.

The smithy is a place for iron, not for tempers. Twinkleheels, you will do well to mind your own affairs. And you, madam," he said, turning his great head toward the mare, "would do well to remember that every horse was a colt once."
The mare grumbled something under her breath about "spoiled ponies," but she turned her head away and said no more.
Just then, the blacksmith came over to Ebenezer. He was a big man with a leather apron and arms as thick as tree branches. He ran a gentle hand down Ebenezer's leg and lifted his foot. Twinkleheels watched, his nervousness returning. The smith took a heavy tool and with a quick twist, pried the old, bent shoe from Ebenezer's hoof. He then took a rasp, which looked like a huge file, and began to smooth the edges of the hoof. A fine, dusty powder floated in the air.
"Does that not hurt?" Twinkleheels whispered to Ebenezer when the smith stepped away.
"Not at all," the old horse replied. "It is like having your fingernails trimmed. See? He is going to the forge now to make my new shoe ready."
Twinkleheels watched as the blacksmith took a new, U shaped piece of iron with a pair of long tongs and pushed it into the glowing coals of the forge. He pumped a great handle on the side, and the coals flared up with a loud whoosh, turning from red to a brilliant orange-white. The iron shoe began to glow with them. When it was as bright as the heart of the fire, the smith pulled it out and laid it on the anvil.
Then the clanging began again. CLANG! CLANG! CLANG! Sparks flew with every blow of the hammer. The smith turned the shoe, struck it again, and then held it up to look at it. He seemed to be shaping it with his eyes as much as with his hammer. He carried it, still glowing a dull red, over to Ebenezer's waiting foot. He held it close for just a second. A wisp of smoke curled up with a sharp smell.
"Sssss!" went the hoof. Twinkleheels jumped.
"He is only checking the fit," Ebenezer said calmly. "It must be perfect."
The smith seemed satisfied. He plunged the shoe into the tub of water with another great HISS! Steam boiled up to the rafters. After it was cool, he brought it back and placed it on Ebenezer's hoof. Then he took several small, thin nails and a smaller hammer. Tap, tap, tap. Tap, tap, tap. With a few quick, expert motions, he nailed the shoe in place. Then he used a tool to clip off the ends of the nails and hammer them flat. He set Ebenezer's foot down on the floor.
"There you are, old friend," the blacksmith said, patting Ebenezer's flank. "Good as new."
He did the same for Ebenezer's other feet, losing two more worn shoes and getting two new ones. The whole time, Twinkleheels watched, his fear slowly turning into a deep fascination.
Then Farmer Green untied Ebenezer and led him to one side. The blacksmith looked at Twinkleheels. "Well now," he said in a booming, friendly voice. "Who is this handsome little fellow?"
Johnnie Green came and stood by Twinkleheels' head, stroking his nose. "This is Twinkleheels," Johnnie said proudly. "It's his first time."
"Is that so?" the blacksmith chuckled. "Well, we will make it a good one. Let's have a look at that foot, little one."
He reached down and tapped Twinkleheels' front leg. Twinkleheels remembered what Ebenezer had said. Pay attention and obey orders. He lifted his foot uncertainly. The blacksmith took it in his big, warm hand and examined it.
"A fine, healthy hoof," the smith declared. "Just needs a bit of trimming and a smart set of shoes to protect it on the hard roads."
Twinkleheels stood as still as he could while the smith rasped and filed his hoof. It tickled a little, but it did not hurt at all. Then came the moment he had been dreading. The smith brought a small, glowing horseshoe from the forge. Twinkleheels' heart began to beat like a drum. He wanted to pull away, but Johnnie's hand was on his neck, and Ebenezer was watching him with a calm, steady gaze. He held his ground.
The smith pressed the hot shoe to his hoof. Sssss! The strange, sharp smell filled his nose again. It was over in a second. He had not felt a thing!
The rest of the shoeing passed in a blur of clanging, hissing, and tapping. The smith was just as careful with him as he had been with Ebenezer. When the last nail was driven home and the last foot was placed on the floor, Twinkleheels felt different. He felt heavier, and taller, too. He shifted his weight from one foot to another, listening to the strange, solid sound his new shoes made on the dirt floor.
"There," said Johnnie Green, giving him a happy pat. "Now you're a real working pony!"
Twinkleheels lifted his head. He felt immensely proud. The scrubby white mare was gone, and he was glad. He would not have wanted her to see how grand he looked. He was a pony with shoes!
Farmer Green paid the blacksmith, and then it was time to go. Johnnie led him out of the dark smithy and into the bright afternoon sun. As his new iron shoes struck the hard packed ground of the village street, they made a wonderful, crisp sound.
Clack! Clack-clack!
Twinkleheels tossed his head and took a few prancing steps. It was the most beautiful sound he had ever heard.
"Listen to that, Ebenezer!" he called out. "I am shod! I am shod!"
"So I hear," the old horse said with a gentle smile in his voice. "It is a fine sound. But mind your footing. A new shoe can be slippery until you are used to it."
But Twinkleheels was too excited to be careful. He trotted behind the buggy, making as much noise as he possibly could. Clack-clack-clack! went his feet on the road. He was no longer just a pasture pony. He was a road horse! Or at least, he felt like one. The journey home seemed much too short.

XVII A CLATTERING CHALLENGE

When they arrived back at the farm, the sun was casting long shadows across the yard. Bright and Broad, the big bay horses, were standing near the barn door, having just been unhitched from the sap sled.
Twinkleheels, still full of pride, did not trot quietly to his stall. Instead, he made a grand circle of the barnyard, picking his feet up high so that his new shoes would clatter and flash in the evening light.
CLACK! CLACK-A-TACK! CLACK!
Bright turned his head. "What is all that racket?" he asked. "Has a woodpecker learned to walk?"
"It sounds more like someone is rattling a bag of stones," Broad added, swishing his tail.
"It is not a woodpecker and it is not stones," Twinkleheels announced, stopping in front of them with his head held high. "It is the sound of proper iron shoes. I have been to the blacksmith's."
"Oh, have you?" said Bright, looking down his long nose. "We go to the blacksmith's four times a year. It is hardly something to make a fuss about."
"Perhaps not for big, clumsy horses like you," Twinkleheels retorted, his pride making him reckless. "But for a pony with quick feet like mine, it is a very important event! Now I can go as fast on the road as I do in the pasture."
"Is that a fact?" Broad said, and he and Bright exchanged an amused look. "You were not so quick in the snow this morning."
"The snow is for oxen and heavy horses," Twinkleheels declared, remembering Farmer Green's words. "But the hard road is for speed! I wager I could beat both of you to the covered bridge and back now!"
The two bay horses threw their heads back and laughed. It was a deep, rumbling sound that made Twinkleheels' ears flatten with annoyance.
"You, beat us?" Bright chuckled. "Why, little one, we would be back in our stalls having our oats before you reached the bend in the road."
"Is that so?" Twinkleheels stamped his newly shod foot. CLACK! "Then you are afraid to race me! You are afraid a little pony will show you up!"
Now, Bright and Broad were young and somewhat flighty, as Ebenezer had said. And they did not like being called afraid by anyone, least of all a pony they thought of as a youngster.
"Afraid?" snorted Broad. "We are not afraid of anything! Very well, pony. You have a race! To the covered bridge and back. We will show you what real speed is!"
"Johnnie! Johnnie!" Twinkleheels whinnied, turning to the barn where Johnnie Green was just putting his halter away. "Bright and Broad have challenged me to a race!"
Johnnie came running out, his eyes wide with excitement. "A real race? On the road?"
"It is more of a lesson than a race," Bright said loftily. "We are going to teach this noisy pony some manners."
Farmer Green had heard the commotion from the farmhouse porch. He came walking over, a thoughtful look on his face. "A race, eh?" he said. "The road is clear, and it is a good way to stretch their legs after a day's work. All right, Johnnie. You may ride Twinkleheels. But you take it easy now. This is not the county fair. Just to the bridge and back, and no foolishness."
"Yes, Father!" Johnnie cried. He quickly slipped a bridle on Twinkleheels and leaped onto his back. Another farm hand who was finishing his chores agreed to ride Broad, while Bright would run alongside, as they often did together.
Ebenezer watched from his stall door, where he had been unharnessed. "Pride is a heavy shoe to wear, Twinkleheels," the old horse called out. "Remember that a long race is won by steady legs, not a quick start."
But Twinkleheels barely heard him. He was quivering with excitement under Johnnie. He could feel the hard road beneath his new shoes, and he was sure he could fly.
The three horses lined up on the road that led away from the farm.
"Ready!" called Farmer Green. "Set! GO!"
Twinkleheheels burst forward. He had always been quick, but on the hard road, his small, light feet gave him a huge advantage at the start.
CLACK-clack-clack-clack! His little hooves drummed a frantic, triumphant rhythm on the packed dirt and gravel. Behind him, he heard the heavier, slower start of the bays. THUD-a-thud, THUD-a-thud!
He was winning! He was actually winning! The wind whistled past his ears. Johnnie was laughing on his back, urging him on. "Go, Twinkleheels! Go!"
He risked a look back. The two big bay horses were farther behind than he had ever dreamed they would be. They looked surprised. He could see their great heads bobbing as they worked to find their stride.
Feeling enormously pleased with himself, Twinkleheels pushed even harder. He was a racehorse! He was the fastest horse on the Green farm! Ebenezer was wise, but he did not understand. Pride was not a heavy shoe; it was a pair of wings!
They thundered past the old stone wall, and Twinkleheels was still in the lead. He could see the dark opening of the covered bridge ahead. That was the halfway mark.
But something was happening behind him. The sound of the bays' hooves was changing. The slow THUD-a-thud had become a powerful, rolling thunder. RUMBLE-rumble-RUMBLE! They had found their rhythm, and now they were working together, their long legs eating up the road.
Twinkleheels began to feel a little tired. His new shoes, which had felt so light and wonderful, now seemed heavy. And each step on the hard road sent a little jolt up his legs. He was not used to this. He was a pony made for soft pasture grass, not for pounding on hard roads.
The rumble grew louder. He did not need to look back to know they were catching up. He could feel their presence, like a great shadow overtaking him. Johnnie leaned forward, trying to encourage him. "Come on, boy! Almost there!"
But Twinkleheels' quick burst of speed was gone. His lungs burned. Just as they reached the entrance to the bridge, a great bay head appeared beside him, and then another. Bright and Broad swept past him, their powerful bodies moving in perfect harmony. They galloped through the echoing darkness of the bridge and turned for the journey home before Twinkleheels had even entered it.
He plodded through the bridge, his hooves making a hollow, lonely sound on the wooden planks. On the other side, he stopped. He was tired and his spirit was gone. He had lost.
Johnnie patted his neck. "That's all right, boy," he said softly. "You gave it your all. They are just too big."
Twinkleheels and Johnnie watched as the two bays grew smaller and smaller on the road back to the farm. Then, slowly, they began the long walk home. The clack of his shoes no longer sounded proud and exciting. It just sounded tired.
By the time they got back to the barn, the bays were already in their stalls, munching happily on their oats. Twinkleheels walked past them without a word and went to his own stall. Johnnie took off his bridle, gave him a long, cool drink of water, and filled his manger with sweet hay and a measure of oats.
Twinkleheels was too discouraged to eat. He just stood there, his head hanging low.
After a while, a soft nose nudged his shoulder. It was Ebenezer, who had been let out into the barn aisle for a moment.
"A good run is a fine thing, even if you do not finish first," the old horse said gently.
"I made a fool of myself," Twinkleheels mumbled into his hay. "I thought my new shoes made me the fastest horse in the world."
"Shoes do not make a horse fast any more than a crown makes a man wise," Ebenezer said. "They help, but the real strength comes from here." He nudged Twinkleheels' side, over his heart and lungs. "And from here." He nudged Twinkleheels' legs. "It is a strength you must build with time and patience. Bright and Broad have it because they work hard together every day. Your strength is different. It is a quickness and a cleverness that they do not have. Today was a race for their kind of strength."
Twinkleheels sighed. It was true. He looked over at the bays. He expected them to be laughing at him. But Broad was looking at him with a thoughtful expression.
"You have a very quick start, pony," Broad called over. "You surprised me."
"He is right," added Bright, his mouth full of oats. "For the first quarter mile, I thought my eyes were playing tricks on me."
Twinkleheels lifted his head a little. They were not making fun of him. They were... complimenting him?
"Thank you," Twinkleheels said in a small voice.
"You see?" said Ebenezer. "Every horse has its place, and every horse has its talent. You learned what yours is not today. And you have also earned a bit of respect. That is a good day's work."
Twinkleheels looked at his oats. Suddenly, he was very hungry. He took a bite, and then another. The hay was sweet, and his stall was safe and warm. His legs were sore and his pride was bruised, but as he chewed, he thought that perhaps Ebenezer was right. He had lost the race, but he felt a little bit wiser. And as he listened to the quiet munching of the other horses in the peaceful barn, he decided that was a fine prize to win.

He spoke a few quiet words to the mare, then lifted her hoof and placed it between his knees. With a rasp, he began to file the hoof, making a dry, scratchy sound.

"That is to make sure the shoe is a perfect fit," Ebenezer murmured.

Twinkleheels held his breath as the smith took a handful of nails from his apron pocket. He placed one against the shoe and with three quick, sharp taps of his hammer, he drove it home. Tap. Tap. Tap. He did this again and again, all around the shoe. When he was finished, he used a special tool to twist off the sharp ends of the nails that had come through the side of the hoof.

The mare stood perfectly still through it all. When the blacksmith set her foot down, she shifted her weight onto it as if nothing had happened.

The blacksmith moved to her other feet, and soon she was shod all around. Her owner paid the smith, and then led the scrubby mare out of the shop. She gave Twinkleheels a final, scornful look as she departed.

"Do not mind her," said Ebenezer. "Some horses believe that size is all that matters. They have not learned that a kind heart is a much greater treasure."

"Now then," said the blacksmith, turning his friendly eyes upon Ebenezer. "What can I do for you, old friend? Lost another shoe, have you?"

"That I have," Farmer Green answered for him. "And the others are worn as thin as a winter leaf. He needs a full set."

"And the little fellow?" the smith asked, winking at Johnnie.

"Twinkleheels is getting his very first pair," Johnnie said proudly. "He's old enough now."

"Is that so!" the blacksmith boomed. "Well, it is a great day for a pony when he gets his first shoes. We shall have to make them extra special."

First, the smith took care of Ebenezer. The old horse knew just what to do. He lifted each foot when asked and stood as quiet as a statue while the smith pulled off the old, worn shoes and fitted him with a sturdy new set. Twinkleheels watched every move, and his fear began to fade. It seemed Ebenezer was right. There was nothing to be frightened of.

Then it was his turn.

"All right, young man," the blacksmith said kindly. "Let's see what we have here."

Johnnie led Twinkleheels to the center of the floor. The pony's heart began to beat a little faster. He remembered Ebenezer's advice. _Pay attention and obey orders._

"Let's start with this one," the smith said, tapping Twinkleheels' front left leg. Twinkleheels wasn't sure what to do. He shuffled his feet.

"Just lift it up," Johnnie said, patting his neck. "Like you do when I clean your hooves."

Twinkleheels lifted his foot. The blacksmith took it gently and examined it. "A fine, healthy hoof," he declared. "It won't take much to get this ready."

He was just as gentle with Twinkleheels as he had been with Ebenezer. He rasped the hoof smooth, and the feeling tickled a little. Twinkleheels wanted to snatch his foot back, but he made himself stand still. He glanced over at Ebenezer, who gave him an encouraging nod.

The smith went to his forge and picked out a very small horseshoe. He heated it until it glowed a cherry red, then laid it on the anvil and began to hammer it. The shop filled with that dreadful clanging noise again, but this time Twinkleheels knew what it was. He watched, fascinated, as the smith shaped the shoe with swift, expert blows. Sparks flew like fireflies with every strike.

When the smith brought the hot shoe towards him, Twinkleheels couldn't help but flinch.

"Easy now," Farmer Green said, holding his halter. "He won't hurt you."

The quick touch of heat and the puff of smoke were strange, but not painful. And when the smith began to nail the shoe on, Twinkleheels was amazed to find he could not feel a thing. It was just a tapping sound, and a slight vibration up his leg. In a few minutes, his first shoe was on.

The blacksmith set his foot down. "There you are," he said. "Take a step."

Twinkleheels carefully put his weight on his newly shod foot. It felt strange. It felt heavier, and when he took a step, it made a sharp _clack_ on the wooden floor. He lifted his foot and put it down again. _Clack!_ It was a wonderful sound! It was the sound of a real, working horse!

He was so pleased that he hardly noticed as the blacksmith shod his other three feet. He stood as still as could be, dreaming of the fine, important sound he would make walking down the road. _Clack! Clack! Clack! Clack!_ He would sound just like Ebenezer.

When they were all finished, Farmer Green paid the blacksmith. "Thank you, Thomas," he said. "You've done a fine job as always."

"My pleasure, Mr. Green," the smith replied, ruffling Twinkleheels' forelock. "You have a good pony here. He stood like a veteran for his first time."

Twinkleheels felt a thrill of pride. A veteran! He puffed out his chest.

Johnnie led him out into the bright sunshine. Twinkleheels lifted his feet high, making his new shoes ring on the hard-packed dirt of the village street. _Clink! Clank! Clink! Clank!_ It was an even better sound than he had imagined.

"You see?" Ebenezer said, walking beside him as they headed for home. "There was nothing to fear."

"It was wonderful!" Twinkleheels exclaimed. "I feel so grown up! I feel as if I could run faster than the wind!"

Ebenezer chuckled softly. "Shoes do make you feel spry," he agreed. "But do not let them run away with your good sense."

But Twinkleheels was too excited to listen to cautionary words. He pranced and danced all the way back to the buggy, his head high, his new shoes striking sparks on the occasional stone. He felt strong. He felt fast. He felt that he was now the most splendid pony in the whole wide world.

XVII A RACE AND A LESSON

The next morning, Twinkleheels could hardly wait to get out of the barn. His new shoes felt like magic slippers. He was sure they had made him at least twice as fast as he was before.

As Johnnie was putting on his bridle, the two big bay horses, Bright and Broad, were being led out to be hitched to a heavy wagon. They were the farm's main team for heavy work, and they were immensely strong.

"Good morning," said Broad, his voice a low rumble. "Those are some very loud shoes you have there, pony."

"They are not loud," Twinkleheels retorted proudly. "They are new. And they are fast."

Bright, the other bay, gave a good-natured snort. "Shoes do not make a horse fast. Strong legs and a big heart make a horse fast."

"I have strong legs!" Twinkleheels insisted. "And my heart is as big as anybody's! I bet I could beat you both in a race!"

The words were out of his mouth before he could stop them. A moment before, he had not even been thinking of a race. But the challenge from Bright, and the power he felt in his new shoes, had made him bold.

Broad looked down at him from his great height. A slow smile seemed to spread across his broad face. "Is that so?" he said. "A race, you say? Where to?"

"To the crossroads and back!" Twinkleheels declared. "The first one back to the barn gate wins!"

Johnnie Green looked a bit worried. "Twinkleheels," he began, "Bright and Broad are very big. And they're used to pulling heavy loads for miles."

"That's why I'll win!" the pony said confidently. "They are big and slow. I am small and quick!"

Farmer Green, who was harnessing the bays, overheard the commotion. He chuckled. "Let them have their race, Johnnie," he said. "It will be good for the pony to stretch his legs. You can ride him. We will follow with the empty wagon."

And so it was decided. Johnnie climbed onto Twinkleheels' back. The little pony was trembling with excitement. He pawed the ground, his new shoes scraping on the stone paving outside the barn.

Bright and Broad stood calmly in their harness, hitched to the big wagon. They looked as solid and unmovable as two red-brown mountains.

"Are you ready?" Broad called.

"I was born ready!" Twinkleheels shouted back.

"On the count of three, then," said Farmer Green from the wagon seat. "One... two... THREE!"

Twinkleheels shot forward like an arrow from a bow. His quickness took the big horses completely by surprise. He was a hundred yards down the lane before Bright and Broad had even gotten the heavy wagon rolling properly.

"Hooray!" shouted Johnnie, clinging to his mane. "We're winning!"

Twinkleheels' little legs flew. His new shoes rang out a glorious, rapid rhythm on the hard road. _Clack-clack-clack-clack!_ It was the sound of victory! He risked a glance behind him. The big bays were far, far back, just beginning to hit their stride.

He laughed to himself. This was easier than he had thought! He would be at the crossroads before they were halfway there. He urged himself on, feeling the wind whistle past his ears. He was the fastest pony alive!

He reached the crossroads in what felt like no time at all. He spun around, ready for the triumphant journey home. And it was then that he saw them.

Bright and Broad were not far behind. And they were no longer moving slowly. They had found their rhythm. Their great legs moved like powerful pistons, eating up the road with every stride. The wagon, which had seemed so heavy at the start, now rolled along smoothly behind them. They were not running, not in the frantic way Twinkleheels had. They were pulling, working together, and they were covering the ground at a tremendous pace.

A little knot of worry tightened in Twinkleheels' chest. He turned for home and urged his legs to go faster. But his first great burst of speed had tired him. His legs were beginning to feel heavy. The magical feeling of the shoes was gone. Now they just felt like four lumps of iron on his feet.

Behind him, the steady, rhythmic pounding of the bays' hooves grew louder. Thump-thump. Thump-thump. It was a relentless, powerful sound, the sound of tireless strength.

"Come on, Twinkleheels, you can do it!" Johnnie encouraged him.

Twinkleheels tried. He flattened his ears and stretched his neck and galloped as hard as he could. But the steady _thump-thump_ grew louder still. Soon, a great shadow fell over him. He glanced to his side. Broad's huge head was right there beside his shoulder. The big horse was not even breathing hard.

"You set a fine pace, little one," Broad said, his voice even and calm. And then, with what seemed like no effort at all, he and Bright surged past.

They did not gallop. They simply trotted on, their powerful stride carrying them ahead with inescapable certainty. Twinkleheels watched them go, his heart sinking with every step. He tried to keep up, but it was no use. His legs ached and his breath came in great, ragged gasps. He slowed to a canter, then a trot, and finally, to a weary walk.

"It's all right, boy," he said softly. "You gave it your all. They are just too big."

Twinkleheels and Johnnie watched as the two bays grew smaller and smaller on the road back to the farm. Then, slowly, they began the long walk home. The clack of his shoes no longer sounded proud and exciting. It just sounded tired.

By the time they got back to the barn, the bays were already in their stalls, munching happily on their oats. Twinkleheels walked past them without a word and went to his own stall. Johnnie took off his bridle, gave him a long, cool drink of water, and filled his manger with sweet hay and a measure of oats.

Twinkleheels was too discouraged to eat. He just stood there, his head hanging low. After a while, a soft nose nudged his shoulder. It was Ebenezer, who had been let out into the barn aisle for a moment.

"A good run is a fine thing, even if you do not finish first," the old horse said gently.

"I made a fool of myself," Twinkleheels mumbled into his hay. "I thought my new shoes made me the fastest horse in the world."

"Shoes do not make a horse fast any more than a crown makes a man wise," Ebenezer said. "They help, but the real strength comes from here." He nudged Twinkleheels' side, over his heart and lungs. "And from here." He nudged Twinkleheels' legs. "It is a strength you must build with time and patience. Bright and Broad have it because they work hard together every day. Your strength is different. It is a quickness and a cleverness that they do not have. Today was a race for their kind of strength."

Twinkleheels sighed. It was true. He looked over at the bays. He expected them to be laughing at him. But Broad was looking at him with a thoughtful expression.

"You have a very quick start, pony," Broad called over. "You surprised me."

"He is right," added Bright, his mouth full of oats. "For the first quarter mile, I thought my eyes were playing tricks on me."

Twinkleheels lifted his head a little. They were not making fun of him. They were... complimenting him?

"Thank you," Twinkleheels said in a small voice.

"You see?" said Ebenezer. "Every horse has its place, and every horse has its talent. You learned what yours is not today. And you have also earned a bit of respect. That is a good day's work."

Twinkleheels looked at his oats. Suddenly, he was very hungry. He took a bite, and then another. The hay was sweet, and his stall was safe and warm. His legs were sore and his pride was bruised, but as he chewed, he thought that perhaps Ebenezer was right. He had lost the race, but he felt a little bit wiser. And as he listened to the quiet munching of the other horses in the peaceful barn, he decided that was a fine prize to win.

Twinkleheels felt a thrill of pride. He stood as still as a statue while the blacksmith measured his hoof, and then went back to the flaming forge. There was the clanging on the anvil again, but this time Twinkleheels knew what it was. And when the hissing sound came, he merely flicked an ear, thinking how foolish he had been to mistake it for a goose.

Soon Mr. Giles came back with a small, perfect horseshoe. He held it against Twinkleheels' hoof. Then, taking a few small nails from his mouth, he took up his hammer. Twinkleheels held his breath. He felt a tiny tap, tap, tap, but it was no more bothersome than a fly landing on him. There was no pain at all! In fact, it tickled a little. Before he knew it, the first shoe was on.

One by one, Mr. Giles shod all four of his feet. With each new shoe, Twinkleheels felt more and more important. When the last one was on, he lifted his foot and peered down at the shiny new iron. It made his hoof look very trim and proper.

"There you are," Mr. Giles said, giving him a final pat. "You're ready for the road now, little fellow."

Johnnie led him out into the sunlight. Twinkleheels took a step, and then another. Clack, clack, went his new shoes on the hard-packed earth. It was a wonderful sound! He felt taller. He felt stronger. He felt faster. He lifted his knees high, just as he had seen the fancy trotting horses do at the county fair.

Ebenezer, who had been shod as well, came out and stood beside him. "They fit you well," the old horse observed.

"They feel wonderful!" Twinkleheels exclaimed. "I feel as if I could fly! I feel as if I could outrun the wind itself! I'll bet I could even beat the bays in a race now!"

Ebenezer smiled his wise, slow smile. "Shoes are a fine thing," he said. "But do not mistake a new hat for a new head. The real race is run with the heart and the legs, not the feet."

Twinkleheels was too excited to pay much mind to Ebenezer's caution. He pranced and strutted all the way back to the buggy. He could not wait to get home and show the bay horses his new shoes. He was quite sure they had never seen such fine shoes on a pony before.

XVII A CHALLENGE GIVEN

The two bay horses, whose names were Rocket and Flash, were in the paddock when Twinkleheels and Ebenezer returned to the farm. They were a matched pair, big and powerful, with glossy coats and muscles that rippled when they moved. They spent their days pulling the heavy wagon and were known for their tremendous strength and stamina.

Twinkleheels, his head held high, trotted straight up to the fence. "Good afternoon," he said, in what he hoped was a very grand and casual tone. "I have been to the village."

Rocket, the larger of the two bays, glanced over. "So we see," he said, his gaze falling to Twinkleheels' feet. "New shoes."

"Yes," said Twinkleheels, lifting a forefoot so the sun glinted on the iron. "They are the very latest style. Very light. For speed."

Flash, the other bay, stopped swishing his tail and looked at Twinkleheels with amusement. "Speed, is it? A pony your size is built for trotting around a pasture, not for speed."

Twinkleheels' pride was stung. "I am much faster than you think!" he declared. "These shoes make me feel as light as a feather. I am certain I could beat you both to the crossroads and back."

Rocket and Flash exchanged a look. Then they both let out a great whinny of laughter.

"Beat us?" Rocket snorted. "Why, little pony, we could beat you with our legs tied together! We are bred for pulling and for distance. You are a pet."

"I am not a pet!" Twinkleheels stamped his newly shod foot, making a satisfying clack. "I am a saddle pony for Johnnie Green! And I am fast!"

"Is that a challenge, then?" Flash asked, a teasing glint in his eye.

"Yes!" said Twinkleheels boldly, before he had even had a chance to think it over. "I challenge you to a race! Tomorrow morning! To the old oak tree at the crossroads!"

"Very well," said Rocket, still looking amused. "We accept. But do not be surprised when we leave you in our dust. It is a long way to the crossroads for such short legs."

Twinkleheels tossed his head, trying to look as if he did not have a care in the world. "We shall see about that," he said. And with as much dignity as he could muster, he trotted away toward the barn, the proud clack-clack-clack of his shoes echoing his confidence.

Inside his stall, however, his bravery began to crumble. What had he done? The bays were so big, their legs so long. Ebenezer's words came back to him. *Do not mistake a new hat for a new head.*

"Oh, dear," Twinkleheels muttered to himself. "I think I have been very foolish."

XVIII THE RACE

The next morning, the whole barn was buzzing with news of the race. Old dog Spot trotted back and forth excitedly, and even the cows in the nearby pasture seemed to be watching the proceedings with interest.

Johnnie Green, who had overheard the challenge, was thrilled. "You can do it, Twinkleheels!" he whispered, brushing the pony's coat until it shone. "You're quick on your feet. Just get a good start."

Farmer Green, however, looked doubtful. He stood with his arms crossed, watching as Johnnie led Twinkleheels to the starting line he had drawn in the dirt at the end of the farm lane. The two big bays, Rocket and Flash, were already there, looking calm and powerful. They were not even breathing hard.

"This is not a race for a pony," Farmer Green said to Ebenezer, who stood beside him. "Those bays have legs like locomotive pistons. It is a race of endurance."

"Every creature must learn its own measure," Ebenezer replied quietly. "Sometimes the lesson is a hard one."

Johnnie gave Twinkleheels a final pat. "Ready?" he called.

"Ready!" Twinkleheels shouted, though his heart was hammering against his ribs like the blacksmith's hammer on the anvil.

"On your mark! Get set! GO!"

Twinkleheels shot forward like an arrow from a bow. His small, light body and quick legs gave him a tremendous advantage at the start. He was a full ten lengths ahead before the big bays had even hit their stride. The wind whistled in his ears. The clatter of his shoes was a glorious, drumming beat of victory. He was winning! He was really winning!

He risked a glance over his shoulder. The bays were far behind, two brown specks in the distance. A surge of pure joy went through him. He would show them! He would show everyone!

The road stretched on. He passed the babbling brook and the clump of birch trees. His legs felt strong, his new shoes barely seemed to touch the ground. But the crossroads still looked a long way off. He began to feel a little stitch in his side, and his breathing was coming in great, ragged gasps.

It was then that he heard it. A new sound, mixing with the clatter of his own hoofs. It was a heavier, thundering rhythm. Thump-thump, thump-thump. It was getting closer.

He flattened his ears and ran harder, pushing with all his might. But the sound grew louder. He dared another look back. The two brown specks were no longer specks. They were huge, powerful horses, their long legs eating up the road. They ran together as if they were one, their strides long and steady and tireless.

Twinkleheels' heart sank. He was beginning to feel very, very tired. His legs ached, and his lungs burned. The crossroads still seemed impossibly far away.

With a great rush of wind, Rocket and Flash thundered past him. They did not even seem to be trying very hard. "Good try, little pony!" Rocket called over his shoulder as they swept by.

Twinkleheels watched them go, his spirit completely broken. He slowed to a trot, and then to a walk. He could not run another step. He had lost. He had made a fool of himself. Defeated, he stood in the middle of the road, his head hanging low. The proud clack of his shoes now sounded mocking and hollow. He had never felt so small.

After a few minutes, he heard footsteps. It was Johnnie Green, who had run after him. The boy put his arm around the pony's neck.

"It's all right, boy," he said softly. "You gave it your all. They are just too big."

The next few days passed quietly. The snow began to melt under a bright, encouraging sun. Johnnie Green came to the barn each morning with a carrot for Twinkleheels and a friendly pat for Ebenezer. He even started spending more time currying Rocket and Flash, who seemed to stand a little quieter for him now.

One morning, Farmer Green came into the barn with a small, wrapped parcel. "Here you are, Johnnie," he said. "Happy birthday a few days early. I thought you might have some use for this, now that the maple sap is running."

Johnnie's face lit up. He tore the paper away to reveal a handsome pocketknife with a smooth wooden handle and two shiny blades. "Oh, Father! It's a beauty! Thank you!" he cried, turning it over and over in his hands. He clicked the larger blade open and shut. It made a satisfying snap.

"It's a good tool," Farmer Green said with a smile. "A man's first knife is a special thing. But it's sharp. You be careful with it."

"I will!" Johnnie promised. He immediately ran to Twinkleheels' stall. "Look, Twinkleheels! Look what I got!" He held the knife out for the pony to inspect. Twinkleheels sniffed the strange, oily smell of the new steel and the faint scent of wood. He could feel Johnnie's excitement, and it made him prick his ears and nicker softly.

"Let's go check the buckets in the sugar-bush," Johnnie said. "I can whittle a whistle while we ride!"

In a few minutes, Twinkleheels was saddled and Johnnie was on his back. They trotted out of the barnyard and up the muddy road toward the woods. The air was sweet with the promise of spring, and the dripping of the sap from the maple trees sounded like a hundred tiny clocks ticking.

Johnnie found a fallen branch of willow, which was perfect for whittling. As Twinkleheels ambled along the path between the trees, Johnnie worked away with his new knife, carefully peeling back the bark. He was so absorbed in his task, and in chattering to Twinkleheels, that he scarcely noticed where his shavings were falling.

They checked all the buckets, and Johnnie was pleased to see many of them were nearly full. "Father will be busy boiling this down tonight," he said. "We'd better head back and tell him."

When they arrived back at the barn, Johnnie slid off Twinkleheels' back. He reached into his pocket to put his new knife away, and his face fell. The pocket was empty.

"My knife!" he gasped. He patted all his other pockets, his face growing paler with each frantic pat. "It's gone! Twinkleheels, it's gone!"

Twinkleheels looked at the boy's distressed face and felt a pang of sympathy. Johnnie ran his hands over the saddle and the saddle blanket, hoping it had just snagged on something. But it was nowhere to be seen.

"It must have fallen out of my pocket in the sugar-bush!" Johnnie cried. His lower lip began to tremble. "Father just gave it to me. He told me to be careful!"

He ran to find his father, who was mending a piece of harness near the woodshed. Twinkleheels, left standing with his reins trailing, could hear their conversation.

"I lost it, Father," Johnnie wailed. "My new knife. I lost it in the woods."

Farmer Green put down his tools. "Now, now, son. Don't take on so. We'll find it. It probably just fell near the path. When I'm done with this harness, we'll take the big horses and go have a look."

"But what if it gets covered in mud? What if we can't find it?" Johnnie sniffled.

"We'll look," Farmer Green said calmly. "There's no use worrying until we've had a good look. Now, put Twinkleheels in his stall and come help me with this strap."

Johnnie returned, his shoulders slumped. He led Twinkleheels into the barn and unfastened his saddle. He was so upset that when he closed the stall door, he forgot to slide the heavy wooden latch all the way into its catch. He gave Twinkleheels a sad, brief pat and went to help his father.

Twinkleheels stood in his stall, listening to the quiet sounds of the barn. He could hear Ebenezer's gentle breathing in the next stall and the rustle of the bays as they shifted their weight. He felt terribly sorry for Johnnie. He remembered the boy's happy face when he first unwrapped the knife, and it made his own heart feel heavy.

He nudged his door with his nose. It swung open a few inches. Twinkleheels blinked. He pushed it again. It swung open wider. He was free! He looked over at Ebenezer's stall. The old horse was watching him with his wise, calm eyes.

"The boy is unhappy," Twinkleheels said softly.

"I heard," Ebenezer replied. "A lost treasure is a heavy burden for a young heart."

"His father said they would look for it," Twinkleheels went on. "But they are going to take the big horses." He paused. "Rocket and Flash are strong, but their hooves are very big. They might step on the knife and push it deep into the mud. And they do not look at the ground so carefully."

Ebenezer nodded slowly. "That is true. A heavy foot sees less than a light one."

"I was there," Twinkleheels said, a thought beginning to form in his mind. "I know the path we took. I am small. I can look closely. Perhaps... perhaps I could find it for him."

"The woods are big," Ebenezer cautioned gently. "And the knife is very small."

"But I want to try!" Twinkleheels declared. "It is my fault, in a way. If he had not been riding me, he would not have lost it."

"It is a kind thought," said Ebenezer. "Kindness is a good compass. But be careful. The melting snow makes for treacherous footing, and not all creatures in the woods are friendly."

Twinkleheels took a deep breath. "I will be careful," he promised. He glanced toward the barn door, which stood open to the afternoon sun. This was his chance. Quiet as a mouse, he pattered across the barn floor and slipped out into the yard. No one saw him. He turned and trotted back up the path towards the sugar-bush.

The woods felt different now that he was alone. The dripping of the sap sounded louder, and the shadows of the trees were longer. Twinkleheels kept his eyes fixed on the path, retracing the steps he and Johnnie had taken only an hour before. He saw their hoofprints and bootprints clearly in the soft earth and remaining patches of snow. He walked slowly, his nose to the ground, sniffing for the scent of steel and wood.

He searched and searched. He looked on the left side of the path and on the right. He peered under ferns and around the roots of large trees. He saw many interesting things: a ladybug waking from its winter sleep, a patch of bright green moss, and a funny-shaped stone. But he did not see a pocketknife.

He was beginning to feel discouraged. Ebenezer was right; the woods were very big and the knife was very small. As he stood wondering what to do next, a rustling in the leaves nearby made him jump. A fat rabbit with twitching whiskers hopped out from under a bush.

"Excuse me," Twinkleheels said politely. "Did you happen to see something shiny fall on the path today? Something with a wooden handle?"

The rabbit wiggled its nose. "Shiny? No, no. Nothing shiny. Lots of roots, though. Good tender roots just under the leaves. Are you looking for roots? I can show you a fine patch." And before Twinkleheels could answer, the rabbit had hopped away, its mind entirely on its dinner.

Twinkleheels sighed and walked on. A little farther along, he heard a furious chattering from a branch overhead. He looked up to see a red squirrel with a bushy tail, scolding him loudly.

"What do you think you're doing?" chattered the squirrel. "Clomping through my forest! Stirring up the leaves! I have nuts buried here, you know! Important nuts! For the winter! For next winter, that is! You'll disturb the whole system!"

"I am very sorry," said Twinkleheels. "I am only looking for a lost knife."

"A knife! Worse and worse!" the squirrel shrieked. "Sharp things! Dangerous things! Go away, you and your sharp things! Find your own forest!" The squirrel stamped its tiny feet and disappeared into a hole in the tree.

Twinkleheels' ears drooped. It seemed no one could help him. He was about to turn back toward the barn, his mission a failure, when a flash of blue caught his eye. A big, noisy bluejay landed on a branch right in front of him.

"Jay! Jay! Jay!" the bird squawked, cocking its head.

Twinkleheels remembered something Farmer Green once said about bluejays. They were clever birds, and they were very fond of anything that glittered or shone.

"Good afternoon, Mr. Bluejay," Twinkleheels said, trying his most charming tone. "You have a very fine view from up there."

The bluejay puffed out its chest, pleased. "The best view!" it agreed. "I see everything. I see the worm and I see the weasel. I see the fox in the brush and the farmer on the road. Nothing happens in this woodlot that I do not see."

"Then perhaps you saw a very special shiny thing fall from the sky this afternoon?" Twinkleheels asked hopefully. "A small, shiny thing?"

The bluejay's bright, black eye glittered with interest. "Shiny, you say? I do like shiny things. Was it very shiny?"

"Oh, yes," said Twinkleheels. "It had two blades that shone like the sun on water."

"Aha!" the bluejay cried. "I did see it! I saw the boy-thing with the clumsy fingers. He was cutting a stick, and then--plink!--the shiny thing flew from his hand. It made a lovely arc in the air."

"Where did it land?" Twinkleheels asked, his heart beating faster. "Can you show me?"

"Perhaps," the bluejay said slyly. "Information like this is not free, you know. What is it worth to you?"

"Worth?" Twinkleheels was puzzled. "I have nothing to give you. Only my thanks."

"Thanks don't fill a belly," the bird grumbled. Then its eyes lit upon Twinkleheels' saddle. Johnnie had tucked a small, leftover piece of a corncob into one of the straps for Twinkleheels to nibble on later. "What about that? That yellow thing. The kernels look delicious."

"It is yours," Twinkleheels said at once. "If you will only show me where the knife is."

"A deal!" squawked the bluejay. It swooped down, expertly plucked the corncob from the strap, and flew back to its branch. "Follow me!"

The bird flew from tree to tree, calling "Jay! Jay!" every so often to make sure Twinkleheles was following. It led him away from the main path, into a part of the sugar-bush where the trees grew close together and thorny brambles covered the ground. They came to the edge of a small, steep gully with a trickle of melted snow running at the bottom.

"There!" the bluejay shrieked, pointing with its beak.

Twinkleheels peered over the edge. There, caught in the thorns of a wild rose bush about halfway down the slippery bank, was Johnnie's knife. The sun glinted off one of its blades. It was hanging in a place where no person could easily climb, and where a big horse like Rocket or Flash would never be able to go.

Twinkleheels felt a surge of triumph. The bluejay, having fulfilled its part of the bargain, finished its corn and flew away with a last, loud squawk. Now it was up to the little pony.

He looked at the steep bank. It was muddy and covered with loose stones. The rose bush was a tangle of sharp thorns. But he was not a big, clumsy workhorse. He was Twinkleheels. He was small and nimble. This was a task for his kind of strength.

Carefully, he placed his front feet over the edge, testing the ground. It held. He slid a little, his new shoes scraping against a rock. He took another step, and then another, moving sideways along the bank. He pushed his head and shoulders into the thorny bush, ignoring the sharp pricks against his skin. He could feel the thorns scratching his nose and his ears, but he kept his eyes on the prize.

He stretched his neck. The knife was just a few inches out of reach. He couldn't grab it with his teeth. He tried nudging the branch it was on, but it was too thick. He took a careful step closer, his back hooves scrabbling for purchase on the muddy slope. He stretched his neck out as far as it would go and, with the very tip of his nose, he gently pushed against the smooth wooden handle of the knife.

It wiggled. He pushed again, a tiny, precise nudge. The knife came loose from the thorns. For a terrible moment it hung in the air, and then it fell... not into the gully, but onto a small, flat ledge of rock just below the bush. It was safe!

Now came the problem of getting back up. Twinkleheels backed out of the thorny bush carefully. He turned his body and, digging his toes into the soft earth, scrambled back up the bank to level ground. He was muddy and scratched, but he was proud.

He knew he could not carry the knife back himself. He would have to lead Johnnie to it. He took one last look at the knife shining on the ledge, memorized the spot, and then turned and trotted as fast as he could back toward the barn.

He burst out of the woods and into the barnyard just as Johnnie and his father were harnessing Rocket to a small cart. Johnnie's face was still smudged with tears.

"Twinkleheels!" Johnnie cried in surprise. "How did you get out?"

Twinkleheels ignored the question. He ran right up to Johnnie and nudged the boy's chest with his muddy nose. Then he whinnied, a sharp, insistent sound, and turned and took a few steps back toward the woods, looking over his shoulder.

"What's got into him?" Farmer Green asked, frowning.

Twinkleheels trotted back to Johnnie, nudged him again, and again gestured with his head toward the sugar-bush. He let out another impatient little whinny.

A flicker of understanding dawned in Johnnie's eyes. "I think... I think he wants to show me something," he said slowly. "I think he's trying to tell me something about the knife!"

"Nonsense, son," Farmer Green said. "He's just a pony."

But Johnnie was already untying Rocket. "Please, Father. Let's just see. He's never acted like this before."

Seeing the hope in his son's face, Farmer Green sighed. "All right. But we're following him on foot. I'm not taking a horse into any thickets after a pony's wild goose chase."

Twinkleheels didn't wait. He started walking, making sure Johnnie and his father were following. He led them along the path, and then turned off where the bluejay had shown him.

"This isn't where we were," Johnnie said, confused.

"Trust me," Twinkleheels thought, and he pushed on through the brush. He led them right to the edge of the gully and stopped, pointing his nose downward.

XVII A QUIET VISITOR

For a long time after Ebenezer had gone back to his own stall, Twinkleheels stood quietly in the deep straw. The cool, wet cloth on his leg felt good, and the sharp pain was turning into a dull, heavy throb. It was a lonely feeling. The barn was full of familiar sounds; the rustle of hay as Bright and Broad pulled mouthfuls from their mangers, the soft cooing of pigeons in the rafters, and the gentle sigh of Ebenezer settling down for a nap. But all these sounds seemed to come from very far away.

Twinkleheels felt as if he were on a small island of straw, cut off from the rest of the world. He was ashamed. He was sore. And most of all, he was dreadfully bored. There was nothing to do but stand still and think. And his thoughts kept circling back to the log, and the mud, and the terrible feeling of being trapped. He had been so sure of himself! He had been so certain that his new shoes made him as strong as Bright or as Broad. Now the single shoe on his good hind foot felt heavy and foolish in the silent straw.

Presently, he heard a soft step beside his stall. It was not the heavy tread of Farmer Green, nor the shuffling gait of Ebenezer. A small, familiar face peered through the slats of the door. It was Johnnie Green. The boy’s face was streaked with dirt, and his eyes were big and worried. He unlatched the door and slipped inside, closing it carefully behind him.

In his hand, he held a large, red apple.

"This is for you," Johnnie said in a small voice. He held it out. Twinkleheels looked at the apple, then at Johnnie, and he took it gently with his lips. He had never known an apple to taste so good.

"I'm sorry, Twinkleheels," the boy whispered, stroking the pony's neck. "It was my fault. I shouldn't have let you try to pull that log."

Twinkleheels crunched his apple and nudged Johnnie's shoulder with his nose. He wanted to tell him that it was not his fault at all. He wanted to say that it was his own foolish pride that had caused all the trouble. But of course, he could not. He could only stand there and let his friend pet him, and hope that Johnnie understood.

"Dad says you have to stay in here for a few days," Johnnie went on, his hand moving to the pony's sore leg, patting it very, very softly just above the wet wrapping. "He says you need to rest it completely. I'll come and see you every day. And I'll bring you apples. And carrots, too."

Twinkleheels flicked an ear and sighed. A few days! It sounded like a lifetime. But seeing the sad look on Johnnie's face, he felt a new feeling stir inside him. It was not pride, and it was not shame. It was a feeling of wanting to make his friend feel better. He whinnied, a soft, low sound, and rubbed his head against Johnnie's chest.

Johnnie seemed to understand. He smiled a little, for the first time. "You're a good pony," he said. "Even if you are a bit reckless." He stayed a while longer, talking to Twinkleheels about nothing in particular, until his father called from the house that supper was ready.

As night fell over the farm, the loneliness returned. The great barn grew dark and quiet, except for the sound of sleepy breathing from the other stalls. Twinkleheels shifted his weight, and the ache in his leg reminded him of his lesson. He was only a pony. A small pony, with three good legs and one very sore one. He closed his eyes and wished for morning to come quickly.

The next day was even longer than the first. After the morning feeding, Farmer Green led Ebenezer and the bays, Flash and Dash, out to the pasture for a few hours of sunshine. Twinkleheels watched them go from his stall door, a great lump of disappointment in his throat. He longed to feel the sun on his back and the soft grass under his hoofs. Instead, he was a prisoner in a box of straw. He could hear their happy whinnies from the field, and he turned his head away, feeling very sorry for himself.

He was dozing lightly when he heard a hesitant shuffling outside his stall. It was not one horse, but two. He opened his eyes and saw two dark heads peering over the door. It was Flash and Dash. Twinkleheels was surprised. He expected them to tease him. He expected them to gloat over his misfortune. He laid his ears back a little, ready for a sharp reply.

But the two big horses did not look proud or boastful. They looked… well, they looked uncomfortable. They fidgeted and shuffled their feet on the hard-packed earth of the barn floor.

It was Flash who spoke first. His voice, usually so bold, was quiet. "We… ah… we heard you were laid up," he said, looking at a spot on the wall just past Twinkleheels' ear.

"Yes," Twinkleheels said, waiting.

Dash cleared his throat. "We saw what happened," he mumbled. "At the creek. We wanted to say… we're sorry."

Twinkleheels stared at them. He was so surprised he could not think of anything to say.

"It was our fault," Flash went on, finally meeting his gaze. "We were bragging. We dared you. It was a stupid thing to do. A pony your size had no business trying to haul a log like that."

"We just wanted to see what you would do," Dash added, his voice full of shame. "We never thought you would actually get hurt. When you went down, and Farmer Green was shouting… we felt awful."

Twinkleheels looked from one bay horse to the other. He saw no trickery in their eyes, only genuine regret. He remembered what Ebenezer had said; that the shoes had not made him a fool, but only showed him where the foolishness was hiding. He supposed that was true for Flash and Dash, too. Their pride had made them act foolishly, just as his had.

"It wasn't your fault," Twinkleheels said, and he was surprised to find that he meant it. "I was the one who did it. I wanted to show off. I thought my new shoes made me as strong as you." He looked down at his hoofs, one of them wrapped in cloth. "I learned that they don't."

A long silence filled the barn. Then Flash sighed, a great gust of air. "Well," he said. "Ebenezer says a lesson learned is worth more than a lesson told. I suppose we all learned one yesterday."

"We did," Dash agreed. "From now on, we'll stick to pulling wagons. And you should stick to pulling Johnnie."

Twinkleheels almost smiled. "That sounds like a good plan," he said.

The bays stood there for a minute more, as if they did not know what else to say. Then, as if they had both decided at the same moment, they turned and walked away, their steps much quieter than usual. Twinkleheels watched them go. The barn did not feel quite so lonely anymore.

XVIII A STORM IN THE BARN

Two more days passed. Johnnie was true to his word, visiting every morning and every afternoon with a carrot or an apple and a good deal of cheerful chatter. Farmer Green changed the wrapping on Twinkleheels' leg each day, murmuring words of encouragement. "It's looking better, little fellow," he would say. "The swelling is going down. Patience is the best medicine for a sprain."

Patience was a medicine that Twinkleheels found very hard to swallow. He was tired of his stall. He was tired of straw. He was tired of watching the other animals go about their business while he could do nothing but stand and wait. Even the sparrows who flitted in and out of the high barn windows seemed to be mocking him with their freedom.

On the third afternoon of his confinement, the world outside the barn began to change. The bright sunshine faded, and the sky, visible through the wide-open barn doors, turned a strange, yellowish grey. A new sort of stillness fell over the farm, a heavy quiet in which the chirping of crickets and the buzzing of bees suddenly stopped.

Ebenezer, who was in his stall across the aisle, lifted his head and sniffed the air. "Storm's coming," he said, his voice low and serious. "A big one, by the smell of it."

Soon, Farmer Green and Johnnie were hurrying all the animals into the shelter of the barn. The chickens were shooed in from the yard, clucking in protest. Old dog Spot trotted in and lay down in his favorite corner. Bright and Broad were brought in from the pasture, their eyes wide as they glanced at the ominous sky. The barn, which had seemed so large and empty to Twinkleheels, was now full of life and nervous energy.

Farmer Green slid the great wooden doors shut, plunging the barn into a dim twilight, lit only by the dusty shafts of light from the high windows. Then the storm broke.

It did not begin with a gentle pitter-patter. It began with a roar. The wind howled around the corners of the barn like a hungry wolf, and the rain came down not in drops, but in solid sheets, drumming against the roof so loudly that Twinkleheels could not even hear Ebenezer breathing.

A sudden, brilliant flash of lightning lit up the entire barn for a split second, followed almost immediately by a crack of thunder so loud it seemed to shake the very foundations. Twinkleheels jumped, his heart pounding in his chest. The chickens squawked in terror, and even the great oxen, Bright and Broad, shifted uneasily in their stalls.

"Easy now," came Ebenezer's calm voice through the din. "It is only noise. We are safe in here."

Twinkleheels tried to believe him. He pressed himself against the back wall of his stall, his ears twitching at every new sound. The storm raged on. The wind screamed and rattled the doors. The thunder cracked and boomed, rolling across the hills like a thousand giant wagons.

Then came a new sound. It was a sharp, splintering crack, followed by a tremendous crash from the direction of the hayloft. A gust of wind and rain suddenly swirled into the upper part of the barn.

"The loft door!" Farmer Green shouted, his voice barely audible above the storm. "The wind's torn it from its hinges!"

Twinkleheels could see him and Johnnie looking up into the dark, swirling chaos of the loft. Rain was pouring in, soaking the precious winter hay. Worse still, the broken door was hanging by a single hinge, swinging wildly in the wind, threatening to crash down into the stalls below.

"We have to get it down!" Farmer Green yelled. "Johnnie, stay back! It's too dangerous."

Twinkleheels watched, his own fear forgotten, as Farmer Green grabbed a long pole and tried to push at the swinging door. But the wind was too strong. The heavy door swung back and forth like a giant's plaything. He saw his master's grim face, slick with rain that was now driving into the main part of the barn.

"It's no good," Farmer Green said, lowering the pole. "I need to get a rope over it and pull it inside. Bright! Broad!"

He quickly led the two oxen out of their stalls. Twinkleheels watched, fascinated. The oxen were not afraid. They moved slowly and deliberately, their big, calm eyes fixed on Farmer Green. They seemed to understand that there was work to be done.

Farmer Green threw a rope up and over a beam, then skillfully looped it around a piece of the broken door. He brought the other end down and hitched it to the oxen's yoke.

"Now, boys," he said, his voice steady and commanding. "Pull easy. Easy now."

Bright and Broad leaned into their yoke. There was no slipping, no frantic struggle. They planted their feet, lowered their great heads, and pulled. Muscles bunched under their hides. For a moment, nothing happened. The door creaked and groaned, fighting against them. Then, with a slow, powerful surge, the oxen began to move backward, step by steady step.

The broken door was dragged inward, away from the fury of the wind, until it crashed down onto the floor of the hayloft with a deafening thud. The wind and rain no longer poured in.

Farmer Green rushed to the oxen and patted their broad necks. "Good boys!" he praised them. "Good work!"

Twinkleheels let out a breath he did not realize he had been holding. He stood on his three good legs, watching the scene. He saw Farmer Green, brave and quick-witted. He saw Johnnie, standing by with a lantern he had lit, ready to help. He saw the slow, immensely powerful oxen, who had done a job no horse could have done. He saw Ebenezer, standing quietly in his stall, a picture of calm reassurance for the more flighty animals.

Everyone had a part to play. Everyone had done their job. And he, Twinkleheels, had done nothing but stand in his stall and watch. For the first time, he did not feel jealous or useless. He felt… observant. He understood, in a way he never had before, how the farm worked. It was not about who was fastest or proudest. It was about everyone doing what they were suited for, especially when things went wrong. He thought of his own job, carrying Johnnie. It was not a big, dramatic job like pulling a broken door in a storm. But it was his job. And he suddenly felt a quiet longing to be well enough to do it again.

XIX THREE GOOD SHOES

The storm passed as quickly as it had arrived. The drumming on the roof softened to a gentle patter and then stopped altogether. A pale, watery sunlight broke through the clouds, and when Farmer Green slid the big doors open again, the world outside was washed clean and glistening. The air smelled of wet earth and fresh leaves.

In the days that followed, Twinkleheels' leg improved steadily. The swelling was almost gone, and the ache had faded to a slight stiffness. He was beginning to put a little weight on it. Farmer Green was pleased.

"One more day of rest," he declared one morning, after unwrapping the leg for the last time. "Then we'll see about getting you back in the sunshine. But we need to do something about this shoe." He tapped the iron on the pony's injured foot. "We can't have you walking unevenly. I'll have the blacksmith come out to the farm."

The idea of the blacksmith visiting him made Twinkleheels feel quite important. It was one thing to go to the smithy; it was quite another for the smithy, in a manner of speaking, to come to you.

The next afternoon, a wagon rattled into the farmyard. It was the blacksmith, a big, broad-shouldered man with a cheerful face and arms as thick as fence posts. He brought with him a small, portable forge and a box of tools. He set up his workplace right there in the barn aisle.

Twinkleheels was not frightened this time. He watched with great interest as the blacksmith built a small, hot fire. He did not jump at the clang of the hammer or the hiss of hot metal in a bucket of water. He knew what all the sounds meant now.

"Well, young fellow," the blacksmith said, running a gentle hand down Twinkleheels' sore leg. "Got yourself into a bit of a pickle, did you?"

Farmer Green explained what had happened. The blacksmith nodded knowingly. "Aye," he said. "Pride and new shoes can be a heady mix for a young one. We'll just take this one off for a while. Let the hoof breathe and the leg heal without any extra weight or pressure."

The blacksmith's hands were surprisingly gentle. He held Twinkleheels' foot carefully and, with a few quick, expert motions, pried the shoe off. It came away with a soft pop. Twinkleheels felt a strange lightness in that foot.

"There now," the blacksmith said, setting the hoof down. "You'll be walking on three shoes for a bit. It might feel a little odd, but it's better for you. We'll leave the others. No sense in taking off good iron."

He packed up his tools, put out his fire, and was soon rattling away down the lane. Twinkleheels stood in his stall, feeling the bare hoof on the cool straw. It did feel odd. Unbalanced.

The next morning, the sun was shining brightly, and the birds were singing with all their might. After breakfast, Johnnie Green came to the stall, his face beaming. In his hand, he held Twinkleheels' halter.

"Dad says you can come out today!" he announced. "Just for a little walk. No running!"

Twinkleheels could have burst with happiness. He pushed his nose eagerly into the halter. Johnnie led him slowly out of the stall and into the bright morning light. Twinkleheels blinked. The air was so fresh, the sky so blue! He took a step, then another.

*Clink. Clank. Clink.*

The sound was strange. With only three shoes, his gait had an uneven rhythm. One footfall was soft and dull; the other three were crisp and metallic. *Clink-clank-clink… thud. Clink-clank-clink… thud.*

He felt a bit self-conscious. What would the other horses think of his lopsided sound? He glanced over at the pasture fence, where Ebenezer, Flash, and Dash were watching him. Flash and Dash did not snicker. They just watched quietly.

Ebenezer ambled over to the fence. "How does it feel to be out in the world again?" the old horse asked.

"It feels wonderful!" Twinkleheels said. "But I sound funny."

Ebenezer smiled his wise, gentle smile. "That sound," he said, "is the sound of a lesson. It is not a perfect sound, because the lesson was not an easy one. Every time you hear it, you will remember not to mistake a new pair of shoes for a new pair of legs."

Twinkleheels thought about that. He took a few more steps, listening to his own uneven footfalls. *Clink-clank-clink… thud.* Ebenezer was right. It was a sound that told a story. It was the story of a foolish pony who had learned something important.

"Come on, Twinkleheels," Johnnie said, giving the lead rope a gentle tug. "Let's walk down to the mailbox and back. Slowly."

Twinkleheels fell into step beside his boy. He walked with his head held not high with pride, but at a comfortable, happy level. He was not trying to be an ox. He was not trying to be a draft horse. He was just Twinkleheels, a pony with a boy to carry, a slightly sore leg, and three good shoes. And as he walked down the familiar lane, the uneven rhythm of his hoofs no longer sounded strange or embarrassing. It sounded like progress. It sounded, in its own way, just right.

The white mare turned her head as far as it would go, which was not very far, and looked down her nose at him. "Sensible is as sensible does," she sniffed. "And a sensible horse would not jump and snort at the honest work of a smithy. It shows a lack of breeding."

"Now, now, miss," old Ebenezer rumbled from his place. "There's a first time for everything and for every horse. A little patience goes a long way. Why, I remember my first visit here. I was so frightened of the fire I nearly backed my master through the wall."

The mare gave a disdainful flick of her ears. "Some of us learn faster than others," she remarked. But she said no more, for just then the blacksmith finished with her last hoof, and her owner led her out into the daylight.

"Pay her no mind," Ebenezer said quietly to Twinkleheels. "She's known all over the county for her sharp tongue and her dull wits."

Twinkleheels felt a little better. But now it was his turn. The blacksmith, a big man with arms as thick as fence posts and a kind smile, came over to him. "Well now, little fellow," he said in a voice that was surprisingly gentle. "Let's see about getting you some traveling shoes."

Johnnie Green stood at Twinkleheels' head, stroking his neck and whispering encouraging words. "It's all right, boy. He won't hurt you."

Twinkleheels wanted to believe him. He remembered Ebenezer's advice. *Pay attention and obey orders.* So he stood as still as he could when the blacksmith ran a hand down his foreleg. "Lift!" the man said in a firm, quiet voice.

Twinkleheels was not sure what to do. He had never been asked to lift his foot in quite that way. He shifted his weight, feeling awkward.

"Just like you're going to take a step," Johnnie whispered.

The blacksmith was patient. He squeezed gently just above the fetlock. "Hup!" he said again. This time, Twinkleheels understood. He lifted his foot. The blacksmith caught it neatly and rested it on his leather-aproned knee.

Twinkleheels felt very strange, standing on three legs with a man holding his fourth foot. He wanted to pull it back, but Johnnie's hand was steady on his neck, and the blacksmith's grip was firm and confident.

First came a funny rasping tool that scraped and filed his hoof. *Scrape, scrape, scrape.* It made a tickling, vibrating feeling that went all the way up his leg. It did not hurt, but it was a very odd sensation. Then the blacksmith set his foot down and went over to the fiery forge. He picked out a small horseshoe with a long pair of tongs. It glowed a brilliant, angry red.

"Goodness!" Twinkleheels thought. "He's not going to put that hot thing on me!" He took a nervous step back.

"Whoa now, son," the blacksmith said calmly. He did not come near with the hot shoe. Instead, he held it near Twinkleheels' hoof, just for a moment, to measure the size. A puff of smoke rose with a strange, burnt smell, and Twinkleheels flinched. The smith took the shoe away at once and went to his anvil.

*Clang! Clang! Clang-clang-clang!* He beat the shoe with his hammer, sending a shower of sparks into the dusky air of the shop. Then came the hissing sound again as he cooled it. After a few trips back and forth, he seemed satisfied. He came back, picked up Twinkleheels' hoof again, and set the cool, dark shoe against it. It seemed to fit perfectly.

"Now for the nails," the blacksmith said, more to himself than to anyone else. He took a few small, thin nails from his apron. Twinkleheels' heart began to beat like a drum. This was the part he feared.

He felt the first nail being placed against his hoof. *Tap. Tap. Tap.* The blacksmith drove it in with light, careful blows. Twinkleheels braced himself for a terrible pain. But there was none! There was only a dull, knocking feeling. It did not hurt at all. He felt the nail come out the side of his hoof, high up where it was all hard and unfeeling. The blacksmith twisted off the sharp point and bent the end over smoothly with his hammer.

One by one, he drove the nails in, until the shoe was firmly attached. Then he set Twinkleheels' foot down on the floor.

*Clink.*

It made a sharp, metallic sound on the hard-packed dirt. Twinkleheels lifted the foot and put it down again. *Clink.* It felt heavier than before. It felt strong. It felt important.

He looked over at Ebenezer with wide, proud eyes. The old horse gave him a slow, approving nod.

The blacksmith moved to the next foot, and then the next, until all four of Twinkleheels' hoofs were shod. With each new shoe, Twinkleheels felt his confidence grow. He was no longer a barefoot pasture pony. He was a working horse, a traveling horse, with shoes just like Ebenezer's.

While the smith tended to Ebenezer's worn-down shoes, replacing the one he had cast and checking the others, Twinkleheels stood practicing. He would lift one foot and set it down. *Clink.* Then another. *Clink.* He loved the sound. It was the sound of being grown up.

Soon, both horses were finished. Farmer Green paid the blacksmith, and Johnnie untied Twinkleheels from the ring in the wall. "Come on, boy," he said proudly. "Let's go home."

Twinkleheels followed Johnnie out of the dim shop and into the bright afternoon. He blinked in the sun and tossed his head, making his new shoes clatter on a loose stone. It was a glorious sound!

XVII A CLATTER OF PRIDE

The journey home was very different from the journey to the village. Twinkleheels did not have to be led. He trotted alongside the buggy, his head held high. He made sure to step on every stone and hard patch of ground he could find, just to hear the music of his new shoes.

*Clink-clank, clink-clank, clink-clank.* It was a wonderful noise.

"You seem pleased with yourself," Ebenezer remarked, glancing over at him. The old horse's own gait was as steady and quiet as ever.

"I feel like a new pony!" Twinkleheels exclaimed. "These shoes make me feel like I could run to the moon and back before supper! I feel so much faster!"

Ebenezer smiled a little. "Shoes don't make you faster, little friend," he said. "They only protect your feet so you can travel farther. The speed comes from your own legs and your own good sense."

But Twinkleheels was too excited to pay much attention to Ebenezer's wisdom. He felt faster. He felt stronger. He felt taller. He was sure that even the bay horses, Flash and Dash, would have to respect him now.

When they arrived back at the farm, Johnnie led Twinkleheels into the barnyard. Instead of putting him right into his stall, he let him stand for a moment. "There now," Johnnie said, patting his neck. "Aren't you a fine-looking pony?"

Twinkleheels certainly thought so. He looked about, hoping someone was there to admire him. And as luck would have it, Bright and Broad, the two big oxen, were just being led from the sugar-bush toward the watering trough. They moved with their usual slow, ponderous rhythm, their great heads nodding with each step.

"Hello, Bright! Hello, Broad!" Twinkleheels called out, much more boldly than usual. He took a few prancing steps, making his shoes ring on the cobblestones near the barn door. "Did you notice anything new about me?"

Bright, the nigh ox, turned his great, placid eye toward the pony. "You seem to be making a good deal of noise," he said in his deep, rumbling voice.

"It's my new shoes!" Twinkleheels announced. "I've been to the blacksmith. I'm shod now. For traveling on hard roads."

Broad, the off ox, swished his tail. "Shoes are good," he stated simply. "Keep the stones from hurting." He did not seem at all impressed. To him, shoes were simply a tool, like a yoke or a chain. There was nothing to be excited about.

Twinkleheels felt a little disappointed. He had expected more of a reaction. "They don't just keep stones from hurting," he said, a bit defensively. "They make you steady. And fast!"

"We do not need to be fast," rumbled Bright. "We need to be strong. The deeper the mud, the slower we go. That is how you pull a load without getting stuck."

This was not the conversation Twinkleheels had hoped for. He wanted admiration, not a lecture on pulling loads through mud. Just then, he caught sight of Flash and Dash, the team of bay carriage horses, being led out of their stalls for a grooming. Here was his chance!

"Flash! Dash!" he called, trotting over toward them. "Look!" He lifted a front foot to show off the new, dark iron.

Flash, who was always looking for mischief, peered at the shoe. "Well, well," he snorted. "Look at the little pony, all grown up with iron on his toes. Do you think they'll make you any faster than a turtle?"

Dash, his brother, laughed. It was a high, whinnying sound. "He probably thinks he can race us now," he neighed.

Ordinarily, Twinkleheels would have been cowed by their teasing. But today, he was filled with the pride of his new shoes. "I could too!" he retorted. "I'll bet I'm just as fast as you are now!"

This was exactly what Flash had been hoping for. "Is that a challenge?" he said, his eyes gleaming with fun. "A race? From here to the old apple tree at the far end of the pasture?"

"A race!" echoed Dash. "What do you say, little pony?"

Twinkleheels' heart gave a little flutter. A part of him, a small, sensible part that sounded a bit like Ebenezer, told him this was a foolish idea. Flash and Dash were full-sized horses, known for their speed. But another, louder part of him, the part that was listening to the clink of his new shoes, could not resist.

"I say yes!" Twinkleheels declared boldly. "A race to the old apple tree!"

"It's a deal!" Flash cried. "As soon as we're unhaltered!"

A short while later, all three of them were turned out into the big pasture for the afternoon. Old Ebenezer was there, grazing peacefully near the fence.

"You're not really going to race those two, are you?" the old horse asked as Twinkleheels trotted past.

"I am!" said Twinkleheels. "And I'm going to show them a thing or two!"

"Be careful," Ebenezer warned. "Pride has a way of tripping a horse up, whether he has shoes or not. And you are not yet used to your new feet."

But Twinkleheels was already gone, cantering toward the starting line he and the bays had agreed upon. He felt the weight of the shoes on his feet. They did not feel light and magical now. They felt a bit heavy, a bit clumsy. But he pushed the thought away.

"Ready?" Flash called, pawing the ground.

"Ready!" Twinkleheels shouted back, trying to sound more confident than he felt.

"On the count of three!" Dash yelled. "One... two... THREE!"

They were off! Flash and Dash leaped forward with the powerful strides of experienced runners. Their legs stretched out, eating up the ground. Twinkleheels did his best to keep up. He pumped his short legs as fast as they would go. The wind whistled in his ears.

For a few moments, he felt like he was flying. The bays were ahead of him, but not by as much as he had feared. "I can do this!" he thought. He pushed himself harder, stretching his neck out, focusing on the distant apple tree.

He was running so hard, and with his head so full of the race, that he did not pay enough attention to the ground beneath him. The pasture was not a smooth racetrack. It was full of little dips and hollows, and here and there a half-buried stone.

His front left hoof, shod in its proud new iron, came down hard on the edge of one of these stones. The iron shoe, which was meant for a hard road, did not give way as a bare hoof might have. Instead, it caught the rock at a bad angle.

Twinkleheels felt a horrible, wrenching twist in his ankle. A sharp pain shot up his leg. He stumbled, his legs tangling beneath him. With a cry of surprise and pain, he went down, rolling over once in the soft grass before coming to a stop.

He lay there for a moment, stunned and breathless. Flash and Dash, hearing his cry, stopped their race and trotted back to him.

"Are you all right?" Flash asked, his teasing manner completely gone.

Twinkleheels tried to get up. He pushed himself onto three legs, but when he tried to put weight on his left front foot, a jolt of pain made him gasp. He looked down. His leg was not broken, but it was already beginning to swell at the ankle. And worse, his beautiful new shoe was gone. It had been torn clean off in the fall, and now lay upside down in the grass a few feet away, two of its nails bent into strange shapes.

Twinkleheels looked at the lost shoe, then at his own bare, throbbing foot. He was not a fine traveling horse. He was a foolish little pony with a sore leg, lying in the middle of the pasture. He had not even finished his first day with his new shoes before his pride had caused him to lose one. He looked at the concerned faces of the two bay horses and wished the ground would swallow him up.

It was lonely, being hurt. And it was embarrassing. He kept his eyes fixed on the spot where his lost shoe lay gleaming in the sun. It looked so useless now, so foolish.

XVII A SLOW RETURN

It seemed like a very long time before help arrived. Twinkleheels was beginning to feel stiff and cold, despite the afternoon sun. His ankle had swollen up so that it looked like a puffy apple stuck on the end of his leg.

At last he heard a familiar voice calling his name. "Twinkleheels! Where are you, boy?"

It was Johnnie Green. And right behind him was Farmer Green, carrying a halter. Dash trotted beside them, looking quite proud of his errand.

"Over here!" Flash whinnied, stamping a foot to get their attention.

Johnnie ran ahead of his father when he saw Twinkleheels standing on three legs. "Oh, Twinkleheels! What happened to you?" he cried, throwing his arms around the little pony's neck.

Twinkleheels leaned into the hug, feeling a great wave of relief. He was so glad to see his boy.

Farmer Green came up, his face serious. He ran a gentle hand down Twinkleheels's leg, his knowing fingers probing the swollen ankle. Twinkleheels flinched, but he stood as still as he could.

"Well, now," Farmer Green said in a quiet voice. "It seems your new shoes were a bit too much for you." He walked over and picked up the cast-off shoe, turning it over in his hand. "You've given yourself a nasty sprain, little fellow. And you have pulled a shoe clean off. That takes some doing."

He looked from the shoe to Twinkleheels's unhappy face, and then he looked at Flash and Dash, who were standing by looking guilty.

"I suppose there was a race," Farmer Green said. It was not a question.

Flash hung his head. Dash shuffled his feet in the grass.

"We did not mean for him to get hurt," Dash mumbled. "It was just a bit of fun."

"Fun can turn to trouble pretty quickly when you are not careful," Farmer Green said. He was not angry; he sounded a bit sad. "A pasture is not a racetrack, and a new shoe is not a toy. It is a tool. You must learn to use your tools properly."

He turned back to Twinkleheels. "Well, we cannot leave you out here. We must get you back to the barn."

He looped the halter over Twinkleheels's head. "Now, Johnnie, you lead him. And walk very, very slowly. I will walk behind him. Flash, Dash, you two can go on ahead. You have done enough for one day."

The two bay horses looked as if they wanted to say something more, but they turned and trotted away, their heads low.

The journey back to the barn was the longest, most painful walk of Twinkleheels's life. Every step was a fresh agony. He had to hop on three legs, leaning on Johnnie for support. Farmer Green walked behind, ready to catch him if he stumbled. It took them a very long time.

When they finally reached the barnyard, old Ebenezer was standing by the door, waiting for them. He had seen them coming from a long way off. He took one look at Twinkleheels's three-legged hop and his swollen ankle, and his wise old eyes were full of understanding.

"Ah," he said softly. "So the new shoes wanted to run before they had learned to walk."

He did not say it in a scolding way. He said it as if it were the most natural thing in the world. Twinkleheels felt a hot blush of shame creep up his neck. Ebenezer had known. He had known all along what might happen.

Johnnie led Twinkleheels into his stall. It was a relief to be back in the familiar, safe space. Farmer Green came in with a bucket of cold water and some soft cloths. He gently bathed the sore ankle, his big hands surprisingly tender. Then he wrapped it in a thick bandage.

"There now," he said, giving Twinkleheels a pat on the rump. "You will have to stay put for a few days. No more racing for you for a while. Just rest and good sense."

He filled Twinkleheels's manger with fresh hay and a measure of sweet oats. But Twinkleheels was too tired and too sore to eat. He just stood there on three legs, his head hanging, and wished he could turn back the clock to that morning, before he had ever heard the word "race".

XVIII A STALL FULL OF THOUGHTS

The next few days were very long and very quiet. Twinkleheels stood in his stall, watching the sun make slow patterns on the barn floor. His ankle was still sore and swollen, and Farmer Green had ordered him to stay off it as much as possible. It was dreadfully boring.

He could hear the other animals outside. He heard the chickens clucking and scratching in the dust. He heard old dog Spot barking at a squirrel. He heard the happy shouts of Johnnie Green playing in the hayloft. But most of all, he heard the sounds of Flash and Dash as they were led out to the pasture each morning. He heard their hooves thudding on the soft earth, and sometimes he heard them whinny to each other. He missed the pasture. He even missed the two bay horses.

He spent a lot of time thinking. He thought about his beautiful new shoes, and how proud he had been. He thought about how that pride had made him foolish. He had been so eager to prove he was as good as the big horses that he had forgotten he was just a small pony. He had forgotten Farmer Green's words about oxen being slow but sure-footed.

Ebenezer was a great comfort to him. The old horse stood in the next stall, and he seemed to know just when Twinkleheels was feeling his lowest.

"A little rest is a good thing for a sprain," Ebenezer said one afternoon, as Twinkleheels shifted his weight uncomfortably. "It is also a good thing for a head full of foolish notions."

"I was very foolish," Twinkleheels admitted in a small voice. "I thought the shoes would make me fast and important. I just wanted to show Flash and Dash that I was not just a little pony."

Ebenezer chewed his hay thoughtfully for a moment. "There is nothing wrong with wanting to be good at something," he said. "The trouble comes when you try to be something you are not. A pony has his own work to do in the world; and it is just as important as the work of a big horse. You are strong and nimble. Johnnie can ride you to places where a big horse could not easily go. That is your strength."

He paused and looked at Twinkleheels with his kind, dark eyes. "As for Flash and Dash... well, they are young. They like to race. But there is more to being a horse than just running fast."

Just then, there was a soft shuffling sound at the barn door. Twinkleheels looked up and saw two familiar heads poking into the dim light. It was Flash and Dash. They stood there uncertainly, as if they were not sure if they were welcome.

"We, ah, we were just passing," Flash said, looking at his hooves.

"We saw some very nice clover near the fence," Dash added. "Very sweet. We thought... well, we thought you might like some."

He had a large mouthful of fresh, green clover, dotted with little pink flowers. He came forward shyly and dropped it into Twinkleheels's manger.

Twinkleheels stared at the clover, and then at the two big horses. He did not know what to say.

"Thank you," he managed at last. "That is very kind of you."

"It was our fault you got hurt," Flash said, finally looking him in the eye. "We should not have dared you to a race. We were just showing off."

"I wanted to race," Twinkleheels said honestly. "I was showing off, too. I wanted to beat you."

Dash let out a little snort, which might have been a laugh. "You never would have beaten us," he said. And then he quickly added, "But you are very fast for your size. You gave us a good run for a minute there."

Twinkleheels found himself smiling. "Maybe when my leg is better," he said, "you could teach me how to run in a pasture without falling over stones."

"It is a deal!" said Flash. "We will make a proper pasture-runner out of you yet."

They stayed for a while, talking in quiet voices. They told Twinkleheels about the fat green flies by the creek and the new calf in the far meadow. It was not exciting news, but it was news from the outside world, and Twinkleheels was grateful for it.

When they finally left, Twinkleheels felt much better. His ankle still hurt, but his heart felt lighter. He nibbled at the sweet clover they had brought him and looked over at Ebenezer.

The old horse was watching him with a gentle smile. "It seems," he said, "that you have lost a shoe, but you have found a pair of friends."

And Twinkleheels knew that he was right.

XIX WISDOM AND MEMORY

"Tell me about when you were young," Twinkleheels said to Ebenezer a few days later. The swelling in his ankle had gone down, and he was now able to put a little weight on it. The boredom, however, was worse than ever. "Were you ever foolish, like me?"

Ebenezer stopped chewing and a faraway look came into his eyes. "Was I ever foolish?" he repeated slowly. "My dear young friend, I was the king of foolishness. I believe I invented new kinds of it."

Twinkleheels looked at the dignified old horse in disbelief. It was hard to imagine Ebenezer as anything but wise and calm.

"Tell me," Twinkleheels urged.

"Very well," Ebenezer said, settling himself more comfortably in his stall. "It happened a long, long time ago. I was not much older than you are now, though I was bigger, of course. Farmer Green's father was the farmer then. And like you, I had just been given my first set of proper shoes. Oh, I was proud! I thought myself the finest horse in the whole county."

He paused, and a soft chuckle rumbled in his chest. "There was a mare in the next pasture, a pretty little thing with a white star on her forehead. Her name was Daisy. I wanted to impress her. So one day, while all the humans were in the farmhouse for their noon-day meal, I decided to show her what a fine jumper I was."

"There was a stone wall between our pastures. It was not a very high wall, but it was old and some of the stones were loose. I had seen the hunters clear it many times, and it looked easy enough. So I took a long run at it. I was thinking so much about how grand I would look sailing through the air that I did not pay any attention to my footing."

"You fell!" Twinkleheels guessed.

"Worse than that," Ebenezer said. "I stumbled on the takeoff. I did not get enough height. My front hooves caught on the top of the wall, and I came down right in the middle of it with a terrible crash. Stones went flying everywhere. I scraped my legs, I cut my nose, and I was tangled up in a pile of rocks. And to make it all worse, I was stuck. Half of me was in one pasture, and half in the other, with a broken wall underneath me."

Twinkleheels could not help but giggle at the picture. "What did you do?"

"I did the only thing I could do," Ebenezer said with great dignity. "I whinnied for help. And who do you think came running? Not just the farmer, but the pretty mare, Daisy. She stood there and looked at me with her big, curious eyes. I have never felt so foolish in all my life. I, the great jumper, was pinned down by a little stone wall."

"Did you get in a lot of trouble?" Twinkleheels asked.

"Oh, yes. The farmer was not pleased about his wall. But he was a kind man. He and his sons spent an hour carefully moving stones to free me. I had a lot of cuts and bruises, and my pride was hurt worst of all. I had to spend a week in the barn, just like you. And Daisy never did look at me in quite the same way again."

Ebenezer was quiet for a long moment. Twinkleheels was quiet, too, thinking about the story. It made him feel much better to know that even the great Ebenezer had made mistakes.

"Pride is a tricky thing," the old horse said at last. "A little bit makes you hold your head up high. But too much makes you blind to the stones at your feet." He looked over at Twinkleheels's bandaged leg. "Those shoes you are so proud of; they are not for racing in a field. They are for pulling a buggy on a hard road, to keep your hooves from wearing down. They are for work. There is a right tool for every job, and a right time for every thing."

Twinkleheels nodded slowly. He was beginning to understand. He looked at his own bare hoof, and then at the three shod ones. They were not medals to be proud of. They were part of a uniform for a job; a job he had not yet learned how to do.

"Thank you for telling me the story, Ebenezer," he said softly.

"You are welcome," the old horse replied. "Sometimes the best lessons are the ones that leave a little scar."
