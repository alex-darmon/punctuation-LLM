I A CURIOUS CONTRAPTION

Chatter-paws the squirrel was quite certain that no creature in the whole wide meadow was as busy as he. He had just finished burying a perfect acorn at the foot of the great maple, patting the earth down neatly with his little paws. "There!" he said to a passing ladybug. "That makes one hundred and seven. The winter will not catch me unprepared!"

He was about to scamper off in search of number one hundred and eight when he saw young Polly Day come out of the farmhouse. She was carrying a little wooden house with glass sides, and she hung it carefully from a low branch of the crabapple tree.

"Now what can that be?" Chatter-paws wondered. Curiosity got the better of his important work. He whisked up the trunk of the crabapple and crept along the branch, his nose twitching. Inside the little house were hundreds and hundreds of tiny black seeds. Just then, a bold chickadee landed on the perch and began pecking away.

"Well, I never!" Chatter-paws chattered indignantly. "Getting your dinner without even having to look for it! What a lazy fellow!"

The chickadee paid him no mind at all. And soon a nuthatch and a finch joined the feast. Chatter-paws flicked his tail in disgust. He felt that such an easy meal was hardly proper. It took skill to find food! It took cleverness and a good memory! This strange contraption, he decided, was nothing but a cheat.

II A BIT OF ADVICE

That evening, as the moon rose over the barn, Chatter-paws paid a visit to old Bartholomew, a wise badger who lived in a tidy burrow under the roots of the oldest oak.

"It simply isn't fair," the little squirrel complained, pacing back and forth. "Polly Day has given those birds a free dinner. They just sit there and eat and eat! They don't have to work for it as I do."

Bartholomew the badger, who was polishing a smooth river stone, looked up with his kind, knowing eyes. "And does their good fortune make your own acorns any less tasty?" he asked in his slow, rumbling voice.

"No, but... but it's the principle of the thing!" Chatter-paws insisted. "I earn my keep. I am an honest worker. They are nothing but beggars!"

"Ah," said Bartholomew. "I once knew a hard winter when the snow was so deep and crusted that no one could dig. Not even me. My own stores were buried beyond my reach. It was a hungry time, a very hungry time indeed. Then, the farmer's boy slipped on the ice and dropped a whole sack of corn. It split wide open. Was I too proud to eat that corn, Chatter-paws?"

The squirrel stopped his pacing. "Well... I suppose not," he admitted.

"A full belly is a blessing, no matter how it is filled," the badger rumbled. "You should not look down on a gift, little friend. You never know when you might be glad of one yourself."

Chatter-paws nodded, but in his heart he was not convinced. "I am much too clever to be caught by a hard frost," he thought to himself. "My winter pantry is the finest in the meadow." And with a flick of his tail, he thanked the badger and scampered back to his own nest, feeling quite sure of himself.

III A LESSON LEARNED

For two days, Chatter-paws ignored the little house in the crabapple tree. He went about his business, gathering nuts and feeling very proud of his growing hoard. But on the third morning, he awoke to a world that was stiff and white. A hard frost had come in the night, freezing the ground as solid as a rock.

Chatter-paws scurried down the tree to dig up a breakfast acorn. He dug and he scratched, but his claws only scraped uselessly against the iron-hard earth. He tried another spot, and another. It was no use! All one hundred and seven of his fine acorns were locked away beneath a frozen lid.

A cold fear began to creep into his heart. He sat shivering on a branch, his stomach rumbling with emptiness. Just then, he heard a cheerful "chick-a-dee-dee-dee!" He looked over and saw his little neighbor from the other day, feasting happily at the little house full of seeds. The chickadee looked quite plump and content.

Chatter-paws watched for a long time. His pride wrestled with his hunger. "Beggars!" he had called them. But oh, those seeds looked so very delicious! Finally, he could stand it no longer. He crept timidly along the branch towards the feeder.

The chickadee looked at him but did not fly away. "Is there room for one more?" Chatter-paws asked in a very small voice.

"There is always room!" the chickadee chirped.

Chatter-paws hopped onto the perch. He took a tiny black seed in his paws. It was an oil-rich sunflower seed, and it tasted wonderful. He ate another, and another. He had never felt so grateful in all his life. "Thank you," he said to the chickadee. "And thank you to Polly Day, too."

He felt a little bit foolish, remembering his proud words to Bartholomew. The old badger had been right, after all. A gift was a wonderful thing, and a full belly was truly a blessing.

The white mare tossed her head. Her eyes, which were a pale and watery blue, widened in surprise. She had clearly expected the little pony to tremble before her, not to answer back. "Well, I never!" she sniffed. "Such impertinence! I am Snowflake, and if you must know, I belong to the doctor. I am a lady of refinement, and I know a spoiled colt when I see one."

"I am not a colt!" Twinkleheels retorted, his small body quivering with indignation. "And I am not spoiled! I am a pony, and my name is Twinkleheels!"

"A silly name for a silly creature," Snowflake said with a disdainful flick of her ears.

Before Twinkleheels could think of another reply, old Ebenezer spoke. His voice was calm and steady, like the deep rumble of a distant wagon. "That is quite enough, Snowflake," he said. "My young friend is here on important business, just as you are. There is no need for unkind words in the smithy. We are all here to be made more comfortable, are we not?"

Snowflake looked at Ebenezer. She could not so easily dismiss the big, dignified horse. She gave a little huff of air through her nostrils. "He is much too young to be shod," she muttered, though with less conviction than before. "He will make a great fuss."

"We shall see," said Ebenezer quietly.

Just then a big man in a leather apron, with arms as thick as tree branches, came over. His face was smudged with soot, but his eyes were kind. This was Mr. Irons, the blacksmith. He had finished with Snowflake's front feet. "There now, old girl," he said to the mare in a cheerful voice. "Just the back two to go. Stand easy." He patted her flank, and she, for all her airs, seemed to quiet down under his touch.

Johnnie Green had been holding Twinkleheels' halter, and he stroked the pony's nose. "Don't you mind her, Twinkleheels," he whispered. "She's just cross."

Twinkleheels watched as Mr. Irons finished with Snowflake. The blacksmith worked quickly and surely. He lifted each of her hind hoofs, pried off the old, worn shoe with a special tool, and then cleaned and trimmed the hoof with a sharp knife and a rasp. The sound of the rasp, a rough zzz-zzz-zzz, made Twinkleheels' ears twitch. Snowflake stood perfectly still through it all. When her new shoes were nailed on, she was led out of the smithy by the doctor's boy, who had been waiting. She cast one last haughty look at Twinkleheels as she left, as if to say, "Now let us see how you manage."

"All right, Ebenezer, you're next," Farmer Green said.

Mr. Irons smiled. "Well, Ebenezer, my old friend. Did you lose another one?"

Ebenezer seemed to nod his head. "The roads are hard and the winter has been long," he seemed to say.

Mr. Irons chuckled and led Ebenezer to the center of the earthen floor. Twinkleheels watched with great interest. He wanted to see everything, so he would know just what to expect. Ebenezer was as calm as if he were standing in his own stall. He lifted his foot the very moment Mr. Irons touched his leg, holding it up patiently for the blacksmith to examine.

"Ah, you've cast the off fore," Mr. Irons declared. He took his tongs and with a mighty tug, pulled the old, bent nail from the hoof where the shoe had been. Then he went to work on the other three. With a few skilled movements, he pried off each shoe and dropped it into a metal bucket with a loud clank.

Twinkleheels flinched at the sound, but Ebenezer did not so much as blink.

"Now for the trimming," the blacksmith said, more to himself than to anyone else. He took up his rasp and began to smooth the edges of Ebenezer's big hoofs. A fine, dusty powder fell to the floor. To Twinkleheels, it smelled strange, like dry, burnt toast. He wrinkled his nose.

"Does that hurt?" he whispered to Ebenezer when the blacksmith moved to the other side.

"Not at all," Ebenezer replied in a low voice. "It is like when Johnnie Green's mother files her fingernails. It merely tidies things up. A well trimmed hoof is a comfortable hoof."

After the trimming, Mr. Irons went to a rack on the wall where dozens of new horseshoes of all sizes were hanging. He selected four large ones and brought them over. He placed one against Ebenezer's hoof, squinting one eye to check the fit. "A bit wide in the heel," he grunted.

He took the shoe over to the forge, that fiery table that had so frightened Twinkleheels. With a long pair of tongs, he thrust the iron shoe into the heart of the glowing coals. He pumped a great handle called a bellows, and the fire roared, turning from red to a brilliant, sun-bright yellow. The shoe began to glow, first a dull red, then cherry bright.

When it was hot enough, Mr. Irons whisked it out with his tongs and laid it upon the anvil. Clang! Clang! Clang! His hammer rose and fell, sending a shower of bright sparks dancing into the smoky air. Twinkleheels jumped at the noise, but he remembered what Ebenezer had said. He forced himself to stand still, though his heart was beating like a drum. The ringing sound filled the whole shop. In a few moments, the blacksmith stopped. He carried the hot shoe back to Ebenezer and held it for a bare second against the hoof.

A puff of white, foul-smelling smoke rose up, and Twinkleheels snorted in alarm. Sssssssss! It was a sound and a smell he had never known.

"Easy, little one," Farmer Green said, laying a hand on his neck. "He is only testing the fit. It does not hurt him. The hoof is thick."

Ebenezer stood as solid as a stone statue. Mr. Irons nodded, satisfied, and plunged the still-glowing shoe into the tub of water. There was that great, loud hiss again, and a cloud of steam billowed up to the rafters. Twinkleheels was beginning to understand. The fire, the clang, and the hiss were all part of the work.

Mr. Irons took the cooled shoe and a handful of nails. He placed the shoe carefully on Ebenezer's hoof. Then he took a nail, placed its point in one of the holes, and with a few short, sharp taps of his hammer, he drove it home. Tap-tap-tack! Tap-tap-tack! He did this again and again, eight times in all. Then he took a special tool and twisted off the sharp points of the nails where they poked out from the side of the hoof. He filed them smooth.

He shod all four of Ebenezer's feet in this way. It seemed to take a very long time. Through it all, the old horse waited with perfect patience, a model of good behavior. When the last nail was driven, Mr. Irons patted his rump. "There you are, old fellow. Good as new."

Farmer Green paid the blacksmith. "Now for the little one," he said, turning to Johnnie. "Bring him over."

XVII
THE FIRST NEW SHOES

Twinkleheels' heart gave a great leap. Now it was his turn. He had watched Ebenezer, and he knew that it did not hurt. He knew what all the noises meant. And yet, his legs felt suddenly stiff and unwilling to move. The smithy, which had begun to seem like an interesting place, was once more a dark and frightening cavern of strange smells and loud sounds.

Johnnie Green gave a gentle tug on his halter. "Come on, Twinkleheels. It's your turn to be a big pony."

Twinkleheels took a hesitant step, then another. He walked to the center of the floor and stood where Ebenezer had been. He looked over at his old friend, who was now standing quietly by the door. Ebenezer gave him a look that seemed to say, "You will do just fine."

Mr. Irons came over and walked all around Twinkleheels, looking him over from head to tail. "Well now, what have we here?" he said, his voice softer than it had been for Ebenezer. "A fine little Shetland pony. You're a handsome fellow, aren't you?" He ran a hand down Twinkleheels' back, his touch firm and sure. "Going to get your first shoes today, are you? It is a very important day."

Twinkleheels stood as still as he could.

"Let's have a look at those feet," the blacksmith said. He bent down and reached for Twinkleheels' left front leg. Twinkleheels remembered what Ebenezer had said about obeying orders, and he tried to lift his foot. But he was nervous, and he lifted it too high and too fast, pulling it away.

"Whoa there, little fellow. Easy does it," Mr. Irons said calmly. He did not get cross. He simply took hold of the pony's leg again, more firmly this time. "Just let me hold it," he said. "I'll do all the work."

This time Twinkleheels let him. The blacksmith cradled the small hoof in his big hand. It felt very strange to have his foot held that way. He could feel the hard calluses on the man's palm. "My, my," Mr. Irons murmured. "Never had a trim in your life, I'll wager. We'll have to fix that right up."

He set the hoof down and fetched his tools. He used a smaller knife and a smaller rasp than he had for Ebenezer. He picked up the foot again, and Twinkleheels braced himself. Then came the scraping of the knife, cleaning out the dirt and overgrown parts of his hoof. It tickled a little. He wanted to snatch his foot away, but he thought of the haughty white mare, Snowflake, and how still she had stood. He would not give her the satisfaction of knowing he had made a fuss. He would be as brave as Ebenezer.

Then came the rasp. Zzz-zzz-zzz. The sound was right next to his ear. He flattened his ears back, but he did not pull away. Johnnie stood by his head, stroking his neck and talking to him in a low, soothing voice. "Good boy, Twinkleheels. You're being so brave. The bravest pony in the whole county."

Twinkleheels felt a little thrill of pride. He _was_ being brave!

When the first hoof was trimmed, Mr. Irons set it down and said, "There. That's how it's done." He did the other three feet, and with each one, Twinkleheels grew a little more confident. He was beginning to feel quite professional about the whole business.

"Now for the shoes," Mr. Irons announced. He went to his rack, but he did not find any shoes small enough. He came back shaking his head. "Nothing in stock will fit this little gentleman," he declared. "We'll have to make them from scratch."

Twinkleheels felt a fresh wave of importance. His shoes would be special! Made just for him!

The blacksmith took a long, thin bar of iron. He measured Twinkleheels' hoof with a piece of string, then measured the iron bar. He marked it with a piece of chalk and put it in the fire. The bellows roared, the coals glowed, and the iron bar turned a brilliant orange.

Then came the song of the anvil. Clang-a-lang-clang! Mr. Irons was not just shaping a shoe; he was creating one. He turned the glowing iron this way and that, hammering it into a perfect little curve. He punched the nail holes with a special tool. It was a wonderful thing to watch. He made one shoe, cooled it with a great hiss, and then started on the next.

One by one, four perfect, tiny horseshoes were made and laid out in a row on the dirt floor. They were the finest, neatest little shoes Twinkleheels had ever seen. He stared at them, filled with admiration. Those were _his_ shoes.

"All right, son," Mr. Irons said to him. "Time for the fitting."

He picked up the first shoe and the first hoof. He held his breath as the blacksmith placed the cold iron against his newly trimmed foot. It felt strange and heavy. Then Mr. Irons took a nail. It looked very long and very sharp. This was the part Twinkleheels had dreaded most. He remembered his question to Ebenezer: "Does it hurt?"

The blacksmith held the nail in place. Tap. A light tap. Twinkleheels trembled. Tap-tap. A little harder. Tack! The nail was going in. Twinkleheels waited for the pain, for the terrible prick that Ebenezer had mentioned. But nothing came. He felt only a slight jarring, a vibration that went up his leg. There was no feeling in his hoof at all!

It did not hurt! It really did not hurt!

He let out a long breath he did not know he had been holding. The blacksmith drove in the rest of the nails, six for each little shoe, and with every tap of the hammer, Twinkleheels' confidence grew. He was being shod! He was wearing shoes!

When the last nail on the last hoof was smoothed down, Mr. Irons gave Twinkleheels a final, friendly pat. "There you are, my boy. All done. You stood better than many a full-grown horse I've shod. You're a credit to your owner."

Johnnie Green beamed with pride. "I told you he was brave!"

Farmer Green smiled. "He did well. He's a good pony."

Twinkleheels felt as if he might burst with happiness. He wanted to prance and kick up his heels. He lifted one foot, then the other, feeling the new weight. They felt odd, but wonderful. He took a step. Clink. He took another. Clink. The sound on the hard earth of the smithy floor was a new and delightful music.

"Thank you, Mr. Irons," Farmer Green said, handing him some money.

"My pleasure," the blacksmith replied, wiping his hands on his apron. "You bring him back when these are worn, you hear?"

Johnnie untied Twinkleheels' halter from the ring in the wall. "Let's go home, Twinkleheels," he said. "We'll show everyone your new shoes."

As they led him out into the bright winter sunshine, Twinkleheels held his head higher than ever before. He passed Ebenezer, who gave a low, soft nicker of approval. Twinkleheels felt ten feet tall. He had faced the fire and the noise and the sharp-looking nails, and he had not flinched. Well, he had not flinched much, anyway.

XVIII
THE SOUND OF PRIDE

The journey home was very different from the journey to the village. Twinkleheels was no longer being led behind the buggy. Farmer Green had decided he had earned a promotion. "Let him walk beside you, Johnnie," he had said. "He can practice walking with his new shoes on."

So Twinkleheels trotted alongside Ebenezer, matching him stride for stride. Or rather, he tried to. His legs felt different. The iron shoes made his feet feel heavy, but also strong and solid. Each time a hoof came down on the hard-packed, frozen road, it made a crisp, ringing click.

Click-clack! Click-clack!

Ebenezer's pace was steady and slow, his own new shoes making a deeper, more resonant clop-clop. The two sounds together made a fine rhythm in the still, cold air.

Click-clack, clop-clop. Click-clack, clop-clop.

Twinkleheels listened to the sound of his own feet with immense satisfaction. It was a grown-up sound. It was the sound of a working horse, a horse of importance. He was no longer a simple pasture pony with soft, bare feet. He was Twinkleheels, the shod pony!

He began to experiment. He lifted his knees a little higher with each step, just to hear the sound more clearly. He was strutting, and he knew it. He remembered how Ebenezer had compared him to Turkey Proudfoot, and now he felt he had every right to be proud.

"You are stepping very high," Ebenezer observed, his voice full of amusement.

"I am getting used to my new shoes," Twinkleheels said in a dignified manner. "They feel very fine. Very strong."

"They are," Ebenezer agreed. "They will protect your feet from stones and ice. You will be able to travel farther and faster on hard roads now. A good set of shoes is a great blessing."

Twinkleheels thought about this. He had only been thinking of how proud they made him feel. He had not thought about how useful they would be. "I suppose I will not slip so much on the ice, either," he said.

"The blacksmith put little sharp bits on the heels, did he not? Those are called calks," Ebenezer explained. "They will give you a good grip. But you must still be careful. Ice is always treacherous."

Twinkleheels promised that he would be careful. He felt very wise and sensible, discussing such things with Ebenezer. He felt he had crossed a great line that day. He had entered a new world, the world of shod horses.

As they turned into the lane that led to the farm, old dog Spot came bounding out to meet them, barking a happy welcome. "You're back! You're back!" he yapped.

"We are!" Twinkleheels called out, and he took a few quick, high steps on the frozen ground of the lane. Click-ety-clack!

Spot stopped barking and cocked his head, his ears perked up. He stared at Twinkleheels' feet. "What is that noise?" he asked, trotting closer for a sniff. He put his cold nose right down to one of the new shoes. "You have metal on your feet!" he exclaimed in surprise.

"Of course," said Twinkleheels, trying to sound as if he had worn shoes his whole life. "These are my new shoes. I have just been to the blacksmith's shop."

Spot was very impressed. He ran in circles around Twinkleheels, barking with excitement. "New shoes! New shoes! Twinkleheels has new shoes!"

By the time they reached the barnyard, Spot had announced the news to anyone who might be listening. The chickens scattered, clucking, and a few sleepy cows in the yard turned their heads to look.

Farmer Green unhitched Ebenezer from the buggy while Johnnie led Twinkleheels toward the barn door. Standing there, yoked together and waiting to be led to the woodlot, were the two great oxen, Bright and Broad. They were huge and slow, and they looked down at Twinkleheels with their great, placid brown eyes.

"So," rumbled Bright, in a voice as deep as the earth. "The little one makes a great clatter."

"He has new shoes," said Broad, who was always a bit more observant. "They are very small."

Twinkleheels did not mind that they called his shoes small. He was proud of them. "I do have new shoes," he announced. "I can walk on the icy roads now without slipping." He remembered what Farmer Green had said about oxen being good in the snow. "Perhaps I shall be as sure-footed as you are now," he added boldly.

Bright and Broad both blinked their slow, long-lashed eyelids. "Perhaps," said Bright. "But can you pull a sled of maple logs through a three-foot drift?"

"Well... no," Twinkleheels had to admit.

"Then you are not as sure-footed as we are," stated Broad, and that seemed to be the end of the conversation. The two great beasts lowered their heads and stood waiting patiently.

Twinkleheels felt a little bit deflated, but Johnnie gave his halter a friendly tug. "Come on," he said. "Let's get you in your warm stall. You've had a big day."

Johnnie led him into the familiar, comforting dimness of the barn. He took him to his own stall right next to Ebenezer's. After he unbuckled the halter, he gave Twinkleheels a lovely, vigorous brushing and a generous measure of oats in his feed box.

Twinkleheels crunched his oats happily, the weariness from his exciting day beginning to settle into his bones. The new shoes felt heavy now that he was standing still.

Ebenezer was in the next stall, munching his own dinner. For a while, the only sounds were the quiet rustling of hay and the contented crunching of oats.

"Well," Ebenezer said at last. "How do you like being a shod pony?"

"I like it very much," Twinkleheels said. "I was a little bit frightened at first. But you were right. It did not hurt at all. And Mr. Irons was very kind."

"He is a good man and a good smith," said Ebenezer. "He knows that a frightened animal is a dangerous animal. Kindness works better than force every time."

"That white mare, Snowflake, was not very kind," Twinkleheels remembered.

"Some horses, like some people, carry their heads so high they cannot see the worth of those around them," the old horse said wisely. "You must not let her foolish words trouble you. You were very brave today."

Twinkleheels felt a warm glow spread through him that had nothing to do with the oats in his belly. Praise from Ebenezer was worth more than praise from anyone else.

"Thank you for coming with me, Ebenezer," he said earnestly. "I do not think I could have been brave if you had not been there to tell me what to expect."

"You would have managed," Ebenezer said kindly. "But it is always easier to walk a new road with a friend who has traveled it before."

Twinkleheels swished his tail. He felt very content. The barn was warm and safe. His belly was full. And on his feet were four new, shiny, wonderful shoes. He lifted a foot, just to feel the solid weight of it once more before he settled into his bedding for a long nap. It was a good feeling. It was the feeling of a pony who was growing up.

The white mare, whose name Johnnie Green's father had called Snowflake, turned her head away with a disdainful flick of her tail.

Ebenezer made a low, rumbling sound in his chest. "Pay her no mind, Twinkleheels," he advised. "Some horses believe a sharp tongue makes them sound important. It only makes them sound unhappy."

Twinkleheels tried to heed the old horse's advice, but the mare's words had stung him. He was not a child! He was a pony who had just survived a snowdrift and was now being shod for the very first time. He felt quite grown up indeed.

"Now for the little fellow," the blacksmith, Mr. Irons, said in a booming, cheerful voice. He was a big man with arms as thick as tree branches and a kind smile. He came over and ran a gentle hand down Twinkleheels' leg. "Let's see what we've got here."

Twinkleheels remembered Ebenezer's advice: pay attention and obey orders. He stood as still as he could, though his heart was thumping like a drum. Mr. Irons lifted one of his front feet. He held it firmly between his knees and began to clean it with a special curved knife. It tickled a little, but it did not hurt. Then he took a tool called a rasp and filed the hoof smooth. The sound was like a loud scratching.

"You're a good lad," Mr. Irons said. "Standing quieter than some full-grown horses I know." He winked at Farmer Green.

Next, he went to the forge. He took a small horseshoe from a hook on the wall and pushed it into the glowing coals. After a few moments, he pulled it out with a long pair of tongs. The shoe was a brilliant, fiery orange. Twinkleheels watched, his eyes wide, as Mr. Irons laid the glowing shoe on the anvil and began to hammer it.

CLANG! CLANG! CLANG!

The noise was deafening, and sparks flew in every direction like angry fireflies. Twinkleheels flinched, but he held his ground, remembering that Ebenezer had told him this would happen.

Mr. Irons brought the hot shoe over and held it against Twinkleheels' hoof for just a second. There was a puff of smoke and a strange, sharp smell. Twinkleheels wanted to snatch his foot back, but the blacksmith's grip was firm and steady. "Just checking the fit," he rumbled. He plunged the shoe into the water tub with a loud HISSSSSS!

Finally, the shoe was cool. Mr. Irons held it against the pony's hoof again and took several small nails from his leather apron. Twinkleheels held his breath. This was the part he had worried about. One by one, the blacksmith drove the nails through the little holes in the shoe and into the hard outer wall of his hoof. It made a small tapping sound: tap-tap-tap. And it did not hurt at all. Not even a tiny bit.

When all four shoes were on, Mr. Irons gave Twinkleheels a pat on the flank. "There you are, young fellow," he said. "Ready for the road."

Johnnie Green came and took his halter. "Thank you, Mr. Irons," he said politely.

Twinkleheels felt tremendously proud. He lifted one foot and then the other, feeling the strange new weight. He took a careful step on the hard-packed dirt floor of the smithy. Clink. He took another. Clink. It was a wonderful, solid, important sound.

"Well, Snowflake," he said, unable to resist trotting past the white mare's stall on his way out. "I am shod now. And I feel very grown up indeed."

Snowflake just snorted, but Twinkleheels did not care. He clinked his way out of the dark smithy and into the bright winter sunshine, feeling like the most important pony in the world.

XVII A NEW SOUND

The next morning, Twinkleheels woke up before the first gray light had touched the barn window. For a moment he lay still in the warm straw, feeling cozy and sleepy. Then he remembered. Shoes! He had shoes!

He scrambled to his feet at once. He lifted a front hoof and looked at it. There, neat and tidy, was the little iron shoe Mr. Irons had nailed to his foot. It was still a bit dark and sooty from the forge, but to Twinkleheels it was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen. He carefully put that foot down and lifted another. Yes, that one had a shoe, too! All four of them did.

He took a step. The shoe made a soft clink on a stone embedded in the dirt floor of his stall. He took another step. Clink. He began to walk in a small circle, just to hear the noise. Clink. Clink. Clink. It was a fine, new sound. It was the sound a working horse made. It was not the soft, dull thud of a bare hoof. It was a grown-up sound.

"What is all that racket?" came a sleepy voice from the next stall. "Is Farmer Green starting the spring plowing before breakfast?"

It was Ebenezer. Twinkleheels stopped his pacing. "Good morning, Ebenezer!" he said cheerfully. "It is not Farmer Green. It is me! I am just listening to my new shoes."

There was a rustling sound as the old horse got to his feet. His head appeared over the top of the stall partition. "Ah, yes," he said, his eyes twinkling. "I remember that sound. The first few days after a new set of shoes, a horse can't help but make a bit of music when he walks."

"It's a wonderful sound!" Twinkleheels declared. "It makes me feel very strong and steady."

"It should," Ebenezer agreed. "But do not forget, the strength comes from your legs and the steadiness comes from your head. The shoes are only helpers."

Before Twinkleheels could reply, another voice, high and mocking, drifted across the barn aisle. "Listen to the little pony! He sounds like a milkman's cart rattling over the cobblestones."

It was one of the bays, a spirited young horse named Rusty. His stablemate, Ginger, let out a whinny that sounded very much like a laugh.

Twinkleheels felt his ears grow hot. He had forgotten about the bays. They were kept in the two stalls at the far end of the barn. They were a matched pair, handsome and strong, but they were also known for their mischief.

"I do not sound like a milkman's cart!" Twinkleheels retorted, though his confidence was somewhat shaken. "I sound like a shod horse! Like you do."

"Ah, but we are used to it," Ginger called back. "We do not prance about in our stalls at dawn, clanking like a broken gate in the wind. We have work to do. We do not have time for such childish games."

This was very unfair, for Twinkleheels knew that he often worked just as hard as they did, pulling the little cart for Johnnie or helping to carry small things around the farm. But the bays always treated him like a foal.

"Never you mind them," Ebenezer said quietly. "Their own shoes were new once, too. They have just forgotten."

But Twinkleheels did mind. He wanted the bays to see that he was not a child. He wanted to show them what his new shoes could do. He pawed the ground, and the sharp clink of iron on stone echoed his frustration. He could hardly wait for Johnnie to come and let them out into the paddock.

XVIII A SLIPPERY PROPOSAL

Soon enough, the big barn doors rumbled open, letting in a flood of cold, bright morning air. Johnnie Green, his cheeks red from the chill, came in with a cheerful "Good morning, everyone!" He went from stall to stall, giving each horse a friendly pat and opening the latches.

Twinkleheels trotted out eagerly. The farmyard was covered in a crisp layer of snow from the other day, but the paths that were used often had been packed down and had frozen overnight into slick, icy patches. The sun was shining brightly, making the ice glitter like a field of diamonds.

Ebenezer moved slowly and deliberately toward a patch of hay that Farmer Green had put out near the fence. He placed his feet with great care, as if he knew the location of every slippery spot.

But the bays, Rusty and Ginger, burst from the barn with a great show of energy. They kicked up their heels and snorted, their breath making white clouds in the air. Then they caught sight of Twinkleheels, who was standing near the watering trough, trying to look very dignified and important.

"Well, well," said Rusty, trotting over. "If it isn't Mr. Clink-Clank himself. Are you planning to stand there all day, or are you going to show us what those fancy shoes are for?"

"They are for being sure-footed," Twinkleheels said proudly. "Farmer Green said so himself. I shall not slip on the ice now."

Ginger came alongside Rusty. He lowered his head and blew a puff of warm air onto a sheet of ice near the trough. "Is that so?" he said slyly. "You think those little bits of iron make you king of the ice? I wager we are still faster than you."

"A race is not about being sure-footed," Twinkleheels said, trying to sound as wise as Ebenezer. "It is about speed. And you are bigger than I am."

"Not a race for pure speed," Rusty countered, a clever glint in his eye. "A race of skill. From here, around the big oak tree at the far end of the paddock, and back to this trough. The path is clear in the middle, but it is very slippery around the tree and right here at the finish."

Twinkleheels looked. The bays were right. The ground around the great oak tree was a treacherous-looking patch of frozen mud and ice. And the finish line was the slickest spot of all. It was a challenge designed to cause a tumble.

Ebenezer, who had been listening from a short distance away, called out a warning. "A wise horse does not test the ice, Twinkleheels," he said. "He respects it."

Twinkleheels heard him. But he also heard the taunting snickers of the bays. He looked down at his own feet. He could feel the solid, reassuring weight of the shoes. He remembered what Farmer Green had told him about oxen being sure-footed in the snow. He remembered what Mr. Irons had said about him being "ready for the road." Surely, with these wonderful new shoes, he would have the advantage on the ice. The bays were just trying to frighten him.

"Alright," Twinkleheels said, lifting his chin. "I will race you."

Rusty and Ginger exchanged a look of triumph. "To the oak tree and back," Rusty declared. "On the count of three! One... two... THREE!"

XIX PRIDE AND A TUMBLE

With a great churning of legs, the two bays shot forward. They were much bigger and stronger than Twinkleheels, and they quickly pulled ahead on the straight, snowy stretch in the middle of the paddock. They galloped with their heads high, their powerful muscles bunching and releasing.

Twinkleheels put his head down and ran as fast as his short legs would carry him. Clink-clink-clink-clink went his new shoes on the packed snow. The sound spurred him on. He was not just running; he was a shod pony, a proper horse, proving his worth!

As they neared the far end of the paddock and the big oak tree, the bays began to slow down. They knew the ground there was dangerous. They spread out, their strides becoming shorter and more cautious as they picked their way around the wide, icy curve. Their speed was gone, replaced by a careful balancing act.

Here was his chance! Twinkleheels saw the wide, clumsy path the bays were taking. And he saw a much shorter path, right up close to the trunk of the tree. It was covered in a sheet of glassy, dark ice, but Twinkleheels was not afraid. 'They have to be careful,' he thought to himself breathlessly. 'But I have new shoes! I am sure-footed!'

He did not slow down. He aimed for the inside track, his heart pounding with excitement. For a thrilling instant, he was right. The sharp edges of his new shoes bit into the ice, giving him a grip he had never felt before. He flew past Ginger, who was picking his way along the outer edge. He was gaining on Rusty! He was going to win!

Pride swelled in his chest. He was faster! He was smarter! He was better!

And in that moment of proud foolishness, he tried to turn even more sharply, to cut inside of Rusty and take the lead. It was too much. His speed was too great for the turn. The new shoes, which had held so well a moment before, suddenly lost their purchase all at once.

For a horrible, sickening second, Twinkleheels felt as if the world had been pulled out from under him. His hind legs slid out to one side, and his front legs went the other. He gave a cry of pure surprise and then went down with a heavy, undignified thump. He did not stop there. The slick ice carried him, and he slid sideways, legs flailing, until he bumped right into the thick, rough bark of the oak tree. WHUMP.

He lay there in a tangled heap, snow and bits of ice clinging to his coat. Nothing was broken, except his pride. That was shattered into a thousand pieces.

Rusty and Ginger had stopped their race. They trotted back, their heads low, and looked down at him. They did not look triumphant now; they looked startled. But then Ginger let out a snort of laughter.

"So much for being sure-footed!" he neighed. "You look like a dropped bundle of laundry!"

Rusty joined in. "I told you those shoes were just for making noise!"

Their laughter echoed across the cold air, and each sound was like a sharp pebble striking Twinkleheels' heart. He just wanted the ground to open up and swallow him.

From across the paddock, old Ebenezer came trotting, his pace urgent but still careful. And from the barn, the door opened, and Farmer Green and Johnnie came hurrying out, their faces filled with concern.

XX THE LESSON OF THE SHOES

Johnnie was the first to reach him. "Twinkleheels! Are you alright?" he cried, dropping to his knees in the snow beside the fallen pony. He ran his hands gently over Twinkleheels' legs and back, checking for any hurt.

Twinkleheels scrambled awkwardly to his feet. He was sore and stiff, and his coat was damp, but he was not truly injured. He just stood with his head hanging low, avoiding everyone's eyes. He felt far too ashamed to even look at Johnnie.

Farmer Green arrived, his expression stern. He took one look at the scene: Twinkleheels covered in snow, the treacherous ice patch, and the two bays trying to look innocent. He understood at once.

"Rusty! Ginger!" he said in a voice that made both horses flinch. "What have I told you about running wild on the ice? You have more years than sense, the pair of you! Go to the far side of the paddock and stay there until I call you."

The two bays, no longer laughing, turned and trotted away meekly. Farmer Green then turned his attention to Twinkleheels. He knelt and picked up one of the pony's front feet, examining the new shoe and the hoof.

"No harm done, it seems," he said, his voice softening. He looked into Twinkleheels' sad eyes. "You gave us quite a scare, little fellow."

"I am sorry," Twinkleheels mumbled, looking at his own feet. "I thought... I thought the new shoes would keep me from slipping."

Farmer Green sighed and stood up. He brushed the snow from his own trousers. "Listen to me, Twinkleheels," he said, and his tone was not scolding, but kind and patient, as if he were explaining a very important secret. "The shoes do help. They give you a better edge than a bare hoof. But they are not magic. They do not turn ice into dry ground."

He pointed to his own heavy leather boots. "I wear these good boots in the winter," he said. "They have thick rubber soles with deep treads. They help me walk in the snow and keep me from slipping most of the time. But if I came to this patch of ice and tried to run across it as fast as I could, what do you think would happen?"

Twinkleheels thought for a moment. "You would fall down," he said quietly.

"Exactly," Farmer Green nodded. "The boots help, but I still have to use my good sense. I have to walk carefully, watch where I am going, and respect the danger. A tool is only a tool, Twinkleheels. It is the wisdom of the one using it that matters."

Twinkleheels finally understood. The shoes were not a magical charm that made him better than everyone else. They were just a help. The real work of being safe and steady still had to happen in his own head.

"I was foolish," he admitted. "I was trying to show off for Rusty and Ginger."

"Pride can be a very slippery patch of ice all on its own," Farmer Green said with a small smile. He gave Twinkleheels a gentle pat on the neck. "Come on. Let's get you back to the barn and brush you dry."

Later, as Twinkleheels stood in his warm stall, munching on a handful of sweet hay Johnnie had given him, Ebenezer spoke from next door.

"It seems you have learned a lesson that is harder than any a blacksmith can nail to your feet," the old horse said gently.

"I have," Twinkleheels agreed. "The shoes do not make the horse."

"No, they do not," said Ebenezer. "They help a wise horse do his work and travel safely. But they will carry a foolish horse to trouble just as quickly as his own bare feet would. To be truly grown up, Twinkleheels, is not about what you wear on your feet. It is about what you carry in your head."

Twinkleheels swished his tail. He still liked his shoes. He liked the solid feel of them and the clinking sound they made. But he saw them differently now. They were not a prize he had won. They were a responsibility he had been given. And he knew, as he settled into his clean straw, that he had a lot more growing up to do on the inside, where it really counted.

Ebenezer looked at the white mare with a calm eye. "He is with his master," the old horse said simply. "And he is learning. We were all young once, madam. Even you."

The mare tossed her head, as if such a thing were impossible to imagine. But she said no more, for just then the blacksmith came for her, and she had to pay attention to her own business.

Twinkleheels felt a surge of gratitude for his old friend. He was still trembling slightly, but he felt braver for Ebenezer's defense of him. "Thank you," he whispered.

"Think nothing of it," said Ebenezer. "Some horses believe that once they are grown, they must forget what it was like to be young. That is a great mistake."

Just then, the blacksmith, a big man with a leather apron and arms as thick as fence posts, came over to them. "Well, Farmer Green," he said in a booming voice, "is this the little fellow who needs his first set of shoes?"

"This is him," said Farmer Green. "His name is Twinkleheels. Be gentle with him, Sam. It is his first time."

"I am always gentle," the blacksmith chuckled. He turned his kind eyes on Twinkleheels. "Now then, young fellow. Let's have a look at that foot."

Twinkleheels looked in panic at Ebenezer. He did not know which foot to lift!

Ebenezer gave a tiny nod of his head towards his own left forefoot. Twinkleheels understood. He stood as still as he could while the blacksmith gently lifted his left front hoof. He measured it carefully with a piece of string and made a mark on a slate. Then he did the same with the right front hoof, and the two hind hoofs.

"He has good, sound feet," the blacksmith told Farmer Green. "It will be a pleasure to shoe him."

Then began the most amazing and terrifying performance Twinkleheels had ever witnessed. The blacksmith took a long bar of iron, thrust it into the roaring fire of the forge, and pumped a great bellows that made the flames leap and roar with a sound like a winter gale. Soon the end of the iron bar glowed a bright, angry orange.

The blacksmith pulled it out and laid the glowing end upon the anvil. Then the terrible clanging began again. With swift, sure blows of his hammer, he beat the iron, sending a shower of bright sparks dancing through the dim air. Twinkleheels watched, his eyes wide with a mixture of terror and fascination, as the straight bar of iron slowly bent and curved into the shape of a tiny horseshoe.

Then came the hiss! The smith plunged the hot shoe into the tub of water, and a great cloud of steam erupted with a sound that made Twinkleheels jump, even though Ebenezer had warned him.

The process was repeated four times, until four perfect little shoes lay cooling on the smithy floor.

"Now for the fitting," the blacksmith said.

This was the part Twinkleheels had dreaded most: the nails. He remembered Ebenezer's words about paying attention and obeying orders. He stood as still as a statue when the blacksmith took his foot again. He felt the cold shoe against his hoof. Then he heard the tap, tap, tap of the hammer. He squeezed his eyes shut, waiting for the pain.

But there was no pain! Not even a prickle. The nails went smoothly into the hard, unfeeling part of his hoof. The blacksmith then snipped off the ends of the nails and smoothed them down.

"There now," he said, setting Twinkleheels' foot down. "One down. Try that on for size."

Twinkleheels tentatively put his weight on his new shoe. It felt strange, solid, and heavy. He lifted his foot and put it down again. Clink! The sound on the hard-packed earth of the smithy floor was new and important.

One by one, the other three shoes were fitted and nailed in place. With each one, Twinkleheels grew more confident and less afraid. When the last hoof was done, Farmer Green paid the blacksmith and led Twinkleheels out into the bright sunshine.

"Well done, little pony," Farmer Green said, patting his neck. "You behaved like a gentleman."

Twinkleheels felt a thrill of pride. He took a step, and then another. Clink, clank, clink, clank! He had never made such a wonderful noise. He tossed his head and pawed the frozen ground, just to hear the crisp, metallic scrape of his new shoes.

Ebenezer, who had been fitted with his new shoes while Twinkleheels was being measured, looked on with an amused expression. "They feel very fine, do they not?" he said.

"They feel wonderful!" Twinkleheels exclaimed. "I feel as if I could run to the top of the big hill and back in a minute! I feel so steady! I do not think I will ever slip again!"

XVII A SLIPPERY LESSON

The next morning, the world was coated in a thin, treacherous glaze of ice. A light rain had fallen during the night and frozen on every branch and twig, turning the farm into a world of glass.

Twinkleheels woke up feeling grand. The memory of the blacksmith shop was now a proud one, and the weight of the four new shoes on his feet felt solid and reassuring.

"I am going out," he announced to Ebenezer. "Johnnie is going to see if the hens have laid any eggs, and I shall go with him."

"Be careful, Twinkleheels," Ebenezer advised from the warmth of his stall. "The yard is like a skating rink this morning. Even with new shoes, you must watch your step."

"Oh, I am not worried about that," Twinkleheels declared loftily. "My shoes have calks on them. Farmer Green said so. They will bite into the ice. I am perfectly safe." He ignored the wise old horse's sigh and trotted to the barn door where Johnnie Green was waiting.

As they stepped outside, Twinkleheels felt the thrill of it. His new shoes did indeed bite into the ice. Where Johnnie had to shuffle carefully, Twinkleheels could walk with a proud, clinking stride.

"See!" he called back into the barn. "I told you so!"

He saw Rusty the rooster and Ginger the hen huddled near the coop, looking miserable as they tried to keep their footing. They slid and slipped with every step.

Twinkleheels could not resist. He trotted over to them, his head held high. His shoes made a confident crunching sound on the ice. "Good morning," he said in a tone that was far too cheerful. "Having some trouble, are you?"

Rusty the rooster glared at him. "It is not easy for a bird to walk on a sheet of glass," he crowed, his voice shaky.

"It is easy for me," Twinkleheels boasted. He took a few high steps, lifting his feet and setting them down with a sharp clink. "These new shoes make all the difference. One must have the proper equipment for such weather."

Johnnie Green had gone into the henhouse. Twinkleheels, feeling very pleased with himself, decided to show off a little more. He started to canter in a small circle, his shoes scattering tiny shards of ice. He felt fast and powerful and completely in control. He felt sorry for all the other animals who had to slip and slide on their bare feet or claws.

"I could probably race an ice boat today!" he thought to himself.

He was so busy admiring his own sure-footedness that he did not notice the one patch of ice near the water trough that was different from the rest. It was not merely frozen rain; it was a thick, smooth sheet where water had overflowed and frozen solid, smooth as a polished mirror.

Full of pride, Twinkleheels cantered right onto it.

For a single, horrifying second, the calks on his shoes found nothing to grip. It was like stepping on air. His feet shot out from under him in four different directions at once. One moment he was a proud, cantering pony; the next he was a confused bundle of legs and tail, crashing to the ground with a thud that knocked the wind out of him.

He lay there, stunned and mortified. His fine new shoes were pointing uselessly at the sky.

Johnnie Green came running out of the henhouse. "Twinkleheels! Are you all right?" he cried, his face full of concern.

Twinkleheels scrambled, but his iron-shod feet could find no purchase on the slick ice. He only slid and spun around helplessly. He could hear Rusty the rooster letting out a series of small, chuckling crows. His cheeks burned with shame.

Farmer Green, hearing the commotion, came out of the farmhouse. He took one look at the situation and his face grew serious. "Stay still, Twinkleheels," he commanded. "You will only hurt yourself."

He went back into the barn and returned with a bucket of sand and an old burlap sack. He carefully spread the sand in a path to where Twinkleheels lay. Then he laid the burlap sack over the ice right beside the little pony.

"Now," he said quietly. "Try to get your feet onto the sack."

With a great deal of slipping and struggling, Twinkleheels managed to get his front feet onto the rough texture of the burlap. With that tiny bit of grip, he was able to heave himself up, trembling all over.

"I am sorry," Twinkleheels mumbled, looking at his own feet. "I thought... I thought the new shoes would keep me from slipping."

XVIII A NEW KIND OF FEAR

The lesson of the ice stayed with Twinkleheels. In the days that followed, a change came over the little pony. The proud strut was gone. The boastful toss of his head was no more. In their place was a caution that bordered on fear.

He tested every step. When he walked from his stall to the watering trough, he moved slowly, his eyes fixed on the ground ahead of him as if he expected it to turn to glass at any moment. He avoided puddles. He gave wide berth to patches of damp hay.

"What is the matter with you?" Ebenezer asked him one afternoon. "You are creeping about like a cat stalking a mouse. But you look more like the mouse."

"I am being careful," Twinkleheels replied in a small voice. "I am using my good sense. I do not wish to fall again."

"There is a difference between being careful and being frightened of your own shadow," the old horse observed kindly. "You have learned not to run on ice. That is wisdom. But now you are afraid to trot on dry ground. That is not wisdom, Twinkleheels. That is fear."

Twinkleheels did not answer. He knew Ebenezer was right. The memory of his fall, of his four feet flying out from under him and the sound of Rusty's chuckling, had become a little monster that lived in his head. It whispered to him every time he thought of moving faster than a slow walk.

A few mornings later, Johnnie Green came into the barn with a happy, excited face. "We are going to the sugar-bush today!" he announced to Twinkleheels, giving him an affectionate rub on the nose. "The sap is running! Father says I can take you with me."

Twinkleheels' heart, which was usually so bold, felt like a cold stone in his chest. The sugar-bush! He remembered his earlier misadventure in the deep snow. He remembered what Farmer Green had said: "It takes oxen in such going. They can go where horses couldn't do anything but flounder."

"I... I do not think I should go," Twinkleheels faltered, speaking to Johnnie in the only way he knew how: by hanging his head and refusing to move.

Johnnie looked puzzled. "What is it, boy? Don't you want to come? It will be fun! You can smell the maple boiling."

But Twinkleheels would not be persuaded. He planted his feet and looked miserable. He was remembering the deep snow and the slippery ice all at once. The sugar-bush sounded like a place full of dangers, a place where a small pony could get stuck and fall down and be very foolish indeed.

Farmer Green came over. "What is the trouble here?" he asked.

"Twinkleheels does not want to go to the sugar-bush," Johnnie said sadly. "I think he is afraid."

Farmer Green looked at Twinkleheels for a long moment. He saw not a stubborn pony, but a frightened one. "Well now," he said gently. "Maybe he is remembering his tumble in the snowdrift. It is all right to be cautious, Johnnie. But we cannot let fear keep us in the barn all our lives."

He took Twinkleheels' halter. "I will not make you pull anything, little fellow," he said, his voice low and comforting. "You will not have to go into the deep drifts. You can just stay with Johnnie near the sugar-house, where the ground is flat. Will you come with us, just for that?"

Twinkleheels looked from Farmer Green's kind face to Johnnie's hopeful one. He still felt the fear fluttering in his stomach, but he also felt a deep loyalty to them. He gave a tiny nod of his head.

"That is a good pony," Farmer Green said.

As they got ready to leave, with Bright and Broad hitched to the big sap sled, Twinkleheels looked over at Ebenezer's stall.

"I am afraid," he whispered.

"I know," said the old horse. "But you are going anyway. Do you know what that is called?"

Twinkleheels shook his head.

"That is called courage," said Ebenezer. "It is a much finer thing than being proud. Remember what Farmer Green told you. It is the wisdom of the one using the tool that matters. Go and be wise, little pony."

XIX IN THE SUGAR-BUSH

The journey to the sugar-bush was slow. Bright and Broad moved with their usual unhurried power, the big wooden sap sled gliding over the packed snow behind them. Twinkleheels, led by Johnnie, trotted nervously behind. He watched every step, placing his newly shod feet with extreme care.

They left the open fields and entered the woods. The air grew still and smelled of damp earth and melting snow. Sunlight slanted down through the bare branches of the maple trees. On many of the trees, wooden spiles had been tapped, and from them hung buckets that collected the clear, watery sap. Plink... plink... plink... The sound was all around them.

They soon came to a clearing where a small, rustic shed stood. A plume of fragrant, sweet-smelling steam billowed from its chimney. This was the sugar-house, where the sap was boiled down into syrup.

"Here we are," said Farmer Green. "Johnnie, you can tie Twinkleheels to that post over there. He will be out of the way and can watch us work."

Twinkleheels was grateful for the instruction. The ground around the sugar-house was well trodden and mostly clear of snow. He felt much safer there. From his spot, he watched as Farmer Green and Johnnie unhitched Bright and Broad and began the day's work. The two great oxen moved through the woods from tree to tree, patient and strong, while Farmer Green emptied the full buckets into a large gathering tank on the sled.

Twinkleheels watched them with a new respect. They were not fast or flashy. They did not prance or show off. They moved without hurry, but also without pause. Their great heads were bowed to their work, their broad backs straining just enough to pull the heavy load. They were steady. They were sure-footed. They were everything Twinkleheels suddenly wished he could be.

Johnnie Green, his own small tasks done for the moment, came and sat on a log near Twinkleheels. He pulled a small block of wood and a knife from his pocket. It was a special knife, with a smooth wooden handle that his father had carved for him.

"I will make you a little horse," Johnnie said to Twinkleheels, and began to whittle, long, thin curls of wood falling onto the snow.

Twinkleheels watched him, soothed by the quiet company and the steady scrape of the knife. The air was full of the wonderful smell of boiling maple. The sun was warm on his back. Perhaps, he thought, the sugar-bush was not such a terrible place after all.

Just then, one of the farm dogs, a young and boisterous collie named Laddie, came bounding into the clearing. He was chasing a squirrel, and his mind was on nothing else. He did not see Johnnie Green sitting on the log. He raced past, barking with excitement, and his wagging tail bumped Johnnie's arm.

"Oops!" cried Johnnie.

The special knife flew from his grasp. It spun through the air and landed not in the soft snow, but on a steep, icy bank that led down to a frozen creek bed. It slid down, down, down, and came to rest on a narrow ledge of rock, just above the ice of the creek. It was a spot no person could easily reach without slipping.

Johnnie scrambled to the edge of the bank. "Oh, no!" he cried. "My knife! My father's knife!"

Farmer Green heard him and came over. He looked down the slippery slope. "It is too slick, son," he said, his voice full of regret. "If I try to go down there, I will end up in the creek myself. And Bright and Broad are much too big to get down that narrow path. I am afraid it is lost until the spring thaw."

Johnnie's face crumpled. A tear traced a path down his cold-reddened cheek.

Twinkleheels saw that tear. He saw the precious knife, so far out of reach. He looked at the steep, icy bank. The little monster of fear in his head screamed at him. It showed him visions of himself, slipping and crashing and lying helpless. His legs began to tremble.

But then he looked at Johnnie's sad face. And he remembered Ebenezer's words: "That is called courage." He remembered Farmer Green's lesson: "Use your good sense... watch where you are going."

Fear told him to stay put, where it was safe. But loyalty to Johnnie urged him forward. He took a hesitant step away from his post. He walked to the edge of the bank and looked down. It was steep. It was slippery. But it was not impossible. Not for a small, clever pony.

He took a breath. Then, very slowly, he began to walk sideways down the slope. He did not try to hurry. He used the lesson the ice had taught him. He bent his knees and lowered his body, keeping his balance. He tested every single spot with his hoof before putting his weight on it. The little calks of his new shoes, which had failed him when he was foolish, now served him when he was wise. They bit into a patch of frozen earth here, a tiny crack in the ice there.

Step by careful step, he made his way down. Johnnie and his father watched in amazed silence.

He reached the ledge. The knife lay there. He nudged it gently with his nose, pushing it away from the edge. Then, he carefully lowered his head and took the leather thong tied to the handle between his teeth.

The climb back up was even harder. He had to pull with his front legs, digging his shoes in for grip. He never let go of the knife. He kept his eyes on Johnnie's face, which was now filled with hope.

Finally, with one last, careful push, he was back on level ground. He walked straight to Johnnie and dropped the knife at his feet.

Johnnie let out a whoop of joy. He threw his arms around Twinkleheels' neck. "Oh, Twinkleheels! You good, brave pony! You saved it!"

Farmer Green laid a hand on Twinkleheels' neck, and the little pony could feel the pride in his touch. "That was not foolishness, little pony," he said, his voice thick with emotion. "That was courage. You used your head. I am very proud of you."

That evening, back in the warm, quiet barn, Twinkleheels told Ebenezer the whole story.

"I was afraid," he finished. "I was afraid the whole time. But I did it anyway."

"That, my little friend," said the old horse, his voice full of a deep and abiding warmth, "is the very definition of bravery. Not the absence of fear, but acting in spite of it. You did not let your fall make you useless. You let it make you wise."

Twinkleheels munched his hay. It tasted better than any hay had ever tasted before. His new shoes felt solid on his feet. They were not a reason to be proud, and they were not a reason to be afraid. They were tools. And today, he had learned how to use them. He had found a new kind of confidence, a quiet, steady feeling that was worth more than all the boastful prancing in the world.

"He's very brave," Twinkleheels whispered to Johnnie.
"He's used to it," Johnnie whispered back. "And the smith is a kind man. You'll see."
Soon it was Twinkleheels' turn. The blacksmith approached him slowly. "All right, little one. Let's have a look at that front foot."

The sound of the rasp filing his hoof was strange, a ticklish, grating feeling. But it did not hurt. The smith measured his hoof with a piece of metal, then went over to the fiery forge. He pumped a great bellows that made the coals glow with a fierce, bright heat. He chose a small, pony-sized shoe with a pair of long tongs and thrust it into the heart of the fire.
Twinkleheels watched, his eyes wide. The iron began to glow, first a dull red, then a cherry red, and then a brilliant yellow-white. The smith pulled it out and laid it on the anvil.
CLANG! CLANG! CLANG!

When the shoe was shaped just right, the smith brought it over. For just a second, he pressed the hot shoe against Twinkleheels' hoof. There was a sizzle, a puff of smoke, and a strange, horny smell. Twinkleheels flinched, but the smith held him steady. It was a hot, brief touch that was gone before he could truly feel afraid.
"Just checking the fit," the man said calmly. Then he plunged the shoe into the water tub.

The smith brought the cooled shoe back and placed it against the hoof. He took a few small nails from his mouth and, with a few quick, skillful taps of his hammer, drove them into the hoof wall. Twinkleheels felt the little taps, but there was no pain at all, just as Ebenezer had promised. The smith clipped the ends of the nails and smoothed them down.
"There," he said, gently placing the pony's foot back on the floor. "One down, three to go."
The rest of the shoeing passed in the same way. Twinkleheels found that if he stood still and did just as he was told, the whole business was not frightening in the least. When the last shoe was on, he lifted a foot to look. The little iron shoe felt heavy and important.
"Go on, try them out," said the blacksmith with a grin.
Johnnie led him in a small circle on the smithy's hard-packed dirt floor. Clop, clop, clop, clop. It was a new sound, a firm, solid sound. It was the sound of a working horse.
"He looks very proud," said Farmer Green, paying the smith.
"As he should," the man replied. "A first set of shoes is a milestone."
Twinkleheels was indeed proud. He felt taller. He felt stronger. As they left the smithy and started for home, he trotted behind Ebenezer's buggy with his head held high, listening to the fine, ringing music his new shoes made on the frozen road.

XVII A NEW TASK

The next morning, the whole barn seemed to know that Twinkleheels was shod. The two bay horses, whose names were Fleet and Flyer, looked him over with condescending eyes.
"Well, look at the little pony," said Fleet. "He thinks he's a real horse now that he has some iron on his feet."
"He'll probably trip over them," Flyer added with a snicker. "They look too big for him."
Twinkleheels felt a flash of anger. He wanted to say something sharp in reply. He wanted to tell them that his shoes were a perfect fit and that he was just as much a horse as they were. But then he looked over at Ebenezer's stall. The old horse was watching him with his calm, wise eyes. Twinkleheels remembered the feeling of foolishness in the smithy. He remembered the quiet pride he had felt after helping Johnnie.
He took a breath and said nothing at all. He simply turned his attention to his morning oats.
His silence seemed to annoy the bays more than any argument would have. "He's too proud to even speak to us," Fleet muttered to Flyer, and they went back to their own breakfast.
"That was wisely done," Ebenezer said quietly a few moments later. "An empty barrel makes the most noise."
Twinkleheels felt a warm glow. The approval of the old horse meant more to him than the opinions of a dozen flighty bays.
Later that morning, Farmer Green came into the barn with Johnnie. They were not carrying the usual buggy harness. Instead, Farmer Green held a small, sturdy work harness made of thick leather, and Johnnie was dragging a very small, low sled with wooden runners.
Twinkleheels looked at the equipment with curiosity. It was much too small for Ebenezer or the bays, and much too big for Spot the dog.
"What's that for, Father?" Johnnie asked, his eyes bright with excitement.
"This," said Farmer Green, stopping in front of Twinkleheels' stall, "is for our newest farmhand." He opened the stall door and patted the little pony's neck. "After the way you handled yourself down by the creek, and now that you've got a good set of shoes on your feet, I think it's time you did some real work."
Twinkleheels' heart gave a happy leap. Real work!
"Are we going to the sugar-bush?" Johnnie cried. "Is he going to help us gather sap?"
"That's the plan," his father confirmed. "Bright and Broad can handle the big sled on the main path, but there are a few dozen trees in the west grove where the path is too narrow for the oxen. I've been carrying those buckets by hand, and it's slow going. I think a small pony with a small sled is just the thing for the job."
Johnnie was thrilled. "Oh, Twinkleheels! We're going to work together!"
Farmer Green fitted the new harness onto Twinkleheels. It felt different from his light riding saddle. It was heavier, more serious. It smelled of leather and oil and honest labor. When it was all buckled, they hitched him to the little sled. It was called a stone boat, Farmer Green explained, though today it would be a sap boat.
"Now, this is an important job," Farmer Green said, his voice serious. "The sap has to be gathered quickly before it spoils, but the work must be done carefully. No foolishness. No racing. Just slow, steady work. Do you understand?"
Twinkleheels nodded his head as if he understood every word. He felt the weight of the sled behind him and the trust of Farmer Green's hands on the harness. He was no longer just a pet or a plaything. He had a responsibility.
As Johnnie led him out of the barn and toward the snowy woods, he passed the stalls of Fleet and Flyer. They watched him go, their expressions a mixture of surprise and envy. Twinkleheels did not even glance at them. He kept his eyes fixed straight ahead, on the path to the sugar-bush, his new shoes making a steady, purposeful rhythm on the hard-packed snow.

XVIII THE SUGAR-BUSH

The sugar-bush was a beautiful and busy place. Tall maple trees stood in neat rows, their bare branches stark against the blue winter sky. A small wooden tap was driven into each trunk, and from each tap hung a metal bucket. Drip, drip, drip. The sound was everywhere, a gentle music of the woods as the clear, sweet sap slowly filled the pails.
In the center of the grove stood the sugar house, a plume of sweet-smelling steam rising from its chimney. Inside, the sap was boiled down in a great, flat pan until it became thick, golden maple syrup.
Bright and Broad, the team of powerful oxen, were already at work. They moved with a slow, ponderous rhythm, pulling a large sled piled high with full sap buckets. They did not seem to notice the cold or the weight of their load. They simply leaned into their yokes and walked, their great hoofs planting themselves with unshakable certainty.
"All right, Johnnie," said Farmer Green. "You and Twinkleheels will take the west path. Just go from tree to tree, empty the buckets into the big can on your sled, and when it's full, bring it back here to the main tank."
Johnnie took Twinkleheels' lead rope, and they started down the narrow trail. Here, the trees grew closer together, and the snow was deeper. It was a path no wagon could travel, and it was plain to see why the big oxen could not come this way.
Twinkleheels felt a surge of importance. This was a job only he could do.
The first few trees were easy. Johnnie would unhook a full bucket, carefully pour its contents into the large collection can on the sled, and hang the empty bucket back on its tap. Then he would say, "All right, Twinkleheels, walk up!" and the pony would take a few steps to the next tree.
But Twinkleheels was a pony full of life and energy. The slow pace was difficult for him. Drip, drip, drip. Step, step, stop. It felt dreadfully slow. He wanted to trot. He wanted to show Johnnie how quickly he could do the job.
At one point, Johnnie was struggling with a particularly full bucket. Twinkleheels, growing impatient, shifted his weight and took a quick step forward without being told. The sled lurched.
"Whoa, Twinkleheels!" Johnnie cried. A little sap sloshed from the bucket in his hands, splashing onto the snow. "You have to wait for me! Father said to be careful."
Twinkleheels drooped his head, ashamed. He had been careless. He had been thinking of speed instead of steadiness. He looked over toward the main path and saw Bright and Broad. They were standing perfectly still while Farmer Green emptied several buckets onto their sled. They did not twitch or shift. They simply waited, patient as the trees around them.
Twinkleheels realized then that their slowness was not a weakness. It was their greatest strength. It was what made them so good at their job.
"I'm sorry," Twinkleheels seemed to say with a soft snort.
Johnnie patted his neck. "It's all right. Let's just go a little slower."
For the rest of the morning, Twinkleheels tried his very best to be like the oxen. When Johnnie stopped, he stood as still as a snow-covered stump. He waited for the command "Walk up!" before he moved a single muscle. He learned to pull the sled smoothly, without jerking, so that the precious sap in the can did not slosh and spill.
It was hard work. His muscles began to ache from the steady pulling. But it was a good kind of ache. With each full sled he brought back to the main tank, he felt a deep and quiet satisfaction.
He even had a chance to speak with the oxen during a short rest.
"You do this all day," Twinkleheels said, his voice full of a new respect.
"All day," rumbled Bright, the ox on the left. His voice was low and slow.
"It is the work," added Broad, the ox on the right, his voice just as deep. "The work must be done."
"But don't you ever want to go faster?" Twinkleheels asked.
Bright turned his great head and looked at the little pony with his large, placid brown eyes. "Fast spills the sap," he said simply.
"Slow fills the barrel," Broad concluded.
Twinkleheels thought about that. Fast spills the sap. Slow fills the barrel. It was a good lesson, just as good as the one he had learned in the blacksmith's shop. He was beginning to see that there was a right way to do things, and it was not always the quickest way. He was learning the wisdom of work.

XIX A SUDDEN DANGER

As the afternoon wore on, a change came over the sugar-bush. The bright blue sky began to cloud over with a uniform sheet of gray. The air grew still and heavy, and a deep cold settled among the trees. The cheerful dripping of the sap slowed, and then stopped altogether as the temperature dropped.
"Looks like a storm's blowing in," Farmer Green said, looking up at the sky. "Let's gather this last load and head in. We've had a good day's work."
Bright and Broad stood patiently with their sled nearly full. Johnnie and Twinkleheels had just arrived from the west path, their own can brimming with the last of the sap from the narrow trail.
But before they could finish loading, the wind began to howl through the tops of the maple trees. Snow began to fall, not in gentle flakes, but in a thick, driving curtain that quickly blurred the world into a swirling whiteness.
Suddenly, there was a terrible sound from deep in the woods. It was a sharp, cracking noise, like a giant whip snapping, followed by a deep groan and a tremendous crash that shook the very ground.
"What was that?" Johnnie cried, grabbing onto Twinkleheels' harness.
Twinkleheels himself had jumped, his heart pounding. The oxen merely shifted their weight and looked calmly in the direction of the sound.
"A tree, or a big limb," Farmer Green said grimly. "Weighted down by the new, wet snow. I hope it didn't fall across the road."
He left the oxen and walked a little way down the main path that led out of the sugar-bush. In a moment he was back, his face etched with worry.
"It's just as I feared," he announced. "A huge maple limb has come down. It's completely blocked the path. We can't get the sled through."
This was terrible news. The large sled held nearly all of the day's sap harvest. If it were left out in the freezing storm, the sap would turn to ice in the buckets and the cans, and the whole day's labor would be lost.
"Can't Bright and Broad pull it out of the way?" Johnnie asked.
Farmer Green shook his head. "It's too big, and it's tangled up with two other trees. If they pull, they might bring more down on top of us. It's not safe. We're trapped on this side of the fall."
They all stood in the swirling snow, a feeling of helplessness settling over them. The wind howled, and the world grew darker.
Twinkleheels looked at the huge sled, piled high with buckets. He looked at the powerful oxen, who stood useless for the first time all day. Their great strength was no good against such an obstacle. He looked at Johnnie's worried face and Farmer Green's troubled frown.
He felt a strange mix of feelings. He was afraid of the storm and the crashing sound he had heard. But he also felt the steady weight of the work harness on his shoulders. He remembered the narrow trail he had been working on all day, a path too small for the oxen.
An idea, small and hesitant at first, began to form in his pony mind. He was small. He was not as strong as the oxen, but he could go where they could not.
He nudged Johnnie with his nose, then looked meaningfully toward his own little sled, and then toward the narrow west path he knew so well.
Johnnie's face, which had been full of despair, suddenly lit up. "Father! I have an idea!" he exclaimed. "The west path! It's narrow, but Twinkleheels and his sled have been using it all day. It goes around the hill and comes out on the main road on the other side of the fallen branch! We could use it!"

When all four shoes were on, Twinkleheels was led outside into the snowy farmyard. He took a careful step. Then another. His feet felt strangely heavy, but also wonderfully solid. He lifted a foot and looked at the neat, dark iron shoe that was now his. He felt immensely proud. He pawed at the packed snow, and the iron rang with a crisp, clear sound. It was a beautiful sound! Ebenezer was soon finished, too, and Farmer Green harnessed him to the buggy again. Twinkleheels trotted behind on the way home, and this time he did not have to be told to keep up. He loved the feel of his new shoes on the road. Clip-clop, clip-clop, they went. It was a grown-up, important sound. He felt he was no longer just a pony, but a real horse with a real job to do.

When they arrived back at the barn, Twinkleheels could hardly wait to show off. The two bay horses, who were in their stalls, looked up as he came in.

"Listen to the noise!" one of them said. "What have you got on your feet, pebbles?"

"They are shoes," Twinkleheels announced, lifting his head high. "I have been to the blacksmith. All working horses wear them, you know." He walked back and forth on the wooden floor, making as much noise as he could. Clip-clop-clatter-clop.

The other bay horse snickered. "He sounds like a whole troop of cavalry!"

Twinkleheels did not care. He knew they were just jealous. He went into his stall next to Ebenezer.

"Well," the old horse said kindly, "how does it feel to be a shod gentleman?"

"It feels wonderful!" Twinkleheels declared. "I feel so much surer on my feet. I don't think I should slip on the ice at all now. I could probably run faster than ever!" He looked wistfully towards the pasture. "It is a pity the snow is so deep. I should like to have another race with you, Ebenezer."

Ebenezer smiled a slow, wise smile. "There will be other days for racing," he said. "A good set of shoes is for work as much as for sport. You will find them very useful."

Twinkleheels was about to say that he would much rather use them for sport than for work when Farmer Green came back into the barn. He was carrying a small harness, much smaller than the ones the big horses wore.

"Johnnie," he called, "come give me a hand. With this storm coming, I want to get the rest of the sap gathered from the sugar-bush before noon. The big sled is too much for this deep snow, but I think Twinkleheels and the little stone-boat sled could manage the west trail."

Johnnie's face lit up. "Can I drive him, Father?"

"You can," Farmer Green agreed. "We'll put some of the empty buckets on his sled, and you can bring them up to the sugar house. Bright and Broad will pull the big collecting tank."

Twinkleheels' heart gave a little leap. A harness! A sled! A real job! It was just what he had been hoping for. He stood very still while Farmer Green and Johnnie fitted the small work harness over his back and fastened the buckles. It felt snug and important.

Soon they were all out in the snow again. The sky had turned a flat, heavy gray, and a few lazy snowflakes were beginning to drift down. The two big oxen, Bright and Broad, were already yoked to a large sled with a huge wooden tank on it. They stood patiently, their breath misting in the cold air.

Johnnie hitched Twinkleheels' harness to a small, low sled called a stone-boat, which was just a set of runners with a flat board on top. He piled a dozen empty sap buckets onto it.

"All right, Twinkleheels," Johnnie said, taking up the reins. "Let's show them what we can do! Giddy-up!"

Twinkleheels leaned into the harness. The sled was not heavy, and it slid easily over the snow. He trotted smartly up the lane, his newly shod hoofs making a satisfying crunching sound in the snow. He felt he could pull this little sled all day. He was so proud that he tossed his head and snorted, sending a plume of white vapor into the air. He was a working horse at last!

They followed the main track into the woods, where hundreds of maple trees stood like gray ghosts. Many of them had buckets hanging from little spouts, or taps, that had been driven into their trunks. Drip, drip, drip, the clear, sweet sap fell into the buckets.

Farmer Green was already at work with the oxen, driving from tree to tree. He would empty the sap from the pails into his own big buckets, and then pour those into the great tank on Bright and Broad's sled.

"You and Twinkleheels can work the west slope," he told Johnnie. "The path is too narrow there for the oxen, and the trees are farther apart. Just gather what you can and bring it back to the sugar house."

The west slope path was indeed narrow, winding between thick fir trees and over little frozen brooks. It was a path Twinkleheels knew well from his romps in the pasture. For a big horse, or for the wide sled of the oxen, it would have been impossible. But Twinkleheels and his little sled were just the right size.

He felt a new sense of importance. Bright and Broad were strong, to be sure, but here was a job that only he could do. He and Johnnie worked as a team. Johnnie would run from tree to tree, emptying the sap into the buckets on the sled. It was hard work. The snow was getting deeper, and the sled, when full of sap, was heavier than he expected. He had to lean into his harness and pull with all his might. His new shoes were a great help. They bit into the packed snow and kept him from slipping on the icy patches. He was glad for them now, not because they made a fine clattering noise, but because they helped him do his job.

After his second trip, as he was resting for a moment, Bright and Broad came plodding past on the main trail.

"Well, look at the pony," Broad, the nigh ox, said to his partner. His voice was a low rumble. "He seems to be making himself useful."

Twinkleheels pretended not to hear, but his ears twitched with pride. To be praised by the great oxen! That was something indeed. He felt his chest swell, and he pulled the next load with renewed energy.

Johnnie's face, which had been full of despair, suddenly lit up. "Father! I have an idea!" he exclaimed. "The west path! It's narrow, but Twinkleheels and his sled have been using it all day. It goes around the hill and comes out on the main road on the other side of the fallen branch! We could use it!"

Farmer Green looked from his son to the little pony, and then toward the faint trace of the west path, which was already filling with drifting snow. He pulled at his chin, thinking hard. The wind tore at his coat and stung his cheeks.

"The path is long," he said slowly. "And the sled is small. That tank holds a mighty lot of sap, Johnnie."

"But it's our only chance, Father!" Johnnie insisted. "We can do it! Twinkleheels is strong, and the path is packed down from our other trips. We can't just leave all the sap here to freeze!"

Farmer Green looked at Twinkleheels. The little pony stood steady in the wind, his head up, as if he understood everything and was waiting for his orders. He looked at the sturdy little sled. He looked at his son's determined face.

"You are right," he said at last, with a firm nod. "It is our only chance. We will have to work like we've never worked before. It will be a race against the cold."

A new energy filled the little clearing. The feeling of helplessness was gone, replaced by a sense of urgent purpose.

"Here's the plan," Farmer Green said, his voice loud and clear above the storm. "Johnnie, you and Twinkleheels will be our transport. I will stay here with Bright and Broad and fill your sled from the main tank. You will take each load around the west path to the main road. I will leave the empty wagon on the road; you can pour the sap into the big cans there. Do you understand?"

"Yes, sir!" Johnnie said, his eyes shining.

"Be careful," his father added, his voice softening. "That path will be getting treacherous. Don't rush. Slow and steady is what we need. The day's work depends on you two."

Twinkleheels felt a thrill go through him. The day's work depended on him! Not on the strong oxen, but on him, the little pony.

Farmer Green took one of the big buckets and filled it to the brim from the spigot on the tank. The clear liquid sloshed as he carefully placed it on the little stone-boat sled. He filled another, and another, until four heavy, full buckets sat on the sled.

"That's a good load," he said. "Don't try to take more. Now go, and be safe."

Johnnie took up the reins. "All right, Twinkleheels," he said softly. "Let's go."

Twinkleheels leaned into the harness. This was a much heavier load than the empty buckets he had pulled before. He felt the runners resist, stuck for a moment in the deepening snow. He bunched his muscles, dug his new shoes in for grip, and pulled. The sled groaned and then began to move.

Their journey was a battle. The wind shrieked and threw handfuls of icy snow into their faces. The narrow path was a white trench, and drifts were beginning to form in the sheltered spots. Twinkleheels had to lower his head against the blast and plod onward, step by careful step. He could feel Johnnie walking close behind him, one hand often resting on his back, a silent encouragement.

He thought of Ebenezer's words. "A good set of shoes is for work as much as for sport." How true that was! Without them, he would be slipping and sliding, wasting his strength. But the sharp edges of the iron shoes gave him purchase, a firm hold on the treacherous ground.

It seemed a very long way. He could hear the sap sloshing in the buckets behind him, a heavy, precious weight. He dared not stumble. At last, through the swirling snow, he saw the darker line of the main road ahead. They had made it!

Johnnie quickly unhitched him and led him to the side. Together, they poured the four buckets of sap into one of the large milk cans that Farmer Green had left in the farm wagon.

"Good boy, Twinkleheels! That's one trip down!" Johnnie said, patting his wet neck. He quickly turned the sled around and hitched the pony up again. "Now, back for more!"

The trip back was easier with the empty sled, but the wind was still fierce. When they got back to the clearing, Farmer Green already had four more full buckets waiting for them.

"Quickly, son!" he urged. "The temperature is dropping fast."

As Twinkleheels stood waiting to be hitched, he saw Bright and Broad watching him.

"The little one is doing it," Broad rumbled, his voice full of surprise. "He is taking the whole harvest out, bit by bit."

"He has a great heart for his size," Bright answered. "A very great heart."

Twinkleheels felt his own heart swell with a pride that was deeper and warmer than any he had felt before. It wasn't the pride of having new shoes or of looking grand. It was the pride of doing a hard job that nobody else could do.

He made a second trip, and a third. A rhythm was established. The perilous journey through the snow, the brief stop to empty the buckets, the quick return. Each time, the path was a little harder to see, the drifts a little deeper. Twinkleheels' legs began to ache, and his breath came in ragged, steaming plumes. But he never faltered. He kept his mind on the steady plod of his own feet and the feel of Johnnie's hand on the reins.

On the fourth trip, they met with a new disaster. A smaller branch, heavy with snow, had fallen directly across the narrow path. It was not big like the one on the main road, but it was too big for the little sled to pass over.

"Oh, no!" Johnnie cried in dismay.

Twinkleheels stopped. He was tired, and his shoulders ached from the pull of the harness. For a moment, he felt like giving up. The barn seemed a world away. But then he looked at the branch. It was wedged between two small trees. He pushed at it with his nose. It was heavy, but it moved a little.

"Wait, Twinkleheels!" Johnnie said. He scrambled over the branch and put his shoulder to it. "If I push and you pull, maybe we can move it just enough!"

He positioned himself, and Twinkleheels backed up a step, then surged forward, pushing with his chest and neck. Johnnie grunted with effort. The branch scraped and groaned against the tree trunks. Slowly, inch by inch, they moved it, just enough to clear a space for the sled to squeeze by.

They were both panting when they got it through. Johnnie threw his arms around the pony's neck. "You did it, Twinkleheels! You're the smartest pony in the world!"

That praise was like a warm meal and a drink of water. It gave Twinkleheels new strength. They carried on. The world grew dark, not just from the storm, but from the coming of evening. Farmer Green had lit a lantern in the clearing, and its small, golden light was a welcoming beacon on each return trip.

"Only two more loads!" he shouted to them as they started off on what felt like their hundredth trip. "The tank is almost empty! You're doing it!"

Those last two trips were the hardest of all. Every muscle in Twinkleheels' body screamed with fatigue. The snow was up to his belly in the drifts, and he had to lunge through them, pulling the sled by sheer will. He could feel that Johnnie was just as tired. The boy stumbled more than once in the deep snow, but he always got up and urged them onward.

At long last, they brought the final four buckets to the wagon on the road. The job was done. All the sap was safe.

Farmer Green came around the blocked path on foot, carrying his lantern. Its light fell on the tired boy and the weary pony, both covered in snow.

"Johnnie. Twinkleheels," he said, and his voice was thick with emotion. "I have never seen such work. You saved the day. You truly did. I am proud of you both."

He unhitched Twinkleheels from the sled and laid a gentle hand on the pony's neck. "Come on, old fellow," he said softly. "Let's get you home to a warm stall and a hot mash. No horse on this farm has ever earned it more."

The journey home was slow. Farmer Green led the oxen, and Johnnie walked beside Twinkleheels, his arm slung over the pony's weary back. Twinkleheels walked with his head low, too tired to even think of trotting. His fine clip-clop was gone, replaced by a slow, dragging shuffle. But in his heart, he felt a deep, quiet satisfaction that was better than any proud feeling he had ever known.

When they finally reached the warm, straw-scented barn, it felt like the finest palace in the world. Johnnie pulled off the wet harness and began rubbing him down with a rough sack, bringing warmth back to his cold muscles.

Old Ebenezer looked over from the next stall. "You are back late," he observed. "And you walk like a horse who has done a long, hard day's work."

"We were," Twinkleheels said simply. His voice was hoarse with weariness. "The snow was deep."

He did not boast. He did not talk about the fallen tree, or the narrow path, or the many trips he had made. He was too tired. And somehow, it didn't seem necessary.

Johnnie brought him a bucket of warm water, which he drank gratefully. Then he returned with a steaming mash of oats and bran, with a sweet trickle of molasses mixed in. It was the most delicious meal Twinkleheels had ever tasted.

As he ate, Bright and Broad were led past his stall on their way to their own. They stopped for a moment. They did not speak, but both great beasts turned their heads and looked at him. It was a long, steady look. And then, in a gesture of respect a horse could understand, Bright slowly nodded his massive head.

Twinkleheels finished his mash and buried his nose in the clean, sweet hay. Johnnie came back one last time, with a thick, warm blanket.

"Good night, Twinkleheels," the boy whispered, fastening the blanket's straps. "You are the best pony in the whole world."

Twinkleheels leaned against Johnnie for a moment, then rested his head on the edge of his stall. The barn was quiet now, except for the soft sound of horses and oxen chewing their hay and the fading howl of the storm outside. He felt the comfortable weight of the blanket on his back and the satisfying ache in his muscles. The new shoes were fine things, he thought drowsily. It was good to feel so solid and sure-footed. But the feeling of a hard day's work well done, of having been trusted and proven worthy, was even better. He closed his eyes and fell into a deep, dreamless sleep.

XVII A NEW RESPECT

The next morning, light streamed through the high, dusty window of the barn, carrying with it the clean, cold scent of fresh snow. Twinkleheels opened his eyes slowly. For a moment, he did not move. A deep soreness ran through his muscles, a sturdy ache that spoke of a hard day's work. He shifted his weight, and his new shoes made a solid, satisfying scrape on the wooden floor. He remembered the deep snow, the great fallen tree, and the quiet nod from Bright the ox. A feeling of contentment, warmer than any blanket, settled over him.

He was not the first one awake. Old Ebenezer was already munching placidly on his breakfast of hay. "Good morning," the old horse said, his voice a low rumble. "You slept the sleep of the truly weary. I did not hear a single stir from your stall all night."

"Good morning," Twinkleheels replied. His own voice sounded a bit stiff. "I feel as if I pulled the whole sugar-bush yesterday."

Ebenezer nodded. "That is how good work feels the morning after," he said. "It leaves its mark on you, so you do not forget. It is a good feeling, is it not? Better than winning a race you did not earn."

Twinkleheels thought about that. It was true. The pride he had felt in his new shoes seemed like a small, silly thing compared to the deep satisfaction that now filled him. Before he could answer, the barn door swung open and Johnnie Green came in, whistling a cheerful tune.

"Morning, Twinkleheels!" the boy cried. He hurried to the pony's stall, his face bright with affection. "How is my brave little horse today?" Johnnie did not wait for an answer but unlatched the stall door and came inside. He ran his hands over the pony's back and legs, checking for any soreness. "You were wonderful yesterday," he whispered, scratching Twinkleheels behind the ears in just the right spot. "Father said he's never seen a pony with so much heart."

Johnnie then produced a crisp, red apple from his pocket. Twinkleheels crunched it gratefully. The sweet juice was a wonderful treat. As Johnnie gave him a thorough brushing, two other horses were led back from the watering trough. They were a pair of handsome bays, sleek and long-limbed, with proud heads and restless eyes. They were the ones Ebenezer had called "young and somewhat flighty."

"Well, well," said the first bay, whose name was Flash. "Look who is getting all the attention. If it is not the little hero of the snowdrift." His tone was not kind.

"I heard he took all day to get to the sugar-bush," said the other bay, Dash. "We could have been there and back three times. We would not have needed the oxen to clear a path for us." He tossed his head and made his harness jingle.

A week ago, Twinkleheels would have been stung by their words. He would have wanted to tell them how fast he could run, to remind them that he had beaten Ebenezer in a race. But now, he simply kept chewing his apple and enjoyed the feeling of Johnnie's brush. He said nothing at all.

His silence seemed to bother the two bays more than any argument would have. "What is the matter?" Flash demanded. "Has the great adventure made you too important to speak to us?"

Just then, Farmer Green's voice called from the far end of the barn. "Bright! Broad! Time to haul some more wood." The great wooden doors opened, and the two huge oxen were led past the stalls. As they walked by Twinkleheels, Bright turned his massive head. He looked at the pony with his great, brown, liquid eyes. Then, slowly and deliberately, he blinked. It was a small gesture, but to the animals in the barn, it was as loud as a shout. It was a sign of fellowship; of shared work and mutual respect. Broad followed with a low, rumbling moo that sounded almost like a word of greeting.

Flash and Dash stood frozen, their mouths slightly open. They had seen it. They had seen the mighty oxen, who paid no mind to anyone, acknowledge the little pony. They looked at Twinkleheels, then at the oxen, and then back at each other. They did not seem to know what to think.

Ebenezer, who had seen it all, gave a quiet chuckle. "There are more ways to measure a horse than with speed," he said softly, more to himself than to anyone else. "And it seems our Twinkleheels is learning all of them."

XVIII A FRIGHT IN THE WOODS

The storm had passed completely. A brilliant sun shone down from a clear blue sky, making the endless fields of snow sparkle as if they were covered in diamonds. The air was still and cold, but the sunshine held the promise of a warmer spring to come.

"It is a good day to check the fences along the north pasture," Farmer Green announced at breakfast. "The snow may have brought down a few rails. Johnnie, I am going to ride Flash. He needs the exercise."

"Can I come too, Father?" Johnnie asked eagerly. "Can I ride Twinkleheels? We will stick to the paths you broke with the oxen. He is very sure-footed now with his new shoes!"

Farmer Green smiled. "Alright, son. But you do as I say. No wandering off the trail. Some of those drifts are deeper than your pony is tall."

Soon they were ready. Farmer Green swung himself onto Flash's back. The big bay horse was full of energy after being cooped up during the storm. He danced and sidled, eager to run. Twinkleheels stood quietly while Johnnie climbed onto his back. The little pony felt strong and steady. The soreness of the day before had faded to a pleasant memory of a job well done.

They set off, Flash leading the way. The bay horse constantly pulled at the reins, wanting to break into a gallop. "Easy there, boy," Farmer Green said, holding him to a brisk trot. "There is plenty of road ahead of you."

Twinkleheels trotted along behind, his new shoes making a crisp clinking sound on the packed snow of the path. He felt Johnnie's light weight on his back and the winter sun on his coat. He was perfectly happy to go at this steady pace. He watched the path carefully, placing his feet with the new sureness he had learned in the sugar-bush.

They left the main road and entered the woods, following a narrow logging trail. Here the snow was piled high on either side, forming white walls that muffled every sound. The branches of the pine trees were heavy with snow, and occasionally a soft clump would fall with a gentle "whoosh."

Flash grew more and more impatient. The narrow path constrained him, and he tossed his head in protest. He saw what looked like a smooth, open clearing just off the trail. It was a patch of unbroken snow, glittering in a patch of sunlight. To the flighty young horse, it looked like the perfect place for a run.

Before Farmer Green could even guess his intent, Flash gathered himself. With a powerful leap, he jumped the wall of snow beside the path and bolted into the clearing. "Flash! Whoa! you foolish creature!" Farmer Green shouted, pulling back hard on the reins.

But it was too late. The smooth clearing was not a clearing at all. It was a deep hollow, filled with soft, bottomless snow. Flash's first leap carried him into the very middle of it. His hooves found no purchase. He plunged downwards with a terrified snort, the snow billowing up around him like a white wave. The sudden stop threw Farmer Green from the saddle. He landed, with a grunt, in the soft, deep snow beside the floundering horse.

Johnnie cried out and pulled Twinkleheels to a halt. The little pony stopped instantly, his feet planted firmly on the solid trail. He looked at the scene before him. The big bay horse was in a panic. He thrashed and kicked wildly, squealing with fright, and only succeeded in sinking himself deeper and deeper into the drift. Farmer Green, covered in snow, was struggling to his feet. "Whoa, Flash! Settle down!" he commanded, but his voice was lost in the horse's terrified cries. Twinkleheels stood perfectly still. He remembered the feeling of yielding snow, the rising panic, and the uselessness of the struggle. But this time, he was not the one in the drift. This time, he was on solid ground. And he was not afraid.

XIX A PONY'S COUNSEL

"Father! Are you hurt?" Johnnie called out, his voice trembling.

Farmer Green brushed the snow from his face and pushed himself to his feet. "I am alright, son. The snow is soft. But this foolish horse is going to hurt himself if he does not calm down."

Flash was in a terrible state. His eyes rolled wildly, showing the whites, and a froth of fear appeared on his lips. He plunged and reared, his powerful legs churning the snow into a frantic mess. He was utterly trapped by his own panic.

"We have to get him out!" Johnnie said. "Should I go for help? I could get Bright and Broad!"

"That would take the better part of an hour," Farmer Green said, his brow furrowed with worry. He tried to step closer to Flash, holding out a hand and speaking in a low, soothing voice. "Easy now, boy. Easy. Just be still." But the bay was too frightened to listen to a man. Every time Farmer Green moved closer, Flash would renew his struggles with a desperate snort.

Twinkleheels had been watching all this with a strange sense of calm. He saw Flash's fear, and he understood it. He remembered his own fear. But he also remembered the quiet waiting, and the path that Farmer Green had dug for him.

He took a step forward on the solid trail, as close as he could get to the edge of the drift. Then, he lifted his head and let out a soft, low nicker. It was not a call of alarm or a sharp command. It was a quiet sound, a sound of reassurance, the kind of sound a mother horse might make to a nervous foal.

The effect was startling. Flash, in the middle of a desperate lunge, suddenly froze. His thrashing stopped. His head, which had been tossing wildly, turned towards the little pony on the path. His ears swiveled forward, catching the sound.

Twinkleheels stood his ground. He looked steadily at the terrified bay horse. Then he nickered again, a gentle, rumbling sound deep in his chest. He did not move a muscle otherwise. He just stood there, a small, solid, and unafraid figure on the edge of the chaos.

Farmer Green stared in amazement. "Well, I will be," he murmured, scarcely daring to breathe.

Slowly, very slowly, the wild look began to fade from Flash's eyes. His heavy, panicked breathing began to slow. He was still sunk to his belly in snow, but he had stopped fighting it. He kept his gaze fixed on Twinkleheels, as if the little pony were an anchor in a swirling sea of fear.

"That is it, boy," Farmer Green whispered, seeing his chance. He began to talk to Flash again, his voice calm and steady. "Look at the pony. See how he stands? He is not afraid. There is nothing to fear. Just be still now. Be still."

With the horse finally quiet, Farmer Green was able to get closer. He unbuckled the saddle, which was twisted at an awkward angle, and pulled it free, tossing it onto the path. Then he took hold of Flash's bridle. "Alright, boy," he said. "We are going to get you out. But you have to help me. No more struggling."

He began to clear the snow away from Flash's front legs, packing it down to give the horse something to push against. It was slow, hard work. All the while, Twinkleheels stood like a small statue, watching. And all the while, Flash kept his eyes on Twinkleheels, drawing courage from the pony's unshakable calm.

Finally, Farmer Green had a rough ramp stomped out of the snow. "Alright, Flash! Now!" he urged, pulling on the bridle. "Come on, boy! Up!"

With a great heave, Flash lunged forward. His hooves found the packed snow. He scrambled, slipped, and then with one last mighty effort, he pulled himself out of the drift and onto the solid path. He stood there, trembling from head to foot, his coat dark with sweat and caked with snow. He had a long, shallow cut on his front leg, where he had struck it with his own shoe in his panic.

Farmer Green patted the horse's shaking neck. "There now. You are safe." He looked at the cut. "It is not deep," he said to Johnnie. "But it is just what I always say. A horse floundering is a danger to himself. We will have to go home and tend to this." He then turned and walked over to Twinkleheels. He put a hand on the pony's neck and looked down at him for a long moment. "You were a great help, little fellow," he said, his voice full of a new kind of respect. "A very great help. You kept your head when he was losing his."

Johnnie leaned forward and hugged his pony's neck tightly. "You are the bravest pony in the world, Twinkleheels," he whispered.

XX A LESSON LEARNED

The journey back to the barn was very different from the journey out. Flash, who had been so eager and impatient, now walked with his head low. He limped slightly on his injured leg and stayed very close to Twinkleheels, as if he did not want to be more than a few feet away from the little pony. Farmer Green walked between the two, leading Flash by the bridle.

When they arrived at the barn, Dash saw them coming from the stable yard. He let out an anxious whinny when he saw his companion's condition; the limp, the trembling, the raw cut on his leg.

Farmer Green led Flash directly into his stall and began to tend to the wound. He washed it carefully and applied a soothing salve. "You were lucky this time, you flighty animal," he said, not unkindly. "Let this be a lesson to you. There is a time for running and a time for walking carefully."

Later, when the chores were done and the barn settled into the quiet of the late afternoon, the horses began to talk. The stalls were arranged in a long row: Ebenezer, then Twinkleheels, then the two bays, Flash and Dash.

It was Flash who spoke first. His voice was low and humbled. He looked across the empty stall between them towards Twinkleheels. "Thank you," he said.

Twinkleheels, who was enjoying a mouthful of hay, stopped chewing. "For what?" he asked.

"For today," Flash said. "In the woods. I was very frightened. I have never been so frightened. I thought I would sink and never get out."

"We saw you," Dash added, his own voice quieter than usual. "You were so calm. You just stood there."

"I was frightened, too, the first time I was stuck in the snow," Twinkleheels admitted honestly. "I struggled just like you did. But it only makes it worse. Farmer Green told me. You have to be still and wait for help. Or sometimes," he added, thinking, "you have to wait for your own fear to grow quiet."

Flash looked down at his clean, bandaged leg. "I see that now," he said. He was quiet for a moment. Then he said, "We laughed at you, you know. For being slow."

"We did," Dash confessed. "We thought working with the oxen was silly. We thought being fast was the only thing that mattered for a horse."

Twinkleheels considered this. He looked over at old Ebenezer, who seemed to be dozing but who, Twinkleheels knew, heard everything. He thought of his new shoes, and the thrill of the blacksmith's shop. He thought of the race in the pasture. And he thought of the heavy sled and the nod from Bright the ox.

"Being fast is a fine thing," Twinkleheels said slowly. "And I like to run as much as anyone. But I am learning that it is not the only thing. It is good to be strong, like Bright and Broad. It is good to be wise, like Ebenezer. And sometimes," he finished, "it is good to just be steady."

From the next stall, old Ebenezer opened one eye. "Knowing when to run and when to walk," the old horse said, his voice carrying through the quiet barn, "and when to simply stand still and offer a friend your courage; that is the mark of a truly fine horse."

Flash and Dash looked at each other. Then they both looked at the small pony in the stall next to theirs. He was not big. He was not the fastest. But he was steady. He was brave. And he was wise in a way they were only just beginning to understand. For the first time, they looked at him not as a little pony to be teased, but as a friend to be admired. And in the warm, quiet barn, Twinkleheels felt a new kind of pride, a quiet and steady feeling that he knew would last for a very long time.

"You see?" Ebenezer said softly to Twinkleheels. "There is nothing to it. The smith knows his work, and I trust him to do it well."

When all four of Ebenezer's feet were newly shod, it was Twinkleheels' turn. Johnnie Green came and untied his halter rope. "Come on, boy," he said gently. "It is your turn to get your new shoes."

Twinkleheels took a deep breath. He remembered Ebenezer's advice. *Pay attention and obey orders.* He thought of the snooty white mare and lifted his head a little higher. He would not let her see him be afraid. He walked calmly to the center of the dusty floor and stood where the blacksmith guided him.

"Well now, what have we here?" the blacksmith said, his voice a low rumble. "A fine little pony. This is your first time, is it?" He ran a hand down Twinkleheels' leg, his touch firm and sure. "Don't you worry. We will take good care of you."

When the smith bent down and reached for his foreleg, Twinkleheels remembered his worry. *I don't know which foot to hold up first!* But just as Ebenezer had said, the blacksmith showed him. He tapped the back of the pony's leg, and Twinkleheels, almost without thinking, lifted his foot.

The process was just as it had been for Ebenezer. There was the strange feeling of his hoof being scraped and filed, but it did not hurt at all. It only tickled a little. When the hot shoe was brought near to test the fit, a pungent, smoky smell rose up, and Twinkleheels sidled nervously.

"Easy now, little fellow. Just for a moment," the blacksmith soothed.

And then came the part Twinkleheels had feared most: the nails. He held his breath as the smith positioned the first nail. Tap. Tap. Tap. The nail slid into the hard, unfeeling rim of his hoof. There was no pain at all! It was no worse than having a burr caught in his mane. He let out his breath in a quiet little puff.

One by one, all four feet were shod. When the blacksmith was finished, he gave Twinkleheels a friendly pat on the rump. "There you go, pony. All set for the hard roads."

Twinkleheels tentatively put his foot down. It felt different. Heavier. Solid. He took a step, then another, listening to the crisp, metallic click of his new shoes on the hard-packed earth of the smithy floor. He looked over at the white mare, who was watching him with a surprised look on her face. Twinkleheels gave a little toss of his head and strutted past her towards Johnnie, his feet making a proud clatter.

"I think he likes them," Johnnie said to his father, laughing.

"He earned them," Farmer Green replied, paying the blacksmith. "He is a good pony."

Out in the bright sunshine, Twinkleheels felt ten feet tall. He walked beside Ebenezer, trying to make as much noise as he could with his new shoes. Click, clack, click, clack! It was a wonderful sound.

"Well done," Ebenezer said, his voice full of approval. "You were as brave as any horse in the smithy today."

"It was not so bad," Twinkleheels admitted, though he was bursting with pride. "But I was glad you were there with me."

The trip home was a joyful one. Twinkleheels no longer felt the need to hurry. He was content to trot along behind the buggy, matching his new, steady rhythm to Ebenezer's own. He was a working pony now. He had shoes. He felt very grown up indeed.

XVII A CHANGE IN THE AIR

The last of the snows melted away under a sun that grew warmer each day. The brown, sleeping earth began to show patches of green, and the brook at the edge of the pasture, which had been locked in ice, now chattered and gurgled merrily. A new feeling was in the air, a feeling of waking up. It made the horses feel frisky.

Flash and Dash, the two bays, were full of restless energy. But something about them had changed since the day they were stuck in the snowdrift. They still loved to run, but their boasting had softened into friendly challenges.

"The ground is firming up, Twinkleheels!" Flash called across the stalls one morning. "Soon it will be dry enough for a proper race! What do you say?"

"I say I will be ready," Twinkleheels replied, feeling a happy thrill at the thought. He and the bays were friends now, in a way they had never been before. Their respect, he was discovering, was worth more than winning any race.

One afternoon, Farmer Green returned from town with his wagon, and in the back was a wooden crate. From inside came a nervous bleating. Johnnie and Twinkleheels, who had been helping clear stones from the near pasture, trotted over to see.

Farmer Green lowered the tailgate and opened the crate. Out stepped a ewe with gentle, worried eyes. And hiding behind her legs was the smallest, fluffiest lamb Twinkleheels had ever seen. It was no bigger than old Spot the dog, and its coat was the color of a fresh snowball.

"This is our new ewe," Farmer Green said to Johnnie. "And her little one. We will call him Woolly."

Johnnie reached out a hand to the lamb, who shied away and pressed closer to its mother. Twinkleheels watched, his heart feeling strangely soft. He knew what it was like to be small in a world of big horses and bigger barns. He took a careful step closer and gave a low, gentle nicker.

The little lamb peeked out from behind its mother. Its tiny black nose twitched. It looked at the small pony, who was not so very much taller than its own mother, and it did not seem so frightened.

For the next few days, Twinkleheels found himself spending his time near the pasture fence where the ewe and Woolly were kept. He would watch the little lamb's antics, how it would take a few wobbly steps and then leap straight up into the air for no reason at all, landing on stiff legs. It made Twinkleheels want to kick up his own heels.

But even as the world turned green and cheerful, a change of a different sort was coming. Old Ebenezer noticed it first.

"I smell rain on the wind," the old horse said one evening, lifting his head and sniffing the air. "A great deal of it."

Twinkleheels sniffed too, but all he could smell was sweet hay and the damp earth.

The next day, the sky was a strange, bruised yellow. The birds were quiet. Farmer Green kept looking up at the clouds gathering in the west, a worried line on his forehead. "This looks like a real gully-washer, Johnnie," he said. "We had best make sure everything is secure."

They spent the afternoon checking the latches on all the barn doors and bringing all the animals inside. The ewe and Woolly were given a snug pen in a warm corner of the barn. By the time the first fat drops of rain began to spatter in the dust, everyone was safe and under cover.

And then the storm broke. It was not a gentle spring shower. The wind howled around the corners of the barn like a hungry wolf, and the rain came down not in drops but in solid, roaring sheets. It hammered on the roof so loudly that the horses had to raise their voices to be heard.

"Goodness!" Twinkleheels exclaimed, his eyes wide. "The sky is falling!"

"It is only a spring storm," Ebenezer said calmly, though even he looked a bit unsettled by the fury of it. "But it is a fierce one. The creek will be rising."

All through the night, the storm raged. Twinkleheels dozed fitfully, waking each time a great clap of thunder shook the old barn to its foundations. He could hear the ewe bleating nervously in her corner, and he felt a pang of worry for little Woolly.

XVIII THE ROARING CREEK

The world that greeted them the next morning was new and strange. The rain had stopped, but a gray mist hung over everything. The air was full of the sound of rushing water.

When Farmer Green opened the big barn door, they all stared. The familiar pasture was half-submerged. The little brook, usually so gentle and polite, had become a muddy, roaring monster. It had swollen over its banks and was tearing at the fields, carrying sticks and clumps of earth with it.

"I have never seen it so high," Johnnie said in an awed voice.

Farmer Green's face was grim. "The old bridge to the north pasture will be gone for certain," he said. He began his morning chores, his movements quick and worried.

It was Johnnie who first noticed something was wrong. "Father!" he cried out. "Look!"

The ewe was running back and forth along the fence line of her pen, bleating in a frantic, heartbreaking way. She would run to one end, then turn and run to the other, calling and calling.

Her pen was empty. Woolly was gone.

"The latch must have shaken loose in the storm!" Farmer Green exclaimed. He vaulted over the fence. "He cannot have gone far."

They began to search. Twinkleheels, from his stall, watched with a growing sense of dread. He could see their footprints in the mud around the barn. They searched the wagon shed. They looked behind the woodpile. But there was no sign of the little lamb.

Then Farmer Green stopped, pointing towards the floodwater. "Oh, no," he said, his voice low.

There, in the soft mud at the edge of the raging creek, were tiny hoofprints. They led towards the water, and then they disappeared. Nearby, a section of the lower fence had been completely washed away. The lamb must have slipped out during the night and wandered straight towards the water.

"He must be on the other side!" Johnnie shouted, running along the bank and peering through the mist. The creek was a hundred feet wide here, a torrent of brown water.

And then he saw him. On the far side, on a small island of high ground that had not yet been swallowed by the flood, stood a tiny, trembling white shape. It was Woolly. The island was shrinking with every passing minute as the water swirled around it, eating away at the edges.

"We have to get him!" Johnnie cried, turning a desperate face to his father.

Farmer Green shook his head, his expression full of despair. "We cannot, son. The bridge is gone. No horse could swim that current; it would sweep even Bright and Broad off their feet. There is nothing we can do."

He put a hand on Johnnie's shoulder. Inside the barn, the ewe's cries were pitiful. Twinkleheels felt a knot of fear tighten in his stomach. To stand by and do nothing while the little lamb was in such peril seemed the worst thing in the world. He strained against his halter, his mind racing. There had to be a way! A full-sized horse could not cross. But what about a pony? No, he was not a strong enough swimmer for such a flood.

He thought of every path and trail he had ever explored with Johnnie. He thought of the woods, the sugar-bush, the high pastures. And then, an image flashed into his mind. It was from last autumn, a chase after a chipmunk that had led him far up the creek, into the thick woods where the banks grew steep and close together. There was a place... a place where a great old oak tree had fallen long ago, its trunk spanning the narrow gorge like a bridge.

It was a rickety bridge, to be sure, and narrow. Too narrow for Ebenezer or the bays. But for a pony? And for a boy?

Twinkleheels began to pull at his halter rope, stamping his feet and whinnying loudly. He tossed his head toward the woods upstream, trying with all his might to tell them.

"Whoa, Twinkleheels, easy now," said Johnnie, coming over to soothe him.

But Twinkleheels would not be soothed. He pulled again, harder this time, looking from Johnnie to the woods and back again. He let out a sharp, commanding whinny. It was not a sound of fear, but of urgency.

Johnnie stared at him, his brow furrowed. He knew his pony. He knew Twinkleheels' every mood. This was different. "Father," he said slowly, "I think he is trying to tell us something. He keeps looking towards the woods."

"He is just spooked by the flood, son," Farmer Green said distractedly, his eyes still on the poor lamb.

"No, it is more than that," Johnnie insisted. He unlatched the stall door. "What is it, Twinkleheels? What do you want to show me?"

Twinkleheels wasted no time. He trotted purposefully out of the barn and started up the muddy track that led towards the woods, stopping every few feet to look back and nicker, urging them to follow.

"I am going with him," Johnnie declared. "Maybe he knows another way."

Seeing the pony's determination, and with no other hope to cling to, Farmer Green sighed and followed his son. "All right," he muttered. "Let us see what has gotten into this pony."

Twinkleheels led them away from the flooded lowlands, up onto higher ground where the walking was easier. He moved with a certainty that was surprising, navigating the muddy patches and fallen branches until they entered the deep quiet of the woods. The roar of the creek was still with them, a constant thunder to their left. After a ten-minute walk, Twinkleheels stopped.

He had led them to a narrow gorge. Below, the creek was a churning chute of water, but it was only twenty feet across here. And spanning the gap was the huge, moss-covered trunk of the fallen oak tree.

"Well, I will be," Farmer Green breathed. "I had forgotten all about this old log." He looked at it critically. "It is slick with moss. And it is a long way down."

Twinkleheels did not wait for a decision. To show them it could be done, he lifted a hoof and carefully, deliberately, placed it on the wide base of the log. He tested his footing. It was slippery, but the rough bark offered some grip for his new shoes. He took another step, and then another, his body balanced and sure. He walked a quarter of the way across the log bridge, then stopped and looked back at Johnnie as if to say, "You see? It is safe."

Johnnie's heart was beating fast. "He is right, Father. I can do it. I am light. I can go across and get Woolly, and Twinkleheels can help me."

"Johnnie, no! It is too dangerous!"

"It is more dangerous to do nothing!" Johnnie pleaded. "Look at him! He is not afraid. He knows he can do it. Please, Father. Let me try."

Farmer Green looked at his son's determined face. He looked at the small, brave pony standing steady on the log over the raging water. He thought of the little lamb, alone and terrified. He took a deep, shaky breath. "Be careful, son," he said, his voice thick with emotion. "Be careful."

Johnnie scrambled onto the log. Twinkleheels waited for him, then slowly began to walk ahead, picking his way with incredible care. Johnnie followed right behind, his hands occasionally touching the pony's flank for balance. Below them, the water roared. But up on the log, all was quiet concentration. They moved as one, boy and pony, their trust in each other a palpable thing.

They reached the other side. Johnnie slid off the log onto solid ground, his legs trembling slightly. "Good boy, Twinkleheels," he whispered, patting the pony's neck.

They made their way quickly downstream to the shrinking island. Woolly was bleating weakly, huddled in the very center. When he saw Johnnie, he cried out. Johnnie waded through the shallow water at the edge of the island and gently scooped the shivering lamb into his arms.

"It is all right, little fellow," he murmured. "We will take you home."

The journey back was even harder. Johnnie now had to balance himself while holding the squirming lamb. But Twinkleheels seemed to understand. He walked even more slowly, a solid, reassuring presence in front of Johnnie. If Johnnie stumbled, the pony would stop and wait, a living anchor in the center of the dangerous crossing.

Finally, their feet touched the home bank. Johnnie almost collapsed with relief as he handed the lamb to his father, who hugged them both, his face wet with more than just the mist.

"You did it," Farmer Green said, his voice choked with pride. "You both did it. My brave boy, and my brave, smart pony."

Back at the barn, the reunion between the ewe and her lamb was a flurry of joyous bleats and nudges. Flash and Dash, who had watched the entire rescue from the pasture fence, looked at Twinkleheels with something akin to awe.

That evening, as the tired animals rested in their stalls, the barn was filled with a contented peace.

"We saw you," Flash said, his voice quiet. "At the creek. I would not have dared to cross that log."

"Nor I," admitted Dash. "My legs would have trembled too much."

Twinkleheels listened, chewing his hay. He did not feel proud in the strutting way he had with his new shoes. This was a different feeling. It was a quiet, deep warmth that settled right in the center of his chest.

From the next stall, old Ebenezer's voice rumbled softly. "Some horses are made for speed on a dry track," he said. "And some are made for pulling a heavy load. But true strength is not always in the size of the muscle or the speed of the leg. Sometimes, the greatest strength is a steady heart, and the courage to walk a narrow path to help a friend."

Twinkleheels looked at his own small feet, now caked with mud. They were not the feet of a racehorse. They were not the feet of a great draft horse. But today, they had been exactly the right feet for the job. And as he drifted off to sleep to the sound of the creek returning to its banks, Twinkleheels knew that he would not trade them for any other feet in the world.
