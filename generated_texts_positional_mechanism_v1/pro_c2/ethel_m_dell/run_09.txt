She had made her choice, or rather, the choice had been made for her, and she was terrified of its consequences.

When he released her, she was breathless, her world narrowed to the circle of his arms. She was in her forties, tall and spare, and dressed in a crisp, grey travelling suit. Her hair was swept up beneath a severe hat, and her face, though not unkind, was all brisk efficiency. She carried a small, professional-looking leather case.

"Mrs. Burke?" she enquired, her voice as crisp as her attire. "I am Nurse Atkinson. Mr. Burke sent me from Brennerstadt. He said the case was urgent."

Sylvia could only stare. He had not said he would arrange for a nurse. He had done it. It was already a fact. The woman was here.

"I... I am Sylvia Russell," she stammered. "Mr. Guy is my brother-in-law. Mr. Burke is... a friend."

Nurse Atkinson's eyebrows rose a fraction, but her expression remained impassive. "I see. My instructions were to report to Mrs. Burke. A slight misunderstanding, evidently. No matter. May I see the patient? Mr. Burke stressed that I was to take over all nursing duties immediately to relieve you. He was most insistent that you were not to be taxed further."

Each word was a small, sharp nail, pinning Sylvia to her new reality. She was being relieved of her post. Burke had not merely suggested it; he had engineered it with swift, irrefutable action. She led the nurse into the house, feeling like a guest in her own home.

The interview between Nurse Atkinson and Guy was brief and, from what Sylvia could glimpse through the half-open door, utterly one-sided. The nurse's quiet professionalism seemed to leave no room for the sort of emotional appeal that Guy had used so effectively on Sylvia. She took his temperature, checked his pulse, and asked a series of brisk questions, her pen scratching in a small notebook. When she emerged, she gave Sylvia a brief, dismissive nod.

"Convalescent debility, exacerbated by a highly strung nervous temperament," she announced, as if diagnosing a faulty engine. "He needs rest, a regulated diet, and a complete absence of emotional excitement. You have done your best, Miss Russell, but this is a case for a firm, professional hand. Now, if you will show me my quarters, I should like to get settled before I prepare the patient's supper. Broth and a dry biscuit, I think. Nothing more."

Sylvia showed her to the guest-hut, her mind reeling. An absence of emotional excitement. A firm, professional hand. Nurse Atkinson, with her starched cuffs and her clinical gaze, was Burke's will made manifest. She was the embodiment of the new order.

Sylvia had just returned to the main house when she heard the familiar sound of a horse's hoofs. This time, her heart did not leap with anxiety, but with a strange, fatalistic calm. Burke swung down from his saddle, his eyes searching for her at once. He took in the scene, the strange motor-car, the open door of the guest-hut, and then looked at her, a question in his eyes.

"Nurse Atkinson has arrived," she said, her voice flat.

"Good," he said, satisfaction in his tone. "That was quick. Is she competent?"

"She seems to believe Guy will survive on a diet of broth and firmness," Sylvia said, with a flash of her old spirit.

He smiled, a brief, understanding smile. "It's probably what he needs. Are you ready? The Merstons will be expecting us for tea."

She looked from his strong, confident face to the house that had been her whole world, the house where Guy now lay under the care of a stranger. The choice had been made for her. The page had been turned.

"Yes," she said, lifting her chin. "I am ready."

He nodded, as if he had never expected any other answer. "Go and change, then. I will wait for you here."

As she went to her room, she did not look back. She did not dare. The time for looking back was over. Burke was waiting. And the future, however strange and terrifying, was waiting with him.

his voice was so low she could hardly catch the words, "I brought you out here to-day because I could not bear to see you in that house another minute. I could not bear it, Sylvia. Do you understand?"

She did not understand; she understood nothing save that his hand was upon her arm, a grip not of restraint but of a strange, urgent possession. It was as if he were holding on to her, not she to him. The world, which had seemed to tilt upon its axis, righted itself with a jarring suddenness. She was aware of the scent of dust and dry grass, of the faint whir of an insect near her ear, of the solid reality of the man before her.

"I don't know what you mean," she whispered, though in her heart a wild, chaotic knowledge was beginning to dawn.

"Don't you?" His smile was gone now, and his face was grim, set in lines of endurance that she had never truly seen before. "I think you do. I think you have known for a long time. You have been giving your life, drop by drop, to Guy. I have watched you. I have watched you grow thinner, paler, your eyes larger with a worry that was not yours to carry alone. And I could do nothing. I was one of his failures, remember? I could not help him. And so I could not help you."

He broke off, his gaze turning towards the window as if the sight of her was too much for him. "That night, when I spoke to you so brutally, it was because I had reached the end of my tether. To see you so convinced of his hopelessness, to see you prepared to throw away your youth, your health, your entire future, for a cause you yourself believed was lost... it was more than I could stomach. I had to do something. I had to break the spell."

Sylvia stared at his profile, the strong, uncompromising line of his jaw, the sheer power in his stillness. "Break the spell?" she echoed, her voice faint.

"Yes. The spell of your devotion. It was killing you, Sylvia. So I went to Kieff. I told him what I believed. That Guy was not physically ill so much as morally bankrupt. That he was feeding on your sympathy like a parasite, and that the only cure was to starve him of it. To confront him. To give him an ultimatum. Kieff would not do it. He said it was too dangerous, that Guy's heart was weak. So I did it myself."

The confession fell into the quiet room with the weight of a physical blow. Sylvia took an involuntary step back. "You?" she breathed. "That night... after you left me... you went to Guy?"

"I did." Burke's voice was flat, devoid of all emotion. "I told him some home truths. I told him he was a coward and a fraud, preying upon the goodness of a woman who deserved better. I told him that his performance was over. That he would either stand on his own two feet like a man, or we would let him sink. I gave him no choice. It was not a pleasant interview."

"His wrath had been so terrible," she remembered saying to herself. But it had not been for her. It had been for Guy. The devil that had gone out of Guy had first been faced by Burke. He had wrestled with him, while she had slept in exhausted ignorance. A shiver, of terror and a strange, unbidden admiration, went through her.

"And it worked," she said, stating the fact as a new, astonishing discovery. "He has come back."

"He has come back," Burke agreed, turning to face her once more. His eyes held hers, and in their depths she saw not triumph, but a vast and weary relief. "The devil has been cast out, for now. And I have brought in a professional to see that he does not return. Nurse Atkinson will not stand for any nonsense. Guy's convalescence will be swift and, I suspect, extremely uncomfortable for him. Which is as it should be. His need is no longer the first consideration here."

He took a step closer, and the space between them seemed to crackle with an unspoken energy. "There are, as I said, other needs." His voice was low, and it vibrated through her. "Sylvia, look at me."

It was the same command he had given before, but this time she did not flinch from it. She raised her eyes to his, her heart hammering against her ribs like a trapped bird. The overwhelming confusion was still there, but it was mingled now with a terrifying clarity. She saw the truth of everything he had said; she saw the cage of duty she had built around herself, and she saw the man who had, with ruthless compassion, just set her free.

"I am looking," she said, and her voice was barely a whisper.

"I am not a patient man, Sylvia," he said, and his tone was rough with feeling. "I have wanted to say this for weeks, since the first day I saw you trying to mother a man who was not worthy of the dust on your shoes. I have held my tongue because his need, as you rightly said, was great. But his need is met. Now it is mine that is paramount. I want you to be my wife."

The words were so stark, so devoid of romance, that they were more shocking than any poetical declaration. It was not a plea; it was a statement of intent, as solid and unadorned as the man himself.

Sylvia's mind went blank. She could not think; she could only feel. The floor seemed to drop away from beneath her feet. "Your... wife?"

"Yes," he said, as if it were the most obvious thing in the world. "I need a wife. This farm needs a mistress. And I find I have no desire for any other woman but you. We can make a good life here, you and I. I can make you happy. I will spend my life making you happy, if you will let me."

He was not touching her, but she felt as if his hands were on her shoulders again, holding her, claiming her. The imperious force of his will was a tangible thing, surrounding her, leaving her with no room to breathe, no room to think.

"I... I can't," she stammered, falling back on the old, familiar excuses. "There's Guy... the farm..."

"Guy will leave as soon as he is well enough," Burke said, dismissing the objection with a wave of his hand. "I will see to it. And the farm is my concern. I am not asking you to be a housekeeper, Sylvia. I am asking you to be my partner. My companion. My wife. The one thing that matters. Will you?"

She shook her head, not in negation, but in sheer, helpless bewilderment. "You don't understand," she cried, a note of desperation in her voice. "I don't... I don't love you!"

The words, once spoken, seemed to hang in the air, a terrible, irrevocable accusation. She had not meant to be so cruel, so blunt. She had simply spoken the first truth that came to her overwhelmed mind.

For a long moment, Burke was utterly still. A shutter seemed to come down over his face, and the light in his eyes died. When he spoke, his voice was cold, clipped. "I see. I had not realised that was a prerequisite. I apologise. I seem to have misread the situation entirely."

He turned away from her then, a movement of such finality that it struck a chill to her very heart. He was going. He was leaving her. The strange, terrifying, and exhilarating world he had opened up for her was closing again, and she was to be left outside, in the cold.

"Burke, wait!" she cried, without thinking.

He paused at the door, his back to her. "What for?" he said, his voice flat. "I have made my intentions clear. You have made yours equally so. There is nothing more to be said."

"But there is!" She took a step towards him, then another, her hands twisting together in front of her. "I... I'm sorry. I didn't mean... it came out all wrong. I am confused. Everything has happened so fast."

He did not turn. "It is a simple enough question, Sylvia. It requires a simple answer. Yes, or no."

"But it isn't simple!" she protested, her voice rising. "Nothing is simple! Yesterday, my whole world was Guy. My whole purpose was keeping him alive. To-day... to-day you have taken that away, and you have put this... this impossible choice in its place! You cannot expect me to just..." She trailed off, unable to articulate the whirlwind in her soul.

"I expect nothing," he said, his voice like stone. "I only hoped. It appears I was mistaken. Now, if you will excuse me, I must check on that horse. And you should get ready. We are still going to the Merstons."

He was gone. The click of the door latch was like a gunshot in the silent room. Sylvia stood frozen, staring at the space where he had been. The interview was over. He had asked for her hand, and she had thrown her answer in his face. A hot wave of shame and regret washed over her, so powerful it made her feel faint. He had bared his soul to her, in his own rough, unadorned way, and she had repaid him with a cruel, childish honesty.

She had told him she did not love him. But was it true? As she stood there in the wreckage of the conversation, she began to doubt it. What was love, if not this overwhelming sense of his presence, this terror at his absence? What was it, if not the strange comfort she felt when he took charge, the unwilling admiration for his strength, the sudden, sharp pang of jealousy when she thought of him needing any woman but her?

Her mind flew back to his words. "I can make you happy." And the most terrifying part, the part that made her tremble, was that she believed him.

She did not know how long she stood there. It was the sound of his voice from the yard, calm and authoritative as he spoke to one of the farm hands, that finally roused her. He had not ridden away in a fury. He was waiting. "We are still going to the Merstons." It had been a command.

With a sudden, decisive movement, she went to her room. Her hands were shaking as she pulled out her best riding habit, a simple thing of dark grey cloth that she had not worn since leaving England. She dressed with a feverish haste, her mind not on what she was doing, but on the man waiting outside. She had given him her answer. But the day was not yet over. And she had a sudden, fierce determination that the matter was not closed. She did not know what she would say, or what she would do. She only knew that she could not let him go away believing that she felt nothing.

When she stepped out into the brilliant afternoon sun, he was standing by the horses, his back to the house. He had not changed his clothes, but he seemed to have himself under a rigid control. He turned as she approached, and his face was an inscrutable mask. He gave no sign that he had even noticed her change of attire.

"Are you ready?" he asked, his voice polite, distant.

"Yes," she said, her own voice small.

He helped her to mount, his touch impersonal, efficient. It was as if the scene in the sitting-room had never happened, as if they were strangers again. They rode out in silence, a different silence from the one before. This one was not charged with unspoken emotion; it was empty, a desolate void.

The ride was an agony for Sylvia. Every rock and tree seemed to mock her. She kept glancing at his stern profile, willing him to look at her, to speak, to show some sign of the man who had laid his heart bare only an hour before. But he rode on, his eyes fixed on the horizon, a stranger she had wounded too deeply.

The Merstons' farm appeared in the distance, a welcoming cluster of buildings in the vast emptiness of the veld. Mrs. Merston, a stout, kindly woman with a face creased by sun and laughter, came out onto the veranda to greet them.

"Burke, my dear boy! And you must be Miss Russell! We are so delighted. Come in, come in! The kettle is just boiling. You look tired, my dear," she said, taking Sylvia's hand in a warm, dry grip. "This dreadful business with young Guy has been a trial for you all, I'm sure."

Sylvia murmured a polite reply, feeling Burke's presence behind her, a wall of cold indifference. Inside, the Merstons' sitting-room was a haven of chintz and polished wood, filled with the comforting smell of baking. It was a world away from the raw, elemental drama of her own home.

Mr. Merston, a quiet man with a pipe, shook Burke's hand warmly and then turned to Sylvia. "A pleasure, Miss Russell. Any friend of Burke's is a friend of ours."

Tea was a civilised torture. Sylvia answered questions about England, made appropriate noises about the quality of Mrs. Merston's scones, and tried desperately not to look at Burke. He, for his part, was the perfect guest. He talked cattle prices with Mr. Merston, complimented the tea, and spoke of the weather with a calm assurance that set Sylvia's teeth on edge. No one would ever have guessed that his proposal of marriage had been flung back in his face less than two hours before.

It was Mrs. Merston who finally broke through the polite facade. When her husband had taken Burke out to see a new borehole, she turned to Sylvia, her kind eyes full of a shrewd sympathy.

"You are not happy, my dear," she said, not as a question, but as a statement of fact.

Sylvia's composure, already stretched to breaking point, finally snapped. To her horror, she felt her eyes fill with tears. "Is it so obvious?" she choked out.

"Only to another woman," Mrs. Merston said gently, patting her hand. "Men like Burke, they see what they want to see. He sees a brave young woman who has had a hard time. He doesn't see that she is at the end of her rope. Or perhaps," she added, with a thoughtful look, "perhaps he does. He is not a fool, our Burke."

"He is the kindest, strongest man I have ever known," Sylvia whispered, the tears now tracing paths down her hot cheeks. "And I have been abominably cruel to him."

"Ah," said Mrs. Merston, with a small, knowing nod. "So that is the way of it. Well, my dear, strength like his can take a little cruelty. It is the strong trees that can withstand the storm. But even the strongest tree can be brought down if the storm rages for too long. Do not let the sun go down on your wrath, child. Or on his."

The simple, biblical wisdom struck Sylvia with the force of a revelation. She had been so wrapped up in her own confusion, her own fear, that she had not considered his pain. She had seen only his strength, not the vulnerability it shielded.

When Burke and Mr. Merston returned, Sylvia had composed herself. But there was a new resolve in her eyes. As they prepared to leave, and Burke was making his farewells, she drew Mrs. Merston aside. "Thank you," she said, her voice low but firm. "Thank you for everything."

The older woman simply squeezed her hand. "God speed, my dear," she whispered.

The ride home began in the same strained silence as the ride out. The sun was low in the sky, painting the clouds in violent strokes of orange and purple. The beauty of it was almost painful. Sylvia rode beside Burke, her heart a battleground of fear and determination. She had to speak. She had to undo the damage she had done.

Finally, when she felt her courage would fail her if she waited another moment, she reined in her horse. "Burke," she said.

He stopped a few paces ahead and turned in his saddle, his face shadowed by the brim of his hat. "What is it?" he asked, his voice weary.

"I need to say something." She took a deep breath. "What I said earlier... in the sitting-room... was unforgivable. It was cruel and it was untrue."

He watched her, his expression unreadable. "Untrue?" he prompted, a faint note of interest in his cold tone.

"I told you I did not love you," she said, forcing the words out, her gaze locked on his. "That was not the truth. The truth is... I do not know if it is love. I do not know anything about love. It is not a subject I have had much time to consider. But I know that the thought of you walking out of that room, of you walking out of my life, was the most dreadful moment I have ever experienced. I know that I admire you more than any man I have ever met. And I know," she finished, her voice dropping to a whisper, "that if your offer still stands... my answer is yes."

He was still for so long that she thought he had not heard. The world seemed to hold its breath with her; the only sounds were the soft stamp of a hoof, the sigh of the evening wind through the tall grass. His face, shadowed by the wide brim of his hat, was a mask, unreadable and remote. The silence stretched, a thin, taut wire of suspense, and her courage, so recklessly spent, began to fray. She had made a fool of herself. She had offered herself out of a desperate, muddled pity, and he, in his wisdom, was according to her the silent contempt she deserved. She dropped her gaze, staring at the pommel of her saddle, her cheeks burning with a shame so hot it brought tears to her eyes.

Then he moved. It was not a sudden action, but a slow, deliberate dismounting. He swung himself from the saddle with the easy grace that marked all his movements and stood for a moment by his horse's head, his back to her. He seemed to be checking the bridle, but she knew it was a pretext. He was giving himself time; or perhaps he was giving her time to retract her wild, impulsive words. When he finally turned and walked towards her, the last of the sun's rays caught the hard line of his jaw. He stopped at her stirrup and looked up. His face was no longer a mask; it was stripped bare, and what she saw there made her heart give a great, painful lurch. It was not contempt. It was a raw, aching hunger, a terrible gravity that seemed to hold all the loneliness of the veld in its depths.

"Say that again," he commanded, his voice low and rough, almost unrecognisable.

Her lips trembled. She could not find her voice. She could only look down at him, her own soul naked in her eyes.

"Say it again," he repeated, more softly this time, but with an intensity that vibrated through her. "I want to be sure I did not dream it."

"My answer... is yes," she whispered, the words a thread of sound in the vast stillness.

He reached up, not to help her down, but to lay his hand over hers where it rested on the saddle. His touch was firm, possessive, a brand of ownership that sent a tremor through her entire body. His fingers were rough, calloused from work, but their grip was infinitely gentle. He did not speak for another long moment, simply holding her hand, his gaze locked with hers. It was as if he were memorising her face, searching for the truth in the very depths of her being.

"You are not sure it is love," he stated, not as a question, but as a fact they both acknowledged. "I know. It does not matter. I will teach you, Sylvia. Or we will learn together. I have enough for us both to start with."

He released her hand then, and stepped back. "Come. It is getting dark. And Guy will be wondering what mischief we are about."

The old, gruff tone was back, but it held a new note now, a deep, resonant undertone of possession and tenderness that was for her alone. He helped her down from her horse, his hands strong and sure at her waist, letting her go the instant her feet touched the ground. There was no lingering, no embrace. He was not a man for easy demonstrations. His courtship would be as stern and uncompromising as he was himself, she knew that. But as he gathered the reins of both their horses in one hand, his other found hers, his fingers lacing through her own in a grip that was both a comfort and a promise. They walked the rest of the way, leading the horses, and the silence that fell between them was no longer a thing of strain or misunderstanding, but a shared space, rich with unspoken things. The world had shifted on its axis. Nothing was the same, and yet everything was blessedly, terrifyingly simple. She was his, and he was hers. The rest was detail.

The light from the farmhouse windows spilled out into the gathering dusk, a welcoming, golden beacon. Inside, she could see a shadow moving. Guy. A flicker of the old anxiety stirred in her breast. How could they tell him? What would he think? He had depended upon her for so long, had viewed her as so completely his own companion in suffering. This would feel like a betrayal.

Burke's grip on her hand tightened, as if he had read her thoughts. "Leave it to me," he said, his voice quiet beside her. "Do not be afraid."

She was not afraid. Not while he was beside her. The realisation was as startling as it was profound. She, Sylvia, who had been afraid for so long--afraid for Guy, afraid of Burke, afraid of herself--was not afraid. She lifted her chin and walked with him into the circle of lamplight, ready for the first trial of their new life.

They entered the sitting-room to find Guy exactly as they had left him, save that he had a book in his hands. He looked up as they came in, a cheerful, easy smile on his face. The change in him was still a source of constant wonder. He looked younger, the deep lines of pain and bitterness smoothed away from his brow.

"Hullo, you two!" he said brightly. "I was beginning to think you had eloped. Mrs. Merston is a dangerous woman. I am sure she was trying to matchmake." He laughed, a light, carefree sound that was music to Sylvia's ears.

She felt the heat rise in her cheeks and was glad of the shadows where she stood. Burke, however, moved forward into the full light of the lamp. He did not release her hand.

"As it happens," Burke said, his voice perfectly steady, "her efforts were entirely successful."

Guy's smile wavered. He looked from Burke's resolute face to Sylvia's, half hidden behind his shoulder. His eyes widened slightly, taking in their joined hands. The book slid from his lap onto the floor with a soft thud.

"What?" Guy said, his voice barely a whisper. "What do you mean?"

"I mean that Sylvia has done me the great honour of agreeing to be my wife," Burke said, his tone leaving no room for argument or misunderstanding. He drew her forward slightly, so that she stood beside him, a silent partner to his declaration.

The silence in the room was absolute. Sylvia's heart hammered against her ribs. She watched Guy, her breath held in an agony of suspense. She saw a flicker of expressions cross his face, one after the other, too swift to name. There was shock, certainly. Disbelief. And then, for a heartbreaking second, a flash of the old, possessive pain, the look of a lost child. Her own heart contracted in a spasm of pity and guilt. She had hurt him. After everything, she had dealt him this new blow.

But then, something else settled in his eyes. It was a kind of weary resignation, but there was a sweetness in it too. A faint, sad smile touched his lips. He looked from her to Burke, and then back again.

"Well, I'll be damned," he said, and the words were soft with wonder. He bent down, with a hint of his old weakness, and retrieved the fallen book. He placed it carefully on the table beside him before he looked up again. "I suppose I should have seen this coming. A blind man could have seen it coming."

He looked at Burke, and his smile widened, losing some of its sadness. "You are a dark horse, Burke. All this time, playing the stern guardian. And all the while..." He shook his head, a ghost of his old, charming laugh returning. "You deserve her. By God, you do. You saved my life, and now you are stealing my nurse. It seems a fair trade."

"Guy..." Sylvia began, taking a step towards him, her voice thick with emotion.

"No, don't," he said, holding up a hand. "Don't you dare feel sorry for me, Sylvia. This is right. It is the most right thing that has happened in this house for a very long time." He stood up, a little unsteadily, and came towards them. He stopped in front of Burke and held out his hand. "Congratulations, you old rogue. You have my blessing, for all that's worth. Be good to her. If you are not, I will personally rise from my sickbed and thrash you."

Burke took his hand, his expression uncharacteristically gentle. "I will," he said. And the two words were a solemn vow.

Then Guy turned to her. He took both her hands in his, his touch light and brotherly. "And you, Sylvia," he said, his eyes suddenly bright with unshed tears. "Be happy. You have earned it more than anyone I know." He leaned forward and kissed her forehead, a brief, chaste salute. "Now," he said, stepping back and attempting a tone of brisk authority, "I am exhausted by all this emotion. And I am sure you two have things to discuss. So if you will excuse me, I shall retire. Do not stay up all night plotting my future. I am perfectly capable of looking after myself for a bit."

He gave them a final, wavering smile and walked towards his room. At the door, he paused and looked back. "Sylvia?"

"Yes, Guy?"

"I always did like Joe," he said, with a flash of his old, wry humour. "But I think you have found a man who is much more capable of putting up a camp-bed. Goodnight, you two."

And then he was gone, closing the door softly behind him.

Sylvia stood where he had left her, tears streaming silently down her face. They were not tears of sorrow, or guilt, but of an immense, overwhelming relief. The devil had truly gone out of Guy. And in its place, a quiet, selfless goodness had taken root.

She felt a touch on her arm and turned to find Burke beside her. He did not speak, but took a handkerchief from his pocket and gently wiped her cheeks. His touch was clumsy, unpractised, but it was the most comforting thing she had ever known.

"He meant it," she whispered, looking up at him. "He is really all right."

"He is a better man than I gave him credit for," Burke said quietly. "He loves you, in his way."

"He is my brother," she said, and knew it for the truth. The tangled, unhealthy bond of patient and nurse had been severed, and in its place was a clean, simple affection.

"Yes," Burke agreed. "And now it is time for his sister to get some rest. You are worn out."

He led her to the long-chair, the one he had so despised, and pushed her gently into it. "Sit. I will make you some tea."

"No, I can do it," she protested, attempting to rise.

He put a firm hand on her shoulder. "You will sit still," he commanded. "This is your first lesson. You are no longer the chief cook and bottle-washer of this establishment. You will allow yourself to be looked after."

She subsided, too weary to argue, and watched as he moved about the room. He was not as clumsy as Joe, but his movements in the domestic sphere had a certain unpractised quality that she found endearing. He lit the spirit lamp with a steady hand, found the tea-caddy and the cups, and moved with a quiet efficiency that was all his own. He was a man who could build a fence, break a horse, or face down a ruffian, and yet he was here, in this shabby sitting-room, making tea for her. The sheer, unbelievable wonder of it washed over her in a fresh wave.

He brought the cup to her, strong and sweet, just as she liked it. He did not sit down himself, but stood leaning against the mantelpiece, watching her as she drank.

"You have not eaten," he said suddenly. "I will find you something."

"No, please," she said. "The tea is enough. I could not eat a thing."

He accepted this with a nod. They were quiet for a while, the only sounds the ticking of the clock and the gentle hiss of the lamp. It was a comfortable, peaceful silence. The long, tumultuous day was over.

When she had finished her tea, he took the cup from her and set it aside. "To bed now," he said. "You are asleep on your feet."

"And you?" she asked. "Where will you sleep?"

"In the guest-hut," he said. "The camp-bed is already in your brother's room. Remember?"

A faint blush touched her cheeks. "Of course. I had forgotten."

He walked with her to the door of her room. The little hallway was dark, and he paused to light a candle for her from the lamp in the sitting-room. He handed it to her, their fingers brushing. The small flame flickered, casting their shadows, huge and distorted, against the wall.

"Sylvia," he said, his voice very low.

She looked up at him. His face was serious in the candlelight, his eyes dark and intense. She thought for a moment that he might kiss her. Her heart began to beat a frantic, hopeful rhythm.

But he only raised her hand to his lips. He did not kiss her palm, or her wrist, but her fingers, one by one, a salute that was both reverent and deeply passionate.

"Sleep well," he said, his voice rough with an emotion he could not quite conceal.

"You too, Burke," she managed to whisper.

He released her hand and was gone, his footsteps receding down the hall and out into the night. She stood for a long moment, the candle trembling in her hand, her fingers still burning from the touch of his lips. Then she went into her room and closed the door, leaning back against it, her heart full to bursting. Guy had come back. And she, in her own way, had come home.
