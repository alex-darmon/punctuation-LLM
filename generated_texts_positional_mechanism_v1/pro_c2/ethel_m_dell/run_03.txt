The last of the winter chill lingered in the stone of the cottage, a dampness that Eleanor felt in her very bones. She knelt before the hearth, coaxing a flame from the obstinate, wet kindling, her brow furrowed with a familiar vexation. A plume of acrid smoke billowed into the room, and she coughed, waving it away with a grim-faced futility. From his armchair, wrapped in a thick woollen blanket, Leo gave a weak laugh. "A losing battle, Nellie," he murmured, his voice still thin from the fever. "Perhaps we should surrender and freeze with dignity." His attempt at humour held a fragile edge. She did not look at him. "The dignity of a common cold is something I should prefer to avoid. Do be quiet, Leo." Her tone was sharper than she intended, born of sleeplessness and a deep, gnawing anxiety that had been her constant companion for weeks. A heavy footstep sounded on the porch, followed by a sharp rap at the door that did not wait for an answer. The latch clicked and Captain Miles entered, bringing with him a gust of clean, cold air that swept the smoke aside. He took in the scene at a glance; Eleanor on her knees, soot smudged on her cheek, the sullen fire, and Leo huddled in his chair. "This will not do," he said, his voice a low rumble that seemed to fill the small room. He did not address either of them directly, but went to the window and threw it open. Then he came to the hearth. "Stand up, Eleanor." It was not a request. She rose stiffly, her knees aching, and moved aside without a word. She went from him without a word, a strange and unsettling lightness in her limbs, as if the chains of duty she had worn for so long had been momentarily struck from her. Her feet carried her up the familiar stairs, her hand skimming the polished banister, but her mind was a whirlwind of confusion. Part of her seethed with a hot resentment. How dare he command her so? How dare he judge her, and judge Leo, knowing nothing of the long, terrible nights, of the fear that had been her constant companion? Yet another, deeper part of her, a part she had long suppressed, felt a guilty, thrilling tremor of release. To be commanded, to have the decision taken from her hands, to be forced out into the sun and the wind; it was a tyranny, but a tyranny for which some secret corner of her soul had been crying out.

In her room, she moved with the automatic precision of long habit. She stripped off the plain dress she wore, stained with dust from the hearth, and opened the great mahogany wardrobe. Her riding habit hung in the back, untouched for months, smelling faintly of cedar and disuse. The sight of it brought a lump to her throat. She remembered the last time she had worn it, riding out with her father not a week before his final illness. They had galloped along the ridge, laughing into the wind, and he had told her she was the finest horsewoman in the county. Now he was gone, and Leo, her only remaining family, was a fragile, broken thing she must tend and protect.

She pulled the heavy skirt and fitted jacket on, her fingers fumbling with the buttons. Her reflection in the long mirror startled her. The woman who looked back was a stranger; her face was thin and shadowed, her eyes wide and dark in her pale face. Miles had been right. She looked exhausted. She looked ill. A wave of anger at her own weakness washed over her, and with it, a stubborn spark of defiance. She would not be an object of pity. She would go with him, and she would show him that she was not some fragile invalid to be coddled. She braided her hair swiftly and pinned it up, then pulled on the sturdy leather boots that had sat so long in the corner. The act of lacing them felt like an act of arming herself for a battle she did not yet understand.

When she came downstairs, he was waiting for her in the hall, his hat in his hand. He did not speak, but his eyes swept over her, a quick, appraising glance that took in everything. He gave a single, curt nod, and turned towards the door. She followed him out into the bright, cool air. The world seemed unnaturally vivid after the dimness of the sickroom. The sky was a vast, brilliant blue, scoured clean by the wind, and the sunlight on the gravel path was almost blinding.

Two horses stood ready, their coats gleaming in the sun. Her own mare, a spirited chestnut named Ariel, whickered softly as she approached, nudging her hand for the sugar she no longer carried. Miles's horse, a powerful black stallion, watched them with an intelligent, almost arrogant eye. Miles helped her to mount, his hands strong and impersonal on her waist, lifting her as if she weighed nothing. For a moment, she was acutely conscious of his nearness, of the scent of leather and fresh air that clung to him. Then she was in the saddle, gathering the reins, and he had swung himself up onto his own horse with an easy, fluid grace.

They set off at a walk, down the long drive and through the gates, turning onto the track that led towards the high pastures. For a long time they rode in silence. Eleanor kept her back straight, her gaze fixed between Ariel's pricked ears. She was intensely aware of him beside her, a solid, implacable presence. The silence was not comfortable; it was charged with all the things that had been said and left unsaid in the house. She could feel his will beside her as surely as she could feel the sun on her skin, a steady, unyielding pressure.

"The gorse is coming into bloom," he said at last, his voice even and conversational.

She followed his gaze to the hillsides, which were indeed brushed with the first bright gold of early blossom. "Yes," she said, her own voice sounding stiff to her ears. "It is early this year."

"The rains were good." He spoke of the farm, of the sheep, of a breach in the wall down by the river. His talk was all of practical things, of the land that was her charge and his life. Gradually, without her quite noticing how it happened, the tightness in her chest began to ease. He was asking her opinion on rotating the grazing, on the price of wool, on whether to hire another hand for the lambing season. He was treating her not as a nursemaid, but as the mistress of the estate, as his partner in the business of its running. It was a role she had almost forgotten how to play. Her answers, at first hesitant, became more confident. The problems of the farm were real, solid things she could grasp, a welcome respite from the nebulous, shifting anxieties of the sickroom.

They rode higher, the wind growing stronger, whipping stray tendrils of hair across her face and bringing a flush to her cheeks. The air was clean and sharp in her lungs, and beneath her, Ariel moved with a joyful energy, eager for a run. The world opened up around them, a vast panorama of rolling green hills and distant, purple mountains.

"You should get out more," he said, breaking a companionable silence. "The land needs you. It does not do well under a mistress who is a stranger to it."

The words were not an accusation, but a simple statement of fact. And yet, they pricked at her conscience. "Leo has needed me," she said, the familiar defence rising automatically to her lips.

"Leo," he said, and his tone changed, the casual air dropping away to reveal the steel beneath. "Let us speak of Leo."

They had reached the crest of a hill, and he reined in his horse, turning in the saddle to face her. The wind tore at his words, but his eyes held hers, and she could not look away.

"You are throwing your life away for him, Eleanor."

"That is not true!" she cried, stung. "He is my brother. He is all I have."

"He is a man grown," Miles said, his voice hard. "And a man who has learned that his weakness is his greatest strength. He has you fetching and carrying, waiting on his every whim. He has you trapped. Can you not see it?"

"You are cruel!" she gasped, her heart pounding. "He was at death's door. The doctor himself said he might not live. He is weak, he is frightened. Of course he needs me!"

"The fever is past," Miles stated, implacable. "The weakness that remains is in his character, not his body. And you feed it, Eleanor. Every time you run to him, every time you let him see the power he has over you, you make him weaker. You are not saving him. You are crippling him."

"And you would have me do what?" she demanded, her voice shaking with a mixture of anger and a dawning, terrible fear that he might be right. "Abandon him? Leave him to fend for himself when he can barely stand?"

"I would have you let him stand on his own two feet," he said grimly. "Or learn that he will fall. That is how a man learns. You have done your duty, and more than your duty. Now it is time he did his." He paused, his gaze searching her face. "And it is time you remembered your own."

"My duty is to my family," she whispered.

"Is it?" he said, and a strange, ironic smile touched his lips. "Or is it your penance? Are you paying for some crime you think you have committed? Do you believe, in some dark part of your soul, that you are responsible for his state?"

The question struck her like a physical blow. A hot, shameful blush flooded her face. How could he know? How could he see into the most secret, guilty corner of her heart? The memory rose up, vivid and sickening; the argument they had had, Leo and she, on the night he had taken the fall. He had wanted to sell the south meadow, to pay his gambling debts. She had refused, had spoken to him with a coldness and a fury that shamed her even now. He had stormed out into the rainy night, drunk and angry, and had been found the next morning at the bottom of the ravine, his horse dead beside him. She had never confessed to anyone the terrible guilt that gnarod at her, the belief that her harsh words had sent him to his near death.

Tears sprang to her eyes, hot and bitter. She turned her head away, unable to bear the searching intensity of his gaze. "You know nothing about it," she said, her voice choked.

For a long moment he was silent. She could feel him watching her, and she braced herself for another harsh truth, another blow. But when he spoke, his voice was unexpectedly gentle.

"I know that you have a heart that is too soft for your own good," he said. "And I know that you cannot carry his burdens for him forever. They will break you." He reached out, not to touch her, but his hand hovered in the space between them, a gesture of stark, unadorned offering. "Let me help you, Eleanor."

She looked at him then, her eyes blurred with tears. The wind whipped his dark hair about his forehead, and his face, which she had always thought so hard and unyielding, seemed in that moment to hold a compassion that stole her breath. He was not being cruel; he was trying to save her. The realization was as overwhelming as it was terrifying.

"I don't know how," she whispered.

"Begin by living again," he said simply. "Begin with this." And with a sharp cry, he dug his heels into his stallion's sides. The horse leaped forward, galloping down the other side of the hill. For a second, Eleanor hesitated. Then, with a sob that was half despair and half exhilaration, she gave Ariel her head.

The mare sprang after him, her powerful muscles bunching and releasing, devouring the ground. The wind rushed past Eleanor's ears, a wild, singing roar that deafened her to everything but the thunder of hooves and the frantic beating of her own heart. She leaned low over the mare's neck, the tears drying on her cheeks, the grief and guilt and exhaustion of months scoured away by the sheer, primal joy of the gallop. She was no longer a nurse, no longer a keeper of a sickroom. She was a woman on a horse, flying over the green earth under a vast blue sky, and she was, for the first time in a very long time, utterly and breathtakingly alive.

They raced for what seemed like miles, until the horses were lathered and breathing hard. Miles finally slowed to a canter, and then a trot, and she drew up beside him. Her hair had come loose from its pins and streamed behind her. Her cheeks were scarlet, and her eyes shone with a light he had never seen in them before.

He looked at her, a slow, unreadable smile playing on his lips. "Better?" he asked.

She could not speak. She could only nod, her breath coming in ragged gasps, a smile of her own, wide and shaky, spreading across her face. He had given her back a piece of herself she had thought was lost forever.

The ride back was different. The silence was easy now, a shared space of contentment. The sun was lower in the sky, casting long shadows before them, and the world was bathed in a soft, golden light. Eleanor felt a deep, bone-weary tiredness, but it was the clean, wholesome fatigue of physical exertion, not the soul-deep weariness of the sickroom. When they reached the stables, she slid from the saddle before he could dismount to help her, her legs feeling unsteady beneath her.

"I will see to the horses," he said. "You go in. Thomas will have made tea."

"Thank you, Miles," she said, and the words felt inadequate for the great, shifting change that had taken place within her.

"Eleanor? Is that you? Where have you been? I've been calling for an hour. My head is splitting, and the fire has gone out. Did you forget my medicine?"

It was Leo.

She stopped on the threshold, the scent of the wind still in her hair, the feeling of the gallop still thrumming in her veins. She looked down, and he was watching her, his face upturned in the lamplight from the hall. Dinner was a strange affair. Thomas served a simple roast chicken and vegetables, food that Eleanor had not tasted in what felt like weeks, having subsisted on broth and toast and whatever scraps she could find time to eat. Leo's place at the head of the table remained empty. His absence was a palpable thing, a brooding presence in the room. They did not speak of him. Instead, Miles spoke of the world beyond the estate. He talked of London, of the theatre and the new motorcars that were frightening the horses in the street. He spoke of a trip he had taken to Egypt, of the heat and the dust and the immense, silent faces of the pharaohs.

Eleanor listened, rapt. She had read of these things in books, but to hear them from the lips of someone who had seen them made them real, made them possible. She found herself asking questions, her voice shy at first, then gaining confidence. She spoke of the books she had read, of the history of the house, of the grandmother she had barely known who had planted the now-grizzled apple trees in the orchard. She spoke of herself, not as a nurse or a keeper, but as a person with thoughts and a past of her own.

Miles was a superb listener. He watched her as she spoke, his chin resting on his hand, his eyes never leaving her face. He seemed to be drawing the stories out of her, giving her the space and the silent encouragement to rediscover the sound of her own voice when it was not pitched to the querulous demands of the sickroom.

When Thomas had cleared the plates and they were left with a glass of port for him and a small cup of coffee for her, a comfortable quiet settled between them. The fire crackled in the hearth, a counterpoint to the ticking of the grandfather clock in the hall.

"You have been a prisoner here for a long time, Eleanor," Miles said at last, breaking the silence.

"I wish it," she said, and the words came from a place so deep inside her she had not known it existed.

He gave a slight bow. "Good night, Eleanor."

"Good night, Miles."

The woman who had woken that morning, weighed down by a thousand small duties and a single, crushing sense of hopelessness, was gone. The old Eleanor would have been up the stairs in a flash, her heart pounding with guilt and fear. The new Eleanor stood perfectly still, her hands clasped before her. With a resolve that felt as if it were forged from steel, she turned and walked towards the hall. She took a lamp from the side table and began to climb the stairs. She was not running to appease. She was going to confront. She was no longer a nursemaid scurrying to a tantrum. She was the mistress of the house, and there had been a disturbance in her domain.

She reached his door and did not knock. She turned the handle and pushed it open.

The room was a mess. A water carafe lay in glittering shards on the floor, a dark patch spreading on the expensive rug. Leo was sitting up in bed, his thin hair dishevelled, his face pale and contorted with fury. He was still in his nightshirt, and the sight of his skinny, shaking frame should have evoked pity. Tonight, it evoked only a weary sort of anger.

He looked up as she entered, his eyes blazing. "So, you decide to grace me with your presence at last! Did you enjoy your dinner? Your cosy little chat with him? While I lay here, dying of thirst, my head splitting open!"

"You had water, Leo. Thomas brought it to you."

He stared at her, his mouth agape. He had never heard her speak like this. It was as if a sheep had suddenly turned and growled like a wolf.

"You have Thomas," she went on, her voice steady and relentless. "And tomorrow I shall send to the village for Mrs. Gable. She is a competent nurse. Between them, you will have everything you require."

"A nurse?" he squeaked, his fury giving way to a new, dawning panic. "I don't want a nurse! I want you!"

"You cannot have me," she said, and the finality in her tone was absolute. "Not in the way you have had me before. I am your sister, Leo, not your keeper. It is high time you learned the difference. And it is long past time that I did."

She looked at the mess on the floor. "Thomas will clear this up in the morning. Try not to destroy anything else. The heirlooms of this house are not yours to break in a fit of pique."

She turned to leave.

"Eleanor, wait!" His voice was different now. All the bluster was gone, replaced by a thin thread of genuine fear. "Don't leave me."

She paused at the door, her back to him. She thought of the wind in her hair, of the hard, honest ground beneath the stallion's hooves, of the look in Miles's eyes across the dinner table. She thought of the future, a blank page waiting to be written.

"I am not leaving you, Leo," she said, her voice softer now, but no less firm. "I am leaving this room. There is a world of difference."

And without looking back, she closed the door behind her, leaving him alone in the quiet, shadowed space with the consequences of his own actions. She walked down the hallway to her own room, the lamplight making a small, steady circle around her. She did not feel triumph, or even relief. She felt a great, aching sadness for the brother she was losing, but an even greater, burgeoning hope for the woman she was finally, at long last, becoming.

The ceremony in the little Brennerstadt church was brief and stark. The parson was a nervous man with a rambling manner, and the only witnesses were his wife and a clerk from the land-registry office. Yet for Sylvia, the moment was charged with a significance that needed no outward pomp. As Burke's deep voice spoke the ancient vows, she felt a sense of peace settle over her, deep and profound. When he placed the plain gold band upon her finger, his hand was as steady as a rock. The ring was cool against her skin. It felt solid, real, an anchor in the shifting tides of life.

Afterwards, they lunched in a private parlour at the hotel. Burke had ordered the meal in advance, and it was served with a quiet efficiency that seemed to be his hallmark. He talked a little, of the journey that lay ahead of them, of the house by the sea.

"It is very simple," he said. "Little more than a cottage. But the sea is at the door, and the air is clean. You will get strong there."

It was always of her that he spoke; of her rest, her comfort, her strength. It was a new and bewildering experience for Sylvia to be the focus of such concentrated care. She, who had always been the carer, found it strangely difficult to be the one who was cherished. She felt a sudden, unaccustomed shyness.

"And you, Burke?" she asked, her voice very low. "What is it that you want?"

He stopped in the act of lighting a cigarette and looked at her across the table. His eyes, so often stern and searching, were filled with a light that made her heart stand still.

"I want you," he said simply. "That is all. It is all I have wanted since the first moment I saw you, standing in the dust and the heat with your chin in the air, defying me."

A hot blush rose to her cheeks. "I did not know," she stammered. "I thought... I thought you despised me."

"I despised the life you were being forced to lead," he corrected her gently. "I hated seeing your strength and your goodness poured out into a hopeless cause. I wanted to snatch you away from it, from the very first day. But it was not my right. I had to wait."

He had waited. He had stood by, silent and grim, while she had struggled and suffered. He had watched her give her heart's devotion to another man. A pang of remorse shot through her.

"Oh, Burke," she whispered. "Can you forgive me? For being so blind?"

He reached across the table and covered her hand with his. His touch was warm and infinitely reassuring.

"There is nothing to forgive," he said. "You did what you believed to be your duty. I honour you for it. But that time is over, Sylvia. Henceforth, your only duty is to be my wife. And to be happy."

The train journey was long and tedious, but Sylvia was scarcely aware of it. She sat in a corner of the carriage, watching the parched landscape of the Karoo slide past the window, but her thoughts were not on the scenery. They were on the man who sat opposite her, reading a day-old newspaper with an air of settled concentration. Her husband. The word was still new and strange upon her mental tongue. She studied him covertly from beneath her lashes. The harsh lines of his face, the square, determined jaw, the eyes that could be so cold and yet held such a capacity for tenderness. He was not a handsome man in the way that Guy was handsome. His was a face carved by hardship and command, a face that had known solitude and responsibility. And it was the strongest, most wonderful face in the world.

He looked up suddenly, as if feeling her gaze, and caught her watching him. She flushed, but did not look away. He put down his paper.

"Tired?" he asked.

"A little," she admitted.

"Rest," he said. He moved to sit beside her and put his arm around her shoulders, drawing her head down to rest upon him. "Sleep. I will watch."

She yielded to his strength, to the solid comfort of his presence. The scent of him, of tobacco and clean linen and something that was just himself, filled her senses. The rhythmic clatter of the train on the tracks was a soothing lullaby. For the first time in longer than she could remember, she felt utterly safe. She closed her eyes, and as she drifted into sleep, her last conscious thought was of his words: "I will watch." He was always watching over her.

She awoke to the scent of the sea.

The train had stopped, and the air that came through the open window was cool and damp and briny. It was a smell utterly different from the dry, dusty air of the veldt. It spoke of vast, open spaces, of freedom, of a world renewed.

Burke was standing on the platform, overseeing the removal of their luggage. She watched him for a moment, a tall, commanding figure in the lamplight of the small, seaside station. He turned and saw her at the window, and came back to the carriage door.

"We are here," he said.

He helped her down, and for a moment she just stood, breathing in the strange, new air. The house was not far. A short drive in a hired trap brought them to a low, white-walled cottage, perched on a dune with the dark, sighing sea just beyond it. An elderly, coloured woman, her face a mass of friendly wrinkles, was waiting at the door, a lamp held high.

"This is Maria," Burke said. "She will look after us."

Maria bobbed a curtsy, her smile vast and welcoming. "Welcome home, Master Burke. Welcome, missus."

Inside, the cottage was simple, almost spartan. The furniture was of dark, heavy wood, the floors were bare and scrubbed clean, and the air was cool. But a fire was burning in the hearth of the main room, casting a warm, flickering glow on the walls, and a meal was laid on the table. It was a haven of peace.

After they had eaten, Burke led her to the French windows that opened onto a small veranda. He opened them, and the sound of the waves filled the room, a steady, rhythmic shushing on the sand. The moon had risen, and it laid a path of silver across the dark water.

"This is your kingdom now, Sylvia," he said, his voice low.

She went and stood beside him, looking out at the immensity of the ocean. It was so different from the farm, from the enclosed, sun-baked world she had always known. It was vast, and powerful, and a little frightening.

She shivered, though not from cold.

He drew her closer, his arm going around her waist. "You are not afraid?" he asked, his perception, as always, unnervingly acute.

"No," she said, though it was not entirely the truth. "It is just... so new. So different."

"All good things are," he said. He turned her to face him, his hands resting on her shoulders. The firelight played over his stern features, softening them. "This is a new life, Sylvia. Our life. There are no ghosts here. No old sorrows. Only you and I."

He looked down at her, and his gaze was so intent, so full of a deep and patient hunger, that it took her breath away. All her defences, all the shy reserves of a lifetime, seemed to melt away before it. This was her husband. And he had brought her here not just to rest, but to be loved.

"Burke," she whispered, her voice trembling.

"My wife," he answered, and his voice was rough with a feeling he no longer tried to conceal.

He did not command her now. He did not need to. When he bent his head and his lips found hers, it was a surrender, and a homecoming. The sound of the sea was in her ears, and the strength of his arms was around her, and she knew, with every fibre of her being, that the book of her life had not just had a page turned. It had been opened at the chapter for which all the others had been a mere prelude. And it was a chapter that would be filled with joy.

Do you think I have not seen what this has done to you? What he has done to you? I will not have it. I will not stand by and watch you waste yourself, watch you fade into a shadow in his service. You matter more than that."

His words fell into the quiet room, each one a separate, weighted thing. They were not words of love, not as she had ever read of them; they were hard, and possessive, and utterly uncompromising. They were a statement of fact, a claim. Her heart began to beat in a wild, frantic rhythm against her ribs, a frantic bird trapped in a cage. She tried to find her voice, to form some protest, but none came. His gaze held hers, demanding, relentless. The world seemed to shrink until it contained only the two of them, and the unbearable tension that stretched between them.

And then, from the other room, a voice floated, thin and querulous. "Sylvia? I say, are you there?"

It was Guy. The sound shattered the spell. With a little gasp, Sylvia pulled back, and this time Burke’s fingers fell away from her arm. The release was as sudden and shocking as the grip had been.

"I must go to him," she said, her voice unsteady.

Burke did not move. He stood like a man carved from granite, his face a thunderous mask. "Let him wait. For once in your life, let him wait."

"But he might need something," she protested, already moving towards the door, her sense of duty a shield she instinctively raised against the turmoil Burke had unleashed within her.

"He needs to learn to fetch it for himself," Burke said, his voice dangerously low. "He is a man, not a babe in arms. Or have you forgotten that?"

She paused at the door, her hand on the frame. "That isn't fair, Burke. He has been terribly ill."

"He is recovering," he shot back. "Thanks to you. And this is your reward. To be at his beck and call, to have your life measured out by the ringing of his bell. I tell you I will not have it."

"Sylvia!" The call came again, more insistent this time, tinged with impatience. "Is Burke keeping you? Tell him to stop being a bully!"

A dark red flush rose under Burke's tan. He took a stride towards her, and for a wild moment she thought he meant to physically bar her way. But he stopped, his hands clenched into fists at his sides.

"You see?" he said, his voice harsh with suppressed fury. "He plays you like a fiddle. And you dance to his tune every time."

"Please, Burke," she whispered, her own nerves frayed to the breaking point. "Not now."

She did not wait for his reply but slipped into Guy's room, her escape driven by a confusion so profound she could not begin to unravel it. She felt like a fugitive, though from what, or whom, she could not say.

Guy was propped up on his pillows, a book lying unheeded on his lap. He looked pale, but his eyes held their familiar, mocking gleam. "Ah, there you are, my ministering angel. I was beginning to think our grim guardian had spirited you away. Was he bullying you? He has a deplorable manner, has our Burke."

"He was not bullying me," Sylvia said, her voice sharper than she intended. She went over to the window and busied herself with drawing the blind a little further against the afternoon sun. "What did you want, Guy?"

"I wanted you," he said simply. "It gets dashedly lonely in here. And I heard voices raised. I was concerned for your welfare, naturally." He smiled, a faint, lazy curving of his lips. "You looked quite magnificent when you were defying him just now. A lioness defending her… well, her charge. It was most impressive. I am only sorry I am the cause of such friction between you."

He did not sound sorry at all. He sounded, as he so often did, faintly amused by the drama he created. A sudden weariness washed over Sylvia, so immense it was almost a physical pain. She saw in that moment, with a dreadful clarity, the truth in Burke’s angry words. She was tired; tired of the tension, tired of being pulled between these two men, tired of the ceaseless anxiety that had been her companion for so long.

"I came to tell you that Burke and I are riding over to the Merstons' after tea," she said, turning from the window to face him. The words came out before she had even consciously decided to say them. It was as if some other, more decisive part of herself had taken control.

Guy's eyebrows shot up. "Are you indeed? All by yourselves? How very enterprising. And what about your poor, helpless patient?"

"Burke says you are convalescent now," she replied, quoting Burke without realising it. "And Joe will be here. You have only to call if you need anything."

"So, the tyrant has issued his decree, and you are to obey," Guy mused, looking at her with a considering light in his eyes. "I wonder. Is it that you are obeying him, Sylvia, or is it that you wish to go?"

The question hung in the air between them. She felt the hot colour rise in her cheeks again, the same blush of guilty confusion that Burke had provoked in her earlier. "The Merstons are our neighbours. It is only polite," she said stiffly.

"Of course," he said, his tone silky. "Politeness above all. Well, you must not let me detain you from your duties. Go and have your ride. But do not be surprised, my dear, if you find the Veldt a little too small for the two of you. It has a way of closing in, with a man like Burke."

She left him then, without another word, and went to her own room to change. She felt a strange, reckless determination. She would go. She would do as Burke wished. Perhaps in motion, in the wide open spaces, she could outrun the turmoil in her own heart.

She put on her riding habit, her hands moving with a swift, jerky competence. When she returned to the sitting-room, Burke was standing by the window, staring out at the sun-drenched compound. He turned as she entered. His face was still grim, but the fury had subsided, replaced by a watchful stillness.

He looked her over, a long, searching look that took in her flushed cheeks and the slight, defiant tilt of her chin. He gave a short, hard nod, as if in approval of something he saw. "Tea is ready," he said. "Mary Ann has made scones."

They had tea in a near-complete silence, a silence that was charged with all the things that had been said, and all the things that had not. Sylvia found she could not eat. She drank her tea in quick, nervous sips, acutely aware of him sitting opposite her. He seemed to fill the room, his presence a powerful, undeniable force.

When they were done, he rose. "I'll have the horses brought round."

She merely nodded, and watched him stride out of the room, his back straight and unyielding. She felt a tremor of something that was almost fear, but it was mingled with a strange, exhilarating thrill. She had crossed a line. She had made a choice, and now she must follow where it led.

The horses were waiting when she came out, their coats gleaming in the late afternoon sun. Burke helped her to mount, his hand firm and impersonal under her elbow. He swung himself into his own saddle, and without a word, they set off at a canter, leaving the small cluster of farm buildings behind them.

The air was warm and smelled of dust and dry grass. The Veldt stretched out before them, a vast, rolling expanse of tawny gold under a limitless blue sky. For a long time they rode in silence, the rhythmic drumming of the horses' hooves the only sound. Sylvia felt some of the tension begin to drain out of her. Out here, with the wind in her face and the powerful animal moving beneath her, the small, stifling world of the sickroom seemed to fall away.

It was Burke who finally broke the silence. "Feeling better?" he asked, his voice less gruff than before.

She glanced at him. His profile was stark against the bright sky, his eyes fixed on the horizon. "Yes," she admitted. "It is good to be out."

"I told you it would be," he said. They rode on for another few moments before he spoke again. "He will try to keep you there, you know. By any means he can."

She did not need to ask who 'he' was. "Guy is not like that," she said, but her protest lacked conviction, even to her own ears. Guy's mocking words were still echoing in her mind.

Burke gave a short, humourless laugh. "Isn't he? He is weak, Sylvia. And weak men are the most selfish of all. They feed on the strength of others because they have none of their own. He has been feeding on yours for months."

They had slowed the horses to a walk now, as the ground became more uneven. A deep, aching sadness welled up in Sylvia. "He has suffered so much."

"We have all suffered," Burke said flatly. "But some of us get on with it. Others prefer to make a career of their suffering, and expect the rest of the world to pay them a salary for it." He turned in his saddle to look at her, his grey eyes piercing. "I saw his kind in the war. Men who found a comfortable wound and clung to it for all they were worth, while better men went back to the line and died."

The brutality of the comparison shocked her. "Burke! You cannot compare Guy to that!"

"Can't I?" he said. "He is my cousin. I have known him all his life. I know exactly what he is capable of."

A thought that had been circling in her mind all day suddenly came to the front. "Then why did he change?" she asked, her voice low. "Yesterday he was… as you say. But today he is different. The devil has gone out of him, just as you said. What happened last night, Burke? What did you do?"

He looked away, his jaw tight. "We had a talk."

"It was more than a talk," she insisted, leaning towards him. "His door was locked this morning. He said he had lost the key. But he was afraid, Burke. I could see it. When he spoke of your wrath… it was real. What did you say to him?"

He was silent for so long that she thought he would not answer. The sun was beginning to sink towards the horizon, painting the western sky in brilliant streaks of orange and crimson. The world seemed to be holding its breath.

Finally, he spoke, his voice quiet and devoid of any emotion. "I told him that if he was not on his feet and behaving like a man by the time I returned today, I would put him in a cart and send him down to the coast. I told him his time here was finished. He could either pull himself together and earn his keep, or he could leave you in peace."

It was an ultimatum, just as she had suspected. Harsh, but perhaps necessary. Yet it did not seem enough to account for the profound change in Guy. There was something more. She could feel it.

"And is that all?" she pressed.

He reined in his horse and turned to face her fully. The fading light cast his face in shadow, making it impossible to read his expression. "No," he said slowly. "That is not all. I told him that I had come back for you. And that I would not allow him, or any man, to stand in my way."

The words struck her with the force of a physical blow. She stared at him, speechless. This was the 'something' he had to say. Not a question, not a plea, but a declaration of intent, as stark and immovable as the land around them.

"I have been patient, Sylvia," he went on, his voice dropping to that low, intense pitch that vibrated through her. "I watched you with Kieff. I have watched you with Guy. I have watched you give your strength, your devotion, your whole heart to caring for others. And I have waited. But my patience is at an end."

He urged his horse closer, until their knees were almost touching. He reached out and took the reins from her unresisting fingers, his touch firm and confident. Their horses stood placidly, side by side, in the vast, silent twilight.

"You asked me if my wishes counted with you," he said. "Now I am telling you what I wish. I wish for you to stop looking at Guy's needs, and to start thinking of your own. Of ours. Look at me, Sylvia."

It was the same command he had given in the sitting-room. And just as she had then, she felt an overwhelming urge to look away, to hide from the intensity of his gaze. But she was not in the stifling confines of the house now. Here, under the immense, open sky, there was nowhere to run. Slowly, she lifted her head and met his eyes.

In their grey depths she saw not the anger and frustration of earlier, but a deep, steady fire. There was hunger there, yes, and a fierce possessiveness that was both frightening and thrilling. But there was also a profound vulnerability, a silent question that all his commanding words could not conceal. He was not asking for her service or her devotion. He was asking for her.

"Guy comes first," he had said, stating it as a fact. And she had been unable to deny it. But was it true now? As she looked at this hard, difficult, imperious man, who had seen her at her most anxious and weary, who had bullied her and commanded her and yet had done it all, she now realised, for her sake, she felt the foundations of her world shift. The duty that had bound her to Guy for so long felt suddenly like a chain, and Burke was offering her the key.

"Burke," she began, her voice a mere thread of sound. "I… I don't know what to say."

"You don't have to say anything," he said, his voice softening. "Not yet. Just ride with me. Let the rest of the world fall away for a while. Let it be just you and I."

He handed her reins back to her, and with a slight motion, set his horse in motion once more. She fell in beside him, her mind a whirl. They rode towards the distant, welcoming lights of the Merstons' farm, two silent figures moving through the gathering darkness. But it was a different silence now. The tension had not gone, but it had changed. It was no longer the prelude to a storm, but the deep, expectant hush that comes after the storm has broken, when the air is clean and the world is waiting to be made anew. Sylvia did not know what lay at the end of the ride, or what the morning would bring. But for the first time in a very long time, she felt not dread, but a trembling, uncertain hope.
