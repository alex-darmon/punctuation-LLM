"He's been out there for an hour, just staring at that old birdhouse," Clara observed, lowering her binoculars. "He hasn't moved a muscle."

"Perhaps he just enjoys birdwatching," Betty suggested, not looking up from her book. "Lots of people do."

"Not like that! Not our new caretaker, Mr. Finch. He looks like a hawk watching a mouse. I'll bet a dollar he's planning something!"

"Planning what? To clean it out for the sparrows?" Betty murmured, turning a page.

"No! I think he's using it as a dead drop," Clara declared, her voice filled with sudden conviction. "He's waiting for his contact to leave a secret message!"

"Oh, Clara, not again," Betty sighed, finally closing her book. "Last week you were certain the chef was poisoning the soup because he used too much salt."

"That was different. This is real! He's been acting suspiciously ever since he arrived. He never talks to anyone, and he's always prowling around the grounds at night."

"He's the caretaker, Clara. Prowling around the grounds is his job."

"Quiet!" Clara hissed. "He's moving!"

The girls watched as Mr. Finch, a tall, gaunt man with a perpetually stern expression, strode purposefully toward the old oak tree. He reached into the birdhouse, fumbled for a moment, and withdrew his hand. He glanced around, his sharp eyes scanning the lodge porch where the girls sat, partially hidden by a trellis of climbing roses. He then turned and walked briskly toward the woods.

"He took something out!" Clara whispered excitedly. "I saw it! Let's go!"

"We can't just go rummaging in his things," Betty protested, but she was already on her feet, her curiosity piqued despite herself.

They waited until Mr. Finch disappeared into the treeline before scurrying across the lawn. Clara, being taller, stood on her toes and peered into the small round opening of the birdhouse.

"Anything?" Betty asked anxiously.

"It's empty. Just some old twigs and feathers." Clara sounded thoroughly disappointed. "He must have taken the message with him."

"Or maybe there was never any message, and he was just clearing out a cobweb," Betty said gently.

"Then why did he look so guilty when he saw us?" Clara countered. "And look at this!" She pointed to the ground at the base of the tree. A small, shiny object lay glinting in the grass. It was a cufflink, shaped like a tiny, intricate silver key.

"He must have dropped it," Betty said.

"Exactly! We'll return it to him. That will give us an excuse to get a look at him up close!"

They found Mr. Finch in the boathouse, mending a fishing net with quick, nimble fingers.

"Excuse me, Mr. Finch?" Clara began, holding out the cufflink. "We found this by the old oak tree. We think it might be yours."

Mr. Finch glanced up, his pale blue eyes fixing them with an unnerving stare. He looked at the cufflink in Clara's hand, and his face seemed to tighten. For a moment, he said nothing.

"It is not mine," he said finally, his voice flat and cold. He turned his attention back to the net. "Now if you'll excuse me, I have work to do."

The girls retreated in silence. Once they were out of earshot, Betty spoke. "Well, that was strange. He seemed almost frightened."

"Frightened? He was furious!" Clara insisted. "And he was lying! I saw the sleeve of his shirt when he reached for his twine. He was missing a cufflink!"

"But why would he lie about something so small?"

"Because it's not just a cufflink!" Clara's eyes were wide with realization. "It's a key! A silver key! What if it opens a safe, or a secret box where he keeps all the things he's stolen?"

"Stolen? Clara, now you're just making things up."

"Am I?" Clara shot back. "Did you forget about the missing statue from the main hall? The little marble one of the dancing faun? It disappeared two days after Mr. Finch started working here!"

"The manager said it was probably just sent out for repairs," Betty reasoned.

"Or so Mr. Finch wants him to think!" Clara declared triumphantly. "The key is the proof! He's not a caretaker, he's a cat burglar!"

Betty stared at her friend, her mouth slightly agape. "A cat burglar? Clara, really! Isn't that a bit dramatic?"

"Is it?" Clara's voice was low and thrilling. "Think about it, Betty! He's new. He's secretive. A valuable object disappears right after he arrives. And now we find this! It's not just a cufflink, it's a key!" She held the small silver object up so it caught the afternoon sun.

"It's a cufflink shaped like a key," Betty corrected gently. "Lots of jewelry is made in fun shapes. It doesn't mean it actually opens a lock."

"Then why did he lie about it?" Clara pressed, her logic unassailable. "Why would a grown man get so flustered over a piece of jewelry unless it was terribly important? He was afraid, Betty. Afraid we had found his secret!"

Betty had to admit that Mr. Finch's reaction had been peculiar. His cold denial had been more unnerving than an angry outburst would have been. "All right, let's say you're right. What does it open?"

"His room, of course! Or something in his room," Clara declared. "He must have a box or a little chest where he keeps his ill gotten gains!"

"We can't just break into his room!" Betty gasped, horrified at the thought. "We'll be thrown out of the lodge if we're caught!"

"We won't get caught," Clara said with an air of confidence she did not entirely feel. "Mr. Finch is down at the boathouse. He'll be there for at least another hour, mending those nets. His room is on the ground floor of the staff wing, number four. I saw him go in yesterday."

"How do you know these things?" Betty asked in exasperation.

"I keep my eyes open," Clara replied simply. "Now, are you with me or not? Think of the little marble faun, Betty! It deserves to be rescued."

The appeal to justice, combined with her own undeniable curiosity, was too much for Betty to resist. With a sigh of resignation that was half excitement, she nodded. "Oh, all right. But if we hear anyone coming, we leave immediately. Agreed?"

"Agreed!" Clara grinned, pocketing the silver key. "Operation Faun Rescue is a go!"

They crept along the side of the main lodge, using the thick hydrangea bushes as cover. The staff wing was a small, clapboard extension added to the back of the building. A narrow hallway was lined with doors, each with a number painted on it. Number four was at the very end.

"Now what?" Betty whispered, as they stood before the plain wooden door. "The main door is locked, I'm sure."

"The window," Clara whispered back, pointing.

The window to room four was slightly ajar to let in the breeze. It was, however, covered by a screen. Clara examined it closely.

"It's only held in by a few little clips," she observed. "If I can get my fingernails under one, maybe I can pop it out."

With painstaking slowness, Clara worked at the screen. Betty stood guard, her heart thumping against her ribs like a trapped bird. Every rustle of leaves, every distant laugh from the direction of the lake, sent a fresh jolt of panic through her. Finally, with a soft pop, the screen came loose in Clara's hands. She carefully set it down in the flowerbed below.

"You first," she urged Betty. "You're smaller."

Holding her breath, Betty hoisted herself up onto the sill and scrambled inside, landing with a soft thud on the floor. Clara followed a moment later, pulling the window almost shut behind them.

The room was disappointingly ordinary. It was small and immaculately tidy. A narrow bed was made with military precision. A simple wooden dresser stood against one wall, and a small writing desk was tucked into a corner. There were no pictures on the walls, no books on the desk, no personal belongings of any kind.

"It's like no one even lives here," Betty whispered, a little shiver running down her spine.

"Exactly!" Clara whispered back triumphantly. "He's covering his tracks. A man with nothing to hide would have a photograph or a letter, wouldn't he?"

They began to search. Betty carefully opened the dresser drawers. They contained only a few neatly folded shirts, some socks, and underthings. Nothing else. Meanwhile, Clara was running her hands over the simple furniture, tapping the walls, and even peering under the thin rug on the floor.

"There's nothing here," Betty said, her voice full of disappointment. "Maybe you were wrong after all."

"Wait," Clara breathed. She was crouched by the writing desk. Tucked away almost out of sight was a small, dark wooden box. It was plain and unadorned, but it had a small brass lock on the front. "Look at this!"

Betty hurried over. Her eyes widened as she saw the lock. It was tiny, almost like a lock on a diary. It was just the right size for a very small, very intricate key.

With trembling fingers, Clara retrieved the silver cufflink from her pocket. She hesitated for a second, then inserted the key-shaped end into the lock. It slid in perfectly. She turned it. A soft click echoed in the silent room.

Lifting the lid, the girls peered inside. They did not find the marble faun, nor a glittering pile of jewels. The box contained only a single, folded sheet of paper.

"Is that all?" Betty murmured, unable to hide her letdown.

Clara carefully lifted the paper out and unfolded it. It was not a letter, but a drawing. It was a roughly sketched floor plan of the main hall of the Black Bear Lodge. In the center of the drawing was a square labeled "Pedestal," and it was circled several times in thick pencil. An arrow pointed from the square to a series of numbers and symbols written in the margin: "14-L-3-R-9."

"What is it?" Betty asked, leaning closer. "A map?"

"It's a combination," Clara whispered, her eyes shining with renewed fervor. "It has to be! Left, Right. See? 14 Left, 3 Right, 9 whatever the last one is. He didn't just steal the statue, Betty. He used it for something! Or maybe, he's planning to put something back!"

"Put what back?"

Before Clara could answer, they heard the distinct sound of footsteps crunching on the gravel path just outside the wing. Heavy footsteps.

"Someone's coming!" Betty hissed, her face pale.

For a terrifying second, they froze. Then Clara sprang into action. She quickly folded the paper, shoved it back in the box, clicked the lid shut, and pushed it back into its hiding place.

"The window!" she mouthed silently.

They scrambled toward it, but the footsteps were closer now, turning onto the wooden porch of the staff wing. There was no time to escape.

"Under the bed!" Clara commanded in a frantic whisper.

They dove for the floor and wriggled under the bed just as the doorknob began to turn. They lay side by side on the dusty floorboards, hardly daring to breathe. The door opened and Mr. Finch walked in.

From their hiding place, they could see his sensible brown shoes and the cuffs of his trousers. He walked to the center of the room and stopped. The silence stretched on. Betty was sure he could hear her heart hammering. She squeezed her eyes shut, wishing she could simply disappear.

Then they heard him moving again. He walked over to the writing desk. There was a faint scraping sound as he pulled the wooden box out from its hiding spot. The girls heard the jingle of keys from his pocket, different from the soft click of their silver key. A lock turned. He had another key!

He must have opened the box and found the paper still there, for after a moment he pushed the box back into place. Then he began to pace. Back and forth, back and forth, his shoes making soft, rhythmic thuds on the floor. It was the pacing of a worried man.

After what felt like an eternity, he stopped. He walked to the door, opened it, and left, closing it firmly behind him.

The girls waited, not moving a muscle, until his footsteps had completely faded away.

"He's gone," Betty finally breathed, her voice shaky.

They crawled out from under the bed, covered in a fine layer of dust.

"That was too close," Betty said, brushing off her skirt with trembling hands. "Clara, we have to stop this. It's too dangerous."

"Stop?" Clara's face was flushed with excitement. "Betty, don't you see? We're closer than ever! He checked on the box! That proves the paper is important. And he has another key, which means he never expected anyone to be able to open it without his."

"But what does the paper mean? '14-L-3-R-9'?"

"It's a safe combination," Clara insisted. "There must be a safe hidden in the main hall, somewhere near where the statue used to be!"

"But there isn't one," Betty said. "I've been in that hall a hundred times. There's just the big stone fireplace, the front desk, and a lot of chairs."

"It must be hidden!" Clara reasoned. "Behind a painting, or under a floorboard. The statue was the clue!"

They slipped out of the room as they had come in, carefully replacing the screen. Their hearts were still racing from the close call, but their minds were racing even faster.

They decided to observe the main hall from the mezzanine, which overlooked the grand, rustic room below. They found a comfortable spot behind a large potted fern and settled in to watch. For a long while, nothing happened. Guests came and went, the clerk at the front desk answered the telephone, and a busboy cleared away some teacups.

Then, Mr. Finch entered the hall. He wasn't carrying his mending or any tools. He simply stood near the enormous stone fireplace, his back to them, and stared at the empty pedestal where the marble faun had once danced. He lingered there for several minutes, looking from the pedestal to the fireplace mantel and back again. He seemed to be measuring something with his eyes.

Just then, a sleek black automobile pulled up to the entrance of the lodge. A man in a dark, expensive-looking suit and a fedora hat got out. He was not a typical guest of the rustic lodge; he looked more like a businessman from the city. He walked briskly into the hall, his sharp eyes scanning the room. He spotted Mr. Finch by the fireplace and walked directly toward him.

The two men did not shake hands. They spoke in low, urgent tones that the girls could not overhear. The man in the suit seemed to be asking questions, and Mr. Finch answered with short, clipped responses, occasionally shaking his head. The man seemed displeased. He gestured impatiently toward the empty pedestal, then toward the woods outside. Mr. Finch gave a slight, almost imperceptible nod. The man in the suit gave him one last, hard look, then turned on his heel and strode out of the lodge as quickly as he had arrived. The black car sped away down the winding drive.

Mr. Finch remained by the fireplace for another moment, his shoulders slumped. He ran a hand over his face, and from their vantage point, the girls could see that his expression was one of deep anxiety. He then turned and left the hall, heading back toward the staff wing.

"Who was that man?" Betty whispered, her earlier skepticism completely gone.

"I don't know," Clara replied, her mind whirling. "But he wasn't happy. And he was interested in the pedestal, too. He's part of it, I know he is!"

"What did his gesture toward the woods mean?"

"Maybe that's where the statue is hidden!" Clara gasped. "Of course! It would be too risky to keep it in the lodge. He's hidden it somewhere in the forest, and now this other man wants it!"

"But why? What's so important about a little marble statue?" Betty wondered. "It was pretty, but it couldn't have been worth that much money."

"It's not about the money," Clara said, her eyes alight with a new, even grander theory. She leaned in close to Betty, her voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. "The cufflink was a key. The statue was a marker. The paper is a combination. This isn't just about a cat burglar stealing art, Betty."

"Then what is it about?" Betty asked, completely under the spell of her friend's deductions.

"Mr. Finch isn't a thief," Clara declared, her voice filled with the thrill of discovery. "He's a treasure hunter! And that statue is the key to a fortune hidden somewhere right here in the Black Bear Lodge!"

Betty stared at her friend, her teacup forgotten on the table. "A treasure, Clara? Are you serious?"

"Of course I'm serious! It's the only thing that makes sense!" Clara insisted, her voice still a hushed but excited whisper. "Think about it, Betty. Why would a cat burglar come back? Why would Mr. Finch be so nervous? And that man in the suit! He wasn't a policeman. He was giving orders!"

"He did look awfully cross," Betty admitted, glancing toward the empty entrance hall. "But a treasure? Like pirates' gold?"

"Maybe not pirates' gold," Clara corrected, her brow furrowed in thought. "But something valuable. The founder of this lodge, old Silas Blackwood, was an eccentric millionaire. I read about him in the brochure in our room. He was a prospector before he built this place. What if he hid his fortune here?"

"And Mr. Finch is his descendant?" Betty guessed, her imagination beginning to catch up with Clara's.

"Or he just found a clue!" Clara leaned forward, tapping the table for emphasis. "The cufflink was the first clue. He found it, and it led him to the statue. But the statue isn't the treasure itself. It's a key, a marker!"

"A marker for what?"

"For whatever the paper with the combination opens!" Clara stood up, her mind made up. "We have to find out. First, we need to examine that fireplace. Mr. Finch was looking at it for a reason."

"But what if he comes back?" Betty whispered, her eyes wide.

"Then we'll pretend we're just admiring the woodwork," Clara said with a confident toss of her head. "Come on. We can't let him get too far ahead of us."

With a determined air, Clara led the way back into the main hall. Betty followed a step behind, casting nervous glances over her shoulder. The great hall was just as they had left it, grand and silent. The afternoon sun streamed through the tall windows, illuminating dust motes dancing in the air. The only sound was the soft crackle of the fire.

They approached the enormous stone fireplace. The empty pedestal to its left seemed like a silent accusation. "He kept looking back and forth," Clara murmured, mimicking the action herself. "From the pedestal to the mantel. What did he see?"

She ran her fingers along the rough wooden mantelpiece. It was thick with age and dark with stain. A few tarnished silver candlesticks and a porcelain vase stood upon it. Nothing seemed out of the ordinary. "There's nothing here," Betty said with a hint of disappointment.

"There has to be," Clara insisted. She got down on her hands and knees and peered at the pedestal's base. Then she crawled over to the fireplace hearth, her eyes scanning the stones. "Look, Betty! Look at the stones!"

Betty knelt beside her. The fireplace was made of large, irregular river stones, fitted together with thick mortar. "What about them?"

"This one," Clara said, tracing the outline of a particular stone with her finger. It was darker than the others and seemed to have a faint, almost invisible carving on it. "It looks like a bear. A black bear, just like the name of the lodge."

Betty squinted. "You're right! It is a bear. But it's so faint, you can barely see it."

"Exactly! It's meant to be overlooked." Clara pressed on the stone. Nothing happened. She tried to wiggle it, to push it from side to side. It was firm. "It must be a lock of some kind. This must be what the paper with the combination is for!"

"But we don't have a paper," Betty pointed out logically.

"Mr. Finch must have it! Or maybe it's hidden somewhere else." Clara stood up and brushed the dust from her knees. Her gaze fell on the empty pedestal again. "The faun was dancing. He was pointing. Where was he pointing, Betty?"

Betty thought for a moment. "He was sort of leaping, with one arm stretched out. I think it was pointed toward the big window that looks out on the woods."

Clara's eyes lit up. "The woods! Of course! The man in the suit gestured to the woods! That's where Mr. Finch hid the statue!"

"You think he took it outside?"

"He had to!" Clara reasoned. "He couldn't risk leaving it here where that man could find it. He took the statue, but he can't get to the treasure without it. The statue must show him where the next clue is, or where the combination is hidden!"

"So what do we do now?" Betty asked, swept up in the chase. "We can't just wander around in the woods. They're huge."

"We won't wander," Clara declared. "We'll follow Mr. Finch. He has to go back for it, especially now that the man in the suit is putting pressure on him. We'll wait and watch, and when he makes his move, we'll be right behind him."

The girls retreated to their observation post in the tearoom. They ordered another pot of tea they did not want and a plate of cookies they barely touched. They sat by the window, their gazes fixed on the staff wing entrance. An hour passed. Then another. Betty was beginning to fidget.

"Maybe he's not coming out," she said with a sigh. "Maybe he went out another way."

"He wouldn't," Clara said with certainty. "This is the most direct route to the woods behind the lodge. He'll come. We just have to be patient."

As if on cue, the door to the staff wing opened. Mr. Finch emerged, but he was not dressed for a walk in the woods. He wore his usual work clothes and carried a toolbox. He headed not for the woods, but for one of the guest cabins on the far side of the property.

"Oh, fiddlesticks," Clara muttered in frustration. "He's just going to fix a leaky faucet or something."

"Maybe your theory is all wrong, Clara," Betty said gently. "Maybe he really is just a handyman."

"No!" Clara shook her head stubbornly. "His face was too worried. And the man in the fedora was too real. We're missing something." She watched as Mr. Finch disappeared into the cabin. "Wait a minute. What if that's just a trick?"

"A trick?"

"To make us think he's busy! He knows people watch him. He's trying to throw us off the scent." Clara’s mind was racing. "He won't go into the woods until he thinks no one is looking. Probably after dark."

"After dark?" Betty squeaked. "I'm not going into the woods after dark!"

"We have to, Betty. It's our only chance."

"There could be bears in there! Or that man in the suit!"

"We'll be careful," Clara promised, though she felt a small shiver of fear herself. "We'll take flashlights. And we'll stick to the main path. The one that starts behind the lodge. It leads toward the old saw mill, remember? That seems like a good place to hide something."

Betty did not look convinced, but she did not argue. She knew that once Clara had a theory, she would see it through to the end.

The rest of the afternoon dragged by. The girls had dinner in the main dining hall, where they saw Mr. Finch again. He was serving water to the tables, his face a mask of polite indifference. There was no sign of the sharp-suited man. As dusk began to settle over the mountains, painting the sky in shades of orange and purple, the girls slipped back to their room.

Clara pulled two flashlights from her suitcase. "Here," she said, handing one to Betty. "Are you ready?"

Betty took a deep breath. "As I'll ever be."

They waited until the lodge had grown quiet. Sounds of dinner service faded, replaced by the chirping of crickets and the distant hoot of an owl. Tiptoeing down the hallway and out a side door, they made their way around the back of the massive log building. The air was cool and smelled of pine needles and damp earth. A faint path, barely visible in the twilight, led away from the manicured lawns and into the dark maw of the forest.

"This is it," Clara whispered, clicking on her flashlight. The beam cut a wobbly circle in the darkness ahead. "Stick close to me."

The woods were even more intimidating at night. Tall pines and firs formed a dense canopy overhead, blocking out the last vestiges of daylight. Every rustle of leaves sounded like a footstep. Every snap of a twig made them jump.

"Are you sure about this, Clara?" Betty whispered, her own flashlight beam dancing nervously among the tree trunks.

"We can't turn back now," Clara replied, trying to sound braver than she felt. "He came this way. I'm sure of it."

They followed the path for what felt like miles. It twisted and turned, climbing steadily uphill. The lodge was soon lost to sight behind them. They were alone in the vast, dark wilderness. After about twenty minutes of walking, the path opened into a small clearing. In the center stood the crumbling remains of what must have been the old saw mill. A single stone wall and a few rotting timbers were all that was left.

"This must be the place," Clara breathed, shining her light around the clearing. Her beam fell upon a patch of disturbed earth near the base of the stone wall. There were fresh footprints in the soft dirt.

"Someone's been digging here!" Betty exclaimed.

Clara rushed forward. She knelt and ran her fingers through the loose soil. "He's already been here! He must have moved it!" She swept her flashlight beam across the ground, searching for any other sign. Her light caught on something small and metallic half-hidden under a fern.

"What is it?" Betty asked, coming closer.

Clara picked it up. It was a small, flat key, the kind used for a luggage lock or a small diary. It was attached to a broken chain. "It's a key," she said, her voice filled with excitement. "He must have dropped it when he was moving the statue!"

"So he did have the statue here!"

"Yes! And now he's moved it somewhere else." Clara stared at the key in her palm. "But why leave this behind? Unless he didn't know he dropped it."

Suddenly, a sharp snapping sound came from the woods just beyond the clearing. The girls froze, their hearts pounding. Clara quickly clicked off her flashlight. Betty did the same. They were plunged into absolute darkness.

"Who's there?" a low, gravelly voice demanded from the trees.

The girls held their breath, too terrified to move or make a sound. They crouched down behind the crumbling stone wall, hoping they could not be seen. Footsteps crunched through the undergrowth, coming closer. A powerful flashlight beam sliced through the night, sweeping across the clearing. It passed just over their heads.

"I know someone is out here," the voice said. It was closer now. "I saw your lights."

Clara recognized the voice. It was Mr. Finch.

The flashlight beam landed on the patch of disturbed earth. The footsteps stopped. There was a long, heavy silence, followed by a frustrated sigh. "It's gone," he muttered to himself. "He took it. After all this."

Clara and Betty exchanged a wide-eyed look in the darkness. He thought someone else had taken the statue. He didn't know it was them who had been there.

Mr. Finch kicked at the dirt. Then, his light began to move again, scanning the perimeter of the clearing. The girls pressed themselves harder against the cold stones of the wall. The beam swept toward them. In another moment, they would be discovered.

Just as the light was about to fall on their hiding place, Betty let out a tiny, involuntary squeak of fear.

The light froze, pointing directly at the wall. "Who is it?" Mr. Finch's voice was sharp now, edged with alarm. "Show yourselves!"

Slowly, knowing there was no other choice, Clara stood up. She fumbled with her flashlight and switched it on, pointing the beam at her own face. "It's just us, Mr. Finch," she said, her voice trembling slightly.

Betty rose beside her, looking like a frightened rabbit caught in the glare.

Mr. Finch lowered his flashlight, his face a mixture of shock, anger, and utter bewilderment. "You girls? What in creation are you doing out here in the middle of the night?"

"We were... we were just taking a walk," Clara stammered, thinking fast.

"A walk? In the dark? Following me?" he scoffed. His eyes narrowed. "You were the ones digging here." It was not a question.

"No, we weren't!" Betty blurted out. "It was like that when we got here!"

Mr. Finch stared from the disturbed earth to the two girls, his expression unreadable. He looked exhausted and defeated. He ran a hand through his graying hair.

"You shouldn't be involved in this," he said, his voice dropping its harsh edge and becoming weary. "It's dangerous. You need to go back to the lodge and forget everything you've seen."

"We can't," Clara said boldly. "Not until we know what's going on. We know about the statue, Mr. Finch. And the man in the suit."

Mr. Finch flinched at the mention of the man. He looked around nervously, as if expecting the man to step out from behind a tree. "You know nothing," he said, but there was no conviction in his voice.

"We know you're looking for the Blackwood treasure," Clara pressed on. "The statue is the key, isn't it?"

The handyman let out a long, slow sigh that seemed to carry all the weight of the forest. He sat down heavily on a fallen log and buried his face in his hands. "Treasure," he mumbled. "I suppose that's one word for it."

He looked up at them, his eyes filled with a desperate sadness. "It's not about gold or jewels, girls. Not anymore. It's about keeping a promise."

"A promise to who?" Betty asked softly.

"To my grandfather," Mr. Finch said. "Silas Blackwood was my great uncle. My grandfather was his younger brother, the one everyone forgot about. Silas didn't trust banks. He didn't trust anyone. When he died, he left behind a puzzle. A trail of clues for my grandfather to follow to his inheritance. But the clues were difficult. My grandfather spent his life searching. He found the first few, but he could never find the last piece. The marble faun."

"So you're trying to finish what he started!" Clara gasped.

"I have to," Mr. Finch said. "The man you saw, the one in the suit, his name is Slade. His grandfather was Silas Blackwood's business partner, a man Silas believed cheated him. Slade's family believes they are the rightful heirs to the fortune. They've been hunting for it for generations, too."

"But you found the statue first!" Betty said.

"I thought I had," Mr. Finch said grimly. "I found a clue in Silas's old journals that led me to it. It was hidden right in plain sight, in the lodge manager's collection. I... borrowed it. I brought it here to examine it. There's supposed to be a map etched onto the base. A map that leads to a lockbox hidden in this forest."

"And the combination paper?" Clara asked, holding her breath.

"The paper with the combination for the stone in the fireplace is inside the lockbox," he confirmed. "And inside the fireplace safe... is the deed to this entire property. That's the real treasure. Silas wanted my family to have the lodge. Not Slade's."

He looked at the disturbed patch of ground. "I brought the statue here last night. I was trying to decipher the map when I heard a noise. I got spooked and buried it, planning to come back. But when I returned just now... it's gone. Slade must have followed me. He has it now."

"No, he doesn't!" Clara declared.

Mr. Finch looked up at her, confused. "What do you mean?"

Clara held out her hand. In the pale glow of their combined flashlights, the small, flat key gleamed on her palm. "You dropped this. But we found something else, too."

"What?" Mr. Finch asked, leaning forward.

"We don't have the statue," Clara explained carefully. "But we might know someone who does. Someone who likes to collect things."

Betty's eyes widened in understanding. "Mrs. Gable! The lady in cabin three!"

"What about her?" Mr. Finch asked.

"She collects salt and pepper shakers," Betty said. "And little glass animals. And... and little statues. We saw her this morning, coming back from a very early walk in the woods. She was carrying a covered basket!"

Clara nodded excitedly. "We thought it was strange then! She wasn't on the path, she was coming from deeper in the woods, from this direction! She must have stumbled upon the spot where you buried it. She's a collector, she wouldn't have been able to resist taking it!"

A flicker of hope ignited in Mr. Finch's tired eyes. "Cabin three? Are you certain?"

"Positive," Clara said. "She probably thinks it's just a lovely antique. She has no idea what it really is."

"We have to get it back," Mr. Finch said, standing up. "Slade is staying at the lodge under a false name. If he finds out an old lady has the statue, he won't be gentle about getting it from her."

"What's the plan?" Clara asked, her mind already spinning with possibilities.

"We need to get into her cabin and get the statue back before Slade realizes it's missing from the woods," Mr. Finch said, his voice now firm and resolute. "And I think I know just how to do it."

Mr. Finch’s plan was surprisingly simple, which in itself made Betty nervous. He proposed to go to the main lodge and speak with the manager, a man he had known for years.
“I’ll tell Henry that Mrs. Gable has won a complimentary dinner at the Pine Ridge Restaurant downtown,” he explained, his voice low and urgent. “It’s the fanciest place in town. Henry can call her cabin and tell her a car is waiting to take her. No one turns down a free meal at Pine Ridge.”
“But will the manager agree to lie for you?” Betty asked, twisting a button on her jacket.
“Henry owes me a favor or two,” Mr. Finch said with a slight, mysterious smile. “And I’ll make sure the dinner is paid for. It’s a small price. The important thing is it will get her out of the cabin for at least an hour, probably more.”
“And that’s where we come in!” Clara said, her eyes alight with excitement. “We’ll use the key I found to get inside and find the statue!” She held up the small, flat piece of metal.
Mr. Finch took the key and examined it under his flashlight beam. “This is a simple back door key for the older cabins. Silas had them all made from the same design. It should work on cabin three.” He handed it back to Clara. “You must be careful. She’s a collector. The statue could be anywhere amongst her things.”
“What if Slade is watching the cabin?” Betty worried aloud. “He might see us go in.”
“That’s a risk we have to take,” Mr. Finch said gravely. “But Slade thinks the statue is still buried here in the woods. He’s more likely to be searching this area than watching an old woman’s cabin. We have the element of surprise. Now, I will go to the lodge. You two circle around and find a place to watch cabin three. Do not, under any circumstances, approach the cabin until you see her leave in the car. Is that clear?”
“Crystal clear,” Clara answered for both of them.
“Be quick and be quiet,” Mr. Finch instructed, giving them one last serious look before he turned and disappeared into the darkened woods, heading toward the distant lights of the main lodge.
Clara and Betty exchanged a look. “Well,” Clara whispered, a grin spreading across her face. “This is certainly more exciting than shuffleboard!”
“I think I’d prefer shuffleboard,” Betty muttered, but she dutifully followed Clara as they made their way through the trees, circling toward the row of guest cabins.

They found a spot behind a large, dense rhododendron bush that gave them a perfect, unobstructed view of cabin three. The cabin was small and rustic, with a single light glowing warmly in the front window. A thin curl of smoke rose from its stone chimney. For what felt like an eternity, nothing happened. The girls crouched in the damp leaves, shivering more from anticipation than from the cold night air.
“Do you think his plan is working?” Betty whispered, her teeth chattering slightly.
“It has to,” Clara insisted. “Mr. Finch sounded very confident.”
As if on cue, the light in the main room of the cabin was joined by another, as if a lamp had been turned on near the telephone. A few minutes later, the cabin door swung open.
Mrs. Gable stood silhouetted in the doorway. She had changed from her walking clothes into a rather formal-looking dress with a floral pattern. She patted her hair, pulled the door shut behind her, and locked it with a key.
“She’s all dressed up!” Betty breathed. “The dinner plan worked!”
Just then, a long, dark car, the kind the lodge used for special guests, pulled up in front of the cabin. A uniformed driver stepped out and opened the rear door for Mrs. Gable. She said something to him, smiled, and then climbed inside. The car door shut with a solid thud, and the vehicle drove off, its red taillights vanishing down the winding lodge road.
“That’s our cue!” Clara said, grabbing Betty’s arm. “Come on!”
They scurried from behind the bush, their sneakers making soft sounds on the pine needle-strewn ground. They kept to the shadows, their hearts pounding in their chests. At the back of the cabin, they found a small, simple wooden door.
“This must be it,” Clara whispered, fumbling with the key. Her hands were shaking so much she had trouble fitting it into the lock.
“Hurry, Clara!” Betty urged, casting a nervous glance over her shoulder into the darkness. Every rustle of leaves sounded like approaching footsteps.
“I’m trying!”
With a final jiggle, the key turned. A quiet click echoed in the night. Clara slowly turned the knob and pushed the door inward. It opened into a small, dark kitchenette. They slipped inside, closing the door gently behind them.
The air in the cabin was stuffy and smelled of lavender and dust. Flipping a small switch by the door, Clara illuminated the room with the dim light of a small stove lamp.
Betty’s gasp was audible.
The cabin was not just cluttered; it was a museum of curiosities. Every available surface was covered. Shelves lining the walls groaned under the weight of countless salt and pepper shakers shaped like everything from kangaroos to lighthouses. A wide mantelpiece was crowded with tiny, gleaming glass animals. Little dolls with porcelain faces stared blankly from a rocking chair.
“Goodness,” Clara murmured, taking it all in. “Where do we even start?”
“She must have hundreds of things,” Betty said, her eyes wide. “The statue could be anywhere.”
“It has to be here,” Clara said, her voice filled with determination. “We’ll split up. You take the shelves on that side, I’ll check the mantelpiece and the tables. Look for something that doesn’t quite fit. A stone bird, remember?”
They set to work, moving as quickly and quietly as they could. It was a maddening task. Each object had to be lifted and examined, for the little stone bird could easily be lost among the menagerie of glass and porcelain. Clara carefully sifted through a collection of miniature ceramic shoes. Betty peered behind a row of porcelain cats with exaggerated grins.
“Anything?” Clara whispered after several minutes had passed.
“Nothing but salt shakers,” Betty whispered back in frustration. “Are you sure she would put it out on display? Maybe she hid it.”
“She’s a collector,” Clara reasoned. “She’d want to look at it. She probably has no idea it’s anything more than a charming little antique.”
Clara moved to a small end table next to an overstuffed armchair. It was covered with a lace doily and another assortment of figurines. There were glass swans, a brass mouse, a wooden squirrel, and then, tucked behind a plump ceramic pig, she saw it.
It was small, no bigger than her palm, and carved from a dark, smooth stone that seemed to absorb the dim light. It was a bird, its head cocked as if listening, its features worn smooth by time. It was heavier than it looked.
“Betty! I’ve found it!” Clara hissed, a triumphant smile lighting up her face.
She held it up for Betty to see. It was undeniably the statue Mr. Finch had described. As Betty rushed over to look, Clara turned the figure over in her hand. The base was flat and smooth, but as she tilted it in the light, she could just make out a series of faint, scratch-like lines.
“The map,” she breathed. “It’s here.”
“We have it!” Betty said, her relief palpable. “Let’s get out of here before she comes back!”
Clara carefully wrapped the statue in her handkerchief and tucked it into her pocket. “Right behind you.”
They turned to go back through the kitchenette, but froze. A key was rattling in the front door lock.
“She’s back!” Betty gasped, her face pale with terror.
The lock clicked, and the doorknob began to turn.
There was nowhere to run. The main room was small, and the kitchenette offered no other exit. With a shared look of panic, Clara grabbed Betty’s hand and pulled her toward the only possible hiding place: a dark space beneath a large, four-poster bed that was covered with a thick quilt that hung nearly to the floor.
They scrambled underneath, pulling the edge of the quilt down just as the front door creaked open. They lay flat on the dusty floorboards, hardly daring to breathe.
A pair of sensible, low-heeled shoes entered their limited field of vision, followed by the hem of the floral dress. Mrs. Gable had returned.
“Now where did I put that pesky thing,” they heard her mutter to herself.
Her footsteps moved across the room, toward the armchair where Clara had found the statue. There was a pause. The girls held their breath. Clara could feel her heart hammering against the floor.
“Oh, for heaven’s sake,” Mrs. Gable said, her voice sharp with annoyance. “I know I left my reading glasses right here. Honestly, if my head weren’t attached…”
Her footsteps moved away, toward a different part of the cabin. There was the sound of a drawer opening and closing.
Beneath the bed, Betty nudged Clara and pointed. Through a small gap in the quilt, they could see out the front window. A man was walking up the path to the cabin. He was tall and broad-shouldered, and even in the dim light they could see he had a hard, unpleasant face. It was Mr. Slade.
Clara’s blood ran cold. He was not supposed to be here. He was supposed to be in the woods. What could he possibly want with Mrs. Gable?
Slade walked directly to the front door, which Mrs. Gable had left slightly ajar. He didn't knock. He simply pushed it open and stepped inside.
“Good evening, Mrs. Gable,” he said. His voice was smooth, but with a menacing undercurrent that made the hairs on Clara’s arms stand up.
“Who… who are you?” Mrs. Gable asked, her voice trembling slightly. “How dare you just walk into my cabin!”
“My name is unimportant,” Slade said dismissively. “I believe you found something in the woods this morning. A little stone statue. I’ve come to collect it.”
“I don’t know what you’re talking about,” she stammered. “I found nothing of the sort.”
“Please, let’s not play games,” Slade said, his voice losing its thin veneer of politeness. “I know you have it. An old woman like you, a collector. You wouldn’t have been able to leave it behind. Now, you can either give it to me, or I can take this entire cabin apart until I find it. I assure you, I will not be as careful with your little treasures as you are.”
From under the bed, Clara and Betty watched in horror. This was a disaster. Mrs. Gable was in real danger.
“I… I don’t have it!” Mrs. Gable insisted, her voice rising in panic. “It’s gone! I don’t know where it is!”
“Gone?” Slade repeated skeptically. He took a threatening step toward her. “What do you mean, gone?”
“It was on the table, and now it isn’t! I don’t know what happened to it!”
Slade let out a short, harsh laugh. “A likely story. You’re a more clever old bird than you look. Hiding it from me, are you? Very well. We’ll do this the hard way.”
He took another step forward, and Mrs. Gable let out a small cry of fear.
Clara knew they had to do something. They couldn’t let him hurt her. An idea, reckless and desperate, flashed into her mind. In the kitchenette, they had passed a stack of metal pots and pans.
Without a second thought, she began to crawl silently toward the back of the bed, toward the opening to the kitchenette.
“What are you doing?” Betty mouthed in silent panic.
Clara put a finger to her lips and continued to crawl. Reaching the kitchenette, she peered around the corner. Slade had his back to her, looming over the terrified Mrs. Gable. On a low shelf near the back door was a large, heavy-looking cast iron skillet.
It was now or never.
Gathering all her courage, Clara darted into the kitchenette, grabbed the skillet, and, with a mighty heave, hurled it against a stack of tin plates on the counter.
The crash was deafening. It sounded like a car wreck inside the tiny cabin.
Slade whirled around, his face a mask of fury and surprise. “What was that?”
“I don’t know!” Mrs. Gable cried.
In that split second of distraction, Clara darted back to the rear door, wrenched it open, and shouted, “The back door, Betty! Run!”
Betty scrambled out from under the bed with a speed she didn’t know she possessed. She practically flew past Mrs. Gable and a stunned-looking Slade and out into the night. Clara was right behind her.
They didn’t stop running. They crashed through the underbrush, ignoring the branches that scratched at their faces and arms. Behind them, they heard Slade shout in anger, but they didn’t look back. They ran and ran until their lungs burned, until the lights of cabin three were long gone, until they finally stumbled into a small clearing and collapsed, gasping for breath.
After a minute, Clara pushed herself up. She reached into her pocket, her hand closing around the hard, smooth shape of the stone bird.
“We did it, Betty,” she panted, a wild, breathless laugh escaping her. “We did it! We got it!”
Betty could only nod, still trying to catch her breath. “That was… that was too close, Clara. He was going to hurt her.”
“I know. But she’s safe now. And Slade doesn’t have the statue. We do.” Clara pulled the statue out and held it tightly. “We need to find Mr. Finch. Right now.”

They found him pacing anxiously near the clearing where they had first met. When he saw them emerge from the trees, relief washed over his face.
“Girls! Are you all right? What happened? I saw a car come back, and then I heard a terrible crash!”
“We’re fine,” Clara said, holding out the stone bird. “And we have it.”
Mr. Finch took the statue from her, his hands trembling slightly as he held the object of his family’s long search. “I can’t believe it. You actually got it.”
“It wasn’t easy,” Betty said, her voice still shaky. “Slade showed up. He was threatening Mrs. Gable!”
Quickly, they recounted the entire terrifying episode in the cabin, from hiding under the bed to Clara’s noisy distraction with the skillet.
Mr. Finch listened, his expression growing darker and more grim with every word. “I put you all in terrible danger,” he said when they had finished. “I am so sorry. I underestimated Slade. He is more ruthless than I imagined.”
“It’s not your fault,” Clara insisted. “We knew it was risky. The important thing is, Mrs. Gable is safe, and he thinks the statue is lost.”
“For now,” Mr. Finch said. “He will soon realize he was tricked. He’ll be looking for us. We don’t have much time.”
He drew his powerful flashlight from his coat pocket and aimed its bright beam onto the base of the statue. The girls huddled close, their earlier fear replaced by a fresh surge of curiosity.
The faint lines Clara had seen were now clearly visible. They were not random scratches. They formed a distinct, if crude, map. There were lines indicating trails, a cluster of triangles that looked like pine trees, and a shape that resembled the river that wound through the forest.
“It’s a map of the woods near the lodge,” Mr. Finch murmured, tracing the lines with his finger.
“But what does it point to?” Betty asked.
Mr. Finch’s finger stopped on a small, peculiar symbol etched near the riverbank. It was a circle with two smaller circles inside it. Beside the symbol were two tiny, barely legible words.
He squinted, bringing the statue closer to his face. “Under the… Owl’s Gaze.”
“The Owl’s Gaze?” Clara repeated. “What does that mean?”
A slow smile of understanding spread across Mr. Finch’s face. “I know that spot. It’s not on any of the official park maps. It’s an old landmark, known only to a few locals. There’s a rock formation down by the river, a large granite outcropping that, if you look at it from just the right angle, looks like the head of an owl.”
“So the lockbox is hidden there!” Clara exclaimed.
“It must be,” he confirmed, his eyes gleaming with renewed hope. “Hidden somewhere under the gaze of the granite owl. Silas was always fond of riddles and poetic clues.”
He slipped the statue safely into his deep coat pocket. “Slade will be searching the woods frantically. Our only advantage is that we know where to look, and he does not. We must go at once.”
“Tonight?” Betty asked, her voice a squeak of apprehension.
“It has to be tonight,” Mr. Finch said firmly. “By morning, Slade might have figured out we were involved. He might even try to force the truth out of the lodge manager. This is our only chance to find that lockbox before he does.”
He looked from Clara’s determined face to Betty’s worried one. “I understand if you’ve had enough adventure for one night. You’ve already done more than I ever could have asked.”
Clara didn’t hesitate. “We started this, and we’re going to finish it. Right, Betty?”
Betty took a deep breath. The thought of venturing back into the dark woods, with Mr. Slade possibly lurking somewhere nearby, was terrifying. But the thought of him getting his hands on the Finch family’s rightful property was even worse.
“Right,” she said, her voice small but steady. “Let’s go find that lockbox.”

Mr. Finch nodded gravely. "Then we haven't a moment to lose. We'll slip out the back way. The kitchen staff should be gone for the night."

He pocketed the powerful flashlight, but not before casting one final, reassuring glance at the girls. Clara, her heart thumping with a mixture of fear and excitement, felt ready for anything. Betty, however, chewed nervously on her lower lip.

"Are we really going back out there?" she whispered. "In the dark?"

"It's the only way, Betty," Clara said, her voice full of conviction. "We can't let Mr. Slade win."

"Mr. Slade won't win," Mr. Finch stated firmly. "Not while I have anything to say about it. Now, stick close to me, and be as quiet as you can."

He led the way from the secluded alcove, down a narrow service corridor they had never noticed before. The air smelled of old wood and floor polish. At the end of the hall was a heavy door with a small, barred window. Mr. Finch carefully turned the handle and peered out into the night.

"The coast is clear," he announced in a low tone. "Let's go."

One by one, they slipped out into the cool, damp air. The moon was hidden behind a thick blanket of clouds, and the surrounding forest was a wall of impenetrable blackness. For a moment, Betty's courage failed her. The woods that had seemed so beautiful and inviting in the daytime now felt menacing and full of unseen dangers.

Mr. Finch must have sensed her hesitation. He clicked on his flashlight, and a bright, steady beam cut a path through the darkness. "Stay within the light," he instructed. "The trail to the river isn't far from here. I used to walk it a dozen times a day as a boy."

They followed him past the empty kitchen loading dock and onto a faint path that led away from the lodge and into the trees. The ground was soft with pine needles, muffling their footsteps. Overhead, the wind soughed mournfully through the high branches.

"Do you think Mr. Slade is looking for us?" Betty asked, her voice barely audible above the sound of the wind.

"I imagine he is," Mr. Finch replied calmly. "But he believes we are still at the lodge. He'll be watching the front entrance and the main roads. He won't think to look for us out here."

"I'll bet a doughnut he's furious about the statue," Clara speculated, a note of satisfaction in her voice. "He thought he had it for sure!"

"He was certainly angry," agreed Mr. Finch. "But a man like Slade doesn't give up easily. He is likely trying to piece together what happened. That's why we must hurry."

They walked on in silence for a time, the beam of the flashlight dancing ahead of them, illuminating gnarled roots and patches of damp moss. Every rustle in the underbrush made Betty jump. A twig snapped nearby, and she gasped, grabbing Clara's arm.

"What was that?"

Clara stopped, listening intently. "I don't know. It sounded close."

Mr. Finch swept the flashlight in a wide arc, its beam probing the shadows between the thick tree trunks. For a long moment, nothing moved. Then, two yellow eyes gleamed back at them from a low branch. A soft "hoo-hoo" echoed through the woods.

"It's only an owl," Mr. Finch said with a quiet chuckle. "He's more startled by us than we are by him."

"An owl!" Clara exclaimed softly. "Maybe it's a good omen! For the Owl's Gaze!"

"I hope so," Betty murmured, though she did not sound entirely convinced. She could not shake the feeling that they were being watched by something far less friendly than a nocturnal bird.

The path grew steeper as it sloped down toward the river. Soon, the gentle sighing of the wind was joined by a new sound, a low, steady rushing that grew louder with every step.

"We're getting close," Mr. Finch announced. "I can hear the water. The rock formation should be just ahead, along the bank."

They emerged from the dense trees onto a wide, pebbly shore. The river flowed swiftly past them, its dark surface churning and swirling in the moonlight that had just begun to break through the clouds. Across the water, the forest rose up again, a solid black mass against the graying sky.

"My goodness, it's so different at night," Clara said, gazing at the scene in awe.

"It's certainly a wild place," Mr. Finch agreed. "Now, according to the map on the statue, the hiding place is near the riverbank. We need to find the rock that looks like an owl."

He began to walk slowly along the shore, shining his light on the various granite outcroppings that dotted the landscape. There were dozens of them, large and small, worn smooth by centuries of wind and water. From a distance, they all looked like gray, featureless lumps.

"They all look the same to me," Betty said with a sigh of discouragement. "How will we ever find the right one?"

"Silas was clever," Mr. Finch mused, playing the beam of light over a particularly large boulder. "The clue was 'Under the Owl's Gaze.' That implies a certain perspective. You have to be looking at it from the right spot."

"Maybe this is it!" Clara cried, running over to a jagged pile of rocks. "If you squint, this tall one kind of looks like a beak!"

Mr. Finch and Betty joined her, but after careful inspection, they had to disagree. The resemblance was faint at best. They spent nearly twenty minutes examining different formations, their hopes rising and falling with each new possibility. The night was growing colder, and the initial excitement of the chase was beginning to wear off, replaced by a weary frustration.

"Perhaps we're on the wrong side of the river," Betty suggested.

"No, the map was very clear," Mr. Finch insisted, though his voice had lost some of its earlier confidence. He stood still, staring out at the dark, rushing water, his brow furrowed in concentration. "Under the Owl's Gaze. What did my father used to say about it? Something about looking backward..."

His head snapped up, his eyes suddenly bright with understanding. "Of course! What a fool I've been! We're looking in the wrong direction!"

"What do you mean?" Clara asked.

"Come with me," he said, his voice brimming with renewed energy. He led them further downstream, away from the main cluster of rocks, to a small, sandy spit that jutted out into the water. "My father brought me here once, long ago. He told me it was his favorite secret spot. Stand here, right beside me, and look back."

The girls did as he instructed. He aimed the flashlight back up the riverbank, illuminating the jumble of granite they had just been searching. From this new vantage point, the scattered shapes suddenly resolved into a single, startling image. Two large, dark hollows in the rock became deep-set eyes. A jutting ledge formed a sharp, hooked beak. The rounded top of the outcropping completed the perfect silhouette of a great horned owl's head, staring out over the river with a stony, silent intensity.

"Gosh!" Betty breathed. "It really does look like an owl!"

"The Owl's Gaze," Clara whispered, her eyes wide with wonder. "It's perfect!"

"Silas knew this spot well," Mr. Finch said with a smile. "Now, the lockbox should be hidden somewhere under its gaze. Let's have a look."

Feeling a fresh surge of adrenaline, they scrambled back up the bank to the base of the massive rock formation. The ground around it was a tangle of overgrown roots and thorny bushes.

"He certainly didn't make it easy," Betty commented, carefully picking her way through the brush.

"A good hiding place never is," Mr. Finch replied. "Spread out and search carefully. Look for anything that seems out of place. A loose stone, a patch of freshly disturbed earth, anything."

They began to search the area methodically. Mr. Finch examined the deep crevices in the rock itself, while Betty cautiously poked at the dirt with a sturdy stick. Clara, being the smallest, was able to crawl into the tight spaces beneath the gnarled roots of an ancient pine tree that grew beside the rock. The air underneath was cool and smelled of damp earth and decaying leaves.

She ran her hands over the ground, feeling for any irregularity. Her fingers brushed against something hard and flat. A stone. She pushed it aside. Then another. And another. Beneath them, the dirt felt looser, softer than the surrounding soil.

"I think I've found something!" she called out, her voice muffled.

Mr. Finch and Betty hurried over. "Where, Clara?"

"Right here! Under these roots!"

Using her hands as shovels, Clara began to dig frantically into the soft earth. It wasn't deep. Scarcely six inches down, her fingers struck something solid and metallic. Working together, she and Betty cleared away the remaining dirt and pulled their prize from the ground.

It was a small metal box, no bigger than a shoebox, and it was surprisingly heavy. It was made of dark, tarnished iron, with a simple, sturdy-looking lock on the front. Green and brown patches of corrosion covered its surface, but it was otherwise intact.

"We found it!" Clara declared triumphantly, holding the box up for the others to see. "We really found it!"

"You did it, Clara!" Betty exclaimed, her face breaking into a wide grin. "I can't believe it!"

Mr. Finch took the box from her, his hands trembling slightly as he wiped away the dirt. "After all these years," he murmured, his voice thick with emotion. "I never thought I'd see it again."

He turned the box over in his hands, examining the lock. "Now, the only question is how to open it."

"Was there a key with the statue?" Betty asked.

Mr. Finch shook his head. "No. But Silas was never one for simple keys. I suspect the trick is in the lock itself." He began to fiddle with the tarnished metal.

SNAP!

The sharp crack of a breaking twig echoed from the trees behind them. It was louder than any sound an owl or a squirrel could make. Instantly, the three of them froze.

"Down!" Mr. Finch hissed, his voice a low, urgent whisper. "Behind the rock! Now!"

Clutching the lockbox, he shoved the girls toward the deep shadow at the base of the granite owl. They flattened themselves against the cold stone, their hearts pounding in their chests. Mr. Finch clicked off his flashlight, plunging them into near-total darkness. Peering around the edge of the boulder, Clara saw another light, a powerful, sweeping beam, cutting through the woods and coming straight toward them.

A man's voice, low and guttural with rage, drifted on the night air. "I know you're out here somewhere, Finch! And you've got those meddling little girls with you! You can't hide from me!"

It was Mr. Slade.

"He found us!" Betty whimpered, pressing her face against Clara's shoulder.

"Shhh," Clara whispered back, her own fear a cold knot in her stomach. "He hasn't seen us yet."

Slade's flashlight beam played across the riverbank, moving erratically. He was clearly searching, but without knowing the secret of the Owl's Gaze, he was merely wandering. He kicked at a loose stone in frustration.

"Where did you go?" he muttered to himself, his voice close enough now that they could hear every venomous word. "It has to be here somewhere. The old fool always talked about this part of the river."

The beam of his light swept upward, painting a brilliant white stripe across the face of the rock formation, not three feet above their heads. Clara held her breath, certain he would lower the light and discover them cowering in the shadows. For a terrifying moment, the light lingered, and she could see the angry, searching expression on his face, illuminated in the reflected glare. He was so close.

But his gaze was fixed on the woods across the river, not on the ground at his feet. After a moment, he grunted in disgust and swept the beam away. He paced back and forth along the shore for another minute, his heavy boots crunching on the pebbles.

"You can't hide forever!" he shouted into the night. "That box is mine! And I will have it!"

Then, with a final, angry curse, he turned and stomped back the way he had come. His light bobbed and weaved as he disappeared into the trees, his muttering growing fainter and fainter until it was swallowed by the sound of the river.

They waited in absolute silence for what felt like an eternity. Mr. Finch was the first to move. He crept to the edge of the rock and peered out into the darkness for a full minute before speaking.

"He's gone," he said, his voice shaky with relief.

Slowly, cautiously, the girls emerged from their hiding place. Betty was trembling from head to toe. "That was too close," she said, her teeth chattering. "He was right there!"

"But he didn't see us!" Clara countered, clutching the precious lockbox to her chest. "And we have what we came for! He was looking in the wrong place the whole time!"

"Thanks to your grandfather's cleverness," Mr. Finch said, placing a steadying hand on Betty's shoulder. "But Clara is right. We have the box. And now we must get as far away from here as possible."

"We can't go back to the lodge," Betty stated. "He'll be watching it for sure now."

"You are absolutely right," Mr. Finch agreed. "We will not go back. I have an old friend, a prospector named Jedediah, who has a small, secluded cabin about two miles east of here. It's well off the beaten path. We will be safe there for the night, and we can decide what to do next."

He switched his own flashlight back on, its warm beam a comforting presence after the terrifying darkness. "Can you two manage a longer walk?"

"We can manage," Clara said with determination.

Betty, still shaken but reassured by Mr. Finch's calm authority, nodded her agreement. Anything was better than staying here.

"Good," he said. "Then let's not waste another second."

Without a backward glance at the stone owl that had guarded its secret for so long, they turned their backs on the river. With Mr. Finch leading the way and Clara holding the mysterious iron box, they plunged back into the deep, dark woods, their minds already racing with the question of what treasures, or further troubles, it might contain.

Penny nodded, though her thoughts were spinning. Mrs. Deline, the package, the spyglass, the secret broadcasts. It all fit together in a pattern too neat to be a coincidence.

Just as she opened her mouth to voice another theory, a sudden crackle of static filled the small space. An operator snatched his headphones, his body tensing.

"Sir!" he called out, his voice sharp. "I'm getting something! It's faint, but it's on the frequency!"

Jerry was instantly at his side. "Is it him? Is it B4?"

"The signature is right, sir. It's the same irregular pulse." The operator twisted a dial, his brow furrowed in concentration. "He's not on for long. Just short bursts."

Another officer at a different set of instruments was already swinging a large, circular antenna mounted on a pedestal. "I've got a bearing!" he announced. "Roughly two hundred ninety degrees. Northwest from our position."

"It's coming from the coastline," Jerry murmured, his eyes fixed on a large map tacked to the wall. He drew a faint pencil line outward from their location. "Just as we thought."

"Signal's gone, sir," the first operator reported, slumping back in his chair. "Lasted less than a minute."

"He's being cautious," Jerry said, tapping the map with his finger. "He knows we could be listening. But he had to make contact. Now all we can do is wait for another listening post to report in. With two bearings, we can triangulate his position to within a few hundred yards."

For ten minutes, the truck was silent except for the low hum of the equipment and the drumming of Jerry's fingers on the chart table. Penny felt she could hardly breathe. They were on the very brink of a discovery.

Then, the field telephone buzzed. Jerry snatched it up. "Livingston here." He listened intently, his expression hardening. "Got it. Southeast, one hundred sixty-five degrees. Thanks."

He hung up the phone and quickly took a protractor to another point on the map, representing a second monitoring station miles down the coast. He drew another line. The two pencil marks intersected at a point on the jagged coastline just north of Sunset Beach.

"There," Jerry said, his voice tight with satisfaction. He circled the intersection with his pencil. "That's where he is. Right in the area the locals call Smuggler's Cove."

"The caves!" Penny gasped. "The old pirate caves!"

Jerry shot her a sharp look. "You know of them?"

"Everyone does," Louise supplied. "They're a local landmark, but people say they're dangerous. The tides come in fast."

"A perfect place to hide a portable transmitter," Jerry mused, more to himself than to the girls. "Easy to get in and out by sea, and a maze of tunnels to hide in. He could be gone by now, but we know his general operating area." He turned to the girls, his manner once again all business. "This is where your tour ends. I have to report this and get a team organized. I'll have the taxi take you back to the hotel."

"But Jerry, can't we help?" Penny pleaded.

"Absolutely not," he said, his tone leaving no room for argument. "This has become an active military operation. I want you two back at the Crystal Inn, and I want you to stay there. Don't go near the beach, don't talk to anyone, and above all, do not try to investigate this on your own. Is that clear?"

"Crystal clear," Penny said, her disappointment plain.

Jerry escorted them back to the waiting taxi. "I mean it, girls," he said, holding the door for them. "This isn't a game. These people are professionals, and they're dangerous."

As the taxi pulled away, Louise let out a long breath. "Well, that was exciting. I'm glad we're out of it. As Jerry said, it's too dangerous."

"Out of it?" Penny cried, turning to her friend in disbelief. "Louise, we're in the very heart of it! Don't you see? Smuggler's Cove! 'Voice from the Cave!' The package! Mrs. Deline! It's all connected!"

"Now, Penny, Jerry himself said there was no reason to suspect her."

"And I'll bet a dozen cookies he was just saying that to put us off!" Penny retorted. "He has to be professional. But we don't! Mrs. Deline buried that package on the beach, and now we know there's a spy sending coded messages from the caves nearby. She was lying about the fish bones! She must have been!"

"It does seem a bit of a stretch," Louise admitted thoughtfully. "Why would she bother to bury garbage for a lighthouse keeper?"

"Exactly! She wouldn't! She was creating a dead drop. A place to leave information or supplies for someone else to pick up. She probably wrote the location on her skirt with a wet finger, just so she could find the exact spot. The mark would have vanished by the time she got back to the hotel."

"That's very clever, if it's true," Louise conceded. "But what do we do about it? We can't go against what Jerry said."

"We won't go against it," Penny declared, a mischievous glint in her eye. "We'll just… keep our eyes open. From the hotel. As instructed."

They arrived back at the Crystal Inn and paid the driver. The veranda was empty now, the rain having driven everyone inside. As they entered the lobby, Penny stopped dead.

"Look," she whispered, nodding toward the far side of the room.

There, seated in a high-backed armchair, was Mrs. Deline. She was not alone. Leaning over her chair, speaking in a low, confidential tone, was old Jake Skagway. The grizzled fisherman, whom Penny had suspected of being a rascal that very morning, now seemed far more sinister. He passed a small, folded piece of paper to the widow, who slipped it quickly into her handbag. She glanced up, her cold eyes sweeping the lobby, and Jake Skagway straightened up, tipped his worn hat, and shuffled away toward the door. He gave the girls a cursory glance as he passed, his face unreadable.

"Did you see that?" Penny breathed. "He gave her a note!"

"I saw," Louise whispered back, her own eyes wide. "So you were right about him being a rascal after all."

"He's more than a rascal, he's part of it! He probably keeps watch on the boats for her. And she's the contact, the one who talks to B4!"

"Quiet, Penny, she'll hear you!"

They moved away toward the elevators, trying to appear casual. Mrs. Deline watched them go, her face a mask of detached indifference.

"We have to do something," Penny insisted once they were safely in their room. "We can't just sit here while she plans who-knows-what with Jake Skagway and that Voice from the Cave."

"But what can we do? Jerry's team is handling it. They'll catch them."

"They'll catch the man with the radio, maybe," Penny reasoned. "But they have no idea Mrs. Deline is involved. She'll slip right through their fingers. And that note Jake gave her… it could be anything! A time for the next broadcast, or instructions for an escaping prisoner!"

She paced the room restlessly, her gaze falling on the window. The sky was clearing, and a pale, watery moon was beginning to show through the breaking clouds.

"The tide will be going out soon," Penny said suddenly. "The caves will be accessible for a few hours."

Louise looked at her in alarm. "Penny Parker, don't you even think it."

"I have to, Lou! What if the note was a summons? What if she's going there tonight? Someone has to watch her! If we could see her go to the caves, or meet someone there, then we'd have real proof to give Jerry."

"And get captured by spies in the process! No, thank you. I'm staying right here."

"But think of it! We could solve the whole mystery! We'd be careful. We'd just watch from a distance. We wouldn't let anyone see us."

Louise shook her head firmly. "It's dark. The beach is rocky and treacherous near the cove. We'd probably fall and break our necks before we even got there. My answer is no."

Penny sighed, knowing it was useless to argue when Louise used that tone. For the next hour, she tried to read a book, but the words blurred on the page. Her mind was on the dark beach, the mysterious caves, and the cold-eyed woman in the lobby below.

At a little past nine o'clock, there was a light tap on their door. It was Louise's father, Mr. Clark, come to say goodnight.

"Everything all right, girls?" he asked, smiling. "You look a little down in the mouth, Penny."

"Oh, it's just the rainy weather," Penny said quickly. "We had to cut our beach adventure short."

"Well, it's clearing up nicely now," he said, glancing out the window. "By the way, I thought you might be interested. Our strange Mrs. Deline just left the hotel. Dressed for a walk, it looked like. Odd time for a stroll on the beach, I must say."

Penny's heart gave a great leap. "Did you see which way she went, Mr. Clark?"

"Toward the north end of the beach, I believe. Headed right for that nasty stretch of rocks. She had a powerful flashlight with her. Well, sleep tight, you two."

The moment the door closed behind him, Penny spun around to face her friend. "You see? I was right! She's going to the cove! We have to follow her!"

Louise's resolve was visibly crumbling. "Oh, Penny… it's so dangerous."

"It's more dangerous to let her get away with it! Please, Lou! We'll be so careful. We'll stay far behind her. We won't even go in the caves. We'll just watch from the dunes. If we see anything, we'll run right back and tell your father."

Louise hesitated, chewing on her lower lip. The thought of what Mrs. Deline might be doing, combined with Penny's infectious determination, was a powerful persuader.

"Oh, all right," she sighed in resignation. "But if I so much as hear a strange noise, I'm turning back, and you're coming with me. Deal?"

"Deal!" Penny cried, grabbing her jacket. "Let's go!"

They slipped out of the hotel by a side door, avoiding the lobby. The air was cool and damp, heavy with the scent of salt and wet sand. The moon was brighter now, casting a silvery sheen on the wet beach and turning the foam of the waves into ghostly lace. Far down the beach, a tiny point of light bobbed and weaved.

"That's her flashlight!" Penny whispered excitedly. "She's already halfway there! Come on!"

Keeping to the soft sand at the top of the beach where their footsteps would be muffled, the girls hurried along. The rhythmic crash of the surf provided a steady cover for any small sounds they made. As they drew nearer to Smuggler's Cove, the smooth beach gave way to a jumble of massive, water-worn rocks and steep, sandy cliffs. Mrs. Deline's light disappeared behind a great outcropping of stone.

"She's gone into the cove," Penny breathed. "We'll have to climb up on the cliffs to see anything."

Carefully, they scrambled up a sandy path that led to the top of the bluffs overlooking the small, hidden bay. From this vantage point, they could see the dark mouths of several caves carved into the base of the cliff on the far side. The tide was low, exposing a narrow strip of rocky shore in front of them.

And there was the light. It wasn't moving now. It shone from within the largest of the caves, a faint, stationary glow that illuminated the entrance.

"She's inside," Louise whispered, clutching Penny's arm. "What do we do now?"

"We wait," Penny whispered back, her eyes glued to the cave. "We watch."

They lay flat on the damp ground, hidden by a clump of coarse beach grass, their hearts thudding in unison. The minutes ticked by. The only sounds were the sea and the wind.

Suddenly, a new sound reached them. It was a low, rhythmic tapping. It came from the direction of the cave.

Tap. Tap-tap. Tap.

It was Morse code. Penny, a dedicated member of her school's signal corps, recognized it instantly. But this was no simple message. It was a fast, complex stream of dits and dahs, sent by a practiced hand.

"She's not meeting B4," Penny murmured in dawning realization. "She *is* B4!"

The tapping continued for another minute, then stopped. Silence descended once more, thicker and more menacing than before. The light in the cave went out.

"She's finished," Louise breathed. "She'll be coming out now. Let's get out of here!"

"Wait," Penny hissed. "Look!"

From the inky blackness of the cave mouth, two figures emerged. Not one. Two.

One was Mrs. Deline, holding the extinguished flashlight. The other was a tall, lean man in dark clothing. In the pale moonlight, the girls could not make out his features, but he moved with a stiff, military bearing.

They spoke together in low voices, too faint for the girls to understand the words. The man gestured out to sea. Mrs. Deline shook her head and pointed back down the beach, toward the hotel. There seemed to be a disagreement.

Then the man's voice rose, carrying on the wind, sharp and guttural. It was not English. It was German.

"Nein! Das Boot wartet!"

Mrs. Deline replied in the same language, her voice sharp and commanding. The man fell silent. He gave a curt nod, turned, and melted back into the shadows of the cave.

Mrs. Deline stood alone for a moment, a dark silhouette against the rocks. Then she switched on her flashlight and began to walk back along the rocky shore, her beam cutting a swath through the darkness.

Penny and Louise stayed frozen, hardly daring to breathe until the light was a considerable distance away.

"Did you hear that?" Penny finally managed to whisper, her voice trembling with the magnitude of their discovery. "She was speaking German! And there was a man with her! One of the escaped prisoners!"

"And he said something about a boat," Louise added, her face pale in the moonlight. "A boat was waiting!"

"This is it, Lou! This is the proof Jerry needs! Mrs. Deline is a spy, and she's hiding a German officer in those caves!" Penny scrambled to her feet. "We have to get back to the hotel and call Jerry before that boat gets here!"

Penny's words hung in the salty air. Louise, still pale, nodded vigorously. "Let's go! Before she circles back and finds us!"

They scrambled backwards, keeping low to the ground. The wet sand and sharp blades of grass scraped at their hands and knees. Every rustle of the wind sounded like approaching footsteps. Every shadow seemed to hold a new threat.

"Which way?" Louise panted, her voice a strained whisper.

"Away from her light," Penny directed, pointing toward the dark shape of the hotel far down the beach. "We'll have to take the long way around, behind the dunes."

It was a grueling trek. They stumbled over unseen rocks and sank into soft pockets of sand. Their breath came in ragged gasps. The moon, their only ally, kept ducking behind thick clouds, plunging them into near total darkness.

"I think my heart is going to pound right out of my chest," Louise confessed as they paused for a moment behind a large drift of sand.

"Mine too," Penny admitted. "But we can't stop. Think of what's at stake, Lou! That man in the cave is an Enemy agent! And Mrs. Deline is his accomplice!"

"I know, I know. I just never thought it would be like this. It's like something out of one of your stories, Penny."

"This story is real," Penny said grimly. "And we're right in the middle of it. Come on."

Energized by the thought, they pushed onward, their only goal the distant, sleeping hotel. They finally reached the rear of the Crystal Inn, skirting the edge of the property until they came to the familiar veranda steps. The lobby was dimly lit and, to their immense relief, empty. Mrs. Deline was nowhere in sight.

"She must have gone straight to her room," Louise whispered, leaning against a pillar to catch her breath.

"Good. Now for the telephone." Penny led the way to the public telephone booth tucked into an alcove near the main desk. "You stand guard and watch the elevator."

Louise nodded, her eyes fixed on the bronze elevator doors as if expecting them to open at any second. Penny fumbled in her pocket for a coin, her fingers shaking so much she dropped it twice on the tiled floor. Finally, she managed to dial the operator and ask for Jerry Livingston's room. The ringing on the other end of the line seemed to go on forever.

"Hello?" Jerry's voice was thick with sleep.

"Jerry! It's Penny! You have to wake up! It's an emergency!"

"Penny? What time is it?" he mumbled. "What's wrong?"

"It's Mrs. Deline!" Penny blurted out, her words tumbling over one another in a frantic rush. "She's B4! We saw her! She was in a cave down the beach, sending a message in Morse code! And there's a man with her, a German! He's one of the escaped prisoners! They're waiting for a boat!"

There was a moment of stunned silence on the other end of the line. Then Jerry's voice came back, sharp and wide awake. "Where are you, Penny?"

"In the hotel lobby. In the phone booth."

"Stay right there. Don't move. Don't talk to anyone. I'm coming down." The line went dead.

Penny hung up the receiver and sagged against the wall of the booth, a wave of relief washing over her. "He's coming," she told Louise, who was still anxiously watching the elevator.

"Did he believe you?"

"He's Jerry," Penny said with a sudden surge of confidence. "Of course he believed me."

Less than two minutes later, the elevator doors slid open and Jerry Livingston stepped out. He was hastily dressed in trousers and a shirt, his hair uncombed and his face a mask of grim intensity. He strode over to the alcove where the two girls waited.

"Tell me everything," he commanded in a low voice. "Start from the beginning. Don't leave anything out."

Standing in the shadowy alcove, Penny recounted their entire evening. She started with their decision to watch Mrs. Deline, their secretive journey to the caves, the pinprick of light, and the rhythmic tapping of the Morse code. Her voice dropped to a conspiratorial whisper as she described the two figures emerging from the darkness and the argument they had overheard.

"She spoke in German," Penny finished, her eyes wide. "And the man, too. We heard him say, 'Das Boot wartet!'"

"Nein! Das Boot wartet!" Jerry repeated softly, a flicker of grim understanding in his eyes. "'No! The boat is waiting!' This is it. This is the break we've been waiting for. You girls are amazing! Truly amazing!"

"So what happens now?" Louise asked, her voice trembling slightly. "Are you going to arrest them?"

"We're going to do more than that," Jerry said, his jaw set. "We're going to catch them all. The man in the cave, Mrs. Deline, and the crew of that boat. I need to make some calls." He glanced around the empty lobby, then looked sternly at the girls. "Go up to your room and lock the door. Under no circumstances are you to leave it. This is going to get dangerous, and I need to know you both are safe."

"But Jerry," Penny began, a protest already forming on her lips.

"No buts, Penny. You've done more than enough. You've given us the key to unlock this whole affair. Now let the Army handle the rest. That's an order."

He gave them a final, non-negotiable look before turning and heading quickly toward the hotel manager's private office. The girls watched him go, then slowly trudged toward the main staircase, the excitement of their discovery now mingled with the disappointment of being excluded from the finale.

Back in their room, Louise immediately bolted the door and sank onto her bed. "Well, I guess that's that. We did our part. I for one am glad to be out of it."

Penny, however, was far too restless to sit still. She paced the length of the room like a caged tiger, finally stopping at the window to peer out into the darkness toward the beach.

"I can't just sit here, Lou," she said, her voice tight with frustration. "Not now. Not when everything is about to happen."

"What do you mean?" Louise asked, sitting up in alarm. "Jerry told us to stay put! He said it was an order!"

"It's our discovery," Penny insisted, turning from the window, her eyes shining with excitement. "We started this, and I have to see how it ends. We won't get in the way. We'll be careful. We'll just watch from a distance, the same way we watched before."

"Oh, Penny, no! It's too dangerous," Louise pleaded. "You heard what Jerry said. And what if they have guns?"

"The Army will have guns too," Penny countered logically. "They'll be all around the place. It will probably be the safest spot on the entire coastline! Besides, we know the layout of the beach better than they do. We can find a perfect hiding spot where no one will ever see us."

Louise looked doubtful, her brow furrowed with worry. But she knew that look on Penny's face. When Penny Parker had an idea, there was little that could be done to dissuade her. "I suppose you're going to go with or without me," she sighed.

"I'd much rather go with you," Penny said, giving her friend a hopeful look. "We're a team, aren't we?"

Louise sighed again, a long, drawn out sound of resignation that was part protest and part surrender. "Oh, all right. But if we get captured by German spies, I'm going to be very cross with you."

"It's a deal," Penny grinned, her good humor restored. "Now, let's wait until we see some activity, then we can slip out the back again. This is going to be the most exciting night of our lives!"

They didn't have to wait long. Within twenty minutes, a flurry of silent, organized activity began below. They saw dark figures moving quietly on the beach, using the dunes for cover. A large Army truck, its headlights off, rolled silently to a stop on the road behind the hotel. Soldiers, barely visible in the dark, fanned out across the sand, moving with practiced efficiency toward the distant, rocky caves.

"That's our cue," Penny whispered, her heart thumping with anticipation. "Let's go."

They crept out of the hotel as they had before, their hearts in their throats. Using the deep shadows of the dunes for cover, they made their way back toward the rocky shoreline. This time, they did not venture onto the open beach. Instead, Penny led them up a steep, sandy path that wound into the cluster of boulders overlooking the caves. They found a high vantage point, a natural nest of rocks well above the beach that gave them a clear, unobstructed view of the main cave entrance.

Below them, the scene was one of tense, quiet readiness. They could see Jerry conferring with a man in an officer's uniform. They pointed toward the largest cave, the one from which the girls had seen the light. The soldiers were in position, forming a tight, inescapable semi-circle around the cave mouth. All was silent except for the endless murmur of the waves against the shore.

The minutes stretched into an eternity. The moon cast a pale, eerie light over the scene. Penny and Louise held their breath, their eyes glued to the dark opening in the rock.

Then, a voice cut through the night, made loud and impersonal by a megaphone. "This is the United States Army! You are surrounded! Come out with your hands in the air!"

There was no response from the cave. The silence that followed was heavy and menacing.

The command was repeated, this time in sharp, authoritative German.

Still, nothing. The dark mouth of the cave remained empty.

"They're not giving up," Penny breathed, her knuckles white where she gripped the cold rock.

"They're trapped," Louise whispered back, shivering from more than just the night air.

Jerry and the officer spoke again. Then, two soldiers, carrying powerful, bulky searchlights, advanced cautiously toward the cave. They switched on their beams, and the entrance was instantly flooded with brilliant white light, turning the dark opening into a starkly illuminated stage.

Suddenly, a figure darted from the mouth of the cave. It was Mrs. Deline. She was not surrendering. In her hand was a small, dark pistol, and she fired it once, twice, into the darkness toward the line of soldiers. The shots echoed sharply off the rocks, loud and violent in the night.

"Get down!" a voice yelled from below.

Penny and Louise flattened themselves instinctively against the cold stone of their hiding place, their hearts hammering against their ribs.

"They got them!" Louise gasped, lifting her head to peek over the boulder. "They got them both!"

"The radio transmitter," Penny murmured, realizing at once what it must be.

"The boat!" Penny and Louise said in unison, looking out at the dark water.

The girls watched, mesmerized, as the swift cutter easily overtook the spy boat, circling it and forcing it to a stop. The trap was complete. Every piece of the puzzle had been put into place.

"I should have known I couldn't order Penny Parker to stay put," he said, managing a weary smile.

"We had to see," Penny said. "We were careful. Did you catch them all?"

"Every last one," Jerry confirmed, sitting down on a boulder beside them. "Thanks to you two. Mrs. Deline was indeed B4. Her real name is Helga von Steiner, a very dedicated enemy agent. The man in the cave was a high ranking German officer who escaped a Canadian prison camp a month ago. Mrs. Deline was sent here to organize escapes for him and others like him. They were to be taken by submarine back to Germany."

"So she really is a spy!" Penny declared triumphantly.

"One of the cleverest we've been up against," Jerry admitted. "She used this quiet little beach town as her base of operations. The short wave broadcasts were to arrange the escape details and rendezvous points. The boat you heard was just a launch to take the officer out to a waiting submarine."

"But what about the package of fish bones?" Louise asked, the one detail that still puzzled her. "Was that a true story she told us?"

Jerry actually chuckled. "That's the clever part. It really was a package of fish bones. She was aware you were watching her that day. "You know," Louise said thoughtfully, "after all this, I still don't think I can bring myself to like Mrs. Deline." Penny laughed, the sound bright and clear in the morning air. "I'll leave that job up to you," she said, echoing her own words from what felt like a lifetime ago. "But I'll bet a cookie our next vacation will be a little less exciting." Louise grinned at her friend, the adventure shining in her eyes. "With you, Penny, I wouldn't be too sure about that."

The Crystal Inn was already humming with the low murmur of the breakfast hour. The aroma of coffee and bacon hung in the air, a welcome scent after a long night of cold sea spray and suspense. Penny's father, Anthony Parker, sat at their usual table by the large window overlooking the beach. He leaped to his feet the moment he saw them.

"Penny! Louise! Jerry, my boy! I was about to send out a search party." His worried expression melted into relief as he took in their tired but triumphant faces.

"We're perfectly fine, Dad," Penny assured him, sliding into a chair. "Just a bit hungry."

"Hungry is the least of it," Jerry said, taking a seat. "Mr. Parker, your daughter and her friend just helped us apprehend a very dangerous enemy agent."

Mr. Parker's eyebrows shot up. He listened intently as Jerry gave a brief, official account of the night's events, from the discovery of the transmitter to the capture of Mrs. Deline and the escaped prisoner.

"I knew that woman was up to no good!" Mr. Parker declared, striking the table lightly with his fist. "I just didn't realize how much. Penny, darling, you were right all along. I'm very proud of you girls. But also very worried! You took a tremendous risk."

"We had to, Dad," Penny said, her mouth full of toast. "No one else knew about the cave."

"Well, all's well that ends well," Mr. Parker conceded. "I'll have a wonderful exclusive for the Riverview Star. 'Girl Detective Unmasks Spy Ring at Quiet Resort'!"

"Oh, Dad, don't be so dramatic," Penny protested, though her cheeks flushed with pleasure.

The rest of the morning passed in a pleasant blur. After their hearty breakfast, the girls retired to their room for a long and much needed sleep. The sun was already dipping low in the western sky when Penny awoke, feeling refreshed and gloriously free of mysteries.

"I could get used to this," she announced to Louise, who was sitting by the window, reading a novel. "A vacation with absolutely nothing to do but swim and lie in the sun."

"That does sound nice," Louise agreed. "I was just thinking the same thing. No more spies, no more digging in the sand, no more lurking in dark caves."

Their peaceful resolution was interrupted by a sudden commotion from the hallway. A woman's voice, high and imperious, echoed down the corridor, followed by a man's nervous, placating tones.

"Do be careful with that trunk, young man! It contains priceless heirlooms! Priceless!"

"Yes, Aunt Elspeth. Of course, Aunt Elspeth."

Penny and Louise exchanged curious glances. Penny, never one to ignore a potential source of interest, tiptoed to the door and opened it a crack.

A bellhop was struggling with an enormous, old fashioned steamer trunk while a large, formidable woman in a purple turban directed the operation. Her face was a mask of expensive powders and rouge, and ropes of pearls were looped around her neck. Tailing her was a thin, anxious looking young man with spectacles, who kept wringing his hands.

"Put it right there," the woman commanded, pointing a jewel encrusted finger toward the room directly across the hall from the girls'. "And for heaven's sake, don't scuff the wallpaper! This hotel is quaint but rather shabby, isn't it, Edgar?"

"It has a certain rustic charm, Aunt," the young man, Edgar, mumbled.

The woman sniffed dismissively. "Rustic charm is just another word for poor plumbing. Now, pay the boy and let's get settled."

Penny quietly closed the door. "Well," she whispered to Louise, "it looks like we have new neighbors."

"She certainly seems like a character," Louise commented. "And that poor young man looks terrified of her."

"I'll bet a cookie she leads him a merry chase," Penny speculated. "Did you see all those jewels? She was sparkling like a chandelier."

The next day, the girls did their best to enjoy a perfectly normal, uneventful vacation. They swam in the ocean, built a sandcastle, and collected shells. But Penny's attention kept drifting back to the hotel. Their new neighbor, a Mrs. Elspeth Abernathy, had made her presence known to the entire establishment. She complained about the food, the service, and the salt in the air, all in a voice that carried from one end of the veranda to the other. Her nephew, Edgar Pym, was her constant shadow, scurrying to fulfill her every demand.

That evening, as the hotel guests gathered in the lounge for after dinner coffee, Mrs. Abernathy was holding court.

"It is my most prized possession," she was announcing to a small, captive audience. "An emerald of magnificent size and clarity, known as the Sea Serpent's Eye. It has been in my family for generations, a gift from a maharajah to my great-great-grandfather."

She opened a velvet case to reveal a necklace. Even from across the room, Penny could see the huge, green stone that glittered under the lamp light. It was indeed a spectacular jewel.

"I never let it out of my sight," Mrs. Abernathy declared, snapping the case shut. "One simply cannot be too careful these days. The world is filled with scoundrels and thieves." As she spoke, her gaze fell upon old Jake Skagway, who had shuffled into the lobby to sell some polished agates to the tourists. He met her suspicious glare with a neutral stare of his own before turning back to his wares.

Later that night, Penny was awakened by a sound she had come to associate with trouble. A shrill, piercing scream.

It was followed by a frantic pounding on a door. "Help! Police! I've been robbed! Robbed!"

Lights flicked on all up and down the hallway. Penny and Louise threw on their robes and hurried out of their room, just as other guests were doing the same. The source of the commotion was, unsurprisingly, Mrs. Abernathy's suite. She stood in the doorway, her face a ghastly white beneath her rouge, her purple turban askew.

"It's gone!" she wailed. "The Sea Serpent's Eye! It's been stolen!"

Mr. Pym was fluttering beside her, trying to calm her down. "Aunt Elspeth, please! Are you certain? Perhaps you misplaced it."

"Misplaced it? Edgar, you fool! I am never careless! I put it in its case on my dresser not two hours ago. Now the case is empty and the window is ajar! A thief has been here!"

The hotel manager arrived, followed shortly by a stout, slow moving man who introduced himself as Constable Dobbs. The constable seemed flustered by the chaos and by Mrs. Abernathy's dramatic accusations.

"Now, now, ma'am," he said, taking out a small notepad. "Let's just calm down and get the facts. You say a jewel was stolen?"

"Not 'a jewel,' you oaf!" she shrieked. "The Sea Serpent's Eye! It's priceless! I demand you search everyone in this hotel! Starting with him!" She pointed a trembling finger at her nephew.

"Me?" Edgar Pym squeaked, his face paling. "Aunt Elspeth, how could you?"

"You've always been jealous of my emerald!" she accused. "You're deep in debt, I know it! You probably saw your chance and took it!"

"That's a terrible thing to say!" the young man stammered, looking utterly miserable.

Penny watched the scene with keen interest. "I'll bet a cookie he didn't do it," she whispered to Louise. "He looks like he'd faint if you said 'boo' to him."

"He does look awfully distressed," Louise agreed. "But she's right about one thing. The window is open."

Constable Dobbs plodded into the room to begin his investigation. He peered at the empty jewel case, stared at the open window, and scribbled a few notes. "No sign of a forced entry on the door," he mumbled. "The thief must have come in through the window."

The window overlooked a narrow balcony which connected to a trellis covered in ivy. It would be a relatively easy climb for an agile person.

"I'll have my men search the grounds at first light," the constable announced. "In the meantime, I'd ask that no one leaves the hotel." He then proceeded to ask a series of questions that seemed to lead nowhere.

Penny, growing impatient, decided to conduct her own investigation. "Come on, Lou," she whispered. "Let's go take a look outside."

Using a back staircase to avoid the crowd in the lobby, the girls slipped out of the hotel. The moon was bright, illuminating the beach and the hotel grounds in a silvery light. They made their way to the side of the building directly beneath Mrs. Abernathy's suite.

"The constable is right," Penny observed, looking up at the ivy covered trellis. "Someone could have climbed up there easily. And down again."

She began to search the flowerbeds and bushes beneath the balcony, her eyes scanning the ground for any sign of a struggle, a footprint, or a dropped object. Louise joined her, carefully parting the leaves of a large hydrangea bush.

"There are some trampled flowers here," Louise noted. "Someone was definitely standing right here. But the ground is too hard for footprints."

"Keep looking," Penny urged. "The thief might have dropped something in his haste."

For ten minutes they searched in silence. Just as Penny was about to give up, her fingers brushed against something small and hard tucked beneath a rose bush. She picked it up and held it in the moonlight.

"What is it?" Louise asked, coming closer.

"It's a button," Penny said slowly. "A very strange one. It's made of carved wood, and it looks like a little fish."

"I've never seen a button like that before," Louise said. "Do you think the thief dropped it?"

"It's possible," Penny replied, tucking the button safely into the pocket of her robe. "It's our first real clue!"

Their search was interrupted by a gruff voice from the shadows of the beach. "What are you two young'uns doing, prowling around at this hour?"

The girls whirled around to see Jake Skagway leaning against the sea wall, a gnarled piece of driftwood in his hand.

"Oh! Mr. Skagway, you startled us," Penny said.

"We were just... getting some air," Louise added quickly.

The old man's eyes twinkled. "Getting air, or sniffing for trouble? Heard the squawking from the fancy lady up at the inn. Lost her shiny bauble, did she?"

"Yes," Penny said, deciding that honesty might be the best approach. "Her necklace was stolen. We were looking for clues. Did you happen to see anyone around here tonight?"

Jake Skagway squinted at the hotel. "Might've," he said noncommittally. "Saw a shadow, is all. Earlier on. Before the screaming started."

"A shadow?" Penny pressed, her excitement growing. "What did it look like? Was it a man?"

"Couldn't say," Jake grunted. "Was over by that trellis. Moved quick, like a sandpiper. Headed down toward the shore. That's all I saw." He gave a shrug and began to amble away down the beach. "Best you girls get back inside. Beach ain't no place for detecting in your night clothes."

Penny and Louise hurried back into the hotel, their minds racing.

"A shadow near the trellis!" Penny whispered as they climbed the stairs. "That proves it was an outside job! And this button must belong to the thief!"

"But who could it be?" Louise wondered. "It seems like everyone in Sunset Beach is either an old fisherman or a tourist."

Back in their room, Penny examined the button again. "It's so unusual. It looks handmade. Like something a fisherman or a sailor might wear."

"Jake Skagway is a sailor," Louise pointed out.

"But we saw him on the beach. He couldn't have been climbing the trellis at the same time," Penny reasoned. "Besides, why would he talk to us if he was the thief? No, it has to be someone else. Someone who moves 'quick as a sandpiper'."

The next morning, the hotel was buzzing with the news. Constable Dobbs had questioned the staff and guests, but had come up with nothing. The suspicion, fanned by Mrs. Abernathy's constant complaints, fell squarely on Edgar Pym. The poor man looked even more haggard than the night before, and kept to himself in a corner of the dining room.

"I still don't think he did it," Penny said, watching him. "He just doesn't seem the type. We need to find the owner of this button."

"How can we do that?" Louise asked. "We can't just go around asking everyone in town if they've lost a fish button."

"No," Penny agreed. "But maybe we can find out where it came from." Her eyes lit up. "I have an idea! There's that little shop by the docks, the one that sells nautical supplies and curios. The 'Salty Dog'. They might have seen a button like this before!"

After a quick breakfast, the girls set off for the waterfront. The Salty Dog was a cluttered, fascinating little shop that smelled of tar, rope, and saltwater. An old man with a captain's hat and a white beard sat behind the counter, mending a net.

"Good morning," Penny said politely. "We were wondering if you could help us."

She placed the wooden fish button on the counter. "Have you ever seen a button like this before?"

The old captain picked it up and examined it closely, his brow furrowed. "Well now, that's a fine piece of carving. Haven't seen one of these in a coon's age."

"You recognize it?" Penny asked eagerly.

"Aye. Not this one specific, mind you, but the style. There was a fellow used to come through here, a merchant seaman from up north. Name of Silas. He used to carve these little things in his spare time. Whales, fish, anchors. Made sets of 'em for his coat."

"Do you know where we can find this Silas?" Louise asked.

The captain shook his head. "Haven't seen him in over a year. He was a restless sort. Never stayed in one port for long. A lean fellow, quiet. Moved quick on his feet, I recall."

Penny's heart leaped. "Quick as a sandpiper?"

"That's a good way of puttin' it," the old man chuckled. "Aye. Quick as a sandpiper."

The girls thanked the captain and hurried out of the shop.

"It has to be him!" Penny declared. "This Silas fellow is our thief! He's a transient, so no one would recognize him. He's lean and quick, so he could easily climb the trellis!"

"But what would he be doing in Sunset Beach?" Louise questioned. "And why steal the Sea Serpent's Eye?"

"Maybe he heard about Mrs. Abernathy and her famous jewel," Penny speculated. "A man like that, a merchant seaman, probably hears all sorts of gossip in different ports. He came here specifically to steal it!"

Their theory, however, hit a dead end. No one at the hotel or in the town had seen a stranger matching the description of Silas. The trail had gone cold.

Dejected, the girls returned to the hotel. They found Mr. Parker on the veranda, talking with Constable Dobbs.

"No luck, I'm afraid," the constable was saying. "We've searched the grounds, the beach, everywhere. The necklace has vanished without a trace. And as for the nephew, Mr. Pym, he has an alibi, of a sort. He was down in the lounge, writing letters, until just before his aunt screamed. Several people saw him."

"So he's cleared!" Penny exclaimed.

"Officially, yes," the constable sighed. "Which leaves us with a thief who came and went like a ghost."

Penny's mind was whirling. The clues didn't add up. If Silas was the thief, where had he gone? And if Pym was innocent, who else had a motive?

Restlessly, she walked to the end of the veranda, her gaze falling on the trellis below Mrs. Abernathy's window. She replayed the events in her mind. The scream. The open window. The trampled flowers. The button. Jake Skagway's 'shadow'.

Something was nagging at her, a small, inconsistent detail.

"Lou," she said slowly. "Let's go back to Mrs. Abernathy's room. I want to look at that window again."

Finding the room empty, as Mrs. Abernathy was currently down in the lobby complaining to the manager again, the girls slipped inside. Penny went straight to the window. It slid up and down smoothly. She examined the sill, the frame, the lock. It was all perfectly ordinary.

"I don't see anything, Penny," Louise said.

"Wait," Penny murmured, her eyes narrowed. She ran her finger along the outside sill. It came away clean, except for a tiny, faint smudge. It wasn't dirt or grime. It was a faint smear of dark purple.

"Purple," Penny breathed. "The same color as Mrs. Abernathy's turban!"

Suddenly, everything clicked into place. It was like a puzzle box snapping open.

"Of course!" she cried, whirling to face Louise. "We've been looking at this all wrong! From the outside in, instead of the inside out!"

"What do you mean?" Louise asked in bewilderment.

"The thief didn't climb *up* the trellis to get in," Penny explained excitedly. "There was no thief! Don't you see? The shadow Jake saw wasn't climbing up. It was Mrs. Abernathy, climbing *down*!"

"Mrs. Abernathy? But why would she climb down a trellis in the middle of the night?"

"To look for something she had dropped!" Penny's voice was electric with discovery. "She wasn't robbed at all! She lost the necklace herself! She probably leaned out the window to look at the moon, and it slipped from her neck. The smudge on the sill is from her turban when she leaned out too far. The trampled flowers are from her searching the bushes. And the story about a thief? She made it all up because she was too proud and vain to admit she had been so careless with her precious 'heirloom'!"

"But what about the button?" Louise asked, the final piece of the puzzle.

"That's the one thing I can't figure out," Penny admitted. "It must be a coincidence. It must have been dropped there by someone else another time. But I'll bet a cookie the rest of my theory is right!"

"There's only one way to find out," Louise said, her eyes shining with the thrill of the chase.

They found Mrs. Abernathy back in her room, dabbing her eyes with a lace handkerchief.

"Is there any news, girls?" she asked in a weak voice.

"Mrs. Abernathy," Penny began gently, but firmly. "We don't think you were robbed. We think you lost the necklace."

The woman's head snapped up, her eyes flashing with anger. "How dare you! Of all the impudent...!"

"You were wearing your purple turban last night," Penny continued calmly. "And there's a purple smudge on your windowsill, right where you would have leaned out. You told everyone you never let the necklace out of your sight. Maybe you were holding it, admiring it by the moonlight, and it fell."

Mrs. Abernathy's face began to crumble. The anger was replaced by a look of sheer panic.

"You climbed down the trellis to look for it," Penny pressed on. "That's the shadow Jake Skagway saw. You searched the bushes, but you couldn't find it in the dark. So you invented the story about the thief to cover your own carelessness. You were even willing to let your own nephew take the blame."

A great, shuddering sob escaped Mrs. Abernathy. "It's true," she whispered, collapsing into a chair. "It's all true. I was looking at how the emerald caught the moonlight... and my fingers fumbled. It fell. I was so ashamed! So horrified! I didn't know what to do!"

"So there was no thief," Louise murmured.

"Then the necklace must still be out there!" Penny exclaimed.

The three of them rushed outside to the spot beneath the window. This time, knowing what they were looking for, their search was more thorough. It was Louise who finally spotted it.

"Penny, look!"

Not in the bushes, but higher up. The heavy emerald pendant had fallen, but the chain had caught on the branch of a sturdy rose bush, where it hung, hidden by the thick leaves, glinting in the afternoon sun.

With a cry of joy, Mrs. Abernathy retrieved her treasure. She turned to the girls, her face streaked with tears and running makeup.

"Oh, you clever, clever girls," she wept. "How can I ever thank you? And how can I ever apologize to poor Edgar?"
