Allie and Daniel pressed themselves flat against the cold, greasy metal of the machine, praying the shadows would be deep enough to conceal them. The beam was getting closer.

"I feel like I've just been shot out of a cannon," Penny confessed, managing a shaky smile.

Their successful run earned them an approving nod from Mrs. Downey when they returned to the top. It also seemed to establish their legitimacy. Even Jake gave them a grudging look of respect.

The time for the official run of the Fergus sled was announced. Maxwell himself would not be riding. He stood by the starting line, his arms crossed, watching as Jake and one of the other burly men climbed into the green sled.

Penny's mind raced. This was their only chance. She looked at the statue, perched on the front of the sled. It was held in place by a simple leather strap buckled around its base. A quick, strong tug might be enough to free it. But how could they get close enough?

"Mrs. Downey," Sara said suddenly, her voice clear and confident. "The Fergus team looks a bit heavy. For a trophy run, you want speed and grace. Why not let a lighter team pace them down the first half of the track? It would make for a better show for the spectators."

Mrs. Downey considered it. "That's an excellent idea, Sara! It would be very exciting! You and Penny can lead them down to Horseshoe Curve, then pull off onto the safety track."

Maxwell opened his mouth to protest, but he was too late. The small crowd of spectators murmured their approval of the dramatic suggestion. To object now would look churlish and unsporting. His face was a mask of fury, but he said nothing.

"Get ready," Sara whispered to Penny as they moved their blue sled into the starting position just ahead of the green one. "When we get to the straightaway before Horseshoe, I'm going to pull up alongside them. That's your chance, Penny. You'll only have a few seconds."

"What if I can't reach it?"

"You have to," Sara said grimly. "Just be ready to grab it. I'll handle the rest."

The starter raised his hand. Penny's heart hammered in her chest. She could feel the intensity of Maxwell's glare burning into her back. Behind her, she heard Jake mutter to his partner, "Just stay on their tail. Don't let them out of your sight."

The hand dropped. The starter shoved, and they were off.

CHAPTER 15

_THE JADE SKIER_

The second run was even faster than the first. The ice was harder now, and Sara, knowing the track, pushed their speed to its absolute limit. Penny felt the crushing force of the turns, but her fear was replaced by a sharp, focused determination. She kept her eyes locked on the green sled that was following no more than ten feet behind them. She could see the statue clearly, its smooth, polished surface reflecting the bright winter sky.

They roared through the first series of S-turns, the two sleds moving almost as one. Penny risked a glance over her shoulder. Jake’s face was a grim mask, his eyes narrowed, focused entirely on the race.

"Get ready!" Sara shouted, her voice nearly lost in the wind and the screech of the runners. "The straightaway is after this next turn!"

They exited the turn, and the track straightened out before them, a long, steep ramp leading toward the great curve. This was the moment.

With a subtle shift of her weight and a delicate touch on the steering ropes, Sara allowed the blue sled to drift slightly to the right. At the same time, she eased their speed just enough for the nose of the heavier green sled to draw level with Penny's seat.

It was now or never.

The jade skier was less than two feet away. Penny could see the buckle on the leather strap. Leaning as far out of the sled as she dared, her muscles screaming in protest, she reached across the gap. The frigid wind tore at her arm. Her fingers brushed against the cold, smooth stone.

"What are you doing?" Jake yelled, his eyes wide with disbelief and sudden alarm.

Penny ignored him. She fumbled with the icy metal buckle. It wouldn't give. Her fingers were numb, clumsy with cold. The end of the straightaway was rushing toward them.

"Hurry, Penny!" Sara cried, fighting to keep the sleds from colliding.

With a final, desperate surge of strength, Penny yanked not at the buckle, but at the statue itself. The leather strap was old. It stretched, groaned, and then snapped with a sharp crack. The jade skier was free!

She clutched it to her chest, the heavy statue shockingly cold against her coat. Jake let out a roar of fury. He reached for her, but Sara instantly veered their sled away. The green sled, now unbalanced and with its driver distracted, wobbled violently.

"Hold on!" Sara screamed.

She didn't steer for the safety track as planned. Instead, she drove the blue sled straight into the heart of Horseshoe Curve, pushing for every last ounce of speed. Behind them, chaos erupted. Jake, trying to regain control and cut them off, overcorrected. The green sled slid sideways, its runners losing their grip on the ice. It shot up the embankment, teetered for a horrifying second at the very top edge, and then flipped over, disappearing from view with a sickening crash of wood and metal.

Penny stared back in shock, clutching her prize.

"Don't look back!" Sara commanded, her voice tight with tension. "We're not clear yet!"

They flew down the remainder of the course, the blue sled a streak of lightning against the snow. Sara didn't brake until they were well past the finish line, skidding to a stop in a flurry of ice near the edge of the woods.

"Quick!" she urged, scrambling out of the sled. "Before anyone gets down here!"

Penny, her legs shaking, followed her. She shoved the heavy jade statue deep inside her ski jacket, the cold weight a solid reminder of their incredible gamble. They abandoned the bob-sled and ran for the cover of the trees, fumbling to strap on their skis.

In the distance, they could hear shouting from the top of the run. A few spectators who had been at the finish line were running toward the abandoned sled, their faces etched with confusion.

"Did you see that?" one of them yelled. "The green sled went over the curve!"

Penny and Sara exchanged a wide-eyed look. They had not intended to cause a crash.

"Are they..." Penny began, her voice trembling.

"Jake's tough," Sara said, though her own face was pale. "And he's a good sledder. He probably just has a few bruises. We can't worry about that now. We have to get away!"

They pushed off, skiing frantically, not back toward the Downey lodge, but deeper into the forest, taking a winding, little known trail that led away from the popular ski routes. They didn't speak, conserving their breath, their only sound the rhythmic hiss of their skis on the snow.

After nearly half an hour of hard skiing, Sara finally led them to a small, deserted hunter's cabin, half-buried in a snowdrift.

"We can rest here for a bit," she panted, leaning against the rough log wall. "No one will find us."

Inside, the cabin was cold and dusty, but it offered shelter. Penny collapsed onto a rickety wooden bench, her adrenaline finally giving way to bone-deep exhaustion. She carefully pulled the jade statue from her jacket and set it on the dusty table between them. In the dim light filtering through the single dirty window, it seemed to glow with a life of its own.

"We did it," Penny whispered in disbelief. "We actually did it."

"We did," Sara agreed, a slow smile spreading across her face. "You were magnificent, Penny! The way you snatched it! I've never seen anything so daring!"

The praise felt good, but Penny's mind was already on their next problem. "What now? We have the proof, but we're fugitives. Maxwell will have the whole mountain looking for us. And my father... if Maxwell's story about me being a 'snooping reporter' gets out, it will look terrible for the _Riverview Star_."

"We have to get the diamonds to the authorities," Sara said practically. "But not the local sheriff. You were right about that. He's probably in Maxwell's pocket."

"The state police, then," Penny decided. "Their nearest barracks is in Northwood, about thirty miles from here."

"Thirty miles!" Sara exclaimed. "We'll never make it on skis before they find us. And the roads will all be watched."

Penny looked at the statue. She remembered the hidden catch on the base. "Maybe we don't have to take the whole statue."

Working carefully, she found the seam and pressed the hidden release. The base sprang open. The tiny, perfect diamonds spilled out onto the rough wooden table, a river of light in the gloomy cabin. They glittered with a cold, pure fire, a fortune in stolen gems.

"So many," Sara breathed in awe.

"If we take just the diamonds," Penny thought aloud, "we can hide them easily. A small pouch would hold all of them."

She looked around the cabin. Her eyes fell on a small leather tool bag left behind by a long-gone hunter. It was perfect. She emptied the few rusty nails it contained and carefully swept the diamonds from the table into the pouch, pulling the drawstring tight. It was a small, heavy bundle that fit easily into the deep pocket of her jacket.

"Now what do we do with him?" Sara asked, nodding at the hollow jade skier.

"We leave him," Penny said. "Let Maxwell find it. Let him think his treasure is still safe inside. It will buy us time."

It was a risky move, but it was the best they had. They left the statue sitting in the center of the table, a silent green sentinel, and slipped back out of the cabin. They had a long and dangerous journey ahead of them, but as Penny felt the reassuring weight of the diamond pouch in her pocket, she felt a surge of confidence. Harvey Maxwell had underestimated her, and now he was going to pay the price. The real race had just begun.

The crisp air stung Penny's cheeks as they pushed away from the deserted cabin. The sun, a pale winter wafer, was already sinking toward the jagged western peaks, casting long, distorted blue shadows across the snow. They had to move, and move quickly.

"Which way?" Sara asked, her breath pluming in the cold. "The main trail is out of the question. Maxwell's men will be all over it."

"I know another way," Penny said, her mind racing. "It's an old logging trace. It's steeper and more difficult, but it will take us down the back of the mountain, away from the hotel and the ski runs."

They poled forward, their skis whispering over the fresh powder. The woods grew thick and silent around them, the heavy-laden branches of the pines absorbing all sound. Penny led the way, relying on a memory of a map she had studied back at the Downey lodge. For the first hour, they saw no one. The only sound was their own rhythmic breathing and the swish of their skis. Penny felt the small, heavy weight of the leather pouch in her pocket, a constant, thrilling reminder of what was at stake.

Just as a sliver of confidence began to warm her, she held up a hand, stopping Sara in her tracks. From the valley below, carried on the still air, came the faint sound of men's voices and the jingling of harness bells.

"Get down!" Penny whispered urgently.

They scrambled off the barely visible trail, plunging into a dense thicket of young fir trees. The snow-heavy branches showered them with icy powder, but provided excellent cover. Peeking through the needles, Penny saw a large sleigh drawn by two horses making its way along a wider path not fifty yards below them. Two men sat in the front, their collars turned up against the cold.

"He found the cabin, I tell you," one of the men grumbled, his voice clear in the cold. "Found the statue sittin' right on the table. But it was empty. The boss went madder than a hornet. He's offering a thousand dollars to the man who finds those girls."

"A thousand dollars," the other man whistled. "For that much, I'd search every inch of this mountain. Where do you suppose they went?"

"Who knows? But Maxwell wants every trail watched. He's got men posted at the train station and watching the main road out of Pine Top. He thinks they're still on the mountain somewhere. He said to check every cabin and shed."

The sleigh passed out of sight, its bells fading into the distance. Penny and Sara waited until the silence was complete before they moved.

"A thousand dollars," Sara breathed, her eyes wide. "Now everyone on this mountain will be a detective."

"It's good and bad," Penny reasoned, brushing snow from her jacket. "It's bad because everyone is looking for us. But it's good because they know the statue is empty. Maxwell won't feel safe until he has the diamonds back. It makes him desperate."

"And it means our plan worked," Sara added, a flicker of her usual bravado returning. "It bought us time."

"Time we have to use," Penny said firmly. They pushed on, their pace now more urgent than before.

The sun disappeared completely, and a vast, star-dusted canopy of black velvet unfolded above them. A bright three-quarter moon rose, bathing the landscape in an ethereal, blue-white light. It was beautiful, but it was also dangerously cold. The temperature had plummeted with the setting of the sun, and a biting wind began to whip through the trees.

For hours they skied in a grim, silent rhythm. The initial thrill of the escape had long since worn off, replaced by a deep, aching fatigue. Penny's legs felt like lead, and her lungs burned with every breath. She glanced back at Sara. The other girl, for all her strength and spirit, was beginning to falter. Her movements were less certain, and she stumbled twice in quick succession.

"We have to rest, Penny," Sara finally panted, leaning heavily on her ski poles. "I can't... I can't go on like this."

Penny knew she was right. Pushing on in their exhausted state would only lead to a mistake, a fall, a twisted ankle. And in this cold, an injury could be a death sentence. She scanned the moonlit terrain. Ahead, nestled in a fold of the mountainside, she saw the dark, angular shape of a roof.

"Look," she said, pointing. "An old lean-to. Maybe for loggers."

It took them another fifteen minutes of grueling effort to reach it. The structure was crude, just three walls and a sloping roof, but it offered a break from the relentless wind. Inside, the ground was covered with old pine needles and packed earth. With numb fingers, they gathered dry twigs and branches from the sheltered underside of fallen logs. Penny, remembering the waterproof matches she always carried in a small tin, managed to coax a tiny flame to life.

They huddled around the small, sputtering fire, shielding it with their bodies from the wind. It offered more comfort than actual warmth, but it was enough.

"I've never been so cold in my life," Sara shivered, her teeth chattering. "Or so hungry."

"Me neither," Penny admitted. They had no food, and no prospect of getting any. The thirty miles to Northwood now seemed an impossible distance.

"Are you scared, Penny?" Sara asked quietly, her usual buoyant tone gone.

Penny looked into the flickering flames. "Yes," she said honestly. "I'm terrified. Not just of getting caught. I'm worried about my father. If Maxwell's story gets out, that I'm just a snooping reporter using my father's paper, it could ruin his reputation." She touched the pocket with the diamonds. "That's why we can't fail. This isn't just a story anymore. It's about justice."

"We won't fail," Sara said, her voice regaining some of its strength. "You and me, Penny. We're a good team."

"The best," Penny smiled, feeling a renewed surge of determination.

They rested for two hours, dozing fitfully in the biting cold, before Penny knew they had to move again. Their muscles screamed in protest as they got to their feet and strapped on their skis. The rest of the night was a blur of silver moonlight, black trees, and the endless, punishing glide and push of their skis.

Dawn broke gray and cheerless, revealing a world blanketed in white. They had descended a great distance and the terrain was beginning to level out. They were weak with hunger and exhaustion, moving on pure nerve. Then, through a break in the trees, Penny saw it. A long, dark line cutting through the landscape.

"A road!" she cried.

It wasn't the main highway from Pine Top, but a smaller, secondary road, barely plowed. It was their best hope. They skied to the edge of the woods and took off their skis, hiding them under a snow-covered bush. They were too weak to ski any farther, and thirty miles was too far to walk. They would have to take their chances.

For almost an hour they stood by the side of the empty road, shivering. Despair was beginning to set in when they heard a distant rattling and grinding. An ancient, mud-spattered farm truck came chugging into view, a plume of exhaust trailing behind it.

The truck slowed as it approached them. The driver, a grizzled man with a face like a wrinkled apple and suspicious blue eyes, leaned out the window. "You girls in some kind of trouble?" he asked, his voice a low grumble.

"Our car broke down about a mile back, sir," Penny said quickly, thinking on her feet. "We were trying to walk to the next town."

The old farmer looked them up and down. "Dressed for skiing, not for walking. You ain't runaways, are you?"

"Oh, no sir," Sara said, trying to look as innocent as possible. "We live in Northwood. We were just visiting friends for the weekend."

The farmer grunted, chewing on his lip. "Northwood, eh? I'm headed to the feed co-op at the junction just before town. S'pose I can give you a lift. Can't leave you to freeze. Get in the back. And stay under the tarp, outta the wind."

Relief washed over Penny so powerfully she felt dizzy. "Thank you, sir. Thank you."

They scrambled into the cold, rattling truck bed and crawled under a stiff, foul-smelling canvas tarp. It was cramped and dark, and the truck bounced over the uneven road, but for the first time in nearly a day, they were not moving under their own power. Penny clutched the precious pouch in her pocket. Hidden in the darkness, lulled by the motion of the truck, she finally allowed her exhausted mind to drift.

It felt like only minutes later when the truck lurched to a halt. The tarp was pulled back, and the farmer's weathered face appeared. "This is as far as I go. Northwood's a mile down that way. You can walk it from here. And you'd best stay out of trouble."

"We will, sir. Thank you again," Penny said earnestly.

He just grunted and drove off, leaving them standing in the slush at the side of the road. They walked the final mile into Northwood on shaky legs. The town was a world away from the glamorous resort of Pine Top. It was a plain, workaday place of brick buildings, hardware stores, and diners. It felt real, and safe. Penny's eyes scanned the street signs until she saw the one she was looking for. A simple blue and white sign: STATE POLICE.

The barracks was a squat, functional brick building. Taking a deep breath to steady her nerves, Penny pushed open the heavy glass door and walked in, with Sara right behind her. The air inside was warm and smelled of coffee and disinfectant. A tall, broad-shouldered trooper with sergeant's stripes on his sleeve and a bored expression sat behind a high desk. He looked them over, from their disheveled hair to their muddy boots.

"Can I help you girls?" he asked, his tone suggesting he doubted it.

This was it. Penny summoned her last reserves of strength and stepped forward, her voice clear and steady despite the tremor of exhaustion running through her.

"Sergeant, my name is Penny Parker. This is Sara Jasko. We have information concerning a major jewel smuggling operation at the Fergus hotel in Pine Top." She paused, letting the words sink in. "And we have the evidence."

The sergeant's expression did not change. He looked from Penny to Sara, his gaze lingering on their worn, anxious faces. "Is that so?" he said slowly, a note of deep skepticism in his voice. "A jewel smuggling operation. And what evidence would two young ladies like yourselves have?"

For an answer, Penny reached into her jacket pocket. Her fingers closed around the familiar shape of the small leather pouch. With a steady hand, she pulled it out, untied the drawstring, and upended it on the polished wooden surface of the desk.

A river of tiny, perfect diamonds cascaded out, scattering across the counter. They glittered with a brilliant, cold fire under the harsh fluorescent lights of the barracks. The room fell silent. Another trooper, who had been typing at a nearby desk, stopped and stared. The sergeant's jaw dropped, his bored expression replaced by one of utter astonishment. He reached out a hesitant finger and touched one of the stones, as if to confirm it was real.

"Good heavens," he whispered.

A wave of triumph surged through Penny. They had done it. They were safe.

At that exact moment, the telephone on the sergeant's desk let out a shrill, piercing ring, shattering the quiet. The sergeant, still staring at the diamonds, answered it reflexively.

"Sergeant Miller... Yes... Who is this?... The Sheriff's office in Pine Top? Yes, go ahead... A what? An All-Points Bulletin... Yes, I see... Two runaway girls? What are their names?... Penny Parker and Sara Jasko... Yes, I understand. Warrants for breaking and entering and grand larceny. You say they're to be considered armed and dangerous?"

Sergeant Miller slowly, deliberately, placed the receiver back in its cradle. The astonishment in his eyes was gone, replaced by a look as hard and cold as the diamonds glittering between them. He fixed his gaze on Penny and Sara, who stood frozen in horror.

"You girls," he said, his voice dangerously quiet, "are going to have a lot of explaining to do."

For a long moment, no one moved. The diamonds on the desk seemed to mock them, their brilliant light a cold counterpoint to the sudden chill in the room. Penny's heart, which had been soaring with triumph only a minute before, plummeted into a black abyss of despair.

"Sergeant," she began, her voice a strained whisper. "Sergeant, there has been a terrible mistake."

"The only mistake was me listening to your little story," Sergeant Miller said, his voice flat and hard. He gestured to the other trooper. "Connors, get on your feet."

The second trooper, a younger man with wide, startled eyes, rose from his chair.

"Armed and dangerous?" Sara shrieked, finding her voice in a torrent of outrage. "They're the dangerous ones! That whole town is crooked! You have to believe us!"

"That's enough," Miller snapped. He walked around the high desk, his movements deliberate and menacing. He was no longer a bored public servant; he was the law, and they were on the wrong side of it. "Turn around. Place your hands on the wall."

Penny's mind raced. To be treated like a common criminal! After all they had been through, after the risks they had taken. A hot surge of indignation fought against the cold tide of fear.

"We won't," she said, her chin held high. "We haven't done anything wrong. You have the evidence right there on your desk."

"I have a pile of shiny rocks and an All-Points Bulletin for two escaped felons from a neighboring county," the sergeant corrected her grimly. "Now, I'm not going to ask you again."

Seeing the look in his eyes, Penny knew it was useless to argue further. Arguing would only make them look more guilty. Defeated, she turned and placed her palms against the cool, painted bricks of the wall. Beside her, Sara did the same, her shoulders shaking with silent sobs.

"This is insane," Sara muttered. "It's a nightmare."

Penny felt a woman's touch, surprisingly gentle, as a matron who had appeared from a back office patted down her ski jacket. The woman removed the green ticket from Penny's pocket and placed it on the desk next to the glittering pile of diamonds. She then did the same for Sara, emptying her pockets of a crumpled handkerchief and a tube of lip rouge.

"What do you want to do with them, Sergeant?" the matron asked.

Sergeant Miller scooped the diamonds back into the leather pouch with a scrape of his hand. He looked at the pouch, then at the green ticket, then at the two girls standing against the wall. A flicker of something, perhaps confusion, crossed his stern features. The story did not quite add up. Two young girls, breaking and entering, grand larceny, armed and dangerous. And yet they had walked straight into a police barracks to turn themselves, and a fortune in jewels, in.

"Put them in Interview Room B," he said at last. "And get the Pine Top Sheriff's office back on the line. I want to confirm this warrant personally with Sheriff Brody."

The matron led them down a short hallway to a small, bare room containing only a metal table and three chairs. The window was high on the wall and covered with a thick wire mesh. The door clicked shut behind them with an air of finality.

Sara immediately sank into one of the chairs and buried her face in her hands. "It's over, Penny. We're going to prison. Grandfather was right. I should never have left the loft."

Penny paced the small room, her mind working furiously. "No, it isn't over, Sara. Don't you see? This is just another one of their tricks. Maxwell is smarter than I thought. He didn't just run. He used his power in Pine Top to discredit us before we could talk."

"Well, it worked!" Sara cried, looking up, her face streaked with tears. "That sergeant thinks we're criminals! They all do!"

"He's confused, that's what he is," Penny insisted, stopping her pacing to look at her friend. "Think about it, Sara. If we were thieves who had just stolen a fortune in diamonds, would we walk into a police station and dump them on the desk? It doesn't make any sense. Maxwell and the Sheriff are counting on the official warrant being enough to make everyone stop thinking."

"But what can we do?" Sara asked miserably. "We're locked in here. They'll be here from Pine Top any minute to take us back there. And once Maxwell has us back in Pine Top, what do you think will happen to us?"

A shiver went down Penny's spine. Sara was right. Being taken back to Pine Top was the worst possible outcome. They would be completely at the mercy of the very men they were trying to expose.

"We need to get a message out," Penny said, thinking aloud. "We need someone on the outside who will believe us. Someone with enough power to make Sergeant Miller listen."

She walked to the door and knocked sharply. After a moment, a small peephole slid open and the matron's eye appeared.

"What is it?"

"I'd like to make a telephone call," Penny said, keeping her voice as calm and steady as she could. "I believe I am entitled to one call."

The eye disappeared and the peephole slid shut. They waited in silence for what felt like an eternity. Penny could hear Sara's ragged breathing in the quiet room.

Finally, the door opened. It was Sergeant Miller. His face was unreadable.

"Sheriff Brody is on his way over from Pine Top," he announced. "He wants to transport you himself."

Penny's heart sank. "Sergeant, you can't let him. If we go back to Pine Top..."

"I'm a State Trooper," Miller interrupted. "I follow procedure. The warrant is valid. Sheriff Brody has jurisdiction." He paused, his eyes lingering on Penny. "You said you wanted a phone call. You have five minutes."

He led Penny back to the main desk, leaving Sara in the interview room with the matron. The younger trooper, Connors, was typing up a report, but Penny could feel his curious eyes on her. The pouch of diamonds sat on the corner of the desk like a paperweight, a silent testament to the strange morning.

Sergeant Miller gestured to the telephone. "Local or long distance?"

"Long distance," Penny said. "To Riverview. I'll pay for the charges."

A new flicker of interest showed in the sergeant's eyes. Riverview was a good two hours away. He nodded his assent.

Penny's fingers trembled slightly as she gave the number to the operator. She willed her voice to be steady. This was their only chance.

"Riverview Star," a crisp voice answered.

"Anthony Parker, please," Penny said. "It's urgent. Tell him it's his daughter."

There was a series of clicks, and then a familiar, welcome voice came on the line. "Penny? Is that you? What's wrong?"

"Dad!" Relief washed over her, so potent it nearly brought tears to her eyes. "Oh, Dad, I'm in so much trouble."

"Where are you? Your mother's been worried sick. You said you'd call yesterday."

"I'm at the State Police barracks just outside of Clear Lake," Penny said, speaking quickly and trying to cram in all the important details. "Dad, they're trying to arrest me. Me and a friend, Sara Jasko."

"Arrest you? For what?" Her father's voice sharpened with alarm and professional interest.

"For breaking and entering and grand larceny at the Fergus hotel. Dad, it's a frame-up. The hotel manager, Harvey Maxwell, he's running a massive jewel smuggling ring. We found the diamonds, thousands of them. We brought them here to the police, but Maxwell and the local Pine Top Sheriff, a man named Brody, they put out a warrant for us. They called us armed and dangerous!"

She heard a sharp intake of breath on the other end of the line. "Harvey Maxwell," her father repeated. "I know that name. He has a reputation. And you say he's in league with the local sheriff?"

"Yes! And the sheriff is on his way here now to take us back to Pine Top. Dad, you can't let them. I don't know what will happen to us if he gets his hands on us." She glanced at Sergeant Miller, who was listening to every word with a stony expression. "We have the proof, but they won't believe us over an official warrant."

"All right, Penny. Stay calm," her father's voice was now a reassuring balm of command. "Don't say another word to anyone. Not a single word, do you understand? I'm going to make some calls. First to a man I know in the governor's office, and then to the best criminal lawyer in the state, a Mr. Abernathy. He's expensive, but he's a bulldog. He's about an hour's drive from you. I'll have him on his way in ten minutes."

"A lawyer?" Penny faltered.

"Yes, a lawyer. You're in a legal jam, Penny, and you need legal protection. This is beyond amateur detective work now. Now listen to me. What is the name of the officer in charge there?"

Penny looked at the sergeant. "It's Sergeant Miller."

"Okay. Put Sergeant Miller on the phone."

Penny hesitated. "Dad, I don't know if he will..."

"Just ask him, Penny. Tell him Anthony Parker of the Riverview Star wishes to speak with him."

Swallowing hard, Penny held the receiver out to the sergeant. "My father would like to speak with you. He's Anthony Parker, from the Riverview Star."

Sergeant Miller's eyebrows raised a fraction. He had heard Penny's side of the conversation, the references to the governor's office and a top lawyer. He stared at the phone for a second, then took it from her hand.

"Sergeant Miller here," he said into the receiver. He listened for a full minute, his expression unchanging, his replies curt. "Yes, sir... I see... Yes, they're here... Yes, the evidence is secured... I understand, sir... It is highly irregular... Yes, Sheriff Brody is en route... I see... Yes, sir, I will."

He hung up the phone and stood for a moment in thoughtful silence. He looked at Penny, and for the first time, she saw not a hostile officer, but a man wrestling with a difficult problem. The simple, black and white situation of two runaway thieves had just become complicated by newspapers, lawyers, and the governor's office.

"Go back to the interview room," he said, his tone no longer harsh, merely professional.

Penny did as she was told, her heart aflutter with renewed hope. When she entered the room, Sara looked up at her anxiously.

"What happened? Who did you call?"

"My father," Penny said, allowing herself a small, triumphant smile. "And he is sending in the cavalry."

They did not have to wait long. Less than twenty minutes later, they heard the sound of a car skidding to a stop on the gravel outside. The front door of the barracks opened with a bang.

A loud, blustering voice echoed down the hall. "Miller! I'm here for your prisoners! Where are the little thieves?"

Penny and Sara exchanged a look of terror. It was Sheriff Brody.

"They're being processed, Sheriff," came Sergeant Miller's calm reply.

"Processed? Don't give me that state police nonsense! They're my fugitives. I have a warrant. Just hand them over and I'll be on my way. I want to get them back to my jail before dark."

"I'm afraid I can't do that, Sheriff," Miller said, his voice firm.

There was a pause. "What do you mean you can't do that? Are you obstructing my investigation, Sergeant?" Brody's voice had a dangerous edge.

"I mean there have been new developments," Miller replied coolly. "I have just been on the telephone with Mr. Anthony Parker, editor of the Riverview Star. He informs me that a Mr. Abernathy, a legal counsel, is on his way here to represent the young ladies. He also informed me that he has been in touch with the state attorney general's office."
