Inside, the truck was a nest of glowing dials and humming equipment. A young officer wearing headphones sat before the main console.

"Anything yet, Lieutenant?" Jerry asked as they climbed inside.

"Not yet, sir," the officer replied. "But the cutter has visual contact. They're keeping a distance of one nautical mile. The target is heading northeast, hugging the coastline."

They waited in the tense, humming silence of the truck. The only sounds were the crackle of the radio and the distant moan of the foghorn. Penny felt as if her nerves were stretched as tight as a violin string.

Suddenly, the officer held up a hand. "I'm getting something, sir," he said, his voice sharp. "A weak carrier wave. He's warming up." He began to turn a large dial, and a faint, rhythmic beeping filled the truck. "It's him. B4. Broadcasting in code."

"Location?" Jerry demanded.

The officer spun the rotating loop antenna on the roof. Another soldier made calculations on a chart. "Bearing three-zero degrees, sir. It's coming right from the target's position! The cutter confirms."

"That's it," Jerry said, his voice grim. He picked up a microphone. "Cutter One, this is Watchdog. We have positive confirmation. The broadcast is originating from your target. Proceed and apprehend. I repeat, proceed and apprehend."

"Roger, Watchdog. Proceeding to apprehend," a calm voice crackled back from the speaker.

For the girls, the next ten minutes were an agony of suspense. They could only listen to the disembodied voices on the radio and picture the drama unfolding out in the fog-shrouded sea.

"Watchdog, we are closing on the target. He is not responding to our signal to heave to."

A pause.

"He's making a run for it! He's faster than he looks."

"Can you intercept?" Jerry asked, his knuckles white as he gripped the microphone.

"We're cutting him off. He's heading for the shallow water around Skully's Reef. He knows these waters too well."

"Don't let him get away!"

Another voice, younger and more excited, cut in from the cutter. "He's turning back toward us! He's not giving up!"

Then, the first voice again, calm and final. "Target has been boarded. Vessel is secure. We have one suspect in custody."

A collective sigh of relief went through the monitoring truck. Jerry sagged back against the wall. "Good work, Cutter One. Bring him in." He turned to the girls, a tired smile on his face. "Well, Penny. Your cookie is safe. You were right."

An hour later, they were all standing on the now brightly lit town pier as the Coast Guard cutter nudged its way to the dock. A handcuffed and defeated-looking Jake Skagway was escorted onto the pier by two guardsmen. He looked smaller and older without his blustering anger. He shot the girls a look of pure venom before he was led away to a waiting car.

Jerry came over to them, having just finished speaking with the cutter's captain.

"You girls were absolutely right," he said. "The radio equipment was brilliant. It was hidden in a false-bottomed fish hold, completely soundproofed. The antenna was disguised as one of the fishing masts. He would motor out into the fog, broadcast his messages for the Enemy, and be back in his bunk before dawn. No one was ever the wiser."

"But why was it called the Voice from the Cave?" Louise asked.

"Propaganda and misdirection," Jerry explained. "They wanted us to think it was a stationary, hidden base somewhere in the hills or the coastal caves. They never wanted us to consider a moving target. It was Jake's own idea, apparently. The old rascal was cleverer than any of us gave him credit for."

"Was he a spy?" Penny asked.

"Not in the traditional sense," Jerry replied. "He had no ideology. He was being paid a great deal of money to relay coded messages from escaped prisoners to the submarines sent to pick them up. He was a traitor for profit."

Jerry drove them back to the hotel through the thinning fog. The sky in the east was beginning to turn a pale gray.

"You two gave me quite a scare tonight," he said as he pulled up to the curb. "But I have to admit, we couldn't have closed this case without you. Your father is going to be very proud, Penny." He fixed her with a serious look. "Just try to have a normal, boring vacation for the rest of your stay. All right?"

"I'll do my best," Penny promised, though a twinkle in her eye suggested her best might not be very boring at all.

The next morning, the sun shone brightly on a clean, quiet Sunset Beach. The whole affair with Jake Skagway and the secret broadcasts already felt like a dream. After breakfast, the girls went down to their usual spot on the sand.

"Well," Louise said with a contented sigh, stretching out on her towel. "I'm glad that's finally over. No more spies, no more secret packages, no more midnight chases in the fog."

"Me too," Penny agreed, though she was already scanning the horizon with a practiced eye. "It will be nice to just relax."

She fell silent for a moment, her gaze fixed on a small, rocky island about a mile offshore. It was a barren place, known to the locals as Gull Island, home to nothing but seabirds. But as she watched, she saw a brief, brilliant flash of light from the highest point of the island, as if someone were reflecting the sun with a mirror. It flashed once, twice, and then a third time in a clear, distinct pattern.

"That's funny," she murmured.

Louise propped herself up on her elbows, a look of weary resignation on her face. "Oh, Penny. What now?"

"Did you see that?" Penny asked, pointing. "A flash of light from Gull Island. Like a signal."

"It was probably just the sun reflecting off a wet rock, or a piece of glass," Louise said, lying back down. "Please, let it be a piece of glass."

"Maybe," Penny said slowly. "But it flashed three times. That seems very deliberate. And Dad said that island used to be a strategic lookout point during the first war. He said there are old fortifications and tunnels up there."

She stood up, her eyes narrowed in thought, the familiar triumphant gleam beginning to dawn.

"Louise," she declared. "I'll bet a cookie that wasn't a piece of glass. I'll bet it's a signal. And I think we'd better find out who is sending it, and why!"

"Louise," she declared. "I'll bet a cookie that wasn't a piece of glass. I'll bet it's a signal. And I think we'd better find out who is sending it, and why!"

Louise groaned and rolled over, putting a forearm across her eyes to block out both the sun and Penny's enthusiastic expression. "Not again, Penny. We just finished with spies and traitors. My nerves need a vacation, even if yours don't."

"But this is important!" Penny insisted, her voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. "Three flashes. That's a code. It could be anything! Maybe it's a new spy, taking Jake Skagway's place!"

"Or maybe it's a very enthusiastic bird-watcher with a shiny pair of binoculars," Louise countered, her voice muffled. "Or a child playing a game. Or a reflection. Honestly, there are a dozen perfectly normal explanations."

"Normal explanations are never any fun," Penny said decisively. "And they're usually wrong. We have to go over there."

"Go over there?" Louise sat up abruptly, her look of resignation turning to one of alarm. "Penny Parker, that's an island! It's surrounded by water! We can't just walk over there and ask a few questions."

"Of course not. We'll need a boat," Penny stated, as if it were the simplest thing in the world. "There must be someone in town who rents them out. Come on!"

Before Louise could protest further, Penny had gathered her towel and was striding purposefully toward the boardwalk, leaving her friend to scramble after her.

Their inquiries led them to a rickety pier at the far end of the beach, where a number of small, weather-beaten fishing boats bobbed in the gentle swell. A grizzled old man with a face as leathery as his boots sat on an overturned crate, mending a net. He looked up from under the brim of his stained cap as the girls approached.

"Looking for something?" he grunted, his voice raspy, like shells grinding underfoot.

"We'd like to rent a boat," Penny said brightly. "We want to go out to Gull Island."

The man stopped his work and squinted at them, his pale blue eyes seeming to take their measure. "Gull Island? Ain't nothin' out there but birds and busted-up rock. And bad luck, some say."

"We're interested in the birds," Louise supplied quickly, giving Penny a warning glance. "And the, uh, rock formations. For a school project."

The old fisherman let out a short, skeptical laugh. "School project, eh? In the middle of summer? You ain't the first ones to get a strange notion about that island. Best to leave it be."

"Why do you say that?" Penny pressed, her curiosity piqued. "What kind of strange notions?"

"Folks see things," he muttered, returning to his net. "Lights. Shadows. Say the old tunnels are haunted by soldiers who never left. Don't believe in ghosts myself, but I do believe in mindin' my own business. And that island ain't good business."

"We'll pay you well," Penny offered, sensing a crack in his resolve. "We just want to be dropped off for an hour or two. Then you can come back for us."

The man, who introduced himself as Silas, chewed on this for a moment. He looked from Penny's eager face to Louise's apprehensive one, then out toward the island, and finally at the ten-dollar bill Penny was hopefully holding out.

"The tide'll be turnin' in three hours," he said at last, slowly taking the money. "I'll take you. But I leave you for two hours, and not a minute more. If you ain't at the landin' spot when I get back, you're stayin' the night with the gulls and the ghosts. Understand?"

"We understand perfectly!" Penny beamed. "When can we leave?"

"Get in," Silas grunted, nodding toward a small boat with a sputtering outboard motor. "Name's The Salty Dog. She's seen worse than a couple of feather-headed girls lookin' for trouble."

The ride over was choppy. The Salty Dog slapped against the waves, sending up plumes of cold spray that dampened the girls' clothes and spirits, or at least Louise's. Penny, however, seemed to thrive on it, her eyes fixed on the rapidly approaching island. It loomed larger and larger, a jagged spine of dark rock rising from the sea, its surface streaked white with guano.

"You mentioned tunnels," Penny shouted over the roar of the motor. "What kind of tunnels?"

"Gun emplacements," Silas yelled back. "From the big war. They say you can get from one side of the island to the other without ever seein' daylight. Dangerous place. Full of pits and sudden drops. You watch your step."

True to his word, Silas deposited them on a narrow strip of pebbled beach and, with a final warning to be on time, turned his boat around and headed back toward the mainland. The sound of his motor faded, leaving the girls in an unnerving silence, broken only by the cry of gulls and the wash of waves against the shore.

"Well, we're here," Louise said, hugging her arms. "Now what, Sherlock?"

"Now we find the highest point," Penny replied, already scrambling up a steep, rocky path. "That's where the signal came from."

The climb was more difficult than it looked. The path was overgrown with tough, thorny bushes, and loose stones shifted under their feet. After several minutes of breathless scrambling, they reached a sort of plateau near the island's peak. Here, the ground was flatter, and they could see the crumbling remains of a concrete structure.

"This must be one of the old fortifications," Penny whispered, her eyes wide with excitement. She peered into a dark, square opening that led into the hillside. "And that must be a tunnel entrance!"

"Let's not go in there," Louise pleaded. "It's probably full of spiders. And ghosts."

"We're not looking for ghosts. We're looking for clues," Penny reminded her. She began to circle the concrete bunker, her gaze sweeping the ground. "Someone was here, and recently. The signal proves it."

Her foot suddenly struck something metallic. She bent down and brushed away the dirt. It was not a mirror or a piece of glass, but a small, flat tin of imported mints. The label was in a language neither of them recognized.

"Look at this!" Penny exclaimed, holding it up. "You don't buy these at the corner store in Sunset Beach. Our signaler has expensive taste."

"Or they're a tourist," Louise pointed out reasonably.

"Maybe. But look here." Penny pointed to a set of footprints in a patch of soft earth near the bunker's entrance. They were faint, but clearly visible. "These are from a man's shoe. A nice shoe, not a hiking boot or a sandal. And they're fresh. The edges are still sharp."

A sudden sound from within the dark tunnel made both girls freeze. It was a soft scraping noise, followed by a cough.

"Someone's in there!" Louise hissed, grabbing Penny's arm. "Let's go! Silas will be back soon."

But Penny was already moving, not toward the beach, but toward the tunnel entrance. "We have to see who it is! Quiet!"

She crept to the edge of the opening and peered into the gloom. At first, she could see nothing. As her eyes adjusted, she made out a faint, flickering light deep within the passage. It seemed to be coming from a lantern. Drawn by an irresistible curiosity, Penny took a cautious step inside, ignoring Louise's frantic, silent gestures to come back.

The air in the tunnel was cool and damp, and smelled of earth and salt. The scraping sound came again, closer this time. Penny flattened herself against the rough wall as a figure carrying a small oil lantern emerged from a side passage.

Penny gasped. It was Mrs. Deline.

She was dressed not in her usual pristine white suit, but in a practical set of dark trousers and a heavy sweater. Her hair was tucked under a scarf. She did not look like a woman enjoying a seaside vacation. She looked like someone at work. In her free hand, she carried a small hammer and what looked like a chisel.

She seemed to be concentrating on the tunnel wall, occasionally tapping it with her hammer and listening intently. She hadn't seen them.

Penny ducked back out of the entrance, her heart pounding. "It's Mrs. Deline!" she whispered to Louise.

"Mrs. Deline? Here?" Louise's eyes were wide with disbelief. "What on earth is she doing?"

"I don't know, but it isn't bird-watching," Penny breathed. "She has tools. A hammer and a chisel. She was tapping on the walls!"

Before they could speculate further, Mrs. Deline emerged from the tunnel into the daylight. She blinked, momentarily blinded by the sun, and then she saw them. For a second, her face registered pure, unadulterated shock, just as it had on the beach. Then, the cold, impenetrable mask slammed back into place.

"Well, well," she said, her voice dripping with ice. "The Parker detective agency, I presume. Your curiosity seems to have no bounds."

"We were just exploring," Penny said, trying to sound casual. Her eyes darted to the tools in the woman's hand.

"Indeed," Mrs. Deline replied, not bothering to hide the hammer. "As am I. I am a geologist, in my spare time. This island has some fascinating stratified rock formations. The basalt intrusions are particularly noteworthy." She gestured vaguely at the tunnel. "One must get into the earth to truly appreciate it."

"The flash of light we saw from the beach," Penny challenged. "Was that part of your geology work?"

A flicker of something, perhaps annoyance, crossed Mrs. Deline's face. "My lantern, no doubt. The reflector caught the sun as I came out of the tunnel. It's a wonder you don't investigate the gulls for sending coded messages with their squawks." She gave them a thin, dismissive smile. "Now if you'll excuse me, my boat is waiting. I do hope you have a reliable way back to the mainland. The currents here are treacherous."

Without another word, she strode down a different, less obvious path that led to a small, hidden cove on the other side of the island. The girls watched her go, then looked at each other.

"A geologist?" Louise said doubtfully. "Do you believe her?"

"Not for a second!" Penny declared. "Geologists use special rock hammers, not ordinary chisels. And why hide her boat in a secret cove? And she didn't explain this!" Penny held up the tin of foreign mints. "I'll bet that belongs to her, or someone she's meeting!"

A low toot from the direction of the sea announced Silas's return.

"We have to go," Louise urged. "Come on, Penny. We've seen enough for one day."

Reluctantly, Penny followed her back down to the beach. The whole way back to shore, she was silent, her brow furrowed in concentration. She didn't believe Mrs. Deline's story about geology. Tapping on the walls, using a chisel, the secret cove, the foreign mints, it all added up to something mysterious. Something that had nothing to do with rocks.

Back at the Crystal Inn, the girls changed out of their damp clothes. Penny paced the room restlessly.

"Her story was too smooth, too practiced," Penny mused, thinking aloud. "It's just like the fish bones. A convenient explanation designed to make us look foolish and stop asking questions."

"She did succeed in making us look foolish," Louise sighed, brushing her hair. "She always does. Maybe we should just leave her alone, Penny. She's obviously a very private person."

"Private people don't go poking around in military tunnels on deserted islands!" Penny retorted. "She was looking for something. Or hiding something. That's why she was tapping the walls. Looking for a hollow spot."

"Hiding what? More fish bones?"

"No! Something valuable. Something that tin of mints is connected to," Penny insisted. "We have to tell Jerry Livingston. He'll know what to do."

Finding Jerry proved difficult. He was not at the hotel, and a call to the local Coast Guard station yielded no information as to his whereabouts. Disappointed, the girls decided to sit in the lobby and wait, hoping he might return.

As they sat pretending to read magazines, the hotel's front door opened and a man entered. He was tall and impeccably dressed in a gray suit that seemed out of place among the casually dressed vacationers. He had dark, slicked back hair and a thin mustache. He scanned the lobby with a sharp, impatient air.

Penny watched him, a prickle of instinct telling her he was not an ordinary tourist. The man walked to the front desk, spoke briefly with the clerk, and then turned. His eyes swept the lobby again and came to rest on Mrs. Deline, who had just emerged from the dining room.

A look of understanding passed between them. It was almost imperceptible, a mere flicker of acknowledgement, but Penny saw it. Mrs. Deline gave a slight, stiff nod and walked toward the veranda. The man in the gray suit waited a moment, then followed her outside.

"Did you see that?" Penny whispered excitedly to Louise.

"See what? That man is probably just another hotel guest."

"No, Lou! They knew each other! That was a signal. He's her contact!"

Forgetting all pretense of casual waiting, Penny crept toward the large windows that looked out onto the veranda. She crouched behind a large potted fern, pulling a reluctant Louise down with her.

Through the leaves, they could see Mrs. Deline and the man in the gray suit standing near the railing, their backs to the hotel. They were speaking in low tones, too low for the girls to hear the words. But their posture was tense and serious. After a minute, the man discreetly passed a small, thick envelope to Mrs. Deline. She slipped it into her handbag without looking at it. The man then gave a curt nod, turned, and walked briskly away down the beach. Mrs. Deline remained at the railing for another moment before returning inside, her face as cold and unreadable as ever.

"An envelope!" Penny breathed, her eyes shining. "She's being paid! Lou, this is bigger than I thought. She's not just hiding something. She's part of a whole operation!"

"Oh, dear," was all Louise could say.

Just then, Jerry Livingston came striding into the lobby, looking tired but cheerful. "Penny! Louise! I was just coming to find you. Heard you girls went on a little boating excursion today."

Penny jumped to her feet. "Jerry! We have to talk to you! It's about Mrs. Deline!"

Leading him to a quiet corner of the lobby, Penny recounted the entire day's events in a torrent of words: the signal from Gull Island, the gruff fisherman, the tunnels, the discovery of Mrs. Deline with her hammer and chisel, her flimsy geology story, the foreign tin of mints, and finally, the secret meeting on the veranda with the man in the gray suit.

Jerry listened patiently, his expression growing more serious as she spoke. He took the tin of mints she offered him and examined it.

"This is interesting, Penny," he said slowly. "Very interesting. You say she was tapping the walls?"

"Yes! As if she were looking for a hollow place. And Jerry, the signal from the island wasn't a reflection from her lantern. She was lying. It was too clear, too deliberate. Three flashes. It was a signal to him!" Penny pointed vaguely in the direction the man in the suit had gone. "To let him know she'd found the spot, or that it was safe to meet."

Jerry rubbed his chin thoughtfully. "Your theory about Jake Skagway was right. And your suspicions about Mrs. Deline and the package on the beach led us right to the monitoring truck. I'm not going to dismiss this." He stood up. "The fact that she met this man right after coming from the island is significant. This might not be a matter of national security, but it's certainly something."

"What do you think she's doing?" Louise asked.

"I have a theory," Jerry said. "During the war, a lot of wealthy families in Europe tried to get their assets out of the country before they were seized. Art, jewels, gold. Much of it was smuggled into neutral countries. Some of it even made its way here, brought by people fleeing the conflict. They hid it, waiting for the day they could safely recover it."

"You think Mrs. Deline is looking for hidden treasure?" Penny asked, her voice filled with awe.

"It's possible," Jerry confirmed. "The 'gun emplacements' would be a perfect hiding place. A man-made cave, secret and secure. Perhaps she has a map, or instructions passed down to her. The man in the suit could be an agent for a collector, or the family of the original owner. The envelope you saw might have been her payment."

"But if it's her property, why be so secretive?" Louise wondered.

"It might not be legally hers to claim," Jerry explained. "Or she might be trying to avoid paying taxes or duties on it. Or maybe," he added, a harder edge to his voice, "it was stolen in the first place. A lot of art and valuables were looted during the war."

He looked at his watch. "It's getting late. Whatever she's doing, she might try to move the items tonight, under the cover of darkness. Penny, your work for today is done. And it was excellent work. Now I want you and Louise to stay here in the hotel where it's safe."

"Oh, but Jerry!" Penny protested. "We want to see what happens!"

Jerry fixed her with a firm look. "No arguments. This could get dangerous. These aren't spies, but people dealing in high-value illicit goods can be just as ruthless. Promise me you'll stay put."

"All right," Penny agreed, though her expression was one of deep disappointment. "We promise."

After Jerry left, the girls went up to their room. For a while, they sat in silence. The moon rose over the sea, casting a silver path on the water.

"I guess that's that," Louise said with a yawn. "Another mystery solved. You were right about Mrs. Deline all along. She's not a spy, but she is a crook."

Penny did not answer. She was standing at the window, staring out at the dark silhouette of Gull Island. Her promise to Jerry echoed in her ears, but a stronger feeling, the pull of an unfinished adventure, was tugging at her.

"Penny," Louise said, a note of warning in her voice. "We promised Jerry."

"We promised we'd stay in the hotel," Penny replied slowly, a mischievous gleam in her eye. "Jerry didn't say we had to go to sleep. And our window has a perfect view of the beach, and the path to the pier where Silas keeps his boat."

Louise sighed. "I knew it. You're not going to listen, are you?"

"We'll just watch," Penny assured her. "From a safe distance. I just want to see if we're right. If she makes a move tonight, we should be there to see it!"

Louise let out a long, theatrical sigh, the kind she reserved for Penny's most audacious plans. "We will do no such thing. We are going to sit right here, like two perfectly normal girls on vacation, and we are going to go to sleep. Jerry is a federal agent, Penny. We can't just ignore a direct order from him."

"It wasn't an order, it was a suggestion," Penny countered, her nose pressed so close to the window pane that her breath formed a small circle of fog. "And he said stay in the hotel. We are in the hotel. Watching from a window is perfectly within the rules."

"Watching is one thing. What you're planning is another," Louise retorted, though she got up from her bed and came to stand beside Penny. The moon was high and bright now, painting the sand dunes in shades of silver and black. The sea whispered onto the shore with a steady, rhythmic hush.

For a long while, nothing happened. The beach was deserted, the hotel silent. Even the restless energy that always seemed to surround Penny began to wane.

"All right, you win," Louise said finally, stifling a yawn. "There's nothing to see. Mrs. Deline is probably sound asleep, dreaming of fish bones and how she fooled two silly girls."

"Shh! Look!" Penny's voice was a sharp, excited whisper. She pointed a finger toward the side entrance of the hotel, the one that led out near the tennis courts and the path to the beach.

A figure had just emerged from the shadows. It was a woman, and even from this distance, the severe, elegant way she held herself was unmistakable. It was Mrs. Deline. She was dressed in dark slacks and a dark sweater, a stark contrast to her usual immaculate white suits. She carried a small canvas bag.

"See! I told you!" Penny breathed, her eyes wide. "She's going back to the dunes!"

As if to prove Penny's point, Mrs. Deline did not head toward the main beach but cut across the lawn, keeping to the shadows of the hotel before disappearing onto the winding path that led toward the drift fence and the dunes where she had buried the package.

"Well, that's that," Louise said, a note of finality in her voice. "She went to the dunes. We saw her. Now we can tell Jerry in the morning. Mystery solved. Bedtime."

"Bedtime? Are you serious?" Penny turned away from the window, her face alight with excitement. "Louise, this is it! This is the moment! She's not burying fish bones tonight. She's going to dig something up!"

"And we are going to stay here and let her," Louise said firmly, crossing her arms. "We promised."

"We promised to stay in the hotel," Penny repeated patiently. "The veranda is part of the hotel, isn't it? We could get a much closer look from there. We wouldn't even have to set foot on the sand."

Louise eyed her friend suspiciously. "Just the veranda?"

"Just the veranda," Penny affirmed, though her fingers were already drumming impatiently on the windowsill. "Scout's honor."

Reluctantly, Louise agreed. They slipped out of their room, their sock-footed feet making no sound on the thick hallway carpet. They took the back stairs, which were dark and smelled faintly of floor wax and old wood. The door to the veranda was unlocked. They crept outside into the cool, salty air.

The wide porch was deserted. The wicker chairs sat like sleeping ghosts in the moonlight. From here, they had a clear, unobstructed view of the beach and the distant, shadowy forms of the dunes. They could just make out a dark figure moving near the spot they had been digging earlier.

"There she is," Penny whispered, ducking behind a large potted fern. Louise crouched beside her. "But wait. She's not alone."

Another figure had joined Mrs. Deline. This one was a man, tall and broad shouldered, wearing a dark suit and hat that made him look like a cutout against the pale sand. It was the man from the pier, the one who had taken the envelope. He was carrying two shovels.

"It is him!" Louise gasped. "You were right, Penny. This is a pre-arranged meeting."

"Of course I was right," Penny said, distracted. "They're not digging where we were. They're heading farther down, toward Gull Island."

The two figures walked with purpose along the shoreline, their shapes shrinking as they moved away from the hotel. They passed the last of the dunes and continued toward the old wooden pier where the local fishermen, including old Silas, kept their boats.

"They're going to the island," Penny stated, her mind racing. "The gun emplacements. Jerry said it was the perfect hiding place." She stood up, peering intently into the darkness. "They're getting into a boat."

Louise pulled on her sleeve. "Penny, get down! What if they see us?"

"They're too far away," Penny dismissed. "They're untying that little motorboat. Not Silas's rowboat. See? They're heading out."

They watched as a small boat puttered away from the pier, its engine a faint, buzzing noise that was quickly swallowed by the sound of the waves. It cut a straight path across the moonlit water toward the dark, humped shape of Gull Island.

For a full minute, neither girl spoke. They were alone again on the veranda, the evidence of their mystery chugging away into the night.

"Well," Louise said, trying to sound relieved. "I guess that really is that. They're gone. We'll tell Jerry everything in the morning. He'll take a Coast Guard cutter out there and catch them red-handed."

Penny turned to her, a strange, determined light in her eyes that Louise knew all too well. "In the morning? Lou, in the morning the treasure will be gone! They'll have loaded it onto their boat and disappeared forever. We have to do something now!"

"Do something? What can we do? We can't swim to Gull Island!"

"No," Penny said slowly, a grin spreading across her face. "But we can row."

"Row? In what?" Louise asked, then her eyes widened in horror as she understood. "Oh, no. No, Penny. Absolutely not. We are not 'borrowing' Silas's boat. That's stealing!"

"It's not stealing, it's commandeering for the sake of justice," Penny corrected her grandly. "We'll bring it right back. Silas will never even know it was gone. It's our civic duty, Louise!"

"Our civic duty is to not get arrested for boat theft or drowned in the Atlantic Ocean!" Louise hissed, her voice a frantic whisper.

"Don't be so dramatic. I'm an excellent rower. We'll be perfectly safe. Think of it, Louise! The story we'll have to tell! We can't just let them get away with it." Penny's voice was pleading, her enthusiasm infectious. "We saw them. We know what they're doing. If we don't act, who will?"

Louise wrestled with her conscience, her fear, and her unshakeable loyalty to her best friend. As always, loyalty and the undeniable thrill of the chase won out. "Oh, all right," she groaned. "But if we get caught, I'm telling everyone it was your idea."

"Deal!" Penny said, already moving. "Now, let's get our shoes. And we'd better grab our sweaters. It's cold on the water."

A few minutes later, two shadowy figures slipped out of the hotel and scurried across the sand toward the pier. The salty spray misted their faces as they crept along the damp, creaking planks. Silas's small, sturdy rowboat, the 'Sea Urchin', was tied securely to a pylon. It bobbed gently in the water, its oars neatly stowed under the seats.

"This feels wrong," Louise whispered as Penny expertly undid the ropes.

"This feels like an adventure," Penny whispered back.

Getting into the boat without falling into the dark, churning water was the first challenge. Penny went first, holding the rope tight, and then helped a trembling Louise aboard. The little boat rocked precariously.

"Sit still," Penny commanded, fitting the oars into the oarlocks. With a soft splash, they were away from the pier.

Penny rowed with long, steady strokes, her back straining with the effort. The lights of the Crystal Inn grew smaller and smaller behind them. Ahead, Gull Island loomed, a black and forbidding silhouette against the starry sky. The only sounds were the rhythmic creak of the oarlocks, the splash of the oars, and the pounding of Louise's heart.

"What if they have guns, Penny?" Louise whispered from the stern, her hands clutching the sides of the boat.

"People looking for old family treasure don't usually carry guns," Penny answered, though she didn't sound quite as certain as she usually did. "They're probably just as scared as we are."

"I doubt that very much," Louise muttered.

They reached the island's small, sheltered cove twenty minutes later. Penny guided the boat toward a rocky part of the beach, away from the sandy spit where the motorboat was pulled ashore. They dragged the heavy rowboat up onto the stones, hiding it as best they could behind a large, gnarled piece of driftwood.

The island was eerily quiet. A narrow path, worn into the ground by generations of fishermen and curious tourists, led up from the beach into a thicket of stunted, wind-blown pines. From somewhere above them, they could hear the faint chink of metal against rock.

"They're up there," Penny breathed, pointing toward the highest point of the island. "Come on. And be quiet!"

They followed the path, moving from shadow to shadow, their hearts in their throats. The air was thick with the smell of pine needles and damp earth. The path grew steeper, and soon they were climbing over large, flat rocks that formed the island's core. They came to a clearing near the summit. Ahead of them was a dark, man-made opening in the hillside, almost completely hidden by overgrown bushes. An old gun emplacement.

Faint light flickered from within. Crouching low, Penny and Louise crept closer until they were hidden behind a dense tangle of wild rose bushes, not twenty feet from the entrance. Peering through the leaves, they had a perfect view of the scene inside.

The space was a small, brick-lined cavern. A battery-powered lantern cast long, dancing shadows on the damp walls. In the center of the cavern, Mrs. Deline and the man in the suit were hard at work. They had cleared away a section of the dirt floor and were prying the lid off a large, metal-banded wooden chest. The man grunted with effort, and with a final, screeching groan of rusted hinges, the lid fell back.

Mrs. Deline knelt, holding the lantern over the opening. Even from their hiding place, the girls could see the glint of gold and the sparkle of jewels. The chest was filled with tarnished silver goblets, ornate candlesticks, velvet bags that were surely full of gems, and strands of pearls as thick as Penny's thumb.

"It's real," Louise mouthed, her eyes as round as saucers. "It's real treasure."

Penny could only nod, completely mesmerized.

Mrs. Deline let out a sound that was half laugh, half sob. "It's all here, Anton. Just as he said. After all these years."

"Your grandfather was a clever man, Baroness," the man, Anton, replied. His voice was deep, with a thick European accent. "To get it out of the country and hide it so well."

"He was a survivor," she corrected him softly, running a hand over a string of pearls. "Now, let's not linger. We must transfer it to the bags and be gone before dawn."

They began to work quickly, carefully lifting the precious objects from the chest and wrapping them in pieces of cloth before placing them in two large duffel bags. Penny and Louise watched, frozen in place, a mixture of awe and guilt warring within them. They were witnessing something incredible, something intensely private.

Suddenly, a new sound broke the night's stillness. It was the crunch of a footstep on the gravel path just outside the cavern.

Anton and Mrs. Deline froze, their heads snapping up. Anton silently reached inside his coat.

"Who's there?" Mrs. Deline called out, her voice sharp and cold once more.

A stooped figure shuffled into the lantern light. He wore a stained fisherman's cap and a sly, gap-toothed grin. It was Jake Skagway.

"Evenin', ma'am," he said, his eyes darting from their faces to the open, glittering chest. "Figured you weren't out here for the night air. That's a mighty pretty collection you got there."

"What do you want?" Anton snarled, stepping in front of Mrs. Deline.

"Now, now, no need to be hasty," Skagway said, holding up his hands. "I just think that since this treasure was found on my stomping grounds, so to speak, it's only right I get a finder's fee. A big one."

"This is private property. It does not concern you," Mrs. Deline said, her voice dangerously low.

"Looks like it concerns me now," Skagway chuckled, taking a step closer. "I could make a lot of noise. Let the Coast Guard know there's some funny business on Gull Island. Or, you could just share a bit of your good fortune. Say, half?"

Anton let out a short, guttural laugh. "You will leave now, or you will regret it."

"I don't think so," Skagway said, and he lunged, not at Anton, but at the chest, his grimy hands grabbing for a silver goblet.

In a flash, Penny knew she had to do something. They were trapped. If a fight broke out, someone would get hurt. And if Jake Skagway won, he would surely find them hiding. Thinking fast, she looked around in the dark. Her hand found a loose, round stone, about the size of an apple.

"What are you doing?" Louise whispered in terror.

"Creating a diversion," Penny whispered back. Taking careful aim at the rocky cliff face on the far side of the clearing, away from everyone, she hurled the stone with all her might.

The stone clattered loudly against the rocks, the sound echoing in the small clearing like a gunshot.

"What was that?" Skagway yelled, dropping the goblet and spinning around.

"Someone else is here!" Anton said, his eyes scanning the darkness.

The distraction worked perfectly. In that moment of confusion, Anton moved. He was surprisingly fast for a man his size. He didn't attack Skagway, but instead grabbed one of the heavy duffel bags. "Helga, now!" he commanded.

Mrs. Deline, her face a mask of fury and fear, snatched up the other bag. "This is not over, you old thief!" she spat at Skagway.

They scrambled out of the gun emplacement and half-ran, half-stumbled down the path toward their boat, leaving the open chest, still a quarter full of treasure, behind them.

Skagway, recovering from his surprise, hesitated for a second. He looked from the fleeing couple to the treasure chest and back again. Greed won. With a triumphant whoop, he dove back into the cavern and began stuffing his pockets with jewels and small golden objects.

"Let's go!" Penny urged, pulling a stunned Louise to her feet. "While he's busy!"

They scurried from their hiding place and ran for their own boat, their feet slipping on the rocky path. They didn't look back. They scrambled into the Sea Urchin, pushed off from the shore, and Penny began to row with a frantic energy she didn't know she possessed.

They were halfway back to the hotel when the engine of the motorboat roared to life. It sped away from Gull Island, not back toward Sunset Beach, but out into the open sea, a dark shape disappearing into the vast, dark expanse of the ocean.

Penny rested on her oars, her breath coming in ragged gasps. The adrenaline was fading, replaced by a deep, bone-weary exhaustion.

"Did you see that?" Louise said, her voice barely a whisper. "They're headed out to sea. Maybe to meet a larger boat."

"Maybe," Penny agreed. She started rowing again, more slowly this time. The lights of the hotel were a welcome beacon.

They secured the rowboat at the pier, their hands shaking so much it took them two tries to tie the knot. They crept back to the hotel, up the stairs, and collapsed into their room, locking the door behind them.

For a long time they just sat on the floor, catching their breath. The adventure was over. The excitement was gone.

"She called her 'Baroness'," Louise said quietly into the darkness. "Mrs. Deline is a Baroness."

"And Anton is her bodyguard, I suppose," Penny added. "And the treasure was her family's. Hidden from the war."

It all made a strange kind of sense. She stood and walked to the window. The moon was beginning to set. In the east, the first faint hint of dawn was coloring the sky. Jake Skagway was still on the island, and a Baroness was fleeing into the Atlantic with a portion of her fortune.

"We have to tell Jerry," Louise said. "Everything."

"We will," Penny agreed. She was staring down at her hand. In the frantic rush to leave the island, she had been holding onto the rock she had thrown. Her other hand, which she'd used to steady herself while hiding, was grimy. And clutched in her palm, stuck to the dirt, was something small and hard.

She walked over to the desk lamp and switched it on. In the pool of light, she opened her hand. It was a small, flat piece of dark wood, intricately carved on one side. It looked like it had broken off something larger.

"What's that?" Louise asked, coming over to look.

"I don't know," Penny said, turning it over in her fingers. "I must have picked it up near the bushes. I think... I think it's a piece of the chest."

She looked closer. The carving was a small bird, a seagull, with a letter 'V' clutched in its beak. It was distinctive. It was a clue.

A slow smile spread across Penny's face, chasing away the exhaustion. "Lou," she said, her voice filled with renewed excitement. "I don't think this mystery is over. I think it's just beginning."

A BROKEN CLUE

She inserted the thin piece of wire into the keyhole and began to jiggle it patiently. For a long minute, nothing happened. Then, with a faint click, the lock gave way.

Penny grinned at Louise and pushed the door open. They were in a large, unfinished attic that smelled of old wood and cedar. Rafters crisscrossed above their heads, and the entire space was cluttered with discarded furniture, trunks, and boxes. But in the far corner, near a dormer window that looked out over the sea, was a sight that made both girls stop dead.

On a simple wooden table sat a compact but powerful-looking radio transmitter. A tangle of wires connected it to a large battery on the floor. A set of headphones and a microphone lay on the table beside a leather-bound logbook. And running from the back of the set, a thick black cable snaked up to the rafters and out a small hole drilled in the roof.

"We found it," Penny breathed, her voice filled with awe. "Mr. Voice from the Cave."

She stepped forward and carefully opened the logbook. The pages were filled with columns of numbers and letters, a code she couldn't hope to understand. But there were also dates and times, and the most recent entry was for tonight.

Suddenly, a floorboard creaked on the stairs below them.

Louise's eyes went wide with terror. "Someone's coming!" she mouthed silently.

Frantically, they looked for a place to hide. An enormous, old-fashioned wardrobe stood against one wall, its door slightly ajar. Without a second's hesitation, Penny pulled Louise toward it. They squeezed inside, pulling the door shut until only a tiny crack remained, and plunged themselves into the suffocating, mothball-scented darkness.

Heavy footsteps ascended the attic stairs. The door creaked open. A dark silhouette was framed for a moment against the hallway light before the man stepped inside, closing the door behind him. He was a tall, thin man with sharp features and cold, calculating eyes. He was not a person they had ever seen before in Sunset Beach.

He moved to the table without hesitation, his movements economical and precise. He flipped a switch on the transmitter, and a low hum filled the attic. Donning the headphones, he picked up the microphone. Peering through the crack in the wardrobe door, the girls watched, frozen with fear.

The man began to speak, his voice low and guttural. He was not speaking English.

"Penny, it's German," Louise whispered, her breath hot against Penny's ear.

Penny, who had taken two years of German in high school, strained to understand. The man's accent was thick, and he spoke quickly in clipped, military phrases. But she caught a few words, words that sent a chill down her spine.

"...rendezvous at the old pier... Mitternacht... das Paket ist sicher..."

Midnight. The pier. The package is safe.

Penny's mind raced. Another package! Not the fish bones Mrs. Deline had buried, but a real one! This was the information Jerry needed! In her excitement, she leaned forward, pressing her eye closer to the crack. Her elbow bumped the inside of the wardrobe door.

It let out a small but horribly loud creak.

In the corner, the man stopped speaking instantly. His head snapped up. His eyes, like chips of ice, scanned the room, finally coming to rest on the old wardrobe. He had heard them.

Slowly, deliberately, he set the microphone down on the table. He rose from his chair, his movements silent and menacing. The girls huddled together in the darkness of the wardrobe, hardly daring to breathe. Penny could feel Louise trembling beside her.

The man took one step toward them. Then another. The floorboards groaned under his weight. He was coming right for them. He reached the wardrobe and his hand closed around the knob.

With a sudden, violent motion, he tore the door open.

For a heartbeat, the spy simply stared at the two girls crammed inside, his face a mask of disbelief that quickly hardened into cold fury.

"Out!" he snarled, grabbing Penny's arm.

Penny reacted instinctively. She stomped down hard on his foot. The man grunted in pain, his grip loosening for a fraction of a second. It was all she needed.

"Run, Lou!" she shrieked.

She shoved past him, and Louise scrambled out behind her. They darted for the door, but the spy recovered quickly. He lunged, his fingers catching the sleeve of Louise's blouse.

Without thinking, Penny snatched a heavy porcelain pitcher from a nearby washstand and hurled it. It shattered against the wall just inches from the man's head, showering him with ceramic fragments. The surprise gave them the precious seconds they needed. Louise tore free, and they scrambled out of the attic, slamming the door behind them.

They didn't wait to see if it would hold him. They flew down the main staircase, their feet barely touching the steps. They could hear the attic door burst open and the man's heavy footsteps pounding after them.

Through the grand foyer they fled, their breath coming in ragged gasps. They burst through the swinging door into the dining room, knocking over one of the sheet-draped chairs. Into the kitchen, past the swinging cellar door, and out onto the sagging back porch they ran, not daring to look back.

They leaped off the porch and tore across the overgrown lawn toward the gap in the stone wall, the spy's angry shouts echoing behind them. He was faster than they were; they could hear him closing the distance.

Just as they reached the edge of the property, two brilliant beams of light swept across the lawn. A dark sedan screeched to a halt on the trail, its doors flying open.

"Freeze! Federal agents!" a familiar voice boomed.

It was Jerry Livingston. He and two other men, all holding pistols, leaped from the car.

The spy skidded to a stop, his face a picture of desperation. He spun around, looking for an escape, but another officer was already circling behind him. He was trapped. With a curse, he raised his hands in surrender.

Jerry rushed over to the girls, who had collapsed against the stone wall, gasping for air.

"Penny! Louise! Are you alright?" he demanded, his face a mixture of anger and profound relief. "What in the name of all that is holy are you two doing here?"

"The... radio," Penny panted, pointing back at the dark house. "In the attic. We found him. We found Mr. Voice from the Cave."

Jerry stared from the girls to the captured spy, then back to the gloomy mansion. A look of grudging admiration dawned on his face. "I don't believe it," he said, shaking his head. "You girls are an absolute menace. You actually did it."

Later that evening, in a private parlor at the Crystal Inn, the final pieces of the puzzle fell into place. Jerry, Penny, and Louise sat around a table. With them was a fourth person: Mrs. Deline. But the cold, distant woman they knew was gone. In her place was a calm, sharp-eyed woman who smiled at them warmly.

"The moment B4 started his broadcast, our monitoring truck got a fix," Jerry explained. "The directional lines crossed directly over the old Von Holst property. We were on our way within minutes. We had no idea we'd find you two in the middle of it all."

He turned to Mrs. Deline. "I believe you have some explaining to do as well, Agent Miller."

Penny and Louise stared. Agent Miller?

Mrs. Deline chuckled. "My real name is Susan Miller," she said. "I'm with a different branch of government intelligence. My assignment was to keep an eye on Sunset Beach. We knew a spy was operating in this area, but we didn't know from where. My cold personality was a cover, to discourage anyone from getting too close or asking too many questions."

"Then the fish bones...?" Penny began.

"Were real, I assure you," Mrs. Miller said with a laugh. "My friend at the lighthouse really did ask me to dispose of them. But it also served as a wonderful excuse to be on that stretch of beach and to throw you two very clever but very meddlesome girls off the trail. You were getting dangerously close to interfering with a federal investigation."

"And when you pretended to write on your skirt?" Louise asked.

"A bluff. I had spotted one of B4's contacts watching from the dunes. I wanted him to think I was leaving a message, to see if I could draw him out. Unfortunately, he was too cautious."

Jerry took over the explanation. "The man you girls encountered, the one we know as B4, was indeed using the house to send coded messages. He was coordinating the escapes of German prisoners from Canada, just as we suspected. The 'package' he spoke of tonight was a set of forged identity papers for an escaped officer who is hiding in the woods nearby. Thanks to you, we'll be able to intercept that delivery at the pier and apprehend him as well."

"And he chose the Von Holst house because of the family connection," Penny concluded, putting the last piece in place. "The seagull carving was the clue all along."

"Precisely," Jerry confirmed. "He's a distant cousin of the original Baron and knew the estate was abandoned. It was the perfect, isolated headquarters."

He looked at Penny, a stern expression on his face that couldn't quite hide his admiration. "You are reckless, impulsive, and you disobey every instruction I give you, Penny Parker. You put yourself and Louise in terrible danger tonight." He paused, then allowed a small smile. "But I have to admit, you get results. You solved a case that had a dozen trained agents stumped for weeks."

Louise let out a long, shaky sigh. "Well, I'm just glad it's over. I've had quite enough of spies and secret radios to last me a lifetime."

Penny grinned, her eyes sparkling with leftover excitement. "Oh, I wouldn't be so sure about that, Lou," she said. "You never can tell what new mystery is waiting for us right around the corner!"

"Yes," Jerry confirmed. "They slip across the border and make their way to coastal areas, hoping to be picked up by submarines or neutral ships. A broadcast station like this one would be vital for coordinating such a pickup."

Penny's mind was racing. Spies, escaped prisoners, a secret radio! It was all more exciting than she could have imagined. Her thoughts flew at once to the strange widow at the hotel.

"Jerry, this changes everything!" she exclaimed. "Mrs. Deline! I'll bet a dozen cookies she's involved!"

Jerry gave her a weary but tolerant smile. "Penny, we've been over this. I've had Mrs. Deline checked out. She's exactly what she appears to be. A wealthy widow on a long vacation."

"But it all fits!" Penny insisted, her voice earnest. "She's always watching the sea with that spyglass. And she hid that package on the beach! Maybe it wasn't fish bones at all. Maybe it was a message for an escaped prisoner who was supposed to retrieve it!"

"And maybe she was telling the truth," Louise put in reasonably. "You have to admit, she certainly made us look foolish."

"That was just a trick to make us stop looking!" Penny retorted. "She's clever, I'll give her that. Cold and clever. Just the sort of person who could be a master spy!"

"I don't think master spies go around burying fish bones for lighthouse keepers," Jerry said dryly. "And they certainly don't sit on the hotel veranda where everyone can see them. Secrecy is the name of the game, Penny. B4 has been very good at it. Mrs. Deline is anything but secretive."

"That's her cover!" Penny declared, undeterred. "She acts obvious so no one will suspect she's being secretive. It's the perfect double bluff!"

One of the radio operators in the truck chuckled softly. Jerry shot him a warning glance before turning back to the girls.

"Look, your imagination is a wonderful thing, Penny, but we're dealing with facts here. The fact is, an enemy agent is broadcasting from somewhere near Sunset Beach. We have to wait for him to transmit again so we can get a fix on his location. That's all we can do."

"What if he never transmits again?" Louise asked, her brow furrowed with worry.

"He will," Jerry said with confidence. "He has to. These operations run on a schedule. He's overdue for a broadcast now. The bad weather may have delayed him, but he'll be on the air soon. And when he is, we'll be ready."

He gestured to the complex array of dials and wires that filled the truck. "This equipment is the best there is. If B4 so much as whispers into his microphone, we'll hear him."

Penny peered at the silent machinery. It all seemed terribly passive. Waiting was not her strong suit. "But while we're waiting, he could be helping more prisoners escape! We should be out there looking for him!"

"And where would you suggest we look?" Jerry asked, a hint of amusement in his voice. "We've combed nearly every abandoned shack and empty building for miles. There are hundreds of little coves and inlets along this coast. He could be anywhere."

"What about a cave?" Penny suggested suddenly.

The question seemed to hang in the air. Jerry and Louise both looked at her.

"A cave?" Jerry repeated slowly. "Why a cave?"

"The name you called him," Penny explained, her eyes bright with the new idea. "You said he was 'Mr. Voice from the Cave'. Was that just a nickname, or did you mean it?"

Jerry exchanged a look with the officer beside him. "It's just a code name the department gave him," he said, though he seemed a bit thoughtful. "There's no specific intelligence to suggest he's actually in a cave."

"But it makes sense!" Penny pressed on. "A cave would be the perfect hiding place. It's isolated. It would muffle the sound of a generator for the radio. And with this jagged coastline, there must be dozens of them!"

"She has a point, sir," the young officer commented quietly to Jerry. "The sound would be difficult to trace, and it wouldn't be a structure we could spot from the air."

"I've explored some of those caves with Dad," Penny added. "There's one in particular, not too far from here, down past the old pier. They call it Smuggler's Cave. It goes way back into the cliff."

"Penny, we can't just go running off to explore a cave based on a hunch," Jerry said firmly. "Our orders are to stay with the monitoring unit. It's our only sure way of finding him."

"But you could send someone to look, couldn't you?" Louise asked. "It seems like a good idea."

Jerry shook his head. "I don't have the manpower to spare for a wild goose chase. Besides, it's getting dark. Exploring caves at night is a good way to get hurt." He looked pointedly at Penny. "I'm taking you girls back to the hotel. And I want you to promise me you'll stay there."

Penny's face fell. "But Jerry..."

"No buts," he said, his tone leaving no room for argument. "This is a serious federal investigation, not one of your high school adventures. People could get hurt. I need you both to stay out of it. Promise me."

"All right," Penny mumbled, staring at her shoes.

"Louise?"

"I promise, Jerry," Louise said sincerely.

"Good." Jerry glanced at his watch. "The taxi should be back by now. Let's go."

The ride back to the Crystal Inn was a quiet one. Louise was content to obey Jerry's orders, relieved to be away from the mysterious Army truck and the talk of spies. But Penny was silent for a different reason. Her mind was a whirlwind of activity. A cave. It had to be a cave. And if the Army wasn't going to look, then someone else would have to.

When the taxi dropped them at the hotel entrance, Jerry saw them to the door. "Remember what you promised," he said, his eyes fixed on Penny.

"I'll remember," Penny said, managing a weak smile.

As soon as Jerry's taxi had disappeared down the drive, Penny grabbed Louise's arm. "Come on!"

"Come on where?" Louise asked in alarm. "Penny, you promised!"

"I promised we'd stay out of it," Penny corrected her. "I didn't say exactly when. A quick look around isn't getting into it. It's just preliminary research."

"That sounds an awful lot like breaking a promise to me," Louise said, digging her heels in. "Besides, it's almost dinnertime. And it's dark!"

"We won't go all the way to the cave now," Penny said impatiently. "But I want to talk to someone. Someone who knows more about this coastline than anyone."

"Who?"

"Old Jake Skagway."

Louise groaned. "Oh, Penny, not him! You thought he was a rascal this morning, and now you want his help?"

"He may be a rascal, but he's lived here his whole life," Penny reasoned. "He rents out the rowboats down by the pier. He'll know every cave and inlet on this beach. He might have seen something."

Reluctantly, Louise allowed herself to be pulled along. The rain had stopped completely, but the sky was a blanket of dark, heavy clouds. The air was thick with the smell of salt and wet sand. They found Jake Skagway by the boat rental shack, hauling a small dory further up the beach, away from the evening tide. He was a wiry man with a face as weathered as an old piece of driftwood and pale blue eyes that missed nothing.

"Evenin', girls," he grunted, not pausing in his work. "Bit of a squall we had."

"We were just wondering about something, Mr. Skagway," Penny began, adopting her most charming manner.

"Folks call me Jake," he corrected her, straightening up and wiping his hands on his trousers. "What's on your mind? Don't tell me you want to rent a boat. Tide's comin' in and the sea's in a foul mood."

"No, not tonight," Penny said. "We were talking about the caves along the shore. The ones past the pier."
