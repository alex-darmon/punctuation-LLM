"That's our assumption. The voice is always the same. He broadcasts on a very tight, powerful beam for short periods of time. He's clever. He knows we're listening."

"Exactly," Jerry confirmed. "We have another truck stationed about twenty miles up the coast. If B4 broadcasts, both trucks will get a bearing on the signal. Where the two lines cross on a map, that's where he'll be."

"The Voice from the Cave," Penny murmured, the name sending a shiver down her spine. "That's a spooky name."

"It's what the men here call him. The first few broadcasts we picked up had a slight echo, as if they were coming from a large, empty space. Like a cave."

"There are dozens of caves along this coastline!" Louise exclaimed. "He could be anywhere!"

"That's the problem," Jerry said grimly. "And until he goes on the air again, we're just waiting."

The girls looked around the truck, at the serious faces of the young soldiers hunched over their silent equipment. The spy game, which had seemed like an exciting adventure on the beach, now felt far more serious and dangerous. This was not about fish bones in the sand. It was about the war.

Their tour of the secret station concluded, Jerry escorted them back to the waiting taxi. The driver, who had been reading a newspaper, looked up without much interest.

"Take us back to the Crystal Inn," Jerry instructed.

As the taxi rattled back toward town, Penny was unusually quiet. Her mind was a whirl of conflicting ideas. Mrs. Deline, the package, the coast watchers, and now a secret radio station and the mysterious B4. Could it all be connected?

"A penny for your thoughts," Louise said softly, nudging her friend.

"I was just thinking, Lou. Mrs. Deline said she came from the lighthouse, from visiting Jim McCoy."

"Yes, to bury his fish bones," Louise chuckled.

"But the lighthouse is north of the hotel," Penny reasoned, staring out the window. "She walked south, quite a long way, to bury that package. She went in the opposite direction of the hotel."

"Maybe she just likes long walks on the beach."

"Maybe," Penny conceded. "But what if the package wasn't fish bones at all? What if she was telling us that story to stop us from digging? She knew we were watching her through the spyglass."

"You mean her spyglass," Louise corrected. "She was watching us."

"It's the same thing!" Penny insisted impatiently. "What if she buried a message for someone else to pick up? Someone connected to B4?"

"Oh, Penny, now you're linking her to the Voice from the Cave? That seems like a real stretch."

"Is it? Jerry said they suspect people connected to the station are helping prisoners escape. That takes organization. It takes spies on the ground, passing information. People who blend in, like a quiet widow staying at a seaside hotel."

Before Louise could argue further, the taxi pulled up in front of the Crystal Inn. The rain had stopped completely, leaving the air fresh and clean. As they paid the driver, Penny's sharp eyes caught a movement on the veranda. It was Mrs. Deline, who had just stood up from her rocking chair. She held a small, dark handbag and was walking toward the hotel entrance with a sense of purpose.

"She's going somewhere," Penny whispered, grabbing Louise's arm. "Let's see where."

They lingered by the hotel steps, pretending to discuss the weather, until Mrs. Deline had passed through the lobby and out the front door. She did not hail a taxi, but instead turned right and began walking briskly down the main street of Sunset Beach.

"Come on!" Penny urged, and the two girls set off in pursuit, keeping a safe distance behind their quarry.

Mrs. Deline did not seem to be heading for the shops or the tea room. She walked past the main commercial block and turned onto a quieter residential street lined with old sea captains' houses. Her pace was steady and she never once looked back.

"Where could she be going?" Louise panted, trying to keep up.

"I don't know, but I intend to find out!"

After several more blocks, Mrs. Deline turned abruptly up the path to a small, weathered cottage set back from the road behind a picket fence. The garden was overgrown with weeds and the paint was peeling from the window frames. A faded sign swayed from a post near the door, its letters barely legible: "JAKE SKAGWAY, MARINE CURIOS."

"Jake Skagway!" Penny gasped. "The old man from the curio shop!"

"The one you thought was a rascal this morning?" Louise reminded her.

"Quiet! This is more important than your jokes," Penny hissed.

They watched as Mrs. Deline knocked firmly on the door. After a moment, it creaked open and the tall, stooped figure of Jake Skagway appeared. He said something to the woman which the girls could not hear, then stepped back to let her inside. The door closed firmly behind them.

Penny and Louise stared at each other, their eyes wide with excitement.

"Mrs. Deline and old Jake Skagway!" Penny breathed. "I knew he was mixed up in something!"

"But what, Penny? What could a rich widow and a poor old junk dealer have in common?"

"I don't know yet. But a package buried on the beach, a secret radio station, and now a clandestine meeting? It's all starting to add up!"

"Add up to what?"

"A conspiracy!" Penny declared. "We have to find out what they're talking about."

The cottage was close to the street, with a large window facing the road. The curtains were thin and yellowed with age. Creeping forward, Penny flattened herself against the picket fence, trying to peer through a gap in the overgrown bushes that crowded the front yard. Louise followed, her heart thumping with a mixture of fear and excitement.

"Can you see anything?" Louise whispered.

"Not much. They're in the main room. It's filled with junk, just like his shop. He's sitting at a table... and so is she. They're looking at something."

Penny strained her eyes. The light inside was dim, but she could just make out the two figures. Jake Skagway was leaning forward, his long, bony finger tracing a line on a piece of paper spread between them. It looked like a map.

Suddenly, a dog began to bark furiously from the backyard of the house next door. Startled, Penny jumped back from the fence.

"We'd better go before someone sees us," Louise urged, pulling at her sleeve. "If he catches us spying, we'll be in real trouble."

Reluctantly, Penny agreed. They scurried back down the street, not stopping until they were safely back on the main road.

"A map, Lou!" Penny said, her voice trembling with excitement. "Just like Jerry's men use. What if they're planning the route for an escaped prisoner? Or figuring out where to land supplies for the Enemy?"

"Or maybe he's just selling her a piece of property," Louise suggested, ever the voice of reason. "It could be perfectly innocent."

"Innocent? Louise, did you see that cottage? It looked like it was about to fall down! And Mrs. Deline did not look like a woman interested in buying a shack. She looked like she was on a mission. And why all the secrecy? Why did she make sure we thought she was just disposing of fish bones?"

"I admit, it is strange," Louise had to concede.

They returned to the hotel, their minds buzzing with the new discovery. They went straight to their room, needing a private place to discuss their next move.

"Okay," Penny began, pacing the floor. "Let's review the facts. Fact one: Mrs. Deline is not just a simple widow. She is secretive and she lied to us about the package."

"Agreed," said Louise, sitting on the edge of her bed.

"Fact two: She is secretly meeting with Jake Skagway, a man who runs a dusty old shop but seems to be involved in something much bigger. They were looking at a map."

"We think it was a map," Louise amended.

"It was a map," Penny insisted. "Fact three: The Army is right here in Sunset Beach, hunting for a secret enemy radio station called the Voice from the Cave."

She stopped pacing and looked at Louise, her eyes shining. "Don't you see? It has to be connected! Skagway's cottage is the perfect place to hide a radio transmitter. It's old, isolated, and no one would ever suspect him."

"But he runs a shop in town. He's there all day."

"He could have an accomplice! Or he could broadcast at night! Jerry said the broadcasts were intermittent. And his cottage is full of old marine equipment, radios, all sorts of things. A transmitter could be hidden in plain sight!"

"So what do we do?" Louise asked. "We can't just barge in there."

"No," Penny said slowly, a thoughtful expression on her face. "We can't. But maybe we don't have to." She walked to the window and looked down at the town. "We need more proof. Something solid we can take to my father, or even to Jerry."

An idea began to form in her mind, a daring and risky plan. "Louise," she said, turning back to her friend. "How do you feel about another night-time investigation?"

"Oh, Penny, no! Not after we almost got drenched on the beach."

"This is different. We're not going to dig in the sand. We're going to pay another visit to Mr. Skagway's curio shop."

"But it's closed! It will be locked."

"Exactly," Penny said with a grin. "But I happened to notice something when we passed by earlier. The window on the side alley. It's a small one, for the back storeroom, and the latch looks old and rusty. I'll bet a whole box of cookies we can get it open."

Louise stared at her, horrified. "You mean break in? Penny, that's illegal! We could be arrested!"

"We're not going to break anything," Penny assured her smoothly. "We're just going to... investigate. We'll find the proof we need, and then we'll get out. No one will ever know we were there."

"I don't like it," Louise said, shaking her head. "It's too dangerous."

"It's more dangerous to let a ring of spies operate right under our noses!" Penny countered passionately. "Think of what Jerry said. They could be helping enemy soldiers! We have to do something."

For a long moment, Louise was silent. She knew Penny was right. The situation was far too serious to ignore. As much as she disliked the idea of breaking into a shop, the thought of what could happen if they did nothing was even worse.

"All right," she sighed in resignation. "I'll do it. But if we get caught, you are doing all the explaining to your father."

"It's a deal!" Penny exclaimed, her spirits soaring. "We'll wait until it's really dark, after ten o'clock. Everyone in Sunset Beach will be asleep by then."

The next few hours passed with agonizing slowness. The girls ate dinner in the hotel dining room, barely tasting their food. Penny kept glancing at the table where Mrs. Deline usually sat, but it remained empty. She had not yet returned from her meeting with Jake Skagway.

After dinner, they tried to read in their room, but neither could concentrate. Every creak of the old hotel in the hallway made them jump. Finally, the clock on the mantelpiece chimed ten.

"It's time," Penny whispered, her voice a low thrum of excitement.

They put on dark sweaters to blend in with the night and slipped out of their room. The hotel corridors were quiet and empty. They crept down the back stairs and out a side door, emerging into the cool, salt-scented night air. The moon was hidden behind a thick blanket of clouds, casting the town in deep shadow. It was perfect for their mission.

Sticking to the shadows, they made their way through the deserted streets toward the curio shop. The town was eerily silent, the only sound the distant wash of the waves on the shore. When they reached the shop, it was a dark, hulking shape against the night sky.

"The alley is this way," Penny whispered, leading the way.

The narrow alley was dark and smelled of damp earth and rotting wood. Just as Penny had remembered, there was a small, high window set into the side wall of the shop.

"Give me a boost," she instructed Louise.

Louise cupped her hands and Penny placed a foot in them, hoisting herself up. She fumbled with the window latch. It was stiff, but not locked. With a loud squeak of protesting metal, it gave way. Penny carefully pushed the window open.

"I'm going in," she breathed. "Stay here and keep watch. If you hear anyone coming, whistle twice."

"Be careful, Penny," Louise pleaded from below.

Penny scrambled through the small opening, dropping silently onto a pile of dusty canvas inside. She was in a small, cluttered storeroom. Moonlight filtered dimly through the grimy window, revealing stacks of old crates, coils of rope, and a jumble of nautical junk. The air was thick with the smell of dust and mildew.

Using a small flashlight from her pocket, she cast its narrow beam around the room. There was no sign of a radio transmitter here. It had to be in the main shop. She found the door to the front room and turned the knob. It was unlocked.

Slipping through, she found herself in the shop itself. It was even more cluttered than the storeroom. In the dark, the piles of fishing nets, ship's lanterns, and mounted fish took on strange, monstrous shapes. Penny moved cautiously, careful not to knock anything over.

She made her way to the counter at the back of the shop. Behind it was a small office area, where Jake Skagway must conduct his business. Her flashlight beam fell upon an old rolltop desk. It was locked. Disappointed, she swept the light around the rest of the office. Her gaze fell upon a heavy wooden sea chest pushed against the far wall. Unlike the rest of the junk in the shop, it looked well-cared for, its brass fittings polished.

A sudden instinct told her this was what she was looking for. She knelt and examined the chest. It was secured with a heavy, modern padlock. There was no way she could open it. Frustrated, she was about to give up when she noticed something tucked behind the chest, almost completely hidden in the shadows.

She reached back and her fingers closed around a metal box. It was cold and heavy. Pulling it out into the beam of her flashlight, she saw it was a military-style ammunition case. Stenciled on the side were letters in a foreign language she recognized as German.

Her heart began to hammer against her ribs. This was it. This was the proof. She tried the lid. It was latched but not locked. With trembling fingers, she unfastened the catches and lifted the lid.

Inside, nestled in a bed of foam padding, was not a radio transmitter, but something far more sinister. It was a collection of forged documents. Passports, identity cards, and travel permits, all bearing unfamiliar faces but expertly crafted to look authentic. Beneath them lay several thick bundles of money in American currency.

Penny stared, speechless. This was bigger than a secret radio. This was an escape operation. Jake Skagway and Mrs. Deline were helping enemy agents or escaped prisoners disappear into the country.

Suddenly, she heard a sound from the street. A low whistle, twice. Louise's signal! Someone was coming!

Panic seized her. She quickly closed the ammo box, shoved it back behind the sea chest, and scrambled back toward the storeroom. She had to get out, now! As she ran, her foot caught on a coil of rope and she stumbled, knocking a stack of old sea charts to the floor with a loud rustle.

From the front of the shop, she heard the sound of a key scraping in the lock. There was no time to get back through the window. Desperate, she looked around the storeroom and saw a tall, empty wooden crate standing in the corner. Without a second thought, she dove inside, pulling a piece of old canvas over the top just as the front door of the shop creaked open.

Through a crack in the canvas, Penny could see a sliver of the storeroom illuminated by a powerful flashlight. Heavy footsteps clumped on the wooden floor.
"Watch your step in here," a gruff voice grumbled. "This place is a worse mess than usual."
Penny's blood ran cold. It was Jake Skagway.
"I am quite capable of navigating a little clutter, Jacob," a cool, familiar voice replied. Mrs. Deline!
Penny held her breath, trying to make herself as small as possible inside the cramped crate. A splinter from the rough-sawn wood dug into her knee.
"Did you lock the back window?" Skagway asked.
"Of course. I am not an amateur. Now, where is it?"
"Same place. Behind the chest. No one's been near it."
Penny heard them move toward the main shop. She risked peeking again. The beam of their flashlight cut a swath through the darkness, landing on the very sea chest she had examined moments before. Mrs. Deline, her white suit now covered by a dark raincoat, knelt down with a grace that seemed out of place in the dusty shop.
"It's still here," she said, her voice low. She did not bother to pull the heavy ammunition case out. She merely ran her hand along its side. "Good."
"What about those girls?" Skagway's voice was laced with irritation. "The Parker girl and her friend. They were on the beach. They saw you."
"They saw nothing," Mrs. Deline corrected him sharply. "They saw a foolish widow burying fish bones. I made certain of it. The one with the wild imagination, Penny, she is the only one to worry about. But she is just a child playing at being a detective. Her little story will sound ridiculous to anyone of authority."
"She's her father's daughter," Skagway muttered. "Anthony Parker is no fool."
"Her father is busy with his newspaper. He has no time for her flights of fancy. Besides, our work here is almost done. Did you receive the signal?"
"The tide broadcast came in an hour ago. Two gulls on a piling. It means the pickup is tomorrow night."
"Excellent. The tide will be high, the moon new. Everything is perfect. You have made the arrangements for the cargo?"
"They'll be brought down to the cove at ten sharp," Skagway confirmed. "Just like the last time. They're getting anxious."
"Let them be anxious. Their freedom is worth a little waiting." Mrs. Deline stood up, brushing dust from her raincoat. "This time, Jacob, there can be no mistakes. The documents must be perfect. The money ready. After tomorrow, this station will go silent for good. We will all disappear."
A shiver traced its way down Penny's spine. So it was an escape operation! And it was happening tomorrow night!
"Don't you worry about me," Skagway growled. "I've handled my end. You just make sure your part of the plan goes smoothly. The less I see of you, the better."
"The feeling is mutual," Mrs. Deline said coldly. "I will be back tomorrow evening to retrieve the case. Have the shop closed up tight. No late customers."
"It'll be closed," he promised.
Penny heard their footsteps moving back toward the front of the shop. The key turned in the lock again, the heavy door groaned shut, and a final click echoed in the sudden, deep silence.
For a long moment, Penny did not move. Her heart hammered a frantic rhythm against her ribs. She was stiff and sore from her confinement in the crate. Slowly, painfully, she pushed aside the canvas and climbed out, her legs tingling with pins and needles.
She had to get to Louise. She had to get to Jerry.
Moving with renewed urgency, she scrambled back to the storeroom window. It was latched, just as Mrs. Deline had said. With trembling fingers, Penny worked the latch free and pushed the window open. She swung her legs over the sill and dropped quietly to the damp ground outside.
A shadow detached itself from the deeper darkness of a neighboring building. "Penny!" Louise hissed. "I was so worried! I saw them go in!"
"Lou, you won't believe it!" Penny whispered, grabbing her friend's arm. "It's worse than we ever imagined! They're helping people escape the country! Enemy prisoners or agents! I found a box full of forged passports and money!"
She quickly recounted the entire conversation she had overheard from her hiding place. Louise listened, her eyes growing wider with every word.
"Oh, Penny! This is serious!" Louise breathed, all of her earlier skepticism gone. "A real spy ring! Right here in Sunset Beach!"
"And the escape is tomorrow night!" Penny added. "They mentioned a cove, and a signal about two gulls. We have to tell someone!"
"We have to tell Jerry," Louise said firmly. "He's the only one who will believe us. The local police might be too slow, or what if one of them is in on it?"
"You're right. Let's get back to the hotel. We have to find him before it's too late!"
The girls hurried through the darkened, sleeping streets, glancing over their shoulders every few feet. Every shadow seemed to hold a threat, every gust of wind sounded like footsteps. When they finally reached the familiar lights of the Crystal Inn, they both breathed a sigh of relief.
The lobby was quiet, save for the hum of a floor polishing machine being run by a night porter. Mrs. Deline was nowhere in sight.
"We can't use the phone in the room," Penny reasoned in a low voice. "The hotel operator might listen in. We have to find Jerry in person."
They settled into a pair of wicker armchairs in a secluded corner of the lobby, pretending to read old magazines. The minutes dragged by like hours. Penny fidgeted, unable to sit still.
"What if he doesn't come back tonight?" Louise worried.
"He has to," Penny insisted, though a knot of anxiety was tightening in her own stomach. "He's staying here, isn't he?"
Just as Louise was about to suggest they go up to their room, the front doors of the hotel swung open and Jerry Livingston strode in. He looked tired but alert, his shoulders set with purpose.
"Jerry!" Penny leaped from her chair, nearly colliding with him.
"Whoa there, Penny!" he said, catching her by the elbows to steady her. "What's the rush? You girls should be in bed."
"We have to talk to you!" Louise said, coming up behind her. "It's important. It's about B4!"
Jerry's casual expression vanished. He glanced around the lobby, then steered them toward the deserted veranda. The rain had stopped, and the air was cool and smelled of salt and wet earth.
"Alright, what is it?" he asked, his voice low and serious. "Start from the beginning."
Speaking in a torrent of words, Penny told him everything. She described breaking into Skagway's shop, the discovery of the ammo case with its sinister contents, and the entire conversation she had overheard between the shopkeeper and Mrs. Deline.
"Forged passports, you say?" Jerry's eyes were intent. "And bundles of cash?"
"Yes! And they mentioned a 'cargo' and a pickup in a cove tomorrow night! Mrs. Deline is coming for the case tomorrow evening. Jerry, they're helping the enemy!"
Jerry ran a hand through his hair. "I knew it," he muttered. "This is the break we've been waiting for. 'B4' isn't just a radio operator. He, or they, are running an entire escape route. It all fits. The coded messages, the drop points, the sudden disappearance of certain individuals we've been tracking."
"Then you believe us?" Louise asked.
"Every word," Jerry said grimly. "You girls have stumbled onto something far bigger than a secret radio station. This ammunition case is the key. We need it as evidence."
"But Mrs. Deline is picking it up tomorrow evening," Penny reminded him. "If it's gone, she'll know something is wrong and they'll all disappear!"
"You're right. We can't just take it. We have to catch them in the act," Jerry reasoned, pacing back and forth on the wet veranda planks. "We need a plan to get into that shop again, but this time we need to be prepared."
He stopped and looked at the two girls. His expression was a mixture of admiration and concern. "You've both been incredibly brave, but this is an official Army intelligence matter now. It's too dangerous for you to be involved any further."
"Not be involved!" Penny cried, her voice full of indignation. "But we are involved! We know where the evidence is! We know their plan! You need us!"
"Penny's right," Louise added, her voice steadier than Penny had ever heard it. "We can help. Jake Skagway knows us, but he just thinks we're nosy tourists. He doesn't suspect we know the truth."
Jerry hesitated, clearly torn. "I can't ask you to put yourselves in more danger."
"You're not asking, we're telling you!" Penny insisted. A plan was already forming in her quick mind. "I know how we can do it! We need a distraction. Louise can go to the shop in the morning. She can keep Mr. Skagway busy at the front counter, asking him a million questions about seashells or fishing lures. He'll be so annoyed he won't notice a thing."
"And while she's doing that?" Jerry asked, a reluctant smile touching his lips.
"You and I can sneak in the back way again!" Penny declared. "The window latch is easy. We can get the box and be out before he knows anything. We could even replace it with a different box so they don't know it's gone until it's too late!"
Jerry looked from Penny's determined face to Louise's resolute one. He sighed, shaking his head in disbelief. "Alright," he said finally. "It's risky, but it just might work. We'll do it first thing in the morning, right after he opens."
"What about Mrs. Deline?" Louise asked.
"I'll have one of my men keep an eye on her," Jerry replied. "She won't make a move without us knowing. Now, the most important thing is for you two to get some sleep. We have a big day tomorrow."
But as Penny and Louise climbed the stairs to their room, sleep was the furthest thing from their minds. They were on the verge of cracking the greatest mystery Sunset Beach had ever known.
The next morning dawned bright and clear. The storm had washed the air clean, and the sky was a brilliant, cloudless blue. Penny, who had tossed and turned all night, was awake before the sun. She and Louise dressed quickly and met Jerry in the hotel dining room for a hurried breakfast.
"Is everything set?" Penny asked, too excited to eat more than a few bites of toast.
"My men are in position," Jerry confirmed, sipping his coffee. "One is watching the hotel, another has Skagway's shop under surveillance. He reported that Skagway arrived and opened up about fifteen minutes ago. The coast is clear."
"I've been thinking about what to ask him," Louise said, trying to sound confident. "I'll ask about the best bait for catching sand crabs. That should take a while."
"Perfect," Jerry said with an encouraging smile. "Remember, Lou, just keep him talking. Don't let him go into the back room for any reason. Penny and I will need at least ten minutes."
They synchronized their watches. Louise would enter the shop at precisely nine o'clock. Penny and Jerry would slip in through the back window two minutes later.
At five minutes to nine, the three of them left the hotel. Jerry went one way, while Penny and Louise headed directly for the main street. As they neared the clutter of shops by the waterfront, their steps slowed.
"I'm a little nervous," Louise admitted in a whisper.
"You'll be wonderful," Penny assured her. "Just think of it as acting in a play. You're a curious tourist, and he's a grumpy old shopkeeper. That's all."
They watched from across the street as the minute hand on the town clock crept toward the hour. The front door of "Skagway's Marine Curios" stood open, a dark maw inviting in the sea breeze.
"Okay, it's time," Louise said, taking a deep breath. She gave Penny a quick, shaky smile. "See you on the other side."
Penny watched as Louise crossed the street, squared her shoulders, and walked into the shop. Now it was her turn. She circled around the block, her heart thumping a steady rhythm of anticipation and fear. She met Jerry in the narrow alleyway behind the shop.
"She's in," Penny whispered.
"Good girl. Let's go," he replied.
Jerry gave her a boost up to the storeroom window. Penny slid it open as quietly as she could and scrambled inside, dropping to the floor. Jerry followed a moment later, moving with a silent, practiced ease that Penny admired.
From the front of the shop, they could hear Louise's voice, high and clear. "But are the red ones better than the green ones? My father told me that sea bass are very particular about color."
"Fish can't see color, girlie," Skagway's gruff voice answered, dripping with impatience. "Bait is bait. Now are you going to buy something or not?"
"It's working," Penny mouthed to Jerry.
He nodded and pointed toward the main room. They crept forward, their sneakers making no sound on the dusty floorboards. The shop was just as she remembered it, a chaotic jumble of maritime junk. The sea chest stood against the far wall, looking solid and immovable.
Jerry knelt beside it and reached into the dark space behind. "Got it," he whispered, pulling the metal ammunition case into the light.
He worked the latches swiftly. Penny held her breath as he lifted the lid. There they were, the stacks of forged documents and the thick bundles of cash, looking even more menacing in the daylight.
Jerry took a small camera from his pocket and quickly photographed the contents of the box. Then, working with incredible speed, he removed one of the passports and a single bundle of currency, slipping them into his jacket. He replaced the bulk of the contents with a small, heavy coil of rope from a nearby shelf to approximate the weight.
"What are you doing?" Penny whispered.
"Taking just enough for definite proof," he murmured. "We leave the rest. When Deline and Skagway open this tonight, they won't notice the difference until it's too late. They'll proceed with their plan, and we'll be waiting for them at the cove."
He closed the lid, fastened the latches, and carefully pushed the box back into its hiding place. The entire operation had taken less than five minutes.
"Now, let's get out of here," he said.
They were halfway back to the storeroom when they heard a change in the voices from the front.
"I've told you everything I know about bait," Skagway was saying, his voice rising in anger. "I have work to do in the back. Now if you'll excuse me."
Penny's eyes flew wide with panic. He was coming! There was no time to get to the window.
"In here!" Jerry hissed, pulling her behind a tall stack of lobster traps just as Skagway's heavy footsteps sounded on the threshold of the storeroom.

Penny's heart hammered against her ribs, so loud she was certain the old man must hear it. Jerry put a steadying hand on her arm, his expression calm but alert. The heavy footsteps stopped just outside the storeroom door. Skagway grumbled something under his breath about nosy summer folk and turned the knob.

The door swung inward with a groan. Jake Skagway stood silhouetted against the light from the shop, a hulking figure with a scowl etched permanently onto his weathered face. He took a step into the dim room, his eyes sweeping over the clutter. Penny held her breath, trying to make herself smaller behind the towering stack of wooden traps. She could smell the faint, musty odor of old rope and dried brine.

Skagway clumped over to a shelf laden with coils of rope and assorted metal tackle. His back was to them. He began rummuring through a box of rusty hooks, his grumbling growing louder. "Can't find a decent gaff hook in this whole mess. Nothin's where it ought to be."

Penny risked a glance at Jerry. He gave a minute shake of his head, his finger pressed to his lips. Every second felt like an hour. Skagway tossed a handful of hooks back into the box with a clatter that made Penny jump. He turned, his gaze sweeping the room again. His eyes seemed to pass right over their hiding spot without seeing them.

Just as he started to move back toward the door, Louise's voice, high and a little shaky, drifted from the front of the shop. "Mr. Skagway! Oh, Mr. Skagway! I forgot to ask you something dreadfully important!"

Skagway stopped, letting out an exasperated sigh. "What is it now, girlie?" he bellowed without turning around.

"Well, it's about sand fleas!" Louise called out. "Do you sell them? And if you do, are they fresh? My Aunt Martha says you can't catch a decent flounder with a stale sand flea!"

A low growl rumbled in Skagway's chest. "I ain't got no sand fleas!" he roared, striding out of the storeroom and slamming the door behind him. "And if I did, they'd be fresher than you are! Now get out of my shop!"

The moment the door slammed shut, Jerry grabbed Penny's hand. "Now! Go!"

They didn't need a second urging. They scurried back to the open window. Jerry gave Penny another boost and she tumbled out into the alley, landing with a soft thud on the packed dirt. Jerry followed with the grace of a cat. They didn't stop running until they were a full block away, where they saw Louise hurrying toward them, her face pale.

"Louise, you were brilliant!" Penny gasped, grabbing her friend's arm. "Aunt Martha and the stale sand fleas! You're a genius!"

"Oh, Penny, he was perfectly horrid!" Louise said, her voice still trembling. "I thought he was going to throw me out into the street! Did you get it?"

"We got it," Jerry said, patting the pocket of his jacket. "We have the proof. And thanks to you, we also got away without being caught. You were very brave, Louise."

Louise blushed at the praise. "I was terrified. His eyes are like little gray stones."

"Well, his spy career is about to hit some rocks of its own," Jerry said grimly. "We need to get this evidence to the proper authorities. Then tonight, we set the trap."

"What's the plan?" Penny asked eagerly, her fear already forgotten and replaced by bubbling excitement.

"My colleagues are already in position around Sunset Beach," Jerry explained as they walked briskly toward the main road. "Tonight, Skagway and Mrs. Deline will go to the cove to make their exchange. They'll be expecting a boat to bring in another escaped prisoner and take out forged documents. Instead, they'll be met by the United States Coast Guard."

"And us?" Penny pressed. "What do we do?"

"You girls have done more than enough," Jerry said firmly. "Your part is over. I want you to go back to the hotel and stay there."

"Oh, but Jerry!" Penny protested. "We can't! We're the ones who discovered the whole plot! We have to see it through!"

"I absolutely forbid it," Jerry said, his tone leaving no room for argument. "This is going to be a dangerous operation. These are desperate people. I won't be responsible for putting you in harm's way."

Penny's face fell. "But that's not fair! We helped!"

"And your help has been invaluable," he conceded. "But a real agent knows when to follow orders. Your orders are to stay at the Crystal Inn, and that's final." He hailed a passing taxi and opened the door for them. "I'll see you both tomorrow morning, after everything is settled."

Dejectedly, the girls climbed into the cab. They watched through the rear window as Jerry got into another car and sped away in the opposite direction.

"I can't believe it," Penny fumed, slumping into her seat. "He's cutting us out right at the end!"

"He's right, you know," Louise said quietly. "It could be dangerous, Penny. What if they have guns?"

"Then it will be even more exciting!" Penny declared. "And we're going to miss it. We did all the hard work, digging in the sand, getting yelled at by that awful Mr. Skagway, and for what? To be told to go to our rooms like naughty children."

Louise sighed. She knew that look in Penny's eyes. "What are you thinking, Penny Parker?"

"I'm thinking that Jerry is a very nice man, but he doesn't know us very well," Penny said, a determined glint in her eye. "He thinks we're just going to sit in the hotel and knit. He doesn't know that we have a mystery to solve."

"But Jerry solved it! He has the proof."

"He has part of the proof," Penny corrected. "But we haven't seen them caught! That's the most important part! We're not going to be in any danger. We'll just watch from a distance. A safe distance."

"I don't know, Penny..."

"Think of it, Lou! A secret cove at night! Spies! The Coast Guard! It's the adventure of a lifetime! We can't just sit it out."

By the time the taxi pulled up to the Crystal Inn, Louise, as usual, had been won over by Penny's infectious enthusiasm. "All right," she agreed. "But if I see so much as a slingshot, I'm running all the way back here."

They spent the rest of the afternoon in a state of feverish anticipation, trying to act as normal as possible. They had dinner with Penny's father, who was preoccupied with a new article he was writing and barely noticed their fidgeting. As darkness fell over Sunset Beach, the girls slipped out a side door of the hotel, clad in dark sweaters against the evening chill.

The cove Jerry had mentioned was a few miles down the coast, a jagged bite taken out of the shoreline. They knew the general area from their explorations. The moon was a pale sliver, offering little light, and a damp, salty mist rolled in from the sea, muffling sound and shrouding the landscape in an eerie silence.

"This is spooky," Louise whispered, clutching Penny's arm as they picked their way over the rocky terrain above the beach.

"It's perfect!" Penny whispered back, her eyes shining with excitement. "No one will be able to see us up here."

They found a secure vantage point, a hollow nestled between two large boulders that overlooked the entire crescent of the cove. Down below, the dark water lapped gently at the sand. The beach was deserted.

"Now we just have to wait," Penny breathed, settling down on the cold stone.

The waiting was the hardest part. Minutes stretched into what felt like hours. The mist grew thicker, and the air grew cold. Louise shivered, pulling her sweater tighter. "Maybe they're not coming," she suggested hopefully.

"They'll come," Penny insisted. "Jerry said they would."

And she was right. Not long after, they saw a faint light bobbing along the beach path. It was a single lantern, held aloft. Two figures emerged from the gloom. Even from their perch, the girls could recognize the stout, rolling gait of Jake Skagway and the tall, straight-backed posture of Mrs. Deline.

"There they are!" Penny whispered, her heart starting to pound.

The two spies did not speak. They walked to the center of the beach, just above the high tide line. Skagway set the lantern down on the sand. Then he reached into his heavy coat and pulled out a small, powerful flashlight. He pointed it out to sea and blinked it on and off. Once. Twice. Three times.

He paused, then repeated the signal. For a long moment, there was no reply. The only sound was the whisper of the waves.

Then, far out in the fog, an answering light blinked back. Once. Twice. Three times.

"It's the boat," Louise breathed.

Mrs. Deline said something to Skagway, her voice too low to carry. He nodded and walked over to the base of the dunes, to the very spot where she had pretended to bury the fish bones. He began to dig with his bare hands, sending sand flying. In a moment, he grunted and pulled the metal ammunition case from its hiding place. He carried it back and set it down beside the lantern.

They stood waiting, two dark silhouettes against the pale sand. The girls watched, spellbound. They could just make out the faint, low thrum of a boat engine growing steadily closer. A dark shape began to resolve itself out of the mist, a small motor launch with its lights extinguished.

This was it. The moment of truth.

"Where is Jerry?" Penny muttered, scanning the dark bluffs around them. "Where is the Coast Guard?"

As if in answer to her question, a voice suddenly boomed through the night, amplified by a megaphone. "STAY WHERE YOU ARE! YOU ARE SURROUNDED! THIS IS THE COAST GUARD!"

Instantly, the peaceful scene erupted into chaos. Powerful searchlights flared to life from the cliffs, pinning Skagway and Mrs. Deline in their brilliant white beams. Men in uniform seemed to materialize from the rocks, running down onto the beach. The motor launch, caught in a spotlight, abruptly cut its engine and then tried to reverse, its propeller churning the water into a white froth. A larger Coast Guard cutter, which had been lying in wait just beyond the fog bank, roared to life and moved to intercept it.

Skagway let out a string of curses and made a clumsy dash for the dunes, but two burly guardsmen tackled him and brought him down. He thrashed and swore, but it was no use.

Mrs. Deline, however, did not panic. With a speed that belied her elegant composure, she spun around and sprinted not toward the dunes, but in the opposite direction, toward a narrow, rock-strewn channel at the far end of the cove. The searchlights were focused on Skagway and the boat, and for a moment, no one seemed to notice her escape.

No one except Penny.

"Jerry! Look!" she shrieked, forgetting all about being quiet. She pointed frantically. "Mrs. Deline! She's getting away!"

Jerry, who was standing with a group of agents near the path, whirled around. His eyes followed Penny's pointing finger. "Cut her off!" he yelled. "She's heading for the channel!"

Two agents broke away and sprinted after the fleeing woman. But Mrs. Deline had a head start and knew the terrain. She scrambled over the slippery rocks like a mountain goat, her white suit a ghostly blur in the darkness. She was about to disappear into a fissure between two massive rock formations.

"She's going to get away!" Louise cried.

"No, she's not!" Penny said through gritted teeth. Without a second thought, she began scrambling down from their hiding place, slipping and sliding on the loose scree.

"Penny, wait! Jerry said to stay here!" Louise called after her, but Penny was already gone.

Penny didn't run toward Mrs. Deline, but parallel to her, staying on the higher ground of the bluff. She knew these cliffs. She knew there was a steep, sandy path just ahead that led right down into the channel. It was a shortcut.

Her lungs burned and her legs ached, but she pushed on, her sneakers finding purchase in the sandy soil. She reached the top of the path just as Mrs. Deline entered the narrow channel below. Without hesitating, Penny launched herself down the steep incline, half running, half sliding, sending a cascade of sand and pebbles ahead of her.

She landed at the bottom of the path in a heap, directly in front of Mrs. Deline. The woman stopped short, her face a mask of fury in the dim light. The two agents were right behind her, closing in. She was trapped.

For a moment, Mrs. Deline just stared at Penny, her chest heaving. Her cold composure had finally cracked. "You meddling little fool!" she hissed.

Then the agents were upon her, grabbing her arms. She didn't struggle. The fight had gone out of her. She drew herself up, her face once again becoming an icy, impassive mask.

Jerry came running up, his face grim. "Penny, I thought I told you to stay put," he said, but there was a note of grudging admiration in his voice. "You could have been hurt."

"But she was getting away," Penny said simply, brushing sand from her sweater.

"Yes, she was," Jerry admitted. He looked at the proud, silent woman between the two agents. "Thanks to you, she didn't. You did well, Penny."

By the time they led a handcuffed Mrs. Deline back to the main beach, the operation was over. The motor launch had been captured, and its two occupants were in custody along with Jake Skagway, who was still muttering darkly.

Jerry took the girls aside as the prisoners were being led away. "Well, that's the end of the Sunset Beach spy ring," he said with satisfaction.

"So Skagway was B4, the Voice from the Cave?" Penny asked.

"He was," Jerry confirmed. "He had a powerful short wave radio hidden in a cellar beneath his shack. That's why our monitoring truck couldn't get a precise fix on him at first. He broadcast from underground. He was sending shipping information and receiving instructions for the escaped prisoners."

"And Mrs. Deline?" Louise asked.

"She was the contact, the paymaster. She used her status as a wealthy widow to travel without suspicion. The money and passports you found were for the next group of escapees. She was a very important part of their operation."

"What about the fish bones?" Penny remembered.

Jerry chuckled. "She admitted that was a complete fabrication, made up on the spot to get you to stop digging. She knew you were watching her. She'd seen you through that spyglass of hers. The story about writing on her skirt was just a diversion as well. She was actually just waiting, watching for a signal from Skagway."

"And she almost got away," Louise marveled, looking at Penny with awe.

"It takes more than a few spies to outsmart Penny Parker," Jerry said, clapping Penny on the shoulder. "My superiors are going to be very interested to hear about the two young ladies who helped us crack this case."

A thrill went through Penny. "Does that mean we can be agents now?"

Jerry laughed. "Let's not get ahead of ourselves. For now, I think you've earned a good night's sleep. And my eternal gratitude."

As they walked back toward the lights of the hotel, leaving the flashing lights of the Coast Guard cars behind, Louise let out a long sigh of relief. "Well, I've had enough adventure to last me a lifetime."

Penny just smiled. The salty night air felt cool on her face, and the sound of the ocean was a comforting roar. "Oh, I don't know, Lou," she said, her eyes already scanning the dark horizon. "I have a feeling our summer at Sunset Beach is just getting started."

The next morning dawned bright and clear, the storm having scrubbed the sky to a brilliant, cloudless blue. The sea, once dark and angry, now lapped gently against the shore in ribbons of white foam. For Louise, the peace was a welcome relief. She intended to spend the entire day with a novel and a comfortable beach chair, soaking up the sun and forgetting all about spies and secret codes.

Penny, however, was another matter. She paced the length of their hotel room, her energy seeming to fill the small space until it hummed like a taut wire.

"I can't believe it's over," she said for the third time, peering out the window toward the now-innocent looking dunes.

"It's a good thing it's over," Louise replied from her bed, where she was examining a collection of seashells. "We were nearly caught in a storm, we dug for fish bones, and you tackled a dangerous spy. I think we've earned a little rest."

"Rest is for when you're old, Lou," Penny declared, turning from the window. "Don't you feel it? The quiet? It's too quiet. After all that excitement, it just feels… wrong."

"It feels perfect," Louise sighed contentedly. "No more theories, no more digging, and no more Mrs. Deline giving us the cold shoulder on the veranda."

Penny made a face. "I suppose. But what are we going to do all day? And tomorrow? And the day after that?"

"Relax," Louise said simply. "It's what people do at Sunset Beach. Come on, let's get breakfast. I'm starved."

Penny reluctantly agreed, and after a hearty meal in the Crystal Inn's sunny dining room, they made their way to the beach. Louise settled into her chair with a satisfied sigh, while Penny stood at the water's edge, hands on her hips, scanning the horizon as if expecting to see an enemy submarine surface at any moment.

The day passed with a slowness that grated on Penny's nerves. Even a visit from Jerry in the afternoon did little to liven things up. He was preoccupied with reports and telephone calls to his headquarters.

"The prisoners have all been transported," he told them, pausing by their beach umbrella. "Skagway is singing like a canary, and we've already rounded up two more members of the ring in the city thanks to the information we recovered. It was a clean sweep."

"Are you sure there aren't any left?" Penny asked hopefully.

Jerry smiled, understanding her perfectly. "Positive, Penny. The Sunset Beach chapter is officially closed. My advice to you two is to enjoy your vacation. You've certainly earned it." With another tip of his hat, he was gone, striding back toward the hotel and his mountain of paperwork.

"You see?" Louise said, looking up from her book. "Closed. Finished. The end."

Penny kicked at a clump of seaweed. "Hmph," was all she said in reply.

That evening, the girls sat by the large window in their room, watching the last colors of the sunset fade over the water. A deep purple dusk settled over the beach, and one by one, stars began to pop into existence.

"It is beautiful here," Louise admitted in a soft voice.

Penny didn't answer. Her eyes were fixed on a distant point down the coast, where the smooth beach gave way to a dark, jagged series of cliffs and rock formations. It was the same area her father had warned was a likely spot for landings.

"What is it?" Louise asked, following her gaze.

"Shhh! Look!" Penny whispered, pointing. "Over there, on the cliffs."

Louise squinted into the gathering darkness. At first she saw nothing but the black silhouette of the rocks against the navy blue sky. Then she saw it. A tiny pinprick of light, flickering weakly. It flashed once, twice, then a third time before vanishing.

"What was that?" Louise breathed.

"A signal!" Penny declared, her voice filled with triumphant excitement. "I knew it! They didn't get everyone! It has to be another spy, signaling a boat out at sea!"

"Oh, Penny, don't start," Louise groaned, slumping in her chair. "It was probably just a camper with a flashlight. Or maybe someone having a little bonfire on the rocks."

"Did that look like a bonfire?" Penny challenged. "It was a distinct series of flashes. One, two, three. That's a code for something!"

"It's a code for you to calm down and get some sleep," Louise retorted, though she could not deny that the sight had been peculiar. "Jerry said the case was closed."

"Jerry can be wrong! Maybe this is a new group. Or someone Skagway and Mrs. Deline never mentioned. We have to investigate."

"We are not going out to those cliffs in the dark," Louise stated firmly. "It's dangerous. Besides, the light is gone now. There's nothing to see."

Penny knew Louise was right about the danger, but the thought of a new mystery, a new clue waiting to be unraveled, was too tantalizing to ignore. "All right," she conceded. "But first thing tomorrow morning, we're going down there to see what we can find."

Louise sighed, knowing there was no arguing with Penny once her mind was made up. "I'll bet a cookie we find nothing but rocks and seagulls," she mumbled.

"And I'll bet you a whole box of cookies we find a clue," Penny shot back, her eyes sparkling with anticipation for the first time all day.

The next morning, Penny was up before the sun, urging a sleepy Louise out of bed. They dressed quickly and slipped out of the hotel while the lobby was still quiet. The air was cool and smelled of salt and damp sand. They walked briskly down the beach, their footsteps the only sound besides the gentle shushing of the waves.

The cliffs were farther away than they had looked from the hotel window. By the time the girls reached the area of jagged black rock, the sun was rising, casting a golden glow over the scene.

"Now what?" Louise asked, looking around at the bewildering jumble of rocks and tide pools. "We don't even know exactly where we saw the light."

"It was high up," Penny said, scanning the cliff face. "Somewhere up there." She pointed to a ledge about halfway to the top. "Let's see if we can find a path."

They found a narrow, winding trail that seemed to be a natural path worn by water and hardy fishermen. It was a steep climb, and Louise complained the whole way, but Penny pushed onward, her eyes searching every nook and cranny. When they reached the ledge she had pointed out, they found a small, flat area, sheltered from the wind.

"Someone's been here recently," Penny announced, pointing to the ground. There were scuff marks in the thin layer of soil and a few faint footprints.

"Could be anyone," Louise said, though she was beginning to feel a prickle of interest.

"But look at this!" Penny knelt down beside a large, flat rock that served as a natural table. Lying upon it was a small object. It was a piece of driftwood, but it was not like any driftwood they had ever seen. It had been smoothed and polished, and carved into the shape of a coiled sea serpent, its tiny eyes inlaid with something dark that glinted in the morning light.

"Well," Louise breathed, picking it up. "That's certainly odd. It's beautiful."

"It's a clue," Penny stated with conviction. "Why would someone leave something so detailed and strange up here? Unless it was a marker. Or a message."

As they were examining the carving, a voice from below startled them. "Findin' treasures, are ye?"

They peered over the edge of the ledge and saw an old man standing on the path below. He was weathered and leathery, with a fringe of white hair sticking out from under a stained cap. He carried a fishing pole and a bucket and looked up at them with startlingly bright blue eyes.

"We, uh, were just exploring," Penny called down.

The old man chuckled, a dry, raspy sound. "Best be careful up there. Them's the Whispering Cliffs. Full o' caves and tricky paths. Easy to get lost." He started to climb up toward them with a surprising agility.

"Whispering Cliffs?" Louise asked as he reached their ledge.

"Aye," the man said, nodding. He introduced himself as Silas, a fisherman who had lived in Sunset Beach his whole life. "The old timers call 'em that. Say that on a windy night, the air rushin' through the sea caves sounds like folks whisperin' secrets to each other. Smugglers' secrets, mostly."

Penny's eyes lit up. "Smugglers?"

"Oh, aye. Long ago," Silas said, gazing out at the sea. "This coast was a haven for 'em. They'd use lanterns to signal ships, bringin' in brandy and silks without payin' the king's tax. They had hidey-holes all through these cliffs. Some say their treasure is still hidden in one of 'em." He winked. "Just a story, o' course."

Penny showed him the carved sea serpent. "Have you ever seen anything like this before?"

Silas took the piece of driftwood, turning it over in his gnarled hands. His expression grew curious. "Well now. That's a fine bit o' whittlin'. Looks like old Cap'n Finn's work, it does."

"Who is Captain Finn?" Penny asked eagerly.

"Not a who. A what," Silas corrected. "The 'Cap'n Finn' was a ship. Wrecked on these very rocks nigh on a hundred years ago. The story goes her captain carved little sea serpents just like this one. Left 'em as markers for his treasure." The old man handed the carving back to Penny. "Like I said, just a story. Probably some tourist dropped it. Well, the tide's turnin'. Good day to ye, girls."

With another nod, the old fisherman nimbly made his way back down the path and disappeared around a bend.

"A treasure map!" Penny whispered, her imagination running wild. "The light wasn't for spies, Lou! It's for treasure hunters! And they're using these carvings as markers to find it!"

"Now you're chasing smugglers' ghosts," Louise said, but she couldn't help but look at the carved serpent with a new sense of wonder. The story was far more romantic than spies, at least. "It's still just a legend, Penny. And an old man's tale."

"An old man who knew exactly what this carving was," Penny countered. "This is a real lead! We have to find where it leads."

They spent another hour searching the cliffs, but found no other carvings or clues. Disappointed but not defeated, they headed back to the Crystal Inn. They decided to tell Jerry about their discovery, certain he would be interested.

They found him on the veranda, finally looking relaxed with his feet propped up on the railing. Penny launched into the story of the flickering light, the climb, and the carved sea serpent, finishing with Silas's tale of smugglers' treasure.

Jerry listened patiently, a small, amused smile playing on his lips. When she was finished, he chuckled.

"Penny, you never quit, do you?" he said, shaking his head. "A light on the cliffs and a piece of carved wood. It's a nice story, but it's not exactly evidence of a criminal conspiracy."

"But what about the light?" Louise chimed in. "We both saw it."

"Probably some kids with a flashlight, just like you thought," Jerry said. "As for the smugglers, that's a local legend you'll hear from any of the old-timers. They like to spin yarns for the tourists. My work here is done. I'm heading back to the city tomorrow. So my final piece of advice is to leave the ghost hunting to Silas and enjoy the sunshine."

Penny's face fell. He was dismissing them completely. "But Jerry..."

"Case closed, Penny," he said, his tone kind but firm. "Stay out of trouble."

As Jerry walked away, Penny's disappointment quickly hardened into resolve. "He thinks it's a joke," she said, her jaw set. "He thinks we're just silly girls playing detective."

"Well, maybe he's right this time," Louise suggested gently.

"No," Penny said, clutching the wooden serpent in her pocket. "He's wrong. There's something going on at those cliffs, and if he won't investigate, we will. We'll go back tonight. If that light appears again, we're going to find out exactly who is making it."

Louise groaned. "I had a feeling you were going to say that. Staking out a dark cliff in the middle of the night. What could possibly go wrong?"

Despite her misgivings, Louise found herself huddled with Penny behind a large boulder on the cliffs that night. A cool wind had picked up, and it whistled through the rocks with an eerie sound, just as Silas had described. They had brought a flashlight, a blanket, and a thermos of hot chocolate that Louise had insisted upon.

For hours, they waited. The moon cast a silvery path across the water, and the only sounds were the wind and the crashing of waves far below. Louise was beginning to doze off.

"This is pointless, Penny," she whispered. "Let's go back."

"Just a little longer," Penny pleaded. "It's not even midnight yet."

And then, it happened. Not from the ledge they had explored, but from a point lower down, closer to the water, a light flickered. Once. Twice. Three times. It was followed by a brief pause, and then two more flashes.

"It's a different code!" Penny gasped, suddenly wide awake.

A few moments later, they saw an answering signal from out on the dark water. A single flash of green light.

"A boat!" Louise whispered, her heart starting to pound. "Penny, you were right!"

A small motorboat with its lights doused was silently approaching the shore. It cut its engine and drifted into a small, hidden cove at the base of the cliffs. Two figures emerged, carrying heavy-looking sacks. They disappeared into the shadows of the rocks. The person who had been signaling from the cliff went down to meet them.

"They're going into one of the caves," Penny breathed, her mind racing. "This is it, Lou! This is the smugglers' hideout!"

"What are we going to do?" Louise asked, her voice trembling slightly.

"We're going to see what's in those sacks," Penny declared, already creeping forward.

"Penny, no! We should go get Jerry!"

"There's no time! By the time we get back to the hotel and convince him, they'll be long gone. We have to be quiet."

Reluctantly, Louise followed Penny as she moved silently from one rock to another, getting closer to the cove. The entrance to the sea cave was a dark slash in the cliff face, partially hidden by an outcropping of rock. The smugglers had disappeared inside. No light was visible from within.

"They must have a shielded lantern," Penny whispered. "Come on."

Holding their breath, the two girls crept to the mouth of the cave. The air that flowed out was cold and damp, smelling of brine and wet stone. From deep inside, they could hear the low murmur of voices.

Penny clicked on their flashlight for just a second, shining it into the cave. The beam revealed a sandy-floored passage that curved away into darkness. Clicking it off, she took Louise's hand. "Stay close to me."

Inch by inch, they moved into the cave, their tennis shoes making no sound on the sand. The passage opened into a larger chamber. A faint, flickering light from around the bend ahead of them cast dancing shadows on the high, vaulted ceiling. As they drew closer, they could make out what the men were saying.

"...got the whole lot this time," a gruff voice said. "The collector in the city will pay a fortune for these."

"Good," a second, younger voice replied. "Let's get them packed in the crates and hidden. The tide will be coming in soon."

Penny and Louise peered around the edge of the rock wall. In the center of the chamber, two men were crouched over the sacks, their faces illuminated by a gasoline lantern. They were emptying the contents onto a canvas tarp. It was not gold or jewels. It was a collection of ancient-looking pottery, jade figurines, and intricate silver ornaments.

"Stolen artifacts," Penny mouthed to Louise.

The younger of the two men looked familiar. Penny wracked her brain, then gasped silently. He was one of the waiters from the Crystal Inn, a quiet, polite young man named Tom. The other man was a burly, rough-looking character she had never seen before.

Suddenly, the burly man looked up, his eyes narrowing as he stared directly toward their hiding spot. "What was that?" he growled.

Penny pulled Louise back just as a small pebble, dislodged by her foot, skittered across the floor.

"Who's there?" the man shouted, his voice echoing in the chamber.

He grabbed the lantern and started toward them. There was nowhere to run. They were trapped. Penny's mind raced. The sea! The tide was coming in! She frantically scanned the chamber. There was another, smaller opening on the far side of the cave.

"This way!" she hissed, pulling Louise toward the other passage.

They scrambled through the narrow opening just as the man's light flooded the spot where they had been hiding. They found themselves in a smaller tunnel, sloping steeply downward. The sound of the surf was much louder here.

"It leads back to the sea!" Louise cried in relief.

But their relief was short-lived. A wave suddenly surged into the tunnel, swirling around their ankles with surprising force.

"The tide!" Penny exclaimed. "It's coming in faster than I thought! This tunnel is going to flood!"

Behind them, they could hear the smugglers shouting. They had no choice but to push forward. The tunnel ended at a small ledge, just above the churning water. They were trapped between the rising sea and the two criminals. The burly man appeared at the entrance to the tunnel, blocking their only way back.

"Well, well," he sneered. "Looks like we've caught a couple of snoopy kittens." He started toward them, a menacing grin on his face.

Just then, a powerful beam of light shot into the cave from the sea entrance, followed by a shouted command. "FBI! Stay where you are!"

Everyone froze. There, at the mouth of the cave, was a Coast Guard patrol boat, its searchlight pinning the smugglers. And standing on the bow, megaphone in hand, was Jerry Livingston.

The burly man uttered a curse and made a desperate lunge for Penny, but Tom, the waiter, seemed to lose his nerve. He threw up his hands in surrender. Before the other man could reach the girls, two agents splashed through the shallow water and had him in a firm grip.

A few minutes later, Penny and Louise were wrapped in warm blankets aboard the patrol boat, watching as the two smugglers, now in handcuffs, were led away. Jerry came over to them, his face a mixture of exasperation and profound respect.

"I ought to lock you two up for obstruction," he said, but his eyes were twinkling. "When you didn't come back to the hotel, I got worried. I remembered what you said about the lights and I had a hunch. I decided to check out your smugglers' legend after all."

"The carvings were a code, weren't they?" Penny asked.

Jerry nodded. "We found two more. We just found out Tom, the waiter, was part of a ring smuggling priceless artifacts stolen from museums in South America. They used the old smugglers' legends as a cover, leaving the carvings as signals for drop-offs. You two led us right to them."

"I told you it wasn't a ghost story," Penny said, nudging Louise.

Louise just shivered, pulling the blanket tighter. "All I know is I've had enough of caves and rising tides to last me a very, very long time."

Jerry laughed. "I think you've both had enough adventure for one summer. My superiors are going to have to invent a new medal for 'unofficial youth assistance'."

As the boat turned back toward the main beach and the distant, welcoming lights of the Crystal Inn, Penny looked back at the dark, whispering cliffs. The mystery was solved, the criminals were caught, and Louise was already planning a long, uneventful day of sleep. But Penny smiled. The salty spray felt good on her face, and the night was still full of possibilities. "Maybe," she said softly to herself, "but the summer isn't over yet."

We believe a well organized escape route is in operation, and that it may run right through this stretch of coastline." "And Mr. Voice from the Cave is helping them?" Penny questioned, her eyes wide with excitement. "He broadcasts in code, telling them when and where to meet the getaway boats?" "That's our working theory," Jerry confirmed. "The only problem is, we can't pin him down. The broadcasts are short, irregular, and we've never been able to get a solid fix on their point of origin. But we will," he added, his jaw set determinedly. "We will."

The return trip to the hotel was a quiet one. The excitement of the hidden radio truck and the talk of escaped prisoners had left the girls with much to think about. Louise, for her part, seemed more than content to have the day's adventures come to a close. But Penny's mind was racing. Spies, secret codes, and a mysterious Mr. Voice from the Cave! It was almost too much to hope for. As the taxi pulled up to the Crystal Inn, she was already planning her next move. The morning after their brush with the law and the revelation of the monitoring truck found the girls in the hotel's sunny dining room. Louise was attacking a large stack of pancakes with the quiet determination of one making up for lost meals. Penny, however, merely pushed a piece of toast around her plate. "Honestly, Penny, you look as if you've lost your last friend," Louise remarked, pausing for a sip of milk. "After last night, I should think you'd be ready for a nice, long, boring day on the beach." "Boring? Louise, how can you even say that?" Penny protested. "There's an enemy spy ring operating right under our noses! Jerry said so himself. And we're just sitting here eating pancakes!" "I am eating pancakes," Louise corrected her primly. "You are making a work of modern art with a piece of toast. Besides, what do you expect us to do? Jerry and the Army have it well in hand." "But they don't!" Penny insisted, leaning forward earnestly. "They've been trying to find that station for weeks and they have nothing. They need help." "And I suppose you intend to provide it?" Louise asked with a skeptical arch of her eyebrow. "You've already accused old Jake Skagway and poor Mrs. Deline of being the Kaiser's personal secret agent. Don't you think you should leave the real spying to the professionals?" "Mrs. Deline is still a puzzle," Penny mused, ignoring the jibe. "Her story about the fish bones was just too convenient. And that spyglass! Why was she really watching us? I'll bet a cookie she's connected to this somehow." "You'll bet a cookie on anything," Louise sighed. "Look, can we please just have one normal day? My nerves are still recovering from that cave." Before Penny could reply, their attention was drawn to a commotion near the front desk. The hotel manager, a portly and usually placid man named Mr. Harris, was speaking in a hushed but agitated tone to one of the bellhops. "No one has seen her since last evening?" Mr. Harris whispered, wiping his brow with a handkerchief. "Her breakfast tray wasn't touched? This is a disaster! A complete disaster!" "Who do you suppose they're talking about?" Louise wondered aloud. "I know who," Penny said, her eyes gleaming with a sudden new interest. "It must be Mrs. Gable. From the suite at the end of the hall. The rich old lady with all the jewels." The girls had seen Mrs. Gable only a few times. She was an elderly woman, always impeccably dressed, who kept to herself and was rumored to be enormously wealthy. She was accompanied by her nephew, a slick, handsome man in his thirties named Arthur Croft. "Maybe she just slept in," Louise suggested. "Wealthy old ladies are allowed to do that." "Not according to Mr. Harris's face," Penny countered. "Something is wrong. I can feel it." Finishing her milk in a single gulp, Penny stood up. "Come on." "Where are we going?" "To the scene of the crime, of course!" "Penny, it's not a crime! It's probably just a misunderstanding." But Louise, with a long suffering sigh, folded her napkin and followed her friend. As they approached the front desk, Mr. Croft came striding toward the manager. He looked annoyed. "What is the meaning of all this fuss, Harris?" he demanded. "I just went to my aunt's suite and the maid told me she was not to be disturbed. Now I hear she's missing?" "We are not certain she is missing, Mr. Croft," the manager stammered, clearly intimidated. "She simply did not answer her door this morning. We did not wish to cause alarm." "Well, you are causing it!" Croft snapped. "My aunt is a creature of habit. She takes breakfast at eight o'clock sharp. If she is not in her room, then where is she?" Penny and Louise exchanged a look. Mr. Croft's concern seemed more like irritation than genuine worry. They watched as the manager used a passkey to open the door to the elegant corner suite. Mr. Croft and Mr. Harris disappeared inside, and the door clicked shut. "Well, that's that," Louise said, turning to go. "The authorities are here." "He's not an authority, he's just a nephew," Penny whispered, her gaze fixed on the closed door. "And I don't like him. He seems more upset about his routine being disturbed than his aunt being gone." "You don't like anyone who might be a suspect in one of your imaginary mysteries," Louise retorted. "For all we know, Mrs. Gable just went for an early morning walk." "Without telling anyone? And without her breakfast? It doesn't fit." Penny's eyes scanned the hallway carpet. "There has to be a clue." Just then, the door to the suite opened again. Mr. Harris and Mr. Croft came out, their faces grim. "Nothing," Mr. Croft said flatly. "The room is in perfect order. Her bed hasn't been slept in. She's gone." "We must call the police," Mr. Harris said, looking faint. "The publicity will be ruinous." As the two men hurried away toward the manager's office, Penny darted forward. Lying on the plush carpet, almost hidden by the edge of the doorframe, was a small, glittering object. With a swift movement, she bent down and scooped it up. "What is it?" Louise asked, hurrying to her side. Penny opened her palm. Lying in it was a single, diamond earring, shaped like a tiny star. "It belongs to Mrs. Gable," Penny breathed. "I saw her wearing them at dinner two nights ago. She must have dropped it when she left the room." "Or when she was taken from the room," Louise added in a hushed voice, her skepticism finally beginning to fade. "Exactly! Why would she be so careless with a diamond earring unless she was in a hurry, or struggling with someone?" Penny carefully wrapped the earring in her handkerchief and tucked it into her pocket. "This proves it, Lou. Something has happened to Mrs. Gable." The girls decided their first course of action was to find Jerry Livingston. They hoped he might take their discovery more seriously than the local police, who would likely be focused on keeping the hotel's name out of the papers. They found him not at the hotel, but down by the docks, overseeing the loading of some supplies onto a Coast Guard cutter. "Penny! Louise!" he called out when he saw them. "To what do I owe the pleasure? I hope you're not here to report another smugglers' cave." "It's worse than that, Jerry," Penny said, her voice low and serious. "It's a kidnapping. Or a murder!" Jerry chuckled. "Hold on, Penny. Start from the beginning." Quickly, Penny recounted the story of Mrs. Gable's disappearance, Mr. Croft's strange attitude, and their discovery of the earring. She finished by presenting the diamond star for his inspection. Jerry examined the earring, his expression growing more thoughtful. "This does change things a bit," he admitted. "A woman like Mrs. Gable wouldn't just lose something like this. But it's still not enough to go on, Penny. We can't launch a full blown investigation based on a missing earring and your intuition." "But Jerry!" "I'm not dismissing it," he said, holding up a hand. "I'll talk to the local chief of police myself. I'll make sure they take it seriously. But my hands are tied with this B4 investigation. It's reached a critical stage. I need you girls to do me a favor." "Anything!" Penny said eagerly. "Stay out of it," he said bluntly. "This could be dangerous. If Mrs. Gable was kidnapped, it wasn't by amateurs. You are to go back to the hotel, enjoy the beach, and let the proper authorities handle it. Is that clear?" Penny's face fell. "Yes, Jerry," she mumbled. "Good. Now I have to get back to work. I'll be in touch." As they walked back toward the hotel, Louise put a comforting arm on Penny's shoulder. "He's right, you know. This isn't like finding an old package in the sand. This is serious." "I know," Penny said glumly. "But what if the police are too slow? What if we're the only ones who can find her in time?" Her eyes strayed toward the Crystal Inn, its white facade gleaming in the sun. An idea began to form in her mind, a long shot, but the only one she had. "Jerry said we should let the proper authorities handle it," Penny said, a mischievous twinkle returning to her eyes. "He didn't say we couldn't do a little unofficial observing on our own." "Oh, Penny," Louise sighed. "What are you planning now?" "Mrs. Gable's room," Penny said decisively. "The police will have it sealed off soon. But Mr. Harris and Mr. Croft were just in there. They might have missed something. Something besides the earring." "And how do you propose we get in?" "I'm not sure yet," Penny admitted. "But where there's a will, there's a way!" Their chance came sooner than they expected. As they loitered in the second floor hallway, trying to appear casual, a maid came out of a nearby room with a large cart of linens. She left the door to the room she was cleaning ajar. Peeking past the cart, Penny could see right into the hallway and, more importantly, right at the door to Mrs. Gable's suite. It was unguarded. "Now's our chance," Penny whispered. "But the door is locked!" "Mr. Harris used a passkey," Penny reasoned. "Which means the maid must have one, too." While Louise nervously kept watch, Penny crept over to the unattended cleaning cart. Draped over the handle was the maid's apron, and hanging from a ring on its belt was a small collection of keys. With trembling fingers, Penny unhooked the master passkey. "Penny, hurry!" Louise urged. It took two tries, but the lock finally clicked. The girls slipped inside the suite and closed the door softly behind them. The air in the room was still and smelled faintly of lavender. It was, as Mr. Croft had said, in perfect order. The silken bedspread was smooth, the pillows plumped. A silver backed brush set lay neatly on the dressing table. It was as if the room's occupant had simply evaporated. "It's spooky," Louise whispered, staying close to the door. "It feels like a museum." "Let's be quick about it," Penny said, her professional instincts taking over. "You check the dressing table and the wardrobe. I'll check the desk." The desk was an elegant mahogany piece. A few letters were neatly stacked to one side, along with a silver pen and an empty inkwell. Penny opened the drawers. They contained nothing but hotel stationery and a few crisp blotters. It was on the last blotter that she saw it. Faint impressions, reversed, left by the last thing written. Holding it up to the light, Penny carefully deciphered the words. It wasn't a letter. It was just a few lines of verse. "Where sunlight cannot fade the rose," she read aloud, "And time itself is held in prose." "What does that mean?" Louise asked, looking up from a drawer filled with silk scarves. "It sounds like a riddle." "It is a riddle!" Penny exclaimed. "Mrs. Gable didn't just disappear. She left a clue!" She reread the lines. "Where sunlight cannot fade the rose, and time itself is held in prose." "A rose that doesn't fade," Louise pondered. "Like a painting of a rose? Or a carving?" "And time held in prose," Penny continued the thought. "Prose means writing. Like in a book!" They looked at each other, the same thought dawning on them at once. "The library!" they said in unison. The Crystal Inn boasted a small but well appointed library on the ground floor, a quiet, wood paneled room filled with leather bound volumes. They hurried down the stairs, their hearts pounding with a mixture of fear and excitement. The library was empty. Sunlight streamed through the tall windows, illuminating dust motes dancing in the air. "Now what?" Louise asked. "We can't possibly search every book in here." "We don't have to," Penny said, her eyes scanning the room. "We're looking for a rose." Her gaze settled on a large, leather bound scrapbook lying on a lectern in the corner. It was the hotel's guest book and historical record, filled with photographs and newspaper clippings from decades past. On its cover, embossed in faded gold, was the hotel's crest: a crystal "C" surrounded by a wreath of thorny roses. "Time itself is held in prose," Penny murmured, walking toward the book. She opened the heavy cover. The first few pages were filled with yellowed photographs of the hotel's construction and its grand opening. But tucked between two pages near the middle of the book was a small, sealed envelope. On it was written a single word: "Arthur." "It's from Mrs. Gable," Penny breathed. "She left it for her nephew." "But he didn't find it," Louise said. "We did." "Maybe she didn't want him to find it," Penny speculated. "Maybe she was testing him. And he failed." Carefully, Penny worked her thumbnail under the seal. Inside was not a letter, but a small, folded piece of paper. It looked like a receipt from a luggage checkroom at the local train station. And stapled to it was a key. "A key to a locker," Louise guessed. "What do you suppose is in it? Her jewels?" "I don't know," Penny said, her mind racing. "But I think we're about to find out." Just as she spoke, the doors to the library swung open. Arthur Croft stood there, his handsome face twisted into a mask of fury. "I thought I might find you two snoops in here," he snarled. "Give me that." He lunged for the paper in Penny's hand, but she was too quick. She dodged behind a large leather armchair. "It's no use, Mr. Croft," she said, her voice surprisingly steady. "We know everything." "You know nothing!" he spat, his eyes darting around the empty room. "Where is she? What did she do with the bonds?" "Bonds?" Penny repeated, stalling for time. "I don't know anything about bonds. We just wanted to know if your aunt was safe." "Safe?" he laughed, a bitter, unpleasant sound. "She's safer than she's ever been. She's run off. She took everything, the bearer bonds her husband left her, and she ran off, leaving me with nothing but a pile of debts." His eyes fell on the receipt in Penny's hand. "That's it. That's the locker key. She left it for me, not for you." He started to move around the chair toward them. Louise let out a small squeak of fear. Penny stood her ground, her mind working furiously. They were trapped. Suddenly, a calm, familiar voice cut through the tension. "Is there a problem here, Mr. Croft?" Jerry Livingston stood in the doorway, two uniformed police officers behind him. Mr. Croft froze, his face paling. "No. No problem, officer," he stammered. "Just a small misunderstanding with the girls." "It looked like more than a misunderstanding to me," Jerry said, his eyes hard. "Penny, Louise, are you all right?" "We're fine, Jerry," Penny said, a wave of relief washing over her. She stepped out from behind the chair and handed him the receipt and the key. "I think this belongs to you." An hour later, the four of them were sitting in the manager's office. Mrs. Gable, it turned out, had not been kidnapped at all. She had, as her nephew suspected, simply left. The local police chief had received a telegram from her that morning from a town fifty miles up the coast. She explained she had grown tired of her nephew's greed and had decided to take an extended holiday, leaving him to sort out his own finances. The locker at the train station contained not priceless jewels or bearer bonds, but a single, leather bound copy of "A Tale of Two Cities," with a note inside for her nephew. It read: "It is a far, far better thing that I do, than I have ever done." "She was leading him on a wild goose chase," Jerry explained, shaking his head in amusement. "Testing him, just as you said, Penny. The riddle, the guest book, the locker... she wanted to see if he was clever enough to figure it out, and if his concern for her was genuine." "And he failed on both counts," Louise added. "He was a nasty piece of work," Penny agreed. "But what about the spies, Jerry? And Mr. Voice from the Cave?" "That," Jerry said, his smile fading, "is still an ongoing concern. In fact, I came here to tell you there's been a development." He leaned forward. "We picked up another broadcast last night. It was very short, but we got a partial fix on it. And the direction points somewhere very, very close to the Crystal Inn." Penny's eyes lit up. The mystery of the missing aunt was solved, but a far greater one remained. And it was closer than they had ever imagined. "Don't worry, Jerry," she said, her voice filled with renewed purpose. "We'll be careful." Jerry looked from Penny's determined face to Louise's resigned one and sighed. "That's what I'm afraid of."
