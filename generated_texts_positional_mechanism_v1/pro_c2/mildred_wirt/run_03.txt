"Are you Ole Swanson?" Penny asked.

"Yeah. Sara said a friend of hers wanted to talk. Said it was worth my while." He looked her up and down. "You're just a kid."

"I'm older than I look," Penny said coolly. "And I can pay. I need a way into the Fergus hotel tonight. Through the service entrance. And I need to know the staff schedule for the west wing."

Ole's jaw dropped. "You want to what? Lady, do you know what Mr. Maxwell would do to me if he found out? I'd be fired. Worse, he'd make sure I never got another job in this county."

"He won't find out," Penny said, trying to sound more confident than she felt. "It will be late at night. And I'll pay you fifty dollars."

The young man's eyes widened. Fifty dollars was more than two weeks' pay for him. He licked his lips, his fear warring with greed.

"Fifty dollars?" he stammered. "For what? What are you going to do in there?"

"I just need to look around," Penny said vaguely. "In the new wing. I won't take anything or break anything. I just need to be inside for about an hour."

"The new wing," Ole muttered, shaking his head. "That's where he has his private games. There's a guard there all night. You can't get past him."

"Let me worry about the guard," Penny insisted. "Can you get me in the back door? The one by the kitchens?"

Ole hesitated for a long time, wrestling with his decision. Finally, he gave a slow, reluctant nod. "The door ain't locked until midnight, when the last kitchen shift leaves," he said in a low voice. "I get off at eleven. I can prop it open with a block of wood before I go. But you're on your own after that. The night watchman makes his rounds at one o'clock."

"That's perfect," Penny said, her heart leaping with success. "And the guard at the Green Room? When does he change shifts?"

"He doesn't," Ole replied. "It's the same man, Frank. He's there from eight at night until six in the morning. He's loyal to Mr. Maxwell."

Penny's face fell slightly. Frank was the same guard who had escorted her out. He would recognize her instantly. Her plan had a major flaw. She would have to think of a way to distract him, a very good way.

"All right," she said, pushing the problem from her mind for the moment. "Meet me by the kitchen door at eleven thirty. I'll give you half the money then, and the other half when I get out safely."

"No," Ole said, shaking his head. "I ain't meeting you. It's too risky. I'll prop the door at eleven, like I said. You get there after I'm gone. Leave the money for me under the third rain barrel from the door. All of it. I ain't trusting you to pay me after."

Penny considered this. It was a risk, but she had little choice. "All right," she agreed. She pulled her wallet from her pocket and was relieved to see she had just enough cash from her travel allowance. "The door will be propped open at eleven. I get in, and I leave the money under the third barrel."

"That's the deal," Ole confirmed, already starting to back away. "And if you get caught, you don't know me. You never saw me. Understand?"

"I understand," Penny said.

Without another word, Ole Swanson turned and hurried away, disappearing around the front of the store. Penny let out a long, shaky breath. Phase two of her plan was in motion. Now she only had to figure out what to do about a loyal guard, a locked door, and a very angry Harvey Maxwell. She walked to the hardware store, her mind already working on the next, most dangerous, part of the puzzle.

Inside the Pine Top Hardware store, the air was warm and smelled pleasantly of sawdust and metal. A pot-bellied stove glowed in the center of the room, and the proprietor, a stout man with spectacles perched on his nose, gave Penny a brief nod before returning to his newspaper. She wandered the aisles, pretending to be a casual browser, though her mind was racing.

What could she possibly use to distract a guard for ten minutes? Her eyes scanned shelves laden with hammers, ropes, and boxes of nails. Nothing there suggested a subtle diversion. She passed a section of plumbing supplies. An idea, vague at first, began to take shape. It was a risky plan, one that could easily fail, but it was better than trying to face down the formidable guard, Frank.

She selected a coil of thin, flexible copper tubing, a small metal funnel, and a can of thick machine oil. To complete her purchases, she added a ball of twine and a small, powerful flashlight, explaining to the man at the counter that her own had stopped working. He barely looked up as he totaled her items.

"That will be two dollars and forty cents," he mumbled.

Penny paid him and tucked the brown paper parcel under her arm. Outside, the afternoon sun was beginning its slow descent behind the snow-covered peaks. She felt a prickle of excitement mixed with fear. There was no turning back now.

She skied back to the Downey lodge at a brisk pace, her thoughts consumed with the night's plan. Mrs. Downey was in the main lodge, supervising the polishing of the great stone fireplace.

"Penny, dear, you're just in time for hot chocolate," she called out cheerfully. "And there's a letter for you. The mail carrier just brought it up from the village."

Penny thanked her and took the offered cup, her hands warming around the thick ceramic mug. The letter, she saw, was from her father. She tore it open, feeling a familiar mix of pride and affection as she read his neat, hurried script. He wrote of mundane matters at the newspaper office, but she could read between the lines. He was worried about her, and he trusted her judgment.

"Tell that old bear Peter Jasko that if he keeps his granddaughter locked up, she'll turn into a sour pickle," he had scrawled at the bottom. "And you be careful, Penny. Maxwell is a cornered badger. They're most dangerous when they think they have nothing left to lose."

His words were a sobering reminder of the risk she was about to take. She folded the letter and put it in her pocket. "I will be, Dad," she whispered to herself.

That evening, a light snow began to fall, muffling the world in a blanket of white. Penny feigned a headache after dinner and retired to her room early, claiming she needed a good night's rest. She laid out her dark ski pants and sweater, then checked the contents of her parcel from the hardware store. Everything was in order.

At a quarter past ten, there was a soft tapping on her window pane. Penny's heart leaped into her throat. She pulled back the curtain to see Sara Jasko perched precariously on a snow-covered branch of the pine tree outside.

"Let me in!" Sara hissed, her breath fogging in the cold air. "Grandfather is snoring like a freight train. I couldn't stand it another second."

Penny hesitated. Sara's presence was a complication she could not afford. "Sara, you shouldn't be here," she whispered, sliding the window open just enough to talk. "If your grandfather wakes up..."

"He won't," Sara said confidently, already trying to swing a leg over the sill. "I'm an expert at this. Now where are we going? You said you had important work, but that was this morning. Tonight is for fun!"

"I can't tonight, Sara. Really," Penny insisted, blocking the girl's entry. "I have... I still have work to do."

Sara's face fell. "Work? At this hour? What sort of work?" she asked, a suspicious gleam in her eyes. "It sounds more like an adventure. Are you holding out on me, Penny Parker?"

"Of course not," Penny lied, thinking fast. "It's for Mrs. Downey. I'm helping her inventory the linen supply for the new wing. It's dreadfully boring. You would hate it."

"Linen?" Sara scoffed. "That doesn't sound like you at all. You're a terrible fibber, Penny." She peered past Penny into the room and her eyes lit upon the flashlight and twine on the bed. "And what's all that for? Counting sheets in the dark?"

Penny knew she was caught. She could either send Sara away, and risk the headstrong girl trying to follow her or investigate on her own, or she could trust her. Her father's words about cornered badgers echoed in her mind. Perhaps having a lookout wasn't such a bad idea.

"Alright," Penny sighed, making a quick decision. "But you have to promise you will do exactly as I say. This is serious, Sara. It's dangerous."

Sara's eyes widened with delight. "I promise!" she breathed. "Cross my heart. What is it? What are we doing?"

Quickly, Penny explained her plan to get inside the Fergus hotel and investigate the Green Room. She left out the part about paying Ole Swanson, simply saying she had found a way in.

"We need to create a diversion to draw the guard away from his post," Penny concluded, holding up the oil can and tubing. "That's where this comes in."

Sara looked at the strange collection of items. "A leak? That's your brilliant plan?" she said, sounding disappointed. "I thought we were going to set off fireworks or something."

"Fireworks will get us thrown in jail, Sara. A small, annoying leak will just get the guard to leave his chair for a few minutes. That's all I need."

Sara considered this for a moment. "It's clever," she admitted reluctantly. "But what's my part in all this?"

"You're the lookout," Penny said firmly. "You stay outside. If you see anyone coming, especially the night watchman, you have to warn me. A low whistle, like a whippoorwill. Can you do that?"

"I can do a perfect whippoorwill," Sara boasted. "But waiting outside is no fun. I want to see what's in this Green Room, too."

"If everything goes well, you will," Penny promised, though she had no intention of letting Sara inside the hotel. "But for now, you are my eyes and ears. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Sara said, her disappointment vanishing as she embraced her important role.

An hour later, two shadowy figures slipped through the softly falling snow toward the back of the Fergus hotel. Penny's heart pounded against her ribs. The kitchen wing was dark and silent. As Ole had promised, the third rain barrel from the door marked the spot. Penny tucked the envelope containing the fifty dollars beneath it, feeling a pang of regret at the expense.

She moved to the door and pulled gently. It swung inward with a soft creak. A block of wood was wedged at the bottom. Ole had kept his word.

"Wait here," Penny whispered to Sara, who was shivering with a mixture of cold and excitement. "Stay in the shadows of the woodshed. And remember, only whistle if there's real trouble."

Sara gave a brisk nod, her face serious in the dim light.

Penny slipped inside, pulling the door closed behind her. She was in a cavernous kitchen. The air was thick with the lingering smells of cooked food. Moonlight filtering through the high windows cast long, eerie shadows across the stainless steel counters and hulking stoves. She clicked on her flashlight, keeping the beam pointed at the floor, and began to navigate her way through the maze of cooking equipment.

She found a service corridor and crept along it, her soft-soled boots making no sound. The hotel was unnervingly quiet. All she could hear was the hum of a distant generator and the frantic beating of her own heart. She soon recognized the hallway leading to the new wing. At the far end, she could see a faint line of light from under the door where the guard, Frank, sat.

Penny backtracked and found a small alcove, half hidden by a tall stack of folded laundry carts. It was about fifty feet from the guard's post, close enough for a noise to travel but far enough to be out of his immediate line of sight. It was perfect.

Working quickly and silently, she fashioned her device. She used the twine to suspend the oil can from a high shelf. Below it, she placed a large metal baking pan she had "borrowed" from the kitchen. She carefully positioned the thin copper tubing to act as a siphon, piercing the can's lid with the point of her ski knife. A dark, thick drop of oil fell, landing on the metal pan with a distinct PING.

Penny adjusted the flow until the drops were falling at a steady, maddeningly slow pace. Ping... ping... ping. It wasn't loud, but in the silence of the night, it was a sound that could not be ignored. Satisfied, she retreated into the shadows at the corner of the main corridor to wait.

The minutes crawled by. Penny watched the sliver of light under the guard's door. For a long time, nothing happened. She began to fear her plan had failed. Perhaps he was wearing headphones, or had fallen asleep.

Then, she heard the scrape of a chair. The door to the wing opened, spilling a rectangle of light into the hall. Frank stepped out. He was a large man, and he looked annoyed.

"What in thunder is that?" he muttered to himself, listening.

Ping... ping... ping.

He started down the hall, his heavy footsteps echoing in the quiet. He was moving away from her, toward the sound. "Now's my chance," Penny thought.

She darted across the corridor to the door Frank had left ajar and slipped into the private wing. The guard's empty chair sat before the infamous Green Door. To her dismay, she saw that the door was not only closed, but sported a shiny new brass lock. Maxwell was taking no chances.

This was a setback she hadn't fully anticipated. Her mind raced. At the hardware store, on a whim, she had also purchased a thin, flexible palette knife used for scraping paint. It was a long shot.

From down the hall, she could hear Frank muttering angrily as he investigated the source of the dripping oil. "Just oil? What kind of prank is this?"

Penny knew she had only moments. She took out the thin blade and inserted it into the space between the door and the frame. She had read about this sort of thing in detective novels. Gently, she worked the blade against the lock's tongue. It didn't budge. She applied more pressure. Sweat beaded on her forehead. The sound of Frank's footsteps started again, this time coming back. He had given up on the leak.

With a surge of desperate strength, she jiggled the knife, and with a soft click, the latch slid back. She did not dare to breathe. She turned the knob, opened the door, and slipped inside, pulling it shut just as Frank's shadow fell across the hallway.

She was in the Green Room.

It was not at all what she had expected. The walls were indeed painted a deep, lurid green, but the room was not a place for gambling. It was an office, and a highly sophisticated one. In the center stood a large desk, but against one wall was a complex array of machinery that Penny recognized at once. It was a shortwave radio transmitter and receiver, far more powerful than any amateur rig. Next to it sat a telegraph key and a machine that looked like a stock-market ticker tape.

Harvey Maxwell was not running a simple gambling den. He was running an intelligence operation.

On the desk, an open ledger lay under a pool of light from a green-shaded lamp. Penny rushed over to it. The pages were filled with columns of numbers and strange letter combinations. It was a code, just as she had suspected. This was the proof her father needed to expose Maxwell's scheme, whatever it was. He was likely intercepting private financial information and using it to play the stock market, or selling it to unscrupulous rivals.

She knew she could not take the ledger. Its absence would be noticed immediately. She had her small notepad and a pencil. She would have to copy a section of it. She quickly opened to a recent page and began to write, her pencil flying across the paper, transcribing the meaningless-looking groups of letters and numbers.

Suddenly, she froze. From outside the door, she heard Frank clear his throat. Then, another sound. Footsteps. A second person was coming.

"Everything quiet tonight, Frank?" a familiar voice asked. It was Harvey Maxwell.

"Quiet as a tomb, Mr. Maxwell," the guard replied. "Except for a strange dripping noise down the hall. I think one of the kitchen boys was playing a prank."

"I'll have Ole Swanson's hide for that," Maxwell snarled. "Did you check the new lock on this door?"

"Yes, sir. Solid as a rock."

Penny's blood ran cold. She was trapped. She looked frantically around the room for a place to hide. There was a tall filing cabinet in one corner, and heavy velvet curtains over the single window. The curtains were her only chance. She dove behind them, pressing herself flat against the cold wall, her notepad clutched in her hand.

She heard a key turn in the lock. The door opened.

"I just want to double check the transmission logs from yesterday," Maxwell said, his voice closer now. "Something about the closing price on United Steel felt... off."

Penny held her breath. Through a tiny gap in the curtains, she could see Maxwell walk to the desk. He sat down in the chair she had vacated only seconds before and pulled the ledger toward him. Her heart hammered so loudly she was sure he must hear it. If he noticed the fresh pencil shavings on the desk, or the slight disarray of the papers, it would all be over.

He flipped through a few pages, his brow furrowed in concentration. He seemed not to notice anything amiss. Penny began to hope she might remain undiscovered.

Then, from outside, came a clear, piercing whistle. A whippoorwill, calling in the dead of winter.

Sara.

Maxwell's head snapped up. "What was that?"

"Sounded like a bird, sir," Frank said from the doorway.

"There are no whippoorwills in Pine Top in January," Maxwell said, his voice sharp with suspicion. He stood up and walked toward the window where Penny was hiding.

This is it, she thought, bracing herself for discovery. My lucky day has run out.

Maxwell reached for the curtains.

His fingers, thick and ringed, brushed against the heavy velvet. Penny squeezed her eyes shut, her knuckles white around the notepad. Any second now, the curtain would be torn aside. Any second, she would be facing the furious hotel owner. Her heart was a wild bird trapped in her ribs.

Thwack!

A sudden, sharp noise cracked against the windowpane, no louder than a snapped twig but startlingly clear in the quiet room. Maxwell flinched back, his hand dropping from the curtain. A spidery starburst of packed snow slid down the outside of the glass, leaving a wet smear.

"What in thunderation was that?" Maxwell spun toward the window, his face a mask of fury. He peered out into the darkness, but there was nothing to see but silent, falling flakes.

"Sounds like more of them kitchen boys," offered Frank from the doorway, his voice uncertain. "They get rowdy sometimes, Mr. Maxwell. Throwin' snowballs at the windows."

"Snowballs," Maxwell repeated, his voice dripping with contempt. "First a phantom bird, now snowballs. This hotel is turning into a nursery." He stalked to the window and tried to shove it open, but it was sealed shut against the winter cold. He rattled the sash in frustration. "I'll have the lot of them fired. Every last one."

This was her chance. While Maxwell's back was turned and his rage was directed at the unseen prankster, Penny took a silent, shallow breath. She slid sideways from behind the curtain, her feet in their soft ski boots making no sound on the thick carpet. Frank, the guard, was still standing in the open doorway, but his attention was fixed on his employer. He was watching Maxwell's tirade, a worried expression on his face.

Penny moved like a shadow. She did not dare to look at the guard. She kept her eyes on the sliver of hallway visible past his shoulder. One step. Two steps. She was past the desk, past the looming filing cabinet. She reached the doorway, her shoulder nearly brushing Frank's arm. He never turned. With a final, desperate burst of courage, she slipped into the hall and pulled the door quietly to, until she heard the soft click of the latch.

She did not wait to hear more. Her legs, which had felt like lead a moment before, were now wonderfully light. She flew down the narrow hall, past the empty guard's chair, and pushed through the unmarked door into the main corridor of the second floor. She didn't pause to listen, didn't glance back. The elevator was out of the question. Spying the red glow of the exit sign above the stairwell, she ran toward it.

The concrete stairs echoed with her hurried footsteps. She took them two at a time, her hand skimming the cool metal railing. One flight, two flights. She burst into the lobby, expecting to see it crowded. But the large room was nearly deserted at this hour. A lone clerk was sorting mail behind the front desk, and two men in heavy coats were talking quietly by the fireplace. Neither of them looked up. Harvey Maxwell's private office was dark.

Penny did not slow her pace. She strode purposefully across the lobby, as if on an urgent errand. She pushed through the heavy oak and glass doors and out into the biting cold of the winter night.

The air was a shock, but a welcome one. It was clean and sharp, and it cleared her head. She took a deep gulp, her breath pluming in a white cloud before her. The snow was still falling, thick and silent, blanketing the world in a pristine layer of white.

"Took you long enough!" a familiar voice whispered from the shadows of the hotel porch.

Penny jumped, her hand flying to her throat. Sara Jasko stepped out from behind a large pillar, a mischievous grin lighting up her face. In her hand was a half-melted snowball.

"Sara! You nearly frightened the life out of me," Penny gasped, a mixture of relief and exasperation flooding through her. "That was you?"

"Of course it was me," Sara said proudly. "I saw the lights go on in the Green Room from my window. When you didn't come out, I figured you needed a little help. Did my whippoorwill call work?"

"It worked all too well," Penny said, grabbing Sara's arm and pulling her away from the hotel and into the deeper shadows of the pine trees lining the drive. "It brought Maxwell directly to the window where I was hiding. Your snowball saved me."

"I thought it was a rather brilliant idea," Sara declared. "Grandfather always says I have more nerve than sense. Where to now, detective Parker? Back to my prison tower?"

"Not yet," Penny said, her mind racing. "We need to go somewhere we can talk. Somewhere safe." She looked back at the Fergus hotel, a formidable block of light and shadow against the dark mountain. "He'll have guards looking for a girl in a red ski suit soon enough."

"The woodshed," Sara said at once. "It's not exactly the Fergus lobby, but it's warm enough, and no one will find us there."

They made their way quickly and silently through the snow, following the path Penny had taken hours before, which now seemed like a lifetime ago. The Jasko cabin was dark, a single flickering light visible in the loft window. Sara led the way to the woodshed, the familiar scent of pine and sawdust greeting them as she pulled the heavy door open. Inside, they found an old lantern. Penny cupped her hands around a match, her fingers trembling slightly, and soon a warm, yellow glow pushed back the darkness.

"All right," Sara said, perching on a stack of firewood. "Out with it. What did you find? Was it worth almost getting caught by that old grump?"

Penny sat down on a chopping block opposite her friend. She pulled the small notepad and pencil from her ski jacket pocket. "I think so, Sara. I think it was."

She opened the notepad to the pages she had filled with her frantic scribbling. In the flickering lamplight, the columns of letters and numbers looked even more mysterious.

GXR-88-PL.
BBD-17-UST.
Talon-4-CFG.

"What is all that?" Sara asked, leaning forward and peering at the page. "It looks like grandfather's list for the hardware store."

"It's a code," Penny explained. "Maxwell is running some sort of secret operation out of that room. He's intercepting information and selling it. I'm sure of it." She pointed to the second line. "BBD-17-UST. I heard Maxwell mention United Steel before he came into the room. I'll bet you anything 'UST' stands for United Steel."

"And BBD?" Sara wrinkled her nose. "Big bad deal?"

Penny laughed, the tension of the last hour beginning to ease. "I don't think so. It's a code name for something. Or someone. If only I could figure out what it means. My father could decipher this in a minute, but I can't very well send it to him."

Sara took the notepad and studied the list, her expression surprisingly serious. She tapped a finger on the page. "GXR... Talon... BBD. You know, 'BBD' sounds a little like 'Bluebird'."

Penny looked up. "Bluebird?"

"Yes. Bluebird," Sara repeated. "That's what everyone in Pine Top calls Jimmy Gannet. He's the pilot who flies the mail and supplies in from the city. They call him that because of his plane, a little blue and white thing. He's always talking about his 'bluebird'."

Penny stared at her friend, a thrill of excitement running through her. "Sara, you're a genius!"

"I know," Sara said with a grin. "It's a terrible burden. So, 'Bluebird' is Jimmy Gannet. Does that help?"

"It helps immensely!" Penny took the notepad back, her mind alight with new possibilities. "If 'BBD' is the pilot, then he's Maxwell's courier. He's flying the information out. 'PL'... 'CFG'... those must be the buyers. And the numbers, '17' and '4', could be prices or dates." She looked at Sara, her eyes shining. "We have to get to that airfield."

Sara's grin widened. "An adventure! I knew you wouldn't disappoint me. We'll ski over first thing in the morning. We can say we're exploring the north ridge."

"It's too dangerous for you, Sara," Penny said, though her words lacked conviction. "If your grandfather finds out..."

"If grandfather finds out, I'll be grounded until spring," Sara finished. "But he won't. He's chopping wood on the south slope all week, remember? It's the perfect opportunity. Besides," she added, her tone becoming more serious, "that Maxwell fellow gives me the creeps. And he's got you in his sights. You need a lookout, Penny. And I'm the best there is."

Penny hesitated for only a moment. Sara was headstrong and reckless, but she was also brave and loyal. And her knowledge of Pine Top and its people had just given them their first real break.

"All right," Penny said, a smile tugging at her lips. "Be ready at dawn. And Sara? Try not to throw any more snowballs unless I give the signal."

"No promises," Sara laughed, her eyes sparkling in the lantern light. "No promises at all."

The next morning broke clear and cold. A fresh blanket of snow lay over everything, muffling all sound and glittering under the early morning sun. True to her word, Sara was waiting, skis and poles in hand, as Penny skied up to the Jasko cabin. The loft window was closed and dark.

"Grandfather is already gone," Sara whispered, her voice full of excitement. "He left a note saying he'd be back after sunset. We have the whole day!"

"Let's not waste it, then," Penny replied, trying to sound more confident than she felt. The memory of Maxwell's enraged face was still vivid in her mind.

They set off at a brisk pace, their skis cutting twin tracks in the pristine snow. The airfield was on a high plateau several miles north of the village, a place few skiers ever visited. Sara, who knew the mountains like the back of her hand, led the way through thick stands of pine and across open meadows. The air was still and the only sound was the rhythmic swish of their skis.

"It feels strange to be having so much fun when we're on a dangerous mission," Sara remarked after an hour of hard skiing.

"Who said it was fun?" Penny retorted, though a smile played on her lips. The beauty of the morning was undeniable, and being out in the open, moving and active, was far better than hiding in a woodshed.

At last, Sara held up a hand and pointed. "There. Through the trees."

Penny followed her gaze. Below them, nestled in a wide clearing, was the Pine Top airfield. It was little more than a single, snow-plowed runway, a fuel depot, and a long, low building that served as a hangar and office. A thin plume of smoke rose from a chimney, and a small, blue and white plane was parked near the hangar doors. The Bluebird.

"We need to get closer," Penny said, her heart beginning to beat faster. "But without being seen."

"Follow me," Sara whispered. "There's a ridge that overlooks the whole field. We'll have a perfect view."

They left their skis hidden in a snowdrift and crept forward on foot, moving from the cover of one massive pine tree to the next. The ridge gave them an excellent vantage point. They were a hundred yards away, but they could see the entire area clearly. A man in greasy overalls was working on the engine of a tractor near the hangar. As they watched, the door to the office opened and two men stepped out. One was the pilot, Jimmy Gannet, a wiry man in a leather jacket and flight cap. The other was Harvey Maxwell.

Penny drew in a sharp breath. "It's him."

Maxwell and the pilot stood talking for several minutes. Maxwell seemed to be giving instructions, gesturing emphatically with his hands. The pilot just nodded, his face impassive. Then, Maxwell reached inside his heavy overcoat and pulled out a slim leather briefcase. He handed it to Gannet. The pilot took it, checked the latch, and then nodded again. Maxwell clapped him on the shoulder, said a few more words, and then turned and walked toward a large black car parked at the far end of the field.

"That's it," Penny whispered, fumbling in her jacket for the small camera she always carried. "That's the handover."

She raised the camera, trying to steady her hands. The men were small at this distance, but a photograph of Maxwell handing that briefcase to the pilot would be undeniable proof. She squinted through the viewfinder, centering the two figures. The light was good. She held her breath and pressed the shutter. The quiet click of the camera seemed as loud as a gunshot in the silent woods.

"Did you get it?" Sara breathed.

"I think so," Penny said, carefully winding the film forward. "One more, just to be sure."

She raised the camera again. But as she looked through the viewfinder, she saw something that made her blood run cold. Another figure had emerged from the trees on the far side of the airfield. It was a woman, dressed in a stylish ski outfit. Even from a distance, Penny recognized the determined stride and the expensive fur-trimmed hood.

Francine Sellberg.

The reporter was not looking at Maxwell or the pilot. She was looking directly up at the ridge where Penny and Sara were hiding. She had her own camera raised, but it was not pointed at the airfield. It was pointed directly at them.

"She's seen us!" Penny gasped, lowering her camera and grabbing Sara's arm. "We have to go. Now!"

They scrambled back from the edge of the ridge, snatching up their skis. As they hurried to buckle them on, they heard a shout from below. It was Francine's voice, carrying clearly in the cold air.

"Penny Parker! I see you up there! What have you found?"

"Don't answer!" Penny urged, pushing off with her poles. "Just ski!"

They plunged back into the forest, no longer caring about noise. The only thing that mattered was speed. Branches whipped at their faces as they raced down the mountainside, their flight made desperate by the knowledge that they had been discovered. They had their proof, but now their rival knew they had it. And Francine was not the only one who had seen them. As they risked a glance back, they saw the black car at the airfield screech to a halt. The door opened and Harvey Maxwell stepped out, his face turned toward the mountain, his expression one of pure, cold rage. He had seen them too.

"Whether you came for the skiing or not, you've meddled in my affairs for the last time," Maxwell snarled. His fingers tightened on the green card, crumpling it into a useless wad. "Now I've warned you. The next time I see you, you'll have more to worry about than a scolding."

As she passed the staircase, she saw Francine Sellberg watching her from the landing below. A triumphant, knowing smirk was on the reporter's face. Francine had seen it all. She had waited, knowing Penny would be turned away, and was now savoring the moment. Penny held her head high and walked past without a word. She would not give her rival the satisfaction of seeing her distress.
"Tough luck, Parker," Francine called after her. "Looks like you're out of the game."
Penny did not reply. She crossed the lobby, retrieved her skis, and stepped out into the brilliant sunshine. Her feeling of cheerfulness from earlier that morning had completely evaporated, replaced by a cold knot of frustration and anger. "So much for my lucky day," she muttered to herself as she buckled on her skis.
Her mind raced as she poled away from the hotel. Maxwell knew who she was. He had connected her to her father's newspaper. Francine was shadowing her every move, ready to capitalize on any mistake. And the secret of the Green Room was still locked away.
"But he doesn't know about the airfield," Penny reasoned, a small spark of hope returning. "He doesn't know I have the photograph."
The film in her little camera was now the most important piece of evidence she possessed. It was the one advantage she held over both Maxwell and Francine. She had to get it developed, and quickly.
Her first instinct was to ski back to the Downey lodge, but she hesitated. Mrs. Downey was kind, but the lodge was a public place. It was the first place Maxwell or Francine would look for her. She needed somewhere safe, somewhere private, to plan her next move.
There was only one other place to go. Reluctantly, she turned her skis toward the Jasko cabin. It was a risk, especially if Peter Jasko was home. But Sara was her friend, and right now, she needed a friend more than ever.
The trip up the mountain was slow and tiring. The elation of her earlier descent was gone, and each push of her poles felt like a heavy burden. When she finally reached the clearing where the cabin stood, she was relieved to see no smoke coming from the chimney. Peter Jasko was likely still out chopping wood.
She approached the cabin cautiously and rapped on the door. There was no answer.
"Sara?" she called softly. "It's Penny."
She waited a moment, then went to the woodshed, her heart pounding. The key was gone.
A terrible thought seized her. Had Peter Jasko discovered the key and taken it? Had Sara been found out?
Penny was about to turn away in despair when she heard a faint tapping sound. It came from above. She looked up at the loft window. It was closed tight, but as she watched, a small, dark object appeared in the corner of the pane, tapping rhythmically. It was the end of a ski pole.
"Sara!" Penny whispered urgently.
The tapping stopped. A moment later, a corner of the window was scraped clear of frost, and Sara's eye appeared, peering down at her. The window slowly creaked open a few inches.
"Penny! Thank goodness," Sara's voice was a strained whisper. "I was afraid you were Grandfather."
"The key is gone," Penny said. "I thought maybe he'd found it."
"He did," Sara said miserably. "He came back early, just after I got back inside. He found my tracks leading to the woodshed. He was furious. He took the key and said I was to stay up here until I learned to obey."
"Oh, Sara, I'm so sorry. This is all my fault."
"It's not your fault I'm a prisoner," Sara sighed. "It's my fault for getting caught. But what are you doing back so soon? Did you deliver the message? What did Mrs. Downey say?"
Penny glanced around nervously. The woods felt quiet, but she couldn't shake the feeling of being watched. "It's a long story. I need to talk to you. Is there any way for you to get down?"
"I haven't had time to make my rope ladder yet," Sara fretted. "And Grandfather locked the trapdoor from the outside and put a bolt on it. I'm sealed in tighter than a canned sardine." She paused. "What's wrong, Penny? You look frightened."
"I am," Penny admitted. "Things have gotten very complicated. And dangerous."
Briefly, she recounted her disastrous visit to the Fergus hotel, her confrontation with Harvey Maxwell, and the smug triumph of Francine Sellberg.
Sara listened, her eyes wide. "He kicked you out? Just like that?"
"He knows who I am, Sara. He called my father's paper a yellow sheet." Penny's voice trembled with indignation. "And Francine saw the whole thing. She's probably at the hotel right now, trying to get in with Maxwell by telling him everything she knows about me."
"This is dreadful," Sara said. "But you still have the picture, don't you? The one from the airfield?"
"Yes, it's in my camera." Penny patted the pocket of her ski jacket. "It's the only thing I have left."
"Then you have to get it developed! It's proof!"
"I know. But I don't dare go back to the lodge. Maxwell might be watching it. And I can't go into Pine Top. He could have someone there looking for me too."
The two girls fell silent, the problem hanging between them in the cold winter air. Penny stared at the snow, her mind a blank. Sara chewed on her lip, her gaze fixed on the woods beyond the cabin.
Suddenly, Sara's face brightened. "I have an idea!" she whispered excitedly. "It's risky, but it might work."
"What is it?"
"Old Man Hemlock," Sara said. "He lives in a little cabin about two miles up the North Ridge trail. He's a prospector, a hermit mostly. He does his own photography."
"He does?" Penny asked, intrigued.
"Yes! He develops his own plates and films. He says the chemists in town ruin his pictures of rock formations. He has a whole darkroom setup in his pantry. He's a bit of a crank, but he's honest. He wouldn't care two cents about Harvey Maxwell."
Penny considered the idea. A private darkroom, far from prying eyes, was exactly what she needed. "Will he help us? I'm a stranger."
"He knows my grandfather," Sara said. "And besides, he loves a good story. If you tell him you're a newspaper reporter trying to expose a scoundrel, he'll probably think it's the most exciting thing that's happened all winter. The only problem is getting you the film."
"I can't just leave the camera with you," Penny said. "It's too risky. And I'll need it to take more pictures."
"You won't have to," Sara said, her eyes gleaming with mischief. "We'll have to get the film out right here. You'll pass it up to me, and I'll hide it. Then, when Grandfather goes out again, I'll slip out and take it to Old Man Hemlock myself."
"Slip out? But you're locked in!"
"Grandfather can't watch me every second," Sara declared. "He's going to Pine Top for supplies tomorrow morning. He said so. It's the perfect chance. You'll just have to trust me with the film, Penny."
Penny hesitated. The thought of separating from her only piece of evidence was terrifying. But Sara was right. There was no other way. She couldn't risk developing it herself, and she couldn't trust anyone in town.
"All right," she said, making her decision. "But how do I get it up to you?"
Sara's gaze fell on Penny's ski pole. "Your pole basket," she said. "Take off the strap. I'll lower a piece of string, and you can tie the film canister to it. I'll pull it up."
"It's broad daylight, Sara. What if your grandfather comes back?"
"He won't," Sara said with confidence. "He took his lunch and his best axe. When he goes to chop in the north woods, he's gone for hours. We have to be quick."
Trusting her friend, Penny unbuckled her skis and moved to a spot directly beneath the loft window. She took off her mittens, her fingers instantly numb in the frigid air. Carefully, she unzipped her pocket and took out the small camera. She worked the latch with fumbling fingers, opened the back, and pressed the release button. The roll of film popped out. It felt small and incredibly vulnerable in her palm.
Just then, a piece of white string, weighted with a nail, dangled down from the window above.
"Quickly!" Sara urged in a hiss.
Penny's hands were shaking, partly from cold and partly from nerves. She tied the small metal canister to the end of the string, making a double knot to be certain it was secure.
"Okay," she breathed, stepping back. "Pull it up."
The canister began its slow ascent, swaying slightly in the breeze. It looked like a tiny silver spider climbing a thread. Penny held her breath, her eyes glued to it. It rose past the first floor window, past the heavy log walls, up, up, up toward the waiting hands in the loft.
It was halfway there when a sharp, cracking sound split the air.
Both girls froze. The sound came from the woods, not far from the edge of the clearing. It was the snap of a heavy branch.
"Grandfather!" Sara gasped in a panicked whisper.
The film canister swung precariously, ten feet from the ground.
"Pull, Sara, pull!" Penny urged, her voice tight with fear.
Sara yanked on the string, but her sudden movement made the canister jerk wildly. It swung out, hit the log wall with a faint clink, and the knot Penny had tied so hastily began to slip.
"It's coming loose!" Penny cried.
Another sound came from the woods, closer this time. A man's cough.
There was no time. The canister slid free from the string and plummeted downwards. Penny lunged for it, her eyes wide with horror. She made a desperate, diving catch, her fingers closing around the cold metal just as her knees hit the hard-packed snow.
She scrambled to her feet, clutching the film, her heart hammering against her ribs. At the edge of the clearing, a figure emerged from the trees. It was not the tall, broad-shouldered form of Peter Jasko. It was a man in a stylish overcoat, his face pale with fury.
Harvey Maxwell.
He must have followed her from the hotel, tracked her up the mountain. He stood there for a second, his eyes fixing on the small object in Penny's hand. His expression shifted from anger to a cold, predatory focus. He knew what it was.
"Give me the film, Miss Parker," he said, his voice dangerously quiet.
Penny backed away, her mind racing. The cabin was behind her, but the door was locked. The woods were her only hope.
"I don't know what you're talking about," she stammered, shoving the canister deep into her jacket pocket.
"Don't play games with me," Maxwell said, taking a step forward. "I saw you at the airfield. I know what you have. I won't let a meddling little girl and her father's scandal sheet ruin years of work."
From the window above, Sara let out a small, terrified cry. Maxwell's eyes flickered upward for a second, then back to Penny.
"It seems your friend is trapped," he said with a cruel smile. "There's no one to help you. Now, be a sensible girl and hand it over."
He started walking toward her, his pace deliberate and menacing. Penny continued to back away, her boots slipping in the snow. Her skis lay on the ground several feet away. She would never reach them in time. She was trapped.
Her back hit the rough log wall of the cabin. There was nowhere else to go.
Maxwell advanced, his hand outstretched. "The film."
Penny's eyes darted around, searching for any escape, any weapon. Her gaze fell on the pile of firewood stacked neatly against the wall. Without a second thought, she bent down, grabbed a thick piece of split birch, and held it up like a club.
"Stay back!" she warned, her voice shaking but determined.
Maxwell stopped, a look of surprised annoyance on his face. "Put that down, you foolish girl. You don't want to make this worse for yourself."
"You come one step closer and I'll scream," Penny threatened. "Mr. Jasko will hear me. He's nearby."
Maxwell laughed, a short, unpleasant sound. "I doubt that. I've been watching this place for half an hour. You're all alone."
He took another step, and Penny tensed, ready to swing the log.
But before he could move again, a new voice boomed across the clearing, a voice as deep and resonant as a thunderclap.
"I would not take another step, if I were you."
Both Penny and Maxwell spun around. Standing by the woodshed, holding a double-bitted axe in one hand, was Peter Jasko. His face was a thunderous mask of cold fury, his pale blue eyes fixed on Maxwell. He had not come from the north woods. He must have been in the shed all along, sharpening his tools.
"Grandfather!" Sara cried out in relief from the window.
Peter Jasko's gaze did not waver from Maxwell. "Who are you?" he demanded. "And what business do you have threatening a guest on my property?"
Maxwell, for the first time, looked uncertain. He was a man used to authority in the city, but here, faced with a grim-faced mountaineer holding an axe, his power seemed to have vanished.
"This is a private matter," Maxwell said, trying to regain his composure. "This girl has stolen something that belongs to me."
"Is this true, Penny?" Jasko asked, his eyes briefly flicking to her.
"No, sir," Penny said, her voice clear. "He's lying. He's trying to take my camera film because it has pictures that will prove he's a criminal."
Jasko's jaw tightened. He took a heavy step forward, the axe held loosely but readily at his side. "I do not know what business you are in, mister. But here in the mountains, we do not take kindly to men who frighten young women. I suggest you leave my land. Now."
Maxwell stared at the axe, then at Jasko's unyielding face. He was clearly not a man to be reasoned with or intimidated. With a venomous look at Penny, he slowly began to back away.
"This isn't over," he snarled at her. "You can't hide forever."
He turned and stalked back toward the trees, his expensive shoes crunching angrily in the snow. In a moment, he was gone.
The clearing was silent except for the sound of Penny's own ragged breathing. Her arm ached, and she let the piece of firewood drop to the ground with a thud. She was safe, for now.
Peter Jasko strode over to her. His expression was still stern, but the immediate threat was gone from his eyes. "Are you alright, child?"
"Yes," Penny whispered. "Thank you, Mr. Jasko. You saved me."
He grunted, then looked up at the loft window where Sara's pale face was still visible. His frown returned.
"As for you, young lady," he said, his voice rumbling with displeasure. "We will have a long talk about locked doors and disobedience. But first, we will get you down from there."
He marched to the front door, produced a heavy, old-fashioned key from his pocket, and unlocked it. Then he went inside. Penny heard the sound of a heavy bolt being drawn back, and the creak of the trapdoor.
A moment later, Sara scrambled down the ladder, her face smudged with dust and tears. She ran to Penny and threw her arms around her.
"Oh, Penny, I was so scared! I saw him coming and I couldn't do anything!"
"It's alright," Penny said, patting her friend's back. "He's gone."
Peter Jasko came and stood before them, his tall frame filling the small cabin. He looked from his granddaughter's guilty face to Penny's shaken one.
"Inside, both of you," he said, his tone leaving no room for argument. "You will tell me everything. From the beginning."

The girls obeyed meekly. He remained standing, his powerful frame seeming to shrink the small space. His piercing blue eyes fixed upon his granddaughter.
"Sara. You will start."
Sara twisted her hands in her lap, refusing to meet his gaze. "I... I wanted to go skiing," she began in a small voice. "Mrs. Downey sent a message with Penny, about the bob-sled run."
"I am not interested in the bob-sled run," Jasko interrupted, his voice low and dangerous. "I am interested in how you came to be locked in the loft, and why a man was threatening your friend in my clearing."
"I made a rope ladder," Sara mumbled, her face flushing with shame. "I got out the window after you left to chop wood. I was going to meet Penny."
"A rope ladder," he repeated, the words sounding like a judgment. He took a deep, slow breath, and Penny feared he might explode with rage. Instead, when he spoke again, his voice was laced with a deep, weary disappointment that seemed far worse. "You deliberately disobeyed me. You risked your life climbing from that window. For what? A morning of sport?"
"I'm sorry, Grandfather," Sara whispered, tears welling in her eyes. "I just hate being cooped up."
"There are worse things than being cooped up," he said sternly. "As you have just seen. Now, what about this man?"
His gaze shifted to Penny. "He called you by name. He accused you of stealing from him."
Penny swallowed hard, knowing everything depended on what she said next. "My name is Penny Parker, Mr. Jasko. The man is Harvey Maxwell, the manager of the Fergus hotel. I did not steal from him, but I do have something he wants very badly."
From the pocket of her ski jacket, she carefully drew out the small metal camera. Its side was dented from where Maxwell had grabbed it.
"It's this," she said, holding it up. "Or rather, the film that is inside it."
Jasko's heavy brows drew together in a puzzled frown. "Film? Why would a hotel manager be chasing you through the woods for a roll of film?"
"Because I took photographs inside a secret room in his hotel," Penny explained, her words coming in a rush. "A place called the Green Room. I think he is running an illegal gambling club there. The men I photographed were very angry. One of them broke my ski pole trying to stop me." She decided it was best not to mention the gun. There was no need to frighten Sara further, or to make Mr. Jasko think she was even more reckless than she already appeared. "He followed me from the hotel. He must have seen me skiing in this direction."
Sara looked from Penny to her grandfather, her own troubles forgotten in the face of this new, exciting danger. "Gamblers! Right here in Pine Top! Oh, Penny, how thrilling!"
"Silence, Sara!" Jasko commanded without looking at her. He took the camera from Penny's hand, turning the small object over with his large, calloused fingers. He seemed to be weighing not just the camera, but her entire story. "You are the daughter of Anthony Parker," he stated, his eyes boring into hers. It was not a question.
Penny's heart sank. She had forgotten that Maxwell had shouted it for all to hear. "Yes, sir."
"The newspaper publisher."
"Yes."
His expression was unreadable. Many people in the mountains had a deep distrust of newspapers and the city affairs they represented. Penny braced herself for his disapproval.
"And he sent you here to get this story?"
"No, sir," Penny said honestly. "He doesn't know anything about it. I came to Pine Top for a vacation, to stay at the Downey lodge. I stumbled onto this myself."
For a long moment, the only sound in the cabin was the ticking of a simple clock on the mantel. Jasko's gaze was thoughtful.
"This Maxwell," he said finally. "He has the look of a city hawk. The kind that preys on weaker folk. I do not like his kind on my mountain."
He handed the camera back to Penny. "The film is still in here?"
"Yes. I have to get it developed. It's the only proof I have."
"And he knows it," Jasko finished for her. He paced the length of the small room, his hands clasped behind his back. "You cannot go back to your lodge. He will be watching for you. He will not be so easily frightened a second time."
"But Mrs. Downey will worry," Penny protested.
"I'll telephone her," he said decisively. "I will tell her you have had a minor skiing accident and that you will be staying here for a day or two to recover. It is a small lie to prevent a larger harm."
Penny was stunned. She had expected to be scolded and sent away, not offered sanctuary. "But... I can't impose on you, Mr. Jasko."
"You are not imposing. That man threatened you on my land. That makes it my affair. You will be safer here." His eyes fell again upon his granddaughter, and his face hardened. "Sara, you will go up to the loft. You will take down your foolish rope ladder and you will stay there until I call you. You will have your meals, but you will have them alone. You will have time to think about obedience, and the trouble your selfishness has caused."
"But Grandfather..." Sara began.
"Go," he said, his voice quiet but absolute.
Sara cast a miserable glance at Penny, then turned and trudged toward the ladder. As she disappeared through the trapdoor, Penny could hear a quiet sob.
Peter Jasko watched her go, then sighed. "She has a good heart, but her spirit is too wild. Like her mother's." He turned back to Penny. "Now, about this film. It is of no use to you undeveloped. And you cannot take it to Pine Top yourself."
"I have to," Penny insisted. "It's my story."
"It is a roll of film," he corrected her gently. "It is evidence. It is more important that it reaches your father safely than for you to deliver it yourself. Pride can be a dangerous thing, Miss Parker."
He went to a small cabinet and took out a tin box. "I have a friend who is going down to Riverview tomorrow with a load of timber. His name is Gus. He is a man who asks no questions and tells no tales. For a small price, he will deliver a package directly to your father's office."
Relief washed over Penny so intensely she felt lightheaded. The plan was perfect. Maxwell would be watching the roads and the train station at Pine Top, looking for her. He would never suspect a delivery via a logging truck.
"Oh, Mr. Jasko, that would be wonderful. I can pay him, of course."
"Gus will be compensated," he said, dismissing the detail. "The difficulty will be in getting the film from the camera. If we open it here, we will ruin it."
Penny's face fell. In her relief, she had forgotten the most basic rule of photography. "Of course. It needs a darkroom."
Jasko tapped his chin thoughtfully. "The root cellar. There are no windows. It is as dark as a tomb down there. I will fetch some tools. We can try to open the camera and transfer the film into a lightproof container." He motioned for her to follow him to a heavy door at the back of the cabin. "Bring the tin box."
He led the way down a short flight of steep, stone steps into the cool, earthy-smelling darkness of the cellar. The air was heavy with the scent of potatoes and damp soil. Jasko lit a single lantern, keeping the flame turned low. Its flickering light cast long, dancing shadows on the stone walls, which were lined with shelves of preserved vegetables and fruits in glass jars.
"We must be quick," he warned. "Even this small light is a risk."
He set the lantern on the floor in a far corner, so that they were working mostly in shadow. Penny's fingers, clumsy with cold and nerves, fumbled with the latch on the camera.
"I'm afraid," she whispered. "If I ruin this, it was all for nothing."
"Be steady," Jasko said, his voice a calm rumble beside her. "Do not think of the man or the hotel. Think only of your fingers and the task."
Taking a deep breath, Penny focused. She worked the latch, and the back of the camera popped open. Working by feel more than sight, she carefully unspooled the finished roll of film. Her heart pounded in her ears. Jasko held the tin box open. With painstaking slowness, Penny placed the roll inside and snapped the lid shut.
A moment later, they were climbing the steps back into the main cabin, blinking in the comparative brightness. Penny clutched the tin box as if it were a priceless jewel.
"Thank you," she said, her voice filled with gratitude. "I could never have done that alone."
"We are not finished yet," he cautioned. "You must write a note to your father. Explain what this is, and tell him you are safe. Do not say where you are. Just that you are with friends."
He provided her with a pen and a sheet of paper from a drawer. Penny quickly scribbled a message, explaining about the film from the Green Room and asking him to have it developed at once. She added that she had run into trouble but was perfectly safe and would contact him again when she could. She folded the note and tucked it into the tin box with the roll of film. Jasko took the box and wrapped it in a piece of plain brown paper, tying it securely with twine.
"I will see that Gus gets this before sunrise tomorrow," he promised, placing the package on the mantel. "Now, you look tired. There is a small cot behind that screen. You should rest."
Penny knew he was right. The morning's events, from the confrontation with Francine to the terrifying chase with Maxwell, had left her utterly exhausted. She felt a profound sense of safety inside the rough-hewn walls of the Jasko cabin. As she lay down on the cot, she could hear Mr. Jasko moving about, adding a log to the fire. The last thing she remembered was the comforting crackle of the flames and the thought that, for all his sternness, Peter Jasko was one of the kindest men she had ever met.

Sometime later, a sharp rapping at the cabin door startled Penny from a deep sleep. She sat up, disoriented. The light in the cabin was dim, suggesting the sun was low in the sky. Peter Jasko, who had been sitting at the table reading a book, rose silently and went to the door.
"Who is it?" he called out, his hand resting on the heavy wooden bolt.
"It's Francine Sellberg," came a breathless voice from the other side. "From the Fergus hotel. I'm looking for Penny Parker. I saw her skiing this way. Is she here?"
Penny's blood ran cold. How had Francine found her?
Jasko glanced over his shoulder at Penny, his expression grim. He put a finger to his lips, signaling for her to remain silent and out of sight.
"I know no one by that name," he said, his voice flat and unwelcoming. "You are mistaken."
"But I'm sure I saw her!" Francine insisted. "She was wearing a red ski jacket. She had a terrible argument with the hotel manager and then she took off. I was worried about her."
"There is no girl here," Jasko repeated firmly. "You are on private property. I suggest you go back to your hotel."
There was a frustrated sigh from outside the door, then the sound of skis crunching in the snow, fading into the distance. Jasko waited a full minute before turning away from the door.
"That was your reporter friend?"
Penny nodded, her heart still pounding. "She's a rival, not a friend. She must have followed my tracks."
"She is persistent," Jasko observed. "Like a weasel on a scent. It is good I am sending that film with Gus. The sooner it is out of these mountains, the better." He looked at Penny with concern. "It is no longer safe for you even to be seen in the valley. Maxwell has his own men, and now this reporter is sniffing around. They will connect you to this cabin soon enough."
A new, more daring plan began to form in Penny's mind. She was a prisoner here, in a way, just as Sara was. But unlike Sara, she was not content to wait for others to solve her problems.
"Mr. Jasko," she said, her voice filled with fresh resolve. "There's one more thing I need to do. Maxwell has a ledger. I saw it on his desk in the Green Room. It must have the names of all the men in his club, and the amounts they've gambled. The film is good, but the ledger would be absolute proof."
Jasko stared at her in disbelief. "You cannot be serious. You want to go back to that hotel?"
"Not to stay," Penny said quickly. "Just to get the ledger. Maxwell thinks I'm hiding. He won't expect me to come back. And Francine's visit proves something. She's not working with Maxwell. She's investigating him, too. She might create a useful distraction."
"It is out of the question," he said flatly. "I will not permit it. It is a fool's errand."
"But if I had that ledger, the police would have everything they need! My father could publish the whole story. Maxwell wouldn't be able to hurt anyone ever again."
She could see a flicker of uncertainty in his eyes. He was a

man of caution, but he was also a man who understood threats. She could see the logic of her desperate plan warring with his protective instincts.

"A fool's errand is sitting here waiting for Maxwell to discover us," Penny pressed, her voice low and urgent. "He knows who I am. He knows I was at his Green Room. He will not simply let that go. He'll have men searching every cabin on this mountain. And when they find me here, what do you think will happen to you and Sara?"

Jasko's jaw tightened. He paced the small floor of the cabin, his heavy boots thudding softly on the wooden planks. "The girl, Sara, is my concern. I will not have her endangered."

"She is already in danger!" Penny retorted. "We all are. But the ledger changes everything. It's not just about a newspaper story, Mr. Jasko. It's irrefutable evidence for the police. With that book, my father can bring in the state authorities. Maxwell's local influence won't protect him then. He'll be finished, and everyone he has threatened will be safe."

She paused, letting her words sink in. Jasko stopped pacing and stared into the cold fireplace as if seeing the future in the dead ashes.

"And how would you get this book?" he asked, his voice rough with skepticism. "Maxwell threw you out himself. The lobby will be watched. Every man in his employ will be looking for you."

"That's why it's the perfect time," Penny explained, her mind racing. "He expects me to run and hide. He doesn't expect me to walk right back into the lion's den. And I won't go through the lobby. There are other ways into a hotel. Service doors, kitchen entrances, windows. And I have an idea for a distraction."

Jasko raised a skeptical eyebrow. "What sort of distraction?"

A mischievous glint, reminiscent of Sara's, appeared in Penny's eyes. "A headstrong girl who is tired of being cooped up in a loft. Sara wants to go to the bobsled run. What if she 'escaped' and did just that?"

The old man's expression darkened. "I will not use my granddaughter as bait."

"Not as bait," Penny corrected gently. "As a diversion. She would be at the Downey lodge, surrounded by people. She would be safer there than she is here. While everyone, including perhaps that reporter Francine, is focused on the commotion of the runaway Jasko girl, the Fergus hotel will be less guarded. You could even go down to Pine Top yourself, pretending to be a frantic grandfather looking for her. It would give you a reason to be in the village, asking questions, making a scene. All eyes would be on you and Sara, not on the back door of the hotel."

Peter Jasko was silent for a long time. He ran a hand over his weathered face, the plan turning over and over in his mind. It was reckless. It was dangerous. But he could also see the sharp, clear logic of it. Keeping Penny at the cabin was a passive risk, a matter of waiting for the inevitable. Her plan was an active risk, but one that offered a swift and permanent solution.

"If you are caught," he said finally, his voice a low rumble, "you will be on your own. I cannot help you."

"I understand," Penny said, her heart leaping with a mixture of fear and excitement. "But I won't be caught."

"Very well," he sighed, the sound heavy with resignation. "We will try your foolish plan. But you will listen to me. You will do exactly as I say until you reach the hotel grounds. This mountain is my home. I know its secrets."

Relief washed over Penny. "Thank you, Mr. Jasko. You won't regret this."

"I regret it already," he grumbled, but there was a new light of determination in his eyes. "Now, let us talk to Sara."

Penny followed him to the trapdoor. He unbolted it and called up into the dim loft. "Sara! Come down. We have something to discuss."

A moment later, Sara's legs appeared on the ladder, and she descended with an agility born of practice. Her eyes were bright with curiosity.

"What is it, Grandfather? Did you catch the bear that's been stealing our supplies?" she asked, looking from his grim face to Penny's determined one.

"Something far more troublesome than a bear," Jasko said. He explained Penny's plan, leaving out no detail of the risk.

As Penny had predicted, Sara's initial look of concern quickly transformed into one of pure, unadulterated delight. The prospect of an escape, a secret mission, and a chance to defy her grandfather's orders all at once was more than she could have hoped for.

"Oh, it's a wonderful plan!" she exclaimed, clapping her hands together. "I'll use the rope ladder I made! I can be down at the Downey lodge in an hour. Mrs. Downey will be so surprised to see me. Should I tell her what's happening?"

"No," Penny and Jasko said in unison.

"You will tell her nothing," Jasko commanded. "You will act as if you have simply slipped away for a bit of fun. You will help with the bobsleds. You will be a girl enjoying the snow. Nothing more. Do you understand me, Sara? This is not a game."

"I understand, Grandfather," Sara said, though her sparkling eyes suggested she still thought of it as a grand game. "When do I leave?"

"As soon as it is dusk," Jasko decided. "The failing light will give you cover. I will go down to the village an hour after you are gone and begin my 'search'. That should be when you arrive at the hotel, Penny. Maxwell and his men will be dining or distracted by my theatrics in the village square."

"And what about Francine?" Penny asked. "She's out there somewhere."

"Let her follow Sara," Sara piped up. "She'll think she's on the trail of a story about my grandfather being a cruel tyrant. It will keep her busy for hours!"

It was a sound idea. Francine, ever the opportunist, would certainly follow the most visible thread of drama. The hours until dusk passed with agonizing slowness. Jasko gave Penny a crust of bread and a piece of dried meat, insisting she eat. He then went over a crude map he had drawn in the dust on the floor, showing her a little used game trail that would lead her to the rear of the Fergus hotel, near the kitchens.

"The delivery entrance is here," he said, pointing with a thick finger. "They receive meat and vegetables in the morning and late afternoon. By the time you arrive, it should be quiet. The door is old. The lock is simple." He handed her a thin piece of bent metal. "This might be of use."

Penny took the makeshift lock pick, her respect for the old woodsman growing. He seemed to have a solution for everything.

When the sun began to dip below the jagged peaks, casting long, purple shadows across the valley, Sara could hardly contain her excitement. She was already dressed in her warmest ski clothes.

"Good luck, Penny," she whispered, giving her a quick, impulsive hug. "Be careful!"

"You too," Penny whispered back. "Don't get into too much trouble."

"I will get into just enough," Sara laughed, and with a final wave, she slipped out the back window and was gone, a phantom in the twilight.

An hour later, Jasko put on his heavy coat. "It is time. Remember, the game trail. Stay off the main paths. If you are not back at the clearing by the old pine tree by midnight, I will assume the worst."

"I'll be there, Mr. Jasko. With the ledger."

He gave a curt nod, his face an unreadable mask of worry, and left the cabin, heading toward Pine Top to play the part of the distraught grandfather.

Penny was alone. The silence in the small cabin was immense. She took a deep breath, marshaling her courage. Her mission had begun. She slipped out of the cabin, strapped on her skis, and found the head of the game trail Jasko had described. The path was narrow and twisted through thick stands of pine, their branches heavy with snow. The only sounds were the soft hiss of her skis and the pounding of her own heart.

The Fergus hotel glowed like a castle in the valley below, its windows blazing with warm, inviting light. To the guests inside, it was a haven of comfort and luxury. To Penny, it was a fortress she had to breach.

She reached the rear of the hotel and found the service area just as Jasko had described. A single dim bulb burned over a sturdy wooden door. The area was deserted, littered with empty crates and vegetable peelings. Sounds of clattering pans and loud voices drifted from a steamed up kitchen window nearby.

Penny glided to the door and removed her skis, hiding them behind a stack of firewood. She pulled out the piece of metal Jasko had given her. Her hands were trembling, partly from cold and partly from nerves. She had read about picking locks in detective stories, but had never actually tried it. The lock was old and crude, however. After a few tense moments of probing and twisting, she felt a satisfying click. The door swung inward with a low groan.

She slipped inside, closing the door silently behind her. She was in a narrow, cold storage corridor that smelled of potatoes and damp earth. Following the sounds from the kitchen, she crept forward until she reached a swinging door. Peeking through its small, circular window, she saw a chaotic scene of chefs and kitchen hands shouting orders and rushing about with plates of food. The dinner service was in full swing. This was no place for her.

She retreated and found another corridor leading in the opposite direction, away from the kitchen. This one was cleaner and warmer, carpeted with a thin runner. It seemed to be a staff hallway. She could hear the faint strains of music from the dining room somewhere above her.

Her goal was the private office near the lobby. Jasko's distraction should be underway, but she could not be certain Maxwell had left his post. She crept along the corridor, her ears straining for any sound of footsteps. She passed a linen closet and a staff break room where two maids were laughing over a cup of coffee. Penny flattened herself into a shallow alcove, holding her breath until they moved on.

The corridor ended at a flight of service stairs. This was her chance. She hurried up the steps, which emerged into another hallway, this one more richly decorated. She recognized it as being just off the main lobby. She could see a corner of the grand fireplace and hear the low murmur of conversation.

From her hiding spot behind a large potted fern, she peered into the lobby. It was bustling with evening guests. The elevator boy stood at his post. A few couples were seated in the lounge chairs. And standing near the front desk, looking agitated, was Harvey Maxwell. He was speaking in a low, angry tone to one of his bellhops. Penny strained to hear.

"...the whole village is in an uproar," the bellhop was saying. "The old man, Jasko, is claiming his granddaughter is missing. The constable is organizing a search party."

"A search party?" Maxwell sneered. "For that wild girl? She probably ran off with a ski instructor. Tell the constable we haven't seen her. I have more important things to worry about."

Penny's heart gave a little jump. The plan was working. Maxwell was annoyed but distracted. More importantly, he was not in his office.

Now was her chance. The office door was just across the lobby, partially obscured by a pillar. She needed to cross twenty feet of open space without being noticed. She waited until a large group of laughing guests moved toward the dining room, creating a momentary screen. Taking a deep breath, Penny stepped out from behind the fern and walked briskly, purposefully, across the marble floor. She kept her head down, praying no one would recognize the girl who had been so violently ejected only hours before.

She reached the office door. Her hand closed around the cool brass knob. She turned it. It was unlocked.

Relief so powerful it almost made her dizzy flooded through her. She slipped inside, closing the door so quietly it made no sound. The office was dark, save for the moonlight filtering through the large window that overlooked the snowy grounds. The air smelled of cigar smoke and leather.

Penny did not dare turn on a light. She moved quickly to the massive oak desk. Her eyes, adjusting to the gloom, scanned the surface. Papers were strewn about, but there was no sign of the ledger. She tried the top drawers. Locked. Of course they would be.

Her gaze fell upon the small, ornate safe tucked into the shadows beside the desk. The ledger had to be in there. A wave of despair washed over her. There was no way she could open a modern safe.

"Don't panic," she whispered to herself. "Think."

Maxwell would not want to be constantly opening and closing his safe. Perhaps the key was nearby. Her hands flew over the desktop, patting and searching. Nothing. She felt along the top of the window frame, under the blotter, behind the heavy velvet curtains. Still nothing.

Then her eyes caught a small detail. On the wall behind the desk hung a framed photograph of the Fergus hotel in its early days. It was hanging slightly askew. It was the sort of tiny imperfection that an orderly man like Maxwell might not tolerate. On a hunch, Penny reached up and lifted the photograph from its hook. Taped to the back of the frame was a small, brass key.

Her fingers trembled as she took it. It was too small for the safe. It had to be for the desk drawers. She tried it on the top right drawer. It slid in smoothly. With a turn, the lock clicked open.

She pulled the drawer open. Inside were stacks of invoices, receipts, and correspondence. No ledger. She tried the left drawer. More papers. Her frustration grew. Where could it be? She was running out of time.

She pulled open the wide, central drawer. It was filled with pens, paper clips, and other office trifles. But underneath a stack of stationary, her fingers brushed against the smooth, leather-bound spine of a book. She pulled it out. It was a heavy, black ledger.

She opened it on the desk, using the moonlight to see. The pages were filled with neat columns of names, dates, and figures. There were names she recognized, prominent businessmen from Riverview and beyond. The sums of money were staggering. This was it. This was the proof.

Clutching the book to her chest, she was about to turn and leave when she heard it: the sound of a key in the office door lock.

Panic seized her. Maxwell was back!

There was nowhere to run. Her eyes darted around the room. The space behind the heavy curtains! It was her only chance. She dove behind them just as the door swung open. The room was flooded with light.

Penny pressed herself against the cold windowpane, her heart hammering against her ribs. The thick velvet concealed her completely, but she was trapped. She held the heavy ledger tightly, praying its bulk would not betray her.

"I tell you, she must have seen it," came a voice. It was not Maxwell. It was Francine Sellberg.

"And I tell you, you are wasting my time," Harvey Maxwell's voice snarled in response. "I have a hotel to run."

Penny peeked through a tiny gap in the curtains. Francine and Maxwell were standing in the middle of the office. Maxwell looked furious, his face flushed with anger.

"You threw Penny Parker out of your hotel this afternoon," Francine said, her voice sharp and accusatory. "Then, just now, I saw her myself. I was coming back from the Jasko cabin, and I saw her tracks leading to the back of this hotel. She's here, Maxwell. And I think I know why."

"You know nothing," Maxwell bit out.

"I know you run a little club in this hotel," Francine pressed on, undeterred. "A very exclusive, very illegal club. Penny stumbled onto it. That's why you are so desperate to get rid of her. She has proof, doesn't she? Something she took from the Green Room."

Penny held her breath. Francine was bluffing, trying to get Maxwell to admit something.

Maxwell let out a short, ugly laugh. "You reporters are all alike. You invent fantasies and call them stories. There is no club. And that Parker girl is long gone. Now get out of my office before I have you thrown out as well."

"Not so fast," Francine said, her eyes scanning the room. They settled on the desk. "You seem to have been doing some work. The desk drawer is open."

Penny's blood ran cold. She watched in horror as Francine walked toward the desk. Maxwell's eyes followed her, a flicker of alarm appearing on his face.

Francine pointed to the open central drawer. "Looking for something, Mr. Maxwell? Or perhaps you've just misplaced something?"

Maxwell strode to the desk and slammed the drawer shut. "My affairs are none of your concern. This interview is over."

"I wonder what a girl like Penny Parker would want in this office," Francine mused, tapping her chin. "Something more valuable than a green ticket, I'll wager. Something that lists names, perhaps? And numbers?"

Maxwell's face went pale. He yanked open the central drawer again, then the one on the right, his movements frantic. He saw the empty space where the ledger had been. A low growl of pure rage escaped his throat.

"It's gone," he whispered, his voice shaking with fury. He spun around, his eyes wild. "It was her! That Parker girl!" He looked at Francine with sudden, venomous suspicion. "Or was it you? A clever trick to distract me?"

"Don't be ridiculous," Francine scoffed, though she took a nervous step back. "I told you she was here."

Maxwell lunged for the telephone on his desk. "I'm calling the constable. I'll have this entire hotel locked down. She won't get away with this!"

This was Penny's only chance. While both their backs were turned toward the desk, she had to move. The door was still open.

Holding the heavy ledger like a shield, Penny burst from behind the curtains. She did not even glance at the two stunned faces that turned toward her. She ran.

"There she is!" Francine shrieked.

"Stop her!" Maxwell bellowed.

Penny did not hesitate. She flew out of the office and into the lobby. A woman screamed. A guest dropped his drink. Penny dodged a luggage cart and sprinted for the main entrance. The revolving doors seemed a mile away.

She could hear Maxwell's heavy footsteps pounding on the marble behind her. He was faster than she expected. His hand snagged the back of her jacket.

"I have you!" he roared.

With a desperate wrench, Penny twisted in his grasp. The fabric of her jacket ripped, but it was enough. She stumbled forward, regaining her balance, and threw herself at the revolving door. She pushed through, bursting out into the frigid night air. She did not stop to find her skis. She just ran, plunging into the deep snow at the edge of the parking lot and scrambling toward the dark line of trees, the precious ledger held tight against her chest. Behind her, the Fergus hotel erupted in shouts and confusion. She had done it. She had the ledger. But now, the real race had begun.

The cold air was a shock against Penny's lungs, burning with every ragged gasp. She did not dare to look back. The sounds from the hotel, a chaotic blend of shouts and alarm, were her only gauge of the pursuit. The ledger, hard and unforgiving against her ribs, was a constant reminder of the prize and the peril.

Her thin shoes, meant for the hotel lobby and not a flight through a winter wilderness, plunged deep into the untracked snow. Each step was a struggle. She floundered, nearly losing her balance, and pushed onward, her eyes fixed on the dark, protective silhouette of the pine forest. Behind her, she heard a new sound, the distinct roar of a man's voice bellowing her name. It was Maxwell.

"He won't give up," she panted, forcing her aching legs to move faster. "He can't afford to."

Reaching the treeline, she stumbled into the relative shelter of the woods. The snow was less deep here, packed beneath the heavy boughs. It was darker, too, with the moonlight struggling to pierce the thick canopy. For a moment, she leaned against the rough bark of a pine, her chest heaving. Where could she go? The Downey lodge was the first place they would look. Mrs. Downey would protect her, but it would put the kind woman in a terrible position. Maxwell would not hesitate to bring the constable to her door.

Her mind raced, sorting through the few options available. There was only one place, one person reckless enough to help her without asking too many questions. Sara.

With renewed purpose, Penny oriented herself by the familiar shape of the mountain against the starry sky. The Jasko cabin was not far, but the path was uphill and treacherous in the dark. She pushed off from the tree, the heavy ledger clutched in her arms. To protect it from the damp, she awkwardly unwound her scarf and wrapped it around the book, tucking the bundle securely inside her ripped jacket.

The journey was a nightmare of slips and stumbles. Branches clawed at her face and hair. Once, she tripped over an exposed root and went sprawling, the impact knocking the wind from her. For a terrifying second, she thought she had lost the ledger, but it was still there, a hard lump against her stomach. Pushing herself up, she brushed the snow from her clothes and pressed on, driven by the image of Harvey Maxwell's furious face.

After what felt like an hour, a faint, welcoming light appeared through the trees. It was the Jasko cabin. Relief washed over Penny, so potent it almost buckled her knees. She crept closer, moving from tree to tree until she was at the edge of the clearing. The lamp was lit in the main room, but the loft window was dark. Peter Jasko must still be out.

Taking a deep breath, Penny dashed across the open space to the side of the cabin, directly beneath the loft window. She scooped up a handful of soft snow and packed it into a ball. Taking careful aim, she tossed it against the glass. It made a soft, wet thud. She waited. Nothing. She threw another, this time with a little more force.

The window creaked and shot upward. Sara's silhouette appeared against the faint light from downstairs. "Who's there?" she whispered urgently. "Is that you, Penny?"

"Yes! It's me," Penny whispered back, her voice hoarse. "Let me in, quickly!"

"What happened to you? I thought you were going to the hotel. Grandfather will be back soon!"

"I know. Please, Sara. It's important."

Sara hesitated for only a second. "The trapdoor. But the key is in the woodshed. I can't get it from up here."

"Don't worry," Penny said, already moving. She ran to the woodshed, her heart pounding a frantic rhythm against her ribs. Her cold fingers fumbled with the familiar loose stone, retrieving the heavy iron key. She raced back, unlocked the thick plank trapdoor set into the cabin's foundation, and pushed it open. The smell of earth and stored potatoes filled the air. She scrambled inside, pulling the door shut just as the sound of a distant snowmobile echoed across the valley.

She found the ladder and climbed into the warm, cozy loft, her relief palpable. Sara was there, holding a lantern, her eyes wide with a mixture of concern and thrilling curiosity.

"Penny! You're soaking wet and your jacket is torn! What on earth happened?"

"I don't have much time," Penny panted, leaning against the wall to catch her breath. "I got what I went for. And Harvey Maxwell is after me."

She unwrapped the scarf and held out the ledger. Sara stared at it, her eyes glittering in the lantern light. "Is that it? The thing you were looking for?"

"It's his account book," Penny explained. "It has everything in it. Names, dates, how much they won or lost in the Green Room. It's proof that he's running an illegal gambling den."

"Oh, Penny!" Sara breathed, her voice filled with admiration. "You actually stole it! No wonder he's furious. Where is he now?"

"He was chasing me. I think he's getting the constable from Pine Top. They'll search the whole mountain."

Sara's expression grew serious, but there was no fear in it, only a focused intensity. "Then you can't stay here. Grandfather is due back any minute. If he finds you, he'll march you right back down to Maxwell himself." She bit her lip, thinking. "We have to hide the book. And we have to get you away from here."

Penny nodded, shivering now as the cold began to seep deep into her bones. "I know. I just needed a place to think."

"Let me see it," Sara said, taking the ledger. She opened it on a small crate that served as her table, the lantern casting a golden glow on the neat columns of figures. "Look at these names! I recognize some of them. They're wealthy men who come here every winter. And the amounts... gracious, Penny! This is a fortune!"

A floorboard creaked downstairs. Both girls froze, their eyes locked in panic. Sara quickly doused the lantern, plunging the loft into near total darkness. They listened, their breathing suspended. Heavy footsteps moved across the main room, followed by the sound of wood being dropped into a woodbox.

"It's Grandfather," Sara mouthed silently in the dark.

Penny's heart sank. She was trapped. Peter Jasko was a stern, unyielding man who would never approve of such goings on. He would see it as his duty to turn her in. They heard him moving about below, stoking the fire, his movements slow and methodical. This was worse than being chased by Maxwell; this was being cornered.

Sara grabbed Penny's arm, her grip surprisingly strong. She pulled her toward the window. "My rope ladder," she whispered, her voice barely audible. "I finished it this afternoon. It's hidden under my mattress."

In the pitch black, they worked with frantic, silent haste. Sara retrieved the coiled rope, which was crudely but effectively knotted every foot or so. One end was tied to a heavy iron bed frame. Unlatching the window as quietly as she could, Sara fed the rope out into the night. It uncoiled with a soft, swishing sound.

"You have to go," Sara urged. "Now. Before he decides to come up here."

"But the ledger," Penny whispered back, gesturing toward the crate where it lay. "I can't climb with that."

"I'll keep it," Sara said at once. "He never comes in the loft without announcing it. He thinks I'm afraid of the dark. I'll hide it. It will be safer here for now than with you. You need to get to the Downey lodge and get help."

Penny hesitated. It was too much to ask of her friend. "Sara, if he finds it..."

"He won't," Sara insisted, her voice full of confidence. "I'm better at hiding things than he is at finding them. Now go! The snowmobile I heard earlier could be the constable."

Realizing her friend was right, Penny gave a quick, grateful nod. She swung a leg over the windowsill, her feet finding the top knot of the rope ladder. The cold air hit her again. The ground looked a long way down.

"Be careful," Sara whispered from the window. "And don't worry about the book. I'll guard it with my life."

Penny began her descent. The rope was stiff and bit into her cold hands. She went slowly, testing each knot before putting her full weight on it. Below, the light in the main cabin window cast a long, yellow rectangle on the snow. She had to stay out of it. She swung her body sideways, keeping close to the dark wall of the cabin.

She was halfway down when she heard a new sound, the baying of a hound. It was distant, but unmistakable. They were using dogs. Her blood ran cold. She quickened her pace, her shoes scraping against the log walls. Her foot slipped on an icy knot and for a heart-stopping moment she dangled by her hands, her muscles screaming in protest. With a grunt of effort, she found her footing again and continued down.

Finally, her feet touched the soft snow. She looked up and saw Sara's head retract from the window. She was alone again, but now she had a new, terrifying problem. She couldn't go back the way she came; the dogs would find her scent in minutes. And she couldn't risk the main path to the Downey lodge.

She stood in the shadows of the woodshed, her mind a whirlwind of frantic calculation. The bobsled run. Mrs. Downey had said it was nearly ready. It started just above the lodge and ran all the way down to the valley floor, not far from Pine Top. It was a straight, fast shot down the mountain. If she could get to the top of the run, she could be at the bottom in minutes, long before Maxwell's search party could work their way up the ski trails.

It was a wild, desperate plan. The run was dangerous even in daylight with a full crew. At night, alone, it was madness. But it was the kind of madness that appealed to Penny's plucky spirit. It was her only chance.

She checked her pockets. The green ticket was gone, taken by Maxwell. But she still had her ski pass and a few dollars. It was not much, but it was something. Making her decision, she skirted the edge of the clearing and plunged back into the woods, heading not down, but up. The start of the bobsled run was near the summit, close to the infamous Horseshoe Curve.

The climb was even more arduous than the journey to the cabin. The snow was deeper here, and the incline was steep. The baying of the dogs grew louder, spurring her on. She imagined Francine Sellberg with them, pointing the way, a triumphant gleam in her eyes. The thought gave Penny an extra burst of adrenaline. She would not be beaten by Francine.

She finally broke through the trees onto the wide, cleared swath of the bobsled track. The moon was bright here, illuminating a trench of packed snow and ice that snaked its way down the mountain like a frozen river. At the top of the run sat a single, dark shape under a canvas cover: a bobsled.

With trembling fingers, Penny pulled back the stiff, frozen canvas. It was one of Mrs. Downey's practice sleds, a heavy, four-man behemoth of steel and wood. It was far too big for one person to handle. She looked around desperately. Then she saw it. Tucked away in a small shed nearby was a smaller sled, a two-man skeleton sled, little more than a plank on runners with a steering wheel. It was light enough to manage.

She dragged the smaller sled to the starting line, her breath pluming in the icy air. The track before her looked terrifying. It dropped away at a steep angle before banking sharply to the left. She had never steered a bobsled in her life. Sara was the one who was supposed to teach her.

"I'll teach you to be my brake boy," Sara's laughing voice echoed in her memory.

There was no brake boy now. There was no one. Just her and the icy track. The dogs sounded closer now, their howling echoing off the silent peaks. There was no more time.

Taking a deep, steadying breath, Penny Parker sat on the sled, gripping the cold metal steering wheel. "This is going to be my lucky day," she whispered to herself, the words a stark contrast to the terror she felt. "I feel it in my bones."

She gave a hard shove with her feet, and the sled began to move. It slid forward slowly at first, then gathered speed with alarming quickness. The wind tore at her, whipping tears from her eyes. The trees on either side of the track blurred into a solid wall of darkness. The sled flew down the mountain, a tiny, solitary projectile hurtling through the cold and silent night. Penny held on, her knuckles white, her gaze fixed on the glistening, treacherous path ahead. She was no longer running from Harvey Maxwell. She was racing for her life.
