The marina was miles away, and his arms felt like lead.

He rowed mechanically, his mind a numb buzz of exhaustion and relief. The moon was his only companion now, casting a silver path across the rippling water. The cold was beginning to seep into his bones, and he shivered in his damp clothes. He reached into his pocket and his fingers closed around the small, hard shape of the jade. He had it. He had the proof. O'Malley was smuggling jade, likely carved from stolen artifacts, and passing it off as common stone. This small chip would prove it.

"Just have to get it to Captain Evans," he whispered, his own voice sounding strange in the quiet.

Captain Evans was the one person in the town of Port Blossom he knew he could trust. A retired Coast Guard captain, Evans was as tough and unshakeable as the granite cliffs that lined the coast. He had no love for men like O'Malley who brought trouble to their quiet fishing town.

The row back to the marina felt endless. Each pull of the oars was an agony. The lights of the town, which had seemed so close from the cannery, now looked a world away. Twice he had to stop, laying the oars across the dory and simply letting his arms hang, burning and useless. But the image of the men's angry faces, and the thought of what they would have done to him, spurred him on.

He thought about the risk he had taken. If his plan had failed, if he had misjudged the channel by a few inches, his dory would have been splintered on the rocks. But it was a risk he had to take. For weeks, he had watched O'Malley's suspicious night time operations, the strange crates unloaded in secret, the guarded men who spoke in whispers. No one else seemed to notice, or if they did, they were too afraid to say anything. Tom, whose father had been a fisherman lost to a sudden storm, had no fear of the sea, and his curiosity was stronger than his fear of O'Malley.

As he neared the marina, the first hint of dawn was touching the eastern sky, turning it from black to a soft, deep gray. A light mist was rising off the water, shrouding the docks in a ghostly veil. This was good. It would help him land unseen. He pulled his collar up and his hat down, trying to make himself as small a target as possible. He could not be sure O'Malley did not have other men watching the marina.

He slipped the dory not into its usual berth, but into a vacant slip at the far end of the docks, hidden behind a large, dilapidated fishing trawler. He tied the painter with trembling fingers, then clambered onto the damp wooden planks of the dock. He moved silently, a shadow among shadows, his worn boat shoes making no sound. The only noises were the gentle lapping of water against hulls and the lonely cry of a gull.

Captain Evans's boat, the 'Sea Serpent', was a sturdy, no nonsense vessel moored at the end of Pier C. A light was on in the cabin, a warm, yellow glow that promised safety. Tom crept along the pier, his eyes scanning the parking lot and the other docks. He saw no one.

He reached the 'Sea Serpent' and hesitated for a moment. He rapped softly on the cabin door, a specific rhythm they had agreed upon. Tap-tap. Tap.

The door opened a crack almost immediately. Captain Evans's weathered face appeared in the gap, his sharp blue eyes taking in Tom's disheveled appearance at a glance.

"Tom! Get in, boy, get in," he said in a low, urgent voice, pulling him inside and quickly shutting the door.

The cabin was warm and smelled of coffee and pipe tobacco. The captain, a man in his late sixties with a shock of white hair and a neatly trimmed beard, guided Tom to a chair.

"You're soaked to the bone," the captain observed, his expression grim. "And you look like you've seen a ghost. Did you get it?"

Tom nodded, too exhausted to speak at first. He fumbled in his wet pocket and pulled out the small piece of green stone. He unwrapped it from the soggy bit of cloth he had used to muffle it and placed it on the polished wooden table.

Even in the dim cabin light, the stone seemed to glow with an inner luminescence. It was a deep, vibrant green, with delicate patterns that spoke of ancient and careful carving. It was unmistakably jade, and of a very high quality.

Captain Evans picked it up, turning it over and over in his calloused fingers. He brought it close to the lamp, his brow furrowed in concentration.

"Well, I'll be," he murmured. "You were right, son. This is the real thing. Imperial jade, or I'm a landlubber. This isn't just stone, it's a piece of a museum-quality artifact."

"They were chipping it off a larger statue," Tom said, his voice hoarse. "Breaking it into smaller pieces to pack in with the regular shipments of quarried stone."

"Smuggling it out in plain sight," the captain finished, his eyes hard. "And O'Malley is the man behind it." He placed the jade chip carefully back on the table. "Did they see you?"

"They saw me," Tom admitted. "I had to run for it. They chased me in O'Malley's speedboat."

The captain's face grew even more serious. "And how did you, in that little dory of yours, get away from a speedboat?"

"I led them into the Dragon's Teeth," Tom said simply.

A slow smile spread across the old captain's face, crinkling the corners of his eyes. "You did, did you? I'll wager their propeller didn't much care for that. Are they still out there?"

"They are," Tom confirmed. "The boat is crippled. But they'll get back somehow. And they know it was me."

"Then you are in a great deal of danger," the captain said, his smile vanishing. "O'Malley can't afford a witness. Especially one who has physical proof." He looked from Tom's pale face to the piece of jade on the table. "We've got him, Tom. This is what we needed to go to the authorities."

"Can we trust the local police?" Tom asked anxiously. "Chief Brody is always playing cards with O'Malley at the club."

The captain shook his head. "No. You're right to be wary. Brody would likely 'lose' this evidence and warn O'Malley you were on to him. We need to go over his head. I have a friend in the federal revenue service, up in the city. A man named Peterson. He's straight as an arrow and he specializes in this kind of smuggling."

He stood up and went to a locked cabinet. He took out a small, velvet-lined box and carefully placed the piece of jade inside it.

"First thing is to get you out of sight," the captain said, turning back to Tom. "You can't go home. O'Malley's men will be watching. You'll stay here, on the 'Sea Serpent'. No one will think to look for you right under their noses. I'll get you some dry clothes and something hot to eat."

Tom felt a wave of relief so powerful it almost made him dizzy. "Thank you, Captain."

"Don't thank me yet, son," the captain said, his hand resting on Tom's shoulder. "We've only won the first skirmish. O'Malley is a powerful man with dangerous friends. Now that he knows he's been exposed, he'll be more dangerous than ever. This whole town could become a battleground."

He looked out the porthole toward the misty, waking town. "I'll call Peterson as soon as his office opens. But it will take him time to get down here, a day at least. Until then, we need to be careful. Very careful."

Tom looked at the small box on the table. It held a tiny chip of stone, yet it felt as heavy as an anchor. He had his proof, but in getting it, he had started a war. And he was standing on the front line.

She grabbed Francine’s arm and pulled her toward the small door in the corner. They scrambled inside just as the main door to the room swung open.

“Housekeeping,” a cheerful voice called out.

They huddled together in the tiny, tile-lined bathroom, pressing the door almost shut but leaving a tiny crack to see through. The maid from the hallway pushed her cleaning cart into the room. She began to bustle about, stripping the sheets from the bed with practiced efficiency.

Penny held her breath. If the maid decided to clean the bathroom, they were caught. Minutes stretched into an eternity. The maid hummed her off-key tune as she dusted the furniture and ran a vacuum cleaner over the rug. The roar of the machine was deafening in the small space, covering the sound of their own frantic heartbeats.

Finally, the vacuum cleaner switched off. The maid gathered her supplies, took one last look around the tidy room, and pushed her cart back out into the hall, pulling the door closed behind her.

They waited a full minute in the silent bathroom before daring to move.

“I think,” Francine said, her voice barely a whisper, “that I am getting too old for this sort of work.”

“Nonsense,” Penny said, a shaky grin on her face. “You’re just getting started. We have everything we need now.”

“We have notes,” Francine corrected. “We need proof. A photograph. One of those ‘electronic counters’.”

“Maxwell said they’re arriving tomorrow morning on a supply truck,” Penny recalled. “Disguised as kitchen equipment.”

“Then that’s where we need to be,” Francine concluded. “The service entrance. Tomorrow morning.”

“But how do we get out of here now?” Penny wondered. “We can’t just walk out the front door. Maxwell’s men are everywhere.”

She crept to the window and peered out. It overlooked the back of the hotel, a less-trafficked area of snowy grounds and pine trees. The drop was still too high to risk a jump. Her gaze fell upon the sturdy drainpipe that ran down the wall, just a few feet from the window. It was wide, old, and securely fastened to the brick.

“I have another idea,” Penny said.

Francine followed her gaze and groaned. “Oh, no. Absolutely not. I draw the line at drainpipes.”

“It’s perfectly safe,” Penny assured her, unlatching the window and pushing it open. A blast of cold mountain air filled the room. “It’s this, or we try to explain to Harvey Maxwell what we were doing in room 214. I’ll go first.”

Before Francine could protest further, Penny had swung a leg over the sill. She grasped the cold, rough metal of the drainpipe, testing its strength. It felt solid. With a last glance back at the reporter, she began her slow, careful descent. Her ski boots were a clumsy hindrance, but her fingers, though numb with cold, held fast.

Francine watched from the window, her expression a mixture of terror and grudging admiration. When Penny was safely on the ground, hidden in the shadow of a large evergreen, she waved up. With a sigh that was visible in the frosty air, Francine Sellberg, star reporter, swung her own leg over the windowsill and began to climb down the drainpipe.

CHAPTER 15

A DANGEROUS DELIVERY

The snow was soft and deep where Penny landed, muffling the sound of her impact. She scrambled behind the thick branches of a towering spruce and peered back at the hotel. A moment later, Francine landed with a soft grunt beside her, brushing snow from her trousers with a grimace.
"I am officially retiring from the newspaper business and opening a flower shop," the reporter declared, her breath coming in ragged puffs. "Something quiet. Something that does not involve climbing down drainpipes in the dead of winter."
"But where is the fun in that?" Penny asked, her eyes twinkling. "We got away clean, Francine. And we know their plan."
"We know a plan," Francine corrected, her voice low and serious. "We still have no proof that would stand up in a newspaper article, let alone a courtroom. Just notes scribbled in a dark bathroom."
"The proof is arriving tomorrow morning on a supply truck," Penny reminded her. "We just need to be there to welcome it."
"And get ourselves thrown in a real jail this time, not just a cozy hotel loft," Francine muttered. "Maxwell isn't a fool. He'll have that delivery guarded."
"Then we'll have to be cleverer than his guards," Penny said with a confidence she did not entirely feel. "First, we need to get back to the Downey lodge without being seen."
They crept through the trees at the edge of the hotel grounds, the shadows of the pines providing excellent cover. The Fergus hotel blazed with light, but out here in the cold, it felt a world away. The back of the property was deserted, and they soon found the ski trail that led back toward Mrs. Downey's lodge. After retrieving their skis from the rack where Penny had left them, they pushed off into the moonlit snow.
The trip was made in silence, each girl lost in her own thoughts. Penny's mind raced, turning over the details of their plan. They needed a camera, for one thing. And they needed a way to get close to the service entrance without being spotted immediately.
When they arrived at the Downey lodge, the windows were dark. Mrs. Downey, an early riser, was also early to bed.
"We can't very well knock, can we?" Francine whispered, leaning on her ski poles. "She'll want to know where we've been, and 'committing breaking and entering' isn't a very good answer."
"I have a key to the back door," Penny said quietly, leading the way around to the kitchen entrance. "Mrs. Downey gave it to me for emergencies. I think this qualifies."
Inside, the lodge was warm and quiet. A single ember glowed in the great stone fireplace. They tiptoed up the stairs to Penny's room, a cozy chamber under the eaves.
"All right, Parker," Francine said, closing the door softly behind them. "Let's hear this brilliant plan." She sat on the edge of the bed, her professional skepticism returning now that the immediate danger was past.
Penny pulled out the notebook she had so carefully guarded. "The delivery is tomorrow morning. We don't know the exact time, so we need to be there at dawn. The truck will pull up to the service entrance at the rear of the hotel."
"The same area we just fled from," Francine noted dryly. "They'll be looking for us."
"They'll be looking for two girls," Penny said. "But what if they only see one? Or what if they're too distracted to see anyone at all?" A new idea began to form in her mind, one that was both brilliant and incredibly risky.
"What are you talking about?"
"We need a diversion," Penny explained. "And I know just the person. Sara Jasko."
Francine stared at her as if she had lost her mind. "The girl who is locked in a loft by her grandfather? The girl you had to sneak out just to talk to? That's your plan?"
"Sara is reckless and she loves adventure," Penny argued. "And more importantly, she's a fantastic skier. She could create a commotion over by the ski trail that would draw a guard's attention for a few vital minutes."
"And what's to stop her from getting caught? Or from getting you caught?" Francine countered. "You said her grandfather is strict. What do you think he'll do if he finds his granddaughter mixed up with Harvey Maxwell's business?"
"It's a risk," Penny admitted. "But it's our best chance. While Sara provides a distraction, I can get close enough with a camera to get a photograph of them unloading the machines. That's the proof you need, isn't it?"
"And where do you plan to get a camera at this hour?"
"Mrs. Downey is an avid amateur photographer," Penny said with a triumphant smile. "I saw a very nice one in her study. I'm sure she wouldn't mind us borrowing it for a good cause."
Francine sighed and ran a hand through her hair. "This entire plan depends on a locked-up teenager, a borrowed camera, and the hope that Harvey Maxwell's hired thugs are easily distracted by ski accidents. You know that, don't you?"
"It's better than no plan at all," Penny replied firmly. "I'll go see Sara first thing in the morning. You get the camera and meet me near the Jasko cabin."
"It seems I have little choice," Francine said with resignation. "But if I end up explaining this to a judge, I'm telling him it was all your idea."
"Fair enough," Penny grinned.

The next morning dawned clear and cold, the sky a pale, cloudless blue. Penny was up before the sun, dressing quickly in her warmest ski clothes. As she crept down the stairs, she saw a light on in the kitchen. Mrs. Downey, her gray hair in a neat bun, was sipping a cup of tea.
"Penny, dear, you're up early," she said with a welcoming smile. "Are you off to catch the best morning snow?"
"Something like that," Penny said, her mind racing for a plausible excuse. "Mrs. Downey, I was wondering if I might borrow your camera. There's a particular view of the mountain in the morning light that I'd just love to capture."
"Why, of course, dear," the woman said, delighted. "It's in the study, on my desk. Just be careful with it. The lens is quite sensitive."
"I will, thank you!" Penny said, relieved at how easily the first part of her plan had fallen into place.
She met Francine a few minutes later on the trail. The reporter looked as though she hadn't slept at all. She held the camera, encased in its leather bag, as if it were a bomb.
"I feel like a common thief," Francine grumbled.
"You're a journalist pursuing the truth," Penny corrected her cheerfully. "Now, you wait here. I'm going to go recruit our diversion."
The Jasko cabin was quiet, a plume of smoke rising from its stone chimney. Penny looked up toward the loft window and saw it. Just as Sara had promised, a makeshift rope ladder, fashioned from bedsheets, dangled from the sill, its end hidden in a snowdrift. A thrill of admiration for the girl's daring shot through Penny.
She gave a low whistle, the sound of a mountain chickadee. A moment later, Sara's head appeared at the window. She grinned and gestured downward. Penny went to the ladder and gave it a tug. It was surprisingly secure. With a deep breath, Sara began to climb down, as nimble as a squirrel.
"I knew you'd be back!" Sara whispered excitedly as her feet touched the ground. "Did you bring me a new adventure?"
"The biggest one yet," Penny said, quickly explaining the situation. She told Sara about the Green Room, the illegal gambling, and the secret delivery expected that morning.
Sara's eyes, already sparkling, grew wide with excitement. "Gamblers! And secret machines! It's just like something out of a book! Of course I'll help you."
"It's dangerous, Sara," Penny warned. "These are not nice men. All I need you to do is create a small diversion. Pretend to fall near the service trail. Just enough to make a guard look away for a minute."
"A fall?" Sara said, looking disappointed. "Oh, I can do much better than that! Peter Jasko's granddaughter doesn't just fall. I'll stage the most spectacular wipeout Horseshoe Curve has ever seen! They'll be talking about it for weeks."
"No!" Penny said, alarmed. "Not Horseshoe Curve. And nothing too spectacular. Just a little slip. We don't want you to actually get hurt."
"Don't worry your head about me," Sara said, waving a dismissive hand. "I know what I'm doing. You just be ready when I give the signal." She winked. "Where do you want this grand performance to happen?"
Penny pointed toward the rear of the Fergus hotel, barely visible through the trees. "Over there. Near the service road. But stay on the main trail, where it's safe."
"Safe is boring," Sara laughed. "You and your friend get in position. I'll give you the show of a lifetime."
Before Penny could protest further, Sara had clicked into her skis and with a powerful push of her poles, shot off toward the hotel, a blur of red ski jacket against the white snow.
Penny skied back to Francine, a knot of worry in her stomach. "Our diversion has been recruited."
"And?" Francine asked, zipping up her jacket.
"And she's either the bravest girl I've ever met, or she's going to get us all captured," Penny confessed. "Let's go. We have to get in place before the truck arrives."
They found a vantage point in a thick cluster of pine trees on a small rise overlooking the hotel's service entrance. The spot gave them a clear, if distant, view of the loading dock. Penny took out the camera and checked the settings, her fingers clumsy with cold and nerves.
"This is a fool's errand," Francine muttered, peering through a pair of small binoculars she had brought. "There are two men already at the loading dock. They look like the kind of men who eat nails for breakfast."
Penny focused on the men. They were large and wore heavy parkas, their faces grim. One of them was the very same guard who had been stationed outside the Green Room.
They waited in the frigid silence. The sun climbed higher, but its rays brought no warmth. Penny's toes were numb. Ten minutes passed, then twenty.
"Maybe they're not coming," Francine whispered, her breath misting in the air.
As if on cue, the low rumble of a heavy engine echoed through the valley. A large, drab green truck, its sides emblazoned with the logo "Apex Kitchen Supply," chugged slowly up the service road and backed toward the loading dock.
"Showtime," Penny breathed, raising the camera.
Harvey Maxwell himself came out of a side door to meet the truck. He spoke briefly to the driver, then gestured to his two men. The back of the truck was unlatched, revealing a cargo of large wooden crates.
"Here we go," Francine said, her voice tense. "Let's see your friend work her magic."
Penny scanned the nearby ski trails, her heart pounding. There was no sign of Sara. Had she gotten cold feet? Or worse, had her grandfather caught her?
The men on the dock began to wrestle the first heavy crate off the truck. It was marked "Industrial Dough Mixer."
"Come on, Sara," Penny whispered. "Any time now."
Suddenly, a flash of red appeared high up on the trail that ran above the service area. It was Sara, skiing at a reckless speed, far faster than anyone would for a casual morning run.
"What is she doing?" Francine gasped, lowering her binoculars.
Instead of faking a simple fall on the trail, Sara veered sharply off-piste, aiming for a steep, tree-lined embankment that dropped down toward the service road. She flew over a small rise, her skis leaving the snow for a heart-stopping moment.
"She's crazy!" Penny exclaimed.
Sara landed with a jarring thud and, as if she had planned it, her skis crossed. She tumbled head over heels in a spectacular crash, sending up a great plume of snow and losing a ski, which cartwheeled through the air and clattered onto the pavement of the service road, not twenty feet from the truck. Sara herself came to a stop in a tangled heap in a snowbank just at the edge of the road.
The effect was immediate. The truck driver, Maxwell, and his two men all spun around at the noise.
"What in blazes was that?" one of the men shouted.
"Some fool kid on the slopes," the other grumbled.
For a moment, no one moved. Then Sara let out a piteous moan. "My leg! I think it's broken!"
Maxwell cursed under his breath. "Go see what's wrong with her," he snapped at one of the guards. "Get her out of here. The last thing we need is a witness."
As the guard lumbered over to the moaning Sara, the other guard and the truck driver were distracted, watching him. Even Maxwell's attention was divided.
"Now, Penny, now!" Francine urged.
This was their chance. Penny broke cover, half-running, half-sliding down the snowy incline toward the loading dock. She kept a line of smaller trees between herself and the men, her heart hammering against her ribs. She was now close enough.
She raised the camera, the heavy lens feeling like an anchor. The men had turned their backs to her as they watched the drama with Sara unfold. The open back of the truck and the crates were perfectly framed. Click. She advanced the film. Click. She took another shot, this time trying to get Maxwell's face in the frame.
Just then, the second crate was being moved. With only one man to guide it, its weight shifted unexpectedly. The crate tilted, slid, and crashed to the pavement with a splintering of wood. A corner broke open, revealing not a dough mixer, but a glimpse of chrome, flashing lights, and a panel of complex buttons.
Penny didn't hesitate. She took two more rapid shots of the broken crate and the exposed machine. That was the money shot. The undeniable proof.
"Got it!" she whispered triumphantly.
Her elation was short-lived. The guard attending to Sara had helped the girl to her feet. Sara, miraculously, was no longer moaning. She was, in fact, brushing snow from her pants and grinning at the guard.
"Just a little spill," she said cheerfully. "No harm done!"
The guard, realizing he had been duped, turned back toward the truck just in time to see Penny scrambling back up the hill. He saw the camera in her hand. His eyes widened in recognition and fury.
"Hey! You!" he roared, pointing a thick finger. "It's the girl! Stop her!"
Harvey Maxwell spun around. His face, when he saw Penny, became a mask of pure rage. "Get her! And get that camera!"
Penny's blood ran cold. She turned and fled, crashing through the underbrush with Francine close behind her. The heavy boots of the second guard pounded on the snow-covered ground behind them. They were exposed, running through the open woods, and their pursuer was gaining.

CHAPTER 16
A DESPERATE GAMBLE

The guard’s heavy boots crunched menacingly in the snow, each step closer than the last. Penny risked a glance over her shoulder. The man was burly and surprisingly fast, his face a grimace of determination. He was no more than thirty yards behind them.

"He's gaining!" gasped Francine, stumbling over a hidden root.

Penny grabbed the other girl's arm, pulling her upright. "Keep going! We can't let him get the camera!"

The precious camera, slung over her shoulder, bumped against her back with every jarring step. It felt impossibly heavy. The woods offered some cover but the deep snow dragged at their legs, stealing their speed and their breath. Every gasp was a plume of white in the frigid air.

"I can't," Francine panted, her voice thin with panic. "My lungs are on fire."

"Yes, you can," Penny said grimly, her own legs burning with effort. "Think of the story, Francine!"

That, at least, seemed to give the reporter a fresh burst of energy. They scrambled onward, their pursuer's labored breathing now audible behind them. He was close. Terribly close. Penny's mind raced. They could not outrun him for much longer. They needed a plan, something more than just a mad dash through the trees.

Ahead, the woods thinned slightly, opening onto a steep, treeless expanse of snow that dropped away sharply. Penny recognized it with a jolt. It was the slope directly above Horseshoe Curve, the treacherous bend in the ski trail Jake had warned her about. The snow here looked different, less packed, with a heavy cornice overhanging the edge where the wind had piled it deep. It looked unstable, dangerous.

A desperate idea, a wild gamble, sparked in her mind.

"Francine, up here!" Penny urged, changing direction and scrambling sideways up the steepest part of the incline. "Hurry!"

"Where are we going?" Francine cried, slipping and sliding on the loose powder. "This is harder!"

"It's our only chance," Penny said between clenched teeth, digging her boot toes in for purchase. She pointed to the thick shelf of snow above them. "We're going to give our friend a surprise."

The guard, seeing their change in tactics, bellowed in frustration. He was directly below them now, beginning his own clumsy ascent. He was heavier, and the deep snow hindered him even more than it had them. Still, he kept coming, his hand outstretched as if he could grab them.

Reaching the top of the rise, Penny and Francine found themselves on a narrow ledge beside the deep, overhanging drift. Below them, the guard was clawing his way up the last few yards of the slope.

"What now?" Francine whispered, her eyes wide with fear.

"We push," Penny ordered, dropping to her knees beside the cornice. She began kicking and shoving at the solid-looking mass of snow. "Help me!"

Francine hesitated for only a second before dropping beside her. Together, they pushed with their hands and kicked with their feet. The snow was more solid than it looked. For a heart-stopping moment, nothing happened. The guard was almost upon them, a triumphant snarl on his face.

"It's not working!" Francine shrieked.

"Harder!" Penny grunted, putting her entire weight into a final, desperate shove.

There was a deep, groaning crack. The entire ledge of snow beneath them shuddered. A fracture line raced across the surface like a bolt of lightning. With a sound like muffled thunder, the massive cornice gave way. A wall of white powder, chunks of ice, and debris cascaded down the slope.

It was not a true avalanche, but it was more than enough. The guard, caught completely by surprise, let out a yell as the wave of snow struck him. He lost his footing, tumbling backward in a flurry of white. The slide swept past him, roaring down the hill and piling up in a deep, impassable drift across the path he had been climbing. He was not buried, but he was half-submerged in the deep powder, floundering uselessly. Their path of retreat was completely cut off.

Penny didn't wait to see more. She grabbed Francine's hand. "Come on! Down the other side!"

They slid and scrambled down the back of the ridge, away from the scene, their escape assured for the moment. They ran until their lungs burned and their legs felt like lead, not stopping until the sounds of the pursuit had faded entirely. Finally, in the shelter of a thick stand of pines, Penny collapsed against a tree, clutching the camera to her chest.

Francine leaned over, hands on her knees, taking in huge, ragged gulps of air. "I thought," she gasped, "I thought we were done for."

"We're not," Penny said, her own voice shaky. The camera was safe. The film was safe. "But he saw us. Maxwell saw us. They know who we are."

Francine straightened up, a flicker of her old professional hardness returning to her eyes. "They know you, you mean. You're the one he threw out of the hotel." Her gaze fell upon the camera in Penny's arms. "But we got it. We got the proof."

Penny nodded, a grim satisfaction cutting through her fear. "We got it."

They stood in silence for a moment, the only sounds the sighing of the wind in the pines and their own harsh breathing. The immediate danger was past, but a new, cold dread was setting in.

"What about Sara?" Penny asked, her voice barely a whisper. "We left her there. With Maxwell."

Francine's face paled. In the thrill of the chase, she had forgotten the other girl. "He was furious, Penny. What do you think he'll do to her?"

Penny pushed herself away from the tree. Her exhaustion was forgotten, replaced by a surge of anxiety. "We have to find out. We have to get to her." But where could they go? The Downey lodge was the first place Maxwell's men would look. The Fergus hotel was out of the question.

There was only one place.

"The Jasko cabin," Penny said firmly. "It's the last place they'll expect us to go."

"Are you crazy?" Francine retorted. "What if her grandfather is there? He hates trespassers! He'll probably turn us over to Maxwell himself."

"He's chopping wood all week, Sara told me. He won't be back until evening," Penny argued. "Besides, we have to take the risk. It's the only place we might find Sara. Come on."

She did not wait for an answer, but started off through the trees, moving with a new sense of purpose. After a moment's hesitation, Francine followed. The desperate gamble had worked, but Penny knew their troubles were far from over.

CHAPTER 17

AN UNEASY TRUCE

The trek to the Jasko cabin was a tense, silent affair. Penny led the way, forcing them to take a roundabout route through the thickest parts of the woods, staying well away from any open trails or clearings. Every snap of a twig made them jump. Every shadow seemed to hold a lurking threat. Penny clutched the camera like a lifeline, its cold metal a constant reminder of the evidence they carried, and the danger it represented.

"This is foolish," Francine muttered for the tenth time as they paused behind a granite boulder overlooking the valley. "We should be heading down to Pine Top, to a telephone."

"And say what?" Penny shot back, her patience wearing thin. "That we took some pictures? Maxwell will say we're hysterical girls making up stories. Without the developed film, we have nothing but our word against his. He'd have the whole thing hushed up in an hour."

Francine fell silent, unable to argue with the cold logic of Penny's words.

At last, the familiar shape of the Jasko cabin came into view, smoke curling from its stone chimney. A curl of fear tightened in Penny's stomach. Smoke meant someone was home. What if Sara's grandfather had returned early?

"Someone's there," Francine whispered, pointing at the chimney.

"Sara must have rebuilt the fire," Penny reasoned, though she did not feel as confident as she sounded. "We have to risk it."

They crept closer, using the woodshed for cover. The cabin was quiet. There was no sign of Peter Jasko's formidable presence, no sound of an axe splitting wood. Taking a deep breath, Penny darted from the woodshed to the side of the cabin, pressing herself flat against the cold logs. She motioned for Francine to follow.

"The key should still be here," Penny whispered, reaching into the familiar hollow log. Her fingers closed around the cold, heavy iron key. Relief washed over her. At least this part of her plan had not failed.

With painstaking slowness, she inserted the key into the lock of the main door. The bolt turned with a loud, grating click that sounded like a gunshot in the winter silence. They both froze, listening. Nothing. Penny pushed the door open a crack and peered inside.

The main room was empty, warmed by a crackling fire in the hearth. A pair of wet mittens and a hat were steaming on a stool near the flames. Sara's.

"She's here," Penny breathed, pushing the door open wider. They slipped inside, shutting and bolting it securely behind them.

For a moment they just stood there, letting the warmth of the cabin seep into their chilled bodies. They were safe, for now. Francine dropped into a rustic wooden chair, her face pale and smudged with dirt.

"I never want to do that again," she declared, her voice trembling slightly.

Penny set the camera down gently on the heavy oak table. Now that they were out of immediate danger, the rivalry that had been simmering just below the surface boiled over.

"Give me the camera," Francine demanded, her voice regaining its sharp edge.

Penny turned to face her. "No."

"That film is my property as much as it is yours, Penny Parker! I was there. I helped you!"

"You helped me by panicking and complaining," Penny retorted, her own temper flaring. "I'm the one who took the pictures. I'm the one who got us away from that guard. This is my father's story."

"Your father's story?" Francine scoffed, getting to her feet. "This is the biggest story to hit Pine Top in years! This is my ticket to a real city paper. I'm not letting a schoolgirl scoop me on it."

She made a lunge for the camera, but Penny was faster. She snatched it up, holding it behind her back. "We're not going to get any story if we start fighting each other! Maxwell is out there looking for us. Don't you understand that? He's not just going to let us walk away."

The gravity of Penny's words seemed to penetrate Francine's ambition. She stopped, her shoulders slumping. "What do we do then? We're trapped here."

"We need a darkroom," Penny said, thinking aloud. "We need to develop this film. We need prints. Hard evidence."

Just then, they heard the sound of the trapdoor to the loft creaking open. Both girls spun around, their hearts leaping into their throats.

Sara Jasko's head appeared in the opening. Her face was white and strained, but her eyes lit up with immense relief when she saw them. "Penny! Francine! You made it!"

She scrambled down the ladder, rushing to Penny and throwing her arms around her. "I was so worried! I saw them chase you into the woods. I thought for sure they'd caught you."

"We're alright, Sara," Penny said, hugging her back. "But what about you? What did Maxwell do?"

Sara pulled away, shuddering at the memory. "He was horrible," she said, her voice dropping. "He grabbed my arm and twisted it. He said he knew I did it on purpose and that I'd pay for it."

"The brute!" Francine exclaimed.

"But then the truck driver, Mr. Henderson, he stepped in," Sara continued. "He lives down in the village. He told Maxwell to leave me alone, that my grandfather was Peter Jasko and wouldn't stand for anyone manhandling his granddaughter. Maxwell looked like he wanted to explode, but I think he realized Mr. Henderson would be another witness. He let me go. He told me if I ever said a word about what I saw, he'd make sure my grandfather lost his trapping license."

Penny's blood ran cold. Maxwell was even more ruthless than she had imagined, willing to threaten an old man's livelihood to ensure a girl's silence.

"After they left, I got my skis and came straight here," Sara finished. "I was terrified they'd go to the Downey lodge looking for you."

"They will," Penny said grimly. "That's probably where they went right after they lost us." She looked from Sara to Francine. The three of them were in this together now. "We have the pictures that can stop him for good. But we have to get them developed."

"There's no darkroom here," Sara said, glancing around the rustic cabin. "Grandfather doesn't even have electricity."

"There must be a place in Pine Top," Francine insisted. "A camera shop, or the local newspaper."

"The _Pine Top Sentinel_," Penny said, an idea forming. "My father knows the editor, a Mr. Abernathy. He's mentioned him before. He said he runs the whole paper by himself."

"He'd have a darkroom," Francine said, a glimmer of hope in her eyes. "We could go there."

"Maxwell will be watching the roads," Penny countered. "And we can't just walk into town. He knows what I look like."

"Then I'll go," Francine declared. "He's never seen me up close. I'll take the film."

"Absolutely not," Penny said flatly. "I'm not letting this film out of my sight."

"Then what do you suggest, Penny?" Francine asked, exasperated. "We can't stay here forever. Peter Jasko will be back this evening."

Penny paced the floor of the small cabin, her mind working furiously. They were trapped on the mountain, with their enemies searching and their time running out. They needed to get to Pine Top, to the safety of the newspaper office and its darkroom, without being seen.

"We won't take the road," Penny said at last, stopping in front of the window that looked out over the valley. The sun was beginning to dip lower in the sky, casting long blue shadows across the snow. "We'll go after dark. And we'll go on skis."

"On skis?" Francine looked aghast. "It's miles to Pine Top! And I'm not a good skier."

"It's mostly downhill," Sara chimed in, her adventurous spirit returning. "And it's faster than walking. We can follow the logging trails. They're never used at night. Grandfather has extra pairs of skis in the woodshed."

Penny looked at Francine. "It's our best chance. We'll leave as soon as it's fully dark. We can be in Pine Top in an hour if we're fast."

Francine looked from Penny's determined face to Sara's encouraging one. She sighed, a long, drawn out sound of resignation. "Alright," she said. "But if I break my leg, you're carrying me the rest of the way. And the story gets a double byline. Parker and Sellberg."

Penny almost smiled. "It's a deal," she said. "For now."

They had a plan. It was risky and it was dangerous, but it was a plan. As darkness began to fall, cloaking the mountain in a blanket of purple and grey, the three girls prepared for their nighttime flight, the roll of film on the table representing their only hope of victory.

The last sliver of the sun vanished behind the western peaks, plunging the valley into a deep indigo twilight. Inside the Jasko cabin, a heavy silence settled over the three girls, broken only by the nervous tapping of Francine's foot against the rough-hewn floorboards. Penny stood by the window, her arms crossed, watching the darkness swallow the familiar landmarks of the mountain. Every shadow seemed to hold a threat, every gust of wind sounded like an approaching footstep.

"How long do we wait?" Francine's voice was a tense whisper. She sat hunched on a low stool, her face pale in the gloom.

"Until it's completely dark," Penny answered without turning. "The moon will be up later. It should give us enough light to see the trail, but not enough to be seen from the road."

Sara rummaged in a small cupboard and produced three slightly bruised apples and a hunk of dry cheese. "It's not much," she said apologetically, "but we should eat something. We'll need our strength."

They ate in silence, the meager meal doing little to warm them. Francine picked at her share, her appetite lost to anxiety. "I still think this is a fool's errand," she muttered, setting her apple down half eaten. "Skiing in the dead of night. We could break our necks."

"It is better than being found by Harvey Maxwell," Sara said quietly. "Grandfather says the old logging trails are safe. No one uses them. We will be ghosts in the forest."

"Your grandfather also locks you in a loft," Francine retorted.

"Girls, that's enough," Penny said, her tone sharp. "We're in this together. Arguing won't help. Francine, you'll be fine. Sara and I are good skiers. We won't leave you behind." Penny's gaze fell on the small, canister of film that sat on the table, a tiny object holding immense power. She picked it up and slipped it deep into the pocket of her ski jacket, zipping it securely. "This is what matters. We get this developed, and Maxwell's reign at the Fergus hotel is over."

Suddenly, a loud snap echoed from the trees just outside the cabin. All three girls froze. Francine let out a small gasp and Sara's eyes widened. Penny held up a hand for silence, her own heart beginning to hammer against her ribs. She crept to the window, peering cautiously into the impenetrable darkness beyond the glass.

"What is it?" Francine whispered, her voice trembling. "Is it them?"

They listened, every muscle tensed. The wind moaned through the pines, a lonely, desolate sound. Then came another, softer rustling, followed by the faint crunch of a hoof on hardened snow.

"It's just a deer," Sara breathed, a relieved smile touching her lips. "They come down from the high ridges at night to look for food."

The collective sigh of relief in the small cabin was audible. The incident, however, served as a stark reminder of their precarious situation. The woods were alive, and not all of its inhabitants would be so harmless.

"It's time," Penny announced, her voice firm. "Let's get the skis."

The air outside was bitingly cold, and the darkness was absolute, a thick, black velvet that seemed to press in from all sides. The stars overhead were brilliant points of ice in the vast sky. Penny led the way to the woodshed, the beam of her small pocket torch dancing ahead of them. Inside, the shed smelled of pine resin and cold earth. Skis of various lengths and ages were stacked against one wall.

"These will do," Sara said, pulling out two pairs of sturdy-looking hickory skis. "They were my mother's and father's. And here's a third pair. They are a little old, but the bindings are strong."

Francine eyed the equipment dubiously. "I haven't been on skis since I was a child. I'm going to be a disaster."

"Just keep your knees bent and your weight forward," Sara instructed patiently, helping the reporter clamp her boots into the bindings. "And don't try to do anything fancy. Just follow in my tracks. Penny can ski behind you, just in case."

"In case of what?" Francine asked nervously.

"In case you decide to have a sudden meeting with a snowbank," Penny said with a grim little smile. She buckled her own skis on, the familiar metallic click a comforting sound. She felt more at home on a pair of skis than she did anywhere else. Tonight, they were not just for sport; they were their instruments of escape.

"Is everyone ready?" Penny asked, pulling her knit cap down over her ears.

Sara nodded, her face alight with a mixture of fear and exhilaration. Francine gave a weak, unconvincing nod, her knuckles white where she gripped her ski poles.

"Alright," said Penny, taking a deep breath of the frigid air. "Sara, you lead. I'll take the rear. And Francine, no matter what, try to keep up and stay quiet. Our lives may depend on it."

Sara pushed off, her skis whispering over the snow as she glided away from the cabin and disappeared into the trees. Penny gave Francine a slight nudge. "Go on. You can do it."

With a visible effort, Francine pushed herself forward, wobbling precariously before finding a semblance of balance. She moved slowly, awkwardly, a dark shape against the snow. Penny followed, her senses on high alert, her eyes scanning the shadowy woods around them, her ears straining for any sound that did not belong. Their desperate flight had begun.

The first part of the descent was through a stand of thick firs where the snow lay deep and soft. Sara moved with a fluid grace, weaving a perfect path between the dark trunks of the trees. The only sounds were the soft swish of their skis and the occasional huff of their own breath in the cold air. A partial moon had risen, casting a pale, ethereal light that filtered through the branches, creating a confusing patchwork of light and shadow on the trail ahead.

For Francine, the journey was a nightmare. She was stiff with fear, her movements jerky and uncoordinated. Within the first hundred yards, her ski tips crossed and she pitched forward into a deep drift of powder, letting out a stifled cry of frustration.

Penny was beside her in an instant, helping her disentangle herself. "Are you hurt?"

"Only my pride," the reporter grumbled, brushing snow from her face. "This is impossible, Penny. I can't see a thing."

"You're doing fine," Penny said, her voice more patient than she felt. "Just try to relax. Don't fight the skis. Let them run."

They started again, but their progress was painfully slow. Francine fell twice more, her poles flailing, her muttered complaints growing louder with each tumble. Sara would ski back to them, her face etched with worry.

"We are moving too slowly," Sara whispered during one such stop. "At this rate, it will take us until morning to reach the valley."

"We have to go faster," Penny agreed, turning to Francine. "We don't have time for this, Francine. You have to try harder."

"I am trying!" Francine snapped, her voice rising. "This is not the press club cocktail hour! It's a dark mountain and I am in over my head!"

"Quiet!" Penny hissed, grabbing her arm. "Do you want Maxwell's men to hear you?"

As if on cue, a distant glimmer of light caught Penny's eye. She pointed down the mountain. Far below, on the winding switchbacks of the main road, a pair of headlights was slowly crawling upwards. Another pair followed behind it.

"Look," Penny said, her voice low and urgent. "They're still searching."

The three girls watched the headlights, tiny beacons of danger in the vast darkness. The sight seemed to drain the last of the fight from Francine.

"They're going to catch us," she said, her voice barely a whisper.

"Not if we move," Sara said, her jaw set. She pointed to a fork in the trail just ahead. "There. The right path is an old ski run. It's much steeper and faster, but it cuts nearly a mile off the journey. It will take us far away from the road."

"Is it safe?" Penny asked.

"It's narrow and it has sharp turns," Sara admitted. "But it is our best chance. We must take it."

Penny looked at Francine's frightened face. There was no other choice. "We'll take it," she said. "Francine, you stay right between us. Sara, you call out the turns. We'll get through this."

The steeper trail was a terrifying ribbon of white plunging down through the dark woods. The pace quickened dramatically. Wind rushed past Penny's ears and stung her cheeks. Sara, skiing just ahead of Francine, became their guide, her voice calling out warnings in the night.

"Left turn coming! Hard left!"

"Watch for ice on the right!"

Penny kept close behind Francine, ready to catch her or block her fall. The reporter, spurred by adrenaline, was skiing better than before. She was still clumsy, but her fear had translated into a desperate focus. They flew down the mountain, a trio of fleeting shadows, their world narrowed to the few feet of moonlit trail in front of them.

They had covered another half mile when Sara's urgent shout cut through the night. "Stop! Trail blocked!"

Penny dug her edges in, sending a shower of snow into the air as she skidded to a halt. Francine, unable to stop so quickly, crashed into a snowbank beside the trail with a soft whump. Ahead of them, a massive pine tree, a victim of some long-forgotten storm, lay directly across their path. Its trunk was as high as their chests, its tangled branches creating an impassable barrier.

"We'll have to climb over," Sara said, already unfastening her skis.

As Penny helped a sputtering Francine out of the snowbank, another sound reached them, a sound that made her blood run cold. It was the sound of men's voices, carried on the still night air. They were close. Terribly close.

"Down! Get down!" Penny whispered frantically, shoving Francine and Sara behind the massive fallen log.

The three girls crouched in the snow, hardly daring to breathe. The voices grew louder. Two men were making their way through the trees, their powerful flashlights cutting swathes through the darkness not fifty feet from their hiding place. Penny's heart pounded so hard she was sure they would hear it. She clutched the canister of film in her pocket, a hard knot of dread tightening in her stomach.

"See anything, Al?" one voice grumbled. He sounded cold and tired.

"Nothin' but trees and more trees," the other voice replied. "This is a waste of time. The boss is crazy. That Parker girl is probably tucked in her bed back at the Downey lodge by now."

"Maybe. But he said to search these woods 'til sunup. Said she was a snooper, and she saw something she shouldn't have." The beam of a flashlight swept over the log, passing inches above their heads. Penny squeezed her eyes shut.

"Well, she ain't here," the first man said with finality. "I'm freezing. Let's check the ridge and then head back to the car. I need a hot coffee."

The voices and the beams of light began to move away, fading back into the forest. Penny, Sara, and Francine remained frozen in place for what felt like an eternity, listening until the last faint echo of their presence was gone.

Only then did Penny let out a shaky breath. "That was too close."

Francine was trembling, her earlier bravado completely gone. "They would have found us," she whispered, horrified. "If it wasn't for this log..."

"We can't stay here," Sara urged, her voice regaining its composure. "They might come back."

With renewed urgency, they clambered over the fallen tree, their movements clumsy in the deep snow. They strapped their skis back on and continued their descent, the near-capture having shocked them into a silent, focused efficiency. Francine no longer complained. She skied with a grim determination, falling only once more and scrambling back to her feet without a word.

The trees began to thin, and soon they emerged from the forest onto a gentle, open slope at the base of the mountain. Below them, nestled in the valley, lay the town of Pine Top. A few scattered lights twinkled in the windows, but for the most part, the town was asleep under a blanket of fresh snow and moonlight. They had made it.

"We did it," Sara breathed, a triumphant grin spreading across her face.

"We're not done yet," Penny reminded her, though she felt a surge of relief herself. "We still have to get to the newspaper office without being seen."

They found a dense thicket of pine trees at the edge of the woods and carefully hid their skis and poles, marking the spot with a broken branch. They would have to come back for them, but that was a problem for later. On foot now, they moved like spies, sticking to the deep shadows cast by the buildings, their boots making soft crunching sounds in the otherwise silent town.

The main street was deserted. A single streetlight cast a lonely yellow glow on the snow-covered pavement. Penny scanned the storefronts, her eyes searching.

"It should be here somewhere," she murmured. "My father said it was a small operation."

"There!" Francine pointed.

Halfway down the block, a modest two-story building had a weathered sign hanging above the door: _PINE TOP SENTINEL_. Just as Penny had hoped, a single light burned in a second-floor window. Mr. Abernathy was working late.

Their goal was just a few yards away. They crept along the wall of the building, staying out of the streetlight's glow. Reaching the recessed doorway, Penny put her hand on the cold brass knob. This was it. The final, most crucial step. She had to convince a man she had never met to help three strange girls in the middle of the night, against a man as powerful as Harvey Maxwell. She took a deep breath, looked at Sara and Francine, and pushed the door. It was unlocked. Slipping inside, they closed it gently behind them, finding themselves in a dark front office that smelled of ink and old paper. A narrow staircase led upwards, toward the light.

The stairs creaked ominously with every step, the sound unnaturally loud in the profound silence. Penny led the way, her hand trailing along the dusty wall, while Sara and Francine followed close behind. The air grew warmer as they ascended, and the smell of hot metal and lead mingled with the ink. Reaching the top of the stairs, they found themselves in a small landing before a door, its glass pane fogged with warmth from within. The name _J. ABERNATHY, EDITOR_ was painted in faded gold letters.

Penny hesitated for only a second before knocking. The sound was a sharp report in the quiet building. From inside, they heard the scraping of a chair and heavy footsteps. The door swung open, revealing a man who seemed to be made of paper and ink himself. He was stoop-shouldered and thin, with an ink-stained apron tied over a rumpled shirt. A green visor was pushed up on his balding head, and his spectacles were perched on the end of his nose. He peered at the three girls with weary, astonished eyes.

"We're closed," he said, his voice a gravelly rumble. "Come back in the morning."

He started to shut the door, but Penny quickly put her foot in the way. "Please, Mr. Abernathy, we must speak with you. It's urgent."

The man's eyes narrowed. He looked from Penny's earnest face to Sara's impatient one, and finally to Francine, who was shivering slightly from cold and nerves. "How do you know my name?"

"My father told me about you," Penny explained, withdrawing her foot as he opened the door a crack wider. "Anthony Parker, of the _Riverview Star_."

A flicker of recognition, and then suspicion, crossed the editor's face. "Parker's girl, eh? I might have known. What sort of trouble are you stirring up in Pine Top?"

"It's Harvey Maxwell," Penny said directly. "He's running a crooked gambling den in the Fergus hotel."

Mr. Abernathy let out a short, harsh laugh. "Is that all? Girl, that's the worst kept secret in this county. Now if you'll excuse me, I have a paper to put out."

"But it's more than that," Penny pressed, refusing to be dismissed. "He's cheating people. And we have proof."

This gave the editor pause. He grunted, studying them for a long moment before finally stepping back. "All right, come in. But make it quick. And don't touch anything. This place isn't a playground."

They filed into the office, a chaotic but strangely organized room. A large flatbed press stood silent in one corner, while stacks of newsprint and trays of lead type covered every available surface. The air was thick with a comfortable, industrious smell. The editor gestured toward a trio of mismatched wooden chairs.

"Sit," he commanded, resuming his own place at a cluttered desk under the single glowing lamp. He leaned back, the chair groaning in protest, and folded his arms. "Now, what is this great proof you're talking about?"

Penny took a deep breath and began the story. She started with her arrival at the Downey lodge, her suspicions about Maxwell, and her discovery of the green ticket. She told him about the Green Room, the rigged roulette wheel, and the specially made fishing rod used to control it.

As she spoke, Mr. Abernathy listened without interruption, his expression unreadable. Sara fidgeted in her chair, while Francine, her reporter's instincts taking over, watched the old editor with keen interest, trying to gauge his reaction.

"We saw it all," Penny concluded. "Francine was there, too. We were hiding."

"Hiding?" Abernathy raised a skeptical eyebrow.

"In a closet," Francine spoke up, her voice clear and professional. "I'm Francine Sellberg, with the _Tribune_. I was following a lead. Everything Miss Parker says is true. The game is crooked. The ball is made of steel, and Maxwell's man uses a powerful magnet hidden in a custom fishing rod to guide it."

Abernathy rubbed his tired eyes. "A reporter from the _Tribune_ and Parker's girl. You two are a fine pair. But it's still your word against his. Harvey Maxwell owns half this town, and the other half is afraid of him. I can't print a story like that based on the word of three girls who were trespassing in his hotel."

"But we weren't just trespassing!" Sara burst out, unable to contain herself any longer. "They chased us! We had to ski down the back of the mountain in the middle of the night! They had dogs!"

The editor's gaze shifted to Sara. "Jasko's girl, aren't you? Peter will have a fit if he knows you're mixed up in this."

"That doesn't matter," Sara said defiantly. "What matters is they tried to catch us because we know the truth. Because we took their precious rod!"

Abernathy's head snapped back toward Penny. His eyes, which had been dull with weariness, now held a sharp, focused light. "You took it? The rod?"

Penny nodded. She reached inside her ski jacket, her hand closing around the two wooden pieces wrapped in her scarf. Carefully, she drew them out and laid them on the edge of Abernathy's desk. The light from the lamp glinted off the metal fittings and the fine wires almost invisible against the dark varnish.

The editor leaned forward, his earlier lethargy gone. He did not touch the pieces at first, merely staring at them. He picked up his spectacles from his nose and polished them with a corner of his apron before setting them back in place for a closer look.

"It broke when I fell," Penny explained. "But you can see how it works. The wires run from the handle to the tip."

With a surprising gentleness, Mr. Abernathy picked up the larger piece, the handle. His thumb found a small, discreet button near the reel seat. He pressed it. Nothing happened, of course, with the wires severed, but he nodded to himself as if confirming a theory. He then examined the tip, peering at the powerful, compact electromagnet concealed within.

"Custom work," he murmured, more to himself than to the girls. "Clever. Very clever."

He set the pieces down and leaned back in his chair again, stroking his chin. The girls watched him, holding their breath. The ticking of a large clock on the wall was the only sound.

"This changes things," he said finally. "This is physical evidence. This isn't just a story, it's an exhibit." He looked at Penny. "Maxwell knows you have this?"

"He knows I'm Anthony Parker's daughter," Penny said. "He threw me out of the hotel tonight. He took my admittance card, but I don't think he knows I have the rod. His men were chasing us for it, but they couldn't have known for sure that we got it. They just knew we were in the room."

"And he doesn't know you're here," Abernathy mused.

"No one does," Francine added. "We were very careful."

The editor stood up and began to pace the narrow space between his desk and the printing press. "Maxwell has had this town in a stranglehold for years. He calls it 'progress'. He brings in the tourists, yes, but he also brings in his own brand of trouble. He's pushed good people out of business. He's corrupted local officials. I've wanted to go after him for a long time, but I never had anything solid. Anything that would stick."

He stopped pacing and jabbed a finger at the broken rod on his desk. "This will stick."

A wave of relief washed over Penny. "Then you'll print the story?"

"Print it?" Abernathy chuckled, a dry, rusty sound. "My dear girl, we're going to do more than that. A story can be denied. Maxwell will just call it 'yellow journalism' from a rival town, an attack on Pine Top's prosperity. No. We need to do this right."

He walked over to a large calendar on the wall, flipping a page. "Tomorrow night is Saturday. It's the biggest night of the week at the Fergus. The stakes are high, and the room is full of Maxwell's special 'investors' from the city. He won't be expecting trouble."

"What are you planning?" Francine asked, her notepad and pencil now appearing as if by magic.

"I'm going to print your story," he said, turning to face them, his eyes gleaming. "All of it. Your eyewitness account, Miss Parker. Your corroboration, Miss Sellberg. A detailed description of this little device." His voice dropped. "But I'm not going to distribute it. Not at first."

"I don't understand," Penny said.

"We're going to print up a special, one-page edition of the _Pine Top Sentinel_," he explained, a conspiratorial excitement entering his voice. "An extra. We'll lay it all out tonight. I can have a few hundred copies run off by morning, before my usual boy comes in. We'll keep them hidden."

"And then what?" Sara asked, leaning forward eagerly.

"And then," Abernathy said with a thin smile, "tomorrow night, at the height of the evening's festivities, we are going to deliver the news. Directly to the Fergus hotel."

The audacity of the plan took Penny's breath away. "You want us to hand them out in the hotel lobby?"

"Precisely! While the game is in full swing. We'll create a sensation. Some of those 'investors' Maxwell has are not the type to appreciate being made fools of. When the story breaks, right in their faces, with you girls there to point the finger, Maxwell won't be able to contain it. The local constable is in Maxwell's pocket, but the county sheriff is an honest man. He'll have to act if there's a public outcry."

"It's risky," Francine stated, but her eyes were sparkling. "It's brilliant."

"It's journalism," Abernathy corrected her. "The kind that gets forgotten when men like Maxwell start signing advertising contracts. It will take all night to set the type and run the press. I'll need your help."

"Of course," Penny said without hesitation.

"I can't set type," Sara admitted, "but I'm strong. I can carry things or crank a handle."

"Good. I'll put you to work," the editor said, already moving toward his type cases. "Miss Sellberg, I want you and Miss Parker to write the account. Every detail you can remember. Be precise. Be factual. The truth is all the weapon we need."

A sudden creak from the staircase below made them all freeze. Abernathy instantly switched off the desk lamp, plunging the room into near darkness, illuminated only by the pale moonlight filtering through the grimy windows.

"Shhh," he whispered, holding up a hand.

Penny’s heart leaped into her throat. Had they been followed? Was it one of Maxwell's men? Or the town constable making his rounds? They stood frozen in place, listening. Heavy, rhythmic footsteps echoed from the street outside, growing louder and then fading away. After a moment of tense silence, Abernathy crept to the window and peered down.

"Just old Officer Smith," he breathed, a sigh of relief in his voice. "He walks like a draft horse. Still, it's a reminder. We're working on borrowed time."

He switched the light back on, and the spell was broken. The office was once again a hive of activity. Abernathy cleared a space for Penny and Francine at a small side table, providing them with paper and pencils.

"Write it together," he instructed. "Combine your recollections. It will be stronger that way."

For the first time, Penny and Francine looked at each other not as rivals, but as collaborators. A small, understanding smile passed between them. "Let's start with the Green Room," Penny suggested.

"Right," Francine agreed, her pencil already poised. "The exact time was..."

While they wrote, Abernathy showed Sara how to lock a chase, the heavy iron frame used to hold the lead type for the press. The old editor worked with a focused, frantic energy, his thin fingers flying across the type case as he selected letters, arranging them on his composing stick. He was a man possessed by a purpose he had long since thought lost.

The hours flew by. The girls, exhausted from their mountain ordeal, found a second wind fueled by coffee from Abernathy’s perpetually hot stove and the thrill of the unfolding plan. Penny and Francine finished their article, a damning, fact-filled account of Maxwell’s entire operation. Abernathy read it, nodding his approval before beginning the laborious process of typesetting it. Sara proved to be a surprisingly helpful assistant, her strength useful in moving the heavy trays and her quick reflexes adept at fetching tools for the printer.

As the first hints of dawn touched the sky, painting the windows a pale, washed-out grey, the final form was locked into the press.

"All right," Abernathy announced, wiping sweat from his brow with a greasy rag. "Time for the magic."

He and Sara inked the rollers and laid the first sheet of newsprint. With a mighty heave on a long lever, the editor brought the press down. He released it, and Sara carefully peeled the paper off the type. There, in bold black ink under the masthead of the _Pine Top Sentinel EXTRA_, was their story.

_MAXWELL'S CROOKED CASINO EXPOSED!_ the headline screamed.

They worked in a steady rhythm, printing copy after copy. The pile of single-page papers grew on a side table, a stack of dynamite waiting for a match. By the time the sun had fully risen, casting long shadows across the valley, they had a formidable bundle of five hundred newspapers.

Abernathy tied them with twine. "We'll hide these in the cellar. I'll bring my delivery truck around to the back of the hotel tomorrow evening about nine o'clock. You girls be there. We'll load you and the papers in the back and get you inside that way."

He looked at the three of them, his weary face holding a newfound respect. "You're brave girls. Your father would be proud, Penny. And your grandfather too, Sara, though he'd never admit it."

He handed Penny one of the fresh, damp copies. She folded it and tucked it securely in her pocket. It felt heavier than a single sheet of paper. It felt like justice.

They slipped out the back door as the town began to stir. The air was cold and crisp, promising another clear winter day. They were exhausted, hungry, and facing the most dangerous part of their plan, but they were no longer just three girls on the run. They were a team with a purpose.

"We have to get back to the woodshed and get our skis before anyone sees us," Penny said, her mind already racing ahead. "Then we need to get some sleep. We have a big night ahead of us."

"A big night," Sara grinned, her eyes flashing with excitement. "We're going to bring down Harvey Maxwell!"

Francine looked from Sara's eager face to Penny's determined one. "This is the biggest story of my career," she said, her voice filled with awe. "And I'm a part of it."

As they hurried toward the edge of town, Penny glanced back at the quiet newspaper office. A thin tendril of smoke was now rising from its chimney, a sign of life, a beacon of defiance. The race against Harvey Maxwell was on, and for the first time, Penny felt certain they were going to win.
