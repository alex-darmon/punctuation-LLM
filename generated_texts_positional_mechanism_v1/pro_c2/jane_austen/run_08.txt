He was not content, however; his amusement required a reply. “One hears so much of this anonymous Poetess,” Mr. Davies continued, turning the pages of the slim volume with a theatrical air. “They say she resides not a hundred miles from this very spot. What a singular coincidence, Miss Morton, that these verses on the melancholy beauty of the changing seasons should appear just after your long visit to your aunt in the North! I dare say the landscapes there provided a wealth of inspiration.” He looked at her with a smile that was all knowingness and no sympathy. Miss Morton, who was engaged in arranging a bowl of flowers, did not look up. “I imagine the North is inspiring to a great many people, sir,” she said, her voice quiet but steady. “The author may as well have been in Scotland, or indeed, in London, dreaming of the country.” “Dreaming, yes! An excellent word. ‘The dreams of a solitary heart,’ is not that one of the titles? I wonder if your aunt, Mrs. Willoughby, has had the pleasure of reading the collection. Does she conjecture as to the author’s identity? Or perhaps she has a closer knowledge of the matter, an immediate intimation of the poet’s labours?” He paused, but Miss Morton merely snipped a dead leaf from a rose stem. She would not be drawn. Catherine, for her part, could not help but be diverted by Mr. Davies’s sport, though she felt a slight pang of guilt for it. It seemed plain enough that Miss Morton, with her romantic notions and her habit of solitary walks, wished to be thought the mysterious authoress, and Mr. Davies was wicked to expose the vanity of it. “You are too severe, Mr. Davies,” she said, playing her part in the charade. “You will make Miss Morton afraid to admire any poetry at all, lest you accuse her of writing it.” “I would never do such a thing!” he exclaimed, placing a hand on his heart. “I only wish I could discover the lady’s name, that I might offer my compliments in person. There, it is settled. I have tidied this little stack of papers for you, Mrs. Allen, and restored order to your correspondence.” He gave a little bow to the lady of the house, who thanked him profusely for so small a service. To escape her gratitude, he moved towards the window where Miss Morton now stood. “If you are very obliging,” said he, in a lower voice, “you will tell me which of these poems is your favourite. Let me guess, is it ‘The Deserted Garden’? You have a look of deserted gardens about you sometimes. I believe you enjoyed your solitude in the North a little too well. I would have given anything, all one ever has to give, for a place in such a peaceful retirement.” She looked at him then, a flash of something, was it anger? or pain?, in her eyes, before she coloured deeply and turned back to the window. “I am sure I have no favourite, sir. I find the whole volume equally accomplished.” “Equally accomplished! What a critic’s praise! No, that will not do.” He reached for the book again, which lay on a small table. “Here is something quite interesting. ‘Printed by J. Wright of Bath.’ Bath! Now that is a clue. One might expect such a thing from a Bath press. A city of invalids and sentiment. Very thoughtful of the Poetess, was it not? She knew the world could not bear to be without her feelings. I admire that in a woman; a desire to share her heart so completely. Nothing held back; nothing kept private. True sensibility could only have prompted it.” Catherine wished he would be less pointed; yet when she glanced at Miss Morton, she saw not the blush of shame she expected, but one of pride, quickly suppressed. She saw that with all the outward appearance of distress, there had been a flicker of secret triumph in her eyes. This quiet, unassuming, penniless Miss Morton was apparently capable of a very deep game indeed. He brought the book to Catherine, and they looked at it together. Catherine took the opportunity to whisper, “You are too plain. She must think you a brute.” “I hope she thinks of me a great deal. I would have her think of me. My meaning is not so very terrible.” “But really, I am half ashamed of our jest. She seems so very wounded by it.” “I am very glad you perceive it. I have now a key to her every mood. Leave the shame to her. If she is putting on an act, she ought to feel it.” “She is not entirely without feeling, I think.” “I do not see much sign of it. She is looking at the print of Derwentwater at this moment, the subject of ‘My Northern Lakes,’ _her_ masterpiece.” Shortly afterwards, Mrs. Allen, from her chair, espied Mr. Hastings riding up the lane. “Mr. Hastings, I do believe! Oh, I must just wave to him. It has been an age. I will not open the window, it lets in such a draught, but I can pop out to the hall. I dare say he will come in when he knows who is here. So delightful to have you all gathered!” She bustled out of the room, and her voice could soon be heard from the open front door, every word as clear to those in the drawing-room as if she had been standing beside them. “How d’ye do? How d’ye do? So good to see you. We were just this moment talking of you. Not two days ago, I said to Mr. Allen, ‘I wonder how Mr. Hastings does.’ Pray come in; do come in. You will find some friends here.” So began Mrs. Allen; and Mr. Hastings seemed quite determined to have his say, for his voice, full of earnest concern, cut through her cheerful prattle. “How is Miss Morton, Mrs. Allen? I was hoping to find you at home, to inquire after her particularly. How is Miss Morton? I heard she was not entirely well after her journey. Tell me how she is.” And Mrs. Allen was obliged to give a direct answer before he would suffer another topic. The listeners were amused; all but Miss Morton, who had gone quite still. Mr. Davies gave Catherine a look of particular meaning. But Catherine only shook her head, a new doubt forming in her mind. “So kind of you! So very kind of you to ask,” resumed Mrs. Allen. He cut her short with, “I am on my way to the village. Is there any service I can render?” “Oh! dear, the village, are you? Mrs. Green was just saying she needed some ribbon from the draper’s.” “Mrs. Green has a pony-trap. Can I be of service to _you_?” “No, I thank you. But do come in. Who do you think is here? Miss Armitage and Mr. Davies; so good as to call and enliven our afternoon. Do tether your horse, and come in.” “Well,” said he, in a considering tone, “for five minutes, perhaps.” “And Miss Morton is here too! Quite restored to us from the North!” “No, not now, I thank you,” he said, his voice changing entirely. “I could not stay a moment. I must get on to the village as fast as I can.” “Oh, do come in! She will be so very happy to see you.” “No, no; your room is full enough. I will call another day, and pay my respects properly.” “Well, I am so sorry! Oh! Mr. Hastings, what a fine sermon on Sunday; how very moving., Did you ever hear such wisdom?, Was not it edifying?, Mr. Davies and Miss Armitage, I am sure, thought it very edifying.” “Oh! very edifying indeed; I can say nothing less, for I imagine Mr. Davies and Miss Armitage can hear every word. And (raising his voice still more) I do not see why Miss Morton should not be mentioned too. I think Miss Morton’s opinion on a sermon is worth having. Now, if your guests have any kindness, they will say something to you about your excellent syllabub in return; but I cannot stay to hear it.” “Oh! Mr. Hastings, one moment; a matter of consequence, so vexed!, That book you procured for her!” “What is the matter now?” “To think you went all the way to Bath for it. You said it was no trouble, and now we hear you missed the mail-coach and had to hire a post-chaise. We really are so vexed! Your man told our gardener. You should not have done it, indeed you should not. Ah! he is away. He never can abide being thanked. But I thought he would have staid, and it would have been a pity not to have mentioned.... Well, (returning to the room,) I have not been successful. Mr. Hastings cannot stop. He is going to the village. He asked me if he could be of any service....” “Yes,” said Catherine, “we heard his kind offers, we heard every thing.” “Oh! yes, my dear, I dare say you might, because you know, the door was open, and Mr. Hastings speaks so clearly. You must have heard every thing to be sure. ‘Can I do anything for you in the village?’ said he; so I just mentioned.... Oh! Mr. Davies, must you be going? You seem but just arrived, so very good of you to call.” Catherine found it was indeed time to take their leave; the visit had been long, and upon consulting their watches, so much of the afternoon was discovered to be gone, that she and Mr. Davies departed, allowing themselves only a brief walk through the gardens before they separated.

The brief walk through the gardens was undertaken, at first, in a profound and not unamusing silence. The gentle crunch of gravel under their feet was the only sound to accompany the residue of Mrs. Gable’s chatter, which seemed to hang in the air long after they had made their escape. It was Mr. Davies who at last gave voice to their shared sentiment, a low chuckle escaping him.

“One must allow,” he began, with the air of a man choosing his words with deliberate care, “that a visit to Mrs. Gable, while trying to the nerves, is seldom an entirely uninformative experience. We have learned more of the village’s doings in this past hour than a month of common visiting could supply.”

“Informative indeed,” replied Catherine, a smile playing upon her lips. “We have learned that Mr. Hastings is capable of a great journey for a small book, that Miss Morton’s opinion on a sermon is of paramount importance, and that Mrs. Gable’s syllabub is in need of commendation. I confess I am quite overwhelmed with the sudden expansion of my knowledge.”

“You are unjust,” said Mr. Davies, though his expression was far from censorious. “You neglect the matter of the post-chaise. That, I think, was the true jewel of the disclosure. It elevates the entire affair from mere civility to the very precipice of romance.”

“The precipice of folly, perhaps,” Catherine retorted, though without any real heat. “To miss the mail-coach! It speaks of an agitation of mind, a want of that composure one expects from Mr. Hastings. And all for a book of verses for Miss Morton.”

They had reached the end of the yew-tree walk and turned to pace its length again, as if unwilling to break the thread of their discourse.

“And what of Miss Morton’s part in this little drama?” Mr. Davies inquired, glancing at his companion. “Did you observe her countenance during Mrs. Gable’s unwitting revelations?”

“I observed it very particularly,” said Catherine, her tone becoming more serious, more analytical. “There was a blush, to be sure. One could not miss it; it was a blush of some significance. But I am not at all certain it was the blush of simple embarrassment. There was, I fancy, a degree of satisfaction in it. She looks down, she plays with her music, but her senses are all alert. She hears every word, registers every compliment, and stores it away. I have never seen a young lady so accomplished in the art of quiet acquisition.”

Mr. Davies was silent for a moment. “That is a severe judgement, Miss Armitage. She has been among us but a short while since her return. Is it not possible that what you term ‘acquisition’ is merely the natural pleasure of a woman who finds herself esteemed by a man of Mr. Hastings’s character? Such esteem is not a thing to be lightly valued.”

“Oh, I do not undervalue it! Nor, certainly, does she. Mr. Hastings is a most eligible man; he is sensible, he is of good fortune, he has a respectable standing and a very handsome person. Any woman might be proud to attach him. But it is the manner of the attaching that I find curious. This return from the North, shrouded in a sort of melancholy mystery; this sudden appearance as a figure of interest, it is all very well managed. She says nothing, and by saying nothing, invites all manner of conjecture. It is a very clever campaign.”

“A campaign!” Mr. Davies exclaimed, half laughing. “My dear Miss Armitage, you make the drawing-room sound like a battlefield. I had thought it was merely a place for tea and conversation.”

“It is a battlefield of a sort,” Catherine insisted. “And you must own, the strategy is effective. Poor Mrs. Gable is made an unwitting lieutenant, broadcasting his devotion to the entire neighbourhood through an open casement. And Mr. Hastings himself, a man usually so possessed of his faculties, is reduced to forgetting the time of the mail-coach. If that is not a successful campaign, I do not know what is.”

“You are too much of a schemer yourself, I believe,” he said kindly. “You imagine these intricate plots because your own mind is capable of conceiving them. I, being of a simpler disposition, am more inclined to believe that a good man has taken a liking to a gentle woman, and she is happy in it. Is that not a more probable, and a more pleasing, interpretation?”

“It is more pleasing, I grant you. As to its probability… we shall see. Time, and Mrs. Gable, will no doubt tell us all.”

They had arrived at the gate where their paths were to diverge. Mr. Davies took her hand with a friendly warmth. “Take care that your imagination does not lead you into uncharitable conclusions,” he said, with a smile that softened the admonition. “Miss Morton may be everything that is amiable and deserving.”

“And I may be everything that is suspicious and unjust,” she finished for him, her good humour quite restored. “I shall endeavour to think better of her; or at least, to wait for more evidence before I condemn her to the ranks of designing womanhood. Good day, Mr. Davies.”

“Good day, Miss Armitage.”

Her walk home was a short one, but it afforded her ample time to reconsider the conversation. Was Mr. Davies right? Was her own mind, so fond of observation and deduction, inventing a complexity that did not exist? She reviewed the scene again. Mr. Hastings’s loud, insistent inquiries after Miss Morton’s health. His abrupt change of heart about entering the house the moment her presence was announced. The business of the book. And Miss Morton’s reaction, that quiet, deep blush, the small, secret smile that Catherine was certain she had seen before it was suppressed.

No, she concluded, as she passed through the gates of her father’s house, Mr. Davies, with all his goodness, was mistaken. He saw the world as he wished it to be, populated by honest hearts and straightforward intentions. She, Catherine, saw it as it was: a theatre of motive and manoeuvre, where a quiet countenance could hide the most active ambition. And she could not, for all her efforts, quite bring herself to believe that Miss Morton was deserving of a man as transparently good as Mr. Hastings. She was too placid, too perfect. It was the flawless surface of a deep and perhaps stagnant pond. This conviction, far from displeasing her, gave a certain zest to her thoughts; she had a puzzle to solve, a character to decipher, and she felt herself uniquely equipped for the task.

She entered the drawing-room to find her father, Mr. Armitage, looking up from his newspaper. He was a man who, having no particular anxieties of his own, took a placid and often ironic interest in the anxieties of others.

“Ah, Catherine,” he said, folding the paper with precision. “You have returned from your mission of mercy. How are the good ladies of the cottage? Is Mrs. Gable’s tongue still hinged?”

“It is, Papa, and as active as ever. We were treated to a most energetic account of the past two days’ events.”

“I do not doubt it. To listen to Mrs. Gable is to have the village gazette read aloud, with rather more exclamation than is common in print. Was there any news of consequence? Has anyone eloped? Has the price of corn fallen?”

“Nothing so dramatic, I assure you. The chief intelligence concerned Mr. Hastings, and his very particular attentions to Miss Morton.”

Catherine delivered this piece of news with a studied neutrality, arranging a bowl of late-blooming roses as she spoke. She was curious to see how her father, who knew Mr. Hastings well, would receive it.

“Hastings? And the little Morton girl?” He considered this, his chin in his hand. “Well, I am not surprised. He has been finding a great many reasons to ride past the cottage of late. A man does not suddenly develop a passionate interest in the state of the hedgerows on a particular lane without a motive of a more pleasing sort.”

“But Papa, do you think her a suitable match for him? For Mr. Hastings?”

“Suitable?” Mr. Armitage raised his eyebrows. “She is a gentlewoman. Her father was a respectable, if uninspiring, clergyman. She has little fortune, to be sure, but Hastings has enough for both. She is reputed to be accomplished, and is certainly not unpleasant to look upon. What is your objection? Does she have a secret vice? A tendency to cheat at cards?”

“No, nothing of the sort!” Catherine laughed. “My objection is not so definite. It is a feeling, an impression. She is so… quiet. So very composed. There is a want of animation, of sincerity, I think.”

“You mistake stillness for a want of feeling,” her father said, picking up his spectacles. “Many a quiet stream runs deep, you know. And as for sincerity, a woman who proclaims all she feels is as wearisome as a woman who feels nothing. You, my dear, have a tendency towards the former; perhaps you are intrigued by a specimen of the latter.”

“I am not wearisome, I hope, Papa!”

“Only when you are dissecting your neighbours’ hearts with such unholy glee. Let the poor man be happy. Hastings is no fool. If he has fixed his affections on Miss Morton, he likely has a better knowledge of her character than you, who have met her but half a dozen times and have already composed a three-volume novel in your head about her secret ambitions.”

This was so near the truth that Catherine could not help but blush. “I have done no such thing. I am merely concerned for your friend. Mr. Hastings is all goodness and openness; it would be a pity for him to be taken in by a pretty face and a show of meekness.”

“Taken in?” Mr. Armitage looked genuinely amused. “My dear Catherine, you speak as if Miss Morton were a fortune hunter from Bath, plotting to seize the family jewels. She is a parson’s daughter who has been living as a companion in the North to oblige her relations. Her greatest crime seems to be a fondness for poetry and a becoming modesty. You must allow that, on the evidence, Hastings is in greater danger of being bored to death than of being ‘taken in’.”

“Her modesty is not becoming, it is calculated!” Catherine insisted, her frustration mounting at her father’s placid view of the matter. “Today, when Mrs. Gable was recounting, in her dreadfully public way, the lengths Mr. Hastings went to for her book, I saw her face. There was triumph in it. I am certain of it.”

“You see what you wish to see,” he replied calmly. “And you have decided you do not like Miss Morton. Very well. It is a woman’s privilege not to like another woman without any cause whatsoever. But do not dress your prejudice in the noble costume of ‘concern for a friend’. It is a disservice to both.” He unfolded his newspaper again, a clear signal that the subject, for him, was closed.

Catherine retired from the conversation, feeling both vexed and defeated. Her father’s common sense was like a bucket of cold water on the bright fire of her theories. He reduced everything to its simplest form, stripping it of all the fascinating nuance that she so delighted in discovering. And yet, his words lingered. *You see what you wish to see.* Was it true? Had she formed a dislike of Miss Morton simply because she presented a puzzle, and Catherine had decided on a dark solution?

She went to the window and looked out upon the garden, her mind still in a whirl. The image of Miss Morton, sitting at the pianoforte, her head bent, a deep blush on her cheek, rose again in her mind. Was it triumph? Or was it, as Mr. Davies had suggested, the simple, overwhelming happiness of being loved? The possibility was irritating. It made her own role in the affair seem meddlesome and small. It was much more gratifying to see herself as a shrewd protector of a guileless friend.

Her reflections were interrupted by the arrival of a servant with the post. Among the letters was a note, which the footman said had just been delivered by a boy from the village. It was a simple, folded sheet, and the hand was unfamiliar.

She broke the seal with a sense of curiosity. The note was brief.

*My Dear Miss Armitage,*

*I hope you will not think me too forward, but I find I must make an inquiry. My aunt, in her flustered state, neglected to mention the matter that was chiefly on her mind when she called Mr. Hastings to the window. We are in a small difficulty concerning the loan of some music for the church concert, and she had hoped to ask his advice. As you are so intimate with the arrangements, I wonder if I might be so bold as to call upon you to-morrow morning, at whatever hour is most convenient, to beg your counsel in the matter. I should be very sorry to trouble you, but I find myself quite at a loss.*

*Yours most sincerely,*
*Catherine Morton.*

Catherine read the note once, and then a second time. Her first reaction was one of pure astonishment. Miss Morton, the silent, the reserved, the object of all her suspicion, was proposing to call upon her, to seek her counsel. It was the most unexpected, the most baffling development. The a-plotter seeking out the detective.

Her second reaction was one of renewed suspicion, sharper and more exhilarating than ever before. What could this mean? The excuse of the church music was flimsy; she knew for a fact that Mr. Davies had settled all those arrangements last week. It was a pretext. But for what? Was it a reconnaissance mission? An attempt to gauge how much Catherine knew, or suspected? Or, more daringly, was it an attempt to win her over, to recruit her as an ally in the ‘campaign’ for Mr. Hastings?

The audacity of it, if true, was magnificent. It confirmed every theory she had entertained. This was no simple, gentle parson’s daughter. This was a player of the game, and a very skilled one at that. Far from being a stagnant pond, Miss Morton’s mind was a creature of startling and intricate movement.

She sat down at her writing desk, a thrill of anticipation running through her. Her father’s words, Mr. Davies’s gentle warnings, they all faded into insignificance before the promise of this new engagement. She was not merely an observer any longer; she had been invited onto the field of battle.

She dipped her pen in the ink, her mind already racing, composing and discarding replies. She must be gracious, she must be welcoming, but she must also be on her guard. The interview of the next morning promised to be an event of the first significance. She would finally have the chance to take the measure of her adversary, face to face.

“My dear Miss Morton,” she wrote, a smile of pure, intellectual delight on her face. “I shall be entirely at your disposal…”

The morning of the interview dawned with a clarity and brightness that seemed to Catherine a most auspicious omen, a celestial approval of the intellectual contest that was to ensue; and she descended to breakfast with a mind so entirely occupied with stratagems and conversational snares that her father’s gentle inquiries as to the quality of her sleep were met with an abstracted air that bespoke a spirit already engaged on a far more significant field.

“You have a visitor this morning, I understand,” said Mr. Armitage, carefully adding a spoonful of honey to his tea. “Young Miss Morton. A very sweet girl, a very proper girl. It is good of you to see her, my dear. The parson’s family have few resources for entertainment.”

“She is not coming for entertainment, Papa,” Catherine replied, a touch of impatience in her tone. “She comes on a matter of business. The church concert.”

“Ah, the music! Yes, yes. I am sure she is in some real difficulty about the choir. A great responsibility for such a young woman. You must be very kind and help her as you can. Her aunt, poor Mrs. Gable, is not a woman of great organisational capacity, I fear.”

Catherine forbore to reply. That her father should take Miss Morton’s flimsy pretext at face value was only to be expected; he was a man of such transparent honesty himself that he could not conceive of artifice in others, particularly in a young lady with a quiet countenance and a respectable father. He saw a timid doe; Catherine saw a panther in sheep’s clothing. This difference in perception was, she reflected, what separated the ordinary mind from the discerning one. She felt a pang of fond pity for his simplicity.

She had chosen the small morning room for the interview. It was less formal than the drawing room, and its two large windows afforded excellent light, a light by which no flicker of an eyelid, no blush of concealment, could possibly pass unobserved. She had arranged the chairs with tactical care: her own was placed with the light behind her, leaving her face in a degree of shadow, while the visitor’s chair would be fully illuminated. It was a small advantage, but in a contest of wits, no advantage was to be despised.

At precisely eleven o’clock, the bell sounded. Catherine, who had been affecting to read a book of verse for the last quarter of an hour, felt her pulse quicken. This was it. The first move of the game. She composed her features into an expression of placid welcome and listened as the footman announced her guest.

Miss Morton entered the room with a hesitation that, to a less astute observer, might have appeared perfectly genuine. She was dressed in a gown of plain grey poplin, its only ornament a simple white collar. Her bonnet was equally unassuming. In her hands she carried a small roll of sheet music, tied with a ribbon. The dress itself was a masterpiece of deception, Catherine thought; a costume so devoid of adornment as to proclaim a humility that its wearer, she was certain, could not possibly possess. It was the calculated simplicity of a woman who knew that her true assets, a pair of remarkably fine, dark eyes and a complexion of delicate purity, required no fashionable embellishment.

“Miss Armitage,” she began, her voice as soft as her attire. “It is so very good of you to see me. I am afraid I am putting you to a great deal of trouble.”

“Not at all, Miss Morton, I assure you,” Catherine replied, gesturing to the designated chair. “I am quite at leisure, and was very happy to receive your note. Pray, be seated. May I offer you some refreshment? A glass of lemonade, perhaps?”

Miss Morton declined with a grateful murmur and took her seat, placing the roll of music on her lap and clasping her hands over it. Her eyes darted about the room for a moment before settling on Catherine’s face with a look of anxious appeal.

“You mentioned a difficulty concerning the church concert,” Catherine began, deciding to confront the pretext at once. “I confess I was a little surprised, as I was speaking with Mr. Davies only on Tuesday, and he gave me to understand that all the musical arrangements were quite settled. He seemed very well satisfied with the programme.”

A faint colour rose in Miss Morton’s cheeks. She looked down at her hands. “Oh. Did he? Indeed. I… I believe my aunt must have misunderstood him. She is so easily flustered, you know, and she came away with the impression that there was still some question about the soprano solos. She was very anxious that they should be appropriate, and she had hoped… she had thought Mr. Hastings might have some particular preference.”

The mention of his name! It had come sooner than even Catherine had anticipated. And the excuse was even weaker than before; to lay the confusion entirely at the feet of poor, flighty Mrs. Gable was a move both cowardly and clever.

“Ah, I see,” said Catherine, with a smile of perfect comprehension. “Your aunt values Mr. Hastings’s judgment very highly.”

“Oh, she does. We all do,” Miss Morton replied, her earnestness a little too pronounced. “He has such a fine ear, and his… his approbation is of great consequence to my aunt. She would be mortified if the concert did not meet with his approval.”

“Naturally,” said Catherine smoothly. “One would always wish for the approbation of one’s friends. But I believe Mr. Davies has selected a piece from Handel, which can hardly fail to please. Mr. Hastings is a great admirer of Handel, is he not?”

She had meant this as a gentle probe, a test of Miss Morton’s intelligence, but the girl’s reaction was one of simple, unfeigned surprise. “Is he? I did not know. That is a great relief, then. My aunt will be very glad to hear it.”

This was not the response of a plotter. A plotter would have seized the opportunity to display her own intimate knowledge of Mr. Hastings’s tastes. Catherine felt a momentary flicker of doubt, but it was quickly extinguished. This was not a lack of cunning; it was a higher form of it. By feigning ignorance, Miss Morton was attempting to paint herself as a disinterested party, a mere messenger for her aunt. It was a more subtle game than Catherine had expected.

“Well, then,” Catherine said, with an air of closing the subject. “It seems your difficulty is happily resolved without any need for my counsel at all. I am delighted to have been of even such passive assistance.”

Miss Morton looked genuinely distressed. “Oh, but I have brought you here under false pretences, Miss Armitage! I feel quite ashamed. I ought not to have listened to my aunt’s anxieties. I have wasted your morning.” She made a motion as if to rise.

“Not at all,” Catherine insisted, with a gracious wave of her hand. “Do not think of leaving yet. Now that you are here, we must have a little chat. It is so rare that we have an opportunity to speak. I was so sorry to hear of your little misadventure at the picnic last week. You turned your ankle, I believe?”

Miss Morton sank back into her chair, a look of gratitude on her face. “It was nothing, truly. I was clumsy. But Mr. Hastings was so very kind. He insisted on fetching the carriage himself, though it was quite unnecessary.”

“He is the soul of consideration,” Catherine agreed. “Always thinking of others. My father says he has never known a young man with such a strong sense of duty.” This was her opening. “And he has been so very busy of late. What with the new enclosure, and the purchase of that new hunter, a very fine animal, I am told, one wonders how he finds the time for such personal attentions.”

She watched Miss Morton’s face closely. Surely now, a flicker of proprietary knowledge, a slip of the tongue would reveal the extent of her information.

“He does seem to take a great deal upon himself,” Miss Morton said softly. “I know the business with the enclosure has given him many sleepless nights. He told my father that he felt the weight of responsibility for the tenant farmers very keenly.”

Catherine’s eyes widened slightly. So, he confided his sleepless nights to the parson, did he? And the parson, no doubt, relayed it all to his daughter. The channel of information was clear.

“Indeed,” Catherine replied, her voice betraying none of her inner excitement. “It is a heavy burden. One hopes he has friends who can offer him some comfort and distraction from such weighty cares. He has been calling at the parsonage a great deal of late, I hear.”

This was a direct shot, and she expected a blush, a stammer, a sign of conscious guilt. Instead, Miss Morton met her gaze with a look of perfect, untroubled frankness. “He has been very good, yes. He comes to read to my father, whose eyesight is failing, you know. He reads the London papers, and sometimes from the classics. It is the greatest kindness. My father looks forward to his visits above all things.”

The simplicity of this explanation was, to Catherine, its most damning feature. It was too perfect, too wholesome. To use her own father’s infirmity as a cover for her campaign, it was an act of breathtaking audacity. The parsonage was not a place of filial duty; it was a theatre of operations.

“How truly benevolent,” Catherine murmured. “And I suppose, while he is there, you and he find time to discuss other matters? Music, perhaps? Or local affairs?”

“Sometimes,” Miss Morton allowed. “He was speaking just the other day of his admiration for your father, Miss Armitage. He said he considered Mr. Armitage the very model of a country gentleman.”

A clever deflection. To turn the conversation to a compliment was a classic diversionary tactic. Catherine smiled, acknowledging the hit. It was time to become more pointed.

“Mr. Hastings is too generous in his praise,” she said lightly. “But it is a mark of his good character that he thinks so well of others. It gives one confidence in his judgment. Indeed, in all his arrangements. One feels that whatever he undertakes, he does so with a clear plan in mind. It is a great comfort when one’s friends have… well-laid plans. It gives such a sense of order to life, do you not find? One almost feels one can conjecture the outcome with some certainty.”

She leaned forward slightly, her eyes fixed on Miss Morton’s, delivering the word “conjecture” with a delicate emphasis that could not be missed. It was a declaration of war, a signal that she saw through the entire charade. She expected a recoil, a moment of panic.

The effect was far more dramatic than she could have imagined. Miss Morton’s face, which had been a mask of quiet composure, suddenly crumpled. Her lips trembled, and her dark eyes, those fine, expressive eyes, filled with tears. She did not weep, but the sheer, shimmering depth of her unshed sorrow was more potent than any sob.

“Plans?” she whispered, and her voice was no longer soft, but brittle with pain. “Conjecture? Oh, Miss Armitage, I am afraid you mistake me entirely. I am not clever enough for plans. I… I only wish to do my duty, and to be a comfort to my father, and not to be a burden to anyone.” She looked down at the roll of music in her lap as if she did not know what it was. “I fear I have troubled you to no purpose. I should not have come.”

She rose from her chair, her movements stiff with a sudden, wounded dignity. One tear escaped and traced a glistening path down her cheek.

Catherine was, for a single moment, struck dumb. She had expected a battle of wits, a parry and thrust of veiled insinuations. She had not been prepared for this, this display of raw, overwhelming vulnerability. It was a masterstroke. It was genius. To feign such profound hurt, such delicate, misunderstood innocence… it was a tactic so brilliant as to be almost beyond censure. It was the ultimate defence: to retreat into a fortress of pathos, leaving the aggressor to look like a callous brute.

“My dear Miss Morton,” Catherine said, recovering herself and rising as well. She adopted a tone of gentle concern, meeting artifice with artifice. “You have misunderstood me. I meant only to praise Mr. Hastings’s foresight. Please, do not be distressed.”

But Miss Morton was already moving towards the door, her head bowed. “You have been very kind,” she said, her voice muffled. “Forgive me. I am not myself today. I am… tired.”

She was gone, leaving a faint scent of lavender and a profound silence in the room.

Catherine stood motionless for a full minute, her mind racing. She walked to the window and watched Miss Morton’s small figure retreat down the drive, her steps quick and uneven. The performance had been flawless. The hesitations, the weak excuses, the praise of Mr. Hastings disguised as her aunt’s concern, the strategic use of her father’s infirmity, and the final, devastating coup de théâtre of the tears, it was a campaign worthy of a general.

Far from exonerating herself, Miss Morton had confirmed every suspicion Catherine had ever held, and had done so with a skill that commanded a grudging, intellectual respect. She was not just a player; she was a master. The quietness, the reserve, it was all a carefully constructed facade to conceal a will of iron and a mind of astonishing cunning. The tears were not a sign of weakness, but her most powerful weapon. She had known Catherine would press her, had anticipated the attack, and had her defence prepared.

A slow smile spread across Catherine’s face. This was better, far better, than she had hoped. The game was more complex, the adversary more worthy, than she had ever dared to imagine. Her own part in it must now be played with even greater subtlety and intelligence. Miss Morton had revealed her strategy: to win by pity, to conquer through the appearance of surrender. Catherine, for her part, would not be so easily disarmed. She would meet pathos with perception, and vulnerability with vigilance.

She returned to her writing desk, her heart beating with the thrill of the chase. The morning had been a triumph. She had taken the measure of her rival, and though she had been momentarily thrown by the sheer brilliance of the display, she now felt more certain, more invigorated, more entirely in possession of the truth than ever before. This amiable, upright, perfect Jane Fairfax, or rather, Catherine Morton, was indeed cherishing very reprehensible feelings, and what was more, she was pursuing them with a genius that was almost sublime. The battle had been joined, and Catherine Armitage had never felt more alive.

The little party proceeded in a silence that was, for a moment, quite comfortable; the shared experience of Miss Bates’s hospitality and Mr. Knightley’s abruptness requiring no immediate comment. It was Mr. Frank Churchill who, with his customary talent for resuming a delightful topic, broke the quiet.

“Well, Miss Woodhouse,” he began, turning to her with a smile of such confidential amusement that it seemed to claim her as his sole partner in wit, “was there ever a more satisfactory morning’s entertainment? I declare, the theatre itself has seldom afforded me such pleasure. We have had mystery, suspense, a most skillful performance on the part of the heroine, and even a dramatic interruption from an unexpected quarter.”

“You are too bad,” said Mrs. Weston, though she smiled as she spoke. “You will make us all feel ashamed of having been amused. I own, Frank, I thought you a little severe upon Miss Fairfax. Her situation is so very delicate.”

“Severe? My dear Mrs. Weston, I was the very model of consideration! I did but offer her opportunities to shine. And did you not see how she took them? That blush when I mentioned Weymouth, was it not perfect? And her composure, her studied calmness when speaking of Colonel Campbell, it is a performance worthy of the highest praise.” He glanced at Emma, his eyes bright with shared understanding. “Miss Woodhouse, I am certain, appreciates the artistry.”

Emma could not deny it. She felt a pleasant glow of vanity in being thus singled out, in being the one person whose perceptions Mr. Churchill trusted. “Indeed,” she replied, adopting a tone of judicious gravity, “one cannot help but admire the consistency of the character. I thought her very accomplished. But you must not give me all the credit for observation. It was your own line of inquiry that brought forth such interesting replies.”

“Ah, but I was merely following your lead. You gave me the key, and I have only been trying the lock. And what a fascinating mechanism it is! The instrument arriving so mysteriously; the Irish melodies; the playing of _Robin Adair_, which she must have known to be a favourite of mine, every piece fits. I begin to feel quite the detective in this little drama of yours.”

Emma’s mind flew back to the moment. He had said, “_his_ favourite.” She had supposed he meant his own, a piece of harmless vanity. But now, his words, and the very particular way he looked at her, suggested a deeper meaning. Could he have meant _another’s_ favourite? Mr. Dixon’s, perhaps? Had he, with his own superior knowledge of the Weymouth party, been testing Jane not for his own sake, but on behalf of the Campbells, to see if her affections were still secretly engaged? This was a level of subtlety that had not occurred to her; it cast Frank Churchill in an even more brilliant light, not merely a flirt, but a man of deep, if playful, loyalty to his friends. The remains of a smile she had caught on Jane Fairfax’s face took on a new significance; it was not a smile of delight in a present flirtation, but a secret, painful memory of a past one. It was all so much clearer now.

“I believe your key fits very well indeed,” she said, her voice warm with approval. “It unlocks a great deal.”

“And what of our other performer?” Mr. Churchill went on, lowering his voice conspiratorially. “Mr. Knightley, bursting upon the scene with his inquiries. ‘How is Miss Fairfax?’, as if the fate of the nation hung upon her health. I have seldom seen a man in such a state of anxiety over a common cold. One might almost suppose he had a particular interest in the matter.”

“Mr. Knightley is very fond of Miss Bates and her mother,” said Mrs. Weston, with a touch of defensiveness. “And you know his way, Frank. He is always direct. It is one of his best qualities.”

“Oh, certainly, a most admirable quality,” returned Mr. Churchill, without the least appearance of sincerity. “It is only that his directness seemed so very… directed. One might have thought Miss Fairfax the only lady in the room. He quite overlooked you, Miss Woodhouse, and you, my dear Mrs. Weston. A shocking want of gallantry.”

“Mr. Knightley does not deal in gallantry,” said Emma, a little sharply. She did not like to hear Mr. Knightley’s manners defended by Mrs. Weston, nor did she enjoy the image of his overlooking her, even in jest. “He prides himself on being above such civilities. I confess, I thought his behaviour very odd altogether. To make such a public and pointed inquiry, and then to refuse to come in, and to be so brusque with poor Miss Bates, it was not at all like him. Or rather,” she corrected herself, “it was exactly like him of late. He has been in a strange humour for some time.”

“Perhaps the air of Kingston does not agree with him,” Mr. Churchill suggested lightly. “Or perhaps he cannot bear to be thanked for his apples. A man of his vast benevolence must find gratitude a terrible burden.”

Mrs. Weston laughed, and the conversation turned to lighter subjects as they reached the gates of Hartfield. Mr. Frank Churchill took Emma’s hand to say his farewells, and the slight pressure he gave it, accompanied by a look of perfect intelligence, seemed to say, “We understand one another completely. We, and we alone, see the truth of things.” It was a most agreeable sensation.

“We shall see you at the Coles’ party, I trust?” said Emma.

“Wild horses could not keep me away,” he declared. “There will be more to observe, I am sure. We must compare notes.”

With a final, charming bow, he and Mrs. Weston departed, leaving Emma and Harriet to enter the house.

“Oh, Miss Woodhouse!” Harriet began, as soon as the door was closed. “What a very eventful morning! And Mr. Frank Churchill is so… so very amusing!”

“He is, Harriet. He has a great deal of perception,” said Emma, leading the way into the drawing-room, her mind still pleasurably occupied with the compliments she had received. She felt a renewed sense of purpose, a desire to arrange her thoughts and observations into a coherent and satisfying whole. It was a satisfaction that could only be completed by explaining it all to a willing, and admiring, audience.

“Sit down, Harriet,” she said, feeling very much the wise and experienced friend. “You were quiet during our walk. I dare say much of what we spoke of was a little obscure. Let me explain. You see, the whole business of this pianoforte is a screen.”

Harriet’s eyes grew wide. “A screen, Miss Woodhouse?”

“Precisely. A deception, to cover a secret attachment. You heard Mr. Churchill mention Weymouth. It was there, you must understand, that Miss Fairfax met with the gentleman to whom she has evidently lost her heart, a Mr. Dixon, the husband, now, of her particular friend.”

She paused to let the shocking intelligence sink in. Harriet gave a little gasp of horrified delight.

“Now, consider the evidence,” Emma continued, warming to her theme. “A pianoforte of immense value arrives, with no one claiming responsibility. Colonel Campbell is the supposed benefactor, yet Jane herself professes complete ignorance. Is it not much more probable that the gift comes from a secret admirer? From Mr. Dixon himself, who, feeling some remorse for his marriage, wishes to make her some token of his continued affection?”

“Oh, but Miss Woodhouse, a married man!” cried Harriet.

“It is very shocking, Harriet, very reprehensible indeed. And that is what makes her conduct so fascinating. She is forced to dissemble, to pretend it is the work of the Campbells, when all the while her heart is rejoicing in this proof of his love. Did you not see her, when Mr. Churchill spoke of the Irish melodies? Mrs. Dixon is Irish, you know. It was a gift from her husband to his wife’s friend, a very pointed and cruel reminder, I think. And Jane’s blush, it was the blush of guilt, of a secret joy she ought not to feel.”

Emma leaned back in her chair, pleased with the elegance of her own reasoning. She had taken the scattered, confusing events of the morning and woven them into a narrative of perfect clarity.

“And Mr. Churchill,” she went on, “he sees it all. He was there, at Weymouth. He knows the whole story. His teasing was not mere sport; it was a series of brilliant experiments, designed to make her reveal herself. And she did! At every turn! It was a triumph of his penetration, and, if I may say so, of mine for having first put him on the scent.”

“It is wonderful!” breathed Harriet. “To be able to see into people’s hearts like that. I should never have guessed any of it. I only thought Mr. Knightley was very loud.”

“Ah, Mr. Knightley,” said Emma, her brow clouding slightly. “That is the one part of the morning that I cannot quite fit into my scheme. His behaviour was most unaccountable. Such extraordinary solicitude for Jane Fairfax! It was almost as if he were trying to rival Mr. Churchill in his attentions.” She gave a little laugh. “Poor Mr. Knightley. He is a very good man, but he has no subtlety. He cannot compete in a game of wit. He would have done better to stay at home and mind his accounts.”

A short while later, Harriet took her leave, full of admiration for her friend’s cleverness, and Emma was left alone, to the quiet satisfaction of her own reflections. The morning had been a decided success. She had seldom felt her own faculties to be in such fine and active order. The world of Highbury, which could sometimes seem so small and placid, had been revealed to her as a theatre of intricate passions and secret designs, and she, Emma Woodhouse, was its most discerning critic.

Her position was a delightful one. In Mr. Frank Churchill, she had found a companion in observation, a mind that moved as quickly as her own, who appreciated her intelligence and deferred to her judgment. Their shared understanding of the Jane Fairfax situation was a secret bond between them, a source of endless, pleasant speculation. It was an intimacy of intellect, far more stimulating than any common flirtation. She felt that they were allies, united against the dullness and deception of the world.

As for Jane Fairfax herself, Emma’s feelings were a complex mixture of pity and a kind of intellectual contempt. She pitied her for her unfortunate attachment and her constrained circumstances; there was no true generosity of spirit, however, in this pity. It was the pity of the fortunate for the unfortunate, of the master of the game for the poor pawn. It made Emma feel benevolent and superior. And beneath the pity was a solid layer of disapproval. This perfect, amiable Jane Fairfax, so universally praised for her elegance and propriety, was, in Emma’s estimation, a creature of profound duplicity. She was deceiving her aunt and her grandmother, she was carrying on a secret, improper correspondence of the heart with a married man, and she was doing it all under a mask of suffering virtue. The smile of secret delight Emma had witnessed was the key; it proved that Jane was not merely a victim of circumstance, but an active, and willing, participant in her own reprehensible feelings. The discovery did not shock Emma so much as it gratified her; it confirmed her initial dislike of Jane, and proved that her own instincts were unerringly correct.

It was only the thought of Mr. Knightley that introduced a note of discord into this harmonious mental concert. His behaviour had been vexing. His loud, commanding inquiries after Jane’s health, his abrupt dismissal of Miss Bates, his refusal to enter, it was all so ill-judged. Why should he make such a fuss over Jane Fairfax? He, who was usually so sensible, so quick to censure any breach of decorum or any hint of affectation. He had always professed to admire Jane’s reserve and accomplishments, but this morning’s display had gone beyond mere admiration. It was… proprietary. It was as if he felt he had a right to know, a right to interfere. Emma paced the drawing-room, her brow furrowed. Could it be that he, too, was falling under Jane Fairfax’s spell? The notion was absurd. Mr. Knightley, a man of thirty-seven, a man of his sense and standing, to be taken in by a pale, reserved girl with a secret sorrow? It was impossible. More likely, his brotherly feeling for the Bateses had been worked upon; Jane’s supposed illness, her delicate situation, had appealed to his sense of duty. He was forever taking up causes, defending the weak, and managing other people’s affairs. Yes, that must be it. He had decided that Jane Fairfax was an object for his particular benevolence, and he was pursuing the project with his usual want of delicacy. He was treating her as he treated his tenants, or William Larkins, or the poor of Highbury, as a problem to be managed. The thought was reassuring; it restored Mr. Knightley to his proper, predictable place, and removed the unsettling possibility of a romantic interest. Emma could be comfortable in her slight irritation with him, so long as he was only being officious.

Her father entered the room then, full of gentle anxieties. “My dear, has your friend Miss Smith gone? I hope she did not find the walk too much for her. There is a decided chill in the air, though Mr. Perry says we are not to expect any more snow.”

“Harriet is very well, Papa, and has gone home quite safely. And we had a very pleasant morning.”

“Ah, that is good. You went to see Miss Bates, I think? I hope you found them well. Their apartments are so draughty. I am always a little concerned for Mrs. Bates in this weather. Did you remember to tell them I was asking after them?”

“Yes, Papa, of course I did.”

“And the new instrument? Is it very loud? I hope it does not give Mrs. Bates a headache. I am not at all fond of loud noises.”

Emma smiled and reassured him on all points, settling him in his favourite chair by the fire with a backgammon board, his anxieties soothed for the present. Her mind, however, was already looking forward. The Coles’ party loomed as an occasion of the highest promise. All the players would be there: she and her clever co-conspirator, Mr. Churchill; the fascinating, deceptive Miss Fairfax; the kind but oblivious Westons; and Mr. Knightley, whose strange behaviour would provide a new and interesting puzzle to be solved. She, Emma, would be at the centre of it all, observing, interpreting, and directing the narrative. It was a delightful prospect. The world was full of interest when one knew how to look at it.

The few days that elapsed between the morning at Mrs. Bates’s and the much anticipated evening at the Coles’ were passed by Emma in a state of the most agreeable speculation. The little dramas of Highbury, which had for a time seemed to languish, were now all astir with a new and invigorating complexity. Her own part in them was, she felt, that of the discerning audience, or perhaps more accurately, the playwright herself, quietly arranging her characters for the most effective display of their humours and passions. Mr. Woodhouse, of course, viewed the impending festivity with his customary trepidation.

“The Coles are very good people,” he would say, sighing gently into his gruel, “but their house is very full on these occasions. So many people coming in and out, bringing the night air with them. And I am not at all certain of their cook. Mrs. Bates once mentioned their mutton was underdone, which is a very serious thing, my dear. I hope you will eat nothing but a little thin soup, Emma.”

“Yes, Papa, you may be sure of it. And Mrs. Weston will be there to see that I do not catch cold, and Mr. Weston will make us all laugh, so we shall be very well off.”

“Ah, the Westons. Yes. It is a comfort that they will be there. But Mr. Weston is so very loud. His laugh, it quite shakes the room. I shall certainly take my handkerchief for my ears. But you must go, I suppose. It would not do to offend the Coles. They are very attentive in sending us the first of their asparagus, which I must say is a great credit to them.”

Emma, promising to be all that was prudent and careful, escaped to her own thoughts. Her chief object of curiosity was no longer the simple question of the pianoforte’s origin, that was settled, at least in her own mind and Mr. Churchill’s, but the far more intricate puzzle of Mr. Knightley. His behaviour had been singular indeed. To have ridden all the way from Donwell, ostensibly to go to Kingston, only to stop and make such particular inquiries after Jane Fairfax, it was a deviation from his usual habits that demanded explanation. Emma had settled upon the theory of his officious benevolence, and yet a small corner of her mind remained unsatisfied. She had never known him to be so… agitated. The force with which he had spoken to Miss Bates through the window, the almost rude interruption of her civilities, it was the manner of a man deeply invested, not merely of a neighbour performing a duty.

She was still turning these inconsistencies over in her mind as she dressed for the party. She chose a gown of pale green crepe, which she knew became her, and had her hair arranged in a style that was both simple and elegant. Vanity was not her chief motive; rather, she felt it her duty to appear to advantage. As the principal lady of Highbury society, she must set a certain standard. She was pleased with the result, and went down to her father with a cheerful countenance.

The short journey to the Coles’ was accomplished with the usual anxieties on Mr. Woodhouse’s part and the usual reassurances on Emma’s. They arrived to find the house brightly lit, and the sound of conversation and laughter spilling out into the hall. Mrs. Cole, a stout, beaming woman who held Hartfield in the highest reverence, met them at the door of the drawing-room.

“Miss Woodhouse! And Mr. Woodhouse! How very good of you to venture out. We are so honoured, so very delighted. Pray come in, come near the fire. I have had a screen set up especially, Mr. Woodhouse, to keep any draughts from the door away from you.”

Her father having been safely established in the warmest and most protected corner of the room, Emma was free to survey the company. It was, as her father had predicted, a very full room. The Westons were there, of course; Mrs. Weston caught her eye and gave a warm smile. Mr. Elton was standing, looking prodigiously self-important, by the side of his new wife, who was engaged in a very animated and rather loud conversation with Mrs. Perry. And there, by the pianoforte, just as she had expected, was the interesting group: Frank Churchill, leaning over the instrument, speaking with great vivacity to Jane Fairfax, who sat before it, her hands resting in her lap.

Before Emma could move towards them, however, she was claimed by Mrs. Elton.

“Ah, Miss Woodhouse! So you are come at last. We had quite begun to despair. I was just saying to Mrs. Perry, one can never be sure of the Hartfield party till one sees them, you are kept such prisoners there! I call Mr. Woodhouse the most charming of gaolers.” She gave a little trill of a laugh. “My caro sposo and I were just remarking on the instrument. A Broadwood, is it not? Very respectable for a country party. We are hoping Miss Fairfax will favour us with a tune later. She plays very prettily, I am told. I have a great mind to form a little musical club here in Highbury. Nothing too formal, you know. Just a few select friends, to meet at Maple Grove and practice. You would join us, of course, Miss Woodhouse.”

“You are very kind,” said Emma, with a coolness that was entirely lost on Mrs. Elton, “but my own playing is not of a standard that I should wish to display.”

“Oh, nonsense! You are too modest. All the young ladies of Hartfield are accomplished, I am sure. But I must not keep you. I see Mr. Frank Churchill is looking this way. Such a charming young man! Quite the talk of Highbury. He has been telling us all about the balls at Weymouth. Such a gay place, it sounds. I quite long to go myself.”

Emma extricated herself as soon as she could and made her way towards the pianoforte, her progress intercepted for a moment by Mr. Weston.

“Emma, my dear! There you are. Is not this a capital party? The Coles do things so well. And have you seen our young man? In high spirits, as usual. He has been teasing Miss Fairfax mercilessly, I am afraid, about her mysterious benefactor. She takes it all in good part, though. A very sweet-tempered girl.”

Emma smiled, thinking she understood the situation far better than Mr. Weston. She finally reached the group. Frank Churchill’s face lit up upon seeing her.

“Miss Woodhouse! You have rescued me. I have been trying to persuade Miss Fairfax to play, but she is inexorable. She claims to have forgotten every piece of music she ever knew.”

“I am sure Miss Fairfax is only being modest,” said Emma, her eyes twinkling as she looked from him to Jane. “Though perhaps you have tired her out with your incessant questions. I heard you from across the room.”

“Guilty,” he said, with a theatrical bow. “I confess it all. I am a creature of insatiable curiosity. When I see a mystery, I must poke and pry at it. It is a terrible fault. You must teach me to be more discreet, Miss Woodhouse. You, who are the soul of discretion.”

He said this with a look so full of shared meaning that Emma could not help but feel a flush of pleasure. Jane Fairfax, meanwhile, had risen from her seat. “If you will excuse me,” she said, her voice very low, “I see my aunt is looking for me.”

“Oh! but you must not run away,” cried Frank. “We have not yet settled the great question of the Irish melodies. Are they to be a tribute to Colonel Campbell’s heritage, or a more particular compliment to… another Irish acquaintance?”

Jane’s colour, which had been pale, rose in a painful blush. She gave him a look of what seemed to Emma to be pure distress, and turned away without another word, moving quickly to where Miss Bates was sitting.

“There,” said Frank Churchill, turning to Emma with a low laugh. “Did you see? A direct hit. She cannot bear the mention of Ireland. The evidence mounts, does it not?”

“You are a sad torment, Mr. Churchill,” Emma replied, though she was very much amused. “You will drive the poor girl to distraction.”

“I only wish to know the truth. And I think we are very close to it. But tell me, who is that vision of elegance who was just speaking to you? She seems a very formidable personage.”

“That is Mrs. Elton, the new vicar’s wife.”

“Ah! The lady of Maple Grove. I have heard of her. She means to organize us all, I am told.”

“She means to try,” said Emma, with a light shrug. “But Highbury is not so easily organized.”

Their tête-à-tête was interrupted by the arrival of Mr. Knightley. He had not been there when Emma first entered, and his appearance caused a little stir. He greeted his hosts, spoke for a few moments to Mrs. Weston, and then his eyes scanned the room. Emma watched him, curious to see what he would do. His gaze passed over her and Frank Churchill with a distinct lack of approval, and settled, as she had half expected it would, on Jane Fairfax.

He made his way across the room, not with the brisk purpose of the other morning, but with a quiet deliberation. Jane was sitting beside her aunt, looking down at her hands. Miss Bates was in the middle of a long and rambling account of the party to a deaf Mrs. Perry, and did not notice his approach.

Mr. Knightley did not speak at first. He simply stood before Jane, until she looked up. Emma, watching from a distance, could not hear what was said, but she could see the whole exchange. She saw the look of surprise on Jane’s face, followed by something else, relief, perhaps. He spoke to her for a few minutes, his expression serious and kind. She answered him, her own expression losing some of its tense watchfulness. He then fetched a chair, and placed it so that it protected her from a slight draught from a nearby window, which Emma had not even noticed. He did not sit himself, but after ensuring she was comfortable, and saying a few more words, he moved away to speak to Mr. Cole.

The entire episode took no more than three or four minutes, but it left Emma feeling profoundly irritated. Frank Churchill, who had been watching along with her, leaned in closer.

“Well!” he whispered. “Mr. Knightley to the rescue. How very chivalrous. One would almost think he had a special interest in the damsel’s distress.”

“Mr. Knightley has an interest in everyone’s distress,” Emma retorted, with more sharpness than she intended. “He cannot see a person in need of a chair, or a screen, or a word of advice, without feeling compelled to provide it. It is his nature.”

“A very admirable nature, to be sure,” said Frank, his eyes dancing with mischief. “Though I did not perceive the draught myself. Miss Fairfax seems a magnet for such knightly attentions. First, a mysterious pianoforte arrives, and now Mr. Knightley himself stands guard. She is a very fortunate young woman.”

Emma did not like the turn of the conversation. She did not like Frank Churchill quizzing Mr. Knightley, and she particularly did not like this new evidence of Mr. Knightley’s unaccountable solicitude for Jane Fairfax. It was all very well to be benevolent, but this was bordering on the particular. It was… pointed. He had barely spoken a word to her, Emma, his oldest friend, yet he had crossed a crowded room to reposition Jane Fairfax’s chair.

Dinner was announced, and the party began to move towards the dining-room. In the gentle confusion, Mr. Elton came bustling up to Emma.

“Miss Woodhouse, may I have the honour? Augusta is being taken in by Mr. Weston, so I am at liberty. I must find a partner for you.”

“Thank you, Mr. Elton, but Mr. Churchill has already offered me his arm,” Emma said, turning with a gracious smile to Frank, who was indeed at her elbow.

Mr. Elton looked put out for a second, but recovered himself. “Ah, of course. Excellent. A very suitable pairing.” And he bustled off, no doubt to report to Augusta that Miss Woodhouse and Mr. Churchill were quite an item.

The dinner was excellent, a fact which would have greatly surprised Mr. Woodhouse, had he been there to witness it. The Coles had spared no expense. Emma found herself seated between Frank Churchill and Mr. Cole, a position which afforded her a great deal of amusement. Frank Churchill kept up a running commentary on the proceedings, his remarks witty and just for her ear. Mr. Cole, meanwhile, was full of compliments about Hartfield and expressions of his good fortune in having secured her company.

From her vantage point, she had a clear view of Mr. Knightley. He was seated further down the table, next to Mrs. Weston. They seemed to be in a serious conversation. Several times, Emma saw Mrs. Weston glance towards her and Frank Churchill, a slight frown on her brow. Mr. Knightley’s expression was unreadable, but he was not, Emma noted, looking her way. His attention seemed more often directed across the table, where Jane Fairfax sat, pale and quiet, next to an effusive Mr. Weston.

After dinner, when the ladies had returned to the drawing-room, Mrs. Weston took the opportunity to have a quiet word with Emma.

“You are enjoying yourself, my dear?” she began, her tone affectionate.

“Very much. The Coles have made us all very comfortable. And Mr. Churchill is in excellent form tonight.”

“Yes,” said Mrs. Weston, a little doubtfully. “He is very charming. But Emma, my love, are you not… encouraging him a little too much? The whole room is watching you. And poor Jane Fairfax, he was teasing her so before dinner.”

“Oh, it is only a game,” said Emma, laughing. “We have a little secret, he and I. A little theory. You must not be concerned for Jane Fairfax. She understands it all perfectly well.”

“Does she?” said Mrs. Weston, looking over at Jane, who was turning the pages of a book of music, though not, it seemed, with any real attention. “She does not look very happy, Emma. She looks worn and anxious. Mr. Knightley was just remarking to me how unwell she appears.”

“Mr. Knightley!” said Emma, unable to keep the impatience out of her voice. “Mr. Knightley seems to have elected himself Miss Fairfax’s personal physician and guardian. He can talk of nothing else.”

“He is concerned for her, as an old friend of the family,” said Mrs. Weston, gently. “As we all should be. Her situation is a very trying one.”

“Her situation is of her own making, I suspect,” said Emma, lowering her voice. “But we shall say no more of it. Ah, the gentlemen are coming. Let us have some music.”

Mrs. Elton immediately took charge. “Now! Miss Fairfax, we have been so patient. You must play for us. No excuses! My caro sposo, Mr. E., insists upon it.”

Jane looked trapped, but Mr. Weston added his own hearty encouragement, and she could not well refuse. She went to the pianoforte and, after a moment’s hesitation, began to play. It was not one of the waltzes from the ball, but a quiet, melancholy air, played with exquisite feeling and a technical perfection that silenced the room. Even Mrs. Elton was obliged to be quiet and listen.

When she had finished, there was a general murmur of applause. Mr. Knightley, who had been standing near the fireplace, did not applaud. He merely watched her, his expression thoughtful. As she made to rise, he moved forward.

“Thank you, Miss Fairfax,” he said, his voice just loud enough for those nearby, including Emma, to hear. “That was beautifully played. But it is a sad piece of music for a party. Will you not play something more cheerful? For your own sake, if not for ours.”

Jane looked up at him, and for a moment, her composure seemed to falter. A look of deep, and what seemed to Emma, resentful weariness crossed her features. “I am not in a cheerful humour, Mr. Knightley,” she said, so quietly that he had to lean down to hear her. “And I do not care to feign one.”

It was a rebuff, and a surprisingly sharp one. Emma was astonished. This was not the meek, perfectly behaved Jane Fairfax she knew. Mr. Knightley, however, did not seem offended. He looked at her with an expression of profound pity.

“I know you are not,” he said, his voice full of a gentleness that struck Emma as utterly misplaced. “Forgive me. It was a clumsy thing to say. You must be very tired. Let me speak to your aunt. It is time you were at home.”

And he turned and walked directly over to Miss Bates, leaving a stunned silence in his wake. Frank Churchill, who had observed the whole thing, appeared at Emma’s side.

“My word,” he murmured. “A lovers’ quarrel, do you think? Or is our Knightley merely being put in his place for his excessive, what was the word you used?, officiousness?”

Emma felt a strange pang of disquiet. She did not know what to think. She saw Mr. Knightley speaking earnestly to Miss Bates, and Miss Bates nodding, her face full of concern. She saw Jane rise, her face a pale, unreadable mask, and take her aunt’s arm. The Westons, seeing that their charges were preparing to leave, began to make their own adieux. The party was breaking up, and all because of this strange, intense little scene between Mr. Knightley and Jane Fairfax.

The journey home to Hartfield was a quiet one. Mrs. Weston was thoughtful, and Emma was too occupied with her own confused impressions to wish for conversation. She had gone to the party feeling that she was in possession of all the secrets, that she held the key to every character’s motives. She left it feeling that she had understood nothing at all. Mr. Knightley’s behaviour was a puzzle wrapped in an enigma. It was not the benevolent concern of a landlord for his tenants; it was not the officious meddling of a busybody. It was something deeper, more personal, and for the life of her, she could not imagine what it was. And for the first time, the thought that she might be entirely, fundamentally wrong about everything, a thought she had not entertained for a very long time, flickered uneasily at the edges of her mind.

She did not wish to admit, even to Harriet, the profound disturbance the evening had caused her.

“Yes, Mr. Knightley is very kind,” said Emma, a little shortly. His kindness was not the point in question; it was the nature of it, the particularity, that perplexed her. It was a kindness that had seemed to her to border on intimacy, a solicitude that had far exceeded the demands of neighbourly benevolence. She could not reconcile the Mr. Knightley who spoke with such grave and gentle authority to Jane Fairfax with the Mr. Knightley who had so often, and with such brotherly bluntness, found fault with herself.

They found Mr. Woodhouse in a state of placid anxiety, wondering if the night air had been too damp, and if they had remembered their shawls. Emma soothed him with her usual cheerful patience, and the evening at Hartfield concluded in its accustomed tranquillity. Yet as Emma retired to her own room, the events of the party at the Coles’ returned to her with renewed force. She sat by her window, looking out into the moonlit garden, and tried to arrange the confusing impressions of the night into a coherent narrative.

Mr. Knightley’s behaviour was the chief puzzle. That he should feel pity for Jane Fairfax was entirely natural; who would not? That he should concern himself with her health was consistent with his character. But the expression on his face when he had looked at her, the tone of his voice when he had spoken to her, these were things that did not fit. There had been a depth of feeling, a personal investment, that was altogether unaccountable. Could it be that Mrs. Weston’s playful suggestion, made so long ago, that Mr. Knightley might be in love with Jane Fairfax, had some truth in it after all?

Emma dismissed the idea almost as soon as it was formed. It was an absurdity. Mr. Knightley, her Mr. Knightley, who was more a brother than a friend, who belonged to Hartfield as much as she did herself, that he should entertain a secret passion for the reserved, and to Emma’s mind, rather cold, Jane Fairfax! It was impossible. He had never shewn the least sign of such a partiality before. No, his concern must spring from some other source. Perhaps he knew more of her situation than others did; perhaps he had heard something from the Campbells that gave him cause for particular anxiety. This was a much more rational explanation. It preserved Mr. Knightley as the sensible, disinterested friend she had always known him to be, and it left the field clear for her own, far more elegant, theory concerning Mr. Dixon.

Yes, it must be so. Mr. Knightley’s officiousness, as Frank Churchill had so aptly termed it, was merely an expression of his upright, conscientious nature, perhaps heightened by some private intelligence. And Jane’s sharp rebuff? That was the product of a proud spirit, chafing under the weight of too much pity, and perhaps, the secret agitations of a heart engaged elsewhere. The more Emma considered it, the more convinced she became that she was right. She had been momentarily shaken, but her judgment, she was pleased to find, was as sound as ever. With this reassuring conclusion, she was able to sleep.

The next morning brought a renewal of her confidence. The sun shone, her father was in good spirits, and the memory of her brief confusion seemed like a foolish fancy. She was engaged in arranging a basket of flowers for the drawing-room when Mr. Knightley himself was announced.

“I was on my way to Kingston,” he said, after paying his respects to Mr. Woodhouse, “but I could not pass your gates without stopping to inquire how you all are after the dissipations of last night.”

“We are perfectly well, I thank you,” said Emma, smiling. “And you? You did not suffer from your gallantry in riding home in the night air?”

“I am not so delicate as your father fears for you,” he replied, with his usual good-humoured irony. He turned to Mr. Woodhouse and engaged him in a conversation about the management of his estate, speaking of tenants and harvests with a calm and practical air that was quintessentially his own. Emma watched him, and felt her last night’s fancies recede still further. This was the real Mr. Knightley: sensible, straightforward, and entirely devoid of romantic mystery.

When Mr. Woodhouse, fatigued by the complexities of crop rotation, had drifted into a light doze, Emma saw her opportunity. She took up her needlework, and with an air of casual inquiry, said, “We were all very sorry that the party broke up so early. Miss Fairfax seemed quite overcome. I hope she is better to-day.”

Mr. Knightley, who had been looking out of the window, turned to face her. His expression was serious. “I imagine she is as she always is,” he said. “Forced to exert herself beyond her strength, and suffering for it in private.”

“Her situation is certainly a trying one,” Emma agreed, carefully. “One must have a great deal of compassion for her.”

“Compassion is not always what is most needed,” he said, his voice retaining that same grave tone she had noted the night before. “Sometimes, what is required is a little forbearance. A little less scrutiny.”

Emma felt a blush rise to her cheeks. Was this a reproof aimed at her? At her and Frank Churchill’s pointed conversation by the pianoforte? “I am sure no one would wish to scrutinise Miss Fairfax,” she said, with a touch of defensiveness. “We were all admiring her performance. Though I confess I thought her choice of music a little melancholy for such a gay occasion.”

“When the heart is melancholy, it is a great effort to feign gaiety,” he replied. “An effort she should not have been called upon to make.”

The censure was plain, and Emma’s spirit rose to meet it. “You are a great champion for Miss Fairfax, Mr. Knightley. One would almost think you were in her confidence.”

She had meant it as a playful challenge, a way of probing him, but he did not receive it so. He looked at her for a long moment, his gaze steady and unreadable. “I am in no one’s confidence,” he said, at last. “I merely use my eyes. It does not take a great deal of sagacity to see when a young woman is in a state of agitation and distress.”

“Agitation? Yes, I suppose so. Though it seemed to me there was pleasure mixed with it. When the music from Ireland was discovered, for instance. I saw her smile then. A secret, happy smile.” She could not resist putting her own theory to the test.

“Did you?” he said, his voice flat. “I saw only a blush of profound embarrassment. It is a cruel thing to be the subject of such public conjecture, whatever its source.”

He was so decided, so stern in his defence of Jane, that Emma felt herself silenced. She could not argue with him without revealing the whole of her suspicions about Mr. Dixon, and she was not prepared to do that. There was a short, uncomfortable silence, which was broken by Mr. Knightley rising to take his leave.

“I must get on to Kingston,” he said, his manner returning to its usual ease. “Give my best to your father when he wakes. I hope to find him as well on my return.”

He was gone, and Emma was left with a renewed sense of disquiet. He had not explained his behaviour, he had only reinforced it. He had set himself up as Jane Fairfax’s protector, her guardian against the impertinent conjectures of the world, a world which, it seemed, included Emma herself. It was vexing. It was more than vexing; it was deeply puzzling. And the more he insisted on Jane’s unmitigated suffering, the more Emma clung to her own belief in a secret, happy love affair to explain it all. Mr. Knightley, for all his good sense, was clearly mistaken.

A few days later, an event occurred which served to confirm Emma in her own opinion, and to drive all thoughts of Mr. Knightley’s oddities from her mind. A letter arrived at Randalls from Frank Churchill, and Mrs. Weston, as was her custom, brought it with her on her next visit to Hartfield, to be read and enjoyed together. It was a charming letter, full of lively descriptions of his journey and witty complaints about the society he was now obliged to keep in Yorkshire. And then, towards the end, came the passage that made Emma’s heart leap with the delight of vindicated judgment.

“You must tell me all the news of Highbury,” he wrote. “Has the new pianoforte been much admired? I trust a certain _personne_ of our acquaintance has recovered from the fatigues of the party, and is delighting you all with her Irish melodies. Does she, I wonder, ever play a particular waltz? If she does, I hope it brings happier memories to her than it appeared to do on the night we danced it. But perhaps some associations are too powerful to be pleasant in public.”

Emma looked up at Mrs. Weston, her eyes shining. “There!” she cried. “What do you say to that? Could anything be plainer?”

“Plainer, my dear Emma? I confess I see nothing very plain, except that Frank is as fond of a little mystery as ever.”

“Oh, but it is as clear as day! ‘A certain _personne_’, that is Jane Fairfax. ‘Irish melodies’, a direct allusion to the music that came with the pianoforte. And the waltz! He remarks that she looked tired when they danced it. And what did he say to her at the pianoforte? ‘You did not enjoy them as I did.’ It all fits together perfectly. He is teasing her about their shared secret. The pianoforte is from the Dixons, the Irish melodies are a nod to Mr. Dixon’s home, and her weariness is because she is dancing with another man while her heart is with the one she loves. The powerful associations are, of course, her feelings for Mr. Dixon!”

Mrs. Weston smiled, though she still looked doubtful. “It is a very ingenious theory, Emma. You have certainly pieced it together with great skill. But it rests upon a great many suppositions.”

“It rests upon the evidence of my own eyes and ears,” said Emma, triumphantly. “And now, upon Frank Churchill’s own words. He has seen it all, just as I have. He understands the whole business. And this proves,” she added, with immense satisfaction, “how very wrong Mr. Knightley was. All his talk of her unmitigated suffering and distress! He does not see the truth of it at all. He sees only what is on the surface. It takes a little more penetration, a little more insight, to understand the true nature of a woman’s heart.”

She felt gloriously, unassailably right. Every piece of the puzzle had clicked into place. Frank Churchill was her ally, her confederate in perception. Mr. Knightley was a dear, but misguided, friend. And Jane Fairfax was the heroine of a secret romance, her blushes and agitations the signs not of misery, but of a clandestine and heartfelt attachment.

So secure was she in this conviction that she determined to seek an opportunity of being particularly kind to Jane Fairfax, the kindness of one who understands and sympathises. An occasion soon presented itself. She had an errand to take a message from her father to Miss Bates concerning some preserves, and she invited Harriet to walk with her into Highbury.

The day was fine, and as they approached the Bates’s lodgings, they saw Miss Bates herself standing at the door, in earnest conversation with William Larkins. She caught sight of them and immediately bustled forward to meet them, her face a picture of delight and agitation.

“Miss Woodhouse! And Miss Smith! How very kind! How very good of you to be walking this way. We were just, William Larkins was just telling me, Oh, it is such a shocking business, I hardly know how to speak of it. You will be so concerned.”

“Good heavens, Miss Bates,” said Emma, alarmed. “What is it? I hope your mother is well? And Miss Fairfax?”

He writes to Mrs. Weston, of course, but he enclosed a little note for us, so very thoughtful of him, he is always so attentive, and the news is so very dreadful. He has given up all thought of coming to Surrey again for the present.”

Emma’s first sensation was one of pure disappointment. No Frank Churchill! All the pleasant parties, the witty conversations, the shared glances of understanding she had anticipated, all vanished. It was a severe blow to the pleasures of Highbury. Her second thought was for Jane Fairfax.

“How very shocking,” she said, her sympathy now fully engaged. “Poor Mr. Churchill. And poor Mrs. Weston. And it will be a great disappointment, I am sure, to all his friends here.” She glanced towards the window of the Bates’s sitting-room, wondering how Jane was receiving this news. “Indeed, a disappointment to us all,” echoed Miss Bates. “We were so looking forward, Jane, you know, particularly values his conversation. He talks to her so much of music. “It is indeed,” said Jane, her voice very low. “One can only pity them.”

There was no secret relief, no sign that the removal of Frank Churchill was a matter of indifference to her. There was only a profound and settled gravity. Emma felt a flicker of her old uncertainty. Could she have been wrong?

At that moment, Mr. Knightley came riding down the street on his way back from Kingston. He dismounted as he reached them, his expression at once concerned.

“Miss Bates,” he said, his eyes going immediately to Jane. “I have just been at Randalls. Mrs. Weston has told me the news from Windsor.” He looked at Jane Fairfax, and his voice softened. “I am very sorry,” he said, and the words, though simple, were imbued with a weight of meaning that seemed directed at her alone.

Jane looked up at him, and for a single, unguarded moment, her composure broke. Her lips trembled, and her eyes filled with a look of such utter, desolate misery that Emma was shocked to the core. It was not the look of a woman indifferent to Frank Churchill’s fate; it was the look of one who was intimately and wretchedly involved in it.

Jane quickly recovered herself, murmuring something inaudible and turning away as if to adjust her bonnet. But Emma had seen it. And in that one look, her entire, carefully constructed edifice of theory and conjecture came crashing down around her.

It was not Mr. Dixon. It had never been Mr. Dixon.

It was Frank Churchill.

The pianoforte, the music, the secret smiles, the pointed allusions, the shared waltz, it had all been about him. Frank Churchill and Jane Fairfax. They were in love, and it was a secret, and from the misery on Jane’s face, it was an unhappy or a forbidden love.

All of Mr. Knightley’s strange behaviour was suddenly, blindingly clear. His pity, his anger, his gentleness, his fierce protectiveness, it was the concern of a man who knew their secret, who saw the pain it was causing her, and who despised the man that was the source of it. He had not been mistaken; he had been the only one, it seemed, who had understood the truth. He had seen Jane’s suffering, while Emma, in her vanity and her love of mystery-making, had seen only a romantic game.

She stood frozen on the pavement of Highbury, the chatter of Miss Bates a meaningless noise in her ears. She felt a wave of shame so hot and overwhelming that she could barely breathe. She had been wrong. Not just wrong in her speculation, but wrong in her judgment, wrong in her feelings. She had misread every one. She had amused herself with Frank Churchill at Jane Fairfax’s expense, believing herself his confidante when in fact she had been his dupe. She had pitied Jane for the wrong reasons, and admired her for the wrong qualities. And she had dismissed Mr. Knightley’s superior insight as mere officiousness.

He was speaking to Jane now, in a low, kind voice, urging her to go inside out of the wind. And Emma, watching them, felt a pang of something she could not name. It was not just shame for her own folly. It was a deep, unsettling realisation that while she had been playing games, real life, with all its real pain and real complexities, had been happening all around her, and she had not seen it at all. The world was not the neat, predictable place she had imagined it to be, and the people in it were not characters to be arranged in a story of her own devising. The thought was humbling, and more than a little frightening. For the second time in a week, she felt she understood nothing at all, and this time, the feeling was not a flicker of unease, but a profound and immovable certainty.

Mrs. Weston, entirely unsuspicious, attributed Emma’s quietness to fatigue, or perhaps a little vexation at Mr. Knightley’s abrupt departure. “It was a most diverting morning, was it not?” she began, with her usual warmth. “So very pleasant to see you all together. Emma could scarce form the words to reply. Good fun! Lively imagination! The words were like poison in her ears. To agree was a falsehood; to disagree, an impossibility. She settled for a faint, assenting sound, which Mrs. Weston, happily, took for a full concurrence.

“I own,” Mrs. Weston continued, lowering her voice a little as they drew near the house, “I did think Mr. Knightley a little short with us all. He is the best of men, of course, but he does not always make allowances for the high spirits of youth. He is perhaps a little too ready to find fault with Frank.”

Emma’s heart gave a painful leap. If Mrs. Weston only knew! If she could but see the truth, she would understand that Mr. Knightley’s fault-finding was the very pinnacle of charity and forbearance compared with the silent judgment now raging in Emma’s own breast.

Frank Churchill himself, walking on her other side, seemed entirely insensible to the change in her. Or if he was not insensible, he was a master of concealing his perception. “A most successful visit, I think,” he said, with all his customary ease and charm. “The pianoforte is a marvel, Miss Fairfax is a treasure, and Miss Bates is… well, Miss Bates is everything one expects, and that is a comfort in its own way. The cruelty of it, spoken with a smile, was almost more than she could bear. This was the man she had thought so superior, so elegant, so very nearly her own equal. This man, who could speak of wounding a woman’s feelings as a diverting sport, and who could use her, Emma Woodhouse, as his instrument and his shield. The full, ghastly extent of her own blindness was revealed to her in that moment. It had been a performance. A cruel, calculated performance, and she had been his most willing audience; he had required a screen for his true attentions, and she had provided it with a vanity that now scorched her memory. Every flirtatious word, every shared glance, every manufactured confidence had been a lie, designed not to captivate her, but to deceive everyone else. And she, in her folly, had believed herself the object, when she was merely the tool.

“You are very quiet, Miss Woodhouse,” he said, his voice softening into a tone of intimate concern that she now found repulsive. “I hope my nonsense has not tired you. This was too much. The audacity of it, the utter misrepresentation of a man whose boots he was not worthy to clean, was the final straw. Emma stopped at the steps of the house and turned to face them. Her face was pale, but her eyes were steady.

“I am a little tired, I believe,” she said, her voice cooler than she had ever used with him before. “It has been a very… instructive morning. Mrs. Weston, I know you must be getting back. Mr. Churchill, I will not keep you.”

The dismissal was polite, but it was unmistakable. A flicker of surprise, perhaps of concern, crossed Frank Churchill’s face. He was not used to being dismissed by Emma Woodhouse. Mrs. Weston, too, looked at her with a new attentiveness. “Are you quite well, my dear? You look pale.”

“Perfectly well,” Emma insisted, forcing a smile that felt like a grimace. “Only in want of a little solitude. My father will be expecting me.”

It was an excuse that could not be argued with. After a few more expressions of concern from Mrs. Weston and a lingering, questioning look from Frank Churchill, they took their leave. Emma watched them go, not with the pleasant feeling of a hostess who has done her duty, but with the desperate relief of a fugitive who has just escaped her pursuers. She did not wait to see them out of sight, but turned and fled into the house, into the sanctuary of her own room, where at last she could be alone with her shame.

She sank into a chair by the window, but she did not see the familiar, beloved view of her gardens. She saw only scenes from the past few weeks, now illuminated by the harsh, unflattering light of truth. The pianoforte! A gift from a secret lover, not a benevolent guardian. The anonymous nature of it, which had so fueled her romantic speculations about Mr. Dixon, was now clearly a necessity to protect the secret engagement. Frank Churchill’s teasing, his pointed questions about Colonel Campbell, his feigned ignorance, it was all a part of his elaborate deception, a way to talk about his own actions while pretending to talk about another’s. He had been enjoying the exquisite torture of it, speaking of his own love affair to the very woman he loved, under the guise of tormenting her.

And Jane Fairfax! Amiable, upright, perfect Jane Fairfax, who Emma had pitied and patronized and secretly disliked. How little she had understood her. The reserve she had called coldness was the necessary armour of a woman in a desperately difficult situation. The blushes and the stammers she had attributed to a guilty conscience over Mr. Dixon were the agonies of a woman forced to listen to her secret lover flirt with another, and to endure his veiled references to their own private history. The smile Emma had caught, the smile of secret delight, had not been reprehensible. It had been the involuntary joy of a heart hearing a beloved voice, even in the midst of pain. Jane’s endurance, her self-command, her quiet suffering, they were not signs of a flawed character, but of a strength and fortitude that Emma could only now begin to appreciate, and feel deeply ashamed of ever having questioned.

And Mr. Knightley. To think of Mr. Knightley! A wave of heat rushed over her, more powerful than any she had felt before. He had seen it. From the very beginning, he had seen something was amiss. He had never liked Frank Churchill. Emma had called it prejudice, the bias of an older man for a younger, more fashionable rival. What a fool she had been! It was not prejudice; it was perception. He had seen the want of principle, the selfishness, the carelessness for the feelings of others, that Emma, blinded by charm and vanity, had entirely missed.

His warnings, which she had called interference; his concern for Jane, which she had branded as a strange and inappropriate fancy; his quiet observations, which she had dismissed as the grumblings of a man too old and settled for society, every instance of his supposed wrong-headedness was now a testament to his superior judgment and his profound goodness. He had tried to warn her. At the Coles’ party, he had cautioned her about playing games with Frank Churchill. “He is a very charming young man,” he had said, and she remembered the weight with which he said it, the implication that charm was not enough, or that it might even be a danger.

And his behaviour that very morning! His pointed inquiries after Miss Fairfax, spoken so loudly for all to hear. It was not, as she had thought, an awkward attempt at courtship. It was a public declaration of support. He was signaling to Jane, in the presence of her tormentor, that she had a friend, a protector. He was using his own social standing and the respect in which he was held to shield her. When he had cut off Miss Bates to ask, “Can I do any thing for _you_?” it was for Jane he was asking. And when he had insisted on Jane’s dancing being praised, it was another arrow from his quiver, another small act of justice in a situation rife with injustice. He had seen her being overlooked, while Emma and Frank Churchill took all the applause, and he had corrected it.

He knew. He must know the whole story, or at least a great part of it. Perhaps he had been taken into confidence. Perhaps he had simply observed, as he always did, with an eye that saw not what people wished to present, but what they truly were. He had seen Jane’s misery and Frank’s cruelty, and his every action had been an attempt to mitigate the one and rebuke the other.

And how had she, Emma, responded? She had been amused. She had sided with Frank Churchill, laughed at his “nonsense,” and felt a spark of triumph when Mr. Knightley had seemed vanquished or out of humour. She had whispered with Frank, “You speak too plain,” enjoying the intrigue, believing herself a partner in a clever game, when she was in fact an accomplice in an act of casual cruelty. The shame was a physical weight, pressing down upon her, making it difficult to breathe. She had been so wrong, so arrogant, so entirely and comprehensively mistaken in every one of her judgments, that she felt she could never trust her own mind again.

She had maligned Jane Fairfax and she had insulted Mr. Knightley. The two people in her circle who possessed the truest moral worth, she had held in the lowest esteem, while elevating a man of superficial charm and questionable principle. The injustice of her own heart was laid bare, and it was a horrifying sight. She had played with people’s lives, with their feelings, as if they were pieces in a game of her own invention. She had invented a love affair for Jane with Mr. Dixon, a fancy for Mr. Elton for Harriet, and a mutual attraction between herself and Frank Churchill. Not one of them was real. And in the midst of all this fiction, a real, desperate love story had been unfolding under her nose, and her only contribution to it had been to make it more painful for those involved.

What was she to do? The question was not of a puzzle to be solved, but of a wrong to be righted, and she had no idea where to begin. Her first impulse was to find Mr. Knightley, to confess her folly, and to ask his pardon for having so wilfully misunderstood him. But the thought of his face, of the calm, knowing disappointment in his eyes, was more than she could face. He would not scold her; that was not his way, not anymore. He would be kind, and his kindness would be the cruelest punishment of all, for she knew she did not deserve it. She had thrown his wisdom back in his face, and he had every right to think her a vain, foolish girl.

No, she could not go to Mr. Knightley. Not yet. The atonement had to begin elsewhere. It had to begin with Jane Fairfax. She had to find a way to show her kindness, a genuine, respectful kindness that was not tainted by pity or condescension. She had to undo, if she could, some of the hurt she had caused, or at least show that she was no longer a part of the conspiracy of silence and deception that surrounded her.

How, though? A formal call would seem like an intrusion. A gift would be an impertinence. Emma, who was so used to arranging the world to her liking, found herself utterly at a loss. Any gesture she could imagine seemed clumsy and inadequate. Perhaps the only thing she could do, for now, was to do nothing that could cause more harm. She would be quieter. She would observe more and direct less. She would withdraw from her alliance with Frank Churchill, creating a distance that would be felt, if not understood, by all of Highbury. That, at least, was a start. It would signal a change.

The resolution, fragile as it was, brought a small measure of calm. The tempest in her mind subsided, leaving behind the wreckage of her pride. She looked around her room, at the elegant furniture, the books, the drawings, all signs of a life of privilege and ease. She had been given everything, and had understood so little. She had lived for twenty-one years and had only just begun to learn the most basic truths about the human heart, starting with the follies of her own.

A tap at the door startled her. It was her father’s man, come to tell her that Mr. Woodhouse was settled in the drawing-room and was asking for her. Duty called. She rose, smoothed her dress, and took a deep breath. She must go down and be cheerful for her father. She must put on a face of contentment, a mask of untroubled ease, much as Jane Fairfax did every day. The thought gave her a new, and deeply humbling, respect for that young lady’s fortitude. For the first time, Emma felt a flicker of true kinship with her, the kinship of shared, though very different, burdens. The world was a more complicated place than she had ever imagined, and her education, it seemed, was only just beginning. And she would endeavour, however painful the prospect, to deserve again the good opinion of Mr. Knightley. That, above all, was the thought that settled in her heart with the most painful, and the most persistent, weight.

Emma, having parted from Mrs. Weston with an affectionate squeeze of the hand and a promise to call upon her very soon at Randalls, walked the short remaining distance to the house in a state of profound meditation. The amusement she had felt in the Bateses’ parlour had long since evaporated, leaving a residue of shame so sharp and unfamiliar that it was almost a physical discomfort. Mr. Knightley’s words, spoken from the lane, echoed in her mind not with the sound of amusement, as they had to the others, but with the force of a gentle, yet undeniable, rebuke. “I do not see why Miss Fairfax should not be mentioned too.” He had defended her; he had been her champion when Frank Churchill was her tormentor and Emma his silent, smiling accomplice. And his enquiry after Jane’s health had not been the idle civility of a neighbour; it had been pointed, particular, and commanding. He had seen, with that perception that saw every thing, that she was unwell, that she was suffering; while Emma, who had been in the same room, had seen only a rival to be suspected and a subject for amusement.

The more she considered it, the more her own conduct appeared contemptible. She had been flattered by Frank Churchill’s preference, had joined in a league of whispered conjecture against a young woman whose only crime was her reserve and her elegance; a young woman whom Emma knew to be in a situation of pecuniary dependence and precarious health. This was not the behaviour of a gentlewoman; it was the behaviour of a spoiled, unfeeling child. And Mr. Knightley had seen it all. The memory of his steady, discerning gaze, which she had so often found vexatious, now seemed the only moral compass in her world, and she had sailed directly against its pointing. The thought of facing him, of meeting that gaze now, knowing what he must think of her, was more than she could bear. His approbation had been the silent, steady background of her life, something she had taken for granted, like the air she breathed; and now she feared she had forfeited it entirely.

She reached her own room and sat down, not to indulge in the comfortable review of a pleasant morning, but to take herself to task with a severity she had never before applied to her own heart. The image of Jane Fairfax, her forced calmness, the deep blush that had spread over her face at the mention of Weymouth, the flicker of a smile that was not of amusement but of some secret, painful delight; it all came back to her with agonising clarity. Emma had interpreted it as the consciousness of a clandestine attachment to a married man; a reprehensible feeling, she had thought with a kind of self-satisfied horror. Now, she was not so sure. What if the secret were something else entirely? What if the pain and the pleasure were rooted in a story quite different from the one she and Frank Churchill had so cleverly invented? The possibility of a deeper, more complicated truth, a truth in which Jane was not a villain but a victim, began to dawn upon her, and with it, a sense of her own monstrous cruelty.

She had to make amends. But how? To go to Mr. Knightley and confess her fault, to seek his counsel as she had in her younger days, was her first instinct. But pride, and a deeper shame, held her back. To appear before him now, so soon after he had witnessed her unworthiness, and beg for his good opinion, the thought was insupportable. She could not. The atonement must begin with Jane Fairfax herself. She must show her some kindness, some genuine, unpatronising respect, to counteract the impertinence she had encouraged. But what could she do? A formal call might seem an intrusion. A gift might be mistaken for charity. Emma, who was so accustomed to arranging every one’s affairs to her own satisfaction, found herself utterly at a loss.

A resolution, however, began to form in her mind. She would withdraw from her alliance with Frank Churchill. She would no longer be his confidante in this cruel sport. She would create a distance, a coolness that he could not fail to notice. And for Jane, perhaps the only thing to be done for the present was to seek an opportunity for some small, practical service; some gesture that spoke of consideration rather than pity. This small plan, fragile as it was, brought a measure of calm. The tempest of her self-reproach subsided a little, leaving behind the wreckage of her pride. She had been given every thing, and had understood so little. Her education, it seemed, was only just beginning. And she would endeavour, however painful the prospect, to deserve again the good opinion of Mr. Knightley. That, above all, was the thought that settled in her heart with the most painful, and the most persistent, weight.

She entered the drawing-room to find her father precisely where she had expected; seated in his particular armchair, not too near the fire, for fear of overheating, but well within its cheerful influence, and with a shawl already arranged about his shoulders by the ever-watchful Serle.

“Ah, my dear Emma, I am so glad you are come,” he began, his gentle, anxious face turned towards her. “I have been very uneasy. A visit to the Bateses is always such a risk. Their rooms are so small, and the draught from that window, I am sure Miss Bates means well, but she is not at all mindful of draughts. I hope you are not chilled, my love.”

“Not in the least, papa, I assure you. We were all very comfortable.” Comfortable! A draught, indeed, was the least of the evils in that little room.

“And Mr. Knightley,” continued Mr. Woodhouse, his mind moving to its next anxiety. “To be out on horseback on such a day! The clouds looked very threatening when he was in the lane. It is so very unwise. I wish he would be more careful. Kingston is a long ride. He will be tired and chilled when he returns, and so very likely to take cold.”

Emma’s heart contracted. “I dare say he is well wrapped up, papa. Mr. Knightley is very sensible.”

“Ah, but sensible people can be careless of themselves. And he was so very loud in his conversation with Miss Bates. It is not good for the throat to exert the voice so in the open air. Mr. Perry says it is one of the surest ways to bring on a fever. I hope he is not already feeling unwell. Did he sound quite himself to you, Emma?”

“He sounded perfectly well, papa,” she said, her voice a little unsteady. To her father, Mr. Knightley’s raised voice was a symptom of impending illness; to Emma, it was the sound of righteous indignation, a sound directed, she felt, entirely at her. She turned away to the window, pretending to observe the very clouds that so worried her father, but in reality to hide the colour that was rising in her own cheeks.

Their quiet morning was, however, soon to be enlivened, if not improved, by the arrival of a visitor. A light tap at the door, and Miss Smith was announced. Harriet entered with her usual glow of artless pleasure, her blue eyes wide with the lingering excitement of the morning. She had been sent home a little before Emma, and had now come, as was her custom, to talk it all over at Hartfield.

“Oh, Miss Woodhouse!” she began, as soon as she was seated, “was it not the most delightful morning? To hear the new pianoforte! And was not Mr. Churchill in such spirits! So droll, about the spectacles! I could not help laughing, though I tried not to, for fear of disturbing poor Mrs. Bates.”

Emma, who a day before would have encouraged this very strain of conversation, now found it almost unbearable. “Mr. Churchill is very lively, certainly,” was all she could trust herself to say.

“Lively! Oh, he is every thing that is charming,” Harriet sighed. “And so handsome when he leans over the pianoforte! Did you notice, Miss Woodhouse, how he seemed to speak to Miss Fairfax with his eyes? Just as we were saying! It is all so clear. I am sure they understand one another perfectly.”

“Perhaps,” said Emma coolly. “My mind was more occupied with Mrs. Bates’s spectacles. It was a kindness in you, Harriet, to show such concern for her.” She was ashamed of the artifice of this praise, but she could not bear to continue the subject of Frank Churchill and Jane Fairfax; she could not bear to hear her own foolish theories parroted back to her by her unsuspecting pupil. “Lively!” she thought, with internal vexation. “He had been cruel. Droll! He had been impertinent.”

Harriet, checked in her raptures, looked a little confused, but was easily diverted. “Oh! Poor Mrs. Bates. Yes. And it was so very good of Mr. Knightley to be so concerned for Miss Fairfax’s health. He asked after her so particularly, did he not? He has a very kind heart.”

“Yes,” said Emma, with a sincerity that surprised even herself. “Yes, he has.”

The conversation turned to other, safer topics; to Mrs. Goddard’s school, to a pattern for a new cap, and to the health of Mr. Perry’s children. Emma listened with more attention than she usually bestowed upon such subjects, grateful for their very insipidity. It was a relief to be spared the necessity of thinking. But when Harriet was gone, and she was once more alone with her father, her thoughts returned with renewed force to the resolution she had made. She could not rest until she had done something.

An idea, which had been vaguely forming, now took definite shape. Jane Fairfax was obliged to walk to the post-office every morning; it was her one fixed, non-negotiable errand, a duty to Colonel Campbell, it was supposed. In fine weather it was a pleasant walk, but in the damp and chill of an English autumn, it was an undertaking of real hazard for one of her delicate constitution. Mr. Perry himself had remarked upon it. Here was an opportunity for a simple, practical kindness.

She went to her writing-desk at once. The composition of the note was a matter of some difficulty. She wished to be kind, but not familiar; helpful, but not condescending. She chewed the end of her pen, a habit from her girlhood that she had long since overcome, but which returned to her now in her anxiety.

Her first attempt was too formal: *Miss Woodhouse presents her compliments to Miss Fairfax, and begs that she will do her the honour of making use of the Hartfield carriage whenever she has occasion to go into the village. Miss Woodhouse is sensible of the inclemency of the weather, and would be happy to be of service.* No, that would not do. It sounded like an order.

Her second was too familiar: *My dear Jane, Do let me send the carriage for you in the mornings. It is madness for you to be walking to the post in this weather. Your health is too precious.* This was worse; an impertinent liberty, assuming an intimacy that did not exist and offering unsolicited advice.

She tried again, seeking a middle way. *Dear Miss Fairfax,, My father and I were speaking just now of the very unsettled weather, and it occurred to me that your morning walk to the post-office must be a very unpleasant, and perhaps an unwise, undertaking. As our carriage is very often at liberty in the morning, I should be extremely happy if you would allow me to send it for you at any time that is convenient. Pray do not think it any trouble; it would be a real pleasure to me., With best regards, I am very sincerely yours, Emma Woodhouse.*

This, she thought, might suffice. It was simple, it put the matter upon the weather and the convenience of the carriage, and it asked for permission rather than bestowing a favour. It was the best she could do. She copied it out in her neatest hand and was just folding the paper when a brisk, familiar step was heard in the hall, a step that a week ago would have brought a smile to her lips, but which now caused her a sensation of something very like dread. It was Mr. Frank Churchill.

He entered with all his customary ease and brilliance, his eyes alight with conspiratorial glee. “Well, Miss Woodhouse! Have you recovered from our morning’s exertions at the Bateses’ little court? I trust you were as diverted as I was. Did you ever see a performance of such exquisite, if unintended, comedy?”

He drew a chair near her, evidently expecting to settle in for a comfortable dissection of the entire visit. Emma forced a polite smile and continued to busy herself with her writing things, placing the folded note carefully to one side.

“The morning was eventful, certainly,” she replied, her tone much cooler than he was accustomed to from her.

He did not seem to notice. “Eventful is a poor word for it! It was a masterpiece. Our friend was in a sad state of confusion, was she not? That blush when I mentioned Weymouth! It was worth the whole journey from London to see. One knows, now, precisely where the land lies.”

“I confess I gave it very little thought,” said Emma, taking up a pen and examining its nib with great attention. “My mind was more occupied with Mrs. Bates’s spectacles. It was a kindness in you to mend them.”

She was rather pleased with this speech. It was a subtle rebuke, shifting the focus from his cruel game to his one genuinely good deed. He, however, took it as a part of their shared joke.

“A kindness! My dear Miss Woodhouse, it was a master-stroke of strategy! It gave me a quarter of an hour to torment her without interruption. You, my dear confidante, must have appreciated the art of it.”

Emma laid down the pen and looked at him directly, her expression perfectly serious. “I am not certain that I did,” she said, her voice quiet but firm. “I believe I have had a surfeit of art and strategy of late. I find I am beginning to prefer plain dealing.”

For the first time, Frank Churchill looked taken aback. He stared at her for a moment, his smile faltering. “Plain dealing?” he repeated slowly. “From you, Miss Woodhouse? That is a new philosophy indeed! Have you been taking lessons from Mr. Knightley? That sounds very much like one of his pronouncements.”

The mention of Mr. Knightley sent a fresh wave of colour to her cheeks, which she knew he could not fail to observe. “Mr. Knightley’s opinions are generally worth attending to,” she said, striving for a dignified tone. “He was certainly correct in his concern for Miss Fairfax’s health. She did not look at all well.”

This was a declaration of allegiance, and Frank Churchill understood it as such. He saw that the ground had shifted beneath him. His partner in mischief was deserting the field. He attempted to recover his playful manner, but there was an edge of vexation in it now.

“Oh, she is always looking unwell! It is her particular style of beauty. A little pale, a little melancholy, it is very captivating, to some. For my part, I prefer a little more spirit, a little more bloom.” His eyes rested on Emma’s face with a meaning that was unmistakable. A week ago, it would have thrilled her. Now, it felt like an impertinence.

“Her spirits might be higher if she were treated with more consideration,” Emma replied, and was herself surprised at her own boldness.

He raised his eyebrows. “Consideration! My dearest Miss Woodhouse, what is this? Have you turned into Miss Fairfax’s champion overnight? I thought we were agreed. I thought we understood one another. I came here this morning counting on your sympathy, your wit, your appreciation for the finer points of a delicate social situation.”

“My appreciation was misplaced,” said Emma. “It is one thing to be amused by a general folly, and quite another to derive entertainment from the particular distress of an individual. I was at fault, and I am ashamed of it.”

The confession cost her a great deal, but it had the desired effect. Frank Churchill was silenced. He saw that she was in earnest, and that his usual weapons of charm, flattery, and shared wit were, for the moment, entirely useless. He rose from his chair and walked to the window, a frown clouding his handsome features. The easy intimacy between them was broken, and he did not know how to mend it.

“Well,” he said at last, turning back to her with a forced smile. “I see I have called at an inopportune time. You are in a very serious mood today. I shall not plague you with my nonsense any longer. Perhaps you will be more yourself when next we meet.”

“I am perfectly myself,” said Emma. “Perhaps more so than I have been for some time.”

He could make no reply to this. He took her hand, bowed over it with a gallantry that now seemed theatrical and hollow, and took his leave, his expression a mixture of puzzlement, frustration, and wounded vanity.

When he was gone, Emma sank back in her chair, her heart beating fast. It had been more difficult than she had imagined. The habit of their flirtatious understanding was strong, and to break it so decisively had required all her resolve. There was a sense of loss; the fun, the flattery, the excitement were all gone. But underneath the loss was a deeper, steadier feeling: the satisfaction of having done what was right.

She picked up the note she had written to Jane Fairfax. It seemed more important now than ever. This was the second step on her new path. She rang the bell and gave it to a servant, with clear instructions that it was to be delivered immediately. As she watched him go, she felt a tremor of anxiety. How would it be received? Would Jane understand the spirit in which it was sent? Or would she see it as just another species of impertinence from the proud, interfering Miss Woodhouse?

She could not know; she could only hope. She had pleased herself, for a moment, in imagining Mr. Knightley’s approval of her conduct towards Frank Churchill. It was a foolish thought, for he would likely never know of it; and yet, the idea that she had acted as he would have wished her to act, brought a warmth to her heart that was more real and more satisfying than all of Mr. Churchill’s flattery had ever been. She had begun to make amends, not just to Jane Fairfax, but to her own better self; the self that Mr. Knightley had always seen, and had always, she hoped, believed her capable of becoming.

The servant returned in what seemed to Emma an age, though it could not have been more than a quarter of an hour. He carried a small, folded note on a silver tray. Emma’s hand trembled slightly as she took it; she dismissed the man and broke the seal with a sense of immense trepidation. The note was brief, the handwriting elegant but severe.

_Miss Fairfax is sensible of Miss Woodhouse’s kind solicitude. She is much recovered from her trifling indisposition of the morning and begs Miss Woodhouse not to bestow upon it another thought._

That was all. There was no warmth, no reciprocal kindness, no acknowledgement of the spirit in which Emma’s note had been sent. It was a reply of perfect, unassailable propriety, and it chilled Emma to the bone. It was a dismissal, polite and complete. For a moment, her old pride surged; she felt the sting of a rebuff, and her first instinct was to be angry, to think Jane Fairfax ungrateful and cold-hearted. Had she not humbled herself? Had she not, at great cost to her own amusement, dismissed the very man whose attentions Jane so clearly coveted? And this was her reward; a note so cold it might have been written in ice.

But then, the image of Mr. Knightley’s steady, disapproving gaze rose in her mind. What would he say? He would say that a good deed is not done for the sake of reward or acknowledgement. He would say that she had done what was right, and that must be its own satisfaction. He would remind her, perhaps, that Jane Fairfax had every reason to be guarded, every reason to suspect the motives of one who had, until this very morning, been a party to her persecution. Emma sighed, and re-read the note. _Sensible of Miss Woodhouse’s kind solicitude._ Perhaps there was more in that than she had first supposed. It was an acknowledgement, however formal. It was not a rejection of the sentiment, merely a very cautious acceptance. Jane was not ready to trust her; and, upon reflection, Emma could not blame her. She had sown the seeds of mistrust for months; she could not expect to reap a harvest of confidence in a single afternoon. Her new path, it seemed, was to be a stony one, and she must learn to walk it with patience. She folded the note, a little sadly, and put it away, resolving to persevere. She would be kind to Jane Fairfax, whether Jane wished her to be or not.

Her reflections were soon interrupted by the arrival of a carriage; it was Mrs. Weston, come, as she often did, for a comfortable coze with her former governess and dearest friend. Emma was always glad to see her, but today a faint sense of apprehension mingled with her pleasure. She knew what the topic of conversation must inevitably be.

“My dearest Emma!” said Mrs. Weston, embracing her. “I could not rest until I had seen you. I am in such a vexation! I met Frank on the road, not half an hour after he left you, and he was in the strangest of moods.”

“Was he?” said Emma, attempting a tone of innocent inquiry as she led her friend to a sofa.

“Indeed. Utterly out of humour. He said he was going back to Richmond immediately, that Highbury had become insupportable. He would give me no proper reason, only muttering something about a change in the climate. A change in the climate! What can that mean? I told him it was nonsense, that his father would be dreadfully disappointed, but he was quite immovable. He looked so unhappy, Emma. Did you quarrel?”

Emma felt a blush rise to her cheeks. “Quarrel? No, not precisely. We had a… a difference of opinion.”

“About what, my love? You know you can tell me. Has he been too pressing in his attentions? I confess, I had hoped… we had all hoped… that his feelings for you were deepening into something serious.”

Here was the difficulty. How could she explain her change of heart without betraying Frank Churchill, or appearing impossibly capricious? “His attentions have been very flattering,” she said carefully. “But I have come to believe, Mrs. Weston, that they are not of a serious nature. They are a part of his general disposition. He is charming and gallant to everyone.”

Mrs. Weston looked puzzled. “To everyone? Yes, he is very agreeable, to be sure. But his partiality for you, Emma, has been marked. Everyone has seen it. Mr. Weston is convinced of it. And I thought… I truly thought you returned his good opinion.”

“I have enjoyed his company very much,” Emma said, choosing her words with the precision of a diplomat. “He is lively and amusing. But one cannot build a life on amusement. I believe I mistook the nature of our acquaintance. It was a game, a very pleasant one, but I have no wish to play it any longer.”

Mrs. Weston was silent for a moment, studying Emma’s face. “Something has happened,” she said at last, with the gentle certainty of one who knew Emma better than anyone. “Something this morning, at the Bateses’. It concerns Jane Fairfax.”

Emma was startled. “Why should you think so?”

“Because, my dear, Frank was full of it. He was tormenting her, was he not? About the pianoforte. I saw it myself, and I felt for her, though I was as much in the dark as anyone. And Mr. Knightley was very… pointed in his inquiries after her. And now you, who were amused by it all, are suddenly so very grave and distant on the subject of Mr. Churchill. It is all connected, I am sure of it.”

Emma saw that further evasion was useless. “You are right,” she said, her voice low. “It is all connected. I behaved badly this morning, Mrs. Weston. I encouraged Mr. Churchill in a kind of teasing that was not merely idle, but cruel. It was directed at Miss Fairfax, and it caused her real distress. When I saw the pain on her face, I was ashamed. I cannot continue to find sport in such a thing. And if Mr. Churchill’s friendship requires that I do so, then I must do without his friendship.”

Mrs. Weston took Emma’s hand. “My dear, dear Emma. I am so glad to hear you say so. Not because I wish you to be at odds with Frank; it grieves me to think of it. But because you have spoken from the heart, and from your own good sense. I was made uneasy by it myself, but I did not know the cause. Poor Jane Fairfax! She has a difficult life. To be the object of such pointed speculation must be dreadful for her.” A thought seemed to strike her. “Do you suppose… is it possible that Frank’s attachment is not to you, but to her?”

The idea was so absurd, so contrary to all appearances, that Emma almost laughed. “To Jane Fairfax? Mrs. Weston, you cannot be serious! Why, they barely speak to one another. They are almost rude in their mutual indifference. No, no. He may have some secret knowledge of her circumstances, some intelligence from the Campbells, which he uses for his own diversion. That is all. His game, I believe, is to make us all believe he is in love with me, while in truth he is in love with no one but himself.”

The harshness of the judgment surprised even Emma herself, but having said it, she found she believed it to be true. Mrs. Weston looked pained. “Oh, Emma. I hope you are wrong. It would be such a disappointment to his father.”

“Perhaps he will learn to be less selfish,” said Emma, a little stiffly. “It is a lesson we all must learn.”

The conversation turned to other, safer topics, but a certain constraint remained. Mrs. Weston was worried for her stepson, and disappointed in a cherished hope. Emma, for her part, felt the loneliness of her new-found rectitude. It was one thing to decide to be good; it was another to find that it set you at odds with your dearest friends. When Mrs. Weston departed, Emma felt a melancholy settle upon her that was not easily shaken off.

The evening was quiet at Hartfield. Mr. Woodhouse, having been assured that no one had taken a chill from Miss Bates’s open window, and that the visit had not been overly fatiguing, was in a placid state of mind. He sipped his gruel and spoke with gentle concern of the poor Bateses and their want of every comfort, a subject which always made him feel both his own good fortune and his benevolent nature. Harriet Smith had been invited to spend the evening, a custom which had become less frequent of late, but which Emma had revived this day with a renewed sense of duty. She found, however, that Harriet’s conversation, which had once been a source of comfortable superiority, was now merely trying. Her endless, artless prattle about Mrs. Goddard’s school, the pattern of a new lace, or some perceived slight from the Martin family, now struck Emma as intolerably trivial. She tried to engage her on other subjects, to speak of books, or of the wider world, but Harriet’s mind was not formed for such excursions; it always circled back to its own small orbit of concerns.

Emma chided herself for her impatience. It was she who had made Harriet what she was; she who had filled her head with notions of rising above her station, only to leave her stranded between two worlds, unfit for the one and unwelcome in the other. Looking at Harriet’s simple, pretty face, her wide blue eyes fixed upon her with such earnest devotion, Emma felt a pang of profound guilt. She had used Harriet as a plaything, a doll to be dressed up and manipulated for her own amusement. It was a part of the same vanity, the same thoughtless exercise of power, that had led her to trifle with Frank Churchill and torment Jane Fairfax. The resolution that had begun this morning deepened within her; she must not only cease to do harm, but she must try to repair the harm she had done. How to help Harriet, truly and lastingly, was a problem of a different order, and one for which she had, as yet, no answer.

They were sitting thus, the three of them, in the comfortable drawing-room at Hartfield, the lamps lit against the gathering dusk, when the sound of a horseman was heard in the drive. Mr. Woodhouse started. “My dear, who can that be at this hour? It is very late. I hope it is no bad news.”

Emma’s heart gave a sudden, inexplicable leap. There was only one person who was in the habit of visiting Hartfield at any and all hours, with no more ceremony than a member of the family. A moment later, the butler announced him. “Mr. Knightley, sir.”

“Knightley! My dear fellow, come in, come in!” said Mr. Woodhouse, his anxieties immediately soothed. “I am very glad to see you, but you must have had a cold ride. You must have something warm. Emma, my dear, ring for some negus.”

“Thank you, sir, but I am not cold,” said Mr. Knightley, though his manner was less warm than his words. He greeted them all, but his eyes, when they met Emma’s, were grave and searching. There was a tension in his face that she had not seen before, a mixture of anger and concern that made her uneasy.

“Did you have a successful trip to Kingston?” she asked, striving for a light, conversational tone.

“My business in Kingston was completed,” he said, his voice clipped and precise. “It was my journey back that was… instructive.”

He sat down, but not with his usual air of being perfectly at home. He seemed restless, his gaze moving from Emma to Harriet, and back again. There was a silence, broken only by Mr. Woodhouse’s gentle inquiries about the dampness of the roads.

“I find,” Mr. Knightley began again, addressing himself pointedly to Emma, “that people are not always where one expects them to be. One imagines a person is on their way to London, for instance; one is told they are on their way to London, in a great hurry. And yet one finds them lingering on a quiet lane a mile from Highbury, in very particular conversation with another individual.”

Emma felt a prickle of alarm. His tone was heavy with meaning. “Indeed?” she said, her voice a little faint. “That seems very odd.”

“Odd is one word for it,” he returned. “Imprudent, I should call it. Deceitful, I might even say.” His eyes were fixed on hers, and she saw in them a deep and startling disapprobation. He believes I am a party to this, she thought with a flash of panicked insight. He believes I know all about it.

“I am sure I do not understand you,” she said, her colour rising. “Of what deceit are you speaking?”

“I am speaking of a young man who pays devoted, public court in one direction, while carrying on a secret, clandestine attachment in another. I am speaking of a want of principle, a thoughtless cruelty, that puts two people, two ladies, in a position of intolerable difficulty and pain.”

He was speaking of Frank Churchill. That much was certain. And he was accusing him, in the strongest terms, of playing a double game. The blood drained from Emma’s face as a horrifying suspicion began to dawn. Mrs. Weston’s question, which she had dismissed as absurd, _is it possible that Frank’s attachment is not to you, but to her?_, returned with stunning force. Lingering on a quiet lane… in conversation with another individual… A clandestine attachment. Jane Fairfax. It had to be Jane Fairfax. The pianoforte, the teasing, the secret looks, the blushes, the smiles of secret delight… it had not been about a gift from Colonel Campbell. It had been about a gift from him.

The whole world seemed to re-arrange itself in Emma’s mind, like the pieces of a kaleidoscope clicking into a new, dreadful pattern. Everything was explained: Jane’s reserve, Frank’s inconsistencies, the strange tension between them that everyone had mistaken for dislike. It was the tension of a perilous secret. And she, Emma Woodhouse, had been his blind, his tool, the convenient screen behind which he conducted his real courtship. The humiliation was so intense it was like a physical blow. She had pitied Jane Fairfax; but all the while, Jane had been engaged to the man Emma thought was courting her. She had felt superior to Jane; but Jane had a real, true, if secret, attachment, while Emma had been merely the dupe of a flirtation.

Mr. Knightley was watching her, his expression softening slightly at the sight of her genuine shock and distress. “You did not know,” he said, his voice quieter now. It was not a question. “I see that you did not know. I have done you an injustice.”

“Know?” she whispered, her voice shaking. “Know what? I know nothing, except that you are accusing Mr. Churchill of some terrible behaviour, and you seem to believe I ought to understand it.”

He looked towards Mr. Woodhouse, dozing gently in his chair, and then at Harriet, who was staring at them both with wide, uncomprehending eyes. He lowered his voice still further. “Miss Woodhouse, Emma. I beg your pardon. My anger has been for him, but also for you. I saw you taken in, and it has vexed me for weeks. I saw him pay you attentions which I believed, which everyone believed, to be serious. To discover that it was all a feint, a performance to mask his true objective… it is an insult to you. An unpardonable one.”

“His true objective?” she repeated, though she now knew the answer.

“I was returning from Kingston,” he explained, his voice low and urgent. “I took the lane by the old mill, as it shortens the way. I saw two people standing by the stile, in what was clearly a secret and agitated conference. I was upon them before they could separate. It was Frank Churchill and Jane Fairfax.”

The confirmation, spoken aloud, was still a shock. Emma felt she could not breathe.

“He had the grace to look thoroughly ashamed of himself,” Mr. Knightley continued, his own anger returning. “She… she looked as if she were about to faint. I did not stop. I would not give them the satisfaction, or the embarrassment, of a conversation. But I saw it all. I understood it all in that moment. The man is a scoundrel, Emma. To trifle with your affections, to use your good name and Hartfield itself as a cover for his deceptions, while he exposes her, a woman of no fortune and delicate health, to gossip and ruin… there are no words for it.”

“My affections?” Emma found her voice at last, and it was sharp with a pain he could not understand. “He has not trifled with my affections. He has trifled with my vanity. He has made me a fool. That is all.”

Mr. Knightley looked at her, his brow furrowed in confusion. “Emma? I do not understand. You cannot mean you have no feeling for him?”

“I have the feeling of one who has been profoundly and thoroughly deceived!” she cried, her voice rising with a passion that made Harriet jump. “I have the feeling of one who has been made a partner in a scheme of cruelty and deceit, without her knowledge or consent! Do not pity me for a broken heart, Mr. Knightley. Pity me for my blindness. Pity me for my arrogance, which was so great that I could not see what was before my very eyes. Everyone, it seems, has been in possession of a secret, while I, who believed I knew everything, have in fact known nothing at all!”

She stood up, too agitated to sit, and walked to the fireplace, her back to him. She rested her forehead against the cool marble of the mantelpiece. The humiliation was a fire in her veins. He thought she was in love with Frank Churchill. He, of all people. The one person whose good opinion she valued above all others. He had come here tonight, angry on her behalf, ready to defend her from a faithless lover. And she had to bear the shame of knowing that the entire drama had been a sham, and she the leading, unwitting actress in the farce.

“I am sorry,” he said, coming to stand near her. His voice was gentle now, full of a perplexed concern. “I have distressed you. That was not my intention. My only wish was to open your eyes. I could not bear to see you so misused.”

“You have opened them,” she said, without turning around. “And I do not thank you for the view.”

The bitterness in her own voice shocked her. It was unfair, and she knew it. He had acted from the best of motives, from a place of long-standing, brotherly, protective regard. But she was too raw, too wounded in her pride, to be grateful. The knowledge he had given her had not only exposed Frank and Jane; it had exposed her to herself, and she did not like the person she saw. The perfect Emma Woodhouse, handsome, clever, and rich, was a fool. And worse, Mr. Knightley, the one man she could never fool, had been a witness to the entire, mortifying spectacle. The path to her better self seemed suddenly, impossibly, long.

A long moment passed, in which the only sounds were the soft crackle of the fire and the almost inaudible sob that escaped from Harriet. Mr. Knightley did not move. He had come to Hartfield a champion, armed with righteous anger on behalf of a wronged and heartbroken Emma; he found himself now entirely disarmed by a truth he had never once conjectured. He, who prided himself on his knowledge of her, on his ability to see through her little vanities to the good and sound heart beneath, had in this greatest of instances misjudged her completely. He had seen a lovelorn girl; she had been acting a part in a play of her own devising, and had only just discovered that the plot was not at all what she had written.

“Emma,” he said at last, and his voice was so altered, so stripped of its earlier certainty, that she flinched. She did not turn. “I beg your pardon. I have been a greater fool, I think, than even Frank Churchill.”

The apology, so unexpected, and the inclusion of himself in the charge of foolishness, did what no amount of scolding could have done; it pierced the armour of her pride. She turned slowly from the mantelpiece, her face pale and stained with the tears she refused to shed.

“No,” she said, her voice scarce a whisper. “You have been my friend. You have been, as you always are, honest. The folly is mine, and mine alone. I am not heartbroken, Mr. Knightley; I am… humiliated. There is a difference. A broken heart may be an object of pity, even of a certain romantic dignity. A fool is merely an object of ridicule.”

“I do not ridicule you,” he said, taking a hesitant step towards her. “I could never ridicule you. I am angry, yes, angrier than I have ever been, but not with you. To think that he should have come here, into this house, and made sport of you all! That he should have used your friendship, your hospitality, your very generosity of spirit, as a screen for his duplicity! And Jane Fairfax, to allow it! To stand by and watch you be so deceived, to accept your kindnesses, small as you might have thought them, while knowing you were the unwitting tool of her own secret happiness. It is a conspiracy of poor character, and you have been its victim.”

His defence of her, even now, was a fresh torment. “A victim who was a willing accomplice in her own deception,” she countered, a little of her customary spirit returning. “Do not make me a blameless heroine in this sordid affair, Mr. Knightley. I am anything but. I did not merely believe he was attached to me; I encouraged the idea. I enjoyed it. I enjoyed the attention, the speculation, the sense that I, Emma Woodhouse, was once again at the centre of a pretty little romance. I built it all up in my mind, just as I did with Mr. Elton, and I find the ruin of my own structures is a poor sort of tragedy for which no one can be blamed but the architect.”

She glanced, almost involuntarily, towards Harriet, who sat hunched upon the sofa, a small, miserable figure of collateral damage. The sight of her was a fresh pang of guilt, sharper and more true than any of her wounded vanity.

Mr. Knightley’s eyes followed hers, and a new understanding dawned in his expression, one of even graver concern. “And Miss Smith,” he said quietly, his voice losing its anger and taking on a note of deep, compassionate regret. “What part has she been made to play in this drama?”

Emma pressed her lips together. Here was the charge she could not answer, the crime for which she had no defence. All her protestations of being a fool might have been a kind of inverted pride, but the harm she had done to Harriet was real, and undeniable.

“She has been made to play the part of my companion in folly,” Emma said, her voice low and heavy with a shame that was, for the first time, entirely pure. “I… I fear I may have given her reason to believe… to hope… that Mr. Churchill’s attentions were not for me, but might be for someone else, if only circumstances were different.” It was a weak, evasive summary of her meddling, and she knew it. The truth was far worse; she had very nearly tried to redirect Frank Churchill’s affections towards Harriet, as one might redirect a stream, without a thought for the landscape that might be flooded in the process.

“You tried to give her Frank Churchill, after Mr. Elton?” Mr. Knightley asked, his voice a study in restrained disbelief. He did not need to say more; he did not need to remind her of his warnings, of his predictions, of the fundamental unsuitability of all her plans for her friend. It was all there, in the quiet, disappointed cadence of his question.

“It was wrong,” Emma said simply. She walked over to the sofa and sat down beside Harriet, taking her cold, limp hand. “It was unpardonable. I have been a wretched friend to you, Harriet. I have played with your happiness for my own amusement. I have used you, and your gentle heart, as a plaything for my schemes. Can you ever forgive me?”

Harriet, who had been weeping silently, looked up, her blue eyes wide with confusion and distress. “Oh, Miss Woodhouse! Forgive you? How could I not forgive you? You have been the best friend in the world to me. It is not your fault. It is he, Mr. Churchill, who is so wicked! And Miss Fairfax… to be so sly! I should never have thought it of her. But you, you are all goodness. You thought only of me.”

This artless, devoted absolution was more painful to Emma than any rebuke. It was the forgiveness of a child for an adult’s careless cruelty, a forgiveness born of an inability to comprehend the offence. Emma looked across at Mr. Knightley, a miserable appeal in her eyes. He saw, she knew, the full depth of her failure. He saw that Harriet’s unshakeable devotion was, in itself, the most damning evidence of the power Emma had misused.

Mr. Knightley, having observed this exchange, seemed to reach a conclusion. He walked back towards the door, retrieving his hat and gloves. His anger had entirely dissipated, replaced by a quiet, thoughtful gravity.

“I must leave you,” he said. “This is a matter for you and Miss Smith to discuss. There is much to be untangled, and my presence is a hindrance.” He paused, his hand on the door. “Emma,” he said, and she looked up, her full attention on him. “You say you have been a fool. Perhaps. We are all fools at one time or another. The measure of our worth is not in the falling, but in the rising. You have a good heart, and a sound understanding, when you choose to use it. Do not let this… this mortification… embitter you. Let it instruct you.”

He looked at her for a long moment, and in his gaze she saw no triumph, no ‘I told you so,’ but a deep and abiding concern that felt, suddenly, like the only solid ground in a world that had turned to quicksand. It was the look of a friend who saw not the fool of the last half-year, but the woman he believed she could yet become.

“Thank you,” she whispered. It was all she could manage.

He gave a slight, formal bow, his eyes lingering on her for a second more before he turned and was gone. The quiet click of the latch echoed in the silent room.

For several minutes, neither Emma nor Harriet moved. The grand drawing-room of Hartfield, usually a place of comfort and cheer, felt vast and cold. Emma was profoundly, deeply tired, as if she had run a great distance. The path to her better self, she thought with a pang of despair, was not merely long; it was a steep and treacherous climb, and she was at the very bottom, with no clear view of the summit.

“Oh, Miss Woodhouse,” Harriet began again, her voice trembling. “What are we to do? To think of it! Mr. Churchill, engaged all this time! And to Miss Fairfax! It seems impossible. She is so… so reserved.”

Emma took a deep breath. Here was her first trial. Her first act of penance. She could not wallow in her own shame. She had a duty to perform. She squeezed Harriet’s hand gently.

“It is not impossible, Harriet, merely secret. And now that it is a secret no longer, we must consider what it means for us. For you.”

“For me?” Harriet looked bewildered. “But he was never for me, Miss Woodhouse. You said yourself his station was too high. I only ever thought… perhaps… if he were not thinking of you…” Her voice trailed off.

“And that is my fault,” Emma said firmly, looking her friend directly in the eye. “I allowed you to think it. I encouraged you to think it. Harriet, I have made a terrible mistake, not just with Mr. Churchill, but in my whole approach to you. I have tried to raise you, to change you, to fit you into a world where you do not belong, and in doing so I have unsettled you and made you unhappy.”

“Unhappy? Oh, no! I have been happier since I knew you than ever in my life!”

“Have you?” Emma asked gently. “Or have you been more anxious? More uncertain? Did you not know a greater peace at Mrs. Goddard’s, before I filled your head with notions of gentlemen and grand marriages?”

Harriet’s face was a picture of conflict. She could not deny the glamour of her time with Emma, but the memory of her simple, untroubled past held its own appeal. “But… I should never have known you! I should never have been to Hartfield!”

“And perhaps you would have been spared a great deal of pain,” Emma replied. “The pain of Mr. Elton’s cruel rejection, and now… this. Harriet, my dear, dear Harriet. My vanity has been your torment. I see it all now. From this day forward, there will be no more schemes. No more matchmaking. I will not attempt to find you a husband. I will not meddle in your life at all, beyond offering you my sincere and steady friendship, if you will still have it.”

The speech was harder to make than any she had ever made. It was a complete abdication of the role she had so delighted in playing: the clever, all-knowing patroness. It was an admission of utter and complete failure.

Harriet began to cry in earnest now, not from a broken heart over Frank Churchill, for her affections had never been truly engaged, but from the shock of this new, humbled, self-recriminating Miss Woodhouse. It was as if the sun had declared itself a mere candle.

“Oh, Miss Woodhouse, please do not say such things!” she wept. “You are not a failure! You are everything that is elegant and clever. If you have been mistaken, it is only because you have too good a heart! You wished for my happiness, and that can never be wrong!”

Emma sighed. It was hopeless. Harriet’s gentle, uncritical nature made a true reckoning impossible. For now, she must simply be soothed. Emma put an arm around her shaking shoulders.

“There, there, Harriet. Do not cry. It is all over now. Mr. Churchill will marry Miss Fairfax, and they will go to Ireland or Yorkshire or wherever they please, and we shall have to be very civil and wish them well. And we,” she said, forcing a note of briskness into her voice, “we shall go on. We shall read and walk and play music, and we shall talk no more of romance and secrets. The world is perhaps a less interesting place than I imagined it yesterday, but it may also be a safer one.”

She led Harriet from the room, promising tea and a quiet evening, her mind already working on the next, most difficult task: how to face the world tomorrow. How to face her father, and Mrs. Weston, and the Bateses, and Jane Fairfax herself. And how, most of all, to face the reflection in her own mirror, which would now and forever show her the face of a fool who had been schooled, at last, by a man she had thought she knew, but had never, until this night, truly understood. The path was long indeed, and she had only just taken the first, painful step.

The evening at Hartfield was one of the longest and most silent Emma had ever known. Harriet, having wept herself into a state of quiet exhaustion, was soon persuaded to retire to her room, leaving Emma to the sole company of her father. Mr. Woodhouse had perceived that something was amiss; Harriet’s red eyes and Emma’s unnatural stillness were not to be overlooked, but his enquiries were, as ever, dictated by his own tender anxieties.

“My dear, I am afraid the evening air from the open window at Mrs. Bates’s was too much for poor Miss Smith. She looks quite faint. Or perhaps the news of this engagement, so very sudden, you know, has been a shock to her nerves. It is a great pity that people must have these sudden engagements. It is so unsettling for everybody else.”

“It is a little unsettling, papa, but we shall all be accustomed to it in a day or two. Harriet is merely tired.”

“Ah, tired. Yes. And you, my dear Emma, you are very quiet yourself. I hope _you_ are not feeling chilled? Perry was saying only last week that these spring evenings can be treacherous. We must not have you catching cold. You have been running about so much of late.”

“I am perfectly well, papa, I assure you. Only thoughtful.”

She was indeed thoughtful; her mind was a whirlwind of mortifying recollections. Every sly look from Frank Churchill, every word of his false confidence, every instance of her own blindness, paraded before her. And through it all, like a steady, reproving bass note to the shrill melody of her folly, ran the memory of Mr. Knightley’s warnings. “He is a man I cannot rightly trust.” “He is reserving his best praise for another.” “She is his object.” How completely, how accurately he had seen it all! He, who was supposed to know nothing of the finer arts of matchmaking or the delicate language of flirtation, had possessed the simple, penetrating truth of the matter, while she, Emma Woodhouse, the clever, the discerning, had been fashioning a romance as flimsy and false as a child’s paper boat, and had sailed poor Harriet’s hopes in it straight into a whirlpool. The shame was almost more than she could bear. She had not only been a fool, but she had been a confident, boastful fool; and he had seen it. He had argued with her, had tried to save her from herself, and she had repaid his solicitude with proud, dismissive opposition. The memory of their last true quarrel, concerning her treatment of Miss Bates at Box Hill, returned to her now with a fresh and agonizing force. He had been right then, too. He was always right. And she, who had valued his approbation above almost any other, had so consistently acted in a way to lose it.

She passed a restless night, and rose the next morning with the settled resolution of a penitent determined upon her penance. The first and most necessary task was a visit of congratulation to the Bateses’. It was a debt of civility, an act of propriety that could not be deferred, and she knew it would be the most difficult visit she had ever been called upon to pay. She would have to see Jane Fairfax, to offer her felicitations, to behave as if she felt a pleasure she could not possibly feel, and to do it all under the observant, if kindly, eyes of the neighbourhood.

She was still fortifying herself for the ordeal when a visitor was announced, and Mrs. Weston entered the drawing-room. Her countenance was a mixture of affection, embarrassment, and sympathy.

“My dearest Emma,” she began, taking both of Emma’s hands, “I could not stay away. I came as soon as I could leave Mr. Weston. You have heard, of course. I can see that you have.”

“We heard it all last night,” said Emma, striving for a composure she was far from feeling. “We had it from the fountain-head.”

“Oh, Emma! What you must think of us, of me! To have been so blind! Frank has written me a letter, such a letter of explanation and apology. He is full of self-reproach for the deception. It was a long-standing engagement, it seems, made in secret at Weymouth last autumn. He felt he could not declare it on account of his aunt’s disapproval. It was a very difficult situation for them both.”

Emma could not answer. A difficult situation! She thought of Jane’s constant headaches, her reserved manners, her evident unhappiness. The difficulty had been all Jane’s. Frank Churchill had amused himself very well.

“He is very sorry for any pain he may have caused,” Mrs. Weston continued, her maternal feelings pleading his case. “Particularly with respect to… to any misunderstandings that may have arisen. He blames himself entirely. He says he was thoughtless, but that he never meant to be deliberately cruel. He esteems you so highly, Emma.”

“I am sure he is very good,” said Emma, her voice cooler than she intended. “And I must own, I am the one who deserves the blame. I was the one who encouraged the misunderstanding. I was a great fool, and I have acted very ill. Pray do not make any excuses for me. I am determined to face my own folly, and I hope, to profit from it.”

Mrs. Weston looked at her with surprise. This was not the Emma she knew. The proud spirit, so quick to defend its own judgment, was entirely gone, replaced by a subdued and steady self-condemnation that was almost more painful to witness.

“My dear, you are too harsh on yourself. We were all deceived. Even Mr. Knightley…”

“No,” Emma interrupted, with a flash of her old energy. “That is the thing. Mr. Knightley was not deceived. He saw it all. He told me so, and I would not listen. There is my greatest fault. I esteemed my own perception so much more than his. I must go to see Miss Fairfax this morning. It is the proper thing to do.”

“Yes, I thought we might go together,” said Mrs. Weston, relieved. “It will be less awkward for you.”

“Thank you,” said Emma, gratefully. “That is very kind.”

The walk to Mrs. Bates’s was accomplished with little conversation. Emma’s mind was too full for trivialities, and Mrs. Weston was wise enough to perceive it. As they approached the door, Emma felt a renewal of all her dread. She was to wish happiness to the woman she had so steadily disliked, and whose reserve and elegance had always been a silent reproach to her own more careless triumphs.

They were admitted, and the scene that met them was precisely what might have been expected. Miss Bates was in a state of flustered, incoherent, joyful agitation. She met them at the door of the parlour, her words tumbling out in a stream of welcome, apology, and exclamation.

“My dear Mrs. Weston! And Miss Woodhouse! So very kind! So very good of you to call! You have heard our news, I am sure. I declare I am so happy I scarcely know whether I am on my head or my heels! My poor mother is quite overwhelmed with joy. And Jane! Well, you must come in and see Jane. She is here with, well, he is here too! It is all so wonderful, I can hardly believe it. We have been so very sly, you know, but it was all for the best, as it turns out. Mrs. Churchill, poor soul, her disapprobation would have made them miserable, but now it is all settled. Do come in, do not stand in the draught.”

She bustled them into the little room, which seemed even smaller and more crowded than usual. There, sitting by the famous pianoforte, were the happy couple. Jane Fairfax rose to greet them, her colour a little heightened but her composure admirable. Frank Churchill sprang up with all his customary animation, though Emma thought she detected a shade of embarrassment under his smile.

“Mrs. Weston! Miss Woodhouse!” he exclaimed, coming forward. “How very good of you. We are the luckiest creatures in the world, are we not, Jane? And the most undeserving of such kind friends, after all our shocking duplicity.”

“We wish you very happy, both of you,” said Mrs. Weston, warmly, kissing Jane on the cheek.

Emma found her voice. “Indeed, I wish you every felicity,” she said, her tone perfectly civil, perfectly correct. She held out her hand to Jane, who took it for a moment. Their eyes met, and in that brief glance, a volume was exchanged: a history of rivalry, misunderstanding, and now, a strange, new, and disconcerting equality.

“You are very kind, Miss Woodhouse,” Jane said, her voice low but steady.

“Kind!” cried Frank Churchill. “She is an angel of forgiveness! I have been a very great rogue, Miss Woodhouse, have I not? I must throw myself on your mercy. My only excuse is that I was a man distracted, and that I could not resist the pleasure of your society, even when my heart was engaged elsewhere. Can you forgive a wretch for finding Hartfield too agreeable?”

His easy charm, which had once delighted her, now struck Emma as insufferably impertinent. He was making light of it, turning his deception into a compliment, and expecting her to be pleased.

“A gentleman may be forgiven for finding any society agreeable,” Emma replied, with a cool smile. “I am only sorry that circumstances should have required such a prolonged secrecy. It must have been a great trial for you both.”

She directed this last sentiment pointedly towards Jane, who coloured deeply but said nothing. Miss Bates, however, was ready to fill any silence.

“Oh! A great trial indeed! My poor Jane! You have no idea, Miss Woodhouse, what she has suffered. Her nerves so shaken, and her appetite so poor! I was sure she was sickening for something dreadful. And all the while it was only love! Is not that the most extraordinary thing? And the pianoforte! To think of him sending the pianoforte! We all thought it was Colonel Campbell, and it was Mr. Churchill all along! He is the most artful, the most generous, oh! it is such a romance! Quite like a book! And Mr. Knightley, did you hear about Mr. Knightley? He was so cross with me yesterday, and it was all because he was so concerned for Jane! He suspected something, I am quite sure of it now. He is so very clever, you know. Almost as clever as you, Miss Woodhouse!”

Emma wished the floor would open. To have her own folly and Mr. Knightley’s superior penetration announced so publicly was the very essence of her mortification. She caught Frank Churchill’s eye and saw a flicker of amusement there, which he quickly suppressed. He knew what she must be feeling, and he was amused. The knowledge was intolerable.

She made her escape as soon as propriety would allow, pleading the necessity of returning to her father. Mrs. Weston accompanied her, and once they were out in the street again, Emma could not help exclaiming, “What a visit! I hope I shall not be required to make another like it in my life.”

“He is a little too easy in his manner, perhaps,” Mrs. Weston allowed. “But he is very happy. And she, did you see how she looked at him when he went to adjust her shawl? There is no doubt of their attachment.”

“No,” said Emma, sighing. “There is no doubt of that. I only wish it had been conducted with more honour.” She would say no more against the son of her friend, but her heart was heavy with a disapproval she could not voice.

As they neared the gates of Hartfield, they saw a familiar figure on horseback approaching from the direction of Donwell. It was Mr. Knightley. Emma’s heart gave a sudden, painful leap. It was the first time she had seen him since he had been proved so entirely, so humiliatingly right. He dismounted as he reached them, giving the reins to a servant.

“Mrs. Weston. Emma.” He bowed, his expression serious. “I was on my way to you. I have been to Kingston, and on my return I heard the news that is now all over Highbury.”

“Yes,” said Mrs. Weston. “We have just been offering our congratulations.”

He looked at Emma, and his gaze was not triumphant, but kind; it was full of a gentle, quiet concern that she found almost harder to bear than any reproach. He saw her pale cheeks and the strain in her eyes.

“Are you well, Emma?” he asked, his voice softer than usual.

“Perfectly well, I thank you,” she answered, forcing a smile.

“We were all taken by surprise,” he continued, turning slightly to include Mrs. Weston, yet his attention seemed fixed on Emma. “It was a well-kept secret. I must own myself one of the deceived. I had my suspicions, but they pointed in another direction entirely.”

Emma stared at him. He was claiming to have been deceived? He, who had spoken with such certainty? She understood him at once. He was trying to spare her. He was attempting, with a generosity that struck her to the very soul, to place himself at her side in her foolishness, to lessen her mortification by sharing in a blame that was not his. The magnanimity of it, the exquisite delicacy of feeling, was too much. Her carefully constructed composure threatened to shatter.

“Indeed?” said Mrs. Weston, looking from one to the other. “I should have thought you the last person to be taken in, Mr. Knightley.”

“We are none of us infallible,” he said, with a slight smile, his eyes still on Emma. “And a young man in love can be very ingenious in his deceptions.”

Tears she had refused to shed for her own folly now pricked at the back of her eyes, tears of a confused and overwhelming gratitude. She could not speak. She gave a little nod, a mute acknowledgment of his kindness, and, turning abruptly, walked through the gates towards the house, leaving him and Mrs. Weston to follow.

She fled to the shrubbery, her customary refuge, and paced back and forth, trying to command her feelings. It was not shame that agitated her now, but something far more powerful and perplexing. His kindness. His tact. The way he had looked at her, as if her pain were his own. He had not come to say, “I told you so.” He had come to stand with her. She, who had so prided herself on her independence and understanding, had been propped up, corrected, and now, sheltered, by him at every turn.

She had been sitting on a bench for some time, her thoughts in a tumult, when she heard his step on the gravel path. He had sought her out.

“Emma,” he said, stopping before her. “I was afraid you were unwell.”

“No,” she said, looking up at him, her face earnest and pale. “I am not unwell. I am only… ashamed. You must not try to spare me, Mr. Knightley. You were not deceived. You knew. You told me, and I did not believe you. You must let me say it. You were right. You were right in every thing, and I was wrong.”

He looked down at her, and there was a gravity in his face she had never seen before. “I would rather have been wrong, Emma,” he said quietly. “To be right in such a case is no great pleasure. I spoke too strongly, perhaps. I have reproached myself for it. At Box Hill… I was angry, and I made you unhappy. It has been on my conscience ever since.”

This was more than she could bear. That he should be reproaching himself, when she was the one so deeply at fault!

“No, no,” she cried, starting to her feet. “You said only what was true. I deserved it. I was behaving abominably. It was your saying it that made me see it. You have always been my best and truest friend. You have always told me the truth, even when I did not wish to hear it.”

They stood in silence for a moment, the air between them thick with unspoken feelings. The social landscape of Highbury, the follies of Frank Churchill, the mortification of her schemes, all of it faded away, leaving only this one, overwhelming reality: his presence, his goodness, his steady, unwavering concern for her. A new and terrifying idea, an idea that had nothing to do with Harriet Smith or Frank Churchill, began to dawn in Emma’s mind. It was an idea so startling, so utterly subversive to her whole sense of self, that she felt her cheeks burn and her heart begin to race. She did not know what to do with it; she did not even have a name for it. She only knew that the approbation of the whole world was as nothing compared to the approbation of the man who stood before her, and that the thought of having lost it was a misery beyond all others.

“I must go,” he said at last, as if sensing her agitation. “Your father will be wanting you. I only wished to be sure that you were not… that you were not suffering too much from this surprise.”

“I am very much obliged to you,” she managed to say. “You are very kind.”

He hesitated, then gave her a small, sad smile, and was gone. Emma sank back onto the bench, her mind in a greater state of confusion than ever before. The mortification over her matchmaking blunders was a simple, straightforward pain. This new emotion was a labyrinth. She had entered the day intending to do penance for her foolishness regarding Frank Churchill, and she was ending it in the grip of a feeling so profound and so alarming that it threatened to overturn her entire world. The path to self-knowledge, she was beginning to understand, was a far longer and more treacherous road than she had ever imagined.
