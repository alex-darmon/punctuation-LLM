He lowered the book with a deliberate slowness, a playful severity in his eye. “It is a great torment to a man’s curiosity, Miss Andrews, when a lady receives a letter of such evident importance and yet betrays nothing of its contents. I am left to imagine all manner of things. A secret summons to court? A proposal of marriage from a Turkish sultan?” He paused. “You may relieve my suffering with but a single word.”

“And what word would you have me say, Mr. Davenport?” she replied, her own composure a source of some private satisfaction. “If I said ‘Tuesday,’ would that suffice?”

“Tuesday! An excellent word. It has a sound of business and purpose. ‘Tuesday,’ he repeated, as if tasting it. “But it solves nothing. Is it the day of a journey, or the day a long-lost cousin is to be revealed? I must conjecture. One conjectures, you know, because one cares. The indifferent man does not trouble himself with wondering. But I, you see, am troubled. Terribly troubled.” He picked up a silver letter opener from the table and began to polish it with his handkerchief. “How to make this shine as it ought. We gentlemen idlers find such tasks to occupy our restless minds. Miss Bennet, I believe you were speaking of conjecture. Now I have quite forgot my point. Ah, there. It is done. I have the pleasure, madam, (to Mrs. Allen,) of restoring your opener to its former glory, ready to unseal a thousand more mysteries.”

He was thanked with a flutter of gratitude from the elder lady. To avoid her further praise, he moved towards the small table where Miss Andrews was arranging flowers. “If you would be so good,” said he, in a lower voice, “you will put that white rose next to the blue delphinium. It is a combination we admired at the assembly last week. Do you not recall? You professed not to enjoy the evening; you seemed fatigued by the noise and the company. I, for one, would have gladly spent another hour in such a place, for all its supposed faults.”

She placed the rose as he suggested. “What a memory you have for such trifles, Mr. Davenport.” She looked at him, and for an instant a warmth rose in her cheeks before she turned back to her task. “If I mistake not, that was the very hour Mrs. Gable was recounting her entire tour of the Lake District.”

He laughed. “Was it? Then my memory is a traitor. Here is a piece of sheet music fallen to the floor. Do you know it? A new sonata from Vienna. And here, a book of Scottish airs. All newly arrived from London, I collect. A very considerate brother, to be sure. He knew you would have little to entertain you here. I admire that quality in a man; a true and detailed attention to a sister’s happiness. It speaks of a careful and deliberate affection. Nothing forgotten; nothing done by halves.”

Eleanor, sitting by the window with her needlework, wished he would be less obvious in his purpose, yet could not suppress a smile; and when she glanced towards Miss Andrews, she saw the blush was now accompanied by the faintest glimmer of a pleased, if flustered, expression. This perfect, sensible Sarah Andrews was not, it appeared, immune to such pointed flattery.

He brought the Scottish airs to the settee, and he and Eleanor looked them over. He took the opportunity to whisper, “You think I am too bold. She must think me odious.”

“I think she must be prodigiously confused,” Eleanor whispered back. “I cannot tell if you mean to praise her brother or yourself.”

“I mean for her to understand that true attention is a rare and valuable thing. I am quite content with my meaning. Let her puzzle it out. If she is half as clever as you say, she will arrive at the proper conclusion.”

“And if she is not?”

“Then she is not the woman I take her for. See, she is humming to herself. ‘The Blue Bells of Scotland.’ A song of loyalty and return. Very telling.”

Shortly afterwards, a commotion was heard in the hall, and Mrs. Allen, who had gone to investigate, returned in a state of high excitement. “Mr. Croft is here! He saw the carriage outside and has stopped to pay his respects. He cannot stay but a moment. He is on his way to Sedleigh to see about a new horse.”

The listeners in the drawing room could hardly fail to hear the exchange that followed, for the hall was all stone and echoes, and Mr. Croft possessed a voice of cheerful authority.

“How is your guest, Mrs. Allen? How is Miss Andrews? I heard she took a small chill at the assembly. Is she quite recovered? I should be very sorry to hear she is unwell.”

And Mrs. Allen was obliged to give a full report on her guest’s health before Mr. Croft would be satisfied.

“So good of you to ask! So very thoughtful of you!” Mrs. Allen was heard to say.

He cut her short with, “I am bound for the Sedleigh market. Is there any service I can offer you?”

“Oh! dear, the market? I believe I need some ribbon from the mercer’s.”

“Your man can fetch that readily enough. Is there any service I can offer to Miss Andrews?”

“No, I thank you. But you must come in! Miss Vance is here, and Mr. Davenport too!”

“Well,” said he, and a thoughtful pause followed. “For a minute, then. Only a minute.”

“They will be so very pleased to see you!”

“No, no, I see I am too late. You have a full party. I should only be in the way. I must not keep the horse waiting.”

“Oh! Mr. Croft, but one word more; we were just speaking of the assembly! Such a fine evening! Did you not think Miss Andrews looked particularly well?”

“Very well indeed,” came the reply, his voice now raised, as if he knew he had an audience. “I can say no less. And I think Miss Vance’s new gown was the prettiest in the room. And Mrs. Allen is the most gracious hostess in the county. Now, if my friends have any sense of justice, they will allow me to depart without making me blush any further.”

“Ah, he is gone! He can never abide a compliment. Well, (returning to the room,) I could not persuade him. Mr. Croft sends his regards, but he must get on to Sedleigh. He asked particularly after you, Sarah.”

“Yes,” said Sarah Andrews quietly, her hands now perfectly still in her lap, “we heard his kind inquiries. We heard everything.”

“Well, Miss Vance,” began Mr. Davenport, as soon as they were out of earshot of the cottage and walking at a brisk pace towards the gates of Vance Hall, “are you not now a complete convert to my theory? Have I not provided you with a key that turns in the lock most smoothly? You saw her face when he mentioned Weymouth; and again, when he spoke of Mr. Croft’s particular inquiries. It was a study in controlled delight.”

“I saw she was discomposed,” replied Miss Vance, feeling a greater degree of compunction than she had in the little room, now that the amusement of the moment had faded. “And I confess I feel we have been most impertinent in our observations. To sit and watch a person so, and to interpret every blush and every tune—it is hardly a generous employment.”

“Generous! My dear Miss Vance, what has generosity to do with it? It is a question of truth. I seek only to understand the little dramas that unfold before me. And where there is such determined secrecy, one is practically invited to conjecture. You saw the smile yourself; you said as much. That was no smile of mere gratitude for a neighbour’s civility. That was the smile of a woman who knows herself to be the true, though unacknowledged, object of all that fuss and ceremony.”

“And Mr. Croft himself,” continued Miss Vance, more to herself than to him, “he is not a man to make a fuss without reason. He is so very direct, so plain-spoken. For him to raise his voice so, to be so insistent in his questions... it was certainly marked.”

“Marked! It was a public declaration in all but name. He wished her to hear; he wished us all to hear. He was asserting a claim. He might as well have posted a notice on the church door. ‘How is Miss Fairfax?’—or Miss Andrews, I should say. Not ‘How is your party?’ or ‘How is Mrs. Allen?’ but a direct, possessive inquiry after the one person who occupies his thoughts. It is as clear as day. They have an understanding.”

They walked on in silence for a few paces, Miss Vance’s mind busy with this new, and to her, rather startling, arrangement of facts. Mr. Croft and Sarah Andrews! It had never occurred to her. Mr. Croft was so established, so much a fixture of their society, a sensible, wealthy bachelor whom all the neighbourhood looked up to. And Sarah Andrews was a visitor, a genteel but penniless dependant. The match was entirely suitable, to be sure, in point of character and, on his side, fortune; yet the secrecy of it struck her as peculiar.

“But if it is so,” she said at last, “why is it a secret? What reason could there be for such concealment? Mr. Croft is his own master. There is no one whose consent he needs to ask. It seems a very strange way to proceed.”

“Ah, now you come to the heart of the matter,” said Mr. Davenport, tapping his cane lightly on the path. “That is the very question that makes the whole affair so diverting. A straightforward courtship would be admirable, but frankly, Miss Vance, it would be dull. This has layers. Perhaps her friends, the Campbells—or their equivalent in this drama—disapprove. Perhaps they have a more brilliant match in mind for her. Or, what is more likely, perhaps there is some particularity on his side. An old promise to his mother, an aversion in his family to a bride without a dowry. These sober, sensible men are often bound by the most nonsensical private restraints. He means to have her, but he must bring his family round by degrees. Hence the clandestine nature of the gift, and the stolen glances, and the playing of ‘Robin Adair.’”

“It all seems very elaborate,” said Miss Vance, still not entirely convinced. “And I cannot quite picture Mr. Croft engaging in such a scheme. He is a man who likes to go to Kingston and come back from Kingston in the most direct manner possible.”

“You underestimate him,” Mr. Davenport returned with a laugh. “You see the landowner, the magistrate, the man who sends his neighbours apples. You do not see the man in love. Love, Miss Vance, makes schemers of us all. It turns the most direct road into a winding lane, full of delightful, and necessary, detours. He cannot openly send her a magnificent instrument, for that would declare his intentions to the world before he is ready. But he can arrange for it to appear, as if by magic, from a mysterious benefactor. He cannot sit by her side and speak soft nonsense, but he can create a situation in which his concern for her is broadcast to the entire room. It is a masterpiece of strategy, and I for one applaud him.”

Miss Vance could not help but smile at his enthusiasm, though her conscience was not quite easy. “You have it all worked out with the precision of a novel.”

“Life, Miss Vance, if you observe it closely, is a novel written by a rather disorganised author. It is our business as intelligent readers to supply the plot. And I am quite certain I have found it here. The only part that puzzles me is her behaviour at the assembly. You said she seemed tired and out of sorts. If she is so happily situated, why was that?”

“Perhaps she was simply tired,” offered Miss Vance.

“No, no, that is too simple. A woman in love is never simply tired. She was vexed, I imagine. Perhaps he had been called away and could not be there. Or perhaps he was there, and was obliged by this very secrecy to pay attention to someone else. Yes, that must be it. He was forced to dance with Miss Carteret, or talk to Mrs. Peterson for half an hour, and poor Miss Andrews had to watch it all and pretend not to care. It is a classic predicament. The suffering is an essential part of the pleasure.”

They had by now reached the gates of Vance Hall. Mr. Davenport paused, his hand on the latch. “Well, I leave you to your reflections. I am very much obliged to you for a most entertaining morning. We have unravelled a mystery, and I have gained a disciple.”

“I am not your disciple,” she said, shaking her head. “I am only a confused observer. I am half-inclined to believe you, and half-inclined to think we are two very foolish people, spinning tales out of nothing.”

“Time will tell,” he said with a confident smile. “But I will wager you my new copy of Cramer against a ribbon from the mercer’s that within a month, the engagement of Mr. Croft to Miss Andrews will be the talk of the neighbourhood. Good day, Miss Vance.”

He was gone, and she walked slowly up the drive, her mind in a whirl. It was plausible; it was all wonderfully plausible. Mr. Davenport’s theory accounted for everything: the pianoforte, the heightened emotions, Mr. Croft’s singular behaviour, the secret smiles. And yet, there was something in it that did not sit right with her. Her own sense of Mr. Croft’s character rebelled against the notion of him as a man of elaborate, romantic schemes. And her sense of Sarah Andrews—that quiet, composed, and dignified young woman—made her reluctant to believe her capable of the sustained deception Mr. Davenport described. Still, the evidence was strong. She had seen the blush, she had seen the smile. An amiable, upright, perfect Sarah Andrews cherishing a secret and possibly difficult attachment—it was a picture her mind could not now easily dismiss.

She was met in the hall by Mrs. Goddard, a sensible and kind-hearted widow who had been her governess and was now her companion.
“My dear, what a long visit! I trust you found Mrs. Allen and her mother well?”

“Perfectly well, and in high spirits,” replied Miss Vance, removing her bonnet. “They have had a new pianoforte delivered, and the whole neighbourhood, it seems, has been called in to admire it.”

“How very nice for Miss Andrews! It must be a great comfort to her. Was it a good instrument?”

“Excellent, I believe. And we had a most... a most eventful morning. Mr. Davenport was there. And Mr. Croft called, though he did not come in.”

“Mr. Croft!” said Mrs. Goddard, her face lighting up with simple approbation. “Now there is a good man. There is nothing I like so much as to see Mr. Croft on his horse. He has such a look of sense and integrity. I hope he is well?”

“He seemed very well. He was on his way to Kingston. He was most particular in his inquiries after Miss Andrews’s health.” Miss Vance could not resist uttering this last part, watching her companion’s face for a reaction.

Mrs. Goddard’s reaction was one of uncomplicated pleasure. “Was he indeed? Well, that is just like him. So thoughtful. He is always thinking of others. I have sometimes thought, my dear,” she added, lowering her voice a little, “that a connection in that quarter would be a most happy thing for Sarah Andrews. She is a charming girl, but her situation is a difficult one. To be so well settled, with a man of Mr. Croft’s worth and fortune—it would be everything her friends could wish for her.”

Miss Vance was struck by the simplicity of this view. To Mrs. Goddard, it was not a secret, intricate drama, but a desirable and straightforward social good. There was no talk of ‘reprehensible feelings’ or clandestine strategies. Hearing it put so plainly made her own and Mr. Davenport’s conjectures seem suddenly rather foolish and overwrought. She felt a blush of shame rise to her own cheeks.

“Indeed, it would be a very suitable match,” she said quietly, and turned the conversation to other things. But her mind was not at ease.

The following morning, having a small errand at the village stationer’s, Miss Vance decided to walk there herself. The air was fine, and she felt a need to be active, to walk off the lingering unease of the previous day’s speculations. She had just left the shop, a small parcel in her hand, when she saw a familiar figure on horseback approaching from the direction of Kingston. It was Mr. Croft. Her first instinct was to turn into the baker’s to avoid an encounter; she felt a sense of guilt, as if her impertinent thoughts must be written on her face. But it was too late; he had already seen her and was reining in his horse.

“Miss Vance,” he said, touching his hat. “A fine morning for a walk. I am glad to have met you.”

“Mr. Croft,” she replied, forcing a smile. “You are returned from Kingston already? You must have been very expeditious.”

“I had but one matter of business to attend to, and it was soon settled,” he said. He paused, and then continued in a frank, earnest manner that was entirely his own. “I wished to say, Miss Vance, that I fear I may have seemed somewhat abrupt yesterday. My call at Mrs. Allen’s was hasty, and my manner, perhaps, too loud. When one is calling from horseback through an open window, one is apt to forget one’s finer tones.”

“Not at all,” she stammered. “We all understood you were in a hurry.”

“I was particularly anxious about Miss Andrews,” he went on, his brow furrowed with a genuine and unembarrassed concern. “I had heard she was overtired from the assembly, and as I was in some part responsible for her being there, I felt a degree of concern for her health. She is a particular friend of my younger sister, Eleanor, from their school days in Bath. Indeed, they are as close as sisters themselves, and when Eleanor knows Miss Andrews is staying so near me, she charges me with looking after her welfare. I feel I stand in the place of a brother to her while she is here.”

Miss Vance stood frozen on the pavement. A friend of his sister’s. He stood in the place of a brother. A perfectly simple, perfectly honourable, and perfectly unromantic explanation for his entire conduct. She felt a wave of such profound mortification that she could not for a moment think of a single word to say. Every elaborate thread of Mr. Davenport’s tapestry had just been snipped in two.

Mr. Croft, happily oblivious to her internal state, continued. “In fact, it was a letter from Eleanor that took me to Kingston. It concerned that very pianoforte you were all admiring yesterday. My sister, knowing Miss Andrews would be quite lost without an instrument, was determined she should have one for the duration of her visit. She and her husband commissioned one from Broadwood’s in London, and charged me with arranging its delivery and settling the account. I was merely the agent in the business. Eleanor was most anxious that it should arrive as a surprise. I hope it gave Miss Andrews pleasure?”

“A great deal of pleasure,” Miss Vance managed to say, her voice sounding faint and distant to her own ears. “She was quite overcome. We all thought it... we all thought it an act of the most remarkable thoughtfulness.”

The mystery of the pianoforte, solved. The secret benefactor was not a lover, but a dear female friend. The whole romantic edifice she and Mr. Davenport had constructed with such glee had crumbled to dust in the space of two minutes, under the simple weight of the plain truth. The secret smiles, the blushes, the playing of telling tunes—it was all now recast in a new, and far more innocent, light. It was the pleasure of friendship, not the thrill of a clandestine love.

“Excellent,” said Mr. Croft, his face breaking into a warm smile. “Eleanor will be delighted to hear it. Well, I must not keep you, Miss Vance. I have business at home. I am glad to have had this chance to explain myself. Good day to you.”

“Good day, Mr. Croft,” she murmured.

He nodded, and with a touch of the reins, his horse moved on, leaving her standing on the pavement with her small parcel and a very great sense of her own foolishness. She thought of Mr. Davenport’s confident wager, of his ‘key to all her odd looks and ways,’ and could not decide whether she was more vexed with him for his wild imagination, or with herself for having been so easily and so willingly led astray by it.



She walked on, her steps considerably slower than when she had set out, her errand completed but her mind in a state of the most complete disarray. The small parcel, which she had carried with such a light and unthinking air, now seemed a dead weight in her hand, a foolish emblem of a foolish errand. How high her spirits had been but an hour ago; how entirely convinced of her own superior penetration and Mr. Davenport’s brilliant sagacity. And now, to be so completely, so thoroughly, so humiliatingly wrong. It was not merely that their theory had proved false; it was that the truth was so very simple, so grounded in wholesome, unimpeachable friendship, that it made their own elaborate conjectures appear not only mistaken, but positively ridiculous.

A brotherly duty. An instrument sent by a devoted female friend. All Mr. Croft’s attentions, his particular inquiries, his ride to Kingston, his very presence at the Bates’s lodgings—all were explained with an almost tedious propriety. She felt a blush of shame creep up her neck for her own thoughts. She had been on the verge of pitying Miss Andrews for a clandestine, and likely unhappy, attachment; and all the while, the poor girl had merely been the recipient of a great and thoughtful kindness from a friend as dear as a sister. That secret smile, which Emma had interpreted as the thrill of a forbidden love acknowledged, was doubtless nothing more than the innocent delight of a pleasant surprise, and a private amusement at the mystery it was causing. Reprehensible feelings indeed! The only reprehensible feelings had been her own; her prying, her amusement at another’s supposed distress, her unpardonable willingness to believe the worst.

And Mr. Davenport! For a moment, her vexation with herself was quite eclipsed by her vexation with him. It was he who had first planted the seed of the idea, who had nurtured it with his confident assertions and his playful, knowing looks. “I have now a key to all her odd looks and ways.” What a fool he had made of her! And yet, she could not entirely acquit herself. She had not only accepted the key, she had helped him to fashion it, and had been all too eager to turn it in the lock. She had wished for a romance, a secret, a drama to enliven the quiet society of Highbury, and had been prepared to invent one where none existed. Mr. Croft, whom they had cast as the clandestine lover, had emerged from the affair as the most rational, the most transparently honest of them all. His steady, sensible character, which she had sometimes found a little wanting in sparkle, now appeared in a far more admirable light. There was a solidity to him that Mr. Davenport, for all his wit and charm, utterly lacked.

By the time she reached the turning to Hartfield, she had resolved on one point: she would tell Mr. Davenport the truth at the very first opportunity, and she would take a certain satisfaction in observing his discomposure. He deserved to feel at least a fraction of the mortification that was now her own constant companion.

She need not have waited long. As she passed through the gates, she saw him, not on his way to Randalls, but pacing the gravel of her own drive, evidently awaiting her return with the most impatient curiosity. He saw her and hurried forward, his face alive with eagerness.

“At last!” he cried, taking her hand with a familiar freedom that she now found slightly irritating. “I thought you would never return. I have been imagining all sorts of triumphs. Did you get her alone? Did you press her? Did she confess all? You look a little grave. Do not tell me she denied it entirely; that would only be a further proof of its truth!”

“If you will allow me to speak, Mr. Davenport,” said she, withdrawing her hand and affecting a coolness she was far from feeling, “I will tell you what I have learnt. There is no need for any further conjecture on the subject of the pianoforte, or of Mr. Croft’s attachment to Miss Andrews. I have had the whole matter explained.”

“Explained! By whom? Not by her, surely! She would never be so weak.”

“No, not by her. By Mr. Croft himself. I met him on the road, not ten minutes since.”

Mr. Davenport’s expression of triumph faltered, replaced by one of keenest interest. “By Croft! And what account does he give of himself? Did he confess his passion? Is he seeking your assistance as a confidante? A capital development!”

“His account,” said Emma, taking a steadying breath, “was perfectly simple. The pianoforte was a gift not from him, but from his sister, Mrs. Hammond, who was a dear schoolfellow of Miss Andrews in Bath. He was merely the agent in the business, charged with arranging its delivery from Kingston. His inquiries after Miss Andrews’s health are on his sister’s behalf. He feels, as she is so far from her own family, that he stands in the place of a brother to her.”

She delivered the words with a deliberate, almost severe, plainness, and watched for their effect. For a moment, Mr. Davenport stood perfectly still. The lively intelligence in his eyes gave way to a brief, comical blankness, as if his mind, running so fast in one direction, had met with a sudden wall. Then, to her infinite astonishment and vexation, his face broke, not into a picture of chagrin, but into a broad, irrepressible smile, which quickly gave way to a peal of the most unfeigned laughter.

“A brother!” he exclaimed, once he could speak. “His sister! Oh, that is beyond anything. It is perfect. We are entirely, magnificently, gloriously wrong. I would not have missed this for the world. You must admit, Miss Woodhouse, it is the best joke imaginable.”

“I confess I am unable to see the humour, Mr. Davenport,” she replied, her colour rising. “I see only that we have both been guilty of a great deal of impertinent speculation, and have made ourselves exquisitely ridiculous.”

“Ridiculous? Not at all! We have been ingenious. Our theory was a masterpiece of construction; that it was built on air only adds to its credit. And as for Croft, to think of him as a brother! It is a role for which he is uniquely suited. All sense and no sensibility. It explains everything even better than our own idea did. But the comedy of it! To have you go to all that trouble, and to be met with such a piece of dull, prosaic truth! I do envy you that moment. What was your face like? I would have given worlds to see it.”

His amusement was genuine, and for that she almost disliked him; her own humiliation was too fresh, too stinging, to be so lightly dismissed. It was not to be borne, that he should find such cause for mirth in what had occasioned her so much embarrassment.

“My face, I imagine, showed the mortification I felt,” she said stiffly. “A feeling of which you seem to be entirely destitute. I for one am resolved not to let my imagination run away with me in such a manner again.”

“Oh, you must not be so severe on yourself,” he said, his laughter subsiding into a look of handsome persuasion. “Where is the harm? We have amused ourselves. And we are the only ones who know how foolish we have been; the secret is ours. And now we must find a new one. Miss Andrews is clearly not the romantic heroine we supposed. But perhaps there is another. What of Miss Smith? Has she no secrets for us to uncover?”

Emma looked at him, and for the first time, she saw his charm not as a delightful enhancement to his character, but as a glittering cloak for a want of real feeling. He did not care that they had misjudged Mr. Croft, or that they had been prepared to laugh at Miss Andrews. The entire affair was, to him, a game, and now that this round was over, he was merely eager to begin another. The whisper she had shared with him in Mrs. Bates’s parlour, “You speak too plain,” now seemed to echo in her mind with a new meaning. It was not his words that had been too plain, but his character.

“I have no appetite for any more such games, Mr. Davenport,” she said, turning towards the house. “I find I have had quite enough of conjecturing for one day. I believe my father will be expecting me.”

She left him there, still smiling, though with a look of some surprise, and went into the house with a sense of disquiet that the simple discovery of the truth could not entirely account for. She had lost her amusement, but she had also, she suspected, lost a little of her good opinion of Mr. Frank Churchill.

The matter might have rested there, a private folly between herself and Mr. Davenport, had not her father, a few days later, decided he was well enough to host a small dinner party. Mr. Woodhouse could not bear large gatherings, but a party of six or eight, with his particular friends, was a pleasure he could sometimes be persuaded to enjoy, provided the weather was mild and the evening did not go on too long. Mrs. Weston was of course indispensable, and Mr. Knightley. To these were added Miss Bates and Miss Fairfax, as a civility on account of the new instrument, and Mr. Frank Churchill, who was so charming a favourite with her father that no party felt complete without him. Emma had at first hesitated over the inclusion of Jane Fairfax, feeling a new and awkward shyness in her company, but Mr. Knightley himself had suggested it, remarking that a little society would do her good, and Emma could not, in politeness, refuse.

The evening began pleasantly enough. Mr. Churchill was in his most agreeable spirits, dividing his attention charmingly between Mr. Woodhouse, whose fears about draughts and unwholesome dishes he treated with the tenderest solicitude, and Mrs. Weston, with whom he spoke of his father with the most becoming filial affection. Emma watched him, and could not deny his charm; yet she found herself viewing it with a new and critical eye. It was all a performance, she thought; a very skillful and pleasing performance, but a performance nonetheless.

Her eye travelled to Mr. Knightley, who was seated on her other side, engaged in a quiet, sensible conversation with Jane Fairfax. They were speaking, she gathered, of Weymouth. Not of dances or flirtations, as she and Mr. Churchill might have done, but of the healthfulness of the sea air and the respective merits of the different libraries. It was not a conversation of any great brilliance, but it was marked by a comfortable sincerity on both sides. Jane Fairfax, in this quiet discourse, seemed less reserved, less of an enigma. She spoke with an intelligent animation that Emma had rarely seen in her, and looked up at Mr. Knightley with an expression of simple, open gratitude when he inquired particularly after a mutual acquaintance of whom she had been fond. There was no blush, no secret smile; only the easy communication of two rational people. How could she have ever mistaken it for anything else?

Dinner was announced, and in the move to the dining-parlour, Frank Churchill found a moment to speak to her privately.

“Our party is almost complete,” he whispered, with a glance towards the others. “We have the mysterious heroine, the false hero—that is myself, I suppose—and the man of sense. If only Mr. Croft were here, we might play the whole comedy out for the entertainment of the company.”

“I believe Mr. Knightley has taken the role of the man of sense this evening,” Emma replied coolly. “And as for heroes, I am quite out of patience with them. They are generally more trouble than they are worth.”

He laughed. “You are severe this evening! Is it my punishment for being so amused at our mistake? You must forgive me. I promise to be more discerning in future. In fact, I have a new theory already, about Miss Bates this time. I am convinced she has a secret sorrow. Did you observe the way she looked at the last of the asparagus?”

Emma could not help a small smile, but she shook her head. “No, Mr. Churchill. I will not be drawn in again. I shall confine my observations to what is plain and declared.”

“How dull!” he sighed, as they took their seats. “You are robbing Highbury of its chief source of entertainment.”

During dinner, Emma made a point of observing Jane Fairfax, not with the prying curiosity of before, but with a genuine desire to understand her. She saw a young woman of elegance and intelligence, whose habitual reserve seemed less a matter of concealment than of a natural quietness of spirit, exacerbated, perhaps, by her dependent situation. She answered when spoken to with great propriety, and though she initiated little conversation herself, she listened to others with an attentive politeness that was in itself a compliment. When Mr. Woodhouse expressed a fear that she had been sitting in a draught at the pianoforte earlier, she reassured him with a gentleness that was very winning. Emma felt a pang of compunction. She had been so busy imagining Jane Fairfax’s faults, she had quite overlooked her virtues.

After dinner, the ladies having withdrawn to the drawing-room, it was inevitable that music should be proposed. Mr. Churchill, with his usual alacrity, went to open the pianoforte.

“Miss Fairfax,” he said, with a bow, “you must do us the honour. After Mr. Knightley’s rapturous accounts of your playing—he has spoken of nothing else—we are all impatience to hear you.”

This was a complete fabrication, as Mr. Knightley had said no more than that Miss Fairfax played very well, but it served its purpose of putting everyone at their ease, and drawing a faint smile from Jane herself. She went to the instrument without any fuss, and after a moment’s thought, began to play a series of lively Scottish airs. The music was brilliant, her touch exquisitely light and firm. Everyone was charmed.

Mr. Knightley had followed them from the dining-room, and now stood leaning against the mantelpiece, listening. His countenance, always serious in repose, was softened with pleasure. Emma, watching him, found her thoughts drawn again to the contrast between the two men. Mr. Churchill hovered near the pianoforte, turning the pages for Miss Fairfax, making little complimentary remarks, his whole person a study in graceful attention. Mr. Knightley, on the other hand, stood apart, offering no such particular address, yet his quiet, steady appreciation seemed to her a truer and more valuable tribute.

When she had finished, and been warmly thanked by all, Frank Churchill picked up a piece of sheet music from the stand. It was a new Italian love song.

“Oh, you must play this, Miss Fairfax,” he urged. “It is full of the most exquisite passion. It requires a performer who can do justice to its sentiment. I am sure you would play it beautifully. Do you know it?”

Jane Fairfax looked at the music, and a slight flush, a genuine blush of what looked like discomposure, rose to her cheeks. “I… I do not think it would suit me, sir. It is a little too… demonstrative.”

“Nonsense!” he cried playfully. “We must persuade you. Miss Woodhouse, will you not add your voice to mine? Compel Miss Fairfax to give us this treat.”

Before Emma could answer, Mr. Knightley spoke from the fireplace. His voice was perfectly mild, but it cut through Mr. Churchill’s charming insistence with an air of quiet authority.

“If Miss Fairfax does not wish to play it, Churchill, you ought not to press her. Perhaps she would prefer something else. I for one should be very happy to hear that piece by Cramer you played the other day, Miss Fairfax. It was an excellent piece, and you played it with admirable taste.”

There was a moment’s silence. Frank Churchill looked from Mr. Knightley to Jane Fairfax, a flicker of surprise in his eyes at this gentle check. Jane looked at Mr. Knightley with an expression of such profound and unmistakable relief that it struck Emma to the heart. It was the look of someone who has been rescued. She immediately sat down and played the Cramer, and as the calm, intricate melody filled the room, Emma felt a new warmth towards Mr. Knightley, a deep and satisfying approval that was worth more than all the amusement Mr. Churchill had ever afforded her. He had not been clever, or witty; he had simply been kind. He had seen a person in a moment of discomfort, and had, without any fuss or show, stepped in to relieve her.

The rest of the evening passed quietly. Mr. Churchill, perhaps a little chastened, devoted himself to a game of backgammon with Mr. Woodhouse. Emma found herself in conversation with Mr. Knightley. They spoke of the poor, of the business of the parish, of a new book of essays he had just finished reading. His mind was clear, his opinions decided, his judgment sound. She found that listening to him was not only pleasant, but improving. There was no artifice in him, no desire to dazzle or to perform. He was simply himself.

When the guests took their leave, and she was alone with her father, she felt a sense of peace she had not known for some weeks. The little world of Highbury seemed to have settled back on its foundations. The pianoforte was an instrument, Miss Fairfax was a gentlewoman, and Mr. Croft was a respectable man of property. There were no secrets, no intrigues. And in this sudden clarity, she saw two things with a certainty that admitted of no doubt. The first was that Mr. Knightley was the truest gentleman of her acquaintance. The second was that Mr. Frank Churchill, for all his charms, had a great deal yet to learn. The key he had offered her had been to a house of cards; she now felt, for the first time, that she might be beginning to understand the architecture of a truer edifice.

The reflections of the succeeding morning were of a character very different from those which had lately occupied Emma’s mind. The amusement she had derived from Mr. Churchill’s supposed attachment, and the key which she imagined he had given her to the mysteries of Jane Fairfax’s conduct, had vanished entirely; and in their place was a sentiment of self-reproach, mingled with a degree of admiration for Mr. Knightley so warm and so novel as to be almost disconcerting. She had been diverted by cruelty; she had mistaken impertinence for wit; she had been a willing accomplice in a scheme of vexation aimed at a person whose circumstances ought to have commanded her pity, if not her friendship. What a fool she had been! To have found sport in such unfeeling persecution, and to have believed Mr. Frank Churchill’s diagnosis of the case, when the true malady was so clearly his own want of principle.

He had simply been kind. The words echoed in her thoughts. Mr. Knightley, whom she had so often thought too plain, too scolding, too little occupied with the elegant trifles that gave society its gloss, had, in one quiet gesture, shewn more true elegance of feeling than Mr. Churchill had displayed in a whole month of charming attentions. He had perceived distress, and had sought only to alleviate it, without a thought for his own consequence, or for the cleverness of the thing. It was the action of a gentleman; not the performance of one. This distinction, which had never before occurred to her with such force, now seemed the most important in the world.

She was so absorbed in these new and improved reflections that she was quite startled when Mr. Frank Churchill himself was announced. He entered with all his usual grace and animation, though she fancied she could detect, under his smile, a shade of inquiry, a desire to ascertain his present standing at Hartfield.

“Miss Woodhouse! I come as a penitent,” he began, taking a seat near her work-table. “I fear I was a sad torment to your friend yesterday. Mrs. Weston has been reading me a lecture all the way from the Bates’s door to Randalls, and I am come to throw myself on your mercy. Am I forgiven?”

“As the offence was not against me, Mr. Churchill, it is not for me to forgive,” replied Emma, with a gravity that was not entirely feigned. “I believe the person you ought to petition is Miss Fairfax.”

“Ah, but your good opinion is what I chiefly value. If you are displeased, I am wretched. Tell me I was a brute, a monster, but tell me you do not think me so past redemption that I may not hope for your smiles again.”

He said it with such a charming air of mock despair that the Emma of a week ago would have been delighted. The present Emma, however, saw only the artifice. He was not sorry for having distressed Jane Fairfax; he was sorry for having potentially displeased Emma Woodhouse.

“I think you are too fond of a joke, sir,” she said, her needle moving rather faster than before. “And sometimes you do not consider enough how your jokes may land with others. Miss Fairfax’s situation is one of some delicacy; she has not the robust spirits of, perhaps, you or I.”

“Delicacy!—yes, that is the very word. She is all delicacy. A perfect model of it. So much so, that I begin to think her nerves are a little too finely strung for this world. Knightley seemed to think so too. Did you observe how he flew to the rescue? Our very own Highbury champion, defending distressed damsels. It was admirably done; though perhaps a little too solemn for the occasion. He looked as if he were pronouncing a sentence in a court of law.”

Emma’s colour rose. She did not like this turn at all. To praise Mr. Knightley with a sneer was worse than to censure him outright.

“Mr. Knightley was acting, I believe, from a simple impulse of compassion,” she said, with more warmth than she had intended. “It is a motive which, I am sure, you can understand, even if you might have expressed it differently.”

“Oh, I understand it perfectly! And I admire it; I assure you, I do. Knightley is the most upright man I know. One could set one’s watch by his principles. I only meant that he, perhaps, lacks a certain lightness of touch. But then, we cannot all be frivolous butterflies like myself; the world needs its solid, dependable oaks, does it not?”

He looked at her, expecting, she knew, a colluding smile. He did not receive one. She was silent, her eyes fixed on her work, and in that silence he might have read, if he had been as perceptive as he was charming, the complete failure of his attempt to recover his ground by diminishing another. There was a short, and for him, an awkward pause.

He tried a new subject. “And the pianoforte! What a magnificent instrument. Colonel Campbell must be the most generous of men. Did you not think there was a particular meaning in Miss Fairfax’s eye when she looked up after playing that Weymouth waltz? Ah, but you did not see it. It was a look of pure, transcendent happiness. My conjectures were not so very wide of the mark, after all.”

“I saw her look up at you with a blush of what seemed to me to be extreme discomposure,” Emma returned coolly. “And I saw her look at Mr. Knightley with an expression of pure gratitude. As to any other looks, I am no longer so fond of conjecturing as I was. It is a hazardous employment, and I have lately found myself to be very unskilled at it.”

This was plain enough, and Mr. Churchill could not mistake it. He saw that the game was up; that his confederate had deserted him. A flash of genuine vexation, so quick that it was instantly gone, crossed his features. He rose to his feet.

“You are severe this morning, Miss Woodhouse. I see I must do a great deal of penance before I am restored. I shall go and walk for an hour in the shrubbery and contemplate my faults. Perhaps by the time I return to Randalls, Mrs. Weston will have thought of some suitable punishment for me.” He bowed, attempted a last winning smile, and departed.

Emma was left with a feeling of profound relief. She had been tried, and she had not been found wanting. She had defended Mr. Knightley, and she had refused to join in teasing Jane Fairfax. She felt she had taken a step towards becoming the woman Mr. Knightley had always wished her to be. This thought was so gratifying that it occupied her until a visit from Mrs. Weston was announced, who, it seemed, had seen her son-in-law depart, and had come, as Emma suspected, to plead his cause.

“My dear Emma,” she began, as soon as they were seated, “I have just had a word with Frank. He is quite miserable. He feels he has offended you dreadfully.”

“He has not offended me, Mrs. Weston. He has only… disappointed me a little.”

“But it is his way! He has such high spirits, he means no real harm. You must not be too hard on him. He lives in a world, with the Churchills, where that sort of playful banter is the common currency. He forgets that our quiet little corner of Surrey is not so accustomed to it.”

“It was not the banter I objected to, so much as its object. And his persistence. There is a point where playfulness becomes persecution. I am only sorry I did not see it sooner. Mr. Knightley saw it at once.”

At the mention of Mr. Knightley, Mrs. Weston’s expression changed from apology to a gentle curiosity. “Ah, yes. Mr. Knightley. Frank said he was quite the hero of the hour. It was very good of him, to be sure. He is always so very correct in his judgments.”

There was an inflection in Mrs. Weston’s voice, a slight emphasis on the word ‘correct,’ that recalled Mr. Churchill’s own manner of speaking. It was clear that he had already been presenting his own version of the story.

“He was more than correct, he was kind,” said Emma, with decision. “And I shall be very sorry, for my own sake, if I ever again fail to distinguish between true kindness and its imitation.”

Mrs. Weston looked at her with affectionate concern. “My dear, you are out of humour with Frank, and it is making you unjust. You are comparing a young man of three-and-twenty, who has been indulged all his life, with a man of Mr. Knightley’s age and established character. It is hardly a fair comparison.”

“Perhaps not,” said Emma. “But I cannot help making it.”

She could see she had not convinced her friend; Mrs. Weston’s partiality for Frank Churchill was too strong. But Emma felt a quiet satisfaction in her own steadfastness. They talked of other things, and soon afterwards, a note was brought in from Donwell. It was from Mr. Knightley.

He wrote to say that his strawberries were now perfectly ripe, and that if the ladies of Hartfield, and their friends from Randalls, would do him the honour of coming to Donwell the next day to eat them, he should be extremely happy. He proposed a morning visit, an informal affair; they could walk about the grounds, and see the house, and eat strawberries and cream in his cool north dining-room.

The invitation was hailed with pleasure by all. A visit to Donwell was always a treat. It was the oldest and most respected estate in the neighbourhood, and Mr. Knightley, though he made no show, was a perfect host. Emma felt a particular pleasure in the prospect. She wished to see Mr. Knightley in his own home, surrounded by the evidence of his respectable, useful life. She wanted to reaffirm the justice of her new opinions.

The next day was as fine as could be wished for. The party from Hartfield and Randalls, which included of course Mr. Frank Churchill, set out to walk the short distance to Donwell. Mr. Churchill, appearing to have adopted a new strategy of quiet attentiveness, attached himself to Mrs. Weston, leaving Emma to walk with her father and Mr. Weston. Emma was quite content with this arrangement. She was in no mood for any more of Mr. Churchill’s charming performances.

They found Mr. Knightley waiting to receive them on the lawn. He greeted them with his usual unaffected hospitality. Miss Bates and Jane Fairfax were already there, having been conveyed in Mr. Knightley’s own carriage; a piece of kindness which Miss Bates was already relating at great length to anyone who would listen.

“So very good of him!—I said to Jane, now, we must not be troublesome, the walk is nothing at all, but he would insist! ‘Miss Bates,’ he said, ‘the dust on the road is not fit for ladies’ shoes.’ So very thoughtful! And the strawberries! Did you ever see such a size? William Larkins says he has never known such a crop...”

The party was soon dispersed about the extensive grounds, some admiring the gardens, others strolling in the shade of the ancient lime-walk. Mr. Knightley intended it to be an exploration at everyone’s liberty, but he found himself, almost by necessity, acting as a guide to Emma and her father. He was particularly attentive to Mr. Woodhouse, finding him the most sheltered seat, and assuring him that there was no risk whatever of a draught.

“You have a beautiful place, Mr. Knightley,” said Emma, looking around her with sincere admiration. The Abbey itself, a large, handsome, stone building, spoke of age and solidity. There was nothing fashionable or modern about it, but it had a dignity and a comfort that was far more impressive. It looked, Emma thought, exactly like its owner: sound, honest, and entirely without pretension.

“It has served the Knightleys well for many generations,” he replied, with a look of quiet affection at the house. “I do my best not to disgrace their memory. It is a great responsibility.” He led them towards the famous strawberry beds, which were indeed a picture of abundance. While Mr. Woodhouse sat on a bench nearby, enjoying the sun, Emma and Mr. Knightley walked between the rows.

“I was very glad of your invitation,” she said, after a moment. “I wished for an opportunity to… to tell you that I thought you were very right the other evening. At Mrs. Bates’s.”

He looked at her, a little surprised by her earnestness. “Right? About what? About Churchill pressing Miss Fairfax to play?”

“Yes. You were not only right, you were very kind. I was ashamed of myself for having been amused by it. I have been thinking about it a great deal since.”

A smile of genuine pleasure softened his features. “Have you? Well, then, the incident was not without its uses. It is not like you, Emma, to be unfeeling. I was sorry to see you led astray by Churchill’s nonsense.”

“He has a way of making everything seem like a joke. But I see now that some things ought not to be joked about.”

“He is a very fortunate young man,” said Mr. Knightley, his tone becoming more serious. “He has been given every advantage; wealth, indulgence, a charming manner. The only thing he has never been taught, I suspect, is to think of others. He finds the world a stage for his own amusement. I hope, for his own sake, he learns a better lesson before it is too late.”

They were interrupted by the approach of Frank Churchill himself, with Jane Fairfax on his arm. It was an arrangement Emma was surprised to see; she had thought Miss Fairfax would avoid him. She looked pale, but composed.

“Knightley, we are all admiration!” Mr. Churchill called out. “Such strawberries! And such a domain! You are the envy of the county. Miss Fairfax and I have been exploring the lime-walk, and were just saying how very romantic it is. It puts one in mind of old stories and secret vows.”

He looked from Mr. Knightley to Emma with a challenging brightness in his eyes. Jane Fairfax said nothing, but quietly disengaged her arm from his.

“It has seen a few generations of lovers, I dare say,” replied Mr. Knightley drily. “Though the Knightleys have generally been too busy with their accounts to be very romantic. Miss Fairfax, you are looking tired. Will you not come and rest with Mr. Woodhouse? There is a glass of my housekeeper’s famous lemonade waiting for you.”

He spoke to her with a gentleness that was almost paternal. Jane gave him a small, grateful smile and went to join Mr. Woodhouse on the bench. Emma felt another pang of admiration for him. He was always thinking of the comfort of others.

Mr. Churchill watched her go, a strange expression on his face. “Your friend is in a very singular state of health,” he said to Emma in a lower voice. “One moment she seems on the verge of collapse, the next she is perfectly composed. It is all very mysterious. She would not say a word to me the entire length of the walk.”

“Perhaps she had nothing to say,” replied Emma, turning to pick a particularly fine strawberry.

“Or perhaps she is saving all her words for a correspondent in Ireland,” he murmured, so only she could hear. “What do you conjecture now, Miss Woodhouse?”

“I conjecture, sir, that you are incorrigible,” she said, and walked away to join the others, leaving him standing alone by the strawberry beds.

The party soon assembled in the dining-room for refreshments. The room was large and cool, with long windows looking out onto a smooth, green lawn. The promised strawberries and cream were delicious, and Mr. Woodhouse himself pronounced the lemonade to be remarkably well-made and wholesome. The whole atmosphere was one of easy, unpretentious comfort. Emma found herself seated next to Mr. Knightley, and they fell into a conversation that was, to her, the most interesting part of the day. He spoke of his plans for the estate, of the families who depended on it, of his duties as a magistrate. He did not speak to impress her, but simply as one rational creature to another. She found his mind to be a constant source of sound sense and clear-sighted integrity.

While they were talking, her eyes happened to fall upon Jane Fairfax. She was sitting a little apart, by the window, and Frank Churchill had just brought her a glass of water. He leaned down and said something to her, his voice too low to be overheard. Emma could not see his expression, but she saw Jane’s. She looked up at him, and for a single, unguarded instant, her face was filled with a look of such agonized pleading, such desperate appeal, that it was like a glimpse into a private tragedy. It was there and gone in a moment. She dropped her eyes, took the glass with a murmured word of thanks, and was once more the pale, composed Miss Fairfax. But Emma had seen it. And it was a look that had nothing to do with Colonel Campbell, or Ireland, or Mr. Dixon. It was a look directed at, and caused by, Mr. Frank Churchill.

The key he had given her was not merely wrong; it was a deliberate deception. The edifice she was beginning to understand was far truer, but also infinitely more complex and alarming, than she had ever imagined. There was a secret, a most unhappy secret, between Jane Fairfax and Frank Churchill. And Mr. Knightley, in his simple kindness, and she, in her foolish vanity, had been playing with fire, all unaware of the true conflagration burning just beneath the surface. A feeling of dread, cold and sharp, pierced through the warm satisfaction of the day. She looked at Mr. Knightley, so honest and so unsuspecting, and felt a sudden, fierce desire to protect him from the network of deceit that seemed to be closing in on their little world. The day at Donwell, which had begun in clarity and approbation, was ending in a shadow of deep and bewildering mystery.

The walk to Hartfield gates was not long, but to Emma it seemed a journey of a most wearisome and perplexing length. The party had divided quite naturally; Miss Bates, full of her unsuccessful attempt to detain Mr. Knightley and of the shocking generosity of his apples, had taken her niece’s arm and set off towards their lodgings with a flurry of good-byes and promises of a speedy visit. This left Mrs. Weston and Frank Churchill to accompany Emma and Harriet, a circumstance that, an hour before, would have promised nothing but pleasant, easy chat. Now, it was a situation fraught with a new and unwelcome tension, a tension of which Emma felt herself to be the sole guardian and, in a way, the sole victim.

Mrs. Weston, in the happy blindness of her affection, saw nothing amiss. “Was it not the most perfect day, Emma?” she began, as they stepped out from the shade of Donwell’s great oaks. “I have seldom passed a more agreeable afternoon. Mr. Knightley’s arrangements are always so good; every thing so well-judged. And dear Frank was in such spirits! He enjoys Donwell almost as much as he does Randalls, I believe.”

“I am glad you were so well pleased, Mrs. Weston,” replied Emma, her voice maintaining a careful, civil evenness. She could not bring herself to look at Frank Churchill, who walked on her other side, but she was acutely sensible of his presence, of the very turn of his head and the light, confident step that now seemed to her to bespeak an intolerable arrogance.

“Pleased! I was delighted. And it was a joy to see Jane Fairfax so much more herself. The pianoforte has been a true tonic. You were very right, Frank,” she continued, turning to her stepson, “in supposing that a little music was all she needed. You seemed to have a particular intuition about it.”

Emma’s heart gave a painful throb. An intuition indeed! An intuition born of the most intimate, secret knowledge. She risked a sideways glance at him. He was smiling, that same easy, charming smile that had always captivated Highbury. To her eyes, it now looked like a mask, expertly worn.

“One does what one can, my dear madam,” he said, with a slight bow to his stepmother. “Miss Fairfax’s friends must all be happy to see her spirits restored. We were all a little concerned, were we not, Miss Woodhouse?” He addressed her directly, his tone pitched in that familiar key of shared confidence and playful conspiracy.

It was a test; she knew it was a test. He was probing the extent of her suspicions, the very suspicions he himself had so artfully planted and nurtured. To answer with any thing but the most placid agreement would be to betray her new understanding. And yet, to agree, to play along with the vile deception, felt like a betrayal of a different kind—a betrayal of Jane Fairfax, of Mr. Knightley, of truth itself.

“Miss Fairfax’s health is a matter of interest to all her acquaintance,” she said, the words feeling stiff and cold in her mouth. She immediately turned to Harriet. “I hope the walk is not too much for you, Harriet. You have been sitting for a long time.”

Harriet, startled and pleased to be so addressed, assured her that she was not in the least tired, and was in fact enjoying the evening air very much. It was a simple, honest reply, and Emma felt a rush of warmth for her friend’s uncomplicated nature.

Frank Churchill was not so easily diverted. He fell back a pace, allowing Mrs. Weston and Harriet to walk on ahead, creating an island of privacy for himself and Emma. “Miss Woodhouse is in a philosophical mood to-day,” he murmured, his voice lowered. “Have my poor attempts at conversation failed to meet the high standards of Hartfield? Or has Mr. Knightley’s sound sense driven out all taste for nonsense?”

The mention of Mr. Knightley, of his sound sense, struck Emma with peculiar force. How right he had been! From the very beginning, Mr. Knightley’s judgment of Frank Churchill had been unwavering and, as she now saw, unerringly accurate. He had disliked him from instinct, from a deep-seated integrity that could not be deceived by superficial charm. And she, in her folly, had argued with him, had defended this man, had even presumed to think Mr. Knightley jealous. The shame of it was acute.

“Mr. Knightley’s sense is always sound,” she said, her voice quiet but firm. “One cannot be in his company for five minutes without feeling the benefit of it.”

This was not the reply he had expected. He looked at her, his brow faintly furrowed with puzzlement. “Indeed? A handsome tribute. I had not imagined you to be so devoted a pupil. I thought you were more inclined to dispute his edicts than to praise them.”

“Then you have imagined a great deal, Mr. Churchill, and to very little purpose,” she replied. The sharpness of her own tone surprised her, but she did not regret it. A line had been drawn, and she was glad to be on the opposite side of it from him.

They walked the rest of the way to the Hartfield gates in a silence that was more expressive than any words. Mrs. Weston chattered on, pointing out the beauty of the twilight and making plans for a future gathering, her voice a cheerful counterpoint to the unspoken drama unfolding behind her. Frank Churchill made one or two more attempts at light conversation, but Emma’s monosyllabic replies offered him no purchase, and he at last desisted, lapsing into a thoughtful quiet that she suspected was more vexation than contemplation.

At the gates, the party finally stopped. “Well, my loves,” said Mrs. Weston, turning to embrace Emma. “We must leave you here. Thank you for a share of your delightful day. Frank, we must not be late.”

“I shall be with you in a moment,” he said. He turned to Emma, and under the guise of a formal farewell, his eyes searched hers. “Miss Woodhouse,” he said, his voice low again. “I seem to have been so unfortunate as to displease you. If I have said or done any thing to give offence, I beg you will tell me. The approbation of my friends at Hartfield is too valuable to me to be lost for want of an explanation.”

It was masterfully done. The humble speech, the look of earnest concern—it was the very picture of misunderstood sincerity. A part of her, the part that had been so easily flattered and deceived for so long, felt a momentary waver. But then she remembered the look on Jane Fairfax’s face—the raw, naked anguish—and her resolve hardened into a cold, polished shield. This man was not sincere; he was an actor, and a very fine one. He was not asking for forgiveness; he was seeking information. He wanted to know what she knew.

“I am not displeased, sir,” she said, with a cool civility that was more damning than any accusation. “Only a little fatigued. It has been a long day, with a great many impressions. I am sure a quiet evening will restore me entirely.” She offered him a small, dismissive smile and turned away to open the gate for Harriet, a clear signal that the conversation was at an end.

She did not look back as he and Mrs. Weston walked away towards Randalls, but she felt his gaze on her, and knew that she had left him in a state of profound and, she hoped, deeply uncomfortable perplexity. She had not exposed him, but she had withdrawn her sanction, and for a man of his vanity, the withdrawal of admiration was perhaps the keenest punishment of all.

As soon as she was within the sanctuary of her own room, the composure she had so painstakingly maintained fell away. She sank into a chair, her mind a tumult of anger, shame, and a dawning, terrible pity. He had used her. From his very first arrival, he had singled her out, flattered her, entertained her, made her believe she was his particular object—and it had all been a lie. A screen. A convenient distraction to hide his true, secret, and undoubtedly improper engagement to Jane Fairfax.

She went over it all in her mind, every incident, every conversation, now viewed through this new, dreadful lens. His eagerness to hear her theories about the pianoforte—what a cruel game! His tormenting of Jane at the Crown ball, his constant, pointed allusions to Mr. Dixon—it was not mere mischief; it was the calculated cruelty of a man trying to maintain power over a woman he was secretly bound to, a woman whose position was so precarious and whose feelings were so utterly at his mercy. And that look—that look of agonized pleading—what had he been saying to her at that moment? Was he threatening her? Reminding her of their secret? Commanding her to maintain her composure? The possibilities were all equally sickening.

And Jane Fairfax! Poor, poor Jane. Emma’s cheeks burned with shame as she recalled her own conduct. All her petty jealousies, her suspicions, her coldness towards a woman who was not a coolly perfect rival, but a fellow creature in a state of almost unbearable suffering. To be secretly engaged, with no prospect of the union being acknowledged; to have to receive attentions from others that she could not want; to have to endure the public flirtations of her intended with another woman, and that woman herself; and worst of all, to have to bear his presence, his daily, casual cruelties, all while maintaining a front of placid elegance. It was a species of torture. The constant headache, the variable spirits, the desire for solitude—it was all explained. It was not the climate of Highbury that disagreed with Jane Fairfax; it was the presence of Frank Churchill.

A profound sense of her own foolishness washed over her. She, Emma Woodhouse, who prided herself on her understanding of people, on her perception and her cleverness, had been the most gullible, the most thoroughly duped person in the entire village. Even Harriet, in her simple-heartedness, had a truer compass. Mr. Knightley… ah, Mr. Knightley. He had seen the truth from the first. Not the whole truth, not the secret engagement—that was a profligacy of deceit beyond even his imagining—but he had seen the man’s character. “A light, trivial, selfish man,” he had called him. He had warned her. He had tried to guide her. And she had laughed at him, had accused him of being prejudiced and unfair.

She paced her room, her agitation too great for stillness. The edifice of her own vanity had been shaken to its foundations. Frank Churchill had not been in love with her; he had simply found her a convenient and amusing tool for his deception. The idea, which would once have been a mortal blow to her pride, was now almost a relief. To have been the object of such a man’s affection would have been a disgrace. To have been merely his dupe was a humiliation she could, and must, overcome.

Her primary feeling, as the first storm of emotion began to subside, was a cold, quiet anger. An anger on behalf of Jane Fairfax, for her suffering. An anger on behalf of Mr. Knightley, for his wisdom being scorned. And an anger on behalf of herself, for being so thoroughly played for a fool. There was a secret, as she had so cleverly conjectured, but it was a squalid, unhappy secret, not the romantic mystery she had woven.

What was to be done? She could not speak. To betray her suspicions now would be to expose Jane Fairfax to a public humiliation far worse than any thing she was currently enduring. It would be a catastrophic breach of confidence, made all the worse because she had no proof, only the evidence of a single, fleeting expression. No, she could not speak. But she could watch. She could be kind. She could, in her own way, try to protect Jane Fairfax from further pain. She would call on her the very next day. She would swallow her pride, ignore Miss Bates’s effusions, and offer Jane a genuine, unfeigned friendship. It was the least she could do. It was a penance, of a sort.

And Mr. Knightley? What would she say to him? The thought of her next conversation with him filled her with a mixture of eagerness and dread. She longed for his counsel, for the simple, clarifying force of his good sense. Yet, to seek it would mean confessing the full extent of her own folly, her own blindness. It would mean admitting, “You were right. I was wrong. About every thing.”

It was a humbling prospect. But as she stood by her window, looking out into the gathering dark towards the distant woods of Donwell Abbey, she knew it was a confession she must be prepared to make. The day at Donwell, which had promised so much simple pleasure, had ended by teaching her a lesson of the most profound and painful complexity. The world was not the simple, malleable place she had imagined it to be, and she, Emma Woodhouse, had a great deal yet to learn. The shadow of mystery had been replaced by the far more sobering light of a partial, and very alarming, truth. And in that new, uncertain light, the steady, unwavering integrity of Mr. Knightley seemed the only fixed point in her entire world.

The morning after her unsettling epiphany found Emma resolved, though not entirely composed; for it is one thing to determine upon a course of right action in the solitude of one’s own chamber, and quite another to execute it under the watchful eyes of the world, and particularly under the eye of the very person one has injured. She dressed with unusual care, not for vanity’s sake, but as an armour against the scrutiny she must face. To call upon the Bateses so soon after the gathering at Donwell might seem particular, but it was a particularity she was determined to embrace. She would go alone, without the buffer of Harriet’s gentle incomprehension or Mrs. Weston’s more discerning gaze. This was a penance, and penances are best performed in solitude.

She found Miss Bates in the midst of a flurry of domestic activity, occasioned, it seemed, by the simple fact that it was Tuesday. “Miss Woodhouse! So very kind! And on foot too! I declare, I was just saying to mother, ‘I wonder if any of our friends will call to-day,’ and she said, ‘My dear, it is only to be expected, with the pianoforte creating such a stir.’ And here you are! The very first. Jane, my dear, Jane, here is Miss Woodhouse come to call upon us. Do put down your needlework; your eyes will be quite spoiled. Not that she ever complains, Miss Woodhouse; she is the most patient creature. But I say her eyes must ache, for she has been mending my shawl, which caught on a bramble yesterday. So very good of her.”

Jane Fairfax did look up, and if her countenance was pale, it was also perfectly composed. She offered a slight, cool bow, her hands remaining quietly in her lap. There was no blush, no sign of the secret delight Emma had witnessed; there was only a profound weariness, a stillness that seemed almost unnatural.

“I was hoping to hear the instrument again,” said Emma, her voice sounding a little too bright, a little too forced, even to her own ears. “Its tone is so very superior. You must be in raptures, Miss Fairfax.”

“It is a very fine instrument, certainly,” Jane replied, her voice low and even. “My debt to Colonel Campbell is greater than I can express.”

“Ah! Colonel Campbell!” cried Miss Bates. “Such a friend! Jane has been writing him the longest letter, a letter of three sheets, to express her gratitude. I said to her, ‘My dear, the post will charge you double!’ but gratitude, you know, Miss Woodhouse, gratitude cannot be weighed in shillings and pence. And such a thoughtful man, to send the music too. All the newest pieces. Jane was just looking at a sonata by Clementi. Or was it Cramer? I am so forgetful. But so very thoughtful.”

Emma felt the conversation veering dangerously close to the subject of Frank Churchill’s pointed remarks. She saw a flicker of something in Jane’s eyes; a tightening of the muscles around her mouth. Emma hastened to change the subject. “The weather is so fine, I thought a walk might be pleasant. Perhaps, Miss Fairfax, when you are feeling stronger, you would allow me to accompany you on a short walk? The air might do you good.”

The offer was met with a moment of pure silence. Miss Bates’s jaw fell slightly ajar. Jane Fairfax looked at Emma with an expression of such frank astonishment that it was almost comical. It was the look one might give to a sworn enemy who had just proffered a bouquet of flowers.

“You are very kind, Miss Woodhouse,” Jane said at last, and the coolness in her tone had not abated one degree. “I am afraid I am not a great walker at present. My physician recommends quiet and rest.”

“Of course,” said Emma, her cheeks warming with the sting of the gentle but firm rebuff. “I should not have suggested it. Rest is certainly of the first importance.”

“So very true!” chimed in Miss Bates, rushing to smooth over the awkwardness. “Rest is what she needs. Though I tell her a little society is a good thing too, and we are so very grateful to our friends for calling. Mr. Knightley, you know, is always so particular in his inquiries. ‘How is Miss Fairfax?’ he says. Always, ‘How is Miss Fairfax?’ He has the kindest heart. He was so concerned after the party at the Coles’. And again, after your ball, Mrs. Weston. He has a great opinion of Jane’s health, you see. Almost as great as my own!”

At the mention of Mr. Knightley’s name, Emma felt a fresh wave of discomposure. To hear him praised for the very perception she had lacked was a trial she had not anticipated. Jane, however, remained impassive, her gaze fixed upon the folded shawl in her lap. It was impossible to know if Miss Bates’s prattling gave her pleasure or pain.

Emma felt her mission of kindness was failing spectacularly. Her presence seemed only to drive Jane further into her shell. She was searching for a new, neutral topic, a task as difficult as finding a smooth stone on a gravel path, when the sound of a firm, familiar tread upon the stair saved her the trouble. The door opened, and Mr. Knightley himself stood before them.

His entrance caused a general start. Miss Bates gave a little cry of pleasure. Emma felt her heart leap into her throat. Jane Fairfax, for the first time, looked visibly flustered, a faint colour rising in her pale cheeks before receding just as quickly.

“Mr. Knightley! I declare! We were just this moment speaking of you,” exclaimed Miss Bates. “What a delightful coincidence! Do come in. Mother will be so pleased. We have Miss Woodhouse with us, you see. A room full of friends!”

Mr. Knightley bowed, his eyes taking in the scene with their usual quick, comprehensive glance. He looked from Miss Bates’s beaming face, to Jane’s renewed composure, and finally to Emma. His expression, when his gaze met hers, was unreadable; it was neither warm nor cold, merely observant.

“I was on my way to Kingston,” he said, addressing Miss Bates but looking at the room in general, “and wished to inquire after your niece. I saw Dr. Perry’s carriage leaving this part of the street not an hour ago, and was concerned.”

“Oh! you are too good, Mr. Knightley. So very considerate,” said Miss Bates. “Yes, Mr. Perry did call. Just a little check, you know. Jane has not been quite herself. A little tired, a little headache. He prescribes rest and asses’ milk. Very strengthening, I am told, though I cannot say I fancy the idea myself. But Jane is a good patient.”

“I am sorry to hear you are unwell, Miss Fairfax,” Mr. Knightley said, and his voice, when he spoke to her, softened with a genuine, unfeigned concern that made Emma’s own clumsy attempts at kindness seem like a mockery.

“It is nothing, sir. A passing indisposition,” Jane replied quietly, not quite meeting his eye.

Emma sat in miserable silence. The very air in the small parlour seemed thick with unspoken things. Mr. Knightley’s presence had changed the entire dynamic of the room. He had brought with him an atmosphere of sense and gravity that made the undercurrents of deception and hidden feeling all the more palpable. She watched him, standing there in his riding boots, his manner so direct and unaffected, and felt the full weight of her own folly. He was everything that was honest and true, and she had spent weeks arguing with him, certain of her own superior insight, while he had been quietly perceiving the truth.

He turned his attention to her. “I did not expect to find you here, Miss Woodhouse.”

“I came to inquire after Miss Fairfax,” she said, the simple truth sounding like a falsehood even to herself.

“Indeed,” was all he said. The word was neutral, but in her current state of heightened self-reproach, Emma heard in it a world of meaning: *Indeed. You, who have been so determined to dislike and misunderstand her, are suddenly full of solicitude. How very curious.*

She could not bear it. The visit, which was meant to be an act of atonement, had become an instrument of torture. She rose to her feet. “I must not keep my father waiting. He will be expecting me for our walk.” This was a complete fabrication; Mr. Woodhouse had not taken a walk of any significant length in a decade, but it was the first excuse that came to mind.

“So soon!” cried Miss Bates. “But you have only just arrived! Mr. Knightley, you must persuade her to stay. We could have a little music. Jane, my dear, perhaps one little tune?”

“No,” said Jane, with a quiet finality. “I am not able to play to-day.”

“Then I must take my leave as well,” said Mr. Knightley, surprising them all. “My business in Kingston will not wait. Miss Woodhouse, if you are walking towards Hartfield, perhaps you will allow me to see you safely to your gate.”

Emma’s heart gave a sickening lurch. This was it. The moment of reckoning she had both dreaded and desired. To refuse was impossible; it would be an act of rudeness so pointed as to be unforgivable. To accept was to submit herself to the judgement she knew she deserved.

“You are very good,” she murmured, unable to look at him.

The farewells were a blur of Miss Bates’s effusive thanks and regrets. Emma barely registered them. She was aware only of Mr. Knightley standing by the door, waiting for her, his presence a solid, imposing fact. She exchanged a final, formal nod with Jane Fairfax, whose expression remained a mask of polite indifference. As she stepped out of the little parlour and into the hallway, she felt as though she were escaping a prison, only to walk directly into the arms of her executioner.

They walked for some minutes in a silence that was more eloquent than any words. The Highbury street was quiet at this hour of the morning. The sun shone, the birds sang, the world went on in its ordinary way, entirely indifferent to the turmoil in Emma’s heart. She kept her eyes fixed on the path before her, counting her steps, trying to arrange her thoughts into some semblance of order.

It was Mr. Knightley who spoke first. “You seem to have taken a sudden interest in Jane Fairfax’s welfare.”

His tone was not accusatory; it was simply a statement of fact, but it cut her to the quick.

“Is it so very sudden?” she asked, trying for a lightness she did not feel.

“I should have called it so,” he replied evenly. “At Donwell, you could scarcely bring yourself to speak to her. This morning, you are making solicitous calls and proposing companionable walks. The change is... marked.”

There was no escape. She must face it. She took a deep breath, the fresh air doing little to calm the frantic beating of her heart. “You are right,” she said, her voice barely a whisper. “It is a marked change.”

He did not immediately reply, allowing her a moment to continue. When she did not, he prompted her gently. “And to what do we owe this marked change, Emma?”

She stopped walking and turned to face him. They were near the edge of the town, with the open fields leading towards Hartfield stretching before them. It was as good a place as any for a confession.

“We owe it to my being a fool,” she said, the words rushing out in a torrent of shame. “A conceited, arrogant, blind fool. You were right about every thing. Every single thing you said. And I was wrong.”

She looked at him, her eyes bright with unshed tears, expecting to see triumph, or at the very least, the cool satisfaction of a man proven correct. Instead, his expression was one of deep, almost pained, concern.

“About what, specifically, were you wrong, Emma?” he asked, his voice losing its edge of irony and taking on a softer, more serious note.

“About Jane Fairfax. About Mr. Frank Churchill. About the whole miserable business. I constructed a narrative, a fantasy, and I believed in it so completely that I could not see the truth, even when it was standing right in front of me, playing the pianoforte.” The last part came out with a bitter little laugh.

Mr. Knightley’s brow furrowed. “I am not sure I follow you completely. What truth about the pianoforte?”

Here was the difficulty. She could confess her own folly, but she could not, would not, betray Jane. “It is not my secret to tell,” she said, shaking her head. “Let us just say that my conjectures, which I was so proud of, were not only incorrect, they were the very opposite of the truth. And my behaviour, based on those conjectures, has been... reprehensible.” She used the word she had thought to herself the night before, and it felt right.

They began to walk again, more slowly now.

“Reprehensible is a strong word,” Mr. Knightley said, after a moment of thought. “Ill-judged, perhaps. Unkind, certainly, at times. But your desire to call upon her this morning, your offer of a walk, clumsy though it may have been... that does not seem reprehensible to me. It seems like an attempt to atone.”

His understanding, his refusal to condemn her when she was so ready to condemn herself, was almost her undoing. A single tear escaped and rolled down her cheek. She hastily brushed it away.

“An attempt that was a complete failure,” she said. “She hates me. And she has every right to. I have made her life more difficult, not less. All my cleverness, all my management... it has been nothing but meddling and mischief.”

“And you regret it?” he asked, his eyes searching her face.

“I have never regretted any thing more in my life,” she said, with a sincerity that was absolute. “To have caused pain to someone already in distress... To have been so wrapped up in my own vanity that I could not see her suffering... And to have argued with you, to have dismissed your judgment when you were only trying to guide me towards the truth... I am so ashamed.”

They had reached the little path that led across the fields to the Hartfield shrubbery. He stopped at the stile.

“Emma,” he said, and his voice was so full of an emotion she could not quite name that she was forced to look at him. “There is no shame in admitting an error. The shame is in persisting in it. You have seen your mistake, and you are trying, however imperfectly, to make amends. That is all that any one of us can do.” He paused, and then added, “I have been... harsh with you on this subject. Perhaps too harsh. My concern for Jane Fairfax’s situation may have made me unjust to you.”

“No,” she said, shaking her head vehemently. “No, you were not unjust. You were honest. It is a quality I have not valued as I ought.”

A small, wry smile touched his lips. “Well. Perhaps we have both learned something, then.” He held out his hand. “Do not be too hard on yourself. Your heart is in the right place, even if your imagination sometimes leads you astray.”

She placed her hand in his, and the warmth of his grip was steadying. It felt like an anchor in the turbulent sea of her emotions. “Thank you, Mr. Knightley,” she said softly. “Thank you for your forbearance.”

“There is nothing to thank me for,” he said, releasing her hand, though his eyes lingered on her face. “Now, you had best go in. Your father will be wondering where you are.”

She smiled, a genuine smile for the first time that day. “My father, who you know perfectly well has not taken a walk with me in years.”

He laughed, a real, unreserved laugh that eased the last of the tension between them. “Go on with you,” he said, his good humour restored. “And Emma... be patient with Jane Fairfax. Her situation, whatever it may be, is clearly a difficult one. Your friendship, if it is sincere, will be a balm to her in time. But you cannot force it.”

“I understand,” she said. And for the first time, she truly did.

As she walked up the path to Hartfield, she did not look back, but she was acutely aware of him, standing by the stile, watching her go. The conversation had been every bit as humbling as she had feared, but it had also been liberating. She had confessed her folly, and she had not been met with scorn, but with a kindness and understanding that was more precious to her than any praise. The world still felt uncertain, and the truth about Jane Fairfax and Frank Churchill was a shadow that had not been fully dispelled. But in the clear, steady light of Mr. Knightley’s approbation, Emma felt she could finally begin to find her way. Her penance was far from over; it was only just beginning. But it no longer felt like a punishment. It felt like a path.

Emma entered the hall at Hartfield, her spirits in a state of such peculiar composure as she had not known for many weeks; a sense of duty performed, however painful, had settled over the agitation of the morning, and the quiet approbation in Mr. Knightley’s eyes seemed to her a far greater reward than all the clever triumphs she had ever contrived. She was met not by her father, but by James, with a message that Mr. Woodhouse was resting in his chair and hoped she had enjoyed her short walk. A very short walk indeed it had been called; Emma smiled to herself at the thought of what a very long and significant journey it had in fact been.

“I shall go to him presently, James. Has anyone called?”

“Miss Smith is in the drawing room, Miss Woodhouse. She arrived not a quarter of an hour ago, in the hopes of finding you at home.”

Harriet. Of all the people she might have wished to see at that moment, Harriet was perhaps the very last. Her little friend was a living monument to her own folly, the chief victim, next to herself, of her ill-conceived schemes and arrogant imagination. A fresh wave of shame washed over her, but it was quickly followed by a new and firmer resolve. If her penance was a path, Harriet was surely the first and most necessary step upon it. She could not undo the harm she had done, but she could, and she must, cease to do more.

She found Harriet seated by the fire, looking over a book of prints with an air of polite but vacant interest. She started up with her usual affectionate eagerness upon Emma’s entrance.

“Oh, Miss Woodhouse! I was so afraid I had missed you. I hoped a little walk might do me good, and my path, you know, so often seems to lead to Hartfield.”

“I am very glad it does, Harriet,” Emma said, taking her hand with a warmth that was entirely sincere. “It is always a pleasure to see you. I was detained a little longer than I expected.”

“Oh, I am sure you were on some kind errand. You are always so busy and so good to everyone.”

Emma’s conscience gave a painful twinge. “Not always so good as I ought to be, I am afraid. But come, tell me what you have been doing with yourself since we last met. Did you enjoy the evening at Mrs. Bates’s?”

The question was a test of her own strength; to speak of that gathering without betraying a hint of her former suspicions, without some arch look or knowing tone, would require a command of her features she was not certain she possessed.

“Oh, yes, very much! That is, for the most part. The music was divine, was it not? To have such an instrument arrive! It is a fairytale. Miss Fairfax is so very accomplished. I felt quite foolish, even thinking of my own poor attempts at the pianoforte afterwards.”

“There is no need to feel foolish, Harriet. We cannot all be masters of the art, and Miss Fairfax has had advantages of education that are very rare.” This was a safe, and true, observation. Emma felt a little more at her ease.

“And Mr. Frank Churchill,” Harriet continued, her cheeks taking on a little colour, “he is so very agreeable! So full of spirits and wit. He was trying to fix Mrs. Bates’s spectacles, you know, and talking such delightful nonsense all the while. He and you, Miss Woodhouse, seem to be of one mind on so many things.”

“Do we?” said Emma, a little drily. “I am not sure we are so very alike. Mr. Churchill’s spirits are rather more unflagging than my own.”

“Oh, but you both have such a clever way of saying things! And he was so attentive to you. Everyone must have remarked it.” Harriet looked at her with an expression of innocent inquiry, waiting, as she always did, to be told what she ought to think. The old Emma would have seized upon this, played with it, and perhaps woven it into some new fantasy for her own amusement. The new Emma, however, was silent. She merely smiled and adjusted a cushion.

Harriet, finding no encouragement in that direction, fell into a brief reverie, from which she was roused by a new thought. “Mr. Knightley was there, too,” she said, her voice dropping a little. “At the window, I mean. It was so very odd. He seemed quite... determined.”

“He is a determined man,” said Emma, her own heart beating a little faster. “He wished to inquire after Miss Fairfax’s health, which was very good of him.”

“Oh, yes, very good. He is always so very good, is he not, Miss Woodhouse?” Harriet’s eyes were fixed on her work-basket, which she was needlessly rearranging. “He sent the apples, you know. All of them. Miss Bates was so shocked, and so grateful. It is just the sort of thing he would do; thinking of others before himself, with no thought for thanks.”

Emma could not but agree. There was a sincerity and a warmth in Harriet’s praise of Mr. Knightley that was both touching and, to Emma’s newly awakened sensibilities, a little alarming. Was it possible? Could Harriet, her fancy once so unfortunately fixed upon Mr. Elton, have now transferred her innocent admiration to a man so infinitely his superior? The idea was so dreadful, so fraught with the potential for misery and mortification, that Emma felt a chill. It would be a calamity beyond all her previous blunders. Mr. Knightley! A man whose judgement she now valued above all others, whose good opinion was her most cherished possession. To have him implicated, even in Harriet’s harmless daydreams, felt like a profanation.

Her first, instinctive reaction was to put an end to it at once; to say something sharp and decisive, to point out the absurdity, the impossibility, of such a notion. But the memory of Mr. Knightley’s own words, his parting injunction to be patient and to do no more harm, held her back. Had she not already meddled enough in Harriet’s affections? Her interference had brought nothing but pain. Perhaps the kindest, the most truly moral thing she could do now, was nothing at all. She must be a silent observer, a passive friend, and trust that Harriet’s fancy, if it existed, would fade of its own accord for want of encouragement. It was a new and difficult role for her to play.

“Yes,” Emma said at last, her voice very steady. “Mr. Knightley is the best of men. There is no one quite like him.” She offered the statement as a simple truth, devoid of any of the little inflections or leading glances that had once been her stock in trade. She would not encourage, but she could not, in good conscience, disagree.

This simple confirmation seemed to be all that Harriet required. She gave a little sigh of contentment and returned to the subject of Frank Churchill’s waltzing and Miss Woodhouse’s unparalleled skill as a partner. Emma allowed the conversation to drift into these safer channels, contributing only what was necessary, and listening with an attentiveness that was in itself a form of penance. The visit, which might once have been a source of amusement and power, was now an exercise in self-restraint. When Harriet finally rose to leave, full of thanks and affection, Emma felt as though she had passed a significant trial. She had not been clever, she had not been entertaining, and she had not managed anyone. She had simply been a friend, and it was a profound relief.

The rest of the day passed quietly. Emma sat with her father, read to him, listened to his gentle complaints about the draughts, and felt a tenderness for him that was unmixed with impatience. Her mind was calmer than it had been for an age. The world, which had seemed a stage for her own clever performances, had resolved itself into a set of duties, small and large. Her duty to her father, her duty to Harriet, and her new, unformed duty to Jane Fairfax.

On this last point, she lingered long. What could she do? A visit, as Mr. Knightley had intimated, might seem intrusive, an attempt to force a confidence that Jane was unwilling to give. To suddenly shower her with attentions after a period of comparative neglect would be awkward and perhaps even insulting. It must be something small, something natural.

The next morning, an idea presented itself. While looking through her own collection of music, searching for a piece her father particularly liked, she came across a set of sonatas she had mastered long ago and now rarely played. They were not new, but they were elegant and of a pleasing difficulty, just the sort of thing a true musician would appreciate. She remembered Jane Fairfax mentioning the composer’s name with admiration some months before. An opportunity.

She sat down at her writing desk, not to compose a flowery note of contrived feeling, but a simple message.

“My dear Miss Fairfax,” she wrote, and then paused, considering her words with a care she usually reserved for the most important of correspondences. “In sorting through some old music, I came upon these pieces and was reminded of a conversation we had some time ago. As you are without your own collection for the present, I thought they might perhaps afford you a little amusement. If you have no use for them, pray do not feel any obligation to keep them. With best wishes for your health, Yours very sincerely, Emma Woodhouse.”

It was plain, it was unadorned, and it was honest. She sealed it, and had James deliver the parcel to Mrs. Bates’s cottage at once, with instructions to simply leave it and not to wait for an answer. The act was done. It was a small, almost insignificant gesture, but it was a beginning. It was a single stone laid on the new path.

She had expected to feel a sense of self-congratulation, the satisfying glow of a good deed done. Instead, she felt nothing but a quiet sense of rightness, as if she had merely balanced a long-unsettled account. She was surprised, later in the afternoon, by a visit from Mrs. Weston. Her former governess arrived alone, Mr. Weston having been called away on business, and her countenance wore an expression of fond and gentle concern.

“My dear Emma! I could not stay away another day. I wanted to see how you were. We were all so amused yesterday at the Bates’s, but I confess, I thought you seemed a little out of spirits towards the end.”

“Did I?” said Emma, attempting a light tone. “I was merely tired, I expect. A room so full of company and conversation can be draining.”

“I was not at all distressed,” Emma replied, truthfully. “Only amused, and a little concerned that he was distressing Miss Fairfax.”

Emma’s curiosity, that old, familiar impulse, stirred within her. “A theory?”

“He has taken it into his head,” Mrs. Weston said, lowering her voice conspiratorially, “that the pianoforte is a gift not from Colonel Campbell at all, but from Mr. Knightley.”

Emma stared at her, utterly dumbfounded. “Mr. Knightley! What on earth would make him think so? It is the most absurd, the most ridiculous notion I have ever heard.”

Mr. Knightley’s known benevolence; his particular friendship with the Bateses, which goes far beyond mere neighbourly civility; his constant, though quiet, solicitude for Jane’s welfare. “He is wrong,” Emma said, with a force that startled Mrs. Weston. “Frank Churchill is completely and utterly wrong. It is an invention, a piece of mischief. Mr. Knightley is not in love with Jane Fairfax.”

“My dear, I am sure you are right,” said Mrs. Weston, soothingly, taken aback by Emma’s vehemence. “It did seem a little far-fetched to me as well. Frank is so romantic. He sees plots and secret attachments everywhere. It is only a bit of fun for him.”

But Emma could not so easily dismiss it. A key to all her odd looks and ways. Mr. Knightley’s own words came back to her. He had been speaking of Jane, but what if the key unlocked a different door altogether? His concern, his anger at Emma’s carelessness, his constant defence of Jane—could it all be explained by this one, terrible, unifying principle? No. It could not. She would not allow it. It was an odious, an insupportable idea.

“Mr. Knightley views Jane Fairfax as a child, almost,” Emma declared, trying to convince herself as much as Mrs. Weston. “He has known her forever. He feels responsible for her, as he does for us all. It is the kindness of an older brother, or an uncle. Nothing more. To suggest otherwise is a discredit to his character and to hers.”

“Well, when you put it like that, it does seem much more likely,” Mrs. Weston conceded. “I shall tell Frank that you have thoroughly dismantled his theory. He will be disappointed to give up his little drama.” She paused, then added, “The reason he has so much time for such speculation, I am afraid, is that his stay has been extended. Mrs. Churchill has had another of her ill turns, and he cannot possibly leave her. He will be with us for at least another fortnight.”

Another fortnight. The news, which a few days ago would have been a cause for celebration, now landed with a thud. Another fortnight of Frank Churchill’s teasing, his games, his unsettling proximity to Jane Fairfax, and his wild, dangerous conjectures. Another fortnight of navigating the treacherous currents of Highbury society with her new, fragile resolutions.

As Mrs. Weston took her leave, Emma was left with a sense of profound unease. She had begun the day on a clear path, guided by the steady light of Mr. Knightley’s approbation. Now, a fog of doubt and suspicion had rolled in, obscuring the way forward. And at the heart of the fog was an image she could not dispel: Mr. Knightley, her Mr. Knightley, secretly in love with Jane Fairfax. It was a nonsense, a fantasy, a product of Frank Churchill’s idle mischief. It must be. And yet, the thought, once planted, refused to be uprooted. It remained, a small, cold stone in her heart, a chilling counterpoint to the quiet satisfaction she had felt only hours before. Her penance, it seemed, was to be more complicated than she had ever imagined.

The very notion was a poison, and it worked its way through her system with a speed that defied all her efforts at reason. That Mr. Knightley, the standard by which she measured all men of sense and principle, could be engaged in a secret courtship, a clandestine attachment of the heart, was an affront to the established order of her world. It was as if Donwell Abbey itself had decided to shift its foundations, or the church spire at Highbury to lean at a precarious angle. All was rendered unstable, untrustworthy.

The invitation to dine at the Coles’, which arrived the very next morning, might ordinarily have been received as a welcome diversion; but to Emma, in her present state of vexed contemplation, it appeared less a pleasure than a trial, an arena in which her newfound suspicions were to be put to a most public and painful test. For she knew, with a sinking certainty, that all the principal parties to this imagined drama would be present. The Coles were too fond of a full and lively table to omit any one of consequence. Mr. Knightley would be there; Jane Fairfax, as a novelty and an object of communal pity, was a certainty; and Frank Churchill, the very author of her misery, would attend with Mrs. Weston, his presence now a source of dread rather than delight.

She dressed for the evening with a feeling of arming herself for battle. Her chief weapon was to be a determined cheerfulness, a mien of such untroubled amusement as to convince not only the world, but herself, that Frank Churchill’s theory was the most arrant nonsense. She would observe, yes; but she would observe with the detached curiosity of a naturalist examining a common beetle, not with the feverish anxiety of one searching for a venomous snake in her own drawing-room.

Upon entering the Coles’ well-appointed parlour, her resolutions were immediately assaulted. The party was indeed assembled, and the very arrangement of the figures seemed a conspiracy against her peace. There was Jane Fairfax, seated near the fire, looking pale and elegant as ever, engaged in quiet conversation with Mrs. Weston. And there, standing before the mantelpiece, his back to the fire in that characteristic manner of his, was Mr. Knightley. He was not speaking to Jane Fairfax; he was, in fact, listening to some long account from Mrs. Cole. But his position commanded a view of the entire room, and Emma could not help but fancy that his gaze rested a moment longer than necessary upon the corner where Miss Fairfax sat.

Frank Churchill was at her side almost at once. “Ah, Miss Woodhouse, you see the tableau I described. Is it not a picture of domestic harmony, albeit one yet to be framed and hung for public view?”

“It is a picture of a gentleman warming himself at a fire, and a lady recovering from a cold,” Emma retorted, with a brightness she did not feel. “I see you have not given up your trade of novel-writing.”

“Never, when I have such compelling characters. Observe his expression. All sense and seriousness. He is weighing some matter of great import. The state of the nation? The price of wheat? Or perhaps the precise degree of draught coming from that window, and whether it poses a threat to a delicate constitution?” He directed a glance so pointedly towards Jane Fairfax that Emma felt her cheeks grow warm on Miss Fairfax’s behalf.

“You are incorrigible,” she said, turning away from him and moving towards her hostess.

Dinner was announced, and the seating arrangements provided fresh material for her disquiet. By some unhappy chance, or perhaps by the artful design of Mrs. Cole, Mr. Knightley was placed on the opposite side of the table, and directly across from Jane Fairfax. Emma was seated between Mr. Cole and a gentleman from a neighbouring village, too far to engage Mr. Knightley in conversation, but perfectly positioned to watch him. And watch him she did.

She saw him press upon Miss Fairfax a serving of the tenderest part of the boiled turkey; she saw him insist that the servant pour her a glass of wine and water, remarking, in a voice that carried just far enough to reach Emma’s ears, “You exerted yourself too much yesterday, Miss Fairfax. You must allow your friends to take care of you.” It was precisely the sort of avuncular kindness he might show to anyone; to Emma herself, even. Yet, filtered through the lens of Frank Churchill’s suggestion, it acquired a different colour, a shade of particular, solicitous intimacy that made her feel a pang of something she could not name.

Jane received his attentions with her usual constrained gratitude, a slight inclination of her head, a murmur of thanks. But did Emma imagine it, or was the blush that rose to her pale cheek something more than simple acknowledgment? Was it not the same blush of consciousness, of secret delight, that she had witnessed at the pianoforte?

“Our friend Mr. Knightley is in a very pastoral mood to-night,” whispered Frank Churchill, who had contrived to be seated next to Mrs. Weston, and thus within easy speaking distance of Emma’s other side. “One might almost imagine he sees a stray lamb in need of protection from the wolves.” He smiled, and his eyes indicated himself as the chief wolf in question.

Emma made no reply. She could not. Her throat felt tight, and the expertly prepared food on her plate might as well have been sawdust. She forced herself to attend to Mr. Cole, to inquire after his greenhouses and to praise his wine, all the while her senses were stretched across the table, straining to catch every word and gesture that passed between Mr. Knightley and Jane Fairfax. He spoke to her of Ireland, of Colonel and Mrs. Campbell, with an air of genuine interest. He even made some light, teasing remark which caused her to smile; a true, unguarded smile for a moment, before her customary reserve descended once more. And that smile, so rare and so lovely, struck Emma’s heart with the force of a physical blow. She had never made Jane Fairfax smile like that. She had only ever made her uncomfortable.

The evening wore on, and when the ladies had retired to the drawing-room, leaving the gentlemen to their port, Emma felt a brief respite. But it was only the calm before a renewed assault. When the men rejoined them, Mr. Knightley, instead of seeking out his usual sparring partner in Emma, walked directly over to the little sofa where Jane Fairfax had been settled by Mrs. Weston. He did not sit, but stood leaning against a nearby bookcase, and resumed his conversation with her in low tones.

Frank Churchill, ever the master of ceremonies, soon approached the pianoforte. “Mrs. Cole, we are all dying to hear your new instrument put through its paces again. And who better to oblige us than its new mistress? Miss Fairfax, will you not grant us the felicity of another tune?”

Jane looked cornered, and glanced, almost appealingly, at Mr. Knightley. It was he who answered for her. “I think Miss Fairfax has played enough for one week, Churchill. She is not so robust as some of you dancers.” The rebuke was gentle, but unmistakable.

“Oh, but I am sure she will not deny us one short piece,” Frank persisted, his eyes dancing with mischief. “Perhaps not a waltz, if it brings back tiring memories. Something tranquil. Something soothing. _Robin Adair_, perhaps? I am told it is a favourite.”

The cruelty of it was breathtaking. Emma saw the colour drain from Jane’s face, and then return in a painful, mottled rush. She looked at Frank Churchill with a flash of real anger. He had gone too far. This was no longer an amusement; it was persecution. She was about to intervene, to say something, anything, to create a diversion, when Mr. Knightley stepped forward.

“My dear Miss Fairfax,” he said, his voice calm and steady, ignoring Frank Churchill completely, “I was just telling you of the collection of Scottish airs my brother sent me from London. They are very fine, and I believe you would appreciate the arrangements. If you are ever at leisure, and would care to look them over, I should be very happy to send the book over to you.”

It was a masterful stroke. He had offered her a refuge, a topic of conversation that had nothing to do with pianofortes or favourite tunes, and had done so with a kindness that was both public and pointedly directed away from her tormentor. Jane Fairfax looked up at him, and her eyes were filled with a gratitude so profound, so luminous, that Emma felt as if the world had tilted on its axis. In that one look, she saw not just thankfulness, but a depth of understanding, of perfect and complete communion, that she had never imagined could exist between them.

The moment was broken by Miss Bates, who, having been holding forth to Mrs. Cole about the excellence of the custards, suddenly became aware of the little drama. “Scottish airs! Oh, Mr. Knightley, how very thoughtful of you! Jane is so fond of Scottish music. You remember, Jane, that little tune Colonel Campbell used to whistle? No, I believe it was Irish. But Scottish is just as lovely, I am sure. Such a very kind offer! I dare say you will have the book worn out in a week, will not she? She has so little to amuse her, you know, and with the pianoforte being so very new and perhaps not quite settled...”

The spell was shattered. The conversation became general again. But Emma could not shake the image of that look from her mind. It was the key. It was the proof. Frank Churchill had not invented a fiction; he had merely perceived a truth that had been hidden from her in plain sight.

The rest of the evening passed in a blur. Emma answered when spoken to, smiled when a smile was required, but her inner life was a maelstrom of confusion and a misery so new and sharp it took her breath away. She felt a chill that had nothing to do with the temperature of the room. When the time came for the carriages to be called, she was relieved.

Mr. Knightley assisted Mrs. Weston and her charge into their carriage first. Then he turned to the Hartfield party. He handed Emma in with his usual careful attention, but she could not meet his eye. As he was about to close the door, he paused.

“Are you well, Emma? You have been very quiet this evening.”

His voice was full of its customary concern, the concern she had always taken for granted, had always prized as her exclusive right. Now it sounded like a death knell.

“Perfectly well, I thank you,” she said, her voice brittle. “Only a little tired. The room was rather warm.”

It was a foolish, flimsy excuse, and she knew it. He looked at her for a second longer, a questioning, almost hurt expression in his eyes. He seemed about to say something more, but her father began a fretful complaint about the night air, and with a slight bow, Mr. Knightley closed the door.

The short journey to Hartfield was made in silence, for Mr. Woodhouse soon dozed off, and Emma was lost in a sea of wretched thoughts. The cold stone in her heart, which had been but a small pebble of unease that morning, had grown to a great, heavy weight, pressing the very life from her. Every event of the evening, every word, every glance, replayed itself in her memory, arranging and rearranging itself into a single, undeniable pattern.

His constant defence of her. His anxiety over her health. His anger at Frank Churchill’s careless flirtations. His quiet, intense attention when she played or spoke. It was not the kindness of an uncle. It was not the concern of a brother. It was, as Frank Churchill had so cruelly and so accurately surmised, the conduct of a man in love. A man who had given his heart, not to the woman who had known him and valued him her entire life, but to a pale, reserved, accomplished newcomer.

Back in the solitude of her own room, she sank into a chair, not even bothering to ring for her maid. She did not weep. The shock was too deep for tears. It was a cold, hollow ache of loss. She felt as if she had been robbed of a great treasure, a treasure she had not even known she possessed until the moment she saw it being given to another. All his reproofs, all his arguments, all his lectures—which she had so often resented—now appeared to her as precious tokens of an affection she had utterly failed to comprehend. He had argued with her because he cared for her. He had corrected her because he saw her as a part of himself, a part of his own moral landscape. And she, in her vanity and folly, had taken it all for granted.

She had been so busy arranging the lives of others, so confident in her own perceptions, that she had been completely blind to the most important truth of her own life. Mr. Knightley belonged to her. Or rather, she had always felt that she belonged to him, in some fundamental way that transcended family or friendship. And now he belonged to Jane Fairfax.

And the most bitter part of it all was that she could not even find it in her heart to blame him. What was there in herself to compete with Jane Fairfax? Emma was spoiled, headstrong, and often foolish. Jane was elegant, accomplished, and bore her misfortunes with a quiet dignity that Mr. Knightley, of all people, would be certain to admire. He had chosen excellence over vanity. He had chosen substance over sparkle. It was a choice entirely consistent with his character. It was a choice that was, in its own way, perfect.

A new resolution began to form in her mind, born of the despairing clarity of her newfound misery. Her penance could no longer be a simple matter of being kinder to Miss Bates. A far greater sacrifice was required now. If Mr. Knightley loved Jane Fairfax, and if, as she suspected from that luminous look of gratitude, Jane Fairfax returned his affection, then it was not for her to stand in their way. More than that, it was her duty, as a friend to them both, to do what she could to facilitate their happiness. She must swallow her own pain, her own mortifying, impossible jealousy. She must befriend Jane Fairfax in earnest. She must promote her cause. She must, if she could, help to bring about the very union that would seal her own desolation.

It was a prospect so bleak as to be almost heroic. The thought gave her a strange, cold comfort. If she could no longer have his love, she could at least strive to be worthy of his esteem. She would act as he would wish her to act: with generosity, with self-command, and with a true regard for the happiness of others. It would be the greatest, most difficult, and most painful undertaking of her life. And as she sat there in the silent, sleeping house, Emma Woodhouse, for the first time, felt the true weight of growing up.

The short walk from the Bates’s door to the gates of Hartfield was accomplished with a vivacity of spirits on the part of Mr. Frank Churchill that seemed little diminished by the morning’s exertions. Mrs. Weston, leaning on his arm, appeared rather to wish for a more placid conclusion to the visit; but her son, it was clear, was not yet done with the subject which had provided him so much entertainment.

“Did you ever see any thing so complete, Miss Woodhouse?” he began, turning back to walk beside her and Harriet. “The blush, the tremor, the feigned composure! I declare, my conjectures were almost too near the mark. I was in danger of being found out as a wizard. One more question about Colonel Campbell, and I believe she might have fainted quite away.”

“You are very cruel,” said Emma, though she could not suppress a smile. “Indeed, you pressed the point most unmercifully. I was in continual terror of what you might say next.”

“Ah, but you enjoyed it! I saw you did. I saw the spark of amusement in your eye when you glanced her way. We are confederates in this, you know. You gave me the key, and I have merely been trying the lock. And it turns, Miss Woodhouse; it turns most beautifully.”

“A key may open more than one door,” said Mrs. Weston, with a gentle, cautionary tone. “My dear Frank, you must have a care. Jane Fairfax’s situation is a delicate one. It is not an object for sport.”

“Sport! My dearest mother, you wound me. Can you call such heartfelt, solicitous attention mere sport? I was doing the work of a true friend—endeavouring to understand, to sympathise. And you must own, it was all done under the guise of the most harmless topick imaginable: a pianoforte.” He looked at Emma with such a significant and diverting air of shared intelligence that she felt her scruples melting away. It was, after all, extremely amusing; and if Jane Fairfax were indeed cherishing a secret and improper attachment, she had very little right to be spared.

“Mr. Knightley did not seem to think it harmless,” said Harriet Smith, in her small, hesitant voice. It was the first time she had spoken, and the observation made them all pause.

“Mr. Knightley?” said Frank Churchill, his eyebrows raised in slight surprise. “Ah, yes. Our good friend Mr. Knightley, riding to the rescue. Or rather, riding to Kingston, but willing to be detained for five minutes by the plight of a fair lady. Do you think he suspected my plot, Miss Woodhouse?”

“I think Mr. Knightley suspects every body of something,” replied Emma, with a laugh that did not feel quite as easy as she intended. “But his concern for Miss Fairfax’s health is of a piece with his concern for all his neighbours. You heard him yourself, he was soon on to the subject of Mrs. Cole’s errands and Miss Bates’s apples. Mr. Knightley’s benevolence is of a very practical, and sometimes very abrupt, nature.”

She said it with an air of complete authority, as one who knew Mr. Knightley best. It was her role to explain him to the world. Yet, Harriet’s simple remark had struck a dissonant chord. His enquiry had not been general; it had been remarkably, almost rudely, particular. “How is Miss Fairfax?”—not once, but thrice, and in a tone that demanded an answer above all Miss Bates’s rambling civilities. Emma had been amused by it at the time, seeing it only as a comic struggle between his impatience and Miss Bates’s garrulity. Now, she was not so certain.

They had reached the gates of Hartfield, and it was time for Mrs. Weston and her son to proceed towards Randalls.

“We shall see you this evening, I hope?” said Mrs. Weston. “Your father was kind enough to say he might spare you for a few hours.”

“If he remains as well as he has been this morning, I have very little doubt of it,” Emma answered.

Frank Churchill took her hand, retaining it a moment longer than was strictly necessary. “Till this evening, then,” he said, his voice lowered for her alone. “We shall have more to conjecture upon, I dare say. Keep your eyes open. Our mystery deepens.” He gave her a final look, full of charm and conspiracy, before turning to follow his mother.

Emma and Harriet passed through the gates and walked up the shrubbery path in a silence that was, for a few moments, quite companionable. Emma was replaying the morning’s scenes in her mind. The more she considered it, the more she was convinced that Frank Churchill, with his worldly sagacity, must be right. Jane Fairfax had a secret. The pianoforte was its symbol, and some unknown gentleman in Ireland—or perhaps, as Frank’s manner sometimes playfully suggested, a certain gentleman in Highbury—was its object. The blush and the smile were undeniable. And Emma, who had so long found Jane Fairfax’s reserve an impenetrable and rather affronting fortress, was delighted to have found a breach.

“It was a very full morning,” she said at last, turning to Harriet. “So many little incidents. I wonder you are not fatigued.”

“Oh! no, indeed,” replied Harriet. “I found it all so... interesting. Everybody was there. And Mr. Knightley, too.”

There it was again. Mr. Knightley. Emma felt a small prick of impatience. “Yes, he made his appearance for a minute or two. He is always so busy with the estate; it is a wonder he finds time to call on any one.”

“He seemed to find time to be very anxious about Miss Fairfax,” Harriet ventured, her cheeks pinkening as if she knew she were treading on delicate ground. “When he called out from the window, his voice was quite different. So strong, and—and so determined. He would not be put off.”

“That is Mr. Knightley’s way,” said Emma, a little coolly. “When he thinks a thing is right, such as asking after a person who was unwell the night before, he will do it, regardless of any other conversation. It is a part of his character. He does not mean to be abrupt, though it often appears so.” She was explaining him again, but this time it was to herself as much as to Harriet. She was putting his behaviour back into the comfortable, familiar box of his known personality.

“Yes, of course,” said Harriet, immediately retreating. “You know him so much better than any one. It was only that I had never heard him speak in quite that tone before. It was as if nothing else in the world mattered but to know that she was well.”

Emma made no reply. An uneasy silence fell between them until they reached the door of the house. As if nothing else in the world mattered. The words were simple, even foolish—the sort of romantic exaggeration to be expected from Harriet—and yet they lodged themselves in Emma’s mind with the discomfort of a splinter. It was a description she would have applied, without a second thought, to a lover.

The rest of the day passed quietly. Mr. Woodhouse had his arrowroot, expressed his gentle anxieties about the dangers of pianofortes arriving in damp weather, and was perfectly content. Emma read to him, arranged her flowers, and tried to dismiss the unease that Harriet’s words had planted. She told herself it was absurd. Mr. Knightley, and Jane Fairfax! The idea was preposterous. He was sixteen years her senior; a brother, a guardian, a mentor to all of Highbury. He viewed Jane with compassion, certainly, and with the admiration due to her accomplishments. But love? The sort of love that made a man’s voice change and his focus narrow to a single object? It was a flight of fancy worthy of Harriet Smith herself.

And yet. The more she tried to dismiss it, the more the evidence arranged itself in a new and alarming pattern. He was always defending her. Even when he agreed with Emma’s criticisms of Jane’s reserve, he would always find some reason to excuse it, to place it in the context of her unfortunate circumstances. He had been the one to insist, with some warmth, that they must be more compassionate towards her. And his behaviour that morning—it was not just the loud enquiry. It was his immediate and pointed dismissal of Frank Churchill’s name from the list of fine dancers. “I do not see why Miss Fairfax should not be mentioned too.” It had seemed at the time like a simple act of justice, of making sure the overlooked person was included, which was so very like him. But what if it were more? What if it were the jealousy of a man who could not bear to hear the woman he admired praised in the same breath as a rival?

Frank Churchill was the rival. Of course. It explained everything. It explained Mr. Knightley’s immediate and uncharacteristic dislike of Frank from the very first. Emma had put it down to a clash of dispositions—the serious and the frivolous. But it was love against love. Frank, with his open flirtations and charming nonsense, was paying court to Emma in public while, she suspected, sending secret signals to Jane. And Mr. Knightley, watching from the side, was consumed with a silent jealousy that broke through only in these odd moments of gruffness and intense, focused concern.

Emma sat at her drawing-table, a pencil motionless in her hand, her mind racing. It was a theory of such beautiful, terrible symmetry. It accounted for every puzzling look, every awkward silence, every moment of inexplicable tension she had witnessed between the three of them. Frank’s game with her was a screen for his feelings for Jane; and Mr. Knightley’s disapproval of Frank was not on her account, as she had proudly imagined, but on Jane’s.

The mortification of this thought was extreme. For weeks, she had been flattering herself that she was the center of this drama; that Frank Churchill’s attentions were for her, and that Mr. Knightley’s anxieties were about her. She had seen herself as the sun around which these other planets revolved. Now, she saw that she was not even a planet, but a distant moon, reflecting a light she had mistaken for her own. She was merely a tool, a convenience; a screen for Frank Churchill and an object of misapprehended solicitude for Mr. Knightley.

And what of Jane Fairfax? Emma’s amusement turned to a feeling much more complex. That smile of secret delight—was it for Frank Churchill’s daring, or was it because she knew another man, a far more considerable man, was watching? And the playing of _Robin Adair_—Frank had called it _his_ favourite. But who was ‘he’? Emma, in her folly, had assumed he meant the secret lover in Ireland, or even himself. But Mr. Knightley had been there. He had heard it. Could it have been meant for him?

The idea was deeply vexing. Mr. Knightley and Jane Fairfax. It was a match she could not approve. Not at all. He, the master of Donwell Abbey, a man of such standing and consequence, to marry a woman with no fortune and no connections, a woman who was all but a governess? It was a degradation. It was beneath him. It was a complete overthrow of the natural order of things in Highbury. And for him to have conducted such a courtship in secret, under all their noses, without so much as a hint to her, to Emma, who had been his friend and confidante since she was a child! It was a betrayal of their intimacy.

A hot flush of indignation rose in her cheeks. She felt an anger so sharp and personal that it surprised her. Why should she care so much? He was a free agent; he could marry whom he pleased. But it felt like a loss. A profound and personal loss. The unique place she held in his life, the easy, argumentative, affectionate footing they shared, would be gone forever. A Mrs. Knightley—and that Mrs. Knightley to be Jane Fairfax, of all people!—would stand between them. The prospect was insupportable.

Her evening at Randalls was transformed by this new, distressing conviction. She watched every interaction with a painful, heightened awareness. Frank Churchill was as charming and attentive as ever, but Emma now saw his performance as hollow. He sat by her, he spoke to her, but his eyes, she fancied, strayed often towards the corner where Mrs. Weston was quietly conversing with Jane Fairfax, who had been prevailed upon to join the party.

Mr. Knightley was not there. He had sent his apologies; business at Kingston had detained him longer than expected. His absence was both a relief and a disappointment. Emma was spared the agony of watching him and Jane together, but she was also denied the opportunity to confirm her suspicions.

“Your friend Mr. Knightley works too hard,” said Frank Churchill to her, in a low voice. “He will wear himself out for the sake of his tenants and his apples. One would think Donwell were a kingdom and he its anxious monarch.”

“There are few monarchs so conscientious,” Emma replied, with a briskness she did not feel.

“Very true. His subjects are fortunate indeed. Miss Fairfax, for instance, seems to enjoy his particular protection. He was quite her champion this morning. I thought for a moment he might call me out for my impertinence about the waltzes.”

Emma’s heart gave a painful throb. So he had noticed it too. Her theory was not a fantasy. “Mr. Knightley is a champion for any one he feels to be slighted,” she said, trying to sound indifferent. “It is one of his better qualities.”

“A very admirable quality,” Frank agreed, with a smile that was just a little too knowing. “Especially when the slighted party is so very handsome. You must allow her to be handsome, Miss Woodhouse.”

“I have never denied it,” said Emma, turning away to adjust her shawl. The conversation was becoming unbearable. Every word he spoke seemed designed to confirm her fears, to hold up a mirror to a truth she did not wish to see.

She was relieved when the time came to go home. The short carriage ride back to Hartfield was a misery of swirling thoughts. The vexation, the anger, the sense of loss—it was a tempest inside her. She felt robbed of something she had never thought to claim, but had always unconsciously possessed: the first place in Mr. Knightley’s estimation. She had taken his affection, his lectures, his constant presence in her life for granted, as she took the sun in the sky. Now it seemed the sun was choosing to shine in another direction, and she was left in a sudden and bewildering cold.

As she lay in her bed that night, unable to sleep, the full picture settled in her mind with the force of absolute certainty. Mr. Knightley was in love with Jane Fairfax. He had been for some time. His criticism of her, Emma, had not been simply brotherly correction; it had been sharpened by the contrast he was constantly drawing between her follies and Jane’s perceived perfection. His defense of Jane was not mere justice; it was the advocacy of a lover. His anxiety was not simple compassion; it was the tender fear of a devoted heart.

And the worst of it, the most galling part of it all, was that she could not entirely blame him. Jane Fairfax was elegant, accomplished, and possessed a quiet fortitude that Emma knew he must admire. What did she have to offer in comparison? Vanity, willfulness, and a history of meddling in the lives of others. He was choosing substance over show, depth over sparkle. It was a choice perfectly in keeping with his character.

A new resolution, born of this painful clarity, began to form. She could not fight this. She could not stand in his way. If this was what he wanted, if this was to be his happiness, then she, as his truest friend, must accept it. More than that. She must rise above her own selfish, wounded feelings. She must be generous. She would not be cold to Jane Fairfax; on the contrary, she would redouble her kindness. She would promote their union. She would help to bring about the very event that felt like the seal on her own desolation. It was a penance, a noble sacrifice that was the only course of action worthy of his esteem. If she had lost his heart, she would not lose his respect. She would act as he would wish her to act. She would be, for once, the woman he had always hoped she would become. The thought brought no comfort, only a bleak, quiet determination. Emma Woodhouse had found a new, and far more difficult, project: the manufacturing of her own misery for the sake of the two people who were now, she believed, dearest to one another in all the world.

The morning light brought no comfort, only the cold, grey reality of her new-found purpose. Emma awoke not rested, but resolved; a state she had often found herself in, though never before with so little pleasure. The feverish activity of her imagination during the night had settled into a kind of stoic ache. Mr. Knightley loved Jane Fairfax. Jane Fairfax, if not in love with him, was at the very least the object of his devoted attention. The facts, as she had arranged them, were immutable. Her duty, therefore, was clear. She would be his friend in this, as in all things. She would aid, not hinder. She would swallow her own bitter portion and smile as she served him his happiness.

She descended for breakfast with a countenance of composed gravity that much perplexed her father. “Is any thing amiss, my dear? Is the air from the passage troubling you? We must have that draught looked to. One cannot be too careful.”

“No, Papa, I am perfectly well. I was only thinking.”

Her project was to be one of secret, noble sacrifice; the outward signs of it ought to be subtle, a quiet dignity that others might remark upon without understanding its source.

The day’s first trial presented itself almost immediately. It was her custom, on a fine morning, to take a walk, and Hartfield was so situated that a walk in any direction was apt to lead towards the homes of her acquaintance. To-day, her feet seemed to know her purpose before her mind had fully commanded them; she found herself on the path to the Bates’s. It was perfect. It was terrible. She must begin her penance at once. She would call on Miss Fairfax, and she would be kind. She would be more than kind; she would be studiously, almost oppressively, attentive.

She found them, as usual, in their small parlour, the new pianoforte occupying a space of such prominence as to make the rest of the furniture seem to be paying it court. Miss Bates, in a flutter of gratitude and delight, met her at the door.

“Miss Woodhouse! How very good of you to call! And so early! We were just saying—were we not, Jane?—how very kind it was of you all to come yesterday, and now to come again! My mother will be so pleased. She is just having her cup of broth, but she will be down in a moment. And how is your dear father? We were so concerned about the carriage last night; I do hope he did not feel a draught.”

“My father is perfectly well, I thank you, Miss Bates. I came, in truth, to inquire after Miss Fairfax. I feared she might be overtired.” Emma directed her gaze towards Jane, who had risen from her seat by the window and now stood, a model of quiet elegance, her hands clasped loosely before her.

“You are very considerate,” said Jane, with a slight inclination of her head. Her voice was as low and musical as ever, but Emma fancied she saw a faintness about her, a weariness in her eyes that confirmed all her suspicions. Jane was suffering; suffering from the strain of a secret, or from the anxieties of the heart.

“Not at all,” said Emma, advancing into the room. “We were all of us concerned. Mr. Knightley, in particular, was most anxious on your account. He quite insisted on being told how you were before he would hear of any thing else.”

She delivered this intelligence with what she hoped was an air of friendly significance, a little nudge to the subject. She watched Jane Fairfax for the expected blush, the momentary confusion that would betray her feelings. And a blush did indeed come, a tide of colour that rose swiftly to her cheeks. But it was accompanied by a look not of pleased consciousness, but of something more akin to alarm. Jane’s eyes darted towards her aunt, and then back to Emma, her lips pressed tightly together.

“Mr. Knightley is always very good,” Jane said at last, her voice barely a whisper. “He is a very kind neighbour to us all.”

“Oh, the very kindest!” burst forth Miss Bates. “Was there ever any body like him? The apples, you know! And then the carriage! And his asking after Jane so particularly—so very particularly! It is just like him. He has a heart of gold. I said to my mother, I said, ‘Mother, if there is a true gentleman in Highbury, it is Mr. Knightley.’ And she quite agreed, did you not, mother? Oh, but she is not here yet. Jane, my dear, you are looking a little pale. Are you sure you are well? Perhaps you should not have sat up so late writing letters.”

“Writing letters?” said Emma, seizing on the new information. “You must have a great many correspondents, Miss Fairfax.”

“Only one of whom I write to with such regularity,” Jane replied, a slight coldness entering her tone, as if she resented the inquiry.

But Emma, in her new role as facilitator, would not be deterred. “A very dear friend from Ireland, I have no doubt,” she said, with a knowing smile. “It is such a blessing to have a friend in whom one can confide every thing.” She paused, and then, unable to resist the opening, added, “Though I imagine your correspondence must be a great tax upon your time and spirits. Mr. Knightley himself has remarked upon it. He worries you exert yourself too much.”

Jane Fairfax looked at her, and for a moment, Emma felt the full force of a gaze that was not grateful, nor pleased, but intensely, searchingly, and almost hostilely questioning. It was as if Jane were trying to look into her very soul and divine the motive for this strange, pointed conversation. Emma felt a twinge of her old dislike, a flash of resentment at this ungracious reception of her well-meant efforts. But she suppressed it. This was part of her sacrifice. She must bear with Jane’s caprices. They were, no doubt, the product of her difficult situation.

Before the silence could become entirely unendurable, the door opened again, and to Emma’s very great surprise, in walked Mr. Frank Churchill.

“I have caught you all,” he said, with his customary easy charm, bowing to the room. “Miss Woodhouse, I had a premonition I should find you here, dispensing sunshine. Miss Bates, your servant. Miss Fairfax… I hope I see you well.” His address to Jane was pitched in a lower, more intimate key, and his eyes lingered on her with an expression that Emma now found merely impertinent. How dare he look at Jane so, when her heart—and Mr. Knightley’s—was so seriously engaged?

Miss Bates was in raptures. “Mr. Churchill! Well, this is a delight! Two such esteemed visitors in one morning. Our little room is quite the fashionable salon to-day. Do take a seat; oh, but we have so few chairs. Jane, my dear, perhaps Mr. Churchill could have your stool. No, no, I shall fetch one from my mother’s room.”

“Do not trouble yourself, I beg you,” said Frank Churchill, smoothly preventing her by taking a place on the window-seat, not far from Jane. “I am quite comfortable here. I came on a mission of mercy. My father and Mrs. Weston are planning a little excursion to Box Hill, and I am sent as ambassador to entreat you all to join us. The day is not yet fixed, but the prospect is held out. What say you, Miss Woodhouse? Does the idea of Box Hill tempt you?”

“It is a charming spot,” said Emma, her mind otherwise engaged. She was observing him, observing Jane. His presence had thrown Jane into a state of visible agitation. She had turned quite pale again and was now intently studying the pattern of the carpet. He, meanwhile, divided his attentions, speaking to Emma, but looking at Jane, a subtle, private war of nerves being waged under the cover of polite conversation.

“Charming is the word,” he continued, his eyes twinkling. “A fine view, fresh air, and good company. We must persuade Miss Fairfax to come. Such an expedition would do her a world of good. Emma did not return it. The jest fell flat. Pining? Of course she was pining. But it was for Mr. Knightley, not for this coxcomb who delighted in tormenting her. Emma felt a surge of protectiveness, a feeling so new and so foreign in its application to Jane Fairfax that it quite startled her.

“I am sure Miss Fairfax has no cause for pining,” Emma said, with a coolness that surprised even herself. “She is surrounded by friends who value her health and happiness above all things. Any paleness is merely the result of fatigue.”

Frank Churchill raised his eyebrows. “Indeed? You are become her champion, Miss Woodhouse? A new and fascinating alliance. And what say you, Miss Fairfax? Are you fatigued, or are you pining? The world—or at least, this small part of it—waits to know.”

Jane looked up, her eyes flashing with a fire Emma had never seen in them before. “My health, sir,” she said, her voice trembling slightly, “is a matter of concern only to my family and my closest friends. I do not find it a suitable topic for general amusement.”

The rebuke was so direct, so sharp, that it silenced even Frank Churchill for a moment. He had the grace to look momentarily abashed. “A thousand pardons,” he said, recovering himself. “You are perfectly right. It was an impertinence. I shall be more discreet. We shall speak of Box Hill instead. Think of it, Miss Fairfax—a whole day of freedom, away from… well, away from Highbury.”

There was a meaning in his tone, a stress on the last words, that was not lost on Emma. It was all part of his cruel game. He knew she was trapped here, and he dangled the prospect of escape only to highlight her confinement. But Emma’s interpretation was different. He was mocking her attachment to Highbury, an attachment necessitated by her duty to her family and, as Emma now believed, her secret devotion to Mr. Knightley, Highbury’s most essential resident.

The atmosphere in the little room had grown thick with unspoken tensions. Miss Bates, blissfully unaware, continued to chatter about Box Hill, hampers, and the likelihood of fine weather. Emma sat in silent appraisal, her resolution hardening with every passing moment. She saw it all so clearly now. Frank Churchill was the villain of the piece; Jane, the suffering heroine; and Mr. Knightley, the steady, honourable hero waiting in the wings. And she, Emma Woodhouse, was the fairy godmother, the secret agent who would bring the hero and heroine together.

It was at this moment, as if summoned by the sheer force of her dramatic plotting, that the man himself appeared. A shadow fell across the window, followed by a firm rap on the door. It was Mr. Knightley.

“I saw Frank Churchill’s horse,” he said, by way of explanation, as Miss Bates ushered him in. “And I thought I might just look in to see that he was behaving himself.” His eyes scanned the room, taking in the scene with his usual quick perception. He nodded to Emma, gave Frank Churchill a look that was decidedly cool, and then his gaze settled upon Jane Fairfax.

“Miss Fairfax,” he said, his voice softening, just as Emma had known it would. “I am glad to see you up and about. I trust you are recovered from last night.”

“Perfectly recovered, I thank you, Mr. Knightley,” Jane murmured, not meeting his eye.

Here it was. The scene she had dreaded and longed for. Her great test. Emma watched them, her heart beating a painful, unsteady rhythm. She must say something. She must play her part.

“We have all been expressing our concern, Mr. Knightley,” Emma said, her voice bright and, she hoped, convincingly cheerful. “I was just telling Miss Fairfax that you were her most anxious inquirer yesterday.”

Mr. Knightley turned to look at her, a strange, quizzical expression on his face. “Were you, Emma? I was an anxious inquirer for everyone. It was a late night for us all.”

“Oh, but you were most particular about Miss Fairfax,” Emma insisted, pushing on with a recklessness born of her conviction. “And you were quite right to be. She is not as robust as some of us.” She smiled sweetly at Jane. “We must all take special care of her.”

A dead silence fell upon the room. Frank Churchill watched Emma with an expression of utter astonishment, a slow smile spreading across his face as if he were witnessing a theatrical performance of the highest comedy. Miss Bates looked from one to the other in a state of happy confusion. Jane Fairfax stood as if turned to stone, her face a mask of incomprehensible emotions.

But it was Mr. Knightley’s reaction that was the most peculiar. He did not look pleased. He did not smile in secret understanding. He looked at Emma, and there was no mistaking it, with a look of profound, almost pained, disappointment.

“Emma,” he said, and his voice was low and stern, “I do not know what you mean.”

“Oh, I think you do,” she retorted playfully, though her heart was sinking. Why was he not playing his part? Was he so determined on secrecy? “But we shall not speak of it. We shall speak of Box Hill! Are you to join the party to Box Hill, Mr. Knightley?”

“That,” he said, his eyes still fixed on her, “will depend.”

“Upon what?” asked Frank Churchill, unable to resist joining the fray. “Upon the company, Knightley? Or upon the conviction that your good advice will be needed to keep us all from mischief?”

“My advice is seldom taken, and less often needed than I am apt to think,” Mr. Knightley replied, turning his sharp gaze upon Frank Churchill. “But my presence is sometimes a useful check upon nonsense.”

The challenge hung in the air, a palpable thing. Emma felt a desperate need to escape. Her grand gesture had gone awry. Instead of producing a moment of tender, unspoken communion between the two lovers, she had created an atmosphere of excruciating awkwardness and drawn Mr. Knightley’s displeasure upon herself. She had meant to be noble, but she had only succeeded in being meddlesome in a new and more painful way.

She rose to take her leave. “I have stayed too long. Papa will be waiting for his walk.”

“So soon, Miss Woodhouse?” said Frank Churchill. “The play was just getting interesting.”

“I am not in a theatrical humour to-day,” Emma said, forcing a smile. She bade her adieus to Miss Bates and Jane, who responded with a quietness that was almost eerie. Mr. Knightley followed her to the door.

“May I have a word with you, Emma?” he asked, once they were in the narrow hallway.

“If you wish,” she said, her head held high. She would not let him see how his disapproval wounded her.

“What was the meaning of that display in there?” he asked, his voice earnest, not angry. “This sudden, public solicitude for Jane Fairfax? It is not like you. And coming from you, it is… it can be misinterpreted.”

“Can it?” she said, feigning innocence. “I was merely being kind. I thought you, of all people, would approve of my taking more notice of Miss Fairfax. You have told me often enough that I should.”

“I have told you to be just to her merits, not to make her the object of a public spectacle. You put her in an impossible position. You put… others in an impossible position.”

“I can imagine nothing of the sort,” Emma said, her voice trembling slightly. “I saw only a friend in need of support, and I offered it. If my methods were clumsy, I am sorry for it. But my motives were of the best.” She was determined to believe it.

He looked at her for a long moment, a deep sadness in his eyes. “I believe you,” he said, so quietly she barely heard him. “I believe you always think your motives are for the best. That is the very heart of the problem.”

He stepped back and allowed her to pass. Emma walked out into the sunshine, but the world seemed to have gone dark. She had tried to be generous, to sacrifice her own feelings for his happiness, and he had met her with censure. He had misunderstood her completely. Or, a dreadful, sickening thought began to dawn, perhaps it was she who had misunderstood every thing from the very beginning. But no. She pushed the thought away. It was too frightening to contemplate. Her theory was sound; the evidence was overwhelming. Mr. Knightley’s strange behaviour was merely a testament to his desire for absolute secrecy. She must be more careful, more subtle. Her work was not yet done. It had only become infinitely more difficult.

He believed her good-hearted, but saw that her methods were a danger to his secret. The problem was not her theory, but her execution. She had been playing chequers, when the game was chess.

Her father’s entrance interrupted these reflections. “Emma, my love, you have been gone a very long while. I was beginning to be afraid. Mrs. Goddard’s little Susan was taken with a sore throat only yesterday. Highbury is so drafty. I hope you did not sit near an open window.”

Here was more proof, if any were needed. The whole world, it seemed, was in a conspiracy of concern for Jane Fairfax. She would not abandon her project. She would simply become a more subtle, a more cunning, a more patient benefactor. She would watch, and wait, and at the perfect moment, she would act in a way so delicate that Mr. Knightley himself would be forced to admire her skill, even as he was surrendering to the happiness she had secured for him. The thought, painful as it was, gave her a strange and solemn satisfaction. She was the secret architect of his future, and he did not even know it.

Several days passed in unusual tranquility. Mr. Churchill was often at Randalls, but made fewer calls at Hartfield, a forbearance for which Emma was truly grateful. She heard, through the inevitable circulations of Highbury gossip, that Mr. Knightley had been to Donwell but once, and had spent most of his time in his study. This, she concluded, was a sign of his troubled mind. He was avoiding society, avoiding the places where he might encounter Jane. How his heart must be aching!

The desire to see for herself, to gather fresh intelligence, became irresistible. On a fine, crisp afternoon, she decided a walk to Randalls was in order. She might learn something from Mrs. Weston, who, though not in on the great secret, was a shrewd observer. “I am sorry to hear it,” said Emma, taking a seat. “I had hoped to find Mrs. Weston at leisure.”

If you are not otherwise engaged, Miss Fairfax, would you allow me to accompany you for a short turn about the shrubbery?”

Jane looked surprised. The invitation was so direct, so personal. Her eyes flickered towards Emma’s face, as if searching for a motive. Emma met her gaze with a look of pure, open friendliness. After a moment’s hesitation, Jane consented. “You are very good. I should like it very much.”

Miss Bates was overjoyed. “A walk! How very delightful! The very thing for Jane. She keeps herself indoors far too much. I am always telling her so. You are so thoughtful, Miss Woodhouse. It is just what I should have proposed myself, if I had but thought of it.”

They had scarce stepped out of the door, however, when their tête-à-tête was interrupted. Mr. Frank Churchill himself appeared, coming towards them with a light, eager step, and a smile that seemed to embrace all of Highbury.

“Miss Woodhouse! And Miss Fairfax!” he exclaimed, with an air of the most fortunate happenstance. “Two goddesses deserting Olympus for a stroll amongst mortals. Whither are you bound? May a mere mortal attend you?”

Emma felt a sharp prick of annoyance. This was precisely what she had not wanted. Her delicate work required privacy, not the disruptive presence of a man who turned everything into a jest. “We were merely going for a quiet turn, Mr. Churchill,” she said, with a coolness she did not trouble to disguise.

“Quiet turns are my particular specialty,” he declared, quite unabashed. “I can be as quiet as a mouse when the occasion demands it. Besides, I have a matter of great importance to discuss. We are planning a little exploration, Mrs. Weston and I. To Box Hill. We must all go to Box Hill. You will come, Miss Woodhouse. And you, Miss Fairfax. It will be an adventure.”

“Box Hill has been there for some time,” said Emma dryly. “I believe it will wait for us.”

“Ah, but the weather will not wait! And the company! The present company is too precious to be risked by postponement. We must seize the day. What do you say, Miss Fairfax? Are you an admirer of fine prospects?”

“I have always heard it is a beautiful view,” Jane said quietly, her eyes on the path before her.

“Then it is settled! We shall form a party. It will be the talk of the county.” He walked between them, directing his conversation first to one and then the other, with a gallantry so universal as to be almost meaningless. Emma was obliged to endure it, her plan for a gentle, confidential talk with Jane utterly ruined. He chattered about the Westons, about the ball, about London, and about Mr. Knightley’s sudden departure.

“And what of our good Mr. Knightley?” he said, with a glance at Emma. “Fled to the dissipation of London. I shudder to think of the dangers to which his sober virtue is now exposed. Do you imagine he will return to us much changed, Miss Woodhouse? A man of fashion, perhaps?”

“I imagine Mr. Knightley will return precisely as he left,” Emma replied, “and I am sure his business is of more consequence than you suppose.”

“Business, of course. We are all men of business, in our way. But a fortnight! It is a lifetime. Highbury is quite bereft. Is it not, Miss Fairfax?”

Jane made a sound of polite assent, but said nothing. Emma watched her, and saw in her silence a confirmation of her own theory. The mention of Mr. Knightley, his absence, the very length of it—it was all painful to her. Frank Churchill’s levity on the subject must be odious to her.

The walk was a failure, and Emma returned to Hartfield in a state of vexation. Her first attempt had been thwarted. Mr. Churchill was a constant impediment, an amiable but maddening complication. She saw now that to win Jane, she must first contrive to separate her from the attentions of this too-assiduous young man.

Her resolution, however, was unshaken. A few days later, she tried a different approach. She sent her carriage to the Bateses’ lodgings with a note, begging the favour of Miss Fairfax’s company for the afternoon at Hartfield. An explicit invitation for one, which neither Mr. Churchill could intercept nor Miss Bates misconstrue. The answer was a polite acceptance, and Jane arrived, looking as elegant and as self-possessed as ever.

Mr. Woodhouse was delighted to see her. “My dear Miss Fairfax, so good of you to come and sit with us. Emma is so often alone. I am sure you will not find us very lively, but the quiet is good for the nerves. I hope you did not find the air too damp coming over?”

The afternoon passed peacefully. Mr. Woodhouse dozed in his chair. Emma and Jane sat with their needlework. The setting was perfect for confidence, yet confidence was not forthcoming. Emma spoke of books, of music, of the little daily occurrences of Highbury. Jane answered with propriety and intelligence, but her reserve was like a wall of glass; Emma could see through it, but she could not pass. She felt a growing impatience. The fortnight was slipping away, and she had made no progress.

She decided a more direct, though still metaphorical, approach was needed. “It must be a great comfort to you,” she began, her eyes on her sewing, “to know you have such steadfast friends as the Campbells. To have a connection of such long standing, to be able to rely on their affection completely.”

“I am indeed very fortunate,” said Jane.

“Affection is a curious thing,” Emma continued, warming to her theme. “Sometimes it comes from the quarters we most expect. But sometimes—and this is more interesting—it springs up where we least look for it. An attachment may be forming silently, secretly, in a heart we believe is indifferent. One might be the object of the most devoted regard, and be entirely ignorant of it.”

She risked a glance at Jane. A faint colour had risen in the girl’s cheeks, and her needle had stopped. Emma felt a thrill of success. She was touching the nerve.

“A person might feel,” Emma went on, lowering her voice slightly, “that their situation is so difficult, so hemmed in by circumstances, that they are not at liberty to even acknowledge such an attachment. They might feel obliged to maintain a distance, a coldness, that is quite contrary to their own heart.”

Jane put down her work. Her hands were trembling slightly. “Miss Woodhouse,” she said, in a voice that was barely audible. “You are—you are speaking in generalities, I suppose.”

“Of course,” said Emma, with a reassuring smile. “Merely a little philosophising on human nature. But do not you agree that such a situation would be a most pitiable one? And that if a true friend could do anything to alleviate it—to bridge the gap, as it were—it would be a work of the greatest kindness?”

Jane looked at her, and for a moment, Emma thought the wall was about to crumble. Jane’s eyes were full of a feeling so deep and so agitated that it startled her. It was not the gentle melancholy Emma had expected; it was something nearer to alarm. “Kindness may be mistaken,” Jane said, her voice strained. “Sometimes the greatest kindness is to—to let things be. To not conjecture.”

She broke off, picked up her work, and seemed to be making a great effort to regain her composure. The moment had passed. Emma was both disappointed and triumphant. Disappointed, because Jane had not confided in her; but triumphant, because her reaction was the most absolute proof imaginable. Jane had understood her perfectly. The allusion to a secret, undeclared love had struck her to the very core. Her agitation was the agitation of one whose most precious, most painful secret is on the verge of being discovered. Emma was more certain than ever: Jane Fairfax knew that Emma was speaking of Mr. Knightley, and she was terrified by the implications.

The rest of the fortnight passed in a similar vein. Emma multiplied her attentions. She walked with Jane; she sent her offerings from the Hartfield gardens; she insisted Jane make free use of the Hartfield pianoforte, which was so much better placed than her own. Frank Churchill remained a frequent and often inconvenient presence, always proposing some new scheme, some party of pleasure. He teased Emma about her newfound devotion to Miss Fairfax.

“You are trying to reform her, I see,” he said to her one day, as they met in the town. “To make her as open and cheerful as yourself. It is a noble project, but I fear you will not succeed. She has a touch of the ice-maiden in her.”

“I have no project at all,” Emma said, annoyed. “I am merely showing a proper civility to someone whose situation deserves our compassion.”

“Compassion! Ah, yes. For her dreadful fate of being young, beautiful, and accomplished. We must all pity her for it.” His tone was light, but his eyes were sharp, and Emma had the uncomfortable feeling that he saw more than he pretended.

At last, the news came. Mrs. Weston called at Hartfield, her face bright with pleasure. “I have had a letter from Mr. Knightley! He returns to-morrow. His business in London is concluded. He says John and Isabella and the children are all well, and send their love.”

To-morrow! The word sent a jolt through Emma. Her time was up. She had tilled the soil, she had planted the seeds of confidence, and now the principal actor was to return to the stage. She felt a nervous excitement, a sense of impending climax. She had done all she could to prepare Jane’s mind. Upon his return, Mr. Knightley, seeing Jane more in society, more under Emma’s friendly wing, would surely find the courage to let his feelings be known. And Jane, prepared by Emma’s subtle hints, would be ready to receive them.

That evening, Emma felt a restlessness she could not subdue. The next day would be momentous. As she sat in the drawing-room with her father, who was complaining of a draught from the window, her mind was entirely at Donwell Abbey, imagining Mr. Knightley’s return. She pictured him riding up to the house, his mind full of London, but his heart full of Highbury—and of Jane Fairfax. She felt a profound and solemn happiness, the happiness of a secret benefactor, a silent architect of others’ joy. The world had gone dark for a moment when he had left, but now the light she had made was about to shine for all to see, and the satisfaction was immense. She had never been so certain, or so pleased with her own benevolent discernment. The play was reaching its final act, and she, the author, was waiting with breathless anticipation for the curtain to rise.
