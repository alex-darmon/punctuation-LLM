Mr. Davies gave a sigh of dramatic contentment, and closed the little book of verses which had been the subject of their conversation. “How very deeply the authoress must have felt, to write with such a spirit! One can scarcely believe them to be the work of a lady, there is such a strength of feeling. I wonder if she is known in London society, Miss Ingram? I dare say you have heard whispers as to her identity.” He fixed his gaze upon her with an arch look, one that spoke of a meaning far beyond his simple words.

Miss Ingram, who had been arranging a bowl of late-blooming roses, did not look up. “I have heard no whispers of the sort, sir. It is published anonymously, and I imagine the author wishes it to remain so.”

“Wishes it, perhaps; but can she expect it?” he continued, turning the book over in his hands. “When one puts such a heart upon the page, a heart so full of particular sorrows and particular joys, one must expect the world to conjecture. Do you not think the gentleman to whom ‘An Ode on Parting’ was written must know it at once?—Do you suppose he has read it yet, and recognised his own portrait, painted in such very flattering, if melancholy, colours?” He paused, but she offered no reply, her attention fixed entirely upon the placement of a single white rosebud. “Come, Miss Ingram, you must have some theory. Is he a soldier, gone to the continent? Or a younger son, sent away for want of a fortune?”

“As I have no acquaintance with the author,” said she, her voice admirably steady, though her fingers trembled just a little, “I cannot possibly form a judgment. It is all fancy.”

“Fancy—to be sure, it is all fancy! And a very pleasant fancy it is, to sit here and guess. One fancies this, and one fancies that. I fancy this binding is rather loose. What a vexation it is, Miss Morton,” said he, turning suddenly to Catherine, “when a book is not properly put together. Your true craftsman takes his time; but we amateurs, we are always in a hurry to see the finished effect.—Miss Ingram spoke of fancy. There, the corner is pressed down. I have the honour, Mrs. Gable, of returning your book to you, a little more secure for the present.”

He was thanked with a gentle smile from the placid lady of the house, and to escape, it seemed, any further remark from Miss Ingram, he moved towards the window-seat where Catherine was tatting lace. “If you are very good,” said he, in a lower voice, “you will tell me what you think of my conjectures. You did not seem to agree with them; you looked quite grave the whole time. I believe you think my theories nonsensical; but I would have given a great deal—all that a man of my small fortune ever has to give—for a single word of confirmation from you.”

She looked up. “What felicity it is to be entirely ignorant!—If I mistake not, you are speaking of matters beyond my comprehension.” She coloured as she spoke, and looked down at her work again with great diligence. He took a newspaper from a nearby table and, turning to Mrs. Gable, said, “Here is news from the city. Do you see it?—An expansion of the Royal Exchange.—And here is a notice of a new shipment of French silks. That, from such a quarter, one might expect. This paper was brought by the morning post. Very thoughtful of your brother, was it not?—He knew you would have no London news here. I admire that sort of attention in a man; it shews a true and constant remembrance. Nothing forgotten; nothing overlooked. Genuine regard only could have prompted it.”

Catherine wished he would be less obvious, yet could not deny a certain thrill at his game; and when she glanced towards Anne Ingram, she saw upon her lips the ghost of a smile, a smile that, for all the blush that accompanied it, seemed to speak of a secret pleasure. This quiet, proper, unimpeachable Miss Ingram was evidently harbouring sentiments of a very interesting nature. He brought the newspaper to her, and they looked over the shipping news together.—Catherine took the opportunity of whispering, “You are too bold. She must guess your meaning.”

“I sincerely hope she does. I would have her guess it. I feel no shame at all in my suspicions.”

“But truly, I am half ashamed for you, and wish I had never mentioned my own idle thoughts on the matter.”

“I am very glad you did, and that you confided them to me. I have now a key to her every glance and sigh. Leave the shame to her. If she has a secret, she ought to feel the weight of it.”

“She is not entirely without feeling, I think.”

“I do not see much sign of it. She is humming that ballad from the opera at this moment—_his_ favourite.”

Shortly afterwards Mrs. Gable, peering from the window, gave a little cry. “Mr. Henderson, I do believe!—Yes, it is he, riding past the laurel hedge. I must just have a word with him, to ask about the parish meeting. I will not open the window in here; it brings in such a damp air; but I can slip into the study. I dare say he will call, when he knows who is visiting. Quite a treat to have you all together!” She bustled into the adjoining room as she spoke, and throwing open the sash, at once hailed the horseman, and every word of their exchange was as perfectly audible to the party in the drawing-room as if they had been standing at her side.

“Mr. Henderson! Good morning to you!—Very well, I thank you. So kind of you to send the preserves. They arrived quite safe; my cook was just saying how fine they are. Pray come in; do come in. You will find some friends of yours here.” So began Mrs. Gable; and Mr. Henderson seemed quite determined to make his own inquiries, for in a most firm and serious voice, he said, “How is your guest, Mrs. Gable?—I wish to know how you all are, but my particular concern is for your guest. How is Miss Ingram?—I thought she looked a little pale at church on Sunday. How is she to-day? Tell me how Miss Ingram is.” And Mrs. Gable was obliged to give him a proper answer before he would attend to another word. The listeners were diverted; and Mr. Davies gave Catherine a look of immense meaning. But Catherine only shook her head with a little frown.

“So good of you!—so very good of you for the preserves,” resumed Mrs. Gable.

He cut her short with, “I am riding on to Ashley. Can I take any message for you?”

“Oh! dear, Ashley—are you?—Mrs. Bryce was just saying she needed a ribbon matched from the draper’s there.”

“Mrs. Bryce has her own footman. Can I do any thing for _you_?”

“No, no, I thank you. But do come in. Who do you imagine is here?—Miss Morton and her brother; so good of them to call to see my new book of verses. Do leave your horse at the inn, and come in.”

“Well,” said he, in a considering tone, “for a moment, perhaps.”

“And Mr. Davies is here too!—Quite a party; so many friends!”

“No, not today, I thank you. I could not stay a minute. I must be on my way to Ashley without delay.”

“Oh! do come in. They should be so pleased to see you.”

“No, no; your drawing-room is quite full enough. I shall call another day, and see the book of verses then.”

“Well, I am so sorry!—Oh! Mr. Henderson, what a beautiful sermon on Sunday; how very affecting.—Did you ever hear such wisdom?—Was not it edifying?—Miss Ingram and I were both so moved by it.”

“Oh! very edifying indeed; I can say no less, for I imagine Miss Ingram is hearing every word I speak. And (raising his voice a little) I do not see why Mr. Davies should not be complimented too. I think Mr. Davies reads poetry with a very fine expression; and Miss Morton is the most attentive listener in the whole county. Now, if your guests have any kindness, they will say something of your excellent tea in return; but I cannot wait to hear it.”

“Oh! Mr. Henderson, one moment more; something of great importance—so vexed!—Anne and I are both so vexed about the ink-stand!”

“What is the matter now?”

“To think of your sending us that handsome silver one. You said you had two, and now we hear you have none for your own desk. We really are so vexed! Your housekeeper mentioned it to our cook. You should not have done so, indeed you should not. Ah! he is gone. He never likes to be thanked. But I was sure he would have stayed, and it would have been a shame not to have said.... Well, (returning to the room,) I have not been successful. Mr. Henderson cannot stop. He is going to Ashley. He asked if he could take any message....”

“Yes,” said Anne Ingram, “we heard his kind inquiries, we heard it all.”

“Oh! yes, my dear, I expect you might, because you see, the door was ajar, and the window was wide open, and Mr. Henderson has such a clear voice. You must have heard it all to be sure. ‘Can I do any thing for you at Ashley?’ he said; so I just thought.... Oh! Miss Morton, must you be leaving?—You seem to have only just arrived—so very good of you to call.”

Catherine found it was indeed time to be returning home; the visit had been a long one; and upon consulting their watches, so much of the day was found to be spent, that Mr. Davies and his sister, taking their leave also, could only offer to escort Miss Ingram to the vicarage gates, before they parted for their separate houses.

The little party of four, having made their adieus with all the requisite civility and protracted farewells that such a visit demanded, found themselves at last in the open air. The change was a welcome one. The small, crowded parlour of the Bateses, though full of the warmth of kindness, had also been full of a more feverish heat, a tension which the fresh December breeze seemed agreeably to dispel. Frank Churchill drew in a deep breath, as if to expel the last remnants of confinement, and turned to Emma with a look of sparkling confederacy.

“Well, Miss Woodhouse,” he began, his voice lowered just enough to create an air of intimacy, yet loud enough for his stepmother to catch if she chose, “I think we may pronounce the morning a success. A new instrument, a mended pair of spectacles, and a mystery most thoroughly, if not altogether solved. I declare I have not been so usefully employed in an age.”

“You were certainly very industrious,” replied Emma, with a smile she could not suppress. “Mrs. Bates will be eternally in your debt for the repair of her spectacles. As for the mystery, however, I fancy your labours only served to make it more perplexing to poor Mrs. and Miss Bates.”

“Ah, but we are not Mrs. or Miss Bates,” he returned quickly. “We see a little farther. We understand a little more. We, I hope, are not perplexed at all. We have, as you so wisely gave me to understand, a key.”

Mrs. Weston, who had been walking a pace or two ahead with Harriet, now fell back to join them, her brow marked with a slight air of maternal concern. “Frank, I hope you were not teasing Jane too much. She looked quite distressed at some of your questions. You must remember her situation, and how delicate her nerves are.”

“Distressed!” he cried, with an affectation of the utmost astonishment. “My dear madam, you quite mistake the matter. Not distressed in the least. Conscious, perhaps. A little conscious, which is all as it should be. And as for nerves, I am persuaded a little of such pleasantry is the very best thing in the world for them. It is stagnation, not stimulation, that is the true enemy of delicate nerves. Is not that your opinion, Miss Woodhouse?”

Emma could only give a slight, assenting nod. She was not entirely comfortable with the recollection of Jane’s deep blush and forced composure; she had felt, even in the moment, that Mr. Churchill’s raillery verged on the cruel. Yet, the memory of that final, secret smile she had intercepted on Jane’s face returned to acquit both him and her own amusement. If Jane Fairfax found a secret delight in the allusions, there could be no true harm done.

“And what of Mr. Knightley?” Frank Churchill continued, his tone shifting to one of mock solemnity. “His own nerves seemed a little agitated. Did you ever witness such a dramatic arrival and departure? One might have thought he was a courier with news of a battle. ‘How is Miss Fairfax?’—as if the fate of the nation depended upon it. And then, off to Kingston, without so much as a how-d’ye-do for the rest of us! I own, I was quite intimidated by his ardour.”

“Now, Frank, that is unjust,” said Mrs. Weston, with a more decided air of reproof. “You know Mr. Knightley. He is the kindest of men, but he does not stand on ceremony. He has known Jane since she was a child; it is perfectly natural that he should feel a particular concern for her welfare.”

“Oh, perfectly natural, I am sure,” said Frank, with a slight, ironic bow. “A concern so very particular, indeed, that it quite eclipsed every other person in the room. He had no inquiries for Miss Smith, who was of the party last night, nor for Miss Woodhouse, whom he professes to have known even longer than Miss Fairfax. No, all his solicitude was for one object. It is a devotion I can only admire. Perhaps there is more in that quarter than any of us have supposed.”

He glanced at Emma as he said this, a sly, questioning look in his eyes. Emma felt a strange, immediate impulse to defend Mr. Knightley, not from the charge of kindness, but from the imputation of a tenderness so particular.

“Mr. Knightley is not a man of professions,” she said, with a coolness she did not entirely feel. “He is universally kind, though his manners may sometimes be abrupt. He probably thought Jane looked unwell, and his sense of duty obliged him to inquire. It is just his way.”

“Ah, his way. A very singular way, if you ask me. Duty is a very powerful motive, to be sure,” said Mr. Churchill, still with that undercurrent of laughter in his voice. “It carries a man all the way to the window of a lady’s lodging, makes him raise his voice to a pitch of commanding anxiety, and then sends him galloping off to Kingston without another word. I must learn this dutiful way of his.”

The conversation was here interrupted by their arrival at the corner where the path to Randalls diverged from the main street of Highbury. Mrs. Weston paused, looking towards her own home and then back at her companions. The walk had been all too short for the discussion she had wished to have, and she was not yet ready to relinquish her stepson to the solitary amusement of his own thoughts.

“We are not so very pressed for time,” she said. “Frank, perhaps you and I will escort Miss Woodhouse and Miss Smith to the gates of Hartfield. It is such a fine day, it seems a shame to go indoors so soon.”

Frank Churchill agreed with the greatest alacrity, and they continued on, a little procession of four that commanded the attention of more than one shopkeeper and passing acquaintance. Emma fell into a reverie. Mr. Churchill’s words, though spoken in jest, had struck a discordant note. Mr. Knightley’s behaviour _had_ been strange. It had been more than his usual brotherly concern, more than his customary Highbury correctness. The urgency in his voice, the repeated, insistent questioning—it was altogether too much for a mere cold. And his pointed inclusion of Jane Fairfax in the dancing compliments, raising his voice so that all might hear—what was that, but a public declaration of his esteem, a direct counter to the neglect he must have perceived from Frank Churchill himself?

Emma’s mind, once set on a path of conjecture, moved with considerable speed. Mrs. Weston’s look of meaning in the Bateses’ parlour came back to her. At the time, she had shaken her head in steady scepticism. But was it so impossible? Mr. Knightley, at seven and thirty, was surely of an age to be thinking of marriage. And Jane Fairfax—elegant, accomplished, and of a serious, sensible disposition so unlike Emma’s own—was she not exactly the sort of woman a man like Mr. Knightley might be expected to admire? He was always praising her, always defending her. He had taken her part against Emma on more than one occasion.

The idea was disagreeable in the extreme. It was not a matter of jealousy; Emma could not for a moment imagine herself feeling any romantic attachment to Mr. Knightley. He was a brother, a mentor, a critic; his place in her life was fixed, familiar, and entirely unromantic. But the thought of him marrying anyone—and especially Jane Fairfax, of all people—was an offense to the natural order of things. It would change everything. Hartfield would lose its first and most constant visitor. Her own consequence would be insensibly diminished. The comfortable, established world she had always known would be overturned. And for Jane Fairfax!—a woman she could not like, a woman she now believed to be cherishing a secret, reprehensible attachment to another man. The impropriety of it all was quite shocking.

She was roused from these unpleasant thoughts by Mr. Churchill’s voice at her side.

“A penny for your thoughts, Miss Woodhouse,” he said softly. “They seem to be of a very serious nature. Are you, perhaps, conjecturing, as Miss Fairfax would say? Are you piecing together the evidence of Mr. Knightley’s secret passion?”

“I am doing no such thing,” said Emma, colouring slightly. “I was thinking of…of the music that came with the pianoforte. The Irish melodies. It was very thoughtful of Colonel Campbell.”

She knew the lie was a weak one, and his smile told her he did not believe it for a moment. “Was it? Yes, very thoughtful indeed. Just as it was thoughtful of Mr. Knightley to be so concerned about the effects of last night’s dancing. Everyone is so very thoughtful towards Miss Fairfax. She is a most fortunate young woman. Perhaps Mr. Knightley means to rival the mysterious sender of the pianoforte. A duel of attentions! It would be a battle worth watching.”

“You talk a great deal of nonsense,” said Emma, forcing a laugh. “Mr. Knightley is not the sort of man to engage in such battles.”

“Is he not? I think you underestimate him. He seemed quite ready for a battle this morning. There was a fire in his eye. Did you not see it, Mrs. Weston?”

“I saw that he was in a hurry, Frank,” said his stepmother, “and I saw that you were being impertinent. I do wish you would let the subject drop. You will make Miss Woodhouse think you have a quarrel with Mr. Knightley.”

“A quarrel? Heavens, no! I have the highest respect for Mr. Knightley. I only wish I understood him. He is a puzzle to me. One moment all warmth and solicitude, the next all coldness and haste. He is a man of contrasts, and I am a simple fellow who prefers things to be plain and consistent.”

This profession of simplicity, from one so obviously complex and artful, was not lost on Emma. It was part of his charm, this playful self-deprecation. He and she were alike in their enjoyment of the subtle complexities of human behaviour, while poor Mr. Knightley, with his straightforward morality and blunt pronouncements, seemed to belong to an older, less sophisticated world. And yet, that world had always been her anchor. The thought of its foundations shifting was profoundly unsettling.

They had now reached the shrubbery that bordered the grounds of Hartfield. Only the gate remained between them and their parting. Harriet Smith, who had said scarcely a word during the entire walk, now ventured a small observation.

“It was a very handsome pianoforte,” she said, in her soft, hesitant voice.

“It was indeed, Miss Smith,” replied Frank Churchill, turning to her with a look of gallant attention. “And I am sure you will agree it is in very good hands. Miss Fairfax plays delightfully.”

“Oh, yes,” said Harriet, blushing at being so directly addressed. “Delightfully.”

Mrs. Weston took the opportunity to make her farewells. “We must not keep you standing, my dear Emma. I shall see you tomorrow, I hope. And Frank, it is time we were on our way back to Randalls.”

But Frank Churchill lingered. As his stepmother walked on a few paces, he leaned towards Emma again, his expression changing from one of public pleasantry to their now-familiar private conspiracy.

“Do not mind my nonsense about Knightley,” he whispered. “It is only a diversion. We know where the real secret lies, do we not? Weymouth. The Irish melodies. The blush. The secret smile. Our key unlocks that door, and that one only. Do not let him distract you.”

Emma felt a warm rush of reassurance. He was right. She had allowed a moment of ridiculous speculation to cloud her judgment. The evidence regarding Jane Fairfax and the Dixons was solid, tangible. She had seen it with her own eyes. Mr. Knightley’s eccentricities were merely a side-show, a distraction from the main drama. Her theory, their theory, was the correct one. The world was back in its proper alignment.

“I will not,” she whispered back, a smile of genuine pleasure returning to her face. “He will not distract me.”

“Good,” he said, pressing her hand for a brief moment before releasing it. “I rely upon your judgment. Till we meet again, co-conspirator.”

With a final, brilliant smile that took in both Emma and Harriet, he turned and followed his stepmother, leaving the two young ladies at the gate of Hartfield.

Emma watched him go, a feeling of perfect satisfaction settling upon her. What a delightful, intelligent, and understanding young man he was! He saw everything just as she did. There was a harmony of mind between them that was truly remarkable. As for Mr. Knightley, she would think no more of his strange behaviour. He was, after all, only Mr. Knightley. He could not be in love with Jane Fairfax, because it was an idea that suited nobody, least of all Emma Woodhouse. And what did not suit Emma Woodhouse could not, in the nature of things, be true.

“Come, Harriet,” she said, her spirits quite restored. “Let us go in. We must tell my father all about the new pianoforte.” And as they walked through the gates and up the pleasant gravel drive to the house, Emma’s mind was already busy, not with the perplexing attentions of Mr. Knightley, but with the far more gratifying and reprehensible feelings of the amiable, upright, perfect Jane Fairfax.

They found Mr. Woodhouse precisely where they had left him, in his comfortable chair by the fire, though he had shifted it a few inches to be farther from a suspected draught. His shawl was drawn more closely about his shoulders, and a book lay unread in his lap. He looked up with an expression of mild relief.

“Ah, my dears! You have returned. I had begun to think you lost. The morning has grown quite old. I hope you are not chilled, Emma. And Miss Smith; it was very good of you to accompany my Emma, but I hope you have not taken any harm from it. Visiting is such a hazardous undertaking.”

“We are perfectly well and warm, papa, I assure you,” said Emma, kissing his forehead and taking a seat beside him. “And we have such news. The pianoforte has arrived at Mrs. Bates’s, and it is a truly beautiful instrument.”

“A pianoforte?” said Mr. Woodhouse, his brow furrowed with concern. “My dear, I hope it was not too loud. The noise of such things can be very trying to the nerves. Poor Miss Bates and her mother; I fear their little room will suffer from the reverberation. And was the instrument very large? It must have been a great trouble to get it into the house. I hope none of the workmen were injured.”

“It all went very smoothly, papa, you need not worry. And Jane Fairfax played for us; so softly, you would have thought it a whisper. We could have stayed and listened all day.”

“Ah, Miss Fairfax. Yes, a very quiet young lady. But she looked so pale last night, Emma. So very pale. I hope she is not exerting herself too much. A new instrument requires a great deal of practice, and practice can be very fatiguing. And you say the room was full? Oh, Emma, that is the worst thing of all. Here, Emma judged it best to be slightly disingenuous. “We were all so comfortable, papa, that we did not notice. I believe Mrs. Bates may have opened a casement in the other room for a moment, but it was done very discreetly.”

“For a moment!” exclaimed Mr. Woodhouse in horror. “My dear, a moment is all it takes! A sudden rush of cold air upon unsuspecting heads. That is how the worst colds begin. “He was only inquiring after Miss Fairfax’s health,” said Harriet, venturing to speak again, for Mr. Knightley’s kindness had made a great impression upon her. “It seemed very considerate of him.”

Emma glanced at her. “Yes, very considerate,” she agreed, though the word felt inadequate to describe the odd intensity of his manner. She steered the conversation back to safer ground. “But you should have seen the music that came with the pianoforte, papa. All the way from Ireland, they believe. From Colonel Campbell. It shewed such particular attention.”

“From Ireland,” echoed Mr. Woodhouse, his mind seizing upon a new source of alarm. “That is a very long way. One hopes everything was packed quite dry. Dampness from the sea voyage can be ruinous, not just for the paper, but for the constitution. There are all sorts of fevers that come from damp parcels.”

Seeing that no part of the morning’s adventure could be related without occasioning some new anxiety in her father, Emma resolved to abandon the subject altogether. She spent the next hour humouring him, discussing the merits of a thinner or thicker slice of bread and butter, debating whether the fire was burning too brightly, and assuring him that she and Harriet had no intention of venturing out again that day.

It was not until Mr. Woodhouse had been settled for his afternoon nap that Emma could at last draw Harriet into her own dressing-room, free from the constraints of paternal solicitude. Here, with the door closed and a cheerful fire of their own, the true business of the day could be resumed.

“Now, Harriet,” Emma began, her eyes bright with the pleasure of a puzzle she felt she had solved, “what did you truly think of the morning? Of all that passed at Mrs. Bates’s?”

“Oh, it was delightful, Miss Woodhouse! To see so many friends, and the new instrument, and to hear Miss Fairfax play so beautifully.”

“Yes, yes, of course,” said Emma, with a little wave of impatience. “But beneath the surface, Harriet. Did you not observe? Did you not see what I saw? What Mr. Churchill and I both saw so very clearly?”

Harriet looked earnest and a little confused, as if she were conscious of some failure in her own powers of observation. “I am not sure, Miss Woodhouse. I saw that Miss Fairfax blushed a great deal when Mr. Churchill was speaking to her.”

“Precisely!” cried Emma, triumphant. “The blush is everything! He put her to the test, and she could not withstand it. When he spoke of Ireland, of Colonel Campbell, of a commission sent from thence—he was speaking of Mr. Dixon, Harriet, and she knew it! Every word was a dart, and every dart found its mark.”

Emma rose and began to pace the room, warming to her subject. “Think of the evidence! A magnificent pianoforte, sent by a secret benefactor. Who could it be but a man deeply in love, and a man who must keep his love a secret? Why must it be a secret? Because he is married to her dearest friend! It is the plot of a novel, but happening before our very eyes in Highbury.”

“Mr. Dixon!” whispered Harriet, her eyes wide with a mixture of shock and vicarious excitement. “But is he not married to Miss Campbell?”

“He is, and that is the pity and the scandal of it! He fell in love with Jane Fairfax at Weymouth, we may be certain of that. Everyone thought him attached to Miss Campbell, but his heart was given to her companion. What could he do? He was entangled. He had to go through with the marriage. But his conscience, his affection, would not let him rest. He sees his beloved Jane, poor and without prospects, and he contrives this grand, anonymous gesture to give her a little pleasure, a little consequence.”

Emma paused, her expression a perfect blend of censure and compassion. “It is very wrong of him, of course. Terribly wrong. A betrayal of his wife. And it is very wrong of her to accept it, to cherish it. Did you see her smile when Mr. Churchill pressed her? A secret smile of delight. She is glorying in it, Harriet! This ‘perfect’ Jane Fairfax is harbouring a secret, illicit attachment. I confess, I find it all rather shocking, but immensely diverting.”

“Goodness,” said Harriet, completely swept up in the narrative. “I should never have imagined such a thing. She always seems so… so proper.”

“That is her art,” declared Emma. “Her reserve is a cloak for her feelings. But we have pierced it! Mr. Churchill, with his wonderful sagacity, saw it at once, and I am very glad he did, for it confirms my own opinion entirely. We have the key, Harriet. We understand Jane Fairfax as no one else in Highbury does.”

She felt a thrill of satisfaction. The little tremor of doubt that Mr. Knightley’s odd behaviour had introduced was now entirely forgotten, smoothed away by the persuasive logic of her own theory, so ably seconded by the charming Mr. Churchill. She and he were partners in perception, an elite of two, with Harriet as their devoted follower.

Their conversation was interrupted by the sound of a firm tread in the hall below, followed by the announcement of a visitor. “Mr. Knightley, ma’am,” said the servant, appearing at the door. “He has returned from Kingston and asks if you are at leisure.”

Emma was surprised, and not entirely pleased. She had consigned Mr. Knightley to the category of a solved, if curious, problem for the day, and had no wish to revisit it. His return was unexpectedly prompt.

“Returned from Kingston already?” she said, half to herself. “He must have ridden very fast. Very well, tell him we will be down directly.”

As she and Harriet descended the stairs, Emma fortified herself. She would be cool and composed. She would not allow him to vex her. She would not even think about his strange enquiries at the Bateses’ window. He was a good friend, a valued brother-in-law, but his judgment was not infallible, and on the subject of Jane Fairfax, it was clearly non-existent.

She found him standing before the fire in the drawing-room, his back to the door, looking not at all as if he had just completed a brisk ride. His posture was thoughtful, almost somber. He turned as they entered, and his expression was one of seriousness, not the cheerful friendliness she was accustomed to.

“Knightley,” she said, forcing a note of welcome. “You have made quick work of your business in Kingston. I hope all was well.”

“My business was not of a sort that required much time,” he replied, his eyes meeting hers with an unblinking directness. “It was an errand of enquiry, and soon satisfied. I came back through the town and thought I would stop. I wished to speak with you, Emma.”

His tone was grave. Harriet, sensing a conversation not meant for her, murmured something about checking on Mr. Woodhouse and slipped quietly from the room. Emma felt a small flicker of annoyance, both at Harriet for abandoning her and at Mr. Knightley for creating an atmosphere that made her feel abandoned.

“You wished to speak with me?” she repeated, raising her eyebrows slightly. “I am at your disposal, though I cannot imagine what subject requires such a solemn preamble.”

He did not smile at her light tone. “I think you can imagine it. I want to talk about this morning. About what happened at the Bateses’.”

“Indeed?” said Emma, her guard immediately up. “I thought it a most amusing morning. Though my father is convinced we have all caught our deaths, thanks to your shouting through an open window.”

“I was not shouting, Emma, I was trying to be heard over Miss Bates. And my enquiry was a serious one. Miss Fairfax looked unwell last night. I was concerned for her.”

“And you shewed your concern most particularly,” Emma could not resist saying. “One might have thought no one else in the room had any claim to health or sickness.”

He ignored the jibe. “My concern is what I wish to speak of. Not only my own, but the lack of it in others. Frank Churchill, for instance. And you, Emma.”

This was too much. “Me? I have known Jane Fairfax my whole life! And Mr. Churchill was paying her a great deal of attention, I thought. More, perhaps, than she found entirely comfortable.” Emma gave him a look full of meaning, hoping he might have the sense to understand her allusion.

But Mr. Knightley’s mind was not running on riddles. “Attention?” he said, his voice sharp with disapproval. “You call that attention? It was persecution. It was the calculated torment of a man who enjoys seeing others squirm. He has some grudge against her, or he is simply a coxcomb who finds his entertainment in the discomfort of a gentlewoman. I do not know which is worse.”

Emma was stunned into silence for a moment. Persecution? Torment? The words were so wildly out of proportion with the elegant, witty flirtation she had witnessed.

“You cannot be serious!” she burst out. “It was a game! A little bit of teasing. And if Jane Fairfax cannot take a jest, it is because she has something to hide! He and I both remarked it. She has a secret, and his questions were drawing nearer and nearer to it. It was fascinating to watch.”

“Fascinating,” he repeated, and the word in his mouth was an indictment. “So it is a game to you. You and Frank Churchill, amusing yourselves at the expense of a young woman who has neither the health, the fortune, nor the temperament to endure it. Emma, look at her situation. She is accomplished and elegant, yes. But she is an orphan, living in cramped lodgings with relations who, however kind, must try her patience at every moment. She has no fortune. Her only prospect is the one she dreads most: to leave the only home she has ever known and sell her talents and her youth as a governess. She is on the very precipice of that fate. And you, and that… that puppy from London, think it a fine sport to poke and pry at her, to lay bare what little privacy of feeling she may have.”

Emma’s cheeks were burning. She had never been spoken to in such a way, not by him, not by anyone. He had scolded her before, about Harriet and Mr. Elton, but this was different. This was a deeper, more wounding censure.

“You have no right,” she began, her voice trembling slightly. “You do not understand. There are things you do not know. There is a whole history there, a story which explains everything.”

“A story you have invented!” he retorted, stepping closer. “A fiction you have concocted to indulge your own fancy, because you are bored and Highbury is too small for your imagination. Do you ever stop to think, Emma, of the real harm you might be doing? You treat people’s lives as a game of riddles, as a puzzle to be solved for your own amusement. You arrange them and pair them and decide their fates in your own mind. First it was Mr. Elton for Harriet, and now it is this fantasy about Jane Fairfax and the Dixons.”

Emma started. “How did you know…?”

“Because I know you, Emma,” he said, his voice softening, but only from anger to a kind of deep, sorrowful regret. “I see how your mind works. And because Frank Churchill is not so subtle a conspirator as he imagines. His looks and whispers to you are plain enough. He flatters you by pretending to share in your superior understanding, and you allow him to lead you into folly and unkindness. He is not a man to be trusted. His charm is a mask for a careless and selfish nature.”

“He is clever, and amusing, and he sees the world as I do!” she defended, feeling as though she were defending a part of herself. “You are prejudiced against him, as you are prejudiced in favour of Jane Fairfax. Why do you defend her so? Why does her situation affect you so deeply? First your strange anxiety this morning, now this… this lecture. One would almost think you had some particular, personal interest in her.”

The old, absurd suspicion flickered again, and in her anger and confusion, she spoke it aloud, a taunt to wound him as he had wounded her. “Perhaps you are the secret-keeper, Mr. Knightley. Perhaps you are the one in love with Jane Fairfax.”

She expected an instant, indignant denial. She expected him to laugh at the absurdity. But he did neither. He simply stood there, looking at her, and the colour slowly rose in his face. He looked not angry, but… exposed. He held her gaze for a long moment, and in his eyes she saw not the brotherly affection or the scolding impatience she had known her entire life, but something else, something deeper and more turbulent, an emotion she could not name but which made her heart stop in her chest.

Then he seemed to recover himself. He gave a short, mirthless laugh. “You have a wild imagination, Emma. One day it will get you into real trouble.” He turned and walked towards the door.

“Is that all you have to say?” she asked, her own voice sounding thin and strange to her ears.

He paused with his hand on the door, his back still to her. “I have said more than enough. It seems I have said too much. I will only say this: be careful. Frank Churchill is not the man you think he is. And Jane Fairfax deserves your compassion, not your suspicion. Try to find it in your heart to be a friend to her, not a detective.”

And then he was gone. The room was silent, save for the crackling of the fire. The perfect satisfaction with which Emma had entered her house was shattered into a thousand pieces. She stood quite still, staring at the door through which he had passed. The world, which had seemed so clear and orderly just an hour ago, was suddenly a bewildering and uncomfortable place. She was vexed with Mr. Knightley beyond measure; his accusations were unjust, his interference intolerable. And yet, beneath her anger, a cold seed of doubt had been planted. Not a doubt about Jane Fairfax and the Dixons; on that she was still certain, for to doubt that would be to doubt Frank Churchill, and to doubt her own sagacity. No, the doubt was about something else entirely. It was the memory of the look in Mr. Knightley’s eyes when she had made her accusation; a look that had not refuted her, but had, in some terrifying and incomprehensible way, almost confirmed the most ridiculous idea that had ever entered her head.

Emma sought her own room, not to think, for her mind was in too great a state of tumult for any coherent thought, but to escape from the possibility of seeing anyone, of having to speak, to smile, to pretend that the world was the same as it had been that morning. The very walls of Hartfield, which usually offered such comfort and security, seemed now to press in upon her. His words echoed in her ears—not the final, dismissive ones, but the silence that had preceded them, a silence more damning than any protestation could have been. And his face; she could see it still, the colour rising, the look of a man caught utterly off his guard, whose deepest secret had been by some unlucky chance dragged into the light.

She began to pace the floor, her hands clasped tight before her. Vexation warred with a feeling so new and unwelcome she could not put a name to it. He was unjust! He was severe! To accuse her of a want of compassion—her, who was always thinking of others, who had done so much for Harriet, who took such care of her father! And to champion Jane Fairfax, a girl who was all reserve and artifice, whose very presence was a quiet reproach to the cheerfulness of Highbury society. It was insupportable.

But her anger could not long hold its ground against the memory of that look. She tried to reason it away, to find another explanation. Perhaps it was not love, but pity, a very deep and particular pity that he felt for Jane’s circumstances. Perhaps her accusation had shocked him by its very crudeness, and he had coloured from embarrassment on Jane’s behalf, not his own. Yes, that must be it. Mr. Knightley, with his fine sense of propriety and his generous heart, would naturally be distressed by such a coarse and unfounded suggestion. He felt for Jane as a man of principle feels for any woman in a dependent and delicate situation. It was a brotherly concern, magnified by the particular hardships of her case.

And yet… and yet, the more she repeated this comforting logic to herself, the less she believed it. It did not account for the turbulence in his eyes, for that fleeting expression of profound, personal pain. It did not account for the way he had turned from her, as if the sight of her was suddenly more than he could bear.

She sat down, her mind beginning its unwilling work of reviewing the past. Every instance of Mr. Knightley’s notice of Jane Fairfax, which before had seemed nothing more than the common civilities of a gentleman, now took on a new and dreadful significance. His sending them his last store of apples—was it truly for Mrs. Bates, or was it a pretext to provide a delicacy for her niece? His anxious inquiries after her health, so loud and insistent at the window—was that merely the concern of a neighbour, or the pointed solicitude of a lover? He had always defended her, she recalled. When Emma had criticised Jane’s reserve, he had spoken of her superior education and unfortunate circumstances. When Emma had made light of her accomplishments, he had been quick to praise them. At the time, Emma had seen it as his usual love of contradiction, his habit of taking the opposite side in any argument with her. Now, she saw it as the consistent, quiet defence of the woman who possessed his affections.

The pianoforte! The whole business of the pianoforte! He had been almost as curious as she, but his curiosity had taken a different turn. He had not been looking for a secret lover in Ireland, but had seemed to fix upon Colonel Campbell, as if to find the most respectable, the most plausible, source for the gift. Was he trying to convince himself, as well as others, that there was no rival? And his conversation with her in the garden at Hartfield, when he had warned her against matchmaking for Mr. Elton—he had spoken then of men of sense not wanting silly wives. He had praised her for having sense, but had he been thinking of another, of a woman whose understanding and accomplishments were universally admired? A woman like Jane Fairfax?

A cold dread, entirely separate from her wounded pride, began to creep over her. If this were true, it explained everything. It explained his severity towards her own speculations, his unusual agitation, his constant, almost paternal watchfulness over Jane’s welfare. It explained why he, a man of thirty-seven, a man of such standing and sense, had never married. He was waiting. But for what? For Jane to be free of her obligations? For his own feelings to be returned?

She felt a pang of something so sharp and bitter, it was almost jealousy. But it was a jealousy of a kind she had never conceived. She was not in love with Mr. Knightley; such an idea had never for a moment entered her head. He was her brother, her friend, her guide. But he was hers. He belonged to Hartfield, to her father, to her. The idea of his affections being so deeply engaged elsewhere, and so secretly, felt like a betrayal. It made her world unsteady. If she could be so wrong about Mr. Knightley, whom she had known her entire life, how could she be certain of anything?

Her thoughts were interrupted by a gentle tap at the door. It was her father.
“My dear, are you quite well? You missed your luncheon, and Mrs. Hodges was concerned. I thought perhaps the walk had been too much for you.”
“I am perfectly well, papa, thank you,” she said, composing her features into a smile. “I was merely occupied with a letter.”
“Ah, a letter. Very good. As long as you are not chilled. The wind had a very brisk feel to it when Mr. Knightley left. Such a hurry he was in. He is always in a hurry when he goes to Kingston. A very busy man, Mr. Knightley.”
Emma felt a fresh wave of vexation. “Yes, papa. He has a great deal to occupy him.”
“He does indeed. And always so good to us. The apples, you know, my dear. I cannot forget the apples. All of them! We must have him to dinner very soon, Emma. He has not dined with us for an age. We must show him we are not ungrateful.”
“Yes, papa,” said Emma, her voice a little faint. “We must certainly have him to dinner.”
The prospect of seeing him, of having to sit opposite him at table, of watching him, knowing what she now suspected, was almost more than she could contemplate.

For the next few days, Emma’s spirits were in a state of curious depression. She told herself it was disappointment in Mr. Knightley’s friendship, and nothing more. She had always relied on his judgment, even when she contested it. To find that judgment clouded by a secret partiality was a blow to her esteem for him. And she was hurt, deeply hurt, by his parting words. He had made her feel foolish and unkind, and Emma Woodhouse was not accustomed to feeling either.

She tried to rally her spirits by thinking of Frank Churchill. He, at least, was all that was agreeable. He understood her, he shared her perceptions, he admired her. His attentions at the Bateses’ had been most gratifying. He had sought her opinion, shared his amusement with her, and made it plain that he preferred her company to all others. And yet, even this comfort was not unalloyed. A small, disloyal thought intruded: his teasing of Jane Fairfax, which had seemed such harmless wit at the time, now appeared in a less favourable light. If Mr. Knightley was indeed in love with Jane, then Frank Churchill’s sport was not merely impertinent; it was cruel. He was tormenting a woman who was not only unhappy in her circumstances, but who was also the object of affection for the best man in their society. Emma felt a flush of shame, as if she herself had been an accomplice. She had laughed. She had encouraged him with her whispers.

She was so lost in these uncomfortable reflections one morning that she was quite startled by the arrival of Mrs. Weston and her stepson, who had walked over from Randalls for a morning call.
“My dear Emma,” cried Mrs. Weston, “we have come to persuade you and your father to join us for a small party on Tuesday evening. Nothing grand, you know. Just ourselves, and the Bateses and Miss Fairfax, and Mr. Knightley.”
Emma’s heart gave a little jump. “That is very kind of you, Mrs. Weston. I am sure papa and I should be delighted.” She said it with as much alacrity as she could muster, for her first instinct was to refuse. But her second was a powerful curiosity. She had to see them together. She had to observe.
“Excellent!” said Frank Churchill, his eyes sparkling. “I was hoping you would agree. A whole evening without your society would be an age. And we shall have music, I trust? Miss Fairfax must play for us again.” He glanced at Emma, a conspiratorial smile playing on his lips, as if to say, ‘We shall have more sport with our mysterious friend.’
Emma could not return the smile. She gave only a weak imitation of it and changed the subject. “And how did you find the party at the Coles’, Mr. Churchill? I hear it was a great success.”
He launched into a lively description of the evening, full of droll observations and witty remarks, and though Emma laughed at all the proper places, her mind was elsewhere. She was already in Mrs. Weston’s drawing-room, watching Mr. Knightley’s face as he looked at Jane Fairfax.

Tuesday evening came, and Emma dressed with her usual care, but with none of her usual pleasure. She felt as though she were going not to a party, but to a tribunal, where some final, painful judgment would be passed. As she and her father were conveyed to Randalls, she steeled herself for the encounter. She would be calm, she would be observant, and she would betray nothing of her inner turmoil.

They were the last to arrive. The little party was already assembled, and as Emma entered, her eyes immediately sought out the two people who occupied all her thoughts. Jane Fairfax was seated near Mrs. Weston, looking elegant, as always, but pale and tired. Mr. Knightley was standing by the fireplace, talking to Miss Bates. He looked up as Emma entered, and their eyes met for a brief moment. He gave her a polite, formal bow, but there was a coolness in his manner, a distance she had never felt before. It was the bow of a mere acquaintance, and it cut her to the quick.

Dinner was a trial. Emma was seated between Frank Churchill and her father, and while the former kept up a constant stream of pleasantries, requiring little more from her than an assenting smile, she was acutely aware of Mr. Knightley, seated opposite. He spoke very little, directing most of his conversation to Mrs. Weston. He did not once address Jane Fairfax directly, nor did he seem to look at her. Emma began to wonder if she had imagined the whole thing. Perhaps his coolness was directed only at her, as a just punishment for her impertinent accusation. Perhaps his feelings for Jane were, after all, nothing more than she had first supposed. She felt a slight lifting of her spirits.

After dinner, when the ladies had withdrawn and the gentlemen rejoined them, Frank Churchill, ever the master of ceremonies, declared that they must have some entertainment.
“Not cards, I beg of you,” he said, with a theatrical shudder. “So dull! So predictable! Let us have something to exercise the wits. Let us play a game of letters.”
He produced a set of ivory letters from a small box he had apparently brought for the purpose, and proposed they form words from a jumble he would spill upon the table. The proposal was met with general approbation, and soon the whole party, with the exception of Mr. Woodhouse who was dozing by the fire, was gathered around the table.
The game began with much laughter and sport. Frank Churchill was in his element, quick, clever, and full of playful challenges. Emma, caught up in the moment, found her spirits reviving. She was quick too, and their minds seemed to dance together, forming words and trading jests.
After several rounds, Frank Churchill pushed a collection of letters towards the centre of the table. “Now,” he said, his voice laced with mischief, “a challenge for the whole company. One word, a very simple word, may be formed from these letters. But it proves surprisingly elusive.”
The letters were jumbled, but Emma’s quick eye soon unscrambled them. They spelled ‘blunder.’ At the same moment, she saw Jane Fairfax, who had been quiet throughout the game, look down at the letters, a faint flush rising in her cheeks. Frank Churchill was looking directly at Jane, a smile of triumph in his eyes. Emma understood at once. It was a reference to some mistake, some slip of the tongue Jane had made. It was a piece of private history being used as a public torment.
Before anyone else could speak, Miss Bates exclaimed, “Blunder! Is it blunder? Oh, dear! I am always making blunders. I remember when Perry told me….”
Her voice was cut short by Mr. Knightley. He had been standing a little apart from the group, observing, but now he moved forward. He did not look at Frank Churchill. He did not look at Jane. He addressed himself to the table at large.
“This is a clever game,” he said, his voice perfectly even, “but a little hard on the eyes by candlelight. Mrs. Weston, you promised us music. Perhaps Miss Fairfax would be so good as to play for us now? I know my friend, Mrs. Cole, was quite disappointed not to hear her the other evening.”
The suggestion was so timely, so natural, that it seemed to arise from nothing but a desire for music. It at once put an end to the game and to Miss Bates’s reminiscences. It rescued Jane from her embarrassment without drawing the least attention to it.
Mrs. Weston eagerly seconded the motion, and Jane, with a look of quiet gratitude towards Mr. Knightley that did not escape Emma’s notice, rose and went to the pianoforte.
Frank Churchill’s face clouded for a moment, like a spoiled child whose toy has been taken away. He had been outmanoeuvred, and by Mr. Knightley, of all people. He quickly recovered his good humour, however, and went to stand by the pianoforte, ready to turn the pages for Jane.

Jane played beautifully, with a skill and feeling that commanded the attention of the whole room. When she had finished one piece, Frank Churchill was quick to place another before her, leaning in to say something in a low voice that Emma could not hear, but which caused Jane to colour and falter for a moment.
It was then that Mr. Knightley, who had taken a seat near Mr. Woodhouse, rose again. He walked over to the pianoforte, not to the side where Frank Churchill stood, but to the other.
“That was beautifully played, Miss Fairfax,” he said, his voice clear and kind. He did not praise her with the extravagant compliments of Frank Churchill, but with a simple sincerity that gave his words great weight. “There is a passage in the middle section that reminds me of a piece by Clementi. It has the same strength and precision.”
He spoke to her for a few minutes about music, about composers, about technique. His manner was that of an intelligent man speaking to an equal. He asked her opinion, listened to her answers, and treated her not as a pretty object of amusement, but as a person of substance and taste. Frank Churchill was effectively excluded from the conversation and, after a moment of looking vexed, he sauntered away to engage Emma in some trivial gossip.

But Emma could not attend to him. She was watching, with a painful, fixed intensity, the scene at the pianoforte. She saw the way Jane’s tense shoulders relaxed as she spoke to Mr. Knightley. She saw the genuine, unforced smile that lit her face, a smile of intellectual pleasure and relief. And she saw the look on Mr. Knightley’s face as he listened to her. It was a look of deep, quiet, and unguarded admiration. It was a look of profound tenderness.

In that moment, all her doubts vanished, to be replaced by a horrible, aching certainty. It was true. All of it. The wild, ridiculous idea she had thrown at him in anger was the plain, simple truth. He loved Jane Fairfax.

The rest of the evening passed in a blur. She spoke when spoken to, smiled when a smile was required, but her inner world had been thrown into chaos. The foundation of her happiness had been her secure and settled place in the world, and the most immoveable part of that foundation had always been Mr. Knightley. Now, he was lost to her. Not to marriage, for that she could have borne, eventually; but to a secret, long-held affection that she had been too blind, too vain, too wrapped up in her own clever games, to see. He had not confided in her. He had not sought her sympathy. He had watched her make a fool of herself over Jane Fairfax, and had borne it all in silence.

When the carriage was announced, she rose with a feeling of profound relief. In the bustle of departure, Frank Churchill took her hand.
“A delightful evening,” he whispered, “though our friend Knightley is in a very solemn mood. He seems to have appointed himself Miss Fairfax’s guardian. Did you see him rush to her defence? Very chivalrous, I am sure.”
Emma pulled her hand away. “He was being a gentleman, Mr. Churchill,” she said, her voice colder than she intended. “Which is more than can be said for everyone.”
She left him looking surprised and a little offended, and took her father’s arm. In the carriage on the way home, Mr. Woodhouse remarked, “A very pleasant evening, my dear. And so kind of Mr. Knightley to think of the draft from the window. He moved Miss Fairfax’s chair himself. He is always thinking of such things. He knew she had a delicate throat.”
“Yes, papa,” Emma murmured into the darkness. “He thinks of everything.”
Back in her own room at Hartfield, the fire long since burnt down to embers, Emma did not pace. She sat in a chair, perfectly still, and looked into the dying glow. The vexation was gone. The anger was gone. In their place was a hollow ache, a sense of loss so profound it frightened her. She had been playing. She had been amusing herself with schemes and conjectures, arranging the lives of those around her as if they were pieces in a game. She had not understood that their hearts were real, that their feelings were deep, and that her cleverness was a kind of cruelty. And Mr. Knightley, her friend, her only true critic, the man whose approval she valued above all others, had seen it all. He had seen her folly, and had turned away from her, towards the quiet, suffering, and superior woman he loved. The perfect satisfaction she had once felt in her own world was not merely shattered; it seemed now a foolish vanity she could hardly remember having felt. The world was not clear and orderly at all; it was a place of secrets and sorrows, and she, Emma Woodhouse, had been living in it like a blind and careless child.

The following morning brought with it no dissipation of the cloud which had settled upon her spirits; rather, it seemed to have condensed during the night into a fine, persistent mist, chilling every thought and dulling the prospect of the day ahead. She descended to the breakfast parlour to find her father already seated, and her first effort was to assume a cheerfulness she was very far from feeling.

“Good morning, papa. Did you sleep well? I trust the little excitement of last night did not disturb your rest.”

“Not at all, my love, not at all. A very quiet evening, on the whole. Mrs. Weston is always so considerate. But you, my dear Emma; you look a little pale. I was just saying to Perry yesterday, the evenings are growing cooler. I hope you have not taken a chill.”

“I have no chill, I thank you, papa. I am perfectly well, only a little tired perhaps.”

She busied herself with the tea-urn, glad of an activity that allowed her to turn away. Her father’s solicitude, usually a source of fond amusement, was now a small, sharp instrument of pain. Every word of kindness felt like a reproach for the unkindness she now saw so clearly in her own heart. To have his concern, when she had so lately forfeited Mr. Knightley’s esteem, seemed a bitter injustice.

The morning papers held no interest; her plans for the poor of Highbury, which usually occupied her thoughts after breakfast, seemed an exercise in self-congratulation. What was the good of her charities, if her daily conduct was marked by such vanity and want of feeling? The approbation of the world, which she had so valued, now seemed as nothing compared to the approbation of the one man whose judgment had always been her truest guide. She had lost it; and she felt, with a sinking of the heart, that she had lost it forever.

She determined upon a walk. To remain within doors was to be prey to every melancholy reflection; to be in motion, in the fresh air, might afford some relief, however temporary. She told her father she was going only so far as the shrubbery, to see how the new plantings were faring, and equipping herself with her bonnet and shawl, she stepped out into the mild autumn day. The grounds of Hartfield, usually a source of pride and pleasure, offered no comfort. Every familiar vista, every well-tended border, spoke of a life of ease and consequence she felt she no longer deserved.

She had not walked far before she saw a figure approaching from the direction of the village. It was Mrs. Weston, come, no doubt, for a comfortable morning chat, a review of the previous evening’s events, and a word in praise of her excellent step-son. Emma’s first impulse was to turn back, to feign an errand in another direction; but she checked herself. This was cowardice. If she was to bear her new unhappiness, she must bear it in society, and not merely in solitude. She must learn to govern her countenance, and to speak on indifferent subjects when her mind was in turmoil. It was a discipline she had never needed, and she braced herself for the trial.

“My dear Emma!” cried Mrs. Weston, her face beaming with affection. “I had hoped I might find you enjoying the air. I have just been to Randalls and was on my way to you. What a very eventful little visit we had yesterday!”

“Eventful indeed,” replied Emma, attempting a smile. “It seems Miss Fairfax’s mysterious benefactor has a taste for grand gestures.”

“Grand, indeed! A pianoforte of that quality! And Frank was in such spirits, was he not? He does so love a little mystery. I confess I was quite struck by Mr. Knightley’s manner yesterday. Such marked attention!”

Emma’s heart gave a painful throb. She kept her eyes fixed on a distant point of the landscape. “Mr. Knightley is always the soul of consideration where anyone’s health is concerned. He knew Miss Fairfax had been unwell.”

“Oh! yes, of course, his kindness to the Bateses is proverbial,” said Mrs. Weston, though with a slight hesitation that suggested she had perhaps meant something more. “But his inquiries were so particular, so urgent. And did you notice how he spoke of her dancing? It was a very pointed compliment, I thought, especially after Frank had been teasing her so.”

“He wished to be kind,” Emma said, her voice betraying a slight tremor which she instantly sought to conceal by a cough. “He could not like to see her distressed.”

“No, certainly not. He has a very good heart,” Mrs. Weston agreed. “And as for Frank, you must not mind his nonsense, Emma. He meant no harm. He is like a playful puppy; he cannot resist a little sport. I hope you were not displeased with him.”

Emma felt a flush of shame. Only the day before, she had been a willing partner in this ‘sport.’ “Mr. Churchill’s spirits are very high,” she said evasively. “It is a happy disposition.”

“He thinks the world of you, Emma. He was saying to me only this morning how your wit and liveliness are the chief delight of Highbury. He quite longs for the next ball at the Crown, that he might claim you for the first two dances again.”

This praise, which might once have gratified her, now felt like a burden. Frank Churchill’s admiration was entwined with the memory of their shared amusement at Jane Fairfax’s expense; an amusement that now seemed to her a piece of odious cruelty. She saw, with a clarity that astonished her, that Mr. Knightley’s displeasure with Frank Churchill had been entirely justified. He had seen the want of delicacy, the want of feeling, from the very first.

“You are quiet this morning, my dear,” Mrs. Weston observed, her tone shifting to one of gentle concern. “Are you sure you are quite well?”

“Perfectly, I assure you,” Emma rallied herself to say. “I was only thinking that I have been a poor neighbour to Miss Fairfax of late. With the arrival of her instrument, and all this speculation, she must be in a very trying position. I believe I shall call upon her this very morning.”

Mrs. Weston looked surprised, and then pleased. “That is very amiable of you, Emma! I am sure she will take it kindly. Your company is just the thing to cheer her. Shall I walk with you? I was not intending a visit myself, but I should be happy to accompany you to the door.”

“No, no, I thank you,” Emma said, a little too quickly. “I believe I should prefer to go alone.”

She could not bear to have Mrs. Weston’s observant eyes upon her during the visit. She needed to be mistress of herself, and that would be difficult enough without a witness. Mrs. Weston, though perhaps a little puzzled by her manner, readily acquiesced, and after a few more minutes of conversation, they parted, Mrs. Weston to her home, and Emma, with a deep breath and a new, painful resolution, towards the village.

Her resolution was this: she must see Jane Fairfax not as a rival, nor as an object of pity, but as the woman Mr. Knightley loved. She must study her with new eyes, and try to understand what he saw. She must, if it were possible, befriend her. It was a penance, and a severe one; but it was the only course that offered itself to her wounded pride and her guilty conscience. To continue to suspect and dislike Jane Fairfax would be to continue in the very error that had cost her so dearly.

As she approached the Bateses’ lodgings, her fortitude almost failed her. The prospect of Miss Bates’s unending stream of chatter, of Mrs. Bates’s placid deafness, and of Jane Fairfax’s elegant reserve, was daunting. She, who had always entered this humble apartment with the unconscious condescension of a superior, now felt like a petitioner, coming to sue for a peace she had herself broken.

She was admitted, and found the scene much as she had anticipated. The small parlour was in a state of happy commotion, centred entirely around the magnificent new pianoforte. Miss Bates met her at the door, her face a picture of bewildered delight.

“Miss Woodhouse! How truly kind, how very obliging of you to call! We were just—I was just saying to Jane, ‘I wonder if any of our good friends will call to-day,’ and here you are! You find us in such a delightful clutter. Pray, come in, come in. Mama, here is Miss Woodhouse, so very good as to come and see the instrument herself. You know you were longing to thank her for her kind inquiries yesterday.”

Mrs. Bates, from her corner, offered a smile and a nod. Jane Fairfax, who had been sorting music at the pianoforte, rose and curtsied. There was a faint colour in her cheeks, but her manner was composed.

“You are very good to call, Miss Woodhouse,” she said, in her low, musical voice.

“I was sorry our visit was so interrupted yesterday,” Emma began, her own voice sounding stiff and unnatural in her ears. “I wished to offer my congratulations properly. It is a beautiful instrument.”

“Oh, is it not?” exclaimed Miss Bates. “A Broadwood, of the very first quality. And the tone! Jane, my dear, you must play something for Miss Woodhouse. She has not heard it properly yet. Just one of the new waltzes. Or perhaps ‘Robin Adair’? Mr. Knightley was so pleased with ‘Robin Adair’ yesterday, was he not? He said it was a particular favourite. So very gratifying, you know, when a gentleman of his taste expresses a preference.”

At the mention of Mr. Knightley, Emma felt the colour rise in her own cheeks. She glanced at Jane, and saw that her blush had deepened. Was it pleasure? Or was it the painful consciousness of a shared secret, now being innocently alluded to by her aunt? The latter, Emma decided. How dreadful for her, to have her most private feelings broadcast in this heedless way.

“Perhaps Miss Fairfax is tired of playing,” Emma said, coming, to her own astonishment, to Jane’s rescue. “She was so obliging to us all yesterday.”

Jane looked at her with an expression of surprise, and something that might have been gratitude. “I am not at all tired,” she said. “But it is you who are the visitor, Miss Woodhouse. The choice of music should be yours.”

This gentle courtesy was another pang. Emma felt herself to be clumsy and ungracious in comparison. “I should like whatever you please,” she murmured, and sat down, determined to be a quiet and attentive listener.

Jane played a short, plaintive Irish air, one of the new melodies that had arrived with the instrument. Her touch was exquisite, her expression full of a feeling that went far beyond the mere notes on the page. Emma listened, and a strange transformation took place in her perceptions. She was no longer listening for technical skill, or for a performance to be judged. She was hearing the expression of a heart, and she imagined it was a heart full of a secret, anxious, but profound happiness. This was the music Jane played for Mr. Knightley in her thoughts; this was the sensibility that he, who was all sense and sincerity himself, must admire. She began to see. She began to understand.

When the piece was finished, there was a moment of silence. Even Miss Bates seemed to have been momentarily hushed by the beauty of it.

“How very lovely, Jane,” Emma said, and her voice was perfectly sincere. “You play with such feeling.”

Jane looked up, and for the first time, she met Emma’s gaze directly, without reserve. “Thank you,” she said simply. “It is a sad little tune, but I am fond of it.”

“‘Sad little tunes’ are often the most beautiful,” Emma replied. “They speak a truth that happier melodies cannot.”

A look of understanding passed between them, so fleeting that Emma almost doubted she had seen it. But it had been there: a momentary recognition of shared sorrow, or at least of a shared capacity for it. In that moment, Emma felt a stirring of something that was not pity, nor penance, but the beginning of a genuine respect.

Miss Bates, recovering her powers of speech, launched into a fresh torrent of thanks and praise. “So very true! What a charming sentiment, Miss Woodhouse! I must remember to tell Mr. Knightley you said that. He appreciates such thoughts. He has such a mind! He was here himself not an hour ago! Can you believe it? So very kind. He brought my mother a basket of his very finest pears from the Donwell Abbey trees. He said he remembered she was fond of them. And he would not stay, would not hear of being thanked. He only asked, ‘How is Miss Fairfax this morning? I trust she is rested?’ and when I assured him you were, my dear, he seemed quite satisfied and went on his way. Was it not thoughtful, Miss Woodhouse? He thinks of everything.”

Every word was a fresh confirmation. He had called that very morning, solely to inquire after Jane. He had brought a gift for her invalid grandmother. These were the attentions of a man deeply and seriously attached. Emma felt the cold hand of certainty clutch at her heart, but she bore it with an outward composure that surprised herself.

“Nobody has a better heart than Mr. Knightley,” she said, and was pleased that her voice was steady. “He is the truest of friends.”

“The truest of friends!” echoed Miss Bates. “You have expressed it exactly! I always say, he is the very pattern of a friend. And a landlord! William Larkins says there is not such another in the whole county.”

The conversation, or rather, Miss Bates’s monologue, continued in this vein for some time. Emma sat through it all, making the proper responses at the proper intervals, but her mind was elsewhere. She was looking at Jane Fairfax, who had resumed her task of sorting the music, her graceful head bent over the pages. There was a serenity about her now that had not been there before. The deep blush of consciousness had been replaced by a soft, settled glow. It was, Emma thought, the look of a woman who is assured of the affection of the man she loves. The arrival of the pianoforte, which had caused such a painful stir, had perhaps been followed by a private word, a quiet look from Mr. Knightley that had set all her anxieties at rest.

The visit had accomplished its purpose. It had been painful, profoundly so, but it had been clarifying. The vague, terrible suspicion of the night before had hardened into an accepted fact. Mr. Knightley loved Jane Fairfax, and Jane Fairfax, it was now clear, returned his affection. They were suited: in sense, in principle, in seriousness of mind. He, with his unimpeachable character and high standing; she, with her elegance, her accomplishments, her quiet fortitude in the face of adversity. It was a perfect match. A match he had chosen, in secret, without a word to the one person who had believed herself his most intimate friend.

The pain of this last thought was the sharpest of all. She had not only lost him; she had been shut out. While she had been playing her foolish games, he had been engaged in the most serious business of his life, and had not seen fit to trust her with even a hint of it. He had judged her, found her wanting, and turned elsewhere for the companionship of the heart.

She rose to take her leave, feeling that she could not endure another moment.

“You are not going so soon?” cried Miss Bates. “Why, you have hardly been here a quarter of an hour! Jane, my dear, Miss Woodhouse is going.”

“I have an engagement at home,” Emma said, the excuse sounding feeble even to her. “I only wished to pay my respects.”

“So very kind,” Miss Bates repeated, following her to the door. “We are so obliged to you for the call. And for your kind words about the music. I shall be sure to tell everyone how much you admired Jane’s playing. And what you said about sad tunes! So very poetical.”

Emma escaped at last, and walked back towards Hartfield with a slow, measured step. The world had not changed. The sun shone as it had before; the houses of Highbury stood as they always had. But for her, everything was altered. The foundation of her world was gone, and she was left standing amidst the ruins of her own vanity. She had wished for a new object of interest, for something to stir the placid waters of her life. She had it now. She had a sorrow, and a secret, and a long, hard lesson in humility to learn. The age of play was over. The business of life, with all its unwelcome truths and painful realities, had begun.

Emma walked on, not with the light, assured step of Miss Woodhouse of Hartfield, but with the heavy tread of one who carries a new and unwelcome burden. The air, which a short while ago had seemed so full of the promise of spring, now felt thin and sharp, and the familiar sights of Highbury, the Crown inn, Ford’s, the very turn in the road towards her own home, struck her with a sense of estrangement. It was as if she were seeing them for the first time through the eyes of a stranger, a lesser person who had no established place of consequence, no undisputed claim to happiness. She had been dethroned; not from any seat of power that others could perceive, but from the quiet, absolute monarchy of her own self-approbation. She had believed herself perceptive, and she had been blind; she had fancied herself important, and had been proved of no account.

The pain of it was a dull, steady ache, a thing quite different from the fleeting vexations to which she was accustomed. This was not the irritation of Mrs. Elton’s forwardness, nor the transient shame of a poorly judged remark. This was a wound to the very centre of her being. Mr. Knightley, who had been the fixed point in her life, the one person whose good opinion was the unspoken standard by which all others were measured, had made the most significant choice of his life without her. He had not only chosen another, he had conducted his courtship in secret, concealing it from her as one might conceal a matter from a child, or from one whose judgment was not to be trusted. And in this, she could not, in her most secret heart, blame him. Had she not proved herself, time and again, to be flighty, and prejudiced, and given to foolish matchmaking? Why should he confide the serious affections of his heart to a friend who treated love as a game?

As she approached the shrubbery of Hartfield, the sight of the house itself, so long the symbol of her security and comfort, brought a fresh pang. It was there, within those walls, that he had scolded her, guided her, and laughed with her, for as long as she could remember. She had taken his constant presence for granted, as she had the sunshine and the air. Now she saw that it had been a privilege, and one she had forfeited. The future unrolled before her as a long, grey vista: Mr. Knightley, married; Mr. Knightley, bringing his wife to Donwell; Mrs. Knightley—Jane Fairfax!—presiding over his table, walking with him in his woods, becoming the first object of his society. And she, Emma Woodhouse, would be a mere neighbour, invited out of propriety, her visits becoming less frequent, her intimacy dwindling to a polite formality. The loss was so immense, so absolute, that she felt a physical difficulty in drawing breath.

She entered the hall and was met by her father, who had been watching for her from the drawing-room window.
“My dear Emma, there you are at last. I was beginning to be quite alarmed. I was sure you would take a chill. You were gone a very long time. I hope you did not find it damp in Mrs. Bates’s little rooms. They are so exposed to the draught from the street.”
“Not at all, Papa, I assure you. We were very comfortable,” said Emma, forcing a brightness she was far from feeling.
“And how were the poor Bateses? And poor Miss Fairfax? So very sad, her situation. Mr. Knightley was saying only the other day—it was just here, by the fireplace—that he pities her exceedingly.”
Every mention of his name was a fresh torment. “They are all quite well, Papa. Indeed, Miss Fairfax seemed in better spirits than usual.”
“Ah, that is the pianoforte. A new instrument is a very cheerful thing. Though I hope she will not overtire herself. Invalids should be very careful not to over-exert. You look a little tired yourself, my love. You have lost some of your colour.”
“I am perfectly well, Papa. Perhaps the walk was a little longer than I am used to.”
She busied herself with helping him back to his chair and arranging his shawl, the small, familiar actions a welcome shield against the necessity of talking. She could not be sad in his presence; his tender anxiety would not permit it. Her own unhappiness must be a secret, her first real secret of the heart, and she was already finding it a heavy one to keep.

She passed the remainder of the day in a state of forced composure, answering her father’s gentle remarks, reading to him from a volume of sermons, and taking her customary turn about the shrubbery. But all the while, her mind was elsewhere, replaying the morning’s events, examining every word, every look, for fresh confirmation of the dreadful truth. Frank Churchill’s pointed speech, Jane’s blush and smile of secret delight, and above all, Mr. Knightley’s singular behaviour outside the window—his loud, determined inquiry after Miss Fairfax, and then his hasty retreat. It all fitted together with a terrible, irrefutable logic. He had sent the instrument, and his subsequent visit was to gauge its reception, to see her, but without the awkwardness of a formal call upon his beloved in so public a setting. His questions, overheard by all, were a lover’s anxieties, disguised as polite concern. Emma’s imagination, once it had found this key, unlocked every mystery. His praise of her dancing, his defence of her character, his very compassion for her situation—it was all the progress of love.

She determined upon her own conduct. She would not betray herself. No one, not her father, not Mrs. Weston, and certainly not Mr. Knightley himself, should ever guess the depth of her mortification. She would be his friend; she would learn to see him and Jane Fairfax together and wish them happy. It was the only course of dignity, the only penance for her folly. She, who had been so free with her advice to others, so confident in her management of their affections, must now learn to manage her own. The task seemed Herculean. It would require a fortitude she had never possessed, but which she now saw embodied in the very woman who was the cause of her suffering. She must try to be more like Jane Fairfax: reserved, elegant, and self-possessed in the face of trial. It was a bitter irony, but a salutary one.

That evening, as she and her father were sitting quietly after tea, Mr. Knightley was announced. Emma’s heart gave a painful leap, and she was grateful for the dim candlelight which might hide the rush of colour to her cheeks. He had returned from Kingston, and called in on his way back to Donwell, as was his frequent custom.
“Well, Emma,” said he, after paying his respects to Mr. Woodhouse, “I did not have the pleasure of a single word with you this morning. You were all so engrossed with Churchill and his nonsense.”
His tone was the same as ever, frank and a little chiding. But to Emma, it sounded different now; it seemed the careful, practised tone of a man concealing a great deal.
“We were tolerably entertained,” she replied, her voice cooler than she had intended. “Mr. Frank Churchill has a lively imagination.”
“He has a talent for making mischief,” said Mr. Knightley, drawing a chair nearer to the fire, and to her. “His performance over that pianoforte was impertinent to a degree. To make such public sport of Miss Fairfax’s private concerns—I was astonished you could encourage him.”
Here was the usual lecture. But instead of feeling the familiar mix of amusement and resentment, Emma felt only a weary pain. He was defending Jane’s delicacy, of course.
“I doubt Miss Fairfax was much distressed,” Emma said, taking up her needlework. “She seemed, on the contrary, rather to enjoy the subject.”
“Enjoy it? Emma, how can you be so unfeeling? To have her obligations to the Campbells, or whoever the benefactor may be, so freely canvassed in company? She bore it with admirable composure, I grant you. She has a degree of self-command which I have rarely seen equalled. But she must have felt it deeply.”
Every word of praise for Jane was another turn of the screw. Emma’s needle moved faster. “You are a great champion for Miss Fairfax, Mr. Knightley,” she said, and the words came out with a little edge of ice.
He looked at her, surprised by her tone. “I am a champion for propriety and feeling, Emma. I should have thought you would be the same. There was a time when you would have been the first to protest such indelicacy on a friend’s behalf.”
The implied criticism, that she had somehow changed for the worse, was more than she could bear with grace. Was it her fault that he had shut her out from his confidence?
“My views of friendship have, perhaps, become a little less romantic,” she said, with a faint, brittle smile. “One learns that it is not always wise to meddle in the affairs of others.”
This was a direct hit, though he could not possibly understand its true aim. She meant to reproach him for his secrecy, but he could only take it as a reference to her own past follies with Harriet. He looked at her for a long moment, a serious, searching look that made her heart beat uncomfortably.
“That is a lesson hard-learned, for some,” he said at last, his voice gentler than before. “But I hope it does not lead you to become cynical. Or cold.”
He seemed about to say more, but Mr. Woodhouse stirred and spoke, asking him if he had found the roads to Kingston very dusty, and the conversation was turned into a safer channel. Mr. Knightley did not stay long. When he took his leave of her, there was a degree of gravity, almost of disappointment, in his manner that struck her. He was puzzled by her new reserve, she supposed. Let him be puzzled. It was better than being pitied. When he was gone, she felt a profound relief, immediately followed by an even more profound misery. This was to be her life now: a series of such guarded, painful encounters, until he was married and they scarcely met at all.

The next day brought Mrs. Weston, full of affection and a gentle curiosity. Frank had returned to Richmond, and she was at liberty to speak her mind more freely.
“I cannot say I was pleased with Frank yesterday, Emma,” she began, as soon as they were alone. “His behaviour towards Miss Fairfax was not what it should have been. It almost seemed as if he were trying to vex her.”
“He was only in fun, I imagine,” said Emma, listlessly.
“Perhaps. But it was not pleasant fun. And you, my dear Emma, you did not seem yourself at all. When Mr. Knightley was speaking so sharply at the window, you looked quite pale. I gave you a look, you know, but you shook your head. I have been thinking about it ever since. What did you mean by it? You still do not believe Mr. Knightley could be the one?”
Emma felt a sudden, desperate need to confide in someone, but pride and shame held her back. How could she confess that she had not only believed it, but that the belief was breaking her heart?
“Oh, it is not that,” she said, trying for a careless air. “I have given up conjecturing entirely. It is a fool’s game. I suppose I was merely tired of the whole subject. The pianoforte has been talked of quite enough.”
Mrs. Weston looked at her with concern. “You are not yourself, Emma. You have been looking out of spirits for two days. Is something vexing you? Have I said something? Has Frank?”
“No, no, nothing of the sort,” Emma insisted, patting her friend’s hand. “I believe I have a slight headache, that is all. Do not plague your good heart about me. Tell me, when do you expect Frank again?”
She succeeded in turning the conversation, but she knew she had not truly deceived her friend. Mrs. Weston left with her kind face full of unresolved anxiety. And Emma was left with the miserable conviction that in her attempt to hide her feelings, she was in danger of alienating the truest friends she possessed. Her new-found fortitude was proving to be a very isolating virtue.

In the days that followed, Highbury society was greatly occupied with the new pianoforte, and, by extension, with Miss Fairfax herself. Mrs. Elton, in particular, had taken her up with a suffocating enthusiasm. She was constantly at the Bates’s, proposing musical evenings, offering to introduce Jane to her acquaintance in Bath, and talking incessantly of her “protégée.”
“It is a charming instrument, to be sure,” she declared to Emma, during a morning call at Hartfield. “A Broadwood, is it not? Very handsome. A gift of Colonel Campbell’s, of course. So very attentive of him. One sees the military man in the precision of the whole affair. My brother, Mr. Suckling, has a man of business who arranges everything for him in just that style. ‘Do this,’ he says, and it is done. No fuss.”
Emma murmured some assent, thankful that Mrs. Elton’s theory of the matter diverted suspicion from the quarter where her own mind so painfully dwelt.
“We must have a musical evening at the Vicarage,” Mrs. Elton continued. “I shall preside, naturally, and Jane Fairfax will be my right hand. You must come, Miss Woodhouse. Though you do not play yourself, you may turn the pages for us. Or perhaps your little friend—what is her name? Miss Smith—perhaps she sings a little? We must all contribute what we can, you know. No passengers allowed on my ship!”
The woman’s vulgarity was almost a relief; it was a vexation so trivial that it served to distract from the greater one. Emma politely declined to enlist in Mrs. Elton’s musical navy, pleading her father’s dislike of evening parties.

A few days later, Mr. Knightley called again. He found Emma in the garden with Harriet, who, upon his approach, blushed and faltered and quickly made an excuse to go inside. Emma watched her go with a pang of pity. Poor Harriet’s hopeless attachment seemed a faint, sad echo of her own.
“You are driving away your companion,” said Mr. Knightley, joining her on the path.
“Harriet is a little shy,” Emma replied.
“She has been looking unhappy, I think. I met her in the lane yesterday, and she seemed much out of spirits. I hope you are not filling her head with any more nonsense, Emma.”
It was his old, familiar, scolding tone. A week ago, she would have risen to the bait, and they would have fallen into one of their customary, lively arguments. Now, she had no spirit for it.
“I believe I have given up the management of other people’s hearts,” she said quietly. “I have found it is an art in which I have no skill.”
He stopped and looked at her. “Emma, what is the matter? This is not like you. For the last week, you have been cold and distant. If I have offended you, I wish you would tell me how.”
His directness was disarming. She felt her resolve begin to tremble. How easy it would be to weep, to confess her misery and her folly. But what would that accomplish? It would only embarrass him and humiliate her. He was asking as a friend, concerned by her change of manner; he could have no idea of the real cause.
“You have not offended me,” she said, forcing herself to meet his gaze. “I have been… reflecting a little, that is all. Upon my own conduct. And I have found it wanting.”
This was true, at least. It was an explanation, if not the whole explanation.
He seemed struck by her seriousness. The searching look returned to his eyes. “We have all cause to do that from time to time,” he said, his voice losing its chiding edge. “But you must not be too severe upon yourself. Your heart is in the right place, Emma, even when your judgment errs.”
His kindness was almost her undoing. It was so much harder to bear than his scolding. To hear him praise her heart, when she felt it was breaking for love of him, was a refinement of torture.
“You are very good,” she said, her voice unsteady. She turned to look at a bed of early tulips, desperate for a reason not to look at him. “These will be very fine in another week. My father is so fond of them.”
He understood that she wished to change the subject. A silence fell between them, not the comfortable silence of old, but one filled with unspoken things. He spoke of business, of his tenants, of the affairs of the parish. She answered in monosyllables. The easy communion between them was gone, replaced by a careful, painful courtesy. When he left, she fled to her room and gave way to the tears she had been holding back for days. She had not been cold enough, or she had not been civil enough. She did not know how to be. She only knew that every meeting was a fresh ordeal, and that the long, hard lesson in humility she had set herself was more difficult than she could ever have imagined. The business of life, it seemed, was a very hard business indeed.

The indulgence of her sorrow, she soon found, was a luxury she could ill afford; for the duties of Hartfield, though gentle, were unceasing, and she could not long neglect her father without bringing upon herself the tenderest of reproaches, which were, in their way, more difficult to bear than the harshest censure. Mr. Woodhouse, having observed her red-rimmed eyes at luncheon, had immediately taken alarm, not for her heart, but for her head.

“My dear, you have a cold coming on; I am certain of it. We must have some of Mrs. Bates’s excellent water-gruel tonight. Not that I mean to imply that our own cook, Serle, is at all deficient; but Mrs. Bates’s has a certain—a certain quality I have always found most beneficial. Perry said last week that draughts were the very worst thing for the constitution at this time of year, and you were sitting by the window with Mr. Knightley only yesterday. I knew no good would come of it.”

“Indeed, papa, I am perfectly well,” Emma had assured him, attempting a cheerfulness she was far from feeling. “I believe I am only a little tired.”

“Tired! Yes, of course you are. All this excitement is too much for you. A new pianoforte for Miss Fairfax, and Mr. Knightley calling, and Mrs. Weston and Mr. Frank Churchill making such a long visit. It is too much for any of us. We shall have a very quiet evening, my dear, with just a little music—if you feel up to it—and no company. I could not bear to think of you exerting yourself further.”

This was a future she could contemplate with something like relief. A quiet evening, with no one to observe her, no one to speak to but her father, was precisely what she craved. The prospect of any society, particularly a society that included Mr. Knightley, was now attended with a dread she had never before known. How was she to act? How was she to seem at ease, when every nerve was stretched to the breaking point? Her resolution to be humble and retiring was easy enough to form in the solitude of her own chamber; to execute it under his watchful eye was another matter entirely.

A few days of this quietude, however, had an effect quite opposite to what she had intended. She grew more restless, not less. The absence of his company, which she had thought would be a relief, was in fact a constant, aching void. She found herself listening for the sound of his horse, starting at every knock upon the door, and then sinking into a deeper despondency when it proved to be only the butcher’s boy or a message from the vicarage. Her life, which had once seemed so full, so complete within the bounds of Hartfield, now felt narrow and empty. She had lost her taste for her own importance, and had found nothing to put in its place.

An invitation to dine at Randalls, a week after her last painful interview with Mr. Knightley in the shrubbery, was therefore received with a mixture of terror and a faint, traitorous flicker of hope. He would be there, of course; he was always at Randalls. She would be forced to endure his presence, to make conversation, to feign a composure she did not possess. But she would see him. For a spirit starved of its chief nourishment, even this small, painful crumb was better than nothing.

“But the night air, my dear Emma!” Mr. Woodhouse lamented, when the note was read aloud. “Mrs. Weston is very kind, very thoughtful indeed, but she does not consider the dangers. And to come back so late! The horses might take a chill.”

“We shall wrap up very warm, papa, and I will be sure to tell James to put on his thickest coat. And it will do you good to see little Anna. Mrs. Weston says she has a new tooth to show you.”

This last was a powerful argument, and after some further negotiation regarding the number of shawls and the precise time of their departure—early enough to avoid the evening damp, but not so early as to appear uncivil—Mr. Woodhouse consented.

Emma dressed for the dinner with a care she had not bestowed upon her appearance for some time. It was not from vanity; or if it was, it was a new and painful kind of vanity, born not of confidence but of a desperate desire not to be found wanting. She chose a simple gown of pale blue muslin, hoping its unassuming colour might allow her to blend into the background. She could not have her old sparkle, her old easy wit; she would therefore aim for a quiet elegance, a dignity that might pass unnoticed, but would, she hoped, escape censure.

As she had anticipated, Mr. Knightley was already there when they arrived, standing by the fire in conversation with Mr. Weston. Her heart gave a ridiculous leap, which she instantly suppressed. She greeted him with a polite inclination of her head, and was grateful that Mrs. Weston’s affectionate welcome provided a distraction.

“My dear Emma! How well you look! And Mr. Woodhouse, it is such a pleasure. Come and sit by the fire; I have had it built up just for you. You must not be allowed to feel the slightest draught.”

Under cover of her father’s elaborate settlement into the most sheltered armchair, Emma was able to regain some measure of composure. She saw that Frank Churchill was present also, looking as handsome and animated as ever, leaning over the back of a sofa where a lady sat. It was not until the lady turned her head that Emma realised, with a jolt, that it was Jane Fairfax. This was an unexpected complication. The last time she had seen them in the same room, Frank had been engaged in a subtle, continuous torment of Jane. Emma felt a pang of sympathy for her, a feeling that was quickly followed by a pang of a different sort, as she saw Mr. Knightley’s gaze follow her own, a thoughtful, unreadable expression on his face. Did he believe she was looking at Frank Churchill? Did he still imagine her heart was engaged in that quarter? The thought was mortifying.

Dinner was announced, and Emma found herself seated between Mr. Weston and Frank Churchill. Mr. Knightley was on the opposite side of the table, next to Mrs. Weston. From her position, Emma could not see him without turning her head, a movement she was determined to avoid. She would devote herself to Mr. Weston, and if she must, she would endure the attentions of Mr. Churchill.

“Miss Woodhouse, you have been a great stranger of late,” Frank began, his voice lowered to a confidential tone. “Highbury has been quite desolate. Have you been unwell? Or merely too happy in your own company to bestow any of it on your poor, admiring friends?”

“I have been perfectly well, I thank you,” Emma replied, her tone cooler than she had intended. “My father has required my attention.”

“Ah, the admirable daughter! It is a role you play to perfection. But surely even the most devoted of daughters must be allowed some recreation. We have all missed your lively spirit. Is not that so, Mrs. Weston?” he called across the table. “Has not Highbury been a desert these last ten days?”

“We have certainly missed Emma’s company at Randalls,” Mrs. Weston said with a smile, though she cast a slightly anxious glance at Emma. “But we do not call Highbury a desert. We have had Miss Fairfax’s new pianoforte to talk of, and that has been a great resource.”

“Indeed!” said Frank. “A most mysterious pianoforte. It arrives, as if by magic, from some unknown benefactor. It is the most romantic of circumstances. I have been attempting to persuade Miss Fairfax to hazard a few conjectures as to the sender, but she is impenetrably discreet.”

Emma saw Jane Fairfax colour slightly. She sat next to her mother, her eyes fixed upon her plate. She looked tired, Emma thought, and thinner than she had been. There were faint shadows under her eyes.

“There is no mystery and no romance about it,” Jane said, her voice very low but clear. “It is the work of a friend, and I am very grateful.”

“A friend!” Frank exclaimed. “But ‘friend’ is such a general term. It might mean a cousin, or a guardian, or—or something more. Come, Miss Fairfax, you must have some suspicion. Is there no one in your acquaintance whose particular regard might prompt such a munificent gift? No one, perhaps, whose affection you have commanded, and whose happiness is bound up in your own?”

The cruelty of it was breathtaking. He was speaking, of course, of himself; every word was a secret message, a private torture inflicted in a public forum. Emma felt a surge of indignation on Jane’s behalf. She risked a glance across the table and caught Mr. Knightley’s eye. He was looking not at Frank, but at her, and in his gaze she saw not the suspicion she had feared, but a shared and unequivocal disapproval. It was a moment of perfect communion, so swift and silent that no one else could have perceived it, yet it sent a warmth spreading through her chest that had nothing to do with Mrs. Weston’s well-tended fire.

Emboldened by that look, she spoke. “I believe,” she said, her voice calm and clear, “that true affection does not require a public acknowledgment. The pleasure of giving is its own reward, and the gratitude of the receiver is a private matter. It is a very indelicate curiosity that seeks to pry into such feelings.”

There was a moment of surprised silence. Mr. Weston looked from Emma to Frank Churchill with a slightly bewildered expression. Mrs. Weston looked at Emma with dawning admiration. Frank Churchill himself had the grace to look momentarily disconcerted. He recovered quickly, with a laugh.

“A very just reproof, Miss Woodhouse! I stand corrected. My curiosity is indeed indelicate. I bow to your superior sense of propriety.” He bowed his head to her with a theatrical flourish, but there was a new respect in his eyes, and he did not return to the subject for the remainder of the meal.

Emma scarcely dared to look at Mr. Knightley again, but she felt his approval as surely as if he had spoken it aloud. She had done it. She had spoken not to draw attention to herself, not to be clever, but to defend another and to express a genuine conviction. And he had understood. It was a small victory, but it was a victory nonetheless, and it gave her the strength to endure the rest of the evening.

When the ladies withdrew to the drawing-room, Mrs. Weston took Emma’s arm. “My dear, that was very well said. Mr. Frank Churchill can be a little too thoughtless sometimes, and it was kind of you to speak up for Jane. She is not in the best of spirits, I fear. This situation in life, waiting for a governess’s post, it is very trying for a young woman of her accomplishments.”

Emma murmured some words of agreement, her mind still on that shared glance. Had she imagined it? Had she read into it a meaning that was not there? She was so lost in these reflections that she did not at first notice that Jane Fairfax had sat down at the pianoforte. She began to play, not one of the new waltzes, but a quiet, melancholy Scottish air. The melody filled the room, and for a few moments, all conversation ceased.

When the gentlemen returned, they found a very tranquil scene. Mr. Woodhouse was dozing in his chair, Mrs. Weston was engaged in her needlework, and Emma was sitting near the pianoforte, her hands folded in her lap, listening. Mr. Knightley came and stood beside her.

“That was bravely done, Emma,” he said in a low voice, so that only she could hear.

She looked up at him, her heart beating a little faster. “I could not hear him go on so. It seemed… unkind.”

“It was more than unkind. It was unfeeling. And your rebuke was as just as it was elegant.” He paused, and then added, “I have been unjust to you in my thoughts of late. I believed—I feared—that you were encouraging his attentions.”

“I have been very foolish,” Emma said, her voice barely a whisper. “But not, I hope, in that way.”

“No,” he said, and the warmth in his voice was unmistakable. “Not in that way.”

He said no more, but moved away to speak to Mr. Weston, leaving Emma in a state of bewilderment and shy, tremulous happiness. He had praised her. He had understood her. He had confessed to being unjust in his thoughts of her. It was more than she had dared to hope for. The evening, which had begun in such dread, had turned into something quite different.

The rest of their time at Randalls passed in a sort of dream. Emma spoke little, but she listened and observed, and for the first time, she felt a sense of peace. She was no longer the centre of her own little world, and she was beginning to find that there was a certain freedom in that.

The journey home was a quiet one. Mr. Woodhouse, refreshed by his nap, was in good spirits, and spoke only of the excellence of the baked apples and the pleasing manners of little Anna. Emma, wrapped in her shawls and her own thoughts, leaned back against the squabs and watched the moonlit landscape slide by. The world had not changed, and yet everything was different. The business of life, she reflected, was indeed a hard business; but perhaps, just perhaps, it was not without its moments of unexpected grace. She had set out to learn a lesson in humility, and she was beginning to suspect that it might be a lesson in happiness as well. But this was a conjecture she hardly dared to form; it was too precious, too fragile, to be exposed to the cold light of day. For now, it was enough to hold it in her heart, a tiny, secret flame against the darkness. It was enough to have seen in his eyes, not the reflection of her own folly, but the promise of a friendship restored, and perhaps—though this was a hope too wild to be entertained for more than a fleeting moment—the dawning of something more. The carriage rumbled on towards Hartfield, and Emma, for the first time in many weeks, felt she was truly going home.

He held her gaze for another instant, a look of serene and steady kindness in his eyes, and then he turned and was gone, his firm tread echoing on the gravel before being lost to the silence of the lane.
Emma followed her father into the house, her mind a whirl of contradictions. She was at once deeply humbled and unaccountably elevated. The praise he had given her for her conduct towards Jane Fairfax, a conduct that had been nothing more than the simple dictate of her own conscience, was a balm to her spirit. He had seen her heart, and had not found it wanting. To have been so mistaken in Frank Churchill was a mortification, certainly; but to be so thoroughly understood by Mr. Knightley was a happiness that outweighed every other consideration.
That night, as she lay in her bed, the moon casting patterns upon the floor, she reviewed her own behaviour not with the sharp sting of immediate regret, but with the sober contemplation of one who has been shown a better path. Her follies with respect to Harriet Smith rose before her, a long and sorry procession. She had been vain, she had been meddling, she had been blind. She had sacrificed Harriet’s real and attainable happiness for a phantom of her own creation, and in so doing, had exposed her friend to the cruelest of disappointments. The memory of Mr. Elton’s proposal in the carriage, and the subsequent misery of Harriet, still brought a flush of shame to her cheeks. And her more recent, though less articulated, hopes of a match between Harriet and Frank Churchill seemed now the very height of absurdity. She had used her friend as a piece in a game of her own devising, and she could not forgive herself for it.
A resolution formed itself in her mind, clear and strong. She would see Harriet the very next day. She would not, could not, confess the whole of her folly; that would be to wound Harriet afresh. But she would be a friend to her, a true and steady friend, and she would meddle no more. Her own happiness, that new and tremulous conjecture, seemed to depend upon her first making amends for the unhappiness she had caused another. With this determination, a sense of peace settled over her, and she fell into a sleep more restful than any she had known for months.

The morning was bright, and Emma, true to her resolve, set out for Mrs. Goddard’s directly after breakfast. She found Harriet in the little parlour, engaged in sorting a box of ribbons, a task of such profound concentration that she did not at first perceive Emma’s entrance.
“Harriet, my dear,” said Emma, coming forward.

“We could not stay away,” said Mr. Weston, rubbing his hands together. “We had but just got home when a letter arrived, a letter of such consequence that my wife insisted we must come and share the news with you all at once. Capital news!”

“News?” echoed Mr. Woodhouse faintly. “I hope it is nothing unsettling. I am not very fond of news.”

“Unsettling? Not at all! The very best sort of news. An engagement!”

Emma’s heart gave a sudden, painful lurch. An engagement? Could it be? After her conversation with Harriet, had Mr. Martin wasted no time at all? She looked towards Mr. Knightley, and saw his gaze fixed upon her, his expression unreadable but intense.

“An engagement in the neighbourhood is always a happy event,” said Mr. Knightley, his voice calm and steady, though he did not take his eyes off Emma. “Pray, tell us who the fortunate parties are.”

Mrs. Weston, holding a letter in her hand, took a deep breath. “It is our own dear Frank,” she began.

A wave of the most extraordinary sensations washed over Emma. Frank Churchill! It was his engagement, then. For an instant, she felt a flicker of the old proprietary vanity; had he written to declare himself to her at last? But the thought died as soon as it was born, supplanted by a dawning dread of a different kind. She had a sudden, certain premonition of what was to come.

“Frank has become engaged,” Mrs. Weston continued, her voice wavering between joy for him and trepidation for her audience, “to Miss Fairfax.”

There was a moment of profound silence in the room. Mr. Weston broke it. “There! The secret is out! A secret engagement, they have had, for months! Can you believe it? Since Weymouth! That young rogue! He has played us all for fools, has he not? And to think of all our worrying on his account, and on Miss Fairfax’s! It is the most famous joke I have ever known!”

Mr. Woodhouse looked from one face to another in gentle bewilderment. “Engaged? Mr. Churchill and Miss Fairfax? But I thought… I thought Mr. Churchill was paying his addresses to you, Emma.”

All eyes turned to her. It was the moment of her supreme test. The blood rushed to her face, a hot, painful blush of shame and embarrassment, not for a broken heart, but for her own past blindness and conceit. She felt Mr. Knightley’s gaze upon her like a physical touch, and she knew that everything depended upon her response. She drew a breath, and forced herself to speak.

“You were mistaken, Papa,” she said, and she was astonished at the steadiness of her own voice. “We were all mistaken. Except, it seems, for the two people most concerned. I am… I am very happy for them. It is a most suitable match.” She turned to Mrs. Weston, her expression clearing into one of genuine sympathy. “But for Jane… for Miss Fairfax! How she must have suffered! To be obliged to endure our conjectures, our impertinence, his very attentions to me… it must have been a continual torment. I am ashamed to think of it.”

There was no acting in this. It was the simple, unvarnished truth of her feelings. The relief she felt at the news was immense, a lifting of a burden she had not even been fully aware of carrying. But it was overshadowed by a wave of real compassion for Jane Fairfax, and a deep, burning shame for her own conduct.

Mrs. Weston, who had been watching her with the most anxious solicitude, seemed to relax entirely. “My dearest Emma,” she said, coming to sit by her and taking her hand. “I knew you would feel so. You have a good heart. Yes, it has been a terrible trial for poor Jane. Frank’s letter is full of self-reproach. He acknowledges that his behaviour has been inexcusable.”

“Inexcusable is the word,” said Mr. Knightley, speaking for the first time since the announcement. His voice was stern, but as Emma glanced at him, she saw that his eyes were fixed on her with an expression of such profound, unequivocal approbation that it sent a shock of pure joy through her. He was not looking at her with pity, or with triumph at his own superior judgement. He was looking at her with admiration.

“Well, well, the boy has been a rascal,” said Mr. Weston, unable to maintain a severe countenance for long. “But all’s well that ends well! A wedding! We shall have a wedding at Highbury! Or perhaps they will be married from Richmond. He speaks of Colonel Campbell’s approval. The Colonel, it seems, knew of it all along, which explains the pianoforte! It all makes sense now. The whole business of the pianoforte!”

The conversation became general then, a flurry of exclamations and explanations, as Mr. and Mrs. Weston recounted the details from Frank’s letter. Mr. Woodhouse, having at last grasped that Emma was not broken-hearted, expressed his satisfaction that there would be no immediate changes, though he was a little concerned that a wedding might involve late hours and rich cake.

Through it all, Emma sat in a kind of daze. She had been publicly humiliated, her supposed conquest revealed as a sham, her judgement proven utterly false. A few months ago, such a revelation would have been a source of the deepest mortification. Now, she felt almost nothing of the sort. The only opinion that truly mattered to her was Mr. Knightley’s, and in his eyes, she had not been found wanting. She had passed the test. She had spoken the truth, felt the truth, and in doing so, had earned his respect in a way that no social triumph ever could.

Presently, the Westons, having discharged their momentous news, took their leave, promising to call again the next day with any further intelligence. When they were gone, a comfortable quiet descended upon the little party in the parlour.

Mr. Woodhouse, exhausted by the excitement, soon began to nod in his chair. Emma rose quietly and adjusted a shawl about his shoulders. As she turned back, she found Mr. Knightley standing by the mantelpiece, watching her.

“You have had a trying afternoon, Emma,” he said quietly.

“I have deserved it,” she replied. “Every moment of it.”

“Perhaps. But you bore it well. You bore it with a generosity of spirit that does you great credit.” He paused, then came a step closer. “I was unjust to you this morning. When I saw Frank Churchill’s attentions, his constant whisperings and pointed looks, I believed… I feared you were encouraging them, that you were taking pleasure in a flirtation that could only cause pain to another.”

“And you were right to believe it,” Emma said, her voice low. “I did take pleasure in it. I was vain, and foolish, and I thought only of myself. I never suspected the truth. I never imagined there could be a real attachment, a real, secret suffering.”

“I suspected it,” he said. “I saw a look on her face, at the Crown ball, when he spoke to her. I have suspected it for some time. Which is why,” he added, and a deeper colour came into his face, “my behaviour to you has been… less than patient. I did you the injustice of believing you saw it too, and were thoughtlessly playing a part in her distress. I ought to have known you better. Your heart is truer than your judgement, sometimes.”

The confession, the apology, the compliment, all delivered in his serious, earnest manner, were almost too much for Emma’s composure. She could only look at him, her eyes shining with unshed tears. “My judgement has been a very poor thing indeed,” she whispered. “But it is improving. I am beginning to see things as they are.”

“Yes,” he said, and his gaze was so full of meaning that she had to look away. “I believe you are.”

A log shifted in the fireplace, and Mr. Woodhouse stirred, murmuring in his sleep. The spell was broken.

“I must not keep you,” Mr. Knightley said, his tone returning to something of its usual register. “Your father will be waking soon and will want his arrowroot. I will see you tomorrow.”

He took her hand, not in the casual manner of a neighbour, but with a gentle pressure that conveyed everything he could not say. “Be happy, Emma,” he said. “You have earned it.”

He was gone. Emma stood where he had left her, her hand still tingling from his touch. The room was quiet, save for the soft breathing of her father and the ticking of the clock. The future, which had so often seemed to her a blank canvas upon which to sketch her own clever designs, was suddenly filled with a single, radiant possibility. It was a prospect so dazzling that it frightened her, yet so right that it calmed her. She was no longer a schemer, no longer a matchmaker, no longer the queen of Highbury society. She was simply a woman who had been a fool, and who had, by some miracle of grace she could not begin to comprehend, been given a chance to be wise. She had lost a fantasy, and in its place, had found a hope as solid and as real as the hand that had just held her own.

She felt a pleasing sense of sagacity, of being admitted, with Mr. Churchill, to a view of the hidden workings of the human heart. “Ah, my dears! I was just beginning to wonder. I hope you have not been taking cold. “We were not in the room with the open window, papa, and Mr. Knightley was in no danger at all. We have had a very pleasant visit and heard the new pianoforte. It has a very fine tone.”

It was not until after Harriet had been sent home in the carriage, and her father had settled to his basin of gruel and an early evening, that Emma found herself truly alone, and able to give her mind over completely to the morning’s events. She sat by the fire in the drawing room, the leaping flames casting dancing shadows on the walls, and replayed the entire scene. Frank Churchill’s words, Jane’s looks, Miss Bates’s chatter, Mr. Knightley’s commanding voice from the street. The more she considered it, the more she was diverted. Frank Churchill was a delightful creature; so full of spirits, so clever in his perceptions. He saw everything. His suspicion of an old attachment between Jane and Mr. Dixon of Balycraig was, she felt, a stroke of genius. It explained everything: Jane’s reticence, her blushes, even the arrival of the mysterious pianoforte. Colonel Campbell, wishing to replace the instrument lost with the Dixons’ marriage, had sent it. It was all perfectly clear, and she gave Frank Churchill full credit for the discovery.

Her amusement, however, was checked by a lingering sense of vexation on another score. Mr. Knightley. Why had he been so… so very insistent? “How is Miss Fairfax?—I hope she caught no cold last night. How is she to-day? Tell me how Miss Fairfax is.” The words echoed in her memory with an emphasis that was almost uncivil. It was an interrogation, not an enquiry. And his sharp interruption of poor Miss Bates, his refusal to come in when he knew she herself was there—it was unlike him. It was a failure in his usual, dependable good manners. While she had defended it to Mrs. Weston as mere benevolence, in the privacy of her own thoughts, she found it disproportionate. Was his admiration for Jane Fairfax’s supposed merits so very great that he must be rude to everyone else on her account? It gave Emma a feeling of dissatisfaction she could not quite define. It was not jealousy; she was perfectly sure of that. She did not want Mr. Knightley’s particular attentions for herself. But she was the lady of Hartfield, and he was their oldest friend. His first civilities should have been for her and her father. To have them all so pointedly directed elsewhere was a slight, and Emma was not accustomed to being slighted.

“Mr. Knightley! I thought you were in Kingston, or long since returned to Donwell.”

“I have been to Kingston and back,” he said, closing the door behind him. “I saw your light from the shrubbery, and as I knew your father retired early, I hoped I might find you alone. May I come in for a few minutes?”

“Of course,” said Emma, her composure a little ruffled by his unexpected appearance and the gravity of his tone. “Pray, come and sit by the fire. You must be cold from your ride.”

He did as she bade, but he did not seem to feel the cold. He looked serious, preoccupied, and for a moment he said nothing, merely gazing into the flames. Emma felt a strange apprehension.

At last he spoke. “Emma,” he said, not looking at her, but addressing the fire, “I was not pleased with what I witnessed this morning.”

Emma’s spirits immediately rose in self-defence. “Indeed? I thought it a most amusing morning. Though perhaps Miss Bates’s parlour is not the place to look for rational entertainment.”

“I do not speak of Miss Bates,” he said, turning his eyes upon her now. They were clear and steady, and held a look of such earnest reproof that she felt herself colour. “I speak of you. And of Mr. Frank Churchill.”

“Of us?” cried Emma, affecting a tone of innocent surprise. “What can you mean? We were doing nothing but listening to the pianoforte, and trying to keep Miss Bates from talking herself into a fever.”

“You were encouraging him,” he said, his voice low and direct. “You were encouraging him in a behaviour that I can only call cruel.”

“Cruel! Mr. Knightley, you are talking in riddles. And you are, I must say, most unjustly severe. What has Mr. Churchill done to earn such a term from you? You have disliked him from the very first, I know, but to call his harmless pleasantry ‘cruel’ is past all reason.”

“Harmless pleasantry?” he repeated, and there was a deep indignation in his voice. “To perpetually hint at a secret, to torment a young woman who is wholly unprotected and in a situation of extreme delicacy, to do so under the guise of compliment and concern, and to make her feelings the sport of a morning visit—you call that harmless? I call it the behaviour of a coxcomb, and a very unfeeling one.”

Emma was stunned into silence for a moment. She had thought Frank Churchill’s teasing clever; she had been his accomplice in it. To have it presented to her in this light was a profound shock.

“You imagine things, Mr. Knightley,” she said, recovering herself, though with less assurance than before. “You mistake the nature of the conversation entirely. There was no torment. It was all a game, a piece of nonsense. Jane Fairfax herself was amused. I saw her smile.”

“I have no doubt you saw her smile,” he returned, his expression softening a little, but not his purpose. “A woman in her situation must learn to smile at many things that gall her to the heart. Did you not see her blush? Did you not hear the tremor in her voice? Or were you too occupied in being amused yourself to notice the distress of another?”

This was too much. To be accused of such unfeeling blindness, and by him, was an injury she could not bear.

“You do me a great injustice,” she said, her voice trembling slightly with anger and mortification. “And you do Mr. Churchill a great injustice. He is all good humour and high spirits; there is not a particle of cruelty in his nature. It is you who are cruel, to impute such motives to him and such insensibility to me. You see everything through a veil of prejudice. Because he is from London, because he is fashionable, because he is not as sober and plodding as the men of Highbury, you are determined to think ill of him.”

“If by sober and plodding you mean a man who understands his duties and respects the feelings of others, then I hope I shall always be found wanting in Mr. Churchill’s style of fashion,” he retorted, his own temper rising to meet hers. “His duties he has shamefully neglected for years, and as for respecting the feelings of others, we have seen a specimen of that this morning. He amuses himself, Emma. That is all. He is amusing himself with Highbury, with the Westons, with Jane Fairfax, and with you.”

“With me!” Emma exclaimed, her cheeks burning. “And what if he is? Is there any great harm in a little harmless flirtation? Must everything be so terribly serious?”

“When it is at the expense of a person’s peace of mind, yes, it must!” he said, rising from his chair and standing before the fireplace, his height and his earnestness seeming to fill the room. “And when it involves you, I will not stand by and see you cheapen yourself by taking part in it. He is not your equal, Emma. Not in sense, nor in principle. His attentions are without meaning, his compliments are hollow, and his whole manner is a performance. He is playing a part, and he is inviting you to be his audience. Is that the role you wish to play?”

Each word was a blow to Emma’s vanity and her pride. She had believed herself Frank Churchill’s confidante, his partner in sagacity. Mr. Knightley was telling her she was merely his dupe, his toy.

“You have said quite enough,” she said, turning her face away that he might not see the tears of vexation that had sprung to her eyes. “You have made your opinion of my friend, and of my own judgement, perfectly clear. I am sorry they do not meet with your approbation, but I am not, I think, accountable to you for my friendships or my amusements.”

The air in the room was thick with their disagreement. Mr. Knightley stood for a long time in silence. When he spoke again, his voice had lost its anger, and was full of a deep, and to Emma, a most surprising, note of pain.

“No,” he said quietly. “You are not accountable to me. I have no right to lecture you. It is a bad habit I have fallen into since you were a child. I forget that you are a child no longer. The simple declaration, uttered in his plain, unadorned way, had an effect on Emma that no flowery compliment from Frank Churchill could ever have achieved. It went directly to her heart. He was the truest friend she had in the world, the one person whose good opinion was essential to her own self-respect.

The choice of a pianoforte, for instance. Everyone is remarking on its elegance.”
She watched him closely as she spoke. A flicker of something—was it surprise? annoyance?—passed over his features before he recovered his composure.
“Ah, yes. The pianoforte,” he said, his voice a little less buoyant. “A happy arrival. It has made Miss Fairfax’s situation so much more comfortable. One is very glad to have been of some small service to Colonel Campbell’s commission.”
He was attributing it all to the Campbells again, just as he had in front of Jane. The denial was too strenuous. Emma’s new-found key, as Mr. Knightley had called it, was turning in the lock. The secrecy was not for her amusement; it was for his own. Why he should be so determined to conceal such a generous gift, she could not imagine; unless, indeed, the generosity was of a kind that required concealment.
The thought was so startling that it drove all other considerations from her mind. Frank Churchill and Jane Fairfax? It was an absurdity. It was an impossibility. He, so open, so animated, so very much devoted to—to herself! And she, so reserved, so correct, so evidently indifferent to him. And yet… and yet. It would explain everything. His constant talk of her, his strange mixture of praise and criticism, his determination to make Emma a confidante in his supposed suspicions about Mr. Dixon. It was a blind. A clever, elaborate, and cruel blind. And she had been his chosen instrument.
“You are very thoughtful,” she said, and the ambiguity in her tone was entirely for her own satisfaction.
He looked at her, a slight frown of puzzlement on his brow. “You are not yourself to-day, Miss Woodhouse. Has something vexed you?”
“On the contrary. I believe my mind has been rather too diverted of late. A little quiet reflection is exactly what I require.”
The hint was plain enough even for the self-satisfied Mr. Churchill. He saw that he would get no entertainment at Hartfield that morning. With a few more pleasantries, which fell rather flat, and a promise to call again when she was in better spirits, he took his leave. Emma watched him go, not with the regret she would have felt the day before, but with a sense of profound disillusionment. The spell was broken. The hero of her imagination had shrunk to the dimensions of a very ordinary, and not entirely honourable, young man.

She sought the quiet of the shrubbery, her mind in a ferment. The hypothesis, once formed, gained strength with every recollection. His eagerness to fix a day for the ball at the Crown, and his subsequent disappointment; had it been for her, or for an opportunity to dance with Jane? His contrived visit to the Bateses to hear the pianoforte; was it to watch her, or to torment the woman he secretly loved? The waltz, Weymouth, the Irish melodies—it was a tapestry of hints and allusions, so obvious now that she wondered at her own blindness. She had been so occupied with the workings of her own imagination, so busy arranging matches for others, that she had failed to see the one great drama playing out directly under her nose.
Her mortification was extreme. Mr. Knightley had seen it all. He had not, perhaps, guessed the whole truth of the attachment, but he had seen the cruelty. He had understood that Frank Churchill’s behaviour was pointed and intentional, while she, in her vanity, had believed it all a game for her benefit. How foolish, how blind, how utterly conceited she must have appeared to him! She paced the gravel walk, her cheeks burning with a shame far deeper than what she had felt the night before. Then, she had been ashamed of a lapse in kindness; now, she was ashamed of a fundamental failure of intellect and perception. He must think her a complete simpleton.

It was in this agitated state that she almost walked into the very man who occupied her thoughts. Mr. Knightley was coming up the path from the direction of Donwell, his steady stride and composed manner a stark contrast to her own inner turmoil. She stopped short, her heart giving a ridiculous leap.
“Mr. Knightley!” she exclaimed. “I did not see you.”
“I was on my way to speak with your father on a small matter of parish business,” he said, his eyes resting on her face with a look of quiet scrutiny. “But I see you are taking the air. I hope you are feeling quite well this morning.”
His tone was perfectly normal, perfectly friendly. There was no trace of last night’s anger, nor of the deep emotion that had followed it. It was as if he had indeed resolved to “speak no more of it.” The calm kindness was more disconcerting than any reproach would have been.
“I am very well, thank you,” she said, her voice not quite steady. “I was… I felt the need of a little air. The house was rather warm.”
“Frank Churchill has been with you, I see. I met him on the road to Highbury.”
“Yes. He called just now.” She hesitated, then felt a sudden, irresistible urge to show him that his lesson had not been in vain. “He was talking again of the pianoforte, and of Miss Fairfax.”
Mr. Knightley’s expression remained guarded. “Indeed.”
“I am afraid,” she continued, looking down at the path, “that I have been very foolish about that whole business. Very foolish, and very unfeeling. You were quite right in what you said last night. There was nothing amusing in it. It was impertinent and unkind, and I am ashamed to have had any part in it.”
It was a great effort to say it, an admission of error more direct than any she had ever made in her life. She risked a glance at him and saw that the gravity of his expression had softened. A slow smile was beginning to dawn in his eyes.
“If you think so, Emma,” he said, and his voice held a warmth that went straight to her heart, “we shall say no more about it. A fault acknowledged is a fault half amended.”
“But I wish you to know,” she persisted, wanting, for once, to be perfectly understood by him, “that I see it all now. His attentions are not what they seem. There is a meaning, a purpose, behind them. And I was his dupe.”
He looked at her with renewed interest. “You think so? You have divined a secret motive?”
“I believe so. I cannot be certain, of course. It is all conjecture,” she said, the word bringing a faint smile to her own lips. “But it is a conjecture that explains a great deal that has been puzzling. It explains his looks, and his words, and his whole manner towards her.”
“And what is this conjecture?” he asked, his curiosity genuinely aroused.
She lowered her voice. “I believe that all this attention he pays me is a blind. I believe his real interest, his real attachment, is to Jane Fairfax herself.”
She had expected him to be astonished, to argue, to dismiss it as another flight of her fancy. Instead, he was silent for a long moment, his brow furrowed in thought.
“Frank Churchill and Jane Fairfax,” he said at last, musingly. “You think there is a secret understanding between them?”
“I do. I think they knew each other at Weymouth. I think this whole performance—his flirtation with me, his teasing of her—is a screen to hide their true feelings from his aunt. It is the only explanation that makes sense of his behaviour.”
Mr. Knightley’s gaze sharpened. “It would indeed explain a great deal,” he said slowly. “His insistence on her coming to Highbury. His variable spirits. His intimate knowledge of the Weymouth party. Emma, I believe you may be right. It is a disagreeable business, if so. To use you, and to torment her, for his own convenience—it is the conduct of a man without principle.”
To be so readily agreed with, to have her perception validated by him, was a balm to her wounded pride. He did not think her a simpleton after all. He saw that she had reasoned her way to the truth.
“I am glad you think so,” she said, with a feeling of relief so immense it was almost painful. “I was afraid you would think it another of my wild schemes.”
“No,” he said, looking at her with an expression she could not quite decipher, an expression of approbation, but with something more behind it. “No, this is not a scheme. This is observation. Clear-sighted observation. You have seen the truth of the situation where I saw only the cruelty of it. You have done Miss Fairfax more justice than I did.”
The praise was so unexpected, and so welcome, that it brought a blush to her cheeks. They walked on in silence for a few paces, a new and comfortable sense of accord between them. The awkwardness was gone, replaced by a shared understanding.
“It makes me pity her exceedingly,” Emma said, her voice soft. “To be forced into such a position. To have to endure his public attentions to another, and his private teasing, and to be unable to show a particle of her true feelings. It must be almost unbearable.”
“It must,” he agreed. “Jane Fairfax has a degree of self-command which I have always admired. If your conjecture is right, I admire it more than ever.”
They had reached the door to the house. Mr. Knightley made no move to enter, but paused on the step.
“Well, Emma,” he said, his tone lighter than it had been. “It seems we are to be fellow-conspirators in this secret, though of a different sort than you and he. We shall watch, and we shall see. And in the meantime,” he added, his eyes twinkling, “we shall endeavour not to be dupes.”
He smiled at her, a genuine, open smile of friendship and understanding, and it was as if the sun had come out from behind a cloud. Emma’s heart felt suddenly light.
“Yes,” she said, returning his smile with one of her own. “We shall endeavour not to be dupes.”
He nodded, touched his hat, and turned to go, leaving Emma on the doorstep with a mind far clearer, and a heart far more dangerously full, than it had been when she left the house. The world had righted itself, but it was not the same world as before. Frank Churchill had been deposed, and in his place, in the very centre of her thoughts, stood Mr. Knightley, no longer merely a critic and a friend, but something else entirely; something she was not yet ready to name.
