Well, I call it a shameful business, and I do not care who hears me say so! To lead a girl on in that manner, and with all the appearance of the most sincere attachment; and then, at the last, to simply send a note, a paltry note! Lord, it is a wonder to me how some men can live with themselves!”

“He has behaved very poorly indeed,” said Annelise, her fingers tightening upon the needlework in her lap, which she had made no progress on for the last hour.

“Poorly! My dear, it is abominable! And for whom? This Miss Goddard, you say? I knew her grandmother; a very worthy woman with a face like a dried apple, but a fortune that might tempt a bishop. They say the girl has twenty thousand pounds, and more to come when her uncle dies. A great catch for a man like Mr. Hensley, who has not two farthings to rub together, for all his fine coats and his talk of London! But what is money, I ask you, when set against true affection? He ought to be ashamed to show his face in the county again.”

“I believe he has no intention of doing so, ma’am. His letter stated he was to join his regiment at once.”

“And a good thing too! The army is the only place for a man of such fickle principles. But what of your poor sister? How does she bear it? I saw she took no soup at dinner, and she has a look about her that is quite pitiable. Is she still in her chamber?”

“She is, ma’am. She finds herself unequal to company at present. The shock has been very great.”

“Poor dear! And she so fond of him! Why, I remember at the summer ball, she would not dance with another man all evening, and he looked at her in a way that, I declare, made old Lady Croft put up her quizzing glass. It is a cruel, cruel thing. But men are all alike, my dear. When a richer prospect appears, they fly to it like wasps to a jam-pot. There is no sentiment left in the world!”

What now,” she said, after a moment’s thought, “do you not think a little broth might tempt your sister? Or some of that baked apple she professes to like? It is quite cruel to think of her up there, all alone with her sorrows.”

“You are very kind, ma’am, but I believe she requires quiet more than any thing. I shall prevail upon her to retire early to bed. Rest is the best physician for a troubled mind.”

“Aye, to be sure. Lord! when I think of it! Sir William was so certain of the match, he was ready to wager his best hunter on it. He will be so dreadfully vexed when I tell him! And my girls will be quite overset for your sister’s sake. For my part, I think the less we say of it, the better. Once a thing is done, what good can talking do? We shall not mention Mr. Hensley’s name again in this house, I am determined on it. It would only be distressing to you, and to poor Catherine.”

“I am very sensible of your consideration, Mrs. Burton. To hear his name, or to have the matter remarked upon, would indeed give my sister a great deal of pain. Her own good sense will in time overcome her feelings, but the process cannot be hurried.”

“Law, my dear, do not talk of good sense now! A young girl in love has no use for it. No, she must be diverted! And what a blessing that Mr. Sterling is to call this evening! Now there is a man of true worth. Not so dashing as that Hensley, I grant you, but his estate at Oakhaven is a model of good management, and his character is beyond reproach.”

Annelise could not forbear a small, sad smile. “Ma’am, I hardly think my sister is in a state to receive the attentions of any gentleman at present.”

“Nonsense, my dear! One fire drives out another, you know. He will not speak to her of love, to be sure; not yet. But his company is so steady and sensible. He will talk to her of books, and of his planting; and she will see the difference between a man of substance and a man of mere show. Mark my words, he will have her in the end. Oakhaven is a thousand times a better prospect than a life in the army, following the drum from one garrison town to another. Well, I shall not press the point; but when he comes, I shall contrive to leave you both together a little.”

“Indeed, ma’am, that is quite unnecessary,” Annelise began, but seeing the resolute benevolence in Mrs. Burton’s face, she let the matter drop.

Rising, she went to her sister’s room. Catherine was not weeping, but sat in a chair by the unlit hearth, her hands lying listless in her lap, and her gaze fixed upon the window, though there was nothing to see in the encroaching dark but her own pale reflection.

“The evening has grown cold,” Annelise said gently, taking it upon herself to light the fire. “You will make yourself ill, Catherine.”

“It does not signify,” her sister answered, in a voice devoid of all its usual life. “Pray, leave me, Annelise. You cannot help me.”

“I will not leave you to sit in the misery of your own thoughts,” Annelise replied, kneeling by her side and taking one of her cold hands. “You must not give way. You must allow your spirit to fight against this affliction.”

For a long moment, Catherine did not answer. Then two tears, slow and heavy, welled in her eyes and traced paths down her cheeks. “He said he should never love another,” she whispered.

Annelise’s heart ached for her. She made no reply, but only chafed her sister’s hand in her own, offering the silent comfort that was all she had to give, and waited for the warmth of the new-lit fire to slowly fill the room.

Finding no polite way to refuse a request so rooted in kindness, however meddlesome, Annelise took her leave and returned to her sister’s chamber. The fire now burned with a cheerful blaze, casting flickering shadows upon the walls, but Catherine had not moved. She sat as one turned to stone, her misery a palpable thing in the small, warm room.

“Catherine,” Annelise said, her voice soft but firm, “you must rouse yourself. Mrs. Burton has sent you some broth.”

“I want nothing,” Catherine murmured, without turning her head.

“You will drink it, nonetheless,” Annelise insisted, setting the tray down and pouring a cup. “I will not stand by and see you make yourself ill for a man who has proven so utterly undeserving of your least regard. To fall into a decline would be to give him a victory he has not earned.”

This appeal to pride seemed to have some small effect, for Catherine stirred at last, and turned her desolate eyes upon her sister. “Do you think I wish to? I cannot help myself, Annelise. My heart is broken. He has broken it.”

“Then you must allow your spirit to mend it,” Annelise replied, holding the cup to her sister’s lips. “Now drink this. For my sake, if not for your own. I cannot bear to see you suffer so.”

Obediently, though with the listless air of one past all caring, Catherine drank the broth. Annelise then persuaded her, with a great deal of gentle urging, to undress and get into bed, drawing the curtains round her as if to shut out the world that had used her so cruelly. She sat by the bedside, holding her sister’s hand, until her breathing grew even and she seemed at last to have fallen into a troubled sleep. Only then did Annelise, with a sigh of profound weariness, quit the room and make her way back down to the drawing-room.

She found Mrs. Burton in a state of some agitation, pacing before the fireplace. “He is come, my dear! Mr. Sterling is here! I have put him in the library for a moment, for I did not know what to say about Catherine. I told him she had a slight headache and was resting. Was that right? He will think it so odd, her not appearing.”

“It was very well thought of, ma’am,” said Annelise, though she felt a fresh wave of exhaustion at the prospect of entertaining a visitor. “Pray, do not trouble yourself. I will explain that my sister is much fatigued from our journey.”

“Well, you must do as you think best. But it is a great pity! However, he will have you to talk to, and that is something. Now, come along, my dear. We must not keep him waiting.”

Mr. Sterling rose as they entered the library. He was a man of perhaps thirty years, with a countenance more sensible than handsome, and a pair of grave, steady eyes that met Annelise’s with an expression of quiet intelligence. His bow was formal but not stiff, and his voice, when he spoke, was low and pleasant.

“Mrs. Burton,” he said. “Miss Annelise. I am sorry to hear that Miss Catherine is unwell.”

“Oh, it is a mere trifle, Mr. Sterling,” cried Mrs. Burton, with a great show of cheerfulness. “A headache, nothing more! You will excuse me, I know.” And with a look of vast significance at Annelise, she bustled out of the room.

A somewhat awkward silence fell. Annelise, feeling the falseness of her position, could think of nothing to say, and sat twisting the ribbon of her sash. It was Mr. Sterling who spoke first.

“Your sister’s indisposition is not serious, I hope?” he asked, his tone gentle and entirely free from idle curiosity.

“She is overwrought and requires rest, sir,” Annelise replied, grateful for his delicacy. “The journey was indeed tiring for her.”

“The city can be an overwhelming place,” he agreed. “I confess, I am always very glad to return to the peace of Oakhaven. Do you and your sister enjoy the country, Miss Annelise?”

“Very much, sir. We have lived in it all our lives, and though a visit to London is a great amusement, I believe our hearts will always remain in the fields and lanes of our own neighbourhood.”

“I am happy to hear it,” he said, with a slight smile that much improved his serious features. “I have a great prejudice in favour of those who love the country. It argues, to my mind, a sound understanding and a steady disposition.”

Their conversation, thus begun, soon flowed with greater ease. They spoke of books, and found that they shared an admiration for the poetry of Mr. Cowper; they spoke of landscape, and Mr. Sterling described, with the quiet enthusiasm of a true lover of nature, the improvements he was making to his estate. He did not boast of its size or its value; rather, he spoke of the satisfaction of planting a stand of oaks that his grandchildren might one day enjoy, and of his efforts to improve the cottages of his tenants.

Annelise found herself listening with an unexpected degree of pleasure. There was a sincerity and a lack of artifice about him that she found deeply restful, particularly after the anxieties of the day. Here was no fashionable flourish, no studied charm, but a plain, honourable man who seemed to say exactly what he thought, and to think on subjects of sense and consequence. She could not help but contrast his solid worth with the glittering, and as it now appeared, hollow attractions of Mr. Hensley.

They were interrupted by the return of Mrs. Burton, who entered with a letter in her hand and an expression of triumphant indignation on her face.

“Well!” she exclaimed, taking a seat and fanning herself with the letter. “If this does not prove the wickedness of the world, I do not know what does! This is from my daughter, Mrs. Palmer, and it is full of the most shocking news about that Mr. Hensley. You will forgive me for speaking of it, Mr. Sterling, but it is a matter that touches these dear girls very nearly.”

Mr. Sterling bowed his head in acknowledgement, his grave eyes resting for a moment on Annelise’s distressed face.

“It seems,” Mrs. Burton continued, lowering her voice to a conspiratorial pitch, “that the whole affair was one of the basest calculation! The man is ruined, completely ruined! My son-in-law, who knows about these things, says he has debts of honour all over town, and his tailor and his boot-maker are on the very point of having him arrested. He has not a feather to fly with, as they say!”

Annelise sat in silent misery, her heart sinking at this public declaration of what she had feared.

“And so,” Mrs. Burton went on, warming to her theme, “he fixed upon this Miss Talbot. And what a creature she is, by all accounts! Charlotte says she has a face like a dried lemon and a temper to match, but her father is a nabob, just returned from India with a fortune that would stagger you. Eighty thousand pounds! And not a penny of it settled on her, so this Hensley will have the free use of it all once they are wed. Is it not abominable?”

“It is a transaction that does little credit to either party, it would seem,” said Mr. Sterling, his voice edged with cold disapproval. “A man’s honour ought not to be so cheaply sold.”

“Cheaply! I do not call eighty thousand pounds cheap!” cried Mrs. Burton. “But you are quite right, Mr. Sterling. It is a shameful business. And to think he was paying his addresses to our dear Catherine all the while, with his pretty words and his soulful looks! Law, it makes one’s blood boil! He deserves to be horse-whipped. Indeed, Charlotte writes that Miss Talbot’s cousin, a Captain Morris, was so incensed by some slight Hensley offered the lady—for he is as careless of her feelings as of everyone else’s, it seems—that there was very nearly a duel! But their friends interfered and patched it up. More’s the pity! A good fright would have done Mr. Hensley a world of good.”

Annelise could bear no more. The image of a duel, of Hensley’s life in danger, even now, sent a fresh pang of terror through her, for she knew what Catherine’s sufferings would be if she were to hear of it. She rose to her feet, her composure failing her at last.

“Ma’am, if you will excuse me,” she said, her voice trembling slightly. “I must look in on my sister.”

Mr. Sterling was on his feet in an instant, his expression full of a concern that was both respectful and sincere. “Miss Annelise, I fear our conversation has distressed you. I should take my leave.”

“No, no, pray stay and finish your visit with Mrs. Burton,” she managed to say. “It is only that I am anxious. Forgive me.” She gave him a fleeting, grateful look, and hurried from the room.

She did not go immediately to Catherine’s chamber, but paused on the landing, leaning her head against the cool wall and struggling to regain control of herself. The whole sordid history, laid bare by Mrs. Burton’s careless gossip, seemed to press in upon her. The debts, the cold-hearted pursuit of a fortune, the ill-tempered heiress, the talk of a duel—it was a tale of such commonplace venality and recklessness that it made Hensley’s betrayal seem at once more brutal and more pitiful. And it was this man for whom her sister was shattering her health and her heart.

When she had composed her features, she crept to Catherine’s door and opened it a crack. All was quiet within. Believing her sister to be still asleep, Annelise returned to the drawing-room, where she found Mr. Sterling on the point of departure.

“I am glad to see you returned, Miss Annelise,” he said, his kind eyes searching her face. “Mrs. Burton has been assuring me that your sister’s complaint is not serious.”

“She is sleeping now,” Annelise replied. “I believe a quiet night will do much to restore her.”

“I sincerely hope so,” he said. He took her hand for a moment. “I trust you will not think me impertinent if I say that if there is any service I can render, any book from my library that might amuse her, or any errand I might run for you in the town, you have only to command me.”

“You are very good, sir,” Annelise answered, touched by his genuine kindness. “I am very grateful.”

After he had gone, Mrs. Burton could no longer contain her satisfaction. “Well, my dear! What do you think of him? Is he not the most sensible, agreeable man? And Oakhaven! It is a paradise on earth, I am told. You see, the wind that blows out one fire may well kindle another!”

“Ma’am, he is a very pleasant, gentlemanlike man,” said Annelise, wearily.

“Pleasant! He is a great deal more than pleasant! And I saw how he looked at you, my dear,” she said, lowering her voice with a knowing smile. “When you spoke of Mr. Cowper, his face quite lit up. “Oh, I shall imagine what I please!” replied Mrs. Burton, laughing. “I have a very good eye for these things. Mark my words, we shall see him again before the week is out. Grateful to be released, Annelise bid her goodnight and went to her own room, which adjoined Catherine’s. But she could not rest. Her mind was a turmoil of thoughts: of Catherine’s pale, sleeping face; of Hensley’s treachery; and of Mr. Sterling’s calm, considerate presence. Mrs. Burton’s suggestion, absurd as it was, brought a strange flush to her cheek. She dismissed it at once as the fancy of a romantic matchmaker. Her own happiness was the last thing on her mind; her only concern was for her sister.

An hour later, a faint sound from the next room disturbed her. It was a low moan, followed by a restless tossing. Annelise rose at once and, taking her candle, went to Catherine’s bedside.

Her sister was no longer sleeping peacefully. Her eyes were half-open, but they were glazed and unseeing. Her cheeks were flushed with a feverish heat, and she was muttering to herself, her hands plucking at the coverlet. Annelise bent closer, her heart clenching with fear.

“He promised,” Catherine whispered, her voice hoarse and rapid. “In the green lane… he promised… Eighty thousand pounds… No, not for that… Annelise, do not let them… do not let them fight…”

She had heard them. She must have been awake, lying in the dark, and had overheard every dreadful word of Mrs. Burton’s report from the drawing-room. The ruin, the heiress, the duel—it had all poured into her wounded mind, and the shock, coming upon her exhausted state, had thrown her into a fever.

Annelise touched her sister’s forehead. It was burning hot. A genuine terror, far sharper than any anxiety she had yet felt, seized her. This was no longer a simple case of a broken heart to be soothed with patience and quiet. It had become an affair for the apothecary.

“Catherine,” she said, trying to keep her voice steady. “Catherine, it is I, Annelise. You are dreaming. All is well.”

For a moment she leaned against the door-frame, her strength failing her. To have a man of sense and action take charge, even in so small a matter, was a relief so profound it brought tears to her eyes. But she brushed them away angrily; this was no time for selfish feelings. Her place was with Catherine.

The next hour was one of the longest of Annelise’s life. Mrs. Burton, having failed to make any impression on her coachman, returned to the drawing-room to keep Annelise company, a kindness the latter would gladly have been spared. The good lady’s anxiety manifested in a ceaseless stream of chatter: of similar fevers she had known, of infallible remedies she had heard of, of the dreadful cost of apothecaries’ bills, and of the lamentable tendency of young ladies to give way to their feelings.

“It is all this sensibility, you see,” she declared, shaking her head. “The modern fashion for it. In my day, a girl was taught to command her spirits. A disappointment in love was a sad thing, to be sure, but one did not immediately take to one’s bed with a brain-fever! But your sister is so very delicate, poor dear. It is just as well that rogue Hensley is out of the picture; he would have been the death of her in a twelvemonth.”

Annelise made no reply. To argue was useless, and to listen was a torment. She sat with her hands clasped tightly in her lap, her ears straining for the sound of Mr. Sterling’s return, while Catherine’s delirious muttering from the next room formed a dreadful counterpoint to Mrs. Burton’s monologue. “Eighty thousand pounds,” the hoarse whisper came, “and he promised… Sir Thomas will kill him… Oh, Annelise, make them stop…”

At last, a carriage was heard to draw up in the street below, and a few moments later, Mr. Sterling reappeared, followed by a small, neat, serious-looking man carrying a black bag.

“Mr. Howell, miss,” Mr. Sterling said quietly, and then, with a tact for which Annelise was unutterably grateful, he retreated to the far side of the drawing-room, leaving the family to their consultation.

The apothecary was a man of few words. He entered the bedchamber, felt Catherine’s pulse, examined her eyes, and listened to Annelise’s account of the evening’s events with a grave, attentive silence. When he emerged, his face was sober.

“It is a fever of the nervous kind, miss,” he said, addressing Annelise. “Brought on by a severe shock to the mind acting upon a constitution already weakened by distress. The brain is inflamed. It is a serious matter, but by no means a hopeless one.”

He spoke of the need for absolute quiet, for cooling barley-water, and for the application of leeches to the temples to draw off the heat. He sat down at the table to write out his instructions and a prescription for a sedative draught. Annelise watched him, a cold dread settling in her stomach. Leeches, an inflamed brain; the words sounded terrifying, a confirmation of her worst fears.

“Will she… will she recover, sir?” she asked, her voice faltering.

“With care and quiet, I have every hope of it, miss,” he replied, sanding the paper. “Youth is on her side. The crisis may come within the next day or two. Everything depends on her strength. I will send the draughts and the… other necessities over at once. And I shall call again in the morning.”

He took his fee, bowed, and was shown out by Mrs. Burton, who immediately began a low-voiced, agitated conference with him on the landing. Mr. Sterling then came forward.

“I will not keep you, Miss Annelise. I only wished to know if there is anything more, anything at all, in which I can be of service.”

“You have done too much already, sir,” she said, looking up at him with a gratitude that she could not, and did not wish to, conceal. “I do not know how we can ever thank you. To go out so late, and to bring Mr. Howell so quickly… you have been our truest friend tonight.”

“Then allow me to continue in that office,” he said gently. “I have settled Mr. Howell’s account for this visit.”

“Oh, no!” cried Annelise, her cheeks flushing with mortification and pride. “You must not! Indeed, we cannot permit it. We are not so destitute as that, sir. I beg you will tell me the sum, that I may repay you at once.”

“It is a trifle,” he said, waving it away. “And I will not be repaid. Consider it a loan, if you will, to be settled when your sister is well and your mind is at ease. Do not, I entreat you, add to your anxieties by thinking of it now. A good night to you, Miss Annelise. I pray you will find your sister improved in the morning.”

He was gone before she could protest further, leaving her in a state of confused emotions. His generosity was as immense as it was delicate; yet to be under such an obligation to a stranger, a man she had known for but a few days, was deeply troubling to her sense of independence. The world, which had seemed only an hour before to be a place of cruelty and deceit, had suddenly shown a face of unexpected kindness, and Annelise hardly knew which aspect was the more discomfiting.

The long hours of the night passed in a miserable vigil. The apothecary’s boy arrived with a terrifying jar of leeches and a bottle of laudanum-laced medicine. With the reluctant help of a grumbling, terrified housemaid whom Mrs. Burton had finally managed to wake, Annelise performed the dreadful task prescribed, her stomach turning at the sight. Afterwards, she administered the sedative, and to her infinite relief, Catherine’s restless tossing subsided, her muttering ceased, and she fell at last into a heavy, unnatural sleep.

Annelise sat in a low chair by the fire, her shawl wrapped tightly around her. She did not dare to go to bed. Every so often she would rise to touch her sister’s brow, which remained alarmingly hot, or to listen to her breathing, which was shallow but regular. In the quiet of the sick-room, with the city’s sounds hushed at last, her thoughts were her only company.

She thought of Hensley, not with the anger of the previous day, but with a cold, hard contempt. His crime was no longer simply one of faithlessness; he was the direct author of this new and terrible calamity. He had not merely broken a heart; he had endangered a life. And for what? For eighty thousand pounds and an heiress who was, by all accounts, not even handsome. The sordidness of it, the common, vulgar cruelty, filled her with a disgust that went deeper than sorrow.

Her mind turned, reluctantly, to Mr. Sterling. Mrs. Burton’s foolish matchmaking words returned to her, and she felt a hot blush rise in the darkness. It was absurd. His actions were those of any man of good feeling and gentlemanly principle. To imagine they proceeded from any particular interest in herself was a vanity she would not permit. Yet she could not deny the comfort his presence had brought her, nor the warmth she felt in recalling his quiet consideration. He was, in every particular, the exact opposite of Mr. Hensley: where one was all show and false sentiment, the other was all substance and true feeling. Where one spoke eloquently of honour and meant nothing, the other acted honourably and said little. The contrast was so stark, so painful, that she pushed the thought away. Her own prospects, her own happiness, were of no consequence. All that mattered now was the pale, still figure in the bed.

The grey light of a London dawn was filtering through the curtains when Annelise, exhausted, finally fell into a light doze in her chair. She was awoken some time later by the entry of Mrs. Burton, who crept in on tiptoe, bearing a cup of tea.

“My dear, you are awake,” she whispered. “You must have some of this. You look as pale as your sister. How has she passed the night?”

“She has been sleeping since the draught was administered,” Annelise replied, her voice stiff from weariness. “The fever is no better.”

“Ah, these things must run their course,” said Mrs. Burton, settling herself with a rustle of skirts into the room’s other armchair. “Mr. Howell said as much. He is a very sensible man. Not like that chattering Mr. Gibbs who attended the Martins. Now, my dear, I have had some news this morning. My maid, Sally, her cousin is a footman at the Ellisons’, you see, where Miss Grey has been staying.”

She hoped they might be contained in the drawing-room, but a moment later, she heard the rustle of silks and a train of footsteps ascending the stairs. Mrs. Jennings appeared at the door, her face beaming. “My dear,” she said, in a loud whisper, “here are my daughters come to call. They are so anxious about dear Marianne. I told them she was much better, and they are longing to see you. Do come out for a moment.” Escape was impossible. With a feeling of profound vexation, Elinor rose and went out into the landing, closing the door softly behind her. There stood Lady Middleton, elegant and erect, her features composed in an expression of formal concern, and Mrs. Palmer, who immediately rushed forward and seized both of Elinor’s hands. “Miss Dashwood! How d’ye do?” cried she, with a laugh. “I am so excessively sorry to hear about your sister! We were on our way to a morning concert, but when Mama’s note reached us, we turned the carriage right around. Is she very bad? I told Mr. Palmer I was sure she had caught the very same complaint that is going about everywhere. He thinks he has it himself, you know! Ha, ha, ha!” “She is a little better this morning, I thank you,” said Elinor, trying to gently disengage her hands. “The doctor is hopeful of her recovery.” “Oh, I am monstrous glad to hear it!” said Mrs. Palmer. “It would be so shocking if anything were to happen to her, you know. Poor Mr. Willoughby! How he would feel it! Have you heard from him? I suppose he is quite distracted?” Elinor felt the blood drain from her face. She could only stare at Mrs. Palmer, for a moment bereft of speech by the sheer, unthinking cruelty of the question. Lady Middleton, perceiving her discomposure, though not its cause, interposed with her cool, placid tones. “Charlotte, you should not ask such questions. Miss Dashwood is naturally distressed. We are very sorry for your trouble, Miss Dashwood. I hope your sister has good advice? We have the greatest faith in Dr. Harris.” “We have a very capable apothecary, ma’am,” Elinor managed to reply, her voice trembling slightly. “And we are most grateful for your concern.” “Well, I am sure you are doing everything you can,” said Mrs. Jennings, bustling forward. “Now, my dears, you must come down and have some refreshment. Miss Dashwood will want to get back to her patient, I am sure.” She ushered her daughters towards the stairs, but Mrs. Palmer lingered, looking towards Marianne’s door. “Do you think one might just peep in at her?” she asked. “Just for a moment? I should so like to see her.” “Certainly not,” said Elinor, with more firmness than she had yet shown. “She is sleeping, and must not be disturbed on any account.” “Oh! Well, if she is sleeping,” said Mrs. Palmer, not at all offended. “That is very good, I am sure. Sleep is such a charming thing, is it not? Mr. Palmer can sleep anywhere, even in the carriage, which is so droll! Ha, ha, ha!” With another laugh, she allowed herself to be led away, and Elinor, with a profound sigh of relief, returned to the quiet of the sickroom. The intrusion, however, had unsettled her. It was a stark reminder of the world outside those four walls; a world that gossiped and laughed and speculated, utterly careless of the real and deep suffering it discussed so lightly. It strengthened her resolve to protect Marianne, but it also filled her with a sense of weary hopelessness. How could her sister ever recover, when every turn was fraught with the possibility of such painful encounters? For the next two days, a fragile peace reigned. Marianne continued to mend, slowly and silently. She began to sit up in bed for short periods, and once, when Elinor was reading aloud, she seemed to listen for a few minutes with a flicker of her old attention, before her gaze drifted away again to the window. She never spoke of Willoughby; she never asked after the post. It was a silence so absolute, so unnatural, that Elinor found it more alarming than any passionate lamentation. It was as if a part of her sister had been extinguished, leaving behind this quiet, listless shell. On the third morning after the visit of Mrs. Jennings’s daughters, Elinor was sitting by the bed, mending a tear in a shawl, when Marianne, who had been watching her with an unnerving stillness, spoke for the first time on any subject of consequence. “Elinor,” she said, her voice a mere thread of its former self, yet perfectly clear and steady. Elinor’s hands froze in her lap. She looked up, her heart beginning to pound with a slow, heavy rhythm. “Yes, Marianne?” “Has any letter come for me?” The question was simple, the delivery calm, but Elinor knew it was the precursor to everything she had been dreading. For a moment, she was tempted to prevaricate, to say she did not know, to buy herself another hour of peace. But looking at the strange, solemn clarity in her sister’s eyes, she knew the time for evasion was past. Marianne was no longer in the delirium of fever; she was in the cold, clear agony of returning consciousness, and she required the truth. “No,” said Elinor, her own voice quiet. “There has been no letter.” Marianne’s gaze did not waver. She seemed to consider this for a long moment. “I have been ill for a long time,” she stated, rather than asked. “More than a week, I think.” “Nearly a fortnight,” Elinor confirmed softly. “A fortnight.” Marianne repeated the word, and a flicker of some unreadable emotion crossed her face. “And in all that time… nothing.” She paused again, and when she next spoke, her voice was lower still, yet possessed of a dreadful intensity. “Elinor. Tell me the truth. Do not spare me now. I have been thinking, in all these quiet hours. I am ready for it. Is he in London?” “Yes,” whispered Elinor. “Is he married?” There it was. The question that had hung in the air between them, unspoken, for so long. Elinor felt a wave of sickness wash over her, but she met her sister’s gaze without flinching. Her first duty now was not to soften the blow, for that was impossible, but to deliver it with the clean, sharp pain of honesty. “He is, Marianne.” For a long, terrible moment, Marianne did not move or speak. Her face, already so pale and thin, seemed to become a mask of stone. She did not cry out; she did not even gasp. She simply stared at Elinor, and in her eyes Elinor saw not the wild tempest of grief she had expected, but the vast, empty desolation of a landscape after the storm has passed and obliterated everything. Then, slowly, as if the movement required an immense effort, Marianne turned her head away and fixed her eyes upon the wall. A single tear welled and traced a path down her temple into her hair. “I am sorry,” Elinor breathed, her own tears now flowing freely. She reached out to take her sister’s hand, but Marianne did not seem to notice. Her body was rigid, her breathing shallow. She lay there, utterly still, as if she had turned to ice. Elinor sat beside her, her heart breaking with a grief that was twofold; for Marianne’s shattered hopes, and for the final, irrevocable loss of the sister she had known. The passionate, vibrant, feeling Marianne was gone, and this silent, frozen creature had taken her place. How long they sat there, Elinor did not know. The morning sun crept across the floor, and the sounds of the household went on below, but in the sickroom, time seemed to have stopped. At last, Elinor, seeing the utter exhaustion in her sister’s frame, gently smoothed her pillow. “You must rest, Marianne,” she said. “Try to sleep.” To her surprise, Marianne gave a slight, almost imperceptible nod. She allowed Elinor to help her lie down more comfortably, and closed her eyes. There was no more weeping; only a profound and terrifying stillness. Elinor watched her until her breathing deepened into what seemed to be a genuine sleep, and then, unable to bear the confines of the room any longer, she stole away. She needed air; she needed a moment to herself, to absorb the shock of what had just passed. In the drawing-room, she found Mrs. Jennings, who looked up from her accounts with a cheerful smile. “Well, my dear? And how is our patient? I am just planning a little party for next week, to celebrate her recovery. A little music, and a hand of cards. It will cheer us all up.” The disjunction between this happy domestic scene and the silent tragedy upstairs was so jarring that Elinor could not speak. She simply sank into a chair and covered her face with her hands. Mrs. Jennings was by her side in an instant, all kindness and concern. “My dear girl! What is it? Is she worse? Has the fever returned?” “No,” said Elinor, her voice muffled. “She is not worse. She is better. She knows.” “Knows?” repeated Mrs. Jennings, bewildered. “Knows what, my love?” “About Mr. Willoughby,” said Elinor, looking up, her face streaked with tears. “She knows that he is married.” “Oh, Lord!” Mrs. Jennings fell back into her own chair as if she had been struck. “Oh, Lord. Poor dear soul. And how… how did she take it?” “Quietly,” said Elinor. “Too quietly. That is what frightens me.” Mrs. Jennings was silent for a full minute, an unprecedented event. All her native flightiness was gone, replaced by a look of genuine, heartfelt compassion. “The brute,” she said at last, her voice thick with indignation. “The unfeeling, wicked brute. To use her so. Fifty thousand pounds! I hope it turns to ash in his hands. And that Miss Grey, I dare say she is no better than she should be, to take a man on such terms. Poor Marianne. My poor, dear girl.” She began to cry, her comfortable frame shaking with sobs. “And you, my dear Elinor. You have had to bear it all alone. Why did you not tell me? I could have helped.” “You have helped, more than you know,” said Elinor, touched by her goodness. “But it was a truth only I could deliver.” Just then, Colonel Brandon was announced. He entered the room to a scene of mutual distress, and stopped short on the threshold, his face a picture of alarm. “Miss Dashwood! Mrs. Jennings! What has happened? Is Miss Marianne…?” “She is alive, Colonel,” said Elinor, rising and trying to compose herself. “But the secret is out. She knows of Willoughby’s marriage.” The colour drained from the Colonel’s face, leaving it grey and strained. He did not speak, but his eyes, fixed on Elinor, were full of a pain that so exactly mirrored her own that she felt a strange, momentary kinship with him. He, of all people, understood. He had lived through this before. “I see,” he said at last, his voice low and grave. He walked to the window and stood for a moment looking out, as he had done once before. When he turned back, his expression was resolved. “Miss Dashwood,” he said. “A change of scene is of the first importance in such cases. The air of London, the constant reminders… they cannot be good for her. My sister and her husband are at my estate, Delaford. It is the quietest place in the world. As soon as Miss Marianne is fit to travel, you must both do me the honour of removing there. My sister, Mrs. Brandon, would be overjoyed to have you, and the country air will be the best physician for her.” The offer was so unexpected, so practical, and so deeply kind, that Elinor was quite overcome. Here was a way forward; a retreat from the world, a place where Marianne might heal in peace, away from the prying eyes and careless tongues of London. It was a reprieve she had not dared to hope for. “Colonel,” she said, her voice unsteady. “Your generosity… I do not know what to say. It is the very thing we need.” “Then it is settled,” he said, with a faint, sad smile. “You have only to name the day. I will make all the arrangements. Delaford, Miss Dashwood, will be entirely at your service.” He took his leave shortly after, and Elinor was left with Mrs. Jennings, who, having dried her tears, was already seeing the advantages of the scheme. “Delaford!” she exclaimed. “Aye, that will be the very thing! A nice, quiet place. And the Colonel there to look after you! Well, my dear, did I not say an ill wind blows nobody any good? He will have her yet, you mark my words! Once that scoundrel Willoughby is forgot, she will see who has the truer heart. Two thousand a year, and a devoted husband! It will all come right in the end, Elinor. You will see.” Elinor could not share her hostess’s romantic predictions. The future seemed to her a vast and arduous path, and she could not see the end of it. But in the Colonel’s quiet offer, she did see a single, steady light. It was not the dazzling, blinding flame of passion that had so tragically consumed her sister, but the calm, enduring glow of true friendship. And for now, that was more than enough.

“Dear Ma’am,” replied Elinor, with a gentle but decided hand, staying Mrs. Jennings’s progress towards the door, “you are all kindness, but I am certain it would be of no avail. Marianne is in no state to take any thing, and least of all wine. Her only desire is for quiet, and her best remedy, I trust, will be sleep.”

Mrs. Jennings looked quite crestfallen. “No wine? Not even a drop of the old Constantia? Well, to be sure, you know best, my dear. It is a sad business, a very sad business indeed. To think of her, poor love, refusing the very thing that always set my poor husband to rights! But there; grief takes us all in different ways, does it not? I shall just put it back in the cabinet, then. But if she should change her mind, you have only to ring for the butler. He knows just where it is kept.”

With this, the good lady bustled out, leaving Elinor to a few moments of solitary reflection before she could summon the fortitude to return to her own duties. The drawing-room, now empty, seemed to echo with the day’s calamitous revelations. Elinor walked to the window and looked down into the dark, glossy street, where the lamps cast long, wavering reflections on the wet pavement. London, with its endless whirl of society and its heartless transactions, had never seemed so alien or so cruel. Colonel Brandon’s offer was a port in a storm, a quiet harbour she had not dared to look for; yet the difficulty of persuading Marianne to accept such a refuge seemed almost insurmountable. To her sister, in her present state of mind, any plan for her relief would appear only as an impertinent intrusion upon the sacred rights of her sorrow.

The next morning confirmed all her fears. Marianne, when Elinor at last ventured to broach the subject, received the proposal with a cold and languid indifference that was more disheartening than any passionate refusal. She was seated by the window of their small sitting-room, her form listless, her eyes fixed on the grey London sky, from which a persistent, miserable drizzle was falling.

“To Delaford?” she said, her voice scarcely more than a whisper. “And why should we go to Delaford? Is one place not as good as another for a wretch to be miserable in?”

“Because London is no place for you now, Marianne,” Elinor said, taking a seat beside her and speaking in the low, steady tones she had found most apt to claim her sister’s attention. “You cannot wish to remain where you must be the object of public curiosity and pity; where every acquaintance we meet will be a fresh trial to your spirits. At Delaford you will have peace, and true retirement.”

“Peace?” repeated Marianne, a faint, bitter smile touching her lips. “Do you suppose that a change of view from a window will give me peace, Elinor? The source of my misery is within me; I shall carry it with me to Delaford, as I should carry it to the farthest Indies.”

“I do not speak of an immediate cure, my dearest,” said Elinor, her patience sorely tried, but her love for her sister prevailing over every vexation. “I speak only of a retreat from present evils. Consider our situation here. We are guests in Mrs. Jennings’s house. Her kindness is unbounded, but we cannot impose upon it for ever; and her spirits, her very good-nature, must be a continual torment to you. How can you bear her conversation, her visitors, the necessity of appearing at her table?”

Marianne shuddered. “I shall not appear. I will keep to my room.”

“And what a life for you! And what a return for all her goodness to us! It is impossible, Marianne. And consider this, too; by removing into the country, we spare our friends the pain of witnessing what they cannot alleviate. Sir John and Mrs. Palmer—they will soon know all; and though their intentions are the best in the world, can you imagine their well-meaning condolences will be any thing but torture?”

This argument seemed to have more weight with Marianne than any that had preceded it. The idea of inflicting her sorrow upon others, or of being subjected to their clumsy attempts at comfort, was insupportable. She turned her head away and was silent for a long time. Elinor, seeing she had made some impression, wisely said no more, but allowed her sister’s mind to work upon the thoughts she had presented.

At length, Marianne spoke, her voice still faint, but with a tone of weary resignation. “If we must go, then let it be so. It does not signify where I am. Do as you think best, Elinor. You are always the one who must think for us all.”

This mournful acquiescence, though it was all Elinor had hoped for, cost her a pang. It was a victory, but a sad one, won from her sister’s utter prostration of spirit. She wasted no time, however, in securing the advantage she had gained. She immediately wrote to Colonel Brandon to accept his generous proposal, and to their mother at Barton to acquaint her with their change of plans, framing the news with all the careful delicacy she could muster.

The next few days were passed in the necessary preparations for their departure, a task which fell almost entirely upon Elinor. Marianne, having given her consent, relapsed into a state of passive quietude, from which nothing could rouse her. She would sit for hours without speaking or moving, and if obliged to give an opinion on some matter of packing, would answer with a listless “It is all one to me,” that wrung her sister’s heart.

Mrs. Jennings, meanwhile, was a whirlwind of activity and advice. “To Delaford! Aye, the very best thing in the world for you both. Now, Elinor my dear, you must be sure to take your warmest shawls; for though the house is mighty comfortable, those old stone passages can have a bit of a draught, you know. And Marianne must have her bottle of hartshorn, and I shall give you a parcel of my own special biscuits; they are wonderfully settling for the stomach on a journey. Lord! I am half tempted to shut up the house and go with you myself, if it were not for Charlotte being so near her time. But the Colonel will be there to look after you; that is one comfort. I shall give him a good talking to before you go, and tell him he is to see that Marianne eats a proper dinner every day. Young men are thoughtless, but the Colonel has a steadiness about him, for all he is not so very old.”

Her discourse ran on in this manner perpetually, a curious and often jarring mixture of practical housekeeping, unsubtle matchmaking, and recycled gossip about Willoughby and his new bride, which Elinor had to bear with what grace she could. She endured it all for the sake of the genuine kindness that lay beneath the chatter, a kindness that showed itself in a thousand ways, from the ordering of a particularly comfortable travelling chaise to the secret pressing of a purse into Elinor’s hand “for any little extras on the road, my dear.”

The day of their journey at last arrived; a grey, overcast morning, entirely suited to the somber mood of the travellers. The farewells were tearful on Mrs. Jennings’s side, and full of repeated injunctions and good wishes. “Now write to me very soon, my dear Elinor, and tell me every thing. And give my love to the Colonel; and tell that poor child to keep up her spirits. It will all blow over, you will see! Lord bless you both!”

The journey itself was as melancholy as Elinor had anticipated. For mile after mile, the carriage rattled along the muddy roads, leaving the smoky confines of London behind for the bare, wintry fields of the countryside. Marianne sat huddled in her corner, a thick veil drawn over her face, a silent statue of grief. She never spoke, and Elinor, after one or two fruitless attempts at conversation, lapsed into a silence of her own, her mind occupied with a thousand anxious thoughts. She looked at her sister’s motionless form, and her heart ached with a sorrow that was all the deeper for being so rigorously suppressed. She thought of Willoughby with a cold, unforgiving anger; of Edward with a steady, secret pain; and of Colonel Brandon with a profound and wondering gratitude. His quiet, unassuming generosity was a beacon in the darkness of their present circumstances.

It was late in the afternoon, as the winter light was beginning to fail, that the carriage turned off the main road and began to proceed down a lane overhung with the bare branches of ancient trees. After some little time, they passed through a simple stone gateway and the house came into view. Delaford Manor was not a grand or imposing edifice; it was a handsome, substantial building of sober red brick, softened by age, its many-paned windows seeming to look out upon the world with a calm and dignified composure. It spoke not of fashion or of modern elegance, but of quiet endurance, of generations of settled, respectable life. A flight of shallow stone steps led up to a heavy oak door, which was opened as they drew up by a neat, elderly woman in a crisp white cap, who curtsied and introduced herself as Mrs. Reynolds, the housekeeper.

The air within the hall was cool and smelled faintly of woodsmoke and dried lavender. It was a large, stone-flagged space, dark with old wood panelling, from which a handsome staircase ascended into the shadows of the upper floor. All was silent, orderly, and peaceful. A moment later, a lady descended the stairs to greet them. This was Colonel Brandon’s sister, Mrs. Patterson, a widow some years his senior. She was a gentle, placid-looking woman, with her brother’s kind eyes and a sweetness of expression that was immediately winning. Her welcome was as different as possible from Mrs. Jennings’s effusions.

“Miss Dashwood, Miss Marianne,” she said, her voice soft and low as she took Elinor’s hand. “I am so very glad you are come. You must be dreadfully fatigued after your journey.” There was no intrusive pity in her gaze, only a quiet, compassionate understanding that Elinor found infinitely soothing. She addressed Marianne with the same gentle courtesy, making no comment on her pale looks or her withdrawn air, but simply expressing a hope that her room would be comfortable.

They were shown at once to their apartments, two spacious, interconnecting rooms on the first floor, which were brightened by cheerful fires and afforded a view over a wide expanse of parkland, dotted with magnificent old oaks. Every comfort had been attended to. Hot water was brought, and tea was laid out for them in their own small sitting-room, that they might be spared the trouble of a formal dinner on their first evening.

Mrs. Patterson’s delicate and unobtrusive kindness was a balm to Elinor’s frayed nerves. She soon perceived that in her, they would have a companion whose good sense and quiet temperament would be a perfect antidote to the well-meaning but exhausting boisterousness they had left behind in London.

The first week at Delaford passed in a profound and almost unbroken quiet. True to her fears, Marianne’s change of scene had effected no change in her spirits. She remained in a state of deep and silent dejection. Most of the day she spent in her own room, sometimes with a book in her lap, though Elinor noted that the pages were seldom turned. When the weather was fine, she would put on her bonnet and walk out alone into the grounds, often staying out for hours, and returning pale and chilled, with a look of fixed and inaccessible misery on her face. She ate little, spoke less, and seemed to find the smallest effort of social interaction an intolerable burden.

Elinor, meanwhile, divided her time between anxious attendance on her sister and the gentle society of Mrs. Patterson. With her, she could converse without strain, and she found a quiet pleasure in learning more about the house and its master. She explored the library, a noble room lined from floor to ceiling with books, and was given leave by Mrs. Patterson to borrow any she chose. She discovered a music-room, containing a pianoforte and a harp, and a pang of memory shot through her as she imagined the pleasure Marianne might have taken in them in happier times. Now, the instruments stood silent and shrouded in dust-sheets.

One afternoon, as she and Mrs. Patterson sat together with their needlework, the elder lady said, with her usual mildness, “My brother writes that he will be detained on business for another week or so, but that he hopes to join us before the end of the month. He is most anxious for news of you both.”

“You are very good to have us here,” Elinor replied. “I do not know what we should have done without your brother’s goodness.”

“It is a pleasure to him, Miss Dashwood, to be of service. He has a great esteem for you and your sister. He has not had an easy life himself, and it has given him, I think, a particular sympathy for the sorrows of others.” Mrs. Patterson looked down at her work, and Elinor felt that though she did not know the particulars of his story, she understood its effects.

About a fortnight after their arrival, a fat letter arrived, bearing a London postmark. Elinor recognised Mrs. Jennings’s untidy scrawl at once and broke the seal with a mixture of apprehension and curiosity. It was, as she expected, a long and rambling account of all the news of Berkeley Street.

“My Dearest Elinor,” it began, “I hope this finds you and your poor sister getting settled at Delaford, which I am sure is a fine place, though I dare say a little dull for you after London. We miss you both terribly. Sir John was here yesterday and declared the house is not the same without his dear Miss Dashwoods to tease. As for news, Lord! where to begin? Charlotte was brought to bed of a little girl two days ago, and both are doing as well as can be expected, though Palmer is in a sad fret, and says the baby cries so much he cannot get a proper night’s rest. I hear that the talk about Willoughby and his marriage has nearly died down, which is a blessing. But Mrs. Taylor told me something very diverting the other day. She had it from Miss Steele, who is a dreadful gossip as you know, but is sometimes right by accident. She says that Miss Grey, or Mrs. Willoughby as she is now, has a temper that would frighten a regiment of soldiers, and that poor Willoughby leads a sad life of it already! They say she keeps him on a very short allowance of money and is terribly jealous if he so much as looks at another woman. Is it not a famous revenge? I am sure if poor Marianne could but hear it, it might do her a world of good to know he has not got every thing his own way. Pray tell me how she does. Has the Colonel been to see you yet? Sir John and I are convinced that is a match, and we drink his health every evening. Now be a good girl and write me a long letter back with all your news. Your Affectionate Friend, M. Jennings.”

Elinor folded the letter with a sigh. The news concerning Willoughby, though it afforded her a moment of grim, uncharitable satisfaction, was something she would on no account repeat to Marianne. To her sister, Willoughby was still a fallen angel, not a hen-pecked husband, and to sully that tragic image with such vulgar realities would be a cruelty. The letter served only to reinforce her conviction that their retreat to Delaford had been the wisest possible course.

Colonel Brandon’s arrival, when it finally occurred, was as quiet and unceremonious as every other aspect of his conduct. Elinor was sitting at the window of the morning-room, looking out at the slow, dripping thaw of a late February afternoon, when she saw a solitary horseman coming up the long drive. There was something in his bearing, in the easy, unaffected way he sat his horse, that was instantly recognisable. Her heart gave a little flutter, a mixture of relief and nervousness, and she rose to inform Mrs. Patterson.

He met them in the hall, his face lighting up with a genuine, quiet pleasure as he greeted his sister and then turned to Elinor. “Miss Dashwood,” he said, taking her hand. “I am very glad to be home. I trust you have been comfortable?” His manner was that of a kind and considerate host, with nothing of the suitor in it.

At that moment, the door opened and Marianne entered, having just returned from one of her solitary rambles. On seeing the Colonel, she stopped short, and a faint colour rose to her pale cheeks. She had not expected him so soon, and his presence was evidently a shock.

The Colonel’s smile faded, and his expression became one of grave concern. He stepped forward. “Miss Marianne,” he said, his voice full of a gentle deference. “I hope I see you well.”

Marianne inclined her head in a slight, cold curtsy. “I thank you, sir,” she murmured, and without another word or look, she swept past them and ascended the stairs to her own room.

An awkward silence fell upon the little group in the hall. Elinor was mortified by her sister’s rudeness, Mrs. Patterson looked distressed, and the Colonel himself stood looking at the empty staircase, a profound sadness in his eyes. He quickly recovered himself, however, and turning to Elinor with a faint, reassuring smile, he said, “Grief, Miss Dashwood, must be allowed its privileges. We must be patient.”

Elinor could have wept with gratitude for his forbearance. In that single, quiet sentence, he had shown more true understanding of her sister’s state, and more compassion for it, than all Mrs. Jennings’s loud-voiced pity or Sir John’s boisterous consolations. Here was a man who knew what it was to suffer, and who had learned from it not bitterness, but a deep and noble charity of heart. As she followed him and his sister into the drawing-room, she felt a renewed sense of security in his presence, a feeling that whatever storms might still lie ahead for them, they had in him a true and steadfast friend.

“Well, well, if you think best. But Constantia wine, my dear, is not to be met with every day. However, you know your sister. The days that followed at Delaford settled into a routine of profound and healing quietness. Colonel Brandon, with a delicacy that Elinor could not but admire, seemed to devote himself entirely to the business of his estate, which had, by his long absence, fallen into some neglect. He was out with his steward from morning till noon, and spent his afternoons occupied with papers in his library, leaving the ladies to their own devices. This conduct, so evidently designed to allow Marianne the space her wounded spirit required, did more to recommend him to Elinor’s esteem than the most devoted attentions could have done. He made no claim on their time, yet his presence was a constant, steadying influence; a background of quiet strength and benevolent authority that made the house feel like a sanctuary.

Mrs. Patterson, his sister, was a gentle, sensible woman of about forty, a widow of some years, who managed his household with quiet efficiency. She possessed none of Mrs. Jennings’s boisterous good-nature, but her kindness was no less sincere for being more reserved. She watched Marianne with an eye of compassionate concern, but forebore to make any direct address to her, confining her attentions to ensuring her niece’s every comfort was met, and engaging Elinor in placid conversation on subjects she judged least likely to cause distress.

Marianne, for her part, remained almost entirely immured in her own sorrow. She spoke little, ate less, and spent the greater part of every day in her room, or, when the weather permitted, in solitary rambles through the bare, wet woods that bordered the park. She moved like a ghost through the comfortable, well-ordered house, her pale face and listless air a continual reproach to the very idea of cheerfulness. She never addressed the Colonel, and if he entered a room where she was sitting, would soon find a pretext to leave it. Elinor, seeing the pain this silent rejection caused him, however he might try to hide it, felt her heart ache with a mixture of pity for him and vexation at her sister.

One afternoon, about a fortnight after his arrival, Elinor, having persuaded Marianne to lie down, put on her pelisse and bonnet to take a turn about the grounds, seeking in the sharp March air some relief from the oppressive stillness of the house. She had not gone far along the gravelled path that led towards the stew-ponds, when she was joined by Colonel Brandon.

“You have left your sister?” he asked, his tone one of gentle enquiry.

“She is resting, I hope,” replied Elinor. “The least exertion seems to weary her to an extent that is quite alarming.”

“It is the malady of the mind, Miss Dashwood, which saps the strength of the body. I know it well,” he said, with a sigh so faint it was barely audible. They walked on for some moments in silence, the only sound the crunch of their feet on the path and the melancholy cawing of rooks in the high elms.

“I fear,” said Elinor at last, feeling a need to apologise for her sister’s continued coldness, “that we are but sorry companions for you and Mrs. Patterson. Marianne’s spirits are so disordered, she is not herself. In other times, you would have found her all life and animation.”

“My dear Miss Dashwood,” he answered, turning to her with a look of the most earnest sincerity, “pray do not vex yourself on that account. To know that you and your sister are safe under my roof is all the companionship I require. I had no right to expect more. There is a sensibility of mind which, whilst it feels happiness with an exquisite poignancy unknown to duller souls, exposes its possessor to the keenest suffering. We must not judge such a nature by common rules.”

Elinor’s heart warmed at this defence. He understood Marianne; he did not condemn her. “You are very good, sir,” she said, her voice trembling a little. “There are few who would show such forbearance.”

“I have not always shown it,” he said, his eyes on the distant prospect. “Experience, Miss Dashwood, is a severe, but a thorough, teacher. It has taught me patience, if nothing else.”

They had reached the old yew arbour of which Mrs. Jennings had spoken with such enthusiasm. It was a gloomy, damp spot at this time of year, but it commanded a fine view of the house and the winding drive. They stood there for a moment, and Elinor, glancing at her companion, was struck by the expression of settled melancholy on his features. It was not the passionate, wild grief of Marianne, but something deeper, a sorrow that had been lived with for so long it had become a part of the man himself. She felt a powerful sense of fellowship with him, a kinship of silent suffering that went beyond words. Here was a man who, like herself, had learned to bear his sorrows with fortitude and to hide his deepest feelings from the world.

“Delaford is a beautiful place,” she said, turning the conversation to a safer topic. “You must be very happy to be home.”

“I am,” he replied, his expression lightening at once. “It is my hope that you and Miss Marianne may in time come to feel some of the same affection for it that I do. It has been the home of my family for generations. There is a permanence here, a sense of continuity, which I find very soothing to the spirits.”

As they walked back towards the house, he began to point out his plans for improvement; a stand of oaks that needed thinning, a cottage whose roof required repair, a spot where he intended to plant a new shrubbery for his sister. He spoke of these things with a quiet enthusiasm that showed his deep attachment to his home and his duties. Elinor listened with genuine interest, her own practical turn of mind finding a satisfaction in these sensible and useful projects. By the time they reached the door, she felt more at ease with him than she had ever done before. The conversation had established a new level of intimacy between them, the quiet understanding of two people who had no need to speak of their own troubles to know they were understood by the other.

A few days later, a letter arrived for Elinor from London, forwarded from Barton. The handwriting was Mrs. Jennings’s, and Elinor retired to her own room to read it. It was, as she had expected, a long, rambling account of all the news of the town, filled with exclamation points and underlined words.

“My Dearest Miss Dashwood,” it began, “I have been prodigiously dull since you all ran away from me, and the house in Berkeley Street is as quiet as a tomb. Sir John and the Palmers are gone back to Barton, and I am left to my own devices, which you know I cannot bear for long! I was in hopes of hearing from you, to know how you all go on, and especially how that poor dear girl does. Has she cried her eyes out? I am sure I should have, in her place. Well, they say it is all settled, and the wedding is to be at the end of April. Mrs. Ellison’s cousin told Mrs. Taylor that Miss Grey has been to the mantua-maker’s and ordered such a quantity of lace as you never saw. All for show, you may be sure, for I will never believe there can be any real affection in it. Fifty thousand pounds may buy a great deal of lace, but it cannot buy a heart, as I always say! Lord! to think of that man, with his fine words and his curricle, selling himself like that! I met Mrs. Ferrars in the park the other day, and you never saw such a sour-faced old creature. She looked as if she had been sucking a lemon. I dare say she is not best pleased with either of her sons. I hear that young Mr. Ferrars, your Edward I mean, is still in town, looking very miserable. I suppose that Lucy Steele keeps him tight to her apron-strings. A sly little minx, if ever there was one! But there, I must not run on. Give my fondest love to your dear sister, and tell her from me to keep up her spirits. The Colonel will make her a better husband by half, and Delaford is a thousand times prettier than a damp old place like Allenham. Write to me soon, my dear, and tell me every thing. Your very affectionate friend, M. Jennings.”

Elinor folded the letter with a sigh. It was a relief to hear that Willoughby’s marriage was a settled thing; the sooner it was over, the sooner the talk would die away and Marianne might begin to forget. But the mention of Edward brought back all her own anxieties with a fresh pang. To think of him in London, miserable and bound to a woman he did not love, was a torment. For a moment, her own situation seemed as hopeless as her sister’s. But Elinor’s was a mind accustomed to self-command. She would not allow herself to sink into despondency. She had a duty to Marianne, and to the kind friends who were sheltering them. She put the letter away and went downstairs, her face composed, if a little paler than usual.

That evening, after dinner, Mrs. Patterson prevailed upon Marianne to go to the pianoforte. She had not touched the instrument since her arrival, and at first she refused. But her sister’s gentle persuasion, added to Mrs. Patterson’s, at last overcame her reluctance. She sat down and, after a moment’s hesitation, her fingers began to move over the keys. She played a wild, melancholy air, full of passionate chords and mournful cadences. It was the music of a breaking heart, and it filled the silent room with an almost unbearable sadness. Mrs. Patterson was obliged to take out her handkerchief, and Elinor’s eyes filled with tears. Colonel Brandon sat in the shadows, his face hidden by his hand, and did not move until the last note had died away.

Marianne rose without a word and glided from the room. A painful silence followed her departure.

“Poor child,” whispered Mrs. Patterson at last. “Her whole soul was in that music.”

The Colonel raised his head. His face was pale, and his voice was unsteady as he said, “She has a gift that is as much a burden as a blessing. To feel so deeply is to live on a knife’s edge.” He then rose and, wishing them a good night, retired to his library.

Elinor saw with a renewed sense of gratitude how perfectly he understood. He did not offer clumsy words of praise or pity; he recognised the music for what it was, a pure expression of suffering, and he respected the privacy of that suffering.

The next morning, Elinor found him in the library when she went in search of a book. He was standing by the window, looking out at the gardens, where the first signs of spring were beginning to appear.

“I hope your sister is not the worse for her exertions last night,” he said, turning as she entered.

“I believe, on the contrary, that it may have done her some good,” Elinor replied. “It is better for her feelings to have some vent, than to be forever preying on themselves in silence.”

“Indeed. I was only sorry that my presence should have driven her away. I would not for the world intrude upon her.”

“You must not think that, sir. My sister’s actions are not governed by reason at present. We must all, as you yourself said, be patient.”

He gave her a grateful look. “You are a most excellent sister, Miss Dashwood. Your own trials, I fear, are often forgotten in your care for hers.”

Elinor blushed, surprised and a little flustered by this direct, personal address. “I have no trials, sir,” she murmured, turning towards the bookshelves.

“Have you not?” he asked, his voice full of a gentle sympathy that was more unnerving than any open question. He did not press the point, however, for which she was thankful. Instead, he said, “Pray, let me help you. Are you in search of anything in particular? My collection is tolerably extensive, though perhaps a little old-fashioned for modern tastes.”

“I am very fond of old-fashioned tastes,” said Elinor, recovering herself. “I see you have a fine set of Pope. And Cowper too; he is a great favourite of mine.”

“And of mine,” he said with a smile. “We are in agreement there, at least. Perhaps you would do me the honour of looking over these new engravings I have just received? They are views of the Lake District. I thought they might please your sister, when she is well enough to look at them.”

He spread the portfolio on the large library table, and for the next half hour, they were pleasantly occupied in examining the prints. They spoke of landscape, and poetry, and the different beauties of a wild and a cultivated scene. Elinor found that his taste was refined, his judgment sound, and his knowledge extensive without the least shade of pedantry. It was a conversation she enjoyed more than any she had had for a very long time. It was a meeting of minds, a quiet communion of shared tastes and principles, which she found infinitely soothing. When she at last returned to Marianne, it was with a sense of refreshment and a feeling of deep and growing respect for the master of Delaford.

A change, slight but perceptible, seemed to have begun in Marianne. The outburst of music, though it had exhausted her, seemed also to have broken the terrible spell of her apathy. The next day, she consented to walk in the garden with Elinor, and though she was silent and listless, she did not immediately retreat to her room afterwards. On the day after that, as Elinor and Mrs. Patterson were sitting at their work in the morning-room, Marianne entered with a book in her hand and, without a word, took a seat by the window. She did not read, but sat gazing out at the clouds, yet her very presence in the room was a victory.

That afternoon, returning from one of her solitary walks, her gown damp with a sudden spring shower and her cheeks flushed with a colour that was not entirely that of health, she encountered Colonel Brandon in the hall. He was about to pass her with his usual quiet bow, but he stopped, his brow furrowed with concern.

“Miss Marianne,” he said, his tone gentle but firm, “you are wet through. You will make yourself ill. Pray go and change your clothes at once.”

Marianne, who would have flown from a word of pity or a look of admiration, was arrested by this simple, authoritative command. She looked up at him, surprised, and for the first time, seemed truly to see the kindness and anxiety in his face. She hesitated for a moment, then murmured, “Thank you, sir. I will.”

She turned and went up the stairs without another word. It was not much; it was the barest civility. But to Elinor, who chanced to be crossing the gallery above and witnessed the little scene, it was a moment of the most profound significance. It was the first time Marianne had voluntarily addressed a single word to Colonel Brandon since his return. It was the first crack in the ice of her despair. And as Elinor looked down at the Colonel, who stood for a moment gazing after her sister with an expression of unutterable tenderness, she felt a sudden, startling conviction that Mrs. Jennings’s vulgar, ridiculous prophecy might, after all, come true. A slow, quiet hope, as tentative as the spring sunshine that was just then breaking through the clouds, began to dawn in her own heart; not only for her sister’s happiness, but perhaps, eventually, for her own.

The days that followed at Delaford passed in a profound and healing quietude. Colonel Brandon, with a delicacy that Elinor could not but admire, seemed to have perfected the art of being present without being intrusive. He spoke little to Marianne directly, but he contrived a thousand small comforts for her, ensuring the fire in the morning-room was always lit, that the freshest flowers from his greenhouse were placed upon the table near her accustomed chair, and that the books he thought might catch her fancy were left, as if by accident, within her reach. He directed his conversation chiefly to Elinor, discussing with her the business of his estate, the news from the papers, or the merits of a new volume of poetry, yet his eyes would often, when he thought himself unobserved, rest upon Marianne’s pensive form with an expression of such tender solicitude that it quite wrung Elinor’s heart.

Marianne, for her part, seemed slowly, almost imperceptibly, to be emerging from the dark abyss of her grief. The violent passions had subsided, leaving behind them a languor and a deep melancholy, but it was a melancholy that had lost its edge of frantic despair. She began to take short, solitary walks in the shrubbery when the weather was fine; and one afternoon, Elinor, coming into the music-room, was startled to find her seated at the pianoforte. She was not playing, but her fingers were resting upon the keys, and as she looked up, her eyes were filled with tears.

“It was his favourite,” she whispered, her voice barely audible. “He gave it to me.”

It was the first time she had mentioned Willoughby by name in a fortnight, and Elinor’s heart contracted with a painful sympathy. She sat down beside her sister, taking her hand. “I know, my dearest. I know it is difficult. But the instrument is not to blame for the faults of the giver. It has brought you much pleasure in the past; perhaps it may do so again, in time.”

Marianne shook her head, but she did not pull her hand away. “All pleasure is gone from it now. It is only a reminder of what I have lost; of what I was foolish enough to believe I possessed.”

They sat in silence for a long time, Marianne’s cold fingers clasped in Elinor’s warm ones. It was a sad, quiet communion, yet Elinor felt it was a necessary one; a lancing of the wound that must precede any true healing. From that day, though Marianne did not play, she no longer shunned the music-room, and once or twice Elinor found her there, looking through the sheets of music with a wistful air.

The change in her was not lost on Colonel Brandon. One morning he approached Elinor as she was arranging a bowl of roses. “I believe your sister is looking a little better, Miss Dashwood,” he said, his voice carefully neutral. “There is a little more colour in her cheeks today.”

“I believe you are right, sir,” Elinor replied. “The quiet and the country air are beginning to do her some good.”

“I have been thinking,” he continued, hesitating a little, “that is, I have a collection of Italian sonnets in the library—Petrarch, you know—which I fancy Miss Marianne might appreciate. They are handsomely bound. I would not presume to offer them to her myself, but if you thought she might be persuaded to look at them…”

“That is very thoughtful of you, Colonel,” said Elinor, touched by his diffidence. “I will certainly mention it to her. Her mind has been too long without employment. A little poetry may be the very thing to draw her out of herself.”

Elinor’s prediction proved correct. When she presented the volume to Marianne, her sister at first turned from it, but the beauty of the tooled leather binding seemed to catch her eye. She took the book, and though she said nothing, she did not immediately put it aside. Later that day, Elinor saw her seated by the window, the book open on her lap. She was not reading, but her gaze was fixed upon the page, as if the very shape of the words could offer some solace.

It was in this state of quiet, fragile convalescence that they were thrown, quite unexpectedly, into a flurry of activity. A letter arrived one morning from London, the familiar, looping hand of which could belong to no one but Mrs. Jennings.

“Lord bless me!” exclaimed Elinor, breaking the seal.

Colonel Brandon looked up from his newspaper with an expression of mild enquiry. Marianne, who was gazing listlessly out at the rain-swept park, did not stir.

“It is from Mrs. Jennings,” Elinor explained, her eyes scanning the page with a growing sense of alarm. “She says that she finds London dreadfully dull without us, and that Sir John is quite miserable without his ‘pretty cousins.’ In short, they propose to join us here at Delaford.”

“Here?” The word escaped Marianne, a faint cry of protest. Her cheeks, so pale a moment before, were flushed with two hectic spots of colour. “They cannot mean to come here.”

“Indeed they do,” said Elinor, reading on with a sinking heart. “‘For,’ she writes, ‘I am persuaded that a little lively company is all that dear Marianne wants to set her to rights again. We shall bring a pack of cards and a new backgammon board, and Sir John insists on bringing his two best pointers, for he is sure the Colonel has some capital shooting. We shall be with you on Thursday, if all goes well.’ Today is Tuesday! Oh, this is the very worst kind of kindness!”

Colonel Brandon had risen to his feet, his countenance a mixture of concern for Marianne and embarrassment on his own account. “Miss Dashwood, Miss Marianne, I beg you will not distress yourselves. It is my house, and while Sir John and Mrs. Jennings are always welcome, your comfort is my first consideration. I will write to them at once and explain that it is not a convenient time.”

“No,” said Marianne, surprising them both. She stood up, her slight figure trembling, but her voice was steady. “No, you will not. You will not turn away your friends on my account. I have been selfish enough. If they are to come, then they must come. I will bear it.” And with a dignity that sat strangely upon her youthful frame, she turned and walked from the room.

Elinor and Colonel Brandon were left looking at one another in shared astonishment.

“Well,” said the Colonel after a moment, a slow smile spreading across his features. “It seems your sister has more of your fortitude than I had supposed, Miss Dashwood.”

“I confess I am amazed,” Elinor replied. “A week ago, such news would have sent her into hysterics, or a relapse of her fever. That she can speak of bearing it, and think of your convenience before her own feelings—it is a greater improvement than I had dared to hope for.”

“It is indeed,” he said softly. And in his eyes, Elinor saw not only admiration for Marianne’s newfound strength, but a deep and abiding love that seemed to encompass both her past follies and her present sorrows.

True to her word, Mrs. Jennings, accompanied by the ever-hearty Sir John Middleton, descended upon Delaford on Thursday afternoon like a benevolent whirlwind. Their arrival was a study in contrasts. Sir John, stamping the mud from his boots in the hall, boomed his greetings to the Colonel, enquiring about his stables and his game covers in the same breath. Mrs. Jennings, meanwhile, enveloped Elinor in a rustling silk embrace, her voice a torrent of questions, condolences, and exclamations.

“My dear girl! How are you? And poor Marianne! Is she about? Lord, I have been thinking of nothing else. Sir John was for coming down the very day we got your letter from Cleveland, but I told him, no, we must give the poor thing time to recover her spirits. And here you are at Delaford! Who would have thought it! The very thing I predicted, you know. Did I not say the Colonel would have her at last? Though not quite at last, I hope! Ha ha!”

“Dear Ma’am,” Elinor began, trying to steer her towards the drawing-room and away from the topic of Marianne’s romantic prospects, “it is very good of you to come all this way. Colonel Brandon has been the most attentive of hosts.”

“Attentive! I should think so! And so he should be. A prettier little mistress for Delaford I never did see. Though she is looking terribly peaky, I must say. We must fatten her up. I have brought a pot of my own damson preserve, it is famous for restoring the appetite.”

At that moment, the door opened and Marianne entered. She had taken great pains with her dress, and though she was still painfully thin and pale, there was a composure in her bearing that commanded respect. She moved gracefully towards their guests, her hand extended.

“Mrs. Jennings. Sir John. Welcome to Delaford.”

Sir John, who had been loudly admiring a set of sporting prints with the Colonel, turned and seized her hand with rough affection. “Miss Marianne! By Jove, it is good to see you on your feet! You gave us all a devil of a scare, you know. A devil of a scare. But you are looking better! A little riding is what you need. The Colonel and I will take you out, we will have the roses back in your cheeks in no time!”

“You are very kind, Sir John,” Marianne replied, withdrawing her hand gently. “But I am afraid I am not yet strong enough for riding.”

Mrs. Jennings, meanwhile, was surveying her from head to foot with an expression of fond and pitying scrutiny. “My poor dear child,” she said, her voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. “You have been through a terrible time. A terrible time. But we will not speak of it. Not a word about that villain Willoughby shall pass my lips. The less said, the better. We shall be as merry as grigs, you will see. I have brought the cards. We shall have a roaring game of lottery-tickets tonight, it will take your mind off things.”

Marianne’s only reply was a faint, tight smile, but she endured Mrs. Jennings’s effusions and Sir John’s boisterousness with a patience that Elinor found nothing short of heroic. Throughout the evening, as Sir John detailed his hunting exploits and Mrs. Jennings recounted every piece of gossip from the past month in London, Marianne sat quietly with her needlework, answering when she was spoken to, but volunteering nothing. It was a performance of heroic self-control, and Elinor could see the effort it cost her in the tightness of her jaw and the shadows beneath her eyes.

Colonel Brandon, for his part, was the model of a gracious host, listening to Sir John with polite interest, laughing at Mrs. Jennings’s jokes, and all the while keeping a discreet but watchful eye on Marianne. When he saw her begin to fade, he intervened with a quiet skill.

“Mrs. Jennings,” he said, “I know you are a great judge of music. I was hoping you might give me your opinion of my new pianoforte. Perhaps Miss Marianne could be prevailed upon to play a little something for us?”

All eyes turned to Marianne. A flush spread across her pale cheeks. “I—I do not play much now, sir,” she stammered.

“Nonsense!” cried Mrs. Jennings. “Of course you will play for us, my dear. A little music is the very thing! It always cheers me up. Play that little Scotch air you are so fond of.”

Elinor held her breath. The Scotch air had been a particular favourite of Willoughby’s. To ask her to play it now seemed a refinement of cruelty, however unintentional.

Marianne looked from Mrs. Jennings’s beaming face to Colonel Brandon’s anxious one. For a moment, she seemed about to refuse, to flee the room as she would have done only weeks before. But then, she took a deep breath, and rose from her chair. “I do not think I remember that piece, ma’am,” she said, her voice low but clear. “But I will play a sonatina by Clementi, if you wish.”

She walked to the instrument and began to play. The piece was simple, elegant, and rather formal; it was nothing like the wild, passionate music she had once favoured. But she played it with a precision and a delicate touch that spoke of a new kind of discipline. There was no soul-baring passion in her performance, but there was a quiet dignity, a profound and hard-won composure.

When she had finished, there was a moment of silence. Sir John, who had been dozing by the fire, awoke with a start. Mrs. Jennings clapped her hands with rather too much enthusiasm. “Lovely, my dear! Perfectly lovely! Though a little melancholy for my taste. Next time you must play us something with a bit more life to it.”

But Colonel Brandon said nothing at all. He simply stood by the fireplace, his gaze fixed on Marianne, and on his face was an expression of such profound tenderness and understanding that Elinor had to look away, feeling herself an intruder on a moment of sacred intimacy. In that moment, she knew, with a certainty that went beyond all reason, that Mrs. Jennings’s vulgar prophecy was not only possible, but inevitable. The ill-wind had indeed blown, and it had blown Colonel Brandon a world of good. For it had blown away the passionate, impetuous girl who had been in love with a dream of Willoughby, and left in her place a woman; a woman who had looked into the abyss of her own heart and, with the help of a good man’s patient love, was slowly, painstakingly, finding her way back to the light.

The next morning found Marianne much as Elinor had left her; pale and listless, but quiet. The violent paroxysms of her grief seemed to have exhausted themselves, leaving behind a heavy, settled languor that was perhaps less alarming, but no less pitiable. She submitted to Elinor’s care, allowing her sister to brush her hair and help her into a fresh dressing-gown, but she spoke not a word, and when Elinor drew back the heavy curtains to let in the thin, grey light of a London morning, she turned her face to the wall with a sigh that seemed to come from the very depths of her soul.

“Will you not try to take some breakfast, Marianne?” Elinor asked gently, indicating the tray she had brought up. “Just a little toast, and some tea.”

For a moment, Marianne did not answer. Then, in a voice hoarse from disuse, she said, “It is all the same to me. Do as you will.”

Elinor took this for consent, and poured out the tea. As she did so, her eyes fell upon the letters lying on Marianne’s bedside table; the cruel one from Willoughby, and the little packet of her own previous notes to him, returned without comment. “It is a wretched affair,” she said, her voice lower than she intended.

Colonel Brandon turned to her, his expression softening with sympathy. “It is indeed, Miss Dashwood. And for you, to have to witness its consequences so nearly… it must be a heavy burden.”

His perception of her own part in the suffering, his recognition that she too was a victim of the situation, was a balm to her spirit. He saw her, not merely as Marianne’s nurse, but as a fellow sufferer. It was a mark of delicacy and understanding which she had met with from no one else, and she could have thanked him with tears in her eyes.

“My sister’s health is my only concern,” she managed to say.

“And it is a concern I share,” he said quietly. “If there is any service I can render, any commission however small, I beg you will command me. To be of use to you, or to her, would be the greatest satisfaction to me.”

His sincerity was so plain, his goodness so evident, that Elinor felt her reserve towards him melt entirely away. Here was a friend. In a world that had so recently shown itself to be governed by vanity and greed, the simple, steadfast worth of such a man seemed a treasure beyond price. It was a strange and disquieting thought to her, that while her heart was secretly and painfully devoted to a man whose future was entangled with another, she could still feel so profound a respect and admiration for a second. But she put the thought from her, as an indulgence she could not afford.

“You are very good, sir,” she said. “Your friendship is a comfort in itself.”

After the Colonel had taken his leave, promising to call again soon, Elinor went upstairs to sit with her sister. She found Marianne lying on her bed, her face turned to the wall. She did not speak when Elinor entered, and Elinor, respecting her silence, took up her needlework and sat in the chair by the window, a quiet and patient sentinel. The day passed slowly. The brief hope of the morning had been extinguished, and the familiar clouds of melancholy had gathered once more. Yet Elinor could not feel that all was lost. A wall that had seemed impregnable had been breached, if only for an hour. A seed of communication had been planted. Though the frost had come again, she trusted that it was not dead, and that with time, and with the careful warmth of affection, it might yet be brought to grow. Her own secret sorrow, the letter from Lucy Steele that lay hidden in her dressing-box, seemed a distant and secondary trouble. To see Marianne restored to herself, to see her able to live and to feel again, without being destroyed by her feelings; that was the first and most pressing object of all her prayers. And in the quiet goodness of Colonel Brandon, she felt she had found an ally, a truth which gave her a strength she had not known she possessed.
