“He is a man of such singular discernment,” said Mr. Alistair Finch, turning the porcelain teacup in his hand as if to inspect its pattern, though his eyes were fixed on Miss Croft. “Your brother, I mean. To have known precisely which shade of blue would most become you. I dare say he consulted every modiste in London to find it. Or was it, perhaps, a happy accident?” He smiled, a smile of such knowing intelligence that it bordered on impertinence. Miss Croft, who had been praised for the new ribbon on her bonnet, coloured faintly and looked down at her lap. “My brother has a tolerable eye for such things, sir. His own waistcoat is proof of it.” “Tolerable! It is genius. Pure genius. He must have given the matter a great deal of thought; considered the light, the time of day, the exact circumstances under which the ribbon might be worn. Did he send it with a particular injunction, I wonder? An instruction that it was to be debuted on a Tuesday, for instance, when a certain company was expected?” He paused, awaiting a reply that did not come. “Well, conjecture is a fruitless exercise, is it not? One may conjecture for a whole morning and be no nearer the truth. I conjecture that this tea has grown cold. Mrs. Pollard, you are too good, but I must refuse another cup. My nerves are quite shattered, you see, from the effort of—of what was it we were discussing, Miss Ainsworth? Ah, yes. Ribbons.” He passed his cup to the servant with a theatrical sigh, and took a seat on the ottoman beside Eleanor Ainsworth’s chair. He was thanked with a flurry of words from Mrs. Pollard; to withdraw from which, he leaned towards Eleanor under the pretence of examining her needlework. “She felt that, I think,” he whispered, with a glance towards the silent Miss Croft. “You are too plain, Mr. Finch. It is almost cruel.” “Cruelty, Miss Ainsworth? I call it a service. A little probing to discover the truth. I am persuaded she has a secret, and I am determined to have it. I have never seen a young lady so furiously placid. There is a story there.” “And you would be the one to read it aloud to the whole room?” “I would have her confess it. My meaning is not so very obscure. If she is embarrassed, she has only herself to blame for the mystery.” “She is not without some feeling on the matter, you see how she blushes.” “A blush is nothing. It is a lady’s business to blush. See, she is talking to her aunt about the price of muslin now, as if her heart were not beating a little faster than it ought. She is a capital actress.” At that moment, a voice from the garden path called out, and Mrs. Pollard flew to the open window in a state of happy agitation. “Mr. Stapleton, is it you? I thought I recognised your horse! Do come in, I pray you, do come in! We are all here, you see. So fortunate a meeting!” Mr. Stapleton, who had dismounted and was tying his horse to the gatepost, looked up at the window, his expression altogether more serious than Mrs. Pollard’s. “I thank you, Mrs. Pollard, but I am on my way to the village. I only wished to enquire after your household. And after your niece.” His voice, a clear and steady baritone, carried into the room with perfect distinction. “How does Miss Croft do this morning? I fancied she had a cough when last we met. Is she quite recovered from the chill?” “Oh! a cough, yes, the smallest thing, you are too kind to remember it. She is very well, I thank you! But you must come in, Mr. Stapleton; Miss Ainsworth is here!” The listeners within exchanged glances of amusement, all except for Miss Croft, whose eyes were suddenly fixed upon the window with an unguarded intensity. “I cannot stay,” came Mr. Stapleton’s firm reply. “But you will tell me truly how Miss Croft is? The air is still damp of an evening. I hope she takes care.” Before Mrs. Pollard could launch into another invitation, he cut her short. “Can I bring you any thing from the village?” “Oh! the village, are you? How good of you! I was just saying to Catherine, we are in want of some grey thread.” “I am not going to the draper’s. Can I do any thing for _you_?” “No, no, I thank you. But Miss Ainsworth and Mr. Finch are here! They will be so disappointed.” “I think not,” he said, and his voice was a little louder, as if for the benefit of those inside. “I am sure Mr. Finch has amusement enough. And Miss Ainsworth is too sensible to value a five minute visit. My compliments to them, and pray assure Miss Croft that I am happy to hear she is so much improved.” “Oh! but Mr. Stapleton, one more moment! about the book—so very grateful! such a comfort to my sister, she…” But he was already untying his horse. “He is gone! Was there ever such a man? Always in a hurry, and never bears to be thanked,” said Mrs. Pollard, turning back into the room. “He was most particular in his questions, Catherine, my love. So concerned about your little cough.” “Yes, aunt,” said Catherine, her voice very low. “We heard his kind enquiries. We heard every thing.” “Of course you did, my dear, the window being quite open, and Mr. Stapleton having such a commanding way of speaking. ‘Is she quite recovered?’ he said, just so. Oh! Mr. Finch, must you be leaving us already? It seems you have only just sat down.” Mr. Finch had indeed risen, and declared he had an engagement at home which could not be delayed. Eleanor, finding the object of her amusement had departed, and the object of her speculation had retreated further into silence than ever, soon found it necessary to make her own farewells.

Mr. Finch, with a step as light as his spirits, matched his pace to Eleanor’s as they made their way from Mrs. Pollard’s gate. He waited only until they were beyond any possible hearing from the house before he spoke.
“Well, Miss Ainsworth! Was it not capital sport? I vow, I have not been so prodigiously entertained in a month. Poor Miss Croft! Did you mark the charming confusion I threw her into? One begins to suspect a very decided partiality in a certain quarter.”
“You are too severe, Mr. Finch,” said Eleanor, though the smile she had suppressed in the parlour still played upon her lips. “Indeed, you pressed the point so far, I was in fear for her. She looked as if she might faint.”
“Oh! not at all. A little agitation does a lady no harm; it heightens the complexion and shows the eyes to advantage. Besides, it is a kindness, you know. If the gentleman in question is to be encouraged, he ought to have some sign; and as she is too shy to give one herself, I provide it for her. I consider myself a friend to all parties.”
Eleanor could not quite agree that tormenting a young woman was the highest office of friendship, but she was too diverted by the accuracy of her own conjectures to protest very much. “A friend to Mr. Stapleton, you mean,” she said, slyly. “It is for his benefit, then, that you conduct these experiments?”
“For his, for hers, for our own! It is all for the general good. But you, Miss Ainsworth, it is you who supplied the key. Until you spoke, I was working in the dark; I saw the lock, but had no notion of how to turn it. Your suggestion that she harbours a secret attachment was an inspiration. All her looks of pained sensibility, her starts and blushes—it is all on account of Mr. Stapleton’s attention! It is as plain as the church tower.”
He spoke with such delighted conviction that Eleanor felt a swell of pride in her own sagacity. “It is plain enough,” she agreed. “His particularity in asking after her, when he would not even come in; his anxiety over a cough she had a fortnight ago—it is all of a piece. And your allusions to his concern for her health, and the book he lent—it was that which brought the deepest blush to her cheek. You played your part to perfection.”
“I live only to be of service,” he said, with a theatrical bow. “And now that we have our theory, we must prove it. We must contrive to see them together more. We must observe. It shall be our private study, Miss Ainsworth; a philosophical inquiry into the progress of the tender affections.”
Eleanor laughed. “You make it sound like a scientific pursuit.”
“And what is it, if not the highest of sciences? At any rate, it is the most amusing. I depend upon your assistance. We shall be confederates.”
They had by now reached the turning for Highcross, the handsome and substantial house where Eleanor had been mistress since her mother’s death some years before. “You may depend upon my discretion, at least,” she said, as they parted.
“That is all I ask!” he called back, and with another wave, he was gone, leaving Eleanor to walk up the neat gravel drive with a great deal to occupy her mind. She was pleased with her morning’s work; pleased with her own penetration, pleased with Mr. Finch’s approbation, and above all, wonderfully diverted by the little drama she had uncovered. Poor Catherine Croft! To be so very much in love, and so very incapable of showing it. And poor Mr. Stapleton, to be so earnest and so entirely unaware of the effect he produced. It was a comedy of the first order, and Eleanor felt she had a ticket for the front row. The slight compunction she had felt in Mrs. Pollard’s parlour had quite vanished, replaced by the pleasant sensation of being in possession of a secret that nobody else, save her clever confederate, even suspected.

She found her father in his library, where he was most days to be found, surrounded by the comfortable and dusty scent of old leather and paper. Mr. Ainsworth was a gentleman of quiet habits and scholarly inclinations, who considered the management of his estate and the business of the neighbourhood a regrettable distraction from his books. He looked up with a mild smile as his daughter entered.
“Ah, Eleanor, my dear. You are returned. Did you have a pleasant visit?”
“Very pleasant, papa. I found Mrs. Pollard and Miss Croft at home, and Mr. Finch was there.”
“Mr. Finch? He is a lively young man. I hope he did not talk too much.”
“He talked a great deal,” said Eleanor, laughing, “but it was very entertaining. And we had a little excitement. Mr. Stapleton rode by, and was hailed from the window by Mrs. Pollard in her usual style. You would have been very much amused.”
“Stapleton is a sensible man,” said Mr. Ainsworth, already turning a page. “He does not waste his time on idle morning calls. Did he come in?”
“No, papa, he was on his way to the village, and would not be persuaded, though Mrs. Pollard tried her utmost. He only stayed to enquire, most particularly, after Miss Croft’s health.”
She paused, hoping her father might catch the significance of this, but he only murmured, “Very civil of him. She seemed a delicate girl, I thought, when she dined here last.” His attention was already lost to his book. Eleanor, sighing at this want of curiosity, but well accustomed to it, knew that if she wished to pursue her new hobby, she must be the sole architect of its opportunities.
An idea began to form, and by the time she sat down to her work-basket, it had solidified into a plan. A small dinner party. It was the very thing. It had been some weeks since they had entertained, and it was a duty she owed to her neighbours. More than a duty, it was a perfect occasion for her ‘philosophical inquiry.’ She must have the Pollards and Miss Croft; she must have Mr. Finch to act as her agent of mischief; and of course, she must have Mr. Stapleton. To see them all together, under her own roof, where she might observe every look, every word, every blush, at her leisure—the prospect was delightful.

The invitations were sent and accepted, and the evening arrived with a most promising crispness in the autumn air. Mrs. Pollard and her charge were the first to appear. The aunt was arrayed in her best silk, full of gratitude for the invitation and apologies for being the first to arrive. Catherine, by contrast, was more silent and pale than ever. She was dressed with her usual simple taste, but there was a nervousness about her, a tremor in the hand she gave to Eleanor, that spoke of a spirit ill at ease.
“So good of you, Miss Ainsworth, so very kind! My sister was just saying, it is such a treat for Catherine to come to Highcross. Our little parlour is very well, but here…” Mrs. Pollard looked around the handsome drawing-room with open admiration.
Mr. Finch was announced next, and he entered with an air of buoyant good humour, paying his compliments to Eleanor with a warmth that was almost conspiratorial, and bowing to the other ladies with polished grace. He managed to direct a look at Eleanor over Catherine’s bent head, a look that said, ‘The subject is present. Let the experiment begin.’
The party was completed by the arrival of Mr. Stapleton, who brought with him, as he always did, an air of sense and sober reality. His greeting to Eleanor was one of friendly respect, and he turned to Catherine with an immediate and gentle kindness.
“I am happy to see you out, Miss Croft,” he said, his voice low and devoid of all theatricality. “Your aunt assured me you were recovered, but I wished to see for myself.”
Eleanor watched, fascinated. Catherine murmured a reply, and a faint colour rose in her cheeks. It was just as she had predicted! Mr. Stapleton’s earnestness, and Catherine’s delicate confusion. Mr. Finch caught her eye and gave a minute, almost imperceptible nod of satisfaction.
Dinner was announced, and Eleanor found herself seated between Mr. Finch and her father, with a clear view of Catherine and Mr. Stapleton, who were placed opposite. The conversation was at first general. Mr. Ainsworth enquired of Mr. Stapleton about a new treatise on crop rotation, and Mrs. Pollard gave a rambling account of a letter she had received from a cousin in Bath. It was Mr. Finch who first steered the talk into more interesting waters.
“Bath!” he exclaimed, when Mrs. Pollard paused for breath. “I was in Bath myself last spring. A dreadful place, I think. Nothing but idle gossip and fortune-hunters. One cannot be there a week without hearing of three or four secret engagements, most of which, I dare say, are entirely imaginary.”
Eleanor saw Catherine’s head lift, her eyes fixed on Mr. Finch with a sudden, sharp intensity.
“Oh, but it is very elegant!” cried Mrs. Pollard. “My cousin says the Pump Room is a sight to be seen.”
“Perhaps,” said Mr. Finch, his gaze sliding from Catherine to the table at large, “but it is a sad place for those of a sensitive disposition. The public nature of society there must be a torment to anyone who has something to hide. A secret affection, for instance. Imagine the misery of seeing the object of one’s regard obliged to dance with another, or of hearing his name spoken of lightly by strangers. It would be an agony.”
He spoke with a philosophical air, as if merely posing a hypothetical case, but Eleanor knew his purpose exactly. She glanced at Catherine. The girl’s hand trembled as she lifted her glass of water, and her pallor was so marked that Eleanor felt a moment’s true alarm. She shot a warning look at Mr. Finch, but he was not looking at her. He was watching Catherine with an expression of cool, clinical interest.
It was Mr. Stapleton who broke the spell. Turning to Catherine with a manner that deliberately excluded the rest of the table, he began to speak to her of the new music Eleanor had mentioned a week before. “Your aunt tells me you have been practising the Irish melodies, Miss Croft. There is one, ‘The Last Rose of Summer,’ that I believe would particularly suit your voice. It requires great feeling.”
His tone was so calm, so respectful, that it seemed to offer a safe harbour from Mr. Finch’s turbulent currents. Catherine looked at him with an expression of what Eleanor could only interpret as profound gratitude. Her colour began to return, and she answered him, her voice still low, but steadier than before. They spoke for some minutes about music, and Eleanor watched, confirming her theory at every turn. Mr. Stapleton’s kindness was the antidote to Mr. Finch’s mischief; it was his attention that she craved, and the want of it, or the fear of losing it, that made her so vulnerable.
When the ladies withdrew to the drawing-room, Catherine seemed to relapse into her silent reserve. She sat on a small sofa, a little apart from Eleanor and Mrs. Pollard, her hands clasped in her lap. Eleanor’s attempts to draw her into conversation were met with polite, but brief, replies. It was only when the gentlemen rejoined them that a degree of animation returned to the party, brought in, as it were, on the tide of Mr. Finch’s irrepressible energy.
“Music!” he declared. “Miss Ainsworth, you must persuade Miss Croft to play for us. After Mr. Stapleton’s eloquent praise of her talents, we are all dying with curiosity.”
Catherine looked distressed. “I do not play well enough for company,” she began.
“Nonsense, my dear,” said Mrs. Pollard. “You play beautifully. Do play something for Mr. Stapleton.”
The directness of the appeal was excruciating. A deep blush spread over Catherine’s face and neck. She looked for a moment as if she might actually refuse, an act of social rebellion that Eleanor could scarcely imagine. But Mr. Stapleton himself came to her rescue, in a way.
“We must not press Miss Croft if she is not inclined,” he said, though he looked at her with an encouraging smile. “Perhaps later in the evening.”
“Oh, but I must insist!” said Mr. Finch, moving to the pianoforte and opening it. “I shall turn the pages. What shall it be? One of the Irish melodies? Or something more cheerful? A waltz, perhaps? We must not be melancholy.” He began to play the opening bars of a lively dance tune, his touch light and confident.
The evening wore on in this fashion: Mr. Finch, all glittering wit and pointed allusions; Catherine, a study in distressed sensibility; and Mr. Stapleton, a steadying presence, frequently intervening with a quiet question or a change of subject to draw the fire from Miss Croft. Eleanor, the observer, found her amusement mingled with a growing sense of unease. Mr. Finch’s game was clever, but it was also cruel. She saw, when she allowed herself to look past the pleasure of her own cleverness, that Catherine was not happily flustered; she was genuinely suffering.
Later, while Mr. Finch was engaging Mrs. Pollard in a debate about the best way to preserve gooseberries, effectively holding her captive with his charm, Mr. Stapleton came to sit by Eleanor on the sofa.
“You have a beautiful home, Miss Ainsworth,” he began, his eyes scanning the room. “And a kind heart. I have always thought so.”
Eleanor was a little surprised by the gravity of his tone. “I thank you, Mr. Stapleton. I hope I am not undeserving of your good opinion.”
“I am sure you are not. Which is why I wonder,” he continued, his voice lowering, “that you can take pleasure in the evening’s entertainment.”
She was completely taken aback. “Entertainment, Mr. Stapleton? I do not understand you.”
“Do you not?” He looked at her directly, and his gaze was not unkind, but it was searching. “I think you do. I speak of the sport Mr. Finch is having at Miss Croft’s expense. And which, I think, you have been encouraging.”
A hot blush rose to Eleanor’s cheeks, a blush of guilt and indignation. “I have done no such thing! Mr. Finch is… he is merely being playful. It is his way.”
“His way, perhaps. But is it a kind way? Is it a gentlemanly way? Look at your guest, Miss Ainsworth. Look at Miss Croft. Does she appear to be enjoying this playfulness? She has been on the verge of tears half the night. You asked her here as your friend. Is this how a friend should be treated in your house?”
Every word was a quiet blow, delivered without anger, but with the force of irrefutable truth. Eleanor’s eyes unwillingly followed his to where Catherine sat, pretending to examine a book of prints, her profile etched with misery. For the first time, Eleanor saw her not as a character in a comedy of her own devising, but as a young woman in real pain. The remains of a smile she had shared with Mr. Finch now felt like a stain upon her conscience.
“I… I had not seen it in that light,” she stammered, feeling her composure and her cherished sagacity crumble. “I believed… I thought the cause of her agitation was… something else entirely.”
“It matters little what the cause is,” said Mr. Stapleton, “if the effect is suffering. And to find amusement in that suffering—that is what surprises me in you, Miss Ainsworth. It is not like you.”
He rose, as if the subject were now closed, and went to make his farewells to her father. Eleanor sat frozen, her cheeks burning. The whole evening, which she had planned with such self-satisfied glee, now seemed a monstrous exercise in casual cruelty. She had been so proud of her ‘key’ to Catherine’s character, but Mr. Stapleton had just shown her that it unlocked nothing but her own vanity. Her confederacy with Mr. Finch no longer seemed a witty alliance, but a partnership in unkindness.
The party broke up soon afterwards. Mr. Finch, on taking his leave, whispered to her, “Our experiment yields most interesting results, does it not?” But Eleanor could not meet his eye. She gave him a cold nod, unable to summon the easy rapport they had shared earlier.
When the last of the carriages had rolled away, and she was left alone in the silent drawing-room, the scene replayed in her mind, but it was no longer a comedy. Mr. Stapleton’s words echoed in the quiet: ‘It is not like you.’ Was it not? She had been so sure of her own judgement, so confident in her reading of everyone around her. And now, she felt she understood nothing at all. The only thing she knew with any certainty was a profound and uncomfortable sense of shame.

The fire in the drawing-room grate had long since died to embers, but Eleanor felt no chill. A heat of a different sort, a burning mortification, was enough to keep her warm. Sleep offered no refuge; for whenever she closed her eyes, she saw not the welcome darkness but the clear, disappointed gaze of Mr. Stapleton, and heard, as if he were in the room beside her, his quiet, cutting pronouncement: ‘It is not like you.’ Was it not, indeed? The question tormented her. She had always supposed herself to possess a superior understanding, a particular gift for seeing into the hearts of those around her; she had moved her friends and acquaintances about in her mind like so many pieces on a chess-board, confident that she alone knew the true object of the game. Now she saw that she had not been playing a game at all, but merely blundering about, knocking over the pieces with a clumsy and unthinking hand. Her clever ‘experiment,’ her witty alliance with Mr. Finch, which had seemed so diverting only a few hours before, now appeared a shabby, contemptible business. To take a young woman, a guest in her own home, and to make her anxieties a source of private sport; it was an action worthy of a heartless flirt, not of a sensible, well-bred lady. She, Eleanor Ainsworth, who so prided herself on her judgment and her kindness, had behaved with the thoughtless cruelty of a schoolboy pulling the wings from a fly.

Morning brought no relief, only the flat, grey light of reality. At the breakfast table, her father, blessedly oblivious, remarked upon the success of the previous evening. “A most agreeable party, my dear Eleanor,” he said, spreading marmalade upon his toast. “Young Finch is a very entertaining fellow. And Mr. Stapleton; a man of sound sense, though perhaps a trifle too serious for such a gathering. Miss Croft seemed a little out of spirits, I thought; a pretty girl, but wants animation.”

Eleanor murmured some vague assent, her coffee growing cold in its cup. Every word of her father’s innocent praise was a fresh reproach. ‘Wants animation.’ And whose fault was that? She had believed Catherine’s quietness a sign of a secret, romantic preoccupation, a charming symptom to be studied. She had not considered that it might simply be the quietness of misery. A resolution, hard and unwelcome, began to form in her mind. There was only one thing to be done, one course of action that might begin to ease the dreadful weight upon her conscience. She must go to Catherine. She must attempt some kind of apology, however humiliating, however inadequate it was certain to be. The thought of it made her cheeks burn anew. What could she possibly say? ‘Pray forgive me, Catherine, for I have been treating your heart as a subject for a scientific inquiry, and have found the results most amusing’? The very idea was monstrous. Yet the alternative, to do nothing, to let Catherine continue to think her a friend while harbouring the memory of such unkindness, was more unbearable still.

By mid-morning, having fortified herself with a renewed sense of duty, she ordered the carriage. The short drive to the Crofts’ lodgings felt a journey of a hundred miles. She rehearsed and discarded a dozen opening speeches, each one sounding more false and contrived than the last. She felt a profound dread of the impending interview, a dread so acute that she was tempted to order the driver to turn back, to plead a sudden headache, to invent any excuse to avoid the meeting. But Mr. Stapleton’s face rose again in her mind’s eye, and she held her resolve.

The Crofts occupied a small but genteel house on the edge of the town, their reduced circumstances making such modest lodgings a necessity. Eleanor was shown into a parlour that was neat and pleasant, but which wanted the spacious elegance of her own home. Mrs. Croft, a gentle, faded woman with an perpetually anxious expression, was seated by the window with her needlework. Catherine was at a small writing-desk, a half-finished letter before her. She looked up as Eleanor entered, and a faint blush of surprise, not entirely of pleasure, rose to her cheeks.

“Miss Ainsworth! Eleanor! This is an unexpected pleasure,” said Mrs. Croft, rising. “How good of you to call upon us so soon after your delightful party.”

“It was no trouble at all,” Eleanor replied, her voice sounding unnaturally high in her own ears. “I was passing, and wished to enquire how you both did after the fatigues of the evening.”

“Oh, we are quite well, I thank you,” said Catherine, her own voice very low. She had not risen, but sat turning a pen over in her fingers. “It was a most… a most beautiful party.”

An awkward silence fell. Eleanor took the seat offered to her, her mind racing. How could she speak to Catherine with her mother sitting there, a witness to every word, every nuance? The conversation that followed was an exercise in polite misery. They spoke of the weather; they discussed the merits of the musicians from the night before; Mrs. Croft enquired after Mr. Ainsworth’s health. All the while, Eleanor was acutely conscious of Catherine’s stillness, of the way she kept her eyes fixed upon the desk before her. She was not taking part in the conversation; she was merely enduring it. Eleanor knew she could not leave things like this. She must find a way to speak to her alone.

“Your garden is looking very pretty, Mrs. Croft,” Eleanor said at last, grasping at a desperate expedient. “I caught a glimpse of it from the carriage. The roses are particularly fine.”

“Oh, do you think so?” replied Mrs. Croft, pleased. “It is my daughter’s chief care. Catherine has a wonderful hand with roses.”

“Perhaps,” Eleanor said, turning to Catherine with a look of entreaty, “perhaps you would do me the honour of showing them to me? The sun is not too strong, and I should so like to see them up close.”

Catherine looked surprised, and for a moment, Eleanor feared she would refuse. A flicker of something—was it suspicion?—crossed her face. But her mother’s fond pride decided the matter. “Of course you will, Catherine my dear. Put on your bonnet. Miss Ainsworth is a great judge of flowers, you know.”

With a quiet sigh of resignation, Catherine acquiesced. A few moments later, they were walking down a narrow gravel path in the small, walled garden behind the house. The air was sweet with the scent of stocks and climbing roses, but Eleanor could take no pleasure in it. Now that she had her opportunity, the words seemed to have deserted her entirely. They walked half the length of the path in silence, Catherine pointing out a particularly fine bloom with a detached air, as if performing a duty for a stranger.

Finally, Eleanor could bear it no longer. She stopped and turned to her companion. “Catherine,” she began, her voice trembling slightly, “I did not ask to see the garden because I care for roses. I asked because I… I must speak to you. I must beg your pardon.”

Catherine stopped also, her expression one of utter astonishment. “Beg my pardon? For what, Eleanor?”

“For last night. For my behaviour. I… I was not a good friend to you. I saw that you were in distress, and I did nothing. Worse than nothing. I allowed… I was amused. It was unforgivable.” The confession, so difficult to contemplate, came rushing out, clumsy and incomplete, but sincere.

Catherine’s eyes filled with tears, but she did not look away. “Amused? I did not think… I thought you simply did not notice.”

“Oh, I noticed,” Eleanor said, her own eyes now stinging. “I noticed, and I drew all the wrong conclusions. I behaved abominably, and I am more sorry than I can say. Mr. Finch… his manner is too bold, and I ought to have checked it. Instead, I encouraged it. It was a contemptible thing to do, and I am deeply, deeply ashamed.”

Catherine put a hand on her arm, a gesture of such simple, unexpected forgiveness that it nearly undid Eleanor completely. “Oh, Eleanor, do not say so. You are not contemptible. You could not be. It is true, Mr. Finch’s raillery was… painful. More painful than he could ever guess. But it was not you. It was my own folly, my own private sorrows that made me so sensitive.”

“But that is no excuse for me,” Eleanor insisted. “A friend should soothe sorrow, not make sport of it. I had formed an idea, a ridiculous, vain idea, about the cause of your agitation, and I acted upon it without a thought for your real feelings. Can you forgive me?”

“There is nothing to forgive,” Catherine said softly, though a tear now traced a path down her cheek. She wiped it away with the back of her hand. “You have always been a kind friend to me. It is just that… at present… my heart is very heavy, and I cannot bear to be teased, even in jest.”

A silence fell between them, but it was no longer the strained silence of the parlour. It was a silence of shared understanding. “Is it a sorrow you can speak of?” Eleanor asked gently, her desire to know now springing not from idle curiosity, but from a genuine wish to comfort. “Perhaps it would be a relief to share it.”

Catherine hesitated, her gaze dropping to a white rose beside the path. “I have told no one,” she whispered. “Not even my mother. She has worries enough of her own. But perhaps… perhaps you are right. You see, when we were living in Bath, before my father’s misfortunes, I… I became acquainted with a gentleman. A Mr. Harris. He is a curate, with no fortune and no prospects to speak of. My father did not approve the acquaintance. And now that our circumstances are so reduced, any… any possibility of a union is quite gone. We are forbidden to correspond. I have not heard from him in six months.”

She paused, taking a shaky breath. “Last night, when Mr. Finch spoke so freely of attachments, and secrets, and letters… it felt as if he knew my very heart, and was mocking me for my foolishness. Every jest was like a fresh wound. I know it is silly to be so affected, but I cannot help it. I believe my affection for Mr. Harris is quite hopeless, and it makes me very unhappy.”

Here was the truth. And it was nothing like the grand, romantic drama Eleanor had imagined. It was a simple, sad story of a thwarted, gentle love. Her elaborate theory, her ‘key’ to Catherine’s character, was not only wrong, it was laughably, humiliatingly wrong. She had been searching for a hidden palace and had failed to see the humble cottage right before her eyes.

“Oh, Catherine,” she said, full of a sympathy that was now entirely unfeigned. “That is not silly at all. It is a very real, and very great, sorrow. I am so sorry. I am sorry for your unhappiness, and I am doubly sorry for my own blindness.”

They stood for a long time in the quiet garden, and for the first time, Eleanor felt a true and equal friendship for the young woman beside her. She had come to perform a humiliating duty, but she had found instead a strange and unexpected relief. The shame had not vanished, but it was now tempered with a sense of purpose. She had made a mistake, a terrible one, but perhaps it was not too late to begin to repair it.

When she took her leave a little while later, Mrs. Croft remarked on how much good the air had done her daughter. “She has more colour in her cheeks than I have seen all week,” she declared. “You must come again very soon, Miss Ainsworth. Your visits are a tonic.”

Eleanor smiled, a real smile this time. “I shall, Mrs. Croft. I most certainly shall.”

As the carriage turned back towards the town, she felt a lightness she had not thought possible that morning. She had debased herself, confessed her own folly, and in doing so, had found a measure of peace. Her penance, however, was not yet complete. As they approached the High Street, she saw a familiar figure strolling towards them, swinging a cane with an air of careless elegance. It was Mr. Finch. He saw the carriage and broke into a wide smile, raising his hand in greeting as if he expected the carriage to stop for him.

Eleanor’s new-found peace immediately evaporated, replaced by a cold resolve. She leaned forward and spoke to the driver. “Do not stop. Drive on.”

Mr. Finch’s smile faltered as the carriage continued past him without so much as slowing. He looked from the driver to the window where he must have caught a glimpse of Eleanor’s face, his expression one of pure bewilderment. Eleanor did not look back. She kept her eyes fixed firmly ahead, her heart beating a little too fast. Their confederacy was over. She knew he would seek an explanation, and she knew she would have to give him one; but for now, this public, deliberate cut was enough. It was a small act, but it felt significant, a drawing of a line in the sand.

Back in the solitude of her own room, Eleanor reflected on the events of the last twenty-four hours. It felt as though a week had passed. The self-satisfied, clever young woman who had planned last night’s party seemed a stranger to her now. She had been so certain of her own world, and her place in it. Mr. Stapleton had shaken that world with a few quiet words, and Catherine, with her simple, honest confession, had turned it upside down completely. She saw now that her ‘sagacity’ was little more than vanity, her ‘experiments’ a mask for a profound ignorance of the human heart.

The memory of Mr. Stapleton’s approbation, which she had once so casually enjoyed, now seemed a thing of immense value, a treasure she had carelessly lost. He had said, ‘It is not like you.’ She had taken it as a condemnation, but as she thought on it now, a new interpretation began to dawn. Perhaps it was not merely a judgment on what she had done, but an expression of faith in what she might be. Perhaps he had seen in her not just the vanity and the thoughtlessness, but a better self which she had betrayed. It was a faint, yet persistent hope. The path back to earning his good opinion, and more importantly, to earning back her own, seemed long and arduous. But for the first time, she felt she could see where it began. It began not with judging others, but with understanding them; and it was a lesson she was determined, at last, to learn.

The following day passed in a quietude that was both welcome and disquieting. Eleanor kept to the house, pleading a slight headache to excuse herself from a planned morning walk with neighbours, a fabrication she felt only a little guilt in employing. The truth was that she did not trust herself in company; she felt too raw, too uncertain of the new person she was attempting to become. Her old habits of speech, of easy wit and sharp observation, lay in wait for her at every turn, and she feared they might spring forth unbidden, undoing the fragile progress she had made. Her father, a kind man whose notice of the emotional weather within his own home was not so sharp as his eye for the weather in the fields, saw only that his daughter was less animated than usual.

“You are very quiet, my dear,” he remarked at luncheon, peering at her over his spectacles. “I hope you are not sickening for something. Mrs. Jennings’s girl is down with a dreadful cough, I hear.”

“I am perfectly well, Papa, only a little tired,” Eleanor replied, forcing a small smile.

“Tired? Well, I am not surprised. All that dancing and carrying on. And then young Finch called this morning while you were still abovestairs. A pity you missed him; he seemed in very good spirits. He said he would call again. A very presentable young man, Finch. Always well-dressed.”

Eleanor’s fork stilled in her hand. A cold dread, sharp and unwelcome, pierced the quiet of her reflections. Of course he would call again. She had delivered a public slight, and his vanity, if not his friendship, would demand an accounting. She had known this, abstractly, but the news of his imminent return made it terrifyingly real. What would she say? How could she explain a transformation of the soul to a man who dwelt so exclusively on the surface of things?

“Indeed,” she said, her voice barely a whisper. “Very well-dressed.”

Her father, satisfied with this endorsement, returned to his mutton. “Yes. Not like that young fellow of the parson’s, who looks as though he has been dressed by a whirlwind. Still, they say he has good Greek. It takes all sorts, I suppose.”

Eleanor spent the remainder of the afternoon in a state of nervous anticipation. Every sound from the hall, every crunch of gravel on the drive, sent her heart leaping into her throat. She tried to read, but the words blurred before her eyes. She took up her needlework, only to find her stitches were uneven and clumsy. The person she had resolved to be—calm, considerate, and self-possessed—seemed to have fled at the first sign of trial, leaving behind the same foolish, frightened girl. She was rehearsing speeches in her mind, alternating between cold formality and earnest appeal, when the drawing-room door opened and the butler announced, “Mr. Finch.”

He entered with his customary air of easy elegance, though Eleanor, in her heightened state of awareness, thought she detected a certain tightness around his eyes, a tension in the set of his smile. He had come for his explanation.

“Miss Lytton,” he began, bowing over her hand with a flourish that now seemed to her entirely theatrical. “I was so sorry to hear from your father that you were indisposed this morning. I trust the malady was not a serious one? I confess, when your carriage passed me by yesterday with such velocity, I feared you had been taken suddenly ill.”

It was an invitation to prevaricate, to blame a headache or a swoon, and restore the comfortable balance of their acquaintance. The old Eleanor would have seized it with glee, perhaps even embellished the story for his amusement. The new Eleanor, trembling and uncertain, knew she could not.

“I am quite well, I thank you, Mr. Finch,” she said, withdrawing her hand and gesturing to a chair opposite her own. She remained standing. “There was no malady yesterday. My actions were my own.”

His smile faltered for a second, the light in his eyes dimming. He chose not to take the offered seat, creating an awkward tableau in the centre of the room. “Your own? Then I am at a loss. I had thought—I had flattered myself—that we were friends. Allies. Have I given some offence of which I am unaware? If I have, I beg you will tell me, that I might atone for it.”

His tone was one of perfect reasonableness, yet it grated on her. He spoke of atonement as if it were a social debt, a matter of a missed appointment that could be corrected with a pretty apology. He did not understand. How could he?

“The offence, Mr. Finch,” she said, her voice gaining a little strength, “has been entirely mine. Not to you, but to myself, and to others.”

He stared at her, his head tilted. “I am afraid you speak in riddles, Miss Lytton. What others? Are you referring to our little jest with Miss Maxwell? Surely you cannot still be thinking on that? It was a harmless diversion. The girl is a simpleton; she will have forgotten it by now.”

“Her name is Catherine,” Eleanor said, her voice sharp and cold. “And she is not a simpleton. She is a good, and honest, and trusting young woman. And what we did—what *I* did—was cruel and indefensible. It was not a jest; it was a piece of casual wickedness, and I am deeply ashamed of it.”

The silence that followed was profound. Mr. Finch looked at her as if she had suddenly begun speaking in a foreign tongue. The look of bewilderment she had seen on his face yesterday had returned, now mingled with something akin to disbelief.

“Ashamed?” he repeated, the word sounding strange in his mouth. “My dear Eleanor, you are being theatrical. It is most unlike you. We were merely amusing ourselves. It is what everyone does. You have always been the most amusing of my acquaintance precisely because you understood the game.”

“Then I am sorry to disappoint you, sir, but I no longer wish to play,” she said. “I have spent the last two days in a state of profound regret for my own behaviour. I have discovered in myself a capacity for vanity and thoughtlessness that I find… reprehensible. The confederacy you and I shared was based upon that vanity. It cannot, and it must not, continue.”

He took a step back, his face a mask of wounded pride. To be seen thus, in her own drawing-room, as a subject of moral lecture by a girl he had considered his most amusing accomplice, was an indignity his pride could scarcely suffer; yet suffer it he must, for her composure was absolute.

“So that is it,” he said, his voice losing its silken edge and taking on a bitter, sarcastic quality. “A fit of conscience. A sudden conversion on the road to Damascus. Or has someone else been whispering in your ear? Has Mr. Stapleton, perhaps, been holding forth on the virtues of a sober and righteous life? I saw you speaking with him. He always looks as if he is composing a sermon in his head.”

The mention of Mr. Stapleton’s name sent a flush of colour to Eleanor’s cheeks, a betrayal she could not prevent. She had been foolish to think her conversation with him had gone unobserved. Mr. Finch’s eyes narrowed, seizing upon her reaction with a flash of triumph.

“Ah,” he said softly. “I see. So it is not a change of heart, but a change of audience you seek. You wish to play a new part, for a new admirer. The penitent. A very fetching role, I am sure. Well, I shall not spoil your performance. I bid you good day, Miss Lytton. I trust you will find your new piety as amusing as you once found your wit.”

He turned on his heel without another word and was gone. The closing of the front door echoed through the quiet house like a judgment. Eleanor sank into a chair, her body trembling. The interview had been worse than she had imagined. He had not understood, and he had twisted her motives into something calculating and ugly. And yet, beneath the sting of his accusations, she felt a strange and liberating calm. The tie was cut. One part of her penance was complete. She had spoken the truth, however clumsily, and stood by it. The thought that he believed her actions were designed to attract Mr. Stapleton was mortifying, yet it contained a sliver of unintended truth. She did want to be worthy of Mr. Stapleton’s good opinion, not by pretending to be someone she was not, but by truly becoming a better person.

Her next duty was clear. An apology to Mr. Finch had been impossible, as he saw no fault; an apology to Catherine was essential. The next morning, without a word to her father, she put on her plainest bonnet and walked to the modest house where Catherine lived with her widowed mother. She was shown into a small, neat parlour where Catherine was sitting alone, mending a linen sheet. The girl looked up in surprise, her cheeks pinkening as she rose to her feet.

“Miss Lytton,” she said, her voice uncertain.

“Catherine,” Eleanor began, her own voice unsteady. “Pray, do not get up. May I sit with you for a moment?”

She took a seat, her gloved hands twisting in her lap. All her rehearsed speeches seemed inadequate. She looked at Catherine’s gentle, open face, and the simple truth seemed the only option.

“I have come to beg your pardon,” she said quietly. “I behaved very ill to you at the party. There is no excuse for my conduct. It was vain, and unkind, and I have been ashamed of it ever since. I led you to believe things that were not true, for my own amusement. It was a contemptible thing to do, and I am more sorry than I can say.”

Catherine stared at her, her blue eyes wide. She seemed more bewildered by the apology than she had been by the original offence. “Oh, Miss Lytton! Pray do not think on it. I—I was a little confused at the time, it is true, but I am sure you meant no real harm. You are very clever, and I am not, and I expect I misunderstood.”

“No,” Eleanor said, shaking her head firmly. “You must not think that. You did not misunderstand. I did mean harm, of a sort. The harm of not considering you, of treating you as a plaything rather than a person with feelings of her own. The fault was entirely mine. Can you—can you forgive me?”

A tear welled in Catherine’s eye and slid down her cheek. “There is nothing to forgive, Miss Lytton. Truly. That you should come here, and speak to me so, is the greatest kindness. I—I have been very unhappy. I believed Captain Mallory admired me, and I felt such a fool.”

“Captain Mallory is the fool,” Eleanor said, with a flash of her old spirit, but it was tempered now with a new softness. “He is a man of little sense and less feeling. You are worth a dozen of him. Your honest heart is a treasure, Catherine, and I was too blind and foolish to see it.”

At this, Catherine could no longer contain her feelings and burst into quiet tears. Eleanor, who had once viewed such displays with a cool detachment, felt an unfamiliar ache in her own heart. She moved to sit beside the girl on the little sofa and, after a moment’s hesitation, took her hand. It was a small gesture, but it felt momentous. They sat in silence for some minutes, the only sound the soft ticking of the mantel clock and Catherine’s quiet sniffling.

When Catherine had composed herself, she looked at Eleanor with a watery smile. “My mother always says that a true lady is known by her kindness,” she said shyly. “You are a true lady, Miss Lytton.”

Eleanor felt her own eyes prickle. This simple, heartfelt praise meant more to her than all the compliments on her wit she had ever received. They spoke for a little while longer, of ordinary things—of Catherine’s mother’s health, and the price of ribbon, and a new book that had come into the circulating library. When Eleanor rose to leave, the atmosphere between them had changed completely. A fragile, tentative friendship had taken root in the soil of a sincere apology.

Walking home, Eleanor felt a lightness in her step. The world seemed brighter, the air clearer. She had faced two difficult confrontations and had not shrunk from them. She had wounded Mr. Finch’s pride, and she had soothed Catherine’s heart. The one was a necessary severance, the other a hopeful beginning. She felt as though she was slowly, painstakingly, mending a part of her own character that she had not even known was broken.

As she turned the corner into her own lane, she saw a figure up ahead, walking in the same direction. It was Mr. Stapleton. Her first instinct was to quicken her pace, to catch up with him, to say… what? She had no idea. Her second, more powerful instinct was to hang back, to let him proceed alone, to avoid an encounter for which she still felt unprepared. But he had paused to inspect a branch of blossom on an overhanging tree, and as he turned, he saw her.

He waited for her to approach, his expression unreadable. Eleanor felt the now-familiar flush rise in her cheeks. She was certain he must have heard of her falling-out with Mr. Finch; news of that sort travelled with remarkable speed. Did he now see her, as Mr. Finch had predicted, as a performer playing a part for his benefit?

“Miss Lytton,” he said, his voice as calm and steady as ever. “A beautiful morning for a walk.”

“Yes, it is,” she managed, her own voice sounding breathless and thin. “I have been to call on Miss Maxwell.”

She had not intended to say it. The words came out of their own accord, a simple statement of fact. She watched his face for a reaction. A flicker of surprise, perhaps? A nod of approval? There was nothing. He merely inclined his head.

“I hope you found her well,” he said.

“I did, thank you.”

An awkward silence fell between them. Eleanor searched her mind for some neutral topic, some observation about the weather or the state of the roads, but her mind was a complete blank. She felt as clumsy and tongue-tied as she had once accused Catherine of being.

“I find,” he said at last, his eyes on the distant prospect of the town, “that a sincere apology is a rare and admirable thing. It is much harder to mend a fault than to commit one.”

Eleanor’s heart gave a great leap. He was not looking at her. His remark might have been a general philosophical observation. But she knew, with a certainty that went deeper than logic, that it was not. He knew. Perhaps Catherine’s mother had told the parson, who had told his wife, who had told… It did not matter. He knew, and he did not disapprove. He had not offered praise, for that was not his way. He had simply stated a truth, and in doing so, had let her know that he had seen her action, and understood its worth.

He turned to look at her then, a long, steady gaze that held no condemnation, and no easy forgiveness either. It was a look of quiet, serious observation. It was the look he might give to a complex problem, or a difficult piece of text. It was a look that acknowledged her complexity, her capacity for both error and correction. And in that moment, Eleanor felt that his observation was not a judgment to be feared, but a standard to which she might, one day, aspire.

“Yes,” she said, finding her voice at last. “Much harder. But I am beginning to think it is the only work that truly matters.”

He gave a slight, almost imperceptible nod. “I must leave you here; my own path lies this way. Good day, Miss Lytton.”

“Good day, Mr. Stapleton.”

She watched him walk away until he was out of sight. The faint hope she had felt in her room two days ago had not flowered into certainty, but it had put down deeper roots. The path ahead was still long, and she knew she would stumble again. But for the first time, she felt she was walking in the right direction, and she was not walking entirely alone.

She returned to the house not with the light step of one whose anxieties had been entirely banished, but with the more measured tread of a traveller who, having lost her way in a thicket, at last espies a reliable landmark in the distance. The path was not yet cleared, but its direction was no longer a matter of pure conjecture. Her mother’s drawing room, a space she knew as well as her own reflection, seemed at once familiar and foreign; the very chairs and tables, in their placid arrangement, appeared to mock the recent turmoil of her soul.

“There you are, Eleanor,” said Mrs. Lytton, looking up from her needlework. “You have been gone an age. I was beginning to fancy you had eloped with a passing stranger, though I own the prospects for such an adventure are rather thin in our neighbourhood.”

“I only walked as far as the upper lane, mama. The air was so fine.”

“And you met no one?” Her mother’s tone was casual, but her eyes were keen. Mrs. Lytton possessed a species of curiosity that operated best when it feigned indifference.

“I happened to cross paths with Mr. Stapleton,” Eleanor replied, moving to the window to adjust the fall of a curtain. She wished to keep her face from her mother’s view.

“Ah, Mr. Stapleton. A very serious young man. Your father esteems him greatly, which is praise indeed. Did he have much to say? I find he is not a great talker, unless the topic be drainage or the price of corn.”

“We spoke very little. He remarked upon the weather.” This was not a falsehood, but it was a severe abridgement of the truth, and Eleanor felt a familiar pang of self-reproach. But how could she explain the significance of a conversation that had consisted of so few words, and so much unspoken meaning? How could she convey that a discussion of mending faults had felt more intimate than any declaration of sentiment? She could not; and so she held her tongue.

A silence ensued, broken only by the rhythmic prick of Mrs. Lytton’s needle. At length, her mother spoke again. “I have been thinking, Eleanor. Now that this… unpleasantness with the Maxwells has been so sensibly resolved on your part—and I do commend you, my dear, it was very well done of you to call—it is high time we shewed them some proper civility. We have not had a dinner party in months. I think we shall have one next week. Yes, on Tuesday.”

Eleanor turned from the window, her heart sinking a little. A dinner party. It was the natural and proper thing to do, the necessary social cement to repair the breach she had made. And yet, the prospect of an entire evening spent in formal company, with Catherine Maxwell’s hurt, watchful eyes upon her, and Mr. Stapleton observing them both, was almost more than she could bear. “So soon, mama?”

“There is no time like the present for doing one’s duty,” Mrs. Lytton declared, with an air of finality that suggested the matter was settled. “We shall have the Maxwells, of course, and Mr. Finch and his wife. And your father will wish to invite Mr. Stapleton; they have some business about a lease to discuss. And perhaps Sir William and Lady Croft to make the numbers even. It will be a small party, but a respectable one. It will shew that all is well between our families. You shall write the invitations this afternoon, Eleanor. It will be a good occupation for you.”

There was no arguing. The invitations were duly written, in Eleanor’s neatest hand, and dispatched by a servant. The intervening days were a trial of her newfound resolve. Every time she thought of the coming Tuesday, her composure threatened to desert her. What a mercy it was that Mr. Stapleton had not witnessed her first, ungracious interview with Catherine; what a still greater mercy that he seemed to comprehend the second. But would his silent approbation, as she chose to interpret it, sustain her through a whole evening of polite performance? She feared it would not.

Tuesday arrived, as Tuesdays must, regardless of the private anxieties of young ladies. Eleanor dressed with unusual care, though with little satisfaction. She descended to the drawing room to find her father already there, discussing the state of the nation with a grim satisfaction that suggested it was precisely as bad as he had always predicted.

“Ah, Eleanor,” he said. “You look very well. Stapleton is a sensible man; he will not be late.”

Eleanor blushed, annoyed at the implication. “I had not supposed he would, papa.”

The first guests to arrive were, as ever, the parson and his wife. Mrs. Finch was a small, bustling woman, whose chief pleasure in life was the observation and gentle management of the affairs of her neighbours. “My dear Mrs. Lytton! So good of you to have us. And Eleanor, my love, you are blooming. Is not she, Mr. Finch? Country air does so agree with our young people.”

The Maxwells were next. Mrs. Maxwell entered with her usual air of flustered goodwill, but Catherine’s demeanour was more difficult to read. She was pale, and her eyes, when they met Eleanor’s, were shuttered and distant. They exchanged the briefest of courtesies.

“How do you do, Miss Lytton.”

“I am very well, thank you, Miss Maxwell. I hope you are the same.”

It was a beginning, but a fragile one. Eleanor felt the awkwardness between them like a physical barrier. She was relieved when Sir William and Lady Croft were announced, their hearty, uncomplicated presences filling the room with the comfortable noise of conventional greetings.

Mr. Stapleton was the last to arrive. He entered with his characteristic lack of fuss, his quiet presence immediately commanding a certain attention. He greeted his host and hostess, and then his eyes found Eleanor. He gave a slight bow, his expression unreadable. “Miss Lytton.”

“Mr. Stapleton.”

Dinner was announced. Mr. Lytton took in Lady Croft, Sir William took in Mrs. Lytton, and Mr. Finch took in Mrs. Maxwell, leaving Mr. Stapleton to offer his arm to Catherine, and Eleanor to follow with her father’s man of business, a silent fellow who had been a last minute addition. Eleanor found herself seated between this man and Mr. Finch, directly opposite Mr. Stapleton and Catherine. It was a position that offered an excellent view, and very little peace of mind.

The conversation at first was general. Sir William held forth on the failings of his tenant farmers, and Mr. Lytton agreed with him. Mrs. Finch enquired after everyone’s health, and Lady Croft described a tedious visit from a cousin. Eleanor said little, applying herself to her soup and trying to appear composed. She could not help but watch Mr. Stapleton. He listened more than he spoke, but when he did speak, it was with a precision that made others attend. He was conversing with Catherine, his manner one of gentle courtesy. He asked after her mother, and whether she had found any new plants for her collection on her walks. Catherine’s answers were brief, but she seemed less tense than she had been in the drawing room. A faint colour had risen in her cheeks.

“It is a true delight,” announced Mrs. Finch, her voice carrying across the table in a slight lull, “to see dear friends reconciled. There is nothing in this world so pleasing as forgiveness and harmony. Is there, Miss Maxwell?”

A dead silence fell upon the table. Catherine looked down at her plate, her blush deepening to a painful crimson. Mrs. Maxwell began to stammer something about the excellence of the fish. Eleanor felt a hot wave of anger and embarrassment, directed as much at herself as at the parson’s wife. It was her fault that had created this situation, her failure that was now being picked over for public consumption.

It was Mr. Stapleton who broke the spell. Without looking at Mrs. Finch, he turned his attention coolly back to Catherine. “You were telling me of the book of Scottish ballads you have been reading, Miss Maxwell. I confess I am not well acquainted with them. Do they compare at all to the English tradition?”

He had not only rescued Catherine; he had done so by taking her seriously, by referring back to a private conversation and treating her as an intellectual equal. It was done so smoothly, so decisively, that the awkward moment was instantly dispelled. The general conversation resumed, flowing around the little island of calm he had created. Eleanor watched him, her admiration warring with a strange and unfamiliar feeling. It was not jealousy, precisely; it was a sort of pained recognition. He was kind to Catherine, as he ought to be. His kindness was a part of the very integrity she admired in him. And yet, she wished his kindness were directed elsewhere. She wished—she hardly knew what she wished.

After the ladies had withdrawn to the drawing room, the atmosphere was thick with unspoken things. Mrs. Lytton and Lady Croft settled on a sofa to discuss the shortcomings of a new housemaid, while Mrs. Finch attempted to draw Mrs. Maxwell into a hushed conference at the other end of the room. This left Eleanor and Catherine standing near the fireplace, in a solitude more daunting than any crowd.

Eleanor knew that something must be said. The stilted courtesy of their greeting would not suffice. “The fire is very warm,” she began, her voice sounding unnatural in her own ears.

Catherine murmured her assent.

Taking a breath, Eleanor tried again. “I was so sorry… that is, I was vexed by Mrs. Finch’s remark at dinner. It was most thoughtless of her.”

Catherine looked at her then, a real look, and Eleanor saw that the hurt in her eyes was still there, but it was mingled with something else; a weariness, perhaps. “Mrs. Finch means no harm,” she said quietly. “She only speaks as she thinks.”

“It is a habit that can cause a great deal of pain,” Eleanor replied, with more feeling than she had intended.

A flicker of something—surprise? sympathy?—crossed Catherine’s face. “Yes,” she said. “It can.” She paused, her gaze dropping to the fan she held in her hands. “Mr. Stapleton was very good to change the subject.”

“Yes, he was.” Eleanor’s agreement was heartfelt. “He is a very thoughtful person.”

“He is not like other gentlemen,” Catherine said, almost to herself. “He does not flatter, but he listens. When he asks a question, you feel he truly wishes to know the answer.”

Eleanor’s heart contracted. Catherine saw it too. Of course she did. Who with any sense or feeling could fail to see it? For a moment, she felt a premonition of a new and more complicated unhappiness, a future in which she and Catherine might become rivals for something far more valuable than social precedence. The thought was so unwelcome that she pushed it away at once. It was unworthy. Had she learned nothing? Her task was not to win Mr. Stapleton’s regard, but to earn back her own self-respect.

She made a small gesture, indicating the empty space beside her on the ottoman. “Will you not sit down? You have been standing a long time.”

Catherine hesitated for a fraction of a second, and then she accepted. She sat, not close, but no longer deliberately far away. It was a small movement, but it felt momentous.

The gentlemen’s return saved them from the need for further conversation. Mr. Lytton and Sir William resumed a political argument, their voices filling the room. Mr. Stapleton did not join them. He made his way directly to the corner where Eleanor and Catherine were sitting.

“I hope I do not intrude,” he said, his eyes moving between them.

“Not at all,” Eleanor managed.

He did not sit, but stood leaning against the mantelpiece, a position that allowed him to address them both. He spoke of a book he had just finished reading, a work of moral philosophy that touched upon the nature of duty and the difficulty of self-knowledge. He spoke not as a lecturer, but as one mind sharing its workings with another. He asked for their opinions. Catherine, to Eleanor’s surprise, offered a shy but sensible observation, which he received with gratifying seriousness.

Eleanor, emboldened, ventured a comment of her own, questioning one of the author’s premises. Mr. Stapleton turned his full attention to her. “That is a shrewd point, Miss Lytton. The author assumes a clarity of motive that I, for one, have seldom found in myself or observed in others. Our reasons for acting are often a tangled web, are they not? And it is the work of a lifetime to begin to unravel them.”

His gaze was direct, and in it, Eleanor saw again that quiet, serious observation she had seen on the lane. He was speaking of the book, and yet he was speaking of her, of them, of the very work she had just begun. He was acknowledging her effort, and inviting her to continue it.

He then took a small, handsomely bound volume from his coat pocket. “I took the liberty of bringing this,” he said, addressing himself more to Eleanor, though not excluding Catherine. “It is a collection of essays by the same author. I thought… it might be of interest. Perhaps you would do me the honour of looking at it, Miss Lytton.”

He held it out to her. Her fingers trembled slightly as she took it. It was not a gift of courtship; it was not a token of affection. It was something more rare and, to her, more precious. It was a vote of confidence. It was a key, offered by a fellow scholar of the human heart.

“Thank you,” she said, her voice low. “I shall be very glad to read it.”

The evening drew to a close soon after. The goodbyes were a flurry of cloaks and carriages. When the Maxwells came to take their leave, Eleanor met Catherine’s eye once more.

“Good night, Miss Lytton,” Catherine said. And this time, there was the ghost of a genuine smile on her lips.

“Good night, Catherine,” Eleanor replied, using her given name without thinking.

The final departure was Mr. Stapleton’s. He shook hands with her father, bowed to her mother, and then turned to her.

“Good night, Miss Lytton. I shall be interested to hear your thoughts on the essays, when you have had time to form them.”

“Good night, Mr. Stapleton.”

She stood in the drawing room long after the last carriage had rolled away, the little book still in her hand. The evening had been an ordeal, but it had not been a failure. The breach with Catherine was not healed, but it was no longer gaping. And the conversation with Mr. Stapleton, that brief, weighted exchange over a book of philosophy, had fortified her more than she could have imagined. She opened the volume. On the flyleaf, in a strong, clear hand, was his name: Robert Stapleton. Beneath it, he had written a single quotation.

“The journey of a thousand miles begins with a single step.”

Eleanor closed the book and held it to her chest. The path ahead was still long, and beset with difficulties she was only just beginning to comprehend. But she had taken another step, and then another. And for the first time, she felt the quiet, steadying presence of a fellow traveller, walking not beside her, perhaps, but on the same arduous and necessary road. The work that truly mattered was indeed the hardest; but she had found, to her great surprise, that she was no longer entirely afraid of it.

The light of the following morning found Eleanor not in the breakfast parlour, but in her own small sitting room, the book from Mr. Stapleton lying open, though unread, upon her lap. The inscription, however, she had perused a dozen times, her finger tracing the firm lines of his script. “The journey of a thousand miles begins with a single step.” It was a simple aphorism, the sort of thing a schoolmaster might write in a copybook; yet from him, it felt less like a platitude and more like a shared secret, a map of the territory she now found herself in. The work, as he had called it, of understanding. The essays awaited, and she felt a tremor of anticipation, a feeling more commonly associated with a new gown or a forthcoming ball, and she chided herself for the comparison. This was a pleasure of the mind; it was of a higher order, surely.

Her solitude was interrupted by a light rap at the door, and her mother entered, her countenance already a study in morning inquiry. “Eleanor, my love, not yet down? Your father is asking for his coffee. You were up so late, I suppose. It was a successful evening, was it not? Mrs. Maxwell seemed in better spirits than usual, though goodness knows it does not take much to put her out of them.”

“It was a very pleasant evening, Mamma.”

“Pleasant, yes.” Mrs. Lytton settled herself into a chair, her eyes scanning the room and landing, as Eleanor knew they would, upon the new volume in her daughter’s lap. “Mr. Stapleton is a most attentive young man. Very well-bred. His manners are not quite so polished as some, perhaps; there is a seriousness about him, a want of that easy nonsense that so often passes for wit, but his principles, I am sure, are sound. Did you notice how he spoke to your father about the new enclosure bill? Your father was most impressed.”

“He speaks very well on subjects that interest him, certainly,” Eleanor replied, her tone more neutral than her feeling.

“And he seems very interested in you, my dear,” her mother returned, with a smile that was half affection and half strategy. “To lend a book! It is a very particular sort of attention. Not flowers, which any young man might send, nor a sheet of music, but a book of essays. It speaks to a respect for your understanding. And his estate, I am told, is entirely his own, and very well managed.”

Eleanor felt a familiar sense of weariness. Her mother, with the best intentions in the world, could reduce any man to the sum of his prospects and any sentiment to a move in the great game of matrimony. It was a language Eleanor struggled to speak with any fluency. “I believe he merely thought I should enjoy the author’s arguments, Mamma. We had been speaking on a similar theme.”

“Of course, my love, of course. And it is a great credit to you that he did. Well, we must not be remiss in our own duties. We must call upon the Maxwells this morning. It is only proper, after enjoying their hospitality. And I should like to have another look at that pianoforte; I wonder if the tone is truly as fine as they say, or if it is merely new.”

To this, Eleanor could make no real objection, though the prospect of another encounter with Catherine so soon was daunting. It was one thing to find a moment of connection in the softened light of an evening party; it was quite another to brave the harsh glare of a morning call, an occasion designed, it seemed, expressly for the measurement of social imperfections.

An hour later, they were shown into the Maxwells’ drawing room, a space that always seemed to suffer from an excess of furniture and a deficit of warmth. Mrs. Maxwell, a thin, anxious woman whose life was a series of small agitations, received them with a fluster of gratitude. “Mrs. Lytton! Miss Lytton! How very good of you to call. I was just saying to Catherine, we must expect a stream of visitors, all curious about the instrument. It is a great trial, you know, to have something new; one is quite put on display.”

Catherine was seated not at the pianoforte, but by the window, a piece of intricate embroidery in her lap. She acknowledged their entrance with a slight inclination of her head, her needle never pausing in its work. The expression on her face was one of resolute neutrality.

“We would not dream of intruding for long,” said Mrs. Lytton, settling her skirts. “We only wished to say again what a delightful evening it was. Your daughter plays with such feeling, Mrs. Maxwell. And Mr. Stapleton, Eleanor was just saying, is a most intelligent young man.”

Eleanor felt the colour rise in her cheeks and saw, or thought she saw, Catherine’s needle jab just a little more forcefully into the taut fabric.

“Mr. Stapleton?” said Mrs. Maxwell, her brow furrowed in concentration, as if trying to place the name. “Oh, the quiet gentleman. Yes. He has not the way of a Mr. Davies, for instance, who is all charm and compliments, but I suppose he is respectable enough. Catherine, you were speaking to him for a time.”

Catherine looked up, her gaze cool and direct. “Mr. Stapleton was kind enough to inquire after my father’s health, Mamma. We did not speak for long.” Her eyes met Eleanor’s for a brief, challenging moment, and in them Eleanor read a clear message: *You may have his attention, but do not imagine it makes you my friend.*

The conversation, or rather Mrs. Lytton and Mrs. Maxwell’s duologue of neighbourhood trifles, meandered on. Eleanor, finding herself quite shut out, and receiving no encouragement from Catherine, allowed her gaze to drift about the room. It was then she noticed, on a small table near Catherine’s elbow, a book of poetry. It was a well-worn copy, its spine broken, and lying beside it was a sheet of paper covered in what looked to be a translation in progress. For all her sharp edges and determined practicality, Catherine, it seemed, had a private world of her own, one not so very different, perhaps, from Eleanor’s. The thought was a strange comfort.

The visit concluded with no further incident, and as they walked home, Eleanor felt a renewed sense of the difficulty of her project. If she was to understand Catherine, she must first breach a fortress of pride and resentment, and she possessed no siege engines, only a nascent and uncertain sympathy.

“A thankless visit,” Mrs. Lytton declared. “Catherine grows more peculiar every year. All that talent, and she hides it under such a disagreeable manner. She will frighten away every eligible man in the county. It is a mercy you have a sunnier disposition, Eleanor.”

Eleanor made no reply. She was thinking of the book of poetry and the needle flashing in the sunlight, two instruments of a very different kind of work.

The day being fine, and her mind too full for the quiet of the house, Eleanor determined upon a walk. She took the path that led towards the woods, relishing the crisp air and the solitude. Her thoughts turned again to the essays, and to the man who had given them to her. What was it about him that was so compelling? It was not handsomeness, though he was not ill-favoured; it was not wit, in the conventional sense. It was, she decided, a quality of attention. When he listened, he seemed to be truly hearing; and when he spoke, his words had the weight of careful thought behind them. He did not, as so many did, speak merely to fill the silence.

She was so lost in this contemplation that she did not at first perceive the figure ahead of her, leaning against a stile and looking out over the fields. It was, of course, Mr. Stapleton. The coincidence was so perfect, so like a scene from a novel, that she almost laughed. He turned at the sound of her approach, and a slow smile lit his features.

“Miss Lytton,” he said, straightening up. “I had hoped the fine weather might draw you out. The world seems a much simpler place out of doors, does it not?”

“Indeed it does, Mr. Stapleton. Indoors, one is surrounded by furniture and expectations. Here, there is only the sky and the path.”

“And which path are you taking to-day?” he asked, his eyes twinkling, as if he knew she would understand the double meaning.

“One that seems to be heading in the same direction as yours,” she replied, emboldened by the fresh air and the freedom from scrutiny.

They fell into step together, and for a time, they walked in a comfortable silence. It was he who spoke first. “I trust your morning was more pleasant than mine. I was obliged to spend an hour with my steward, debating the merits of a new drainage system. It is necessary work, but I confess my mind wandered.”

“My morning was spent in paying a call upon the Maxwells,” Eleanor said.

He gave her a quick, perceptive look. “Ah. Then I believe my hour with the steward was the more agreeable of the two. Miss Maxwell was in a good humour?”

“Miss Maxwell’s humours are not easily read,” Eleanor said carefully. “She was occupied with her embroidery.”

“She is always occupied,” he observed. “I have sometimes thought that she uses her industry as a shield. If one is always busy, one need never be available for conversation. It is a formidable defence.”

His insight was so exact that Eleanor looked at him in surprise. “You have observed her so closely?”

“One cannot help but observe what is interesting, Miss Lytton. And a young woman of her intelligence and education, confined to such a narrow sphere and with such evident vexation of spirit, is an interesting study. Do you not find it so?”

Here it was again: the invitation to think, to analyze, to work at the problem. “I do,” she said, her voice earnest. “I find it a very perplexing study. I wish I understood her better.”

“And have you begun your other studies?” he asked, his gaze dropping for a moment to the empty air where the book had been in her lap that morning. “Did the essays prove as dense as I warned?”

“I have only had time to read the preface,” she admitted. “But the author speaks of ‘the architecture of the self,’ the way we build the person we present to the world. It is a compelling notion.”

“It is,” he agreed. “And a frightening one. For it implies that the structure might be unsound. That we might build our house upon the sand.” They walked on for a few paces. “The author argues that true self-knowledge requires us to examine the foundations, to knock on the walls and listen for the hollow places. Most of us, I think, are afraid of what we might find. It is easier to busy ourselves with decorating the rooms.”

His analogy was so apt, so perfectly descriptive of the morning’s visit to the Maxwells, and indeed of so much of the society she knew, that Eleanor felt a thrill of recognition. “Decorating the rooms,” she repeated, half to herself. “That is exactly it. We discuss the new curtains and ignore the cracks in the plaster.”

“Precisely. And to point out the cracks is considered a gross breach of decorum.” He stopped and turned to her, his expression serious. “Which is why I suspect your work with Miss Maxwell may prove more difficult than my work with the drainage of the north field. You are not merely trying to improve the landscape; you are challenging the architecture of a person’s self-defence.”

The force of his understanding was almost overwhelming. He saw it all: her ambition, her difficulty, the very nature of the task she had set for herself. It was as if he had been reading her own thoughts.

Before she could formulate a reply, the sound of hoofbeats and cheerful whistling broke the quiet of the lane. A moment later, a rider on a handsome grey mare came into view. He was a young man, fashionably dressed, with a sunny, open countenance and an air of immense self-satisfaction. He drew rein upon seeing them, sweeping his hat from his head in a theatrical bow.

“Miss Lytton! What a happy chance! And Stapleton, good day to you.” The young man’s smile was brilliant. “Out enjoying the rustic charms, are we? I have just been to the village to procure some ribbons for my sister. A man’s duty is never done, eh?” He dismounted with an easy grace, looping the reins over his arm.

“Mr. Davies,” Eleanor said, summoning a polite smile. Mr. Davies was the son of a manufacturer who had recently purchased a large estate nearby; he was new to the neighbourhood, and very determined to be liked.

“You are acquainted with Mr. Stapleton?” she asked.

“Oh, Stapleton and I are old neighbours, in a manner of speaking,” said Davies, clapping Mr. Stapleton on the shoulder with a familiarity that was not returned. “Our lands march together. He concerns himself with the soil, and I with the society. A perfect division of labour! But Miss Lytton, you are looking thoughtful. Has Stapleton been boring you with talk of crop rotation and hedgerows?”

“On the contrary, Mr. Davies,” Eleanor said, a little coolly. “We were discussing philosophy.”

“Philosophy!” Mr. Davies laughed, a loud, hearty sound that seemed to startle the birds in the trees. “Good heavens, Stapleton, you will frighten the ladies away. No, no, Miss Lytton, the only philosophy a lady needs is that of a cheerful heart and a well-chosen partner for the dance. Are you to be at the assembly in town next week? My sister and I shall be there. You must save a waltz for me.”

“I have not yet decided if I shall attend,” Eleanor replied.

“Oh, you must! It will be a sad affair without you. The prettiest flower in the county must not hide herself away.” He beamed at his own compliment, then turned his attention back to Mr. Stapleton, who had remained silent and still throughout the exchange. “Well, Stapleton, I must not keep you from your… philosophy. And I must deliver these ribbons before my sister despairs of me entirely. A pleasure, Miss Lytton. Your servant.”

With another flourish of his hat, he remounted and cantered off down the lane, resuming his cheerful whistle as he went. A profound silence descended in his wake.

Eleanor felt a mortifying blush creep up her neck. The contrast between the two men, and the two conversations, was so stark it was painful. One spoke to her mind; the other to her face. One offered a key; the other, a compliment.

It was Mr. Stapleton who finally broke the silence, his voice devoid of its earlier warmth. “Mr. Davies is considered a great prize in the neighbourhood, I believe.”

The statement was flat, an observation without commentary, yet Eleanor felt a sudden, fierce need to refute it. “He has a new fortune and a good tailor,” she said, more sharply than she intended. “Some may call that a prize. I call it a very loud suit of clothes.”

A flicker of something—amusement? surprise?—passed over his face. The stiffness in his posture seemed to relax. “A very loud suit of clothes,” he repeated softly. He looked at her then, and the quiet, serious observation was back in his gaze. “You are not easily taken in by decoration, are you, Miss Lytton?”

“I am trying not to be,” she said. “I am trying to learn to check for cracks in the plaster.”

A genuine smile, broad and transformative, spread across his face. It made him look younger, less burdened. “Well,” he said, his good humour quite restored, “that is a work I should be very glad to help you with.” They resumed their walk, the interruption forgotten, the shared understanding deepened, the path ahead clearer than ever.

The silence which followed his departure was of a different quality entirely; no longer the comfortable quiet of shared thought, but the slightly strained quiet of a moment concluded, which, however satisfying, must inevitably give way to the more prosaic business of parting. They had reached the small gate that led from the lane into the gardens of Lytton House, Eleanor’s home. The house itself, a handsome, sober structure of grey stone, was visible through the trees, its windows catching the afternoon sun.

“I must not keep you longer, Miss Lytton,” Mr. Stapleton said, his hand resting on the latch of the gate, though he made no move to open it. “Your father will be wondering where his walking companion has strayed.”

“My father is accustomed to my straying,” Eleanor replied, a small smile playing on her lips. “He knows that a good book or a new prospect can detain me for an hour. A new philosophy, I imagine, might be considered grounds for a whole afternoon’s absence.”

His answering smile was immediate and full of warmth. “Then I am glad to have provided you with a sufficient excuse. I hope the philosophy was not too… dry.”

“Not at all. It was a great deal more refreshing than talk of ribbons, I assure you.”

The unspoken name of Mr. Davies hung in the air between them for a moment. Mr. Stapleton’s expression became serious once more, his gaze steady and searching. “Miss Lytton,” he began, then paused, as if weighing his words with great care. “The world is full of loud suits of clothes. It is a rare and valuable thing to find someone who prefers to inspect the quality of the cloth.”

Eleanor felt a warmth spread through her that had nothing to do with the sun. She could not think of a reply that would not seem either too forward or too coy, and so she simply met his gaze and hoped her own expression conveyed the pleasure his words gave her. It seemed it did, for the gravity in his face lessened, and he finally lifted the latch.

“Your servant, Miss Lytton.” He gave a slight, formal bow, which seemed to her more full of genuine respect than all of Mr. Davies’s flourishing.

“Mr. Stapleton.” She inclined her head and passed through the gate. She did not turn to watch him go, but walked with a steady, measured pace up the gravel path towards the house, though every sense was alert to the sound of his retreating footsteps on the lane behind her. Only when she heard them no more did she allow her composure to soften, a sigh of some unnameable emotion escaping her. She had a great deal to think on; a very great deal indeed.

She entered the house to find her mother in the drawing room, engaged in a furious battle with a length of embroidery which did not seem to be submitting to her will. Mrs. Lytton was a woman for whom the world was divided into two distinct categories: things which were advantageous, and things which were not. Her judgments were swift, her opinions firm, and her chief concern in life was seeing her only daughter well settled; a concern which manifested in a constant and wearing anxiety over Eleanor’s prospects.

“Eleanor! There you are. I was about to send James out to look for you. You have been gone an age. Your hair is a fright, my dear, and there is a smudge of dirt upon your cheek. Where have you been?”

“Only for a walk, Mama. As far as the old stone bridge.”

“The stone bridge! What is there to see at the stone bridge but crumbling mortar and nettles? You met no one, I suppose?” The question was put with an air of casual indifference that betrayed a burning curiosity.

Eleanor, who was making her way to the mirror to inspect the alleged smudge, paused. “We met Mr. Davies, in fact. He was on his way back from the village.”

Mrs. Lytton’s needle stopped its frantic dance. Her whole countenance brightened. “Mr. Davies! Did you? Was he on his new bay hunter? A very fine animal, your father says. And what a fine fortune! They say he has ten thousand a year, quite unencumbered. Did he speak to you, Eleanor? What did he say?”

“He said he was delivering ribbons to his sister, and he enquired if I should be at the assembly next week.”

“Excellent!” Mrs. Lytton declared, all vexation with her needlework forgotten. “Of course you must go. It is the perfect opportunity. He is clearly taken with you, Eleanor. To stop and speak in that way; it shews a particular attention.”

Eleanor, having removed the smudge with her handkerchief, turned back to the room. “He also told me that the only philosophy a lady requires is a cheerful heart and a well-chosen partner for the dance. I confess, Mama, I do not find such particulars to be a great recommendation.”

“Nonsense,” Mrs. Lytton said briskly, though a little of her animation had faded. “That is merely the way gentlemen talk. He means to pay you a compliment, to say you are too pretty to trouble your head with serious matters. You take things too literally, child. He is a most eligible young man, and you would do well to encourage him.”

“And what of his philosophy, Mama? Should I not examine that a little, before I decide if he is a well-chosen partner?”

“Oh, philosophy!” Mrs. Lytton waved a dismissive hand. “What has that to do with a good settlement and a comfortable home? You have been spending too much time in your father’s library. It gives you these odd notions.” She sighed. “You were not alone when you met him, I think. You said ‘we’.”

Eleanor had hoped to avoid this. “I was walking with Mr. Stapleton.”

The effect of this name was precisely the opposite of Mr. Davies’s. Mrs. Lytton’s face clouded over. “Oh. Mr. Stapleton,” she said, her voice flat. “I might have known. He is always wandering about the lanes as if he has nothing better to do. For a man with such a large estate, he seems remarkably idle.”

“He is not idle, Mama. He concerns himself a great deal with his land, and with his tenants. We were discussing… improvements.” This was not, strictly speaking, a falsehood.

“Improvements,” Mrs. Lytton sniffed. “I wish he would begin by improving his own coat. It is quite threadbare at the elbows. And he is so… quiet. So solemn. I am sure a conversation with him must be very dull work indeed. I cannot imagine what you find to talk about.”

“We find a great deal, Mama.”

Mrs. Lytton gave her daughter a sharp, assessing look. “Eleanor, you must not be foolish. Mr. Stapleton is a gentleman, I allow. The Stapletons are an old family, very respectable. But he has no fortune to speak of, not compared to Mr. Davies. His father left the estate much encumbered, as I have heard it told. He is a serious, melancholy sort of man, not at all the husband for a girl of your spirits. You must put him out of your head.”

“He is not in my head, Mama,” Eleanor said, her cheeks growing warm, “except as a neighbour and a pleasant acquaintance.”

Before Mrs. Lytton could press the point further, the drawing room door opened and the butler announced, “Mr. Davies and Miss Davies.”

Eleanor’s heart sank. She felt a desperate, childish urge to flee, to run up to her room and feign a headache. But it was too late. There they were, sweeping into the room, Mr. Davies all smiles and polished boots, and his sister, a younger, sharper-featured version of him, her eyes darting about, taking in every detail of the room and its occupants.

“My dear Mrs. Lytton! So good of you to be at home,” Mr. Davies boomed, taking her hand. “My sister was determined to pay her respects, and I could not deny her the pleasure of making your acquaintance, and of renewing my own with Miss Lytton.” He turned his bright, confident gaze on Eleanor. “Miss Lytton! We meet again. Providence is smiling upon me to-day.”

“Mr. Davies, Miss Davies,” Mrs. Lytton said, positively beaming with delight. “How very kind of you to call. You find us in a state of quiet domesticity. Pray, sit down.”

The next half-hour was an exercise in torment for Eleanor. Miss Davies, having seated herself on the very edge of her chair as if poised for flight, launched into a breathless account of the latest gossip from the town, her brother punctuating the narrative with loud laughter and observations which he clearly considered to be the height of wit. They spoke of the upcoming assembly, of who was to be there, of what dresses were being made, of a rumoured engagement, of a suspected flirtation. It was a torrent of triviality, a flood of insignificant detail, and Eleanor felt her spirits sink lower with every passing moment.

“And you will be there, Miss Lytton?” Miss Davies asked, her sharp eyes fixing on Eleanor. “Charles has talked of nothing else. He is quite determined to claim you for the first waltz.”

“My brother is too bold,” Mr. Davies said, though he looked immensely pleased with himself. “But I cannot deny it. I have set my heart upon it. You will not disappoint me, I hope?”

“I have not yet decided if I shall attend,” Eleanor repeated, feeling her mother’s disapproving gaze upon her.

“Oh, but you must!” cried Miss Davies. “It will be the event of the season. Everyone will be there. Even, I hear,” she added, lowering her voice to a conspiratorial whisper, “Mr. Stapleton.”

Eleanor could not prevent the slight start she gave.

“Indeed?” said Mr. Davies, raising an eyebrow. “Stapleton at a dance? The man is more suited to a funeral. He will stand in a corner and scowl at the music, I expect.”

“Charles, do not be horrid,” his sister chided, though she giggled. “They say he is very learned. He was at Oxford, you know. But he never dances. It is a great shame, for he is not at all ill-looking, in a severe sort of way. Though his coat, as I was saying to Mrs. Pringle only yesterday, is a positive disgrace.”

Eleanor felt a hot surge of anger. To hear him discussed so, by these people, as if he were a piece of furniture to be appraised and found wanting, was intolerable. She opened her mouth to make some sharp retort, to defend him, but her mother spoke first.

“Gentlemen who are concerned with philosophy and books often neglect such worldly matters as fashion, I find,” Mrs. Lytton said, with a pointed look at Eleanor. “Their minds are on higher things.” There was a distinct edge of sarcasm in her tone, which the Davieses were too self-absorbed to notice, but which Eleanor felt like a slap.

“Higher things indeed!” Mr. Davies laughed. “The height of his own hedgerows, perhaps. Well, he may have his books; I shall have the waltz. And I trust, Miss Lytton, that when the music begins, you will not find me wanting.” He gave her a look of such confident, possessive admiration that Eleanor had to turn away, her mind in a turmoil of vexation and dismay.

When they were finally gone, leaving behind a faint scent of pomade and a profound sense of relief, Mrs. Lytton turned to her daughter. “Well! Was that not a delightful visit? Such lively, charming people. And it is quite clear, Eleanor, what Mr. Davies’s intentions are. Quite clear. Any girl in the county would be thrilled with such attentions.”

“I am not any girl in the county, Mama,” Eleanor said, her voice trembling slightly. “And I was not thrilled. I was mortified.”

“Mortified! What can you mean? He paid you every possible compliment.”

“He treated me like a fool. He spoke to me as if I were a child who could be placated with a sweetmeat. His sister is a gossip, and he is a… a loud suit of clothes.”

“Eleanor!” Mrs. Lytton’s voice was sharp with disbelief and disappointment. “You will throw away the best opportunity you are ever likely to have with these nonsensical, romantic notions you have picked up from your father’s books. Ten thousand a year! You will not find another man of that fortune who will look at you twice, if you persist in this… this criticism of everything!”

She swept from the room, leaving Eleanor standing alone in the centre of the floor. The sun had moved, and the room was now in shadow. The visit, so far from promoting Mr. Davies’s cause, had served only to cement her opinion of him, and in doing so, to clarify her feelings in another direction entirely. The contrast between Mr. Davies’s booming compliments and Mr. Stapleton’s quiet observation, between an inspection of her face and an inspection of her mind, had never been more stark. She would go to the assembly. She would go, not to please Mr. Davies, but to see Mr. Stapleton, to see him in that setting, to confirm for herself that the quiet man who spoke of philosophy was as real in a ballroom as he was in a country lane. The thought gave her a small, secret thrill of determination. She would go, and she would see.

The evening of the assembly arrived sooner than Eleanor had supposed possible, a fact for which she was not entirely grateful. The intervening days had been filled with a quiet, simmering tension between herself and her mother. Mrs. Lytton spoke of little else but the assembly, and, more particularly, of Mr. Davies; of the absolute necessity of securing his interest, of the folly of indulging in fanciful notions, and of the elegance of a new set of pearl ornaments she had procured for the occasion. Her lectures were delivered not with anger, but with a weighty sense of maternal duty that Eleanor found infinitely more oppressive. To argue was to be childish; to be silent was to acquiesce. Eleanor, therefore, took refuge in a kind of polite impenetrability, agreeing to the pearls, commenting on the weather, and all the while feeling as if she were preparing for an execution rather than a party.

The assembly rooms at Fordbridge were already crowded and exceedingly warm. The mingled scents of perfume, hot wax, and damp wool assailed them upon entry, and the sound of the tuning orchestra was nearly drowned by the cheerful din of fifty conversations at once. Eleanor, feeling her resolutions waver in the face of such overwhelming sociability, instinctively searched the crowd, but her eye met only a sea of unfamiliar or indifferent faces. Before she could look for long, a large, confident figure was before them.

“Mrs. Lytton, Miss Lytton! I had begun to despair.” Mr. Davies’s voice boomed, cutting through the general noise. He was dressed in a coat of the most fashionable blue, with a waistcoat so elaborately embroidered it might have served as a sampler. “I trust your journey was not disagreeable? I have, of course, saved the first two dances for you, Miss Lytton. I would not have forgiven myself otherwise.”

He did not ask if she wished to dance; he informed her that she would. Eleanor, with her mother’s sharp gaze upon her, could do nothing but give a slight inclination of her head and a murmur of assent.

“Excellent!” he declared. “My sister is just over there, renewing her acquaintance with half the county. She will be delighted to see you. But the music is beginning; we must not delay.”

He led her to the floor. The dance was a lively one, requiring a great deal of movement and leaving little opportunity for conversation, for which Eleanor was thankful. Mr. Davies, however, was not a man to be silenced by a contredanse. He filled every available pause with commentary on his own agility, compliments on her appearance, and conjectures as to which of the other dancers were likely to make a misstep. His conversation was a monologue of self-approbation, and Eleanor found that by offering an occasional smile, she could retreat entirely into her own thoughts, using him as a mere shield against the greater crowd. All the while, her eyes scanned the room, searching for a quiet figure in a plain coat, a face more given to thought than to vacuous smiles. But she could not see him. A cold knot of disappointment began to form in her chest. Had she been a fool? To imagine he would even attend such an event; to build such hopes upon a chance encounter in a lane.

The first dance concluded, and without a moment’s pause, he claimed her for the second. “A waltz!” he announced with satisfaction. “Now this is a true test of a partner. You will find I do not disappoint, Miss Lytton.” His arm encircled her waist with a familiarity that made her stiffen, and they were off. The waltz, being a more intimate affair, did not permit the same mental escape. He held her more closely, and spoke more directly to her.

“You are very quiet this evening,” he observed, steering her narrowly past another couple. “Saving your wit for a more deserving subject, perhaps?”

“The music is very fine, sir. I am merely enjoying it.”

“Fine, yes, but not as fine as the company. I hope you will not be so cruel as to deny me a third dance later. Perhaps after supper? We might take a turn about the room. I have a great deal I wish to say to you.”

The possessiveness in his tone, the implication that her evening was his to command, was more than Eleanor could patiently bear. “You are very good, sir, but I believe I am engaged for the first dance after supper.” It was a lie, but a necessary one.

Mr. Davies’s smile tightened almost imperceptibly. “Engaged? Indeed? And might I be so bold as to ask with whom?”

Before she could invent a partner, the music came to a merciful end. She curtsied, murmuring a polite falsehood about the pleasure of the dance, and made her escape on the pretext of finding her mother. She did not look back to see his expression. Her one desire was to lose herself in the crowd, to find a quiet corner where she might recover her composure. The room seemed hotter than ever, and her head began to ache. And still, there was no sign of Mr. Stapleton.

She found her mother deep in conversation with a triumphant Miss Davies, who immediately turned to Eleanor. “My dear Miss Lytton! Was not that a picture? My brother quite worships the waltz, you know. He says it is the only dance that truly allows a gentleman to shew his admiration for his partner. He was quite desolated that you could not promise him the dance after supper.”

“Miss Lytton is a very popular young lady,” Mrs. Lytton said, her voice betraying a hint of warning. “She must be allowed to oblige her other friends.”

Eleanor felt a blush of shame and irritation creep up her neck. She had been caught in her lie, and her mother’s defence felt more like a reprimand. It was at that moment, as she stood trapped between the two of them, feeling foolish and cornered, that she saw him.

Mr. Stapleton was standing near the doorway, as if he had only just arrived. He was not looking at the dancers, but at the assembly itself, with the air of a naturalist observing a strange and chaotic species. His coat was dark and perfectly plain; his cravat was simply tied. He looked, Eleanor thought with a sudden lift of her heart, exactly like himself. He was not handsome in the way Mr. Davies was handsome; his face was thinner, his features more irregular, but his expression held a quiet intelligence that she found infinitely more compelling. He seemed entirely out of place, and yet entirely self-possessed.

Her heart gave a ridiculous leap. As if he had felt her gaze, his eyes moved from the general crowd and settled, for a moment, upon her. He gave a small, almost imperceptible nod of recognition, a gesture so understated it might have been imagined. It was enough. The heat, the noise, the vexation of Mr. Davies, all of it seemed to recede.

“Eleanor, are you attending?” her mother’s voice cut through her reverie. “Miss Davies was just remarking on the lace of your gown.”

“It is exquisite,” Eleanor said, her attention still across the room. “You must excuse me, I find I am in need of some air.”

She slipped away before either of them could stop her, her objective clear. She did not move towards the retiring-room, but skirted the edge of the dance floor, using the columns that supported the gallery as cover, making her way circuitously towards the entrance. She felt a thrill of rebellion, a sense that she was taking her evening into her own hands at last.

She found him examining a rather poor landscape painting that hung near the door, a depiction of the local countryside that owed more to imagination than to geography.

“A faithful representation of our hills, do you not think, Mr. Stapleton?” she said, her voice steadier than she had expected.

He turned, and a slow smile lit his face. It was a smile of genuine pleasure, and it transformed his rather serious features entirely. “Miss Lytton. I had wondered if you were here. As for the painting, I believe the artist must have been conjecturing. I have never known the river to run uphill, even in the most violent of floods.”

“Perhaps he is a philosopher, like your friend in London, and sees things from a higher plane.”

He laughed, a quiet, pleasant sound. “I suspect he is merely a poor painter. But I am surprised to see you away from the dancing. I saw you just now, engaged in what appeared to be a very energetic waltz.”

“I have done my duty for the present,” she said, a little boldness entering her tone. “I did not take you for a man for assemblies, Mr. Stapleton.”

“And you were right not to. I confess I am here on a matter of business; my sister’s husband, who is a stranger to Fordbridge, required an introduction to a certain solicitor who he was told would be in attendance. Having performed that office, I was on the point of making my escape.”

“And have I delayed your escape, sir?”

“You have made it a matter of some debate,” he said, his eyes meeting hers with a directness that made her heart beat faster. “A man may value his solitude, Miss Lytton, but he is a fool if he prefers it to good company.”

The compliment was so simply, so sincerely delivered, so unlike the florid pronouncements of Mr. Davies, that she felt herself colour with pleasure. “And what are your observations of our little world here?” she asked, gesturing to the swirling crowd. “Does it provide much for a philosopher to study?”

“A great deal,” he replied, leaning against the wall beside her. “It is a perfect microcosm of society. See how they arrange themselves; by fortune, by fashion, by family. See how they vie for position, for notice, for the best partners. It is a dance far more complicated than any the musicians are playing.”

“And we are all merely dancers in it?”

“Some of us, perhaps. Others seem to be attempting to choreograph the entire affair.” His gaze flickered for a second towards Mr. Davies, who was now holding court near the supper-room, and Eleanor knew he had understood her situation perfectly.

“I am afraid I do not have a head for choreography,” she said, her voice low. “I am too apt to miss a step.”

“It is often the prescribed steps that are the most unnatural,” he said. “A simple walk in a straight line is sometimes the most graceful movement of all.”

They were silent for a moment, the noise of the party a distant murmur. To be understood so completely, without having to explain herself; it was a novel and a deeply comforting sensation. A set was forming for a new country dance, and a thought, bold and reckless, came to her.

“Are you so determined on your escape, Mr. Stapleton,” she asked, looking at the floor, “that you could not be persuaded to walk in a straight line with me for one dance?”

There was a pause. She dared not look at him. Had she been too forward? Had she mistaken his quiet sympathy for something more?

“Miss Lytton,” he said, and his voice held a note of surprised pleasure. “Nothing would give me greater felicity.”

The dance was a simple one, one she had known since childhood. But dancing it with him was an entirely new experience. He was not a showy dancer like Mr. Davies; he was steady, correct, and attentive. And he did not feel the need to fill every moment with chatter. When the figures of the dance brought them together, he spoke to her, not of the other dancers or of himself, but of a book he had just finished, asking her opinion on a point of character, as if her opinion were a thing of value. When the dance separated them, she found his eyes following her, and when they met again, he would resume their conversation as if they had not been parted. It was a conversation held in fragments, a quiet thread of sense and sensibility woven through the public tapestry of the dance.

For fifteen minutes, she was perfectly happy.

But as the final chord sounded, and he was escorting her from the floor, they were met by a formidable obstacle: Mr. Davies, his face a mask of jovial displeasure.

“Ah, Stapleton! I should have known,” he said, his voice loud enough for several neighbours to hear. “Stealing away the prettiest girl in the room for a dull country dance. I was just telling Miss Davies that you would likely be found in a corner discussing crop rotation or some such dreary business. I see you have brought your business to the dance floor.”

Eleanor froze, mortified by his rudeness, both to her and to Mr. Stapleton. She felt a protective anger rise within her.

Mr. Stapleton, however, remained perfectly composed. He looked at Mr. Davies with an expression of mild inquiry. “On the contrary, Davies,” he said, his voice calm and clear. “Miss Lytton and I were engaged in a matter of far greater complexity. We were conjecturing upon the precise relationship between social harmony and a well-executed hey. A subject of which you, I am sure, have a profound and practical understanding.”

The reply was so unexpected, so perfectly aimed, that it left Mr. Davies momentarily speechless. He had been prepared for a defense or a retreat, not a piece of intellectual wit that gently mocked his entire sphere of existence. Several people nearby, who had overheard the exchange, failed to conceal their smiles. Mr. Davies, sensing he had lost the advantage, flushed a dull red.

“Well,” he stammered, “I suppose there is no accounting for taste. I prefer to dance, not to lecture. Miss Lytton, your mother is looking for you. She wishes to go into supper.” He turned on his heel and strode off, the very picture of affronted dignity.

Eleanor turned to Mr. Stapleton, her eyes shining with gratitude and admiration. “Thank you,” she whispered.

“He is not a man who enjoys conjecture,” Mr. Stapleton observed with a small smile. “He prefers a world in which everything is already known.” He offered her his arm. “Shall I return you to your mother? I believe my business here is now quite concluded.”

The walk back across the room was a silent one, but it was a silence full of meaning. When he left her with her mother, he bowed, his eyes meeting hers for a brief, significant moment. “Good evening, Miss Lytton. I shall trust that the rest of your dancing is not interrupted by lectures.”

Mrs. Lytton had seen it all. Her face was a study in controlled fury. She said nothing as they joined the crush for the supper-room, nothing as she procured a plate of cold chicken and a glass of negus for her daughter, but the silence was more damning than any tirade. Mr. Davies, having recovered his poise, joined them, and pointedly ignored the events of the last half-hour, launching into a tedious story about a horse he had recently purchased. Eleanor listened, or pretended to listen, her thoughts elsewhere entirely. The assembly, which she had dreaded, had become the scene of a quiet, momentous victory. The contrast was complete; the choice was made.

The journey home was as silent as she had predicted. The carriage rattled over the dark country roads, the rhythmic clopping of the horses’ hooves the only sound. Mrs. Lytton sat bolt upright, a rigid silhouette in the moonlight. Just as they were approaching their own gates, she finally spoke, her voice cold and sharp as ice.

“I hope you are satisfied, Eleanor. You have made a public spectacle of yourself and of me. You have insulted a man of ten thousand a year, and for what? For a brief flirtation with a man who has nothing. Nothing but his books and his insolence.”

“He was not insolent, Mama. He was a gentleman. Mr. Davies was the one who was rude.”

“Mr. Davies has the right to be whatever he pleases! You have thrown away such an opportunity, an opportunity I have worked so hard to secure for you. It is a willful, selfish, and foolish act. You will live to regret this night, I assure you.”

The carriage stopped. They had arrived. Eleanor stepped out into the cool night air, her head held high. She did not feel regret. She felt a strange, new, and exhilarating sense of freedom. She had not thrown anything away; she had simply refused to pick it up. As she walked into the house, leaving her mother to pay the driver, she knew her life had just become a great deal more difficult. And yet, for the first time in a very long time, she felt that it was truly her own.

He called her Emma, a liberty he had taken up with a charming assurance that made it difficult to resent. She felt her opinion was of consequence to him, and it was a pleasing sensation. “I saw a man who was perhaps a little vexed at being kept waiting on his horse,” she said, with an air of judicial gravity, “and who wished to conclude his business with Miss Bates as swiftly as possible. His enquiries were loud because he was speaking from the street to an upper window.”

She was rather pleased with this rational explanation, but Mr. Frank Churchill would not have it. “No, no, that is too simple, too logical. You are robbing the scene of all its poetry. Mrs. Weston shook her head. “You build a great deal upon very little, Frank. I believe the simple truth is that Mr. Knightley was in a hurry, and that he is a kind man who thinks of his neighbours’ welfare. Is not that your opinion, Miss Woodhouse?”

Emma found herself in a slight difficulty. Her own private theory, the one she had so long cherished, was that Mr. Knightley must marry somebody, and that as he had shewn no inclination for anyone else, he must surely, one day, marry her. His present attentions to Jane Fairfax were therefore a matter of polite fiction, a kindness that could mean nothing. “I believe,” she said at last, “that Mr. Knightley’s motives are very often exactly what they appear to be. He is kind, as you say, Mrs. Weston. But he is also a man who enjoys having his own way. He wished for a direct answer from Miss Bates, and he took the most direct means of getting one. I do not think we need to look for any deeper romance in it.”

She delivered this verdict with a smile, but inwardly, she was turning over Frank Churchill’s words. A rival? He had cast himself as Mr. Knightley’s rival for Jane Fairfax’s affections. It was a jest, of course, but a curious one. He seemed to take such an inexhaustible pleasure in discussing Jane, in praising her, in conjecturing about her. It was almost as if he were trying to convince them all, and perhaps himself, of an attachment that did not exist. It was an odd sort of game, and Emma was not entirely sure she understood the rules.

They had by now reached the gates of Hartfield. Mrs. Weston and her companion were to continue on their way, and the farewells were made.

“We shall see you at the Coles’ party, if not before,” said Mrs. Weston, pressing Emma’s hand. “Do not let Frank persuade you of any more nonsense.”

“I shall do my very best,” Frank Churchill replied for her. “I have a whole volume of nonsense prepared for the occasion. Miss Woodhouse, Miss Smith, I wish you a very good morning. Pray do not let the memory of our little drama prevent you from your own conjectures. It is the finest sport in a quiet neighbourhood.”

“Mr. Churchill enjoys making sport of everything, Harriet. You must not take him too seriously. He has a great deal of imagination, and not very much to occupy it here in Highbury.”

“But Mr. Knightley did seem so very… loud,” Harriet persisted. “And he asked after Miss Fairfax so many times. Do you think… do you think he can really be so very attached to her?”

There was a little tremor of anxiety in Harriet’s voice. Emma understood it at once. Her foolish, delightful project of attaching Mr. Elton to Harriet had ended in disaster; but the faint, more recent, and far more speculative hope concerning Mr. Knightley had been a quiet comfort. She had planted the seed herself, with a careless word here and there about his kindness, his admiration of Harriet’s sweet temper. “Attached?” Emma said, with a light laugh. “My dear Harriet, you mistake the matter entirely. Mr. Knightley has known Jane Fairfax since she was in leading strings. He looks upon her as a niece, or a ward. His concern is that of a guardian, nothing more. Indeed, his manner proved it. Would a man truly in love shout his affections for all of Highbury to hear? No, he would be quiet, reserved, fearful of betraying himself. Frank Churchill has it all backwards, as usual.”

“Oh,” said Harriet, visibly relieved. “Yes, I suppose that must be it. It was more the manner of a schoolmaster checking on a pupil.”

“Precisely! An excellent comparison, Harriet. A schoolmaster, exactly. He wishes her well, he is concerned she might have caught a cold, just as he would be for you, or for me, or for any of his friends. There is nothing in it of a tenderer feeling, I assure you.”

She spoke with such confidence that Harriet was entirely convinced. Her face cleared, and her natural good humour returned. “How clever you are, Miss Woodhouse, to see the truth of things so clearly. I should have been puzzling over it all day.”

“It is only a matter of observation,” Emma replied, feeling very wise and very much in control. “One must learn to distinguish between genuine attachment and mere neighbourly duty. Mr. Knightley is all duty and principle.”

“My dear, you are returned,” he said, his voice full of gentle reproach. “I was beginning to be very anxious. Perry was here, and he said the wind has a very treacherous quality to-day. I hope you were well wrapped up. And poor Miss Smith—she is so delicate.”

His attentions to Miss Fairfax are a mere blind; his true object is to ascertain the feelings of another, to provoke a little jealousy, perhaps, and thereby to receive a little encouragement. He uses her as a subject, a topic of conversation, that he might engage in a discourse with somebody else. It is very cleverly done. He is a young man of infinite resource.”

The implication of who that ‘somebody else’ might be was too strong to be missed, even by Harriet. She looked at Emma with sincere admiration. “Oh, Miss Woodhouse! How you understand every thing! And he was talking to you a great deal. I did notice that.”

“He was,” said Emma, with a smile of modest triumph. “We have an understanding. And I am very glad of it, for it makes his behaviour, which might otherwise seem unkind to Miss Fairfax, perfectly excusable. When one has the key, it all becomes a most amusing game.” She felt a renewed satisfaction in her key. It was so elegant, so comprehensive; it unlocked every difficulty and explained every contradiction. The world of Highbury was a complex piece of machinery, and she alone knew how to operate it.

Their conversation was interrupted by the arrival of tea, and the subject was dropped, but Emma’s mind continued to dwell upon it with unabated pleasure. The next day, she promised herself, she would devote to a deeper consideration of the matter, and to preparing Harriet for the part she must play at the Coles’ upcoming party.

The following morning was as fine as the one before, and Emma was just settling at her drawing-table, contemplating a new portrait of Harriet that should entirely eclipse her former, less successful attempts, when a visitor was announced. It was Mr. Frank Churchill himself, who, finding himself with a morning of leisure, had ridden over from Randalls to pay his respects at Hartfield.

His reception was, from Mr. Woodhouse, one of polite misgiving—“So early, Mr. Churchill? I hope you are not chilled from your ride. The wind has a little touch of east in it, I fancy”—but from Emma, it was one of unfeigned pleasure. Here was a delightful opportunity to put her theories to a further test.

Frank Churchill, with that happy address that was his peculiar charm, soon pacified Mr. Woodhouse by expressing a profound horror of draughts and a decided preference for a seat at the greatest possible distance from the door. Having thus established himself as a man of sense and prudence, he turned his attention to the ladies.

“I find you recovered, I hope, Miss Woodhouse, from the fatigues of yesterday’s adventures? It was a morning of great incident. I have thought of little else since.”

“We are perfectly recovered, I thank you,” replied Emma, with a playful look. “Though I own, it was more eventful than one might expect from a visit to Miss Bates.”

“Ah, Miss Bates!” he exclaimed. “An excellent woman, but one lives in a perpetual state of surprise in her company. And her niece! She is the true source of the drama. I have been wondering, did Mr. Knightley succeed in his quest? Did he return from Kingston with a full and satisfactory report on Miss Fairfax’s health, to be proclaimed to the neighbourhood at large?”

Emma laughed. “We have not heard. He is, I imagine, still collecting his intelligence. You seem very much struck by Mr. Knightley’s concern.”

“I am struck by its openness!” said he, leaning forward slightly. “One cannot but admire a man of such decided, open attachments. He wishes to know how Miss Fairfax is, and the whole of Highbury must know it too! It is a candour I can only envy. My own feelings, alas, are of a more timid and retiring nature. I should not dare to have my inquiries shouted across a street.”

He said this with a glance towards Emma that was so full of meaning, so clearly inviting her to share in his satirical view of Mr. Knightley’s conduct, that she felt her convictions hardening into certainty. He was jealous. Not of Jane Fairfax, but of Mr. Knightley’s supposed place in her, Emma’s, estimation. He wished to set his own, more subtle and refined, mode of attachment in opposition to Mr. Knightley’s blunt and public solicitudes.

“Every one has his own style,” she said, taking up a crayon. “Mr. Knightley’s is the style of a long-standing and privileged friend. He has known us all our lives, and does not scruple to speak his mind.”

“A very happy privilege,” said Frank Churchill, his eyes still on her. “And does it extend, may I ask, to all the young ladies of his acquaintance? Is Miss Smith, for instance, subject to such public inquiries when she has a cold?”

Harriet, thus unexpectedly addressed, blushed and looked down at her work, murmuring something about Mr. Knightley’s always being very kind.

Emma was delighted. The question was perfect. It neatly separated his view of Mr. Knightley’s general benevolence (towards the likes of Harriet) from the more particular, and therefore suspicious, interest in Jane Fairfax, which he was using as a cover for his rivalry.

“Mr. Knightley is kind to every body,” said Emma. “But his interest in Jane Fairfax, I suspect, arises from a particular compassion for her circumstances. He pities her.”

“Pity!” he repeated, taking up the word with an eagerness that she found most telling. “Aye, that is it. Pity. It is a powerful emotion. And it accounts for a great deal. To be so accomplished, so elegant, and yet so dependent. One must pity her. And to be the object of so much conjecture! The pianoforte, the mysterious benefactor… it is a trying situation. I pity her myself, from the bottom of my heart.” He sighed, a theatrical sigh that Emma understood was meant entirely for her. He was pitying Jane Fairfax to shew how very far his feelings were from any warmer sentiment.

“Indeed,” said Emma. “Her situation is unfortunate. We must all feel for her.” She now felt a degree of compassion for Jane herself, not only for her circumstances, but for her role as the unwitting pawn in this complex courtship.

“And tell me,” he continued, lowering his voice again, as if sharing a great secret, “does she continue to play _Robin Adair_? Or has Mr. Knightley’s very public approbation frightened her away from it? I confess, the repetition of one particular tune can sometimes signify a great deal. It can be a sort of signal, can it not?”

He was looking directly at her, and Emma felt a blush rise to her own cheeks. A signal! He was speaking of signals! What could he mean, but to invite her to look for the signals in his own behaviour? It was an intimacy so refined, so far beyond the plain-speaking of a Mr. Knightley, that it charmed her completely.

“One can interpret it so,” she allowed, her heart beating a little faster. “If one is disposed to look for such meanings.”

“I am always disposed to look for such meanings,” he answered, his voice soft but emphatic. “It is the only thing that makes society interesting. To read the hidden story beneath the plain text. I am persuaded that you, Miss Woodhouse, are a very expert reader.”

Never had a compliment been more welcome, for it was a compliment not merely to her beauty or her agreeableness, but to her intellect, to that very penetration on which she most prided herself. She felt that they were two of a kind, two minds moving in a sphere of sophisticated perception high above the common world of Highbury.

The time passed with astonishing rapidity. He talked of the upcoming party at the Coles’, expressing a fervent hope that he might be permitted to claim her for the first two dances, a request made with an air of gallantry that seemed to set them apart from all the other potential dancers in the room. He talked of music, and asked her what she had been playing, and begged to see it, turning the pages with an attention that seemed to value the notes only because her fingers had touched them. Harriet was not ignored; he paid her the correct degree of polite notice, inquiring after her drawings and admiring her needlework, but it was all done with a civility so general and disengaged that it only served to highlight the focused, particular nature of his attention to Emma.

When at last he rose to take his leave, Emma felt that her theory was no longer a theory at all, but a confirmed and established fact. He was in love with her, and was pursuing a plan of courtship as ingenious as it was flattering.

As soon as he was gone, she turned to Harriet. “Well, Harriet! What do you say now?”

“Oh, Miss Woodhouse, he is so very charming! The most agreeable young man I have ever seen.”

“Is he not? But charming and agreeable is only the surface. It is his depth, his discernment, that is so remarkable. You see now, do you not, how every thing he said about Jane Fairfax was designed to convey a meaning to me?”

“Yes,” said Harriet, though her understanding was far from complete. “He did look at you a great deal. And he seems to think Mr. Knightley very… very loud.”

“Exactly!” cried Emma, satisfied with this summary. “He perceives the absurdity of it. He sees that Mr. Knightley’s manner is not that of a lover. And he takes care to shew me that his own is entirely different. We may be perfectly easy about Mr. Knightley, my dear Harriet. I think we need never give his matrimonial schemes a second thought.”

Harriet sighed, a little wistfully. “No, I suppose not. It is a great relief.”

Emma, in the full glow of her success, was inclined to be generous. She surveyed Harriet’s sweet, bewildered face, and felt a rush of matchmaking ambition, a desire to secure for her little friend a happiness as certain as her own. Mr. Elton was a disaster, Mr. Knightley an impossibility. Who was there for Harriet? For a moment, her mind scanned the eligible gentlemen of the district. The eldest son of Mr. George Otway of Maple Grove? He was said to be a sensible young man, and his father’s estate was respectable. But it was too soon. Harriet’s heart, she knew, was a tender and impressionable thing. First, it must be thoroughly cleansed of any lingering nonsense about Mr. Knightley. Then, and only then, could a new, suitable object be introduced.

She spent the rest of the day in a state of happy contemplation, her mind entirely occupied with the brilliant prospects unfolding before her. Frank Churchill’s visit had been the master-stroke. He had all but declared himself. His cleverness, his elegance, his decided preference for her, filled her with a delightful sense of her own consequence. She thought of Jane Fairfax with a kind of benevolent pity; poor Jane, with her reserved manners and her secret anxieties, how little she could guess of the great drama of which she was the unwitting instrument! And she thought of Mr. Knightley with an affectionate, sisterly indulgence. Dear Mr. Knightley, so good and so thoroughly predictable, with his loud inquiries and his charitable projects. It was a comfort to have him so well understood. He would marry no one, of course. He belonged to Hartfield and to Donwell Abbey, a permanent and useful fixture in their lives, a brother, a critic, and a friend. The idea of him as any thing more was now so ludicrous that she could smile at her own past arguments against it.

The Coles’ party now loomed in her imagination as an event of the first importance. It would be the scene of her triumph. There, before the eyes of all Highbury, Frank Churchill’s preference would be made manifest. He would dance with her, he would talk to her, he would seek her out. Jane Fairfax would be present, of course, playing her part with her usual quiet grace, and Mr. Knightley would be there, observing them all with his keen, critical eye. Emma looked forward to that. She would enjoy letting him see that she, and not he, was the true judge of character and the true mistress of the social game. The party would settle every thing. She had arranged it all so perfectly in her mind that the event itself seemed but a formality, a public ratification of the truths she had so cleverly discovered.

“We shall meet at the Coles’, I trust; till then, Highbury will be a desert,” said Frank Churchill, bowing over Emma’s hand with a look that managed to convey both public gallantry and private meaning.

Emma could not but feel that his words, though spoken for Mrs. Weston’s ear as well, were intended chiefly for her own; and the glance that accompanied them seemed to promise a renewal of that particular, pointed conversation which had so diverted her in Mrs. Bates’s little parlour. She watched him walk away with Mrs. Weston, his figure so elegant, his air so distinguished, and felt a swell of pride in having captured the attention of such a man. He was, in every respect, precisely the person she could have wished for.

Once the gates were closed and they were walking up the shrubbery path to the house, she turned to her companion. Harriet’s eyes were still fixed upon the road, with an expression that was not entirely happy.

“Well, Harriet,” Emma began, with the gentle authority of one who has a pleasing but necessary truth to impart. “I think we may be quite satisfied with our morning’s work. The whole business of the pianoforte is as clear as day.”

“Yes,” said Harriet, though without much energy. “Mr. Churchill is very—very amusing.”

“He is more than amusing; he is clever. He sees every thing. You must have observed how perfectly he understood the situation with Jane Fairfax, and how he played upon her feelings. A little cruel, perhaps, but all in good sport. And did you not hear Mr. Knightley, Harriet? His inquiries were all for Miss Fairfax. ‘How is your niece, Miss Bates? Tell me how Miss Fairfax is.’ It could not have been more plain.”

Harriet gave a small, resigned sigh. “No, indeed. He seems very anxious about her health.”

“Exactly so. Anxious as a friend, or a guardian, or—as we once feared—something more. But I am now convinced it is all of a piece with his general benevolence. He is anxious for every body. You heard Miss Bates herself say he had sent them all his store of apples. It is his way. He would be just as concerned for Mrs. Bates’s cat, were it to catch a cold. We have been quite mistaken in attributing any particularity to his attentions. He belongs to all Highbury, not to any single person.”

She delivered this speech with such cheerful confidence that Harriet could not but be affected by it. A little of the bewilderment left her face, replaced by a look of timid gratitude towards her friend.

“If you are sure, Emma, then I am very glad. It is a great weight off my mind. I should not like to think of Mr. Knightley—of any body—being unhappy.”

“Depend upon it, he is perfectly content,” said Emma warmly. “His happiness consists in managing his estate, scolding his friends, and sending apples to his neighbours. He will never marry. Some men are not of a marrying disposition, and Mr. Knightley is one of them. He is a confirmed bachelor, and we must all be thankful for it; for what would Hartfield do without him?”

This settled the matter. Harriet was convinced, and Emma was delighted with her own sagacity. The path was now clear. With Mr. Knightley safely removed from the field of matrimonial speculation, her mind was free to return to more promising subjects: namely, the Coles’ party, and the continued cultivation of Frank Churchill’s decided partiality.

The intervening days were passed by Emma in a state of the most pleasant and settled anticipation. The weather was fine; her father was in tolerable health and spirits, requiring only a little more reassurance than usual about the perils of an evening engagement; and her own mind was a perfect picture of serene self-satisfaction. She thought of her dress; a new muslin, trimmed with lace, which she knew to be exceedingly becoming. She thought of her hair, and decided upon a style that was both elegant and fashionable. She pictured the scene: the Coles’ drawing-room, brightly lit; the assembly of all the first society of Highbury; and herself, entering with her father, the object of general admiration, and of one gentleman’s very particular regard.

The evening arrived. Mr. Woodhouse, having been coaxed and cosseted into his carriage with the promise of a very early return and no exposure to drafts, was safely conveyed, with his daughter, to the door. The Coles’ house was a blaze of light and activity. The sound of voices and a harp tuning met them in the hall. Mrs. and Mr. Cole received them with a bustle of joyous hospitality, full of gratitude for their coming, and pride in their handsome, crowded rooms.

“Miss Woodhouse! and Mr. Woodhouse! This is so very kind! We were almost afraid—the night air—but we are so honoured, so truly gratified. Every body is here, I believe. Do let me find you a comfortable chair, Mr. Woodhouse, quite out of the way of the door.”

The Coles’ drawing-room, though not so large as that at Hartfield, was furnished with a degree of modern elegance that spoke of new money and a great desire to please. It was, as Mrs. Cole had said, very full indeed. Emma’s eye swept the room in a quick, comprehensive glance. The Westons were there, of course; Mrs. Weston was in conversation with Mrs. Goddard. Miss Bates and her niece were seated near the fireplace, Miss Bates already in full flow of discourse to a patient Mrs. Perry. Harriet Smith was looking pretty and timid by Mrs. Goddard’s side. And there, standing by the mantelpiece, talking to Mr. Weston, was Frank Churchill. He saw her at once, and his face lit up with a smile that sent a flush of pleasure through her. At the same moment, she saw Mr. Knightley. He was not smiling. He was standing rather apart from the main groups, observing the room with his characteristically keen and serious eye, and his gaze, she noted, was fixed not upon her, but upon the very group she had just noticed: Frank Churchill and his own father.

Her triumph, however, was not to be delayed. Frank Churchill excused himself from his father with a pleasant nod, and was at her side in a moment.

“At last,” he said, his voice low and for her alone. “I have been watching the door for this half-hour. The party may now begin.”

“You are very good,” returned Emma, laughing. “I hope you have not been suffering too much in the interval. I see all our friends are here.”

“Yes, all our friends,” he said, and his eyes flickered for a second towards Jane Fairfax. “It is quite a Highbury collection. And your friend Miss Smith is looking very charming to-night. You have been taking good care of her, I see.”

“Harriet always looks well when she is happy,” said Emma, pleased by the compliment.

For the next quarter of an hour, he was all that she could wish: attentive, lively, and devoted. He spoke of Randalls, of his father and Mrs. Weston, of the dullness of Enscombe and the delights of Surrey; and in every thing he said, there was an implied compliment to her, a suggestion that her society was the one thing that made Highbury not merely tolerable, but delightful. Emma was in her element. She was conscious of being observed; she saw Mrs. Weston’s approving looks, and felt sure that the rest of the room must be drawing the same, inevitable conclusion.

It was only when Mr. Cole came to lead her to the pianoforte, begging for a song, that Frank Churchill’s attention was drawn away. As she moved towards the instrument, she saw him make his way across the room to where Mr. Knightley was still standing. She felt a little prick of curiosity as to what they might say to one another. They were so very different; she could not imagine them having much in common.

Mr. Knightley had indeed been observing, and his observations had not pleased him. He had seen Frank Churchill’s performance with Emma, and had thought it a piece of practised, meaningless flirtation. He had also seen Frank Churchill’s eyes, when he thought no one was watching, stray again and again to Jane Fairfax, with an expression of an entirely different character. He greeted the young man’s approach with a cool civility that bordered on sternness.

“A very pleasant party,” said Frank Churchill, with his easy smile. “The Coles have done it very handsomely. You are not dancing, Mr. Knightley?”

“I do not dance with married women, and there are not a great many unmarried ladies here to-night,” replied Mr. Knightley, his tone dry. “Besides, I leave that to you younger men. You seem to be doing your duty by the neighbourhood very thoroughly.”

The sarcasm was not lost on Frank Churchill, but he chose to ignore it. “One must be agreeable, you know. It is expected. Miss Woodhouse is in excellent voice to-night, is she not? She is the queen of Highbury, and presides over her court with admirable grace.”

“Miss Woodhouse has been queen of Highbury long before you arrived to admire her,” said Mr. Knightley, more shortly than he had intended. “I am glad you appreciate her. I hope your admiration is not of a kind to cause more pain than pleasure in the end.”

Frank Churchill’s smile tightened just a fraction. “Pain, Mr. Knightley? What a severe word. I cannot imagine causing pain to any one, least of all to Miss Woodhouse. I am merely enjoying myself. Surely there is no harm in that.”

“There is no harm in it, provided you are the only one whose feelings are engaged. But you are a great deal too practiced, sir. You have a look and a tone for every body. I see you look at Miss Woodhouse, and then I see you look at Miss Fairfax, and I am at a loss to know which is the real performance.”

At the mention of Jane Fairfax, a singular change came over Frank Churchill’s countenance. The easy charm vanished, and was replaced by a look of genuine seriousness, almost of alarm. “Miss Fairfax? I have barely spoken two words to Miss Fairfax. You must have mistaken my glance. She seems in very poor spirits this evening.”

“She does,” said Mr. Knightley, his gaze unwavering. “And you, I think, have a very good idea of the cause. Your conjectures the other day at her aunt’s house were remarkably pointed. A man does not torment a woman in that way without some reason.”

“You give me credit for a great deal of subtlety,” Frank Churchill said, attempting to recover his former lightness, but failing. He looked across the room towards the pianoforte, where Emma was just finishing her song to a chorus of applause. “I must go and offer my thanks. It was a beautiful performance.” And with a stiff little bow, he left Mr. Knightley’s side.

Mr. Knightley watched him go, his expression troubled. He was more convinced than ever that something was amiss. The young man’s denial had been too quick, his change of manner too pronounced. He was playing a game, a double game, and Mr. Knightley’s sense of justice and propriety was deeply offended. He looked at Emma, radiant and smiling as she received Frank Churchill’s effusive praise, and he felt a profound sense of vexation and concern. She was being deceived; her vanity was being flattered, and her good sense put to sleep. She was so much cleverer than this, so much better. Why could she not see it?

Emma, meanwhile, had noticed nothing of this. She was only aware that Frank Churchill had returned to her side, and that his praise was music to her ears.

“You have made the evening complete,” he whispered. “Cramer himself could not have played it with more feeling.”

“You are too partial a judge,” she replied, her heart fluttering with pleasure.

The company began to move; there was talk of cards in the adjoining room, and dancing for the younger set. Frank Churchill immediately claimed Emma’s hand for the first two dances. Here was the triumph she had anticipated! They took their places, a handsome and conspicuous couple. He danced with an energy and grace that few in Highbury could match, and his conversation was a stream of witty nonsense and flattering attention. Emma was perfectly happy.

During a pause in the music, she happened to glance towards the side of the room. Jane Fairfax was not dancing. She was sitting beside her aunt, looking pale and weary. Just at that moment, Frank Churchill’s attention seemed to be caught by something in the same direction. He followed Emma’s gaze, and his own expression changed.

“Miss Fairfax looks unwell,” he said, his voice dropping. “I believe the heat of the room is too much for her.”

“She has not been well for some time,” said Emma. “She professes to enjoy these parties, but she never seems to derive much pleasure from them.” Emma’s tone held that faint note of criticism that she often used when speaking of Jane.

“Perhaps the pleasure is of a kind we cannot see,” he replied, rather enigmatically. “Some people feel most deeply when they appear most composed.”

The music began again, and the remark was forgotten by Emma in the pleasure of the dance. But when the set was over, and he had returned her to a seat beside Mrs. Weston, she observed his next actions with interest. He did not, as she might have expected, remain with her. Instead, after a moment’s hesitation, he procured a glass of negus and carried it across the room to Jane Fairfax.

Emma watched. She saw him bend down and speak to her in a low voice. She could not hear the words, but she saw Jane’s reaction. A deep blush spread over her pale cheeks; she looked up at him with an expression of what seemed to be pained surprise, and at first refused the glass. He persisted, with a look of earnest entreaty. Miss Bates, of course, immediately began to thank him profusely. “Oh! Mr. Churchill, you are too good! So very thoughtful! Jane, my dear, you must take it. Mr. Churchill is so very obliging. Is he not the most considerate young man, Mrs. Cole?”

Under the pressure of her aunt’s gratitude and his quiet insistence, Jane finally accepted the glass. She murmured something, her eyes cast down, and he replied, lingering for a moment longer than was strictly necessary before retreating.

Emma processed the scene through the lens of her own settled theory. It was all perfectly clear. He had, no doubt, made some further allusion to Weymouth, to Mr. Dixon. The blush, the distress, the reluctance—they were all symptoms of her guilty conscience. Poor Jane! It was rather unkind of him to persecute her so publicly, but it was also vastly amusing, and the strongest possible confirmation of his own freedom from any attachment in that quarter. He was demonstrating, for all to see, that Jane Fairfax and her secrets were merely a source of entertainment to him.

Mrs. Weston, however, seemed to see it differently. “Frank is very good-natured,” she said to Emma, her eyes following her stepson. “He is always thinking of other people’s comfort.”

“Yes, he is very attentive,” said Emma, with a private smile.

The rest of the evening did not quite live up to the promise of its beginning. Frank Churchill did not ask her to dance again, devoting himself instead to his duties as a visitor, dancing with Miss King, with one of the Miss Coxes, and even, for a short and rather awkward country-dance, with Harriet Smith. He was civil and pleasant to all, but he did not again seek out Emma for any particular conversation. He seemed, she thought, a little distracted, his earlier high spirits having given way to a more thoughtful, almost troubled, air. She told herself that this was only natural; a man of feeling could not be perpetually at the height of vivacity. Perhaps his conscience was pricking him for his treatment of Jane.

Mr. Knightley, having done his duty by asking Harriet to dance—an act of kindness which Emma noted with approval as further proof of his general, undirected benevolence—approached her where she sat.

“You are not dancing, Emma?”

“I have danced. I am resting now. It is a very pleasant party, is it not?”

“So it seems. I hope you are enjoying it.” His tone was serious.

“Very much. Though I think your friend Mr. Frank Churchill’s spirits seem to have deserted him a little in the last hour.”

“My friend?” said Mr. Knightley, with emphasis. “He is no particular friend of mine. And as for his spirits, I imagine they are as changeable as the wind. I should not pay them much heed, if I were you.”

“You are determined to dislike him, I see,” said Emma, half amused, half irritated. “You find fault in every thing he does.”

“No, not in every thing,” he answered, and his eyes fell for a moment on Jane Fairfax, who was now being persuaded by Mr. Weston to take a turn at the pianoforte. “His asking Miss Fairfax to take some refreshment was a kindness. I only wish all his actions were as easy to interpret.”

He said no more, but moved away to speak to his brother. Emma was left with a faint, indefinable feeling of dissatisfaction. Mr. Knightley’s disapprobation always had the power to unsettle her, even when she was most convinced he was wrong. She looked towards the pianoforte. Jane Fairfax was playing, something soft and melancholy. And Frank Churchill, who had been standing near the door as if contemplating his departure, had stopped, and was listening with an air of rapt attention. He did not approach her; he simply stood and listened, his posture betraying a feeling that went far beyond polite appreciation.

The party began to break up. The Woodhouses’ carriage was one of the first to be announced. As they were making their adieus, Frank Churchill came forward to help Emma with her shawl.

“Leaving so soon?” he said, his voice once more low and intimate. “The best part of the evening is gone with you.”

It was prettily said, but it felt, to Emma, a little mechanical. It lacked the spontaneous warmth of his earlier address. She thanked him, allowed him to arrange the shawl, and then took her father’s arm. Mr. Knightley was in the hall, seeing them to the door.

“I hope you will not take cold, sir,” he said to Mr. Woodhouse. “The carriage is drawn up as close as possible.”

“Thank you, Mr. Knightley, you are always so very thoughtful,” chirped Mr. Woodhouse.

As they stepped into the night air, Mr. Knightley’s hand briefly detained Emma’s arm. “Emma,” he said, his voice unusually grave. “Be careful. Do not build your happiness on a foundation of sand.”

Before she could frame a reply, he had released her and stepped back. She was bustled into the carriage, the door was closed, and they were on their way back to Hartfield.

Her father soon fell into a gentle doze, and Emma was left to her thoughts. The brilliant triumph she had so confidently arranged in her mind had not quite come to pass. There had been moments of it, certainly; the first dance, the devoted conversation. But they were interspersed with other moments that were confusing and contradictory. Frank Churchill’s strange conversation with Mr. Knightley; his pointed, yet perplexing, attention to Jane Fairfax; his variable spirits; Mr. Knightley’s solemn, repeated warnings. The perfect clarity of her morning’s conclusions had become clouded. A disagreeable flutter of uncertainty had taken the place of her former serene self-satisfaction. She tried to re-establish her theory, to fit all the pieces back into their proper places, but they resisted. The image of Frank Churchill listening to Jane Fairfax’s playing, and the echo of Mr. Knightley’s parting words, “a foundation of sand,” returned to her again and again, disturbing the perfect structure of her plans, and leaving her with a sense of doubt that was as novel as it was unwelcome.
