Mr. Ambrose turned the small, silver locket over in his fingers, a glint of mischief in his eye. “It is a very pretty thing, Miss Verity, very pretty indeed. But so plain, so unadorned. One might almost suppose its value to be in what it conceals, rather than in what it displays.” He held it out towards her, yet did not quite relinquish it. “I wonder if your good aunt in Bath has seen it? I am certain she would be forming a thousand conjectures as to its origin. Do you suppose she would guess it to be a recent acquisition, or a long cherished keepsake?”

Miss Verity, whose composure was visibly strained, could only reply, “I am sure I cannot say. My aunt’s conjectures are her own.”

“Her own, to be sure,” he answered, finally placing the locket on the table beside her work-basket. “But we all must have our little theories. I have a theory, for instance, about this clasp. It is very stiff. You see? A gentleman’s fingers, Miss Bennet, are often quite clumsy with such delicate mechanisms. One needs practice. I should be very glad to offer my assistance whenever it is required.” He smiled, a knowing, familiar smile that brought a faint colour to Miss Bennet’s cheek, though she was merely an observer. He then addressed Miss Verity again. “You were very quiet at dinner last evening; I believe you did not say above three words to poor Mr. Green. Were you thinking, perhaps, of how soon you might be able to retire and look upon your treasure in peace?”

“I was tired, sir. The walk from the village was a long one.”

“Ah, yes, the walk from the village,” he repeated, as if the words themselves held some particular significance. He moved from the table towards the window, his work of torment seemingly done. “How brightly the sun shines on the meadows to-day. It quite puts one in mind of that picnic at Box Hill. Do you remember the view from Box Hill, Miss Bennet?”

Before she could form an answer, he was speaking again. “We were all so happy then. Or, at least, most of us were. Miss Verity, you were rather pensive that day too, if I recall. You preferred sketching the ruins of the old priory to joining our games. A very romantic occupation, sketching ruins. It speaks of a fondness for the past, for things that are lost.” He glanced at her, but she had taken up her needlework and would not meet his eye.

Eleanor Bennet wished he would be silent, yet a part of her was compelled by the little drama. When she saw Miss Verity’s fingers tremble almost imperceptibly as she drew the thread, she felt a pang of sympathy, but it was mingled with an irresistible curiosity. This quiet, reserved Miss Verity was clearly possessed of a story, and Mr. Ambrose, for all his impertinence, was determined to bring it to light.

He came to sit in the chair next to Eleanor’s. “You think me cruel,” he whispered, under the cover of fetching a dropped ball of yarn.

“I think you too bold,” she whispered back. “You will distress her to no purpose.”

“Oh, I believe there is a purpose. I would have her know that she is understood. Secrets are a heavy burden, Miss Bennet, and are best shared.”

“You presume to know her secret, then?”

“I know that she looks at this locket when she believes no one is watching, and I know that she received a letter with a London postmark this morning. I am a great conjecturer, you see. There, her needle is still. She is listening. I believe she values my attention more than you suppose.”

Just then, a carriage was heard on the gravel drive, and soon after the parlour door opened to admit Mr. Davies. He was a man of fewer words and a more serious countenance than Mr. Ambrose, and his entrance seemed at once to clear the air. “Ladies,” he said with a general bow. “I was at the bookseller’s in town, and I remembered, Miss Verity, your mentioning an interest in botany. I took the liberty of bringing you this.”

He presented a slim, plainly bound volume. Miss Verity looked up, her expression one of genuine surprise and, it seemed to Eleanor, of immense relief. “Oh, Mr. Davies! How very thoughtful. I had quite forgotten I had mentioned it. I am very much obliged to you.”

The warmth in her voice was unmistakable. She took the book with a smile so open and unguarded that it quite transformed her face. Mr. Ambrose observed it all, his own smile fading into something more contemplative.

“Yes,” said Mr. Davies, his gaze resting on her with a quiet satisfaction that had nothing of triumph in it. “I am happy to have remembered it correctly. I am going on to the Grange now. Can I take any letters for you?”

“No, I thank you,” said Miss Verity, her eyes still on the book. “Nothing for the post to-day.”

When he had gone, a silence fell upon the room, quite different from the tense quiet that had preceded it. Mr. Ambrose walked back to the table. He picked up the silver locket, looked at it for a moment, and set it down again, this time with a decided lack of ceremony. “Well,” he said, turning to Eleanor with a shrug. “It seems some gentlemen have a talent for giving gifts that may be acknowledged. A skill I have yet to acquire.” And he said no more on the subject for the rest of the afternoon.

The two gentlemen, Mr. Churchill and Mr. Knightley, what a study in contrasts they presented! “A very singular young man, your Mr. Churchill,” Eleanor ventured to say to Mrs. Weston, as they began the last leg of their journey.

“Is he not?” replied Mrs. Weston, with the fond, slightly anxious smile of a new mother. “He has a spirit and vivacity that cannot be suppressed. I own, I sometimes wish he would be a little less… pointed in his observations. He does so love to guess at a secret.”

“He seems to have fixed upon a very particular one with respect to Miss Fairfax,” said Eleanor.

“Ah, yes. That is Emma’s influence, I am afraid. She has a theory, you see, about a secret attachment in Ireland, and she confided it to Frank. He has taken up the notion with a relish that quite alarms me. I do not believe a word of it, for my part. Jane Fairfax is too good, too sensible, to be engaging in some clandestine affair. Her reserve comes only from her unfortunate situation; it is the natural consequence of dependence.”

Eleanor considered this. It was a very rational, very kind explanation, and one she would have accepted without question only a day before. But having seen Jane Fairfax’s deep blush, the remains of that secret smile, and the tremor in her hand as she played a song designated as “his favourite,” she could not be so certain. There was a story there, of that she felt sure; the only question was whether Mr. Churchill’s version, or some other, was the true one. She thought of Mr. Knightley’s solicitude, his commanding tone at the window, demanding to know of Miss Fairfax’s health above all else. Could _he_ be a part of the story? The idea was so startling, so contrary to his established character as the sensible, impartial friend of the family, that she dismissed it almost as soon as it was formed. No, Mr. Knightley’s concern was merely that of a benevolent neighbour for a deserving but unfortunate young woman. It was a kindness of principle, not of passion.

The next day brought a fine, crisp morning, and Eleanor, feeling the need for air and exercise to clear her head of its many conjectures, set out for a brisk walk towards Donwell. She had not gone above a half-mile when she heard the sound of a horse behind her, and turned to see Mr. Frank Churchill himself, riding at a gentle pace. Upon seeing her, he dismounted with an easy grace and, looping the reins over his arm, fell into step beside her.

“Miss Woodhouse’s friend, Miss Bennet, if I am not mistaken,” he said, with a smile that invited confidence. “Walking to enjoy this admirable weather? Or, perhaps, walking to escape a house full of pianofortes and endless chatter about apples?”

Eleanor laughed. “The weather is certainly my primary motivation, sir. Though I confess the quiet is also welcome after such a full morning yesterday.”

“Ah, yes. A full morning indeed. We unearthed a few treasures, did we not? Or at least, we polished them until they shone. You thought me very cruel to Miss Fairfax, I am sure.”

He looked at her with such directness that a simple denial would have been an obvious falsehood. “I thought you very persistent,” she replied carefully. “I confess I was not certain of your object.”

“My object? Why, it is the simplest one in the world: truth! I have a passion for it. When I see a mystery, a puzzle, a set of odd looks and carefully guarded words, I must know the reason. Miss Woodhouse gave me the scent, and I am merely following the trail. Do you not find it a fascinating sport?”

“I am not sure I should call it a sport when it may involve the feelings of another,” said Eleanor.

“Oh, but the feelings are the very heart of it! Without them, it would be no more diverting than a game of chess. And I believe her feelings are not so pained as you imagine. Did you not see her, when I mentioned Weymouth? Such a blush! It was not a blush of anger, Miss Bennet; it was a blush of memory. She was pleased. And the song… _Robin Adair_! It was a direct signal, a message to me—or rather, to the idea of me, the one who understands—that I was correct. She was confirming my conjecture.”

He spoke with such confidence, such an air of absolute certainty, that Eleanor was almost swayed. He made the whole business sound like a delightful intrigue, a game played between two clever people with the rest of the world as their audience. “You believe she wishes her secret to be known, then?”

“Not known, precisely. But understood. There is a world of difference. To have it blurted out by Miss Bates would be a catastrophe. But to have it delicately hinted at by a sympathetic soul… that is a comfort. She is isolated, you see. She cannot speak of the man she loves, and it must be a terrible burden. I am offering her a sort of silent communion.”

This was a very romantic, very generous interpretation of his own actions, and Eleanor could not help but be a little suspicious of it. “A communion she did not seem to welcome when you spoke of the commission from Colonel Campbell.”

He winced, a flicker of genuine chagrin crossing his features. “Ah. Yes. That was a misstep. I was too direct; I approached the matter like a lawyer cross-examining a witness. A clumsy error. I saw it at once. One must be oblique. One must approach the subject through music, through shared glances, through praises of a third party’s ‘true affection.’ That is the language she understands.” He paused, and then looked at her with renewed animation. “But you are a new observer, Miss Bennet. Your eye is fresh. What did you make of Mr. Knightley’s performance?”

“Mr. Knightley?” said Eleanor, surprised. “I saw no performance. I saw only a man very anxious for his friend’s health.”

“Oh, it was a performance, I assure you! And a masterly one at that. To stand outside a window and bellow one’s concern for all the world to hear! ‘How is Miss Fairfax? Tell me how Miss Fairfax is!’ My dear Miss Bennet, when a man of Mr. Knightley’s reserve and propriety makes such a public spectacle of his concern for a young lady, you may be sure there is more to it than neighbourly kindness. “A claim? But he is like an elder brother to her, is he not? He has known her since she was a child.”

This was an entirely new and dizzying possibility. Mr. Knightley, the pillar of sense and reason, as a romantic rival to the dashing Mr. Churchill, for the affections of the reserved Miss Fairfax, who was supposedly pining for a secret lover in Ireland? It was the plot of a novel, and a rather overwrought one at that.

“You have a very lively imagination, Mr. Churchill,” Eleanor said, unable to keep a note of scepticism from her voice.

“I do,” he replied cheerfully, not at all offended. “And I am rarely wrong. Watch him, the next time they are together. Watch him when he is not speaking to her, but when he is looking at her. There is a gravity, an intensity there, that you do not see when he looks at anyone else. Not even when he is scolding Miss Woodhouse, which I am told is his favourite occupation.” He smiled, a flash of pure mischief in his eyes. “But I have detained you long enough. I must get on to Hartfield and report my theories to my commander-in-chief, Miss Woodhouse. He mounted his horse with a nod and a smile, and was gone, leaving Eleanor standing in the lane with her head spinning. Mr. Knightley and Jane Fairfax! It was preposterous. It was impossible. Mr. Knightley, who valued openness and sincerity above all things, secretly in love with a woman so reserved she was almost a statue? And yet… and yet… his behaviour at the window had been singular. There was no denying it. His voice had carried a note of command, of possession almost, that went beyond simple politeness.

She continued her walk, her mind a whirl of competing theories. Mr. Churchill’s version of events was so compelling, so full of dramatic flair, but was it true? He saw the world as a stage, and everyone in it as an actor in a play of his own devising. It was an amusing worldview, but a dangerous one. He was playing with real people, real feelings; and if he was wrong, the consequences could be far from entertaining. She resolved to follow his advice, however. She would watch. She would observe Mr. Knightley with a new and sharpened attention.

Her opportunity came sooner than she expected. The arrival of the pianoforte had made Miss Fairfax an object of renewed interest in Highbury, and this dinner was clearly intended to be a celebration of sorts.

Eleanor found herself seated at table between Mr. Woodhouse, whose anxieties about drafts and undercooked mutton required her constant and gentle reassurance, and Mr. Knightley. For the first part of the dinner, he was occupied in a conversation with Mrs. Weston about parish matters, and seemed his usual, sensible self. “Oh, Mr. Knightley! You must call upon us, you really must. To hear Jane play! It is such a sweet-toned instrument. So very thoughtful of Colonel Campbell. Jane was just saying, was she not, my dear, how very much she feels the kindness of it.”

Jane Fairfax, who had been listening with a polite but distant smile, murmured something inaudible.

Eleanor felt a thrill of discovery. Mr. Churchill was right. There was something here, a hidden current of feeling that ran deep beneath the placid surface of Highbury society.

“Mr. Churchill is very fond of dancing,” said Emma, from her seat beside Mr. Weston, a playful light in her eye. “He seems to think of little else.”

“He is a young man, and it is natural for him to be fond of dancing,” said Mr. Knightley, his voice a little sharper than usual. “But a fondness for dancing does not always imply a sound judgment in other, more serious matters.”

The rebuke was ostensibly for Emma, a continuation of their familiar pattern of sparring, but Eleanor felt its true target lay elsewhere. He was speaking of Mr. Churchill’s character, of his fitness as a suitor, and he was speaking with the authority of a man who felt he had a right to do so.

The conversation moved on, but the impression remained with Eleanor for the rest of the evening. When the ladies withdrew, she observed Jane Fairfax, who, pleading a slight headache, did not join the others at the card table but sat by the fire with a book. She looked pale and weary, and Eleanor felt a pang of compunction for her own prying curiosity. This was no game; this was a young woman in a state of real distress.

Later, when the gentlemen rejoined them, she watched as Mr. Knightley, instead of joining the card players or engaging in general conversation, walked directly over to the fireplace. He stood for a moment, looking down at Jane.

“Your aunt tells me you have a headache, Miss Fairfax,” he said, his voice low and gentle, entirely stripped of the sharpness it had held at dinner. “I am not surprised. You were fatigued last night, and the exertions of yesterday, with the arrival of that… instrument, must have been too much for you.”

There was a slight, almost contemptuous emphasis on the word “instrument.”

“You are very kind, sir,” she replied, not looking up from her book. “It is of no consequence. It will soon pass.”

“I hope you will not be persuaded to play for us to-night. You are in no state for it. Mrs. Weston will understand.”

“I believe Mrs. Weston has already asked Miss Woodhouse,” she said, her voice still quiet and controlled.

Eleanor had seen enough. The mystery of Jane Fairfax was not one story, but two. There was the secret of the pianoforte, of Weymouth, of _Robin Adair_—a secret of which Mr. Frank Churchill was the gleeful, self-proclaimed master. And then there was this other secret, a silent, unhappy drama playing out between Jane and Mr. Knightley, a story of unspoken feelings, of disapproval, and of a deep, sorrowful concern that looked so very much like love. How these two stories connected, she could not begin to imagine; but she was now certain that to understand one, she would have to understand them both. And she felt, with a certainty that was both exciting and daunting, that the key to it all lay not with the brilliant, conjecturing Mr. Churchill, but with the quiet, steadfast, and deeply unhappy Mr. Knightley.

The succeeding days passed quietly. Emma was thoughtful, and less inclined than usual to her schemes and speculations. She spoke of Mr. Churchill with admiration for his wit and manners, but with a degree of reservation that Eleanor had not previously observed. She spoke more of Mr. Knightley, wondering at his strange, almost proprietary concern for Jane Fairfax.

“It is so unlike him,” she said one afternoon, as she and Eleanor sat with their needlework. “Mr. Knightley is kind to everyone, I know; his benevolence is not confined to his particular friends. It was, ‘How is Miss Fairfax?’ again and again. It is very odd.”

“He has known her since she was a child, I believe,” Eleanor offered gently. “Perhaps he feels a brotherly concern for her welfare, seeing her in such a delicate position.”

“Brotherly concern?” Emma repeated, looking doubtful. “No, it is not that. There is a gravity in his manner, a seriousness that goes beyond mere neighbourly kindness. He looked as if he had bitten into one of his own sour apples.” She paused, her needle still. “I confess, it vexes me. I cannot make it out. Mr. Knightley, who is sensibility and integrity itself, to be so singularly focused on a girl as reserved and—and, I must say it, as tiresome as Jane Fairfax! It is a puzzle not worth the solving.”

Yet Eleanor could see it was a puzzle that occupied her friend’s mind more than she was willing to admit. Emma’s world was a small one, and Mr. Knightley was its fixed star; any deviation in his orbit was a matter of profound and personal significance. For him to show such marked attention to another, and that other Jane Fairfax, was a disruption of the natural order of Highbury.

Thursday arrived, and with it the dinner at Randalls. Mr. Woodhouse had, predictably, declined the invitation, finding the prospect of a night excursion altogether too hazardous. So it was that Eleanor, Emma, and Miss Smith made the short journey alone. They were the first to arrive, but the other guests soon followed: Miss Bates and her niece, looking elegant but pale in a simple white gown; Mr. Knightley, his countenance as serious and composed as ever; and finally Mr. Elton, who, having just returned to the neighbourhood, had been included at the last moment. Mr. Churchill, of course, was already at home.

The party was, as Mrs. Weston had promised, a comfortable one. Mr. Weston was in his usual boisterous spirits, delighted to have his house full of friends. Mr. Elton attached himself to the married ladies, paying them elaborate compliments, while studiously ignoring Miss Smith. Frank Churchill, true to his word, was the model of propriety. He devoted himself almost entirely to Emma, his conversation free from any pointed allusions, full of amusing anecdotes of London life, and altogether designed to charm.

Eleanor, seated for a time near the fireplace, observed the tableau. She saw Jane Fairfax, placed between her aunt and a benevolent but deaf old lady, speaking little and smiling less. She saw Mr. Knightley, standing apart from the main group, conversing quietly with Mr. Weston, yet his eyes strayed, more than once, towards Jane’s corner of the room. It was a brief, unguarded glance, but it held a world of troubled thought. He was not looking at a tiresome girl; he was looking at a source of deep and abiding anxiety.

At dinner, fortune, or perhaps Mrs. Weston’s careful design, placed Eleanor next to Mr. Knightley. On her other side was Mr. Weston, who was soon engaged in a loud and cheerful debate with Mr. Elton about the price of hay. This left her at liberty to speak with her other neighbour, should she find the courage to do so. For a time they ate in silence, with only the requisite civilities passing between them. At last, seeking a neutral topic, Eleanor remarked on the beauty of the view from the dining-room window, even in the twilight of a winter’s day.

“Donwell Abbey must command an even finer prospect,” she said.

“It does,” he replied. “From the upper part of the grounds, one can see a great distance. It is an old-fashioned view; there is nothing of the modern picturesque about it. But I am fond of it. It is the view my father, and his father before him, looked upon.”

There was a sincerity in his tone that invited confidence. “It must be a great comfort,” Eleanor ventured, “to belong so completely to a place. To have such deep roots in the neighbourhood.”

“It is a comfort, and a responsibility,” he said, his expression softening a little. “One has a duty to the land, and to the people who live on it. Not all of us are so fortunate as to have such ties. Some are forced to wander, to seek their fortune among strangers.”

His gaze flickered for a single instant towards the other side of the table, where Jane Fairfax sat. The movement was so slight, so swift, Eleanor might have doubted it, had she not been watching for it.

“Indeed,” she said, keeping her voice level. “It must be a very painful prospect for a young woman of genteel birth but no fortune. The world can be a cold place for a governess.”

She felt, rather than saw, him turn to look at her fully. She kept her own eyes on her plate, fearing she had been too bold.

“You speak with feeling, Miss Morton,” he said, his voice low. “It is a subject on which, I believe, too few people of fortune reflect. We are apt to take our own security for granted. For a young woman of superior mind and accomplishments, to be at the beck and call of spoiled children and their arrogant mothers—it is a kind of slavery. A genteel slavery, but slavery nonetheless.”

“One must hope for a kind family, at least,” Eleanor murmured.

“One may hope,” he answered, a note of bitterness in his voice. “But hope is a poor substitute for independence. Miss Fairfax, for instance, possesses a degree of talent and a strength of character that would do credit to any station. To think of her abilities being wasted, her spirit broken, in such a life… it is a matter of real regret to all who know her worth.”

He spoke as if concluding a public statement, a formal acknowledgement of a neighbour’s merits. But Eleanor heard the undertone of personal pain. He was not speaking of “all who know her worth”; he was speaking only of himself. Here was the source of his sorrowful concern: not just love, but a love sharpened by a profound pity for her circumstances and an impotent anger at the injustice of her fate. He loved her, and he could see the path of misery that lay before her, a path he seemed powerless to alter.

Their conversation was interrupted by Mr. Weston, calling down the table to Mr. Knightley on some matter of parish business. Eleanor was left to her thoughts, her theory no longer a conjecture, but a certainty.

When the ladies withdrew to the drawing-room, the atmosphere shifted. Mrs. Weston was eager to know Emma’s opinion of her son.

“Is he not delightful, my dear? I knew you would find him so. He has such spirits, and such a good heart.”

“He has been very pleasant this evening,” Emma agreed, though with a moderation that seemed to disappoint Mrs. Weston a little.

Miss Bates, meanwhile, had begun a long and rambling account of a letter she had just received from her sister in Ireland, full of news of the Campbells, and how they were expected in London very soon, and how very much they longed to see Jane, and how Colonel Campbell had been suffering from a touch of the gout. Jane sat beside her, her hands folded in her lap, her face a mask of patient endurance.

“And Mrs. Dixon, you know—the late Miss Campbell—writes that they are so very happy,” Miss Bates chirped, “though she does miss her father’s company. And she asks particularly after you, Jane! She hopes your health is quite restored, and she says—let me see, where is it?—ah, yes! She says she hopes you have been making good use of the… of the new music that you have.” Miss Bates faltered for a moment, suddenly conscious, it seemed, of the dangerous ground she was treading. “Such a thoughtful man, Colonel Campbell!” she hurried on, a little too loudly. “Always thinking of what will give pleasure. Jane is so very fortunate in her friends.”

During this speech, Eleanor watched Jane Fairfax. At the mention of Mrs. Dixon, a flicker of something—was it pain? resentment?—crossed her features. She remained perfectly still, but her knuckles were white where she clasped her hands.

The gentlemen’s return saved her from any further commentary. Mr. Churchill went directly to Emma’s side.

“Your friend Miss Smith is to be pitied,” he whispered, with a mischievous smile. “Mr. Elton has been talking of nothing but tithes and turnpikes for the last half-hour. I believe Knightley encouraged him, merely to observe his suffering.”

“Mr. Knightley is not so cruel,” said Emma.

“Is he not? I think him capable of any degree of sober cruelty, if he believes it to be for one’s own good. He has the air of a man who would prescribe a very disagreeable medicine with the greatest satisfaction.”

Before Emma could form a reply, Mr. Churchill raised his voice. “Mrs. Weston! The evening is growing solemn. We are in need of a little music to enliven us. Miss Fairfax was so kind at her aunt’s the other day; perhaps she would oblige us again?”

A sudden silence fell upon the room. All eyes turned to Jane. Eleanor felt a surge of indignation at his casual cruelty. After his promise of discretion, this was a deliberate and public provocation. Jane Fairfax coloured, a deep and painful blush that spread from her neck to her brow.

“I… I am not feeling very well this evening, sir,” she stammered, her voice barely audible. “I must beg you to excuse me.”

“Not well?” said Mr. Churchill, his face a picture of innocent concern. “I am grieved to hear it. But sometimes a little music is the very best remedy. A simple country dance, perhaps? Or one of those waltzes we all so enjoyed? I am sure it would restore you.”

He was smiling, a charming, handsome, and utterly merciless smile. He was enjoying her distress; he was displaying his power over her to the entire room, and most particularly, to Emma. Miss Bates began to flutter, “Oh, indeed, sir, she is not quite the thing, her head has been plaguing her…” but no one attended to her. Jane seemed to shrink into herself, cornered and exposed.

It was then that Mr. Knightley, who had been standing by the mantelpiece, moved. He did not raise his voice; he did not betray any sign of anger. He simply walked across the room and stood before Mr. Churchill.

“Mr. Churchill,” he said, and his voice, though quiet, carried to every corner of the room, cutting through the strained silence. “You must allow me to intercede on Miss Fairfax’s behalf. She has told you she is unwell. To press her to perform would be an unkindness I am sure you do not intend.”

The final words were spoken with a deliberate and chilling politeness. It was a perfect rebuke; it accused Mr. Churchill of nothing, yet it condemned him entirely. It offered him the chance to retreat with honour, while making it plain that to continue would be an ungentlemanly outrage.

For a moment, Frank Churchill was nonplussed. He looked from Mr. Knightley’s grave, unyielding face to Jane’s averted one, and for the first time that evening, his mask of cheerful ease slipped. He looked surprised, and then deeply, profoundly annoyed. But he was too skilled in social arts to betray it for more than an instant. He recovered himself with a short laugh.

“You are right, of course, Knightley. How thoughtless of me! My apologies, Miss Fairfax. My desire for music overcame my good manners.” He executed a graceful bow. “In that case, we must impose upon Miss Woodhouse. I know she will not refuse to save us from melancholy.”

All eyes now turned to Emma, who found herself the unexpected centre of this silent drama. She was vexed with Mr. Churchill, shocked by his conduct; she was startled by Mr. Knightley’s intervention, and mortified to be made a part of it. She felt her cheeks burn, but she rose to the occasion with her natural grace.

“I will play with pleasure,” she said, moving towards the pianoforte, “though I cannot promise to play anything as delightful as a waltz from Weymouth.”

The subtle sting in her words was not lost on Mr. Churchill, who merely smiled and followed her to the instrument. The crisis was over. Conversation resumed, and Emma began to play. But the harmony of the evening had been broken beyond repair. Eleanor looked at Mr. Knightley. He had returned to the mantelpiece, his posture unchanged, but his face, in the firelight, was filled with a sorrow so deep it seemed to age him. He had won the battle, but he looked like a man who knew he was losing the war. He had protected Jane Fairfax for a moment, but he could not protect her from the secret that bound her to a man like Frank Churchill, or from the miserable future that awaited her. It was the torment of loving a woman he could not respect, and whom he could not save.

When at last the final chord faded, a polite murmur of applause ensued. Mrs. Weston, ever the peacemaker, was quick to praise. “My dear Emma, you played beautifully, as you always do. Miss Bates, for once, was nearly silent, her usual stream of chatter reduced to a few anxious whispers to her mother.

It was Eleanor, Mrs. Weston’s young cousin, visiting from a quieter corner of the country, who perceived the full extent of the evening’s fracture. She had observed the entire exchange with an outsider’s keen eye, unclouded by the intimacies and assumptions that governed the Highbury set. She saw not gallantry in Mr. Knightley’s action, but desperation; she saw not thoughtlessness in Mr. Churchill’s cruelty, but a calculated and possessive malice. Soon, the necessity of departure provided a welcome relief. Carriages were announced, shawls were fetched, and the little room, which had felt so suffocatingly close, began to empty. Mr. Frank Churchill was all obliging charm, assisting Mrs. Weston with her wrap, sharing a jest with her husband, and bowing over Emma’s hand with a look that seemed to solicit her forgiveness and admiration all at once.

“I have been a brute, Miss Woodhouse,” he murmured, his voice just for her. “Say you are not entirely out of charity with me.”

“You were certainly not at your best,” Emma replied, unable to resist a touch of severity, though she was already half-inclined to forgive him his trespasses, as she so often was. “Miss Fairfax looked quite unwell.”

“She did, she did indeed,” he said with a sigh of theatrical regret. “It is her nerves. They are not strong. I ought to have remembered. You, Miss Woodhouse, have nerves of steel, as well as a touch of gold.”

He was incorrigible; and being incorrigible, he was, in Emma’s estimation, halfway to being forgiven. She allowed a small smile, and he took it for a full pardon, pressing her hand for a moment before relinquishing it.

Mr. Knightley’s farewells were of a different character altogether. He spoke briefly to Mr. and Mrs. Weston, his manner perfectly civil but lacking its usual warmth. He nodded to Frank Churchill with a coldness that was almost a physical blow. When he came to Emma, he paused.

“A trying evening, Miss Woodhouse,” he said, his eyes not quite meeting hers.

“It became so,” she answered, feeling a blush rise again. She wished to defend herself, to explain that she had no part in Mr. Churchill’s ill-judged attack, but the words would not come. Before Mr. Knightley, she often felt a discomfiting sense of being entirely seen through, and tonight was no exception.

“I trust you will get home without trouble,” he said, his gaze moving past her to the door. “Good night.”

He was gone, and Emma was left with a feeling of dissatisfaction so potent it was almost an ache. He had disapproved of her, she was certain of it; he had lumped her in with Frank Churchill, as a partner in folly.

The walk back to Hartfield was short, but it offered ample time for reflection. Mrs. Weston had elected to return with her husband, leaving Emma to be escorted by Eleanor, with a servant following at a respectable distance. The night air was cool and restorative after the close atmosphere of the Bateses’ parlour.

“Well!” said Emma, as soon as they were out of earshot. “I have never known an evening to take such a turn. Frank Churchill may be the most charming man in England, but he has moments of the most astonishing want of sense.”

“His behaviour was very singular,” Eleanor agreed, her voice carefully neutral.

“Singular! It was dreadful. To press her so, when she was so obviously distressed! And all that nonsense about Weymouth. As if we did not all know the source of her attachment lies in Ireland! He has no delicacy. And yet,” Emma mused, her thoughts taking a familiar turn, “one cannot help but feel for him. He must see how her affections are engaged elsewhere, and it piques him. It is vanity, of course, but a very understandable vanity.”

Eleanor made no reply. She was thinking of Mr. Knightley’s face in the firelight, of that deep, private sorrow. To hear Emma so blithely construct a narrative of wounded vanity for Frank Churchill, while the real, profound heartache of the evening went entirely unobserved, was a lesson in the limits of human perception. Emma, so clever, so confident in her judgements, was utterly blind.

“And Mr. Knightley!” Emma went on, warming to her subject. “Did you ever see anything like it? To step in like that, so formally. It was very well done, I suppose; very correct. But it was so very unlike him. So stern! I declare, he quite frightened me. He puts himself forward as Jane Fairfax’s champion, and I am sure I do not know why. He is always finding fault with me, yet he treats her with this solemn reverence, as if she were a queen in disguise. It is a mystery to me.”

“He seems to value her comfort a great deal,” Eleanor ventured.

“Oh, he values everyone’s comfort but his own, and mine!” Emma said with a laugh that did not quite ring true. “He would have us all sit in perfect, sober sense from morning till night. He cannot appreciate a little harmless amusement. Frank Churchill and I were only conjecturing, after all. It is what he does not understand.”

They had reached the gates of Hartfield. The great house was dark, save for a light in the hall and another in her father’s room. The familiar sight was a comfort. Inside those walls, she was mistress of her own small world, a world of order and affection where such jarring notes were seldom struck.

“I am very glad to be home,” she said, more to herself than to Eleanor. “I have had quite enough of other people’s nerves and tempers for one night.”

Eleanor’s rooms were in a quiet wing of the house, and as she prepared for bed, she found she could not so easily dismiss the evening. Emma could attribute the discord to a simple clash of tempers and move on; but Eleanor, burdened with her new and unwelcome insight, felt the weight of it settle upon her. What a wretched business it was! To see a man of Mr. Knightley’s worth so entangled in a hopeless affection was a sorrow indeed; but to see the object of that affection, Miss Fairfax, so bound to a man who seemed to take pleasure in her pain was a matter of true tragedy. She did not, like Emma, believe Jane Fairfax to be perfect; no one who kept such a secret, and endured such public torment from its keeper, could be entirely without fault. But she saw in her a desperation that she was beginning to understand. Jane Fairfax was not merely in love; she was trapped.

The following morning brought with it a return to tranquility, at least on the surface. Mr. Woodhouse had heard a garbled account of the party from his daughter, which had focused principally on the dangers of evening air and the doubtful quality of Mrs. Bates’s seed-cake, and he was quite content. “So good of you to stay at home with your old father, my dear,” he said to Emma. “Parties are such unsettling things. I am sure poor Miss Fairfax has caught a terrible cold.”

“Mr. Churchill! So good of you to call. I hope you are not tired after your exertions. Emma tells me you were all at the Bateses’. A very worthy family, but their rooms are so small. I always fear for the draughts.”

“It is I who should fear, sir,” said Frank, approaching Mr. Woodhouse with a winning smile. “For I have come to throw myself on the mercy of the court at Hartfield. Emma, despite her lingering vexation, could not help but be pleased by this appeal. It was a drama tailored for her, positioning her as judge and confessor. She led him to the drawing-room, leaving her father to his gruel, with the promise that Mr. Churchill would not stay long enough to bring in a chill from the outside world.

“Now,” she said, once they were seated. “Explain yourself.”

“I cannot,” he declared, striking a pose of despair. “There is no explanation, only the sad truth of my own flawed character. I was in high spirits; I saw Miss Fairfax looking, as she always does, a little too serious; and I thought, in my folly, that a little gentle teasing might enliven her. It was a gross miscalculation. I am like a clumsy giant who does not know his own strength. When I saw the pain in her eyes—and, I might add, the thunder in Knightley’s—I knew I had gone too far.”

He watched her carefully as he spoke, measuring the effect of his words. The mention of Mr. Knightley was a masterstroke.

“Mr. Knightley was very severe,” Emma admitted.

“Severe! He was Jupiter hurling a thunderbolt. And quite right, too. I deserved it. To be reprimanded by a man of his standing—it is a lesson I shall not soon forget.” He leaned forward, his voice dropping to a confidential tone. “But tell me truly, Miss Woodhouse—you, whose judgment I value above all others—was I entirely to blame? Is there not something in Miss Fairfax’s manner, a kind of proud reserve, that invites misunderstanding? One feels she is always holding something back, guarding some secret.”

He had her. He was echoing her own thoughts, her own theories about Jane’s secret attachment to the married Mr. Dixon. He was not just apologising; he was reinforcing their alliance.

“She is certainly very reserved,” Emma allowed, feeling her censure of him melt away. “It makes her difficult to know.”

“Exactly! And one tries to break through the ice, and instead one falls into the river. I am properly chastened, I assure you. My only hope is that you do not think too badly of me.”

“I think you are too fond of amusement,” Emma said, her voice now gentle. “But I cannot believe there is any real harm in you.”

His victory was complete. He stayed for another half an hour, charming her with gossip from London, inquiring tenderly after her father, and behaving in all respects as the perfect, repentant friend. By the time he left, Emma had quite convinced herself that the incident had been a mere trifle, magnified by Jane Fairfax’s over-sensitivity and Mr. Knightley’s over-seriousness.

She was in the midst of this comfortable conclusion when another visitor was announced. It was Mr. Knightley himself.

His entrance had none of Frank Churchill’s easy grace. He seemed to bring the raw, damp air of the outdoors in with him, and his expression was as sober as the grey sky.

“Mr. Knightley,” said Emma, surprised. “Two visits in one morning. Hartfield is quite the centre of society today.”

“I have been to see the Bateses,” he said, declining her offer of a seat and taking up his customary position on the hearthrug. “I wished to inquire after Miss Fairfax.”

“And how is she?” Emma asked, a little defensively. “Has she survived the ordeal of being asked to play the pianoforte?”

He gave her a sharp look. “She has a headache and is keeping to her room. Miss Bates says she slept very little.”

“I am sorry to hear it,” Emma said, though her tone suggested it was only what one might expect from someone of Jane Fairfax’s delicate constitution.

A tense silence fell between them. Mr. Knightley seemed to be wrestling with something he wished to say. Emma, for her part, felt the return of that unpleasant sensation of being judged.

“Churchill has been here,” he stated, rather than asked.

“He has. He called to offer his apologies.”

“And you have accepted them, I suppose.”

“He was very penitent,” Emma replied, lifting her chin. “He sees that he was in the wrong.”

“Does he?” said Mr. Knightley, his voice laced with a skepticism that Emma found intensely irritating. “Or does he simply see that it is advantageous to appear penitent in your eyes? He plays a part, Emma. He plays it very well. But you must not mistake the performance for the man.”

“And you mistake gravity for virtue!” she retorted, her patience finally snapping. “You think that because Frank Churchill laughs and jests, he must be a villain. And because Jane Fairfax sighs and looks pale, she must be a saint. You are unjust, Mr. Knightley. You have always been unjust to him.”

“Have I?” He looked at her, and for a moment, the anger in his face was eclipsed by that same look of profound sorrow Eleanor had witnessed the night before. It unsettled Emma more than his anger. “Perhaps I have,” he said, his voice quiet again. “But I have seen men like him before. All charm, all surface. They take what they want, and they do not count the cost, because it is always others who are made to pay it. I had thought you, Emma, had better sense than to be taken in.”

He turned as if to leave, then hesitated. “I would ask one thing of you. Be kinder to Jane Fairfax. Your cleverness and Churchill’s wit make a formidable combination. Do not turn it upon a woman who is, for whatever reason, friendless and unhappy. It is unworthy of you.”

Without another word, he left the room. Emma stood frozen, her heart beating a painful tattoo against her ribs. Unworthy of you. The words echoed in the silent room. He had not shouted; he had not condemned her outright. He had, instead, appealed to her better self, and in doing so, had delivered a far more devastating blow than any lecture. For the first time, a sliver of doubt penetrated her formidable self-assurance. Could he be right? Was Frank Churchill’s charm a mask? Was Jane Fairfax’s reserve not pride, but pain? She remembered the flash of a secret smile on Jane’s face that she had once seen, and the blush that followed. She had interpreted it as delight in a forbidden love. Now, for the first time, she wondered if it might have been something else entirely; not the smile of a lover, but the grimace of one who is playing a part, just as Mr. Knightley had said. The harmony of her own mind, so recently restored, was once again broken, and this time, she feared, the discord ran deeper than she knew.

Emma, however, felt no inclination for the walk back to Hartfield. Mrs. Weston’s gentle presence would have been a comfort, and Mr. Weston’s cheerful nonsense a distraction, but she felt a pressing need for solitude that outweighed even the claims of politeness. To be free of society, even the society she most loved, seemed at that moment the greatest blessing imaginable.

She did not return to the drawing room, where the lingering scent of Mrs. Bates’s shawl and the disarray of the music still spoke of the morning’s company. Instead, she passed through the hall and into the garden, seeking the cool air and the familiar geometry of the shrubbery walks. The day was fine, one of those mild February days that offer a false promise of spring, yet Emma took no notice of it. Her mind, usually so alive to the pleasures of her own domain, was turned entirely inward, grappling with the harsh echo of Mr. Knightley’s parting words. _Unworthy of you._

He had judged her, and found her wanting. This was not, in itself, a novelty; Mr. Knightley had been judging her, and generally finding her wanting, for as long as she could remember. But this was different. There had been no teasing in his tone, none of the half-scolding, half-affectionate manner that usually characterised their disagreements. There had been only a grave, sorrowful disappointment, a look in his eyes that suggested she had not merely been foolish, which he could forgive, but that she had been unkind, which he seemed to find unpardonable.

And to be classed with Frank Churchill! To be told that their cleverness made a “formidable combination” to be used against a poor, defenceless woman. The injustice of it stung her. Was she not Jane Fairfax’s defender? Had she not, a hundred times, silenced the petty criticisms of others, praised her accomplishments, and insisted upon her elegance? It was true she did not like her; she found Jane’s reserve impenetrable and her composure irritating. But she had never been actively cruel. Or had she?

Her conscience, now thoroughly awakened, began to present a most unwelcome catalogue of evidence. Had not her own amusement at Frank Churchill’s pointed torments been a form of cruelty? She had perceived his meaning, had understood the double-edged nature of his compliments about the pianoforte and its mysterious donor, and she had done nothing to stop him. Indeed, she had been his accomplice, sharing whispered asides and amused glances. She had watched Jane Fairfax’s composure fray, seen the colour rise in her cheeks and the tremor in her hand, and had felt a thrill of satisfaction, a sense of being on the inside of a delightful secret. Now, seen through Mr. Knightley’s eyes, it was not delightful at all; it was vulgar. It was contemptible.

She paced the gravel path, her hands clasped tightly behind her. Could he be right about Frank Churchill? She tried to summon the young man to her mind’s eye, to see him not as the charming, devoted admirer she had imagined, but as the heartless performer Mr. Knightley described. She recalled his easy grace, the way he could make the most mundane event seem like an adventure, the flattering intensity of his gaze when he spoke to her. Surely that could not all be artifice. A man could not feign such warmth, such an open and engaging spirit. And yet… there was his strange, inexcusable behaviour regarding his visit; the sudden trip to London; the almost too-perfect apologies. There was his constant, playful, yet somehow relentless, persecution of Jane Fairfax. Was it truly play? Or was there a vein of malice beneath the polished surface?

The discord in her mind was excruciating. To believe Mr. Knightley was to admit not only that she had been unkind, but that she had been a fool, completely duped by a handsome face and a silver tongue. Her vanity, her formidable self-assurance, rose up in rebellion against such a conclusion. It was far more likely that Mr. Knightley was simply prejudiced. He was a man of sense, to be sure, and his principles were of the highest order; but he was also a creature of habit, and suspicious of novelty. Frank Churchill was all novelty, and so Mr. Knightley disliked him. It was as simple as that. And as for Jane Fairfax, he pitied her, and his pity had blinded him to the possibility that she was not a saint, but a woman of secrets and perhaps of faults, just like any other.

Having thus reasoned her way back to a state of precarious equilibrium, Emma resolved to think no more on the matter. She would be civil to Jane Fairfax, of course—more than civil, she would be kind. She would prove Mr. Knightley wrong by the sheer exemplary nature of her conduct. And she would continue to enjoy Frank Churchill’s company, though perhaps with a new, secret layer of observation. She would study him. She would be the dispassionate judge of his character, and she would arrive at the truth on her own terms, not on Mr. Knightley’s.

Feeling much restored by this resolution, she turned back towards the house, only to be met by her father, who was shuffling towards her down the path, shawled and hatted against the imaginary dangers of the air.

“My dear Emma,” he began, his face a picture of gentle anxiety. “I was afraid you had taken a chill. You were so hasty to go out after your headache. A headache is a very serious thing, you know. Mrs. Bates’s mother had a headache once that lasted for three days. Perry had to be sent for twice.”

“I am perfectly well now, Papa, I assure you. The air has done me good,” Emma said, forcing a cheerful smile and taking his arm.

“If you are sure, my dear. But the ground is damp. I am certain the ground is damp. You will ruin your shoes. And Mr. Knightley was so determined to go to Kingston! On such a day. I do think he is a little thoughtless of his own health. Kingston is a very draughty place.”

At the mention of Mr. Knightley, a flicker of the morning’s discomfort returned. “I dare say he will manage, Papa. He is not so delicate as we are.”

“No, indeed,” sighed Mr. Woodhouse. “Very few people are. It is a sad thing. But you, my dear, you must not overtire yourself. We have had a great deal of company. All those people in the little room at the Bateses. I cannot think how they managed. And then Mrs. Weston and Frank Churchill here… it is all very delightful, but it is a great deal.”

“Yes, Papa,” Emma agreed, her mind wandering again. A great deal, indeed.

They had nearly reached the house when they saw another figure approaching the drive: a small, neat figure in a plain pelisse, walking with a timid but determined air.

“Goodness,” said Mr. Woodhouse, peering. “Is that not Miss Smith?”

It was indeed Harriet Smith, come, as she so often did, to seek the wisdom and bask in the glory of her friend Miss Woodhouse. Emma’s heart sank a little. An hour with Harriet’s simple, earnest prattle was the very last thing she felt equal to just then. But it would be cruel to turn her away, and so she composed her features into a welcoming smile and went forward to meet her.

“Harriet! How good of you to call. You find us just returned from our own morning excursions. You must come in and rest.”

Harriet’s face lit up with pleasure. “Oh, Miss Woodhouse! I hoped I might find you. I would not for the world have disturbed you if you were busy.”

“Never too busy for you, Harriet,” Emma said, leading her inside and helping to divest her of her bonnet and shawl, while Mr. Woodhouse fussed about her, insisting she take the chair farthest from the door and closest to the fire.

Once they were settled, and Mr. Woodhouse had been satisfied with a glass of his particular sweet wine and a slice of seed cake, the conversation, as Emma had dreaded, turned immediately to the events of the morning.

“Oh, Miss Woodhouse,” Harriet began, her blue eyes wide with remembered delight. “Was it not the most wonderful morning? To hear the new pianoforte! It has such a sweet tone. And Miss Fairfax plays so beautifully.”

“She does,” said Emma, with careful neutrality. “She has had the very best masters.”

“And Mr. Churchill!” Harriet continued, a slight blush rising in her cheeks. “How very droll he is! The things he said about the spectacles, and making the rivet firm! I declare I almost laughed aloud, but I was afraid it would be improper.”

“Mr. Churchill has a lively sense of humour,” Emma conceded, her eyes on her needlework. She was determined to be observant, even with Harriet.

“And he is so very handsome, is he not?” Harriet sighed. “The way he looks at you, Miss Woodhouse. Everyone must see how much he admires you.”

This, in former times, would have been the sweetest music to Emma’s ears. Now, it grated. Was her supposed conquest of Frank Churchill a matter of such common gossip that even simple Harriet had taken note of it? And was it admiration he showed, or was it a performance for the benefit of the room? “Mr. Churchill is very gallant,” she said coolly. “It is his way. I should not read too much into it.”

Harriet looked a little crestfallen, as if she had been gently reproved. “Oh! No, of course. It is only… you and he seem so perfectly matched. When you were looking over the music together, you made such a handsome couple.”

“We were merely looking at Cramer,” Emma said, with more sharpness than she intended. “There is nothing very romantic in that.”

A short silence fell. Harriet, sensing she had somehow displeased her oracle, looked down at her hands. Emma felt a pang of guilt. It was not Harriet’s fault that her own mind was in such a turmoil. She tried a kinder tone.

Harriet’s face brightened at once. “Oh, yes! More than I can say. Though I was so very nervous when Mr. Knightley asked me. I was sure I would tread on his toes or forget the figures.”

Emma listened to this tribute with a strange mixture of feelings. She was pleased, of course, that Mr. Knightley had been kind to her protégée. It was just what she would have expected of him. Yet there was something in Harriet’s earnest, slightly breathless praise that she found… unsettling.

“Mr. Knightley has always been a friend to you, Harriet,” she said.

“Oh, the very best friend!” Harriet declared. “Except for you, of course, Miss Woodhouse. After the… the unpleasantness with Mr. Elton, he was so very considerate. I saw him this morning, you know, when Miss Bates called to him from the window. He was so very particular in asking after Miss Fairfax, but he looked up at the window where we were all sitting too. I am sure he saw me. He has a very kind look in his eyes, do not you think?”

Emma did not answer immediately. She was picturing the scene: Mr. Knightley on his horse, his gaze directed upwards. She had assumed, everyone had assumed, his concern was entirely for Jane Fairfax. But Harriet had felt herself included in his notice. The thought was absurd. Harriet was a sweet girl, but for her to imagine any particular regard from Mr. Knightley was the height of folly. It was Emma’s own fault, she supposed, for filling the girl’s head with romantic notions. She had taught Harriet to look for signs and meanings where there were none, and now the poor girl was seeing them everywhere.

“Mr. Knightley is kind to everyone, Harriet,” she said, her voice more didactic than she intended. “He is the master of Donwell Abbey and feels a responsibility for the whole neighbourhood. We must not mistake a general benevolence for a particular partiality.” She winced inwardly as she heard the pompous echo of her own past lectures on Mr. Elton.

“Oh, no, I am sure I do not,” Harriet said hastily, blushing again, this time with what looked like confusion. “It is only… he is so very good. Not so witty and fashionable as Mr. Churchill, perhaps, but there is a goodness about him that… that one can rely on.”

_A goodness one can rely on._ The phrase struck Emma with unexpected force. It was the perfect description of Mr. Knightley. It was the very quality that made his disapproval so painful. Frank Churchill was a summer’s day; Mr. Knightley was the solid earth beneath one’s feet. And she, in her folly, had been ready to trade the one for the other.

She looked at Harriet, truly looked at her, and for the first time saw not a project or a pupil, but a young woman with a heart of her own, a heart that was perhaps more discerning in its simplicity than Emma’s was in all its cleverness. Harriet, left to her own devices, would never have aspired to Mr. Elton. She had been content to admire Mr. Martin. And now, she was admiring Mr. Knightley. Both were men of sense and substance. It seemed Harriet had a natural inclination for worth, an inclination that Emma had done her very best to corrupt.

The harmony of her mind, which she had so painstakingly tried to restore in the garden, was now utterly shattered. The doubt Mr. Knightley had planted had taken root, and Harriet, in her complete innocence, had watered it. Emma felt a wave of vexation so strong it was almost despair. She was vexed with Frank Churchill for his ambiguity, vexed with Jane Fairfax for her infuriating reserve, vexed with Mr. Knightley for being so stubbornly, inconveniently right, and vexed with Harriet for holding up a mirror to her own misguided meddling. But most of all, she was vexed with herself. For she was beginning to suspect that she, Emma Woodhouse, who had thought she understood the workings of every heart in Highbury, understood nothing at all.

She, Emma Woodhouse, had been the dupe of a young man whose principal object, she was now convinced, was to amuse himself, and to carry on a secret correspondence of the heart under the very noses of the whole society of Highbury. The headaches, the variable spirits, the weary looks at the dance; they were not the effects of a delicate constitution alone, but the inevitable anxieties of a clandestine engagement. The blush and the secret smile that Emma had witnessed were not for an absent Mr. Dixon, but for the present, persecuting, beloved Mr. Churchill. Jane Fairfax was engaged, secretly engaged, and to a man who was paying devoted court to another. How reprehensible indeed! Emma had actively sought to manipulate destinies; Jane had merely hidden her own. The comparison brought a fresh wave of colour to Emma’s cheeks, though there was no one to witness it.

Then there was Mr. Knightley. Here her vexation was of a different order entirely. It was the vexation of being utterly in the wrong, and of knowing that he had been consistently, provokingly in the right. His warnings about Frank Churchill, which she had dismissed as the prejudices of a man unused to the polished manners of London, now sounded in her mind with the clarity of prophecy. His solicitude for Jane Fairfax, which she had interpreted as a nascent romantic interest, a notion that had unaccountably displeased her, was nothing more than his usual clear-sighted benevolence. He had always been her superior in judgment. She had known it, in a theoretical way, all her life. She felt humbled, and she did not like the feeling. It was as if she had been playing a game of chess, moving her pieces with what she believed was brilliant strategy, only to look up and find that her opponent had been observing a different board entirely, a board on which the real game, the one of true consequence, was being played.

Her thoughts circled back, inevitably, to Harriet. _A goodness one can rely on._ Harriet had said it of Mr. Knightley. Simple, unassuming, yet perfectly true. Harriet, whom she had deemed a sweet-natured simpleton, possessed a sort of instinct for genuine worth that she, with all her cleverness, had utterly lacked. The mortification was complete. “My dear, I was just telling Mr. Perry that you were a little fatigued by your walk. He quite agrees with me that these morning visits may be over-long. Mr. Perry, a man whose profession it was to agree with the master of Hartfield, bowed and confirmed this diagnosis. “A little quiet, a little rest, Miss Woodhouse. We cannot have our bloom deserting us.”
Emma assured them both that she was perfectly well, and that her bloom would undoubtedly return after luncheon; but she was grateful when her father’s anxieties led him to his armchair by the fire, allowing her to escape to the quiet of her own apartment.

She did not reappear until the family assembled for dinner. She had resolved to be more cheerful, or at least more tranquil. She had made her mistakes, but she was Emma Woodhouse; she would not be long vanquished by them. Her new course was clear: she would practice the simple art of observation. She would meddle no more. She would watch, and she would learn, and she would hold her tongue. It was a resolution so novel, and so contrary to her nature, that it gave her a momentary sense of purpose.

Her resolve was to be tested sooner than she imagined. The very next afternoon, as she was cutting flowers in the shrubbery, she saw Mr. Knightley himself entering the gates. Not on horseback this time, but on foot, having walked over from Donwell. Her first instinct was to retreat into the house, but he had already seen her, and altered his course to join her among the roses.
“A fine afternoon for your work,” he said, his eyes taking in her basket of flowers and her slightly disordered hair with an expression she could not quite decipher. It was not his usual look of brotherly approval; there was a shade of enquiry in it.
“Very fine,” she replied, busying herself with a particularly stubborn rose stem. “I did not expect to see you again so soon. Is all well at Donwell?”

He said the last word with a slight emphasis, and looked at her directly. Emma felt the colour rise in her cheeks. She snipped the rose with a vicious snap of her scissors.
“Mr. Churchill is very fond of such games,” she said, striving for a tone of indifference. “It is the way of the world he is used to, I suppose.”
“Is it?” said Mr. Knightley. “I should not have thought it the way of any world to cause a lady distress for one’s own amusement. Miss Fairfax did not seem to me to be enjoying the game.”
Here it was. The censure she had been dreading. And she could not, in good conscience, contradict it.
“No,” she said, her voice lower than she intended. “No, she did not.”

She could not bring herself to say more. She could not expose her new suspicions, which were still too raw and too humiliating. To admit to Mr. Knightley that she believed Frank Churchill and Jane Fairfax to be secretly in love would be to admit the full extent of her own blindness. “Ah, Knightley! Very good of you to walk all this way. I hope you are not heated. Emma, my dear, is there not a draft from that window? Perhaps Mr. Knightley would feel it.”

Emma’s newfound peace was instantly shattered. The compliment, so casually delivered, struck her with the force of a blow. It was precisely what he would say of a woman he was beginning to notice. A woman who was not clever, or accomplished, or of his own rank, but who was good, and unassuming, and pretty. All the things Harriet was. All the things Emma had tried to teach her to overcome.
“Yes,” she managed to say. “She is a very good girl.”
“She is,” he agreed, and with a final nod to her and her father, he was gone.

Emma stood motionless for a full minute after the door had closed. The monster she had been trying to keep at bay had just been fed. Harriet and Mr. Knightley. The idea had been absurd, a fleeting, foolish fancy. But Mr. Knightley himself had just given it substance. ‘A very modest, pleasing countenance.’ He, who never gave unmerited praise. He had noticed Harriet. He approved of her.
A cold dread, entirely new and deeply alarming, began to creep over her. Her mortification over Frank Churchill, her shame at her own meddling, all of it paled in comparison to this new, unimagined horror. It was one thing to be wrong about Frank Churchill’s heart; it would be quite another to be right about Harriet’s and Mr. Knightley’s. She had resigned herself to no longer being the first object of interest to a fashionable young man from London. She had been vexed with herself for her folly; but this new feeling was not vexation. It was a sharp and jealous pain. And as she stood there, staring at the door through which he had just departed, Emma Woodhouse began to suspect that in her grand project of understanding every heart in Highbury, the one she had understood least of all was her own.

The sound, when it came, was so precisely what she had been dreading that it seemed a thing of her own fevered imagination; the steady, familiar crunch of a horse’s hooves on the gravel of the drive. Her heart, which had been beating so uncomfortably, gave one great leap and then seemed to cease altogether. Her father, however, heard it with uncomplicated pleasure. “There, my love! That must be him. That is Mr. Knightley. I should have been quite alarmed had he been any later; the night air is beginning to grow so very damp. I do hope he has not taken a chill.”

Serle announced him, and he entered the room, bringing with him a welcome breath of the cool evening and an air of such robust, untroubled sense that Emma’s own turmoil felt, for a moment, like a foolish malady. He was looking remarkably well; his ride had given him colour, and his eyes, as they met hers, held their usual keen, intelligent light. It was a look she had basked in, argued with, and fled from her entire life; now, it was a look she could scarcely bear.

“Mr. Knightley,” said Mr. Woodhouse, extending a feeble hand. “So good of you to come. But you must be tired after your journey. I am sure you must. Emma, my dear, is not Mr. Knightley looking fatigued?”

“On the contrary, papa, I think he looks very well,” she managed to say, her voice sounding thin and unfamiliar in her own ears. She busied herself with the tea things, an occupation she generally left to the servants but which now offered a welcome shield for her face and a purpose for her trembling hands.

“Fatigued? Not in the least, I thank you, sir,” said Mr. Knightley, his gaze moving from Mr. Woodhouse to rest on Emma’s bent head. “The ride was pleasant, and my business in Kingston dispatched with more efficiency than I had dared to hope. I met Weston on the road, who told me Frank Churchill is expected to depart for Yorkshire very soon. It seems his aunt’s health is perpetually in a state of convenient crisis.” There was a dry note in his voice that would normally have prompted a rallying reply from Emma, a defence of the charming Mr. Churchill, however insincere. Tonight, she said nothing. She merely continued her careful arrangement of the cups.

A small silence fell. It was not a comfortable one. Mr. Knightley looked at her, a question forming in his expression. He seemed about to speak directly to her, to challenge her quietness, when her father, oblivious, rushed to fill the void.

“Yorkshire! So very far away. And the sea air there can be so treacherous. Poor Mrs. Churchill. She requires a great deal of care, I am sure. I hope he takes a flask of warm gruel with him for the journey. There is nothing so settling as gruel. Emma, my love, you must remind me to send the recipe to Mrs. Weston.”

“Yes, papa,” was all Emma could reply.

Dinner was announced, and the meal that followed was, for Emma, an exercise in the most refined torture. Every word Mr. Knightley spoke seemed freighted with a new and terrible significance. When he spoke of his tenants, of their wants and their welfare, she saw only the benevolent master of Donwell, the man so fit to be a husband and a guide. When he inquired, with his customary kindness, after the health of her old governess, she heard the voice that had soothed Harriet’s fears. And when he turned to her, his brow slightly furrowed with concern, and said, “You are uncommonly quiet this evening, Emma. Is anything amiss?” she felt a blush of such painful heat rise to her cheeks that she was sure it must betray everything.

“I am a little tired, I believe,” she said, forcing a smile that felt like a grimace. “Miss Smith’s visit, perhaps, as papa suggested. We had a great deal to talk of.”

“Ah, yes. Miss Smith,” he said, and Emma’s blood ran cold. But he did not continue. He did not mention the gipsies, or the rescue, or anything that had passed. He merely looked at her for a moment longer with that same inquiring gravity, and then turned his attention to carving the chicken for her father. The reprieve was so immense it was almost as painful as the suspense had been. She had wanted to know his thoughts, to gauge his feelings, and yet, the moment the topic of Harriet was approached, she had recoiled in terror. She was a coward. She had resolved to fight, but at the first sight of the enemy—or rather, of the prize—she had laid down her arms and hidden behind the tea-tray.

When he took his leave, it was with a parting glance at her that was full of a gentle, puzzled concern. “You must take care of yourself, Emma,” he said, his voice softer than it had been all evening. “Do not allow the troubles of all Highbury to weigh upon you so.”

The kindness of it was her undoing. The moment the door closed behind him, the composure she had so painfully maintained shattered completely. She wished her father a hasty good-night, pleading a headache, and fled to the sanctuary of her room. But there was no sanctuary to be found. Her own mind was a place of torment, replaying every look, every word, every missed opportunity of the evening. His concern was not that of a lover; it was the affectionate, almost brotherly anxiety he had always shown her. And Harriet? He had not mentioned her name with any particular warmth or interest. Perhaps there was no immediate danger. Perhaps she had imagined it all.

But the relief this thought might have offered was instantly consumed by another, more dreadful one. If he was not thinking of Harriet, he was most certainly not thinking of her either. He saw her as a friend, a neighbour, a slightly spoiled young woman to be advised and corrected. The change must come from her. She had been passive and foolish; now she must be active, however reprehensible the action.

The next morning found her resolve hardened into a grim certainty. She would not allow herself the luxury of hesitation. After breakfast, a meal she barely touched, she announced her intention to walk into the village.

“To the village, my love?” said her father, looking up from his toast with alarm. “But the ground will be damp. And you said you had a headache. I cannot have you taking a chill. Why do you not send one of the servants?”

“The air will do me good, papa. My headache is quite gone, and I shall wear my stoutest boots. I only mean to call at Mrs. Goddard’s for a moment.”

She knew this would not entirely soothe him, but she could not be deterred. She needed to see Harriet. She needed to begin her terrible work. Every moment she delayed was a moment in which Harriet’s tender hopes might take deeper root, nurtured by the memory of a gentleman’s kindness.

The walk, beneath a sky of watery, uncertain blue, did nothing to calm the agitation of her spirit. She felt like a general marching to a battle she was ashamed to fight, armed with weapons she despised. She was going to wound a gentle, loving heart, to trample on a delicate affection, all for her own selfish ends. She thought of Frank Churchill’s deception, his secret engagement to Jane Fairfax while trifling with her own feelings, and for the first time, she felt a flicker not of anger, but of a horrid, shared understanding. Was she not about to engage in a deception of her own, a calculated manipulation of a friend’s affections? The comparison sickened her, yet she walked on.

She found Harriet in the small parlour at Mrs. Goddard’s, not occupied with any useful work, but sitting by the window, a piece of half-finished embroidery lying forgotten in her lap. Her expression was one of dreamy abstraction, and a soft, happy smile played about her lips. She started when Emma entered, her cheeks flushing with pleasure.

“Miss Woodhouse! I did not dare to hope I should see you again so soon. This is such a kindness!”

Emma returned the greeting, forcing herself to appear composed. “I was taking a walk, Harriet, and thought I would see how you were. You have recovered from your fright of two days ago, I trust?”

Harriet’s blush deepened, and her eyes shone. “Oh, yes! Entirely. I believe I was very foolish to be so very frightened. And so very, very fortunate.” She looked down at her hands, her smile returning. “I can think of nothing else. To have been rescued in such a way! It was like something from a book.”

“It was a very alarming experience, and you were very brave,” Emma said, choosing her words with a dreadful precision. “And Mr. Knightley, of course, acted just as a gentleman ought.”

“Oh, he was more than a gentleman!” Harriet exclaimed, her voice full of fervent adoration. “He was—he was everything that is good and kind! The way he spoke to those horrid people, so firm and commanding, and then his gentleness to me... Miss Woodhouse, I shall never, ever forget it.”

Here was the opening, as clear and as terrible as a chasm at her feet. Emma took a breath. “His kindness is indeed very great, Harriet. It is a part of his nature. He is a friend to all the parish. I have known him to be just as concerned for the welfare of old Mrs. Bates, or for one of the farmer’s children who has taken a fall. It is his way. He cannot see distress, in any quarter, without wishing to alleviate it.”

She watched Harriet’s face. A little of the radiant happiness faded, replaced by a look of slight confusion. “Yes,” she said, more slowly. “Yes, of course. He is so very good.”

“It is a universal benevolence,” Emma continued, hating the sound of her own calm, pedantic voice. “One must be careful not to mistake it. A man of Mr. Knightley’s position and character feels a sense of duty to everyone in his sphere. To be kind to those less fortunate, to protect a woman in distress—it is as natural to him as breathing. He would have done the same for any young woman of Highbury whom he happened to find in such a predicament.”

The confusion in Harriet’s eyes was now tinged with a faint, dawning hurt. “For anyone?” she repeated, her voice small.

“But of course, my dear,” Emma said, with a dreadful, bright cheerfulness. “Can you imagine Mr. Knightley doing any less? Can you imagine him riding past and leaving you to the mercy of those dreadful people? It is not in his nature. It has nothing to do with the particular person, and everything to do with his own principles. That is what makes him Mr. Knightley.”

She was, she knew, presenting a perfectly logical and admirable portrait of their friend. She was also, with every word, draining the colour and hope from Harriet’s world. She was taking the unique, magical event that Harriet had been cherishing and recasting it as a commonplace act of public duty.

Harriet was silent for a long moment, twisting the ribbon on her abandoned needlework. “But—but you thought—,” she began, then stopped. “You always said...”

“I have been a great fool, Harriet,” Emma broke in, seizing the opportunity to condemn her former self, a far more comfortable task than directly wounding her friend. “My judgment has been shockingly in error, as you have reason to know. I fancied I could see things that were not there. I have been arrogant and presumptuous. My only excuse, and it is a poor one, is that I wished for your happiness. But I have been a very poor guide. You must not listen to my fancies any longer. You must listen to reason.”

“Reason?” Harriet whispered.

“Yes. And reason tells us that there are certain distinctions in society which, while we may not always like them, we cannot simply ignore. Mr. Knightley is the master of Donwell Abbey; he is of the first rank of society in this part of the country. His friends, his connections, his entire world—it is all of a piece with that. A woman who marries him must be his equal, not only in sense and education, but in birth and consequence.”

It was the most brutal, the most direct contradiction of everything she had spent months teaching Harriet to believe. She was tearing down the very edifice she had so proudly built, stone by painful stone.

Harriet looked up, and Emma was shocked to see that her eyes were full of tears. One spilled over and traced a slow path down her cheek. “But you said none of that mattered,” she said, her voice trembling. “You said elegance of mind was what was important. You said I was your friend, and that I was fit for any gentleman.”

“And I was wrong, Harriet,” Emma said, her own heart aching with a mixture of pity and shame. She reached out and took Harriet’s hand, a gesture of comfort that felt like the grossest hypocrisy. “I was wrong. I let my affection for you cloud my judgment. I have done you a great disservice. My friendship has been a curse to you, not a blessing. I wanted to see you elevated, and instead I have only made you unhappy. Forgive me.”

The plea for forgiveness was genuine, the only truly honest thing she had said all morning. But Harriet, in her simple, wounded misery, could not see the nuance. She only saw her friend, her guide, her oracle, suddenly and inexplicably turning on her, dismantling her hopes, and calling her foolish for ever having entertained them. She pulled her hand away.

“I do not understand,” she said, the tears now flowing freely. “Yesterday, you were happy for me. Yesterday, everything was full of hope. And now... now you say it was all nothing. You say he thinks of me no more than—than old Mrs. Bates!”

The comparison, which Emma herself had supplied, struck Harriet with its full, humiliating force, and she broke into a quiet, desolate sob. Emma stood, helpless and miserable. She had succeeded. She had planted the seeds of doubt and disillusionment. She had begun her dreadful work. But the sight of Harriet’s pain was almost more than she could bear. Every decent instinct screamed at her to retract her words, to comfort her friend, to restore the hope she had so cruelly extinguished.

But the image of Mr. Knightley, his kind, concerned face as he had left her the night before, rose in her mind. The thought of losing him, of seeing him bestow that kindness permanently on another, was a terror that outweighed even her present self-loathing. She could not go back. She had set her course.

“I am sorry, Harriet,” she said softly, her voice barely audible. “I am truly sorry. I must go. My father will be expecting me.”

She left the room without another word, not daring to look back at the small, weeping figure by the window. She walked out of Mrs. Goddard’s and into the Highbury street, feeling like a criminal escaping the scene of her crime. She had done it. She had behaved reprehensibly. And as she hurried back towards the cold, stately comfort of Hartfield, she did not know which was the greater misery: the pain she had inflicted, or the chilling certainty that, given the chance, she would do it all over again.

The short walk from Mrs. Goddard’s to the gates of Hartfield had never seemed so long, nor the familiar aspect of her home so accusing. Each stone in the wall, each neatly trimmed shrub, seemed to stare at her with the quiet condemnation of long and faithful service. They were part of a life of propriety, ease, and unblemished good standing; a life from which she had just taken a shocking departure. She, Emma Woodhouse, who had always prided herself on her integrity, her benevolent intentions, however faulty their execution, had acted with the deliberate, cold-hearted malice of a villain in a novel. The thought was so unwelcome, so utterly alien to her conception of herself, that she physically recoiled from it, pausing for a moment on the gravel path. But the image of Harriet’s tear-stained face was not to be so easily dismissed; it was imprinted upon her mind’s eye with a terrible clarity, a portrait of the ruin she had wrought.

She entered the house, divesting herself of her bonnet and pelisse with a mechanical stillness, hoping to glide unobserved to the sanctuary of her own room. Her plan, however, was immediately thwarted by the appearance of her father, who was making a slow and cautious pilgrimage from the drawing-room to the breakfast-parlour.

“Emma, my dear! There you are. I was beginning to be uneasy. Perry said the air had a dampness to it this morning. You have not taken a chill, I hope? You look a little pale.”

“Not at all, Papa, I thank you,” she replied, forcing her features into a smile that felt a grotesque parody of her usual cheerfulness. “The air is quite refreshing, I assure you. I was only taking a short walk.”

“To Mrs. Goddard’s, I suppose,” he said, with a little sigh of resignation. He held a very low opinion of the intellectual benefits of Mrs. Goddard’s school, but a very high one of the risks of its draughty passages. “Well, I hope you found them all well. So many young people crowded together; it cannot be very wholesome. And little Miss Smith—you did not sit near an open window with her, I trust?”

“We were very careful, Papa. And everyone is perfectly well.” Everyone but Harriet, whose wellness she had just shattered. The lie came easily, too easily, and she felt another tremor of self-disgust. To soothe her father’s anxieties, she offered him her arm, and they completed his journey together, his gentle, fretful monologue on the perils of morning walks and the virtues of gruel providing a bizarre counterpoint to the tempest in her own soul.

She settled him in his favourite chair with his newspaper, and made her escape at last. In the drawing-room, she sat down at her table, took out her portfolio, and attempted to busy herself with the selection of a new head for her collection of portraits. But the faces of her acquaintance swam before her, and all of them seemed to wear expressions of judgment. She saw Mrs. Weston’s kind disappointment, Mr. Knightley’s stern disapproval. With a shudder, she pushed the portfolio away. She could not draw; she could not read; she could only think, and her thoughts were the worst possible company. She had done a terrible thing, and for a terrible reason: for the hope of securing the affections of a man who had likely never thought of her as anything more than a spoiled child in need of correction. And what if her scheme should fail? What if, after all this, he married Jane Fairfax? Or some unknown lady from London? Then she would be left with nothing but her guilt, and the ruin of two friendships—her honest, if unequal, friendship with Harriet, and her long, invaluable friendship with him. The risk was appalling; the misery, certain. Yet the alternative, the vision of Mr. Knightley taking Harriet’s hand in his, congratulating her on her good fortune, was so intolerable that it justified, in a dark corner of her heart, any measure taken to prevent it.

She was startled from this wretched reverie by the sound of the door-bell. A visitor. Her first instinct was to flee, to claim a headache and retire to her room. She was in no state to receive company; she could not trust herself to speak or to listen with even the semblance of composure. As she hesitated, James entered the room.

“Mr. Knightley, ma’am, to wait upon you and Mr. Woodhouse.”

It was as if the world had stopped turning. Mr. Knightley. Here. Now. A wave of panic, so intense it was almost sickness, washed over her, followed by a frantic, desperate hope. He had come. Had he come for her? Did he guess? Or was this merely a casual call, a neighbourly duty that would turn into an instrument of her torture?

“Show Mr. Knightley in, James,” she said, and was amazed at the steadiness of her own voice. She rose and stood by the fireplace, one hand resting on the mantelpiece, hoping the pose suggested a graceful nonchalance she was a thousand miles from feeling.

He entered, and the room seemed at once smaller and more vividly alive. He was all health and good sense, his cheeks fresh from the outside air, his eyes clear and direct. He paid his respects first, as always, to her.

“Emma. I was not sure I should find you at home. I thought you might be out enjoying this fine morning before the weather turns.”

“I have already had my walk,” she replied, her heart beating a frantic rhythm against her ribs. “And now I am enjoying the comforts of being indoors. My father is in the breakfast-parlour, if you wish to see him.”

“I shall see him presently. It was you I particularly wished to speak with.”

Her breath caught in her throat. This was it. This was the confrontation she had dreaded and longed for. She gestured towards a chair, and took one opposite, her hands clasped tightly in her lap to still their trembling.

“I have come from Donwell,” he began, settling himself with an air of purpose. “And on my way here, I passed Robert Martin. He was returning from the Crown, where he had gone to settle a bill, and he wished me to convey his thanks to you.”

“To me?” said Emma, bewildered. “For what should he be thanking me?”

“For your great kindness to his sister, it seems. Mrs. Martin was taken ill two days ago—nothing serious, a heavy cold—and you were so good as to send a pot of your own special preserve from Hartfield, and to inquire after her yesterday. He was most gratified. He said it was a mark of attention they should not soon forget.”

Emma stared at him, her mind a blank. The preserve, the inquiry—it had been the work of five minutes, an automatic gesture of charity, carried out by a servant and forgotten almost as soon as it was ordered. She had been thinking only of Harriet, of Mr. Elton, of a thousand foolish things, when she performed this small, thoughtless act of neighbourhood civility. And now, for this she was being thanked, while the morning’s true and significant labour, the deliberate crushing of a human heart, remained her guilty secret. The irony was so profound, so bitter, that she could have wept.

“It was nothing,” she said, a faint blush rising to her cheeks. “A common courtesy. I am glad to hear Mrs. Martin is recovering.”

“He was very earnest in his gratitude,” Mr. Knightley continued, his gaze fixed upon her with an expression she could not quite decipher. It was not his usual look of indulgent reproof, nor was it simple friendliness. There was a warmth in it, a softness she had seldom seen. “He spoke of your goodness of heart. I confess, I was pleased to hear it. It confirmed an opinion I have long held, even when we have been at variance.” He paused, and then added, in a lower voice, “You have a better heart than you will sometimes allow yourself to show, Emma.”

This was too much. To be praised for her goodness of heart, on this day of all days, by the very man for whose sake she had just forfeited her claim to any such quality—it was a refinement of agony she had not anticipated. Her composure, so painstakingly assembled, began to crumble. She looked away, unable to meet his eye.

“You are mistaken in me, Mr. Knightley,” she said, her voice strained. “You are very much mistaken.”

“Am I?” He leaned forward slightly. “I do not think so. I saw you yesterday, at Mrs. Bates’s. I saw you endure Frank Churchill’s nonsense with a patience I could not have mustered. And I saw you glance towards Miss Fairfax with what looked very much like compassion. You are not as unfeeling as you would sometimes have us believe.”

He was determined to misunderstand her, to paint her in colours of virtue she had just proved she did not possess. Every word of praise was a fresh stab of guilt. She had to change the subject, to divert him from this perilous course of commendation.

“My feelings about Jane Fairfax are of no consequence,” she said, a little sharply. “She has her secrets, and I have my conjectures. It is a tiresome business. Did you find your ride to Kingston satisfactory yesterday?”

He seemed a little surprised by her abruptness, but answered readily enough. “Perfectly. The business was concluded more quickly than I had hoped. It gave me time for some reflection on my way home.” He hesitated, as if considering his next words. “Emma, I hope you will not think me impertinent. I know your friendship with Miss Smith is a very particular one. I would not intrude for the world. But I happened to see her at her window as I rode past Mrs. Goddard’s not half an hour ago. She looked… deeply distressed.”

The blood drained from Emma’s face. He had seen her. He had seen the result of her work. Her carefully constructed facade of indifference was gone, replaced by a stark, undeniable terror.

“Did she?” she managed to say, her mouth suddenly dry.

“She did,” he confirmed, his brow furrowed with concern. “I was so struck by her appearance that I almost dismounted to inquire if she were ill. But it did not seem my place. You had just left the house; I saw you walking up the street. I trust nothing is amiss? Has she had some bad news?”

Here was her moment, her trial by fire. Her mind raced, sifting through a dozen possible lies and evasions. She could feign ignorance, but he had seen her leaving; the connection was too plain. She must be bold. She must take the narrative and shape it to her own ends.

She took a deep breath, and met his concerned gaze with a look of manufactured gravity and confidence. “No, not bad news, Mr. Knightley. On the contrary. I believe one might call it the very best sort of news in the long run. Harriet has been coming to a realization. A very sensible, and I must say, very necessary realization.”

“Indeed?” he said, his attention now fully engaged. “And what is that?”

“She has been reflecting,” Emma continued, choosing her words with the care of a general positioning his troops, “upon the… the folly of certain youthful fancies. We have been talking this morning, she and I. A great deal of nonsense has been cleared away. She has come to see that an attachment she was in danger of forming was not only unwise, but quite impossible. She sees it all now in its proper light.”

“Does she indeed?” Mr. Knightley looked at her, then down at his hands, then back at her. His expression was one of intense concentration. “The attachment to Mr. Elton, you mean? But I thought that was long past. I was under the impression there was some new… idea.”

“Ideas are like weeds, are they not?” Emma said, affecting a light, philosophical tone. “They spring up where they are least expected, and must be rooted out before they choke the entire garden. Harriet’s imagination is a very fertile ground. But she has, I believe, resolved to cultivate it more carefully in future. She understands now that one must look to one’s own sphere, and not entertain hopes that are… entirely out of keeping with one’s situation.” She delivered this last phrase, a cruel echo of her own words to Harriet, with a sense of grim purpose. She was burning her bridges, and she wanted him to see the flames.

Mr. Knightley was silent for a long moment. He seemed to be weighing her words, examining them from every angle. Emma held her breath. Had she been too obvious? Too cruel? Would he see through her, and despise her for the puppet-master she was?

When he finally spoke, his voice was unexpectedly gentle. “And this realization has caused her the distress I witnessed?”

“A momentary pang, perhaps,” Emma conceded, daring to hope. “It is never pleasant to give up a cherished dream, however foolish. But the pain will pass. It is the sting of a necessary cure. She is a good girl, a sensible girl at heart. She will be happier for it in the end. I am convinced of it.”

“A sensible girl,” he repeated thoughtfully. “Yes. Yes, she is. And you have been a true friend to her, Emma, in helping her to find her sense. A truer friend than you were, perhaps, in that business with Elton.”

The relief was so sudden, so overwhelming, that Emma felt almost faint. He did not suspect. He had not only accepted her account, he had approved of it. He was praising her for the very act that she herself had labelled a crime. The world had turned upside-down.

“I learned my lesson from that affair,” she said, her voice now much more confident. “I hope I shall not meddle so foolishly again. My only wish was to see Harriet settled in her own mind.”

“And you have succeeded,” he said, and he smiled. It was not a wide smile, but it was genuine, and it was directed at her with an warmth that made her treacherous heart leap with triumph. “You have done the right thing. It is a difficult path, to guide a friend towards a painful truth, but it is the only honourable one. I am glad you had the courage to walk it.” He rose from his chair. “Well. I have taken up enough of your time. I shall just say a word to your father and be on my way.”

She rose as well, a little unsteadily. “You are not leaving so soon?”

“I must. There is business at the Abbey farm that will not wait. But I am very glad I called. Very glad indeed.” He held her gaze for a moment longer, a look of such undisguised approval and affection that Emma felt a dizzying surge of joy, so potent it almost erased the memory of Harriet’s tears. Almost.

He left her then, and she sank back into her chair, her mind a whirlwind of conflicting emotions. She had won. She had faced the lion in his den, and she had tamed him with a story. She had turned her great sin into a virtue in his eyes. He approved of her; he admired her; he had looked at her as she had always dreamed he might look. The prize was within her grasp.

But as the initial euphoria began to fade, the chill returned. She had lied to him. She had built this new, hopeful foundation with him upon a bedrock of deceit. And in the drawing-room at Mrs. Goddard’s, a simple-hearted girl was weeping, her dreams shattered not by the gentle hand of a guiding friend, but by the cold, calculated blow of a rival. Emma had secured her ground, for the moment. But she had a dreadful, sinking feeling that the cost of this victory was one she would be paying for a very long time to come.

Emma remained in her chair, the echo of his approbation a sweet and venomous music in her ears. He was gone; the interview was over; and she had, by all accounts, emerged victorious. She had not only preserved his good opinion, she had enhanced it. He, who was so often her censor, her judge, her superior in all matters of sense and conduct, had pronounced her actions wise and her motives pure. He had called her a true friend. The words reverberated in the silent room, a testament to her cleverness, her dexterity, her complete and utter fraud.

A shudder passed through her. It was not of cold, for the fire burned brightly, but of a deep, internal chill that the finest hearth in Surrey could not hope to dispel. The triumph, which had for a moment seemed as solid and brilliant as a diamond, now appeared a thing of glass, fragile and sharp, which she dared not hold too tightly for fear of it shattering and cutting her to the bone. She had lied to him. Not a small, social falsehood, a little white lie to spare a feeling or smooth an awkward moment, but a great, structural lie, a falsehood that altered the very architecture of their friendship. She had taken his trust, his earnest and open nature, and used it as a shield for her own vanity and misjudgement.

“My dear, are you quite well?”

Emma started. Her father had entered the room so quietly she had not perceived him. He stood by the door, his face a picture of gentle concern. “You look a little pale, my love. I fear Mr. Knightley has been tiring you with his talk of business. He is a very good neighbour, but he does not always consider how fatiguing such subjects can be for delicate constitutions.”

“Not at all, Papa,” said Emma, forcing a smile. “We had a very pleasant conversation. I am perfectly well, I assure you.”

“Well, you do not look it. A little of Mrs. Bates’s excellent arrowroot would be the very thing, I fancy. Or perhaps we should have the fire built up. Perry was saying only yesterday that these sudden thaws are the most dangerous time for catching a chill.”

“I am quite warm, Papa, thank you.” She rose and went to him, linking her arm through his. “Let us not think of chills and arrowroot. Let us think of a game of backgammon before dinner. Shall we?”

Her father was easily diverted. “Backgammon! Yes, my dear, that would be delightful. Provided we are not too near the door. There is a shocking draught when the wind is in the east.”

As she sat with her father, moving the pieces on the board and listening to his gentle prattle about the iniquities of draughts and the virtues of thin gruel, Emma felt a new and unwelcome dimension to her guilt. It was one thing to deceive a man as discerning as Mr. Knightley; it was another, and somehow baser, thing to sit here, the picture of filial devotion, while her mind was a hornet’s nest of intrigue and self-reproach. Her whole life at Hartfield, so placid, so comfortable, so secure, seemed suddenly a stage set, and she an actress playing a part she no longer quite understood. She had wanted excitement, she had wanted to be first in consequence; and now that she had entangled herself in a plot worthy of a novel, she found she wanted nothing more than the simple, uncomplicated peace she had so carelessly thrown away.

The next day brought with it the inevitable necessity of a visit to Mrs. Goddard’s. Emma had put it off as long as she could, pleading a slight headache to her father—a fiction which he accepted with such alarming seriousness that he nearly summoned Mr. Perry on the spot. But she knew she could not abandon Harriet entirely. Her new role, the one she had so artfully described to Mr. Knightley, demanded it. She was to be the kind, sensible friend, guiding the heartbroken girl towards acceptance. It was a part she must now perform, however little she relished the prospect.

She found Harriet in the small parlour, ostensibly mending a chemise, though the needle was idle in her lap and her eyes, red-rimmed and swollen, were fixed on some distant point of misery. At Emma’s entrance, she started, and a fresh wave of colour flooded her pale cheeks.

“Miss Woodhouse,” she stammered, rising hastily. “I did not… I was not expecting…”

“My dear Harriet, do not get up,” said Emma, taking a seat beside her and affecting a gentle, sisterly air. “I could not stay away another day. I have been so thinking of you.”

This expression of sympathy was, unfortunately, the very worst thing she could have said. Harriet’s composure, fragile at the best of times, dissolved completely. She burst into tears, covering her face with her hands. “Oh, Miss Woodhouse! You are too good! I am so ashamed, so miserable!”

Emma’s heart sank. This was going to be even more difficult than she had imagined. “There, there, my dear girl,” she said, patting Harriet’s shoulder with a feeling of profound hypocrisy. “There is no need for tears. We must be rational. We must be strong.”

“But how can I be strong?” sobbed Harriet. “To have my hopes raised so high, only to be dashed! And by him! He, who I thought the very soul of kindness and consideration. And to think… to think that you knew all along!”

Emma froze. “Knew what, Harriet?” she asked, her voice a little sharper than she intended.

“That he did not care for me! That it was all a mistake! You must have seen it. You see every thing. Why did you let me go on, making such a fool of myself?”

Here was an accusation Emma had not anticipated; that she had been not a malign influence, but merely a negligent one. It was a lesser charge, to be sure, but it stung nonetheless. “My dear Harriet, it is not so simple as that. One cannot always read the heart of another. I confess, I may have been mistaken in my interpretation of some of his actions. I am as disappointed as you are; more so, for I feel I led you astray.” This, at least, was true, and she delivered the line with a pleasing sincerity.

“But what am I to do?” Harriet wailed, her grief now taking a practical turn. “I shall see him everywhere! At the Crown, in the street, at church! How am I to bear it? Every time I look at him, my heart will break all over again. And to know that he… that he prefers… someone else.”

Emma felt a prickle of alarm. “Someone else? What do you mean, Harriet? Did he… did he say something to that effect?”

“No, not in so many words,” Harriet admitted, dabbing her eyes with her handkerchief. “But his manner, Miss Woodhouse! The way he spoke of… of you.”

“Of me?” Emma’s breath caught in her throat.

“Yes! He said he hoped your little friend would soon find a suitable match, as you had enough on your hands in managing your own happiness. He said it with such a look! And then, when I told him how much I admired you, how you were the most perfect creature in the world, he said, ‘She is indeed.’ Just like that. ‘She is indeed.’ Oh, Miss Woodhouse, the way he said it! Anyone could see what he meant. How can I possibly compete with you?”

And with that, Harriet dissolved into a fresh paroxysm of weeping, leaving Emma in a state of the most bewildered, terrified, and triumphant confusion she had ever known. He had been speaking of her. While she was confessing Harriet’s misguided affection for him, he had been nurturing his own. The world tilted on its axis. Her great deception, her terrible crime, had been perpetrated in the service of a truth she had not even suspected. She had cleared the field of her rival, not for Mr. Knightley’s sake, but for her own.

She stayed a little longer, offering what comfort she could, which was in truth very little. Her mind was elsewhere, soaring in a dizzying flight of speculation. She walked back to Hartfield in a daze, her feet barely touching the ground. The guilt, the fear, the remorse—they had not vanished, but they were now overlaid with a new and powerful emotion: hope. A thrilling, dangerous hope.

The fine weather held, and a few days later, Emma was persuaded to join a party walking towards Donwell. Mrs. Weston had proposed it; Frank Churchill, ever eager for any social diversion, had seconded the motion; and Emma, whose spirits were now in a state of treacherous elevation, had readily agreed. They were to call upon Miss Bates on the way, a prospect that usually filled Emma with a kind of weary resignation, but which today seemed merely a minor inconvenience on the path to greater things.

They found the little party, as expected, in a state of flurry and talk. Miss Bates was overflowing with gratitude for some small attention from Mr. Frank Churchill, while Jane Fairfax sat at the pianoforte, looking pale and elegant, and, Emma thought, decidedly unhappy. Frank Churchill immediately attached himself to Emma’s side, his conversation a low-voiced, confidential stream of wit and flattery.

“You see our fair recluse,” he murmured, with a glance towards Jane. “She looks as if she is composing an elegy on the death of joy. One almost expects her to produce a cypress wreath from her reticule. And yet, she plays divinely. A strange creature.”

“She has not been well,” said Emma, with a pang of something that felt surprisingly like compassion. “Highbury does not, perhaps, agree with her.”

“Or perhaps it is not Highbury, but a certain gentleman’s absence from it, that disagrees with her,” he returned, his eyes twinkling with mischief. He was, of course, referring to the endless speculation about Jane and a mysterious Irish suitor, a theory Emma herself had once championed. Now, it seemed tiresome and a little cruel.

“We all have our secret sorrows,” she said, with a gravity that surprised herself.

Frank Churchill looked at her, his expression momentarily serious. “Do we indeed, Miss Woodhouse? And what, I wonder, are yours?”

Before she could frame a reply, the door opened, and the object of her own secret, tumultuous thoughts stood before them. Mr. Knightley had come to call on Mrs. and Miss Bates.

His entrance caused the usual happy stir. Miss Bates was ecstatic. “Mr. Knightley! How good of you! We are all here, you see! Such a party! Mrs. Weston, and Mr. Frank Churchill, and dear Miss Woodhouse! You are just in time to give us your opinion of a new piece of music.”

Mr. Knightley’s eyes, however, had already found Emma’s. He gave her a smile—that same warm, approving smile that had so undone her at Hartfield. “I see you are recovered from your headache, Emma,” he said, his voice loud enough for the room to hear. “I was sorry to learn from your father this morning that you had been indisposed.”

Emma felt the blood rush to her face. Frank Churchill shot her a quick, inquisitive look. “A headache?” he said softly, for her ears alone. “Is that what you call your secret sorrow? You are more poetical than I knew.”

Mr. Knightley, having paid his respects to Mrs. Weston and exchanged a civil, if not entirely cordial, greeting with Frank Churchill, was now being waylaid by Miss Bates near the window. Emma, flustered and overly conscious of being the centre of a drama only she understood, turned her attention to the music on the pianoforte, pretending a deep interest in a page of Cramer.

It was then that she saw it. Through the window of the Bates’s parlour, she had a clear view of the street. And walking slowly along it, on the opposite side, was Harriet Smith. She was alone, her head bowed, a picture of forlorn dejection. As Emma watched, Harriet looked up, her gaze sweeping across the street, and her eyes met Emma’s through the glass. There was no accusation in her look, only a profound and bottomless sadness. And then her gaze shifted, moving past Emma, to the figure standing just behind her. A flicker of recognition, of fresh and agonizing pain, crossed her face. She had seen Mr. Knightley. She turned away quickly, almost stumbling, and hurried off down the street, a small, pathetic figure of misery.

No one else had seen. Frank Churchill was attempting to engage Jane Fairfax in conversation; Mrs. Weston was speaking with Mrs. Bates; and Mr. Knightley himself was still captive, his back to the window, listening with what seemed an heroic patience to Miss Bates’s latest intelligence from the post-office.

Only Emma had witnessed the little tragedy. And it struck her with the force of a physical blow. The cost. The dreadful, sinking feeling. Here it was. It was not some future reckoning, some dramatic unmasking. It was this. It was the sight of that poor, simple girl’s suffering, a suffering that Emma had directly and knowingly caused. It was the knowledge that her own burgeoning happiness, the hope that was making her heart beat so wildly, was being bought at the price of Harriet’s tears.

“Miss Woodhouse, you are a thousand miles away.” Frank Churchill’s voice broke through her reverie. “Are you contemplating the complexities of Cramer, or something else entirely?”

She looked up at him, her face a mask of polite indifference, but her mind was in turmoil. She had been so pleased with her own cleverness, so thrilled by the prospect of winning Mr. Knightley’s affection. But the foundation of her castle in the air was the wreckage of another’s heart.

The walk to Donwell was, for Emma, an exercise in torture. The sun was bright, the air was fresh, and her companions were in the highest spirits. Frank Churchill was at his most charming, directing his attentions so equally between herself and Mrs. Weston that it was impossible to say which he preferred, though Emma felt he looked at her with a new, more pointed curiosity. Mrs. Weston was happy in the company of her husband and stepson, her gentle face beaming with contentment. And Mr. Knightley… Mr. Knightley walked beside her.

He had manoeuvred it quite deliberately, detaching her from the others on the pretext of pointing out some improvement he had made to a hedgerow. And now they walked together, a little apart from the rest. The very proximity she had dreamed of was now a source of acute discomfort.

“You are very quiet today, Emma,” he said, after a few moments of silence. “I had thought the fine weather and good company would have quite restored your spirits.”

“My spirits did not need restoring,” she said, a little defensively. “I am perfectly well.”

“Are you? You seem… preoccupied. I hope my calling upon you the other day did not distress you. We touched upon a sensitive subject.”

“Not at all,” she lied, staring intently at the path before them.

“I am glad to hear it,” he said, and his voice was so full of genuine warmth that she could not bear to look at him. “I have been thinking a great deal about our conversation. About your friendship with Miss Smith. You have acted with a kindness and wisdom that do you great credit. It is a rare thing, to be a true friend.”

Every word was a fresh turn of the screw. He was praising her for the very qualities she had so spectacularly failed to exhibit. He was building a pedestal for her, and she knew that at any moment it might crumble to dust, revealing the clay feet beneath.

“And how is your friend?” he continued, his tone one of genuine, compassionate inquiry. “I trust she is beginning to feel the benefit of your good counsel?”

Emma’s mind flew back to the image of Harriet’s stricken face, seen through the window. “She is… resigned,” she managed to say. “It will take time, of course.”

“Of course. A disappointment in love is not a thing to be gotten over in a day. Poor girl. She has a feeling heart, I am sure. One would wish to see her comfortably settled. Perhaps with a good, honest man who would appreciate her sweet temper. The farmer, Robert Martin, for instance. He seemed a very respectable young man.”

Emma’s blood ran cold. The final, unbearable irony. He was now, in his innocence, proposing the very match she had so arrogantly and disastrously scotched. The whole sorry affair had come full circle, and she was trapped in its vortex.

“Yes,” she said, her voice barely a whisper. “Perhaps.”

He glanced at her, a slight frown creasing his brow. “You are pale, Emma. The walk has been too much for you.”

“No, I am quite well. Only a little tired.” She felt a desperate need to change the subject, to escape the suffocating web of her own deceit. “Look,” she cried, with a brightness she did not feel, “how well the Abbey looks from this vantage! You must be very proud of it, Mr. Knightley.”

He followed her gaze to where the old, venerable pile of Donwell Abbey stood among its trees, solid and reassuring. “It has been my family’s home for a great many years,” he said simply. “I am not proud of it, so much as I am… its servant. I try to do my duty by it, and by the people who depend on it.” He turned to her, his earnest brown eyes holding hers. “That is all any of us can do, Emma. To know our duty, and to do it.”

To know our duty, and to do it. The words seemed to echo in the still air. And Emma Woodhouse, handsome, clever, and rich, who had never known a real sorrow or a day’s want in her life, felt a sudden, piercing understanding of her own character. She had been selfish, and vain, and cruelly thoughtless. She had played with the hearts of others as if they were pieces on a backgammon board. And in her pursuit of a momentary triumph, she had sacrificed the one thing she ought to have valued most: her own integrity. The prize she thought she was winning was not worth the cost of the game. And she knew, with a certainty that was as cold and hard as the stones of Donwell Abbey itself, that the bill for her education was only just beginning to come due.

The little party of four, now reduced from the larger group, proceeded at a more leisurely pace. The silence that fell upon them was of a different character from the animated, if occasionally fraught, conversation that had filled Miss Bates’s small parlour. It was a silence of reflection, and, on Emma’s part at least, of some slight discomposure. Frank Churchill was the first to break it, and he did so with the same air of unassailable good humour that had carried him through the morning.

“Well!” he exclaimed, with a light laugh. “I think we may say the new instrument has been thoroughly welcomed to Highbury. It has caused a stir, introduced a new subject, and even, it seems, mended a pair of spectacles. A most accomplished pianoforte.”

“You were very useful, certainly,” said Mrs. Weston, though her tone lacked its usual warmth of approbation. “Mrs. Bates was extremely grateful.”

“And Miss Fairfax?” he asked, turning to Emma with a glance of shared amusement. “Was she grateful, do you think? It is a little difficult to ascertain the true state of Miss Fairfax’s feelings.”

Emma felt a prick of conscience. She had been his accomplice, and now felt a desire to distance herself from the transaction. “I imagine she is very happy with the instrument,” she replied, with a careful emphasis. “It is a beautiful gift. Anyone must be sensible of such kindness.”

“Oh, indisputably. The kindness of Colonel Campbell is not to be questioned. One only wonders at the particulars. But conjecture, as she so rightly observed, is a hazardous business.” He said this with a look so full of meaning, so clearly referencing their own shared conjectures about the origin of the gift, that Emma could not help but feel a return of her former amusement, mixed with a growing sense of its impropriety. Mr. Knightley’s stern countenance rose in her mind’s eye, and she felt her cheeks grow a little warm.

Mrs. Weston, who had been walking a pace or two ahead with Harriet, now fell back to join them. “Mr. Knightley seemed in a great hurry to be at Kingston,” she observed, her voice pitched for Emma and her stepson alone. “It is not his usual way to be so abrupt. He scarcely gave poor Miss Bates a moment.”

“He had business, he said,” returned Frank Churchill lightly. “And we must own, the room was rather full. A man of his consequence cannot be expected to push his way into a crowded parlour without an invitation of some days’ standing. Besides, he made his inquiries most particularly. One cannot fault his neighbourly concern for Miss Fairfax’s health.”

The manner in which he said this, with a slight, almost imperceptible stress on the name, and another quick, intelligent look at Emma, was not lost upon her. It was as if he were testing the very idea that Mrs. Weston had put into Emma’s head with her significant glance earlier. The idea of Mr. Knightley and Jane Fairfax. Emma felt a strange sort of indignation rise within her. It was one thing for her to entertain a foolish notion from Mrs. Weston for a moment, but quite another to have it served up to her as a jest by Frank Churchill.

“Mr. Knightley is always attentive to the health of his friends,” said Emma, with a degree of stiffness. “He considers the Bateses his particular charge, and as Jane Fairfax is their guest, his concern is perfectly natural. It is just his way.”

“Ah, is it?” said Frank Churchill, his tone of innocent inquiry a masterpiece of art. “You know him so well, of course. Forgive me; as a comparative stranger, I am apt to misinterpret your Highbury manners. I might have supposed such very pointed and public solicitude to proceed from a warmer interest. But if it is merely ‘his way,’ then the matter is explained.”

He fell silent, but the seed was planted anew, and in the most unwelcome soil. Emma found herself vexed. She was vexed with Frank Churchill for his impertinence, with Mrs. Weston for her foolish glance, and most of all, she believed, with Mr. Knightley for behaving in a manner so open to misconstruction. To be so loud, so commanding, in his questions from the street! It was unlike his usual judgment. It was almost… ridiculous.

They had by this time reached the shrubbery walk that led to the Hartfield gates. Here, Mrs. Weston and her companion were to part from them.

“We shall see you this evening, I hope?” said Mrs. Weston to Emma. “Your father was so good as to say he would spare you to us for a few hours.”

“Yes, if nothing prevents it. We shall be with you for tea.”

“Excellent. Till then.” Mrs. Weston smiled, and extended her hand to Harriet, who curtsied with her usual timid gratitude.

Frank Churchill, however, lingered for a moment beside Emma. “I am afraid I have fallen into disgrace with you,” he said in a lower voice, full of persuasive charm. “You think me a great brute for teasing your ‘amiable, upright, perfect Jane Fairfax.’” He used her own former words with a gentle irony that was difficult to resent. “But you must confess, she gives one very little to work with. Such reserve is a challenge to a man’s ingenuity. And did you not see that smile? You yourself pointed it out. She was not entirely displeased.”

“She was embarrassed,” said Emma, though with less conviction than she could have wished. “You pressed her unmercifully.”

“Do you think so? Perhaps I did. But a little pressing is sometimes necessary to get at the truth of things. And I have you to thank for the key. Without your suggestion, I should have been entirely in the dark. Now,” and here his voice dropped still further, becoming earnest and confidential, “I have something to build upon. You will not be too severe upon me, will you? You will allow me some follies?”

He looked at her with an expression that bespoke so much reliance on her good opinion, so much admiration and affection, that Emma’s displeasure quite melted away. It was impossible to be long angry with him. He was faulty, no doubt; he was trifling, and perhaps a little unfeeling; but he was devoted to her, and his faults were all of a lively, interesting kind. They were the faults of a young man of spirit and fortune, not the solemn, sermonizing faults she was accustomed to from Mr. Knightley.

“I cannot promise to approve of all your follies,” she said, a smile playing on her lips in spite of herself. “But I may perhaps be persuaded to forgive them.”

“That is all I can ask,” he replied, pressing her hand for a moment before turning to follow Mrs. Weston. “Adieu, till the evening.”

Emma and Harriet watched them go, and then turned in at the gates of Hartfield. The short walk had been more eventful than many a long morning, and Emma’s mind was in a whirl of conflicting thoughts and feelings.

“Mr. Churchill has such very pleasing manners,” said Harriet, with a sigh that seemed to come from the depths of her being. It was a sigh that spoke of admiration for a happiness and ease she could never attain, and of a general, gentle melancholy that had become her chief characteristic of late.

“Yes, he is very agreeable,” replied Emma, her thoughts still on his parting words.

“And Mrs. Weston is so very kind. It was a pleasant visit, was it not, Miss Woodhouse? Though I felt very sorry for Miss Fairfax. Mr. Churchill did seem to tease her a great deal.”

“Miss Fairfax knows how to take care of herself, Harriet. You need not be concerned for her. She is not a person to be easily overcome.” Emma spoke with a confidence she was beginning not to feel. The image of Jane’s deep blush and forced calmness was more persistent than she liked.

“And Mr. Knightley,” Harriet ventured, after a moment’s hesitation. “He spoke so very loud. I was quite startled. He seemed almost angry.”

Here it was again. The subject was inescapable. Even Harriet, in her simplicity, had noted the singularity of his behaviour.

“Angry? Oh, no, not angry,” said Emma quickly. “Mr. Knightley is never angry without a very good cause. He was merely… emphatic. He has a powerful voice, and when he is on horseback he perhaps forgets how easily he is heard. He wished to be sure that Miss Bates understood his inquiries after her niece. It was all kindness, I assure you.”

“Oh, yes. I am sure of it,” said Harriet, readily convinced, as she always was by Emma’s assurances. “He is so very good. He sent them all his apples, you know. It was what Miss Bates was so shocked about. Is it not just like him?”

“Exactly like him,” said Emma. And it was. It was just like him to be quietly, substantially good, without any show or parade. The thought gave her a moments pause. His goodness was of a very different order from Frank Churchill’s charm. It was like the difference between the solid, enduring stone of Donwell Abbey and the elegant but temporary decorations of a ballroom. The comparison was not entirely in Frank Churchill’s favour, and it vexed her.

She shook off the feeling. “But we must not loiter here. Papa will be wondering where I have been so long.”

They entered the house, and after seeing Harriet settled with a book in the drawing-room—a book which Emma suspected would not be much read—she went to her own room to prepare for the latter part of the day.

Alone, with the door closed, Emma allowed her composure to dissolve. She walked to the window, looking out over the neat gardens of Hartfield, but seeing nothing of them. Her mind was a theatre, replaying the scenes at the Bateses’ and on the walk home. Every word, every look, was subjected to a rigorous and not altogether comfortable examination.

Frank Churchill’s conduct, she was forced to admit, had bordered on the cruel. To so relentlessly probe a wound, for that was what she suspected it was, in a person of Jane Fairfax’s constrained circumstances and delicate health, was not the behaviour of a true gentleman. It was amusing, yes; his wit was quick, his perception sharp; but the object of his wit had been a helpless woman, compelled by propriety to sit and endure it. And Emma had been his smiling confederate. She blushed now to think of it. She had whispered, “You speak too plain,” not from any real compassion for Jane, but from a slight fear of discovery, a fear for the delicacy of their own private amusement.

And what had been the result? Mr. Knightley’s appearance. His extraordinary, public, and very pointed display of concern. Emma paced the length of her room. Why had he done it? Her first explanation, that it was merely his usual kindness, now seemed insufficient. There had been a forcefulness, a command in his voice as he cut through Miss Bates’s chatter, that spoke of something more than simple neighbourly feeling. There had been a protective quality in his repeated question, “How is Miss Fairfax?” as if he were publicly taking her part against some unseen antagonist.

The antagonist, she knew with a sinking feeling, must have been Frank Churchill; and by extension, herself. Mr. Knightley must have heard, as they had all heard, Frank’s pointed speech about conjectures and commissions from Ireland. He had seen Jane’s distress, and had acted to put a stop to it, or at least to offer a public counterweight of support. It was a reproof. A silent, indirect, but perfectly clear reproof aimed over Jane Fairfax’s head at the occupants of the parlour.

The thought was mortifying. To be corrected by Mr. Knightley was an experience she was well used to, and one she had always resented; but to be corrected by him on a point of feeling, of common humanity towards another woman, was a new and deeper humiliation. He, from the street, had shown more true compassion for Jane Fairfax than she, sitting a few feet away, had been able to muster. He, with all his supposed bluntness, had acted with a delicacy of feeling she had failed to exhibit.

And then, Mrs. Weston’s absurd idea recurred to her. That look of particular meaning. Could it be possible? Mr. Knightley and Jane Fairfax. She tried to picture it. Mr. Knightley, so sensible, so judicious, so much a part of her own family, of Hartfield itself. He was sixteen years her senior; he had held her in his arms as a baby. He was her brother, her friend, her critic. And Jane Fairfax—so elegant, so accomplished, so infuriatingly reserved and universally praised.

No, it was an absurdity. It was a match that made no sense. There was no warmth, no spark between them. They were both too sensible, too proper. Their conversations were always models of rational discourse, on literature, or music, or the management of a parish. There was none of the playfulness, the teasing, the delightful nonsense that marked her own interactions with Frank Churchill. A union between Mr. Knightley and Jane Fairfax would be a partnership of mutual esteem, certainly; but it would be cold, so very cold. The notion was disagreeable in the extreme. It would change everything. It would take Mr. Knightley from Hartfield. It would take him from *her*.

She stopped her pacing, struck by the force of her own feeling. Why should the idea be so peculiarly odious to her? It was not her business whom Mr. Knightley married, if he ever chose to marry at all. She had always imagined him a confirmed bachelor, a permanent fixture of their society, belonging to Donwell and to Hartfield, and to no one else. The idea of another woman—any woman—having a primary claim on his time, his attention, his affections, was… unsettling. It was as if a piece of her own world, a fundamental and unexamined part of her own happiness, were being threatened.

She quickly chided herself for such selfishness. It was only that a marriage to Jane Fairfax would be so unsuitable. He deserved better. He deserved a wife of a more open, cheerful temper; someone who would brighten his home, not add to its sobriety. Jane Fairfax, with her pale cheeks and her perpetual air of mystery and silent suffering, was the very last woman for him.

Thus, she reasoned herself back into a state of composure. Mrs. Weston was a romantic, and had been led astray by a moment of unusual behaviour. Mr. Knightley’s concern for Jane was that of a compassionate friend and nothing more. And Frank Churchill—dear, charming, faulty Frank Churchill—was in love with her, Emma Woodhouse, and his teasing of Jane Fairfax was merely a blind, a clever diversion to conceal his true feelings from the prying eyes of Highbury. This was a much more rational, and infinitely more agreeable, explanation of the morning’s events. It restored everyone to their proper places, and reaffirmed her own central importance in the drama.

She was content with this conclusion. And yet, as she began to dress for the evening at Randalls, a small, unwelcome image persisted in her mind: the image of Mr. Knightley’s face, earnest and resolute, as he looked up from the street, and the sound of his voice, raised not in anger, but with an authority and a tenderness that had, for a moment, silenced everyone. She could not quite dismiss the feeling that she had, on this occasion, judged everything entirely wrong.

The drive to Randalls was short, and served rather to fix her conclusions than to dislodge them. The steady motion of the carriage, the familiar sight of the Highbury road, the very presence of her father beside her, lamenting the chill of the evening air and the lateness of the hour, all contributed to a sense of rightness and order. The world was as it should be. Mr. Knightley had been unusually solicitous, that was all. Jane Fairfax was secretive and perhaps a little artful. And Frank Churchill was everything that was charming and agreeable. By the time they drew up to the welcoming light of Mrs. Weston’s door, Emma felt her spirits quite restored, and her judgment perfectly vindicated.

She was met with the usual warmth. Mrs. Weston, her dear former governess, embraced her with an affection that never failed to comfort, and Mr. Weston, all cheerful bustle and welcome, declared her arrival the very thing needed to make the evening complete.

“Ah, Emma! Here you are! We were just saying, were we not, my love, that the party was not quite a party until Miss Woodhouse arrived? And Mr. Woodhouse, so good of you to venture out! We shall take the greatest care of you; no draughts, I promise. The fire is built up, and your corner is ready.”

Frank Churchill was there, of course, leaning against the mantelpiece with an air of fashionable languor that dissolved at once into delighted animation upon seeing her. He came forward immediately, his eyes speaking a volume of flattering things before his lips had formed a single word.

“At last,” he said, in a low voice meant only for her. “I had begun to despair. An evening without you is a book without a frontispiece; it may contain some sense, but it lacks all its beauty and interest.”

Emma could not forbear a smile. “You are very wicked,” she whispered, though she felt more pleased than censorious. “To think that you were just as happy an hour ago without me.”

“Never happy. Merely existing. I passed the time in a sad contemplation of my own mortality, and in wondering at the extreme slowness of Highbury clocks. But tell me, have you recovered from the great spectacle of the morning? The mysterious pianoforte, the bewildered Miss Bates, the heroic spectacle-mender?”

“I am quite recovered,” said Emma, laughing. “And you? You seemed to work very hard at your riveting.”

“Oh, intolerably! A gentleman is not made for such labour. But it was worth the effort, to be so situated. One learns a great deal, you know, when one appears to be entirely occupied with a trifle. One hears things, one sees things.” He gave her a look of immense significance. “One confirms one’s conjectures.”

Here was the confirmation she needed. He felt as she did; he saw what she saw. Her alliance with him in this matter of Jane Fairfax was a source of private satisfaction. Mrs. Weston approached them, her kind face wearing a look of gentle inquiry.

“Well, you two,” she said, her smile including them both. “You seem to have secrets already. We were just speaking of the morning. Was it not an extraordinary scene? Poor Jane! I own, I felt for her. Mr. Churchill’s teasing was almost too pointed.”

“My teasing?” exclaimed Frank Churchill, with an air of perfect innocence. “My dear Mrs. Weston, I protest! I was merely making conversation. The arrival of a pianoforte is, after all, a matter of some note in a quiet place like Highbury. I cannot be held accountable for the over-sensitivity of others.”

“Indeed,” said Emma, coming to his defence. “It was all done in the spirit of good humour. And as you saw, Jane Fairfax was not so very distressed. I caught her smiling myself.”

“A smile?” said Mrs. Weston, looking doubtful. “I must have missed that. I saw only a blush of what looked very like pain.”

“Ah, but a blush and a smile may have the same source,” said Frank Churchill philosophically. “One may blush from pleasure as well as from mortification. And we cannot deny there must have been pleasure. Such a gift! From so devoted a friend and guardian as Colonel Campbell. Or,” he added, dropping his voice again as he turned to Emma, “from someone else entirely.”

Emma’s heart gave a little leap of shared understanding. This was the language she understood, the playful, intelligent probing of a mystery that occupied them both. Before she could reply, however, the drawing-room door opened, and Mr. Knightley was announced.

His entrance had an immediate and curious effect on the room. Mr. Weston greeted him with boisterous friendship, Mrs. Weston with quiet pleasure. Emma felt her own composure falter for a moment. He had said he was going to Kingston; she had not expected to see him again so soon. He looked serious, more serious than usual, and his eye, as it swept the room, seemed to seek for something, or someone. It rested for a moment on her and Frank Churchill, standing together, and a slight, almost imperceptible, hardening came into his expression. He gave them a brief, comprehensive nod, and turned to speak to Mrs. Weston.

“Knightley! So you got my message!” cried Mr. Weston. “I sent a boy over to Donwell on the off chance you might have returned early. I knew you could not resist us!”

“My business in Kingston was concluded more quickly than I anticipated,” Mr. Knightley replied, his voice calm and even. “It was no great matter.”

“And you are come to play a rubber of whist with Mr. Woodhouse, I am sure,” said Mrs. Weston. “He has been hoping for it.”

“I shall be very happy to,” said Mr. Knightley. But his attention seemed divided. He was listening to Mrs. Weston, but his gaze kept returning to the corner where Emma and Frank Churchill had resumed their low-toned conversation.

“He does not look best pleased to see me,” murmured Frank Churchill in Emma’s ear. “I believe your Mr. Knightley holds me in some disfavour. Do you think I have offended him? Perhaps he disapproves of my methods of repairing spectacles.”

“Do not be ridiculous,” said Emma, though she felt a prickle of unease. “Mr. Knightley disapproves of a great many things. It is his way. Pay it no mind.”

“I shall try, for your sake. But his very presence seems to cast a sort of gravity over the room. See, even the candles seem to burn more soberly. We were so merry before he came.”

It was true. A subtle shift had occurred. The light-hearted atmosphere had been tempered by something more serious. Emma felt a childish resentment towards Mr. Knightley for spoiling their fun. It was he who had created the awkwardness at the Bateses’, with his loud and pointed inquiries after Jane; and now he had brought that same mood of uncomfortable earnestness to Randalls.

The card tables were set up, and Mr. Woodhouse was settled with his three friends for whist. Mr. Knightley played with his customary attention and skill, yet Emma, observing him from across the room, could not but feel that his mind was not entirely on the game. He answered her father’s gentle ramblings with unfailing patience, he sorted his cards with precision, but there was a watchfulness about him that was directed not at the table, but at the little group by the fire.

There, she and Frank Churchill were engaged in looking over a book of prints, their heads bent close together. Mr. Weston had joined them, and was contributing his own cheerful, if not always insightful, commentary.

“What have we here?” he said, peering over their shoulders. “Views of the Lakes. Very fine, very fine indeed. But not so fine as our own Box Hill, eh, Emma? We must have our party to Box Hill soon, now that Frank is here to make us all merry.”

“An excellent idea, sir,” said Frank Churchill. “I am at Miss Woodhouse’s command. We could make it a party of exploration. We might even discover a new prospect, or a hidden spring, or perhaps,” he glanced at Emma with a smile, “the secret source of a certain anonymous pianoforte.”

Mr. Weston laughed heartily. “Aha! You are still on about that! I confess, it is a capital mystery. Knightley,” he called across the room, “we are trying to solve the case of the pianoforte. Have you any theories?”

Mr. Knightley looked up from his cards. A small frown was set between his brows. “I have no theories,” he said, his voice carrying with perfect clearness in a sudden lull in the conversation. “I have only the belief that Miss Fairfax’s private affairs are her own, and that speculating upon them in her absence is an exercise in idle cruelty.”

The silence that followed was profound. Mr. Weston looked utterly chapfallen, as if he had been unexpectedly struck. Frank Churchill raised an eyebrow, a picture of amused surprise. Emma felt a hot flush of anger rise to her cheeks. The reproof was not for Mr. Weston, not really. It was for her, and for Frank Churchill. He had said “idle cruelty.” He had as good as called her cruel.

Mrs. Weston, ever the peacemaker, quickly interposed. “Well, I am sure no cruelty was intended. It is only natural to be curious about one’s friends. Shall we have some music, Frank? You have deprived us for too long.”

Frank Churchill rose with alacrity, seemingly unshaken. “With the greatest pleasure. But it is Miss Woodhouse who must play. I am a mere listener in her presence.”

Emma allowed herself to be led to the instrument, but her mind was in turmoil. The injustice of Mr. Knightley’s remark stung her deeply. Idle cruelty! When her only fault was a lively imagination and a desire for a little amusement. And for him to say it so publicly, to embarrass Mr. Weston and to shame her before everyone! It was unpardonable.

She played a few popular airs, her touch more spirited than usual, almost defiant. Frank Churchill stood by her, turning the pages, his handsome face expressing nothing but admiration. He was her ally. He understood her. He would never have subjected her to such a cold, public rebuke. The contrast between the two men had never been more stark in her mind. One was all warmth, indulgence, and shared wit; the other was all censure, principle, and uncomfortable truth.

When the rubber of whist was finished and Mr. Woodhouse was beginning to show signs of fatigue, Mr. Knightley did not join the group around the pianoforte. Instead, he came to stand near Emma as she finished a piece, while Frank Churchill was momentarily distracted by Mr. Weston.

“You play with a great deal of feeling tonight, Emma,” he said, his voice low and devoid of its earlier harshness.

“I am glad you find something to approve of in my performance,” she replied, not looking at him, her fingers still resting on the keys.

There was a pause. “My words earlier were perhaps too strong for the company,” he said, “and for that, I beg your pardon. It was not my intention to cause a general discomfort.”

The apology was offered, but Emma was not ready to receive it. “Not general,” she said coolly. “The discomfort was, I believe, intended for a very particular audience of two.”

“And if it was?” he said, his voice regaining a little of its firmness. “Can you say, in your own heart, that the subject of your amusement was a worthy one? Can you say that your conjectures, as Mr. Churchill calls them, have been guided by kindness?”

“Kindness!” Emma turned on the stool to face him, her eyes flashing. “And was it kind, your own performance this morning? To call out to Miss Bates from the street, to make a public spectacle of your concern for her niece? You speak of idle cruelty, but what of such pointed, ostentatious charity? You made everyone uncomfortable, and for what? To show yourself the only person in Highbury with a proper sense of duty?”

She had not known she was going to say it, but the words, once spoken, felt deeply true. She was shocked at her own audacity, but also fiercely glad to have finally turned the tables on her accuser.

Mr. Knightley was taken aback. He looked at her, not with anger, but with a kind of pained astonishment. “So that is how you saw it,” he said slowly. “As a performance. As a bid for admiration.” He shook his head, a wry, unhappy smile touching his lips. “You know me very ill, Emma, if you can believe that. I wished only to know if she was well. My inquiries were loud because Miss Bates was in another room and I was in the street, and I am not a young man of fashion who thinks it a virtue to be inaudible. My concern was for a young woman who looked ill and tired, and who I feared had been made more so by the very party that should have given her pleasure.”

“She is always ill and tired,” Emma retorted, her vexation making her unjust. “It is her way. She cultivates a delicate air to attract sympathy. And she seems to have succeeded with you.”

“Succeeded with me?” Mr. Knightley repeated, his voice dangerously quiet. “What is it you imagine she is trying to succeed in? What is this grand scheme you and Frank Churchill have concocted? That she is trying to ensnare a husband with her pale looks and her musical talents? That she means to take Mr. Dixon from his wife? The idea is as absurd as it is uncharitable. The poor girl is in a situation of immense difficulty; dependent, isolated, with a future that is precarious at best. And your response, the response of a person to whom she should be able to look for friendship and support, is to make her the object of your idle, and I will say it again, cruel speculation.”

“Frank Churchill thinks—” she began, but he cut her short.

“Frank Churchill!” he said, with a contempt he made no effort to disguise. “Do not quote Frank Churchill to me. He is a child. A charming, amusing child, who cares for nothing but his own entertainment. He would set fire to a house for the pleasure of seeing the flames, and call it a capital joke. That you, Emma, you with your good heart and your sound understanding, should allow yourself to be led by him into such thoughtless behaviour is what I cannot comprehend. He is not a standard for you to follow. He is a warning.”

He had never spoken to her like this before. The force of his conviction, the sheer weight of his disappointment in her, was like a physical blow. All her easy confidence, her self-satisfaction, evaporated, leaving behind a cold, hollow feeling of shame. She had no answer for him. She looked down at her hands, which were trembling slightly.

At that moment, Mrs. Weston, seeing their tense, private conference, came towards them with a gentle smile. “Mr. Woodhouse is asking for you, Emma, my dear. I believe he is ready to go home.”

The interruption was a relief, a reprieve she desperately needed. She rose without another word to Mr. Knightley, and went to her father’s side. The farewells were made with all the usual civilities, but for Emma, they were a blur. She was aware of Frank Churchill pressing her hand and whispering, “Until tomorrow,” of Mrs. Weston’s anxious, searching look, of Mr. Weston’s unabated cheerfulness. She did not look at Mr. Knightley again.

The ride home was silent. Mr. Woodhouse, tired from his exertions, soon dozed off. Emma sat staring out at the dark shapes of the trees, her mind replaying every word of her conversation with Mr. Knightley. “You know me very ill.” Had she misjudged him so completely? “A warning.” Was that what Frank Churchill was? And the most painful charge of all, that she, Emma Woodhouse, who prided herself on her judgment and her standing in their little world, had been led. Led, like a child, into folly and unkindness.

The agreeable explanations she had so carefully constructed for herself that afternoon now lay in ruins. She had seen Mr. Knightley’s concern for Jane as a romantic absurdity, his censure of herself as mere crossness. Now, she was forced to see it differently. His concern had been genuine compassion; his censure had been a moral judgment, delivered from a place of deep, personal affection for her. He was not angry with her because she had broken a rule of etiquette; he was disappointed in her because she had failed a test of character.

And what of her own feelings? Why had she been so fiercely, so unreasonably angry at the idea of his caring for Jane Fairfax? Why had his good opinion become, in a single evening, the only thing in the world that seemed to matter? The questions were deeply unsettling, and pointed towards a truth she was not yet ready to face.

She had entered Randalls that evening feeling like the heroine of the story, the clever, admired orchestrator of events. She left it feeling small, and foolish, and profoundly ashamed. The image that now occupied her mind was not of Mr. Knightley’s sternness, but of the pained astonishment in his eyes when she had flung her accusation at him. “So that is how you saw it.” In that moment, she had not only misjudged him; she had wounded him. And the realization of that fact was more painful than any reproof. The world, which had seemed so right and orderly just a few hours before, now felt entirely, and frighteningly, out of place.
