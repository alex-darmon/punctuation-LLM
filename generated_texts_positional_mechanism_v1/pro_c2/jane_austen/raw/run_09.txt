At the sound of his name, a violent shudder passed through Marianne’s frame. “Do not speak of him,” she whispered. “I cannot bear it.”
“And yet we must speak of him, at least once,” said Elinor, with gentle firmness. “We must face the truth, however painful. He has proved himself to be altogether unworthy of your affection. His conduct has been that of a man without honour, without principle, without feeling. To have allied yourself to such a man would have been to ensure a life of misery. You have had a most fortunate escape.”
“An escape!” cried Marianne, bitterly. “Do you call this an escape? To be rejected, despised, held up to the ridicule of the world! To have my affections trifled with, and my heart broken! Oh, Elinor, you talk of what you do not understand. You have never loved.”
The injustice of this accusation, at a time when her own heart was so secretly and so painfully oppressed, was almost more than Elinor could bear. The colour rose to her cheeks, and a sharp retort was on her lips. But a look at her sister’s wretched face checked it at once. What right had she to add to that misery by any complaint of her own? It was her part to endure, and to console.
“You are mistaken, Marianne,” she said, in a voice that cost her a great effort to keep steady. “I do not pretend to judge the greatness of your suffering. But I know that it cannot, and must not, be eternal. You owe it to yourself, to our mother, to all who love you, to combat this affliction. You have duties, you have affections, which are not all buried in one grave.”
Marianne only wept in reply. Elinor, seeing that no more could be done at present, did not press the subject. She persuaded her sister at last to drink a little tea, and to allow her to brush her hair. These small attentions, by proving her love, seemed to soothe Marianne more than any arguments. By the time Elinor was obliged to go down to dinner, she had the comfort of seeing her sister lying on the sofa, if not less miserable, at least more tranquil.
Mrs. Jennings was full of the Palmers, whom she had called upon that afternoon in Conduit Street.

This intelligence, which Mrs. Jennings imparted with great satisfaction, gave Elinor none at all. The character of Miss Grey was of no consequence to her. Willoughby’s guilt was not to be lessened or increased by the qualities of the woman he had preferred. That he had acted from the meanest of motives was already plain enough.

It leaves the coast clear for the Colonel, and I am more convinced than ever that it will all end in a wedding at Delaford before the year is out. We must have him here tomorrow. I shall send him a little note myself, to make sure of him.”
Elinor gave up the contest. She felt that to oppose Mrs. Jennings’s matchmaking was as fruitless as to reason with Marianne’s grief. She could only arm herself with patience, and wait for time to bring a cure for both.

She began to scratch away with great energy, her face screwed up in concentration, a spectacle of such earnest and ill-judged contrivance that Elinor knew not whether to be more pained by its purpose or amused by its execution. To argue further was useless; the note was being written, and it would be sent. Elinor could only hope that Colonel Brandon’s own discretion would lead him to decline an invitation so evidently patched together for an occasion he could not fail to guess at. But then, she remembered his particular regard for Marianne, and feared that the very hint of her being in distress would be the one thing most certain to bring him.
“There!” said Mrs. Jennings, folding the paper with a triumphant rap of her knuckles on the desk. “John shall take it round directly. And we shall see what we shall see. Now, my dear, do not you fret. All will be well. A young man like Willoughby! with his curricles and his debts! He would have been a terrible husband. All show and no substance. The Colonel is worth a dozen of him. Two thousand a year, you know; and Delaford! Did I tell you about the mulberry tree?”
Elinor was spared the necessity of a reply by the entrance of the servant to clear the table, and she took the opportunity of slipping away once more to her sister’s chamber. She found Marianne just as she had left her, her eyes closed, but her countenance still bearing the impress of a deep and settled misery. She did not seem to sleep, but to be lost in a stupor of grief. Elinor sat by her side for nearly an hour, in perfect silence, her heart aching with a sorrow that was twofold; for her sister’s suffering, and for the heavy consciousness of her own, which, in the constant demand for exertion, she was forced to suppress. Her own disappointment, her own anxieties respecting Edward, seemed to belong to another life, a matter of secondary import which had no right to claim her thoughts while Marianne was so wretched. It was a cruel comfort, but a comfort still, to feel that her own trial had, by its slower and more secret nature, fortified her with the patience and self-command so desperately needed now.
The next morning brought with it no alleviation of their distress. Marianne, when she awoke, was feverish and weak, and declared herself utterly unable to rise. Her eyes, heavy and inflamed with weeping, were turned from the light, and she spoke only in whispers, begging to be left alone. Elinor, however, would not consent to this. She insisted on staying with her, on bathing her temples with lavender-water, and persuading her to take a few spoonfuls of broth. Every attention was received with a mournful passiveness that was more alarming to Elinor than any complaint. It was the absolute surrender of a spirit that had once been all fire and animation. She saw with terror that Marianne was not merely indulging her sorrow; she was sinking under it.
Mrs. Jennings, on hearing of Marianne’s state, was all kindness and consternation. She would have sent for half the physicians in London, had Elinor not calmly and firmly prevented her.
“It is the effect of a great shock on a delicate frame, ma’am,” she said. “Rest and quiet, I am convinced, are all she needs. If she is not better by tomorrow, then we may consider a physician.”
“Well, you know best, my dear. But she looks shockingly. And to think of her having no appetite! Poor thing. I have a great mind to go and sit with her myself; it would be a charity.”
“You are very good,” said Elinor, with an inward shudder at the thought of Mrs. Jennings’s well-meant conversation in that sick-room, “but the slightest exertion, even that of listening, is too much for her at present. I am to sit with her, and I believe she prefers it to be no one else.”
Mrs. Jennings was forced to be content with this, though she declared it a thousand pities that so much good nursing should fall upon one pair of shoulders. Her active benevolence, thus thwarted in one direction, soon found another outlet.
“Well, my dear, the Colonel’s answer is come! He will be here this evening. He says he is honoured by my invitation and will do himself the pleasure of waiting on us after dinner. There now! Is not that good of him? I knew he would come!”
Elinor’s heart sank. “Did he… did he ask after my sister?”
“Oh! Lord, yes, in the most particular manner. Here is his note; you may see for yourself.”
She handed Elinor a small, neat folded paper. The hand was elegant and firm. After the formal acceptance of the invitation, was written, “I hope I may find that you and your young friends continue in good health. Pray be so good as to assure Miss Dashwood and Miss Marianne of my constant and sincerest good wishes for their happiness.”
The delicacy of it, the careful inclusion of both sisters, and the avoidance of any particularity that might betray knowledge or suspicion, struck Elinor very forcibly. Here was a consideration, a gentlemanlike feeling, which she had not dared to expect. It was a style of sympathy as different from Mrs. Jennings’s as light from darkness; and for a moment, the prospect of his company was a relief rather than a burden. He, at least, would not talk of Willoughby; he would not plan for the future, or exult in the downfall of a rival. He would understand, as far as a man could, the necessity for silence.
The evening came, and with it, Colonel Brandon. He was shown into the drawing-room, where Elinor was sitting alone, Mrs. Jennings having just gone up to her own room to make some alteration in her dress. He looked paler than usual, and his customary gravity was deepened into an expression of genuine concern. He approached Elinor, not with the briskness of a common visitor, but with a quiet hesitation that spoke his respect for the sorrow of the house.
“Miss Dashwood,” he said, his voice low and earnest, “I was very sorry to hear from Sir John of the… of the intelligence you have lately received.”
Elinor looked up at him, her own composure for a moment shaken by his evident sympathy. “You have seen Sir John, then?”
“I met him this morning. He was… much incensed. It was from him I learnt that you were in town, and that your sister was unwell.” He paused, and then added, with a visible effort, “I will not offend you by saying what I feel. The world is full of such instances of… of profligacy. But for your sister, whose sensibilities are so exquisitely fine, the blow must be doubly severe. If there is any service, any office, however small, that I can perform, I hope you will believe that my greatest happiness would be to be employed by you.”
“You are very good, sir,” replied Elinor, her voice trembling a little. “I am very grateful for your kindness. At present, nothing can be done. Time, and my sister’s own excellent understanding, must be her only friends.”
“Her understanding, yes,” said the Colonel, with a sigh, “but her heart may resist its counsel for a long time. I know something of such afflictions.”
There was a sadness in his tone that went beyond mere sympathy, a personal echo that made Elinor look at him with a new interest. Before she could make any reply, however, Mrs. Jennings came bustling into the room, all smiles and apologies for her absence.
“Colonel! So good of you to come on such short notice! We are but a dismal party, I am afraid, for my poor Marianne is quite laid up. But Elinor and I will do our best to amuse you. Did she tell you? Poor girl, she has had a dreadful shock. That scoundrel Willoughby…”
“Ma’am,” said Elinor, in a low but firm voice, touching her arm, “Colonel Brandon is already aware of the circumstances. We need not speak of them.”
“Oh! very well, my dear, very well. As you please. The less said the better, as I always say.” Mrs. Jennings, though momentarily checked, was not to be silenced for long. “But it alters nothing of what I think, Colonel. An ill-wind, you know! But there, I will not say another word. Shall we have a game of cards after tea? Or do you think a little music? Elinor, my dear, perhaps you would play for us later? It would be a treat for the Colonel.”
Elinor, seeing no escape, gave a faint assent. The evening proceeded much as she had anticipated. Mrs. Jennings talked enough for three, her conversation skipping from the price of fish to the latest fashions, from a remedy for coughs to the architectural beauties of Delaford. Colonel Brandon spoke little, but his attention to Elinor was constant and unobtrusive. He enquired after her mother, her little sister; he spoke of books he had been reading, and of public affairs, subjects on which he could converse with her without touching on any painful topic. When tea was over, Mrs. Jennings renewed her request for music.
“Now, Elinor, my love, you promised us a tune. The Colonel, I know, is a great lover of music.”
Elinor would have given anything to refuse, but she saw no civil way of doing so. She went to the pianoforte, her mind in a tumult. To play, to be forced into an employment so associated with domestic peace and cheerfulness, seemed a mockery of her real feelings. Her fingers trembled as she touched the keys. She chose a quiet and simple piece by Haydn, hoping to get through it quickly and without notice. But as she played, the music began to work upon her own overwrought nerves. The harmony, so ordered and so pure, was a painful contrast to the chaos of her thoughts. She thought of Marianne, who at that moment was probably weeping in the room above; she thought of Willoughby, and the evenings at Barton when he and Marianne had sung together; she thought of Edward, and the mysterious sorrow that stood between them. Her self-possession, so long and so painfully maintained, began to give way. She played on, but her eyes filled with tears, which she dared not wipe away for fear of drawing attention.
She was not unobserved. Colonel Brandon had drawn his chair near the instrument, and was watching her with an expression of the deepest and most compassionate concern. He saw the struggle, the trembling of her hands, the single tear that escaped and traced a path down her cheek. When she had finished, and sat for a moment with her head bowed over the keys, unable to speak, he did not distress her with common praise. He was silent for a moment, and then said, in a tone of quiet respect, “You are very fond of Haydn, Miss Dashwood. There is a melancholy in his best adagios that, I confess, I find more soothing than any lighter music.”
His words, by acknowledging her sadness without naming its cause, were the truest kindness she had received all day. She was able to look up and give him a grateful, though watery, smile. “I believe you are right, sir. It speaks a language which does not require a cheerful heart to understand.”
At this moment, a servant entered with a note for Mrs. Jennings. “From Mrs. Palmer, ma’am. The boy waits for an answer.”
Mrs. Jennings tore it open. “Lord! what is this? Charlotte wants us all to come to her tomorrow evening! A little party. She has some friends coming, and she says it will be the very thing to cheer us up. And she particularly begs that Marianne will come, if she is well enough; she thinks a change of scene would do her all the good in the world.” She looked at Elinor. “Well, my dear? What do you say? It is very kindly meant of Charlotte.”
Elinor’s heart failed her at the very idea. To take Marianne, in her present state, into a room full of strangers! To subject her to the curious eyes, the whispered comments, the cheerful nonsense of Mrs. Palmer herself! It was an impossibility.
“Dear ma’am, you must make our apologies to Mrs. Palmer. It is very good of her, but my sister is in no condition to go into company. Nor am I, indeed. You must be so good as to go without us.”
“Go without you! Indeed, I shall do no such thing. What, and leave you two to mope here by yourselves? No, no, that will never do. If Marianne cannot go, you must, at all events. The Colonel will escort us, I am sure. Will you not, Colonel?”
Colonel Brandon, who had listened to this exchange with a grave face, now spoke. “I am at your service, Mrs. Jennings, as you know. But perhaps, in this instance, Miss Dashwood is the best judge. To force society upon those who are in affliction is often a mistaken kindness.”
“Well, I never!” cried Mrs. Jennings. “You are both of you in a league against me. A mistaken kindness! When my own daughter begs to see you! She will be monstrously disappointed.”
“I will write to Mrs. Palmer in the morning,” said Elinor, wearied beyond measure by the perpetual contest. “I will explain it to her myself. I am sure she will understand.”
“Oh, well, if you take it upon yourself, I have no more to say,” said Mrs. Jennings, in a tone of resignation. “But I think it is a great pity.”
The Colonel, seeing Elinor’s evident fatigue, rose soon after to take his leave. His parting words to her were as considerate as all his previous conduct. “I shall be in town for some time,” he said. “If I can be of any use—if, for instance, there are any books from a library that your sister might be persuaded to look at—I should be very happy to procure them.”
This small, practical offer of service, so different from the vague and noisy sympathy of others, touched Elinor deeply. As she returned to the drawing-room after seeing him out, she felt that in Colonel Brandon, she had a friend whose worth she had never before so fully appreciated. His presence had been a shield, and his quiet understanding a balm. Yet the reflection that followed was a bitter one; it was for Marianne’s sake that he showed this kindness, and it was Marianne who, in her blind devotion to a worthless man, was incapable of valuing it. For a moment, Elinor allowed herself to wish that the Colonel’s affections had been fixed on a heart more ready to welcome them, a heart that was not already broken, or, like her own, secretly and hopelessly engaged elsewhere. But the thought was dismissed as quickly as it came, an indulgence she could not afford. Her duty was plain; it was to Marianne, and to Marianne alone, that all her care and all her fortitude must now be devoted.

Elinor endured it all with what patience she could command, answering in monosyllables whenever an answer was required, until at length, pleading a headache, she was permitted to retire.

She stole first to Marianne’s room, and opened the door a crack. All was dark and silent. Hoping that her sister had indeed found the oblivion of sleep, she repaired to her own chamber, not to rest, but to think. The events of the day passed before her mind in a painful procession: Willoughby’s cruel letter, Marianne’s utter collapse, Mrs. Jennings’s suffocating kindness, Colonel Brandon’s quiet dignity. On all of this she pondered, her heart aching with a sorrow that was twofold; for she grieved not only for Marianne’s transparent misery, but for her own, which must be forever concealed. The knowledge that the man she loved was engaged to another was a burden she must bear in silence, without the luxury of a single confidante, or the solace of open grief. While Marianne could indulge her despair to the full, Elinor must be the pillar of strength, the voice of reason, the manager of all the practical difficulties that such a blow entailed. She felt a weariness of spirit that was almost too great to be borne.

The night was long. Several times, she crept to Marianne’s door to listen, but heard nothing but the sighing of the wind and the distant rattle of a late carriage. When at last the grey light of a London morning began to filter through her curtains, she had not slept at all.

She found Marianne already awake, or rather, as it seemed, not having slept. She was sitting in a chair by the window, wrapped in a shawl, her hair uncombed, her eyes staring blankly at the row of houses opposite. She looked pale and fragile, as if a breath could shatter her. She did not turn her head when Elinor entered, nor give any sign that she was aware of her sister’s presence.

“Marianne,” Elinor said softly, “you will take cold. Let me help you back to bed.”

There was no answer. Elinor went to the bed and smoothed the pillows. “Come, dearest. A little more rest.”

Slowly, as if in a trance, Marianne allowed herself to be led back to bed. She lay down and pulled the covers up to her chin, turning her face to the wall. Elinor brought her a cup of tea, but she would not touch it. She would not speak. She seemed to have retreated into a world of her own, a world of silent, inconsolable woe.

Breakfast was a trying meal. Mrs. Jennings, refreshed by a good night’s sleep, was full of plans for the day.

“Well, my dears, I have been thinking. What poor Marianne needs is a little distraction. We cannot have her moping in her room all day. What do you say to a drive to Kensington Gardens? The fresh air would do her a world of good. Or we could go and look at the shops in Bond Street. A new ribbon can sometimes do wonders for a lady’s spirits.”

“I thank you, ma’am,” said Elinor, “but I am afraid my sister is in no state for any such excursion. She is not well enough to leave her room.”

“Not well enough! Lord, child, you will make her ill by coddling her so. The more she stays in her room, the more she will think about her troubles. She must be roused. If she will not go out, we must bring the company to her. I shall send a card to the Middletons. Lady Middleton is so placid and quiet, her company cannot possibly overset her. And Sir John is always so cheerful. He would make her laugh, I am sure of it.”

The thought of Sir John’s boisterous pleasantries being inflicted on Marianne in her present state made Elinor shudder. “On no account, ma’am, I beg you. You must not send to the Middletons. The sight of any one, even her oldest friends, would be more than my sister could bear. You promised yesterday that you would be discreet.”

“And so I have been!” cried Mrs. Jennings, somewhat offended. “I have not said a word to a soul, except to Sir John and my daughters, as I said I would. It was only right that they should know. And I have had a note from Charlotte this very morning. Poor girl, she is quite distracted about it. Here,” she said, pulling a crumpled sheet of paper from her pocket, “you may read it yourself.”

Elinor took the note with a sinking heart. It was, as she had feared, a perfect specimen of Mrs. Palmer’s thoughtless good-nature.

‘Dearest Mama,’ it began, ‘How shocking and dreadful about poor Miss Marianne! I cried for a whole five minutes when I read your letter. Mr. Palmer said I was a goose, and that men jilted girls every day, but I am sure it is the most horrid thing I ever heard of. Willoughby must be a monster! And to think how we all liked him! You must bring the poor Dashwoods to us at once. We are having a little party on Tuesday, only a few friends, and it will be the very thing to cheer them up. We can play lottery-tickets, and Mr. Palmer has promised to read to us from a very funny book. Do come, and tell Miss Marianne not to fret. There are plenty more fish in the sea, as Mr. Palmer says, though I tell him it is a very vulgar expression. Your most affectionate daughter, C. Palmer.’

Elinor folded the note and handed it back. “Mrs. Palmer is very kind,” she said, in a voice that was colder than she intended. “But you must see that it is impossible. To take Marianne to a party!”

“Oh, as to the party, perhaps that would be too much just at first,” conceded Mrs. Jennings. “But a quiet dinner with them. What harm could there be in that?”

“The harm of being seen, of being spoken to, of being an object of pity,” said Elinor with some warmth. “My sister’s affliction is not a thing to be paraded in company. She requires privacy and quiet. If her friends wish to show their kindness, they will show it by leaving her alone.”

“Well, I am sure I do not know what is to be done,” said Mrs. Jennings, looking quite downcast. “I only wish to help. It seems a strange way of helping, to do nothing at all.”

The morning passed heavily. Elinor sat in the drawing-room with her needlework, a book lying unread in her lap, while Mrs. Jennings wrote letters and lamented the perversity of young people in love. Every half hour, Elinor would go to Marianne’s room, only to find her in the same state of silent, motionless despair. On one of these visits, however, as Elinor was about to withdraw, Marianne spoke for the first time.

Marianne had turned from the wall and was looking at her. Her eyes were large and dark in her pale face, and held an expression of such profound misery that Elinor’s heart contracted with pity.

This was the question that Elinor had been dreading. She had hoped, for Marianne’s own sake, that she would not be forced to answer it. But there was no avoiding it now.

“But no promise? No formal, binding promise?” Marianne persisted, her eyes fixed on Elinor’s face.

Elinor hesitated. To admit the truth would be to strip Marianne of her last defence, the belief that she was a wronged and betrayed fiancée. It would be to reduce her to the status of a foolish girl who had given her heart away too easily. Yet to lie was impossible.

“There was no positive engagement,” she said at last, choosing her words with care. “But his attentions were such, his behaviour so particular, that any one would have been justified in supposing his intentions to be serious. He is the one at fault, Marianne, not you. He has behaved without honour.”

A single tear rolled down Marianne’s cheek. “So I have no claim,” she whispered. “I have no right even to my grief. It is all my own folly, my own vanity. I imagined it all.”

“No, Marianne, you did not imagine it. You did not imagine his daily visits, his constant companionship, his declarations of admiration. You did not imagine the lock of hair, or the drive to Allenham. He is a villain. Let that be your comfort.”

“Comfort!” cried Marianne, with a sudden burst of energy that startled Elinor. “What comfort can there be in knowing that the man I love, the man I thought a paragon of all virtues, is a heartless, calculating villain? It would be easier to bear if he had died. Then I could mourn him openly, and cherish his memory. But this! To know that he is alive, and happy, and with another woman, while I… while I am nothing to him. It is more than I can bear.”

She fell back against the pillows, sobbing, not with the wild, convulsive grief of the day before, but with a deep, hopeless anguish that was even more painful to witness. Elinor did not attempt to check her tears. She knew that they must flow, and that words of comfort were useless. She sat beside her, holding her hand, her own heart overflowing with a love and pity so intense that it was almost a physical pain. She felt a rising tide of anger against Willoughby, so fierce and so personal that it surprised her. It was one thing to disapprove of his conduct on general principles; it was another to see its devastating effect on the person she loved most in the world. At that moment, she felt she could have forgiven him any crime but this.

To keep up her spirits! Elinor, on her way upstairs, could not forbear a melancholy smile at the utter inadequacy of such a message. How little did Mrs. Jennings, with all her genuine kindness, understand the nature of such a grief as Marianne’s; a grief that fed upon itself and scorned the very notion of commonplace comfort.

The next morning brought with it no abatement of Marianne’s misery. She remained in her room, and in her bed, declaring herself unequal to the exertion of rising. She would take nothing but a little tea, and when Elinor attempted to draw back the heavy curtains to admit the morning light, she turned her face to the wall with a moan of such profound despair that Elinor’s resolution almost failed her. She felt that to press her sister was a kind of cruelty; yet to leave her to the unchecked indulgence of her sorrow was, she knew, a still greater danger.

“You must make an effort, Marianne, for my sake, if not for your own,” she said, sitting on the bed beside her and taking her cold hand. “This excessive grief is not only a torment to you, but an agony to me. I cannot bear to see you so.”

“Can you not?” replied Marianne, in a voice hollow and faint. “Then you must leave me. My wretchedness is not fit for any one to see. It is my own, and I must bear it alone.”

“Never alone, while I am with you. But you must allow me to help you bear it. And you cannot do so by resigning yourself entirely to it. We must leave this house, Marianne; we must return to Barton. We cannot stay in a place that must be, every hour, a fresh source of pain to you.”

“Leave London!” cried Marianne, starting up with a flash of her former energy. “Never! How can we leave? Is he not here? I must be near him. I may see him; perhaps in his carriage, or walking in the Park. To be in the same city with him is something; to leave it would be to sever the last thread. No, Elinor, I cannot go.”

Elinor’s heart sank. To remain in London, in the very neighbourhood of the man who had so injured her, and of the woman he was to marry, seemed to her the height of imprudence and the most certain way to prolong Marianne’s suffering. Every street, every shop, every public walk would be a torment. But she saw that to argue the point now, in Marianne’s present state of agitation, would be both fruitless and unkind. She therefore said no more of it, and confined her efforts to persuading her sister to take some nourishment, an object she at last achieved, though with great difficulty.

The day passed in this melancholy manner. Elinor divided her time between sitting in silence in Marianne’s darkened room, and enduring the well-meant but wearying conversation of Mrs. Jennings in the drawing-room below. The good lady’s mind was still full of Willoughby’s misconduct and Colonel Brandon’s prospects.

“And so she will not leave her room, poor dear,” she said, as Elinor entered after a long vigil. “Well, it is to be expected. But depend upon it, my dear, a little time is all that is wanted. And I have been thinking, it is the luckiest thing in the world that Colonel Brandon was called away to the country. If he were in town, he would be calling here, you know, and that would hardly be proper, so soon. But when he comes back, things will be different. He will find her softened, you see; more ready to listen to reason. Men do not like a girl to be too high in her spirits. A little melancholy is very becoming, I have often thought.”

Elinor could make no reply to a speculation so unsuited to her own feelings. She was spared the necessity of it by the arrival of visitors. A carriage stopped at the door, and in a few moments, Sir John Middleton and Mrs. Palmer were announced. Mrs. Jennings was all delight and bustle.

“My dear Sir John! Charlotte, my love! I am so glad to see you. I was just this moment wishing for you. Here is poor Miss Elinor, quite worn out with nursing her sister.”

Sir John, whose honest, rosy face was clouded with a look of unwonted solemnity, shook Elinor’s hand with great warmth. “Miss Dashwood,” he said, in a low and confidential tone, “I am devilish sorry for this business, upon my soul I am. I would rather have had my best hunter shot than have had it happen. A damned shabby trick of Willoughby. I always thought there was something a little wanting in him, for all his fine talk.”

Mrs. Palmer, meanwhile, had run up to Elinor and seized both her hands, her face a strange mixture of concern and her habitual glee.

“My dear Miss Dashwood, I am so dreadfully shocked! I have done nothing but cry ever since Mama’s note came this morning. It is the most abominable thing I ever heard in my life! I declare I am quite of your sister’s way of thinking, I should have died on the spot. And to think of his preferring Miss Grey! A poor, sallow-faced thing with no conversation at all, they say. But then, fifty thousand pounds! Lord, what a sum of money! It is a great temptation, you must own. I do not know that I could have resisted it myself, if I had been a man.” And at this idea, she could not suppress a little titter, which she immediately checked with a look of contrition.

“Charlotte!” cried her mother, “how can you run on so? You see how you distress Miss Dashwood.”

“I am sure I would not distress her for the world,” replied Mrs. Palmer, with perfect sincerity. “But one cannot help thinking of it all. And is it true that they are to be married next month? Mama said so in her note. How I should like to see the wedding! I wonder what she will wear.”

“The less said upon the subject the better, Charlotte,” said Sir John, with a gravity that did not often adorn his features. “We are come to see if we can be of any use to the young ladies. My dear Miss Dashwood, if a change of air would do your sister good, Lady Middleton and I would be proud to have you both at Barton Park for as long as you please.”

Elinor, though touched by the kindness of the offer, knew that a removal to a house within sight of their own cottage, and in the very heart of the country they had so lately traversed with Willoughby, was the last thing to be desired. She thanked him with real gratitude, but declined the invitation, giving as a reason her sister’s unfitness for any removal at present.

The conversation then, under Sir John’s well-meant but clumsy guidance, turned to other matters; to the state of the roads, the price of horses, and a new pack of hounds he was about to purchase. Through it all, Elinor sat with an appearance of attention, answering when she was addressed, and trying to be grateful for their intention to divert her, while her thoughts were perpetually returning to the still, silent figure in the room above. Mrs. Palmer continued at intervals to whisper to her observations upon the affair, such as, “Do you think she has his picture? I should certainly have returned it, all in little pieces!” and, “I suppose she will wear black now for a while. It would be very becoming to her complexion.”

Elinor was beginning to feel that she could endure it no longer, when another visitor was announced, and Colonel Brandon entered the room.

His arrival produced an immediate change in the party. Mrs. Jennings’s face brightened with renewed hope; Sir John greeted him with boisterous pleasure; and even Mrs. Palmer’s flightiness was for a moment subdued by the Colonel’s grave and quiet presence. He had returned to town that morning, and had come, as he said, to pay his respects. His eye went at once to Elinor, and then glanced round the room, as if in search of another. A single look at her pale, composed face, and the absence of her sister, was sufficient to inform him that something was amiss.

He took a seat near Elinor, and while the others were engaged in a loud and disjointed conversation, he said to her in a low voice, “I hope Miss Marianne is not unwell?”

The simple question, and the tone of sincere, unobtrusive concern in which it was asked, affected Elinor more than all the noisy sympathy she had yet received. Her composure for a moment deserted her.

“She is very unwell, Colonel,” she replied, her voice trembling slightly. “She has been greatly shocked, and her spirits are unequal to the blow.”

Colonel Brandon’s face, always serious, became sterner. He did not press her with questions, for which she was infinitely thankful. He seemed to understand at once the nature and the cause of the distress. He looked down for a moment, and when he spoke again, it was with a gentleness that conveyed the deepest sympathy.

“I was afraid of this,” he said, almost to himself. “His character was known to me. Had I been in town, I might… but it would have been an interference I had no right to make.” He paused, and then added, with more firmness, “Miss Dashwood, if in any way I can be of service to you, or to your sister; if there is any commission, however difficult, that a friend can execute, I beg you will command me. My time, and my utmost exertions, are entirely at your disposal.”

Elinor was deeply moved. In the midst of the frivolous chatter of the Palmers and the rough good-nature of Sir John, this quiet, manly offer of support was a comfort beyond expression. Here was a sorrow that was understood without being named; a delicacy that shrunk from intrusion, and a strength that could be relied upon.

“I thank you, Colonel,” she said, with a warmth that her usual reserve seldom permitted. “Your kindness is a very great comfort. At present, nothing can be done. Time, and her own good sense, must be her only remedies.”

“Her good sense I have no doubt of,” he replied, with a faint, sad smile, “but in sufferings of this nature, sense is not always the first counsellor to be heard. I, of all men, should know that.”

A look of profound, settled melancholy passed over his features, and Elinor was reminded of the history of his first love, which he had once confided to her. She felt at that moment a bond of fellowship with him; a fellowship of those who had learned to suffer in silence.

Mrs. Jennings, whose eye had been upon them, now broke in. “Well, Colonel, so you are come back at last! And you find us all in a sad taking here. But you must not be down-hearted. We shall all be merry again by-and-by, I have no doubt.”

The Colonel bowed, but made no reply, and after a short time, he rose to take his leave. His adieu to Elinor was marked by the same quiet respect. He simply bowed over her hand and said, “Remember that you have a friend.”

When he was gone, Elinor felt as if the only person in the world who could truly comprehend her situation had left the room. The conversation of the others seemed more than ever vapid and unfeeling. She soon made an excuse to retire once more to her sister’s chamber, leaving Mrs. Jennings to give her daughter and Sir John a more detailed, and doubtless a more highly coloured, account of the whole affair.

She found Marianne lying just as she had left her, her eyes open and fixed upon the ceiling, with a look of hopeless vacuity. Elinor sat down beside her, not to talk, but simply to be present, a silent guardian against the utter solitude of her despair. The afternoon wore on; the sounds of the city grew fainter; and the light in the room began to fade. In the gathering twilight, Marianne at last spoke, without turning her head.

“Is he gone?”

“Colonel Brandon? Yes, he left above an hour ago.”

There was a long silence. Then Marianne said, in a whisper, “And the others? Sir John? Mrs. Palmer?”

“They are gone too. We are quite alone, dearest.”

Marianne turned and hid her face in the pillow. Elinor could hear the stifled sobs that shook her frame. It was a different kind of weeping from the passionate outbursts of the day before; quieter, deeper, and to Elinor, infinitely more alarming. It was the weeping of a heart that had looked its own ruin in the face, and had found no hope. Elinor said nothing. She only stroked her sister’s hair, and waited; waited for the storm to pass, with a patience that was born of the deepest love, and a strength that was drawn from her own secret, and long-practised, self-command. She knew that the night would be long, and the morning no brighter; but for Marianne’s sake, she must not, and she would not, falter.

It was a task of the utmost delicacy, and Elinor’s head ached with the effort of composing her sentences. She sealed the letter, her heart heavy with the knowledge of the sorrow it carried, and resolved to post it herself in the morning, before any of the servants were about.

The following day broke grey and cheerless, a true London morning. Elinor, who had slept but little, was down early, and had the satisfaction of dispatching her letter before Mrs. Jennings had made her appearance. When that lady did at last descend to the breakfast-parlour, it was with an air of determined cheerfulness that Elinor found almost as trying as her more open expressions of sympathy.

“Well, my dear,” she cried, bustling up to the fire, “what a dismal day! I declare, it is enough to give one the vapours. But we must not let it get the better of us. And how is your sister this morning? A little better, I hope?”

“She is still asleep, I believe,” said Elinor. “I looked in upon her just now, and she seemed to be resting quietly.”

“Ah, that is a good sign! The very best sign in the world. There is nothing like a good long sleep for chasing away the blue devils. And you, my dear, you are looking a little pale yourself. I hope you have not been fretting. You must not, you know. It will do no good. We must all have our troubles. Lord knows, I have had my share. But it is no use dwelling upon them. What is done, is done; and as for Mr. Willoughby, he is a scoundrel, and not worth another thought. You must make your sister see that.”

“I am afraid that is a lesson she will be some time in learning,” said Elinor, quietly.

“Oh, nonsense, my dear! She will come round. They all do. Why, I remember when my Charlotte was so taken with that Captain Prentiss, the one who turned out to have a wife and six children in Ireland, she vowed she would never love another. And yet, six months later, she was as merry as a grig, and engaged to Sir John! It is always the way with young people. Their hearts are so resilient. Now, what shall we do to amuse ourselves today? We cannot go out in this weather. Shall I send for the fish-monger? A nice piece of salmon for dinner might tempt your sister’s appetite.”

Elinor could not but be sensible of the good-nature that prompted these suggestions, however ill-judged they might be; and she was endeavouring to frame a suitable reply, when the door opened, and Marianne herself entered the room.

She was dressed, but with a negligence that showed how little she cared for her appearance. Her face was deathly pale, her eyes were heavy and swollen, and she moved with a languor that was painful to behold. She took her seat at the table without speaking, and when Mrs. Jennings pressed her to take some toast, she shook her head in silent refusal.

A most uncomfortable silence fell upon the little party. Mrs. Jennings, for once, seemed at a loss for words. She fidgeted with her tea-cup, glanced from one sister to the other, and at last, unable to bear the gloom any longer, she burst out, “Well, I must say, this is a pretty state of affairs! Three of us, and not a word to throw at a dog! Come, Marianne, my dear, do try to eat a little something. You will make yourself ill. A little bit of muffin, now? Or a nice fresh egg?”

Marianne looked at her with an expression of such settled misery that even Mrs. Jennings was silenced. “I am not hungry,” she said, in a low, toneless voice. “I wish for nothing.”

And so the miserable meal proceeded, in almost total silence, broken only by Mrs. Jennings’s occasional sighs, and the clatter of her spoon against her saucer. As soon as it was over, Marianne rose and returned to her room.

“Poor dear soul!” exclaimed Mrs. Jennings, the moment the door was closed. “It is worse than I thought. She looks quite ghastly. What is to be done with her, Elinor? We cannot let her go on like this. She will fret herself into a fever.”

“We must have patience, ma’am,” said Elinor. “It is all that can be done at present. Her feelings are very strong. It will take time for her to recover from such a blow.”

“Time! yes, but in the meantime, what a life for us all! Not but what I would do any thing in the world for her. But it is so melancholy to see her so. I think, my dear, we must have some company to cheer us up. I will send a note to Charlotte and Sir John. They will be sure to come. And perhaps the Colonel may look in. A little cheerful society is what she needs.”

Elinor could have told her that cheerful society was the very last thing Marianne needed, or would endure, in her present state; but she knew that it was useless to argue. She could only hope that Mrs. Palmer’s high spirits, and Sir John’s boisterous good-humour, would not prove altogether unbearable. She was not, however, prepared for the visitor who was announced a short time later.

It was Colonel Brandon.

He was shown into the drawing-room, where Elinor was sitting alone, attempting to read, though her thoughts were constantly straying to her sister’s chamber. He came forward with an air of quiet concern that was in itself a comfort.

“I was afraid I might be intruding,” he said, his grave eyes resting upon her with an expression of the deepest sympathy, “but I could not rest without enquiring after your sister. And after you,” he added, in a lower voice.

“You are very good,” said Elinor, her own voice unsteady. “My sister, I am sorry to say, is no better. She is very ill.”

“Do not despair,” he said, taking a seat beside her. “I have seen something of this kind of grief before. It is terrible in its first violence, but it exhausts itself by its own force. You must be patient. And you must take care of yourself. You look as if you had not slept.”

There was a warmth, a sincerity, in his manner that touched Elinor to the heart. For the first time since the fatal letter had arrived, she felt the relief of tears. They started to her eyes, and though she strove to repress them, she could not. She turned away her head, that he might not see her emotion.

Colonel Brandon, with a delicacy that she could not but appreciate, rose and walked to the window, as if to observe the weather, leaving her time to recover her composure. When she was herself again, he returned to his seat.

“I have pained you,” he said, in a tone of self-reproach.

“No, no,” she replied. “You have been very kind. I do not know what we should do without our friends. It is only that—I am a little over-wrought.”

You may have heard it said that a living in my gift, the living of Delaford, was promised to Mr. Willoughby. It was so. But under the present circumstances, of course, that engagement is at an end. The living is now vacant, and at my disposal. It occurred to me that—that it might be of service to your brother, Mr. Ferrars.”

Elinor stared at him, her mind in a tumult of surprise and gratitude. Edward! To have a living—a home—a competence! It was the very thing she had been secretly longing for, the solution to all his difficulties. But how could she accept such a favour, from such a quarter, and at such a time?

“Colonel Brandon,” she stammered, “I—I do not know what to say. You are too generous. But I cannot—we cannot—accept such an obligation.”

“It is no obligation,” he said, earnestly. “It is a pleasure. A very great pleasure. I know something of Mr. Ferrars’s situation, of his worth, and of the difficulties under which he labours. To be able to assist such a man would be a source of the purest satisfaction to me. I beg you will not refuse me this gratification. Say nothing of it at present, if you think it better not. But when the time is ripe—when your own affairs are a little more settled—you will perhaps allow me to renew the offer?”

He spoke with so much genuine kindness, so little of the air of a patron, that Elinor found it impossible to persist in her refusal. To be able to carry such news to Edward, to be the instrument of his happiness—it was a prospect too delightful to be relinquished.

“You are the best of men,” she said, her voice trembling with a mixture of emotions. “I can only say, that you have given me the first moment of happiness I have known for many days. And for Edward’s sake, I will not say no.”

A faint smile, the first she had seen, lit up his grave features. “I am answered,” he said. And then, as if unwilling to prolong a scene that had already been so emotional, he rose to take his leave.

“Remember,” he said, as he pressed her hand at parting, “you have a friend. Do not scruple to call upon him.”

He was gone. And Elinor, left to herself, sat for a long time without moving, her mind in a whirl. Colonel Brandon’s generosity, his delicacy, his quiet, constant friendship—all this, contrasted with Willoughby’s selfish perfidy, made a deep impression upon her. He had called himself her friend; and for the first time, she felt that he was indeed something more than a mere acquaintance. He was a man to be esteemed, to be trusted, to be relied upon. Amidst the wreck of her sister’s happiness, and her own secret sorrows, the knowledge of his steady friendship was a rock to which she could cling. It did not make her own burden any lighter; but it gave her strength to bear it. And as she sat there, in the deepening twilight, a new feeling began to mingle with her gratitude; a feeling of respect, of admiration, almost of tenderness, for the man who had shown himself so true a friend in her hour of need.

Dear Ma’am,” replied Elinor, with a civility that cost her a great effort, as she gently took the glass from the other’s hand, “I am very grateful for your kindness, but I fear it would be of no avail. Marianne is not in a state to be soothed by wine; her affliction is not one which such remedies can reach, and I believe the best service we can do her is to allow her the quiet she desires.”

“Well, my dear, you know best, to be sure,” said Mrs. Jennings, a little disappointed at having her cure rejected, but by no means silenced. “If she will not have the Constantia, she will not. But it is a sad business, a very sad business, to see a young creature so dreadfully cast down. And to be alone, too! It is the very worst thing for her. Now if she would but come down, we might have a little music, or a game of speculation. That would take her out of herself a little, would it not? Elinor shuddered at the thought of Marianne being subjected to a game of speculation, or indeed to the society of anyone, in her present state. “I believe, ma’am, that solitude is the only comfort she can at present receive. Lord! if she had but a little of Miss Grey’s fortune, she could have her pick of the dukes in the land, never mind the Willoughbys.”

With this comforting assurance, Mrs. Jennings at last took her leave for the night, and Elinor was left to the quiet of the drawing-room, and the far less comfortable society of her own thoughts. To be obliged to hear Marianne’s most sacred sorrows treated with such coarse familiarity, to be forced into a continual dissimulation of her own feelings, and to stand as a perpetual guard between her sister’s sensibility and the clumsy well-meaning of the world, was a task that exhausted her spirits and tried her patience to the utmost. She sat for a long time, her hands clasped in her lap, looking into the fire, and considering the full extent of their misfortune. Willoughby’s desertion was a blow from which Marianne might never recover; and their situation in London, so far from offering any comfort, seemed only to expose them to fresh sources of pain and humiliation. They were guests in a house where every thing was meant kindly, but where nothing was understood; where delicacy was a thing unknown, and the deepest griefs were matter for common gossip and unthinking advice.

Her own anxieties, too, pressed heavily upon her. The secret of Edward’s engagement, a secret which she had borne so long and with such fortitude, now seemed an almost intolerable burden. While Marianne could give free vent to her despair, Elinor was condemned to a silence and a composure that were every moment on the verge of giving way. To have no one to confide in, no one to offer a word of sympathy, was a solitude far more profound than that which her sister had sought in her own chamber. She thought of her mother, and wished, for a moment, that she had never left Barton. But then, the remembrance of Colonel Brandon’s visit that very afternoon, and of his extraordinary, his unlooked-for generosity, came to her mind. Though the prompt had been misleading about the timing of such a conversation, the essence of his character, his quiet, steadfast friendship, was a truth she had come to feel deeply. His goodness was a single point of light in the surrounding gloom; a proof that integrity and honour were not entirely banished from the world. The thought of him, so calm, so considerate, so different from the thoughtless crowd around them, brought a faint sense of comfort to her weary heart. It was a comfort she sorely needed, for she knew that the trials of the next day would be greater still.

The morning brought with it no abatement of misery. Marianne, who had passed a sleepless night, appeared at the breakfast table, pale and silent, with a fixed look of despondency that went to Elinor’s heart. She would eat nothing, and replied to Mrs. Jennings’s solicitous enquiries with a distant civility that was more expressive of suffering than any complaint. Mrs. Jennings, who had, as she assured Elinor, “thought of nothing else all night,” was full of schemes for Marianne’s relief.

“Now, my dear,” she began, as soon as the tea things were removed, “I have been thinking. We must not let you mope. What you need is a little distraction. Sir John and Lady Middleton are coming to town next week, and the Palmers are already here. We shall have a nice little party. And I was thinking, we might take a drive to the shops this morning. A new bonnet, now, or a length of ribbon, does wonders for the spirits, I always say.”

Marianne’s only reply was to rise from the table and walk to the window, where she stood looking out at the grey London street with an air of such profound wretchedness that even Mrs. Jennings was, for a moment, silenced. Elinor, seeing her sister’s distress, quickly intervened.

“I am afraid, ma’am, that Marianne is not equal to any exertion this morning. Perhaps a little later in the day she may feel better.”

“Well, just as you please, my dear. But I do think it is a mistake to give way. When my poor husband was taken, I was in a dreadful state, I can tell you. But I said to myself, ‘Bridget, this won’t do.’ And I made myself get up and see people, and in a month, I was as cheerful as ever. Not that I had forgotten him, poor man. But what is the use of grieving? It will not bring him back, will it?”

Elinor, despairing of making her understand, was about to lead her sister from the room, when a loud rap at the door announced a visitor. Mrs. Jennings’s face brightened.

“There now! Who can that be, I wonder? So early, too! It must be somebody with news.”

A moment later, the servant entered, announcing not Sir John, nor Mrs. Palmer, but a name that struck both sisters with a very different degree of emotion.

“Colonel Brandon, ma’am.”

Mrs. Jennings was all delight. “Colonel Brandon! Lord, how fortunate! The very man we want to see. Show him up, Thomas, show him up at once.”

Elinor felt a blush of something very like pleasure rise to her cheek. His presence, she knew, would be a support. He, at least, would understand. Marianne, on the other hand, turned from the window with a look of impatience, and seemed about to make her escape. But it was too late. Before she could reach the door, the Colonel was in the room.

He entered with his usual gravity, but his eye immediately sought out the two sisters, and his expression, as it rested upon them, was one of the kindest and most compassionate concern. He bowed to Mrs. Jennings, and then advanced towards them, his manner conveying a sympathy so sincere and so unobtrusive that it could not possibly give offence.

“Miss Dashwood,” he said, addressing himself to Elinor, but with a glance that included her sister, “I was sorry to hear that you had been unwell. I hope I do not intrude.”

“Not at all, Colonel,” replied Elinor, grateful for his delicacy in thus sparing her the pain of any direct allusion to their troubles. “We are very happy to see you.”

Marianne, who had stood, cold and averted, since his entrance, made a slight, involuntary movement, as if to leave. The Colonel saw it, and his next words were evidently intended to detain her.

“I came,” he said, his voice taking on a slightly more formal tone, as if to put them all at their ease, “on a matter of business, in which I was willing to hope I might be of some small service to you, and to Mrs. Dashwood.”

He paused, and looked at Elinor, who, surprised and curious, begged him to go on.

“It is a matter,” he continued, “concerning a living. The incumbency of Delaford, which is in my gift, has lately become vacant. The parish is small, the duties are light, and the parsonage-house, I am assured, is a very comfortable one. It has occurred to me that it might, perhaps, be an acceptable home for a clergyman of merit, who is at present without preferment.”

He stopped again, and the colour deepened in his face. Elinor looked at him, bewildered. What could this possibly have to do with them? She could not imagine. Mrs. Jennings, however, who had been listening with the most eager curiosity, had no such doubts.

“A living!” she cried. “In your gift, Colonel! And a comfortable parsonage-house! Lord, how charming! And who is the lucky man to be? Some friend of yours, I suppose? A deserving young man, no doubt. I do love to see deserving young men provided for.”

Colonel Brandon did not immediately reply. He seemed to be struggling with some embarrassment. At last, turning to Elinor with an air of resolution, he said, in a low voice, “The gentleman I have in mind is a Mr. Ferrars.”

Elinor’s heart gave a sudden, violent leap. Edward! A living for Edward! The blood rushed to her face, and then as quickly receded, leaving her pale and trembling. She could not speak. She could only stare at him, her mind in a tumult of astonishment, gratitude, and a thousand other conflicting emotions. To have this, the one thing she had most desired for Edward, offered to him now, at such a time, and from such a quarter! It was almost more than she could bear.

Marianne, who had heard the name with indifference, was struck by her sister’s violent agitation. She looked from Elinor to the Colonel, and back again, a dawning suspicion in her eyes. What connection could there be between this Mr. Ferrars, and her sister, to cause such a powerful emotion?

Colonel Brandon, seeing Elinor’s distress, and mistaking its cause, hastened to add, “I fear I have been too abrupt. I should have prepared you. But I had heard—I was led to believe—that Mr. Ferrars was a person in whom you and your family were… interested. If I have been misinformed, I can only beg your pardon for my intrusion.”

“No, no,” whispered Elinor, recovering herself with a great effort. “You have not been misinformed. He is… he is a very old friend. A friend of my brother’s. We are all… very much attached to him.”

“Then you will not, I hope, be displeased at my interference?”

“Displeased! Oh, Colonel Brandon,” she cried, her voice trembling with a sincerity that could not be mistaken, “how can you say so? It is the most generous, the most considerate thing… I do not know how to thank you.”

Tears sprang to her eyes, and she was forced to turn away to hide them. For the first time since Willoughby’s letter, she felt a ray of genuine happiness. The prospect of being able to bestow such a blessing on Edward, of being the instrument of his independence and his comfort, was a joy so pure and so unexpected that it almost overwhelmed her.

Colonel Brandon was deeply affected. To see Elinor, the calm, the self-possessed Elinor, so moved, was a sight that touched him to the quick. He said nothing, but his look of quiet satisfaction was more eloquent than any words.

Mrs. Jennings, who had been watching this scene with open-mouthed astonishment, now broke in. “Mr. Ferrars! Good Lord! You mean Lucy Steele’s Mr. Ferrars? The one who is engaged to her? Well, I never! So you are giving the living to him! What a good, kind soul you are, Colonel. To be sure, he will need it, poor young man, for they say his mother has cut him off without a shilling. And to think of you doing it! Well, it is the handsomest thing I ever heard of. He will be able to marry his Lucy at last. And they will have a nice little house, and every thing comfortable about them. How happy they will be! And it is all your doing. I declare, you are the best man in the world.”

At the mention of Lucy Steele, a change came over the whole party. The brief moment of joy was gone. A chill, as of death, fell upon them. Elinor, who had forgotten every thing but Edward’s happiness, now remembered it all. Edward was not free. He was engaged to another. And this living, this gift of a benevolent friend, would only serve to hasten a marriage which would be his misery, and her own. The blood drained from her face, and she sank back in her chair, feeling as if she had received a mortal blow. The contrast between the bright hopes of a moment before, and the bleak reality that now confronted her, was too cruel. She covered her face with her hands, unable to bear the sight of the others’ eyes upon her.

Marianne, who had been observing her sister with a growing sense of alarm, now sprang to her side. “Elinor! What is it? Are you ill? Speak to me!”

Colonel Brandon, equally shocked and bewildered, looked from one sister to the other in silent consternation. He had intended to give pleasure; he saw that he had inflicted the most exquisite pain. What was the meaning of it? What was this mystery of which he was so ignorant?

Mrs. Jennings, alone, was unembarrassed. “Poor thing!” she said, in a low, pitying voice. “It has been too much for her. First her sister’s troubles, and now this. To be sure, it is a sad thing for her to think of him marrying another woman. For you know, Colonel,” she added, turning to him in a confidential whisper, “we all thought it was to be a match between him and Miss Elinor. And so it would have been, I dare say, if it had not been for that artful little minx, Lucy Steele. But there! it cannot be helped now. The young man must keep his word, I suppose. And it is a great comfort that you have provided for them so handsomely.”

The Colonel heard her with a cold civility, his eyes never leaving Elinor’s averted face. He was beginning to understand. The whole miserable story, which had been a puzzle to him for so long, was now becoming clear. Elinor’s reserve, her occasional sadness, her want of spirits, all were explained. She loved this Mr. Ferrars, and he was engaged to another. And he, Brandon, in his blundering kindness, had come to announce the very event that would seal her unhappiness. The thought was agony to him. He would have given worlds to recall his words.

He rose from his seat. “I have been unfortunate,” he said, his voice husky with emotion. “I have caused a distress which I would have given any thing to prevent. Forgive me. I will take my leave.”

He moved towards the door. But Elinor, hearing his words, roused herself from her stupor. She could not let him go thus, with such a mistaken idea of her feelings. It would be an injustice to his generosity, and a betrayal of her own self-respect. With a powerful effort, she mastered her agitation, and rose to her feet.

“Colonel Brandon, you must not go,” she said, her voice low but firm. “You must not misunderstand me. I am not… I am not ungrateful. Your offer… it is everything that is kind and noble. And for Edward’s sake… for Mr. Ferrars’s sake… I thank you from the bottom of my heart. If I have been… overcome… it is only because… because I am not very well today. My sister’s illness… has upset me. Do not think any thing more of it.”

It was a brave speech, but it did not deceive him. He saw the effort it cost her, and he honoured her for it. He saw the pale, composed face, the tearless eyes, and the slight trembling of the hands, and he understood, better than any one else in the room, the depth of the suffering that lay beneath that calm exterior. He bowed, without speaking, and his look of silent, respectful compassion was more comforting to Elinor than any words could have been.

Marianne, meanwhile, was lost in a maze of conjecture. Elinor in love with Mr. Ferrars! The man she had always considered so dull, so spiritless, so wanting in every thing that could engage a woman’s affections! And Elinor, her calm, her prudent Elinor, to have a secret attachment, an unhappy love! It was a revelation that overturned all her preconceived ideas. It gave her a new and startling insight into her sister’s character. For the first time, she began to suspect that Elinor’s composure might not be the result of a cold heart, but of a strong mind; that her own sorrows, which she had thought so unique and so overwhelming, might not be the only sorrows in the world. She looked at her sister with a new feeling of respect, of pity, and of awe. And in that moment, a bond of sympathy was forged between them, stronger than any that had existed before.

Elinor did not immediately return to her sister’s room. She stood for a moment in the passage, gathering her thoughts, the fine cut of the glass cool against her trembling fingers. Colonel Brandon’s hasty departure, his look of pained understanding, had unsettled her far more than she had allowed to appear. He knew. And Marianne knew. The secret she had guarded for so long, with such painful diligence, was now no secret at all to the two people, besides herself, whom it most concerned. There was a strange mixture of terror and relief in the thought.

When she re-entered the bedchamber, she found Marianne no longer huddled by the fire, but sitting upright on the edge of her bed, her hands clasped in her lap. The wildness of her grief had subsided, replaced by an expression of deep and wondering thought. She looked at Elinor, not as she had ever looked at her before, but with an enquiry so searching, so full of a new and timid sympathy, that Elinor’s own composure was threatened anew.

“He is gone?” Marianne asked, her voice low.

“Colonel Brandon? Yes, he has taken his leave.”

“And he knows,” Marianne continued, less as a question than a statement. “He understood it all. Your face, Elinor, when he spoke of Mr. Ferrars…”

Elinor set the wine-glass down on a small table. “I was surprised by his intelligence, nothing more. It was not a subject I expected to hear of from him.”

“No, it was not surprise,” Marianne insisted, shaking her head. “It was pain. I saw it. I, who have been so blind, so wrapped in my own selfish misery that I could not see the sorrow in my own sister’s heart. Oh, Elinor, how you must have suffered! And in silence! While I have paraded my grief for all the world to see, you have borne a heavier burden without a single complaint.”

Tears, no longer for herself, but for her sister, began to stream down Marianne’s face. She rose and threw her arms around Elinor’s neck, weeping with a contrition that was as passionate as her grief had been. “Forgive me! How could I be so unfeeling, so neglectful? To think that all this time, while I was accusing you of coldness, of being without feeling, you were concealing a sorrow perhaps greater than my own.”

Elinor returned the embrace, her own eyes at last filling with tears. It was a comfort so exquisite, so long unsought and unexpected, that it quite overcame her. To be understood, to be pitied by Marianne, was a balm to her wounded spirit. “My dearest, kindest Marianne,” she whispered, “you have nothing to forgive. How could you guess what I took such pains to conceal? It was my own choice. I could not have done otherwise.”

They sat down together upon the bed, hand in hand, and in the dim light of the single candle, the barrier of reserve that had grown between them was at last broken down. Marianne, with the gentleness of her new-found empathy, did not press with impatient questions, but waited for Elinor to speak. And Elinor, feeling the relief of confession after so long a silence, began her story.

She spoke of her first acquaintance with Edward Ferrars at Norland, of the quiet affinity that had grown between them, of the diffidence and melancholy that she had at first attributed to his nature, but which she soon suspected had a more particular cause. She recounted, in a low and steady voice, the dreadful afternoon at Barton when Lucy Steele, with cruel cunning, had confided the secret of her four-year engagement to Edward.

“An engagement of honour, Marianne,” Elinor explained, her voice faltering for the first time. “Made when he was a boy, and she a girl with no fortune and few connections. A promise he feels he cannot in honour break, though I know, I believe, that his heart is no longer hers. He is bound. And I, by my knowledge of it, am bound to silence.”

Marianne listened with an attention that was wholly new to her. She, who had always scorned concealments and measured attachments, now saw a heroism in her sister’s conduct that put her own romantic ideals to shame. Elinor’s steady endurance, her quiet loyalty not only to Edward but to his promise, her refusal to betray a confidence even to her dearest sister; it was a display of fortitude that Marianne could only look upon with awe.

“And you have known this all winter?” she asked in a hushed voice. “Since the Steeles came to Barton? And you have borne it all, and borne with me, too?”

“I would have told you,” Elinor said, squeezing her hand, “if I had thought it would do any good. But to what end? It would only have distressed you, and I could not have borne to see your indignation on my behalf. There was nothing to be done, but to wait.”

“To wait! Oh, Elinor, what a lesson you have taught me. I thought my own situation desperate, but at least it was decisive. Willoughby is lost to me forever; it is a clear and certain misery. But you have been condemned to the far worse torture of uncertainty, of hope warring with principle. And Colonel Brandon! His offer today was for Mr. Ferrars, was it not? To give him a living, to make it possible for him… for him to marry Miss Steele.”

The irony was not lost on Elinor. “He could not have known. His motive was pure kindness; a desire to assist a man he thought deserving, and perhaps,” she added with a faint blush, “to do me a service. I believe he has some notion of my… my regard for the family.”

“He has a notion of more than that,” Marianne said, with a flash of her old perceptiveness. “He esteems you, Elinor. I saw it in his eyes. He is a good man. How strange that we have both been so mistaken in our judgements of men.”

The conversation might have continued in this vein for much longer, for Marianne, having been admitted to her sister’s confidence, had a thousand questions to ask and a thousand protestations of sympathy to offer. But they were interrupted by a gentle tap at the door.

“Elinor, my dear, are you there?” It was Mrs. Jennings. “I have brought up a little warm broth for your sister. She must have something to sustain her, poor love.”

Elinor rose and opened the door to find Mrs. Jennings standing there with a steaming bowl. Her face, which a moment ago had been alight with concern, was now beaming with a new piece of intelligence.

“Well, my dears, what do you think?” she began, hardly waiting for Elinor to take the broth from her. “Lady Middleton has sent a card! An invitation for a small musical evening a week from Tuesday. She particularly requests your company, for she has heard so much of your playing, Marianne, and she is quite determined to hear you. Is it not the very thing to cheer us all up?”

Marianne, who would formerly have declared such an evening to be an insupportable torment, looked at Elinor. She saw the weariness in her sister’s face, and the effort with which she was maintaining her composure before Mrs. Jennings.

“That is very kind of Lady Middleton,” Marianne said, her voice surprisingly steady. “We are much obliged to her.”

Mrs. Jennings was taken aback. She had expected a refusal, tears, a declaration that music was now a source of pain, not pleasure. To receive such a calm, if not enthusiastic, reply was most unexpected.

“Well, to be sure it is,” she said, recovering herself. “And you will go, will you not? It would be a shame to disappoint her. Sir John will be there, and you know how he enjoys a bit of company. It will do you a world of good, Marianne, to get out of this house. A change of scene is the best remedy for low spirits, I always say.”

“If Elinor thinks it proper, we shall be happy to attend,” Marianne replied, her eyes still fixed on her sister.

Elinor felt a surge of gratitude. This was a new Marianne indeed; one who could think of another’s feelings, who could sacrifice her own inclinations for the sake of propriety and kindness. “I believe it would be a very good thing,” Elinor said, turning to Mrs. Jennings. “We should not wish to appear unsociable, and Lady Middleton’s kindness deserves a proper acknowledgement.”

“There now!” cried Mrs. Jennings, triumphant. “Did I not say so? Excellent! I shall send a reply at once. And you, Marianne, you must drink this broth. And Elinor, my dear, you look quite done in. You have been playing the nurse for too long. It is time someone took care of you.”

She bustled away, full of plans and satisfaction, leaving the sisters once more alone. The invitation, a trifle in itself, had served to solidify the new understanding between them. It was a step back into the world, but a step they would now take together, with a shared knowledge and a mutual support that had been wanting before.

In the week that followed, a subtle but profound change was apparent in the little household in Berkeley Street. Marianne, though still pale and often silent, had lost the air of restless, romantic affliction. She no longer sought solitude to nurse her grief, but remained with her sister, assisting her in small household tasks, reading aloud, or simply sitting in quiet companionship. She observed Elinor with a new and tender solicitude, watching for signs of fatigue or sadness, and trying, in her own small ways, to lighten her sister’s load. She even began to take an interest in the affairs of others, listening with a surprising patience to Mrs. Jennings’s daily budget of news and gossip.

Elinor, for her part, felt the immense relief of a burden shared. Though her anxiety for Edward was in no way diminished, it was no longer a solitary anxiety. She could speak of him to Marianne, and find in her sister not the impetuous, judgmental critic she might once have been, but a sympathetic and sensible friend. Their conversations were a source of infinite comfort to her, and she found that in explaining her own feelings, she came to understand them better herself.

Colonel Brandon called again within a few days, a visit which Elinor had been dreading, but which passed off with less awkwardness than she had feared. He directed his conversation chiefly to Mrs. Jennings, but his manner towards both sisters was marked by a quiet, respectful gravity. He made no allusion to his previous visit, but his eyes, when they rested on Elinor, were full of a compassion that she found more comforting than any words. To Marianne, he showed a particular gentleness, as if he understood the change in her, and wished to acknowledge it. He was no longer the silent, melancholy older man she had once dismissed; she saw him now as her sister did, as a man of deep feeling and unwavering integrity. She found herself able to speak to him without embarrassment, and even to feel a sense of gratitude for his steady, unobtrusive friendship.

The evening of Lady Middleton’s party arrived. Marianne, who had not appeared in company since the news of Willoughby’s marriage, dressed with a simple elegance that was far more becoming than her former fashionable attire. She allowed Elinor to arrange her hair, and submitted to Mrs. Jennings’s admiring inspection with a patient smile.

“Well, you look charming, my dear,” that good lady declared. “Quite yourself again. And so you should. There is no sense in letting a rascal like Willoughby spoil your pretty face. There will be other young men, you know. Plenty of them! And who knows, Colonel Brandon may be there tonight! I gave him a hint that you were going.”

Marianne flushed, but did not reply. The time was past when such a speech would have thrown her into an agony of agitation. She merely glanced at Elinor, who gave her a small, encouraging smile.

The party was much as they had expected: a crowded room, a great deal of polite but meaningless conversation, and a general air of stuffy formality. Lady Middleton greeted them with a cool civility, Sir John with his usual boisterous good-nature. Elinor found herself seated next to Mrs. Palmer, who immediately began to question her, in a series of loud whispers, about Marianne’s health.

“I am so glad to see your sister out and about! Poor thing, she has been looking shockingly. But it is all over now, is it not? Sir John told me he is married. A rich girl, they say. Well, it is all for the best, I am sure. Men are so tiresome, are they not? Mr. Palmer is forever complaining about something. But I tell him, it does no good to complain. One must just make the best of things.”

Elinor, by long practice, was adept at making the sort of noncommittal replies that such a conversation required. Her chief concern, however, was for Marianne. She saw her seated at the pianoforte, surrounded by a small group of admirers who were pressing her to play. She saw the hesitation in her sister’s face, and her heart went out to her. To play the very airs that she had once played for Willoughby, in a room full of strangers, must be an ordeal of the first magnitude.

But then she saw Marianne lift her head, and a look of quiet determination settled on her features. She looked across the room and met Elinor’s eye. And in that glance, Elinor read a message of courage and resolve. Marianne was no longer the slave of her own sensibility. She was her own mistress.

Her fingers touched the keys, and after a moment’s pause, the first notes of a simple, plaintive melody filled the room. It was not one of the passionate, complicated pieces she had once favoured. It was a gentle, unassuming air, full of a sweet and tender melancholy. She played it with a purity and feeling that silenced the idle chatter in the room, and drew all eyes to her. There was no theatricality in her performance, no trace of the self-conscious artist. She was simply making music, and finding in it a source of solace and expression.

When she had finished, there was a moment of respectful silence, followed by a murmur of genuine admiration. Colonel Brandon, who had been standing near the door, made his way to her side. He did not compliment her on her playing, but simply said, in a low voice, “Thank you. That was beautifully done.”

Marianne looked up at him, and for the first time, smiled a genuine, unforced smile. “I am glad you enjoyed it, Colonel,” she said.

Not like some I could name,” she added, with a very pointed look at the Colonel. “No, no, a steady, respectable man with a good fortune and a good character is worth a dozen of them.”

Elinor felt an agony of embarrassment, both for herself and for the Colonel, who looked as if he would willingly have been anywhere else in the world. He stood quite still, his hat in his hand, his eyes fixed on the carpet. “Is Miss Marianne… is she unwell?” he asked at length, in a low voice.

“Unwell! Poor soul, she is heartbroken,” replied Mrs. Jennings. “Gone to her room, and will not see a soul. Elinor has just put her to bed. It is a mercy she has such a sister to look after her. But she will get over it. Lord, yes! Girls always do. And there are as good fish in the sea as ever came out of it, as I always say. She will be married before the year is out, you mark my words.”

“Ma’am,” said Elinor, her voice trembling slightly with suppressed vexation, “I am sure Colonel Brandon is very sorry for our trouble, but it can be of no amusement to him to hear it discussed so freely.”

“Not at all,” said the Colonel, recovering himself a little and taking a seat at Elinor’s gentle invitation. “I assure you, Miss Dashwood, I feel nothing but the deepest sympathy. If there is any service, any assistance I can offer, you have only to command me.”

“You are very kind, Colonel,” Elinor replied, her voice softening. “But there is nothing to be done. The harm is complete, and we must only endeavour to bear it with as much fortitude as we can.”

A short but heavy silence fell upon the room. Mrs. Jennings, for once, seemed to have been silenced by Elinor’s gentle rebuke. The Colonel, for his part, seemed lost in his own grave reflections. It was he who finally broke the silence. “I believe,” he said, looking at Elinor, “that I have some small acquaintance with the family of the lady in question. The Greys. I knew her uncle, Mr. Henshawe, many years ago.”

“Indeed!” said Elinor, with a flicker of interest.

“Yes. A man of considerable property. Miss Grey, as I understand it, is his principal heir.”

“Fifty thousand pounds!” interjected Mrs. Jennings, her spirits reviving at the mention of so specific a sum. “And a stylish girl, they say, though not a beauty.”

The Colonel looked pained. “I believe her to be a perfectly amiable young woman,” he said, with a certain coldness. “But her personal attractions, or want of them, can have little to do with the matter. A fortune of that size is an attraction in itself.” There was a bitterness in his tone that surprised Elinor, and she looked at him with a new curiosity. He seemed to feel her gaze, for he immediately added, in a lighter tone, “But this is all idle speculation. My chief concern is for your sister. I hope she will soon recover her spirits.”

He did not stay long after that. He seemed to feel, as Elinor did, that his presence, however well-intentioned, could only add to the awkwardness of the situation. He took his leave with the same quiet gravity with which he had entered, pressing Elinor’s hand with a warmth that spoke more than words of his sympathy and regard.

When he was gone, Mrs. Jennings could no longer contain herself. “Well, my dear! What did I tell you? He is head over ears in love, that is plain to see. Did you mark how his face changed when he spoke of her? And his coming here tonight, so late! It can only mean one thing. Oh, it will be a match! I am sure of it. Delaford is a thousand times better than Allenham, and he is a thousand times the better man. It is all for the best, you will see.”

“Dear ma’am,” said Elinor, wearily, “pray do not speak of it. It is too soon. It is cruel to Marianne, and it is unfair to Colonel Brandon. Let us not be so ready to dispose of their hands and hearts before they have even had time to recover from this day’s events.”

“Well, well, you are always so sensible, Elinor. But you cannot stop an old woman from hoping. And now, my dear, you look quite worn out. You must go to bed yourself. Things will look better in the morning, they always do.”

Elinor doubted it very much, but she was too tired to argue. She bid Mrs. Jennings good-night and retired to her own room, which adjoined her sister’s. She listened at the communicating door for a moment, but heard no sound. Marianne was either asleep, or lying in that silent, motionless misery which is worse than any outcry. Elinor’s own heart was heavy, not only for her sister, but for the general folly and injustice of the world. She thought of Willoughby, of his charm and his deceit; of Miss Grey, with her fifty thousand pounds and her want of beauty; of Colonel Brandon, with his quiet sorrow and his patient love. And she thought of herself, and of Edward, and a sigh escaped her that was as full of pain as any that her sister had uttered. But she did not allow herself to dwell on her own troubles. She had a part to play, a duty to perform. She was the sentinel, and she must not sleep at her post.

The next morning brought with it no abatement of their troubles. Marianne, when she at last emerged from her room, was a picture of such pale and settled despondency that Elinor’s heart ached to see her. She would not eat; she would not speak; she sat by the window, her eyes fixed on the dreary London street, as if she saw nothing of the busy life that passed before her. Mrs. Jennings, with a tact for which Elinor was profoundly grateful, forbore to make any direct mention of the previous day’s events. She kept up a steady stream of cheerful, inconsequential chatter, relating anecdotes of her daughters, describing the latest fashions, and planning a series of excursions and entertainments for the coming weeks. To all of this, Marianne paid not the slightest heed. She did not seem to hear it.

Elinor tried to persuade her to go out, to take a little air, but Marianne refused with a faint shake of her head. “Why should I go out?” she said, her voice a mere whisper. “There is nothing to see. And I do not wish to be seen.”

“It would do you good, Marianne. The fresh air would revive you.”

“Nothing will revive me,” she replied, and turned her face again to the window.

The day passed in this melancholy fashion. In the afternoon, they were subjected to a visit from Lady Middleton and Mrs. Palmer. Sir John, with a delicacy that did him credit, had stayed away, but his wife and his mother-in-law, prompted by a curiosity that they disguised as concern, had come to see for themselves the extent of the disaster. Lady Middleton was all cool, elegant sympathy. She said very little, but her manner conveyed a gentle, yet unmistakable, condemnation of Marianne’s want of prudence. Mrs. Palmer, on the other hand, was all volubility and thoughtless good-humour.

“My dear Miss Dashwood!” she cried, seizing Elinor’s hand. “I am so excessively sorry to hear of your trouble! It is so shocking! But I am not at all surprised. I always thought Mr. Willoughby was a sad flirt. But men are all so provoking, are they not? Mr. Palmer is forever in a bad temper about something. But I tell him it does no good to fret. One must just laugh at it all.”

She then turned her attention to Marianne, who had not risen at their entrance, and who sat, pale and silent, in her corner. “Poor Miss Marianne! You look shockingly. But you must not mind it so much. There are plenty of other young men. Sir John says there is a Colonel who is very much in love with you. Is that not droll? I should like to be in love. It must be so amusing.”

Elinor felt her blood run cold. She cast a desperate look at Mrs. Jennings, who, to do her justice, immediately came to the rescue. “Charlotte, my love, you are talking nonsense. Come and look at this new screen I have bought. It is the prettiest thing you ever saw.” And she drew her daughter away to the other side of the room, leaving Elinor to deal with Lady Middleton.

That lady, however, had no intention of engaging in a difficult conversation. She made a few polite inquiries about Mrs. Dashwood’s health, expressed a hope that they would soon see them at a little party she was planning, and then rose to take her leave. Mrs. Palmer, recalled to her duty, followed suit, bestowing on Marianne a final piece of advice. “You should wear a little rouge, my dear. You are so excessively pale. It would make all the difference.”

When they were gone, a profound silence filled the room. Elinor dared not look at her sister. She felt a burning indignation against their visitors, and a deep and painful pity for Marianne, who had been forced to endure such an exhibition of unfeeling curiosity. At last, she ventured to speak. “I am sorry, Marianne. I am so very sorry you were exposed to that.”

To her surprise, Marianne turned from the window and looked at her. There were no tears in her eyes, but a look of such utter weariness and despair that it was more moving than any tears. “It does not signify,” she said, in a low, flat voice. “Nothing signifies now. They may say what they please. They may think what they please. It is all one to me.”

And then, for the first time, she began to weep; not with the passionate, theatrical grief of the day before, but with a quiet, hopeless sobbing that seemed to tear at Elinor’s very heart. Elinor went to her, and put her arms around her, and for a long time they sat thus, the two sisters clinging to each other in a world that had suddenly grown cold and hostile. In that silent embrace, Elinor found a small and bitter comfort. The storm had not passed. It was breaking over them now, in all its fury. And they could only hold on, and wait for it to end.

Elinor, to gratify her, drank a little of the wine, which was indeed of a remarkable quality; but its warmth offered little comfort to a heart so chilled with anxiety. Mrs. Jennings, seeing her comply, seemed satisfied, and began to bustle about the room, tidying what was already tidy, and talking all the while.

“Well, I must say, it is a shocking business altogether. I have been thinking it all over, and the more I think of it, the more abominable Mr. Willoughby’s behaviour appears. To think of his trifling with a dear sweet girl like your sister in such a way! And for what? For a Miss Grey with fifty thousand pounds, who by all accounts is as plain as a pikestaff and has a temper to match. But that is the way of the world. I have seen it a hundred times. A young man gets his head turned by a fortune, and all his promises go for nothing. Well, he will live to regret it, I have no doubt. A man who begins his life by behaving so ill to one woman, is not likely to make another happy. You may be sure Miss Grey will have her troubles with him. He will spend her fifty thousand pounds in a twelvemonth, with his curricles and his high-flying ways, and then where will she be?”

Elinor listened with a heavy heart, wishing that Mrs. Jennings would choose any other subject for her discourse, yet knowing that to stop her was beyond her power. She made no reply, hoping her silence might at length discourage her; but Mrs. Jennings was not to be so easily silenced.

“And to think of the letter he sent! I did not see it, of course, but from what you said, it must have been a very cold, unfeeling sort of thing. Not even a proper apology, I dare say. Just a few lines to say he was going to be married, and that was all. It is just like a man. They have no notion of what a woman suffers in such a case. They break your heart, and then they expect you to be perfectly composed and wish them joy. Lord, I should like to give Mr. Willoughby a piece of my mind! I should tell him what I think of him, and his Miss Grey too. A man has no right to make a woman love him, and then cast her off for a richer match. It is downright wicked, and the law ought to prevent it.”

“Indeed, Ma’am,” said Elinor, rousing herself at last, “there is no law that can reach such an offence. It is a breach of honour, not of contract. And as I mentioned before, there was no positive engagement.”

“No positive engagement! My dear, you are too good. You would find excuses for the devil himself. Why, Sir John was speaking of it only last week as a thing quite settled. He will be in such a passion when he hears of it! He was so fond of Mr. Willoughby. But he will be ready to call him out now, I’ll be bound.”

“I hope Sir John will do no such thing,” cried Elinor in alarm. “For my sister’s sake, there must be no public quarrel. The less this unhappy affair is talked of, the better for her. Her peace of mind is my first concern; and it can only be promoted by silence and privacy.”

“You are right, my dear, you are quite right. We must hush it up as much as possible. I shall tell Sir John he must not say a word. And as for my daughters, they will be as secret as the grave. It is a great pity, though, that we cannot give Mr. Willoughby the punishment he deserves. But we must think of your poor sister. And what is to be done for her? She cannot mope in her room for ever. We must try to divert her. Perhaps a little drive in the Park tomorrow, if the weather is fine?”

“I think not, Ma’am. She is in no state for driving, or for any kind of exertion. Rest and seclusion are all she needs at present.”

“Well, you know best, my dear. But I cannot help thinking that a little fresh air would do her good. And it would be a change of scene, at any rate. But we shall see how she is in the morning. And now, my dear, you must go to bed yourself. You look quite worn out. And do not fret too much. Things will come round, you will see. Your sister is young, and she will get over it. And who knows? Colonel Brandon may have her yet. He is a much better man than Mr. Willoughby, I can tell you. And Delaford is a much prettier place than Allenham. So it may all be for the best in the end.”

With this comfortable reflection, Mrs. Jennings at last took her leave, and Elinor was left to the solitude she had been craving. But solitude brought no relief. Her mind was a chaos of painful thoughts. She thought of Marianne, and her heart ached with a pity so intense that it was almost a physical pain. She thought of Willoughby, and a cold, hard anger settled in her breast. She thought of his protestations, his seeming tenderness, his irresistible charm, and she could not reconcile them with his present conduct. Was he a cold, calculating villain, who had deliberately trifled with her sister’s affections? Or was he a weak, unprincipled young man, who had yielded to the temptation of a great fortune? Either way, his guilt was undeniable, and the misery he had caused was immeasurable.

And then, in the midst of her anger and her grief, another thought intruded, a thought she had been trying to suppress all day. She thought of her own secret sorrow, of Lucy Steele and her engagement to Edward. And for a moment, her fortitude deserted her. A wave of self-pity, as bitter as it was unfamiliar, washed over her. She too had a heart that was wounded, a hope that was blighted. But she had no right to indulge her own grief. She must be strong for Marianne. She must conceal her own feelings, and devote herself to soothing her sister’s. With a sigh that seemed to come from the very depths of her being, she rose and went to her own room, not to sleep, but to watch and to wait.

The next morning brought no improvement in Marianne’s condition. She remained in her room, refusing all nourishment, and answering Elinor’s anxious inquiries with a listless indifference that was more alarming than any passionate grief. She would not get up; she would not speak; she would only lie with her face turned to the wall, as if she wished to shut out a world that had become unbearable to her.

Mrs. Jennings, on being informed of this, was all for sending for a doctor. “Depend upon it, my dear, the girl is in a fever. Her pulse must be very high, and her blood is all in a ferment. We must have Mr. Harris come and see her. He will order her to be bled, and that will relieve her head at once.”

Elinor, however, who knew that her sister’s malady was of the mind, not the body, would not hear of it. “Indeed, Ma’am, you are mistaken. Marianne has no fever. She is suffering from a great shock, and her nerves are over-wrought. A doctor could do her no good. All she needs is time and patience.”

“Time and patience! Lord, my dear, you will be the death of her with your time and patience! When a person is ill, they need a remedy. And what remedy is better than a good bleeding and a few calming draughts? I shall send for Mr. Harris this very instant.”

It was with the greatest difficulty that Elinor could prevent her from carrying out this alarming resolution. She was obliged to represent to her, in the strongest terms, the terror that such a visit would inspire in Marianne, and the certain injury it would do her. She was forced to promise that if Marianne were not better by the evening, she would consent to a doctor being called. And even then, Mrs. Jennings yielded with a very bad grace, declaring that she washed her hands of the consequences, and that if Miss Marianne were to die, it would not be her fault.

The day passed heavily and anxiously. Elinor divided her time between her sister’s bedside and the drawing-room, where she was obliged to endure the well-meant but wearisome consolations of Mrs. Jennings. In the afternoon, they were visited by Sir John Middleton, who had just heard the news from his mother-in-law, and had come, as he said, to see how the poor girls were bearing up.

His sympathy, though sincere, was of a boisterous and unthinking kind, that was almost as trying as Mrs. Jennings’s. He cursed Willoughby for a scoundrel and a rascal, and swore that if he ever met him, he would horsewhip him within an inch of his life. He declared that Marianne was a thousand times too good for him, and that she was well rid of such a villain. And then, by way of comfort, he assured Elinor that there were plenty of other young men in the world, and that her sister, with her beauty and her accomplishments, was sure to get a husband before six months were out.

“There’s Brandon, now,” he said, lowering his voice to a confidential whisper. “He’s the very man for her. A better fellow never lived. And he has been in love with her from the first. I knew it all along, though he never said a word. He is a little melancholy, to be sure, but that’s because he has had his troubles. But she would soon cheer him up. And Delaford is a very pretty place. Not so large as Barton Park, perhaps, but very snug and comfortable. They would be as happy as the day is long. You must talk to her about it, Miss Dashwood. You must tell her what a fine fellow he is, and what a good husband he would make.”

Elinor, inexpressibly pained by this coarse and unseasonable matchmaking, could only reply that her sister was in no condition to think of Colonel Brandon, or of any man. She begged him, as she had begged Mrs. Jennings, to forbear any mention of the subject to Marianne, and to use his influence to prevent it from becoming a topic of general conversation.

Sir John, though a little disappointed that his scheme was not more warmly received, was good-natured enough to promise compliance. “To be sure, to be sure,” he said, “we must not plague the poor girl. She must have her time to get over it. But when she is a little recovered, you will not forget to put in a good word for Brandon. He is a capital fellow, and I have set my heart on seeing him married to your sister.”

He then took his leave, and Elinor was left once more to her anxious watch. The evening brought no change for the better. Marianne remained in the same state of torpid misery, and Elinor’s heart began to fail her. The prospect before her was dark and cheerless. She saw her sister sinking into a settled melancholy, from which it might be impossible to rouse her. She saw herself condemned to a long and painful attendance, with no one to share her anxieties, or to offer her any real comfort or support. The weight of her own secret sorrow, added to her fears for Marianne, seemed almost too heavy to be borne.

It was in this state of dejection that she was sitting, late in the evening, by the drawing-room fire, when Colonel Brandon was announced. His visit was unexpected, but not, at that moment, unwelcome. There was a quiet gravity in his manner, a deep and unobtrusive sympathy in his look, that was more soothing to Elinor’s spirits than all the noisy condolence of Sir John, or the bustling kindness of Mrs. Jennings.

He made no direct allusion to the subject that was uppermost in their minds. He spoke of general topics; of the weather, of politics, of a new book he had been reading. But his eyes, whenever they rested on Elinor, were full of a compassionate inquiry, and his voice, when he addressed her, had a gentleness that betrayed his knowledge of her distress.

Elinor, on her side, felt an unwonted relief in his society. With him, she was not obliged to be on her guard. She knew that he understood and respected her feelings, and that he would make no clumsy attempts to console her. There was a silent sympathy between them, a communion of feeling that needed no words to express it. They were both sufferers, though from different causes; and in their mutual compassion, they found a partial alleviation of their own pain.

After he had sat with her for some time, he rose to go. As he took her hand, he said, in a low voice, “If there is any way in which I can be of service to you, or to your sister, I trust you will not hesitate to command me. My time, my fortune, my best exertions, are all at your disposal.”

Elinor could not speak. The tears, which she had so long restrained, rushed to her eyes. She could only press his hand, and look the thanks she was unable to utter. He seemed to understand her. With a look of the deepest commiseration, he bowed and left the room.

His visit had done her good. It had broken the lonely uniformity of her sorrow, and had given her the comfortable assurance that she was not entirely without a friend. The kindness of Mrs. Jennings was a perpetual trial to her patience; the sympathy of Sir John was an offence to her delicacy. But the grave, unobtrusive friendship of Colonel Brandon was a balm to her wounded spirit. It did not remove her anxiety, but it helped her to bear it. She felt that, with such a friend to rely on, she could face the trials that were before her with a little more courage, and a little more hope.

She knew that Marianne, in her present state of determined wretchedness, would reject the wine with an impatient sorrow, and Elinor had not the heart to press it upon her, nor the strength to endure another scene of her sister’s unchastened grief. She would dispose of the Constantia herself in the morning, and give a vague and satisfactory account to their hostess.

Such were the small deceptions that her situation now constantly required of her; such were the minute and wearying labours of her love. To shield Marianne from the officiousness of others, and to shield others from the unreserve of Marianne, was a task that demanded a perpetual vigilance and a fortitude that Elinor felt was beginning to desert her. The short-lived comfort she had derived from Colonel Brandon’s visit was already fading, giving way to the returning tide of her anxieties. He had offered his help, his time, his fortune; but what help could he give against a sorrow that resided so deep within the heart? What could his fortune buy, that could restore her sister’s peace, or her own?

The following morning brought no relief. Marianne remained in her room, and to Elinor’s gentle enquiries, would return no answer but a faint sigh, or a request to be left alone. She refused all nourishment, save a little tea which Elinor prevailed on her to swallow, and spent the hours in a state of listless inertia, her eyes fixed upon the window, as if watching for a hope she knew would never come. Mrs. Jennings, upon hearing this account, was all bustling concern.

“Poor dear soul! to take on so! It is just as I feared. Well, we must not let her mope, my dear. We must find some amusement for her. Sir John and Charlotte are to call this morning; that will liven things up a little. Charlotte, you know, has such spirits! She can laugh at any thing; and a good laugh is the best medicine in the world, I always say.”

Elinor could not be of the same opinion. The prospect of Mrs. Palmer’s spirits, and Sir John’s boisterous good-humour, filled her with dread. She knew that their attempts at cheerfulness would be as ill-judged as they were well-intentioned, and would only serve to irritate the raw wound of her sister’s feelings. She endeavoured to suggest that company was the very thing Marianne was least fit for; but Mrs. Jennings was not to be persuaded.

“Oh! pooh, pooh, my dear. You are too anxious. Solitude is the worst thing for a low-spirited person. It gives them time to think, and that is what we must prevent at all costs. We must keep her busy, keep her diverted, and before she knows it, she will have forgot all about this worthless young man.”

Argument was vain, and Elinor resigned herself to the coming trial. Punctual to their appointment, Sir John and Mrs. Palmer arrived, their entrance into the drawing-room announced by a peal of laughter from the latter, and a hearty halloo from the former.

“Well, here we are!” cried Sir John, rubbing his hands together. “Come to cheer up the poor invalid! How is she, hey? How is Marianne? Moping, I’ll warrant. Told you so, Mrs. Jennings, didn’t I? Said she’d be all in the dumps. Well, we’ll soon have her out of them. Got a plan, you see. A little party to the theatre tonight. Nothing like a good play to take one out of oneself.”

“Oh! Lord, Papa, how you do go on!” cried his daughter, sinking into a chair and laughing immoderately. “So sorry, Miss Dashwood, so very sorry to hear about your sister. It is quite shocking, is it not? And so very droll! I declare, I have not been able to stop laughing since I heard of it. The idea of Willoughby throwing her over for a Miss Grey with fifty thousand pounds! It is the most ridiculous thing I ever heard in my life.”

“Charlotte!” exclaimed her mother, though with more indulgence than reproof in her tone. “You must not say such things. You will distress Miss Dashwood.”

“Oh! but it is droll, you know, Mama,” persisted Mrs. Palmer, wiping her eyes. “And I am sure Miss Dashwood thinks so too, though she is too polite to say it. And as for Marianne, I dare say she will have a good laugh about it herself, by and by. I always laugh at my own disappointments. There was that time when my new pelisse was quite ruined by the rain; I thought I should have died of laughing.”

Elinor, to whom this comparison of a ruined pelisse to a ruined heart was addressed, could only reply by a cold and distant civility. She felt a contempt for such thoughtless insensibility that almost overcame her habitual self-command. To hear her sister’s deepest affliction treated as a subject for merriment, a droll anecdote to be laughed over and forgotten, was an insult that stung her to the quick. But she knew that to show her resentment would be to betray her sister’s secret to the world, and to expose them both to the pity and curiosity of their acquaintance. She therefore forced herself to maintain an outward composure, and to answer Mrs. Palmer’s unthinking chatter with a semblance of patience.

Mr. Palmer, who had followed his wife into the room, had, during this time, been standing by the fire, surveying the company with his usual air of sullen indifference. He now condescended to take notice of the conversation.

“What is all this fuss about?” said he, without troubling to lower his voice. “Some girl has been jilted, I collect. Well, what of it? It happens every day. She will get over it. Or she will not. In either case, it is of no consequence to any one but herself. I cannot see why we must all be disturbed by it.”

“Now, Palmer, don’t be a brute,” said Sir John. “The poor girl is unhappy, and we must do what we can to help her.”

“Help her?” retorted his son-in-law. “And how do you propose to do that? By dragging her to a play she does not want to see, to be stared at by a parcel of fools who know her story and will whisper about her behind their fans? A very sensible plan, upon my word. If you wish to be of service to Miss Marianne Dashwood, I should advise you to leave her alone.”

There was so much truth in this ungracious speech, that Elinor could not but feel a momentary gratitude to the speaker. Mr. Palmer’s rudeness was, at that moment, more welcome to her than the kindness of his relations. It was a relief to hear the voice of common sense, even though it spoke in the accents of cynical misanthropy.

The project of the theatre was, for the time, abandoned; but the determination to “amuse” Marianne was by no means given up. The next few days were a torment to Elinor, a succession of futile attempts on the part of Mrs. Jennings and her daughter to draw Marianne from her seclusion. Parties were planned, excursions proposed, visitors invited, all with the professed object of cheering the poor invalid. Each new scheme was a fresh source of vexation to Elinor, who was obliged to act as the mediator between her sister’s obstinate sorrow and her friends’ relentless benevolence.

One afternoon, Mrs. Jennings, having failed in all her other attempts, declared that a little air and exercise was what Marianne needed, and that a drive in the park would do her a world of good.

“The carriage is at the door, my dear,” she announced to Elinor, “and I will take no refusal. You and Marianne must both come. It is a lovely day, and the fresh air will bring the colour back to her cheeks. Now, go and fetch her, and do not let her say no.”

Elinor, weary of fruitless opposition, and seeing a sort of desperate resolution in Mrs. Jennings’s eye, thought it best to comply. She went to her sister’s room, and with gentle but firm persuasion, at last prevailed on her to leave her solitary chamber and prepare for the unwelcome outing. Marianne yielded, not from any revival of spirit, but from the sheer exhaustion of her will. She suffered herself to be dressed, as a child might do, with a passive indifference that was more painful to Elinor to witness than any outburst of grief.

The drive began in silence. Marianne, her face half-hidden by her veil, leaned back in her corner of the carriage, insensible to the passing scene. Mrs. Jennings kept up a desultory stream of chatter, pointing out the equipages of their acquaintance, and commenting on the dress and appearance of the ladies who thronged the fashionable drive. Elinor, seated opposite her sister, answered in monosyllables, her thoughts divided between her anxiety for Marianne, and her own secret and unacknowledged sorrows.

It was in the midst of this dreary excursion that an incident occurred, which, for a moment, threatened to shatter the fragile composure of both sisters. As their carriage drew to a halt, blocked by the press of vehicles, Elinor’s eye was caught by a familiar figure in a phaeton that had stopped beside them. It was a gentleman, whose face, for a dreadful instant, she took to be that of Willoughby. Her heart gave a sudden, sickening leap; a cold dread seized her. But a second glance showed her her mistake. The gentleman was a stranger, and the resemblance, which had seemed so strong at first, was but a fleeting and fanciful one. The relief was so great, that she felt a momentary dizziness, a weakness that almost overcame her.

She glanced at Marianne, to see if she too had been struck by the same resemblance. But her sister’s face was turned away, her gaze fixed upon the distant line of trees that bounded the park. She had seen nothing. Elinor breathed again. But the shock had unnerved her. It had brought home to her, with a new and terrifying force, the precariousness of their situation. What if they were to meet him? What if, at any moment, in any public place, they were to come face to face with the man who had wrought such ruin in their lives? The thought was insupportable. She felt a sudden, urgent desire to be gone, to escape from this scene of public display, and to hide themselves once more in the sad security of their own apartments.

As if in answer to her unspoken wish, the line of carriages began to move. Their own coachman touched his horses, and they rolled slowly forward. At that moment, a voice from the pavement cried out, “Why, Miss Dashwood! Miss Elinor! Is it you?”

Elinor turned, and to her infinite vexation, beheld the smiling face of Miss Steele, who, with her sister Anne, was strolling along the edge of the drive. To have avoided them was impossible. The carriage was moving at a snail’s pace, and they were already at the door.

“How do you do? How do you do?” cried Anne, pressing forward with her usual forwardness. “We were so surprised to see you! We had no notion you were in town. And dear Miss Marianne too! How sad she looks! But that is not to be wondered at, to be sure, after all that has happened.”

“Anne, you forget yourself,” said her sister, with a look of reproof that was belied by the keen curiosity in her own eyes. “Pray excuse her, Miss Dashwood. She has no discretion. But we were indeed most concerned to hear the news. The whole world is talking of it, you know. And to think of his marrying Miss Grey! A poor, plain little thing, they say, with not half the beauty of your sister. But fifty thousand pounds! Well, there is no accounting for the taste of gentlemen, is there?”

Lucy delivered this speech with an air of the most sympathizing concern, while her eyes darted from Elinor’s face to Marianne’s, eager to catch every flicker of emotion. Elinor felt her blood run cold. To be thus accosted, thus publicly pitied and condoled with, by a person whom she so thoroughly disliked and distrusted, was a refinement of torture she had not anticipated. She saw Marianne shrink back into her corner, her whole frame trembling with a mixture of shame and agony.

“You are very good,” said Elinor, in a voice that she strove to keep steady, “but you are misinformed. My sister has been unwell, and is still suffering from the effects of a severe cold. We must beg you to excuse us. Good day to you.”

She made a sign to the coachman to drive on. But Mrs. Jennings, who had been listening with undisguised interest, leaned forward and called out, “Now, my dears, don’t be in such a hurry. Why should not the Miss Steeles get in and take a turn with us? There is plenty of room.”

The invitation was no sooner given than it was accepted. To Elinor’s unutterable dismay, the two sisters, with many expressions of gratitude, were handed into the carriage, and seated themselves opposite to the two Dashwoods. The situation was now become intolerable. To be thus shut up in a confined space with the last people on earth she wished to see; to be forced to endure their conversation, to submit to their scrutiny, to witness their covert triumph in her sister’s misery, was a trial beyond even her powers of endurance.

“Well, this is comfortable!” exclaimed Anne, settling herself with a bounce. “I do love a ride in a fine carriage. And so, Miss Marianne, you have been ill? I am sorry for that. But you must not give way to melancholy, you know. It will spoil your looks. And there are as good fish in the sea as ever came out of it, as my mother always says.”

Marianne made no reply. She had drawn her veil more closely over her face, and sat as still and silent as a statue. Elinor, seeing that her sister was on the verge of a complete collapse, felt that she must put an end to this scene, at whatever cost to politeness.

She turned to Lucy, whose eyes had not once left her face, and said, with a coldness she did not attempt to disguise, “My sister, as you see, is in no condition for company. Your presence, I am sure, is well-meant; but at this moment, it can only give her pain. I must therefore request that you will be so good as to leave us.”

There was a moment’s astonished silence. Even Mrs. Jennings was taken aback by a tone so foreign to the gentle and ever-polite Miss Dashwood. Lucy’s sallow cheeks flushed with anger. But she was too artful to betray it.

“Why, to be sure,” she said, with a sweet and forgiving smile. “If our company is disagreeable, we will not impose it. We would not for the world add to dear Miss Marianne’s distress. Come, Anne, we must be going. I am sure Miss Dashwood will make our apologies to Mrs. Jennings.”

She pulled the check-string, and the carriage stopped. With a great show of civility on all sides, the Miss Steeles were once more set down upon the pavement. As the carriage drove off, Elinor could hear Anne’s loud and complaining whisper, “Well! I am sure! Of all the proud, ill-tempered…”

The rest was lost in the noise of the wheels. Mrs. Jennings was full of apologies and regrets. “My dear, I am so sorry! I had no idea! I thought it would be a pleasant surprise. But you were quite right to send them packing. A pair of chattering, impertinent girls! And to talk of Willoughby and Miss Grey before your very faces! It was abominable. I shall take care not to be so civil to them in future.”

Elinor could make no answer. The effort of the last few minutes had completely exhausted her. She leaned her head back against the squabs, and closed her eyes, a silent prayer on her lips that the day, and all its trials, might soon be over. The remainder of the drive passed in silence. When they reached home, Marianne, without speaking a word, flew to her room, and locked the door. Elinor, too weary to follow, and knowing that any attempt at comfort would be rejected, sought the solitude of her own chamber. She sat down by the window, and looked out upon the gathering twilight, her mind a tumoil of painful reflections. The events of the day had stripped away the last of her defences, and left her exposed to the full force of her own unhappiness. The world seemed to her a place of heartless cruelty and unthinking folly; and she felt, for the first time in her life, a sense of utter and hopeless desolation. The friendship of a Colonel Brandon might be a balm, but the world was full of Palmers and Steeles; and against their combined assault, what fortitude could long prevail?
