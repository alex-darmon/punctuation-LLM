Catherine smoothed the covers over her, and had the satisfaction of seeing her lay her weary head upon the pillow, hoping she might find a little peace in sleep. When she returned to the drawing-room, she was met by Mrs. Finch, who was advancing from the sideboard with a small glass of something dark and potent in her hand. “My dear,” she said, her face beaming with purpose, “I have just remembered my late husband’s famous cherry cordial! replied Catherine, with a gratitude that was more for the intention than the offer, “you are too good; but I am certain my sister is in no state to take any thing of the kind. The greatest kindness she can now receive is quiet, and I hope she is, by this time, on the point of finding it in sleep.”

“Sleep! Lord, my dear, a glass of cordial is the very thing to help one to sleep! My poor Mr. Finch swore by it. He would take a glass every night of his life, winter and summer, and he slept like a top, I assure you. But if you think it best not, you know your sister best. Perhaps you are right. A young constitution throws things off so easily. And a sad heart does make one feel a little hollow. Now, my dear, what are we to do with ourselves? I cannot abide a moping evening. I told Sir William and Lady Croft they might look in on us for a hand of cards, and it would be a shame to disappoint them. Sir William is always so eager for his whist.”

“You are very good to think of our amusement, Ma’am,” said Catherine, her heart sinking at the prospect of company, “but for this evening, I believe my own thoughts, and perhaps a book, would be company enough. I should not wish to be a burden on your friends’ pleasure.”

“A burden! Nonsense, my dear. Lady Croft is the most good-natured creature in the world, and she is monstrously fond of you both. And Sir William, well, Sir William is Sir William; he cares for little beyond his cards and his newspaper, so you need not trouble your head about him. They will be full of concern for poor Eliza, of course, but I shall give them a hint directly they arrive. A single look from me will be enough to tell them to say nothing of the matter. People of sense, you know, can take a hint. It is only the fools who must have every thing spelled out for them.”

Catherine, who had very little faith in the subtlety of Mrs. Finch’s hints, could only resign herself to the evening. To argue would be to appear ungrateful, and to retreat to her room would be to leave Mrs. Finch to her own devices with her guests, a circumstance which she feared might lead to a more complete and unvarnished account of Eliza’s sorrows than any she cared to imagine. Her duty, therefore, was to remain, and to guard the conversation as best she might. She sat down with her needlework, a task which always helped to compose her thoughts, and prepared for the trial ahead.

Mrs. Finch, having fortified her spirits with the cherry cordial, was now in full flow. “And as for Mr. Armitage,” she continued, returning to her favourite subject with renewed vigour, “it is, as I said, an ill-wind. He is such a steady, respectable man; not one of your flashy, dashing types who thinks of nothing but his own pleasure. He has a proper sense of things. I remember when his father died, he managed the whole estate with such prudence; paid off all the debts, which were not inconsiderable I can tell you, and has put the whole place on a capital footing. And he is so attentive to his old aunt, Miss Armitage. To be sure, she is a trial; always complaining of draughts, and can never remember who one is from one day to the next; but he is patience itself with her. That is the sign of a good heart, my dear. A man who is kind to a deaf old aunt will make a good husband, you may depend upon it.”

“Mr. Armitage’s character is, I am sure, everything that is admirable,” Catherine allowed, taking a careful stitch.

“Admirable! That it is. And you must not think him dull, my dear. He can be very lively when he chooses. I have seen him at the Christmas assemblies, and he cuts a very fine figure in a country dance. He is not, perhaps, what a very young girl, full of romance, might call handsome, for his face is a little too serious; but it is a good, honest face, and his countenance lights up wonderfully when he smiles. If Eliza would but give him a chance to make himself agreeable! We must contrive to throw them together. A little picnic to the ruins at Abbey-gate, perhaps, when the weather is finer? Or a musical evening? Eliza plays so prettily, and I know Mr. Armitage is very fond of music, though he does not perform himself.”

Before Catherine was obliged to feign enthusiasm for these schemes, the sound of a carriage arriving, followed by a brisk rap at the front door, announced the arrival of the Crofts. Mrs. Finch bustled to the drawing-room door, her face a mixture of social excitement and conspiratorial importance.

“Now, my dear, you will see,” she whispered to Catherine, “I shall manage it all perfectly.”

Sir William and Lady Croft were announced. Sir William was a stout, red-faced gentleman whose chief expression was one of mild and settled complacency. His wife was a thinner, sharper woman, with quick, intelligent eyes that seemed to take in the entire state of the room, and of Catherine’s countenance, in a single glance.

“My dear Mrs. Finch! So good of you to have us,” said Lady Croft, giving their hostess’s hand a brief squeeze. “Miss Catherine, I hope I see you well. But where is your charming sister? Not unwell, I trust?”

Here was the moment. Catherine braced herself, but Mrs. Finch was before her. “Poor dear Eliza has a sad headache,” she declared, placing a finger to her lips and giving Lady Croft a look of the most profound and mysterious significance. “She has retired to her room. We are not to speak of it,” she added, in a whisper that was perfectly audible to everyone, including Sir William, who was still shrugging himself out of his greatcoat by the door.

“Headache? Ah, very sorry to hear it. Bad things, headaches,” boomed Sir William, advancing towards the fire. “A change in the weather, I expect. The wind is getting up from the east.”

Lady Croft, however, was not so easily diverted. Her keen eyes rested on Catherine’s face. “Indeed, a headache may be a very trying complaint,” she said, her tone a little softer than usual. “We shall miss her at the card-table. Sir William has been looking forward to his revenge upon you all evening, Mrs. Finch.”

“And he shall have it!” cried Mrs. Finch, all conspiracy forgotten in the thrill of the challenge. “But he will find me a formidable opponent tonight, I promise you! Sir William, will you be my partner? Miss Catherine, you and Lady Croft will play against us. Come, let us not waste a moment.”

Catherine was grateful for the immediate business of the card-table, which for a time held all conversation in check, confining it to the declarations and tricks of the game. Sir William played with a silent, solemn concentration, while Mrs. Finch accompanied her own play with a running commentary of triumphs and tribulations. Lady Croft played with a quiet skill, her eyes occasionally straying to Catherine’s face with an expression of thoughtful sympathy which was more comforting than any words. Catherine tried to fix her own attention on the cards, but her thoughts continually drifted back to the room upstairs, and to the letter which had brought such ruin upon their peace.

They had been playing for near an hour when another visitor was announced. The name, “Mr. Armitage,” caused Catherine’s head to lift with an involuntary start, and she saw Mrs. Finch’s face light up with an expression of pure, unadulterated delight.

“Mr. Armitage! How very good of you to call!” she exclaimed, rising from the table. “You are just in time to tell me what I ought to have played in that last hand. Sir William is a tyrant, and will tell me I have thrown the game away.”

Mr. Armitage was a man of about thirty, with a tall, gentlemanlike figure, and the serious but pleasant countenance Mrs. Finch had described. He bowed to the company, his gaze lingering for a moment on Catherine with an expression of polite enquiry.

“I was at the library in town, and on my way home thought I might presume to ask after your health, and that of Miss Eliza,” he said, his voice calm and steady. “I had heard she was not perfectly well.”

“Oh, she is suffering sadly, my dear sir, sadly!” cried Mrs. Finch, before Catherine could frame a more guarded reply. “A grievous disappointment, you know! Young people’s hearts are so tender. But she will get over it! Lord, yes, she will get over it. Time, you know, and a change of scene! And good friends! That is what she needs.”

Catherine felt a blush of mortification stain her cheeks at this dreadful speech. She saw a flicker of pained understanding in Mr. Armitage’s eyes as he glanced from Mrs. Finch to herself. Lady Croft coughed delicately and began shuffling the cards with unnecessary vigour.

“Mrs. Finch,” said Catherine, her voice low but clear, “I believe Mr. Armitage was referring to the cold my sister has been suffering from this past week.”

“The cold! Oh, aye, the cold, to be sure,” said Mrs. Finch, though with a look that suggested this was a very poor and insufficient explanation for the state of affairs.

Mr. Armitage, with a tact that did him credit, immediately took Catherine’s lead. “Indeed, that was what I had understood,” he said smoothly. “These spring colds can be very persistent. I hope a few days of rest will see her quite restored.” He then turned to Sir William and made an enquiry about a matter of parish business, effectively shifting the conversation onto safer ground.

Catherine felt a powerful wave of gratitude towards him. He had not only understood her distress, but had acted instantly to relieve it. He had seen the truth of the situation, she was sure of it; the gossip of a small town travels fast, and Mrs. Finch’s performance would have enlightened anyone of common sense. Yet he had shown no unseemly curiosity, nor offered any pity, but had behaved with a quiet, feeling propriety that was the truest form of kindness.

The card game was not resumed. Sir William and Mr. Armitage fell into a conversation about agriculture and local politics, while Lady Croft engaged Catherine in a low-voiced discussion of a new book she had been reading. Mrs. Finch, for her part, hovered between the two groups, occasionally offering an opinion to the gentlemen or a piece of news to the ladies, and casting frequent, approving glances at Mr. Armitage, as if he were a prize she had just procured for the family’s benefit.

“A thousand a year, you know, Miss Catherine,” she murmured, as she passed behind her chair on the pretence of mending the fire. “And High Cross is a picture. You must see his new pinery. It cost him five hundred pounds, Sir William was telling me.”

Catherine merely inclined her head, her feelings a strange mixture of exasperation at her hostess and a grudging respect for the object of her matchmaking. She could not deny that Mr. Armitage’s presence was a comfort. His steady sense seemed a rock in the midst of the emotional turmoil of the day. To compare him with Davies was impossible; they were men of different species. Davies was all fire and charm, a meteor that had blazed across their lives and left darkness behind. Mr. Armitage was a fixed star, less brilliant, perhaps, but constant and true. Could Eliza, so lately scorched by the one, ever learn to navigate by the other? Catherine feared not. Her sister’s heart, so passionate and so absolute in its affections, was not formed for a sensible, second attachment. It would either heal and love again with its old ardour, or it would break.

When the visitors at last took their leave, Lady Croft pressed Catherine’s hand with a warmth that spoke volumes. “Give my love to your sister,” she said quietly. “Tell her I shall call upon her in a day or two, when her headache is quite gone.” The emphasis on the word ‘headache’ was slight, but it conveyed to Catherine a perfect understanding and a promise of discretion.

As soon as the door had closed behind them, Mrs. Finch burst forth. “Well! Was I not right, my dear? Is he not a charming man? So sensible, so well-bred! And did you see how concerned he was for Eliza? He asked after her the moment he came in. Oh, it will do, it will do, I am certain of it. He is half in love with her already, and once that worthless Mr. Davies is forgot, she will see what a treasure she has been overlooking.”

“He is a very good man, ma’am,” said Catherine wearily. “And he was very kind this evening.”

“Kind! He is the soul of kindness. And his conversation is so improving! Did you hear what he said about the new enclosure bill? It shows a great grasp of affairs. He will be a magistrate before he is forty, Sir William says. That is the sort of husband to make a woman happy, my dear. Not a man who can write a pretty sonnet and nothing else. Depend upon it, Eliza has had a lucky escape, though she may not see it so just at present. Well, I am quite tired out with all this emotion. I shall go straight to my bed. And you must do the same, my dear. You look quite worn to a thread.”

Left at last to herself, Catherine took up her candle and went softly back to the bedchamber. She had half expected, half hoped, to find Eliza asleep, but as she entered, she saw her sister’s form silhouetted against the window. She had risen from the bed and was once again sitting on the window seat, wrapped in a shawl, gazing out at the dark, windswept garden.

Catherine came and stood beside her, saying nothing, but laying a hand gently on her shoulder. For a long time, neither of them spoke. The only sound was the sighing of the wind in the great elm tree outside.

At last, Eliza’s voice, low and toneless, broke the silence. “Was anyone here?”

The question was the one Catherine had dreaded. Her sister had not asked *who* was here, but used the more general, more hopeful, ‘anyone,’ as if her heart, against all reason, still harboured a desperate fantasy of a repentant visitor.

“Sir William and Lady Croft came for cards,” Catherine answered, keeping her voice even. “And Mr. Armitage called in on his way home.”

There was another long silence. Catherine felt a slight tremor pass through her sister’s frame under her hand.

“Mr. Armitage,” Eliza repeated at last, the name a mere breath. “How kind of him.”

There was no irony in her tone, only a profound and desolate emptiness. It was as though the very name of another suitor, of a respectable, sensible, worthy man, served only to illuminate the completeness of her loss. It was not kindness or worthiness she mourned; it was the loss of a vibrant, thrilling, and deeply felt love, a love which, however ill-founded, had become the whole of her existence.

Catherine’s heart ached for her. “He asked after you with great concern,” she added softly.

Eliza turned her head slowly and looked at her sister. In the faint candlelight, her face was pale as marble, her eyes vast and dark with a sorrow so deep it seemed beyond the relief of tears.

“He is a good man,” Eliza said, in that same flat voice. “You have always said so. You were right about him. You have been right about every thing.”

And with that, she turned back to the window, her gaze fixed once more on the darkness outside. Catherine knew that there was nothing more to be said. She pulled a thicker quilt from the bed and wrapped it carefully around Eliza’s shoulders, before taking a seat in a low chair nearby, determined not to leave her sister alone with her grief again that night. The house was silent, but for the wind that moaned around its corners, a fitting music for the sorrow it contained.

The world, for a time, seemed a place of such profound disappointment.

Marianne, when Elinor went to her, was awake, but lay perfectly still, her face turned to the wall, and would not be prevailed upon to speak. She accepted a cup of tea from her sister’s hand, but would touch no food, and when Elinor gently proposed that she might rise later in the day, she gave a slight, negative shake of her head, a gesture of such profound listlessness that it went to Elinor’s heart.

Leaving her to the quiet she seemed to crave, Elinor went down to the breakfast parlour, where she found Mrs. Jennings already presiding over the tea-things, her cheerful countenance a jarring contrast to the sorrow that pervaded the house.

“Well, my dear,” she began, as soon as Elinor entered, “and how is the poor patient this morning? Has she had any sleep? I was dreaming of her all night. I dreamt she had married Colonel Brandon, and that Willoughby had shot himself in a duel with the Colonel on account of it! Lord! was not that a strange thing? But I suppose it came of my thinking so much of the Colonel’s good fortune. Has she eaten any thing?”

“She has taken some tea,” replied Elinor, sitting down and feeling a fresh wave of irritation at the indelicacy of such a dream being recounted at such a time.

And you, my dear, you are eating nothing yourself. You must not allow yourself to pine away for your sister’s troubles. You have troubles enough of your own, I dare say, if the truth were known.”

This last remark, though spoken with a vague and general sympathy, struck Elinor with such force that she coloured deeply and bent her head over her cup, hoping Mrs. Jennings would not observe her confusion. That good lady, however, was already embarked on another train of thought.

“I have been thinking, my dear, that what Marianne needs is a little distraction. Lying in a dark room is the worst thing in the world for a broken heart. We must have some company for her. I am expecting Charlotte and Sir John to call this morning; Sir John will be so concerned, he is so fond of you both. And a little lively chatter from Charlotte is just the thing to shake her out of her melancholy. Mrs. Palmer laughs at every thing, you know; she would laugh at a funeral if she went to one, I do believe. It is her way, and though it can be trying at times, I dare say it is better than crying.”

Elinor could think of nothing worse for Marianne, in her present state, than the boisterous, unthinking pity of Sir John, and the giddy, incessant laughter of Mrs. Palmer. “Indeed, ma’am,” she said, with as much firmness as she could muster, “I am sure you mean it for the best, but my sister is in no condition to see visitors. The very sound of a strange voice would, I am certain, be too much for her. Her spirits are entirely unequal to the exertion.”

“Nonsense, my dear! What she needs is to be forced into a little exertion. Depend upon it, I know what is best for young people in these situations. When my poor cousin, Fanny, was used so ill by Captain Davies, we had the house full of people for a fortnight, and played cards every night, and by the end of it she was quite herself again. We shall have Marianne downstairs this very afternoon, you will see.”

Elinor saw, on the contrary, that she would have to stand guard over her sister’s chamber for the rest of the day, and the prospect exhausted her. She was saved from having to argue the point further by a great noise in the hall, which announced the arrival of the very guests she had been dreading. A moment later, the door flew open, and Sir John Middleton and Mrs. Palmer burst in, followed by Mr. Palmer, who looked, as usual, as if he had been brought there against his will and wished for nothing more than to be somewhere else.

“My dear Miss Dashwood!” cried Sir John, seizing both her hands in his. “Jennings has told us! The saddest business! Poor Miss Marianne! By heavens, if I were a young man, I would call Willoughby out tomorrow morning! A scoundrel! A complete scoundrel! But we will get her over it. We have come to cheer you all up. We shall have a party! A little music this evening, eh? That will liven her spirits!”

“Oh, la, yes!” cried his sister, Mrs. Palmer, sinking into a chair and giving way to a peal of laughter. “How very shocking! To be jilted! I declare I have not heard any thing so droll in an age! Poor Miss Marianne! And they say the other lady, Miss Grey, is quite plain! Is not that the best part of the whole story? Oh, I shall die of laughing. Papa, is it not excessively amusing?”

Mr. Palmer, who had taken up a newspaper by the window, looked over it with an air of supreme indifference. “I am not acquainted with the gentleman’s taste in drollery, Charlotte,” he said drily, before returning to his reading.

“Elinor, my dear,” said Mrs. Jennings, bustling about, “do not mind them. Sir John means well, and Charlotte cannot help her way. Now, I shall go up myself and see if I cannot persuade Marianne to join us. A new face is what she needs.”

“No, indeed, ma’am, you must not,” cried Elinor in real alarm. “I beg you will not. She is trying to rest. To disturb her now would be the greatest cruelty.” Her voice was so earnest, her look so distressed, that Mrs. Jennings was for a moment checked.

“Well, my dear, if you are so set against it, But I think you are quite wrong. What do you say, Sir John? Ought we not to get her down?”

“To be sure!” said Sir John heartily. “No sense in moping. We’ll have her out for a drive this afternoon. A spin in my curricle round the park will blow the cobwebs away, what!”

“Oh, la, a drive!” giggled Mrs. Palmer. “Perhaps she will see Mr. Willoughby with his new heiress! How awkward that would be! I should sink with confusion!” And she laughed again, louder than before.

Elinor felt a despairing sense of being surrounded by fools. She could not speak for vexation. Her silence and her pale, distressed face seemed at last to make some small impression upon Sir John.

“Come, come, Miss Dashwood,” he said, his tone a little softer. “Do not you be downhearted too. We are all your friends here. We will take care of you. But Willoughby! I shall never forgive him. To think of it! After all the time he spent with you at Barton. Seemed as good as engaged. And what of the horse? Did he give Miss Marianne the horse he promised her?”

It will be a blessing when you are safe at Cleveland, away from all this chatter. You look quite worn out. You must let my maid sit with Marianne tonight, and get some proper rest yourself.”

This time, Elinor did not refuse. The prospect of another sleepless night spent watching Marianne’s fitful, miserable slumber was more than she could face. The strain of the last two days had pushed her to the very limit of her endurance. She agreed that the maid, a sensible, quiet woman, might watch in her stead for the first part of the night.

After a silent dinner, of which she ate little, Elinor repaired to her sister’s room. Marianne was awake, but lay as still as before, her face turned to the wall.

“Marianne,” Elinor said softly, “I have something to tell you. We are to leave London. Mrs. Jennings has offered us the use of her house at Cleveland, and we shall go there the day after tomorrow.”

There was a long silence. Elinor was not certain she had been heard. But then, Marianne spoke, her voice muffled by the pillow. “Any where. It does not signify.”

This utter indifference was, to Elinor, the most painful reply imaginable. The Marianne she knew would have had a thousand opinions, a thousand feelings, about such a plan. This ghost, who did not care where she went, was a stranger.

“It is a quiet place,” Elinor continued, forcing a note of gentle persuasion into her voice. “You will be disturbed by no one. You can walk in the gardens, and have perfect peace to… to recover your strength.”

Marianne made no answer. Elinor sighed and began the small tasks of preparing for the night, moving about the room with a heavy heart. She explained to Marianne that the maid would be sitting in the adjoining dressing-room, and that she herself would be just across the hall. Marianne seemed not to notice. Her gaze was fixed on some point in the middle distance, lost in a sorrow so vast it had consumed the entire world.

Leaving the maid with her instructions, Elinor retired to her own small chamber. For the first time in nearly forty-eight hours, she was truly alone. The door was closed, the house was quiet, and there was no one to witness her collapse. She sat on the edge of her bed, the candle-flame flickering on her weary face, and the self-control she had so rigorously maintained at last gave way. Her own sorrow, so long suppressed, rose up and overwhelmed her. It was not only for Marianne that she wept, but for herself; for Edward, for the letter she still had not received, for the cruel entanglement with Lucy Steele, and for the profound loneliness of her situation. Her tears fell, hot and silent, in the solitude of the little room. They were not the passionate, stormy tears of her sister, but the quiet, bitter tears of a grief that had been denied, and which, in the darkness, could finally be acknowledged. She did not know how long she sat there, but when she finally crept into her cold bed, it was with a sense of utter desolation, and the chilling thought that while Marianne’s heart was broken, her own was merely being slowly and inexorably crushed.

Mrs. Jennings, all bustle and command, was in her element directing the servants, sending messages, and making lists for their departure the following day. Elinor was glad of the occupation; to be busy, even in the melancholy task of packing away the dresses and ornaments that had been brought to London with such different expectations, was a relief from the torment of idle thought. The moment the door closed behind her, Elinor sank into a chair, her hands trembling with suppressed rage and misery. The visit had been a refinement of torture. To have her own secret sorrow so cruelly played upon, under the guise of sympathy for her sister, was an insult almost greater than the original injury. For a few moments, she allowed herself to despise Edward for his weakness, for having entangled himself with such a creature. But her sense of justice soon returned. He was weak, but not wicked. He was trapped, and he was suffering too. Her anger softened into a profound and pitying grief for them all.

The carriage, they had learned, could not be repaired until morning, so they were forced to spend the night. The room they were given was small but clean, with a large, curtained bed and a scent of lavender. As Elinor helped her sister to undress, she found herself reflecting on the strange turn their day had taken. What had begun as a journey of despair had ended in this quiet, unexpected haven. The encounter with Mr. Davies had been a balm to her spirit. It was a reminder that the world contained more than just Willoughbys and Lucy Steeles; that there were still quiet, kind, and intelligent people to be found. It was a small thing, perhaps, but at that moment, it felt like a great one. The next morning, the rain had stopped, and a watery sun was struggling to break through the clouds. The carriage, they were informed, was mended and ready. As they were preparing to leave, Mr. Davies came to bid them farewell. “I trust your journey onwards will be less eventful,” he said to Elinor, with his gentle smile. “I am travelling in the opposite direction, or I would offer you my escort.” “You have been very kind, sir,” Elinor replied, extending her hand. “Your company was a great comfort to us last night.” He took her hand for a moment. “The comfort was mutual, I assure you,” he said. “Safe travels, Miss Dashwood.” And with another bow, he was gone. As the carriage pulled away from the inn, Elinor looked back, but he was no longer in sight. She settled back against the cushions, feeling a strange sense of peace. The brief, chance encounter had been a respite, a moment of calm in the midst of the storm. It had not changed her situation, or her sister’s; the sorrows they carried with them were as heavy as ever. But it had, for a little while, made them easier to bear. They arrived at Cleveland late that afternoon, the grand house rising before them, solid and imposing. Their arrival was met with a flurry of activity, and in the midst of it all was Mrs. Palmer, as effusive and thoughtless as ever. “Oh, you are come at last!” she cried, rushing to embrace them. “We had quite given you up! I am so glad to see you! But Lord, Marianne, you look perfectly dreadful! So pale and thin! I declare, London has not agreed with you at all!” Elinor steeled herself. The peace of the King’s Head was over. Their new life, and their new trials, were about to begin.

Their apartments at Cleveland were spacious and handsomely appointed; a world away from their little cottage at Barton, and a great deal more grand than their lodgings in town. But their size and elegance offered no comfort to a heart oppressed by sorrow. Marianne, when Elinor returned to their chamber, had not moved from her position, huddled in a chair before the grand, unlit fireplace, a shawl wrapped tightly about her shoulders as if she were chilled to the very bone, though the room was not cold.

“Marianne,” said Elinor, her voice full of a tenderness she seldom allowed herself to show so openly, “you must not sit there. You will take a dreadful cold. Come, let me help you to bed. You promised me you would rest.”

The argument was useless, and Elinor knew it. She reverted to persuasion of a simpler kind, and after much gentle entreaty, at last prevailed upon her sister to allow herself to be put to bed. As she tucked the covers around her, she saw with alarm how feverish and flushed Marianne’s cheeks were, and how bright her eyes. A dread, formless but heavy, began to settle in Elinor’s own breast.

The next morning brought with it Mrs. Palmer, full of a new scheme for their amusement. “My dear Miss Dashwood!” she cried, bursting into their sitting-room where Elinor sat alone at a writing-desk, attempting to compose a letter to their mother. “I have had the most famous idea! We shall have a little party, a dance, perhaps! Nothing grand, you know, just a few of the neighbours. It will be the very thing to cheer Marianne up. She loves dancing, does she not?”

Elinor looked up, her pen suspended. “Ma’am, I am very sensible of your kindness, but I do not think Marianne is in any state for a dance. Indeed, she has not yet risen.”

“Not risen! Lord, how lazy! But that is no matter. She can rest all day, and be quite fresh for the evening. I shall send out the cards at once. The name struck Elinor with a peculiar force. “Mr. Davies?” she repeated, a slight colour rising in her own cheeks. “Did you say he has taken Combe Magna?”

“Yes, for a year. Do you know him, then?” asked Mrs. Palmer, her curiosity instantly piqued.

“We encountered a gentleman of that name by chance, on our journey,” Elinor replied, striving for a tone of indifference. “Our carriage broke down, and we were forced to spend a night at an inn near Marlborough.”

“Lord, how shocking! And you met him there? Was he handsome? Is he agreeable? You must tell me every thing! I am dying to know what he is like before he comes.”

“He was very civil,” said Elinor, retreating at once from the subject. “But as to a dance, Mrs. Palmer, I must beg you to reconsider. My sister’s health is truly not equal to it. The exertion, the necessity of appearing cheerful… it would be a misery to her.”

Mrs. Palmer’s cheerful countenance fell. “Well, if you truly think so,” she said, disappointed. “But it seems a great pity. And what are we to do to amuse ourselves? Mr. Palmer does nothing but read the papers and complain about the government, and it is so very dull!”

Her husband, who had entered the room in time to hear her last complaint, now spoke from the doorway. “An accurate summary of my existence, my dear. I would add only that I also find opportunities to despair of the general state of society. Your plan for a dance, for instance, filled me with a particular kind of dread.” He gave a slight bow towards Elinor. “Your intervention, Miss Dashwood, has earned you my sincerest gratitude. The prospect of seeing my wife’s acquaintance attempt to be merry is one I would gladly avoid.”

Mrs. Palmer paid him no mind. “Oh, stuff, Mr. Palmer! You are always so disobliging. Well, if we cannot have a dance, we must have a dinner party at least. No one can object to a dinner party. We will have the very same people, and it will not be half so fatiguing.” And before Elinor could frame a new objection, she had bustled out of the room to give her orders, quite satisfied with her amended plan.

Mr. Palmer looked at Elinor with an expression of dry, sympathetic amusement. “You see, Miss Dashwood, resistance is futile. My wife’s desire for company operates with the force of a natural law. You have merely exchanged a convulsion for a slow fever. I cannot say which is the more tedious.”

“Your concern for my sister’s comfort is appreciated, sir,” Elinor said, unable to suppress a small smile.

“I have no concern for it whatever,” he replied coolly. “My concern is entirely for my own. A young lady in the throes of a romantic disappointment is a spectacle I find uniquely trying. Her silence is reproachful, and her conversation, should she vouchsafe any, is invariably centered upon the miseries of the human condition. I prefer my miseries at a political, and therefore a more distant, remove.” He picked up a newspaper from the table and, with another slight bow, retired to a chair by the window, effectively ending the conversation.

Elinor sighed and turned back to her letter, though her thoughts were now in a greater state of disarray than before. The prospect of a dinner party was scarcely less dreadful than that of a dance. Marianne would be forced to appear, to sit through multiple courses, to endure the cheerful chatter and the curious glances of strangers. And among these strangers would be Mr. Davies. The thought of seeing him again, in such a formal and public setting, brought with it a strange mixture of pleasure and apprehension. She remembered the quiet intelligence of his conversation, the kindness in his eyes; a kindness that had seemed to spring from a genuine understanding of their distress. To meet him again would be a comfort. And yet, to have that meeting under the watchful, matchmaking eye of Mrs. Palmer was a mortification she could hardly bear to contemplate. She found herself wishing he would decline the invitation, and then was ashamed of the wish, for it felt like a rejection of the only society she had found truly congenial in many weeks.

The day of the dinner party arrived all too soon. Marianne had at first declared her utter inability to attend, but Elinor, with a mixture of gentle reasoning and firm persuasion, had convinced her that a public appearance was not only necessary for the sake of propriety, but was a trial that, once endured, would grant her a greater claim to privacy and retirement for days to come. She consented at last, with the air of a martyr going to the stake, and allowed Elinor to help her dress, submitting to the arrangement of her hair and the choice of a gown with a listless indifference that was more painful to witness than any outburst of grief. When she was ready, Elinor looked at her with a pained heart. The beauty was still there, undeniably, but it was the beauty of a statue; her skin was unnaturally pale, her eyes were shadowed with sorrow, and she held herself with a rigidity that spoke of a spirit stretched to its very limit.

They descended to the drawing-room together, a little before the first guests were to arrive. Mr. and Mrs. Palmer were already there, she in a flutter of excitement, he in a state of resigned boredom.

“Ah, here you are!” cried Mrs. Palmer. “Marianne, my dear, you look charming! Does she not, Mr. Palmer? A little pale, perhaps, but that is so very fashionable. The gentlemen will be quite smitten.”

Marianne flinched as if struck, and turned away towards the window. Mr. Palmer observed her for a moment, and then said to Elinor in an undertone, “My wife possesses a singular gift for applying salt to a wound.”

Before Elinor could reply, the first guests were announced: a Mr. and Mrs. Croft, neighbours from a nearby estate. He was a bluff, hearty gentleman who spoke loudly of crop prices, and she a quiet, faded woman who seemed to have little to say on any subject. They were soon followed by the vicar, Dr. Bailey, a portly man with a booming laugh and an inexhaustible store of local anecdotes. With each arrival, Elinor felt a fresh wave of anxiety on her sister’s behalf. Marianne remained by the window, a solitary and unapproachable figure, her back to the room.

Then, the butler announced, “Mr. Davies.”

Elinor’s heart gave a small, involuntary leap. She looked towards the door and saw him enter. He was dressed, as was proper, in formal evening attire, which suited his tall, slender frame and gave him an air of quiet distinction. He paused for a moment on the threshold, his intelligent eyes surveying the room and its occupants. He bowed to Mrs. Palmer, who greeted him with effusive delight, and exchanged a civil nod with Mr. Palmer. Then his gaze found Elinor.

For a moment, their eyes met across the room, and she saw a look of surprise, immediately followed by one of quiet pleasure, dawn on his face. He made his way towards her at once.

“Miss Dashwood,” he said, with a low bow. “This is a most unexpected pleasure. I had no notion, when I accepted Mrs. Palmer’s kind invitation, that I should have the good fortune of meeting you and your sister here.”

“Nor I you, sir,” Elinor replied, her voice more composed than she felt. The simple, sincere pleasure in his greeting was a balm to her frayed nerves. “I was not aware that Combe Magna was in this part of the country.”

“Indeed it is. I have been settled there for only a fortnight. It is a very pleasant old house, though I confess I have found the neighbourhood rather quieter than I am used to. Your presence, if I may say so, greatly improves its prospects.”

Mrs. Palmer, her duties as hostess momentarily forgotten, had been watching this exchange with the keenest interest. She now bustled over to them. “Oh! So you are old friends!” she exclaimed. “Miss Dashwood was so very sly about it, I had no idea! Lord, what a romantic story! Meeting at a broken-down carriage! It is just like something out of a novel!”

Elinor felt a hot blush of mortification spread over her cheeks. “We are not old friends, ma’am,” she said, with more sharpness than she intended. “We are barely acquaintances. Our meeting was, as I told you, entirely by chance.”

“Oh, pooh!” said Mrs. Palmer, waving a dismissive hand. “That is how all the best friendships start. Mr. Davies, you must take Miss Dashwood in to dinner. I insist upon it. And you must sit by me, so that you can tell me all about your new house.”

Mr. Davies caught Elinor’s eye, and she saw in his a flicker of shared amusement at Mrs. Palmer’s complete want of subtlety. He bowed his assent. “I should be honoured,” he said, and the simple words, spoken with a quiet gravity, seemed to rob the command of its embarrassing officiousness.

When dinner was announced, he duly offered Elinor his arm. As they walked towards the dining-room, he said in a low voice, “I must ask your pardon, Miss Dashwood, if our chance meeting has exposed you to this… unsolicited interest. Mrs. Palmer seems a most vivacious hostess.”

“She is very good-natured,” Elinor replied, feeling the need to defend her hostess even while suffering under her actions. “Her intentions are always kind.”

“I do not doubt it,” he said. “Kind intentions, however, can sometimes produce the most awkward effects. I hope your sister is feeling recovered from the fatigues of your journey?” He glanced towards Marianne, who was being led in by a reluctant Mr. Palmer, her face pale and set.

“She is not as well as I could wish,” Elinor admitted, her voice dropping. “She finds society a great trial at present.”

“I can well imagine it,” he said, with a sympathetic glance that told her he understood more than he said. That, she thought, was the singular comfort of his company: he did not require things to be explained. His perception was as quick as his manners were delicate.

The dinner was as tedious as Elinor had feared. Mrs. Palmer chattered incessantly to Mr. Davies, demanding to know every detail about his life, his fortune, and his opinions on matrimony, all while casting significant looks towards Elinor. Mr. Croft talked across the table to Dr. Bailey about a dispute over a hedgerow, while their respective wives sat in almost total silence. Mr. Palmer, seated at the head of the table, looked thoroughly miserable and ate his dinner with a grim determination, speaking only to request more wine. Through it all, Elinor was acutely conscious of Marianne, seated opposite her, who neither ate nor spoke, but merely toyed with the silver on the table, her eyes fixed on her plate with an expression of profound, unbreachable misery.

Elinor did her best to maintain a polite conversation with Mr. Davies, turning his attention, whenever she could, from Mrs. Palmer’s interrogations to more general subjects, such as literature and the beauties of the local landscape. She found his mind to be as well-furnished as she had suspected; he had read widely, and his opinions were thoughtful and original, offered with a diffidence that was the very opposite of presumption. In his company, she felt her own intellect, so often starved for want of real conversation, revive and awaken. For moments at a time, she could almost forget the weight of her anxieties.

But then her eyes would fall on Marianne, and the weight would descend again, heavier than before. She saw that others were beginning to notice her sister’s strange behaviour. Mrs. Croft cast nervous, pitying glances at her, and even the booming Dr. Bailey seemed to lower his voice when he looked in her direction. The dinner became a torment, a public exhibition of her family’s private sorrow. She longed for it to be over.

When the ladies at last retired to the drawing-room, leaving the gentlemen to their port, the situation hardly improved. Mrs. Palmer immediately drew Elinor aside.

“Well, my dear! He is quite charming, is he not? And I am sure he is taken with you. Did you see how he looked at you when he came in? And he hardly spoke to another soul at dinner, besides myself, of course. Combe Magna is a very pretty estate, they say, and I should not wonder if his income was upwards of two thousand a year. It would be a capital match for you! Far better than that other business, which has come to nothing, I hear.”

This unthinking allusion to Edward was the final straw. “Mrs. Palmer,” Elinor said, her voice trembling with suppressed indignation, “I must beg you. You are mistaken in your suppositions, and the subject is a deeply painful one to me. Pray do not speak of it again.”

She turned and walked away, her heart beating fast, leaving a very surprised and slightly offended Mrs. Palmer in her wake. She sought refuge near the fire, her mind in a tumult of embarrassment and anger. She felt exposed, her feelings handled and examined with a rough, careless curiosity that was a thousand times worse than outright malice.

Her one comfort was the sight of Marianne. Her sister, finding a moment’s respite from the public gaze, had sunk into a large armchair in a shadowy corner of the room and, overcome by exhaustion and despair, had fallen into a light, uneasy sleep. Her head was tilted back against the velvet, her hands lying limply in her lap. In sleep, the rigid control she maintained over her features was gone, and the pure, unadulterated misery was plain for anyone to see. Her breathing was shallow, and a faint flush, the same feverish colour Elinor had noted before, was visible even in the dim light.

When the gentlemen rejoined them, Elinor saw Mr. Davies’s eyes go at once to the sleeping figure in the chair. He came over to where Elinor was standing.

“Your sister is unwell,” he said, not as a question, but as a statement of fact. His voice was low and full of a concern that was blessedly free from curiosity.

“I fear she is,” Elinor replied, her own voice unsteady. “The day has been too much for her. I must see if I can persuade her to go to bed.”

“Allow me,” he said, and before she could protest, he had crossed the room to Mr. Palmer. After a brief, quiet exchange, Mr. Palmer approached his wife. A moment later, Mrs. Palmer, looking rather subdued, came to Elinor.

“My dear, Mr. Palmer tells me your sister is tired. Pray do not feel you must stay on our account. Take her to her room. It is a great pity the evening should end so, but her health is the first thing to be considered, to be sure.”

Grateful for this release, however it had been procured, Elinor went to her sister’s side. She touched her shoulder gently. “Marianne,” she whispered. “Come. It is time for bed.”

Marianne started, her eyes flying open, wide and disoriented. “What is it? Where am I?”

“You are at Cleveland. You fell asleep. Come with me.”

Supported by Elinor’s arm, Marianne rose unsteadily to her feet. She seemed hardly aware of the other people in the room as she allowed her sister to lead her towards the door. As they passed, Elinor’s eyes met Mr. Davies’s once more. He gave her a small, almost imperceptible nod, an acknowledgement of their shared understanding in a room full of incomprehension. It was a gesture of quiet support, a confirmation of an unspoken alliance, and Elinor carried the memory of it with her as she guided her poor, shivering sister up the grand staircase and away from the noise and light of the party below.

She knew Marianne valued her solitude, even in misery, and a too-hasty return would only seem like surveillance. Instead, she paused by a large window at the end of the corridor, pressing her forehead against the cool glass. The night was dark and starless, a heavy blanket of cloud obscuring the moon. Outside, the park at Cleveland stretched away into blackness, its familiar shapes lost to the gloom. The view suited her spirits perfectly. The events of the day, of the week, of the last several months, crowded in upon her, a dizzying collage of hope and disappointment, of love and betrayal. Willoughby’s letter, with its cold, formal dismissal, was seared into her memory. She could feel the stiff paper in her hand, see the careless scrawl of his signature. It was a pain so profound, so all-encompassing, that Elinor felt her own sorrows shrink to a trifling insignificance. What was her own quiet, protracted anxiety over Edward Ferrars compared to this sudden, violent severing of all Marianne’s dearest hopes? Her own trial was a slow, wearing illness; her sister’s was a mortal wound. And she, Elinor, was the sole physician, a physician with no cure to offer, only the weak poultice of patience and the bitter draught of reason. She sighed, a long, shuddering breath that misted the window pane. It was not only Marianne’s heart that was in peril; it was her health. The feverish colour in her cheeks, the unnatural brightness of her eyes, the shivering that had seized her, these were not the signs of a common grief. A soft footstep on the carpeted runner behind her made her start. She turned from the window, composing her features with an effort that was becoming second nature. It was Mr. Davies, the gentleman whose quiet intervention had secured their release from the drawing-room. He had a book in his hand, as if on his way to his own chamber, but he stopped at a respectful distance.

“Miss Dashwood,” he said, his voice low and measured. “I hope I do not intrude upon your reflections.”

“Not at all, sir,” Elinor replied, her own voice betraying a little of her weariness. “I was merely taking a moment before returning to my sister.”

“I trust she is resting more comfortably now?” There was no hint of the idle curiosity that had so vexed her in Mrs. Palmer’s questions; his enquiry was simple, direct, and imbued with a gravity that invited a sincere answer.

“She is in her bed, which is the best place for her,” Elinor said, choosing her words with care. “Whether she rests is another matter entirely.”

He gave a slight nod, his gaze thoughtful. “Grief is a poor bedfellow. It allows little peace to body or mind.” He looked at Elinor, and she felt, for the first time that evening, that she was being truly seen. Not as the sensible older sister, not as a figure in a romantic drama, but as a person bearing a heavy load. “And it is a burden that often falls most heavily on those who must watch it in another.”

The astuteness of this remark, and the gentle sympathy with which it was delivered, very nearly undid Elinor’s composure. She felt an absurd impulse to confide in this relative stranger, to pour out the whole wretched story of Willoughby’s perfidy and her fears for Marianne’s sanity. She mastered it almost at once, but the effort left her feeling breathless.

“You are very right, sir,” she managed to say. “It is a difficult thing to witness.”

“Mrs. Palmer has been good enough to explain to me, in a general way, you understand, that your sister has received some distressing news,” he continued, as if sensing her struggle and wishing to put her at her ease by making his own knowledge plain. “I will not pretend to an ignorance I do not possess. But I hope you will believe that my concern, and I think I may speak for Mr. Palmer as well, is solely for your sister’s well-being, and for your own comfort while you are guests in this house.”

“You are very kind,” Elinor murmured, genuinely touched. “Mr. Palmer has been most considerate.”

“He is a man of fewer words than his wife,” Mr. Davies observed, a faint, wry smile touching his lips for the first time, “but his feelings are no less sincere for being less spoken. If there is any thing, any service at all, that we may provide, you have only to ask. A message to be sent? An errand to be run in the village? You must not feel that you are alone in this, Miss Dashwood.”

The offer of practical help was so unexpected, and so welcome, that Elinor could only look at him in gratitude. “Thank you, Mr. Davies. I shall remember your offer. At present, there is nothing to be done. We must simply… wait.”

“Then I will not keep you any longer from your vigil,” he said, bowing. “I wish you, and your sister, a more peaceful night than you may expect.”

He turned and walked quietly down the corridor, leaving Elinor with a strange mixture of feelings. She was grateful for his kindness, surprised by his perception, and faintly troubled by the ease with which he had drawn from her a more honest response than she had given to anyone else that day. He was, she judged, a man of some sense and true breeding, a world apart from the boisterous Sir John or the vapid Mrs. Palmer. His presence was a small, steadying point in a world that seemed to have been thrown into chaos.

Shaking off these thoughts, she turned towards Marianne’s room. She opened the door softly and crept inside. The fire had been made up by a servant and now cast a warm, flickering light over the chamber. Marianne was lying on her side, her face turned away from the door, but her posture was rigid, and Elinor could hear the sound of small, suppressed sobs. She had not been sleeping, but weeping, hoarding her sorrow as if it were a treasure.

Elinor went to the bedside. “Marianne,” she said softly. “You will make yourself ill. You must try to sleep.”

“Sleep!” Marianne’s voice was muffled by the pillow, thick with tears. “How can I sleep? How can I ever know peace again? Oh, Elinor, he is gone. He is gone forever, and he never loved me.”

The words were a fresh torrent of grief. Elinor sat on the edge of the bed and put a hand on her sister’s shaking shoulder. “He is not worth your tears, Marianne. A man who can act with such cruelty, such duplicity, ”

“Do not speak ill of him!” Marianne cried, turning over with a sudden, fierce energy. Her face, tear-stained and pale in the firelight, was a mask of tragedy. “You do not understand. I do not understand. There must be some mistake, some dreadful misunderstanding. His letter… it was not his hand, it could not be his heart! Someone has forced him to it. His aunt, perhaps! “Sometimes, it seems there is very little else,” Elinor admitted, her own heart aching for Edward, for the tangled web of duty and prior engagement that kept them apart. “But we cannot know Willoughby’s motives for certain. We only know his actions. And his actions have been unpardonable.”

“You are so calm,” Marianne whispered, her anger spent, her voice sinking once more into despair. “You do not feel it as I do. Your heart is not broken.”

“My heart,” Elinor began, but she could not go on. What could she say? That her own heart was bruised and weary from its own silent battle? That she envied Marianne her freedom to weep, to rage, to display her suffering for all the world to see? No, it would be no comfort to her sister. She was the strong one. She had to be.

“We will speak of it no more tonight,” she said, her tone gentle but firm. “You are exhausted, and you have a fever coming on. You must rest.” She smoothed Marianne’s hair back from her hot forehead. “I will stay with you until you fall asleep.”

She sat by the bed for a long time, listening to Marianne’s ragged breathing as it slowly, gradually, deepened into the troubled sleep of exhaustion. Only when she was certain her sister was truly unconscious did she allow herself to move. She adjusted the blankets, lowered the lamp, and sank into a wing-backed chair by the fire, her mind too active for sleep. The night stretched before her, long and silent, and she prepared to keep her lonely watch.

The next morning confirmed all of Elinor’s fears. Marianne awoke from her stupor with a high fever and a racking cough. Her words were disjointed, alternating between pleas for Willoughby and confused muttering about her home at Barton. She did not seem to recognise Elinor, or if she did, it was only as a nameless, comforting presence. Alarm seized Elinor, sharp and cold. This was no longer a matter of a broken heart; it was a serious, physical illness.

She rang for a servant and sent an urgent message to Mrs. Palmer, asking that an apothecary be summoned from the village at once. Then she set about the business of nursing. She bathed Marianne’s face with cool water, tried to coax a few sips of barley-water between her parched lips, and spoke to her in low, soothing tones, though she doubted her sister could comprehend the words.

Mrs. Palmer, her usual ebullience quite gone, soon appeared at the door, her face a picture of distress. “Oh, my dear Miss Dashwood! This is dreadful! Quite dreadful! I came as soon as I got your note. Poor, poor Marianne! Is she very bad?”

“She is very ill, Ma’am,” Elinor said, her voice strained with lack of sleep and anxiety. “I have taken the liberty of sending for Mr. Jones, the apothecary.”

“Mr. Jones! Yes, of course, the very thing! Mr. Palmer has already sent a man on his fastest horse. He should be here within the hour. Oh, you poor dear,” she added, looking at Elinor’s pale, tired face. “You look as ill as she does. You must have some breakfast. I will have a tray sent up to you.”

“You are very good, but I have no appetite,” Elinor replied. “I cannot leave her side.”

At that moment, Mr. Palmer himself appeared behind his wife. He looked from Elinor to the moaning figure on the bed, and his florid, usually impassive face creased with concern.

“This is a bad business,” he said gruffly. “A very bad business. Jones is a competent fellow, but the girl looks to be in a high state. Cleveland is known for its dampness in the spring. We should have thought of that.” He seemed to be reproaching himself, a thing so unheard of that it silenced his wife completely.

From the hallway, Mr. Davies’s voice could be heard. “Is there any news of Miss Dashwood?”

Mr. Palmer turned. “She is very ill, Davies. Very ill indeed. We have sent for the apothecary.”

Mr. Davies came to the doorway, his expression grave. He looked past the Palmers directly at Elinor. His gaze was not intrusive, but steady and questioning, as if to ask what was truly needed.

“Is there a physician in the area of better reputation than the local apothecary?” he asked quietly, addressing Mr. Palmer but clearly for Elinor’s benefit. “In a case of such sudden fever, a second opinion might not be amiss.”

“There is Dr. Harris in Exeter,” Mr. Palmer said, pondering. “A good man, they say. But that is a journey of several hours.”

“If you will permit me, sir,” Mr. Davies said at once, “my horse is fresh. I could be in Exeter and back before nightfall. I would be glad to go, if Miss Dashwood thinks it wise.”

All eyes turned to Elinor. The responsibility of the decision was hers alone. To send for a physician was to admit the gravity of the situation, to cause further trouble and expense to her hosts, and perhaps to alarm Marianne, should she have a moment of lucidity. But to delay… to delay might be to risk everything. She looked at her sister, tossing and muttering on the pillows, her skin flushed and hot to the touch. The decision was made.

“I would be eternally grateful, Mr. Davies,” she said, her voice trembling slightly. “If you would be so good.”

“Consider it done,” he said, with a brief, reassuring nod. He did not waste words on sympathy or offer false hope. He simply accepted the task, and with a word to Mr. Palmer about his departure, he was gone.

Elinor felt a small, solitary tear escape and trace a path down her cheek. It was not a tear of sorrow for Marianne, but a tear of gratitude for an act of plain, sensible kindness in a world that had suddenly become a terrifying and lonely place. She wiped it away impatiently. There was no time for tears. There was only the long wait for the apothecary, the longer wait for the doctor, and the endless, terrifying space in between, filled with the sound of her sister’s fevered breathing.

At last, a quiet shuffling was heard in the passage, and Mr. Palmer entered, followed by a small, neat-looking man with a serious face and spectacles perched upon his nose. This, Elinor deduced, must be Mr. Jones. He bowed to her with a grave air, and without any unnecessary preamble, moved directly to the bedside.

Elinor’s heart sank at the grim certainty in his tone. “We were caught in the rain some days ago, on a long walk,” she admitted, her voice scarce above a whisper. “But she seemed to suffer no ill effects at the time.”

“There is always hope, Miss Dashwood, while there is life. But I will not disguise from you that the case is a very serious one. The fever is high, and must be broken. At this, Elinor could not suppress a shudder. The remedy seemed to her almost as barbarous as the disease, but she knew it was the common practice, and she was in no position to argue. “As you think best, sir,” she murmured, turning her face away so that he might not see the terror in her eyes.

Mr. Palmer, who had been observing all with a grim and silent attention, spoke up. “Do what you must, Jones. We have also sent to Exeter for Dr. Harris, for a second opinion. I trust you do not object.”

And so the grim work began. Mr. Jones, with the assistance of the maid and a footman, performed the bleeding, a procedure from which Elinor was obliged to turn away, feeling more faint and sick than she would have thought possible. She could only pray that this draining of her sister’s already taxed vitality would have the desired effect, and not hasten the very end they all so feared. Afterwards, the apothecary mixed his powders and draughts, leaving precise instructions with Elinor for their administration every hour, and promising to call again before sunrise.

His departure left a new kind of silence in the room, a silence filled not with mere waiting, but with the active and dreadful business of sickness. Elinor sat by the bed, her watch on the small table beside her, marking the passage of each hour with a dose of the chalky, bitter-tasting medicine, which she had the greatest difficulty in getting Marianne to swallow. For the most part, her sister remained lost in a feverish stupor, muttering incomprehensible phrases, her hands constantly plucking at the bedclothes. Once, however, with a terrible clarity, she cried out a single word: “Willoughby!” The name fell like a stone into the quiet room, and Elinor felt a fresh stab of pain, a mixture of anger and pity so intense it nearly choked her. To think that he, the author of all this misery, was at that very moment, in all likelihood, enjoying the comforts of London and the society of his wealthy bride, while the girl he had ruined lay struggling for her very life. The injustice of it was almost too much to bear.

Some time after midnight, Mrs. Jennings crept into the room again, this time bearing a bowl of steaming broth. “You must eat something, my dear Elinor,” she whispered, her voice stripped of its usual boisterous cheer and full of a genuine, motherly concern. “You will do yourself a mischief if you go on like this. You cannot help her if you fall ill yourself.”

Elinor, exhausted and heartsick, could not refuse such a kindness. She allowed Mrs. Jennings to take her place by the bed for a few moments, and forced herself to swallow some of the soup. It gave her a little strength, but could do nothing to warm the chill that had settled deep in her soul.

“Has there been any word from Exeter?” Elinor asked, returning to her post.

“Not yet, my love. It is a long ride, and the roads are not the best. But Mr. Davies is a determined young man, I am sure he will make all the haste he can. Lord, what a blessing that he was here! So sensible, and so ready to be of service. Not at all like some young men, who are all show and no substance.” The implied comparison with Willoughby was not lost on Elinor.

Mrs. Jennings settled herself in a chair on the other side of the bed, declaring her intention to sit with them for a while. “It reminds me so of my poor husband’s last illness,” she said, her voice dropping to a confidential murmur. “He had a fever just like this, after a terrible attack of the gout. The doctors bled him, and purged him, and gave him powders enough to stock a shop, but it was no use. He just slipped away. But Marianne is young and strong; her constitution is not worn out like his was. She will pull through, you will see.”

Though her stories were rambling and her comparisons often grim, Elinor found a strange comfort in her presence. Mrs. Jennings’s steadfast refusal to despair, her tireless and practical kindness, was a rock to cling to in this sea of uncertainty.

“There is something we must do, Elinor,” Mrs. Jennings said after a period of quiet reflection. “We must send for Colonel Brandon.”

Elinor started. “For Colonel Brandon? Oh, no, ma’am, we could not. It would be to give him so much pain, to no purpose.”

“To no purpose? My dear, the man is devoted to her. He would never forgive us if he were not told, if he were not here. He has a right to be here, I think, as much as any of us. He would move heaven and earth for her, you know he would. And who can say? Perhaps his presence might be of some use. If she were to have a lucid moment, to see a friendly, faithful face… it could only do her good. Besides,” she added, with a return to her more practical tone, “he is a man of resources and sense. It is good to have such men about you in a crisis. Mr. Palmer is well enough, but the Colonel has a different sort of gravity. We must send a man to Delaford at first light.”

Elinor hesitated. Her first instinct was to shield the Colonel from the distress this news would inevitably cause him. She knew the depth of his feelings for Marianne, and the thought of his suffering was an added burden. Yet, she could not deny the justice of Mrs. Jennings’s words. He would want to know. He would want to be there. To keep it from him would be a false kindness, an insult to his sincere attachment.

“You are right, ma’am,” she said at last. “He ought to be told. I will write a note, if Mr. Palmer can spare a messenger.”

“Of course he can,” said Mrs. Jennings with decision. “I will speak to him myself. You write your letter, my dear.”

With a trembling hand, Elinor composed a brief, carefully worded note to the Colonel. She stated the fact of Marianne’s grave illness plainly, for she knew he was not a man to be trifled with, but she refrained from any dramatic embellishment, trusting the simple truth to be powerful enough. The act of writing his name, of dispatching this summons to one who loved her sister so truly, seemed to make the situation horribly official, and she had to fight back tears as she sealed the letter.

The hours that followed were the longest of Elinor’s life. The house sank into the profound stillness of the very deep of night, a quiet broken only by Marianne’s restless moans, the ticking of the clock, and the occasional creak of a floorboard as a servant passed on some hushed errand. Mrs. Jennings eventually retired, promising to relieve Elinor at dawn, leaving her once again to her solitary vigil.

The first grey light of morning was beginning to filter through the curtains when Elinor, who must have dozed for a moment in her chair, was startled by a sound from the bed. Marianne was trying to sit up, her eyes wide open, though they seemed to see nothing in the room.

“Mama?” she called out, her voice thin and reedy. “Mama, is it you? I am so cold.”

Elinor was at her side in an instant, taking her burning hands. “It is Elinor, my dearest. Mama is not here. But I am here. I will not leave you.”

Marianne stared at her, a flicker of recognition in her eyes, which was almost immediately clouded over again by the fever. “He is gone,” she whispered, her gaze turning towards the window. “He is gone, and he will not come back. It is all my fault. I drove him away.”

“No, my love, no,” Elinor soothed, trying to ease her back against the pillows. “It is not your fault. You must rest.”

But Marianne would not be pacified. She began to weep, not with the violent passion of her earlier grief, but with the weak, helpless sobbing of a frightened child. The sight of it was more than Elinor could endure. All her carefully constructed composure crumbled, and she knelt by the bed, her own tears flowing freely as she held her sister’s hand, feeling the life within it flutter like a trapped bird. How long she remained so, she did not know, but she was roused by a gentle touch on her shoulder. It was Mrs. Jennings, her face full of pity.

“Go and rest, my dear child,” she said softly. “You have done all you can. I will watch with her now. Go and lie down for an hour. The doctor cannot be far off now.”

Elinor allowed herself to be led from the room, too exhausted to protest. She lay down upon the bed in her own chamber, but sleep would not come. Her mind was a torment of fears and prayers. She thought of her mother, so far away and ignorant of her daughter’s peril. She thought of Colonel Brandon, riding through the dawn towards this house of sorrow. And she thought of Mr. Davies, whose return with the physician from Exeter was now their only tangible hope.

It was perhaps another two hours before she heard what her ears had been straining for all night: the distant but distinct sound of a carriage approaching at speed. She sat bolt upright, her heart hammering against her ribs. She flew to the window, which looked out over the drive. It was not a carriage, but two riders, galloping up the long approach to the house. One she did not recognise, but the other, even at a distance, was unmistakable. It was not Mr. Davies. It was Colonel Brandon.

He must have ridden without stopping, for his horse was lathered in sweat, and he himself looked pale and dishevelled. He dismounted before the horse had even come to a complete halt and threw the reins to a groom who had come running from the stables. Elinor felt a renewed sense of crisis. His arrival, so much sooner than she had anticipated, brought home the terrible urgency of their situation. She smoothed her hair, splashed her face with cold water from the basin, and went downstairs to meet him.

“There, there, Charlotte,” he said. “Crying will do no good. The girl is in the best hands.”

“But it is all my fault!” wept Mrs. Palmer. “We should never have left Barton! It is this horrid, damp house! I knew it was unhealthy! And for her to be so ill, under my roof… oh, it is too dreadful!”

“Nonsense, Charlotte,” said Mr. Palmer, with a return to his usual dryness, though it was tempered with concern. “You could not have foreseen it. No one could.”

Mrs. Jennings, meanwhile, had taken charge of Elinor. “You are to have some broth, my love, and a glass of the Constantia. The doctors said so, you know! I heard them myself. And you are to lie down. We have had a bed made up for you in the little dressing-room next to Marianne’s, so you can be close by. You must not fret. The Colonel is with her, and that Mr. Donovan seems a very capable man. Lord! what a blessing that the Colonel brought that Dr. Lacey. Two heads are better than one, as my poor husband used to say, especially when one of them belongs to a physician from Bath! They are all the fashion, you know.”

Elinor submitted to it all. She drank the broth and the wine, which gave her a little artificial strength, and allowed Mrs. Jennings to lead her to the small dressing-room. It was a comfort to know she was only a few feet away from her sister. The room was prepared, the bed turned down, but she knew she could not sleep. She sat in a chair by the window, looking out into the impenetrable blackness of the night, and gave herself over to her thoughts.

Her mind was a tumultuous sea of fear for Marianne, and gratitude for Colonel Brandon. His quiet, unassuming devotion shone like a beacon in the darkness of her despair. He had come without a moment’s hesitation, had brought help, had taken on the burden of informing her mother, and was now sitting by her sister’s bed, asking nothing, only giving. When she contrasted this selfless conduct with the selfish, cruel behaviour of Willoughby, a feeling of indignation so powerful it almost choked her rose in her breast. It was Willoughby who had done this. It was his heartless desertion that had broken Marianne’s spirit and laid her low, a ready victim for the first fever that came her way. He was, in Elinor’s eyes, her sister’s murderer, as surely as if he had administered poison. And yet, even as she condemned him, she heard in her memory Marianne’s delirious, fevered cry of his name, and a wave of pity, unwanted and unwelcome, complicated her anger. Whatever his crimes, Marianne loved him still; and for that alone, Elinor could almost have found it in her to forgive him.

The three hours passed with excruciating slowness. At last, she heard a soft tap at her door. It was Colonel Brandon. His face, in the candlelight, was pale and etched with fatigue, but his eyes were calm.

“It is time,” he said. “There is no change. She is very restless, but the apothecary has administered the laudanum, and she is a little quieter now. You should go to her. I will try to get some rest myself.”

Elinor went at once to the sickroom. The scene was as she had left it, save that Mr. Donovan was now seated in a chair by the fire, dozing lightly. The air was thick with the scent of vinegar and herbs. She approached the bed and looked down at her sister. Marianne’s breathing seemed, if anything, even more shallow, her skin still burned with fever. Elinor sat down in the chair the Colonel had vacated, the chair that was to be her post for the next three hours. She took her sister’s hot, dry hand in her own. It lay there limply, without response.

“Marianne,” she whispered, “I am here. I will not leave you.”

There was no answer but the faint, rasping breath and the ticking of the clock. The long, terrible night stretched before her, an abyss of waiting and praying, with nothing to hold onto but the faintest glimmer of a doctor’s hope, and the steadfast, unwavering constancy of a good man’s love.
