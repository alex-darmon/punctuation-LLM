He held the chronometer to the light of the single oil lamp, and its polished brass case seemed to drink the flame, to hold it captive in its warm and glowing heart. The rest of the small cabin, a chaotic museum of sextants, rolled charts, and curious bits of scrimshaw, fell away into a soft and whispering darkness. He was a man made of sea-salt and old sorrows, his face a creased and yellowed map of journeys I could not imagine.

“When I first saw her papers,” he began, his voice a low rumbling that was part of the ship’s own groan, “I thought my fortune was made. A charter to the Sunda Islands, carrying a cargo I did not ask about, for a fee that made my ears ring. She was a pretty brig, the ‘Aurelia’; I can see her now, sitting so calm on the water in that dusty Eastern port. I had been a long time without a command, a very long time. I had told myself stories of such a moment in the deep of the night watch, and here it was, handed to me on a platter by a fat Dutchman with sweat on his lip--for me!” He adopted a tone of mock reverence. “‘The ship, she is yours, Captain,’ he tells me, ‘so long as the tide holds.’” He set the chronometer down with a delicate click, its ticking suddenly loud in the silence he had made. He began to fill a small clay pipe, his thumb tamping the dark tobacco with a slow, methodical rhythm.

“Yes, my young friend. In that moment, the world was a perfect instrument; I was master of my own fate; I had a ship, a crew that did not hate me yet; I had the wind” (he pronounced it “vint”) “in my future sails, a woman I had, in a port I had left, whose memory was a comfort--and even the impossible thing I had wished for had come to pass!” He struck a lucifer against the underside of the table. The sudden flare cast his face in a mask of stark ridges and deep gullies. His calm blue eyes did not blink. “‘Ship, crew, woman,’” he said, his voice barely a whisper into the cupped flame--“pfft!” The match went out, leaving a ghost of sulphur in the air. He sighed, a sound like ropes straining. “I turned back to the wharf. The dark water sloshed against the pilings, and for a moment I felt as if the pretty ship and the fat Dutchman had been a dream called up by my own breathing.”

“The voyage,” he continued, pointing a stem of his pipe towards a discoloured chart pinned to the bulkhead, “is where the trouble begins. It is all in the planning. . . . So! And what is the storm on your horizon?”

“To be truthful, Captain,” I said, and the words felt heavy, like pulling up a fouled anchor, “I came to you to report a faulty instrument. . . .”

“Compass?” he asked, a glint of technical curiosity in his eye.

“Something less reliable,” I answered, a wave of hopelessness washing over me. “A man!”

“Ah,” he breathed out, a plume of smoke obscuring him for a second. When it cleared, his expression was one of profound and weary recognition. He looked at me for a long moment, then nodded slowly. “Well--I am a man too.”

There you have the heart of him; he had a way of stating the obvious that made it sound like a revelation, an offer of complicity that made a man feel both understood and utterly exposed. But if he saw my hesitation, he gave no sign.

He heard me out, his pipe fuming, his chair creaking in time with the slow roll of the ship. A sympathetic grunt or a cloud of smoke was his only interruption. When my sorry tale of a man caught between duty and desire was finished, he laid his pipe in a ceramic dish, leaned his weathered forearms on the cluttered table, and laced his fingers together.

“I see very well. He is lost.”

He had plotted my position for me, and I was startled to find my complex anxieties reduced to so simple a nautical crisis; and the whole interview had taken on the aspect of a consultation with a ship’s surgeon--the Captain, with his air of ancient knowledge, behind his desk; I, the patient, on a stool before him--so that it felt natural to ask--

“What is the cure for it?”

He raised a single, salt-stained finger. “There is only one chart! One course alone can us from being lost deliver!” The finger tapped the table with a sharp report. The problem, which had seemed so vast and tangled, became at once brutally simple--and entirely insoluble. There was a long pause, filled only by the ship’s timber and the ticking clock. “Yes,” I said, “but the question is not how to be found, but how to sail on.”

He nodded his head, a gesture of sad agreement. “Ja! ja! In the words of your own great playwright: That is the question. . . .” He continued to nod, as if to a silent rhythm. . . . “How to sail! Ah! How to sail.”

He pushed himself to his feet, his tall frame unfolding in the cramped space.

“We want our course to be so many things,” he mused. “This little barnacle finds a solid piece of hull and holds fast for its life; but man he will never on his own safe course hold still. He want to go this way, and he want to go that way. . . .” He gestured with his hand, a ship tacking against an unseen wind. . . . “He wants to be an admiral, and he wants to be a pirate--and in every storm he closes his eyes and sees himself as a great navigator--so great as he can never be. . . . His voice, coming from that gloom where he seemed to be wrestling with the shades of forgotten coastlines, was no longer sharp, but took on a deep and hollow resonance--muffled by the distance.

“And because you cannot always keep your charts rolled up, there comes the true reckoning--the heart’s current--the soul’s gale. The shadow, moving amongst the ghosts of dead voyages, gave a short, bitter laugh.

“Yes! A terrible and funny thing this is. A man that is born is set adrift on a dream, like a man cast away in a lifeboat. His voice rose with a sudden, startling power, as if in that darkness some truth had whispered its name to him. “I will tell you! For that too there is only one way.”

With a scrape of his boots he was back in the ring of light, looming over the table. His life had begun with a command, with a passion for the well-made ship; he had sailed very far, by many routes, on strange tides, and whatever he sought it had been with his whole soul, and therefore without apology and without regret. When at last I found my voice it was to suggest that no one could be more lost than he.

He made a small, weary gesture with his hand, as if brushing away a chart that was not there. ‘“A man makes his plan. He has a compass, a chronometer . . . he has the whole world in a little brass-and-glass box. And the sea says nothing. It lets him believe in his little box. It lets him feel very clever. But the sea has its own time, which is no time at all, and its own direction, which is all directions at once.”’

His eyes, which had been fixed on the polished surface of the table as if it were a flat, calm sea, lifted to mine. ‘“He believed in it more than anything,”’ I said, and the sound of my own voice seemed thin and inadequate in that room, a place of echoes and immense silences. ‘“He saw the course laid out clear before him. A matter of honour. A straight line.”’

‘“Ah, honour,”’ Kruger murmured, nodding slowly. ‘“A straight line. The straightest line is the one that leads a ship onto the rocks. The sea has no straight lines. Inside, suspended on fine wires from unseen supports, was the model of a ship. It was not a grand clipper or a man-of-war; it was a small, sturdy barque, rendered in exquisite detail, with rigging as fine as spider-silk and sails no bigger than a child’s palm, neatly furled.

‘“There was a man once,”’ he began, his voice muffled by his proximity to the glass, ‘“a first mate. On a ship very like this one. Not this one, no . . . this one was my first command. He paused, and from the shadows came the small, familiar sounds of his pipe being scraped and filled. I waited, the ticking of the chronometer on his desk seeming to count out the seconds of a long-dead voyage.

‘“Heller was a man who loved a straight line,”’ Kruger continued, his voice taking on a new texture, a kind of grim animation. ‘“He had the finest set of instruments I had ever seen. He polished the brass on his sextant every day. Every day. At noon he would be on the deck, a high priest of the sun, and woe to the man who cast a shadow on his ritual. He could fix our position to within a hundred yards, he said. A master of his craft. And he had his chart, with the island marked in red ink. A perfect circle. Paradise.”’

A match flared in the dusk, illuminating for an instant his strong, weathered features, the deep lines etched around his eyes. He drew on the pipe, and the bowl glowed like a distant port light. ‘“For two weeks, it was a perfect voyage. The sea was glass. The wind was a friend. The believers sang hymns on the deck in the evening. Heller would show me our progress on the chart, the little pencil marks marching in a straight line towards the red circle. He was a happy man. He had the world in its place. Then the sky turned yellow.”’

The glow of the pipe pulsed in the darkness. ‘“Not a storm. Worse. A calm. One of those dead, flat, oily calms where the air is too thick to breathe and the sun is a hammer. The sails hung like wet rags. For a day. For three days. For a week. The water in the casks grew warm and began to smell. The children cried. The believers stopped singing their hymns. And Heller . . . Heller kept taking his sights at noon, making his little pencil marks. But the marks did not move. We were a fixed point in a universe of glittering, hateful emptiness.”’

He came forward and sat down again, the pipe smoke wreathing his head like a spectral mist. ‘“Then the fever came. Not a bad fever at first. Just a few of the colonists. Then a sailor. Then another. And the water was going fast. I knew this sea. I had been a trader in these islands for years. I knew there was a current, a deep, slow current that the charts did not show, that ran west towards the Macassar Strait. If we put the boats in the water, we could tow ourselves into it. It would take us away from the island. It would ruin Heller’s straight line. But it would take us towards water, towards life.”’

‘“I gave the order. To put the longboats over the side. Heller came to my cabin. He was very pale, but his eyes were shining with a terrible sanity. He held his chart in his hand. ‘Captain,’ he said to me, ‘this is a deviation. The owner’s instructions are explicit. We are to proceed to this position.’ He pointed to the red circle. As if we could proceed. As if his pointing finger had the power to move a ship of four hundred tons.

‘“I told him, ‘Mr. Heller, the chart is paper, and the sea is God. We will follow the sea.’ He looked at me as if I had gone mad. ‘That is romantic nonsense, sir,’ he said. ‘We have instruments. We have discipline. We are not savages who pray to the water.’ He believed in the plan, you see. He believed in the dream of the straight line. To abandon it was to abandon his very self. He could not do it. He would not.”’

Kruger laid his pipe down, the embers dead. His face in the lamplight was like a mask of tragedy, ancient and deeply felt. ‘“The men, they were afraid. Torn. Half of them were old hands who knew I was right; the other half were new, and they believed in Heller’s brass and his charts. The colonists, they wanted only to get to the island, the paradise they were promised. They saw me as the man who was turning them away from their dream. Heller spoke to them. He told them I had lost my nerve. That I was surrendering to chaos. He showed them the chart. The beautiful, clean, straight line. And the little red circle. So near. So very near.”’

He leaned forward, his elbows on the desk, his hands clasped so tightly that the knuckles were white. ‘“It came to a choice. They would not put the boats over. Heller had them. He had their fear and he had their hope. He would wait for the wind, he said. The wind would come. It was a matter of discipline. Of holding the course. Of not being a romantic. I looked at their faces . . . the faces of the believers, the ones who wanted paradise. And I knew what you know now. I knew what it is to describe a specimen.”’

My own heart was beating heavily. It was as if he had been on my own ship, had seen my own man. ‘“What did you do?”’ I asked, my voice a whisper.

‘“What could I do?”’ he said, his shoulders slumping. ‘“A captain cannot fight his whole ship. A man cannot force another man to be saved. He can only show the way. So I did the only thing I could. I surrendered. Not to Heller. No. To the sea.”’ He looked up, and his eyes had a strange, distant glimmer, the light of an old memory seen across a great expanse of years. ‘“I provisioned one of the boats. My own boat. In the night. With a little water, a little food. I took a compass, but not a chronometer. I took no charts. I went to the cabin of a woman, one of the colonists. Her child was sick with the fever. Her husband was a believer, one of Heller’s men. I told her, ‘The ship is a grave. But there is a current. It will take us west.’ She looked at her child, and then she looked at me. And she chose.”’

He fell silent for a long time. The room was so still I could hear the faint hum of the oil in the lamp. The ship in the glass case seemed to float in its own private darkness, a vessel on an eternal, motionless sea.

‘“We slipped over the side in the darkest hour, before the moon rose. The woman, her child, and myself. Three souls in a longboat. As the dawn came, a grey, sullen dawn, we saw the ship. A black shape, motionless, with its sails hanging like shrouds. It looked already like a wreck. We rowed for a full day to find the current. And on the second morning, we saw it. A line on the water. A disturbance. We entered it, and it took us. For five days we drifted. The child was very bad. I thought we had only exchanged one grave for another, a slower one. But on the sixth day, we saw the sail of a prau, a Bugis trader I knew. He gave us water. He took us to Macassar. The child lived.”’

‘“And the ship?”’ I asked. ‘“Heller?”’

Kruger shook his head slowly, a gesture of immense finality. ‘“Nothing. She was never seen again. No wreckage. No word. She was found, I think. By the sea. Heller held his straight course into the heart of the calm. He kept his dream, you see. He kept it to the end. He would not surrender. He would not immerse. He tried to climb out into the air, as I told you. And he drowned. Not in water, but in the lack of it. Drowned in a perfect calm, under a perfect, empty sky. A martyr to his own chart.”’

He stood up and walked once more to the window, peering out into the night which was as black and featureless as the sea of his story. ‘“I lost my ship,”’ he said, his back to me. ‘“I lost my career. The owner in Batavia called me a deserter, a coward. I had nothing. But I was not lost. For I had learned the way. How to be. You surrender to the currents that you cannot fight, and you row with all your strength in the direction they are taking you. That is all. That is the only way.”’

He turned back to me, and his face was composed, the storm of memory passed. ‘“So. Your man. This romantic. He has a dream of a straight line, of honour. He has fallen into the sea. And he is trying to climb out, is he not?”’

‘“Yes,”’ I said, the word costing me a great effort. ‘“He is trying to climb out.”’

‘“And he will drown,”’ Kruger said, not as a question, but as a statement of fact. ‘“Unless. . . .”’ He let the word hang in the air. He came close and put his hand on my shoulder again, the gesture now feeling less like one of comfort and more like the steadying hand of a pilot on a helmsman’s arm. ‘“You cannot take the dream from him. To do that is to kill him as surely as Heller’s calm killed him. The dream is the wind. Without it, he is becalmed. But the dream is not the course. That is the secret. He must learn to use the dream to sail the sea as it is, not to sail the sea as he wishes it to be.”’

His grip tightened for a moment. ‘“You cannot give him a new chart. You cannot show him a safe harbour. You must tell him to go on. To follow the dream, yes. Always to follow the dream. But to trim his sails to the wind that is, not the wind he prays for. You must tell him to immerse. That is the only way he can make the deep, deep sea keep him up. It is a terrible thing to tell a man. It is like telling him to drown in order to learn how to swim.”’

He let go of my shoulder and sat down, a great weariness seeming to settle over him, the telling of his tale having taken some vital energy from him. The light from the lamp cast long, dancing shadows that made the room seem once more vast and uncertain. His whispered conviction, born of a failure that was also a kind of victory, a shipwreck that was also a salvation, had not provided an answer. It had only illuminated the terrifying nature of the question. The great ocean on which my friend drifted remained just as empty, just as menacing. But now, it seemed to me, I could see the faint, almost imperceptible line of a current in the water. It was not a path to safety. It led away from every known shore. But it was a current. It was a movement. It was, perhaps, a way. To follow it was an act of faith not in a destination, but in the journey itself. An act of submission that was also the highest form of resistance. I looked at Kruger, this man who had lost everything to find himself, and I understood that my question had been wrong from the start. I had come to ask how my friend could be saved. The real question, the only question, was how he was to live. And as I rose to take my leave, the answer, in all its terrible and liberating ambiguity, seemed to whisper from every shadow in that quiet room: ‘“Ewig--usque ad finem.”’ Forever--unto the end.

How could I go to him, to this boy who was a man, and deliver Kruger’s message? For it was a message, of a sort; a terrible gospel of immersion. ‘“You must tell him to immerse.”’ The words echoed in my head, not with the force of a command, but with the hollow resonance of a philosophical proposition, elegant in its symmetry, and utterly, hopelessly impractical. It was not advice; it was a paradox, a riddle posed by a sphinx who has already devoured all who failed to answer. I imagined myself facing him, his clear, honest eyes fixed on mine, waiting for a word of hope, a promise of redemption, a chart to a safe harbour. And what would I offer? For what, after all, was he, this romantic, this Jim, but a man who had believed too much in the promises? Not the promises of others, which are easily discounted, but the promises he had made to himself. He had seen himself as a very fine fellow, as Stein--or was it Kruger now? the voices were beginning to merge--had said, and the world had conspired to show him that he was not. It was a common enough story. But for him, the dream had been everything; it was not a pleasant fiction to be enjoyed in idle moments, but the very framework of his existence. To have it shattered was to have the world itself dissolve into a meaningless chaos. He was not a man who could pick up the pieces and build a smaller, more sensible shelter. He wanted the palace of his dreams or nothing; he wanted to be a hero, or he would be a pariah. There was no middle ground for him. And we, his friends, his well-wishers, we who saw the good in him, the fine material that had been so tragically flawed in the firing--what could we do? We wanted to save him. But what Kruger’s words had laid bare, with the precision of a surgeon’s knife, was that we could not save him *from* himself. The only way was *through* himself.

My walk had, without my conscious decision, taken me along the waterfront. The dark, solid shapes of the ships at anchor rose against the lesser darkness of the water, their riding lights like captive stars. I could hear the gentle lapping of the tide against the pilings, a soft, insistent, whispering sound. The sea. The great, deep sea, which keeps you up, if you submit. I looked out at its vast, inscrutable face, and I felt a chill that had nothing to do with the night air. This was the element. This was the reality he had to face. It was not hostile, not malicious; it was merely indifferent, a force of nature that would support you or swallow you with equal unconcern. It was in its remorseless impersonality that its terror lay. To submit to it was to abandon the idea of a special destiny, of a privileged fate, of a world that cared whether you swam or drowned. It was to accept that you were, in the end, alone with the exertions of your hands and feet.

And the dream? What of the dream? ‘“The dream is the wind,”’ Kruger had said. ‘“But the dream is not the course.”’ A fine distinction, and one, I suspected, that only a man who had spent a lifetime at the helm could truly grasp. For most of us, the wind and the course are one and the same; we pray for a fair wind to blow us to our desired destination. The idea of using an adverse wind, of tacking against the gale of one’s own imagination, of trimming the sails of one’s most cherished illusions to navigate the ugly, brute facts of the sea as it is--this was a feat of seamanship of the highest, most esoteric order. It was the secret of a lifetime, learned in shipwreck and despair. And he was to learn it now? In his youth, in his disgrace, with the ink still fresh on his cancelled certificate? It was too much to ask. It was like asking a blind man to appreciate the subtleties of a sunset.

And yet, as I turned away from the water and began to walk inland, into the narrower, more constricted streets of the European quarter, another thought began to form itself in my mind, a thought less hopeless, if no less grim. Perhaps the message was not for him at all. Perhaps it was for me. Perhaps the consultation with Kruger--with Stein, with all the wise, weary men who have seen the world and found it wanting--was not to procure a remedy for another, but to understand the nature of the disease in oneself. For was I not also a man who dreamed? Did I not also, in the quiet moments, see myself as a very fine fellow, a man of insight and sympathy, a reliable friend, a teller of truths? And here I was, balking at the first real test, shrinking from the task of speaking a difficult truth, seeking a way to soften the blow, to find a compromise, to offer a palliative. I was trying to climb out. I was looking for a safe harbour for my own conscience.

The recognition was so sudden, so unwelcome, that it stopped me in my tracks once more. It is… an opportunity. To go to a place so far removed from this world, so completely its own, that all the judgments, all the opinions, all the failures and the memories… they cannot reach it. They have no currency there.”

I could see the first spark of that dangerous, romantic fire returning to his eyes. He was listening now with his whole being.

“It is a place called Patusan,” I continued, my voice steady, almost cold. I would not embellish it; I would not soften it. He had to see it for what it was. “It is a native state, up a river, lost in the forests. It is run by a Rajah, a drunken old tyrant, and his son, a suspicious and violent man. The place is stagnant, forgotten by trade, riddled with factional strife between the people of the river and the inlanders, led by a man they call Doramin, and his son, Dain Waris. It is a place of intrigue, of sudden death. It is not safe. It is not respectable. It is, in every sense of the word, a wilderness.”

I paused, letting the unvarnished truth of it sink in. I had offered him no promise of success, no hope of comfort. I had painted a picture of a pit, a morass, a destructive element.

He stood perfectly still, his gaze locked on mine. He seemed to be looking straight through me, at the vision I had conjured. His lips were slightly parted. I had expected questions, doubts, hesitation. I had expected the scrupulous man to hesitate on the brink. But he was not a scrupulous man, not in that way. He was a romantic.

“And?” he breathed, the word a demand for the heart of the matter. “What would I be there?”

“You would be Stein’s man,” I said simply. “His agent. He has a trading post there, a relic from older, better days, now gone to seed. You would be in charge of it. You would be alone, absolutely alone, with a few guns, some trade goods, and your own wits. You would be the only white man for hundreds of miles. You would be… a power, for good or ill. Or you would be dead in a week. It is a place where a man is what he can make himself, and nothing more.”

I had said it. I had laid the abyss at his feet.

For a long moment, the silence in the room was absolute, broken only by the distant, muffled rumble of a cart in the street below. Jim’s face was a mask of concentration, his brow furrowed. He was seeing it, I knew; he was seeing the river, the jungle, the faces of the men he would have to master or serve. He was seeing the chance, not to erase his past, but to overwhelm it with a future so immense, so demanding, that the echo of the Patna would be drowned out.

Then, a slow, profound change came over him. The tension in his stance eased, replaced by a kind of strange and terrible elation. A faint colour rose to his cheeks. His eyes, when he finally blinked, seemed to have been washed clean of all their despair, and shone with a light that was both brilliant and profoundly unsettling. It was the light of an absolute conviction, the gaze of a martyr who sees the flame and walks towards it without a tremor.

“When?” he asked. His voice was not the defeated whisper of before. It was clear, steady, and resonant with a purpose I had not heard in it for a very long time.

“The schooner that can take you leaves in a fortnight,” I answered, feeling my own heart beat a little faster.

“A fortnight,” he repeated, as if it were an eternity. He took a step towards me, then another, until he stood very close. He did not offer his hand. He simply looked at me, and in his look there was a gratitude so vast and so silent that it was more potent than any words. He was not thanking me for a job. He was thanking me for believing in his dream, for taking it seriously, for feeding it with the only substance it could live on: an impossible reality.

“I will be ready,” he said. And in those four simple words, the Jim who had been adrift, the ghost haunting the edges of the Eastern ports, was gone. In his place stood the man he had always seen himself to be in his dreams: resolute, poised on the edge of action, facing a destiny of his own choosing. He had made the leap. He was already falling towards the sea.

I left him then. There was nothing more to say. Our conference, so unlike the one with Stein, had also reached its conclusion. I had delivered the remedy, or the poison. I walked out of that transient hotel and into the fading light of the afternoon, and I felt as if I had just stepped away from a momentous and irreversible act of creation; or perhaps, of destruction. I had taken a human life, a life already shipwrecked, and set it upon a new and infinitely more perilous voyage. I had not given him a chart, or a compass, or a safe harbour to aim for. I had simply pointed to a dark patch of the ocean, a place marked on the old maps with ‘Here be Dragons’, and told him to sail.

In the weeks that followed, I saw to the arrangements. There were supplies to be bought, letters from Stein to be delivered to the schooner’s captain, a taciturn Swede who looked at me as if I were mad for sending a man to such a place. He had been there once, years ago, and spoke of it with a shudder. “Is no place for white man,” he grunted, chewing on his cold pipe. “The fever and the kris. Is all they have there.” I said nothing. How could I explain to him that the fever and the kris were precisely the point?

I saw Jim only once more before he sailed. I had gone down to the wharf to make sure all was in order. He was there, standing by the rail of the shabby little vessel, looking out towards the open sea. He was dressed in simple white drill, and he looked younger, stronger than he had in years. He saw me and raised a hand, a gesture not of farewell, but of acknowledgement. I did not go aboard. I felt, in that moment, like a spectator who has bought his ticket and must now take his seat. The drama was his, and his alone. He was no longer my responsibility; he was Patusan’s.

As I watched the schooner being warped out from the quay, a strange sense of detachment came over me. I had done my part. I had taken Stein’s terrible, magnificent philosophy and made it flesh. I had taken a man’s romantic soul, which the world saw as a flaw, a weakness, and given it a world to match its own immensity. I had, as I had foreseen with that chilling clarity, pushed him in a little deeper.

Would he swim? Or would he drown? I did not know. I knew only that he would not, could not, try to climb out. He had submitted to the destructive element. He was following the dream, and again, following the dream.

I turned away from the harbour, from the sight of that small ship dwindling into the vastness of the sea, and walked back into the city’s familiar clamour. But the city was no longer the same. Its certainties, its routines, its solid, dependable realities seemed to me flimsy and unsubstantial. My mind was elsewhere. It was on a river winding through a jungle, in a place of shadows and violence, where a solitary young man with a clouded past was about to attempt to be. The name of the place was a whisper in my mind, a promise of either sublime triumph or utter catastrophe. Patusan. It had an echo, you see. An echo of a name that, for good or ill, was not yet ready to be forgotten. And I, who had sent him there, was condemned to listen for that echo for a very long time, across the immense and silent distance. I had made myself the keeper of his story, and I knew, with a certainty that was as profound as it was sorrowful, that the most extraordinary part of it was only just beginning.

It was a question that hung in the air between us long after I had taken my leave of him that night; it followed me through the familiar, lamp-lit streets and into the solitude of my own rooms. He, in the calm of his vast collection, surrounded by the ghosts of desires fulfilled and laid to rest; and I, in the restless tide of the port’s affairs, ever watching the horizon for a sail, ever listening in the babble of the shipping offices for a word, a whisper, a rumour from that forgotten corner of the world.

The weeks turned to months. The southwest monsoon broke, and for a time the roads ran with liquid mud and the sky was a uniform grey sheet from which water fell with a dreary and persistent drumming. Then the winds shifted, the sun returned with a vengeance, and the harbour once more became a frantic hive of steamers and praus, of schooners with peeling paint and luggers from the outlying islands, their crews chattering in a dozen dialects on the crowded quays. Of Patusan there was nothing. It was as if the place did not exist, or existed only in the philosophical schema of an old entomologist and in the anxious recesses of my own mind. It was a name without a geography, a destination without a return. I began to feel the weight of my action not as a risk, but as an ending. I had sent the man into a void, and the void had simply swallowed him. The silence was absolute, profound; it was the silence of a grave.

And then, one sweltering afternoon when the air was so thick with heat and the smell of spice and dust that it seemed a man could chew on it, the silence was broken. Not by a letter, not by an official report, but by a man. He was a Dutchman, the master of a small, disreputable-looking brig that traded in guttapercha and rattan where it could find them. I knew of him by sight; a thick-set fellow with a fiery red face and pale, suspicious eyes. He sought me out in the very exchange where I conducted my business, approaching my table with a sort of lumbering diffidence, as if he were at once determined to speak and uncertain of his welcome.

He introduced himself as Jansen and came to the point with a bluntness that was almost startling. “They tell me,” he said, his voice a low rumble, “that you are the man who sent the Englishman to Patusan.”

I admitted that I was. I felt a sudden coldness in my chest, a premonition of disaster so sharp it was like a physical blow. The void was about to speak, and I was not at all sure I wished to hear what it had to say.

He pulled out a chair without being asked and sat down heavily, mopping his brow with a large, soiled handkerchief. He stared at me for a long moment, his pale eyes narrowed in a look of frank bewilderment. “I have just come from there,” he said. “I was… becalmed. For a week. Off the mouth of the river. I went ashore for water.” He paused, took a deep breath, and leaned across the table. His voice dropped lower still, becoming conspiratorial, incredulous. “You know what they call him there? They call him Tuan Jim. Lord Jim.” He uttered the title as if it were a profanity, or a joke of the most grotesque kind.

I said nothing. I merely watched his face, waiting. The coldness in me was spreading, but now it was mixed with a current of something else, something hot and fierce: a wild, improbable hope.

“The place is a pit,” Jansen went on, shaking his head slowly, as if to dislodge the memory. “A nest of snakes. When I was last there, two years ago, you could not go ten yards up the river without paying tribute to that old scoundrel the Rajah Allang, and another ten before you had to bow and scrape to that murdering fanatic, Sherif Ali, who had his stockade on the hill. Between the two of them they bled the people white. No trade, no peace. Nothing but fear.” He took out a plug of tobacco and bit off a piece, his jaw working methodically. “Is changed now. All changed.”

He told his story in fragments, in a tone of perpetual astonishment, as if he were recounting a fever dream. He had found the river open, the settlement busy. The people, he said, moved with a new confidence, a purpose he had never seen in them. The Rajah was a toothless old man, confined to his compound, stripped of all but his nominal title. And Sherif Ali… well, that was the heart of the matter. Sherif Ali was gone. His hill-fortress, which had for years dominated the surrounding country, was a ruin of charred timbers and fallen earthworks.

“I ask them what has happened,” Jansen said, leaning forward again, his eyes wide. “They tell me a story. A fairy tale. They say this Englishman of yours, this… this Jim, he did it. He came with nothing. No men, no guns to speak of. He lived among them for a few weeks, quiet as a mouse. He learned the talk. He made friends with the old chief of the Bugis, a man they call Doramin. A wise old head, Doramin, but slow. Cautious. And then, one day, this boy of yours… he decides to make war.”

He laughed, a short, harsh bark without any mirth. “War! On Sherif Ali! A boy with a revolver and a handful of Bugis fishermen who were more afraid of the Sherif’s shadow than of a crocodile. I told them they were mad. They just smiled. They said, ‘The Tuan had a plan.’ A plan! To take that hill! I have seen it. It is a fortress. Steep sides, two stockades. You would need a hundred soldiers and a cannon.”

My own heart was beating a slow, heavy rhythm against my ribs. I could see it. I could see him, standing before them, slender and erect in his white clothes, his head held high, his voice steady, laying out the impossible. I could see the doubt in their faces giving way to the sheer force of his conviction, the magnetism of a man who has found the one stage in all the world perfectly suited to his role. He was not making a plan; he was sketching the geography of his dream.

“He did it at night,” Jansen continued, his voice hushed with a kind of grudging awe. “In a thunderstorm. He took fifty men. Doramin’s son, Dain Waris, went with him. They climbed the back of the hill, the steepest part, where no one ever watches. He led them himself. They went over the first wall like ghosts, he and the boy Waris side by side. The guards were sleeping. They were inside the main stockade before anyone knew what was happening.” He shook his head again, a gesture of utter disbelief. “There was a fight. A terrible fight, they say, in the dark and the rain. But he had them. He had them by the throat. The Sherif’s men panicked. They broke. Some ran into the jungle. Some were cut down. Sherif Ali himself, they say he escaped over the wall with a few followers, like a thief in the night. And that was it. It was over.”

He fell silent. He stared into his empty glass as if the story were written in its dregs. The clamour of the exchange, the shouts of clerks, the rattle of telegram machines, all of it seemed to recede, to become a distant and meaningless noise. There was only the small circle of our table, and the incandescent image of that night attack: the rain, the mud, the flashing of steel, and in the centre of it all, a young man finding his salvation in the heart of the storm. He had submitted to the destructive element. He had made the deep, deep sea keep him up.

“And now?” I asked, my voice sounding strained and unfamiliar in my own ears. “What of him now?”

Jansen looked up at me. The incredulity in his eyes was slowly being replaced by something else, a kind of grudging respect, the respect of a practical man for a miracle he cannot explain but whose effects he cannot deny. “Now?” he grunted. “Now he is the lord of the place. Doramin and the Bugis, they would die for him. The people of the river, they look to him for justice. He settles their disputes. He advises them on planting. He has even started to build a new pier. A pier! In Patusan! He is… he is a king. A king of mud and savages, ja. But a king all the same.” He paused, and then added the one detail that, for me, illuminated the whole fantastic tale with a single, brilliant flash of light. “And he is happy.”

He said the word simply, as a statement of fact. He did not seem to grasp its significance, its utter and profound impossibility. For him it was just another strange detail in an inexplicable business. For me, it was everything. It was the vindication of Stein’s terrible wisdom. The man who had been hounded from every decent corner of the earth by the ghost of his failure, the man who was so fine, so romantic, that he could never be what he dreamed himself to be, had found a place where the dream itself was the only reality that mattered. He had not been cured; he had found a world that was afflicted with the same magnificent disease.

“Happy,” I repeated softly. It was not a question. It was an affirmation.

Jansen shrugged, finished his drink, and pushed his chair back. His mission was accomplished. He had delivered his news, discharged his burden of astonishment onto the one man who might be expected to understand it. “I do not know what you saw in him,” he said, standing up. “To me he looked like any other young fool with his head in the clouds. But there is something there. A fire. Is not a safe thing, a fire like that.” He gave me a final, searching look. “You sent him there. His fate is on your hands. I hope you can sleep at night.”

He turned and walked away, a solid, earthbound figure swallowed up by the crowd. I remained at the table for a long time, watching the patterns the afternoon sun made on the dusty floor. Sleep? Oh, I would sleep. For the first time in months, I felt I could. The echo I had been listening for had come back, not as a whisper, but as a thunderclap. My part in the affair, which had felt like a condemnation, a sentence to perpetual anxiety, now seemed something else entirely. I had not been a gaoler or an executioner. I had been a navigator. I had taken a vessel that was destined to founder in the shallow, charted waters of our world and guided it towards a distant, stormy sea where it might ride out the tempest of its own nature.

The great plain on which men wander remained as desolate as before, its pitfalls as deep, its graves as numerous. But now, in one remote corner, a single figure stood illuminated. He was no longer wandering. He had built his house upon his heap of mud, and he had, against all odds, made it a fortress. He was no longer dreaming of being a fine fellow; in the eyes of a whole people, he was one. He had followed the dream, usque ad finem, and for him, the end was a lordship. I thought of Stein, and the frail, beautiful butterfly in its glass case. My head went round, just as his had. The specimen was not merely perfect; it was alive. And its wings, so delicate and so strong, were beating steadily in the heart of a darkness I had sent him to, a darkness he had, by the sheer force of his romantic will, begun to fill with light. The story was not over; no, it was far from over. The perils of success, I reflected, are often more subtle, and more deadly, than those of failure. But for now, he was swimming. By God, he was swimming. And I, his distant, solitary spectator, felt a surge of pride so fierce and so painful it was almost indistinguishable from love. The boy was safe. Tuan Jim was home.

It was not a thing to be kept, this sudden fierce clarity; it was a fire, as Jansen had said, and like a fire it consumed the air around it, leaving a man breathless in the vacuum of his own thoughts. I paid my bill, a triviality that seemed almost insulting in the face of the monumental transaction that had just occurred in my soul, and stepped out into the street. The noise of the port was a welcome intrusion; it was the sound of the world carrying on, indifferent and robust. Men shouted, carts rumbled, and the air was thick with the smell of salt and tar and spices--the honest, uncomplicated perfume of endeavour. I had a ship to rejoin, a command to resume. My own life, which for months had seemed a mere shadowy anteroom to the drama of Jim’s, now beckoned with the solid promise of routine, of duties to be performed and distances to be measured.

I walked towards the harbour, my mind a churning sea of its own. Stein’s words about the butterfly’s short, brilliant life echoed with the sinister chime of prophecy. Had I, in seeking to save Jim from the ignominy of one fate, merely delivered him up to the treacherous brilliance of another? That fortress built on mud… how long could it stand against the rising tide of… of what? Of reality. Of the destructive element itself, which he was not merely to submit to, but to rule. It was a preposterous notion, a paradox that would have been laughable if it were not so sublime. To be king of the very thing that seeks to drown you. Ha! It was a story for the gods, and we, mere mortals, were left to watch and wonder at the spectacle.

My ship was a dark, solid shape against the gaudy colours of the sunset. She was called the *Vigil*, a name that had always struck me as sober and fitting for a vessel engaged in the steady, unglamorous work of trade. Tonight, it seemed to take on a new, more personal meaning. I felt like a watchman, a sentinel posted on a distant shore, my gaze fixed on a single, flickering light in the immense darkness of the Archipelago. My first mate, a steady, unimaginative man named Harrison, met me at the gangway. His face was a study in placid reliability. He saw the world in terms of cargo manifests, weather reports, and the crew’s payroll. For him, a man was a man, a fact as simple as a sounding or a celestial observation.

“All ready to sail on the morning tide, sir,” he said, his voice devoid of all curiosity about my long sojourn ashore. “The last of the freight is stowed.”

“Very good, Mr. Harrison,” I replied, the familiar words a comforting anchor in the swirl of my thoughts. “See to it the watches are set. We’ll have a clear night, I think.”

He nodded, touched his cap, and went about his business, a man perfectly adapted to his heap of mud, and all the more admirable for it. I stood for a moment on the deck, my deck, feeling the gentle thrum of the ship’s life beneath my feet. This was my world. A world of straight lines, of known quantities, of cause and effect. And yet, I had become entangled in a story that defied all navigation, a story of curves, of imponderables, of effects whose causes were hidden in the deepest recesses of a single, tortured soul.

We sailed as planned, slipping out of the harbour in the grey, liminal hour before the dawn. The land fell away, a dark smudge that dissolved into the vast, shimmering plain of the sea. For days, I immersed myself in the rhythm of the ship. I took my sights, I inspected the holds, I stood my watches on the bridge, feeling the salt spray on my face. I tried to lose myself in the simple, demanding reality of my profession. I tried to forget Jim. But it was no good. He was there in the vast emptiness of the horizon, in the way the sun struck the water, in the profound, unsettling silence of the night watch. He was a presence, an obsession. The specimen I had come to describe to Stein had instead taken me over, had become the lens through which I now saw the world.

My pride in his success, that fierce, painful emotion I had felt in the hotel, began to cur-dle into a more complex, more troubling feeling. It was anxiety. A deep, abiding anxiety not for his failure, but for the very nature of his success. He was swimming, yes, but the sea is patient. It has currents and eddies and sudden, unforeseen squalls. To follow the dream… usque ad finem. The end. That was the word that haunted me. What was the end for a man like Jim? A lordship was a beginning, not an end. It was a perch, a promontory, and from such a height, the only way to fall is down.

About a week out from port, we ran into a storm. It was not one of your theatrical tropical tempests that tears the sky to shreds and is gone in an hour. No. This was a different sort of beast. It was a sullen, methodical, and utterly relentless assault. The sky turned the colour of old pewter, and the sea, which had been a placid blue, became a churning, malevolent grey-green. The sea did not rise; it awoke. And it seemed possessed of a cold, intelligent fury.

For two days and two nights, the *Vigil* was tossed and battered. The wind shrieked through the rigging with the sound of a thousand mourning voices, and great mountains of water, capped with sickly white foam, would rise up before us, hang there for a heart-stopping moment, and then crash down upon our decks with the force of a physical blow. In the midst of it all, I stood on my bridge, a prisoner in the roaring, chaotic heart of the destructive element. I did my duty. I gave my orders. I watched my men, their faces pale and set, perform their tasks with the grim competence of their trade. We were all swimming, you see, every one of us, with the exertions of our hands and feet, making the deep, deep sea keep us up.

But as I held the ship to her course, my mind was not on the immediate danger. It was in Patusan. I saw Jim, not on his verandah as a lord, but in a small, struggling boat in a sea like this one. I saw the dream being washed away, the fine fellow revealed as just another drowning man. The metaphor had become a terrifying reality. Stein’s words, spoken in the calm, dusty air of his study, took on a new and dreadful power in the howl of the wind. *If he tries to climb out into the air as inexperienced people endeavour to do, he drowns--nicht wahr?* What was his lordship, his fortress on the mud, if not an attempt to climb out of the sea of his own nature? An attempt to find solid ground where there was none?

The thought was a cold knot in my gut. I had sent him there. Jansen was right. His fate was on my hands. And in that moment, with the deck heaving under my feet and the world dissolved into a chaos of wind and water, I felt the full weight of that responsibility. I had not just been a navigator; I had been a creator. I had taken the raw material of a romantic fool and helped fashion him into a myth. Tuan Jim. Lord Jim. The words themselves had a terrible, binding power. They were a cage, a beautiful, gilded cage built of the expectations of others, and I, more than anyone, had helped to forge the bars.

The storm passed as suddenly as it had begun. The wind dropped, and the sea, exhausted, subsided into a long, oily swell. The sun came out, and the world was once again a place of dazzling light and colour. We had survived. The *Vigil* was battered but sound. We had submitted, and we had been allowed to live. But the peace I had felt after Jansen’s visit was gone, shattered beyond repair. It was replaced by a gnawing, perpetual vigilance.

We made port a few days later, and there, amongst a stack of mail that had been waiting for the ship, was a letter. The handwriting on the envelope was familiar, a precise, elegant script that seemed wholly out of place amidst the mundane commercial correspondence. It was from Stein.

I took it to my cabin and opened it with a sense of foreboding. The letter was short, and written in his peculiar, philosophical style.

“My dear friend,” it began, without preamble.

“I have been thinking of your specimen. You will forgive the term; it is the habit of a lifetime. The collector sees the world as a cabinet of curiosities. It is a limitation, I know.

I have been thinking of that moment when the magnificent creature is captured. The chase is over, the net has fallen. There is a moment of stillness, of triumph. The wings are perfect, the colours are vibrant. It is, for that one instant, a perfect thing. But then, the work begins. The specimen must be preserved. It must be protected from decay, from the dust, from the simple passage of time which is the enemy of all perfection. It must be pinned.

Your Jim… he is in his moment of perfection now. He has been captured by his own dream. The people of his dark corner of the world, they are the velvet upon which he is now displayed. But a pin is a sharp thing, my friend. It holds the specimen in place, yes. But it also pierces its heart.

The wings are beautiful, yes. But they are made for the air, for the flight. Not for the cage, even if the cage is a kingdom. The dream has found its substance. This is the most dangerous moment. A shadow that takes form can also be caught, can be held. And a thing that can be held can also be broken.

I look at my butterflies, and I see the beauty of what was. You look at your man, and you see the beauty of what is. But both are an illusion. There is only the process. The becoming, and the un-becoming. He is swimming, you say. I say, let us hope the sea remains kind. But the sea has no memory and no heart. You know this. You live upon it.

Be watchful. The most perfect specimens are always the most fragile.”

He signed it simply, “Stein.”

I read the letter twice, then folded it carefully and put it in my pocket. It felt heavy, like a stone. He had taken my fragile hope and dissected it with the calm, precise cruelty of a scientist. Every word was a confirmation of the fear that had taken root in me during the storm. A pin. A sharp thing that pierces the heart. It was a horrifying image, and an entirely accurate one. The adulation of the people of Patusan, the title of “Lord,” the very success I had celebrated--it was the pin. It was the thing that held him fast, that fixed him in his role, that transformed him from a living, breathing, erring man into a static, perfect object. A specimen.

I went out on deck. The night was clear and starry, the air warm and humid. I looked out over the dark water of the harbour, towards the east, towards that immense and shadowy part of the world where Jim was playing out his impossible destiny. The relief, the pride, the fierce and painful love--all these had been burned away, leaving only the cold ash of knowledge. The story was not over; it was only now truly beginning. He had his people to watch him, and I had him. I was the collector who refused to use the pin. The tale was mine now, in a way it had never been before. It was not a memory to be recounted, but a vigil to be kept. The journey back to my ship was a passage through a world that seemed suddenly unreal. The smells of the harbour, the distant shouts, the clanking of a winch--they were the sounds of a life that moved to a different rhythm, a life blessedly ignorant of the destructive element and the terrible necessity of immersion. The night was much like the one on which I had visited Stein, thick and star-dusted, the air heavy with the scent of cloves and damp earth. He came aboard with an air of crisp, unruffled purpose, as if the humid night were a minor inconvenience to be ignored. He was a Dutchman, a Mr. Heemskirk, and he presented an aspect of solid, unassailable prosperity; a man whose substance was measured in ledgers and whose soul, one suspected, was bound in the same sturdy leather. He was not old, but he had the settled, confident air of a man who has found his place in the world and intends to profit from it. He carried a dispatch case and an aura of chilly pragmatism that seemed to lower the temperature of the air around him.

‘Marlow?’ he said, extending a hand that was both firm and faintly moist. His English was precise, with a gutteral edge that gave his words a certain weight, a certain gravity. ‘I am Heemskirk. He accepted both with a curt nod, as if they were his due. He sat with a straight back, his knees together, the dispatch case resting on them like a shield.

‘I have come on a matter of business,’ he began, after a perfunctory sip of his whisky. ‘Business is my... speciality. I hear things. Rumours. Of new opportunities. My heart gave a lurch, a wholly involuntary and ridiculous reaction. It was as if he had named a secret lover. I kept my face impassive, I hope. He smiled, a thin, bloodless affair that did not reach his eyes. ‘Precisely. Unexploited. That is the most beautiful word in the language of commerce, is it not? It was not a question. It was a statement of fact, a move in a game I was only just beginning to understand. This was the world, the real world that Stein had left behind, come knocking. It was not interested in dreams or in the state of a man’s soul. It was interested in gutta-percha.

‘Good. Excellent.’ He leaned forward, the leather of his chair creaking in protest. His voice dropped to a confidential, conspiratorial level. ‘This is the situation, Marlow. I felt a cold anger begin to build within me, a frigid and useless fury. Here was the pin. Not the pin of a naturalist’s adoration, but the pin of a bookkeeper’s calculation. It was cruder, and perhaps for that reason, even more horrifying.

‘And what role do you envision for the “professionals”?’ I asked, the words tasting like ash in my mouth.

‘To regularise,’ he said, with an air of profound satisfaction. ‘To systematise. To bring Patusan into the fold. A proper trading station. A contract with this... this Jim. A percentage for him, of course. A generous one. He remains the local headman, the face of the operation. We provide the capital, the shipping, the access to the world market. An Englishman who had gone a bit eccentric in the tropics, but who could still be brought to heel by the promise of a steady and significant income. He could not, in his wildest imaginations, have conceived of the truth: of a young man fleeing a shadow, of a dream of honour so delicate that a single misplaced word could shatter it, of a promise made to a village of brown-skinned people that was more real and more binding than any contract countersigned in triplicate in a boardroom in Amsterdam.

A finder’s fee. For delivering Jim, bound and gagged by percentages and clauses, into the hands of men who saw the world as a vast, divisible asset. I looked at Heemskirk’s solid, confident face, at the self-assurance that radiated from him like heat from a stove, and I felt a despair so profound it was almost a kind of peace. I thought of Stein’s words. To follow the dream, and again to follow the dream. I thought of the destructive element. Heemskirk was frowning now, a small crease appearing between his pale eyebrows. He was a man encountering an unfamiliar variable, a number that would not fit in his neat columns.

‘This is sentiment,’ he said, with a dismissive wave of his hand. ‘Sentiment does not last. Business does.’

‘Perhaps,’ I said. ‘But you are asking me to be the man who introduces the serpent into this... this rather shabby Eden. You are asking me to go to him and say, “Your dream is over. He becomes just another white man exploiting them. The magic, you see, vanishes. And all you are left with is a man who has sold his soul for a percentage. He could not put ‘the magic’ on a balance sheet, but he could, perhaps, dimly perceive its effect on the bottom line.

‘You paint a very... complicated picture,’ he said at last, his voice flat.

‘It is a complicated place,’ I answered. ‘It runs on a different kind of currency. A currency of trust and illusion. It is not traded on your exchange.’

He looked at me, and for a moment I saw something other than calculation in his eyes. It was not understanding, not by a long shot. It was a kind of weary bafflement, the look of a man who has travelled to the edge of his known world and found that the maps are wrong. He saw that I would not be his bridge. I had protected the flame of Jim’s improbable sanctuary from a cold and methodical wind. I had, in a sense, done my duty as the chronicler, as the friend who watched from a distance. I had kept the world at bay.

The disease was the world itself, with its inexorable logic of profit and progress. For every Heemskirk I turned away with tales of mist and shadow, there would be another, and another. A man, as Stein had said, will never on his heap of mud keep still. And the world, with its voracious appetite for new heaps of mud to exploit, would never leave a man like Jim alone for long.

The encounter had changed things. The threat was no longer an abstract fear born of Stein’s philosophy. It had a name and a face. It wore a suit and carried a dispatch case. The world had located Patusan on its map. It would be back. I thought of Jim then, and the contrast was so violent it was like a physical blow. Jim, for whom the question of how to be was the only question, the very air he breathed, the fever in his blood. He had tried to be a ship’s officer once, and the simple, solid world of my mate had broken him. He had not been able to live with its plain facts. And so he had fled into a dream, a place where facts were pliable, where a man could remake himself, where a single act of courage could, he hoped, blot out a lifetime of shame. He had fallen into the sea and, scorning the bitter water, had tried to build a raft of moonbeams and hold it together with the sheer force of his will.

My conversation with Heemskirk had been an attempt to describe that raft, to present it as a sovereign territory, a fortress of the spirit. But in doing so, had I not simply reinforced the delusion? I had described a specimen, as Stein had said, but I had described it as a creature of myth, a thing not subject to the ordinary laws of nature. I had spoken of his exalted reputation, his honour, his power over the hearts of men. I had painted a portrait of a king in a secret kingdom. He was performing the role I had helped to write for him.

I turned from the rail and walked the length of the poop deck, my footsteps unnaturally loud in the stillness. The ship slept around me, a slumbering world of wood and iron, its purpose clear, its destination set. I was its master, the man in whom all its purpose was concentrated. Yet I felt like the most purposeless man alive, a mere spectator, a messenger carrying a tale whose ending I dreaded but could not alter.

I went below, into the narrow confines of my cabin. The air was close, smelling of varnish and old papers. The lamp on my desk cast a familiar, steady glow, illuminating the clutter of my trade: charts, dividers, a half-written letter to my owners in London. It was a small, contained world, a world of straight lines and precise calculations. On the chart table, secured by a lead weight, lay the track for our passage south. A neat, pencilled line bisecting the blue expanse of the sea. There was the coast, a jagged, intricate fringe of land. And there, tucked into a deep bay, marked only by a tiny, almost invisible cross, was the river mouth that led to Patusan.

I stared at it. A cross on a map. The mark of a grave, or of treasure. With Jim, it was impossible to say which. It was to this small, insignificant mark that Heemskirk’s cold logic had been directed. It was this cross that represented the point of intersection between two irreconcilable worlds: Jim’s world of romantic striving and Heemskirk’s world of commercial fact. And I, Marlow, a man who belonged fully to neither, stood at the crossing.

My victory, as I had called it, now seemed not just hollow but perverse. I had not saved Jim. I had only made him more interesting. I had transformed him from a simple fugitive into a complex political problem. Heemskirk had come looking for gutta-percha and tin; he had left with a story about a mysterious white rajah, a man whose honour was a commercial liability. He would tell this story in the boardrooms and clubs of Singapore and Batavia. He would dine out on it. The story would take on a life of its own. It would become a legend. And the legend would attract not men like Heemskirk, who could be deterred by a lack of clear profit, but another kind altogether. It would attract the other romantics, the adventurers, the men who seek not profit but glory, the men who are themselves trying to climb out of the destructive element. It would attract men exactly like Jim.

What would happen then? When one dream collides with another? The thought was appalling. Jim’s authority rested on his uniqueness, on the fact that he was the one, the only one. He was the white man who had kept his word, who had brought them peace. His power was a house of cards built on a foundation of myth. What would happen when another white man appeared, a man with his own dream, his own desires? Would he be seen as a brother, or as a rival? A threat?

I sat down heavily in my chair, the one in which Heemskirk had sat, and I felt the weariness from the deck return, tenfold. I had tried to build a wall of words around Patusan, a verbal mist to obscure it from the world. But all I had done was build a lighthouse. I had made it a beacon.

And there was another, more intimate, fear. My own role in this. I was the chronicler. The keeper of the flame, I had told myself. But was I not also the one who supplied the fuel? I was the friend from the outside world, the link, the one who brought news and took it away. I was the one who listened to his stories, who nodded sympathetically at his protestations of faith and honour. My visits, my listening, my very presence, confirmed him in his dream. I was his audience. And what is a performer without an audience? He might cease to believe in the performance himself.

I had tried to be a friend. To help him. But how do you help a man like that? Stein’s words came back to me, not as a philosophical abstraction this time, but as a diagnosis, precise and terrible. ‘He is romantic.’ And the only cure was the one that could not be taken. To cease to be himself. So the only help one could offer was to support the romance, to shore up the dream. But the dream was the disease. The dream was the magnificent butterfly, and it was also the heap of mud on which it sat. It was the sainthood and the devilment. It was the vision he saw every time he shut his eyes, of himself as a very fine fellow.

My intervention with Heemskirk had been a masterpiece of this flawed therapy. I had confirmed his fineness. I had insisted on it. I had told a hard-headed man of business that Jim’s value lay precisely in the fact that it could not be calculated, that his kingdom was not of this world, or at least not of the world of profit and loss. And in doing so, I had sentenced him. I had locked him more securely into his gilded cage. He could never leave now. He could never go back on it. To do so would be to admit that the exalted reputation was a sham, that the man who could not afford to be insulted was, after all, just a man. Just Jim.

The silence of the ship pressed in on me. I could hear the tiny, rhythmic creak of a bulkhead, the faint whisper of water against the hull. Sounds of a world moving on, a world of physical laws and inexorable motion. It was the world that had thrown Jim out, and it was the world that would, sooner or later, come to claim him back, not with the cold calculation of a Heemskirk, but with the blind, crashing force of an accident, a misunderstanding, a chance encounter. The sea he had fallen into was patient.

I thought of the last letter I had received from him, months ago. It had been full of the usual things. Details of some local dispute he had settled; a proud account of a new stockade he had built; a complaint about the quality of the last batch of gunpowder I had sent him. It was the letter of a busy and responsible administrator. But between the lines, in the very ferocity of his attention to detail, in the slightly too-insistent cheerfulness of his tone, I had read a desperate, unspoken plea. A plea for validation. A plea for me to see how well he was playing the part, how completely he had become the man he had always dreamed of being. ‘You would be proud of your handiwork,’ he had written, in what was meant to be a jest. It was not a jest. It was a cry for help.

And what had been my response? I had brought him gunpowder. I had brought him news of the world he had left behind. I had, in short, treated him as if his kingdom were real, his authority absolute, his dream a solid and enduring fact. I had humoured him. Like a doctor administering a placebo to a dying man.

The flame in my lamp flickered, and a grotesque shadow danced on the wall behind me. It seemed to be mocking me, a huge, stooping shape with gesturing arms. It was Stein’s shadow, prowling amongst the graves of butterflies. It was my shadow. It was the shadow of the world, a shapeless dusk that was slowly closing in.

‘In the destructive element immerse.’ Stein’s voice. I could hear it as clearly as if he were in the cabin with me, his pipe in hand, his wise, sad eyes fixed on my face. That was the way. But I saw now, with a clarity that was a form of despair, that we had all misunderstood him. Or perhaps he had not been clear. Or perhaps the truth was a thing that could never be told, only lived.

We had thought the destructive element was the world, the sea of life, the judgment of men. And for a man like me, perhaps it was. For a man who lived in that world, who navigated its currents, the only way was to submit, to learn its tides and use them to keep afloat. But for Jim? The world was not his destructive element. He had already drowned in that world, on the day the *Patna* inquiry had branded him. No, for Jim, the destructive element was something else entirely.

It was the dream.

The dream itself was the treacherous sea. His fall had not been from the bridge of the *Patna*. That was merely the prelude. His real fall was into the dream of Patusan, the dream of redemption, the dream of becoming a fine fellow. And into this sea he had plunged, and he was, with the frantic exertions of his hands and feet, making it keep him up. He was succeeding. But for how long? And to what end? The dream was not water, which is yielding and supportive if you trust it. The dream was a more subtle poison. It was a mirage in which a man could die of thirst. To follow the dream, and again to follow the dream. Ewig. Usque ad finem. To the end.

And the end of a dream, when it is lived in the waking world, is always the same. It is a collision with a fact. A fact as hard and brutal as a submerged rock. For Jim, the facts were all around him, held at bay only by the force of his personality, by the fragile web of loyalty and fear he had spun. The jealousy of a local chief, the greed of a trader, the sudden arrival of a gunboat, the simple, stupid chance of a misfired rifle. Or the advent of a man like Heemskirk, who, though turned away, had left a scent on the trail, a signpost for others to follow.

I got up and went to the porthole, unscrewing the heavy brass clasps to open it to the night. The air that came in was cool and clean, smelling of salt and immense distances. The sea was calm, its surface a black mirror reflecting the pinpricks of the stars. It was a vision of perfect, cosmic indifference. And in the face of that indifference, the struggles of one man on a remote coast seemed a thing of infinitesimal smallness. A faint, almost imperceptible agitation on the surface of the deep.

But it was not small to me. It was everything. Because I was implicated. I had seen him. I had listened to him. I had judged him and pitied him and, yes, in a way, admired him. I had made his story a part of my own. His struggle was not just his own; it had become a question I had to answer for myself. What is the value of a man’s honour? What is the price of a dream? Is it better to drown in the sea of facts or to be consumed by the fire of a romantic illusion?

I had no answer. There was no answer. There was only the story, unfolding towards its inevitable conclusion.
