We were to have reached the mining survey by nightfall, but the chief mechanic, a gaunt man with the desert burnt into his skin, advised against it. He gestured at the engine housing, from which a faint, almost invisible shimmer of heat still rose. The condensers, he said, were labouring under a strain he did not like; another three hours of this infernal heat and they would surely fracture. To be stranded out here, in the middle of the Akada Flats, without power was a sentence I did not care to contemplate. This was sensible, of course. We were a hundred miles from the nearest brackish well, and the sun was already a swollen, malevolent eye hanging low on the horizon. Nevertheless, the delay was a physical irritant, a kind of fever in the blood, though why one more night should signify after so many weeks was a question I could not reasonably answer. Caution being the only virtue in that forsaken country, I gave the order to halt.

The great land-crawler sighed to a stop, its iron treads sinking a few inches into the baked salt crust. The plain stretched out on all sides, white and absolutely featureless, as if the world had been scoured down to its last blank page. The dusk did not fall here; it seeped up from the earth itself, a violet stain that bled into the horizon long before the light had truly failed. A profound stillness took hold, but it was not the stillness of peace; it was a watchful, expectant immobility. The very air seemed crystalline, and so silent you fancied you could hear the blood moving in your own ears. You would stare out at the nothingness until your eyes ached, and begin to feel that you alone were the only moving, breathing thing left in creation, then the darkness would snap shut, and the universe was gone. Sometime deep in the night the metal frame of the crawler groaned as it contracted in the cold, a sound so loud and sudden it made me start from my cot as if a rifle had been discharged at my ear.

When the dawn came it was not with light but with a low, spectral mist, a ground-hugging vapour that did not so much obscure the view as it did erase the world. It clung to us, cold and tasting of salt and dust. It was more disorienting than the absolute dark. At seven, or perhaps it was eight, the sun burned a hole in the top of it, a perfect circle of white fire hanging in a grey void, and we saw the endless flat floor stretching away, and then the grey shutter swirled and closed once more. I had just given the order to begin the pre-heating of the engine when the sound began. It was not a cry, not a shriek, but a low, resonant hum that seemed to come from all directions at once, to rise from the very salt beneath our feet. It was a single, sustained note of immense and mournful power. It vibrated in the fillings of my teeth. It made the skin on my arms prickle and tighten. ‘Great heavens! What is that?’ cried the company geologist from the cabin door, his voice thin and reedy. He was a small, precise man, with spectacles that seemed permanently fogged and a habit of clutching a leather-bound satchel to his chest as if it were a child. Another of the passengers, a surveyor, simply stood with his mouth agape, his face a pale oval in the gloom.

What we could see of ourselves seemed unreal; the crawler’s sharp lines were softened by the mist, its solid bulk made to look porous, almost ghostly. Beyond the rail was a yard or two of cracked, white earth, and then nothing. The world had been pared down to our precarious island of iron. Gone, as though it had never been. ‘Will they find us?’ a voice whispered, full of a nameless dread. ‘Who is there to find us?’ another answered, his tone pitched on the edge of hysteria. The faces of the white men were a study in strain; they looked not so much frightened as they did personally offended by this inexplicable resonance. Our local crew, the men recruited from the hill-tribes of the interior, showed a different aspect. They were as new to these flats as we were, their homes a thousand miles east, in the green country. Their expressions were not of fear, but of a deep and solemn attention. They stood quietly, their dark faces tilted as if listening to a familiar piece of music. Their headman, a tall, sinewy fellow whose face was a web of fine ceremonial scars, stood by the forward winch. ‘Well?’ I said to him, affecting a nonchalance I was far from feeling. ‘The salt sings,’ he said, without looking at me. His voice was a low rumble. ‘It remembers the water.’ ‘Remembers?’ I asked. He nodded slowly, a single, grave motion. ‘It is thirsty,’ he added, and then fell silent, looking out into the white void with an air of profound, philosophical acceptance.

I ought to have been unnerved by this pronouncement, but my mind had been diverted by a more immediate and practical thought: that these men must be famished. We had signed them on three months prior, with the usual farcical assurances from the company agent. For their labour they received, with a punctuality that was in itself a kind of mockery, a weekly issue of company scrip, a currency utterly worthless until we returned to the port. For sustenance, they had been given a supply of dried meat and meal, which had been consumed in the first month. After that, they were expected to ‘forage’, or to trade their useless paper with the nomads we occasionally sighted, mirage-like figures who would vanish at our approach. You can imagine the success of that. The geologist and I, we had our tinned beef and hard biscuits, a diet of dreary but reliable substance. The crew had nothing but a few handfuls of a coarse, reddish flour they mixed with water into a paste and baked on hot stones. It was a miracle of restraint they had not yet risen up and taken what they needed; they were fifteen to our three, strong men whose muscles had only just begun to lose their firm contours. I saw then that some inscrutable principle, some deep-seated human discipline that defies all logic, was at work. I looked at them with a sudden, sharp curiosity, not because I feared being clubbed and eaten, though I confess it did cross my mind how pale and soft the geologist looked, and I found myself hoping, with a queer vanity that was of a piece with the dream-state of my whole existence in that place, that I presented a stringier, less palatable aspect. Perhaps it was a touch of the sun sickness. One cannot go through such days without acquiring a little of the land’s madness, those first gentle nudges from the wilderness before it decides to strike in earnest. Yes; I watched them as one might watch figures in a dream, trying to understand the secret grammar of their endurance.

Their headman, who had spoken of eating his enemies with such a dispassionate finality, had resumed his pose by the rail, a figure of archaic dignity carved out of the shifting vapour; and it was to him that my eyes returned, not with the apprehension of a potential meal, but with the vexing curiosity of a man who has stumbled upon a cipher to which he has no key. He stood with his back to me, but I could feel his attention, a palpable force directed out into the white abyss from which that terrible cry had issued. The world had been snatched away, and we were left floating in a pearly void, a forgotten speck of rust and steam, our existence testified only by the clammy chill of the air and the frantic thumping of our own hearts.

Then a sound began, or rather, inserted itself into the profound stillness without any discernible start. It was not a drum, for it lacked the sharp report, the echo. It was lower, more intimate, a dull, resonant thudding that seemed to come from all around, from the very substance of the fog. Thump. A pause. Thump. This new absurdity, the idea of an amphibious assault, should have been comical, but in that place, it was not. The imagination, cut adrift from the anchor of reality, was capable of producing horrors more potent than any physical threat. I could almost see them, dark forms swimming just beneath the surface, propelled by some supernatural force. The thudding continued, patient, inexorable. I looked at my crew. Their faces showed no new alarm. A few of them had cocked their heads, listening, but their expressions remained placid, attentive. The headman had not moved. I stepped towards him, my boots making no sound on the damp deck. ‘Your people?’ I asked, my voice low.

A faint, almost imperceptible smile touched his lips, a flicker of something that might have been pity, or perhaps contempt. ‘Not you,’ he said. ‘The river. I stared at him, at the intricate scarring on his cheeks, the oily ringlets of his hair, the profound, unshakeable certainty in his eyes. I was dealing not with a simple savage, but with a theologian, a metaphysician of the jungle. His explanation, which should have been meaningless, resonated with the monstrous, animate quality of the landscape. The river *was* hungry. The forest *was* a thing of stone. The mist *had* screamed. To my over-strained senses, it was all of a piece, a coherent and terrible system of belief in which I was the only unbeliever. I felt a sudden, dizzying vertigo, the feeling of a man looking down from a great height, not into a chasm of rock and air, but into a gulf of time. These men, with their talk of hungry rivers, were not my contemporaries. They were ancient, their thoughts moving in grooves worn by millennia of such mists, such silences.

The thudding grew nearer. You could feel it now, a subtle vibration through the hull of the steamer, a bass note that resonated deep in your chest. The pilgrims were becoming undone. The fat man in pyjamas was openly weeping now, his face buried in his hands. The other two had flattened themselves behind a stack of wood, their rifles poking out like the horns of some grotesque mechanical snail. It was a particular brand of terror, you see, the terror of the enlightened man who finds his enlightenment counts for nothing, his science a child’s toy, his reason a flickering candle in a hurricane. They were not afraid of being killed; they were afraid of the universe that this place revealed, a universe that was not empty and indifferent, but sentient, malevolent, and utterly mad.

‘I can’t stand it!’ one of them shrieked from behind the woodpile. ‘I’m going to shoot!’

‘Hold your fire,’ I commanded, my voice sharper than I intended. ‘You’ll hit nothing but fog and waste our cartridges.’

‘We have to do something!’ he wailed. ‘Make a noise! Blow the whistle!’

The idea was tempting, a blast of steam, a shriek of familiar, man-made noise to tear this suffocating fabric of sound and silence. But I hesitated. It seemed like a profanity, an act of gross sacrilege. To answer that deep, rhythmic pulse with our own shrill whistle felt like a child shouting in a cathedral. Moreover, I had a sudden, vivid conviction that it would be a mistake, a fatal error in etiquette. We were guests here, uninvited, and we did not know the customs.

Just then, a change occurred in the fog. It did not lift, not really, but a section of it, directly ahead, seemed to become less dense, to thin into a gauzy curtain. The thudding was loudest from that direction. And through the thinning whiteness, we began to see things. At first, they were just vertical lines, dark strokes against a grey canvas. Poles? The bank? Then the lines acquired shape, detail. They were paddles. Dozens of them, rising and falling in perfect, unhurried unison. They were long paddles, wielded by men standing in long, narrow canoes, so narrow they were barely visible. And as the mist swirled and eddied, we saw the men themselves. They were naked, their bodies lean and black, glistening as if oiled. They were not looking at us. Their heads were bowed, their movements fluid and concentrated as they struck the surface of the water with the flat of their blades. Thump. Thump. Thump.

The sheer number of them was staggering. There must have been thirty or forty canoes, perhaps more, arrayed in a wide, silent crescent before us. Yet there was no sense of menace. It was not a war party. It was a procession, a ceremony of some kind, slow, solemn, and utterly absorbing. The faces of the paddlers, when we could glimpse them, were masks of rapt concentration. The air, which had been filled with our own frantic fear, now seemed charged with a different emotion entirely: a profound, collective grief, an immense and patient sorrow. That cry we had heard, that sky-filling lament of infinite desolation, it had not been a threat. It had been its true self: a cry of pain.

The white curtain swirled again, and for a few seconds, we saw the bank. It was not twenty yards away. We saw the steep, muddy slope, the wall of vegetation, and a village, or what passed for one. A few wretched-looking huts on stilts, their thatch dark with moisture. And figures. Scores of them, standing motionless at the water’s edge, men, women, children, all silent, all watching the canoes. They were like statues, part of the petrified landscape. And in their midst, on a small, cleared patch of ground, was a pyre. Not of wood, but of belongings, it seemed: gourds and woven mats and what looked like ivory tusks, piled high. But there was no fire. They were simply waiting.

Then the mist, as if jealous of its secrets, descended once more, thicker than before. The vision was gone. The canoes, the village, the silent watchers, all of it swallowed back into the opaque void. Only the sound remained, the slow, rhythmic heartbeat of their sorrow. Thump. Thump. Thump.

It was too much for the little fat man. With a strangled sob, he threw his rifle to the deck, where it landed with a clatter that was shocking in the muffled air. He scrambled to his feet, his eyes wild, the pink of his pyjamas a lurid splash of absurdity in the grey monochrome. ‘I’ve had enough!’ he screamed, his voice a high-pitched tear in the sonic tapestry. ‘Enough of this! I’m from Brussels! I have a haberdashery! I sell gloves!’ He began to run in a small, frantic circle, his sidespring boots slipping on the wet planks. ‘Gloves! Silk and leather! For ladies!’

It was the breaking point, the moment the fragile shell of our shared sanity finally cracked. His hysteria was infectious. One of the men behind the woodpile began to pray aloud, a babble of panicked Latin. I knew that in another moment, someone would start shooting, not at any target, but simply at the world, at the fog, at the sound. And I knew, with a certainty that chilled me more than the fog itself, that this would be the final blasphemy, the act that would turn this sorrowful mystery into a bloody and meaningless skirmish. We would kill, and be killed, for no reason other than our own failure to be quiet and to listen.

I moved. I crossed the deck in three strides and grabbed the haberdasher from Brussels by the collar of his ridiculous pyjamas. He was surprisingly strong, flailing with a lunatic energy. ‘Be still!’ I roared, the words torn from my gut. I shook him, hard, as one might shake a man out of a nightmare. ‘Be still, damn you!’ His head snapped back, his eyes rolling. For a moment he stared at me, his mouth open, the words about gloves dying on his lips. Then he seemed to collapse, all the tension going out of him, and he slid to the deck in a boneless heap, sobbing quietly.

The sound of my own voice shocked me. It was the voice of command, a tone I had not used in months, a relic from another life, a life of clear orders and predictable consequences. The effect was immediate. The praying man fell silent. The two with rifles slowly lowered them. They all looked at me, their faces slack with a kind of desperate hope, as if I, by shouting, had somehow taken control not just of them, but of the situation, of the fog, of the river itself. It was a terrible burden, that look.

I turned to the headman of my crew. He was watching me, his expression unreadable. ‘What was that cry?’ I asked again, my voice now quiet, spent. ‘A death?’

He nodded slowly. ‘A great man,’ he said. ‘The river has taken him.’ He gestured out into the whiteness where the canoes were. ‘They ask it to be satisfied. They give him back to the water with their voices.’

So that was it. A funeral. A king, or a chief, had drowned, and this was his wake. This vast, coordinated expression of grief. The cry of desolation, the beating of the water, the silent vigil on the shore. It was all part of it. We had blundered into the middle of a nation’s mourning. Our little steamboat, with its noisy engine and its cargo of greedy fools and its pathetic pretensions of power, had anchored in the heart of their sacred sorrow. And our response had been to tremble and gibber and prepare to kill.

The rhythmic thudding began to fade. The canoes were moving on, taking their grief with them down the river, or back into the labyrinthine creeks. The silence that returned was different this time. It was softer, gentler. The tension on board the steamer had broken. The pilgrims, exhausted by their paroxysm of fear, were quiet, slumped in attitudes of utter dejection. The fat man had curled into a ball, his pink pyjamas already stained with the grime of the deck. They looked like broken dolls, their strings cut.

I felt a profound weariness, a fatigue that went deeper than bone and muscle. I looked again at my crew. They were beginning to stir, to speak to one another in low, grunting tones. Their ordeal, it seemed, was over. They had understood. They had listened. They had not been afraid; they had been respectful. Their restraint, the thing I had been trying to fathom, was not just about hunger or discipline. It was knowledge. It was a kind of grace. They knew they were small and the wilderness was great, that their lives were brief and the river was eternal. They lived inside this reality, and so they were not maddened by it. We, on the other hand, we who thought we were masters of the world, were driven to insanity by the first hint that we were not.

I went and stood by my headman at the rail. We were silent for a long time, listening to the last faint heartbeats of the paddles die away in the distance. The fog was still absolute. The world was still nowhere. But it no longer felt menacing. It felt…pensive.

‘He must have been very hungry,’ I said, almost to myself, my mind returning to that earlier, more practical thought, the thought of my own starving crew. The contrast was too stark, too grotesque. The immense spiritual dignity of the ceremony we had just witnessed, and the sordid fact of the empty bellies of the men who stood beside me.

The headman did not seem to think the change of subject was strange. He simply nodded. ‘Yes,’ he said. ‘We are hungry.’ There was no complaint in his voice, merely a statement of fact, as plain and solid as the deck beneath our feet.

‘Why?’ I asked, the question that had been tormenting me. ‘Why have you not taken our food? There are thirty of you. There are three of us, and one is a fool who sells gloves. You are strong. Why?’

He looked at me, and this time there was no mistaking the expression in his eyes. It was a profound, weary, ancient pity. He seemed to be weighing his words, or perhaps weighing my capacity to understand them. He touched his own chest, a gesture of surprising gentleness.

He turned away then, his duty as an interpreter for his world complete, and resumed his watch over the invisible river. I was left with his words, and with the immense, appalling silence. He had given me the key to the cipher, and the message it revealed was more terrible than any I could have imagined. Their restraint was not a secret of endurance. It was a moral choice. They had looked at us, at our guns, our tinned food, our frantic terror, our whole shabby enterprise, and they had chosen to starve rather than to join it. In that moment, floating in the white, sightless fog, I felt a shudder of horror, but it was not for them. It was for myself, and for the hollow men who lay defeated on the deck around me. The real savages were not the ones beating the water in their canoes, but the ones cowering on our steamboat, and I, their captain, was the chief among them. ‘Get out of the way, you fool,’ he snarled, and tried to push past me. I caught his arm. It was surprisingly soft and fleshy, like a woman’s. But it was too late. The report of the Winchester cracked the air, a sharp, profane sound that shattered the hypnotic rhythm. It was followed instantly by two more shots from his companions. The three reports echoed in the confined space of the reach, a series of imbecile bangs that chased each other up and down the river. For a heartbeat, there was a stunned silence. I looked at the bank. The figure was gone. There was only the mud promontory, empty under the swirling fog. There had been no cry, no fall, no sign that he had been hit. He had simply vanished, as if he had been an illusion all along, a projection of our own fevered imaginations, dispelled by the crude reality of gunpowder. The fog, as if its purpose were now complete, began to close in again, the translucent patches thickening, the rifts sealing up, until the bank was once more swallowed by the white emptiness.

The fat pilgrim lowered his rifle, a look of stupid triumph on his face. ‘See?’ he panted. ‘Scared him off. That’s how you deal with them.’

I looked at him, at his ridiculous pyjamas, at the faint wisp of smoke curling from the barrel of his gun, and a wave of such profound, bottomless contempt rose in me that I felt physically sick. He had not scared anything off. He had committed a sacrilege. He had burst into a temple he could not see, and desecrated an altar he could not comprehend, and thought he had performed an act of courage. It was the same blind, blundering arrogance that had brought us all here, the same belief that a loud noise and a piece of pointed metal could solve any mystery, tame any wilderness.

I turned away from him, unable to bear the sight, and my eyes fell on the headman. He was looking at me, and the placid mask had fallen from his face. For the first time, I saw an expression of pure, unadulterated disgust. It was not the weary pity of before. It was the revulsion of a sane man forced to witness an act of obscene madness. He did not say a word. He simply held my gaze for a long moment, then spat, a clean, sharp gesture of contempt, onto the deck between us. Then he turned his back on me once more, resuming his watch over a world that we had just proven, beyond any shadow of a doubt, we were unworthy to see.

His spit seemed to dissolve a hole in the deck. It was not a physical mark, of course, but it felt like a brand, a permanent stain on my command. He had not spat at the pilgrim. He had spat at me. I was the one who had brought these chattering monkeys into his world. I was the one who was paid to keep them in check, and I had failed. His contempt was not for their sickness; it was for mine, for the weakness that allowed their sickness to fester and erupt. I thought again of his words: ‘To eat that is to eat despair.’ The pilgrims had not eaten despair. They had fired it from their guns. They had filled the air with it. And I, by my presence, by my inaction, was choking on it.

The silence that followed was worse than any of the sounds that had preceded it. It was not the silence of peace, or of nature. It was a violated silence, a silence filled with the echoes of a crime. The fog was absolute again, heavier than before, it seemed, and carrying a new chill. We were alone, more alone than ever, because now we were not just lost in the wilderness; we were exiles from it. We had been offered a glimpse into its heart, and we had answered with a volley of rifle fire. There would be no more invitations.

I walked away from the pilgrims, away from the symbolic stain on the deck, and went to the bow. I needed to be by myself, to breathe air that was not thick with the stench of their panic and their pathetic victory. The anchor chain hung limp and silent, a dead nerve connecting us to the riverbed. I leaned on the forward rail, my forehead pressed against the cool, damp metal. The fever, I was sure, was rising in me again. The playful paw-strokes were over. I felt its teeth begin to probe for a hold. My thoughts grew disjointed, drifting like the fog itself. I thought of Kurtz. Was this what he had discovered? Was this the knowledge that had sent him raving into the darkness? Not that the wilderness was hostile, but that it was indifferent, and that its indifference was a mirror in which a man could see the utter, contemptible smallness of his own soul. He had not been corrupted by the jungle. He had been revealed by it.

‘He is not dead.’ The voice came from beside me, so quiet I almost thought I had imagined it. It was the headman. He had followed me to the bow, his bare feet making no sound on the deck. He stood a few feet away, not looking at me, but out into the same blank whiteness that I was staring at.

‘How do you know?’ I asked, my own voice a croak.

‘The sound stopped,’ he said, as if this were a complete and logical explanation. ‘If he were dead, the sound would change. It would become a cry. There was no cry. Only silence. They offended the place. So the place is silent.’

‘Offended the place,’ I repeated, the words tasting like ashes in my mouth. It was so simple, so devastatingly accurate. We had not fought an enemy. We had committed a social gaffe on a cosmic scale.

‘They will move on now,’ he continued, his voice still a low monotone. ‘They will not attack. There is nothing here for them to find. Only bad spirits.’ He glanced at the pilgrims, who were now huddled together near the cabin, muttering amongst themselves, their brief moment of triumphant aggression having already evaporated into a new kind of anxious dread. ‘They carry the bad spirits with them.’

He fell silent again, and we stood there for what felt like an eternity, two sentinels at the edge of nothing, our shared vigil a strange, unspoken truce. My contempt for the pilgrims was a shallow, noisy thing compared to his deep, quiet certainty. He did not hate them. They were simply a fact, a disagreeable element in the landscape, like a patch of foul-smelling mud. He navigated around them, and through them, with an economy of motion and of spirit that I was beginning to envy. It was a kind of freedom I had never imagined.

Slowly, almost imperceptibly, a change began. A faint luminosity grew in the air above us. The oppressive whiteness thinned, not in patches this time, but as a whole, as if a great lamp were being turned up behind a vast, frosted-glass dome. The outlines of the steamer sharpened. The water around us became a visible, tangible element again, grey and slick. Then, with the silent, swift motion of a theatre curtain rising, the fog lifted.

The scene it revealed was one of such profound and utter stillness that it took the breath away. The reach of the river stretched before us, a corridor of polished steel between two towering, silent walls of green. The sun, a pale, watery disc, hung directly overhead, its light seeming to be absorbed by the immense density of the foliage, leaving the air in the gorge dim and cool, as if we were at the bottom of a well. The banks were empty. The mud promontory was there, but there was no sign that it had ever been occupied. The jungle was a solid, impenetrable mass, giving up no secrets. The thrumming, the shrieks, the figure in the white clay; it all might have been a hallucination, a collective nightmare brought on by the fog and the fear.

But I knew it was not. I knew because of the stain of spittle I could still feel on the deck behind me, and because of the new, heavy knowledge in my own heart. The world had returned, but it was not the same world we had lost a few hours before. A line had been crossed. A question had been asked, and we had given our answer.

I straightened up, my hands gripping the rail. The dream-sensation was gone, replaced by a grim, clear-headed weariness. There was work to be done. We had to get to Kurtz.

‘Heave in the chain,’ I said to the headman, my voice flat.

He nodded once, and without looking back at the cowering pilgrims, he walked to the capstan and began to give the orders to his men. They moved with a quiet efficiency, their bare feet slapping softly on the wet deck, their voices low and guttural. The chain began to clatter and groan, the sounds of mundane work a welcome intrusion into the monumental silence.

I remained at the bow, watching the jungle slide by as the steamer slowly gathered way. We were moving again, pushing on, up the river, deeper into the heart of the continent. But I no longer felt the impatient annoyance of the previous day. The delay, I now understood, had not been a delay at all. It had been a destination. We had arrived at a place of truth, and had failed the test it presented. And I had a chilling premonition that the rest of the journey, the meeting with Kurtz, the resolution of our sordid little enterprise, would be nothing more than an epilogue to the events of that single, blinding morning in the fog.

The day wore on, a long, monotonous procession of hours marked only by the shifting angle of the light. The river remained a straight, unvarying corridor. The jungle on either side was not a collection of individual trees, but a single, living entity, a vast, cellular organism of leaf and vine and shadow, breathing out a faint, sweet smell of decay. The sun climbed higher, burning off the last of the cool air, and the heat descended like a physical weight, pressing down on the deck, on our shoulders, on our very thoughts. The silence deepened, became more absolute. Even the thrum of the engine seemed to be absorbed by it, muffled and distant. We were a tiny, noisy speck crawling along the bottom of a deep, green chasm, and the world outside had ceased to exist.

My thoughts returned, as they always did, to Kurtz. The manager’s words, intended to condemn, had had the opposite effect. They had given Kurtz a new, more formidable dimension in my mind. A man whose ‘unsound methods’ could provoke the very mist to scream, a man who could inspire such a paroxysm of grief or fury, what kind of man was he? The Kurtz of the letters, the painter of fine pictures, the writer of soaring, eloquent prose about the civilizing mission, seemed to be dissolving, replaced by a figure of immense, shadowy power, a being who operated on a different plane of existence from the petty schemers and grasping functionaries of the Company. The screams had not been for us, I felt sure of it. They were part of a drama in which we were merely accidental, and profoundly unwelcome, spectators. They were an echo of Kurtz’s soul, a lament for him, or a raging against him. He was the centre of this world, the dark sun around which everything revolved.

It was in the late afternoon that we saw the book. It was floating near the bank, a small, dark object bobbing gently in the slack water of a shallow cove. It was the headman who spotted it, his keen eyes missing nothing. He pointed, a silent, almost imperceptible gesture. I ordered the steamer to slow and edged it closer to the shore. One of the crew, a lanky youth with long, nimble fingers, leaned far out over the side with a boat-hook and, after a few tries, snagged it and brought it aboard.

It was a book. An actual, honest-to-God book, bound in what had once been sturdy brown cloth, now warped and stained by the water. The title on the cover was still legible, in faded gold lettering: *An Inquiry into some Points of Seamanship*, by a man named Tower, or Towson. It was a technical manual, a sailor’s companion, something solid and practical and sane from a world of clear rules and measurable distances. The sight of it, here in this place of screaming mists and incomprehensible passions, was like finding a signpost for a familiar road in the middle of a nightmare.

I opened it carefully, my fingers trembling slightly. The pages were swollen and soft, the print blurred in places, but it was readable. And then I saw the notes. In the margins, in the spaces between the lines, everywhere, there were annotations, written in a small, precise hand. They were not corrections or comments on the text. The manager, who had approached, a figure of starched and sweating propriety, peered at the object with a kind of proprietary suspicion, as though any flotsam in this river belonged by rights to the Company, and its discovery by another was a minor act of insubordination. He looked at the black fellows, then at the pilgrims who were still clutching their rifles with a theatrical desperation. ‘Discipline,’ he sighed. ‘It is all about discipline.’

I almost laughed. Discipline. Here, in this place that was the very negation of all orderly principle, he was invoking discipline. It was like whispering of table manners in the belly of a whale. I turned away from him and listened again to the silence. It was no longer a simple absence of sound. It was an active, listening silence. It had weight. It pressed in on the steamboat from all sides, a pressure of immense, patient contemplation. The headman had been right. We had insulted them. We had made a great, foolish noise, and in response they had simply withdrawn the honour of their notice, leaving us to stew in our own insignificance.

And then, out of that profound stillness, a new sound arose. It was not the drum. It was not the tumultuous shrieking of before. It was a single voice, a man’s voice, singing. It was a long, melancholy note that hung in the damp air like a thread of smoke, rising and falling in a cadence of unspeakable sadness. It was a lament, a dirge. It was not directed at us; it seemed a purely internal expression, a sound meant only for the ears of the jungle itself. It went on and on, a single, looping phrase of sorrow, weaving itself into the very texture of the fog. It was infinitely more unnerving than the drums had been. The drums had been a challenge, a declaration. This was a verdict.

The pilgrims were petrified. I saw the fat one, the man in the pink pyjamas, his mouth hanging open, his face a blotchy, pale mask of terror. The song seemed to be dismantling him, stripping away his ridiculous costume and his flimsy sense of self. It spoke a language of primordial grief that his soul, if he had one, could not comprehend but which his body understood perfectly. It was the sound of a world that had no place for him, that had existed for millennia before his sidespring boots ever trod the earth, and would exist for millennia after.

Suddenly, one of the others, a lean, twitchy fellow with a narrow face and darting eyes, gave a choked sob. ‘I can’t bear it!’ he gasped, and before anyone could move, he raised his Winchester to his shoulder. He did not aim; he could not have seen anything to aim at. He simply pointed the barrel into the white void and pulled the trigger.

The crack of the rifle shot was a shocking profanity in that sacred quiet. It was a thin, hysterical sound, utterly alien and meaningless. It ripped a hole in the fabric of the lament, and for a second, there was nothing but the ringing in our ears. The song stopped. It did not fade; it was cut off, as if with a knife. The silence that rushed back in was ten times more profound, more absolute than before. It was a dead silence, a void. The heartbeat was gone. We had shouted into the void, and the void had answered by turning its back on us completely.

The pilgrim stood there, the rifle still smoking, his whole body trembling. The manager strode over to him, his face purple with a rage that was almost comical in its impotence. ‘You fool!’ he hissed, his voice a strangled whisper. ‘You imbecile! What have you done? Do you want to bring them all down on us?’ He was not angry about the sacrilege; he was angry about the breach of his precious discipline, the tactical error. The fool did not even seem to hear him. He just stared into the fog, his eyes wide with a horror that had finally found its name.

I looked over at the headman. He had not moved from his position near the stern. He stood with his back to us, a severe, dark-blue silhouette against the grey. He had not flinched at the gunshot. He did not turn. He simply stood there like a statue of judgement, his posture an indictment of our frantic, noisy terror. He knew what we had done. We had not merely been ignorant; now we had been violent in our ignorance. We had answered a lament with a gunshot. There could be no greater misunderstanding, no more profound a declaration of our own savagery.

We waited. The minutes stretched out, each one a small eternity of suspense. Nothing happened. The fog did not stir. No cry answered the rifle’s report. No arrows flew out of the whiteness. The jungle had absorbed our petty violence as a swamp absorbs a stone, without a ripple, without a trace. It was the ultimate contempt. We were not worth answering.

Slowly, the tension on the boat began to change. The sharp edge of fear dissolved into a dull, weary dread. The pilgrims lowered their rifles, their faces slack and empty. The charade of defence was over. There was no enemy to fight, only an immense, disapproving presence that refused to engage with us. We were left alone with ourselves, with the echo of that gunshot and the memory of that song.

The fog began to thin as the morning wore on, not lifting, but becoming translucent. Shapes began to emerge from the whiteness, ghostly and distorted. The high banks of the river appeared as dark, wet smudges. The trees were not trees but spectral pillars, their branches dripping with a moisture that was not rain but the condensation of our own clammy fear. The world was reassembling itself, but it was a changed world. It was a landscape seen through a fever dream.

‘Get up the anchor,’ I said, my voice sounding rough and loud in the quiet. ‘We are going on.’

The manager nodded eagerly. ‘Yes, yes. We have lost too much time.’

The chain rattled as the crew hauled it in. The sound was comforting, a piece of familiar, mechanical reality in this phantasmagoria. The headman’s crew worked with a quiet efficiency, their faces inscrutable. They did not look at the shore. Their world seemed to have shrunk to the confines of the steamboat, to the task at hand. The headman turned and watched them, his expression severe. I walked over to him.

‘Will they be back?’ I asked him quietly.

He looked at me, his bloodshot eyes calm and ancient. ‘They are not gone,’ he said. ‘They are watching. They always watch.’ He gestured with his chin towards the bank, which was now a solid line of impenetrable green, veiled in mist. ‘This is their place. You do not hear them now. That is bad. When you hear them, you know where they are. When you do not hear them…’ He left the sentence unfinished, turning his gaze back to the river ahead. The implication was clear enough.

The steamboat shuddered as the engine engaged, and we began to move slowly forward, pushing into the current, into the silence. The reach was still narrow, the water sliding past us like oil. Every turn brought a new vista of grey mist and green shadow, each one identical to the last. It was like travelling through a single, endlessly repeating image. The unreality of it all was beginning to wear on me. I felt a disorienting sense of detachment, as if I were not truly the captain of this boat but a mere passenger in a dream, my own and someone else’s.

We steamed for an hour, perhaps two. Time itself had become fluid, another casualty of this place. The pilgrims huddled together near the boiler, seeking a warmth that was more than physical. The manager stood at my elbow, peering ahead, his little eyes scanning the banks for any sign of a station, for any landmark that might restore his sense of a world governed by maps and ledgers. He saw nothing. There was nothing to see but the river, the jungle, and the fog.

Then, for a moment, the mist swirled and parted ahead of us on the left bank. It was as if a curtain had been drawn aside for our benefit. We saw a clearing. A few dilapidated huts, their thatched roofs sagging. A tangled patch of what might have been a garden. In the centre of the clearing, a tall pole had been erected, and from its top, something dark and ragged fluttered. It was not a flag. The whole place had an air of utter desolation, of things left to rot.

‘A village,’ the manager breathed, a note of relief in his voice. ‘They have fled.’

But they had not fled. As my eyes adjusted to the details, I saw them. They were not moving. They were standing at the edge of the trees, in the deep shadows between the huts, just standing there. Dozens of them. Men, women. Perfectly still. They were not armed, not that I could see. They were simply watching us pass. Their faces were blurs at this distance, indistinct patches of darkness in the greater darkness of the forest. But I could feel their eyes. It was a collective gaze, a silent, unified stare of immense, dispassionate curiosity. They were not threatening. They were not welcoming. They were merely observing, like scientists observing an insect under glass. We were the spectacle. Our noisy, puffing little steamboat, our pale, anxious faces, our whole frantic enterprise, it was a fleeting curiosity in the long, slow life of the forest.

The headman saw them too. He made a low sound in his throat, a soft grunt of acknowledgement. He was not surprised. This was what he had meant. They are always watching.

The steamboat chugged past the clearing. The curtain of fog drifted shut again, hiding the village from our sight. But the image was burned into my mind: the silent watchers at the edge of the world. It was a more profound isolation than I had ever known. We were not adventurers exploring a new land; we were a contamination, a strange disease floating past on the river, and the land’s immune system was holding a silent, clinical conference to decide what to do with us.

‘They did not look hostile,’ the manager said, trying to convince himself. ‘They were probably just frightened. Our whistle, the gunshot… it’s only natural.’

I did not bother to reply. To him, they were a factor in a calculation of risk, a potential impediment to the flow of ivory. To me, they were… what were they? They were the reality of the thing. They were the truth that lay coiled at the heart of the great lie. The lie was the trading company, the civilizing mission, the philanthropic pretence. The truth was that we were interlopers, fools playing a deadly game whose rules we could not begin to understand, and our only advantage, our steam-power, our firearms, was a kind of noisy irrelevance.

I thought of Kurtz. He was waiting at the end of this river. He had lived here, among these silences, among these watchers. He had not been content to merely pass by. He had, by all accounts, plunged in. He had torn down the veil. The ivory was incidental. The manager and his kind saw only the ivory, the profits. But Kurtz, that ‘universal genius,’ that voice, he had sought something else. He had gone to confront the thing itself. And I was now beginning to suspect that the drums, the lament, the silent stares, they were all part of a conversation he had joined.

A shiver ran through me, a tremor that had nothing to do with the morning’s chill or the lingering fever. It was a tremor of anticipation. I was not just going to rescue a company agent. I was going to find an answer. I was going to meet the man who had listened to that song and understood it, who had looked into the faces of those watchers and seen not a threat or a resource, but perhaps a reflection. The absurd little steamboat felt like a projectile, and I was strapped to its nose, being fired into the centre of a great and terrible secret. The journey was no longer about getting to a place on a map. It was about getting to Kurtz. He was the destination. He was the heart of the darkness.

Yes; I looked at them as you would on any human thing that has suffered, and that has kept its form, its coherence, its inexplicable and stubborn poise in the face of annihilation. What was it? An idea, a principle of conduct, something remote and obscure which they served, not with the blind devotion of fools, but with a patient, weary fidelity. They had a bargain, you see, a contract of a kind; and in the face of the gnawing emptiness in their bellies and the profound absurdity of their situation, they held to their side of it. This restraint of theirs, it was not cowardice. It was, if anything, a kind of formidable, archaic pride. It was a thing the pilgrims, with their little anxieties and their backing of farcical laws, could never understand. They saw only black, hungry savages; I saw men who, in their own way, possessed a mastery over themselves that was positively regal. It was a power that did not need to shout, a strength that resided in the quiet endurance of their frames, in the steady, patient look of their eyes.

The silence returned, pressing in on us once more, a heavy blanket woven of fog and suspense. It was a more dreadful silence than before, because now it was filled with the memory of that scream. It had a voice. We had heard it. The manager, who had been peering into the white void with a sort of proprietary disapproval, as if the fog were a breach of contract, turned to me. ‘An unfortunate incident,’ he muttered, his voice low and confidential. ‘We must expect a little excitement. These people are excitable.’ He wanted to dismiss it, to file it away under an appropriate heading. I said nothing. Excitement was not the word I would have chosen. Desolation, I thought, that was the word. A cry of utter, cosmic desolation.

I kept my eyes on the headman. He stood unmoving, a statue of dark, polished wood, his gaze fixed on the impenetrable whiteness. The grin had vanished from his face, replaced by a look of intense, listening concentration. He was not afraid; he was attentive. He was part of this world, and he was listening to its speech, a language we whites could only guess at. He knew the cry, I felt. He knew its source and its meaning, as a man knows the sound of the wind in his own country. I felt a sudden, strange kinship with him, with his grim patience, his profound connection to the very thing we were trespassing upon. We were both, in our different ways, waiting for a sign.

And then it came. Not a sound, at first. A flicker of movement in the air, a whisper against the skin. A soft, feathery hiss, and then a sharp thud into the wood of the pilot-house just beside my head. I did not flinch; I was too astonished. I reached out a hand and touched it. An arrow. A slender slip of cane, feathered neatly, with a sharpened wooden point. It had no weight, no menace in itself. It was almost a plaything. Then another hissed past my ear and clattered on the iron deck. A third struck the awning above the pilgrims with a sound like a dry twig snapping.

For a moment, there was a perfect, frozen stillness on board. The pilgrims stared at the flimsy shafts as if they were apparitions. The little fat man in pink pyjamas took a hesitant step back, his mouth a perfect ‘O’ of surprise. Then all at once, chaos erupted. ‘We are attacked!’ one of them shrieked, and it was the signal for a bedlam of futile activity. The two who had rushed for their Winchesters now raised them, pointing them blindly into the fog. ‘Don’t shoot!’ I yelled, ‘You’ll hit nothing!’ But it was no use. The manager was shouting something about protecting the company’s property. A shot rang out, a flat, foolish crack that was immediately swallowed by the fog. Then another, and another. They were firing at the whole continent of Africa, a ridiculous fusillade against a hostile universe.

The arrows were coming faster now, not a storm of them, but a steady, thoughtful, unnervingly accurate drizzle. They came from all around, from the invisible banks, from the very air it seemed. You heard the whisper, the thud, and then you saw the thing quivering in a deck plank or a bulkhead. It was a ghastly, silent warfare. There was no enemy to see, only these slight, persistent messengers of death arriving out of a white emptiness. One of the pilgrims let out a thin, bleating cry and slumped against the rail, clutching at his arm where a dark little stick had appeared as if by magic. His fellows paid him no mind; they were too busy reloading, their faces slick with sweat and contorted with a sort of panicky rage. They were making a great deal of noise, a clatter and shouting that seemed to me more terrifying than the silent arrows.

Through it all, my crew were magnificent. At my sharp command, they sprang to the anchor chain, their movements economical and sure. There was no panic in them, only a grim, focused energy. The headman was there, his voice a low, guttural bark, directing them, and their muscles bunched and strained as they began to haul. They were in their element, not the element of fighting, perhaps, but of work, of concerted effort in the face of peril. Their faces were alive with a strange light, an expression of what I can only call professional interest. This was a thing that happened. This was a problem to be dealt with. ‘Heave!’ I shouted, ‘Heave and get it short!’

I turned to the pilot-house. A dark figure stood at the wheel, my helmsman, a native from some coastal tribe, a man I had trained myself. He was steering by the feel of the current, his knuckles white on the spokes. His eyes were fixed forward into the blankness, but his whole body was attuned to the river, to the faint signs of its channel that were imperceptible to me. He was a fine helmsman. As I watched, an arrow struck the glass of the window in front of him, starring it, but he did not so much as blink. He just shifted his weight, as if to get a better feel of the boat’s drift. There, I thought, is a man.

‘Full steam ahead,’ I said to the engine-room tube, though the words were almost lost in the crackle of the Winchesters. I felt the faint tremor beneath my feet as the tired old engine responded. We began to creep forward, a blind snail nosing its way through a world of milk. The arrows kept coming. They were aiming for the light of the pilot-house now, for the sound of my voice. The air was full of their soft, deadly whispering. I had a sudden, morbidly clear vision of Kurtz. Had he faced this? Had he stood on a riverbank and watched these arrows fly? Or had he sent them? The thought was a cold spike in my brain.

‘Can you see the channel?’ I yelled at the helmsman.

He shook his head, a single, sharp movement. But then he pointed with his chin. ‘There,’ his voice was calm, almost conversational. ‘The trees are a darker shadow there. The current pulls.’ He had seen something I could not, a subtle thinning in the fog, a faint difference in the texture of the universal grey. He was reading the river’s soul. I trusted him. I trusted him completely.

The pilgrims were going mad. They had used up their ammunition and were now cowering behind the deck-house, their bravado evaporated, leaving only a residue of pasty fear. The manager was stamping his foot, demanding that I do something, that I ‘disperse the savages.’ Disperse the fog, he might as well have said. I ignored him. My whole being was concentrated on the helmsman, on the faint vibration of the deck, on the slow, agonizing forward creep of our boat. We were in a narrow passage; I could feel it from the way the water slapped back against the hull.

Then, out of the white screen before us, a new object materialised. It was not an arrow. It was long, heavy, a dark streak flying through the air with a purpose that was horrifyingly direct. It was a spear. It came straight for the pilot-house. There was a crash of splintering wood, a sound of breaking glass, and then a choked, guttural gasp from my helmsman.

I saw him slump, his hands sliding from the wheel. The spear had taken him in the side, pinning him to the flimsy wall behind him. His legs gave way, and he slid down, held up only by the thick shaft. He looked at me. His eyes were wide with a kind of immense, final surprise, not of fear, but of profound, animal astonishment. A dark stain was spreading rapidly across his loin-cloth. He opened his mouth as if to speak, but only a soft sigh came out, a bubble of blood at his lips. He gave a single, violent shudder, and then his head fell forward. The steamboat, deprived of his guidance, began to swerve towards the invisible bank.

For an instant I was paralyzed. The world had gone silent again. The pilgrims, the manager, the arrows, all forgotten. There was only the dead man at my feet, the spear, and the sudden, sickeningly sweet smell of his blood. My shoes were sticky with it. Then the boat scraped against something, a submerged branch probably, and the jolt threw me back into the reality of my position. I was the captain. The boat was adrift. The enemy was all around.

I lunged for the wheel, my feet slipping in the spreading pool on the floor. I had to step over his legs. I gripped the spokes, still warm from his hands. The spear shaft was in my way; I had to lean awkwardly around it. As my hand closed on the wheel, I felt a new determination, a cold, hard rage that burned away the dream-like trance. I was not going to die here. I was not going to let this ridiculous, scrap-heap of a steamer be finished by a volley of sticks and a single well-aimed spear.

I spun the wheel, fighting the current. I could feel the river’s power trying to drag us ashore, to deliver us into the hands of those who waited in the mist. At the same time, I reached for the cord of the steam whistle. I gave it a pull, a short, angry blast. It was a pathetic sound. It was not enough. I gathered all my strength and hauled on the cord, holding it down.

The whistle let out a shriek, a long, piercing, inhuman scream of tortured metal and escaping steam. It was a sound from another world, a voice of iron and fire hurled into the primeval stillness. It tore through the fog, blasted the silent trees, echoed and re-echoed until the very air vibrated with its pain. It was a howl of defiance, of grief, of sheer, mechanical fury. I held it, letting it scream and scream, my own throat tight with an unuttered cry.

And the effect was instantaneous. The arrows stopped. The whispering in the air ceased. A profound, ringing silence fell, broken only by the dying echo of the whistle and the chugging of our own engine. I had deafened the wilderness. I had answered its cry of desolation with a cry of my own, a manufactured, soul-less, and yet terribly effective shriek of power. We were moving again, steadily now, in the centre of the channel. The helmsman’s intuition had been right.

I let go of the cord, my arm trembling. The silence that rushed back in was different. It was not a listening silence; it was a cowed silence. The attack was over.

For a long time, I just stood there, steering, my shoes squelching softly. The pilgrims began to emerge, their faces slack with relief. The manager came to the door of the pilot-house, peered in, and saw the body. He looked at the spear, at the blood, and then at me. ‘Well, I’m, this is very regrettable,’ he said, as if commenting on a clerical error. ‘He was a serviceable native.’ He looked at the dead man with a kind of abstract annoyance. An instrument was broken, and it was an inconvenience. ‘We’ll have to throw him overboard, I suppose. Can’t have this sort of thing on deck.’

A wave of nausea and fury rose in me. I looked at the man I had trusted, the man who had died at his post, his body still held upright by the weapon that had killed him. Throw him overboard. Like a piece of garbage. I thought of the headman, of his curt ‘Eat ’im!’. Which was worse? The simple, brutal hunger of the cannibal, or the cold, tidy, heartless expediency of this civilized manager? I could not say. They both seemed to emanate from the same profound darkness.

‘Get out,’ I said, my voice a low rasp.

He blinked. ‘I beg your pardon?’

‘Get out of my pilot-house.’

He hesitated, a flicker of outrage on his bland face, but then he saw something in my eyes and thought better of it. He withdrew, muttering about my nerves.

I was alone with the dead. The fog was thinning, not lifting, but becoming translucent. I could see the vague shapes of the banks, the towering, ghostly forms of trees. The world was returning. And with its return came a devastating certainty. I had been on my way to find a voice, a man, an explanation. But this attack, this blind, furious assault, it could only mean one thing. The natives had broken loose. Their restraint, whatever it was that Kurtz had imposed on them, was gone. Therefore, Kurtz himself must be gone. He was dead.

The thought did not come as a shock. It settled upon me with the quiet, inexorable weight of a tombstone. All this way, all this effort, all this feverish anticipation, for nothing. To arrive at a ransacked station and a shallow grave. I had lost him. I had never even seen him, but the sense of loss was immense, personal, profound. It was as if I had been promised a great revelation, and had arrived to find the shrine empty and the oracle slain. The goal of my pilgrimage had vanished. There was no longer a Kurtz at the end of the river. There was only his memory, and the savage, mournful uproar his death had unleashed.

I looked down at the dead helmsman. His service was done. He had brought me this far. I felt a sudden, morbid impulse to give him a proper burial. But where? On this hostile shore? The manager was right in his brutal logic. I could not keep him. I reached out and, with a deep reluctance that felt like a betrayal, I put my hands on his shoulders. He was still warm. I pulled his body free of the spear. It made a horrid, sucking sound. He was heavy, a dead weight of flesh and bone. I dragged him to the edge of the low deck, averting my eyes from the pilgrims who watched with a stupid curiosity. I let him slip over the side. He entered the water with a quiet splash, and the brown, swirling river took him. He was gone.

I went back to the pilot-house and kicked his spear overboard after him. Then I busied myself with my charts, with the mechanics of navigation. I did not want to think. I wanted to focus on the journey’s end, on the practical matter of reaching Kurtz’s station. But the purpose had been hollowed out of it. I was now merely the captain of a small, shabby steamboat, going to collect some ivory and the bones of a dead agent. The great, terrible secret I had felt myself approaching had resolved itself into a simple, sordid tale of failure and death. The darkness had won. It had swallowed Kurtz, and now it was just digestsing him. And I, I was left with the taste of ashes in my mouth, and the blood of a good man on my shoes.

There was in their eyes a profound, bewildered inquiry, a quiet waiting for some incomprehensible doom which was to descend upon them, and which they would meet with the dumb resignation of animals. They were not enemies; they were not criminals; they were not even savages in any sense that made them accountable. They were, nothing. Nothing but a great, muscular fact, a force of nature that had been captured and harnessed to a fantastic and cruel enterprise. And the restraint; yes, the restraint. It was not fear. We were five, armed with rifles which we did not know how to use with any certainty, and they were thirty, armed with the primal weapons of hands and teeth and the desperate courage of starvation. It was not prudence, for they were men of the forest, not of the counting house; what did they know of weighing consequences? No, it was something deeper, an inscrutable principle. Perhaps it was the sheer alienness of our lunacy that held them in check. They could understand a blow, a spear-thrust, a cry of rage. But the methodical, passionless, and utterly insane logic of our enterprise, the patient collection of ivory for a world they could not imagine, the payment in bits of useless wire for a life of slow starvation, the whole farcical charade, perhaps this was so profoundly unnatural to them that it paralysed their will, as a snake is said to paralyse a bird. They could not fight it because they could not understand it; they could not rebel against a system whose rules were not of this earth. They were caught in the web of an incomprehensible witchcraft, and they were dying of it, quietly, with their faces turned to the impenetrable fog.

The manager appeared at my side, a spectre in the pearly gloom, his white clothes making him seem disembodied. He did not startle me. My senses were attuned to a different sort of threat. ‘He is dead,’ he said, with no preamble. It was a statement, not a question; a conclusion delivered with a chilling finality. I did not answer. I was watching the headman, who had not moved, his profile a stern carving against the grey void. ‘This attack,’ the manager continued, his voice low and confidential, as if we were coconspirators, ‘it proves it. The station has been overrun. The savages have turned on him. There is nothing left for us there but his bones, if that. We must turn back.’ He said it smoothly, the words sliding out as if they had been rehearsed. There was a little tremor of eagerness in his tone, a ghastly corporate cheerfulness. The chief obstacle to his promotion, the brilliant and troublesome Kurtz, had been removed by a convenient act of providence.

‘Turn back?’ I repeated, turning to look at him for the first time. His face was a pale oval, indistinct, the features blurred by the mist and by his own inherent featurelessness. ‘We are eight miles from the station. We came here for ivory and for Mr. Kurtz.’

‘The ivory is lost, my dear fellow,’ he purred, laying a clammy hand on my arm. I shook it off. ‘And Mr. Kurtz is dead. To proceed would be suicide. Think of the Company’s property. Think of our lives.’ He gestured vaguely at the invisible pilgrims huddled somewhere behind us. ‘We have a duty to get this boat and ourselves back down the river safely.’

A murmur of assent rose from the gloom, a pathetic chorus of frightened men. ‘He’s right.’ ‘Madness to go on.’ ‘Let’s get out of this dreadful place.’ The little fat man with the pink pyjamas materialized at the manager’s elbow, his face glistening with sweat. ‘We’ll all be murdered,’ he whispered, his voice cracking. ‘For what? For a man who was trading with the savages, who went native, they say. He brought it on himself.’

‘Did he?’ I asked, my voice louder than I intended. The sound of it seemed to cut through the fog. ‘And you, have you brought your salvation on yourselves, with your piety and your sidespring boots?’ He recoiled as if I had struck him. I felt a savage satisfaction. These men, with their sordid little souls and their gross appetites, passing judgment on a man like Kurtz. The absurdity of it was monstrous. I believed him to be dead, yes, but my belief was a kind of mourning, a recognition of a great light extinguished. Theirs was the petty glee of jackals finding a lion’s carcass.

‘I am going on,’ I said, turning my back on them and facing the bow. ‘I will take the boat up to the station myself. It is my charge. Those of you who wish to can take your chances on the bank.’ It was an empty threat, of course. The bank was death. But it silenced them. The manager made a small, frustrated sound, like a pained grunt. He knew I had him. The boat was under my command, and my stubbornness was the one factor his neat calculations had not accounted for. He hated me for it then, I think; hated me with a passion that was almost respectable in its intensity. It was the most real emotion I had ever seen him display. He faded back into the mist, his agent at his heels, leaving me to my mad resolve.

The silence that followed was of a different quality. It was no longer the silence of nature, but a human silence, thick with resentment and fear. I stood alone in the pilot-house, gripping the wheel, staring into the blank wall ahead. Why was I doing it? It was not for the Company. It was not for the ivory. It was not even for Kurtz, not anymore. It was, I think, for the helmsman I had just tipped into the river. He had died at his post, trying to get us here. To turn back now would be to make that death utterly meaningless, a mere bloody incident in a squalid farce. It was a question of faith. He had kept his faith with me, with the journey. I had to keep faith with him, with the destination. I would go on, if only to see the place where he was meant to arrive. I would look upon the empty shrine. That was my pilgrimage now.

An hour passed, or perhaps it was a century. Time itself seemed to have dissolved in the fog. Then, without warning, a breath of air touched my cheek. It was not a wind; it was more like a sigh from the land itself. The solid-seeming wall of white began to lose its density. It thinned, became translucent, broke apart into colossal, drifting shapes, like icebergs of vapour. Patches of the river appeared, dark and slick. Then a line of trees emerged on a bank, then another. The world was being reassembled before my eyes, piece by piece, out of nothingness. And at last, as the final curtain of mist drew back, we saw it.

The inner station. It sat on a small, cleared rise, overlooking the river. A long, low building of mud and thatch, with a high, collapsing verandah. And all around it, a fence. A crude fence of rough-hewn stakes. My eyes were drawn to it, I don’t know why. There was something odd about the posts. Each one was topped with a round, dark object. I raised my binoculars, my hands unsteady. The glass was misty; I wiped it on my sleeve. I looked again. The objects were not ornamental knobs, as I had first supposed. They were heads. Dried, shrunken, human heads, fixed on the stakes, their faces turned towards the house.

I lowered the glasses slowly. My heart was not pounding; it was quite still. I felt a peculiar calm, the lucidity that comes after a great shock. The pilgrims behind me had seen it too. A gasp, a choked sob, a frantic babbling of prayers. The manager was silent, but I could feel his presence, his rigid, calculating horror. This was beyond the pale. This was not just unorthodox; it was unproductive. Such practices were bad for business.

But my own horror was of a different kind. It was not revulsion. It was a kind of dreadful, intellectual curiosity. I found myself studying the heads. They were not all alike. Some were old, with scraps of white hair clinging to the scalp. Some were young. Some had their eyes closed, as if in sleep. Others stared out with a sightless, agonised intensity. They were, I surmised, the heads of rebels. This was the method Kurtz had used to maintain order, to inspire devotion. And looking at them, at their pathetic, defeated agony, I understood something. This was not the act of a madman. It was the act of a god, a terrible, petty deity of this dark corner of the world, who had looked into the abyss and found it resonant with his own soul. He had not gone native; he had taken the wilderness and bent it to his own magnificent and terrible will. He had kicked himself loose of the earth, and he was alone, exalted, and utterly damned. The heads were not trophies. They were offerings. They were prayers.

And then I saw that one of the stakes, the one nearest the house, was bare. The place where a head should have been was empty. The thought that had settled on me like a tombstone was wrong. It was all wrong. Kurtz was not dead. He could not be. A man who could do this, a man who could inspire this, did not simply die. The uproar in the fog, the attack, it was not a sign of his end. It was a symptom of his power, a convulsion in the land he ruled. He was here. He was alive.

As if in answer to my thought, a figure appeared on the verandah of the house. It was not Kurtz. It was a man, or rather a boy, dressed in a fantastic patchwork of clothes. A jacket of blue cloth, a pair of canvas trousers, and a shirt made of what looked like a piece of sail. He was all angles and joints, like a marionette, with a boyish, beardless face and a shock of fair hair. He waved his arms at us, a frantic, joyous gesture of welcome. He looked utterly out of place, a clown who had wandered into a charnel house. He seemed ridiculously fragile, absurdly cheerful. He hopped from one foot to the other, beckoning us ashore.

The manager let out a breath. ‘Who in God’s name is that?’ he whispered. But I knew. I knew with a certainty that had no basis in logic. This was a disciple. This was the one who tended the shrine.

‘We’re going ashore,’ I said, my voice hoarse. I rang the engine-room telegraph for ‘Slow Ahead.’ The little steamer shuddered and began to creep towards the bank. The pilgrims clutched their rifles, their faces a mixture of terror and avarice. They were afraid of the heads on the stakes, but they could smell the ivory. The manager stood beside me, his lips thin, his eyes darting from the house to the shore to my face, trying to formulate a plan, to regain control of a situation that had spiralled into a waking nightmare.

The patchwork puppet on the shore was shouting something, but his voice was too thin to carry over the churn of the paddle-wheel. He looked like a castaway greeting a rescue ship, and yet there was something in his frantic energy that was more than just relief. It was adoration. He was not welcoming us; he was welcoming the world that had produced his master.

We dropped anchor in the shadow of the bank, the chain rattling out with a sound that seemed profane in the profound stillness. The crowd of natives I had half expected to see was not there. The shore was empty, save for the boy. The heads on the stakes watched us, their silent testimony more eloquent than any shout. The wilderness itself seemed to be holding its breath, waiting.

The boy, he could not have been more than twenty-five, had scrambled down the bank and was now splashing through the shallows towards us, heedless of crocodiles, of anything. ‘You’ve come!’ he cried, his voice a high, reedy tenor, with a strange accent I could not place. ‘You’ve come at last! I knew you would! He said you would!’

‘Who said?’ the manager barked, leaning over the rail. ‘Who are you?’

The boy looked up, his face alight with a delirious, unreasoning joy. He paid no attention to the manager. His eyes, wide and blue and startlingly innocent, found me in the pilot-house. ‘You are the captain,’ he stated. ‘English. I can tell. He needs you. Oh, you cannot imagine how he has been waiting!’ He spoke as if Kurtz were a king, and we were a long-overdue embassy. ‘They attacked,’ he said, gesturing vaguely into the forest. ‘The people from the woods. They do not understand. They are his enemies. But his own people, ’ he swept a hand towards the silent, watching village behind the house, ‘, they are faithful. They fought for him. Oh, they fought!’

‘Where is Mr. Kurtz?’ I asked, the words feeling heavy and strange in my mouth.

The boy’s face clouded over, the joy replaced by a sudden, anxious concern. ‘He is ill,’ he whispered, as if the river itself might overhear and take offence. ‘Very ill. He has been, exacting himself. His great work. The ideas. Sometimes the body cannot keep up with the spirit. But your arrival, the medicines you have brought, the contact with Europe, it will restore him. You will see. He is a universal genius.’

He said this with such passionate conviction that for a moment I almost believed him. He was a believer, a true believer, and there is nothing in the world so convincing as faith. He was Kurtz’s last convert, and his first apostle. He saw before him not a dilapidated trading post and some shrunken heads, but a new civilisation, a new gospel.

‘The ivory,’ the manager cut in, his voice grating with practicality. ‘Is the ivory safe?’

The boy looked at him with a sort of baffled pity, as one might look at a man who asks the price of the candlesticks on an altar. ‘The ivory?’ he repeated. ‘Oh, yes, piles of it. He is a prodigy. He has collected more ivory than all the other stations put together. But that is not the point. You do not understand. The ivory is just a trifle. It is his words you should be seeking, the greatness of his thoughts. He has enlarged my mind.’

He gazed back towards the house with a look of pure rapture. I looked too. And I knew that I had to get Kurtz out of there. Not for the Company, not for the ivory, not even for his own sake. But for mine. I had to get him on my boat, to hear his voice, to look upon the man who had built this kingdom of horror and worship. I had come all this way seeking a voice, and I had found instead an idol, worshipped with bloody rites by this boy-priest and a congregation of cannibals. I had to take him away, to bring him back into the world of time and consequences, if only to see what would happen. To see if the god could survive being a man again. The journey was not over. It had just begun.

The boy started, as if waking from a dream. His face, which had been a mask of ecstatic contemplation, took on a look of protective alarm. ‘No, no, you must not. He is resting. The excitement, it is too much for him. You must wait. I will tell him you are here. I will prepare him.’

‘Prepare him?’ the manager sneered. ‘He is an agent of the Company. He is not a king receiving tribute. I am his superior. I will see him at once.’

This clash of two worlds, the world of ledgers and authorities against the world of visions and disciples, was almost comical in its intensity. The manager, with his neat little suit and his air of fretful importance, was utterly out of his depth. He was trying to apply the rules of a game that was not being played here. The boy, with his patched clothes and his burning eyes, was the keeper of a different flame altogether. He drew himself up, a scarecrow filled with sudden dignity. ‘You do not understand,’ he said, and there was a note of pity in his voice. ‘You cannot simply walk in. He has been… communing. His thoughts are on a different plane. To interrupt would be a sacrilege.’

I stepped between them, partly to prevent the manager from doing something foolish, and partly because I felt a sudden, cold certainty that the boy was right. One did not simply walk in on Kurtz. One approached, as one might approach a primordial shrine, with a sense of dread and fearful anticipation. ‘Let us go ashore,’ I said. ‘We can wait there. It is better than this fog.’

My suggestion seemed to offer a practical compromise, and after a moment’s hesitation, they both agreed. We lowered the small boat, a flimsy cockleshell in that immense silence, and made for the bank. The manager, the boy, and I. The pilgrims remained on the steamer, their rifles held at the ready, their faces pale and strained, peering into the fog as if expecting an attack at any moment. They were like men trapped in a bad dream, and in a way, they were not wrong.

The moment my foot touched the shore, the unreality of the place took on a new, more tangible quality. The ground was soft and yielding, a black mud that smelled of decay and ancient water. A narrow, trodden path led up from the river’s edge, through a screen of withered reeds. And as we emerged from the reeds, I saw them. I had been prepared for something of the sort, of course. The boy had spoken of enemies, of severity. But the reality was, as it always is, a thing apart. They were stakes, set in a semi-circle before the house, and on top of each stake was a dried, shrunken head. They were not pleasant to look at. They were black, withered, with sunken cheeks and grinning, teeth-baring expressions. They seemed to be whispering to one another in the still air, their dead lips twisted into a permanent mockery. What was most shocking was not the barbarity of it, but the thought behind it. They were not the heads of enemies, I felt instinctively. They were the heads of heretics. Rebels against the new creed. They had been turned to face the house, as if in perpetual, silent adoration of the man within. They were a warning, a symbol of a power that had gone beyond all known limits.

The manager stopped dead. ‘My God,’ he whispered, his face turning a sickly shade of grey. ‘What is this? Is he mad?’

The Russian boy glanced at him, his expression one of faint annoyance, as if the manager had made a terribly impolite remark. ‘He has had to be firm,’ he said calmly. ‘These were criminals. They resisted the light. They wanted to steal his ivory.’ He seemed to think this a perfectly reasonable explanation. I looked from the heads to the boy’s innocent, fanatical face, and I felt a chill that had nothing to do with the damp air. He had helped to put them there, I had no doubt. He had probably sharpened the stakes. For him, this was not horror, but justice. A necessary part of the great work.

He led us on, past the grisly sentinels, towards the house. It was a long, low building of clay and reeds, with a verandah that sagged in the middle. It was in a state of advanced decay, the thatch falling in, the walls streaked with green mould. And yet, there was an air of habitation about it, of a fierce, concentrated life burning within its crumbling shell. On the verandah, in the deep shade, figures stirred. They were the natives the boy had called ‘his people.’ They were armed with spears and old muskets, their bodies decorated with streaks of white and yellow clay. They watched us approach with a profound, unnerving stillness, their eyes black and unreadable. They were not hostile, not exactly. They were simply there, a silent, watchful congregation guarding their deity.

‘We will wait here,’ the boy announced, gesturing to a rough-hewn bench on the verandah. ‘I will see if he is awake.’ He disappeared into the darkness of the doorway, leaving me and the manager alone with the silent watchers and the grinning heads.

The manager sat down heavily, wiping his brow with a silk handkerchief. ‘This is monstrous,’ he kept muttering to himself. ‘Completely monstrous. The man has no method. No method at all.’ It was the cry of a bookkeeper who has discovered a universe that cannot be audited. He was not horrified by the moral implications; he was outraged by the lack of procedure. The ivory, where was the ivory? How was it accounted for? These were the questions that tormented his small, tidy soul.

I did not sit. I stood by the rail of the verandah, looking out at the river. The fog was beginning to thin, patches of it dissolving to reveal the dark, oily water and the immense, brooding forest on the other side. The silence was absolute, save for the manager’s fretful muttering and the faint, almost imperceptible hum that seemed to come from the house itself. It was the sound of a life burning itself out with ferocious intensity. I thought of the man inside, this Kurtz, who had come into the wilderness with high ideals, with, I had been told, a mission to enlighten and improve. And this was the result. A kingdom of skulls, a congregation of armed savages, and a boy-priest who spoke of his master’s thoughts as if they were holy writ. What had happened here? What secret had the wilderness whispered to him that had stripped away the veneer of civilisation and left this terrible, primal truth?

I had to know. My own journey, my own descent into this heart of darkness, seemed to culminate in that single, obsessive question. I had not come for the ivory, or for the Company. I had come for this. To hear the voice, to understand the fall.

After what seemed an eternity, the boy reappeared. He was flushed, his eyes shining with a kind of holy excitement. ‘He will see you,’ he breathed. ‘He is weak, but his mind is clear. Clearer than ever. He has been waiting for an audience, for a man from the real world. A man like you.’ He looked at me, not the manager. ‘He says you are a man who can understand.’

The manager rose, bristling at the slight. ‘It is about time. I have a great deal to say to him.’

The boy ignored him, his gaze fixed on me. ‘Be prepared,’ he whispered. ‘What you will hear… it is not for small minds.’ He held back the crude animal hide that served as a door, and gestured for us to enter.

The manager went first, his footsteps hesitant on the uneven floor. I followed, my heart beating a slow, heavy rhythm against my ribs. The interior of the house was even darker than the verandah. It was one long room, cluttered with a bizarre assortment of objects: crates of cartridges, books piled in chaotic stacks, scientific instruments covered in dust, and everywhere, scattered on the floor, on the table, leaning against the walls, were elephant tusks. Great, curving arcs of old ivory, gleaming with a pale, ghostly light in the gloom. It was an astonishing display of wealth and rapacity, a king’s ransom lying in the dirt.

And in the furthest corner of the room, on a low pallet of straw and blankets, lay Kurtz.

My first impression was of an immense, skeletal frame, draped in rags. He was incredibly tall, but so emaciated that he seemed little more than a collection of bones. His head was a skull, bald and yellowed, like ancient ivory. But it was the eyes that held you. They were set deep in their sockets, and they burned with a feverish, consuming intelligence. They were not the eyes of a sick man. They were the eyes of a seer, a man who has looked into the abyss and has not flinched.

He stirred, and a hand, a mere bundle of bones held together by dry skin, rose and pointed a long, skeletal finger at the manager. And then he spoke. His voice was the most astonishing thing about him. It was not loud, but it had a deep, resonant quality that filled the room, that seemed to vibrate in the very air. It was a voice made for pronouncements, for commanding multitudes, for whispering terrible secrets.

‘You,’ he said, and the word was an indictment. ‘You have come to judge me. You, with your little rules and your petty concerns. You, who crawl on the face of the earth and call it progress.’

The manager recoiled as if struck. ‘Mr. Kurtz, I…’

‘Silence,’ the voice commanded, and the manager fell silent. Kurtz’s burning eyes left him and found me. They seemed to probe, to search, to weigh. I felt like a specimen under a microscope. I stood my ground, my hands clenched at my sides.

‘And you,’ the voice went on, softer now, but no less intense. ‘You are the sailor. The man of surfaces, of tides and currents. But you have a soul that listens. I have felt you coming. All this way.’ He paused, and a faint, terrible smile touched his lips. ‘You have come for the truth. You have come to the very heart of it.’

He tried to raise himself on one elbow, and the effort caused a paroxysm of coughing that shook his whole frame. The Russian boy rushed to his side, holding a cup of water to his lips. Kurtz pushed it away impatiently. His eyes never left me.

‘They tell you I am mad,’ he whispered, the sound a dry rustle. ‘The mad are those who cannot see. I see everything. I have seen the horror. The horror. And I have understood it. I have embraced it. It is not a thing to be fought. It is a thing to be… used.’

The manager found his voice again, a thin, reedy sound. ‘The ivory, Mr. Kurtz. The reports. The Company demands an explanation for your methods.’

Kurtz turned his head slowly, and the look he gave the manager was one of sublime, infinite contempt. ‘The ivory?’ he said, and the resonant voice was laced with a chilling amusement. ‘The ivory is mine. I won it. Not for the Company. Not for any tin-pot king in a distant land. For myself. I am the king here. My methods are my own. They are successful. Is that not all your Company cares for? Success?’ He gestured with his bony hand towards the pile of tusks. ‘There is your explanation. Take it and go. Leave me to my work.’

‘Your work?’ the manager stammered. ‘What work? This… this butchery?’

A fire blazed in Kurtz’s eyes. ‘Butchery? No. It is creation. I am forging a new reality. I am teaching these people the meaning of power. True power. Not the pathetic charade you play with your contracts and your laws. I give them a god they can see, a will they can fear. And they give me… absolute obedience.’ He fell back on the pallet, exhausted. ‘You cannot understand,’ he murmured. ‘You are a child playing with toys.’

His gaze returned to me, and there was a strange, pleading quality in it now. ‘You understand, don’t you? You have seen the darkness in the river, in the forest, in the hearts of men. I have not created it. I have only given it a voice. My voice.’

And at that moment, from outside, a new sound arose. A low, mournful wail that grew in volume until it was a chorus of profound and desperate grief. The natives on the verandah were crying out. The Russian boy ran to the doorway and peered out. ‘She is here,’ he said, his voice tight with fear. ‘She has come.’

Kurtz’s head turned towards the sound. A look of extraordinary complexity passed over his face: a mixture of desire, of fear, of triumphant possession. ‘Ah,’ he breathed. ‘My queen.’

Through the doorway, I could see her. She stood at the edge of the forest, a tall, magnificent figure. She was draped in dark cloths, her arms laden with brass and ivory bracelets. Her face was a mask of fierce, sorrowful beauty. She did not look at us. She looked only at the house, at the man within. The wailing of the crowd rose around her, a wave of sound that beat against the flimsy walls of the hut. It was not a cry of welcome. It was a cry of protest, of lamentation. They knew we had come to take him away. And she, this savage and splendid queen, had come to forbid it. She raised her arms, and the crowd fell silent. It was a gesture of absolute authority.

The manager, utterly terrified, shrank back into the shadows of the room. ‘We must go,’ he hissed. ‘Now. Take the ivory and go.’

But Kurtz was not looking at him. He was looking at me. ‘You see?’ he whispered, and his voice was a thread of sound. ‘You see the choice? Their world, or yours? The darkness with a throne, or the light with its lies?’ He struggled to sit up again, his eyes blazing. ‘Take me with you. Take me back. I must speak to them. I have so much to say. But do not let them win.’

I did not know who ‘they’ were. The company? The savages? Or the encroaching madness itself? I looked from the skeletal figure on the pallet to the magnificent woman at the forest’s edge, from the pile of dead ivory to the grinning heads on the stakes. The choice he spoke of was not a choice at all. It was a descent. And I was already on the path.

‘We will take you,’ I said, and the words felt like a sentence pronounced upon us all. The journey was not over. No. It was just sinking into its true, its final, and its most terrible stage. I had found the voice. And now I had to carry it, with all its attendant horrors, back into a world that had no place for it. I had to see if the god could survive the voyage. I had my doubts.

They looked at the manager, who nodded reluctantly, his avarice for the ivory warring with his fear of the woman. Two of them, with a great deal of shuffling and muttering, produced a canvas stretcher and entered the hut. The transfer of Kurtz from his wretched bed to the stretcher was a horrid business. We began our slow, absurd procession towards the river bank: the two trembling pilgrims carrying the stretcher, the manager scurrying ahead as if to clear a path that was already clear, the Russian boy trailing behind like a lost dog, and I walking beside Kurtz. A grotesque parody of a royal procession. Kurtz’s head lolled on the canvas, but his eyes were fixed on the woman. He was taking his leave of his kingdom.

She had not moved. As we drew nearer, I could see the intricate scarring on her cheeks, the sombre fire in her dark eyes. She was beautiful, yes, but it was a beauty that had nothing to do with grace or comfort; it was the terrible, awe-inspiring beauty of a storm, of a panther, of the implacable jungle itself. She watched us come, her expression unchanging. The crowd was utterly still, their silence a wall around her. My hand strayed towards the revolver at my hip, a completely futile gesture. What was a pistol against this solid wall of grief and devotion?

When we were but twenty yards from the steamboat, she moved. She did not cry out, she did not rush forward. She simply raised her arms, her hands open, palms upward, to the grey, unforgiving sky. It was a gesture of immense and tragic grandeur, a silent appeal to forces I could not name. It might have been a curse. It might have been a farewell. It might have been a surrender to a fate she understood far better than I. The mass of natives behind her stirred, and for a heart-stopping moment I thought they would charge. But they did not. They merely echoed her gesture, a forest of dark arms rising in unison, a silent chorus of despair.

At that, Kurtz made a final effort. He tried to raise himself on one elbow. A word, a sound, bubbled on his lips, but it was lost, a faint guttural noise that the river breeze snatched away. His head fell back, and his eyes closed. Whether he had fainted or merely surrendered to his profound weariness, I could not tell.

‘Quickly! On board!’ the manager yelped, his voice cracking. We bundled the stretcher onto the deck, a clumsy and undignified affair. The pilgrims immediately abandoned it to cast off the mooring ropes. The Russian boy stood at the rail, waving his tattered cap at the shore, a gesture of such forlorn and ridiculous loyalty that it was almost poignant. He was staying. He belonged here, in this madhouse, more than he could ever belong anywhere else.

I ordered the steam up. The chugging of the engine began, a familiar, mechanical heartbeat in the overwhelming silence. The paddle-wheel churned the brown water, and the steamboat began to move away from the bank, away from the silent, accusing figures. The woman stood as we had left her, with her arms raised, a tragic and magnificent statue carved against the backdrop of the immense, indifferent forest. She watched us go until the steamboat rounded a bend in the river, and the station, the natives, the ivory, and that unforgettable figure of savage sorrow were swallowed up by the jungle.

The world shrank to the confines of our little steamer. The manager, his relief palpable, immediately began to fuss over the ivory, counting the tusks, assessing their weight, his soul entirely absorbed by the calculation of his commission. The pilgrims, their immediate fear assuaged, went about their duties with a sullen reluctance, their faces still bearing the shadow of the unnerving spectacle. And in the small cabin, which I had cleared for his use, Kurtz lay dying.

The journey downriver was the grim antithesis of the journey up. Before, there had been a sense of mystery, of striving towards a goal, however ill-defined. Now, there was only the oppressive weight of the goal achieved, the mystery unveiled to reveal a horror. The river was no longer a path into the unknown, but an escape route from a known damnation. Yet, we were not escaping. We were carrying the damnation with us, locked in the cabin, breathing its pestilential air.

Kurtz drifted in and out of a feverish consciousness. Sometimes he would lie for hours, silent and still as the dead, his breath so shallow I had to bend close to be sure it was there at all. At other times, he would talk, his voice a reedy thread of sound, but his words possessed a terrifying clarity. He was no longer the master of ceremonies, the orator holding a crowd rapt. He was a man stripped to his essential ghost, and that ghost was articulate.

‘They listen,’ he whispered one afternoon, as I sat by his side, swabbing his forehead with a damp cloth. The heat in the cabin was suffocating. ‘All Europe contributed to my making… and I have nothing to say to them now but a whisper. They sent me for ivory, for progress, for enlightenment. And I brought them… myself.’ He tried to laugh, a dry, rattling sound from his ruined chest. ‘A bargain, don't you think?’

He would speak of his plans, his immense, philanthropic plans that had somehow curdled into grandiose schemes of terror. ‘I would have made them see,’ he insisted, his eyes glowing with a momentary spark. ‘A new faith, a new order. But there was no time. The pettiness… the jealousy… that manager…’ His voice would trail off into muttered imprecations against the manager, against the company, against the very fabric of a world that was too small for his colossal ego.

And he spoke of the wilderness. He did not speak of it with love or with hatred, but with the profound, intimate knowledge of a man who has surrendered himself to a lover and been consumed by her. ‘You can’t judge it,’ he told me, his gaze fixed on the endless green wall sliding past the porthole. ‘It whispers things to you. Things you have forgotten. It has a voice. And if you are alone, and you listen… you answer. Your own voice, which you thought was yours, becomes an echo of its voice.’ He paused, and a look of profound surprise crossed his face, as if he were discovering this for the first time. ‘My voice,’ he breathed. ‘It was not my voice.’

The steamboat broke down. Of course it did. It was inevitable, a final, farcical touch from the presiding genius of this whole affair. A cracked steam-pipe. We drifted helplessly for two days, anchored in a wide, muddy stretch of the river, surrounded by the same oppressive silence, the same impenetrable green walls. The manager was beside himself with rage, not at the delay, but at the thought of the steamboat’s repair bill diminishing the profits. The pilgrims lounged, sharpening their knives, their eyes occasionally straying towards the cabin where their former tormentor now lay helpless. There was a calculating look in their eyes that I did not like.

During those two days, Kurtz sank rapidly. The flashes of lucidity became rarer, the periods of fevered delirium longer. He was haunted by images, by sounds. He would call out for his queen, his savage consort, in a tone of desperate longing. Then he would speak of his Intended back in Europe, and his voice would be filled with a kind of hollow, conventional tenderness that was more chilling than his ravings. He saw them both, I think, as reflections of his own greatness, two different altars for the same worship.

He had become my secret, my responsibility. I fed him, I listened to his terrible confessions, I protected him from the hungry glances of the pilgrims. Why? I ask myself that now. Was it loyalty? Was it pity? No. It was a kind of fascination, a morbid kinship. I had to see it through to the end. I had to hear the final words. I felt that in his downfall was a reflection of some secret truth about us all, and I could not turn away. I was the chosen confessor to a faith of which he was the only adherent and the only god.

On the second night of our enforced halt, the fever rose to a new height. The cabin was an oven, thick with the smell of mortality. I sat on a stool by his cot, the oil lamp casting long, dancing shadows on the bulkheads. Kurtz was rambling, his voice a continuous, monotonous murmur. He spoke of wealth, of love, of justice, of fame; the words were just sounds, empty vessels from which all meaning had drained away. He tossed his head from side to side, his hands plucking at the thin blanket.

Suddenly, he fell silent. His eyes opened, wide and clear, and they were filled not with fever, but with an immense, lucid terror. He stared straight ahead, at the cheap wooden partition, but I knew he was not seeing it. He was seeing something else, something that had been waiting for him in the depths of his own soul, something that had now, at the last, come to claim him. His whole body stiffened. He tried to sit up, his mouth agape.

A whisper, so faint it was barely more than a breath, yet it filled the tiny cabin, it filled the world. It was a judgment, a verdict, a summary of a life, of a world, of a truth.

‘The horror! The horror!’

I blew out the lamp and let the darkness rush in. It was a relief. There was nothing more to see. I sat there in the dark, my heart pounding, the two words echoing in the utter stillness. It was not a cry of fear. It was a statement of fact. He had pronounced a judgment upon the adventures of his soul on this earth. He had summed it all up, and it was a verdict of unutterable horror.

I remained there, a sentinel in the dark, for how long I do not know. The mechanical throb of the crickets outside seemed to mark a different kind of time. The manager’s boy, a young native from the coast, poked his head in. ‘Mistah Kurtz, he dead,’ he announced in a flat, dispassionate tone. I went out onto the deck. The stars were out, clear and brilliant, indifferent. The pilgrims were clustered by the rail, whispering. They glanced at me, then at the cabin door, and a sort of collective, reptilian relief passed over their faces. The nightmare was over. Their small, greedy world could resume its normal course.

But for me, it was not over. The voice was silent, but I had heard it. I had it with me now, a permanent resident in my own heart. I was the keeper of those last words, the custodian of that final, terrible illumination. The journey was not over. No. It was just sinking into its true, its final, and its most terrible stage. I had found the voice. And now I had to carry it, with all its attendant horrors, back into a world that had no place for it. I had to see if I could survive the voyage. I had my doubts.

I found myself watching the manager. He stood a little apart, near the flimsy railing of the upper deck, his hands clasped behind his back. He had not flinched, not cried out. His was the irritation of a man whose careful schedule has been disrupted by an act of God, or, what was to him the same thing, an act of sheer, unmanageable savagery. The precious, mythical ivory, waiting upriver, and here we were, stopped, deafened, and blinded by a wilderness that refused to play by the rules. He caught my eye and made a small, impatient gesture with his head, as if to say, ‘Well? Get on with it.’

Get on with what? Plunge blindly into a wall of milk? Offer up the steamer as a sacrifice to whatever devils were making this appalling row? Caution, he had said last night. Caution was the word. My gaze went back to the river, or what little of it I could see. The current, invisible but strong, tugged at the anchor chain, making the steamer sheer and swing in a slow, uneasy arc. We were not still. We were being moved by forces we could not see, a perfect metaphor for our whole ridiculous enterprise.

Then it came. A whisper of movement in the air, a sound too slight for the ear, more a feeling in the teeth, and then a slender stick, a mere toy of a thing, appeared, quivering, in the woodwork of the pilot house not a foot from my head. It was an arrow. A long, thin shaft of cane with a nasty-looking, blackened point. It had made no sound on its arrival, save for a faint, final ‘thwick’. It was utterly silent, utterly deadly, a message from the invisible world.

The sight of it broke the spell. It was a tangible object, a piece of evidence. The world was not entirely dissolved, after all. The open-mouthed pilgrim gave a gurgling cry and fired his Winchester into the fog. The other man with a rifle started blazing away, levering and firing as fast as he could, the hot cartridges spinning out onto the deck. The little fat man scrambled to his feet, fumbled in a bag, and produced a revolver, which he began to discharge into the white emptiness with a series of hysterical pops. It was a farcical ballet of terror. They were firing at a sound, at a memory, at their own fear. The waste of cartridges was appalling.

I bellowed at them to stop, but my voice was lost in the din. I saw the manager making sharp, angry gestures, his lips moving, but no sound came. The black fellows of the crew had dropped the chain they had been hauling and were crouching low, their eyes wide, not with the panicked terror of the whites, but with a keen, professional interest. They were watching the fog, not firing into it. The headman was still by my side. He had not moved, but he was no longer pensive. He was alert, poised, his nostrils flared as if he could scent the danger.

I had to do something. The random firing was more dangerous to us on board than any number of arrows from the bank. I lunged for the lanyard of the steam whistle. I gave it a long, hard pull.

A shriek tore through the fog, a blast of sound so immense, so brutally industrial, it seemed to rip the very fabric of the silence. It was a voice of brass and steam, our voice, a scream to answer the one from the wilderness. It drowned out the paltry popping of the rifles, it vibrated in the deck plates, in the bones of your skull. I held it, a continuous, deafening bellow, a statement of our own capacity for creating a meaningless and terrifying noise. The pilgrims clapped their hands to their ears, their faces masks of agony. Even the manager winced.

For a moment, it seemed to work. The air, which had been full of an unseen menace, was now filled with our own. There was a pause, a stillness in the fog. I let go the lanyard, and the ensuing silence was deeper, more absolute than before.

And in that silence, it came again. Not the scream this time, but a shower of whispers. A flight of arrows, a soft, feathery rustle that ended with a dozen dull thuds into the wood of the deckhouse and the canvas awning. One clattered onto the deck at my feet. It was longer than the first, a proper spear-like affair. The silence from the banks was over. Our whistle had been answered.

‘We must go on!’ the manager shrieked, his voice thin and high. ‘Full steam! Get away from the bank!’

‘Which bank?’ I shouted back, my patience, which had been worn thin for months, finally snapping. ‘And in which direction? Would you have me run us aground on a bank we cannot see?’

He stared at me, his face a contortion of fury and impotence. But there was no time for argument. Another flight of arrows arrived. This was no longer a warning. It was an attack, however blind. We were a huge, helpless target sitting in the middle of a shooting gallery where the shooters were invisible. I had to move. Caution was dead; survival was all that mattered.

‘Haul in that chain!’ I roared at the crew, pointing. ‘Lively now!’ The headman grunted a phrase in his own tongue, and the crew, his chaps, jumped to it with a will, their movements swift and economical. The whites were still fumbling with their hot rifles, useless. I spun the wheel, trying to guess the direction of the main channel. It was a gamble, a desperate one. I would head for the source of the ripples I had seen last night, trusting to a sailor’s instinct that no book, no chart, had ever prepared me for.

The anchor came up with a clatter and a suck of mud. I rang for full steam ahead. The old steamer shuddered, her paddles began to churn, and slowly, agonizingly, we began to move. We were a blind man running in a crowded room. I was peering into the white glare, my eyes straining until they burned, trying to make out a shape, a shadow, anything. The helmsman, a tall fellow from a river village further south, stood beside me, his hands gripping the spokes of a smaller, auxiliary wheel. He was a good man, silent, competent, one of the few on this trip who simply did his job without complaint or commentary. He stared into the fog with me, his brow furrowed in concentration.

We were moving, yes, but the world remained a blank. The arrows had stopped, perhaps because our movement made us a harder target, perhaps because of the churning noise of the paddles. I listened with every fibre of my being, not for screams, but for the tell-tale scrape of our hull on a sandbank. The pilgrims were huddled now, a miserable knot of pyjamas and fear. The manager stood at my elbow again. ‘Faster,’ he breathed. ‘You must go faster.’ I ignored him. Any faster and we would tear our bottom out on the first snag we met.

Then, a dark shape loomed, for an instant, in the mist ahead and to our left. A tree? A piece of the bank? It was impossible to say. Before I could react, a long, thin object flew out of the whiteness. It was not an arrow. It was a spear, heavier, thrown with great force.

I saw it coming. It seemed to move with a dream-like slowness. I had time to think, ‘So, this is it.’ I braced for the impact. But it was not for me.

The helmsman beside me made a soft, grunting sound, like a man who has lifted something too heavy. He did not cry out. He fell against me, a dead weight, and I felt a sudden, surprising warmth spread across my shoes. I looked down. The spear had gone clean through him. The dark wood shaft stuck out of his chest, and the point, a sharpened piece of iron, protruded from his back. He slid from me to the deck, his hands falling away from the wheel, leaving bloody fingerprints on the spokes. His eyes were open, full of a vast, uncomprehending surprise. He looked at me, a mute question in his gaze, and then a little trickle of blood ran from the corner of his mouth. He was dead.

I was spattered with his blood. It was on my hands, my trousers, soaking into my canvas shoes. I stared at the body at my feet, at the absurdly long spear. The attack was no longer an abstract event. The unseen enemy was no longer a phantom. The wilderness had struck back, and it had drawn blood. My blood, it felt like.

A sort of cold fury seized me. I kicked the spear shaft where it protruded from his body, trying to break it, to push it away, but it was lodged fast. The manager yelped and jumped back. The pilgrims moaned. I grabbed the wheel myself, my hands slipping on the spokes his own had just left. The warmth from my shoes was turning cold and sticky. I spun the wheel hard over, turning the steamer’s nose away from the unseen bank.

I pulled the whistle lanyard again, not with any plan, but in a paroxysm of rage. I let it scream and scream, a howl of defiance and grief and pure, mechanical power. I would show them. I would show the wilderness, the fog, the unseen savages, the manager, all of it. I would get this wreck of a tin-pot steamer up the river. I had to. The truth was not a thing to be found; it was a thing to be confronted. And Kurtz was there. I had to hear the voice. That had become the whole point. The ivory, the pilgrims, the company, it was all a sideshow. There was only the river, the fog, and the promise of a voice that had something to say.

And so I steamed on, with a dead man at my feet and his blood on my shoes, towards a man who was perhaps more dead than he, a voice that was waiting to pronounce its own epitaph. The pilgrims huddled behind me, their fear a palpable thing, a foul smell in the air. The manager had retreated to the far side of the deck, staring into the fog as if he could will Kurtz’s station into existence. The black crewmen, my crewmen, worked in silence, their faces grim, occasionally glancing at the body of their fallen comrade and then at me, at the helm, as if to gauge my resolve. I did not know what they saw. I only knew what I felt: a strange, exhilarating clarity. The last vestiges of the mundane world had been stripped away. There were no more laws, no more rules, just the river, the steam, and the will to go on. The fog was no longer a shroud; it was a veil, and I was determined to tear it aside, no matter what horror lay on the other side. The horror. It was not just waiting up the river. It had boarded the steamer. It was at the helm.

The paddle-wheels churned, their slaps and gasps muffled by the fog, but the boat was moving with more purpose, pushing harder against the current. The silence from the banks continued. The world was reduced to the throb of the engine, the groan of the rudder chain, and the small, circumscribed space of the steamer’s deck. The headman had resumed his post near me, not speaking, just being there, a dark, solid presence in the universal flux. His proximity was a strange comfort. It was he who saw it first. He made a low noise, not of alarm, but of interest, and pointed. I followed his gesture. For a moment, I saw nothing but the usual swirling whiteness. Then, a subtle change. The fog was thinning, not lifting, but becoming translucent in one area off the port bow. It was like a cataract dissolving from an eye. They had been placed on the stakes, each one impaled from below, looking out from their high perch with an air of profound, eternal melancholy. Their faces were turned away from the river, towards the house, as if they were its guardians, or its congregation. I swung the glasses along the line. There were perhaps a dozen of them. Even at this distance, I could see the expression on one of them, the mouth drawn back in a rictus of agony or ecstasy, it was impossible to tell. They were not the heads of enemies. ‘Good God,’ a voice croaked behind me. The manager had taken his own pair of glasses. ‘The man is utterly mad.’

The arrangement was too deliberate, too…artistic. It was a statement. This was Kurtz’s postscript to the wilderness. He had taken its heart of darkness and put it on public display. The silence from the banks now had a new meaning. It was not the silence of ambush. It was the silence of desolation. There was no one left to scream.

‘We are going in,’ I said, my voice a stranger to my own ears. The decision was made not by reason, but by a deeper, more compelling instinct. I had to see. I had to know the man who could do this, who could turn human heads into decorative fence-posts and still command, I was sure of it, the fealty of the very wilderness that had produced them. The horror of it was not a deterrent; it was a magnet. It pulled me forward with the force of a revelation.

We were no longer creeping; we were charging, straight towards that ghastly, ornamented clearing. The manager yelped something, a protest, a warning, I did not care which. And I had a terrible, thrilling suspicion that his judgment, and the wilderness’s, and my own, were now one and the same.

The steamboat shuddered, a tin-pot kettle boiling over with a fury it was never designed to contain. We were eating up the distance. The ripples I had seen earlier were now a distinct churn of water near a crude landing stage, a flimsy structure of logs and planks that looked as if the first good push of the current would sweep it away. And the heads, they grew clearer, more horribly detailed. They were not fresh; the sun and the rain had done their work, leaving them drawn and shrunken, with an expression of agonized surprise fixed upon them for all eternity. They were ranged in a semi-circle, their vacant sockets turned towards the main building, a long, low clay structure with a sagging veranda. The station itself was a revelation of decay. The jungle was not so much held at bay as it was invited in; creepers coiled around the posts of the veranda like green serpents, and a section of the thatched roof had caved in, leaving a dark, gaping wound open to the sky. The station was not a fortress; it was a shrine, and those silent sentinels on the fence were its votaries, or perhaps its sacrifices.

Then I saw a man. He emerged from the shadow of the main building and walked slowly towards the landing stage. He was a white man, which was a shock in itself, and he was alone. As we drew nearer, his figure resolved itself into a picture of such fantastic incongruity that for a moment I felt the dream-sensation of my fevered days return with overwhelming force. He was a scarecrow of a man, tall and gaunt, dressed in clothes that were a carnival of patches. His jacket, originally of some stout material, was a mosaic of colours and textures; his trousers were ragged at the knee, and on his head he wore a cap that might once have belonged to a ship’s officer, but was now stained and misshapen. He waved at us, a wide, enthusiastic gesture, as if we were a pleasure-boat arriving for a regatta. There was no menace in him, only a frantic, joyous energy that was more unnerving than any show of hostility.

‘You see,’ the manager spluttered, pointing a trembling finger. ‘One of his men. A deserter, no doubt. A scoundrel. We shall have him put in irons.’ He seemed to find a sliver of comfort in the thought, a return to the familiar world of rules and punishments.

I cut the engines, and the sudden silence was as profound as the earlier noise. The steamboat, its momentum spent, drifted sideways with the current, nudging gently against the flimsy jetty. The patched man stood on the bank, his face alight with a kind of holy ecstasy. He was young, younger than me, with a wisp of fair beard and wide, blue, utterly mad eyes. He looked like a runaway apprentice, a novice monk who had bolted from his cloister and found a new, more terrible faith in the wilderness.

‘He is magnificent,’ the young man called out, his voice thin but clear across the water. ‘You have come to see him. He has been expecting you.’

The manager puffed himself up. ‘I am the manager of this district,’ he announced, his tone laden with all the pomposity of his office. ‘I have come to relieve the station and receive Mr. Kurtz’s report, and his ivory.’

The man on the bank gave a short, barking laugh. ‘Ivory,’ he said, as if it were the most absurd word he had ever heard. ‘Oh, there is ivory. An immense quantity. But that is nothing. You do not understand. You have come for him. The words. The ideas. He has so much to say.’ He clasped his hands together, his gaze directed not at us but at the ramshackle house behind him, as if he were speaking to an invisible presence there. ‘He has enlarged my mind.’

I threw a line to him, and he caught it with an eager, fumbling motion, making it fast to a post. I stepped ashore, my feet sinking slightly into the soft, muddy bank. The air was thick, heavy with the smell of damp earth, woodsmoke, and something else, something cloying and vaguely sweet, the smell of decay. The pilgrims followed me, clutching their rifles, their eyes darting about nervously, while the manager strode up to the patched man with a show of blustering authority.

‘Who are you? What is your name? You are an employee of the Company, I presume?’ he demanded.

The young man looked at him with an expression of pitying disdain. ‘I am with him,’ he said simply, as if that explained everything. ‘He saved me. I was shipwrecked up-river. My captain was a Dane. ‘Adore him?’ the manager scoffed. ‘That uproar this morning, was that adoration? We thought we were being attacked.’

A shadow crossed the young man’s face. ‘Ah, that,’ he said, lowering his voice. ‘They do not like to see him go. He was planning to leave, you see. For the good of the Company. To bring his ivory down. But they begged him to stay. Sometimes they get…excited. They do not want him to be taken away. He is their god.’ He looked at us with a sudden, sharp suspicion. ‘You have not come to take him away?’

Before I could answer, a procession emerged from the path leading to the station. It was not a war party. It was something far stranger. A group of dark figures, walking slowly, carried a litter, a crude stretcher made of poles and animal hides. On the stretcher lay a man. My heart, which had been beating with a dull, steady rhythm of dread, gave a violent leap. The figures moved with a somnambulistic grace, their faces impassive, their bodies adorned with strange fetish objects, bits of bone, twists of wire, and coloured rags. They were not the muscular, glossy-skinned men of my crew; they were gaunt, their ribs showing, their eyes burning with a feverish light in their dark faces. And as they drew nearer, the silence was broken by a sound, a low, collective hum that seemed to be the source of the very vibration in the air.

‘They are bringing him out to greet you,’ the patched man whispered, his voice trembling with reverence. ‘He is very ill. The wilderness has…it has taken a toll. But his spirit, his spirit is immense.’

The manager and the pilgrims recoiled as the procession drew level with us. They stopped, a respectful distance away, and lowered the stretcher to the ground. And there he was. He was little more than a skeleton, a frame draped in skin the colour of old ivory. His head was bald, a dome of bone that gleamed in the hazy light. His arms, lying outside the blanket that covered him, were mere sticks. But the life in him was concentrated in his eyes, which were enormous, dark, and luminous with a terrifying intensity, and in his voice. For he spoke, and the sound of it silenced every other thought. It was not loud, but it had a depth, a resonance, that seemed to come from the very depths of the earth.

‘Let them come no closer,’ the voice commanded, and it was not a request. It was a decree. The bearers, who had been looking at us with a blank, animal curiosity, instantly became still as statues.

The manager, recovering a fraction of his courage, took a hesitant step forward. ‘Mr. Kurtz, I presume? I am the manager…’

The head on the litter turned slowly, and the great, cavernous eyes fixed upon him. A faint, ironic smile touched the bloodless lips. ‘So you are the manager,’ the deep voice rumbled. ‘You have come to see the results of my method.’ He made a slight, almost imperceptible gesture with one skeletal hand towards the fence posts. ‘There is my postscript to your instructions. An expression of the ultimate sincerity.’

The manager stammered, his face turning a mottled purple. ‘This is…this is irregular. Unspeakable. The heads of rebels…’

‘Rebels?’ the voice of Kurtz echoed, imbued with a profound and chilling amusement. ‘There are no rebels here. There is only my will. Those were men who lacked…restraint. An essential quality. I have supplied it for them.’

I stood apart, a silent witness to this incredible scene. I looked from the shrivelled heads on the poles to the living head on the stretcher, and it seemed to me that they were all part of the same terrible truth. This was the man who had written of the brute force that must be used in the cause of progress, the man whose eloquence had so impressed the highest councils in Europe. And here he was, the logical conclusion of his own philosophy, a disembodied voice ruling over a kingdom of decay and death. He had not been conquered by the wilderness; he had married it, consummated a union with its dark heart, and this station was the issue of that marriage.

The patched man, the frantic acolyte, knelt by the side of the stretcher, not daring to touch his master but gazing at him with an expression of absolute devotion. ‘They have come with the steamer,’ he said softly. ‘You can go home now. You can be cured.’

Kurtz’s eyes flickered towards him, and for a moment a look of something like affection, or perhaps just weariness, softened their terrible fire. ‘Home,’ he murmured, and the word sounded alien, a syllable from a forgotten language. ‘I have no home. My home is here.’ His gaze shifted, sweeping over the faces of the pilgrims, who shrank from it as from a physical blow. Then his eyes found me. They paused, and in their depths I saw not madness, but a terrifying lucidity, a recognition. It was as if he had been waiting for me, specifically for me, all this time. He saw, I am certain of it, the kinship between the darkness that had consumed him and the darkness that I had felt stirring in myself all the way up this river.

‘You,’ he said, the voice gathering a scrap of its former power. ‘You are the sailor. The one who brought this heap of scrap iron up my river.’ He seemed to look right through me, to the very marrow of my bones. ‘You have seen things. The river has spoken to you.’

I could only nod, my throat suddenly dry.

‘And what did it say?’ he pressed, a faint, ghastly smile playing on his lips. ‘Did it whisper of profits? Of civilizing missions? Of progress?’

‘It whispered of you,’ I said, the words coming out before I had time to consider them.

His smile widened. It was not a pleasant sight. It was the grin of a skull. ‘Ah,’ he breathed. ‘Then you have understood. You are a man to whom I can speak.’ He tried to raise himself on one elbow, a movement of immense and painful effort. ‘The others,’ he said, with a gesture of contempt that encompassed the manager and his coterie, ‘they are fools. They have no imagination. They see the surface, the ivory, the…abominations. They do not see the truth. The horror.’ He fell back, exhausted, his breath coming in ragged gasps. The patched man fussed over him, trying to rearrange his blanket.

‘Take him inside,’ I said to the bearers, my voice sounding harsh and unfamiliar. ‘He must be put to bed.’

My sudden assumption of command seemed to startle everyone. The manager opened his mouth to protest, to reassert his authority, but no words came out. The pilgrims just stared. The bearers, however, looked from me to Kurtz, and then, after a moment’s hesitation, they obeyed. They lifted the stretcher and, with that same slow, dignified pace, carried their living god back into the gloom of his dilapidated palace. The patched man followed them like a faithful dog.

We were left standing on the riverbank, the manager, the pilgrims, and I. The immediate spell was broken, but the air was still charged with the presence we had just witnessed. The silence that descended was heavier, more meaningful, than before. It was the silence of a mystery that, having been partially revealed, had only become deeper and more appalling.

‘The man is mad,’ the manager finally managed to say, wiping his sweaty brow with a handkerchief. ‘Completely, dangerously mad. And the ivory… my God, the ivory must be immense. We must secure it at once. That is our first duty.’

I looked at him, at his round, foolish face, his eyes gleaming with greed even in the shadow of such a horror, and I felt a wave of profound, bottomless contempt. He was the true savage, the one who could stand in the heart of a great and terrible mystery and see only the potential for profit. He was blind and deaf to the voice that had spoken to me, the voice that had whispered from the jungle, from the fog, from the shrunken heads on the poles, and finally, from the lips of Kurtz himself. He saw the madness, but he could not see the truth it contained, the awful, universal truth about what lies hidden in the heart of a man when he is stripped of all the familiar supports of law, society, and opinion. I had journeyed all this way not for ivory, not for the Company, but for this. To hear a voice. And I had heard it. And I knew, with a certainty that chilled me to the bone, that I would never be able to un-hear it. The rest of my life would be a mere echo of that resonant, terrible whisper from the stretcher. From the edge of the clearing, just where the jungle wall seemed to pause before devouring the last vestiges of man’s endeavour, I thought I saw a flicker of movement. It was not a man, not an animal, but something of the shape and colour of a shadow detaching itself from other shadows, a darker patch in the universal gloom. The mystery of the land was not in its immensity, but in its intimacy; it did not ignore you, it enveloped you, it breathed on you, it waited for you to make a mistake.

I turned at last and walked, not towards the station house where the sounds of avarice were already beginning to rise, an exclamation of wonder, the thud of a dropped tusk, but towards the hut that served as Kurtz’s throne room and, I suspected, his prison. The two bearers stood outside, motionless, like caryatids of ebony supporting a roof of invisible despair. They did not look at me as I approached, their gaze fixed on some point in the far distance, or perhaps the near future. I pushed aside the ragged blanket that served as a door and stepped into the gloom.

A single candle, stuck in the neck of a bottle, threw a struggling, uncertain light. It was a scene of fantastic squalor. The place was a store-room, a study, a bedroom, and a tomb all at once. In one corner, piled nearly to the ceiling, was a hoard of ivory, tusks of all sizes, a pale, curving, spectral treasure gleaming in the weak light. The pilgrims had evidently missed this private cache. But it was not the ivory that held my attention. It was the papers. They were everywhere: scattered on a rough wooden table, piled in a corner, weighted down with a revolver. Sheets upon sheets of close, frantic script, a testament to an unhinged and eloquent mind. It was his report for the ‘Society for the Suppression of Savage Customs.’ I saw a page that had come loose, and read by the flickering light a single, scrawled sentence at the very end, a postscript written in a shaky hand, utterly at odds with the flowing, confident script of the preceding pages: ‘Exterminate all the brutes!’

Kurtz himself lay on a low pallet, a heap of blankets from which his head emerged like a skull from a grave. His eyes were open and burning with a feverish light. The patched man, the harlequin, was there, kneeling beside him, attempting to coax a few drops of water between his cracked lips. He looked up as I entered, his boyish face a mask of anxiety and fear.

‘He is very bad,’ he whispered, scrambling to his feet. ‘The journey…the excitement…it was too much. You must be quiet.’

‘I came to see,’ I said, my own voice a low murmur.

Kurtz’s eyes found me in the gloom. A flicker of recognition, of intelligence, of something more, a kind of desperate camaraderie, passed over his features. ‘They…are stealing my ivory,’ he whispered, the sound a dry rustle in his throat.

‘It is the Company’s ivory,’ I said, for what reason I do not know. Perhaps to test him, perhaps merely to state a fact in a world that had dissolved into nightmare.

A faint, terrible smile touched his lips. ‘The Company. Ah, yes. The pious ceremonies of bookkeeping. But the ivory is mine. I won it. I suffered for it. I… I am it.’ He seemed to lose the thread of his thought, his gaze wandering to the ceiling of thatch, as if he could see through it to the stars he had meant to conquer. ‘My Intended,’ he murmured. ‘My station. My ideas. It all had to be…perfect. A light. A beacon.’ His voice gained a sudden, surprising strength, a resonance that filled the small hut. ‘And I was that light!’

The harlequin wrung his hands. ‘He talks like this for hours. The wilderness has found him out, you see. It has whispered things to him that a man should not hear. And he listened. I tried to get him away. But he would not leave his work.’

‘His work?’ I asked, looking around at the squalor, at the pile of bones, no, tusks, gleaming like a heap of dead men’s limbs.

‘The enlightenment,’ the Russian said, with a simplicity that was more shocking than any cynicism. ‘He was bringing the light. They adored him.’

‘Adored him enough to put shrunken heads on poles,’ I muttered.

‘A question of method,’ the harlequin insisted, his face earnest. ‘You don’t understand. He had to be severe. There were rival factions. It was a matter of political necessity. He was forming a society. A new world.’

A shadow fell across the doorway. The manager stood there, his round face slick with sweat, his eyes darting from Kurtz on the pallet to the pile of ivory in the corner. Greed, triumph, and a kind of nervous fear warred in his expression.

‘Ah, here you are,’ he said to me, ignoring the other two. ‘A good haul. A very good haul. More than we could have hoped.’ He took a step inside, his nose wrinkling at the smell. ‘We will begin loading at once. We must leave on the morning tide. The man is a liability.’

Kurtz’s head turned slowly on the pallet. His eyes, sunk deep in their sockets, fixed on the manager with an expression of such pure, unadulterated contempt, such regal and profound disdain, that the manager actually took a step back. For a moment, the dying man on the pile of rags was a king, and the manager was a grubby, trespassing commoner.

‘You are meddling,’ Kurtz breathed, and the word was a condemnation, a sentence. ‘You are meddling with my methods.’

‘Your methods,’ the manager stammered, recovering a little of his bluster, ‘your methods are unsound. Decidedly unsound. The district is a ruin. I shall have to make a full report.’

‘Make your report,’ Kurtz whispered, his eyes closing as if the effort of looking at the man was too much. ‘The world is full of reports. And darkness.’ He fell silent. The candle flame guttered, and for a moment the hut was plunged into almost total blackness. When it flared up again, his face seemed to have shrunk, to have become more skull-like than ever.

We spent the rest of that day and the long, hot hours of the next morning transferring the ivory to the steamer. It was a labour of ants, a Sisyphean task under the blank, white eye of the sun. The pilgrims, spurred on by the manager, worked with a feverish energy, their pyjamas and their sidespring boots stained with the dust of the station. They carried the tusks down the bank, their faces flushed with exertion and a kind of holy ecstasy. They were bearing the cross of profit. The black crew, my cannibals, worked too, but with a different air. They were efficient, silent, their movements economical. They handled the tusks as if they were logs of wood, with no more reverence than they would give to a piece of firewood. They were outside the whole farcical religion of the enterprise.

I divided my time between the engine-room, where I soothed my old boiler with a gentle hand, and the deck, from where I watched the proceedings on shore with a detachment that felt like a new skin. I was a part of it, and yet I was not. I was the ferryman, charged with carrying a cargo of ghosts and their loot away from the land of the dead. All the while, the jungle watched. No one came out, no one made a sound, but you could feel the weight of their gaze. The clearing was an empty stage, but the theatre was packed.

The Russian came to me, furtively, as I was supervising the coiling of a rope. He was terrified. ‘That man,’ he whispered, nodding towards the manager, ‘he means to have me hanged. I know it. I have heard him talking with the others. For being a competitor. For…for knowing too much.’ His eyes were wide with a simple, animal fear. ‘You must help me. You seem to be a man one can trust.’

‘What can I do?’ I asked.

‘Give me some cartridges for my rifle. And a little tobacco. And let me slip away. I will take my chances in the forest. It is better than their justice.’ He looked at me, a young, lost soul in absurdly patched clothes. He was a fool, perhaps, but he was a fool who had loved something, who had admired, who had believed. In the moral desert of that place, it was a kind of virtue.

I went to my cabin and got him what he asked for. He took the items with grateful, trembling hands. ‘You have a good heart,’ he said. ‘Do not let them corrupt you. And listen. Be careful of what he has written.’ He gestured vaguely towards Kurtz’s hut. ‘He has entrusted it all to you. His reputation. His soul. Do not let that fat man get his hands on it. He would not understand.’ He wrung my hand, and then, with a last, scared look over his shoulder, he melted into the scrub at the edge of the river. I never saw him again. I imagine him still, a colourful ghost wandering an immense green wilderness, forever loyal to a memory of a great voice.

By midday, the last of the ivory was aboard. The steamer sat low in the water, weighted down with the price of a soul. Kurtz had been brought out on his stretcher and placed in the pilothouse, which I had cleared for him. It was the only place with a semblance of air, and from it, he could look out over the river. He lay there, silent, a brooding, skeletal presence, his eyes following our movements. The manager wanted to set off at once, his anxiety to be gone a palpable thing. The steam was up. The anchor was weighed. And then, as the first slow turn of the paddle wheels began to churn the brown water, a sound arose from the shore.

It was a cry, not of anger, but of lamentation, of profound and utter bereavement. The clearing, which had been empty for so long, was suddenly filled with people. They emerged from the jungle as if by magic, hundreds of them, warriors with spears and shields, women adorned with brass wire, their bodies gleaming with oil. They stood in a dense, silent throng, their faces turned towards our departing vessel. And at the very front, walking to the water’s edge with a slow, deliberate grace, was a woman.

She was magnificent and terrible. Her arms were covered in bracelets, her proud head held high. She was draped in brilliantly coloured cloths, and her expression was one of inscrutable sorrow and fierce pride. She raised her arms, not in a gesture of threat, but as if in an invocation, a farewell, a curse. The crowd behind her moaned, a low, resonant sound that seemed to make the air itself vibrate. It was the congregation mourning the theft of their god.

The pilgrims on deck panicked. They raised their Winchesters, their faces pale with fear. ‘Don’t shoot,’ I yelled, my voice hoarse. ‘You’ll have them all on us.’

The manager was wringing his hands. ‘Go on, go on,’ he urged me. ‘Faster.’

I pulled the whistle-cord, and a long, piercing blast of steam shrieked across the water, a mechanical scream answering the human one. For a moment, it startled the crowd. They wavered. The woman did not flinch. She simply stood there, her arms outstretched, her eyes fixed on the pilothouse.

And in the pilothouse, a ghost stirred. I saw Kurtz struggling to raise himself on one elbow. He was trying to get to the window, to answer that call. His face, a mask of bone and shadow, was filled with a terrible, conflicting agony. He was being torn apart. One foot in the wilderness, one foot on the deck of my steamer. He looked from the woman on the shore to the river stretching ahead, the path of retreat, of civilization, of forgetting. I saw his mouth open, but no sound came out.

I stepped into the pilothouse. ‘You will be lost,’ I said, my voice quiet. ‘Utterly lost.’

Did he hear me? I don’t know. But he collapsed back onto his pallet, his face turned away from the shore, from his kingdom of darkness. He had made his choice. Or perhaps the last of his strength had simply failed him.

We steamed on, gathering speed. The station, the crowd, the magnificent, savage woman, all grew smaller, receding into the vast, indifferent green of the jungle. The sounds of their sorrow faded, replaced by the rhythmic thump of the engines and the churn of the paddle-wheel. We were escaping. But I had the distinct and unnerving feeling that we were taking the heart of the darkness with us, locked in the pilothouse, breathing its last in faint, feverish whispers about ivory and fame and a fiancée waiting in a city that seemed as unreal as a dream. The river was no longer a path to a goal, but a retreat, a flight from a truth so overwhelming that to look at it for too long was to go mad. We were running away, and the silence of the wilderness, which had been so full of menace on the way up, was now the silence of an accomplice, a conspirator who had shown you a secret and now watched, with a dumb immobility, to see what you would do with it. The horror. It was not on the shore. It was on the boat. And I was its keeper.
