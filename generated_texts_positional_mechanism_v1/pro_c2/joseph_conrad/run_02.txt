He had the chart unrolled on the great mahogany table when I entered, its corners weighted with what looked to be fossilised eggs of a strange, polished grey. The room smelled of old paper and beeswax, and a single lamp threw a cone of buttery light onto the vast, yellowed expanse, leaving the rest of the study in a profound and expectant gloom. He did not look up at once, but continued to trace a faint coastline with the tip of his forefinger, a digit gnarled and stained as if it had been dipped in walnut ink.

‘When I first laid eyes on her,’ he began, his voice a low burr that seemed to emanate from the very floorboards, ‘she was being used to wrap fish in a market in some forgotten port. A crime, you see. A desecration.’ His finger paused over a spidery archipelago. ‘I had been searching for a true rendering of these particular shoals, a thing of myth among my kind. I had read of it in the journals of a long dead captain, a man who went mad with certainty. In his own script’ (he said it “screept”) ‘he wrote, “‘Twas not a map I made, but the very soul of the sea I ensnared.’” He gave a dry little cough, a sound like a dead leaf skittering across pavement, and finally raised his eyes to meet mine. They were the colour of a storm-clouded sea, and just as unreadable. ‘So you see. For a few worthless coins, I had it. The thing I had chased across half the world; the thing that had ruined a better man than I. For myself.’ He straightened up and began to fill a small, blackened clay pipe, his movements deliberate and silent. Tamping the tobacco with his thumb, he fixed me with a look of shrewd inquiry.

‘Yes, my young friend. That day, my quest was over; my greatest rival had been proven a fool; I was hale, I was known in every port; I had camaraderie; I had the esteem’ (he pronounced it “esteem”) ‘of my fellows, a ship under my feet, to make my life seem a very solid thing, and then the ghost I had been chasing was mine to hold too!’ He struck a lucifer against the underside of the table; it erupted with a sulfurous hiss. His severe, weathered face seemed to flicker. ‘Quest, rival, ship,’ he said slowly, staring into the tiny, angry flame, ‘pfft!’ The match was pinched out between his fingers. He gave a sigh and turned back to the chart. The brittle paper seemed to tremble in the still air, as if the ghost of that long dead captain had been stirred by his breath.

‘The coast,’ he said abruptly, gesturing at the intricate lines, and in his usual quiet, measured tone, ‘is making its argument. I have been this particular passage interrogating. . . . So! And what is your squall warning?’

‘To tell you the truth, Kaelen,’ I said, my own voice sounding thin in the heavy silence, ‘I came here to interrogate a passage myself. . . .’

‘A channel?’ he asked, with a kind of cynical curiosity.

‘Nothing so clear,’ I answered, suddenly feeling the immense weight of my own foolish errand. ‘A man!’

‘Ach, so!’ he breathed, and the hard lines of his face softened into something like pity. He looked at me for a long moment before he spoke again, slowly. ‘Well, I am a man too.’

There you have Kaelen as I knew him; he understood how to offer just enough sympathy to make an earnest man feel his own simplicity; but if I wavered in my purpose it was not for long.

He listened to my whole sorry tale without a word. He sat with one leg thrown over the other, the lamplight catching the worn leather of his boots, and from time to time he would draw on his pipe, exhaling a plume of smoke so dense it seemed to possess its own substance, a solid thing that obscured him entirely. When I had finished, he set the pipe down in a ceramic dish, leaned forward until his face was once more in the bright circle of the lamp, his elbows on his knees, his stained fingers steepled before him.

‘I see it very well. He is adrift.’

He had named the condition for me, and I was for a moment startled by its plainness; and indeed the whole setting had the air of a visit to a ship’s surgeon, Kaelen, with his charts and his air of grim knowledge; I, the nervous sailor, presenting some unnameable malady, so that it seemed quite natural to ask,

‘What is the cure for it?’

He raised a long forefinger. ‘There is only one chart! One thing alone can from our own shoals save us!’ The finger came down and tapped the paper map. The problem which he had made to seem so plain before became at once plainer, and yet utterly insoluble. There was a pause. ‘Yes,’ said I, ‘but to put it plainly, the question is not how to be saved, but how to sail.’

He nodded his head, a gesture of profound weariness. ‘Aye. Aye. In the words of your great navigator: That is the heading. . . .’ He continued to nod, as if to a rhythm only he could hear. . . . ‘How to steer! Ach! How to steer.’

He rose and rested his fingertips on the chart. ‘We want in so many ways to steer,’ he began. ‘This little fish is content with his patch of reef; but man he will never in his safe harbour stay put. He want to go north to the ice, and he want to go south to the sun. . . .’ He moved his hand one way, then the other. . . . ‘He wants to be an admiral, and he wants to be a pirate, and in the dead of night, in the lonely watch, he sees himself as a master mariner, so skilled as he can never be. . . . In a fantasy. . . .’

He rolled the chart carefully, the dry paper whispering, and slid it into a long leather tube. It had a strange finality, as if he had just sealed a man’s fate within it. His tall frame, deprived of its central purpose, seemed to fade as he moved out of the lamp’s bright ring into the darker space near the shelves; his voice, when it came from that dimness where he fumbled with unseen objects, was no longer sharp, seemed to gain a new resonance from the distance.

‘And because you cannot always keep your charts unrolled there is the true storm, the soul’s tempest, the world’s gale. I tell you, my friend, it is not good for you to find you cannot weather the storm, for the reason that your ship not strong enough is, or your crew not loyal enough. . . . Ja! . . . And all the time you are such a master mariner too! Wie? Was? God in Heaven! How can that be? Ha! ha! ha!’

The shadow moving amongst the ghosts of old voyages laughed, a harsh, grating sound.

‘Yes! Very funny this brutal thing is. A man that is born is set adrift on an ocean like a man who falls overboard in a storm. If he tries to climb back on the deck as a landsman might try to do, he is dashed against the hull, nicht wahr? . . . No! I tell you! The way is to the indifferent ocean surrender yourself, and with the frantic kicking of your own feet in the water make the cold, cold sea keep you afloat. So if you ask me, how to steer?’

His voice rose with a sudden, astonishing power, as though some truth had whispered to him there in the darkness. ‘I will tell you! For that too there is only one way.’

With a soft scrape of a boot on the floorboards he emerged from the gloom and stood again at the edge of the light. He held out the leather tube, aimed at my chest like a spyglass; his shadowed eyes seemed to see right through my flimsy composure, but his lips, set in a hard line, said nothing, and the austere conviction I had heard in his voice seemed to drain away from his face. The hand holding the tube lowered, and after a moment, he took a step closer and laid a hand on my arm. There were truths, he said, his voice now quiet and full of sorrow, that perhaps could not be charted, only he had sailed so long alone that he sometimes forgot, he forgot. The lamplight had dissolved the certainty that the shadows had given him. He sat down and, with his head in his hands, rubbed his temples. ‘And yet it is so, it is so. In the indifferent ocean immerse.’ . . . He spoke in a low murmur, not looking at me. ‘That was the course. To follow the heading, and again to follow the heading, and so, ewig, usque ad finem. . . .’ The whisper of his belief seemed to reveal before me a vast and chartless sea, a grey horizon at dusk, or was it, perchance, at the first hint of dawn? One lacked the courage to be certain; but it was a beautiful and treacherous light, casting the impalpable poesy of its dimness over hidden reefs, over scuttled ships. His own life had begun in ambition, in a passion for discovery; he had navigated very far, by many routes, by strange stars, and whatever heading he had followed it had been without hesitation, and therefore without shame and without regret. In that much he was right. That was the course, no doubt. Yet for all that, the great ocean on which men drift between reefs and wrecks remained utterly desolate under the impalpable poesy of its grey light, darkest in the centre, ringed with a terrible brightness as if surrounded by a world on fire. When at last I found my voice it was to suggest that no one could have a clearer heading than himself.

He shook his head slowly, and then looked at me with a patient and questioning gaze. It

was the kind of gaze that does not so much question your words as the very ground upon which you stand to speak them, and I felt for a moment the disconcerting sensation of a man who, having ventured an opinion on solid earth, finds himself suddenly afloat.

‘No,’ he said, and the word was not a contradiction but a gentle correction, a shifting of a chart by a fraction of a degree. ‘Not romantic. That is the point. That is where the danger lies for your . . . for your specimen. He is one of us, you see, but he has not learned the way. He has fallen into the sea, as I said, but he thinks the sea is a dream of his own making. He thinks he can command the waves because he has a great idea of how the sailing should be done. Ach! That is not how it is.’

He leaned back, the old wicker of his chair groaning a soft complaint, and picked up his cold pipe from the desk. ‘A man may follow a dream,’ he went on, tamping the bowl with a thoughtful thumb, ‘but the romantic, he thinks he *is* the dream. He sees himself as the hero in a tale, and when the tale goes wrong, when the world, which is a very rough and dirty place, does not behave like a well written story, he is . . . lost. He is offended. It is a personal insult to him, you understand? He has been betrayed not by his own weakness, or by the cruelty of other men, but by the universe itself.’ He made a small, eloquent gesture with the pipe stem, as if dismissing a preposterous argument. ‘And the universe, my friend, it does not care for our opinion of its story. It has its own business to attend to.’

‘So what is he to do, Stein?’ I asked, my voice betraying a weariness that went deeper than the late hour. ‘If the dream is a lie and the world is a betrayal, what is left for a man like that?’

‘A lie?’ he murmured, his eyes widening a little as if I had said something truly shocking. ‘Who has said it is a lie? I did not say that. The dream is not a lie. The dream is true. The dream is the most true thing there is. It is the sea. The trouble is that your friend wants to walk upon it, as if he were a god in a book. But he is a man. And a man must swim. That is all.’ He paused, and a faint, sad smile touched his lips. ‘And sometimes, the swimming is not a glorious thing. Sometimes it is only a desperate kicking in the dark to keep your nose above the water. There is no glory. There is only the water, and the breath in your lungs.’

He seemed to sink into his thoughts, his gaze fixed on some point in the shadows beyond the lamplight, a place crowded, no doubt, with the ghosts of his own desperate moments. The silence in the room grew heavy, thick with the faint scent of camphor and dead things, with the weight of all the unspoken histories pinned behind the glass of his collection. I had come for a remedy, for a clear course to plot, and he had given me an ocean.

‘You showed me the butterfly,’ I said at last, gesturing towards the corner where he had put away that perfect, iridescent marvel. ‘A dream you caught. A moment of perfection. Is that not what he is seeking? What you yourself once had?’

For a long time he did not answer. He rose from his chair without a sound, a tall, stooping shadow, and moved with that curious, gliding step of his towards a far wall, a wall I had not much noticed, lined not with the brilliant lepidoptera but with squat, dark-wood cabinets, all meticulously labelled with neat, spidery script. He did not need the lamp, it seemed; his hands knew their way in this mausoleum of his passions. I heard a small key turn in a lock, the quiet scrape of a drawer. He returned to the circle of light not with a flash of colour, but with a small, black case, no bigger than my palm, made of some polished, funereal wood like ebony.

He placed it on the desk between us, his long fingers resting on the lid. ‘You think of that butterfly,’ he said, his voice very low. ‘And I too, I think of it. It was a day. Yes. A day when the world gives you everything. But a man’s life is not made of one day, or one butterfly. A man’s life is made of all the days. The good, and the bad. The perfect specimens, and . . . this.’

With a slow, deliberate movement, he lifted the lid. Inside, on a bed of faded velvet, was a beetle. It was a stag beetle, of a kind I had never seen, large and formidable. Its carapace was the colour of jet, but with a strange, oily sheen that caught the light in shifting patterns of deep blue and violet, like a patch of oil on a black puddle. Its great mandibles were curved and wicked, branching near the tips like the antlers of some miniature, monstrous deer. It was a magnificent creature, a thing of darkness and armour, a perfect embodiment of brutal, chitinous strength. Yet it was not perfect. On the left side, one of the great mandibles was flawed. Near its base, a small but undeniable splinter of the horn was broken off, leaving a rough, pale scar against the polished black. And one of its legs, the middle one on the same side, was missing entirely, the neat, articulated socket empty.

‘It is *Lucanus-imperator*,’ Stein said, his voice a reverent whisper. ‘The Emperor. For thirty years, it was thought to be only a legend, a tale told by the Dyak hunters in the deep interior. A beetle so large it could snap a man’s finger. A ghost of the deep forest. The professor in Europe, he laughed at the idea. He wrote a paper, a very clever paper, on the credulity of native populations. But I had heard the stories. I had seen a drawing once, scratched on a piece of bark. I knew. I knew it was there.’

He looked up from the case, and his eyes had a fire in them that was not merely a reflection of the lamp. It was the old fire of the chase, the fever of the collector. ‘I was not the only one. There was another, a German named Schultz. A good man, in his way. Very determined. Very correct. He had more money, a better expedition. We were rivals, but in that place . . . in that green hell, there are not enough white men to be rivals for long. We became friends. The way men do when they are the only two people for a hundred miles who remember what a paved street looks like. We shared our quinine. We hunted together. And we both hunted for this.’ He tapped the black case.

‘For a year we were in the interior. A year of fever, and leeches, and bad water. We lost our porters. We were near to death more than once. And all the time, we were looking. Every night we would light the lamps to draw the moths, and we would search. In the day, we would strip the bark from rotten trees. Nothing. The legend remained a legend. Schultz, he began to doubt. He said we were chasing a phantom. But I . . . I could feel it. I knew it was near.’

He fell silent, and in the quiet I could almost hear the maddening drone of the jungle, feel the suffocating heat. His face, usually so placid, was tense with the memory.

‘Then one night, there was a great storm. The heavens opened. A tree fell on our hut, a small thing, but it sent us out into the rain. And in the lightning, for just a second, I saw it. On the trunk of a great ironwood tree. It was crawling, very slow. Majestic. Gott im Himmel! It was blacker than the night, and the rain on its back shone like jewels. I had him.’

His hand, lying on the desk, clenched into a fist. ‘I shouted. I do not know what I shouted. I ran to the tree. And then I heard another shout. Not of triumph. A shout of pain. It was Schultz. The branch that had hit our hut, it had pinned his leg. A bad break, I could see it at once. He was in the mud, the water rising around him. And he was looking at me, and then looking at the tree where the beetle was. I saw the look in his eyes. He knew. In that moment, we were not friends. We were rivals again, to the death.’

Stein took a deep breath, a long, shuddering sigh that seemed to drain the energy from him. ‘Here is the moment, you see. The moment for the romantic. He sees the prize. The thing he has dreamed of, suffered for. It is the justification for everything. A perfect specimen. An immortal fame. He would take the beetle. Of course. What is a friend’s broken leg compared to a legend in your hand? The story demands it. The dream must be made real, complete, perfect.’

He looked at me intently. ‘But I am not a romantic. I am a man who has learned to swim in the sea. The sea that night was a storm, and a fallen tree, and my friend with his leg broken and the bone showing white in the dark. That was the water I was in. So . . . I went to Schultz. I left the beetle on the tree. We worked for an hour, with a knife and a piece of wood, to get him free. I thought my heart would break, hearing him groan. And all the time, I could see the beetle. It was crawling up the tree, very slow. Towards the darkness. Towards escape.’

‘When Schultz was free, when I had bandaged his leg as best I could, I went back to the tree. It was almost gone, high up. I had to climb. The bark was wet, slippery. I was in a hurry. I was a fool. I did not use the net. I used my hand. I was shaking, with the fear and the hurry. I grabbed it.’ He opened his fist, as if re-enacting the moment. ‘It was so strong. Unbelievable. It twisted in my grip. And the mandible, the big horn, it snapped against the wood of the tree. I felt it break. I felt a piece of my dream break in my hand. And in the struggle, its leg . . . it came off. Phoo. Gone in the mud.’

He looked down at the flawed creature in its velvet-lined coffin. ‘So. I had him. But he was not perfect. I had saved my friend, but I had damaged my prize. Schultz, he lived. I got him back to the coast. He was grateful. He said I was a good man. He said I had sacrificed my ambition for our friendship. He was a romantic, you see. He made it into a fine story. But it was not a sacrifice. What choice was there? A man drowns, you pull him out. That is all. You do not think about the beautiful shell you were about to pick up. You just do it. You immerse yourself in the thing that is happening. You do what a man has to do.’

He closed the lid of the black case with a soft, final click. The flawed Emperor was hidden once more. ‘This beetle,’ he said, his voice now quiet and even, ‘is more valuable to me than the butterfly. The butterfly is a memory of a perfect day. A gift. This . . . this is a memory of a choice. It is the thing I have. Not the perfect thing I dreamed of. The real thing that I got, with my own hands, in the mud and the rain. It is broken, and incomplete. Like me. Like you. Like every man who has lived a life and not just a dream.’

He pushed the case towards me across the polished surface of the desk. ‘Your friend,’ he said, with an emphasis that was both gentle and immensely sad. ‘He wants the butterfly. The perfect specimen of his honour. He wants to be the hero he sees in his dream. But he has fallen into the sea. And the sea has damaged him. It has broken a piece of his soul. He cannot accept it. He wants to pretend he is still the perfect, unbroken thing he was in his dream. And so he will drown. Because he is trying to climb out of the water, instead of learning to swim with what is broken.’

‘So the only remedy,’ I said, understanding at last, my heart sinking with the weight of this knowledge, ‘is to . . . accept the damage? To live as the flawed specimen?’

‘To live,’ he corrected me, his forefinger rising and falling in that familiar gesture. ‘Just to live. Not as the flawed specimen. As a man. A man who has been in a storm. The flaw is not the man. The flaw is the proof that he has lived. The scar is the story. Your friend must learn to look at his own soul the way I look at this beetle. Not with shame at what is broken, but with . . . with a kind of courage. The courage to say: This is what the sea has left of me. And I am still here. I am still swimming.’

He stood up and carried the black case back to its cabinet on the far wall, his tall form once again dissolving into the productive gloom. His voice came out of the shadows, no longer the voice of a philosopher, but the simple, weary voice of an old man who had seen too much. ‘Tell him that. Tell your specimen . . . tell the poor boy that Stein, who is a man too, has a collection of broken things. Tell him it is a very fine collection.’

He did not come back into the light. I sat for a while longer, alone in the bright circle of the lamp, the silence of the great house pressing in on me. The air was still, but the vast and uncertain expanse he had spoken of was no longer a distant horizon; it was all around me, a chartless sea of doubt and difficult choices. He had given me no simple cure, no easy heading to follow. He had given me only a story about a broken beetle, and the terrible, liberating truth that in the destructive element, survival itself was the only form of perfection. I rose, and quietly let myself out, leaving him there in the darkness, the prowling shadow amongst the graves of his dreams, both the perfect and the damned. The great plain on which men wander remained as desolate as before, but it was no longer empty. It was filled with the quiet, stubborn courage of broken things.

He wants to be what he is not. And the world, my friend, the world is a very poor place for such ambitions. It has a way of breaking the wings of even the most magnificent specimens.’ He sighed, a profound and gentle sound that seemed to rise from the very depths of his experience. ‘It is not a crime to be so. Perhaps it is a kind of nobility. But it is a great, great misfortune.’

I took my leave of him not long after, feeling the weight of his words as a physical burden. The gate of his compound clicked shut behind me, a small, final sound in the immense quiet of the tropical night, and I was alone again with the immense, unsolved problem of Jim. Stein’s house, a sanctuary of ordered shadows and philosophic gloom, receded behind a wall of dark foliage, and I found myself walking under a sky that was a vast, indifferent canopy of stars. They burned with a cold, remote fire, their light too ancient and too distant to illuminate the crooked path at my feet. The air, heavy with the scent of unseen blossoms and the faint, miasmic odour of the sleeping town, was a tangible element; it seemed to press upon me, a warm and humid breath that promised no relief.

Stein had given me a diagnosis, a name for the ailment, but the remedy he proposed was a thing of terrifying subtlety. ‘In the destructive element immerse.’ The words echoed in my mind, a Delphic pronouncement that was at once a command and a riddle. It was the wisdom of a man who had made his peace with the sea, who had learned to draw his breath in the very medium of his drowning. But how was one to translate this profound and terrible secret to another? How could I, a mere messenger, carry this chalice of bitter knowledge to a man like Jim, who thirsted only for the clear, sweet water of redemption? He wanted a plank, a spar, a solid piece of wreckage to which he could cling; Stein was offering him the abyss itself.

My steps led me, as if by their own volition, through the deserted streets towards the waterfront, to the hotel where he had his rooms. A single lamp burned in his window on an upper floor, a solitary point of yellow light in the great, dark block of the building. It seemed to me a beacon of his own sleeplessness, a testament to the ceaseless vigil he kept over his own tarnished honour. He would be there, I knew, pacing the narrow confines of his room, a prisoner of his own past, waiting for a word, a sign, a key that would unlock the future. And what had I brought him? A handful of shadows, a whisper of metaphysics, the story of a butterfly with perfect wings.

He opened the door before I had knocked, as if he had sensed my approach on the creaking stair. The light from within fell upon his face, and I saw at once that he had not been resting. His fair hair was disordered, his eyes had the wide, strained look of a man who has been staring too long into the dark, and his shirt was open at the throat, as if he were struggling for air. He was a magnificent specimen indeed, even in his distress; a figure of youth and strength made tragic by some invisible flaw in his conception.

‘You have been long,’ he said, and his voice was taut, vibrating with a nervous energy that filled the small room. He stepped back to let me pass, and I saw that the place was in a state of barely controlled chaos. A chart was spread on the table, held down by a book and a coffee cup; his few belongings were not so much unpacked as they were strewn about, the flotsam of a life cast suddenly adrift.

‘I was with a man,’ I began, taking the single chair he gestured me towards. ‘An old friend. A man of some wisdom.’

He did not sit. He began to pace again, from the window to the door, his steps restless and impatient on the bare wooden floor. ‘Wisdom?’ he repeated, and there was a note of scorn in his voice. ‘I have had enough of wisdom. What I need is a chance. Did you ask him? Did you tell him my case?’

‘I told him,’ I said slowly, trying to choose my words, feeling the immense gulf between Stein’s contemplative chamber and this bare, desperate room. ‘I described the specimen, as he would say.’

He stopped his pacing and turned to face me, his body rigid. ‘The specimen?’

‘It is his way of speaking,’ I explained hastily. ‘He is a naturalist. He collects things. Butterflies, beetles... he sees the world in terms of its... its variations. Its unique forms.’

‘And what did he say about this... this specimen?’ The question was a challenge, flung at me across the small space.

I hesitated, the memory of Stein’s quiet, shadowed face before me. ‘He understood,’ I said at last. ‘He understood you very well. He said... he said you are romantic.’

Jim stared at me, his expression unreadable. For a moment, I thought he might laugh. Instead, a faint colour rose in his cheeks. ‘Romantic,’ he echoed, the word a soft exhalation. It was as if I had named a secret and cherished part of himself. He seemed to stand a little taller. ‘Well. And what of it? Is that a crime?’

‘No,’ I said, my heart sinking a little. ‘Not a crime. A... a condition. He said there is only one remedy for it.’

His eyes lit up with a sudden, desperate hope. He took a step towards me, his hands clenched at his sides. ‘A remedy? What is it? For God’s sake, man, tell me. Is there a place I can go? A ship I can get? I would do anything... anything to... to be myself again.’

Here it was, the precipice. I had to deliver the message, the whole of it, or betray the trust of both men. ‘The remedy,’ I said, my voice sounding strange and hollow in my own ears, ‘is not to be cured. The remedy is to... to live with it.’

The hope in his face died as if I had struck him. It was extinguished utterly, leaving behind an expression of blank, uncomprehending shock. ‘Live with it?’ he whispered. ‘Live with... this?’ He gestured vaguely around him, at the room, at himself, at the whole sorry mess of his life. ‘What kind of a remedy is that? That is a death sentence.’

‘No,’ I insisted, leaning forward in my chair. ‘You do not understand. He spoke of a man who falls into the sea. If he tries to climb out, into the air, where he thinks he belongs, he will drown. The way to live... the way to survive... is to submit to the destructive element. To immerse. To learn to swim in it.’

He looked at me as if I had gone mad. His face, which had been pale, was now flushed with a dark, angry red. ‘Swim in it?’ he burst out, his voice rising in outrage. ‘Swim in this filth? Wallow in it? Is that his advice? To accept that I am... that I am what they say I am? A coward? A failure? Never! I will never accept that.’

‘That is not what he meant,’ I tried to argue, but the force of his indignation was like a gale.

‘Then what did he mean?’ he demanded, standing over me, his tall frame radiating a heat of fury and wounded pride. ‘What is all this talk of the sea and drowning? It is just words. Poetry! I am a plain man. I made a mistake. A terrible, unforgivable mistake. But I am not that mistake. I am not... broken.’ He spat the word out as if it were poison. ‘I need a way to prove it. Not a lecture on how to accept it.’

He turned away from me and went to the window, staring out into the blackness of the harbour. The silence that fell was more terrible than his anger. I could see the rigid set of his shoulders, the tension in his whole body. He was a man on a rack of his own making, and I, with my borrowed wisdom, had only tightened the screws. I felt a profound sense of failure, of my own utter inadequacy. Stein’s philosophy, so luminous in the scholarly dusk of his study, seemed here, in the raw presence of Jim’s pain, a cruel and elaborate sophistry.

When he finally spoke, his voice was different. The anger was gone, replaced by a cold, hard resolution that was somehow more alarming. ‘He is wrong, your wise man,’ he said, without turning around. ‘There is another way. Not to immerse. Not to accept. But to go so far away that the sea itself cannot find you. To find a place where the dream is not a memory, but a possibility. A place for a fresh start.’

He turned back to face me, and in the lamplight his eyes had a strange, feverish glitter. He was no longer the despairing man I had found upon my arrival. A new energy, a new purpose, had taken hold of him. It was the terrible, unquenchable energy of the romantic.

‘I have an offer,’ he said, his voice low and quick. ‘From a man I met here. A trader. He needs an agent for a post up-country. A place so remote they hardly know the King’s name. A place called Patusan.’ He spoke the name as if it were a spell, a word of power. ‘It is a forgotten corner of the world. A river, a settlement, natives... dangers, he said. A man could get lost there. A man could... begin again.’

I looked at him, and I saw it all with a dreadful clarity. He had not understood Stein’s words at all. He had taken the part that suited him-- ‘to follow the dream’-- and discarded the rest. He was not going to immerse himself in the destructive element; he was going to flee from it, to find a new, unsullied ocean in which to drown himself all over again. He was going to climb out of the sea, just as Stein had warned, but he was convinced he had found a shore where the air was pure, where his lungs would not fail him.

He had answered not my question, but the one he heard in its place: not whether I was romantic, but whether I was alive in the way he understood life--as a prolonged, noble, and ultimately doomed struggle within one’s own dream. My pronouncement had left him silent, his gaze fixed on me as though I were a new, unclassifiable specimen brought before him, one that presented a curious and perhaps insoluble puzzle.

“Romantic,” he said at last, the word a soft exhalation of breath, a whisper almost lost in the great stillness of the room, surrounded as we were by the ghosts of his own dead ardours. “You say that of me. And you . . . you are the one who has come here with a man’s soul in his hand, asking what to do with it. Who is the romantic, my friend? The man who collects the butterflies, or the one who wishes to preserve the man?” He made a small, weary gesture, a motion of his hand that seemed to dismiss the whole tangled affair, to dismiss me, and Jim, and the very world that had produced such a lamentable and magnificent problem. It was the gesture of a man who has lived long enough to see the same tragedy play out in a hundred different costumes, and who knows the final act by heart.

The days that followed my interview with the Dutchman were of a piece with that desolate dawn; they were grey, uniform, and possessed of a stillness that was not peace but the held breath of an expectant dread. I remained in the port, a place that was itself a kind of purgatory, a waiting room between the solid, charted world of commerce and the vast, unknown archipelagos of adventure and ruin. I watched the steamers come and go, their funnels trailing plumes of solid, purposeful smoke against the indifferent sky; they were assertions of a world that worked, a world of schedules and bills of lading, a world in which a man like Jim was an anomaly, a rounding error in a final accounting. The harbour was a great, grimy machine, and every turn of a winch, every bellow from a captain on his bridge, seemed to me a reiteration of the Dutchman’s brutal philosophy. Stay on the ship. Mind the cargo.

I would sit for hours on the verandah of the hotel, an establishment that catered to the transient servants of this machine, and I would feel myself a ghost amongst them. They spoke of freight rates, of the quality of rattan from a particular river, of fevers and the unreliability of native crews. Their talk was a litany of the concrete, a bulwark against the immense and threatening ambiguity of the East. And all the while, my mind was far away, in that hidden river kingdom where one man, for the sake of his own idea of himself, was attempting to hold back the tide not with a bulwark of facts, but with the fragile dam of his own honour. He was, as Stein had said, a man who would not keep still on his heap of mud. And I, who had been given the two irreconcilable prophecies, the philosopher’s and the trader’s, could only wait for the news of the inevitable flood. Stein had given him a destiny; the Dutchman had given him a price, and found it wanting. Both, I saw with a terrible clarity, were pronouncing a sentence of death.

The news, when it came, did not arrive on the steady pulse of the mail steamer, a predictable and orderly event. It came as Patusan itself might have come, had it broken loose from its moorings in the jungle and drifted out to sea; it came in the form of a small, bedraggled prau that appeared one morning on the burnished, heat-hazed expanse of the sea. It was a vessel of a build I had seen only in the remotest corners of the islands, with a tattered, patched sail the colour of dried blood and a hull that seemed held together by little more than determination and a thick coat of marine growth. It moved with a kind of desperate languor, a wounded creature dragging itself towards the shore, and it was ignored by the busy, self-important steam launches that criss-crossed the harbour. It belonged to another age, another reality, and its appearance was an intrusion, a note of wild, discordant music in the monotonous hum of the port.

I watched it from the verandah, my heart a cold, heavy weight in my chest. I knew, with the certitude of a man who has been waiting for a blow to fall, that this was it. This frail vessel was the answer to my vigil, the messenger from the specimen I had left under glass. It took an eternity for the prau to creep across the intervening water and find a resting place amongst the filth and flotsam of a small, forgotten inlet used by local fishermen. For a long time, nothing happened. The boat simply lay there, looking exhausted and final, as if the effort of arrival had been its last. Then, a single figure emerged from the low, palm-thatched shelter amidships. He stood for a moment, unsteady on his feet, a silhouette against the blinding glare of the sun on the water, and surveyed the shore with the bewildered air of a man cast up from the depths.

I did not wait for him to come to me. I descended the steps of the hotel and made my way along the waterfront, my boots stirring up the dust and refuse of a civilisation I suddenly found abhorrent in its stolid security. The man was still by his boat when I reached him. He was a Bugis, one of Jim’s own people, and his face, when he turned it to me, was a mask of exhaustion, grief, and a profound, uncomprehending loyalty. His sarong was torn, his skin etched with the marks of sun and salt. He recognised me at once, and in his eyes I saw a flicker of that same absolute trust that Jim inspired in all his followers; it was a look that made me feel like an accomplice in a great and terrible betrayal.

He did not speak, but reached inside the folds of his tunic and produced a small object wrapped in oilcloth. It was a letter. He held it out to me with a hand that trembled slightly, a gesture of immense and solemn finality. I took it. The oilcloth was still damp, and the package felt heavy, as if weighted with more than just a few sheets of paper.

“From Tuan Jim,” he said, his voice a dry rasp. It was all he said. There was no need for more. The story was in his haunted eyes, in the slump of his shoulders, in the very presence of this derelict boat so far from its home.

I retreated to the shade of a godown, the stench of dried fish and tar thick in the air, and I unwrapped the letter. It was not long. The handwriting was Jim’s, bold and clear, the strokes firm, almost defiant. It was not the letter of a man asking for help; it was the letter of a man reporting, with a kind of terrible pride, on the successful management of a crisis.

It began without preamble. ‘There was a party of whites. Scum of the earth, by the look of them, but whites nonetheless. Came up the river in a stolen schooner’s boat. Starving, armed, and desperate. The sort of men we both know, the kind that give us all a bad name. The leader, a fellow who calls himself Brown, a brute with the manners of a gentleman fallen on very hard times. He demanded food, a clear passage out. Threatened fire and slaughter.’

I had to stop reading for a moment. I could see it. I could see the entire scene: the sudden appearance of these spectres from the outer world, a brutal reminder of the very life Jim had fled. They were his past, arriving to judge his present. They were the destructive element, made flesh. I read on.

‘Doramin and the others wanted to wipe them out. A simple solution. And, I suppose, a just one. They had killed some of our people on their way upriver, out of pure malice it seems. There was blood to be paid for. But I could not do it. You would understand that. One cannot simply kill men who are at your mercy, however worthless they may be. They were my kind, in a way. I stood for a fair chance. I told Doramin that. A fair chance.’

A fair chance. The words seemed to lift from the page and hang in the hot, still air before me. It was the cry of his soul. It was what he had never been given, what he had always felt was his due. Now, in his own kingdom, he had become the dispenser of that elusive justice. He would give to these ruffians the very thing the world had denied him. It was magnificent. It was insane. It was pure Jim.

‘I went to them myself,’ the letter continued. ‘Unarmed. I told this Brown he would have his passage. I gave him my word they would be unharmed if they left peaceably. He looked at me as if I were a new sort of fool he had just discovered. Perhaps I am. He agreed. He gave me his word in return. I am sending them down the river tomorrow, on the ebb tide. The affair is settled. I have kept the peace. I have shown that we here are not savages, that we can be just. I felt I had to write and tell you this. It seems important. It feels like a victory.’

The letter ended there. A victory. He had faced the grubby, violent reality of the world in the person of this man Brown, and he had imposed upon it his own romantic code. He had stared into the abyss of his past and, instead of recoiling, had offered it terms of surrender based on his own private articles of war. And he called it a victory. I folded the letter slowly, the paper crackling in the silence. It felt like an epitaph.

I looked up at the messenger, Kassim, who had been watching me with a dog-like patience. His face was a tragic refutation of the letter’s triumphant tone.

“He sent them away?” I asked, my voice flat.

“Yes, Tuan,” he whispered. “He gave them the clear river. He spoke to them as a brother.”

“And they left?”

The man’s composure broke. A tremor ran through his body, and he looked away from me, towards the open sea, as if searching for an escape from the memory he had to deliver. “They left, Tuan. But they did not go to the sea. They stopped by the spit of land, where the channel is narrow. They hid their boat. They waited.”

I knew the rest. I did not need him to tell me, but I had to hear it, to have the account rendered in full. The balance sheet of Jim’s victory. “Go on,” I said, and my voice was harder than I intended.

“Dain Waris,” the man stammered, his eyes filling with tears. “The son of Doramin. Our young chief. Tuan Jim had sent him with his best men to watch the river mouth, to make sure the scoundrels went out to sea. He told him to post his men on the far bank, to let them pass. To show trust. Dain Waris did as Tuan Jim commanded. He always did. He loved him.” The man choked on the word. “He waited on the far bank. The white men, the devils, came down the river. When they saw him, they opened fire. They shot him from their boat as they passed. Him, and two others. They slaughtered them, Tuan. For sport.”

He fell silent. The air hummed with the heat. A fly buzzed insistently around my head. So that was it. The fair chance had been given. The word of a gentleman-brute had been accepted. And the price had been paid, not by Jim, but by the one who represented the future of his people, the son of his staunchest ally, the embodiment of the trust he had so carefully built. The destructive element had not been the sea, or the dream, but the simple, predictable treachery of a man like Brown, a man for whom a ‘fair chance’ was merely another name for an opportunity.

“And Jim?” I finally managed to ask. “Tuan Jim?”

The messenger looked at me then, and his expression was no longer one of simple grief. It was something more complex, a mixture of awe and terror. “When the news came, he became stone. He did not speak. Doramin… old Doramin sits with the body of his son. He has the two silver pistols Tuan Jim gave him, the ones that were a promise of protection. He has laid them before him. He waits.”

He waits. The whole world, it seemed, was waiting. I was waiting in this port. Doramin was waiting by the body of his son. And Jim, I knew, was preparing to walk out and meet his fate. He would not run. He was not a man who ran, not anymore. He had run once, from the *Patna*, and had spent the rest of his life running towards a chance to stand still, to face the bill when it was presented. And now it had been presented. The cost of his victory had been calculated, and the debt was due.

“I was sent to bring you this news,” Kassim said, his voice regaining a sliver of its formal composure, as if he were delivering a final, official report. “And to tell you… to tell you that he said you would understand. He said it was the only way. To follow the dream.”

To follow the dream. The words echoed Stein’s, but they were no longer a whisper of charming, deceptive light. They were a blaze, a conflagration. He had followed it, indeed. He had immersed himself, not in the sea, but in the fiery crucible of his own idealism. He had treated the world of men as if it were a gentleman’s club, and had been answered with a volley of rifle fire from a dark riverbank.

I gave the man some money, told him where to find food and rest. He took it without a word, his eyes vacant, his duty done. He had delivered his terrible message from that other world, and now he was adrift in mine, a piece of wreckage like his battered boat. I watched him shamble away, a man whose heart had been broken by a loyalty he could not renounce.

I stood there for a long time, the letter in my hand, the heat pressing down on me like a physical weight. The Dutchman’s words came back to me, no longer cynical, but prophetic. An investment that would not pay out. He was right, of course. From the world’s point of view, Jim was the worst kind of investment; he had gambled everything, his life and the lives of those who trusted him, on a principle that had no market value. He had tried to be a very fine fellow, and in his pursuit of that dream, had brought ruin upon the only world that had ever accepted him.

He was romantic. And the remedy, as Stein had said with that terrible, simple finality, was the one thing that could cure us from being ourselves. I knew then that Jim had gone to seek that cure. He would go to Doramin. He would face the pistols that were the symbol of their broken trust. He would not offer an excuse, or a defence. He would offer himself. It was the only coin he had left with which to pay. He would at last keep still upon his heap of mud, not as a king upon a throne, but as a sacrifice upon an altar. And in that final, awful act, he would perhaps, in his own eyes, cease to be a specimen for me to describe, or a problem for Stein to diagnose, and become, at last, what he had always dreamed of being: a man who was, in a certain sense, perfect. The thought brought me no comfort at all. It was a perfection bought at too high a price, a consummation that left behind only a great and desolate silence.

His voice came from that uncertain region, no longer the doctor’s, but the sage’s, the old man who had seen too many specimens, too many dreams.

“He paid the price,” the voice rolled, voluminous and grave. “That is something. That is the way. He did not try to parley with the destructive element; he did not ask for terms. He presented himself. He made of his life the one thing he had left to give that was of absolute value--a perfect and complete gesture. To a man of the East, a man like Doramin, a man of old truths, that would be understood. A life for a life. An honour for an honour. He did not cure himself, you see. He… completed himself.”

The shadow stopped its prowling and for a moment I could feel its gaze upon me from across the room, a gaze that was not of the eyes but of the mind. “And you are left with the story. You came to me to describe a man, and now you have the whole of him, from the first beat of the wings to the last. It is a terrible and beautiful thing, a complete specimen. What will you do with it?”

The question hung in the air, in the thick, fragrant smoke of his pipe. What would I do with it? I had carried it across the sea, I had written it down in letters, I had turned it over and over in my thoughts until its sharp edges had worn smooth paths in my mind. It was a part of me, a weight in my soul, a lens through which I now saw the world of men. I saw them all as romantics of one kind or another, falling into the sea of their own desires, some drowning quickly, some learning the grim stroke that keeps the head above the water, and some, the rarest, like Jim, diving deep to find a strange pearl in the abyss, and never coming up again.

“I suppose,” I said, and the sound of my own voice was strange to me, a visitor in that room of profound silences, “I suppose I will tell it. To… to see if anyone understands.”

There was a soft, rumbling chuckle from the darkness. “To see if you are alone in your sickness. Yes. That is the way. One tells the story to find one’s brotherhood. It is what the sailor does, telling of the storm. It is what the soldier does, telling of the battle. You will find them, your brothers. Men who have looked into the abyss, even for a moment. But do not expect them to give you a remedy. They will only nod their heads, and fill their pipes, and look at you with… with recognition.”

He loomed up again in the circle of light, his face deeply lined, his eyes holding a great and sorrowful wisdom. He laid his hand not on my shoulder this time, but on the scattered papers of his work, on the descriptions of his own captured dreams. “The work must go on,” he said, in his old, gentle tone, as if waking from a trance. “We must describe. It is how we… how we keep still for a moment on our own heap of mud. It is how we make a little order in the chaos. This butterfly… and your… your Jim. They are not so different. Both flew towards a light. Both were… magnificent. And both are now… still.”

He picked up the glass case that had started it all, the one with the rare, perfect specimen. He did not open it. He held it up to the lamp, and the light caught the iridescent dust of the wings, making them shimmer with a spectral, unearthly life. “So beautiful,” he whispered. “And so… finished. There is a peace in that, is there not? The struggle is over. The dream is captured, forever. Ewig. Usque ad finem.”

He looked at me, and his gaze was a dismissal, a final prescription. The consultation was over. He had given me the only consolation he possessed: the grim fellowship of the observer, the lonely peace of the collector. He had pronounced my condition chronic, and my only palliative the telling of the tale.

I left him there, a tall, stooping figure in the ring of lamplight, holding a dead butterfly as a priest might hold a sacred relic, a man surrounded by the gorgeous, silent ghosts of concluded lives. I walked out into the humid night, under a sky that was a vast, dark, and indifferent sea. The sounds of the distant port, which on my arrival had seemed a profanation, were now just a part of the great, unceasing machinery of existence, a world that had no time for the tragedies of romantic men.

I found my way back to my ship. The familiar scent of tarred rope and salt water was a comfort, a reality that pushed back against the phantoms of the evening. My men looked at me, I think, with a certain curiosity. I had been gone a long time, and I suppose my face must have carried the marks of my conference, the shadow of that room where a man’s life had been weighed and classified like a rare insect.

We sailed with the morning tide. As the coastline of that land of dreams and disillusionment fell away, a pale green smudge dissolving into the heat-haze, I stood on the poop deck and felt the finality of it. I had left him there, the man of shattered trust and fulfilled honour, a king in a land of ghosts, a memory in the heart of a broken old chief. And I had left Stein, the philosopher of butterflies, the man who saw the truth so clearly that it had bleached all the colour from his own hopes. They were two poles of the same terrible world, one who had immersed himself to the point of extinction, and one who had withdrawn to a high, dry place to catalogue the drownings.

And I? I was the messenger, the medium, the one in between. I was sailing away with the specimen, not pinned in a glass case, but alive and fluttering in my own mind, a restless ghost that would not be still. I had sought an answer, a cure, a simple truth to hold on to, and I had been given instead a vast and uncertain expanse, a crepuscular horizon. How to be? The question was no longer Jim’s alone. It was mine. It was branded upon me.

Months later, in a grimy, fog-bound London public house that smelled of stale beer and wet wool, I found one of my brotherhood. He was an old acquaintance, a man who had seen his share of the world’s grim edges, his face a roadmap of forgotten ports and weathered disappointments. I told him the story, all of it, from the jump off the *Patna* to the final walk into Doramin’s campong. I spared him none of the details, none of the philosophy, none of the doubt.

When I had finished, a great silence fell between us. The cheerful din of the pub faded into a distant murmur. He did not speak for a long time. He stared into his glass as if it held the secrets of the universe, then slowly, he lifted his head. His eyes, small and shrewd and weary, met mine across the table. He did not offer condolences. He did not pronounce a judgment on Jim’s character. He did not attempt to solve the riddle.

He only nodded, a single, slow, profound gesture of recognition. Then he said, in a voice so low it was almost lost in the noise, “Yes. By God. Yes.”

And in that moment, in that dingy, commonplace room so far from the sun and savagery of the East, I understood what Stein had meant. I was not cured. I was not absolved. But I was not alone. The story was a bridge, a fragile span across the chasm of our separate solitudes. It did not lead to a safe shore, to a place of certainty and rest. It led only to other wanderers, other watchers on the edge of the abyss, their faces lit for a moment by the flickering flame of a shared, unspoken truth.

The truth was that the destructive element is not only the sea or the jungle or the dark passions of men. It is the dream itself. And to be a man is to be born into it, to fall into that dream as into the sea. You can paddle and splash and try to climb out, and you will drown from the exhaustion of your own terror. Or you can submit, and learn its ways, and let it hold you up, even as it chills you to the bone. Jim had submitted at last. He had let it carry him to its deepest, most silent floor. And I, left on the surface, had been given the task of charting its currents, of mapping its shores, of telling the tale of its depths to any who would listen.

The man across from me raised his glass in a silent, solemn toast. I raised mine in return. We drank to the specimen, and to the collector, and to the teller of the tale. We drank, in short, to the whole damned, glorious, and heartbreaking business of how to be. “That was all,” Davies confirmed. “And that was everything. The man had seen his dream. Not a dream of being a hero, like your Jim. A quieter dream. A dream of a life where things were not measured, where a sad, gentle smile held more truth than a pressure gauge, where you could be wrecked by a glance from a painted face. He had seen it, and he had turned his back on it because he was a 'very fine fellow' of a different sort. He was a man of purpose. He kept his eyes wide open.”

The story hung in the air between us, as palpable as the smoke from a just-extinguished match. It was a quieter tragedy than Jim’s, a failure not of nerve but of imagination. A drowning not in the sea, but in the desert of a life without it.

“What became of him?” I asked, after a time.

“Oh, he went on,” Davies said, with a shrug that was infinitely weary. “He got his promotion. Became chief on one of the premier liners. The brick house got bigger. The daughters married well. He succeeded, you see. By every measure he valued, he was a complete success. I saw him once more, years later, in Southampton. He was standing on the quay, watching his ship being loaded. He had grown stout. His face was heavy, impassive. The look of astonishment was gone. In its place was . . . nothing. A perfect, polished emptiness. Like a house with all the furniture removed. We spoke for a few minutes, of the usual things. He did not remember me at first. Then he did, and he asked me what I was doing, and he nodded, and his eyes looked straight through me, as if I were a ghost. He had won his battle. He had kept the floating rubbish out. He had made his heap of mud into a fortress. And he was serving a life sentence inside it.”

Davies finished his drink in one long, final pull. He set the empty glass down. “He didn't fall into the sea,” he said softly. “He walled it off. And the destructive element, denied its flood, seeped in through the foundations, brackish and slow, and rotted the whole structure from within. He didn't drown. He just . . . dried up. Which is worse? I confess, I have never been able to decide.”

He looked at me, a direct and challenging look. “Your Jim submitted at last, you say. He went to the bottom. But this man, Hendricks . . . he never even got his feet wet. He stayed on the shore, and died of thirst. Another specimen. For the collection.”

Here it was again, the same terrible and simple question, posed in a different key. How to be. How to live. To follow the dream and risk immolation, or to deny it and risk petrifaction. Jim and Hendricks. The butterfly and the beetle. Both perfect in their way, both pinned to a board by the fact of their own natures, both victims of the terrible, funny thing that is a man born into a dream he can neither live up to nor live without. Stein’s words came back to me, rolling out of the dusk of memory, mellowed by distance. *The way is to the destructive element submit yourself*. Jim had, in his own final, catastrophic way. Hendricks had refused, and in his refusal had found a different, quieter, but no less absolute destruction.

“So you see,” Davies said, pushing his chair back. The scrape of it on the floorboards was loud in the near-silent room. “It is not just the romantics. The abyss is for all of us. It is the thing we are all trying to cross. Your Jim tried to leap it. My Hendricks tried to pretend it wasn’t there. And both of them are gone. And here we are, you and I, telling their stories, trying to make a bridge of words. A fragile sort of engineering, isn’t it?”

He put some coins on the table, more than enough for the drinks. “The story is the only thing that is left,” he murmured, more to himself than to me. “The specimen is gone, but the description remains. A slip of paper in the great, dark cabinet.”

He gave a short, almost formal nod. “It was a good talk.”

“Yes,” I said, my own voice sounding strange and distant. “A good talk.”

He turned and walked towards the door, his steps steady and even on the worn floor. He did not look back. He opened the door, and for a moment he was silhouetted against the damp, dark glitter of the street, a patch of London night full of its own shapeless dusk. Then he was gone, and the door swung shut, its automatic lock clicking sharply.

I was alone with the ghosts. With Jim, that very fine fellow, and with Hendricks, that very empty one. With Stein, prowling amongst the graves of his butterflies, and with Davies, a man who navigated the sea of stories with the same grim competence he navigated the sea of water. I sat for a long time, in the deepening silence, under the watch of the sleepy barman who was wiping down his counter with a slow, circular motion. The charming and deceptive light seemed to grow dimmer, the poesy of its dimness more pronounced, throwing its faint and melancholy glow over the new grave that had just been dug on the plain of my thoughts.

Was Davies right? Was it worse to dry up than to drown? The question was a pitfall, another one of the endless traps laid for a man who tries to understand. The answer, I felt, was that it did not matter. The end was the same. The failure was the same. The failure was to believe there was a remedy, a cure for being ourselves. The only way was to submit, to immerse. But how? Stein’s hand, pointing like a pistol, had faltered. His certitude had vanished in the light. And Davies, for all his hard-won knowledge, had offered only another question, another tale of a specimen pinned and catalogued.

My gaze fell upon the coins he had left. They gleamed softly on the dark wood. A simple, practical transaction. An exchange of metal for spirits. And yet, it was also a part of the story. A small, concrete fact in a sea of immense and troubling uncertainties. Hendricks would have understood the coins. He would have counted them, judged their worth. He would not have understood the conversation they had paid for. He would have called it more floating rubbish.

And in that moment, in that dingy, commonplace room so far from the sun and savagery of the East, a new and disquieting thought came to me. We, the tellers of tales, the collectors of specimens, were we any different? We who watch the Jims and the Hendricks of the world, who diagnose their romanticism, who chart their destructions--what were we doing but turning them into butterflies, into beetles, pinning them down with our words? We immerse ourselves, not in the destructive element of life itself, but in the telling of it. We make the deep, deep sea keep us up by turning it into a story. Perhaps that was our own way of building a wall. A more elaborate wall, certainly, decorated with the impalpable poesy of crepuscular light, but a wall nonetheless. A way of keeping our own eyes shut, of seeing ourselves as very fine fellows--so fine as we can never be--the wise observers, the sad philosophers, the ones who understand.

The barman called out that he was closing. His voice broke the spell. I stood up, feeling a sudden chill, a weariness that went deeper than the late hour. The great plain on which men wander remained as desolate as ever, but its bright edge, which I had imagined as an abyss full of flames, now seemed to be something else. It seemed to be a ring of mirrors, in which every wanderer, every collector, every teller of tales, saw only the reflection of his own face, lit for a moment by the flickering flame of a story, before the darkness closed in again. I picked up my hat and coat, and walked out into the night, leaving the coins and the empty glasses and the ghosts to the silence. The story was not a bridge, after all. It was just another room, with its own pitfalls, its own graves, in the vast and inescapable solitude of how to be.

I stepped out into the darkness, and the door closed behind me with a quiet, decisive click. The sound was not unlike the one his automatic lock had made on the glass case. I was outside again, but the world had changed. The path back was no longer just a path; it was a part of the great plain. The shadows under the trees were not just shadows; they were pitfalls. The stars, glittering like sharp fragments of ice in the blackness, were no longer distant and beautiful; they seemed to be the cold, watchful eyes of an immense and silent audience, waiting for the story to continue, waiting for the collector to find his next specimen, or perhaps, to become one himself.

The silence he had spoken of did not feel like an absence of sound. It felt like a presence, a weight. Walking through the sleeping settlement, I felt as if I were wading through it, this immense, profound silence. The story of the man I had come to describe, the man Stein called the butterfly, was no longer a puzzle to be solved. It was a mirror. And in it, I saw not his reflection, but my own, a figure with a net, standing on the edge of an abyss, mesmerized by the fall of another, calling it romance, calling it destiny, calling it a story. Stein was right. The failure was to believe there was a remedy. There was only the work. And my work, I saw with a sudden and chilling clarity, was not to save the man, not to understand him in any way that might be of use to him. My work was to pin him to a page, to preserve the tragic beauty of his flight for my own collection. And I knew, with a certainty that was altogether hopeless, that I would do it. I would do it because it was the only way I knew how to be. I would immerse myself not in his dream, but in the telling of it, and in that telling, I would make the deep, deep sea keep me up, even as it swallowed him whole. The night was vast, and the path was long, and I walked on, a scrupulous man who had hesitated on the brink of confidence and had been pushed, gently, over the edge, into the heart of his own inescapable dream.

The journey back was a descent into a kind of waking trance. The familiar sounds of the settlement, the chirping of nocturnal insects, the distant bark of a dog, the rustle of unseen things in the undergrowth, all seemed to be parts of a language I was only just beginning to comprehend, a language that spoke of continuance, of the blind, unthinking surge of life that paid no heed to the agonies of consciousness. My own thoughts circled, hawk-like, over the landscape of the preceding hours: Stein’s face in the lamplight, a mask of thoughtful placidity; the glint of his spectacles; the frail, quivering wings of the butterfly, a dead thing that seemed to hold more life than the living; his voice, pronouncing judgment, ‘He is romantic,’ and then the correction, the terrible amendment, ‘We are the romantics.’

To follow the dream… and again to follow the dream… Was that the way? Or was it to stand apart, and to tell the story of the following? I thought of the man, the ‘specimen’. I was seeking a man named Robinson, a ship-chandler with whom our man, in his capacity as a water-clerk, had dealings. ‘Well?’ he grunted, without preamble. His voice was a rasp, as if rusted by years of shouting against the wind.

‘I listen to them,’ I corrected gently.

‘Same thing.’ He waved his pipe dismissively. ‘Well, there’s a story for you. The story of the man who was too good for the job. Too fine for the work. He’s all yours. Make him a hero in a book. It’s the only place he’ll ever be one.’

The finality of his judgment was like a door slamming shut. There was no appeal, no room for doubt. In the world according to Robinson, the case was closed. I thanked him for his time, a formality that seemed absurd in the face of such brutal candour. As I stood up to leave, his muddy eyes held mine for a moment longer.

‘One more thing,’ he rasped, leaning forward over the chaos of his desk. He had already dismissed me, already returned to the solid, quantifiable world where a man’s failure was a simple debit in a column of figures.

I descended the rickety staircase and stepped back into the blinding glare and noise of the port. The whole encounter had lasted no more than twenty minutes, but it had altered the light. The world seemed harder, its edges sharper. Stein’s crepuscular horizon, with its impalpable poesy, had receded, replaced by the unforgiving midday sun on Robinson’s world of pitiless facts. The two visions existed side by side, irreconcilable. One man saw a dream that could not be made true; the other saw a machine that had been broken.

And because you not always can keep your eyes shut there comes the real trouble. Stein’s voice returned to me, a ghostly whisper in the clamour of the quay. I had opened my eyes to Robinson’s reality, and the pain of it was undeniable. The man, my specimen, was not just a romantic soul grappling with an ideal of conduct. He was a man who lost vital documents because he was entranced by a sunset. The poetry and the prose of his existence were horribly, inextricably tangled. To follow the dream… and so… ewig… usque ad finem. But what if the dream led, not to a heroic sacrifice, but to a misplaced sheaf of papers, to a shipment of rotting pepper, to the contemptuous pity of a man like Robinson? The abyss full of flames seemed a noble, almost desirable alternative to that small, sordid hell of incompetence.

I walked on, aimless, through the sweltering streets. The story was growing, but it was not becoming simpler. It was becoming denser, more opaque. Each new testimony added a layer, not of clarity, but of complexity. Stein’s butterfly, Robinson’s chronometer, the man himself… they were all true, and all false. The truth was not a point of light to be discovered, but a vast, shadowy expanse to be navigated, a destructive element in which one had to learn to swim. The work, the telling of the tale, was not a process of revelation, but a process of immersion in this very uncertainty. It was the only way. To describe the dream, and in describing it, to live it, in a way; to feel the spray from a shore I could never bring myself to leave. I had another slip to add to the scattered pile on my desk, another voice to weave into the chorus, another shadow to cast upon the great, desolate plain. And the man himself remained, as ever, one of us, and yet terribly, luminously, alone.

was a pity he could not be made to see that. It was a pity, I thought, that one could not simply take a man to Robinson and say, ‘Look. Here is the unvarnished truth of your life. The air here was cooler, heavy with the scent of spices and decay, a smell that was the very essence of this port, the smell of fortunes made and lost on the turn of a tide. At the end of the lane, a wooden veranda, its paint peeling in long, leprous strips, offered the promise of shade and drink. It was the entrance to a hotel, one of those establishments that seem to exist in a perpetual state of twilight, having long ago given up any pretension to respectability. The name, ‘Hôtel de la Marine,’ was painted in faded gold letters on a board above the door, a name that spoke of a certain clientele, of men who followed the sea and carried its vast indifference in their eyes. The only other occupant was a man sitting alone at a table by the far wall, a glass and a bottle before him. I hesitated, then took the plunge. It was a long shot, but the name of the hotel, his uniform, the whole atmosphere of the place seemed to point in one direction. ‘It concerns a ship,’ I said slowly. ‘An old steamer, carrying pilgrims. A ship named the Patna.’

Here it was. Another piece of the puzzle, delivered into my hands by the sheer, blind chance that governs the affairs of men in the East. A man who had seen the thing itself, the abandoned ship, the very corpus delicti of my specimen’s fall from grace.

‘You saw her?’ I asked, leaning forward.

‘We saw her,’ he confirmed, nodding. ‘A black shape on the horizon at dawn. We could not imagine what she was. No smoke. No sign of life. A derelict. We approached with caution. One does. They were lying everywhere, on every deck, in every patch of shade. Not dead, no. But in a state of utter exhaustion, of passive despair. They had been waiting for the end, and the end had not come. He fell silent, and I did not press him. I could see it all, the French gunboat, sleek and grey, coming upon that rusty, forsaken hulk; the young officer, this man before me, climbing the rope ladder, his hand on his pistol, expecting pirates or mutineers, and finding instead this… this profound and silent accusation.

‘And the ship itself?’ I prompted gently, after a moment. ‘Was she… sinking?’

He gave a short, sharp laugh, a bark of pure, unadulterated irony. ‘Sinking? Monsieur, that is the cream of the jest. That is the point of the entire vaudeville. We examined her. She was, in the words of our engineer, ‘la plus dégoûtante et la plus solide des vieilles boîtes.’ The most disgusting and the most solid of old boxes. An ugly phrase, for an ugly truth.’

He looked at me directly now, his gaze sharp and analytical. ‘So. Your story is about the man who ran away from a ship that was not sinking. Your romantic. I presume he is a romantic. He would have to be. Only a romantic could construct such a magnificent catastrophe in his own mind, a catastrophe so grand that it justifies abandoning eight hundred people to their fate. His words landed like stones, each one chipping away at the impalpable poesy of Stein’s crepuscular horizon. Stein saw the dream; this man saw the vaudeville. Robinson saw the broken chronometer; this man saw the disgusting but solid old box.

‘You think it was simple cowardice, then?’ I asked.

‘Cowardice?’ He considered the word. ‘No. Cowardice is too simple. Cowardice is an instinct. A man is afraid, he runs. That is an animal reaction. Understandable. Contemptible, but understandable. This… this was something else. This was a failure of the imagination, masquerading as an excess of it. He did not see what was there, a rusty plate and a slow leak. He saw a vision of the abyss. He became the hero of his own private apocalypse. He must have seen himself, a solitary figure, tragically forced to save his own precious skin from the general doom he had himself invented. It is a very… English conception, if you will permit me to say so.’

‘I have heard it called a dream,’ I said, my voice quiet.

‘A dream,’ he mused, swirling the amber liquid in his glass. ‘Yes. A dream. But you see, for a man in my profession, a man who wears this uniform, a dream is a luxury one cannot afford at sea. A ship with eight hundred souls on board is a fact, a terrible, weighty fact. To be a sailor is to submit oneself to these facts. To serve them. Your man did not want to serve the facts. He wanted the facts to serve his dream of himself. And when they would not, when the ugly, solid old box refused to sink gracefully into the abyss for him, he ran away from it. He drained his glass and set it down with a click of finality. He had diagnosed the case, just as Stein had, but his remedy was not immersion; it was a cold, brutal acceptance of the facts. What’s good for it? I had asked Stein. This man, this Lieutenant Dubois, would have had a simple answer. A court martial. A broken sword. The end of the story.

Terribly, luminously, alone. The words echoed in my own mind. This Frenchman, with his cold logic and his Gallic irony, had arrived at the same destination as Stein, the German mystic. They stood on opposite sides of the globe, staring at the same enigma, and saw the same solitude at its heart. For Stein, it was the solitude of the dreamer who can never make his dream come true. For Dubois, it was the solitude of the deserter who can never rejoin the world of men, the world of shared, solid facts. Both visions were true, and both were terrible.

‘He is trying to live,’ I said, more to myself than to him. ‘He is trying… to be.’

‘Ah. How to be,’ Dubois murmured, and for the first time his smile seemed to possess a flicker of something that was not irony, but a deep and profound sadness. ‘That is always the question, is it not? For all of us. The ones who run, and the ones who stay. We all have our own Patna, our own moment where the dream and the fact diverge. Most of us are fortunate enough that the moment passes in the dark, unobserved. Your man… he had the bad luck to have his moment under a vast and starry sky, with the whole world watching. You can pick up the pieces, but you can never make them tell the same time again.’

He gave me a final, formal nod, turned, and walked out of the bar, his white uniform a ghostly shape swallowed instantly by the gloom of the louvered doors. I was alone again, in the cavernous room, with the sluggish fan and the dregs of my brandy. But the room was not empty. It was filled with shadows, with the ghosts of abandoned ships and abandoned duties, with the memory of eight hundred silent people staring from a deck, and with the presence of a man who had chosen a dream over the solid world, and in doing so, had fallen out of it altogether.

Dubois was wrong about one thing. The pieces did tell the same time. They all told the same, impossible time. It was the moment of the jump. The work, my work, was not to choose between their visions, but to hold them all at once, to feel their equal weight, their equal truth. To describe the specimen was to become him, in a way; to stand on that phantom deck, to feel the vibration of the engines stop, to see the calm, indifferent stars above and the dark, patient sea below, and to face the question, not of how to be, but of what, in God’s name, one was to do. I pushed my glass away. The brandy had lost its consolation. The sun was beginning to set, and the light filtering through the door was turning a bruised, crepuscular purple. It was the hour of ghosts. I felt it was time I went to find my own.

It was clear that he had immersed himself, not in the sea that had been his downfall, but in the intricate, destructive element of this small, isolated society. He was, in his own way, still trying to make the deep, deep sea keep him up.

‘This Sherif Ali,’ I said, venturing into deeper water. ‘He is a danger to you?’

He looked away from me then, his gaze fixed on the wall of green that pressed in on his clearing. ‘He is a danger to the idea of this place,’ he said slowly. ‘He lives by force, by taking. And there it was. The heart of the matter. The romantic. He was not merely managing a trading post; he was defending a principle. He was a knight in a land without chivalry, the guardian of a fragile Camelot built on a foundation of mud and rattan. The world had judged him and found him wanting in a moment of crisis, and so he had found a place so far from the world that he could create his own crises, and this time, meet them. ‘And how does one meet a man like that?’ I asked.

His face became closed, resolute. ‘One must be… decisive,’ he said. ‘One must show that one is not afraid.’ He said it with such simple conviction that it was impossible, at that moment, to think of him as the man who had jumped from the *Patna*. That man was a ghost, a story told in smoky bars by men who had never seen the heat of this sun, who had never felt the weight of this silence. This man before me was real, solid, burdened with the present. The past was a dream; this, this was the waking world.

‘You have a plan, then,’ I stated, rather than asked.

He gave a short, humourless laugh. ‘A plan. Yes. One always has a plan. It is a matter of… opportunity. He had fallen into the dream, as Stein had said, and finding he could not climb out, he had resolved to make the dream real, to force the world to conform to the shape of his own romantic vision.

That evening, as a thick, velvety darkness descended, we sat on the verandah again. The compound below was lit by a few flickering torches, casting long, dancing shadows. The sounds of the village, a snatch of song, the cry of a child, a brief argument, came to us softened by the distance. It was peaceful, an idyll of sorts. But I could feel the tension beneath it, the presence of the hills, of Sherif Ali, of the unresolved conflict. I felt like an observer in a laboratory, watching an experiment whose outcome was terribly uncertain. He had been describing a specimen, this man Jim, and I had come to do the same, but I found the roles reversing. He was the one conducting the experiment; I, and all his people, and the very land itself, were the elements he was working with.

I dared to touch the nerve. ‘I knew a man once,’ I began, my voice sounding unnaturally loud in the stillness, ‘who served on a ship called the *Patna*.’

He did not flinch, but a change came over him. It was as if a shutter had come down behind his eyes, leaving the surface calm, but hiding whatever turmoil lay within. He picked up a small palm-leaf fan from the table beside him and began to move it with a slow, steady rhythm. ‘A pilgrim ship,’ he said, his voice perfectly even. ‘A long time ago.’

‘Yes,’ I said. ‘A very long time ago. There was an incident.’

‘There is always an incident,’ he murmured, his eyes on the darkness beyond the torchlight. ‘The world is full of them. Things happen. A man… a man does what he does. And then he must live with it.’

‘This man,’ I pressed on, feeling a kind of cruel necessity, ‘he was judged for what he did. Or for what he failed to do.’

‘Judged.’ He stopped fanning himself. He placed the fan down with deliberate care. ‘Yes. He was. By men who had never felt the deck shudder under their feet. ‘He jumped,’ I said, the words falling like stones into the quiet.

‘He jumped,’ he agreed, his voice dropping to a whisper. He turned to look at me, and in the flickering torchlight his face was a mask of ancient pain. ‘He saw a vision of all those people drowning. He saw it as if it had already happened. And in that moment… the world split in two. There was the world where he stayed and drowned with them, a hero in the dark, a hero to no one. And there was the world where he lived. He chose to live. Was that so wrong? To choose life?’

‘He left them,’ I said.

‘He did,’ Jim conceded. His shoulders slumped. ‘He left them to a fate he was certain of. He saved himself from a ship he was certain was sinking. The crime… the crime was that the ship did not sink. The fact would not align with the dream. Or with the nightmare. He was judged not for his action, but for a miracle he could not have foreseen.’ He stood up and walked to the edge of the verandah, his back to me, a lonely, rigid figure outlined against the night. ‘I try not to think of it. The work here… the work is everything. It is real. It is now.’

‘Stein would say,’ I found myself saying, the words coming unbidden, ‘that the way is to the destructive element submit yourself.’

He turned, a dark silhouette. ‘Stein.’ A flicker of recognition. ‘The man with the butterflies. Yes. He would say that. He sees things. But he sees them under glass, perfectly preserved. Here… here things are not preserved. They live, and they die. They struggle.’ He came back and stood before my chair, looking down at me. ‘You want to understand. I can see it. You collect men’s stories, like he collects his specimens. You want to pin me to a board, put a label on me. Romantic. Coward. Hero. Deserter.’

‘I want to know,’ I said honestly, ‘how to live.’ The words came out, an echo of my conversation with Stein, but this time they were not a philosophical parry; they were a genuine question, addressed to the man who was living the answer.

He looked at me for a long time, his expression unreadable. ‘How to be,’ he corrected me gently, his voice once more his own. ‘That is the question, is it not? How to be the man you see when you shut your eyes. I will tell you. You cannot. Not all the time. But you can build a place where, perhaps, you have the chance to try. You can find a struggle that is worthy of the man you dream yourself to be.’ He pointed out into the darkness, towards the unseen hills. ‘My struggle is out there. With Sherif Ali. When that is done… when this place is truly safe… then perhaps the man who jumped can finally be at peace.’

I saw then the terrible, simple logic of his existence. He had not escaped his dream; he had merely transposed it to a new, more dangerous stage. His honour was not a thing to be polished and displayed; it was a thing to be forged anew in the heat of conflict. He had to face another test, a test of his own choosing, to erase the one he had failed. The specimen was not content to be described. It was determined to evolve. I stayed for two more days, a silent observer of his small, ordered world, a world held together by the force of one man’s will, by the sheer power of his need to be, at last, a fine fellow.
