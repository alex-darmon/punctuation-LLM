The air in Volkov’s study hung thick with the ghosts of forgotten voyages, a compound of old paper, stale tobacco, and the faint, sharp scent of dried ink. He was bent over a great vellum sheet, spread like a pale skin beneath the cone of a green-shaded lamp, the only real illumination in the vast, book-crammed darkness of the room. He traced a line upon it with a finger whose nail was cracked and yellowed, a finger that had known the grip of a sextant in a black squall.

‘You see here,’ he murmured, without looking up, his voice a low rumble from a cavernous chest. ‘This is a masterpiece of conviction. The man who drew this, he did not guess; he knew. He knew the shape of a coast he had never seen, the temperament of a current he had never felt. He put a dragon here, you see, not because he was afraid of the unknown, but because he was certain of the monster.’ He tapped a fantastically embellished creature writhing in a corner of the parchment. ‘He had to give his fear a name, and so he made it beautiful, yes? He gave it scales of gold and a breath of fire, and in so doing, he believed he had contained it. So it is with men.’ He straightened up slowly, his great, shaggy head turning to me, his eyes, under their heavy white brows, full of a sad, ancient humour. ‘He has it in a cage of ink, and so he thinks he is the master of the beast.’ He sighed, a sound like gravel shifting. ‘And the sea, my friend, the sea does not read the charts.’

He moved away from the table, a great bear of a man in a waistcoat that had seen better decades, and began to fill a small, dark-wood pipe with deliberate, powerful movements of his thumbs. ‘So. You did not come here to look at my old follies. You have a beast of your own you wish to cage, I think.’ He paused, the pipe clenched in his teeth, and struck a match on the heel of his boot; it erupted with a sulphurous hiss, briefly illuminating a face like a weathered cliff.

‘To be honest, Volkov,’ I began, feeling the familiar inadequacy one always felt in his presence, ‘I came to you to describe a coastline.’

‘A woman?’ he asked, the flame of the match dancing in his pupils.

‘Worse,’ I said, a wave of despair washing over me at the sheer simplicity and impossibility of my errand. ‘A conviction.’

‘Ach!’ The match went out. In the renewed dark I could only sense him, a block of solid shadow. ‘The most dangerous reef of all. So you have a navigator who steers by a star only he can see.’ There was no question in his tone, only a statement of fact.

‘He will wreck himself on it,’ I said. ‘He will wreck everyone.’

He was silent for a long time, the only sound the gentle tap-tap-tap of his pipe against a brass ashtray. Then he spoke from the darkness, his voice losing its worldly weariness and taking on a different quality, something more remote, prophetic. ‘And you want to take from him his star? You would ask the sailor to look down at the boards of his own deck, and not up at the heavens? You would tell him the sea is only salt and water and death, and not a road to glory?’

He loomed suddenly back into the circle of the lamp, his face drawn and earnest, his hands clasped before him. ‘There is no cure for this! You cannot make a man unsee the thing he has seen in a dream!’ He struck the table with the flat of his hand, making the lampshade tremble and the shadows jump. ‘No, the question is not how to cure. The question is how to sail. You see your star; the storm comes. The wind does not care for your star. The waves, they do not care. They want to pull you down, to make you part of the senseless water. What do you do?’

I stared at him, mute.

‘You think you fight the storm?’ he scoffed, a deep, bitter laugh rumpling in his chest. ‘No. You do not fight the storm. You cannot. You do not fight the sea. That is a fool’s thought. A landsman’s thought. No.’ He leaned closer, his voice dropping to a conspirator’s whisper, intense and intimate. ‘You let the sea have you. You give yourself to it. You join its dance. You use the wave that wants to swamp you to lift you higher. You use the wind that wants to tear your sails to drive you faster. You see?’ His eyes bored into me, fierce and alight with a terrible, joyous wisdom. ‘You must make the destructive thing the thing that saves you. You must make the storm your engine.’

He stood like that for a moment, a man possessed by his own sermon, his body taut with the force of his conviction. Then, as if a string had been cut, he slumped. The fire went out of his eyes, leaving only the old, tired sadness. He turned away from the light and walked back towards the shadows where his charts lay like sleeping dragons.

‘But it is a hard thing,’ his voice came, muffled and distant, from the other side of the room. ‘To give yourself to what wishes to destroy you. To follow the dream, yes, always to follow the dream... but to trim your sails for the truth of the wind. To know the chart is a lie, and yet to steer by it. How to do both? Gott in Himmel. How to do both.’ He made a small, indistinct movement in the gloom, a stooping gesture as if he were gathering up the scattered pieces of an impossible idea. ‘And in the end,’ the voice rolled back to me, softened by the space between us, ‘perhaps the sea always wins. But the trying... the trying to sail... that is the thing, is it not? That is the only thing.’

was a fine distinction to make, a silken thread of an idea spun in the darkness, and for a moment I felt I could grasp it; I could see the terrible beauty of such a seamanship. The silence that followed his words was not an emptiness, but a presence; it filled the room with the weight of unseen oceans, with the pressure of immeasurable depths where his voice had just been. It seemed to me that I could hear the very sigh of the tides he spoke of, the eternal, mournful whisper of the destructive element itself, calling to the dreamer in every man. The lamplight on his scattered papers seemed for an instant to be the phosphorescence on a black wave, and the faint, dusty scent of the entombed butterflies carried a whiff of salt and decay.

‘And yet,’ I said, my own voice sounding thin and reedy in that profound stillness, a land-bound creature’s cry, ‘one sails towards a port, does one not? The chart is a lie, you say, and the wind has its own will. But the sailor... the sailor must have a destination in his heart, even if it is only a dream of a place. Without that... is he not just a derelict? A piece of wreckage for the storm to play with until it tires of the game?’

He did not turn from the shadows. For a long while the only sound was the rustle of paper, a dry, whispering sound like the stirring of dead leaves. I imagined him over there, his tall form bent over those maps of forgotten coastlines and treacherous passages, a ghost communing with other ghosts. When he spoke again, his voice had lost its prophetic fire and had taken on a tone of weary pedagogy, the voice of a teacher who has explained the same impossible lesson a thousand times.

‘A destination,’ he murmured, the sound rolling out of the dusk. ‘Ja. A destination. You think the destination is the thing. You think it is a place on a map, with a name and a longitude... a place where you can drop anchor and say: I am here. It is finished. The voyage is done.’ A faint scratching sound reached me, the tap of his fingernail on brittle parchment. ‘I too once believed in such places. I had charts... oh, so many charts. More beautiful than this one.’

He emerged then from the gloom, holding not a chart, but a rolled-up cylinder of what looked like darkened vellum, tied with a faded green ribbon. He walked slowly back to the circle of the lamp and laid it on the desk between us, his hands moving with a strange reverence, as if he were handling a sacred relic or perhaps the limb of a child. He did not unroll it. He simply rested his fingers upon it.

‘This,’ he said, looking at me with a gaze that seemed to hold all the sorrows of the East, ‘was a chart to a paradise. A kingdom of islands, rich beyond telling, ruled by a wise old king who had a daughter... ach, you have heard such stories. The sailors tell them in the grog-shops. But this was a true story. The man who drew this chart, he had been there. He swore it on his life. He gave it to me when I was young, like your specimen. And I believed.’

He paused, his thumb tracing the shape of the tied ribbon. ‘I believed so much, I bought a ship. A small brig, not much, but she was sound. And I gathered a crew of men who also wanted to believe. We set sail for this paradise. We had the dream... a very fine dream. We would be rich, we would be kings ourselves, we would find a place where a man could be... as he sees himself in his sleep.’

His eyes left my face and went to the glass case where the great butterfly rested, its wings a silent testament to a captured ideal. ‘The first storm we met took our mainmast. We did not fight it; we submitted. We cut it away and we sailed on under jury-rig. We were proud. You see? We were learning to dance with the sea. The second storm took two of my best men. Washed from the deck as if they had never been. The sea took them. We grieved. We sailed on. The destructive element... it makes you strong, or it breaks you.’

He sighed, a deep, cavernous sound that seemed to drain the very air from his lungs. ‘We ran out of fresh water. Then the sickness came. The men, they began to look at me. The belief was going out of their eyes. And still I held to the dream. I looked at this chart every night, in my cabin, by the light of a swaying lantern. The lines, they were promises. The names of the islands, they were like a prayer. I held to it. I steered by it.’

He leaned forward, his face close to mine, the lamplight carving deep shadows in the hollows of his cheeks. ‘And one day, we saw it. Land. An island, just where the chart said it would be. The men cheered. They wept. They had seen the destination. They forgot the lost mast, the dead men, the thirst, the fever. The dream was real.’ He tapped the rolled chart with a long forefinger. ‘So we thought.’

‘What happened?’ I whispered, caught in the web of his telling.

‘It was the wrong island,’ he said simply, with no drama, no emphasis. The words fell into the room with the finality of stones dropped down a well. ‘Or the chart was the wrong chart. Or the man who drew it was a liar, or a madman, or perhaps himself only a dreamer who had drawn his sleep on a piece of skin. It was an island, yes. A heap of rock and twisted trees, with no water, no harbour, no king, no daughter. Nothing. Just... rock. And the sea crashing on it, laughing at us.’

He sat back in his chair, the story over. ‘The men... they did not want to dance with the sea anymore. They wanted to kill me. They said I had stolen their lives for a lie. And in a way... in a way, they were right. So I submitted to that too. I gave them the ship. I let them put me ashore on that rock with this chart, and one cask of water, and a pistol with one shot. It was all very... romantic, in its own terrible way.’

My God. The scene rose before my eyes: the solitary figure on the barren shore, the hostile faces looking back from the deck of the departing ship, the vast, indifferent ocean all around. It was a vision of ultimate despair, the absolute ruin of a man’s dream.

‘But you are here,’ I managed to say. ‘You survived.’

‘Survived?’ He looked at me with a faint, quizzical smile. ‘I am an old man who collects butterflies and tells stories to strangers. Is that survival? Perhaps.’ He picked up the rolled-up chart and held it as if weighing it. ‘I was on that rock for seventeen days. On the tenth day, a prau of Bugis traders, men I had once considered my enemies, found me. They were off course, blown by the same currents that had fooled me. They took me aboard. They did not ask about my dream. They gave me water and rice. They were... men. Living. Not dreaming.’ He gave the chart a little toss, and it landed on a pile of papers with a soft thud. ‘And I learned something on that rock. With this lie in my hand.’

‘What did you learn?’

‘That the destination is not the thing,’ he said, his voice regaining a measure of its earlier strength. ‘The destination is the lie we tell ourselves so that we can bear the journey. The journey... the sailing... that is the truth. That is the only thing that is real. The feel of the tiller in your hand, the spray on your face, the song of the wind in the rigging... the terror and the beauty of it. The dream of the port is only there to make you hoist the sail. It is the fuel for the engine. Whether the port exists or not... Gott im Himmel, who can say? And perhaps it is better if it does not. A port is an end to sailing.’

He stood up and began to pace again, but this time within the circle of light, a lion in a cage of his own making. ‘Your man,’ he said, stabbing a finger in my direction. ‘Your specimen. He has been shipwrecked on his rock. He had a chart, a very fine chart of what it is to be a good seaman, an honorable man. And he found it was a lie. Or he thinks it was. He has lost his ship. The crew... the whole world... has condemned him and sailed away. And he sits there, on his barren rock of shame, with the ghost of his dream in his hand.’

He stopped before me, his face a mask of intense concentration. ‘You ask me for a cure. You ask me what is good for it. You want me to give you a new chart to a new paradise for him to sail to. Is that not so?’

I could only nod, feeling the foolishness of my errand, the utter inadequacy of any help I could offer.

‘There is no new chart!’ he declared, his voice ringing with a sudden passion. ‘That is the first thing he must learn. To burn the old chart, and to resist the temptation of any new one. There are no more paradises. Not for him. Not in that way.’

‘Then what is left for him?’ I asked, a sense of desolation creeping into my heart. ‘To sit on the rock until he perishes?’

‘No!’ Stein almost shouted the word, his body tense. He strode to the other side of the desk and leaned over it, his hands flat, his eyes burning into mine. ‘He must learn to live on the rock. He must learn the tides that surround it, the fish that swim in its shadow, the birds that nest in its crevices. He must learn the rock itself, its every crack and flaw, its hardness, its unforgiving truth. He has been given this rock. It is his, now. He must make it his home.’

He straightened up, his breathing heavy. ‘And then... one day... when he has forgotten the dream of paradise, when he is no longer looking for a ship to rescue him... maybe he will see a piece of driftwood float by. And he will have an idea. Not a dream. An idea. A small, practical idea. To tie that piece to another piece. To make a raft. Not to sail to any paradise. No. Just to get off the rock. Just to feel the water under him again. To go... somewhere. Anywhere. To be in motion again. To be a sailor again, even on a raft made of debris.’

He fell silent, and the energy seemed to drain from him as swiftly as it had come. He walked over to his armchair and sank into it, a man suddenly grown old and frail before my eyes. He passed a hand over his face.

‘That is the way,’ he whispered, to himself more than to me. ‘It is not to find the dream again. It is to find the work again. The small work. The building of the raft. The knotting of the rope. The patient, hopeless, necessary work. And in that work... in the submission to that small task... a man can find a way to live. He can... in a certain sense... be.’

He looked up at me, and his eyes were filled with a profound and gentle pity, not only for my friend, but for me, for himself, for all of us who fall into the sea of our own making. ‘Your specimen... is he a worker? Can he learn to see the driftwood, or does he only see the ghost of his lost ship? That is the question you must ask.’

I considered this. It was a new light on the affair, a light as harsh and clear as the sun on a barren rock, stripped of all the crepuscular, deceptive charms of his earlier philosophy. The destructive element was no longer a grand, romantic sea to be danced with; it was a pile of broken pieces on a desolate shore. And the remedy was not a heroic submission, but a humble, painstaking labour. It was in its own way a much more terrible, and perhaps a more hopeful, prescription.

‘He was a worker,’ I said slowly, thinking of the man in question, of his almost boyish devotion to the minutiae of his craft before the fall. ‘He loved the... the knotting of the rope, as you say.’

Stein nodded, a slow, deep nod of understanding. ‘Ja. Then there is a chance. For the dreamers who are not also workers, there is no hope. They are the ones who drown. They leap into the sea, but their hands are soft, they do not know how to pull, how to kick, how to use the water. But a worker... a worker has hands. A worker has muscle. A worker knows that things are built piece by piece. He has a chance to build his raft.’

He fell silent then, and seemed to slip away into his own thoughts, his gaze fixed on some point in the middle distance. The great conference was over. The diagnosis had been given, and the prescription, as bitter and difficult as it was, had been written. I felt a strange sense of exhaustion, as if I myself had been swimming for my life in a raging sea all evening. The air in the room was thick with unspoken histories, with the ghosts of failed dreams and the stubborn, quiet persistence of life.

I rose to my feet. ‘I... I have taken too much of your time, Stein,’ I said. ‘You have been more than generous.’

He did not seem to hear me at first. Then his eyes focused on my face, and he gave a faint, courteous smile. ‘Time,’ he said softly. ‘There is always time for such... specimens. They are the most interesting of all. More interesting than butterflies, even.’ He made a small, weary gesture with his hand. ‘Because they are not yet finished. Because the wings are still wet, you see. One does not know... one can never know... if they will fly, or if they will fall to the dirt and be... spoiled.’

He too got to his feet, and walked with me to the door, his slippers whispering on the polished floor. The house was utterly silent around us, a tomb of wonders. As I stood on the threshold, ready to step out into the warm, damp night of the tropics, he laid a hand on my arm. His touch was light, almost tentative, the touch of a man used to handling fragile things.

‘Tell your friend,’ he said, his voice a low, confidential murmur, ‘tell him not to look at the sea. Not for a while. Tell him to look at his hands. That is where he will find the beginning of his new chart.’

He withdrew his hand and gave me a slight bow, a gesture of old-world formality that seemed both absurd and deeply moving in that place. ‘Go well, my friend. And when you next see your specimen... observe him. Observe him as you would a rare... a difficult... creature. And have a little... pity. We are all such fine fellows, in a dream.’

With that, he stepped back and closed the door, not shutting it, but drawing it to, so that the click of the latch was almost inaudible. I was left alone on his veranda, facing the darkness of the garden. The night was alive with the chittering of insects, a million tiny, insistent lives asserting themselves against the immense, silent indifference of the universe. I looked up at the sky. The stars were out, scattered like a handful of diamonds on black velvet, cold, remote, and impossibly beautiful. They were the dreams, I thought. And down here, on the dark earth, was the place for the work.

I walked away from the house of wonders, the house of dreams and of dust, feeling the weight of Stein’s words settle upon me. It was not a comfort, what he had given me. It was a burden. A burden of difficult knowledge, a chart not to a paradise but to a stark and lonely shore. And yet, as I made my way back towards the harbour, towards the world of men and ships and compromises, I felt that in that burden there was also a kind of strength. The question remained, as Stein had put it: How to be? He had not provided an answer, not a simple one. Instead, he had pointed to a path, a narrow, stony track between the abyss of the dream and the hard rock of reality. To walk it required a courage of a different sort, not the courage to fight the storm, but the courage to pick up the broken pieces. As I walked, the phrase repeated in my mind, a mantra against the dark: To build the raft. To knot the rope. The patient, hopeless, necessary work.

was a look that conceded nothing, yet seemed to invite the uttermost confidence; it was the glance of a man who had looked so long into the heart of things that he had come to find the agitated surfaces of human intercourse a matter of simple, if melancholy, interest. ‘Romantic,’ he repeated softly, as though testing the word against the silence of his museum. ‘No. Not romantic.’ He made a small, almost imperceptible gesture with his hand, a gesture of quiet dismissal. ‘Only I have lived a long time. And I have learned that the young dream of the mountain peak, and the old dream of the road. One is a dream of conquest; the other, a dream of return. There is no romance in that. Only… a journey.’

He did not offer to shake my hand, and I would not have presumed to offer mine. Our parting was of a piece with our meeting: an affair of distances, of unspoken understandings, of a communion that had traversed the gulf between us without the need for the common bridges of familiarity. He simply inclined his head, a tall, stooping shadow once more as he turned back towards his desk, towards the scattered slips of paper and the memory of a perfect specimen. I let myself out, the heavy door closing behind me with a sigh of well-oiled timber, and found myself again under the stars. The air, thick with the scent of unseen blossoms and the faint, pervasive odour of decay that hangs over all life in the tropics, felt like a physical substance, a warm, wet cloth pressed against my face after the cool, dry stillness of that house. I had gone to him seeking a light, a signpost for a man lost in the dark, and I had been given instead a handful of gorgeous, contradictory shadows: a butterfly on a heap of dirt, a man drowning in the sea as a way to salvation, a dream to be followed even unto the end… usque ad finem.

My gharry was waiting where I had left it, the driver a motionless heap on the front seat, asleep in that complete abandon which the East reserves for its moments of repose. The pony, head bowed, stood as if turned to stone. The whole world, it seemed, slept, all except me and the thoughts that swarmed in my head like the invisible insects in the foliage overhead. As we jolted back towards the town, each rut in the dark road seemed to shake another fragment of my conversation with Stein to the surface of my mind. In the destructive element immerse. A fine phrase. A phrase of a poet, or a philosopher, a man who could afford to look at life from the high window of a life already lived. But for the man in the water, for the man struggling for his next breath with the taste of salt and failure in his mouth, what then? Was it not simply a counsel of despair, a beautiful, literary way of saying, ‘Give up and sink’?

I tried to picture it, to translate this Delphic utterance into the crude prose of the Merchant Service. How was one to immerse? Did it mean to seek out failure, to court disgrace, to live defiantly in the very heart of one’s shame? The idea was monstrous, an offence against every instinct of a seaman, whose whole existence is a battle against the destructive element, a perpetual effort to keep the water on the outside of the ship. We live by rules, by charts, by the solid certainties of compass-headings and dead-reckoning. To be told to submit, to let the chaos in… it felt like a betrayal of a fundamental faith. And I was to be the priest of this new, terrible creed. I was to go to Jim and say to him—what? ‘My dear boy, the fact that you are a pariah, that you are haunted and hunted and unable to look a decent man in the eye, is in fact your only hope. Embrace it. Wallow in it. Therein lies your salvation.’ I almost laughed aloud in the darkness of the carriage. The driver stirred, muttering in his sleep. To laugh at such a thing felt like a kind of blasphemy.

For there was a truth in it; I could not deny it. My own soul, a thing I had long considered a serviceable, if unadventurous, instrument, responded to the profound and terrible beauty of Stein’s pronouncement. Had not Jim already tried the other way? Had he not tried to climb out, to scramble ashore on the mud-flats of one commercial house after another, only to be swept away again by the first high tide of gossip, of recognition? He was trying to live as if the *Patna* had never been, to deny the very event that had made him who he was. He was, in effect, trying to cure himself of himself. And as Stein had said, with the simple, final rap of his finger on the desk, for that there was no cure. There was only the question of how to live.

The lights of the waterfront began to appear, at first a scattered few, then a smudged line of yellow along the blackness of the shore. The smells changed: the scent of flowers and earth gave way to the more robust odours of tar, brine, stale beer, and humanity. Here was the world of facts, the world where a man’s worth was measured by his last command, by the papers in his pocket, by the name he signed at the bottom of a manifest. In this world, Jim was a finished man. A specimen to be described, as I had put it to Stein, and then, presumably, discarded. And yet… Stein, that old romantic, that collector of dead beauty, had seen him not as an end but as a beginning. ‘He is romantic,’ he had said, and in that diagnosis lay the faintest glimmer of a possible course. A romantic hero cannot be given a clerk’s stool in a ship-chandler’s office. It is not a fitting stage for his drama. He requires a world as vast, as lonely, as uncompromising as the dream in his own head.

In the destructive element immerse.

The phrase came back to me, but this time with a different resonance. Perhaps the destructive element was not the sea of public opinion, the endless, churning disgrace. Perhaps it was something more fundamental. The sea is a place of solitude. It is a place without history, without signposts. Each wave is new, each crest is virgin. It is a world of immense, impersonal forces, where a man is thrown back entirely upon himself, upon the strength of his own hands and the courage in his own heart. To immerse in that… To send him away from all the judges and the juries, away from the pointing fingers and the knowing looks, to a place where the only judgment to be faced was his own, reflected in the silence of an indifferent nature. To put him in a place where he was the only white man, the only representative of that ideal of conduct he had so spectacularly failed to live up to. To make him, in effect, the sole custodian of the very law he had broken.

The carriage drew up before the hotel, a great white block of a building humming with a life that seemed at once feverish and somnolent. A knot of officers from a French mail-boat stood on the steps, their voices sharp and clear in the night, their cigarette-tips glowing like angry fireflies. From the open doors of the billiard-room came the clean, sharp click of ivory balls and a gust of laughter. This was the world Jim was trying, and failing, to remain a part of. A world of standards and codes, of men who had not jumped. He could not live in it; he could not live without it. He was suspended, a ghost haunting the edges of his own life.

I paid the driver and walked, not into the hotel, but across the road, to the edge of the esplanade. The harbour lay before me, a vast expanse of dark, oily water, pricked with the riding-lights of ships. A faint breeze, the first sigh of the land-wind that precedes the dawn, stirred the surface, making the reflected lights tremble and shift as if the very foundations of the world were unstable. The great plain on which men wander… overshadowed in the centre, circled with a bright edge as if surrounded by an abyss full of flames. Stein’s words. I saw it then, not as a metaphor, but as a literal description of the choice before Jim. He could remain here, on the bright, burning edge of the civilized world, forever circling the abyss of his past, or he could step off, into the darkness at the centre.

My mind, with the slow, inexorable logic of a man fitting together the pieces of a difficult puzzle, began to move. I had connections, of a sort. There were parts of the archipelago known to me, and to few others, places that had dropped off the charts of commerce, backwaters of existence where time itself seemed to have grown sluggish and uncertain. There was, in particular, a trading post, a venture of Stein’s as it happened, in a place whose name was little more than a whisper on the lips of a few desperate traders. Patusan. A native state, deep in the interior, reached by a long and winding river. A place of mists and ancient feuds, of sudden violence and brooding quiet. A place forgotten by the world. It was a grave for a white man, they said. Or a kingdom.

The thought, once it had entered my head, was terrifying in its absolute fitness. It was perfect. It was a solution so complete, so poetically just, that it felt like an inspiration, like a whisper of knowledge from the dusk, as Stein would have it. To send him there would be to take Stein’s philosophy to its ultimate conclusion. It would be an immersion indeed. There, the past would have no voice. The name of the *Patna* would be an empty sound. He would be judged not by what he had been, or by what he had failed to be, but by what he could do, by the man he could become in that crucible. He would be alone with his dream of himself, the fine fellow he saw every time he shut his eyes. In Patusan, he would have to open them. He would have to confront the dream with the daily, grinding reality of being. He would have to make it true, or be destroyed by it.

It was an experiment upon a human soul; and I, who had gone to seek a cure, found myself in the position of the vivisector, knife in hand, with only a philosopher’s whisper for a guide. The audacity of it took my breath away. Who was I to chart such a course for another man? Who was I to send him to his potential doom or his problematic salvation on the strength of a conversation about butterflies? I was a seaman. A plain man, accustomed to plain dealing. And yet, I could not shake the conviction that this was the way. It was the only way that was not a lie. Every other option—a new job, another fresh start—was merely a postponement, another turn on the circle of shame. This was a straight line, leading into the heart of the darkness.

I turned my back on the harbour and walked into the hotel. The lights were brilliant, the noise was jarring. A man I knew, the master of a small coaster, hailed me from the bar. ‘Hullo, Marlow! You look as if you’ve seen a ghost. Come and have a drink.’

I shook my head. ‘Not tonight, Patterson. I have things to do.’

He looked at me with a sort of bibulous concern. ‘Business? At this hour? Nothing good ever happens after midnight, old man.’

‘Perhaps not,’ I said, more to myself than to him. ‘And perhaps it is not goodness I am after.’

I left him looking perplexed and went up the broad, silent staircase to my room. Jim was not there. He was quartered in a smaller, cheaper room at the back of the hotel, a room that befitted his diminished status. I had an impulse to go to him then and there, to burst in upon his doubtless troubled sleep and lay before him this mad, magnificent, terrible plan. To say to him, ‘I have found it. A place where you can be.’ But I hesitated on the landing. The assurance I had felt by the dark water’s edge began to seem, under the steady, uncompromising glare of the gas-lamps, like a form of lunacy. The light, as it had for Stein, seemed to destroy the certitude born in the shadows.

This is the price. When you want to be a very fine fellow, a hero, a Tuan, you must pay the price. The price is that you cannot ever be a little bit of a scoundrel. Not even once. Not even for a good reason. For him, there is no compromise. If he compromises, the dream is shattered. And if the dream is shattered... what is left of him?”

He stood and walked towards the window, a tall, stooping shadow against the deep, star-dusted velvet of the tropical night. “He will let this Brown go,” he said, not to me, but to the darkness outside. “He must. To kill a man in a trap, a man who speaks his own tongue, who appeals to his own history of being an outcast... for your Jim, that would be a betrayal of the dream itself. It would be an admission that he is no better than the forces he has conquered. It would be like a man who has climbed a great mountain, only to find at the top that he is still standing on the mud. No. He cannot do it. He will give the man a chance. It is his nature. It is the very engine of his romance.”

“And Brown will betray him,” I stated flatly.

“Ja,” Stein’s voice was barely a whisper. “Of course. The world is as it is. A snake is a snake. It does not understand gratitude. It understands only hunger and fear. This Brown is hungry. Your Jim will feed him with his own heart.”

There was nothing more to say. We had dissected the specimen, laid its soul bare on the polished wood of his desk, and the diagnosis was immutable. The very qualities that had allowed for his magnificent, improbable success were the same ones that ensured his final, tragic failure. He was a man caught in a logic of his own making, a logic the world did not share. I left him then, standing by his window, a lonely scholar of human folly, and I went back to the indistinct clamour of the port, to the life of compromises and small, necessary dishonesties that men like me must live.

The days that followed were a torment of stillness. The sun beat down upon the roofs of the town with a white and silent fury, a fury that seemed to have exhausted itself, leaving behind only a great and heavy quiet. The harbour water lay flat and oily, reflecting the glare with a merciless intensity. I waited. It was all I could do. I felt like a man on a cliff’s edge, watching a ship sail towards a known reef, powerless to shout a warning that could be heard. The clerk’s words, Stein’s words, my own fears--they all swirled together into a dreadful certitude. The experiment, as I had called it, was running its course.

It was on the third day that I saw it, a mere speck at first, labouring in from the east against the tide. It was a small prahu, and it moved with the erratic, wounded motion of a thing in its death throes. It did not steer a course; it drifted, it wallowed, it seemed to be pushed and pulled by currents of which it had no mastery. My heart, which had been a heavy, inert thing in my chest, gave a painful lurch. I knew, with the kind of knowledge that requires no proof, that this was it. This was the wreckage.

I took a boat out myself, with two Malay rowers who grumbled at the heat. As we drew closer, the condition of the craft became pitiable. Its sail was a shredded rag, hanging from a splintered mast. There was no sign of life at the tiller. It was a ghost ship, a piece of flotsam from a forgotten disaster. We hailed it, and our cries echoed over the glassy water, answered only by the gentle slap of waves against its hull. It was only when we were alongside that we saw him.

He was lying in the bottom of the boat, amidst a coil of rope and the foul bilge water. He was a Patusan man, one of Dain Waris’s men I thought I recognised, though it was hard to tell. He was little more than a skeleton, his skin stretched tight over his bones, his lips cracked and black. But he was alive. His eyes, when they opened, were vast, dark pools of fever and memory. He tried to speak, but only a dry rasp emerged. We got him into my boat, giving him a little water, and as we rowed back to the shore, he seemed to rally, a last flicker of a dying flame. He clutched at my arm, his grip surprisingly strong, a grip of utter desperation.

“Tuan,” he whispered, the word a struggle. “Tuan... he is gone.”

We got him ashore and into the shaded quiet of my rooms. For a day and a night, he hovered between this world and the next, his sleep troubled by muttered phrases and sudden, sharp cries. I sat with him, bathing his forehead, forcing a little broth between his lips, waiting for the story that I already knew in its dreadful outline. When the fever finally broke, he began to speak, his voice a reedy thread of sound, but clear, horribly clear.

His name was Karim, and he had been with Dain Waris. He told the story not as a man analyzing a tragedy, but as a devout follower recounting the desecration of a shrine. He spoke of the great council, where Tuan Jim had stood before the people and argued for mercy. He described the white pirate, Brown, with his sick, knowing eyes and his voice that was like a file on stone, a voice that spoke of shared hardship, of the injustice of the world, of the brotherhood of all white men who were outcasts.

“The Tuan Jim,” Karim whispered, his eyes fixed on some point beyond the wall of my room, “he listened. He saw not a serpent, but a man like himself, cast out. His heart was too great. He told us, ‘We have been victorious. We can afford to be merciful.’ He saw a chance to show that his world, our world, was better. That it was a world of high trust and great deeds, not a world of traps and killing.”

He spoke of Jim’s absolute conviction, of how he had overruled the cautious Doramin and the suspicious Dain Waris. He had staked his own life on the promise. “I shall be responsible,” he had declared to them all. He had provisioned Brown’s ship. He had given them a clear passage down the river, ordering his guard-posts to stand down. He had stood on the riverbank and watched them go, a solitary figure, his white clothes a beacon of his own immaculate belief in the rightness of his act.

And then Karim’s voice broke. He told of how Dain Waris, ever loyal, ever dutiful, had taken a party of men in canoes to shadow the pirates downstream, just to be certain they kept to their bargain and did not turn back to trouble the villages on the coast. It was a precaution, a final act of guardianship. They had waited at a narrow bend in the river, hidden in the mangroves.

“We heard them coming,” Karim choked, his body trembling with the memory. “But they were quiet. Too quiet. No sound of oars. They were drifting. And then... and then the world exploded.”

Brown, of course, had not gone. He had merely drifted out of sight of Patusan, waited for the dark, and then, with his men, had crept back up a small, unguarded tributary that rejoined the main river below Jim’s fortress. He had known, with the cunning of a predator, that Jim’s honour would not be enough. He knew there would be a watcher. He laid his ambush for the watcher.

“They found them by the river bend,” I murmured, finishing the thought for him.

“Yes,” he wept, openly now, the tears of a strong man which are a terrible sight. “A slaughter. Not a fight. Dain Waris... he stood up in his canoe when the first shot came. He did not hide. He was the son of a chief. He was the friend of Tuan Jim. He fell... he fell into the water.”

Karim had survived only because his canoe had been overturned in the first volley, and he had been carried by the current into the thick roots of the mangroves, wounded but unseen. He had watched the pirates loot the bodies and then slip away, back towards the sea, their laughter echoing in the sudden, awful silence.

The rest was a blur of grief and horror. The news, carried by the few other survivors, had reached Patusan like a thunderclap. The wailing of the women, the stunned disbelief of the men, the terrible, accusing silence from the house of old Doramin. And in the centre of it all stood Jim.

According to Karim, he did not rage. He did not despair. A great and profound stillness had settled upon him. He had received the news standing in the courtyard of his own house, and for a long moment he had not moved, his face a mask of white stone. The dream was shattered. The fine fellow he had seen in his own eyes was gone, replaced by the man whose scrupulous romanticism had led his best friend, the brother of his heart, to his death. The destructive element had not been the sea this time; it had been his own soul, and in submitting to it, he had brought ruin.

“He said nothing,” Karim whispered. “He just turned and walked towards his house. Jewel, the Tuan’s woman, she tried to stop him. She cried out his name. He did not look back. He went inside. When he came out again, he was dressed as he was when he first came to us. All in white. Unarmed.”

He had walked through the village, and the people had fallen back before him, their faces a mixture of awe and accusation. He was no longer Tuan Jim, their lord and protector. He was a man walking to his own execution. He walked straight to the stockade of Doramin.

The old chief was there, sitting in state, surrounded by his armed retainers. Before him, on a piece of silk, lay the body of his only son, Dain Waris. The air was thick with a grief that had curdled into a desire for vengeance. And into this silence, Jim walked.

He stopped before the old man. He did not beg. He did not explain. He did not try to climb out of the consequences of his actions.

“He looked at the chief,” Karim’s voice was now a faint, awed breath. “And he said, ‘I have come. I am responsible. The promise was my life.’ He said that the debt was now due.”

And so he had stood, solitary and unflinching, a romantic to the last, paying the final price that reality demanded. He had fallen into a dream, as Stein had said, like a man falling into the sea. This time, he had not tried to climb out. He had submitted himself to the element. He had followed the dream, and again he had followed the dream, unto the very end.

“And Doramin?” I asked, though I knew the answer.

Karim closed his eyes. “The old chief had his pistols. The ones the Tuan Jim had given him long ago. A gift of trust. He took one up. He looked at the Tuan Jim, who did not flinch. And Doramin, raising the silver-mounted pistol, the one he had once given in trust... he did not hesitate.”

The whisper of that final consummation seemed to hang in the hot, still air of my room. He was gone. He had passed beyond my sight and my judgment, into the keeping of a dream he had successfully followed to its appointed end. The wreckage had washed ashore, and it was the story of a flawed and magnificent soul who had sought to be a very fine fellow and had paid for it with everything he had. Stein was right. That was the way. To immerse. He had not drowned, not truly. He had found his own dark, exalted way to be. And I, who had been his first and last witness in this perplexing world, was left with nothing but the echo of his conviction, a vast and uncertain expanse, a crepuscular horizon overshadowed in the centre, but circled, for a brief, splendid moment, with a bright edge of impossible light. Was it a victory? I do not know. I only know that for a time, in that far-off corner of the world, a man who was one of us had touched greatness. And I am left here, in the common light of common days, to wonder if the price was worth the dream. Perchance it is a question only the romantic can truly answer. And he is beyond all questions now.

He began to scrape the dottle from the bowl of his pipe with a small, specialized knife, the movements precise and economical. “I had a dream like that once. A dream of the land. A wife... a child... a place where the heart could be very full, as I told you. It was my shore. I had collected it, you see, piece by piece, like a specimen. The rarest of all specimens. And I put a glass case over it in my mind, to keep it safe.”

His hands stilled. He held the pipe in one hand and the small knife in the other, and for a long moment he stared into the shadows beyond the lamp’s bright circle, as if discerning there the ghosts of his past. The silence in the room grew heavy, populated by the faint, nocturnal rustlings of the tropical night outside and the immense, quiet presence of all those dead butterflies, each one the relic of a desire fulfilled.

“There was a man,” he began again, his voice lower, more intimate, “my principal enemy, as I said. It was a matter of trade, of course. Always, it begins with trade. He was simply a man like me, who also dreamed of the land. Our shores, it seemed, overlapped. For a long time we fought in the water, as is the way. A shipment lost here, a trading post burned there. He laid down the knife and the pipe and leaned forward, his elbows on the desk, his face earnest and drawn in the concentrated light. “He spread a rumour. A lie. It was a clever lie, because it was made of a small piece of truth, polished until it looked like the whole thing. It concerned my wife. A lie about her fidelity, you understand. In that place, at that time, such a thing was not a small matter. It was a poison that could dissolve the very ground from under your feet. It could turn your ‘lof’ into ashes. I watched him, fascinated and appalled. The placid philosopher was gone, and in his place was a man reliving the sharp, bitter taste of an old wound. The skin around his eyes was tight, his mouth a thin line.

“What did I do?” he asked, as if I had spoken the question aloud. “What would your romantic friend do? Your Jim? He would make a great gesture. He would challenge the man to a duel, perhaps. He would make a speech to the whole world declaring his wife’s honour and his own. He would try to build the shore up again, higher than before, with grand words and noble postures. He would fight the lie. He would try to climb out of the sea of doubt and suspicion. Ja?”

He nodded slowly. “And he would have failed. Because you cannot fight a whisper. You cannot shoot a shadow. The more you protest, the more people believe there is something to protest about. You thrash, and you sink. I have seen it. Many times.”

“So what did you do?” I asked, my voice hushed.

He picked up his pipe again, as if for comfort, holding the smooth, cool bowl in his palm. “I submitted myself to the element,” he said simply. “I did not deny the rumour. I did not affirm it. When people looked at me with those eyes, the curious and the pitying and the triumphant, I did not look away. I looked back, and I said nothing. I let the poison in. I let the doubt wash over me, over my house, over everything. It was... cold. It was the deep, deep sea. My wife... she was a brave woman. She looked at me, and she did not ask. She waited. But the silence... the silence grew between us. The lie was a third person in our bed. The child... a child feels such things. The house was no longer full. It was... hollow.”

He fell silent. The flame in the lamp seemed to burn with a preternatural stillness. I could picture it all: the tropical heat, the whispers in the marketplace, the averted eyes, the terrible, corrosive silence in that house which he had dreamed of as a sanctuary, his shore.

“For months,” he went on, his voice a near-whisper, “I lived in this sea. I did my work. I ate my meals. I spoke to my men. And all the time, the water was up to my chin. My enemy, he was very happy. He thought he had won. He saw me drowning. But I was not drowning. I was learning to breathe underwater. I was making the deep sea keep me up. I was accepting the world as it was, not as I wished it to be. The world is a place where such lies exist, where a man’s happiness can be taken from him by a puff of bad air. To deny this is to deny the nature of the water you are in. It is to be... romantic.”

He gave the word a soft, almost pitying weight. “And then, when my enemy was most certain of my defeat, when he was standing on his own little patch of shore laughing at the fool in the water... I acted. Not against the lie. The lie was now my element, my home. I acted against him. Directly. It was a matter of trade, you remember. I found a weakness in his affairs, a place where he had overextended himself, a thing he had done that was... not quite legal. A small thing. But I had been patient. I had been watching from below. I used my knowledge of the currents, you see. I gave a little push. A quiet word in the ear of a different kind of chief, a man in a uniform in Batavia. That was all. It was not a grand gesture. It was a small, precise, and... wet act. An act of the sea.”

He shrugged, a small, final movement. “He was ruined. He had to leave. His shore dissolved. He was the one who drowned, in the end. In a sea of debt and disgrace.”

“And you?” I asked, my throat dry. “Your shore? Your specimen?”

Stein’s face, for a fleeting instant, seemed to collapse. The lines deepened, the placid mask fell away, and I saw a grief so profound, so ancient, that I felt like an intruder, a voyeur of a sacred sorrow.

“Ah, yes,” he breathed out, and the sound was like the one he had made before, the one that had blown out the match. It was more than a sigh; it was the sound of a world emptying. “The shore... the specimen. The cure came too late. The specimen was already... damaged. You see, to live in the water for so long, you must become a creature of the water. Cold. Hard. You must grow gills, and scales. And when you finally crawl back onto the land... you find you cannot breathe the air anymore. The silence... it had done its work. The poison had been in the house too long. My wife... she looked at me after he was gone, and she saw what I had become to survive. She saw the man who had let the lie live in his house, who had made the deep sea keep him up. “She left me,” he said, and his voice was utterly devoid of emotion, a flat statement of a scientific fact. “She took the child. They went back to her people, across the sea. I never saw them again. The child... the child died of a fever, a year later. On the land, you see. In the clean air.” He smiled, a terrible, bloodless smile. “So you see, my friend. I saved my business. He made the sound again, a soft explosion of breath, and it was the most desolate sound I had ever heard. He had immersed himself in the destructive element, and it had kept him up, yes. But the shore he had dreamed of was gone forever, washed away not by the enemy’s wave, but by the very currents he had used to survive. He had won the battle and lost the world.

“So when I say to you, ‘in the destructive element immerse’,” he said, his voice regaining some of its didactic strength, though it was now freighted with an unbearable weariness, “I do not say it is a path to happiness. He finally picked up a match, struck it on the underside of the desk. The flare was violent, a sudden birth of light that made the shadows leap and shudder. The philosopher was back, seated behind a parapet of smoke.

“The question for your specimen,” he said, his voice a low rumble, “is not how to get his honour back. That is the thrashing. That is the dream of the shore. The question is, what can a man who has lost his honour do? What work can be done by a man in the water? How can he use the weight of his own fall, the very element that surrounds him, to exert a force? To hold something up? Perhaps not himself. Perhaps... something else.”

He gestured with the stem of his pipe towards the door, towards the world outside his strange sanctuary. “There is much work to be done in the world. And much of it must be done by men who are... damaged. Men who are not ‘fine fellows’. Men who have learned to breathe underwater. Your Jim... he is now one of them. He has been initiated. He has a new strength, if only he knew how to use it. The strength of having nothing left to lose. No shore to dream of. Tell him that. Tell him to stop looking for the shore. He fell silent, puffing on his pipe, his eyes half-closed in thought. The conference was over. He had given me not a cure, but a diagnosis so profound and so terrible that it felt like a sentence. To live, one had to die first. To be, one had to accept the impossibility of being the fine fellow one dreams of. It was a bleak and powerful wisdom, born of a desolation I could barely comprehend.

I stood up, my legs stiff. The air in the room felt thick, heavy with unspoken grief and the ghosts of dead dreams. “And you, Stein?” I asked softly. “What work did you find? In the sea?”

He opened his eyes and looked at me. It was a long, steady look, without artifice, without philosophy. It was the look of a man. “I collect butterflies,” he said. “Ah,” he said, and a faint, arid smile touched his lips. “The troubles of others. They are the most expensive kind. You are the English captain, are you not? The friend of the disgraced officer. The one who jumped.” He said the word “jumped” with a particular finality, as if it were a transaction that had been irrevocably closed.

“I am,” I answered, feeling a familiar prickle of defensiveness.

“I am Vermeer,” he said, as if that explained everything. He laid his pen down, aligning it perfectly with the edge of his ledger. “Schomberg’s partner. I handle the accounts. He handles the noise. A necessary division of labour. So. You have been to see the old butterfly man.”

It was not a question. In this town, the movements of a white man were tracked as closely as the barometer. Every visit, every conversation was noted, filed, and turned into a piece of social currency.

“I have,” I said.

“And he filled your head with poetry,” Vermeer stated, his tone flat. “He spoke of dreams, I imagine. Of destiny. Of the soul. He is very good at that. He talks of a man’s inner world as if it were a rare beetle from a distant jungle; something to be admired, classified, and pinned to a board. It is a harmless hobby. But it has nothing to do with this.” He gestured with a long, bony finger at the room around him, at the mountains of paper that documented the inexorable, unpoetic flow of goods and money. “This is the world, Captain. The world is a ledger. On one side, assets. On the other, liabilities. Everything, and everyone, ends up in one column or the other.”

“And a man like Jim?” I asked, drawn into his bleak catechism against my better judgement. “In which column do you place him?”

“Ah, your specimen,” he said, and the dry smile returned. “He is a simple entry. He was an asset; a ship’s officer, a tool of the company. A predictable component in the great machine of trade. Then, he performed an unpredictable action. He jumped. In that moment, he ceased to be an asset and became a liability. Not just to himself, but to the entire system. The system does not tolerate such things. It has a way of balancing its books.” He picked up a heavy, black-lacquered abacus from his desk, its wooden beads worn smooth with use. With a flick of his finger, he sent a bead clattering from one side to the other. “A debt is recorded. And a debt must be paid.”

His words, so devoid of emotion, so coldly practical, were in their own way more chilling than Stein’s tragic philosophy. Stein saw Jim as a romantic soul struggling in the sea; Vermeer saw him as a faulty piece of equipment that had been written off the books. “Stein believes there is a way to live with it,” I said, compelled to offer a counter-argument, to hold up the faint glimmer of starlight against this absolute darkness. “To submit to the destructive element.”

Vermeer gave a short, humourless laugh, a sound like dry leaves skittering across pavement. “The ‘destructive element’,” he scoffed. “A fine phrase. What does it mean? It means failure. It means you have fallen off the side of the ledger that matters. Submitting to it is not a philosophy, Captain; it is an accounting term for a bad debt. You write it off. You cut your losses. You move on to the next transaction. Stein can afford his poetry. His money is old. His fortune was made long ago, in ways we do not speak of, when the books were kept in blood, not ink. He collects butterflies because he can. He has balanced his own ledger. But your man, Jim… he has nothing. He has only the debt.”

He leaned forward, the lamplight glinting on his spectacles, and for the first time his voice took on a hint of something more than dispassionate calculation. It was the faintest trace of urgency, the kind a man might show when pointing out a fatal error in a sum. “Tell me, Captain. In all your discussions with the philosopher, did you speak of the Patna? Not the jump, not the shame, not the dream. The ship itself.”

“The ship?” I was taken aback. “What of it?”

“It is a ghost,” he said simply. “A ghost that still sails. It was salvaged, you know. Towed to port. Refitted. It has a new name now, a new flag, but it is the same hull. The same iron heart. It carries pilgrims again, year after year. It steams through these very waters. Schomberg saw it himself last month, passing in the strait. He said its new name was the ‘Vajra’.” He let the name hang in the air. “Imagine that. The ship he abandoned as sinking, sails on. Whole and profitable. While he, the man who was meant to command her, is sunk. Is that not a fine piece of irony? It is the kind of thing the universe enjoys. A perfect, symmetrical piece of accounting.”

The image was devastating. I saw it at once; Jim, marooned in his own failure, forced to watch the very vessel of his disgrace sail past, sound and intact, a monument to his own inadequacy. It was a detail so cruel, so exquisitely tailored to torment, that it could only be true. “He does not know this,” I murmured, more to myself than to Vermeer.

“He will,” Vermeer said, his voice returning to its flat, clerk-like tone. “Truth has a way of arriving. Like a bill for services rendered. It always finds you. And you are going to him, are you not? To Patusan. To that little kingdom he has built for himself on a swamp. Another dream. Another fiction he has constructed to avoid the facts.” He picked up his pen again. “Be careful what news you carry, Captain. Words are also assets and liabilities. You came from one man who sells dreams. You came to another who counts costs. Which will you be? A poet, or an accountant?”

He bent his head back over his ledger, the arc of his spine a question mark against the clutter of the room. The audience was over. I was a figure that had been entered, assessed, and now dismissed. I turned and walked out of the office, leaving the small, precise man to his endless sums, to his balancing of the world’s grim accounts.

The air outside felt colder. The darkness was no longer mysterious; it was simply empty. Stein’s last words, which had followed me from his house like a faint, spectral music, were now drowned out by the harsh, clattering sound of Vermeer’s abacus. Stars in the water. The phrase now seemed a mockery. What good were stars when you were chained to the bottom, watching the ship that should have been yours sail on under a different flag?

I had set out that evening to find a way for Jim to live, and I had been given two answers, two impossible paths. One was to immerse oneself in a dream, to follow it to the end, a path that led through pitfalls and over graves, illuminated by a deceptive, crepuscular light. The other was to face the ledger, to see oneself as a sum of one’s errors, a liability to be accounted for, a debt to be paid. One was the way of the poet, the other the way of the accountant. Both, in their own fashion, were sentences of death. One a death by drowning in visions, the other a death by a thousand cuts of fact.

And I, the messenger, the go-between, stood on the dark waterfront with these two poisons in my hands. The weight of my task had become immense, unbearable. To describe a specimen, I had told Stein. And he had replied, “Well--I am a man too.” But was Jim? Or was he, as Vermeer had so brutally insisted, merely a ghost already? A walking deficit, a footnote in a ledger full of other men’s profits.

I looked up at the sky. The true stars were out, a brilliant, indifferent spray of diamonds on black velvet. They offered no guidance. They did not seem to care whether a man drowned or swam, whether he dreamed or counted costs. They were simply there, shining with a cold, ancient light on the sordid little heap of mud where men like me, and men like Jim, struggled to find a way to be. And the terrible thought occurred to me, a thought that was neither Stein’s nor Vermeer’s, but entirely my own; that perhaps the most destructive element of all was not the sea, or the world, or the facts, but the very light of consciousness that forced a man to see his own drowning, and to see, at the very same moment, the cold, impassive beauty of the stars above.

was the light of a certain knowledge; the kind of knowledge that does not illuminate but only serves to define the vastness of the surrounding darkness. I stood there for a time that had no measure, caught between the abyss of stars above and their mocking reflection in the harbour’s foul water, a man suspended between two infinites of indifference. The counsel of Stein, with its poetic and terrible grandeur, and the judgment of Vermeer, with its cold and unassailable logic, were like two millstones, and I felt my own soul being ground to a fine dust between them. There was no escape in either. To follow the dream into the destructive element was a kind of magnificent suicide; to accept the ledger’s tally was to be executed by a thousand facts, to have one’s ghost audited and found wanting. I had been sent for a remedy, and I was returning with two distinct forms of poison.

The weight of this knowledge pressed upon my shoulders, a physical burden, as I finally turned my back on the deceptive peace of the waterfront. The narrow streets of the port town coiled around me like the intestines of some great, sleeping beast. The air, which by the water had tasted of salt and distance, was here thick with the odours of stale arrack, of frying fish, of damp earth and unwashed humanity; the very breath of life in its most squalid and insistent form. From the lighted doorways of grog-shops, bursts of discordant music and coarse laughter spilled out, fragments of a world that had made its peace with its own heap of mud, a world that had no time for fine fellows who fell into the sea. These men, with their sun-blackened faces and their loud, simple desires, were they not also following a dream? A dream of a full bottle, a willing woman, a night’s oblivion before the next day’s toil. Perhaps Stein’s formula was more universal than I had supposed; perhaps it was only the quality of the dream that differed, and the depth of the sea.

I was making my way towards the hotel where I had left him, a place of transient respectability that seemed to hold its breath against the encroaching chaos of the port. Its white facade was a ghostly presence in the gloom. As I approached, a man emerged from the entrance, a solid, red-faced man in a crisp white suit, and I recognised him at once. It was a Captain O’Brien, master of a fine steamer that had departed that very afternoon on the tide. He was a man of unshakeable certainties, a man for whom the world was a simple chart of channels and shoals, of profitable cargoes and predictable monsoons. He saw me and his broad face broke into a grin of uncomplicated fellowship.

“Marlow! By all that’s holy. I thought you’d be halfway to tomorrow by now. Missed your berth, did you? Or found some local attraction too powerful to resist, eh?” His voice was a hearty boom that seemed to sweep the philosophical cobwebs from the air. He clapped me on the shoulder with a hand that felt like a side of beef.

I managed a smile, a thin and inadequate thing. “Just some business, O’Brien. Tying up a loose end.”

“Loose ends! The devil of a life you lead, Marlow. Always poking about in the queer corners of this world. Me, I like things shipshape. Accounts settled, papers signed, and the open sea ahead. No room for loose ends on my bridge.” He puffed out his chest, a monument to satisfactory conclusions. He was not a Vermeer, for he lacked the accountant’s venomous precision, but he belonged to the same species; the men who knew the rules and for whom the rules worked. He had never, I was certain, had to ask himself the question of how to be. He simply was, as solid and unquestionable as his ship’s engine.

“You’re sailing with a good crew, I trust?” I asked, for the sake of saying something, for the sake of delaying for one more moment the interview that awaited me.

“The best! Not a single man jack of ‘em I’d have to think twice about in a blow. Know their work, do their work, and draw their pay. That’s the way of it. You get one of those dreaming types on board, one of those fellows who’s always staring at the horizon as if he expects to see God Almighty step out of a cloud, and you’re asking for trouble. Had one once. A good sailor, mind you, but his head was full of books and grand notions. Nearly lost us the ship off the Palawan coast because he was wondering about the meaning of a squall instead of shortening sail.” He laughed, a great, rumbling sound. “You have to be in the world, Marlow, not in your head. That’s my motto.”

He clapped my shoulder again, a gesture of finality. “Well, I’m for a last drink before I find a bed. The agent’s putting me up. A pity you’re not coming with us. Good voyage to you, whenever you do sail.” He gave me a last, cheerful nod and strode off into the darkness, his solid form a reassurance, a proclamation that the world, for all its mysteries, was a manageable and profitable enterprise. He was a man who did not need to immerse himself in the destructive element; he sailed confidently on its surface, and the sea, for him, was a servant, not a god.

His hearty presence had been like a blast of cold, clean air, but it left the atmosphere of my own thoughts more rarefied, more suffocating than before. He was the proof that a man could live without being a romantic, without being a specimen for analysis. He was the simple, brutal fact against which Jim’s entire existence seemed a fantastic and sorrowful fiction.

I went into the hotel. The air was still and cool, scented with floor wax and fading flowers. The clerk at the desk, a sallow Eurasian with indifferent eyes, gave me a key without looking up from his newspaper. The silence of the upper corridor was profound, a silence of waiting. I felt like a physician approaching the bedside of a patient for whom he has no cure, only a diagnosis that is also a death sentence.

He was not in his room. The bed was untouched. I found him where I might have known he would be, on the deep, shadowed veranda at the back of the building, a place that overlooked a tangle of overgrown garden. He was standing at the balustrade, a white figure against the nearly absolute blackness, looking out not at the garden but up at the sky, at those same impassive stars that had offered me no comfort. He did not turn as I approached, but I knew he had heard me. The very stillness of his shoulders was a sign of his awareness.

“They are a long way off,” he said, his voice quiet, without its usual boyish resonance. It was the voice of a man who has been keeping a long and lonely vigil.

“Yes,” I said, coming to stand beside him. The air was heavy with the scent of night-blooming jasmine, a sickly sweet perfume that seemed to rise from the dark garden like a vapour from a grave.

He was silent for a while, and in the faint light that filtered from the hotel windows I could see his profile, clear-cut and fine, a youthful head on which a great and invisible weight had been placed. He was still one of us, still a man who could command a ship, still a man who could be trusted, and yet... he was not. He was set apart.

“You saw them,” he said at last, not a question but a statement. He turned to face me, and his eyes, in the deep shadow, seemed enormous, dark pools of inquiry. “You have the answer for me. How to live.”

The directness of the question was a blow. How could I present him with Stein’s crepuscular horizon, with Vermeer’s merciless balance sheet? How could I say: one man of wisdom tells you to drown, and another tells you that you are already drowned, a ghost haunting the world of solvent men?

“I have... opinions,” I said, the word sounding feeble and absurd. “Two of them. From two very different men.”

“Only two?” he asked, with a ghost of his old, confident irony. “I should have thought the world was full of opinions on a man like me.”

“These were... considered ones,” I said, struggling for the right tone. “From men who have looked at the world for a long time.” I paused, gathering my thoughts, searching for a way to translate the untranslatable. “One man... a man of business... a man who sees the world as a set of accounts... he sees a... a liability. A risk. He sees the fact of what happened. For him, the fact is everything. It is a debt that can never be paid, only carried. His advice is to... to accept the accounting. To be what the world has judged you to be.”

He listened without moving, his face a pale oval in the gloom. When I finished, a tremor went through him, a faint, visible shiver. “To be a coward,” he said, the words barely audible. “To wear it like a brand for all to see. For ever.”

“It is not what I...”

“No,” he interrupted, his voice gaining a sudden, hard strength. “But it is what he means. It is the world’s way. I know it. I have seen it in every eye that has looked at me for months. The little smiles. The careful questions. The sudden silence when I enter a room. I am a fact. A shameful fact.” He turned away from me again, gripping the wooden rail of the balustrade so hard his knuckles shone white. “And the other? The other opinion?”

Here was the true difficulty. How to offer the dream without sounding like a madman or a tempter? “The other,” I began slowly, my voice feeling thick in my own throat, “is a man who collects... beautiful things. Perfect things. He is a philosopher, a man who has lived a great deal. He believes... he believes that the world of facts, the world of ledgers and judgments... is not the only one. He believes in the dream.”

At that word, “dream,” he turned back to me, his whole attitude one of intense, almost desperate concentration. “The dream,” he repeated softly.

“Yes. He says a man is born and falls into a dream as a man falls into the sea. And to try to climb out, to struggle against it... that is the way to drown. His advice... his remedy...” I took a breath. “Is to submit. To immerse yourself in the destructive element. To let the sea, the dream, keep you up. To follow it. To follow the dream, and again to follow the dream... to the very end.”

I had delivered it, Stein’s terrible and magnificent gospel. For a long moment, Jim stood utterly still, as if he had been turned to stone. The sweet, heavy scent of the jasmine seemed to thicken around us. Then he let out a long, slow sigh, not of despair, but of a profound, an infinite relief. It was the sound of a man who, having been told he is condemned to die, is suddenly offered a reprieve, however strange, however perilous.

“Yes,” he whispered, and his face was illuminated by a smile of such dazzling, tragic beauty that it pierced me to the heart. “Yes. That is it. That is the way.”

I was aghast. He had seized upon it with the blind ardour of a zealot. He had seen the poetry and missed the peril. He saw the dream, the following of the dream, but he did not see the graves and pitfalls that lay under Stein’s crepuscular light. He did not understand the nature of the destructive element. For him, it was not the sea of life’s brutal compromises and failures, but the shining ocean of his own romantic fantasy.

“Jim,” I said, my voice urgent. “You must understand. It is not simple. This man, Stein... he speaks of a way to live, but it is a way that is... fraught with danger. It requires a kind of strength, a kind of... fidelity to the dream that is almost impossible.”

“But I have it!” he declared, taking a step towards me, his eyes blazing with a renewed fire. “Don’t you see? That is all I have ever had! It was the dream that I followed. The dream of being... of being the right sort. The man who is always ready, who never fails. And I did fail! I fell out of my dream. And now... now this man, this wise man of yours, he has told me how to get back. Not to climb out, but to go deeper! To immerse! Of course!”

He was transfigured. The brooding, haunted figure was gone, replaced by the old Jim, the Jim of the training ship, the Jim I had first known, full of confidence and a magnificent, unthinking belief in himself. He had taken Stein’s complex, despairing philosophy and turned it into a simple, heroic creed for his own justification. He had found not a way to live, but a way to redeem his own image of himself in his own eyes. He saw himself as the romantic hero, submitting to the great, tragic forces of his destiny.

“He is a fine fellow,” he said, more to himself than to me. “This Stein of yours. He understands.”

“He understands that it is a terrible thing,” I said quietly.

“Terrible? No. It is glorious.” He paced the veranda now, his white suit a restless gleam in the darkness. “To follow the dream... usque ad finem. To the end. Yes. I will.” He stopped and looked at me, his face alight with a kind of holy enthusiasm. “I will. I will find a place where the facts don't matter. A place so remote that a man can live by his dream alone. There must be such a place, Marlow. A corner of the world the accountants have not yet reached.”

He was describing Patusan, though neither of us knew it then. He was describing the life and the death that awaited him. He had taken the two poisons I had brought him, and spat out Vermeer’s bitter dose to swallow Stein’s ambrosia whole, without tasting the hemlock mixed within it.

I felt a profound and weary sadness. I had come to describe a specimen, and the specimen, upon hearing the description, had declared itself a creature of a higher order, a being not of flesh but of spirit, destined for a special fate. A moth, hearing the naturalist describe its attraction to the flame, and flying into it not with blind instinct, but with a passionate and reasoned conviction that the fire was its true and only home.

“So you have your answer,” I said. There was nothing more to say. The conference was over. He had his remedy.

He came and put his hand on my shoulder, a gesture of warm, almost pitying friendship. “You have been a good friend to me, Marlow. The very best. You brought me the only words that could have saved me from... from the other thing. From the ledger. You must not look so grim. It is a victory.”

A victory. Perhaps it was. A victory of the imagination over the sordid facts of existence. But as I looked at his exalted face, I saw not a victor, but a man who had just signed his own death warrant with a flourish, believing it to be a declaration of independence. The light of consciousness, which I had cursed on the waterfront, had in his case been turned into a spotlight for his own heroic drama. He did not see the drowning; he saw only the magnificent gesture of the immersion. He did not see the cold stars; he saw a celestial audience for his final act.

I left him on the veranda, a solitary white figure charting his course for a sea of dreams, and I went to my own room, not to sleep, but to sit by a dark window and confront the nature of my own complicity. I had been the messenger. I had delivered the words. And the words had done their work. Stein, the wise and lonely man among his dead butterflies, and Jim, the flawed specimen who refused to be pinned, were bound together now by a philosophy that was as beautiful and as deadly as one of those perfect, rare creatures under the glass. And I, the go-between, was left with the knowledge that in trying to find a way for a man to live, I had only succeeded in showing him a more splendid way to die. The heap of mud remained, but Jim was determined to build his castle on it, a castle of dreams from which he would, in the end, surely fall.

“We are all part of the apparatus of each other’s fate, my friend,” he replied softly. “That is the real destructive element. Not the sea, but the other swimmers. He turned back into the light of his study, back to his silent companions under the glass, and I was left alone, facing the night. The path away from his house was a narrow track through a riot of vegetation that whispered and rustled with a million tiny lives. The air was thick, heavy with the perfume of frangipani and the faint, underlying scent of mud and decay, the smell of life and death inextricably mingled. It was a long walk back to the harbour, and with every step I felt myself moving from one world to another; from the realm of shadows and vast, noble, and terrible ideas, to the world of hard surfaces, of money, of cargo, of men who lived by the clock and the tide table.

The waterfront, when I reached it, was a different kind of darkness. It was a place of stark contrasts; pools of harsh, yellow light from the open doors of godowns and grog-shops, spilling out onto a quayside of profound blackness, a blackness made more absolute by the faint, greasy sheen of the water beside it. The air here was soured with the smells of rope tar, stale beer, and the brackish exhalations of the harbour mud at low tide. The silence of Stein’s hill was replaced by a discordant symphony: the distant clang of metal on metal from a ship’s engine room, a burst of drunken laughter from a doorway, the mournful creak of a mooring rope taking the strain of the tide. This was the world of fact, the heap of mud from which Jim wished to ascend.

I was seeking my own ship, my own familiar berth in this concrete and perplexed world, when a voice called my name. It was a voice I knew, a thick, guttural sound that seemed to have been aged in brine and cheap brandy. “Marlow! So, you too are a creature of the night. Come, share a glass. The night is long, and the company of honest men is scarce.”

It was Reynaud, captain of a small, disreputable French steamer that seemed to be permanently engaged in litigation and rust. He was a man built on a square plan, with a face like a weathered map of some forgotten and inhospitable coastline. He sat alone at a rickety iron table outside a cafe that catered to the less fastidious officers of the merchant marine. On the table was a bottle of absinthe and a single, cloudy glass. He was the absolute antithesis of Stein; where Stein collected butterflies, Reynaud collected grievances.

I was in no mood for his particular brand of cynicism, but the alternative was my own company, which at that moment seemed even less palatable. I pulled up a chair. “Reynaud,” I acknowledged. “You look as though you are holding a wake.”

He gave a short, barking laugh. “Every night is a wake, my friend. A wake for the profits that were not made, for the cargoes that were damaged, for the owners who are all thieves and accountants. What else is there to do in this life but to mourn its absurdities?” He poured a measure of the green liquid into a second glass he had procured from somewhere, and pushed it towards me. “But you, you have the look of a man who has been communing with spirits. Have you been up the hill to see the old German and his dead insects?”

The question was so direct, so uncannily perceptive, that I was taken aback. “You know Stein?”

“Everyone knows Stein,” he grunted, splashing water into his glass and watching the green fluid turn a milky, malevolent white. “The philosopher of the jungle. The man who talks of dreams. I have heard the sailors’ tales. They say he is a magician. I say he is a man who had one piece of good luck a long time ago and has been dining out on it ever since. Dreams.” He spat the word as if it were a piece of grit. “Do you know what a dream gets you in this world, Marlow? It gets you a berth on a leaky ship with a lying owner and a cargo of wet copra. It gets you a fever in a port where the doctor is drunk. It gets you a knife in the back for a woman who will be with another man before your body is cold.”

He drank, his eyes, small and hard as pebbles, fixed on me over the rim of the glass. “I had a dreamer for a first mate once. A young man, from a good family in Brest. He had read too many books. He believed in the ‘romance of the sea.’ He saw poetry in a squall and destiny in a landfall. He used to stand on the bridge in a gale, his hair plastered to his forehead, with a look of ecstasy on his face, as if God were speaking to him personally through the wind.”

“What happened to him?” I asked, feeling a cold premonition, a sense of an inexorable pattern repeating itself.

“What do you think happened to him?” Reynaud said, his voice flat. “We were running before a monsoon in the Sunda Strait. A big sea was coming up behind us, a great wall of black water with a white crest, the kind of sea that has no poetry in it at all, only weight. Tonnes of it. I am at the helm, my hands and my eyes and every muscle in my body focused on one thing: keeping the stern of this old tub square to the wave. It is not a matter of dreams, you see. It is a matter of physics. Of angles. Of a few degrees of rudder to the right or to the left. But my romantic, he is not looking at the compass. He is not watching the sea. He is looking at the sky, which has turned a rather dramatic shade of purple. He is having a moment. An experience. And in that moment, a cross-swell, a smaller, meaner wave from the side, catches our quarter. The ship gives a sickening lurch. He is not braced. He is dreaming. He goes over the side as easily as a man tipping his hat.”

For a moment, the two visions merged in my mind: Jim, striding purposefully into a sea of dreams, and Reynaud’s mate, tumbling accidentally into a sea of water. Both were gone. “You paint a grim picture, Reynaud,” I said, pushing my untouched glass away.

“I paint the world as it is,” he retorted. “It is you, and your friend the butterfly catcher, who insist on painting it as you wish it were. You are artists. I am a navigator. He had, with the unconscious cruelty of a completely practical man, diagnosed Jim’s case as surely as Stein had. A beautiful, noble shipwreck. That was it. That was the goal. The difference was that Stein saw the nobility, and Reynaud saw only the wreck. And I... I saw both. I saw the tragic grandeur and the pathetic waste, all at once.

I stood up, the sour taste of Reynaud’s worldview in my mouth. “I must be going,” I said.

“Of course,” he said, not bothering to rise. “The righteous must get their rest. But remember this, Marlow. The sea does not care about your dreams. The rocks do not care about your honor. This, and a healthy sense of your own insignificance.”

I left him there, a square, solid man nursing his bottle of green poison under the indifferent stars, a high priest of the faith of futility. I walked along the dark quay, the sound of the lapping water beside me. Reynaud’s words had not dispelled the power of Stein’s philosophy; they had, in a strange way, reinforced it. They had laid bare the alternative. On the one side was Jim, seeking to immerse himself in the dream, to become a legend, a magnificent butterfly. On the other was Reynaud, immersing himself in absinthe, seeking only to endure the sordid reality, a crab scuttling in the mud. To live as a god, or to live as a beast. And between them, nothing. Was there no third way? No way to live as a man, on the solid ground of the world, with your eyes open to both the mud and the stars? It seemed not. It seemed the choice was absolute. You had to submit to one element or the other. I thought of my own life, of the ships and the rivers, of the faces seen in the glow of binnacle lamps, of the tales heard and the silences kept. I was the witness, as Stein had said. Brown, retreating down the river, had turned his guns on the party of Dain Waris, who had been sent to watch his departure. A volley in the dark. A senseless slaughter. I sat in the deepening twilight, the fan overhead stirring the hot, heavy air. I had the end of the story. The ledger was complete. Jim had not tried to climb out of the sea. He had seen the magnificent shipwreck, and it had not confirmed his faith; it had revealed its utter hollowness. To win the argument was to be left with nothing but the bleak, sterile truth of it, alone in the dark.

Perhaps we are all of us forced to choose: the glorious, fatal immersion in the sea of dreams, or the long, slow petrification on the shore of reality. I, the witness, the keeper of the ledger, was left to walk the tideline between them, forever gathering the beautiful, broken shells washed up by the tide, telling their stories to the wind. They introduce uncertainty. They act out of ‘honour,’ out of ‘love,’ out of some ‘idea,’ and in so doing they sink the ship for everyone. It is not magnificent. It is inefficient.”

I sat there, silent, transfixed by the sheer, unadorned horror of his vision. It was a third way, a way I had not considered. Not the glorious drowning in the sea of dreams, not the sterile petrification on the shore of reality, but the paving over of both sea and shore to create one vast, endless marketplace. “And a man?” I asked, my voice a whisper. “What is a man in this world of yours?”

“A man?” Bjerregaard looked genuinely puzzled. “A man is a producer. Or a consumer. Or a unit of labour. He is an asset or a liability. I stood there for a long time, on the edge of the land, the smell of the water in my nostrils, the sound of it in my ears. I felt like a man caught between three warring kingdoms. Behind me, the kingdom of the ledger, of Bjerregaard, all straight lines and hard numbers, a place of immense, soul-crushing order. My work. What was it, in the end? It was not to choose. It was not to judge. Bjerregaard’s world was a kind of hell, but a man might live there a long and prosperous life. To tell the tale was, I began to understand, my own way of navigating these treacherous waters. It was my own form of immersion. I could not be a Jim, for I lacked his magnificent, fatal innocence. I could not be a Reynaud, for the memory of the dream, once seen, could never be entirely scoured away. And I could not, God forbid, be a Bjerregaard, for what profit is it to a man if he gains the whole world and loses his own soul’s capacity for wonder and for sorrow?

No, I was the storyteller. And the story was my raft. By clinging to it, by shaping it, by turning it over and over, I was not submitting to the destructive element, perhaps, but I was using its own energy to keep my head above water. To tell the story of Jim was to keep him from being merely a bad debt in Bjerregaard’s ledger. To tell the story of Reynaud was to remember that even on the driest shore, a man can still weep. To tell the story of Stein and his butterfly was to affirm that the desire for the perfect, the unattainable, was in itself a thing of terrible, fragile beauty.

The first hint of dawn was beginning to touch the sky, that pale, uncertain, crepuscular light I knew so well. It was the colour of ambiguity, the colour of a question that had no answer. It fell without prejudice on the calm water, on the solid mass of Bjerregaard’s warehouses, and on my own tired face. It was the light in which I did my work. And I saw, with a clarity that was neither joyful nor despairing, but simply was, that the work would never be finished. There would always be another Jim, another specimen to describe, another story that demanded to be told. The abyss would remain, circled with its bright and menacing flames. And on the desolate plain, new graves would be dug, and new dreamers would stumble towards them, their eyes fixed on a star. And I would be there to watch. To record. To testify. It was not a life for a poet, no. Nor for a man of business. It was the life of a witness. And for me, in the end, it was the only way. To look at it all, and to speak. To speak into the wind, to the waves, to any man who would listen. To speak, and so, somehow, to stay afloat.

To be romantic is to be in love with the dream itself. I am not in love with the dream, my friend. I am in a state of war with it. A war that I know I cannot win, and so I must negotiate a truce. That is not romance; it is... a kind of politics. The politics of the soul.”

He leaned back, the old wicker of his chair groaning a weary protest. The smoke from his pipe, which he had finally managed to light, rose in a placid column, a fragile pillar supporting the vast, invisible ceiling of his thoughts. “You see the butterfly,” he said, gesturing with the stem of his pipe towards the glass case, now returned to its place of honour on a shelf crowded with the silent, jewelled dead. “You see it and you think, ‘Ah, the dream made real!’ A moment of perfection. But that is the end of the story, not the beginning. The beginning is the desire. The crawling on the earth. The great privations. The risk. The romance is in the wanting, you see. The man who wants. But the man who has... what is he? He is a man with a dead thing in a glass box. A beautiful dead thing, yes. A perfect memory. But the wanting is finished. The dream is over. And he must go on living.”

He fell silent for a long moment, his eyes half-closed, contemplating the blue-grey wreath of smoke as if it were the very ghost of his discourse. I felt a pressure to speak, to defend my choice of word, but a profound stillness had fallen upon the room, a stillness that seemed to emanate from him, from the quiet certitude of his disillusionment. It was he who broke it, his voice shifting from the philosophical to the practical, though with no discernible jolt.

“This specimen of yours,” he said, his gaze returning to me, sharp and focused now. “This man. You say he is not so perfect. Tell me. In what way is he broken?”

The question was so direct, so like a physician inquiring after a symptom, that it took me aback. I had come to describe a state of being, a spiritual quandary, and he was asking for a clinical assessment. “Broken?” I repeated, fumbling for a reply. “He has lost his way. He has... failed his own idea of himself. He fell into the sea, as you say, and the fall has shattered him.”

“Shattered,” Stein echoed thoughtfully. He took the pipe from his lips and examined the bowl with an air of intense concentration, as if the secrets of the human heart might be read in the glowing embers of the tobacco. “Shattered is one thing. A vase that is shattered is only fit for the rubbish heap. But a ship that is shattered... ah, that is another matter. From the wreckage of a ship a man can build a raft. If he has the tools. If he has the will. Does your man have the will?”

“He has the will to suffer,” I said. “He punishes himself without end. He lives in a hell of his own making, because he cannot forgive himself for not being a hero.”

“Ach, a hero,” Stein sighed, a sound of immense weariness. “Another word. Like ‘romantic.’ A word for songs, for stories told to children. In real life there are no heroes. There are only men who do what must be done, and men who do not. And sometimes the thing that must be done is a terrible thing. And sometimes the thing that is not done is the thing that will haunt you forever. It is not about being a hero. It is about... how to be. You were right. That is the question.”

He stood up then, not with the sudden energy he had shown before, but with a slow, deliberate movement, as if his limbs were heavy with the weight of his years, of his memories. He walked away from the lamplight, his slippers whispering on the floorboards, and disappeared into the same dusk where he had earlier held his spectral conversation with the ghosts of his butterflies. I heard the faint click of a cabinet latch, the rustle of papers or cloth, a soft metallic clink. The sounds were mundane, yet in that charged atmosphere they seemed invested with a mysterious significance. He was not gone for long. When he returned to the circle of light, he was carrying something in the palm of his hand.

He did not present it with the flourish he had used for the butterfly. He simply came back to his chair, sat down, and placed the object on the desk between us. It was a ship’s compass. It was old, the brass casing greened with the patient corrosion of sea-salt and time. The glass was thick and scratched, and the liquid within, in which the card was suspended, had yellowed to the colour of old varnish. It was a heavy, solid, utilitarian thing, the very antithesis of the airy, transcendent beauty of the butterfly.

“You see this?” he asked, his finger tapping the scarred glass. I nodded. “This is not a perfect specimen. Far from it. The housing is dented here, you see? From a fall. The liquid is clouded. Sometimes, in a storm, the card sticks. It does not point true. It is a flawed instrument. And yet...” He paused, and a strange, faint smile touched his lips, a smile of affection for this battered object. “And yet, it saved my life. And the lives of three other men.”

He leaned forward, his voice dropping into a confidential, narrative tone. “This was long ago. Before the butterfly. Before the wife, the child. When I was still collecting for the professor. We were in the interior, a terrible place of mud and fever, searching for a species of orchid that no white man had ever seen. The professor, he was a man possessed by a dream. A dream of a flower. For this flower he would have given his life, and ours too, I think. We found it, after weeks of misery. A thing of such beauty... it made you believe in God, and in the Devil too, for such a perfect thing to grow in such a hell. We had it. The dream. In a special box. But on the way back, our boat was overturned in the rapids. All was lost. The specimens, the food, the rifles. Everything. All except what we had on our bodies. And me... I had this.”

He laid his hand flat over the compass, a gesture that was both protective and proprietary. “The professor, he was like your man. Shattered. His dream was at the bottom of the river. He wanted to lie down and die. He said there was no point. The others, they looked to him, their leader, and they saw only despair. They too began to lose hope. They talked of walking east, towards a sunrise they could not see. Of following the river, which twisted like a dying snake. They had lost their dream, you see, and so they had lost their way. They had fallen into the sea.”

He lifted his hand and pointed a finger at the compass. “But I had this. This imperfect, damaged, obstinate thing. I did not have a dream anymore. The orchid was gone. What I had was a direction. North. I knew that if we walked north, for one hundred miles, through a forest no one had ever mapped, we would strike the coast. Maybe. It was not a certainty. It was not a promise of paradise. It was just... north. It was a fact. A hard, stubborn, stupid fact. And I said to them, ‘The dream is dead. Now we must live.’ And I held up this compass. I made them believe in it. Not because it was beautiful. Not because it was perfect. But because it was all we had. We walked for twenty-seven days. We ate things I will not speak of. The professor died on the tenth day. He simply sat down and would not get up. He was still dreaming of his flower. But the other three, and me, we kept our eyes on this needle. We submitted to its tyranny. And we lived.”

He pushed the compass slowly across the desk towards me. It slid over the polished wood with a low, rasping sound. “Your man,” he said, his voice quiet but with an edge of steel. “He is in love with his own fall. He is in love with the memory of the height from which he fell. He is like the professor, dreaming of a flower at the bottom of a river. He sees himself as a fine fellow who failed. He must be made to see himself as a man who survived. That is all. Survival is not a romance. It is a grim business. It is the business of a faulty compass and a long walk in the dark.”

I looked from his face, etched with the memory of that terrible journey, to the object before me. It lay there, squat and uncompromising, a piece of irrefutable reality. The butterfly was a poem. This was a tool. The butterfly was the leap of faith; this was the plodding, step-by-step trudge that followed. The contrast was so stark, so absolute, that it seemed to contain the whole of his philosophy, the whole of the bitter wisdom he had purchased at such a price.

“So the remedy...” I began, my voice trailing off as I tried to grasp the immensity of what he was suggesting. “The remedy is to... to give up the dream?”

“No!” he exclaimed, striking the desk with the flat of his hand, making the old compass jump. “Never! To give up the dream is to die. Like the professor. The dream is what makes you fall into the sea in the first place. You cannot un-dream it. Gott im Himmel, no! The remedy is to find a compass. The remedy is to stop looking at the sky where you thought you belonged, and to start looking for a direction, any direction, that leads through the water. He wants to be a fine fellow? Good! Let him be a fine fellow in the midst of his disgrace. Let him be the best man who ever drowned and yet learned to breathe water. The dream is the ‘why.’ The compass is the ‘how.’ His dream of himself, the fine, honourable man, that is his butterfly. He caught it, and it was killed in his hands. Now he sits staring at the dead thing. You must tell him to put it away in a box. To remember its beauty. But to navigate by something else. By a duty. By a promise. By a piece of work. By a direction. North. It does not matter what, so long as it is not the dream itself. He must immerse himself not in the memory of the fall, but in the task of swimming.”

He leaned back, his sudden burst of energy expended. The silence that followed was different from the last. It was not empty, but filled with the weight of this new, terrible, and practical knowledge. The image of Jim, as I had last seen him, rose before my eyes: a man adrift, his gaze fixed on the unreachable shore of his own past. Stein was right. He was a man staring at a dead butterfly, believing it was the only thing of value in the universe.

“And what if he has no compass?” I asked softly. “What if the wreck left him with nothing?”

Stein’s eyes, which had been contemplating the shadows, now fixed on me with a look of profound, almost sorrowful, pity. “Then he must make one,” he said. “From the wreckage. From a piece of rusted iron and a splinter of wood and a thread from his own coat. He must magnetise the needle with his own will. It will be a poor compass. It will stick. It will lie. But it will be his. And it will be better than nothing. It will be a start. The way is to the destructive element submit yourself... yes. But to submit is not to surrender. It is to accept the medium in which you find yourself. And then you must work. With your hands and feet. With your mind. With your compass. You must make the sea keep you up.”

He reached out and laid his hand on my arm. His touch was surprisingly firm, the grip of a man who had held on to things, to life itself, by sheer force. “You are his friend,” he said, and the simple statement felt like both a judgment and a commission. “You are the one who has come here. You are a part of his world now. Perhaps... perhaps you are his compass. For a time. You can show him a direction. Not to the man he was. That country is lost, a drowned island. No. A direction towards the man he could be. A man who lives. A man who works. A man who has looked into the abyss and has not thrown himself in, but has instead learned the grim art of keeping his balance on the edge.”

He withdrew his hand and sighed, a long, slow exhalation that seemed to carry with it the last of his strength. He looked old, suddenly, and very tired. The lamplight carved deep shadows under his eyes and in the hollows of his cheeks. He had given me all he had to give: the story of his triumph in the butterfly, and the story of his survival in the compass. One was the story of a dream fulfilled, the other the story of a life pieced together after the dream was lost. And it was the second story, I knew, that held the key.

“Go now,” he said, his voice a near whisper. “It is late. The night is almost over.” He made a small, dismissive gesture with his hand, as if to ward off any thanks. He had not been speaking to me, not really. He had been speaking to a ghost, the ghost of his younger self, and through that ghost, to the ghost of the man Jim might have been. The consultation was over. He turned his chair slightly, away from me and towards the serried ranks of his silent collection, a king returning to his court of the dead.

I stood up, my legs stiff. I picked up the old brass compass from the desk. It was heavy in my hand, a solid, dense reality. I looked at Stein’s back. He did not turn. I placed the compass back on the desk, beside the scattered slips of paper on which he had been describing his latest treasure. A tool for living, next to a testament of a perfect death. I left it there. It was his, not mine to take. The wisdom, however, I carried with me as I walked out of the bright circle of the lamp, through the familiar ring of fainter light, and towards the door. The path of my own duty seemed, for the first time, to have a direction. It was not an easy one, nor a pleasant one. It was a long, dark walk, and I did not know if it led to any coast worth reaching. But it was north. It was a start. And as I stepped out into the pre-dawn gloom of Patusan, into that uncertain, crepuscular light that was the colour of all human endeavour, I felt the immense, crushing weight of the message I had to deliver. I had to teach a man in love with his own ghost how to build a compass from the shards of his soul.
