His potted ham from the night before, I reflected with a kind of detached cruelty, was a salty, greasy poison in this heat. It was sucking the very moisture from his cells. He was being undone by his own civilized provisions. He stumbled more and more frequently, and his breathing became a ragged, painful pant.

After what seemed an eternity, a subjective measure of time that had lost all connection to the sun’s steady progress, he fell. He did not cry out. He simply crumpled, his legs folding beneath him, and lay still in the red dust.

I stopped and went back to him. The guide paused a few yards ahead, watching us, his form wavering in the heat. Hargreaves’s eyes were closed, his lips cracked and blackened. I knelt and shook his shoulder. ‘Hargreaves. Get up. We are not halfway there.’

His eyes fluttered open. They were glassy, unfocused. ‘Water,’ he whispered, the sound a dry rustle. ‘I need—water.’

‘We need to save it,’ I said, though the words tasted like ash in my own parched mouth. ‘We must keep going.’

‘I can’t,’ he breathed. ‘Leave me. Just—go.’

It was the classic, melodramatic plea of the exhausted traveller, the sort of thing you read in adventure stories. But looking at his face, at the slackness of his jaw and the terrifying vacancy in his eyes, I suspected it was terribly, horribly sincere. He was giving up. His mind, which had recoiled so violently from this world, was now simply shutting it out, retreating into the darkness.

The guide walked back to us. He looked down at Hargreaves, his expression unchanging. He then unslung the small pack from his back. He rummaged inside it and produced—a piece of the creature. It was wrapped in the same large leaves as before, raw, pale, and glistening faintly. With his small, sharp knife, he sliced off a chunk and held it out. Not to me this time, but to Hargreaves.

Hargreaves stared at the offering. He seemed not to understand at first. Then a flicker of his old self returned, a spark of pure, uncomprehending horror in his glazed eyes. He tried to shake his head, a feeble, palsied movement.

The guide did not retract his hand. He simply waited, a figure of infinite, patient reality. He nudged the meat against Hargreaves’s cracked lips. ‘Eat,’ he said. It was the same word the headman had used. ‘Eat.’ But this was not a cry of savage hunger. It was a command, a diagnosis, a prescription. The meat was moist. It was sustenance. It was life.

I watched, frozen in a strange state of clinical detachment. Here it was. The farce had reached its climax. The line drawn in the sand of the mind, and the man dying of thirst on the wrong side of it. All his life, all his education, all his beliefs, all the accumulated wisdom and prejudice of his culture, had brought him to this single, impossible moment: to eat the abomination, or to die.

Hargreaves’s eyes darted from the glistening meat to my face, then to the guide’s. They were filled with a desperate, pleading terror. He was begging for another option, for a miracle, for the world to suddenly make sense again. He was begging to be saved from the truth. But there was no other option. The desert did not offer alternatives. The sun did not negotiate. I looked at him, lying there in the dust, a sacrifice to his own fastidiousness, and I felt nothing. No pity, no sympathy. Only a vast, empty curiosity. I wanted to see what he would do. I wanted to know, in the final accounting, what a man was. Was he a set of principles, or was he just a piece of thirsty flesh? I waited for the answer, and the silence stretched on, as vast and as merciless as the sky above.

His cheek settled into the red dust, and he closed his eyes.

The guide did not sigh. He did not shrug. He displayed no emotion whatsoever that I could interpret. His face remained a mask of calm, impassive reality. He simply looked at the piece of glistening flesh in his hand for a moment, as if assessing its value, and then, with a neat, economical motion, he folded the leaf back over it and tucked it away into his pack. It was a gesture of profound and terrible sanity, an act of conservation in a world where waste was the ultimate sin. He had made the offer; it had been refused. The matter was closed. The resource would be preserved. I watched him, and it seemed to me in that moment that all the laws and ethics of my world were a kind of elaborate and fragile theatre, and that I had just witnessed a scene from the true and terrible play that went on behind the painted scrim.

Hargreaves did not move again. A faint, shuddering breath escaped him, and then another, shallower still. I found myself counting them, a morbid and useless accounting. The third did not come. The silence that followed was different from the silence of the desert; it was a small, personal, and dreadfully hollow silence that settled around his still form. He was gone. And my great, empty curiosity was answered. He was a set of principles, after all. A man of such delicate and refined sensibilities that he would rather perish than offend them. It was not a tragedy: it was a miscalculation of the highest order, a fatal piece of bookkeeping in which the soul was valued higher than the body, and both were lost as a result. I felt a sudden, cold and lucid perception of the utter absurdity of his position, and my own, and the whole farcical expedition into this heart of emptiness.

The guide touched my arm. It was a light touch, but it had the weight of an undeniable command. He pointed, not with his hand, but with his chin, towards the west, where the sun, a merciless white disk, had begun its slow descent. There was no time for burial, no time for sentiment. The rituals of my world had no currency here. To stop was to die. To linger over the dead was to join them. I had a brief, rebellious flicker of indignation, a protest rising in my throat against this casual, brutish dismissal of a man’s passing. A good man, a colleague. But the words turned to sand in my mouth. I looked at Hargreaves’s still form, already seeming to shrink, to become one with the dust and stone, and I knew that any gesture I made would be for my own benefit, a hollow performance for an audience of one. The desert would not notice. The guide would not care. I gave a short, jerky nod and turned away, leaving Hargreaves to the silence and the coming chill of the night.

We walked. The word is insufficient. We trudged, we staggered, we endured the act of putting one foot in front of the other while the sun beat upon our backs like a molten hammer and the ground threw that heat back up at us. My own body was a foreign country to me, a place of strange aches and unfamiliar weaknesses. My tongue was a swollen thing in my mouth, my skin was a cracked and burning parchment, and my thoughts, ah, my thoughts were a disordered and mutinous crew. They scurried about in the darkness of my skull, whispering of cool water, of green English lawns, of faces I had once loved, all of it insubstantial, a dream from another life. Was I that man who wore polished shoes and worried about the price of wool? It seemed impossible. That man was a fiction, a character in a book I had read long ago. The only reality was the next step, and the next, and the implacable back of the guide moving before me.

He, of course, was an entirely different creature. He moved with a rhythm that seemed attuned to the desert itself, a steady, unhurried pace that consumed the miles without any visible sign of effort. He was not walking across the landscape; he was a part of its grammar, a moving verb in a sentence of stone and sun. He never looked back to see if I was following. He knew I was. Where else would I go? I was tethered to him by the invisible leash of my own helplessness. I began to study him, not as a subordinate, not as a hired hand, but as one might study a strange and potent natural phenomenon. He would pause, occasionally, and stoop to examine a rock, or the lie of the dust, or a withered, skeletal bush. He was reading the land, I realized, reading a text that was utterly inscrutable to me, a text written in a language of heat and shadow. My own skills, the ability to navigate by sextant, to calculate a ship’s position, to command men, were as useless here as a book of poetry in a furnace.

Sometime in the late afternoon, when the air was a shimmering, visible thing and the sky was a pale, washed-out blue, a city appeared on the horizon. It was a fantastical creation of towers and ramparts, shimmering with a pearlescent light, impossibly grand and beautiful. I knew it was a mirage. My rational mind, what was left of it, told me it was a trick of the heat and the light and my own desperate, thirsting imagination. And yet, I could not tear my eyes from it. It was the promise of everything I had lost: shelter, society, water, reason. A part of me, a traitorous, childish part, wanted to run towards it, to fling myself upon its mercy. I even took a stumbling step in its direction before I caught myself. I glanced at the guide. He had not even broken his stride. His gaze was fixed on some point on the ground twenty yards ahead of him, his face as expressive as a stone. The city was not on his map. It did not exist. For him, the distinction between the real and the unreal was absolute, and it was a matter of life and death. For me, the line was becoming dangerously blurred.

As dusk began to fall, painting the sky in lurid shades of orange and violet, the character of the terrain changed. The endless, rolling expanse of sand and gravel gave way to a region of fractured rock, a jagged and broken landscape that looked like the aftermath of some titanic, geological war. Enormous slabs of stone, worn smooth by an eternity of wind, were piled one upon another in chaotic formations. It was a dead, silent place, a skeleton of a world. Yet, as we penetrated deeper into this labyrinth, I began to feel a strange and growing unease. There was a quality to the formations, a hint of pattern in the chaos, that pricked at the edges of my perception. The stones were not just piled; they seemed arranged. We passed between two towering pillars of black rock that might have been a gateway. We walked down a long, straight corridor of stone that felt unnervously like a street. It was all a trick of the light, of course, and my over-strained nerves. It had to be.

The guide, however, seemed to find this place to his liking. He led me to a narrow fissure in a vast wall of rock, a dark slash that was almost invisible until you were upon it. He slipped inside without a word. I hesitated for a moment at the threshold. The air that breathed out from the opening was cool, but it carried a dry, ancient, and dusty smell, the smell of a tomb. The thought of Hargreaves, lying alone out in the open, flashed through my mind. I pushed the thought away and followed the guide into the darkness.

Inside, it was not a cave, not really. It was a chamber, high-ceilinged and surprisingly spacious, its walls unnaturally smooth. In the gloom, I could just make out that the space was not entirely natural; there were angles here, corners that seemed too perfect, a suggestion of artifice that was more terrifying than any natural formation could be. It felt like a room. A dwelling. But a dwelling for what? The guide paid no mind to the unsettling architecture. He set his small pack down on a flat ledge of rock and, in the gathering darkness, began the business of making a camp. He did not light a fire. He simply sat, a compact shadow against the deeper shadows of the chamber, and I knew he was preparing to eat.

He unwrapped the piece of creature again. The faint, pale gleam of the meat was just visible in the gloom. He used his small, sharp knife, the same one I had seen before, and sliced a piece from it. He ate it slowly, methodically, with a quiet concentration that was almost devotional. The faint, wet sounds of his chewing were the only noises in the vast, echoing silence. I sat a few feet away, my back against the cold stone wall, and tried not to listen. My own hunger was a dull, gnawing ache in my belly, a constant and miserable companion. But it was more than hunger. It was a deeper craving, a depletion of the very stuff of my being. I was emptying out, becoming a hollow man in a hollow land.

When he was finished, he did not re-wrap the meat. He held it in his hand for a moment, a pale offering in the darkness. Then, he shuffled over to me. I did not see him move so much as feel his presence beside me, a sudden condensation of the shadows. He held out the leaf-wrapped package. I could not see his face. I could not see the meat. I could only smell it, a faint, wild, and not entirely unpleasant odour, like raw game, but with a strange, sweetish undertone.

‘Eat,’ he said.

The word was not a question. It was not a command. It was a simple statement of fact, a presentation of the only available option in a world that had been stripped of all others. Here it was again. The same test, the same choice that had been presented to Hargreaves. But it was different now. Hargreaves had been a man of principles, of culture, of fastidious taste. What was I? I looked into the darkness where the guide’s face would be, and I thought of the long journey back, of the offices and the ledgers, of the men in stiff collars talking of profits and percentages. I thought of the entire, intricate, and utterly insane edifice of my world, a world where men threw good hippo-meat overboard and paid other men in useless brass wire to starve quietly and without complaint. I thought of my own ridiculous vanity, my hope that my aspect was not ‘unappetizing’.

And then I thought of the city in the clouds, the beautiful, lying mirage. I had known it was not real, but I had wanted it to be. I had wanted the lie. Hargreaves had died for a truth of his own making, the truth of his own delicate soul. And what a paltry, useless truth it had been. This, on the other hand. This dark chamber, this silent guide, this piece of strange flesh in my hand. This was a different kind of truth. A harder truth. A truth that had weight and substance. It was sustenance. It was life.

My hand moved, seemingly of its own volition. My fingers closed around the cool, damp leaves. The guide’s hand withdrew, leaving me with the package. It felt heavy, impossibly heavy, weighted with the full gravity of the choice I was making. I hesitated for a long moment, listening to the frantic, useless protests of the man I used to be. He was screaming in my head, a tinny, distant voice, shouting about decency, about propriety, about the natural order of things. He was a fool. He was already dead. He had died somewhere back there in the dust, next to Hargreaves.

Slowly, deliberately, I raised the package to my mouth. The silence in the chamber was absolute, a watchful, waiting presence. It was not sleep; it was a state of trance. And in that trance, I, too, prepared to eat.

The flesh yielded, dense and fibrous, and with each movement a new wave of sensation flooded my senses, not merely taste, but a kind of knowing. I was not simply consuming; I was being consumed, my own identity dissolving into this primal sacrament. The guide stood motionless, a darker patch of black against the blackness, his presence a silent affirmation. He was not my keeper, not my tempter; he was a fellow communicant, a witness to my final, irrevocable initiation.

When I had swallowed the last morsel, a profound quiet descended upon my soul. It was not the quiet of peace, but the quiet of emptiness, of a space that has been cleared to make way for something new. I felt the substance of it settle within me, a dense, warm weight in the pit of my stomach. It was not food as I had known it; it was ballast, anchoring me to this dark, breathing world. The chamber, which had seemed a prison, now felt like a womb. The darkness was no longer a void, but a plenum, a rich and fertile medium in which new perceptions could take root. I sat there for a time that had no measure, adrift on a calm, black sea. My own past seemed a remote and nonsensical story, a tale told to a stranger. The man who had worried about his starched collars, who had dreamed of making a name for himself, who had felt annoyed by a delay of one night: he was a comical figure, a puppet whose strings had been cut. I felt a flicker of something like pity for him, but it was a distant, academic pity, the sort one might feel for a character in a poorly written play.

The guide moved, a faint rustle of his cloth garment, and a sliver of grey light pierced the gloom. He had shifted the stone at the entrance. It was dawn. The light did not flood in; it seeped, tentatively, a thin, watery gruel that diluted the perfect blackness. I looked at my hands. They were my own, yet they seemed unfamiliar, the hands of a stranger. The skin was scored with the grime of the river, the nails were broken, but there was a new stillness in them. They did not tremble. They did not fidget. They were simply there, tools that had been put to their proper use and were now at rest. The guide becked me with a slight inclination of his head. I rose, my limbs moving with a languid, deliberate grace I had never before possessed. The stiffness was gone from my joints. The weariness that had been my constant companion for months had vanished, replaced by a deep, quiet energy, a feeling of being profoundly and unshakably centred.

We emerged not into the blinding fog of the previous morning, but into a world of crystalline clarity. The mist had been scoured away, and the river lay before us, a polished ribbon of silver under a sky of pale, luminous pearl. The jungle on the opposite bank was a solid wall of incandescent green, each leaf, each vine, each blossom rendered with an almost painful precision. It was as though a veil had been lifted, not from the world, but from my own eyes. I could see the tremor of a spider’s web glistening with dew fifty yards away. I could hear the faint, dry rustle of a lizard moving through the dead leaves on the bank. The world had not changed, but my capacity to perceive it had been altered, magnified to a terrifying degree. The clamour of the previous day, the shrieking that had stirred my hair, now seemed a distant memory, a childish tantrum. The silence that followed it was the true voice of this place, and it was a silence I could now understand.

I walked back towards the steamer, the guide trailing a few paces behind me, a silent, unobtrusive shadow. The pilgrims were on deck, blinking in the sudden brightness, their faces pasty and unnerved. They looked at me with a mixture of suspicion and relief. The little fat man in the pink pyjamas, his absurd sidespring boots caked with mud, took a half-step towards me. ‘Well?’ he stammered, his voice thin and reedy in the clean air. ‘What was it? Did you find out?’

I looked at him, at his sandy whiskers twitching with anxiety, at his wide, fearful eyes, and I felt nothing. No contempt, no pity, not even irritation. He was simply a fact, a piece of the landscape, no more or less significant than the rotting stump by the water’s edge. ‘The fog has lifted,’ I said. My voice sounded different to my own ears, deeper, more resonant, as if it were being drawn from a well that had not been there before.

The manager appeared from his cabin, his white suit immaculate, his face a mask of sour impatience. He strode towards me, his boots making a crisp, authoritative sound on the deck planks. ‘At last,’ he said, not looking at me but at the river ahead. ‘We have wasted enough time. Get the anchor up. We must push on to the station. Kurtz is expecting us.’ He paused, and his pale, fish-like eyes flickered towards me. ‘Where were you, captain? Consorting with the natives?’ There was a sneer in his voice, a casual, ingrained contempt that had once seemed to me a mark of his authority. Now it sounded merely petty, the yapping of a small dog.

‘I was ensuring the safety of your vessel,’ I replied, my gaze level. I did not elaborate. I did not feel the need to. The old habit of deference, the instinct to placate and explain, was gone.

He stared at me for a long moment, his mouth a thin, bloodless line. He was trying to re-assert his dominance, to put me back in my place. But the place he was looking for no longer existed. He must have seen something in my eyes, some new and unsettling stillness, for he looked away first. ‘See that you do,’ he muttered, turning to glower at the crew, who were beginning to move towards the anchor chain. ‘And tell your headman,’ he gestured dismissively towards the broad-chested man who now stood near the bow, ‘that their ridiculous hunger-pains are no excuse for shirking. There will be work to do at the station.’

The headman had not moved. He stood watching me, his fierce nostrils flared, his eyes no longer bloodshot but clear and dark as river stones. There was a question in them, a silent inquiry. I gave him a slight, almost imperceptible nod. A flicker of understanding passed between us, a current of silent communication that bypassed the need for words, for promises, for the farcical contracts written on paper. He understood. I understood. We were bound now, not by brass wire, but by a shared knowledge, a shared meal. He turned and let out a sharp, guttural cry, and his men, the men who had looked upon us with hungry eyes, sprang to the chain with a will. The grins were gone, replaced by a focused, formidable purpose. The chain rattled in, the links clattering a swift, rhythmic tattoo, a song of labour that was no longer sullen, but had a kind of grim, terrible joy in it.

‘Will they attack?’ the awed voice from the fog whispered again at my elbow. It was one of the other pilgrims, a thin, sallow man with a perpetually worried expression. He was still clutching his Winchester as if it were a holy relic.

‘They are hauling the anchor,’ I said.

‘But the shrieking,’ he insisted, his voice dropping. ‘The cannibals.’

I looked from his twitching, fearful face to the powerful, glistening backs of the men at the chain. I thought of the headman’s curt phrase: ‘Eat ’im!’ I thought of the earthy, vital taste of the meat in the dark chamber. Cannibals. The word seemed absurd, a label from a child’s adventure story, utterly inadequate to describe the complex reality I had just glimpsed. What was it to eat a man? Was it any more savage than to watch a man starve while you fed from tins? Was it any more barbaric than to pay a man for his life’s labour with useless scraps of metal? The questions arose in my mind, but they were no longer troubling. They were simply facts, aspects of a truth that was far larger and more terrifying than the pilgrim’s simple-minded fears.

‘They are hungry,’ I said, and the simplicity of the statement seemed to silence him. He backed away, clutching his rifle, his eyes wide with a new kind of fear, not of the jungle, but of me.

The anchor came free with a muddy sucking sound, and I went to the pilothouse. The little steamer shuddered as the engine engaged, the propeller churning the silver water to a muddy brown. I took the wheel. It felt solid and real in my hands, a point of contact with the mechanical world I still nominally inhabited. But as I guided the boat into the main channel, my eyes were not on the gauges or the compass. They were on the river, on the jungle, on the vast, silent world that stretched away on either side. I was no longer a stranger here. I was no longer a detached observer, a tourist of the abyss. I had taken the wilderness into myself, and it had taken me in return. The journey up the river had been a stripping away, a shedding of the layers of illusion that had made up my life. Now, I was peeled down to the raw, essential man.

We steamed for hours, the engine a steady, throbbing pulse beneath my feet. The sun climbed higher, beating down with a fierce, impartial heat. The pilgrims hid in the shade of the awnings, sweating and listless, their conversation a desultory murmur. The manager stood on the forward deck, his arms crossed, his white suit a defiant blot against the overwhelming green. He was staring upriver, towards his precious Kurtz, his paragon of productivity, his agent of enlightenment. I wondered what he would find when he got there. I wondered if he had any conception of the changes that such a place, such an isolation, could work on a man. I thought of Kurtz, a man who had come here with high ideals, with plans for a new kind of civilization. And I thought of the path he must have walked, a path that led from lofty pronouncements to the savage discords in the fog. I was beginning to understand that path. I had taken the first step onto it myself.

The river was my map now. I read its currents, its eddies, its subtle shifts in colour, not as a pilot, but as a creature of the river itself. The suspicious ripples I had feared the day before now seemed like a familiar language, telling me of sandbars and hidden snags. I navigated not by charts, but by a kind of instinct, a deep, resonant feeling for the water’s flow. The eight miles to Kurtz’s station, which had seemed an arduous journey, now felt like an inevitable unfolding, a final, fateful approach.

As the sun began to dip towards the western bank, painting the sky in lurid shades of orange and purple, we rounded a final bend. And there it was. Not a station, not a settlement, but a scene of such utter, surreal desolation that it stopped the heart. A clearing had been hacked from the jungle, a raw, red-earth wound in the green flesh of the land. In the centre stood a long, low building of mud and reeds, its roof sagging, its verandah crumbling. But it was not the decay that was so shocking. It was the fence. The clearing was enclosed by a fence made of rough wooden stakes, and atop each stake was a head. They were not skulls, bleached and clean. They were fresh, some with remnants of flesh still clinging to the bone, others with wisps of hair stirring in the faint breeze. Their faces, what was left of them, were turned inwards, towards the house, as if in a posture of eternal, silent adoration. They were the sentinels of this kingdom, the grotesque ornaments of Kurtz’s domain.

A gasp went through the pilgrims. The little fat man made a choking sound and turned away, his hand clapped over his mouth. The manager stared, his face a mask of disbelief, his jaw slack. He had come for ivory, for reports, for profits. He had not come for this. This was an outrage against procedure, a flagrant and unforgivable breach of commercial decorum.

But I, I looked upon the heads, and I felt a strange, cold sense of recognition. This was not madness. This was a kind of logic, a terrible, unassailable logic that had followed its premises to their final, grisly conclusion. Kurtz had simply gone further. He had not stopped at the first step, at the secret meal in the dark. He had walked the path to its very end. He had cast off the last of his illusions, the last of his pretences, and embraced the truth of this place in all its naked, horrific glory. The shrieking in the fog, the heads on the stakes, it was all part of the same thing: a complete and utter surrender to the wilderness.

There was no war-cry, no shouting, just this persistent, whispering rain of tiny wooden darts. And yet, it was more unnerving than a full-throated charge would have been. It was an attack by phantoms, by invisible enemies who were all around us, who could see us when we could not see them, who were methodically, patiently, trying to kill us from the depths of a spectral world.

The pilgrims’ reaction was immediate and entirely predictable. A wild, ragged volley of rifle-fire erupted from the deck. They fired blindly into the fog, their Winchesters cracking and booming in the stillness. It was a hysterical, senseless outburst, a tantrum of noise against the suffocating silence. The manager shouted something, but his voice was lost in the din. They were possessed, firing at their own fears, at the formless horror of their situation. The little fat man was particularly energetic, working the lever of his rifle with frantic haste, his face contorted with a kind of ecstatic terror. I saw the flash of their guns lighting up the fog in brief, lurid bursts, revealing for an instant their own strained, sweat-slicked faces. It was a spectacle of supreme imbecility, a mad pantomime of defence. They might as well have been throwing stones at the moon. The cannibals, by contrast, had simply ducked. They lay flat on the deck, presenting the smallest possible target, their movements economical and precise. They did not utter a sound. The headman had pressed himself against the bulwark, his head tucked down, but his eyes were open, watchful, scanning the impenetrable white wall.

I was in the pilot-house with my helmsman, a fine, strapping fellow from some coastal tribe, whom I had trained myself. He was steering, his hands firm on the spokes of the wheel, his body swaying gently with the motion of the boat. He was, I believe, the only man on board who was actually doing his job. The arrows were thicker around us now, pattering against the thin wooden walls of our shelter. One pierced the canvas screen in front of me and stood vibrating, its point a few inches from my face. I could have touched it. It was a very personal, intimate sort of threat. The helmsman grunted, and opened his mouth as if to speak. He had a foolish, surprised look on his face. His hands slid from the wheel. He collapsed slowly, deliberately, not like a man but like a sack of grain, slumping to the deck in a boneless heap. A long, dark arrow, much larger than the others, was sticking out of his side, just below the ribs. It had gone in deep. He looked up at me, his eyes wide with an uncomprehending appeal. A dark stain was spreading across the front of his worn cotton tunic. He tried to say something again, but only a soft, bubbling sigh came out. He shuddered once, violently, and then was still.

The steamboat, left to itself, began to swing into the current. I had to act. I pushed the dead man’s legs aside—his blood was warm and sticky on my shoes, a shocking, disconcerting sensation—and grabbed the wheel. The bank, a dark, blurry line, was looming out of the fog. I spun the wheel hard, and the boat responded sluggishly, groaning in protest. All the while, the pilgrims kept up their insane fusillade. The noise was deafening, the air thick with the smell of burnt powder. They were achieving nothing, of course, except to advertise our exact position to our invisible assailants. And then an idea came to me, an inspiration born of sheer exasperation. I reached up and grabbed the lanyard of the steam-whistle. I gave it a long, hard pull.

The sound that erupted was monstrous, a shriek of tortured metal that tore through the fog and the pathetic popping of the rifles. It was a sound of the industrial world, a voice of iron and steam, hurled into the heart of the primeval forest. It was a brutal, soulless, and utterly terrifying noise. It drowned out everything: the gunfire, the whispering arrows, the very silence of the jungle. It was as if a god of the machine had screamed in rage. On deck, the firing stopped abruptly. The pilgrims froze, stunned by this new, more potent horror. And from the shore, the effect was just as immediate. The shower of arrows ceased as if by magic. A profound, echoing silence fell, broken only by the dying echoes of the whistle bouncing between the unseen banks. I had given them a voice more terrible than their own. I had scared them with a sound they could not comprehend, a manifestation of a power beyond their experience. It was a victory, of a sort. A farcical victory, won by a puff of steam.

I held the steamboat steady in the middle of the channel, my hands sticky on the wheel. The dead helmsman lay at my feet, a silent reproach. I looked down at him. He had been a part of my little world, a reliable piece of the human machinery that kept us going. He had had a name, a family, a life of his own, and now he was just a heap of cooling flesh, a problem to be disposed of. The pilgrims began to stir again, congratulating themselves on their heroic defence. ‘Gave them a good scare, what!’ the fat man shouted, his face beaming with pride. He seemed to think his Winchester had won the day. I felt a wave of nausea, a profound weariness of the soul. I looked from the self-satisfied faces of the pilgrims to the dead man on the floor, and it seemed to me that the real savagery was not in the jungle, but on the deck of my own boat.

The manager came up to the pilot-house, stepping delicately over the body. ‘A regrettable incident,’ he said, glancing at the helmsman with mild distaste, as if he were a piece of spoiled cargo. ‘But you handled it well, captain. Very resourceful.’ He was looking at me with his pale, unblinking eyes, and I knew what he was thinking. He was thinking that the attack confirmed his worst fears, that Kurtz was surely dead, that the whole enterprise had been a failure. And I found, to my own surprise, that I was thinking the same thing. The journey had been for nothing. The man I had come all this way to meet, the voice I had longed to hear, was silent, swallowed up by the darkness he had sought to conquer. A sense of immense futility washed over me. All this striving, this suffering, this petty scheming and grandiose folly—all for a dead man. I felt a sudden, morbid curiosity. What had been his last words? What final wisdom or final madness had he uttered at the end?

‘We should turn back,’ the manager said. It was not a suggestion; it was a test. He wanted to see if I had given up, if I was ready to admit defeat. And perhaps I should have. It would have been the sensible thing to do. But the thought of turning back now, with the goal so near, was intolerable. It was not about Kurtz anymore, not really. It was about finishing the journey, about seeing the thing through to its bitter end. It had become a personal matter, a question of my own integrity. ‘No,’ I said. ‘We go on.’ The manager shrugged, a barely perceptible movement of his shoulders. ‘As you wish, captain,’ he said, and turned away. He was content. The responsibility was now entirely mine.

I had to get rid of the body. The cannibals were stirring, their eyes drawn to the dark shape on the floor of the pilot-house. The headman had risen to his feet and was looking at the dead helmsman with a thoughtful, speculative expression. I could guess what was in his mind. I had seen that look before. ‘Eat ’im!’ He had said it curtly, as a simple statement of fact. I could not let that happen. It was a matter of sentiment, perhaps, a foolish tribute to the man who had shared my watch, but it was a sentiment I could not ignore. I motioned to two of the crewmen. They understood at once. They picked up the body, one at the head, one at the feet, and carried it to the side. I looked away. I did not want to see his face again. There was a brief, heavy splash. I looked back. The brown water swirled for a moment, and then was smooth again. He was gone, vanished without a trace, as if he had never been. The river had taken him, just as it had taken so many others. And we steamed on, into the heart of the white, silent fog, leaving nothing behind but a few spent cartridges and the lingering echo of a scream.

The white shutter did not lift this time; it dissolved, turning from solid milk to a watery wash, like ink bleeding into paper. The jungle was a solid wall of vegetation, dripping, silent, a colossal and indifferent spectator to our passage. And through the tattered curtain of the mist, we saw it.

It was not a station, not in any sense I understood. It was a clearing, a raw wound carved into the flesh of the wilderness. A long, low building of mud and thatch, sagging in the middle like a tired beast, stood at the edge of the bank. To one side, a flagpole, stark and bare, pointed a finger at the grey sky. But it was the space before the house that held the eye. A row of slender posts had been driven into the ground, and on top of each post was a ball, a dark, round shape. I had been looking for a trading post, for signs of commerce, for the mundane evidence of profit and loss. Before I could fully absorb this spectacle, a figure burst from the doorway of the central hut, an impossible apparition that did not seem to belong to the scene, or to any scene I had ever witnessed. He was a young man, though it was hard to tell his age. He was gaunt, with a thin, fair beard and wide, feverish eyes. But it was his clothes that arrested the attention. He waved his arms, both of them, in a gesture of frantic welcome. He was shouting something, but his voice was too thin to carry over the churn of the water as I brought the steamer in towards the bank.

‘My God,’ a pilgrim muttered behind me. ‘He’s white.’

The man on the shore, this incredible patchwork creature, ran down to the water’s edge as our anchor chain rattled out. He was all nervous energy, a bundle of quivering excitement. As we drew closer, his words became clear.

‘You have come for him! I knew you would come!’ he cried, his voice high-pitched and ecstatic. ‘You are English! He has been waiting. Oh, the loneliness! The solitude!’

He was beside himself with joy, or relief, or some other emotion I could not name. I stared at him from the pilot-house, and he stared back, his face a mask of pure, unadulterated devotion. He seemed to have forgotten the heads on the stakes, forgotten the oppressive silence of the jungle, forgotten everything but the fact of our arrival.

The manager, ever practical, was the first to speak to him as the gangplank was lowered. ‘Are you in charge here?’ he asked, his voice cold and flat.

They paid me no mind. They were in the grip of a frenzy. It was their answer to the incomprehensible, their retort to the shrieks and the arrows and the fog. The manager was shouting at them too, but his voice was thin and reedy, lost in the din. I was alone in the pilothouse with a dead man, surrounded by lunatics, drifting blind. The air grew thick with the acrid smell of burnt powder. And through it all, the cannibals remained quiet, watchful. My headman stood by the rail, his expression unreadable. He looked at the frantic pilgrims, then at me, then back out into the fog. A flicker of something—was it contempt? amusement?—passed across his features.

The firing stopped as abruptly as it had begun. The two men stood panting, their rifles hot in their hands, staring into the fog as if expecting a round of applause. The silence that rushed back in was even more profound than before. It was a hollow, ringing silence, the kind that follows a great explosion. And in that silence, I made a decision. It was a desperate, illogical gamble, born of the same madness that had gripped the pilgrims. ‘Get below,’ I said to the manager, my voice hoarse. ‘Tell them to give me all the steam they can. I’m going to blow the whistle.’

He looked at me as if I had lost my senses. ‘The whistle? At a time like this? It will deafen us! They’ll know exactly where we are!’

‘They know where we are already,’ I said, gesturing with my head towards the dead man. ‘They are listening. I want to give them something to listen to.’ I had a mad idea that the sheer, unearthly power of a steam whistle, a sound that had no place in this ancient forest, might be more effective than a hundred rifles. It was the voice of another world, a voice of brass and fire and irresistible force. It was our magic against theirs.

He scurried off, and I waited, my hands tight on the wheel, the dead man a heavy presence at my side. I felt the vibrations of the deck change their rhythm, a deeper, more urgent thrumming from the boat’s iron bowels. A plume of steam hissed from a release valve nearby. I reached up and grabbed the lanyard, a greasy, braided cord that hung from the ceiling. I gave it a hard, vicious pull.

The sound that erupted was not merely a noise; it was a physical assault. A prolonged, deafening shriek that tore through the fog and the silence, a blast of pure, mechanical fury that seemed to shake the very air, the water, the trees on the invisible banks. It went on and on, a single, unwavering, cataclysmic note of industrial rage. I felt it in my bones, in my teeth. I saw the pilgrims clap their hands over their ears, their faces screwed up in pain. Even the cannibals flinched. I held the lanyard, letting the whistle scream its defiance into the white emptiness. I was trying to frighten the wilderness itself, to tear a hole in the fabric of this dreadful dream.

When I finally let go, the ensuing silence was absolute. It was the silence of a dead world. Nothing moved. Nothing breathed. We floated there, stunned, our ears ringing, waiting. I waited for a new flight of arrows, a new chorus of shrieks. But there was nothing. Only the gentle gurgle of the river against the hull and the fading echo of that colossal sound in my head. Had it worked? Had I scared them away, these men with their toy arrows and their terrible cries? Or had I simply confirmed for them that we were a prize worth taking, a source of unimaginable power and noise?

There was no way to know. But the attack did not resume. Minute after minute crawled by, and the world remained still. And then, a change. It was not a sound, but a shift in the quality of the light. The fog was thinning. Not lifting, but becoming less opaque, turning from a solid wall of white into a translucent, luminous haze. Shapes began to emerge, dark, blurry phantoms solidifying into reality. I could see the full length of the steamboat now, the arrows sticking out of her sides like porcupine quills. I could see the far bank of the river, a dark, muddy smear, and above it, the jungle, a solid, towering mass of green, dripping with moisture.

A deserted village of dilapidated grass huts stood on a small clearing at the water’s edge. There was no sign of life. A few dugout canoes were pulled up on the mud. A cooking fire smouldered, sending a thin wisp of smoke into the damp air. It was as if our attackers had simply vanished, dissolved back into the forest like the mist that had concealed them. They had screamed, they had shot their arrows, they had killed my helmsman, and then they had gone, leaving behind only a profound sense of bewilderment and violation.

I gave the wheel a spin and angled the boat back towards the center of the channel. The current took us, and the village began to slide astern. The pilgrims, their panic replaced by a swaggering relief, chattered excitedly, pointing to the dents in the funnel, congratulating each other on their marksmanship. The manager appeared at my side again, his face a mixture of relief and self-importance. ‘A narrow escape, Captain,’ he said, as if he had personally directed the entire operation. ‘A very narrow escape. My decision to hold our fire until absolutely necessary was, I believe, the correct one.’

I did not have the energy to argue. I looked down at the man at my feet. We had to do something with him. ‘Get a couple of your men,’ I said to the headman, who had approached the pilothouse. ‘Take him. Bury him in the river.’ The term ‘bury’ was a euphemism, of course. He nodded, his face impassive. He and another of his crew came forward, and with a surprising gentleness, they lifted the body. They did not seem horrified or disturbed. It was a job, like hauling wood or heaving the anchor chain. As they carried him to the side, one of the pilgrims, the fat one, trotted after them, peering with a morbid curiosity. ‘Be careful,’ he chirped, ‘you might get some of that blood on you.’

The headman stopped and turned his head slowly to look at the pilgrim. He said nothing. He simply looked, and in his eyes there was a depth of primeval contempt that a thousand years of civilization could not have produced. The pilgrim faltered, his face paling, and took a hasty step back. The cannibals proceeded to the rail, and with a coordinated heave, they tipped the body over the side. There was a single, lonely splash. The river took him without a ripple, swallowing him up as if he had never been. And we steamed on.

I stayed at the wheel, my mind a dull blank. The sun was burning through the last of the mist, and the heat was becoming intense. The river ahead was a broad, glittering pathway of light. We were getting close now, very close to Kurtz’s station. The manager was talking again, something about a report I would have to write, about the loss of company property—the helmsman, presumably, being classified as such. I was not listening. I was thinking about the arrows, light as toys, and the man they had killed. I thought of his patient, steady hands on this very wheel. He had died for me, in a way. He had been steering my course. And for what? So that I could get to Kurtz. So that the manager could get his ivory. So that these gibbering pilgrims could get whatever it was they were seeking in the heart of this darkness.

My gaze fell upon the crew, who were now receiving their weekly pay of brass wire from the manager’s clerk. They took the useless metal with grave expressions, tucking it away in their loincloths. The headman stood apart, watching the proceeding with that same air of detached, philosophical interest. ‘Eat ’im,’ he had said. The words echoed in my mind, not with the horror I might have felt before, but with a new, chilling clarity. It was not a savage boast. It was a simple statement of a different kind of logic, a different set of rules. We, with our steam whistle and our rifles and our farcical salaries, we were the ones who were truly mad, thrashing about in a world we could not comprehend. They, with their hunger and their unnerving restraint, possessed a kind of terrible wisdom. They knew the rules of this place. The first rule was to survive. The second, perhaps, was that everything was edible. I felt a cold knot in my stomach, and it was not from fear. It was a kind of recognition. The wilderness had not screamed. The people had. And in their cry of infinite desolation, I had heard a voice that was profoundly, terrifyingly human. The journey was not over. The worst, I felt, was yet to come. The river flowed on, and we with it, deeper and deeper into a darkness that was no longer merely around us, but within us as well.

The manager, however, was not given to such useless reflections. He reappeared at my side, his face a mask of strained composure, and cleared his throat with a dry little cough that was lost in the vast, listening silence.

‘The fog is thinning, I believe,’ he remarked, in a tone one might use to comment on the weather in a suburban garden. ‘We must be ready to proceed. This incident—unfortunate, of course—must not delay us further. Mr Kurtz is waiting.’

His voice, so flat and mundane, was an obscenity in that charged atmosphere. It was as if a man were to begin discussing accounts in a cathedral during a moment of divine revelation. One of the pilgrims, the one in the ridiculous pink pyjamas, still clutched his Winchester, his eyes wide and vacant. ‘But what was it?’ he whispered, his voice trembling. ‘What was that cry?’

The manager gave him a glance of cold disdain. ‘Natives, naturally. Exuberant, no doubt. Their customs are… noisy. Pay it no mind. The important thing is the station.’ He gestured vaguely into the white wall before us, as if Mr Kurtz and his ivory were a tangible certainty just beyond the veil.

And then, as if summoned by his will, the veil began to part. It did not lift or dissipate in the way of an honest sea-fog; it seemed instead to grow thin, to become translucent, like a sheet of frosted glass slowly clearing. The solid white became a milky grey, and through it, dark shapes began to resolve themselves, phantoms taking on the substance of reality. The high bank opposite us emerged first, a dark, unbroken line. Then the trees, not as a solid mass, but as individual titans, their lower trunks still wrapped in wisps of mist, their immense crowns clear against a sky of washed-out blue. The sun, which had been a mere suggestion of brightness, now asserted itself, pouring down a heat that was immediate and oppressive. The water around us turned from a ghostly strip to a wide, brown, sluggish expanse, its surface shimmering with a kind of oily lethargy.

And there it was. Kurtz’s station. It was not a station, really; it was a scene of ruin so complete and yet so strangely inhabited that it arrested the mind. A long, low building of clay and reeds stood near the bank, its roof sagging in the middle as if under the weight of some immense sorrow. A portion of the flimsy stockade that had once enclosed the compound had collapsed, its posts leaning at weary, defeated angles. To the right of the main building, a smaller hut stood isolated, looking like a forgotten sentry-box. But it was not the decay that held the eye. It was the silence, the utter absence of any sign of the life one would associate with a trading post. No smoke rose, no figures moved, no sounds of work or speech carried across the water. The whole place seemed to be holding its breath, preserved in a state of arrested decomposition under the pitiless glare of the sun.

‘There,’ the manager breathed, a note of possessive triumph in his voice. He raised his binoculars to his eyes. ‘At last. We must land at once.’

But I hesitated. Something was wrong. The stillness was not the stillness of desertion; it had a watchful quality, a tension that radiated from the shore like heat. My eyes scanned the edge of the jungle, the clearing, the dilapidated buildings. And then I saw it. Or rather, I saw them. Perched atop the posts of the broken stockade were a series of round, dark objects. At this distance, they were just knobs, punctuating the line of the fence. I raised my own glass, my hand unsteady. The lens swam into focus, and the world contracted to a small, sharp circle of appalling clarity. They were heads. Dried, shrunken heads, stuck on poles, and turned, all of them, to face the house. They were not festive ornaments, not symbols of power in any way I could comprehend; they seemed rather like votive offerings, placed with a kind of lunatic care, the last vestiges of some incomprehensible ceremony. Their faces were black, with gaping mouths and sunken eye sockets, expressing in their final contortion a wild variety of emotions: anger, surprise, despair, and one, I saw with a jolt, had an expression of what looked like serene, unholy ecstasy.

A cold sweat broke out on my forehead. This was the place Kurtz had ‘civilized.’ This was the fruit of his benevolent intentions, of the light he was to bring into the darkness. ‘My God,’ I whispered, lowering the glass. The pilgrims had not noticed yet; they were chattering with relief, seeing a destination, a tangible end to their fears. The manager, however, had seen. He let his binoculars fall, and they dangled from the strap around his neck. His face was a study. It was not horror I saw there, nor shock. It was a kind of calculating annoyance, the look of a man who has discovered a flaw in an otherwise profitable enterprise.

‘Really,’ he muttered to himself, ‘this is most irregular. His method is… unsound.’ He turned to me, his little eyes glinting. ‘You see? I was right to be concerned. The man has gone completely mad. Utterly mad.’ There was a vicious satisfaction in his tone. This was what he had wanted, what he had journeyed all this way to find: not a successful rival, but a ruined one.

It was then that a figure emerged from the doorway of the main hut. It was not Kurtz. It was a man so unbelievable, so fantastically out of place, that for a moment I thought the fever had taken me for good and I was seeing things. He was dressed in a motley collection of garments, a patchwork of colours and fabrics that seemed to have been scavenged from a dozen different sources. He had on a brown jacket with bright yellow patches, a pair of what looked like blue canvas trousers, and gaiters wrapped around his thin legs. On his head he wore no hat, and his fair hair stood up in a boyish shock. He looked young, incredibly young, with a frank, open face, a snub nose, and a pair of astonishingly blue eyes. He stood there for a second, blinking in the sun like an owl, then he saw us. His whole body gave a start of joyous, incredulous surprise. He began to wave his arms, both of them, in wide, frantic circles. He shouted something we could not hear. Then, as if overcome by an excess of emotion, he started running towards the river bank, stumbling over the uneven ground, his arms still flailing.

‘Who on earth is that?’ the manager said, his voice sharp with suspicion. ‘A subordinate? I have no record of one.’

We brought the steamer in closer, the churn of the propeller a deafening intrusion into the profound silence of the place. The patchwork man did not stop waving until we were within hailing distance. His face was alight with an ecstatic welcome.

‘You have come!’ he yelled, his voice high and carrying clearly over the water. His English was perfect, accented with the slightest hint of something guttural and foreign. ‘You have come at last! It is a miracle!’

‘Who are you?’ the manager called back, his tone brooking no nonsense. ‘Identify yourself.’

The man seemed not to hear the question, or to consider it irrelevant. ‘Is he with you?’ he cried, his gaze sweeping our decks with a desperate eagerness. ‘The Company sent you for him? You must be careful! He has been very ill, very ill indeed!’

I ordered the steam-whistle to be blown, a short, sharp blast to announce our official arrival and to warn away whatever unseen listeners might be lurking in the trees. The sound tore through the air, echoed once from the wall of the jungle, and then died, leaving a silence that felt deeper than before. The man on the bank flinched as if struck.

‘Don’t do that!’ he shouted, his voice suddenly filled with alarm. ‘For God’s sake, don’t make such a noise! It agitates them!’

‘Agitates who?’ the manager demanded.

But the man was already pointing, not at the jungle, but back towards the hut. ‘Him!’ he said. ‘You must be quiet. He needs rest. He has been… exerting himself. His discourses… they take a great deal out of him.’

This was beyond belief. We were surrounded by a silent, watching wilderness, in a station decorated with human heads, after having been attacked by a screaming horde in the fog, and this… this harlequin, this jester at the court of horror, was concerned about his master’s rest. I brought the steamer alongside the crumbling bank, and two of the crew jumped ashore with ropes. The man scrambled aboard with the agility of a monkey, his face beaming, his blue eyes darting from face to face. He was a Russian, he told us, a sailor who had deserted from a foreign ship and had wandered inland, drawn by stories, by whispers, of the great Mr Kurtz.

‘He is a prodigy,’ the Russian declared, looking at us as if we were children who needed to be instructed in a great truth. He clutched my arm, his grip surprisingly strong. ‘He has enlarged my mind. You should hear him. His ideas… you can’t imagine. He speaks of everything! Of love, and justice, and duty… He has to be heard.’

The manager cut him short. ‘Where is the ivory?’ he asked, his voice like a chip of ice.

The Russian looked at him, his expression momentarily blank, as if the question were in a language he did not understand. ‘The ivory?’ he repeated. ‘Oh, yes, the ivory. There is a great deal of it. A magnificent quantity. He has been very successful. The tribes adore him. They bring it to him as a gift.’ He gestured towards the hut. ‘He is a god to them.’

‘A god,’ the manager echoed slowly, and he exchanged a quick, meaningful glance with me. He was thinking of the heads on the poles. I was thinking of them too. I looked at the Russian, at his boyish, innocent face, his shining eyes full of a frantic devotion.

‘Those… decorations,’ I said, my voice sounding hoarse in my own ears. ‘Were they part of the adoration?’

The Russian’s expression did not change. There was no flicker of shame or horror. He looked at me with a kind of earnest candour. ‘Oh, that,’ he said, with a dismissive wave of his hand. ‘Those are rebels. It is a local custom. He has to show his power, you see. It is necessary. They were criminals.’

‘Criminals,’ I repeated, my mind reeling. The word, in this context, was so grotesquely inadequate, so fantastically civilized, that it bordered on the insane.

‘Yes, yes,’ he insisted eagerly. ‘He has had to be severe. This is a savage country. You cannot come here with your European notions of… of procedure. He has understood this. He has a strength of will you cannot imagine. But the wilderness… the solitude… it has preyed on his mind. He has no one to talk to, you see. For months, no one but me. And I…’ He looked down at his own patched clothes with a sudden, touching humility. ‘I am not a worthy intellectual companion. He needs to go home. He needs to be saved.’

‘Saved from what?’ I asked.

He leaned closer, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper, his blue eyes wide with a kind of terrified awe. ‘From himself,’ he said. ‘He has no restraint. There is no one here to… to tell him no. The darkness… it has whispered things to him, things I cannot understand, and he has listened. I have watched him, out there in the forest at night… I have heard him. He is a great man, but he is… hollow. He is lost.’

The manager, who had been listening to this with growing impatience, stepped forward. ‘Enough of this chatter,’ he said. ‘We will take Mr Kurtz on board, along with his ivory, and we will leave this… unhealthy place at once.’ He turned and began issuing orders to the pilgrims, who were now staring with a mixture of fear and fascination at the heads on the poles. They were to form a party, armed, and prepare to bring a stretcher to the main hut.

As they bustled about with a show of nervous efficiency, I remained near the rail, talking to the Russian. He was a fount of information, a torrent of words that flowed out of him as if a dam had broken. He had nursed Kurtz through two illnesses, had listened to his poetry, had been the sole audience for his magnificent, terrifying monologues on a civilizing mission that had somehow transformed into a reign of terror. He told me of raids for ivory, of midnight ceremonies in the forest, of a power Kurtz held over the local population that was absolute and hypnotic. He spoke of it all with a strange mixture of pride in his master’s greatness and a simple, boyish fear of his madness.

‘They do not want him to go,’ he said, glancing nervously towards the jungle. ‘He is more to them than a man. Last time he tried to leave, they surrounded the hut. I thought we would be killed. But he went out and spoke to them. Just spoke. And they… they crawled away. They let him be.’

I looked from his earnest, troubled face to the impassive faces of my cannibal crew, who were watching all of this from the foredeck. They had not moved. Their headman stood as before, his arms folded, his gaze fixed on the shore with a pensive, unreadable expression. He had seen the heads on the poles, of course. He had heard the Russian’s frantic talk. And yet his face showed nothing. No surprise, no disgust, no fear. It was the look of a man observing the predictable, if lamentable, behaviour of a particularly strange species of animal. He was not a part of our drama; he was a spectator, and his silence was a judgment more profound than any words.

The manager’s party was ready. He stood at their head, a rifle in his hand, his face set in a grim expression of purpose. ‘Come,’ he said to me. ‘You are to witness the transfer. Company regulations.’

I followed him down the makeshift gangplank onto the soil of Kurtz’s kingdom. The ground was littered with debris: broken pottery, discarded bits of cloth, the bones of small animals. The air was thick with a faint, sweetish odour of decay that caught in the back of the throat. As we approached the hut, a deep, resonant voice suddenly boomed from within, a voice that seemed to have no source, to come from the earth itself. It was not speaking words, but was engaged in a kind of declamatory groaning, a sound of immense, articulate despair.

The Russian paled. ‘He is awake,’ he whispered. ‘He knows you are here.’

The manager paused, his hand raised. The pilgrims froze behind him, their faces slack with fear. The voice from the hut rose in pitch, modulating into a string of what sounded like furious, impassioned pronouncements in a language I did not know. It was a voice accustomed to command, to being heard, a voice that had shaped the world around it through sheer sound. Even in its current state of ruin, it possessed a terrifying and undeniable authority.

Then, as suddenly as it had begun, it stopped. The silence that rushed back in was absolute. We stood there, a ridiculous little party of armed men, held captive by the will of a disembodied voice. The sun beat down. A fly buzzed near my ear. From the steamer, I heard the faint rattle of a chain. The world was holding its breath. And in that moment, I knew with a certainty that went beyond reason that we had not come to rescue Kurtz. We had come to be judged by him. We had travelled all this way, not to the heart of a continent, but to the bottom of a human soul, and the darkness we were about to confront was not the darkness of the jungle, but the darkness that waits, patient and triumphant, at the end of every illusion. The door of the hut remained a dark, uninviting rectangle. We waited.
