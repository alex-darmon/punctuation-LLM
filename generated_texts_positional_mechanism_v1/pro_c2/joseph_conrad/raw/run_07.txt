We were to have made the caldera by nightfall. I had meant to press on; but Hargrove, his face looking bleached and pinched in the dying light, insisted the ground ahead was unstable, that the saline crust was known to give way without warning into the shimmering brine pits below. He argued, his voice thin with a corporate sort of prudence, that to navigate such a place in the gloom would be an act of madness, not of courage. This was, I suppose, a reasonable point. The last six miles of that white, crystalline plain had taken us nearly four hours, the wheels of the great steam-wagon crunching with a sound like grinding teeth. I could see, even from our slight elevation, a strange glitter in the distance that was not the reflection of the sky. Still, the delay chafed at my spirit, a senseless irritation, for what was one more night in a wasteland that seemed to exist outside of time itself? As we had a surplus of water for the boiler, and as caution had become Hargrove’s watchword, I ordered a halt in the centre of that immense, flat pan.

The plain was featureless, a sea of salt that stretched to a horizon which quivered and danced. The sun did not set so much as dissolve, bleeding its colour out into the pale sky until the whole world was a symphony of pearl and ghost-grey. A silence fell, a silence so profound it seemed to have a texture, to press in on the eardrums like a physical weight. The living men of my crew, the porters we had hired from the last dusty outpost of civilization, fell still, their chatter ceasing as if a switch had been thrown. It was not the quiet of rest; it was a held breath, an absolute and unnatural stillness. You stared out into the grey, and felt your own substance begin to fray, to dissipate into the shimmering air. Then the stars appeared, not with the gentle grace of a country night, but with a sudden, hard intensity, like chips of ice flung against black glass.

It must have been sometime after midnight when the first note sounded. It was not a noise, precisely, but a vibration, a high, clear resonance that seemed to come not through the air but up through the soles of my boots, through the very iron frame of the wagon. I sat bolt upright in my cot, my heart hammering. It was a pure tone, like a wet finger being drawn around the rim of an impossibly large crystal glass. It held for a moment, beautiful and unnerving, and then it was answered by another, a perfect third above it. Then another, and another, until the whole plain was singing. It was a celestial choir, a cacophony of pure tones that rose and fell in a kind of terrifying harmony, seeming to emanate from all points of the compass at once. The sheer, glorious alienness of it made the skin on my arms prickle. I do not know how it affected the others; to me it was as though the salt-flats themselves had found a voice, a voice that was ancient and utterly indifferent to our presence. The sound swelled to an almost unbearable crest of interwoven notes, and then, in an instant, it stopped, leaving a silence that was now a wound, a vacuum more shocking than the sound had been.

‘Saints above, what was that,’ whispered Hargrove at my shoulder; he was a portly man, with a neat little moustache that now seemed to be trembling on its own account, and he had pulled on a dressing gown of a startlingly inappropriate floral pattern. Two of the other company men, prospectors, stood dumbstruck by the rail, their mouths agape, before scrambling for the gun-locker and emerging with gleaming repeaters clutched in their white-knuckled hands. What we could see was only our own wagon, its form indistinct in the starlight as if it were half-dreamed, and the flat, pale ground extending a few yards in every direction before dissolving into impenetrable gloom. The rest of the universe had ceased to exist, erased without so much as a sigh.

I went to the front of the wagon, where my own crew, the dark-skinned men of the interior, were gathered. ‘Will it come back?’ one of the prospectors asked, his voice cracking. ‘We’ll be torn to pieces out here,’ another muttered. Their faces were taut, their eyes wide and unblinking. It was a curious thing to see the difference between the company men and my porters, who were no more native to this particular salt-pan than we were, though their villages were a thousand miles closer. The whites were, of course, terribly unnerved, but more than that they looked personally offended, as though the universe had committed a gross breach of etiquette. The others, my porters, had a look of deep, sober attention; their faces were fundamentally calm, even the one or two who showed their teeth in expressions I could not decipher as fear or excitement. They murmured to one another in their own tongue, short, guttural phrases that seemed to bring them some sort of consensus. Their leader, a tall, gaunt man with ritual scars on his cheeks, wrapped in a single piece of dusty grey cloth, stood beside me. ‘Well,’ I said, for the simple sake of making a sound. ‘That was something.’

He turned his head slowly, his eyes seeming to absorb the starlight rather than reflect it. ‘The salt sings,’ he said, his voice a dry rasp. ‘It calls for what is lost.’ ‘Lost?’ I asked; ‘what is lost out here?’ ‘Water,’ he said simply, and turned back to his silent vigil, looking out upon the plain with an air of profound and sorrowful understanding. I might have been more unsettled by his words, had it not then occurred to me what these men were paid for this journey. They had been signed on for four months, and of course, as long as a contract existed, bearing the Company’s seal in smudged ink, it did not occur to Hargrove or his superiors to wonder how they might eat. They had been given an advance, not of currency, but of tokens, small disks of tin stamped with the Company’s crest, which were redeemable, in theory, at Company stores. You can imagine how many of those we had passed. So, unless they planned to chew on the tin itself, or perhaps use them as lures to attract the salt-crickets which we sometimes saw, I could not imagine what use their salary was to them. For sustenance, I had seen them carry only small, hard cakes of milled grain and dried meat, which they ate in portions so meagre it seemed a gesture, a ritual of eating rather than a means of survival. Why, in the name of all the hollow gods of starvation, they did not simply overpower us—they were twenty to our four—and seize our well-stocked larder, is a mystery to me even now. They were wiry, resilient men, with little to lose and a strength that had not yet been fully leached by the sun. And I saw that some principle, some obscure code of honour that defied all logic, was at work. I looked at them with a renewed and sudden interest, not because I feared being clubbed for a tin of biscuits, though I admit I suddenly saw, in a new and startling light, how soft and fleshy Hargrove looked, and I hoped, yes, I genuinely hoped, that my own lean frame did not present such a—how to put it?—such a succulent appearance: a flicker of grotesque vanity that seemed to belong to the dream-like state I had inhabited for weeks. Perhaps I was running a fever. One does not travel in such places with a thermometer perpetually under one’s tongue. I often had ‘a touch of the sun,’ or a slight tremor in the hands—the soft inquiries of the wasteland, the preliminary courtesies before the true assault which I felt was surely coming. Yes; I looked at them as one looks at any profound human puzzle.

The singing, as the leader had called it, did not cease. It rose and fell in a slow, unearthly cadence, a vibration that seemed to travel not through the air but through the very ground beneath our feet, a low hum that resonated deep in the bones. Hargrove, who had retreated into the command tent we had pitched on the flat roof of our largest vehicle, emerged now, his face puffy with sleep and indignation. He was a man for whom any phenomenon that could not be logged in a ledger was a personal affront. ‘What is that infernal noise?’ he demanded, not of me, but of the universe at large. ‘Can’t a man get a decent night’s sleep?’

I pointed out that the sound appeared to be coming from the salt itself. ‘It’s a natural occurrence, I suppose,’ I said. ‘Some sort of geological resonance. Wind over the crystals, perhaps.’

‘Wind?’ he scoffed, gesturing at the dead-still air. ‘There isn’t a breath. It’s those men of yours,’ he said, with a nod towards the crew, who sat in a silent circle, their heads cocked as if listening to a familiar, melancholy story. ‘It’s some sort of pagan ritual. I won’t have it. It’s unsettling. Tell them to stop.’

The sheer, imbecile fatuousness of the command left me speechless for a moment. To ask them to stop the plain from singing was like asking them to halt the rising of the sun. ‘They are not making the noise, Hargrove,’ I said, my voice flatter than I intended. ‘They are listening to it.’

‘Listening? What’s there to listen to?’ He squinted out at the immense, pale emptiness, a great artist surveying a canvas he found offensively blank. ‘It sounds like a thousand groaning ghosts. It’s bad for morale.’ He adjusted his nightshirt, a garment of improbable whiteness in that world of grey dust. ‘We push on at first light. I want to be shot of this place.’

With that, he retreated once more to his sanctuary, leaving me to the stars and the sound. I walked over to the circle of men. The leader looked up, his scarred face a map of shadows in the starlight. I did not need to ask. He spoke as if continuing a conversation we had started days before. ‘The old ones say it is the sound of the sea remembering itself,’ he murmured, his voice a dry rustle. ‘Before the sun burned it away, this was the great water. The salt is what remains of its body. The sound is its spirit, crying out.’

A spirit crying out. It was no more fantastic, I thought, than the Company’s belief that one could draw wealth from this desolation with paper contracts and tin tokens. It was, in its own way, a far more sensible explanation, for it acknowledged the power and the sorrow of the place, qualities entirely absent from Hargrove’s ledgers. I stood with them for a time, a silent interloper, and felt the vibration not as a noise, but as a presence, a vast and ancient grief that surrounded our little, temporary camp. The night passed in this sonorous vigil. We were a tiny island of human warmth in a sea of spectral lamentation.

At dawn, the singing faded with the stars, leaving behind a silence that felt heavier, more absolute than before. The sun, when it came, was not a gentle arrival but a sudden, violent assertion of power, striking the salt flat and throwing back a glare that was instantly, painfully blinding. We ate our meagre breakfast, the crew their hard cakes, Hargrove and his two assistants their tinned ham, and I a biscuit that tasted of dust and futility. As ordered, we broke camp. The vehicles, two hulking, six-wheeled tractors of a design that seemed both futuristic and already obsolete, coughed to life with a roar that was a vulgar desecration of the morning’s profound quiet. We began to move, our tracks leaving dark, wounded lines on the white crust of the salt.

For hours we drove, and the landscape did not change. It was a journey through a state of being rather than a physical space. The sun climbed, the heat became a palpable entity, a shimmering, distorting lens through which we viewed the world. And then, around noon, we saw it.

It began as a tremor on the horizon, a darker line against the uniform glare. It was Hargrove who spotted it first, his field glasses glued to his face. ‘There!’ he cried, his voice shrill with a kind of greedy triumph. ‘A settlement! Or trees, at least. An oasis! I knew it. My projections were correct.’

I looked. Through my own glasses, I could see it too: a smudge of cool grey and dark green, the suggestion of stone towers and the deep shade of palms. It seemed to shimmer and shift, to possess a liquid instability, but it was undeniably there, a promise of water and shelter in that hell of light and salt. A wave of relief, so potent it was nauseating, washed over me. The prospect of a cool drink, of a wall to cast a shadow, was a vision of paradise.

The crew had seen it too. A murmur went through them, but it was not one of excitement. It was a low, guttural sound of suspicion, of deep-seated doubt. The leader stood on the running board of the lead vehicle, his hand shielding his eyes, his body as rigid as iron. He said nothing, but his silence was more eloquent than any protest.

‘Full speed!’ Hargrove yelled, thumping the roof of the cab. ‘Don’t spare the fuel! There will be a depot there, I’m certain of it.’

The driver, a nervous young man from the coast, looked at me, his eyes wide with a question. I gave a slight, weary nod. An order was an order, and the logic of our mad enterprise demanded we chase any phantom of success. The engines whined, and our speed increased. The salt crust crunched and popped under our wheels. The heat was ferocious, a solid wall we seemed to be driving into.

For an hour we drove towards the shimmering vision, and a strange thing began to happen. The oasis, the city, whatever it was, it did not seem to get any closer. It remained on the horizon, a fixed and taunting promise. Yet, it grew clearer. I could make out distinct shapes now, or I thought I could: the high, crenellated walls of a fortress, the graceful curve of a bridge, the dense, impossible green of a forest clinging to its walls. It was a city from a dream, an architect’s fantasy set down in the middle of nowhere. It was too perfect, too detailed.

‘It’s a mirage,’ I said to Hargrove, who had joined me in the forward cab.

‘Nonsense,’ he snapped, his face slick with sweat, his eyes fixed and feverish. ‘It’s real. It’s got to be real. It’s Fortitude. The old Company maps show a Fortitude. A forward station.’

‘Those maps were drawn fifty years ago,’ I replied. ‘Based on rumour. This is an illusion, Hargrove. A trick of the heat.’

‘I am the station chief here,’ he said, his voice trembling with a rage born of desperation. ‘And I say it is real. We will reach it by sundown.’

We drove on. The sun passed its zenith. The air in the cab was like the blast from a furnace; to touch any metal surface was to be seared. The city on the horizon continued to mock us. It shimmered, it wavered, it seemed to breath. At one point, I could have sworn I saw flags fluttering from its towers, bright splashes of colour against the grey stone. My own mind, starved of stimulus, began to crave the illusion, to want it to be real with a fierce, illogical passion. I found myself tracing the lines of its impossible architecture, populating its silent streets in my imagination. Perhaps I had a fever. The thought was a familiar one.

Then, as the sun began its descent, painting the western sky in brutal streaks of orange and purple, the city began to change. The clean lines of the towers warped, stretching upwards like melting wax. The cool grey of the stone bled into a sickly, feverish yellow. The lush green of the trees turned black, the colour of rot. The entire vision seemed to curdle, to decay before our eyes, transforming from a promise of salvation into a spectre of disease and ruin. It was no longer a city, but a carcass.

A cry went up from the crew. Not of fear, but of grim, sorrowful recognition. This they understood. This was a truth the salt plain offered up. The leader spoke a single, sharp word in his own tongue, and the driver of the lead vehicle, without waiting for an order from me or from Hargrove, began to slow down.

Hargrove was frantic. ‘Keep going!’ he shrieked, hammering on the dashboard with his fists. ‘It’s a trick of the light! The sunset! Keep going, damn you!’

But it was no use. As the last sliver of the sun disappeared, the spectral city dissolved. It did not fade; it vanished in an instant, as if a lantern had been extinguished, leaving only the unbroken, infinite line of the horizon. We were exactly where we had been hours before: in the centre of a vast and featureless plain, with nothing before us but more of the same. The fuel gauges showed we had wasted nearly a quarter of our reserves.

We stopped. The sudden silence after the roar of the engines was deafening. No one moved. The immensity of our failure, our foolishness, settled upon the little convoy like a physical weight. We had chased a ghost and found only our own exhaustion.

Hargrove was the first to break. He let out a sound that was half a sob, half a snarl of fury. He scrambled out of the cab and stood, a ridiculous figure in his sweat-stained clothes, shaking his fist at the empty horizon where his city had been. ‘A trick,’ he muttered, his voice hoarse. ‘A filthy, native trick.’ He turned on me, his eyes wild. ‘This is your fault. Your men. They willed it so. They don’t want us to succeed.’

‘They warned us,’ I said quietly. ‘In their own way.’

‘Warned us? They sat there like idols! They knew! They let us waste the fuel!’ He took a step towards the crew, who were climbing stiffly from their perches. ‘I’ll have them flogged. Every last one of them. For insubordination. For sabotage.’

The leader of the crew stepped forward, placing himself between Hargrove and his men. He was not a large man, but in that moment, he seemed to possess a stature that dwarfed the fleshy, quivering Company man. He did not speak. He simply stood there, his gaze level, his expression not of defiance, but of a profound and unshakable certainty. It was the look of a man on his own ground, confronting a lost and hysterical child.

Hargrove faltered. He blustered for a moment, his threats dissolving into incoherent muttering, and then, with a final, pathetic glare, he turned and stumbled back to his tent, a defeated king returning to a canvas castle.

A profound weariness fell over me. The charade was becoming too difficult to maintain. The ‘enterprise,’ the ‘mission,’ the very words themselves tasted like ash in my mouth. I looked at the crew. They were gathering up what little equipment they had, their movements economical, resigned. The long, useless drive had taken its toll on them too. That night, the singing of the salt did not return. The silence was absolute, a crushing void. We made a cold camp. There was no talk, no pretence of fellowship. We were a collection of isolated anxieties, marooned together in the dark. I watched the crew share their last few grain cakes. The portions were minuscule, a sacrament to a god who had long since abandoned them. The leader broke his own cake into several pieces and gave them to the youngest boys, keeping none for himself. He then found a place apart from the others and sat, his back against a wheel, and stared out into the darkness with an air of complete and utter stillness.

The question returned to me, more forcefully this time. Why? Why this restraint? They had been cheated of their wages, starved, and now led on a fool’s errand that had brought them closer to death. They had the numbers, and we had the food. Our strength was a sham; Hargrove was a bundle of nerves, his assistants were terrified clerks, and I was a hollow man, a cog in a machine I no longer believed in. The answer was there, in the leader’s posture, in the quiet dignity of his self-denial, but it was an answer written in a language I could not read. It was a human secret, as you say, a mystery that stood in defiance of the simple, brutal calculus of survival.

I felt an impulse to go to him, to ask, to demand an explanation for this baffling honour. But what could I say? ‘Why have you not murdered us for a can of beans?’ The absurdity of it stopped me. Such a question would be an obscenity, a final, unforgivable intrusion into a world of principles I was not fit to comprehend. And so I, too, sat in silence, and felt a new kind of fear, a fear not of being eaten, but of my own utter incomprehension. I was a stranger here, more alien than I had ever known.

It must have been well past midnight when a new sound broke the silence. It was not the singing of the salt. It was a scraping, dragging noise, coming from somewhere out on the plain. It was faint at first, then grew steadily, horribly, closer. I grabbed my rifle. The two clerks, Hargrove’s men, scrambled from their sleeping bags, their faces pale in the starlight. The crew were on their feet in an instant, not with alarm, but with a tense, listening alertness.

The dragging sound stopped a few hundred yards from our camp. There was a moment of perfect silence, and then a low moan, a sound of such utter, final despair that it seemed to freeze the blood. It was not the spectral, inorganic lament of the salt; this was the sound of a living throat, of a suffering that was immediate and real.

Against all reason, I took a lantern and began to walk towards it. Someone had to. Hargrove remained in his tent, a fortress of cowardice. The clerks huddled by the vehicle, armed but useless. I walked out onto the salt, my boots crunching, the lantern casting a small, timid circle of light around me. The leader of the crew fell into step beside me. He carried no weapon, only a long, sturdy stick he used for walking. He did not look at me, but I was glad of his presence.

We found him at the edge of the lantern’s glow. A man. He was crawling, or had been. He lay now on his side, his body a tangle of rags and bones. He was a European, from his features, though his skin was burned black by the sun and stretched so tight over his skull that he looked like a mummified corpse. One of his legs was twisted at an unnatural angle, the bone clearly broken. He was dragging it behind him. It was he who had been making the sound.

He lifted his head as we approached. His eyes were cavernous holes in his face, but in their depths a flicker of sentient awareness still burned. The man on the promontory—Kurtz—raised a single, emaciated arm. It was not a gesture of greeting, nor of warning. It was a gesture of command, of benediction, of absolute possession. As we came abreast of the promontory, I could see Kurtz clearly for the first time. He was not a man anymore. He was a fever-dream, a figure carved from bone and shadow. His eyes were sunk deep in his skull, but they burned with a terrifying, luminous intelligence. They were the eyes of a fallen angel, of a being who had seen the bottom of the pit and found it to his liking. He opened his mouth, and for a moment I thought he would speak, that some profound, terrible utterance would issue from that skeletal frame. But no sound came out. It was as if his voice had been used up, exhausted by the shrieks we had heard earlier. He was a voice, and the voice was now silent.

I took them for decorative knobs at first. But as the light improved, and as I raised my binoculars again, I saw that they were not wood. They were heads. Human heads, dried, shrunken, and turned to face the house. They were black, and their expressions were of an eternal, uncomprehending anguish. They were, I surmised, the heads of rebels. These were the ornaments of Mr. Kurtz’s home. This was how he had administered justice, how he had imposed his will. The horror of it was not in the act itself—I had seen enough of the Company’s work to be past simple shock—but in the terrible, intimate domesticity of it. These were not trophies of war. They were decorations.

I felt a touch on my arm. It was the manager. His face was the color of old tallow. ‘We must proceed with extreme caution,’ he stammered. ‘The man is clearly mad.’

I looked at him, at his clean, starched collar, his plump, fearful face, and I felt a wave of contempt so profound it was almost dizzying. ‘Mad?’ I said. ‘Is that what you call it? We brought the steamer alongside the muddy bank. A path led up to the main building. There was no one to be seen. The station itself was silent, deserted. The crowd of natives remained on the opposite bank, watching, waiting. They made no move to interfere. Their part in the drama, it seemed, was over. Now it was our turn. I gave the order to tie up, and prepared to go ashore. I had to go. I had to speak to him. I had to hear the voice that had presided over these horrors. I had come all this way for the voice. Whatever else happened, I had to hear what it had to say. For my sins, I had to. It was my reward, my punishment, the culmination of my journey into the heart of an immense darkness.

A collective gasp went through the pilgrims. For all his bluster, he was a man of paper, a creature of regulations and procedures. A situation like this, a voice calling out of a white void, was beyond his comprehension.

Not to go would be the greatest insult.’ I did not add that my curiosity had become a fever, a gnawing hunger that had to be satisfied, even at the risk of my life. To turn back now, with the voice so near, would be a kind of spiritual suicide.

The manager, seeing my determination, seemed to make up his mind. He was a company man, after all, and Kurtz, for all his eccentricities, was the company’s most successful agent. There was ivory to be secured, a station to be taken over. He puffed out his chest. ‘Very well,’ he said, with an air of reluctant courage. ‘I will accompany you, of course. The world shrank to the small circle of our boat, the grey, greasy water, and the white, impenetrable curtain ahead. The voice did not speak again. It hung in the air, a memory and a promise. The boat scraped against mud. We had arrived. We stepped out onto a bank of black, stinking ooze that sucked at our boots. There was a narrow, trodden path leading up from the water’s edge, disappearing into the fog after a few yards. We followed it, the pilgrims in the lead, their rifles pointed at nothing. The air was thick, heavy, and smelled of damp earth, of decay, and of something else, something faintly sweet and cloying, like overripe fruit. It was the smell of death, but a perfumed, decadent sort of death.

We had not gone ten yards when a figure emerged from the mist. He was a young man, shockingly young, with a smooth, boyish face, fair hair plastered to his forehead with sweat, and wide, feverishly bright blue eyes. He was dressed in a bizarre collection of clothes: a patched jacket of what looked like blue silk, dirty white trousers, and on his feet, incredibly, a pair of embroidered slippers. The whole outfit was a riot of colour, a gaudy splash against the grey and white of the fog. ‘You have come,’ he said, his voice breathless, exultant. ‘He said you would come. I am so glad. You must not mind the noise earlier. That was nothing. They do not want him to leave, you see. They adore him.’

The manager, recovering a little of his official manner, stepped forward. ‘Who are you?’ he demanded. ‘I am the manager of the Central Station. We are here to relieve Mr. Kurtz.’

The young man’s smile did not falter. He looked at the manager with a kind of pitying amusement, as if a child had announced he was the king of the world. ‘Relieve him?’ he said, with a little laugh. ‘You cannot relieve him. He is a force. You can only listen to him. You can only learn. I have been with him for months. He has enlarged my mind.’

I looked past him, into the fog, expecting to see Kurtz himself. ‘Where is he?’ I asked.

The young man turned his brilliant, unsettling gaze on me. It was a look of intense, fanatical devotion. ‘He is resting. He has been ill, very ill. But his mind… his mind is magnificent. The things he has spoken of… you cannot imagine. He has talked to me of love, of justice, of duty. You should have heard him. I have written it all down. ‘We heard a cry,’ I said, my eyes drawn to the path behind him. ‘A great tumult.’

‘Ah, that,’ he said, waving a dismissive hand. ‘That was the natives. They attacked you, did they not? It was foolish of them. They thought you had come to take him away. They cannot bear the thought of his departure. He has been a god to them. Sometimes he would go out among them, and they would crawl. I have seen it.’ He said this with a simple, unadorned pride, as if stating a self-evident fact of nature.

It was a post, and on top of it was a round object. I walked closer. It was a human head, dried and shrunken, facing the path. Its eyes were closed, and its lips were drawn back in a silent, eternal snarl. It was one of the ‘rebels’ I had seen from the steamer. Close up, it was not an ornament. It was a warning. ‘An expression of his will,’ the young man said, following my gaze. He was not ashamed. He was proud. ‘He has to be severe sometimes. It is necessary. There are no laws here. There is only the will. And his will is a light in this darkness.’

The pilgrims raised their rifles, but he seemed not to notice. His whole being was focused on defending his master, his god.

‘Enough,’ I said, my voice sharp. ‘Take us to him.’

The young man’s fury subsided as quickly as it had arisen. He looked at me, and his eyes were pleading. ‘You will understand,’ he said, almost in a whisper. ‘You are not like them. I can see it. You have looked into the darkness. You will understand him. You must.’

He turned and led the way. We followed him up the path, past more of the grisly signposts, each one a testament to Kurtz’s ‘will’. The fog began to thin a little, and a large, low building loomed before us. It was the main station house, a long, dilapidated structure of wood and thatch, with a wide, dark veranda. The whole place was in a state of shocking disrepair. The railings were broken, the roof was sagging, and the ground was littered with empty tins, broken bottles, and the scattered detritus of a life lived without care or order. Yet, through an open doorway, I could see a table piled high with books and papers, and on a rough-hewn shelf, a row of what looked like scientific instruments. It was the dwelling of a civilized man who had forgotten or disdained the outward forms of civilization. It was the lair of a brilliant, savage hermit.

Our guide, the harlequin, put a finger to his lips. ‘He is in here,’ he breathed. ‘Be quiet. He may be sleeping.’

He led us onto the veranda and into a large, gloomy room. The air inside was thick and hot, filled with the smell of sickness and unwashed linen, and something else, the faint, metallic scent of blood. The room was dark, the only light coming from the doorway and a few cracks in the walls. At first, I could see nothing but a jumble of shadows. Then, as my eyes adjusted, I made out a makeshift bed in the far corner, a simple cot draped with a dirty blanket. On the cot lay a figure.

It was not a man. It was a skeleton. A specter. The form under the blanket was so emaciated it seemed impossible that it could contain a living being. All I could see clearly was a head, a head of startling baldness, like a ball of ivory, and a long, thin arm lying outside the blanket. The arm was nothing but bone and sinew. At the end of it, a hand, a great, bony hand, lay open, as if in a gesture of offering or supplication.

I took a step closer. The manager and the pilgrims hung back, huddled by the doorway, their fear a palpable thing in the stifling air. The harlequin stood by the bed, his face a mask of anxious adoration. I approached the cot as one might approach an altar on which a terrible sacrifice had been made. I looked down at the face. The eyes were closed. The cheeks were hollowed out, the mouth a black, cavernous hole. It was the face of a man who had been consumed from within, eaten alive by his own passions, by the wilderness, by the very darkness he had come to conquer.

I thought he was dead. I thought we had come all this way only to find a corpse, a final, silent mockery of our quest. But then the eyes opened. They were not the dull, glazed eyes of a dying man. They were luminous, intense, and terrifyingly intelligent. They fixed on me, and in their depths, I saw a flicker of immense, weary, and profound triumph. They were the eyes of a fallen angel who remembered heaven and was not sorry for his fall.

A voice, a whisper, but a whisper that filled the entire room, that silenced the beating of my own heart, came from the black hole of the mouth. It was a mere thread of sound, yet it held all the resonance, all the commanding power of the voice I had heard from the river.

‘I have been waiting,’ the whisper said, ‘for a man who could understand.’

‘To guide them,’ he whispered, a ghostly irony touching his lips. ‘To show them the path. But I have seen further now. The philanthropic mask had not just slipped; it had been burned away, leaving the terrible, grinning skull of his true belief.

Before I could process the monstrousness of this postscript, the manager, having recovered a measure of his courage, spoke again. ‘We must prepare to move him,’ he announced, his voice regaining its official tone. ‘The steamer must leave on the morning tide. We will make a stretcher.’

‘He is a Company agent,’ the manager said, his jaw set. ‘And this station must be tidied. He must be brought back to civilization. It is my duty.’ His eyes, however, were not on Kurtz, but on the padlocked chest and the piles of ivory I could now discern in the deeper gloom of the hut, stacked like firewood. His duty was to his cargo.

Kurtz seemed to pay them no mind. His whole being was once again concentrated on me. The skeletal hand lifted from the blanket, a gesture of immense and pitiable effort. It hovered in the air between us, a fragile, bony claw. I did not know what he wanted. Did he want me to take it? To swear an oath? To accept the terrible burden of his legacy? I found myself unable to move, transfixed by the sheer force of the will that radiated from him.

‘You will help me,’ he stated. It was not a question. ‘They do not understand. They hear only the words. You—you hear the silence behind them. The truth.’

The truth. His truth was a thing of darkness and whispers, a philosophy born of isolation and absolute power, a creed written in the blood and terror of the land he had come to enlighten. And he was right. I heard it. I felt it. I understood it not with my mind, but with some deeper, more ancient part of myself that had been awakened by the journey up this river, by the silence of the forest, by the rhythmic beat of the drums in the night.

I gave a slow, almost imperceptible nod. It was an act of surrender. I had enlisted. I was now First Mate on a voyage into the heart of his particular darkness.

A faint, ghastly smile, a mere stretching of the skin over the teeth, appeared on his face. ‘Good,’ he breathed. Then the energy seemed to drain from him completely. The hand fell back onto the blanket with a soft, dead sound. The eyes closed, and the ivory mask of his face became utterly still, so still that for a moment I thought the final transaction had been completed. But the shallow, almost imperceptible rise and fall of the dirty blanket told me that the specter still clung to its last thread of existence.

The manager, taking this for his opportunity, turned to me. ‘Right,’ he said, with brisk authority. ‘You will see to the stretcher. Two poles and a blanket will do. The pilgrims can help you. I will see to the—the assets.’ He strode towards the great chest, his purpose clear.

The harlequin was wringing his hands, his face a mess of tears and despair. ‘He will not survive it,’ he moaned, over and over. ‘You are murdering him.’

I saw the heads on the stakes then. In the gathering twilight, I had at first mistaken them for ornamental knobs on the fence posts around the hut. They were not knobs. They were dried, shrunken heads, turned to face the dwelling. They were black, dried, shrunken, with closed eyelids, a dreadful stillness upon them, and all of them, I noticed with a jolt, had their mouths stuffed with what looked like river clay. They had been silenced. These were the rebels, I surmised. The symbols of a will that had dared to oppose his. This was the fruit of seeing ‘further’.

Kurtz had looked into the abyss, and the abyss had looked back, and in that moment of mutual recognition, he had found not terror, but a terrible, ecstatic freedom. And I, who had just pledged myself to him, I had taken my first step onto that same ledge.

Two of the pilgrims, their faces pale and sweaty, came towards me, their expressions a mixture of fear and a kind of ghoulish curiosity. ‘The manager says—a stretcher?’ one of them asked, his voice trembling slightly.

‘Yes,’ I said, my own voice sounding distant, as if it belonged to someone else. ‘A stretcher. We are taking him with us.’

We found two sturdy saplings and tore a thick blanket from a bale of trade goods. As we worked, the humming from the village grew louder, more insistent. It was joined by the slow, rhythmic beat of a single, large drum. Boom. Boom. Boom. It was a sound that seemed to measure the slow, inexorable pulse of the wilderness itself. The pilgrims kept casting fearful glances over their shoulders towards the sound, their hands fumbling with the knots.

When the makeshift stretcher was ready, we carried it back to the hut. The manager was standing over the now open chest, his hands plunged deep into a glittering, cascading river of ivory trinkets, his face rapt, like a priest before an idol. The harlequin was crouched by the bed, weeping silently. The drumbeat from outside seemed to make the very air in the room vibrate.

‘It is time,’ I said.

The manager looked up, his eyes glazed with avarice. ‘Yes. Time. Get him on board. Quickly. I don’t like the sound of that.’ He gestured with his head towards the village.

They were cloudy now, unfocused. He seemed to be looking past us, through the dilapidated walls of the hut, towards the river.

‘The river,’ he murmured, his voice a dry rustle of leaves. ‘The current waits. We must go with it. Out—out to the sea.’

We carried him out into the twilight. The moment he cleared the doorway, a great cry went up from the direction of the village. It was not the shriek of fury I had heard from the fog, but a sound of a different quality altogether: a deep, prolonged, and utterly mournful wail. It was a sound of immense, collective grief, the cry of a people witnessing the abduction of their deity. Figures began to emerge from the trees, hundreds of them, their bodies gleaming in the dying light, their arms laden with spears and bows. They did not rush us. They simply stood there, a silent, menacing wall of humanity, their faces turned towards the pathetic, skeletal figure on our stretcher.

The pilgrims froze, their faces turning the colour of putty. One of them dropped his corner of the stretcher, and I had to lunge to catch it before Kurtz was tipped to the ground. ‘Keep moving!’ I hissed, my heart hammering against my ribs. ‘To the boat! Now!’

At the center of the throng of warriors stood a woman. She was magnificent and terrible to behold, draped in dark cloths, her arms covered in bracelets of brass and ivory. Her face was a mask of fierce, sorrowful pride. She took a step forward, her hand raised, and the wailing of the crowd fell silent. The drum stopped. In the sudden, profound stillness, her eyes met Kurtz’s.

And he, on his rude litter, dying, defeated, a captive being carried away by hollow men, he made one last effort. He raised his head a fraction of an inch from the blanket. A word formed on his lips, a name perhaps, or a command. But no sound came out. He just stared at her, and in his gaze was a universe of shared secrets, of dark triumphs and a final, agonizing farewell. Then his head fell back, and a tremor ran through his frail body.

The woman did not move. She simply watched us, her expression unreadable, as we scrambled down the muddy bank and onto the deck of the steamer. The manager was already there, shouting orders, frantic to be gone. The pilgrims tossed the stretcher onto the deck with a clumsy thud and ran for their rifles.

I stood for a moment by Kurtz’s side. His eyes were closed again. His breath was a shallow, ragged whisper. The jungle, the crowd, the wild and beautiful woman on the shore, all of it seemed to recede, to become part of a dream from which I was struggling to wake. I looked down at the face of the man who had waited for someone to understand, and I felt the full weight of my complicity settle upon me, a burden heavier than all the ivory in the Congo. I had looked into his darkness, and I had not turned away. Now, I had to carry it with me, down the river, and for all the days of my life that were to follow. For two more days we steamed downriver. Two days that stretched into an eternity, measured not by the rising and setting of the sun, but by the waxing and waning of Kurtz’s fevered consciousness. He would lie for hours in a stupor, so still I would have to bend close to catch the wisp of his breath. He was back in the interior, haranguing unseen multitudes, whispering promises to spectral lieutenants, arguing with unseen rivals. He gave me papers, a bundle of them, thrusting them into my hand with a grip of surprising strength. ‘For you,’ he rasped, ‘Keep them from the manager. He would not understand. The method… the method must be preserved.’ It was a slim packet, mostly a rambling, incoherent report for the ‘Society for the Suppression of Savage Customs,’ filled with soaring eloquence about the civilizing mission, but with a final, chilling postscript scrawled in an unsteady hand: ‘Exterminate all the brutes!’ There was also a photograph, faded and worn, of a woman with a placid, conventional beauty, her hair piled high, a look of serene faith in her eyes. His Intended, I presumed, the one waiting back in that other world, the sepulchral city.

I hid the papers. I listened. I became his confessor, his audience, the last sane man in his kingdom of one. The pilgrims avoided me, and the cabin, as if it were a leper’s hut. He knew I had the papers; he could smell the potential for some final, inconvenient truth that might disrupt his neat columns of profit and loss. We spoke little, but a silent battle was being waged between us over the legacy of the man who lay dying between decks. He wanted to bury Kurtz, not just the man but the entire uncomfortable episode. I… I don’t know what I wanted. I felt a strange, perverse loyalty to the voice, to the magnificent ruin of the man. He had, after all, lived. He had not merely existed, like the others. He had stepped over the edge, and had found something there, something that had consumed him, but something he had at least faced.

On the third night, the end began. The air in the cabin had become unbreathable, a heavy, physical presence. The steamer was tied up to the bank, the fires damped down. The silence of the jungle pressed in on us again, but it was a different silence now, not threatening, but expectant. It was waiting. Kurtz had been quiet all day, a deep, moribund stillness. As I sat by the lamp, watching the play of shadows on his sunken face, his eyes suddenly snapped open. They were no longer focused on any vision of the past. They were fixed on me, and then beyond me, as if looking at something just over my shoulder. There was no terror in them, not at first. ‘I have seen it,’ he breathed, the words so faint I had to lean in close, my ear almost to his lips. ‘The darkness. It is not what you think. It is not the absence of light. It is a presence. It… it looks back.’ He paused, gathering the last dregs of his strength. The steamer creaked. A log shifted in the firebox. He had looked over the edge of the world, and now the abyss had given him his final, irrefutable truth. A whisper, a breath of air, escaped his lips, yet it filled the tiny cabin, it filled my world, it seemed to vibrate in the very structure of the universe.

‘The horror! The horror!’

I stood at the wheel, my hands numb, my mind a blank. The responsibility for the boat, for these lives, was mine, and I had no idea what to do. To go forward was to steam into a trap. To go back was unthinkable. To stay put was to be a sitting target for an enemy we could not see. The current was the only thing with a purpose, pulling us steadily downstream, deeper into this land of shrieking mists and silent, deadly arrows.

It was the fog itself that made the decision for us. It began to lift for good. Not suddenly, as before, but with a slow, majestic deliberation. It rose in great, silent banks, unveiling the world piece by piece. First, the full width of the river appeared, a broad, brown artery. Then the near bank resolved itself out of the mist, a solid wall of green, no longer a phantom but a real, physical barrier. And finally, the far bank came into view. There was a clearing. A raw, red-earth gash in the jungle. At the top of a low hill, a long, low building stood, with a verandah and a high-pitched roof of reeds, all of it in a state of astonishing disrepair. A flagpole, askew, stood bare before it. This, then, was Kurtz’s station. This was the place I had struggled towards for so many months. It was so plentiful it had lost all value; it was just a mass of dead matter, a hoard of pale, curving shapes. And it was all stained with the darkness of the place, with the memory of the shrieks in the fog and the sight of the heads on the stakes. The manager was running his hands over a particularly large tusk, his face flushed, his eyes gleaming. ‘We will get it all,’ he whispered, as if in prayer. ‘Every last piece.’

I turned away in disgust and went back into the oppressive sunlight. The harlequin was wringing his hands. ‘You will take him away, won’t you? He must go home. This place will kill him. But he does not want to go. The work is not finished, he says. The Company has interfered with his plans. He has no medicine. I nursed him through two fevers. I have nothing left.’ He patted his patched pockets, which were visibly empty. ‘He gave me his last quinine.’

A deep, resonant chanting began to rise from the jungle, a low, rhythmic sound that seemed to make the very air vibrate. Sometimes he goes out to them. They have ceremonies. It is… impressive.’

Just then, a commotion came from the main hut. Two of the pilgrims, who had ventured inside, came backing out hastily, their faces pale. And then, framed in the dark doorway, a stretcher appeared, carried by four of Kurtz’s silent followers. On the stretcher lay a man. Or rather, the shadow of a man. I have seen sick men in my time, dying men, but I have never seen a being so utterly consumed, so devoured from within, that only the burning intensity of his will seemed to hold his frame together. He was impossibly long and thin, his skin stretched like old, yellowed parchment over his bones. He was nothing but a skeleton animated by a malevolent fever. And his head. His head was astonishing. It was bald, as bald as one of the spheres of ivory in the shed, and his face was all brow and jaw, a structure of immense power and intelligence. His eyes were sunk deep in their sockets, but they were not the eyes of a sick man. They were luminous, burning with a terrifying energy, and they fixed upon us, upon the steamer, with an expression of intense and ravenous desire.

A voice, not loud but of an immense and weary resonance that seemed to come from the depths of the earth, issued from this spectral figure. ‘Close the shutter,’ it commanded, and the word was not a request, but a decree. One of the natives moved to obey, trying to pull a makeshift blind of reeds over the doorway to shield him from the light, from our sight.

The manager stepped forward, his practical little soul reasserting itself. ‘Mr. Kurtz, I presume,’ he said, with an attempt at a formal bow.

The eyes on the stretcher moved to fix upon him, and a faint, contemptuous smile touched the bloodless lips. The voice came again, a mere whisper, but it carried across the clearing with perfect clarity. ‘At your service.’

The manager launched into a prepared speech, something about the Company’s concerns, about reports, about the unfortunate necessity of his removal for his own health. He spoke of schedules and profits and regulations. And all the while, Kurtz watched him, his gaze unwavering, his expression one of profound, bottomless disdain, as if he were a god listening to the chattering of an insect. The harlequin stood by, shifting his weight from foot to foot, like a disciple witnessing a sacrilege.

My own men, the cannibals, had gathered near the gangplank, watching the scene with their alert, quiet faces. The headman stood with his arms crossed over his broad chest, his gaze fixed on the figure on the stretcher. There was no fear in his eyes, but a look of deep, professional appraisal, the same look he had given the heads on the stakes. He was measuring, I think, one form of power against another.

I found myself walking towards the stretcher, drawn by an impulse I did not understand. It was not pity, not curiosity in the ordinary sense. It was the feeling a man might have approaching the epicentre of an earthquake, a desire to see the source of the devastation. As I came closer, I could see the details of his ruin. His breath was a faint, rattling sound. A tattered blanket covered his skeletal form, and one arm, a mere bundle of sticks and sinew, lay outside it. On his little finger, a plain gold ring looked absurdly large.

He turned his head slowly, and his burning eyes met mine. For an instant, the world seemed to fall away. The chattering manager, the ridiculous pilgrims, the silent jungle, it all vanished. There was only that gaze, a look that held knowledge of things I could not even imagine, a look of triumphant despair. He had kicked the world to pieces, and he was now sovereign of the ruins.

‘You,’ he whispered, and the sound was intimate, conspiratorial. ‘They sent you.’

‘I am the captain of the steamer,’ I replied, my own voice sounding hollow.

He made a faint gesture with his bony hand, a gesture that encompassed the whole station, the river, the immense, waiting continent. ‘My station,’ he breathed. ‘My ivory. My Intended. My…’ He paused, and his gaze drifted past me, towards the dark wall of the forest. A look of extraordinary, terrible longing came into his face. ‘My jungle.’

The chanting from the forest grew louder, more insistent. A woman appeared at the edge of the clearing, walking with a slow, regal gait. She was draped in dark cloths, her arms were covered in brass bangles, and her face was a mask of proud, brooding sorrow. She stopped and raised her arms towards the hut, a gesture of supplication, of command.

‘His woman,’ the Russian breathed in my ear. ‘She is a queen. She has caused trouble. She does not want him to go.’

Kurtz’s eyes followed her, and for a moment, his face softened. It was a fleeting, human expression, and it was almost more shocking than the horror. Then the mask of power descended again. He tried to raise himself on one elbow, a flicker of his old, indomitable energy returning. ‘I will not be taken,’ he said, his voice gaining strength. ‘This is my kingdom. I must finish the work.’

The manager, seeing his ivory about to stage a rebellion, began to shout orders to the pilgrims. ‘Get him on board! Now! We are leaving at once!’

The pilgrims, emboldened by the man’s obvious physical weakness, surged forward. Kurtz’s followers, the silent watchers, took a step forward as well, their spears held ready. The air crackled with a tension that was about to snap. For a heart-stopping second, I saw it all dissolving into a bloody, pointless skirmish on this forgotten mudbank, another verse in the sordid epic of greed.

He held them all, friend and foe, in the palm of his skeletal hand. He looked at me, and his lips curled into that faint, terrible smile. ‘One must have a choice of nightmares,’ he whispered. Then he let his head fall back onto the rough pillow, his eyes closing as if in exhaustion. The decision, it seemed, had been made.

The Russian, the harlequin, had accosted me on the deck just as the anchor was being weighed. He was in a state of high agitation, his boyish face streaked with dirt and tears. ‘You will look after him?’ he implored, clutching at my sleeve. ‘He is a great man. A universal genius. The manager—he would destroy it.’ I took the packet; it felt heavy, not with paper, but with the weight of an immense and terrible idea. He was an absurdity, a joke; and yet, in his pure, unthinking devotion, there was a kind of faith that I, in my grim, weary knowledge, could only envy and despise.

The journey downriver was a strange and haunted affair. The sun, when we saw it, was a coppery disk, veiled by a perpetual, feverish haze. The river was no longer a highway into the unknown, but a corridor of escape, and the jungle on either side seemed to press in on us, its silence no longer passive, but watchful, patient, waiting. It seemed to know we carried its tainted heart with us, and it was in no hurry. It knew the river had its own slow, inexorable logic. The paddle-wheels beat their monotonous tattoo, a funeral drum marking our retreat. The pilgrims, their immediate fear assuaged, resumed their usual state of listless, grasping discontent. They argued over scraps of food, polished their rifles, and spoke in hushed, excited tones of the fortunes in ivory stowed in the hold. They avoided the pilothouse as if it were a leper’s hut. Only the manager would occasionally appear at the door, his thin lips drawn into a semblance of a smile, his eyes cold and calculating as they flicked from my face to the figure on the couch and back again. ‘How is our—guest—today, Mr Marlow?’ he would ask, his voice dripping with a solicitude that was more insulting than open contempt. I would grunt some noncommittal reply, and he would nod, satisfied, and retreat, already mentally counting the tusks, weighing the profit from this man’s soul.

And Kurtz; he lay there, a wraith in the stifling heat, his body consumed by the fever, but his mind, his terrible mind, burning with a bright, erratic flame. He talked. He talked for hours, his voice a reedy whisper that filled the small cabin with grandiose plans, with bitter accusations, with fragments of poetry, with pronouncements of breathtaking arrogance. He spoke of his ‘Intended,’ a distant, pure image he had somehow managed to preserve amidst the squalor of his own degradation. He spoke of the ‘brutes’ he had come to civilize, his voice laced with a venomous hatred that was terrifying in its intensity. He spoke of his methods, of the necessity of exerting a will of iron, of the station as a beacon of progress in the benighted wilderness. One afternoon, when the heat was at its most oppressive, and the boat seemed to be crawling through a river of warm oil, he called me over to the couch. I looked up from the paper and met his gaze. There was no shame in his eyes, no apology; only a fierce, triumphant clarity. It was as if he had finally stripped away all the cant, all the hypocrisy, and arrived at the one brutal, undeniable truth that lay at the heart of his experience. He saw my shock, and a faint, spectral smile played on his lips. ‘You see,’ he breathed, ‘one must not be sentimental.’ It was the cry of a soul that had tasted absolute power, had looked into the abyss, and had found it not just habitable, but congenial. I felt a cold dread creep over me, a dread that was not for him, but for myself; for in that moment, I understood him. I did not condone, you understand; but I understood. I saw the straight, logical, terrible path from the high-minded ideals of the Society to the shrunken heads on the stakes and the scribbled words in my hand. The manager came running, his face a mask of fury and panic. ‘What is it? What have you done?’ he shrieked at me, as if I had personally willed the breakdown. We buried him the next morning. If you can call it a burial. There was no ceremony, no word spoken. The pilgrims stood about in a small, nervous cluster, their faces a mixture of relief and a kind of morbid curiosity. Two of the crew, their black skins gleaming with sweat, wrapped the body in a length of old canvas, weighting it with a few scraps of iron from the engine room. They carried him to the rail. For a moment, he rested there, a shapeless bundle poised between the steamboat and the immense, silent jungle. Then, with a quiet heave, they pushed him over. There was a surprisingly small splash. The brown water swirled for an instant, a single bubble rose to the surface and popped, and then he was gone. The river took him without a ripple, swallowing him down into its muddy, inscrutable depths as though he had never been. The current flowed on, smooth and implacable. The journey was over, and the man was erased.

I gave the order to get underway. The steam-whistle shrieked, a thin, hysterical sound that tore through the morning stillness and sent a cloud of startled birds rising from the trees. The paddle-wheels began their rhythmic, churning beat, and the little steamboat, clumsy and insignificant, turned its nose downstream. We were going home. We were being swept away from the heart of a mystery, back towards a world of facts that seemed, by comparison, utterly false. The current was with us now, and the banks slid by at a deceptively brisk pace. The very landscape seemed to sigh in relief, eager to be rid of us. Yet for me, the journey back was infinitely longer and more arduous than the journey in. Then, I had been driven by a purpose, a curiosity to meet the man, Kurtz. Now, I was adrift, carrying the weight of his final confession, a passenger in a boat piloted by a victorious mediocrity.

The days that followed were a grey blur of steaming down that monotonous, snake-like river. The manager was insufferably cheerful, a model of efficiency and good order. He had what he wanted. Kurtz was dead, the troublesome natives of the Inner Station were leaderless and would soon be brought to heel, and he, the prudent, patient manager, would be lauded for restoring discipline. He made several more attempts to get the papers from me, approaching the subject with a variety of clever, indirect methods. He spoke of the need for a comprehensive report for Brussels, of the value of Kurtz’s ethnographic notes for future operations, of his concern for the man’s reputation, which, he hinted, could be protected only by his careful editing of any… indiscretions.

I held fast. I do not know why, precisely. It was not a matter of loyalty in the ordinary sense; it was my duty to Kurtz; a duty to a dead man which felt more binding than any contract I had ever signed with the living. I kept the packet of papers, wrapped in an oilcloth, under the mattress of my cot. It felt at times like a sacred relic, at others like a bomb.

One afternoon, about a week into our return, the inevitable happened. There was a groaning shudder that ran through the whole vessel, a series of metallic coughs from the engine room, and then a juddering halt. Silence. The paddle-wheels stopped their churning. We drifted, turning slowly in the current, once more at the mercy of the river. The manager came storming out of his cabin, his face purple. He cursed the chief engineer, a melancholy, bearded man who simply shrugged and spoke of a cracked cylinder head, of the impossibility of making repairs without a proper workshop. We were stranded.

The delay, which enraged the manager and the pilgrims, was to me a kind of reprieve. It was another immersion into that profound, waiting silence of the wilderness. But this silence was different. It was no longer pregnant with a hidden menace or a secret promise; it was simply empty. The heart had stopped beating. I spent the long, hot hours of our immobility in my cabin with Kurtz’s legacy.

There was a bundle of personal letters, mostly from his Intended, full of a touching, innocent faith in his goodness and his civilizing mission. Reading them felt like an act of sacrilege, like peering into a clean, well-lit room from a place of utter filth. And then there was the report. It was a magnificent piece of writing, seventeen pages of close script, written for some ‘International Society for the Suppression of Savage Customs.’ It began with a soaring, eloquent argument that we, the emissaries of light, must approach the natives with the godlike power of our benevolence; that by sheer example and superior wisdom we could guide them out of their darkness. It was vibrant with a magnificent, if naive, belief in the power of words, in the ideals that were supposed to be the foundation of the whole enterprise. It made you believe, for a moment, in the fantasy. It was inspiring, and it was also mad. Because at the very end, scrawled in a shaky but determined hand, as if an afterthought that contained the whole truth of the matter, were the words: ‘Exterminate all the brutes!’

There it was, the abyss between the man’s soaring rhetoric and his final, whispered verdict. The horror. He had seen it all, the lofty ideals and the savage reality, and his soul had been the battleground. The man who had written of sympathy and progress was the same man who had presided over unspeakable rites, who had adorned the fence posts of his station with human heads. He had not been corrupted; he had been revealed. The wilderness had not whispered lies to him; it had told him a truth about himself, and he had found in his own heart a perfect echo. He had looked into it, and it had looked back, and in that final moment of lucidity, he had judged. The eloquence was the mask, the civilized lie; that brutal scrawl was the naked, shivering soul.

I sat there in the stifling heat of the cabin, the eloquent report in one hand, the memory of that final cry in my mind. What was I to do with such a truth? The manager wanted the document so he could excise the postscript, to present the noble sentiments to the Company as proof of Kurtz’s value, to bury the truth just as we had buried the man. And I knew that when I returned to that city of whited sepulchres, to the world of his Intended, I would be expected to offer up a similar lie, to preserve the beautiful illusion of the man she thought she knew.

The repairs took an age. The chief engineer and his crew toiled in the hellish heat of the engine room, their banging hammers a feeble protest against the vast indifference of our surroundings. The pilgrims grew listless, their faces slack with boredom and the humidity. They gambled with greasy cards, and their low squabbles were the only human sound to disturb the peace. The manager paced the deck like a caged animal, his eyes fixed downstream, calculating the cost of the delay in francs and centimes. As for the cannibals, our crew, they sat quietly on the lower deck, watchful and patient. Their hunger, which had seemed so pressing on the journey up, had subsided into a kind of remote disdain. They had seen our god die, and they were not impressed. Their headman, when he passed me, would sometimes give me a long, searching look, not of fellowship, but of a deep, ancient, and unfathomable appraisal. I had the feeling he knew. Not the details, not the words, but the essential fact of the collapse, the hollowness at the centre of our enterprise. He knew the witchcraft had failed.

One evening, as the sun was setting in a smear of lurid red and orange, the manager approached me again. The air was thick and still, promising another suffocating night. He did not mention the papers directly. He spoke instead of Kurtz’s fiancée, of the sad duty that awaited me in Europe. ‘A terrible blow for the poor woman,’ he said, his voice oozing a counterfeit sympathy. ‘One must be… delicate. One must protect her from the more… distressing details of his illness.’ He looked at me, his little eyes glinting. ‘His reputation is in your hands, Marlow. A few ill-considered words, a single unfortunate document, and a great man’s memory could be tarnished. The Company would be grateful for your discretion. Very grateful.’

It was a threat wrapped in a promise. He was offering me a pact, a place in the conspiracy of silence. Give me the papers, he was saying, and we will all agree on the story. We will make Kurtz a hero, a martyr to the cause. We will preserve the fiction. And in that moment, looking at his self-satisfied, prudent face, I felt a kinship with Kurtz that was stronger than ever. I, too, was being tempted. Not by ivory, or power, or savage rites, but by the simple, comfortable lure of the lie. The temptation to smooth things over, to make it all neat and tidy, to spare the feelings of a mourning girl and to earn the gratitude of a powerful company. It was the whisper of the profane world, a temptation far more insidious than the frank brutality of the jungle. I saw then that the darkness was not just in the heart of the continent; it was everywhere, in boardrooms, in drawing-rooms, in the careful, calculated phrases of civilized men. It was a darkness of a different kind altogether, a grey, pervasive twilight of compromise and evasion.

‘I will be as discreet as the situation warrants,’ I told him, and turned my back on him to watch the last of the sun drown in the black water of the river. The choice had been made for me, it seemed. I would not give him the truth, because he would only twist it into a more profitable lie. I would have to carry it myself, a secret infection, back to the world that had sent us here, a world that thrived on its own carefully constructed illusions. I was Kurtz’s heir, not to his station or his ivory, but to his appalling vision, and to the necessity of finding a lie to tell.

I stood clutching the rail, and the thick, tepid air of the river seemed to press in on me, heavy with the weight of unspoken things, of words I now had to carry in place of the ivory I had come to fetch. The journey downriver, I perceived, was to be a different kind of trial. The journey up had been a slow, grinding approach to a mystery, a pulling against the current towards a name, a voice, a darkness. This, the return, was a surrender to the current, a flight, but a flight with the core of the mystery now lodged inside me, like a piece of shrapnel working its way deeper towards the heart.

The steamer itself felt different. On the way up, for all its wretched slowness and propensity for breaking down, it had been a vessel of purpose, a tool for penetration. Now it was a hearse. It carried a dead man’s memory, a dead man’s secrets, and a crew of living phantoms, black and white, all of us sliding down the immense, silent stream, away from a horror that we could not comprehend and back towards a civilization that would not believe. The pilgrims, I noticed, had already begun to recover their spirits. The immediate threat being removed—the threat of shrieks in the fog—they reverted to their natural state of petty squabbling and sanctimonious greed. I heard them muttering about the lost ivory, the missed opportunities, the sheer inconvenience of Kurtz’s unorthodox methods which had, it seemed, culminated in the supreme inconvenience of his death. His value, you see, had been reduced to a ledger entry, and the final sum was a disappointment.

I left them to their calculations and went to my cabin, a space no bigger than a cupboard, and oppressively hot. The air within was stale, smelling of rust and damp blankets and the faint, lingering scent of the fever I had only just shaken off. There, by the light of a flickering oil lamp that swung with the gentle motion of the boat, I took out the packet of papers. It was a meagre bundle, tied with a bit of string. There was the pamphlet, of course, that eloquent, magnificent, and terrifying piece of prose on the ‘Suppression of Savage Customs,’ written for the International Society for that very purpose. I untied the string and spread the pages. The lamplight gave the ink a brownish, faded look, the look of something already ancient. His words soared, they flew, they spoke of a benevolent light, of progress, of weaning these primitive souls from their dark ways. It was beautiful, it was convincing, it was a lie of the highest order, the kind of lie that can only be constructed by a man who has glimpsed a profound truth.

And then, at the bottom of the last page, that postscript, scrawled in a shaky but determined hand, a frantic addendum that negated all the soaring prose that came before it. ‘Exterminate all the brutes!’ There it was. The truth in nine words. The final synthesis of his experience, the practical application of his lofty ideals when faced with the reality of the jungle, the reality of his own soul. The lamp cast my shadow, huge and distorted, upon the wall, and for a moment I felt as if Kurtz himself were in the cabin with me, a gaunt, whispering presence, nodding his ivory head in approval. He had trusted me with this. Why me? Perhaps because in my own fumbling, skeptical way, I too was a man who looked for a truth behind the surface of things. And I had found one. I had found his.

A sudden, violent shudder ran through the steamboat, nearly throwing me from my stool. The engine had stopped. Of course. It was its nature. A profound silence descended, broken only by the gurgle of the current against the hull. The night outside the small porthole was absolute, a solid wall of blackness against which the river’s faint, phosphorescent gleams were like the last dying thoughts of the world. I sat there in the dark, the papers in my hand, and felt the immensity of my task. To preserve the lie of the pamphlet while concealing the truth of the postscript. It was not a matter of simple omission. It was a work of creative deception. I had to build a new Kurtz from the ruins of the old one, a Kurtz fit for public consumption, a Kurtz for his fiancée, for the Company, for the world that had made him and sent him out, and which was now so eager to disown the consequences.

The silence on deck grew restive. I could hear the shuffling of feet, anxious whispers. The manager would be looking for someone to blame. The pilgrims would be certain this was a new, insidious attack. I tucked the papers inside my shirt, the stiff pages scratching against my skin. They felt both dangerous and precious, a kind of sacred, damning relic. When I emerged, the air was thick enough to chew. The stars, which had been brilliant a moment before, were gone, smothered by a low-lying bank of cloud. The headman of my crew was standing at the prow, his silhouette motionless against the slightly less black expanse of the river. He was not looking for enemies. He was listening. His whole body was an instrument of perception, attuned to the river’s moods in a way I could never be.

‘She’s gone again,’ I said, stating the obvious. It was a habit one fell into out here; speech was a way of confirming your own existence against the overwhelming silence.

He turned his head slowly. He did not speak for a long moment. Then, ‘The river is tired,’ he said, in his deep, guttural voice. It was not a mechanical diagnosis. It was a statement of profound, spiritual weariness. The river was tired of us, of our noisy, stinking, fire-breathing canoe, of our frantic comings and goings. It simply wished for us to be still. I had the absurd thought that the breakdown was not mechanical at all, but a deliberate act of will on the part of the stream.

Then the rain began. It did not start with a few tentative drops. It was as though a celestial dam had burst. A solid, vertical wall of water slammed down upon the steamboat, a deafening, continuous roar that blotted out all other sound. It was not cleansing; it was an assault. The deck instantly became a shallow, fast-running river of its own. Lightning split the sky, not in clean forks, but in great, searing sheets that lit up the scene for a ghastly, prolonged second: the high, glistening black walls of the jungle on either side, the thrashing, pock-marked surface of the river, and the faces of the men on the boat. The pilgrims were a tableau of comic terror, huddled under the flimsy awning, their mouths open in silent shouts. The manager was nowhere to be seen; he would be in his cabin, no doubt, counting his tins of meat.

My crew, the dark, powerful men from the coast, were another matter. They worked. Under the headman’s quiet commands, they moved with a frantic, beautiful efficiency, securing loose gear, checking the woodpiles, their bodies gleaming like polished bronze in the strobing light. They were not fighting the storm. They were moving with it, a part of its wild energy. And I, the captain, stood there, drenched to the bone in seconds, feeling the thunder of the rain on my skull, and a strange, feverish elation rose in me. This was real. This elemental fury was a thing of absolute purity compared to the murky, equivocating darkness I carried in my shirt. The lightning flashed again, and in that instant, I saw Kurtz’s face, not as I had last seen it, emaciated and dying, but as it must have been in his prime: wide-eyed, ecstatic, terrible, his mouth open, shouting into a storm just like this one. ‘The horror! The horror!’ was not a confession; it was a judgement. He had judged it all, the jungle, the river, the natives, the pilgrims, the Company, himself, and found it all, all of it, wanting.

I felt a touch on my arm and started violently. It was the headman. He was holding out a tin mug. Steam rose from it, instantly whipped away by the wind and rain. I looked at him, and his face in the intermittent glare of the lightning was unreadable, a mask of ancient wisdom and profound indifference. He pointed at my mouth, then at the mug. I took it. The liquid was hot, bitter, and foul-tasting, some brew of leaves and bark he had kept simmering. I drank it down, the warmth spreading through my chest, a small, solid anchor in the chaos. He gave a short, sharp nod, a gesture of completion, not of servitude, and turned back to his work. It was a moment of communion, more real than any conversation I had ever had with the manager or his acolytes. He and I, we were the only two men on that boat, perhaps, who understood that the world was not a sane or orderly place. He had his rituals, his restraints, the inherited experience of his people; I had only the appalling vision of a dead man and a promise to tell a lie.

The storm passed as suddenly as it had arrived. The roar ceased, replaced by the dripping of a million leaves, a sound like the whole world weeping quietly to itself. The clouds broke, and a sliver of moon appeared, casting a sickly, anemic light on the scene. The engine, by some miracle of its own perverse constitution, chose that moment to sputter back to life. The monotonous thud-thud began again, the heartbeat of our ridiculous enterprise. We were moving.

I went back to my cabin, my clothes plastered to my body, but the shivering had stopped. The headman’s brew, or perhaps the storm itself, had burned away the last of the fever and left behind a cold clarity. I knew now what the lie had to be. It could not be a small, timid lie. A small lie would be found out, it would be sniffed at and worried and torn apart by the hounds of rectitude and jealousy. No, it had to be a grand lie, a noble lie, a lie on the same scale as Kurtz’s own magnificent delusions. He had not died of savagery and greed, a hollow man devoured by the wilderness. No. He had died a martyr. A great emissary of light, struck down by a terrible illness while on the very cusp of achieving a great work. His last words had not been a cry of despair, but a whisper of love and loss, a name on his lips. Her name.

It was perfect. It was what she would want to hear, what the Company could accept, what the world would applaud. It transformed his squalid end into a tragedy, his madness into a noble, suffering sensitivity. It was a work of art, in its own way. I would take his soaring, eloquent pamphlet and present it as his final testament. I would take the memory of his voice, that deep, compelling organ, and I would make it speak my words. I would become Kurtz’s last and greatest apostle, preaching a gospel in which I did not believe, to a world I had come to despise. The difficulty, I saw, would not be in the telling. The difficulty would be in living with the echo of it afterwards, in the quiet rooms of a Europe that seemed to me now like a vast, well-appointed tomb. I had looked into the heart of a great darkness, and it had tasked me with becoming a purveyor of light. The irony was so profound, so complete, that it bordered on the sublime. I almost laughed. It was the only response that made any sense. The steamboat chugged on, carrying me and my revelation down the dark, indifferent river, back to the world of men.

The others, with their Winchesters, had the look of children playing a dangerous game, their eyes wide not with courage but with a sort of theatrical horror, as if they were already composing the story they would tell of it later, provided they survived.

The headman had turned away from me. His remark, ‘Eat ’im!’, hung in the air between us, a stark and brutal piece of honesty in a world built on evasions and polite fictions. It was not a threat, I began to realise, so much as a statement of fact, a simple, logical conclusion drawn from the premises of his existence: there is a source of meat; we are starving; therefore, let us eat. The horror of it was not in his desire, but in the impeccable, unshakable logic of it. He was not a monster. He was merely hungry. And I, with my tins of preserved food and my ridiculous salary paid in drafts on a European bank, I was a creature from another universe, a universe where such logic was inadmissible, unthinkable. The gap between us was not a matter of miles or of skin colour; it was a chasm of ages, a difference in the fundamental assumptions one made about the world. He still lived in a world of immediate and terrible necessities; I lived in a world of deferred consequences and abstract principles. And I was not at all sure which of us was the more deluded.

The silence returned, but it was a different silence now. The first one had been empty, a void. This one was full, pregnant with listening. We were all listening, the pilgrims for the rush of feet, the twang of bowstrings; the crew for whatever their instincts told them to listen for; and I, I was listening to the frantic thumping of my own heart, a foolish, agitated little drum beating against the immense, patient stillness of the continent. The fog held us in its soft, clammy embrace, a blind and deaf god who had decided to take an interest in our absurd little steamboat. You felt a desire to shout, to make a noise, to prove your own existence against this overwhelming negation. But we were all struck dumb, caught in the trance that had fallen upon the land itself.

Then a new sound began, a sound so faint at first that I thought it was a ringing in my own ears, a product of the strain and the fever. It was a low, monotonous hum, a vibration rather than a sound, something you felt in the deck plates and in the bones of your skull. It grew steadily, a deep, resonant drone that seemed to come not from the banks but from the very air, from the fog itself. It was not a warlike sound. It had none of the fury of the earlier shrieks. It was something else entirely: a sound of immense, unutterable sorrow, a collective groan of a world in pain. It rose and fell in a slow, lugubrious rhythm, a funeral dirge for a dying god.

‘What is it now?’ whispered the fat pilgrim, his voice trembling. He had given up on the crate and was now crouched by the rail, peering into the white nothingness as if he could force it to yield its secrets by the sheer intensity of his gaze.

I did not answer. I was listening to the headman, who had begun to hum along with the sound, a deep, quiet vibration in his chest. His eyes were closed, and his face, which a moment before had been a mask of fierce hunger, was now serene, almost contemplative. One or two of the other crewmen joined in, their voices weaving a soft, intricate counterpoint to the great, mournful sound from the shore. They were not afraid. They understood. This was not a prelude to an attack. It was a ceremony.

The manager, who had been hiding in the pilot-house, now chose to make his appearance. He was a man whose authority was directly proportional to the visibility of his surroundings; in the clear light of day, he was a petty tyrant, but in this fog, he was just a frightened man in a clean collar.

‘What are you doing, Mr. Marlow?’ he demanded, his voice a squeak of indignation. ‘Why are we sitting here? We should fire into the banks! A volley from the Winchesters, that will scatter them!’

‘Scatter what?’ I asked, without turning my head. ‘The fog? Or the echoes?’

‘The savages, man, the savages!’ he hissed, gesturing vaguely into the white wall around us. ‘Can’t you hear them? They are preparing to attack! We must show strength!’

It was the classic creed of the weak and frightened: when in doubt, make a loud noise and shoot at shadows. It had probably been the guiding principle of more follies and disasters in this land than hunger or disease.

‘There will be no firing,’ I said, and the words came out with a cold flatness that surprised even myself. ‘We will wait. The sound is not hostile.’

‘Not hostile?’ he sputtered. ‘Mr. Marlow, I hold you responsible for the safety of this expedition! I am the manager here!’

‘You are the manager,’ I agreed, turning to face him at last. His face was pale and sweaty, his eyes darting about like trapped animals. ‘But I am the captain of this steamboat. And on this steamboat, for as long as this fog lasts, the only authority is the river, and the only sensible course is to listen to it. Now get back in the pilot-house before you catch a chill.’

He stared at me, his mouth opening and closing silently. For a moment I thought he would explode in a fury of impotent rage. But there was something in my eyes, I suppose, a touch of the feverish, dreamy indifference that had taken hold of me, that gave him pause. Or perhaps it was simply that in this strange, disembodied world of white fog, the normal hierarchies had dissolved. He was not a manager and I was not a steamboat captain; we were just two men adrift in a cloud, and my particular brand of madness seemed, for the moment, more functional than his. He gave a little grunt of disgust, turned, and disappeared back into the relative safety of the pilot-house.

The chanting from the shore continued, a vast, hypnotic wave of sound that washed over us, eroding the sharp edges of our fear, lulling us into a state of weary acceptance. I found myself swaying slightly in time with the rhythm. The world had been reduced to this: a circle of grey water, the blurred shape of a steamboat, and a sound that might have been the breathing of the earth itself. It was strange, but I felt a kind of peace. The striving, the pushing on, the desperate race against time to reach Kurtz—all of it seemed suddenly foolish and irrelevant. What was one man, however remarkable, compared to this ancient, timeless sorrow?

The sun must have risen higher, though we could not see it. A change came over the fog. It did not lift, but it grew brighter, suffused with a pale, pearly luminescence. The white walls became translucent, and vague, ghostly shapes began to appear in their depths. Trees, impossibly tall and slender, emerged like phantoms, then faded again. It was like watching a world being born, or perhaps remembered, in a dream.

And then, as if a switch had been thrown, a section of the fog directly ahead of us dissolved. It did not drift away; it simply vanished, leaving a perfectly clear, circular window in the white curtain. And in that window, framed like a picture, was the source of the sound.

It was a village, or what was left of one. A collection of miserable, conical huts huddled by the riverbank. But the people were not in the huts. They were gathered in a wide circle in a clearing, and in the centre of the circle was a great, dark shape. It was a man, I saw, a chief or an elder, laid out on a bier of woven reeds. And the people, men, women, and children, were swaying and chanting, their bodies painted with white clay, their faces masks of ritualised grief. The great, soaring cry we had heard earlier had not been a war cry; it had been a cry of mourning, the first, piercing note of a long and elaborate farewell. The intolerably excessive shrieking had been the climax of their shared sorrow. We had not blundered into an ambush; we had blundered into a funeral.

The pilgrims stared, their mouths agape. The Winchesters hung limp in their hands. This was a savagery they could not comprehend. It was not the thrilling, bloodthirsty savagery of their fireside stories. It was the savagery of a grief so profound it had to be given a voice, a body, a ritual. It was organised, it was communal, it had a purpose. It was, in its own way, terribly civilised.

I looked at my headman. He was standing very still, his head bowed. He was not one of them, his home was hundreds of a miles away, his people were different, his language was not theirs. But he recognised the shape of their sorrow. He was a man who understood death. He looked at me, and his eyes were no longer bloodshot with hunger, but clear and deep. He gave a slight, almost imperceptible nod, not to me, but to the scene before us, a gesture of respect, of acknowledgement.

The window in the fog remained open for perhaps five minutes. We were like spectators in a theatre, watching a play of which we did not understand the language, but whose tragic import was unmistakable. Then, as smoothly and silently as it had opened, the window closed. The fog rolled back in, the picture vanished, and we were once again alone with the sound, which now seemed to us not menacing, but profoundly, deeply human.

A great sigh seemed to go through the pilgrims. It was the sigh of an anticlimax, of a tension released without a satisfying explosion. They had been cheated of their battle. They looked vaguely foolish, standing there with their guns and their fearful expressions. The fat pilgrim slowly, self-consciously, began tucking his pink pyjamas back into his socks, as if restoring this little piece of order would help to restore the world to its proper shape.

I turned to the engine-room telegraph and rang for ‘Slow Ahead’. The anchor chain rattled as the crew, with a new energy, began to haul it in. The monotonous thud-thud of the engine resumed, and the steamboat began to creep forward, pushing its nose into the white blanket. We were moving again.

The manager reappeared, his confidence restored by the sound of the machinery. ‘Ahem, Mr. Marlow,’ he began, adopting his official tone. ‘A most peculiar incident. Most peculiar. It seems you were right, for once. A good thing we did not fire. Wasting ammunition, you know. And it might have provoked them.’

‘It might have,’ I said.

He looked out into the fog, a slight frown on his face. ‘Still,’ he said, as if to himself, ‘one cannot be too careful. A strange people. All that noise. Very unseemly. When my time comes, I hope for a bit more dignity.’

I thought of the man on the bier, of the immense, communal outpouring of grief, and then I thought of the lonely, sterile rooms in Europe where death was a whispered, hygienic affair, a private embarrassment to be tidied away as quickly as possible. And I wondered which was the more dignified.

‘I am sure you will have it,’ I told him.

We steamed on in silence for another hour, the mournful chanting slowly fading behind us until it was swallowed by the fog and the noise of our own passage. The sun was hot now, and the fog began to thin, not by lifting, but by turning into a fine, warm drizzle that plastered our clothes to our skin and made the deck slippery. Great, dark shapes of trees loomed and receded on either side. We were still in a world of ghosts.

I found myself standing next to the headman again. He was staring out into the mist, his pensive attitude from before returned, but with a difference. It was no longer the pensiveness of hunger, but of thought.

‘A sad business,’ I said, for the sake of saying something.

He turned his head and looked at me. ‘He was a great chief,’ he said, and I was startled, for I had not thought he would know. But news, like the river, has its own currents in this land.

‘They are mourning,’ I said, stating the obvious.

‘They mourn,’ he agreed. Then he paused, and added, ‘And they will choose a new chief. Then they will feast.’

He said it so simply, so matter-of-factly. Mourning, choosing, feasting. The cycle of life and death, stripped of all sentimentality. And suddenly his earlier words came back to me, ‘Eat ’im!’. It had not been a joke. It had not been just about his own hunger. It had been about a deeper, more primal understanding of the world. In this land, nothing was wasted. Not even sorrow. Not even death. The life of a fallen enemy, or perhaps even a fallen chief, was absorbed, literally, back into the tribe, a transfer of strength, of spirit. It was a kind of sacrament. A horrible, unthinkable sacrament.

I looked at him, at his strong, calm face, at the oily ringlets of his hair, at the fierce nostrils that seemed to sniff the very secrets of the air. He was not a savage. He was a man with a different set of truths, a different and perhaps more terrifyingly coherent philosophy than my own. My morality, the morality of the white men on this boat, was a thing of prohibitions and negations: thou shalt not. His was a thing of affirmations, of necessities, of an ironclad logic that led from the grave to the cooking pot.

And Kurtz, I thought, Kurtz, the man of soaring eloquence, the emissary of pity and science and progress, what would he have made of this? He had come here to enlighten, to improve. But the wilderness had a logic of its own. It did not listen to eloquent phrases. It listened to the rumbling of an empty stomach. It presented you with a choice: adapt or perish. And I had a sudden, chilling premonition that the man I was going up the river to find had not perished. He had adapted.

The fever, which had receded, seemed to touch me again, a light, playful stroke on the back of my neck. I shivered in the warm rain. The world was not a sane or orderly place. It was a place of blinding fog and terrible, lucid moments of insight. It was a place where a man could, in all sincerity, offer his deepest condolences and then ask for a piece of the deceased. The thought was so monstrous, so utterly beyond the pale of my own experience, that it ought to have horrified me. But it did not. It merely interested me, with a detached, clinical curiosity. I was learning the language of this place, and it was a language in which words like ‘horror’ and ‘pity’ and ‘civilisation’ had a different, more fluid, and altogether more terrifying meaning. The steamboat chugged on, a noisy, futile little beetle crawling into the heart of an immense mystery, and I, its foolish master, was no longer sure I wanted to find the answers. I was becoming part of the mystery myself.

Their quietude was not one of fear, but of acceptance. They had heard the cry of the wilderness before, in many forms, and would hear it again. Time itself seemed to have dissolved. There was no morning, no noon, only this perpetual, luminous twilight. We ate, not because we were hungry, but to perform an act of normality, a ritual to assure ourselves that the ordinary world still existed. ‘They have fled,’ the manager announced with satisfaction. ‘Our noise, the whistle perhaps, has frightened them off.’ He seemed to take a queer sort of pride in this, as if the power of our steam-whistle to depopulate the countryside was a testament to the might of his enterprise. ‘We should have a look,’ he continued, his eyes gleaming with a new light. ‘There might be ivory. I did not like the idea. The place had a pestilential air, a look of profound and settled misery. It felt like a place of death, not of commerce. ‘It might be a trap,’ I said, the words feeling feeble even as I spoke them. ‘Perhaps we should just push on.’

He gave me a sharp, sideways look. ‘And leave the company’s property to be reclaimed by the jungle? I think not, my dear captain. We have a duty. We will take a small party. You, me, and two of my men. Armed, of course. Just to see.’

There was no arguing with him. His authority, flimsy as it was, was the only thing that held this absurd expedition together. To defy him was to invite chaos, and I had a feeling that chaos, in this part of the world, would be a swift and final affair. So I gave the order to lower the small boat, a leaky, miserable little dinghy that was barely fit to cross a pond. The two pilgrims he selected, the same two who had dashed for their Winchesters, clambered in with an air of nervous bravado, their rifles held awkwardly. The manager settled himself in the stern, arranging his white linen suit so as not to stain it. I took the oars.

The water between the steamer and the shore was like black glass, reflecting the sullen grey sky. The only sound was the drip of water from my oar blades and the creak of the rowlocks. As we drew closer, the silence of the village deepened, became more absolute. On the steamer, the faces of those we left behind, black and white, watched us go with a uniform stillness. They were spectators at a play whose ending they could not guess. The headman stood apart, his arms folded. He did not watch us; he watched the jungle behind the village, his gaze fixed and intense, as if he saw the actors who had just left the stage.

We grounded the dinghy on a bank of sticky, red-brown mud. The air was thick, heavy, filled with the smell of woodsmoke, decay, and something else, something faintly sweet and cloying, like overripe fruit. The huts were even more dilapidated up close, with gaping holes in the thatch and crumbling walls. In the central clearing, the ground was littered with debris: broken pottery, gnawed bones, a few tattered pieces of cloth. It looked as though the inhabitants had left in a great hurry, dropping whatever they were holding. There was a large, blackened circle in the centre of the clearing where a great fire had burned. The ashes were cold.

The pilgrims tiptoed about, poking their rifles into the dark doorways of the huts, their faces pale with a mixture of fear and greed. ‘Nothing,’ one of them whispered, his voice cracking. ‘It’s all empty.’

The manager, however, was not looking at the huts. His eyes were fixed on something at the far end of the clearing, near the edge of the forest. It was a row of slender poles stuck in the ground. I had noticed them from the boat, but had taken them for part of a fence. Now I saw that they were not part of a fence. Atop each pole was a small, round object.

We walked towards them, a slow, hesitant procession. The manager led the way, his step surprisingly light. They looked different. Rebels, the manager muttered under his breath. Criminals. An object lesson.

This was Kurtz’s work. I knew it, with a certainty that went beyond evidence. This was his method. This was the fruit of his soaring eloquence, his emissary of pity and science and progress. He had not come here to enlighten; he had come to rule. And his rule was absolute. The silence of the village was not the silence of abandonment; it was the silence of terror. These people had not fled; they had been subjugated, their spirits broken against this display of implacable power. The cries we had heard had not been for a dead chief; they had been for a way of life, for a world that had been shattered.

The writing was elegant, surprisingly so, a fine, looping script that spoke of a cultured hand. It was a message, an inscription. It read: ‘Observe the law. The law is mine.’

The law is mine. It was not a plea, not a warning, not a cry for help. It was a declaration of sovereignty, a statement of absolute, divine right. Here, in the heart of this immense darkness, one man had declared himself a god, and had provided the evidence of his divinity on the tops of these poles. The manager picked up the board, a flicker of something unreadable in his eyes. Approval? I felt a sudden, violent urge to snatch the board from him and smash it to pieces, to obliterate this obscene testament. But I did nothing. I was paralyzed, not by fear, but by a profound and weary fascination. I was a witness. That was my role. I was the audience for this grand, horrific drama. I looked from the shrunken heads to the manager’s covetous face, to the frightened, greedy pilgrims, and back to the inscription. The law is mine. Kurtz had not just adapted to the wilderness; he had absorbed it, reshaped it in his own image. He had found in it a freedom that the civilized world could never offer: the freedom to become a law unto himself.

And then I saw it. On the back of the same board, almost as an afterthought, was another message, scrawled in the same hand but more hastily, as if the writer were in a great hurry. It was just two words. ‘Hurry up.’

Hurry up. The contrast with the magisterial proclamation on the other side was so jarring, so utterly bizarre, that I laughed. It was not a laugh of mirth; it was a dry, cracked sound, the laugh of a man on the edge of a precipice. The manager shot me a venomous look. One of the pilgrims jumped, thinking the sound was a prelude to an attack. Hurry up. Was it a plea? An order? A desperate cry from the man who had set himself up as a god, a sudden crack in the facade of his omnipotence? Was he in danger? Or was he impatient for our arrival, for fresh disciples to witness his glory? The ambiguity of it was more terrifying than the heads on the poles. It was a glimpse into a mind that had soared to the heights of absolute power and was now, perhaps, teetering on the brink of an equally absolute abyss.

‘What is it?’ the manager demanded, turning the board over. He read the two words, and his face clouded. ‘Impertinent,’ he muttered. ‘He is expecting supplies, no doubt. The man has become too accustomed to getting his own way.’ He tucked the board under his arm, a frown of displeasure on his face. The practicalities were now complicated by this note of human need, of urgency. It spoiled the clean, brutal simplicity of the other message.

We turned to go. The place had yielded its secrets, such as they were, and I wanted nothing more than to be back on the grimy deck of my steamboat. The sun had finally broken through the clouds, and the clearing was flooded with a harsh, unforgiving light. It made the scene look even more squalid, more pathetic. As we walked back toward the river, one of the pilgrims stumbled over something half-buried in the mud. It was a book. An old, battered copy of a manual on seamanship, of all things. I stood there, staring at the book, at this relic of a former life, a life of rules and tables and predictable currents. The freedom of the depths. He had found it. I was going to meet a man who had dared to look into the abyss, and the abyss, it seemed, had looked back and found a brother.

The manager ducked into the cabin, emerging a moment later with a rifle of his own, though he did not fire it. He merely held it, as if it were a symbol of his authority, his right to be there.

For my part, I had no time for such theatricals. My concern was the boat. The current was still swift, and we were drifting. I looked at my helmsman. He was a fine fellow from the coast, a man whose dark skin shone with an almost purple lustre. He stood beside me at the wheel, his hands gripping the spokes, his body tense. He had not flinched. I reached above my head for the cord of the steam-whistle. As my hand closed on the cord, I saw the helmsman’s posture change. It was a subtle thing, a slight stiffening, a sharp intake of breath. His gaze was fixed on a point in the fog just off the port bow. He opened his mouth as if to speak, but no sound came out. I followed his gaze, but saw nothing, only the swirling, featureless white. Then, for an instant, the mist seemed to thin, to part like a curtain, and I saw a dark shape, a fleeting glimpse of a naked, leaping body, a face smeared with white clay, a mouth open in a silent yell. It did not make a whistling sound. It was utterly silent. It struck my helmsman in the side, just below the ribs. He let go of the wheel with a soft grunt of surprise. He had been a part of this world, and this world had turned on him, had struck him down for no reason he could ever understand. The shriek that tore through the air was unearthly. It was a blast of pure, mechanical agony, a sound that seemed to rip the very fabric of the world. The real tragedy was the potential loss of profit.

I had been longing to hear Kurtz’s voice, to meet this prodigy, this emissary of pity and science and progress. I had imagined him as a great intellect, a beacon of light in the heart of this darkness. And now, according to the manager’s impeccable logic, he was dead. He had been swallowed by the wilderness before I could even reach him. All this journey, all this toil, all the absurdities and the dangers, had been for nothing. I felt an immense, hollow disappointment. I had lost something, something I had never possessed. I had been cheated. I felt as though I had been denied a glimpse of the final, terrible truth. All that was left was the sordid business of getting back down the river, with a cargo of petty intrigues and a manager whose soul was a balance sheet.

Just then, as if on cue, the fog began to lift. It did not drift away; it rose, like a great theatre curtain being drawn upwards. The white shutter, as I had called it, ascended smoothly, revealing the scene it had concealed. We saw the banks of the river, the immense, tangled wall of the jungle, dripping and silent. There was no sign of life. The trees stood motionless, impassive spectators to our little drama. The attackers had vanished. They had melted back into the forest, leaving no trace behind them but a few dozen arrows stuck in our steamboat and a dead man on our deck. It was as if they had been a hallucination, a fever-dream conjured by the fog itself. The sun, a pale disc, appeared overhead, casting a weak, watery light on the scene. It illuminated the splintered woodwork, the brass shell casings littered across the deck, the dark, sticky pool of blood that was slowly spreading from beneath the body of my helmsman.

I turned away. I could not look at him any longer. I walked to the edge of the boat, my feet leaving red prints on the planks. I sat down and began to unlace my shoes. One of the pilgrims stared at me as if I had gone mad. Perhaps I had. It was a gesture of profound futility, a symbolic washing of the hands that changed nothing. But it was all I could do. I felt an overwhelming desire to be rid of everything, to throw it all overboard: the pilgrims, the manager, the ivory I knew we would eventually find, the whole stinking, farcical enterprise. I stood up, barefoot on the bloody deck, and looked upriver. The way was clear now. There was nothing to stop us from reaching Kurtz’s station. But the man I had come to see was dead. The voice I had longed to hear was silent. All that remained was to go and look upon the sordid wreckage of his dream. And I knew, with a certainty that chilled me to the bone, that the journey into the heart of darkness was not over. It was only just beginning.
