His workshop, a cavern of wood shavings and the ghosts of turpentine, was a world unto itself; and he, its sole architect and prisoner. Old Kaelen was bent over a vast table, a single low hanging lamp turning the crown of his bald head into a polished knuckle of bone. He was not planing or sawing; his work was of a finer sort. With a tiny brush, he was tracing a line of gold leaf along the equator of a half finished globe, a perfect miniature of a world he had long since abandoned. I waited, content for the moment to breathe the sanctified air of his solitude.

“The first one I made,” he began, without turning, as if I had been a party to his thoughts for the last hour, “was for a sea captain. A man with more courage than sense. He wanted the world, you see, but a world of his own liking, with the dangerous reefs not marked and the trade winds blowing always in his favour.” He set the brush down with an incredible delicacy and picked up a soft cloth to wipe his fingers. He looked at me then, his eyes small and bright in their deep sockets, like two embers in a cave. “I gave him what he asked. A beautiful lie. He paid me in silver and sailed off the edge of his own chart. They found his ship, the ‘Vagrant Star,’ broken on the teeth of a basalt island a year later. “Not a chart, Kaelen,” I said, and the sound of my own voice seemed an intrusion. “A man.”

“Ah,” he murmured, the sound a long exhalation of smoke. “The most difficult map of all. Full of forgotten territories and sudden storms.” He gestured with his pipe stem to the chair opposite. “Tell me of his voyage.”

I told him everything; of young Copeland, who had come out east with a head full of grand notions and a heart full of fire; of his brilliance, his charm, his damnable, incurable belief that he could bend men to his will as easily as he bent the rules of the trading company. I spoke of his successes, which were dazzling, and of his failures, which were ruinous and always the fault of some lesser man’s caution or some trick of fate.

Kaelen listened, his head wreathed in a slowly dissolving cloud. Now and then a sympathetic grunt, a short “So,” would escape him. When I had finished, a profound silence descended, broken only by the faint hiss of the lamp.

“He is a swimmer,” Kaelen said at last, his voice quiet but possessed of a strange finality. He leaned forward, the pipe smoke trailing from his lips like a departing spirit. “Yes. A very strong swimmer. But the sea is wide, and he is swimming for an island that is not there.”

The diagnosis was so stark, so simple, that it left me breathless. It was as if this old man, with his tools and his globes, had navigated the whole complex mess and located the one fatal error in the calculation. “What is the cure, then?” I asked, feeling foolish, like a boy asking a fortune teller to change his fate.

He stood up and walked away from the circle of the lamp. His form became a tall, stooping shadow moving amongst other shadows, the ghosts of unfinished projects and abandoned plans. “Cure?” his voice came back to me, altered by the distance, seeming to rise from the very dust of the floor. “You do not cure a man of being himself! You do not tell the tide to stop. No!”

He paused, and I could just make out the indefinite motion of his hand brushing against a stack of rolled charts. “He wants to command the ocean. To stand on the shore and shout, ‘Be still!’ and have the waves obey him. A fine ambition, for a god. For a man…” His voice trailed off. “For a man, it is madness. It is the dream that breaks you. The dream that you are the master.”

His slippers whispered on the wooden floor as he returned. He did not come all the way back into the light, but stood on its dim edge, a figure both present and remote. “No, there is no cure. There is only a way. A way to live.” His hand, when he raised it, was a pale shape in the gloom, pointing not at me but at some unseen horizon. “You do not command the sea. You learn her. You give yourself to her, and you make her currents your strength. You must go with her to get where you are going. You must be part of the thing that seeks to destroy you.”

He stepped forward, and the lamplight caught his face, erasing the prophetic certainty, leaving behind only a deep and patient sorrow. He leaned back, the old wood of his chair creaking in protest. The consultation, it seemed, was over. Kaelen’s face, emerging from the smoke, was full of an immense, patient sadness. He did not answer at once. He merely looked at me, and in his gaze I felt the weight of all the choices he had made, all the souls he had tried to ferry.

He moved from behind the desk, his slippers making a soft, shuffling sound on the polished floorboards, a sound that seemed to measure the profound stillness of the house. “You mistake the symptom for the man. The romantic--the real romantic--is not the one who pursues the dream; he is the one who has been captured by it. He does not run towards the light; the light, you see, has found him in his darkness and holds him fast. He has no choice. It is a condition of his being.” He stopped before a tall cabinet, its glass doors reflecting the lamplight in distorted, elongated shapes, a funhouse mirror for our earnest faces. “Like this one,” he murmured, his long finger tracing the outline of a shadow-box within. I could not see what it contained, only the dark shape of it against the velvet backing. “He too wanted to be so, and again he wanted to be so. He flew very high. And the sun was very bright.”

He turned back to me, his face half in shadow, half in light, an emblem of the very duality he described. “Your friend--this Jim--he has seen the light. Perhaps it was the light of a star he thought he was steering by, or perhaps only the flare of a match in the great dark. It does not matter. What matters is that he has seen it, and now he is blinded. He cannot see the mud on his boots for the memory of the light on his face. This is not romance; it is a tragedy of perception. He is not a specimen to be described. He is a soul to be saved--or, if not saved, then to be witnessed with compassion as it seeks its own form of salvation.”

He gestured me towards the door with a slight inclination of his head, a dismissal that was also an entrustment. Our consultation was over. “You must go to him,” he said, not as a command, but as a statement of indisputable fact, as one might say, “The tide is turning.” “You are now part of his dream, whether you wish it or not. You carry a piece of his light, or his darkness. Be careful with it. It is more fragile than the wings of any butterfly.”

And so I found myself once more on the verandah, under a sky pricked with the indifferent brilliance of a thousand stars. The air was thick with the scent of unseen blossoms and the faint, pervasive odour of decay which is the very breath of the tropics. Stein had not given me a map; he had not given me a cure. He had simply deepened the mystery, had wrapped the plain, sordid facts of a young man’s disgrace in the gauzy, spectral fabric of metaphysics. In the destructive element immerse. The words echoed in my head, not as a solution, but as a sentence. It was a philosophy for saints or for madmen, for men who had nothing left to lose. And Jim, it seemed, was such a man.

The journey to find passage to a place that few had heard of, and none wished to go, was in itself an immersion into a certain kind of destructive element--the element of human apathy and cynical self-interest. The port was a hive of commerce, a place where anything could be had for a price, but a journey to Patusan was apparently priceless, in that no one would name a figure. I went first to the grand shipping offices, where immaculate clerks in white drill suits looked at me over the tops of their ledgers as if I had inquired about a steamer service to the moon. They would consult their vast, leather-bound atlases, their fingers tracing the familiar, profitable routes, and then shake their heads with a sort of polite pity. Patusan? The name, when they repeated it, sounded like a malady of the throat. It was not on their charts. It did not exist.

I moved then to the dingier, more odorous waterfront establishments, where the maps on the wall were stained with tea and nicotine, and the air was thick with the ghosts of a thousand dubious ventures. Here the name was met not with ignorance, but with a knowing, unhealthy sort of interest. Men with shifty eyes and too much knowledge of the archipelago’s dark corners would lean closer, their breath smelling of stale spirits, and whisper tales of pirates, of a mad rajah, of fever that killed a man between one sunrise and the next. They knew of the place, yes; they knew of it as a sailor knows of a reef that is marked on no chart but is legendary for the ships it has devoured. They would take my money, certainly, but they would not take my ship.

It was in one such establishment, a den of smoke and quiet desperation masquerading as a ship chandlery, that I was directed to a man named Robinson--Captain Robinson. The name was delivered with a kind of grudging respect, as though he were the final court of appeal for all hopeless causes. “If anyone will take you, it’ll be Robinson,” the chandler had said, a desiccated creature who seemed to be made of little more than yellowed parchment and suspicion. “He fears neither God nor the devil, but he has a healthy respect for the Dutch gunboats. Patusan is beyond their usual patrol. It might just appeal to his sense of… enterprise.” The man’s smile was a crack in his dry face, revealing nothing but a profound disbelief in the success of my mission.

I found Robinson on the deck of his vessel, the *Orion*, a vessel that looked as though it had been cobbled together from the wreckage of a dozen other, better ships. She was a squat, graceless steamer, her funnel coated with a scab of rust, her decks warped and stained, her very posture in the water suggesting a weary resignation to her own decrepitude. Robinson himself seemed to have been cast in the same mould. He was a thick, bull-necked man with a face burned the colour of brick by a lifetime of suns and a short, grizzled beard that failed to conceal a mouth set in a permanent line of truculence. He was overseeing the loading of a cargo of something noxious that came in leaking barrels, and he greeted my approach with a grunt that was a masterpiece of non-committal hostility.

I stated my business. I spoke of a passenger and a certain amount of cargo to be landed at the mouth of the Patusan river. I named a price that I thought generous, a figure that should have at least earned me his full attention. He listened, or seemed to listen, his small, piggish eyes never leaving my face. They were eyes that had seen too much and believed in too little; they were the eyes of a man who had long ago traded his dreams for a profit and had found the profit wanting.

When I had finished, he spat a stream of tobacco juice over the rail, a gesture of supreme contempt for my proposition, for me, for the world in general. “Patusan,” he said, the word coming out like a piece of grit he was trying to dislodge from his teeth. “I’ve heard of it.” He squinted at the sun, a fiery orb in a brassy sky. “Heard a story once. About a man who went up that river looking for diamonds. Or was it orchids? Something foolish. They found his boat a year later, drifting in the straits. Empty. Except for the ants. The whole damn boat was crawling with them.”

He turned his gaze back to me, a flicker of something--was it curiosity?--in their depths. “What’s your game? Not diamonds, you don’t have the look. Not a missionary, I hope? They’re the worst. Full of faith right up until the moment they start screaming. No, you’re one of those… gentlemen. A gentleman with a problem. And your problem has a name, and you want to bury it in a place no one can find. Am I right?”

“It’s not quite like that,” I began, but he cut me off with a wave of his thick hand.

“It’s always like that,” he growled. “The world is full of places like Patusan. Sinks. Holes where you can pour the things you don’t want to see anymore. Men, mistakes, memories. I’m a practical man, Mister… I did not catch your name.”

“Marlow,” I said.

“Marlow.” He savoured the name as though it had a bad taste. “I’m a practical man, Marlow. I haul cargo. I take it from a place where it is worth less, to a place where it is worth more. That’s the beginning and the end of my philosophy. The risks I take have to be weighed against the profit. Your profit is… sentimental. There’s no percentage in sentiment.”

“I am prepared to increase my offer,” I said, feeling a familiar wave of weariness. I was bartering for a man’s soul with a man who believed only in balance sheets.

He laughed, a short, barking sound without a trace of mirth. “You could offer me the crown jewels, and I wouldn’t go up that river. You know why? Because I’ve been there. Not to Patusan, not exactly. But to a hundred places like it. Places where the maps go blank. And you know what’s in those blank spaces? Nothing. Nothing but heat, and mud, and fever, and men who have forgotten they are men. It’s a place that sucks the life out of you, the hope out of you. It’s the sea’s bilge, Marlow. And I’ve spent my life trying to stay on deck, not swim in the bilge.”

He took a step closer, and his voice dropped to a conspiratorial whisper, a foul intimacy that was more offensive than his shouting. “I had a dream once, you know. Everyone does. I was a young mate on a fine clipper, all brass and white canvas. Thought I’d be an admiral. Thought I’d end up with a big house on a hill overlooking the sea. Look at me now. Look at this… this floating coffin. The sea doesn’t reward dreamers, Marlow. It drowns them. It takes your clipper ship and gives you this. It takes your dreams and gives you… this.” He gestured around at the squalor of his ship, the stinking barrels, the listless, sullen crew. “It’s an accommodation. A compromise. You learn to live with what you’ve got, not what you wished for. This Jim of yours… he hasn’t learned that yet. And you, you want to help him keep on dreaming. That’s not a kindness, Marlow. That’s a cruelty.”

He turned away from me then, his broad back a final, unarguable dismissal. He bellowed an order at a cowering deckhand, and the business of the ship, the real, grimy, profitable business, resumed. I stood there for a moment, an absurdly solitary figure on that deck of disillusionment, feeling the heat of the sun on my neck. He was right, of course. From his point of view, he was entirely, unimpeachably right. He was a man who had not only immersed himself in the destructive element; he had become it. He was a part of the dirt and the heat and the cynical, grinding reality that Stein had spoken of. He was the mud on which the butterfly would not sit still.

I left the *Orion* without a backward glance. The encounter had left a bad taste in my mouth, a film of grime on my very soul. Robinson’s cynicism was a more potent poison than any fever in Patusan. It was the disease of a world without dreams, a world reduced to a ledger of profit and loss. And yet, as I made my way back through the teeming, indifferent streets of the port, I felt not defeat, but a strange, hard-edged clarity. Robinson had shown me the alternative. He was the ghost of a future that awaited any man who submitted too completely, who gave up the struggle and allowed the deep, deep sea to drag him down to the lightless bottom.

Stein had told me to immerse. But he had also said to make the sea keep you up with the exertions of your hands and feet. Robinson had simply let himself sink. He had made his peace with the depths, and it was a peace that looked remarkably like death.

My resolve hardened. This was not just about Jim anymore. It was a battle against the Robinsons of the world, against the creeping, soul-destroying paralysis of his brand of realism. If Jim was a romantic, then his romance was a flicker of defiance in the face of a vast, encroaching darkness. And I, for reasons I was only beginning to understand, had been appointed the keeper of that flame.

It took another week, a week of fruitless inquiries and dashed hopes, before I found my ship. She was not a ship so much as a rumour, a vessel spoken of in whispers, owned by a man even more spectral. She was called the *Avon*, and she lay at anchor in a forgotten reach of the harbour, far from the main shipping lanes, as if ashamed of her own existence. Her owner was a reclusive Arab trader, a man of immense age and dignity, who received me not in an office, but in the cool, shaded courtyard of his house, a silent oasis in the heart of the city’s clamour.

He heard me out in silence, his dark, ancient eyes fixed on mine. He asked no questions about my motives, expressed no surprise at my destination. He seemed to exist on a plane far beyond the sordid calculations of men like Robinson. For him, Patusan was not a sink or a blank space; it was simply a place. A place to which a man wished to travel. And he, as a man of the sea, would provide the means. A price was agreed upon, a sum that seemed to me to be more of a symbolic tribute than a commercial transaction. His ship, he said, would be ready to sail with the evening tide in two days’ time.

The interview was over. As I rose to leave, he made a small gesture with his hand, a gesture that bade me stay a moment longer. A servant brought us small glasses of mint tea, thick and sweet.

“You seek a man,” the old trader said, his voice a dry rustle, like leaves in a desert wind. It was not a question.

“Yes,” I said.

“Men are often lost,” he mused, staring into the depths of his glass. “And sometimes found. But the journey changes them. The man you find at the end of the river will not be the man you lost at its beginning. Allah is great.”

It was the second piece of advice I had received, as cryptic and as profound as Stein’s. The one, a German-Jewish naturalist and philosopher; the other, an Arab mystic and man of trade. Both saw the world not as a chart of land and sea, but as a map of the human heart, a place of treacherous currents and hidden archipelagos.

Two days later, I stood on the deck of the *Avon* as she slipped her moorings and slid out into the channel. She was an old wooden steamer, smaller even than the *Orion*, but she was clean, and she moved through the water with a quiet grace. Her crew were silent, watchful men, Malays and Bugis, who went about their tasks with an unhurried efficiency. There was a sense of peace about the vessel, a stark contrast to the angry, frantic energy of Robinson’s rust-bucket.

As the lights of the port receded, melting into a fuzzy golden haze on the horizon, I felt a profound sense of release. The world of ledgers and practical men was behind me. Ahead lay the open sea, and beyond that, the blank space on the map. I was committed now. I had stepped off the solid shore of fact and plunged into the uncertain waters of another man’s dream. The night was vast and full of stars. The sea was a dark, whispering immensity. I was alone with my thoughts, and with the echo of Stein’s voice, a murmur in the rush of the wind and the throb of the engines. To follow the dream, and again to follow the dream. The destructive element. The words had become a kind of mantra, a compass pointing not north, but inwards, towards a destination that was not a place, but a condition of the soul. The *Avon* ploughed a lonely furrow in the dark water, a small, determined vessel heading towards the dawn. Or was it, perchance, towards the coming of the night? I had not the courage to decide. It was enough that we were underway.

The first days at sea passed in a profound and shimmering calm. The sun climbed the sky each morning, a disc of white fire that bleached the colour from the heavens and beat down upon the deck with a physical weight. The sea was a sheet of polished turquoise, its surface unbroken save for the creamy wake that trailed behind us, a transient scar that healed itself almost as soon as it was made. The throb of the engine was the only constant sound, a steady, rhythmic heartbeat in the vast silence. It was a world reduced to its elements: sea, sky, and the hot, still air. On the second day, Abdullah joined me where I leaned on the rail, gazing out at the empty horizon. He did not speak for a long time, but stood beside me, a still, robed figure, his presence as unobtrusive and as natural as the ship’s mast.

“The sea is patient,” he said at last, his voice so low it was almost a part of the engine’s hum. “It waits. It has seen all the ships of the world. The great war canoes, the galleons of the Portuguese, the trading brigs of your own people. It sees us now. A little smoke, a little noise. And then we are gone. The sea remains.”

“It feels . . . empty,” I said, the word inadequate for the scale of the solitude that surrounded us.

He made a small, slow gesture with his hand, a gesture that encompassed the entire world. “To you, it is empty. To the fish, it is full of life. To the believer, it is full of the signs of God. Emptiness is in the eye, my friend, not in the sea.” He drew on his narghile, which a boy had brought him, and the smoke mingled with the salt air. “You are restless. You look for the shore. But the shore is only a line between two mysteries. Stein . . . your friend the butterfly man . . . he told you to immerse yourself. That is the wisdom of a man who fights. He sees the world as a thing to be conquered, even in submission. He would make the sea his servant.”

I was startled that he knew of my conversation with Stein, though I should not have been. In these small, interwoven communities of the East, secrets were a current that flowed beneath the surface of things.

“And what is your wisdom, Abdullah?” I asked.

He looked at me with his ancient eyes, and for the first time, a flicker of something like amusement touched their depths. “My wisdom? It is very simple. It is to know that you are not the sea. You are a man in a boat. The boat is strong, the hand on the tiller is steady, but the sea will do as it wills. The storm will come, or the calm will stay. It is not our choice. Our choice is only to be ready. To have our sails mended and our hearts at peace. The rest . . . is in the hands of Allah.”

He gave a slight bow of his head and moved away, leaving me to ponder his words. Two philosophies for the same journey. Stein’s was a call to heroic struggle, a wrestling with the very element of destruction to force it to yield a meaning, a victory. Abdullah’s was a creed of acceptance, of a profound and humble surrender to a power so vast that to fight it was not courage but foolishness. And I, caught between the two, felt the pull of both. The romantic in me, the man Stein had diagnosed, thrilled to the idea of wresting life from the jaws of the destructive sea; but the weary sailor in me, the man who had seen too many ships and too many men go down, felt the deep, quiet truth in the old Arab’s words.

The calm held for two more days. The heat became a torment. The pitch in the deck seams softened and bubbled. The air was thick, heavy, difficult to breathe, as if the sky were a great woollen blanket pressing down upon us. The crew, who had been models of quiet industry, became lethargic, moving about their few tasks with a dream-like slowness. They would retreat to the slivers of shade cast by the deckhouse and the awnings, where they would sit for hours, motionless, their dark eyes gazing out at the glittering, pitiless water. I saw them at prayer, their foreheads touching the hot deck in unison, a ritual of serene devotion in the midst of a world that seemed to have forgotten motion. Their stillness was a reproach to my own pacing, my own impatient scanning of the horizon for a wisp of cloud, a sign of change.

Then, on the evening of the fifth day, the change came. It began not with a wind, but with a colour. The serene turquoise of the sea deepened to an inky, bruised purple. The horizon, which had been a sharp line, grew blurred, indistinct. The air, which had been heavy, grew heavier still, charged with a strange, electric tension. A swell began to run, long, oily, and unnerving, lifting the *Avon* with a slow, sickening motion, though not a breath of wind stirred. The crew came to life. Ropes were checked, hatches battened down, loose gear secured. Their movements were still unhurried, but now they were imbued with a definite purpose. Abdullah stood on the bridge, a silhouette against the strange, lurid light of the dying sun, his voice carrying across the deck in short, guttural commands.

The wind, when it came, did not rise; it struck. It was as if a solid wall had hit the ship. The *Avon* shuddered from stem to stern, heeling over so far that I thought she would never right herself. The sea, which had been merely sullen, exploded into a fury of white water. Rain followed, not in drops but in solid sheets, a deluge that was instantly whipped into a horizontal spray, stinging the face and blinding the eyes. The world dissolved into a roaring chaos of wind and water. The steady throb of the engine was lost in the shriek of the gale through the rigging and the thunder of waves crashing over the bows.

I found a precarious handhold near the deckhouse, drenched and half-blinded, possessed by a kind of awed terror. This was it. This was the destructive element, made manifest. This was the reality behind Stein’s metaphor, and there was nothing poetic about it. It was a mindless, impersonal violence that sought only to obliterate. The little steamer, which had seemed so sturdy in the calm, was now a terrified living thing, groaning in every timber, fighting for its life against an enemy of incomprehensible power.

Through the spray and the gathering darkness, I could see Abdullah at the helm. He had sent the helmsman below and taken the wheel himself. He was not fighting the sea; he was yielding to it, anticipating the blow of each wave, easing the ship’s head into it, using the water’s own force to keep his vessel from being swamped. His face, illuminated now and then by a flash of lightning, was a mask of intense concentration, but it held no fear. It was the face of a man engaged in a difficult and familiar task, a craftsman at his work. He was not wrestling with the sea; he was dancing with it, a grim and terrible waltz in which a single misstep meant oblivion.

The night was an eternity of noise and motion. The ship was tossed about like a cork, now lifted to a terrifying height on the crest of a wave, from which I could see a wilderness of black, mountainous water, now plunging into a trough so deep that the world seemed to be made entirely of two roaring, liquid walls. I thought of Stein’s words: *submit yourself, and with the exertions of your hands and feet in the water make the deep, deep sea keep you up*. And I looked at Abdullah, his hands firm on the wheel, his body moving with the ship, and I saw another kind of truth. He was not exerting himself against the sea. He was part of it. He had submitted so completely that the distinction had ceased to exist.

Sometime before dawn, the fury of the storm began to abate. The wind’s shriek lowered to a mournful howl, and the waves, though still immense, lost their vicious, breaking crests. By sunrise, we were wallowing in a heavy, confused swell under a sky the colour of lead. The rain had become a steady, dispiriting drizzle. The *Avon* was a wreck. Her port-side rail was a twisted ruin of stanchions and splintered wood, one of the lifeboats was gone, and the forward awning was a tangle of shredded canvas. But she was afloat. The engine still throbbed, a weary but defiant pulse. The crew, haggard and soaked, were already at work, clearing the debris with a quiet fatalism.

Abdullah relinquished the wheel to his serang and came down from the bridge. He moved stiffly, like an old man, and his face was grey with exhaustion. He saw me clinging to the rail and came over, steadying himself against the lurch of the deck.

“A difficult night,” he said, his voice hoarse.

“You saved the ship,” I answered, the words sounding foolish and small.

He shook his head. “Allah saved the ship. The sea was angry, and now it is tired. Like an old man after a rage.” He looked out at the grey, heaving water. “It has taken its tribute.” He pointed with his chin towards the empty lifeboat davits. “It is always so. You must give something, so that you are not given entirely.” He looked at me then, a long, searching look. “You have seen it now. The thing you speak of with your friend. The destructive element. “No,” I said honestly. “It is not.”

“It never is,” he murmured. “A dream is a dream. A storm is a storm. To confuse them is to drown.” He nodded, as if to himself, and walked away towards his cabin, his bare feet leaving wet prints on the deck.

The rest of the journey was slow. The battered *Avon* laboured in the swell, her pace much reduced. But three days later, a smudge of land appeared on the horizon, a low, dark line that slowly resolved itself into a solid wall of green. This was the coast, the edge of the great island. We followed it for another day, the water around us changing colour, losing its deep-sea blue and taking on a brownish, muddy tinge. The clean smell of salt gave way to a new scent, an odour of damp earth, of vegetation, of decay. It was the breath of the land, and it was heavy, suffocating, full of a rank and ancient life.

We reached the river mouth in the late afternoon. It was not a grand estuary but a narrow, sullen gap in the mangrove swamps, a wound in the flesh of the land from which a sluggish brown current bled into the sea. The water here was as still as a pond, reflecting the unbroken wall of green with a sinister perfection. The silence was absolute, a thing far more profound than the silence of the open ocean. It was the silence of a waiting thing.

Abdullah brought the *Avon* to anchor a cable’s length from the shore. The journey with him was over. A longboat was lowered, a small canoe manned by two of his Bugis sailors, who would be my guides for the first stage upriver. My few belongings were placed in it.

I stood with Abdullah at the rail for the last time. I tried to offer him more money, for the damage to his ship, for the danger he had endured. He waved it away with a gesture of his hand.

“It was written,” he said simply. “Our bargain is complete. I have brought you to the door. You must walk through it alone.” He looked from my face to the dark opening of the river. “Remember what I told you in the port. The man you find will not be the one you lost. This river… it is a different kind of sea. Here, the dangers are not from the wind and the waves, but from the stillness. And from the hearts of men. Including your own.”

He placed his hand on his heart in a gesture of farewell. “Ma’a as-salāmah. Go with peace.”

I climbed down into the canoe. The two Bugis sailors dipped their paddles into the brown water without a sound, and the small craft glided away from the steamer’s side. I did not look back. I kept my eyes fixed on the gap in the trees, the dark throat of the river. We slipped from the murky light of the open anchorage into a green twilight. The immense trees joined overhead, shutting out the sky. The air was hot and motionless, thick with the smell of mud and unseen blossoms. The world of the sea, of vast horizons and cleansing winds, was gone, as if it had never been. I was in a tunnel, a passage into a world that was secret, enclosed, and watchful. The only sounds were the soft dip of the paddles and the hum of a million insects, a sound that was not a noise but the very vibration of the silence itself. I had immersed myself, as Stein had advised. But this was not the open, raging sea. This was a place of shadows, of creeping, silent things. This was the heart of the dream. And as we moved deeper into the gloom, a profound and chilling doubt took hold of me. Was this the way? To follow the dream, and again to follow the dream, into a darkness from which there might be no return? The conviction that had seemed so clear in Stein’s lamplit room, so tested and true in the heart of the storm, now seemed a fragile and desperate faith. The crepuscular light I had imagined on a great plain was here a suffocating reality, a perpetual dusk under a roof of leaves. And it was no longer a horizon I was facing, but an ending. Or was it, perchance, a beginning?

was to this question that my mind returned, again and again, with the monotonous regularity of the paddles dipping into the water that was the colour of rust and secrets. A beginning. No, it felt like no beginning I had ever known; for beginnings are marked by a clarifying light, a sense of a path opening, however arduous. This was a closing in, a narrowing, a descent into a place that had already reached its conclusion and was now mouldering in the long afternoon of its own decay.

The two Bugis sailors were a part of this landscape, as silent and as purposed as the river itself. Their bare backs, slick with sweat, rippled with a contained and economical strength. They never spoke, not to me, nor to each other. Their communication was a thing of slight glances, of a minute shift in posture, a language of shared existence that had no need for words. The man in the bow, older, with a face like a weathered chart of these very waterways, would sometimes lift his paddle without a sound and point with the blade towards a half submerged log, a swirl of current that betrayed a snag beneath the surface. The man in the stern, younger and stronger, would respond with an imperceptible adjustment of his own stroke, and the canoe would glide past the danger without a tremor. They were not fighting the river; they were a part of its flow, a thought within its sluggish mind. They had submitted, and in their submission, they were masters. I, sitting in the middle on my piled belongings, was the only alien object, a piece of flotsam from another world, ridiculously clad and burdened with a purpose they could not comprehend and would not have cared for if they could.

Hours passed. The sun, which had been a furnace in the open sea, was here only a theoretical presence, a diffused, oppressive heat without a source. Its light filtered down in sickly green shafts, striking the water and turning it into a pool of liquid jade for a moment before being swallowed again by the brown gloom. The jungle was not a collection of individual trees but a single, vast organism, a fortress of intertwined roots, lianas thick as a man’s arm, and a canopy so dense it was a second earth, teeming with its own unseen life. From it came a ceaseless, low-pitched hum, the vibration of a million tiny wings, the scraping of a million unseen legs: the sound of life at its most voracious and indifferent. And other sounds, too, that pricked the ear and unsettled the soul: the sudden, maniacal cackle of a hornbill, a sharp cry that was cut off as if a hand had been clapped over a mouth, the rustle of some heavy body moving through the undergrowth just beyond the curtain of leaves. At each sound, my hand would twitch, my muscles would tense; the sailors, however, gave no sign, their paddles rising and falling with that same indefatigable, hypnotic rhythm.

I thought again of Stein, in his room full of dead, beautiful things, his butterflies pinned and classified, their tragic, brief flight arrested and made eternal. He had captured his dream. He held it in his hands, a perfect specimen. But this… this was not a specimen to be pinned. This was a living, breathing entity, and its life was a suffocating force. Was this what he had meant? ‘In the destructive element immerse.’ I had imagined a heroic struggle, a defiant battle against the waves, a man proving his worth against the fury of the sea. I had lived through that very thing, had felt the deck heave and the wind scream, and I had found a strange, fierce joy in it, a clarity. But this was different. This was not a roaring, open destruction. It was a slow, silent, creeping dissolution. The element here was not the clean, salt water of the ocean, but this thick, warm, viscous fluid, and the air itself, which seemed to clog the lungs and dull the mind. To immerse oneself here was not to swim, but to sink.

Abdullah’s words came back to me as well, spoken in the clean air of the harbour: ‘The man you find will not be the one you lost.’ I had dismissed it then as the fatalistic poetry of an old sailor. Now, it seemed a piece of stark, practical wisdom. A man could not live in this, I thought, and remain the man he had been. This place did not change a man; it absorbed him. It leached the very substance of his old self, the convictions, the memories, the sense of a world beyond the green wall, and replaced it with something else, something that belonged to the river, to the gloom. I was here to find a man who had fallen into a dream, a man who, as Stein had diagnosed, was ‘romantic.’ And I, following him, had brought my own romantic notions, of friendship, of rescue, of a duty to be done. Here, under the oppressive weight of the canopy, these ideas seemed thin and brittle, like the wings of Stein’s butterflies, liable to crumble to dust at the slightest touch.

Sometime in the late afternoon, the rhythm of the paddling changed. The man in the bow gave a low grunt, the first sound I had heard from him, and pointed. Ahead, the river was completely blocked by a colossal fallen tree, its crown of leaves and vines a tangled wreck on the far bank, its massive trunk lying across the water like a dam. Its roots, torn from the earth, reached up to the sky like the skeletal fingers of a giant hand, caked with mud and festooned with debris. The current, baulked by the obstacle, eddied and swirled against the trunk in a low, angry murmur.

For a moment, I felt a surge of something like despair. It was a final, impassable barrier, a definitive statement from the river itself: you shall not pass. But my guides were unperturbed. The older man steered the canoe expertly towards the bank, into a shallow inlet where the water was still. Without a word, they both stepped out, sinking to their knees in the soft, stinking mud. They motioned for me to remain seated. With an ease that belied the effort, they began to haul the canoe out of the water, sliding it up the muddy bank. I made to get out, to help, but a sharp gesture from the younger man stopped me. It was not a gesture of courtesy, but of command. I was cargo. I was to remain with the other belongings. They were the force, the knowledge; I was the inert object to be transported.

They dragged the heavy canoe through the clinging undergrowth, sweat glistening on their bodies, their feet finding purchase in the treacherous ground as if by instinct. They hacked a path with the short, heavy knives they wore at their belts, clearing a way around the root-ball of the fallen tree. It took them the better part of an hour. All the while I sat, useless and watched, a silent spectator to their quiet, effective struggle. They were not fighting the obstacle; they were accommodating it, flowing around it as the river itself would eventually find a new channel. When they finally slid the canoe back into the water on the other side of the tree, their breath was coming in deep, regular gasps, but their faces were as impassive as ever. As I clambered back to my seat, my foot slipped on the wet gunwale and I almost fell, splashing mud on my trousers. The younger sailor looked at me, and for the first time I saw something in his eyes, a flicker of what might have been contempt, or perhaps only a profound and weary pity. In that moment, I felt the full weight of my inadequacy, of my utter foreignness.

We journeyed on, and the light began to fail in earnest. The green twilight deepened to a murky grey, and the sounds of the jungle shifted, the day-creatures falling silent, the hunters of the night beginning to stir. A deeper, more resonant hum rose from the forest, and the air grew cooler, if no less heavy. I had begun to resign myself to a night spent in the canoe when the man in the bow pointed again, this time to a faint glimmer of light on the right bank. It was not the angry glare of a fire, but the soft, uncertain glow of a single lamp, a solitary spark of human life in the immense darkness.

As we drew closer, I could make out the shape of a small landing, a rickety structure of bamboo poles driven into the mud, and beyond it, a clearing hacked out of the forest’s edge. A few huts stood there, raised on stilts, dark and silent shapes against the black wall of the trees. The light came from the largest of these, shining feebly through a wide, open doorway. There was no sound of welcome, no barking of dogs, no voices. The place seemed to be holding its breath.

The canoe slid alongside the landing with a soft bump. The sailors secured it with a length of rattan and then, at last, they spoke to me. The older man turned his head, his face a mask of shadows. “Sini,” he said, his voice a dry rasp. Here. He pointed towards the lighted hut. It was a dismissal. Their part of the journey was over. I gathered my things, my limbs stiff and awkward. I offered them money, pulling out a handful of silver dollars that seemed absurdly bright and out of place in the gloom. The younger man simply looked away, but the older one took one coin, just one, holding it in his palm for a moment as if weighing not its value but its meaning, before tucking it into the fold of his sarong. It was not a payment; it was a token, an acceptance that some form of exchange had, after all, taken place.

I climbed onto the landing, which swayed alarmingly under my weight. The two Bugis pushed the canoe back out into the current, and without a backward glance, began to paddle back the way we had come, their forms dissolving into the darkness until they were gone completely. I was alone.

The silence that descended was absolute, a palpable pressure against my eardrums. The single lamp in the hut seemed not to be a beacon of safety but an eye, watching me. I stood for a long time on the creaking bamboo, wrestling with an almost overwhelming urge to call out after the canoe, to beg to be taken back to the sea, to the world I understood. But the silence held me, and the knowledge of my purpose, however frayed and doubtful it had become, held me as well. To follow the dream. I had to walk towards the light.

With a deep breath that was more of a sigh, I made my way along the landing and stepped onto the damp earth of the clearing. The ground was littered with refuse, the air thick with the smell of woodsmoke, dried fish, and something else, something acrid and vaguely medicinal. As I approached the hut, a figure appeared in the lighted doorway, a silhouette against the warm glow within. It was a man, short and broad, clad only in a sarong. He held a long spear in one hand. He did not move, did not speak, simply stood there, blocking the way.

“I am looking for a man,” I began, my voice sounding unnaturally loud in the stillness. “An Englishman. He came this way…”

The man in the doorway shifted the spear, its point glinting in the lamplight. He said something in a guttural tongue I did not understand. I tried again, using the few words of Malay I had picked up. “Orang putih… Tuan…” A white man. A master.

He grunted, a sound of dismissal. He seemed about to turn away when another voice came from the darkness within the hut. It was a voice that was old and dry, like rustling leaves, and it spoke in a corrupted, halting form of English that was almost as alien as the man’s own tongue.

“He asks… for the one who lives with ghosts. The one who fell from the sky.”

The man in the doorway turned his head, spoke rapidly. The voice from within answered. The exchange was brief. The man with the spear stepped aside, grudgingly. The gesture was not an invitation, but an allowance. I hesitated on the threshold. The interior of the hut was smoky and dim, lit by a single oil lamp that sat on a low table. The air was thick with the smell of opium. In the far corner, on a pile of mats, an old, skeletal man lay propped on his side, his head resting on a wooden block. It was he who had spoken. His eyes, small and black in his wrinkled face, were fixed on me with a disturbing intensity.

“You seek him,” the old man stated. It was not a question. “Many have sought him. Few find him. And those who do… they do not find what they seek.” He took a long, slow pull from a pipe that lay beside him, and a sweet, cloying smoke filled the air.

“Do you know where he is?” I asked, stepping fully into the hut.

The old man laughed, a dry, rattling cough. “Know? Here, we do not ‘know.’ We see. We wait. The river brings things. The river takes things away. He is… upriver. Always more upriver.” He gestured with a thin, trembling hand into the darkness beyond the hut, a darkness that seemed to have no end. “He is at the heart of his kingdom. Or his prison. It is the same thing, is it not?”

His eyes strayed from my face to my hands, my clothes, my boots. “You are from the sea,” he said, his voice taking on a new tone, one of keen, almost hungry, interest. “You carry its smell. He was of the sea, once. Before he tried to become a god. Or a devil. He has been both.” The old man’s eyes glazed over for a moment, as if he were seeing things far beyond the walls of his hut. “He built a dream here. A great dream. It was beautiful. And then… phoo!” He made a gesture with his hand, like Stein blowing out the match. A gesture of sudden, complete annihilation. “The destructive element. It is not just the water, Tuan. It is in the heart. He taught us that.”

He fell silent, his gaze lost in the flickering flame of the lamp. I stood in the centre of the hut, a stranger in a strange land, having arrived at what was not a destination, but merely another question. The silence stretched, filled only by the hum of the forest outside and the old man’s shallow breathing. The first stage of my journey was over. I had immersed myself. I had come through the door Abdullah had shown me. And here, in this smoky hut, in the cryptic words of a dying opium-eater, I had found not an answer, not a path, but only a more profound and intricate statement of the problem. I had come seeking a man, and I had found a myth, a ghost, a story told in whispers in the dark. The great plain remained desolate, the pitfalls were deeper than I had ever imagined, and the crepuscular light was now a darkness so complete it seemed a solid thing, a wall against which I could only beat my fists in vain. Or was it, perchance, a curtain, waiting to be drawn? I had not the courage, then, to decide. I only knew that I had to go on. Upriver.

He did not wait for my answer. With the same grave, ritualistic care, he carried the case back into the darkness. I heard the faint click of a cabinet door. The audience was over. The lesson, for what it was worth, had been given.

The door closed behind me with a soft click, and I stood for a moment under the stars, which seemed in that clear tropical night to be not lamps but pinpricks in a vast, oppressive blackness. The air was thick with the scent of unseen blossoms and the faint, ever present odour of decay, the two mingled into one, the very perfume of life itself. Stein’s words echoed in my head, not as a solution, but as a deeper, more intricate framing of the puzzle. To follow the dream. To immerse. But what if the dream was to sit upon a heap of filth? And what if the one who followed, the one who sought to understand, was himself the destructive element, the man with the net?

I had come to him for a remedy, and he had given me a poem, a parable of a butterfly on a dung heap. He had shown me his own life, a life of action and success, of love and loss, all distilled into the single, sharp exhalation: “phoo!” He had looked at the very heart of the thing, and he had laughed. A man that is born falls into a dream like a man who falls into the sea. The way is to the destructive element submit yourself.

My own journey had brought me here, to the edge of a great and unknown continent of human despair. I had a name, a vague direction whispered by men who feared to speak it aloud, and a conviction, as stubborn and as irrational as the romanticism Stein had diagnosed in my quarry, that I had to go on. Upriver.

The path from Stein’s bungalow led down to the native town, a sprawling collection of huts on stilts that crowded the muddy banks of the river. It was a place of shadows and flickering torchlight, of half-seen faces in doorways and the murmur of a language I did not understand. Here the great plain of humanity, with its pitfalls and its graves, was not a metaphor but a teeming, breathing reality. I sought out the man I had been told of, a man named Abdullah, who, for a price, could arrange things, a man who knew the currents, both of the river and of men’s affairs.

I found him in a smoky room behind a shop that sold cheap cloth and tinned goods, a portly, impassive Arab who listened to my request with his eyes half-closed. He did not seem surprised. He named a price, a formidable one, and when I agreed without haggling he opened his eyes and looked at me properly for the first time.

‘He is a long way,’ Abdullah said, his voice soft, almost greasy. ‘The river does not want to go there. The men do not want to go there.’

‘But they will go,’ I said.

‘For money, a man will go into the house of a devil,’ he conceded with a slight shrug. ‘I will find you a prahu, and a crew. Men from the sea, who do not know the stories of the river. But you will need a guide. A pilot. The river has many teeth.’

‘Can you find such a man?’

He stroked his short, grizzled beard, his dark eyes appraising me. ‘There is one. He is not a man of this place. He came down the river, many years ago. He came from… up there. Two days later I stood on the rickety wharf as the sun rose, a blood-red smear across a sky the colour of a bruise. The prahu was a long, narrow craft, little more than a hollowed log with outriggers and a makeshift palm-thatch roof amidships. The crew, four impassive Bugis sailors, moved with a silent economy of motion, their faces like masks. And then a man detached himself from the shadows of a nearby warehouse and came towards me. It was Kassim.

He was not what I had expected. He was small, almost frail-looking, and his face was a web of fine lines, though it was impossible to guess his age. He might have been thirty; he might have been sixty. His eyes were the most remarkable thing about him; they were a pale, washed-out grey, the colour of a northern sea, and they seemed to hold a perpetual distance, as if he were always looking at something far away, something only he could see. He carried nothing but a small, tightly wrapped bundle.

He stopped before me and gave a slight, almost imperceptible nod. His gaze was not on me, but on the river, on the thick, brown water that slid past the pilings with a quiet, menacing strength.

‘You are the man who wishes to go upriver,’ he said. His voice was toneless, flat.

‘I am.’

‘The river is long. The sun is hot. The night is full of ghosts.’

‘I am not afraid of ghosts,’ I said, perhaps too quickly.

The river was not water; it was a thick, living thing, a serpent of mud and secrets coiling its way into a heart of darkness. On either side, the forest rose like a solid green wall, impenetrable, silent, hostile. Great trees draped in creepers stood like mourning giants, their roots clutching at the crumbling banks. Now and then a flash of colour, a bird with plumage like a jewel, would dart across our path, its cry a brief, sharp note in the immense silence. The heat was a physical weight, a wet blanket that smothered thought. My Bugis crewmen paddled with a steady, hypnotic rhythm, the dip and splash of their oars the only sound besides the drone of insects and the secret rustlings from the unseen world behind the screen of leaves.

Days melted into one another. We travelled from dawn until the swift tropical dusk fell, then we would tie up to the bank, in a small cove or under the branches of an overhanging tree. The nights were worse than the days. The silence of the day was replaced by a chorus of a million living things, the croaking of frogs, the sawing of crickets, the sudden, terrifying scream of some hunting creature. Through it all, Kassim remained impassive, a carved figurehead at the prow of our vessel. He seemed not to eat, not to sleep. He would sit for hours, unmoving, his pale eyes scanning the river, the banks, the sky. He navigated not by any map, but by an instinct born of some terrible familiarity. He would point with a thin finger, ‘There. The channel is deep’, or hold up a hand for the paddlers to stop, his head cocked, listening to a sound I could not hear. He was a part of this world, and I was the specimen, the alien, the man in a glass case of his own ignorance.

One evening, as the light was failing, he spoke to me without turning his head. ‘You are a friend of his?’ he asked, the words falling without inflection into the stillness.

‘I do not know him,’ I answered honestly.

‘Then you are an enemy.’

‘No. I am... nothing. I have come to see.’

He made a small, dry sound, a huff of air through his nostrils. It might have been a laugh. ‘Many have come to see. He likes to be seen. The next day, the character of the river began to change. The banks grew higher, steeper, like canyon walls. The current grew stronger, fighting our progress. We began to see signs of human life, but of a strange, unnerving kind. A small landing stage, rotted and half-submerged, leading nowhere. A single, tall pole driven into the mud of the bank, from which something fluttered in the breeze; as we drew closer I saw it was not a flag, but a collection of brightly coloured rags, tied together with vines, like a madman’s prayer offering.

Kassim saw me looking at it. ‘One of his follies,’ he said, his voice flat. ‘He wished to mark his domain. To make the land beautiful. He believed beauty could conquer the jungle.’ He gestured with his chin at the silent, green walls around us. ‘The jungle has a different idea of beauty.’

Later that afternoon, we rounded a sharp bend and the paddlers stopped without a command from Kassim. There, in the middle of a wide, placid stretch of the river, was an island. And on the island was a house.

It was not a native hut, but a European-style bungalow, large and sprawling, with a wide verandah and a roof of corrugated iron, now red with rust. Or rather, it was the ghost of such a house. The jungle had begun its patient work of reclamation. Creepers snaked up the pillars of the verandah, their green tendrils probing the windows. A section of the roof had collapsed, leaving a gaping, black wound. The garden that must have once surrounded it was now a riot of untamed growth, a fever-dream of monstrous flowers and strangling vines. It was a place of profound and terrible desolation, a monument to a defeated dream.

‘His first place,’ Kassim said softly, as if speaking to himself. ‘The place of the woman. And the child.’

He fell silent, and we drifted past the island in the hushed stillness of the afternoon. The house stared at us with its blind, broken windows. It was here, then. Here that the love he had told Stein about, the full heart, had existed. And it was here that it had been consumed, annihilated, reduced to this ruin. Phoo. The word echoed in my mind with the force of an incantation. I saw Stein’s face, thoughtful and placid, twitching once as he blew out the match. Friend, wife, child.

I felt a sudden, almost overwhelming wave of despair. The great plain on which men wander was not a metaphor; it was here. It was this river, this jungle, this ruin. The crepuscular light was the hazy, feverish glare of the tropical sun. The pitfalls were real. And I was following a man who had not only fallen into them but had, it seemed, dug them himself.

‘We are close now,’ Kassim said, his voice pulling me back from the abyss of my thoughts. He pointed ahead, to where the river narrowed again, disappearing between two towering cliffs that seemed to lean together, as if to guard whatever lay beyond. ‘The heart of the kingdom,’ he murmured, and for the first time, I heard a note of something like emotion in his voice, a vibration of awe, or of terror. ‘Or the prison.’

I remembered Stein’s butterfly, his magnificent specimen, sitting still on its little heap of dirt. That was its way of being. But man, he said, will never on his heap of mud keep still. Perhaps for the true romantic, the one who falls into a dream like a man who falls into the sea, such a place is not a prison but a liberation. The flame threw his young features into sharp relief, carving shadows under his cheekbones, in the hollows of his temples. It was the face of a man perpetually on trial before a tribunal of his own devising.

‘One imagines a certain sort of conduct for oneself,’ he began again, as if there had been no interruption, his voice low and confiding in the enclosed space. ‘It is the first and greatest dream, is it not? Not the dream of what one will *do*--a soldier, a sailor, a king--but of what one will *be*. Unflinching. Honourable. True. One draws a line in the sand of one’s own soul, a line one swears never to cross. You stand with your back to it, facing the world, ready for any test. And then… one day, you look down, and your feet are on the other side. You are not even sure how it happened. A moment of panic; a failure of imagination; a simple, human terror. And the shocking thing, the truly damnable thing, is not the crossing itself. It is that the world does not end. The sun rises again. The birds sing. Men go about their business. And you are still there, on the wrong side of the line, alive.’

He leaned forward, and his blue eyes, dark pools of light in the lamp’s glow, seemed to burn with a cold fire. ‘That is when the test truly begins. Not the grand, heroic test you prepared for, but the squalid, daily one. The test of living with yourself. That is the thing they do not understand, those on the shore. They see the single act, the fall. They do not see the long, slow, desperate swim afterwards. They pronounce their judgment and sail on, and they have the comfort of their righteousness. But here… in the water… righteousness is a luxury. Here, there is only the next stroke, and the one after that. You build a raft of what you can. You make an order. You become… necessary. Not for your own sake, you understand. That is long gone. But for the sake of the others who are in the water with you.’

‘The people here,’ I said.

‘They were drowning too,’ he said simply. ‘In a different sea. A sea of fear, of petty tyrannies, of endless, meaningless violence. I offered them a different dream. My dream. A dream of order, of a sort of justice. It was a fair exchange, I think. They gave me a reason to keep my head above water. I gave them a rock to cling to.’ He laughed, a short, bitter sound that was startling in the quiet room. ‘And so I became a king on my little heap of mud, as Stein would put it. A lord of flotsam.’

He stood up and walked to the door, gazing out into the impenetrable darkness. His white shirt was a ghostly blur against the night. ‘Stein is a good man,’ he said, his back to me. ‘He believes in cures. In remedies. He believes you can catch a butterfly without smudging the dust on its wings. He lives in a world of beautiful, dead things, perfectly preserved. He does not understand that life, real life, is a messy, smudging business. The dust comes off on your fingers. You cannot help it. The only way to preserve the dream in its perfect state is to never try to live it.’

He turned back towards me, and his face was in shadow now, his expression unreadable. ‘He sent you to describe a specimen. To see if the butterfly has survived its flight. To see if it is still… perfect.’ The word was heavy with an irony so profound it was almost a physical weight in the room. ‘And you? You who come from him, from that world of solid certainties and cherished specimens, where things have names and their place is known… What do you call it? Now that you have seen it, living and breathing?’

The question hung in the air between us, direct, unadorned, and inescapable. He was asking me to be his judge, his confessor, his historian. He was asking me to place the label on him, to put him in his case. He, who had just decried the very act, was now demanding it of me, with an intensity that was a kind of prayer. He wanted to see himself, for a moment, through my eyes; he wanted to know how he appeared from the shore I had so recently left.

I thought of Stein’s whisper: *usque ad finem*. To the very end. And I saw that for this man, the end was not an event but a condition. His life was the ‘again and again’ of which Stein had spoken. To follow the dream, and again to follow the dream. He had made his dream of honour real, but only by building it upon the foundations of his dishonour. He had created a perfect, tiny kingdom of order as a penance for a moment of chaos. He was the living embodiment of Stein’s paradox, a man held up by the very element that was destroying him.

‘I am here to describe a specimen,’ I answered, the words coming out more slowly than I intended, feeling for their truth as I uttered them. ‘I find it… not perfect. No. Nothing so simple. I find it… alive. And I do not think I have the words to describe that.’

A great stillness fell upon him. He stood by the door, a tall, white figure at the edge of his small circle of light, and I had the distinct impression that he was holding his breath. It was as if my refusal to name him, to pin him down with a simple judgment, was a thing so unexpected that it left him, for a moment, without a defence. Then he let out the breath in a long, slow sigh, a sound of immense weariness, but one not entirely without a grain of relief.

‘Then you are a kinder man than I am,’ he said softly. ‘And perhaps a wiser one than Stein.’

A shout from the village below, sharp and sudden, a questioning cry that sliced through the stillness, broke the spell. It was followed by another, and then the agitated barking of a dog. The man’s posture changed in an instant. The look of the confessor, the philosopher, the lost soul, vanished, replaced by the attentive, slightly strained expression of the guardian. The king of flotsam was needed on his raft.

‘It will be nothing,’ he said, though his body was already tensed for action. ‘A dispute over a goat. Or a shadow that looked like a rival. In a place like this, fear has a thousand fathers.’ He looked at me, and his brief, sad smile returned. ‘You see? The tide. It never rests.’

He picked up a broad-bladed sword that had been leaning against the doorpost, a formidable piece of local steel, and it looked both terrible and natural in his hand. ‘You are safe here. My people know you are my guest. Sleep if you can. The night is long, but the morning always comes. That is the one promise that has never been broken. Not yet.’

And with that, he was gone, stepping out of the light into the darkness that was his domain, his substance, his work. I was alone in his room, with the smell of the lamp, the silent reproach of the uneaten food, and the heavy presence of his story. I sat for a long time, listening to the silence that had returned, a silence now freighted with a new and more complex meaning. He had submitted, and the sea was holding him up. Of that, I had no doubt. But as I sat there in the heart of his precarious kingdom, a question formed itself in my mind, a question Stein had not answered, perhaps had not even thought to ask. For how long, I wondered, for how long can a man tread water? And what happens when he grows tired? The night was falling in earnest now, and the abyss full of flames which had circled Stein’s world seemed here to have swallowed the horizon whole, leaving only this one small, flickering point of light in an ocean of darkness. And I, who had come merely to observe, felt its terrible, lonely warmth, and knew myself to be, for a time, a keeper of its flame.

was not his romance I questioned, but its substance. A man may fashion a crown of driftwood and call himself a king, and in a world of wrecks and castaways the title might even hold a certain truth, a certain grim authority. But a crown is a heavy thing to wear, even a phantom one, and a kingdom built upon the shifting sands of misfortune is a desperate sort of dominion. I sat for a time, a very long time it seemed, and the silence that had followed his departure was not the silence of peace but of a held breath, a a pause in a struggle whose nature I could only guess at. The shouts from below had ceased as abruptly as they had begun, and the night resumed its immense, watchful quiet, punctuated only by the faint, incessant chorus of insects and the distant, almost inaudible sigh of the surf on the outer reef. His world, which for a moment had seemed so solid in his presence, so completely his own, now felt fragile again, a solitary perch in a vast and indifferent darkness.

I found my eyes drawn to the simple cot in the corner, to the crude table, to the few objects that constituted the furniture of his life. There was a brutal simplicity to it all, a stripping away of everything that was not essential for the mere act of existence. And yet, this was the man who had spoken of love, of a child, of a past that had once been full. Where were the ghosts of those things? Did they prowl here with him in the dark, or had he, by some tremendous act of will, drowned them too in the element he had made his own? Stein had his butterflies, his carefully preserved specimens of a beauty that had once lived. This man, it seemed, had only the living, breathing, and profoundly imperfect specimens of humanity that the sea washed up at his feet. He was a collector of a different sort, a curator of failures, of which he himself was the principal exhibit.

The thought had a certain bleak grandeur that was not without its appeal to a mind like mine, a mind that had looked too long into the perplexities of the human heart. To submit. To immerse. It was a creed of sorts, a doctrine for the hopeless. But even as I turned the idea over, I felt its terrible flaw. To submit to the sea is to surrender to a thing without a mind, without malice or intent. It is a force, pure and simple. To submit to the destructive element in men, to the swirling currents of greed and fear and despair that make up a community of outcasts. . . that was another matter altogether. That was to surrender to a thousand petty wills, a thousand selfish dreams. It was not a sea, but a whirlpool, and at its centre was this strange, solitary man, treading water not for himself, but for them all.

I must have dozed, for I was startled back to full consciousness by a sound at the door. It was not the confident stride of his earlier return, but a slow, burdened shuffle. The door swung inward, scraping softly on the rough floorboards, and for a second he stood silhouetted against the profound blackness of the night, a figure broader and more misshapen than I remembered. He stepped into the ring of lamplight, and I saw why. He was not alone.

He carried a person in his arms, with a desperate sort of care, the way a man might carry a child that has been snatched from a fire. He moved past me without a word, his face a mask of grim concentration, and laid his burden gently on the cot. It was a woman. Her head fell back against the coarse pillow, and a great mass of dark, wet hair spilled out, glistening like seaweed in the lamplight. Her face was pale, impossibly so, with the translucent quality of fine porcelain, and her eyes were closed. A dark stain, which I had at first taken for a shadow, spread across the thin fabric of her blouse, high on the shoulder, near the collarbone. She was small, and seemed in her stillness to be already a thing of the past, a memory given form.

He stood over her for a moment, his broad back to me, his shoulders heaving as he caught his breath. Then he turned, and the placid exhaustion of the philosopher-king was gone. His face was drawn, etched with new lines of strain, and his eyes, when they met mine, held a darkness that was not of the night outside, but from some deeper, more personal abyss.

‘More flotsam,’ he said, and the words were not a cynical pronouncement but a statement of bitter fact. He gestured with his chin towards the cot. ‘Her boat was found capsized on the rocks at the northern point. The work of a squall that came and went in a moment. Her companion. . . the sea kept him.’ He walked to the table and poured a measure of water from a clay jug into a cup, his hand unsteady. He drank it down in one go.

‘The shouts?’ I asked.

‘A dispute over the boat’s salvage,’ he said, with a flash of his old weariness. ‘They were picking it clean before I arrived. My people. They understand wreckage.’ He looked back at the woman on the cot. ‘They are less certain what to do with the survivors.’ He seemed to speak to himself as much as to me. ‘It creates complications. It introduces a past. Every piece of flotsam has a past, you see, a history of the storm that wrecked it. And they bring that storm with them.’

He was right, of course. She was not merely a body, an object. She was a story, a complication, a demand. I looked at her still form, at the faint, almost imperceptible rise and fall of her chest. She was a specimen, like my Jim, like Stein’s butterfly, but she had washed up in a kingdom where the collector had grown tired of his collection.

He busied himself then, finding clean rags and a bowl of water, and with a surprising gentleness, he began to tend to the woman’s wound. It was a deep gash, ugly and dark against the pallor of her skin, but as he worked, his hands were deft and sure. He had done this before. He was a physician to the wrecked, a mender of broken things. As he cleaned the wound, the woman stirred, and a low moan escaped her lips. Her eyes fluttered open.

They were dark eyes, wide with a terror that seemed to have no object, the abstract fear of one who has looked into the void and found nothing there to look back. They darted around the room, over the rough walls, the flickering lamp, and then they found me. For an instant, the terror sharpened, became personal and acute. I was the stranger, the unknown quantity. I was the new danger. Then her gaze slid past me and fixed upon the man leaning over her. And the fear died. It did not recede or fade; it died, snuffed out in an instant, and in its place came an expression of profound and unnerving stillness. It was not relief. It was not gratitude. It was recognition.

‘You,’ she whispered, and the word was a bare thread of sound.

The man’s hands froze for a heartbeat. He did not look at her face, but continued his work, his head bowed. ‘I am here,’ he said, his voice low and toneless.

‘Always,’ she breathed, and her eyes closed again, not in a faint, but in a kind of surrender, a willed and final acceptance. A single tear traced a path from the corner of her eye through the grime on her temple. He finished dressing the wound, pulling the torn blouse aside with an impersonal touch, and wrapping a strip of cloth firmly around her shoulder. Then he straightened up and turned away, as if he could no longer bear to look at her.

He came back to the table and sank into the other chair, the one he had offered me earlier. He leaned forward, burying his face in his hands, and the silence in the room became heavy, charged with all the things that had not been said. The drama of the rescue, the dispute on the beach, it was all nothing. The real event was here, in this room, a confrontation with a ghost that had, impossibly, taken on flesh and blood.

‘You know her,’ I stated. It was not a question.

He lifted his head, and his face was ravaged. It was the face of a man who has had his last defence stripped away. ‘I do not know her,’ he said, his voice a harsh rasp. ‘I know her kind. I know the wreck. I have seen it a thousand times.’ He made a dismissive gesture with his hand, but it was a gesture of profound weakness. ‘She is from a port to the north. A trader’s wife, perhaps. Or daughter. Running from something. Or to something. It is always the same story. They think the sea will wash them clean. They think a new shore means a new life.’

‘And does it not?’ I asked, my voice softer than I intended.

He gave a short, bitter laugh that was more like a cough. ‘A new shore is just a new place to drown. Some do it quickly, in the water. Others,’ he glanced at the cot, ‘take a little longer.’ He fell silent, staring at the flickering flame of the lamp, as if it held the answer to some unanswerable question. The light cast shadows that danced on his face, making him look old, then young, then old again.

‘When I was young,’ he began, his voice taking on that distant, confessional tone I had heard before, ‘before this… this kingdom of mine, I was on a ship. A trading vessel in the eastern passages. A proper ship, with clean decks and a captain who believed in God and the owners. We took on a passenger. A man running from a debt of honour, he said. A gambler. He had a wife with him. She had eyes like that.’ He nodded almost imperceptibly towards the cot. ‘Eyes that had already seen the end of the world and were just… waiting for the paperwork to be filed.’

He paused, and I waited. The story was being pulled from him, piece by agonizing piece.

‘There was a storm, a real one. Not a squall. A tyrant that held us for three days. The ship was breaking up. The captain, the man of God, put a pistol to his head when the mainmast went. The gambler, the man of honour, was the first to fight for a place in the longboat, and he left his wife standing at the rail. He did not even look back.’ The man’s hands had clenched into fists on the table. ‘I was young. I was strong. I still believed a man could climb out of the sea. I got her into the boat. There were too many men, and the waves were mountains. I held onto her. I told her to hold onto me. For hours. In the water, in the dark.’

His voice dropped to a whisper, and he was looking not at me, but through me, into the heart of that long-ago darkness. ‘She was so quiet. She never cried out. Never complained. At dawn, when the sea finally grew calm, I was still holding her. But she was gone. Sometime in the night, she had simply… let go. Without a sound. Without a struggle. She had submitted. And I, who had tried to save her, I was left holding the dead. I was the flotsam then, clinging to a corpse.’

He looked at me then, and his eyes were burning. ‘Do you understand? To submit is one thing. That is the way. Stein is right. But to drag another down with you? To offer them your hand and then have them let go? Or to be the one who lets go? That is the other side of it. That is the part of the dream no one speaks of. You think you are treading water, but you are in a sea of ghosts. And they are so very, very tired.’

He stood up, and for a moment he seemed to swell to an immense size, a figure of terrible and tragic power. ‘I built this place,’ he said, his voice ringing with a sudden, fierce energy, ‘so that there would be a shore for them to find. A place where they would not have to let go in the dark. I would be the raft. I would be the rock. I would hold them all up!’ He laughed, a wild, broken sound. ‘A fine fellow, eh? A saint, perhaps. A very fine fellow in a dream.’

His energy collapsed as suddenly as it had come. He seemed to shrink, to become once more a weary man in a small, lamplit room. He walked over to the cot and stood looking down at the woman. Her breathing was even now, deep and peaceful. She was safe, for a time. He reached down and pulled the thin blanket up to her chin, his movements infinitely tender.

‘But the tide always comes in,’ he murmured. ‘And it always brings more.’ He turned and looked at me, and his face was calm again, but it was the calm of a vast and desolate sea after the storm has passed. There was nothing left to fight. ‘You asked what happens when a man grows tired. You are looking at it, my friend. You are looking at it.’

He walked to the door. ‘You should sleep,’ he said, in his old, quiet tone. ‘I must sit with her. It is my watch to keep.’ He did not invite me to stay, nor did he bid me to go. He simply stated a fact. His place was here, with this new ghost, this new burden. My place was elsewhere.

I rose from my chair, my limbs stiff, my mind reeling. The conference, the consultation, was over. The case had been diagnosed, the prognosis given. There was no remedy. There was only the watch, and the waiting, and the inexorable tide. I walked to the door of my own small hut, a few paces away, and turned back. In the frame of his doorway, he stood, a dark shape against the lamplight within. Behind him, on the cot, the woman slept, a still form in a sea of shadows. He was a guardian at the gates of a hospital for souls, and I saw with a terrible clarity that he was both its keeper and its oldest patient. The abyss had not swallowed the light; the light was merely a candle burning in the heart of the abyss, and its fuel was the life of a single, tired man. And as I finally turned away to seek my own uneasy rest, his question to Stein echoed back to me across the gulf of my own experience. How to be? It was not a question for a single man. It was the question for all of us who wander, romantic and doomed, across the great plain, amongst the graves and the pitfalls, under the deceptive poesy of a crepuscular light. It was the only question there was. And there was no answer. There was only the dream, and the sea, and the long, long watch of the night.
