The crew, however, was far from neutral. My climb up the mast had become the stuff of fo'c's'le legend. I was no longer just the "gentleman apprentice," but "the lad who bridled the main-yard." The true test, I knew, would come with the sextants. For several days, the sky was overcast, and no proper observation could be taken. The sky was a brilliant, endless blue, and the sea a deep indigo, flecked with the white crests of flying fish. The ship, the beautiful _Esmeralda_, settled into the steady rhythm of a long voyage; the creak of the timbers, the hum of the wind in the rigging, and the gentle sigh of the bow wave became the soundtrack to our days. It was an idyllic life, and for a time, even the tension between myself and Macdougall seemed to fade. The change came with the subtlety of a thunderclap. One afternoon, the sky to the west, which had been a perfect, cloudless dome, began to show a strange, greenish-yellow haze along the horizon. The air grew thick and humid, and an unnatural silence fell over the ship, broken only by the slatting of the sails against the masts and the nervous murmurings of the men.

The storm hit us during the first watch. It began with a low moan that grew into a deafening shriek, and the ship was struck by a squall so violent it felt as if we had run into a solid wall. Rain, driven horizontally, lashed the decks with the force of buckshot. The _Esmeralda_ heeled over so far that her lee rail was buried in the churning foam, and for a heart-stopping moment I thought she would never come back. But she was a fine ship, and she righted herself, shaking the water from her decks like a wet dog. The watch on deck, under Mr Ohlsen's command, fought like demons to shorten sail, their shouts swallowed by the roar of the wind. I was with them, my hands raw from hauling on wet canvas, my body soaked to the skin, but my heart pounding with a strange and terrifying exhilaration.

For two days we ran before the gale, a tiny, insignificant speck in a world of raging water and screaming wind. The sea was a nightmare of mountainous, grey waves, their crests torn into white spindrift that flew across the deck like snow. Sleep was impossible, and hot food a distant memory. Pat Doolan, bless his heart, managed to keep a fire going in his galley stove, brewing endless pots of coffee that were passed hand to hand along the streaming decks. Captain Billings seemed to be everywhere at once, his oilskins streaming, his face grim but calm, his presence a rock of certainty in the chaos.

The crisis came on the third night. I was on the poop deck, standing the eight-to-midnight watch with the first mate. Mr Ohlsen had finally been forced to his bunk by sheer exhaustion, and Macdougall was in command. The storm, if anything, had worsened. Every now and then, a colossal wave would board us over the stern, a solid wall of black water that swept the decks and threatened to tear a man from his grip. We had been forced to heave to, with just enough storm canvas set to keep the ship's head to the sea. Macdougall was in a foul temper, his face pale and drawn in the flickering light of the binnacle lamp. He was, I realised with a jolt, afraid. His seamanship, like his navigation, was a thin veneer, and the storm had stripped it away, leaving only a scared, incompetent bully.

Suddenly, there was a crack like a pistol shot from forward, followed by a tremendous tearing sound. Jorrocks, who was at the helm, shouted, "Fore-topmast staysail's gone, sir! It's flogging itself to pieces!" In the next flash of lightning, I saw it; the sail had been torn from its hanks and was now a thrashing, cannonading mass of canvas and rope, a wild beast that threatened to bring down the whole fore-topmast with it. If that went, the main-topmast would likely follow, and we would be dismasted and helpless. "We've got to cut it away!" I yelled to Macdougall over the wind. "We need men aloft to cut it away!" "Are you mad, boy?" he screamed back, his face a mask of terror. "Send men up there in this? It's suicide! Let it go! Let the mast go with it, for all I care!" "Sir, we can't!" I pleaded. "We'll lose the foremast! The ship will be crippled!" "I've given my order!" he shrieked, his voice cracking. "Silence! Or I'll have you in irons!"

The journey forward along the main deck was a battle in itself. The waist of the ship was a torrent of swirling water, and I had to time my movements between the waves that crashed aboard. I was joined by Haxell, the quiet carpenter, his solemn face set with determination. We began the terrifying climb up the foremast shrouds. The wind tore at me, trying to rip me from my hold. The rigging was slick with rain and spray, and the mast whipped back and forth like a coachman's whip. Below me, the deck seemed a mile away, a chaos of white water and darkness. Above, the remnants of the sail boomed and cracked like thunder. Every instinct screamed at me to go back down, but the thought of the captain's trust, of Jorrocks's steady gaze, of Pat Doolan's biscuit warming my pocket, pushed me onward.

We reached the fore-top, and from there had to crawl out along the swaying footropes of the yard. The flogging sail was a monster, a fury of canvas that could break a man's arm or knock him into the sea with a single blow. Working in the brief, terrifying illuminations of the lightning, Haxell and I began to slash at the lines and canvas that held the wreckage to the mast. It was desperate, brutal work. A corner of the heavy sail caught me a blow on the shoulder that nearly sent me flying, but I clung on, my fingers locked around the iron jackstay. At last, with a final, mighty slash from Haxell, the main body of the sail tore free. For a moment it hung in the air like a vast, white ghost, and then the wind caught it and hurled it away into the darkness of the sea. It was gone.

Panting, bruised, and utterly spent, we made our way back down to the deck. The fore-topmast was still standing. The ship was safe. As I stumbled aft, soaked and shivering, I saw Captain Billings standing at the break of the poop, a lantern held high in his hand. He had clearly been roused by the commotion. The _Esmeralda_, relieved of the pressure on her foremast, rode more easily, her timbers groaning a song of weary survival rather than one of imminent peril. We spoke little, the captain and I. They knew, as sailors always do, that a change had occurred in the ship's unseen hierarchy; they had witnessed the test, and they had seen who had passed and who had failed.

When our watch was relieved at four bells by Mr Ohlsen and his men, the captain clapped me on the shoulder. "Well done, lad," he said. "You have a sailor's heart in you. Go below and get some dry clothes and some hot coffee from the cook. You've earned it."

The next morning, the ship's world had been turned entirely upside down. Captain Billings called all hands aft after breakfast. The sea was calm now, a deep, placid blue, and the sun shone down as if no storm had ever dared to trouble the heavens. Mr Macdougall was there, standing apart from the rest, his eyes fixed on the deck planks, his face a mask of sullen resentment.

"Men," the captain began, his voice ringing out in the clear morning air, "as you know, we suffered some damage to the fore-topsail last night. Mister Macdougall, as first mate, was in charge of the watch." He paused, letting the silence hang heavy. "He has been relieved of his duties as first mate. He will remain on this vessel as a passenger. Mister Ohlsen, you are promoted to first mate. I expect you to perform your duties with diligence."

Ohlsen, a steady, quiet man of few words, simply grunted his assent, his face betraying nothing.

You will keep a log, you will take the noon sight, and you will answer to the first mate and myself for the good conduct of your watch and the safety of the ship."

A murmur went through the assembled crew, a sound of approval and satisfaction. I felt a blush creep up my neck, but I stood straight and tall, my heart thumping with pride. "Aye, aye, sir," I said, my voice coming out a little huskier than I intended.

And that was how it was. Macdougall was a ghost, a nonentity who kept to his cabin and was seen only at mealtimes, where he ate in silence, his food seemingly turning to ash in his mouth. The steward, Williams, the "flabby half-and-half sort of Welshman" who had been his creature, now found himself without a patron, and his attitude towards me became one of fawning servility, which I found even more distasteful than his previous insolence. The ship settled into a new and more harmonious routine. Ohlsen was a capable, if unimaginative, first mate, and with Jorrocks as boatswain, the work of the ship went on like clockwork.

My life was transformed. I moved my sea-chest from the apprentices' berth in the half-deck to the small but private cabin of the second mate. I took my meals in the main cabin, not as a guest, but as an officer of the ship. I had my own watch to command, my own men to direct, and the immense responsibility of the ship's safety in my hands for eight hours out of every twenty-four. It was a heavy weight for a lad of my years, but I gloried in it. I was determined to prove Captain Billings's faith in me was not misplaced. I studied my navigation books until my eyes ached, and I spent hours with Jorrocks, learning the practical seamanship that no book could teach: the particular way to splice a new rope, the signs in the clouds that foretold a shift in the wind, the name and purpose of every block and tackle on board.

We sailed south, out of the boisterous Atlantic and into the torpid heat of the doldrums. The wind deserted us. For days on end, the _Esmeralda_ sat becalmed on a sea of polished glass, the sun beating down with a relentless, brassy glare that bleached the very colour from the sky. The sails hung limp and useless from the yards, and the pitch in the deck seams softened and bubbled. The men grew listless and irritable in the oppressive heat. Tempers, shortened by sweat and boredom, began to fray. It was in this stagnant atmosphere that a new and more insidious trouble began to brew.

It started with grumbles in the fo'c's'le, whispers that reached me through Jorrocks. The provisions, never lavish, seemed to be getting scantier. The biscuit was harder and more weevilly than usual, the salt pork tougher, the water ration tasting a little more stale each day. "It's that Williams, the steward," Jorrocks said to me one evening, as we stood on the poop deck, watching a blood-red sunset that offered no promise of wind. "He was Macdougall's man, and he's takin' his spite out on the crew, now his master's been disrated. Thinks he can curry favour with the captain by showing how stingy he can be, I reckon. He's skimming the stores, Mister Leigh, mark my words."

I had noticed it myself. The duff on Sunday had been smaller, and the tea was little more than brownish water. It was a dangerous business, for nothing sours a crew faster than the belief that they are being cheated of their rightful grub. Under Macdougall, the men would have borne it with sullen resentment; but now, with the change in command, they looked to me and Ohlsen to see justice done.

I resolved to investigate the matter myself, without making a formal complaint to the captain until I had proof. I knew that the steward served out the week's provisions every Monday morning, and I made it my business to be present the following week, ostensibly to check some detail in the ship's manifest, but really to observe. Williams, a man with a perpetual sheen of grease on his face and an air of wounded piety, greeted me with an oily smile.

"Mister Leigh, sir! To what do we owe the honour?" he asked, his Welsh accent more pronounced than usual.

The mood of the ship was lifted, the oppressive gloom of the doldrums dispelled not by a wind, but by an act of justice.

And as if the ship herself approved, that very night, a faint cat's-paw of a breeze rippled the glassy surface of the water. The deck was a scene of organized chaos. Lanterns swung wildly, casting a confusing, demonic light on the scene. Men shouted, waves crashed, and above it all was the unholy shriek of the wind and the thunder of the flogging canvas. I gathered my watch, a half-dozen good men including Jorrocks himself, and we fought our way forward to the foremast.

Laying aloft in a gale is the nearest thing to hell I have ever experienced. The mast whips back and forth in an arc that seems a hundred feet wide; the wind tries to tear you from your precarious perch on the foot-rope; and the sail, which on a calm day is a sheet of stiff but manageable canvas, becomes a living, malevolent beast, hard as a board and beating with enough force to break a man's arm.

We fought it for what seemed an eternity, clinging to the yard, our fingers raw and bleeding, trying to gather in the wild, thrashing sail. Below us, I could see Mr Macdougall's party struggling with the mainsail with little success; the first mate seemed to be doing more shouting than working, and his men were disorganized and fearful.

We had almost subdued our own canvas, and were passing the gaskets to secure it, when a cry of real terror went up from aft. I looked over my shoulder and my heart went into my throat. The main-topgallant sail, which Macdougall's men had evidently failed to secure properly, had broken loose from its gaskets. It bellied out with a tremendous bang, and then, with a sound of splintering wood that was audible even over the storm, the yard-arm to which it was attached snapped clean off.

The broken yard, a massive piece of timber weighing several hundred pounds, did not fall. It hung precariously from the mast, held by a tangle of rigging, swinging back and forth with the violent roll of the ship like a gigantic scythe. With every swing, it smashed against the main-topmast, threatening to bring the entire top-hamper down on our heads in a fatal cascade of wood, rope, and canvas.

A paralysis seemed to seize the deck. The men stared up in horror at the swinging pendulum of death above them. I saw Mr Macdougall standing near the mast, his face a mask of terror in the lantern light, pointing and shouting, but his orders were lost in the gale. Captain Billings was roaring something through his speaking-trumpet from the poop, but no one seemed to hear, or if they heard, they did not know what to do.

"Cut it away! Cut it all away!" I heard Macdougall shriek, his voice high with panic. He was ordering the men to cut the rigging that held the mast itself, an act of sheer madness that would have brought the whole thing crashing down, likely killing half the men on deck and crippling the ship beyond repair.

In that instant, Jorrocks's words came back to me: "He'll wait for the dark and the foul weather." This was the moment. This was the test. I saw Jorrocks beside me on the yard, his face grim. He looked at me, and in his eyes, I saw not a question, but a confirmation.

"No! Belay that!" I roared, my voice cracking with the strain. I slid down a backstay to the lower yard, landing with a jarring impact. "Jorrocks! Take two men and get a preventer-brace on that swinging yard! Quick now, before it parts!" Then I turned to the men cowering on the deck. "The rest of you, with me! We're not cutting it, we're saving it!"

For a second, no one moved. They just stared at me, a mere boy, giving orders in defiance of the first mate. Then one of them, a stout Able Seaman named Evans, nodded once. "Aye, sir!" he said. "What's the plan?"

It was a desperate plan, born of a lesson the captain had given me on stresses and strains weeks ago. The swinging yard had to be controlled and then lowered to the deck. "We need to get a line around the broken end," I yelled over the wind. "We'll run it through that block on the main mast, and use the men's weight to steady it! Then we can lower it down! Macdougall! Get your men to clear the deck below!"

I did not wait to see if he obeyed. With Evans and another man right behind me, I began to climb again, up the heaving, treacherous shrouds of the mainmast, towards the splintered, swinging yard. The wind tore at me, trying to pluck me from the ropes. The ship rolled, and the broken spar swung sickeningly close, its splintered end like a jagged spear. It was the most terrifying thing I had ever done. But as I climbed, I felt a strange calm descend upon me. I was no longer a boy playing at being an officer; I was a seaman with a job to do, and the lives of my shipmates and the safety of the vessel depended on it. As I reached the topmast crosstrees, I looked down and saw, to my immense relief, that Jorrocks had succeeded in getting a line on the spar, and the men on deck, my men and Macdougall's men now working as one, were hauling on it, steadying its wild swing. The first mate stood apart, his hands hanging uselessly at his sides, a spectator at his own abdication. The ship was mine to command.

The captain took a step closer, his voice dropping to a low, dangerous tone that cut through the cabin's confines like a sharpened blade. "You are mistaken, Mr Macdougall," he said, with a terrible quietness. "You will not be wanting a new first mate, for you are no longer the first mate of this ship. The turkey-egg colour drained from his face, leaving it a pasty, mottled white. Without another word, he turned on his heel and stumbled out of the cabin, slamming the door behind him with such force that the very timbers of the ship seemed to groan in protest. The skipper turned to me, a wry smile touching his lips, though his eyes were still flashing. "Well, Mister Leigh," he said, "it seems you are to be promoted sooner than even I had intended. Come, lad, sit down. The steward will be in with our dinner shortly, and after that day's work, I daresay you have an appetite." And so it was that I, a mere first-class apprentice, sat down to dine with the captain, not as a guest, but as his second officer, while the man who had sought to make my life a misery sulked in his cabin, a captain in name only over his own boiling anger.

The wind tore at me, seeking to rip me from the crosstrees as if I were no more than a dead leaf clinging to a branch. Below, the deck was a scene of organised chaos, a testament to the change in command. Men, who moments before had been shrinking from the wrath of the storm and the madness of the first mate, now worked with a desperate purpose, their movements guided by Jorrocks's bellowed instructions. He had indeed managed to get a preventer-brace rigged, a heavy rope running from the deck to the far end of the swinging yard, and the strain of a dozen men hauling upon it had dampened the spar's wild, destructive arc, though it still bucked and plunged like a harpooned whale. My task, however, was the more perilous. It was to get a stout hawser around the splintered stump of the yard, close to where it had snapped, so that we might use it as a sling to lower the whole broken affair to the deck. With me was Evans, the seaman who had first answered my call; his broad, weather-beaten face was set in a mask of concentration, his eyes fixed not on the raging sea below, but on the job at hand. "A bit more to port, sir!" he yelled, his voice barely audible over the gale. "When she swings back this way!" I had the coiled end of the hawser in my hands; it was as thick as my wrist and stiff with salt and cold water, feeling more like a dead serpent than a rope. The ship rolled heavily to leeward, and the broken yard swung away from us into the darkness, its splintered teeth gnashing at the black air. Then, as she rolled back, it came hurtling towards us. "Now!" I screamed, and together, Evans and I threw our weight into the heave. For a heart-stopping second, I thought we had missed. The loop of rope seemed to hang in the air, caught by the wind, before it fell short. But Evans, with a seaman's instinct, had held onto a trailing length of the line; with a flick of his powerful wrist, born of a thousand such moments on a thousand such nights, he flipped the rope up and over the yard-arm. It caught. "Haul, sir, haul!" he roared in my ear. We pulled with the strength of desperation, taking in the slack, fighting the rope as it threatened to slip from the splintered wood. We had it. I fumbled with numb fingers to make the line fast around the mast itself, creating a secure point from which to control the descent. Looking down, I could just make out the pale blur of faces on the deck, all turned upwards towards us. "On deck there!" I bellowed, cupping my hands to my mouth. "Jorrocks! Take the strain on the hawser! Ease off your preventer-brace as we lower! Slow and steady now!"

There was a responding roar from below, and I felt the hawser in my hands go taut as the men on deck took the full weight of the broken spar. It was a delicate and terrifying operation, like trying to tame a furious beast with threads of twine. Every time the ship plunged into a trough, the yard would drop with a sickening lurch that threatened to snap the rope; every time she rose on a crest, the strain on the mast and rigging was immense. I could hear the timbers of the mainmast groaning in protest, a deep, resonant sound that vibrated through my very bones. I remained aloft with Evans, my job now to watch the sling we had fashioned, to shout warnings if it began to slip or chafe. Macdougall's mad plan to cut it all away would have taken minutes; my plan, I realised, was taking an eternity of heart-in-mouth terror. "She's holding, sir! She's holding!" Evans shouted, a grin splitting his face. Below us, under Jorrocks's masterful direction, the two teams of men on the separate ropes worked in perfect concert, one group easing off while the other hauled, and inch by painful inch, the great broken spar was guided down towards the comparative safety of the deck. I saw Mr Ohlsen now, the second mate, directing a party of men to clear a space, his easy-going nature vanished in the face of the emergency, replaced by a quiet, Scandinavian efficiency. And then I saw another figure emerge from the companionway onto the poop deck. It was Captain Billings. He stood for a moment, his oilskins glistening in the spray, taking in the whole scene with a single, sweeping glance: the men working with disciplined fury at the ropes; Jorrocks, legs braced against the roll of the deck, conducting them like an orchestra; and me, high above in the rigging, clinging to the mast. He said nothing, his face was a shadow beneath his sou'wester, but I could feel his eyes on me. His gaze then shifted to the break of the poop, where Mr Macdougall stood, a solitary, useless figure, watching the saving of his ship by the very boy he despised. He was no longer a participant; he was merely a spectator to his own irrelevance. With a final great heave and a scraping of wood against wood, the broken end of the yard was brought to rest on the deck. A ragged cheer went up from the men, a sound that was immediately snatched away and shredded by the wind, but it was a cheer nonetheless. The immediate danger was past. Only then did I allow myself to feel the exhaustion that flooded my limbs, and the tremor in my hands that was not entirely from the cold. As Evans and I began our slow, careful descent, I saw Captain Billings walk over to the first mate. He did not raise his voice; he did not need to. He spoke a few words, his finger pointing once, decisively, towards the cabin. Macdougall's shoulders slumped. He turned without a word and walked away, a man utterly defeated, swallowed by the shadows of the deckhouse. The ship was still in the grip of the storm, but the mutiny of incompetence had been quelled.

When my feet finally touched the solid planking of the deck, my knees nearly buckled under me. Jorrocks was there to clap me on the shoulder, his hand like a slab of granite. "Well done, Mister Leigh! Well done, my lad!" he boomed, his face split by a wide grin that showed his tobacco-stained teeth. "You handled her like a veteran! I couldn't ha' done it better myself!" The men of the watch crowded around, their earlier fear replaced by a boisterous admiration. One of them pressed a steaming mug of something hot into my hands, and I realised it was Pat Doolan, the cook, who had no business being on deck at all. "The finest brew for the finest officer, Mister Leigh, sir!" he said, with a wink. "The captain's own private stock, it is, though he doesn't know it yet!" I took a gulp of the scalding, sweet tea, and it felt as if it were melting the ice in my veins. At that moment, Mr Ohlsen approached and offered his hand, a gesture of profound significance from a man of few words. "Good work, Mister Leigh," he said, his Swedish accent thick. "You saved us much trouble. The ship is grateful." Just then, a call came from the poop deck. "Mr Leigh, the captain wants a word with you in his cabin!" I handed the mug back to Pat, straightened my weary shoulders, and made my way aft, the men parting for me with a newfound respect that was more gratifying than any praise. The captain's cabin was an oasis of calm, the light from the gimballed oil lamp swinging gently, casting steady shadows. Captain Billings had shed his wet oilskins and was standing by his chart table. "Close the door, Mr Leigh," he said, his voice quiet but resonant. "And help yourself to a proper drink. You've earned it." He indicated a decanter of brandy and a glass. My hand shook as I poured a small measure, the spirit's amber glow a warm and welcome sight. "I saw what happened on deck, lad," the captain continued, turning to face me. "I saw it all. Macdougall's panic, and your... resolution. You countermanded a direct order from your superior officer." I stood straighter, the brandy untouched. "Yes, sir. I did." "It was an act of gross insubordination," he said, his face unreadable. My heart sank. "An act which, had you been wrong, would have seen you in irons for the rest of the voyage. An act that saved my ship, my cargo, and the lives of my men." A slow smile spread across his face. "Jorrocks was right about you, Mister Leigh. You have the right stuff in you. You did not just do your duty; you did what was right, and that is the mark of a true commander." He took a step forward and placed a hand on my shoulder. "From this moment, you are no longer a 'prentice lad playing at being an officer. As far as this ship and this crew are concerned, you are my second mate in fact as well as in name. You will take the second mate's cabin, you will stand the second mate's watches, and you will draw the second mate's pay." He paused, his expression growing grim. "As for Mr Macdougall," he said, the name tasting like poison in his mouth, "he is confined to his quarters. He has forfeited his position and my trust. He will remain a passenger, and a prisoner of his own cowardice, until we reach Callao, where I shall put him ashore and strike him from the ship's articles for gross dereliction of duty." The finality in his voice was absolute. The Scotsman's reign of petty tyranny was over, brought down not by a grand confrontation, but by a moment of foul weather, just as Jorrocks had predicted. "Thank you, sir," I stammered, overwhelmed. "I will not let you down." "I know you won't, lad," he said, his tone softening. "Now get some rest. Mr Ohlsen and Jorrocks have the deck. You've a watch to stand at four bells, Mister Mate." He called me "Mister Mate." The words rang in my ears, sweeter than any music. I downed the brandy in one go, the fire of it chasing away the last of the chill, and walked out of the cabin, not as the boy who had entered it, but as an officer of the ship _Esmeralda_.

The dawn broke grey and weary, the sky the colour of unpolished pewter. The fury of the gale had passed, leaving behind a heaving, slate-coloured sea and a sky bruised with clouds. The ship, wounded but alive, rolled on the long, heavy swell, the sounds of hammering already echoing from the deck where Haxell, the carpenter, and his mate were fashioning a temporary repair for the broken yard. When I came on deck to take my watch, the change in my station was palpable. The seaman at the wheel, a man who a week ago would have given me a surly nod at best, touched his forelock and said, "Morning, Mister Leigh, sir. Wind's backing to the west-sou'west. She's steering well enough." The respect was no longer feigned, nor was it offered to the captain's favourite; it was earned, paid in the currency of a shared and survived danger. I saw Macdougall only once that day. He emerged from his cabin near midday, his face pale and unshaven, his eyes red-rimmed. He looked like a ghost haunting the scene of his own demise. He walked to the rail and stared out at the grey water, a man marooned on a ship full of people. No one spoke to him. The crew, when their duties brought them near, moved around him as if he were a rock, an obstacle to be avoided, their faces blank and uninterested. He had ceased to exist for them. I felt a momentary pang of something that might have been pity, but it was quickly extinguished by the memory of his sneering face and his panicked shrieking in the storm. He had forged his own isolation, and now he was condemned to live in it. He caught my eye for a moment across the deck; a flicker of pure, unadulterated hatred passed across his features before he turned away. I knew then that this was not over. Jorrocks found me later that afternoon, as I was overseeing the re-stowing of some gear that had come loose in the hold. He spat a stream of tobacco juice over the rail into the sea. "Well, Mister Leigh," he began, "you've made your berth, right and proper. But you've made a powerful enemy, too." "He's no threat, Jorrocks," I said, with more confidence than I felt. "The captain has relieved him of his duties. He's nothing now." Jorrocks shook his grizzled head slowly. "Ah, that's where you're wrong, lad. A man with power who's a bully is one thing; you can see him coming. But a man who's lost his power, who's been shamed in front of the whole crew... he's something else entirely. He's poison. He has nothing to lose now, and a man with nothing to lose is the most dangerous animal aboard a ship, I tell you." He leaned closer, his voice a low rumble. "He's still got one friend on board, mind. That flabby Welsh steward is still thick as thieves with him. Been taking him his meals, whispering with him in his cabin. You watch your back, Mister Leigh. The storm's passed, but the sea ain't always the most dangerous thing on a long voyage." He gave me a meaningful look, then straightened up and ambled off to attend to some rigging, leaving me alone with my thoughts. The sun, for the first time in days, broke through the clouds, bathing the deck in a pale, watery light. The _Esmeralda_ sailed on, but I knew, with a cold certainty that settled deep in my gut, that Jorrocks was right. The battle with the storm was over; but the battle with the Scotsman had only just begun.
