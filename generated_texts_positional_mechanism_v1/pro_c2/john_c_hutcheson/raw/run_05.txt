"What's the matter, Grummet?" I yelled over the wind. "Afraid of getting your feet wet?"

"Look out!" someone screamed.

I lay there for a moment, gasping for air, my head throbbing and my arm screaming in agony. When I finally pushed myself up, my vision clearing, I saw my rescuer. It was Grummet. He was leaning against the rail, his face pale, his chest heaving. He had saved my life.

Before I could speak, Sykes was there, his face a mask of concern. "Mister Davies! Sir, are you alright?" He saw my arm, which was hanging at an unnatural angle. "Sweet mother, your arm's broken!"

"I'll live," I gasped, the pain making me feel faint. I looked at Grummet, who was watching me with an unreadable expression. "You... you pulled me back."

He just grunted, not meeting my eye. "Would've been a man short," he muttered, and then turned away to check a straining stay.

There was no time for sentiment. The ship was still in peril. Gritting my teeth against the pain, I got to my feet. "The scuppers," I said, my voice hoarse. "We still have to clear them."

"You're in no fit state, sir!" Sykes protested.

"I'll be the judge of that, Bosun," I retorted, though the world was starting to spin. "Let's get it done."

With Sykes and Young Billy's help, we managed to clear the blockage. The water on deck began to recede. We worked through the rest of the storm, a nightmare of wind and water and aching pain. I did what I could with my one good arm, directing the men, securing lines, my mind curiously clear and focused despite the agony radiating from my broken limb. And through it all, I saw Grummet, working with a silent, ferocious energy he had never shown before, always seeming to be in the right place at the right time.

When the grey, miserable dawn finally broke, it revealed a scene of devastation. The _Sea Serpent_ was still afloat, but she was a wounded beast. One of the lifeboats had been smashed to kindling, a section of the rail was gone, and the decks were littered with the detritus of the storm. But we were alive. All hands were accounted for.

As soon as the sea had calmed enough, Captain Fowler relinquished the helm and came to find me. He took one look at my face, my makeshift sling, and the exhausted crew, and his own weary expression softened.

He stopped his work and looked at me, really looked at me, for the first time. The sullen mask was gone, replaced by a weary respect. "Don't thank me, sir," he said, his voice rough. The days that followed settled into a routine. We left the English Channel behind and stood out into the broad Atlantic, the last smudge of land sinking below the eastern horizon. With every day that passed, my duel with Mr Macdougall continued, though it took on a more subtle form. He no longer confronted me openly; instead, he sought to catch me out in small ways, to find fault with my work and hold me up to ridicule.

He would appear on deck during my watch and question my entries in the ship's log, peering at my figures as if searching for a mistake. He set me to tedious and unnecessary tasks, such as re-stowing coils of rope that were already perfectly in order, or having me inspect the lashings of the spare spars, hoping, I suppose, to find some neglect on my part. The hands on watch, seeing his game, would often lend me quiet assistance, one of them giving a final tightening to a knot, another pointing out a spot I might have missed. They had taken my measure during that first squall, and their silent support was a constant source of strength to me.

My friendship with Pat Doolan, the cook, also flourished, much to my benefit. True to Jorrocks's prediction, a pannikin of hot, strong coffee was always waiting for me when I came off the cold morning watch.

One afternoon, about a fortnight out from England, we were becalmed. The wind, which had been blowing a steady trade, died away completely, leaving the _Esmeralda_ rolling lazily on a long, glassy swell under a sky of brass. The heat was intense. The pitch in the deck seams grew soft and sticky, and the sails hung limp and useless from the yards. There is nothing more trying to a sailor's patience than a dead calm, and the tempers of the men soon began to fray.

Mr Macdougall seemed to thrive in this atmosphere of indolence and irritation. With no sailing to be done, he turned his energy to making work for the crew. He had them scouring the brasswork until it blazed in the sun, holystoning the decks until they were white as bone, and tarring the standing rigging, a hot and messy job at the best of times. His voice, sharp and grating, was everywhere, finding fault, hurrying the men, and allowing them no rest.

"Get a move on there, you slugs!" he'd bawl at a group of men painting the bulwarks. "D'ye think we're paying ye to stand about and admire your own handiwork? This is a ship, not a picture gallery!"

The men worked with a sullen resentment, their faces grim under the merciless sun. Even Jorrocks, usually so philosophical, was out of sorts.

"He's a proper driver, is our Mister Macdougall," he muttered to me as we worked together, applying a fresh coat of Stockholm tar to the forward shrouds. "Works a man for the sake of working him. He'd have us polish the anchor if he thought of it. It's in a calm like this that a bad officer shows his true colours."

"It's his way of keeping busy, I suppose," I said, trying to be charitable.

"It's his way of being a bully," Jorrocks corrected me. "He can't drive the ship, so he drives the men instead. You mark my words, Mister Leigh, there'll be trouble afore this calm breaks."

His prophecy was very nearly fulfilled that same evening. At the change of the watch, one of the hands, a young fellow named Green, was slow in coming on deck. He had been feeling the effects of the sun and had fallen into a heavy sleep in the fo'c's'le. Mr Macdougall, seeing him emerge rubbing his eyes a few minutes late, descended upon him in a fury.

"So, you choose your own time to turn to, do ye, Green?" he snarled, seizing the lad by the collar of his shirt. "Think you're a passenger, eh?"

"No, sir," the lad stammered, pale with fright. "I was asleep, sir. I didn't hear the call."

"Don't give me your excuses!" Macdougall roared, shaking him violently. "I'll teach you to sleep when you should be on duty! I'll log you for a day's pay, and if you're not smarter in future, I'll have you up at the gangway!"

Haxell the carpenter and his mates were busy from dawn till dusk repairing the shattered bulwarks and replacing the stove-in jolly-boat; while Sails, who had been an uncommonly quiet man during the blow, now recovered his cheerfulness, and could be heard from his little canvas-scented cabin, stitching and patching away, his voice raised in some long-forgotten sea-chanty. For my own part, I felt as if I were walking on air. The change in my standing on board was as clear as the tropic sky above us. The men, who had before been civil enough, now greeted me with a genuine warmth, touching their caps and calling out "Mornin', Mister Leigh!" with a respect that had nothing to do with my rank and everything to do with the events on the storm-lashed poop. Even Ohlsen, my watch-mate, who had always been friendly in his quiet, easy-going way, now treated me as a true colleague. We would stand our four hours on deck together, talking of ships and the sea, and of his home in Sweden, and he took pains to show me the little tricks of the trade, how to judge the weather by the look of the clouds, or how to get the last ounce of speed out of the vessel by a slight adjustment of the yards.

Only one man stood apart from this general harmony, and that was Mr Macdougall. He was a changed man, though not for the better. The open bullying was gone, for the captain's words had put a stop to that; but in its place was something I liked even less, a sort of watchful, waiting stillness. He performed his duties with a sullen precision, his lips set in a thin line, and his pale eyes followed me about the deck with an expression I could not fathom, though I knew it boded me no good. He spoke to me only when necessity demanded it, and then in a voice devoid of all inflection, as if he were addressing a block of wood. Jorrocks's words came back to me often in those days: "He's like a shark, that one; he'll circle and circle, and the first drop of blood he sees, he'll strike." I knew I was being circled, and I trod very carefully indeed.

The opportunity for him to strike, or so he thought, came about ten days later, as we were approaching the Equator and the region of unpredictable winds and currents known to all sailors as the doldrums. The air grew heavy and humid, the sea turned to a sheet of polished glass, and our sails, which had been so bravely filled, now hung in limp, useless festoons from the yards. The ship lay almost becalmed, rising and falling on the long, oily swell, her timbers groaning in protest. It was a time that tried the patience of any crew, a time of endless, monotonous waiting, with the sun beating down mercilessly from a white-hot sky.

It was in such conditions that accurate navigation became a matter of the utmost importance. A mistake of a few miles in one's reckoning could mean being trapped for weeks in this watery prison, while a ship just over the horizon might pick up a stray cat's-paw of wind and leave you wallowing in her wake. Captain Billings, aware of this, and perhaps also wishing to keep our minds occupied, declared one morning that he wished for a full and proper accounting of our position before the sun reached its zenith.

"Mr Macdougall," he said, coming out on deck after his breakfast, "you will take a sight at noon and work out our position. And Mister Leigh," he added, turning to me with a nod, "you will do the same. We will see if two heads are better than one in finding a way out of this confounded mill-pond."

A murmur of interest went through the hands who were idling on deck, pretending to mend ropes or tar rigging. This was to be another contest, a trial of skill between the first mate and the new-fledged third. I saw Macdougall's thin lips tighten, and a strange, triumphant little gleam flicker in his eyes before it was extinguished. "Aye, Cap'en," he said, with a show of obedience. "The lad needs his practice, to be sure."

I said nothing, but went to my cabin to fetch my sextant, the very one the captain had helped me select in London. I cleaned the mirrors and checked the vernier with a careful hand, feeling a thrill of anticipation. This was the ground on which I felt most secure; this was a contest of pure mathematics and skill, where bullying and bluster counted for nothing.

A few minutes before noon, we stood ready on the poop deck, Macdougall and I, a little space apart. The captain stood by the binnacle, observing us with a neutral expression. Ohlsen was at the wheel, and Jorrocks, who had no official business there, was nevertheless loitering nearby, ostensibly checking the lashing on a spare spar. The ship rolled lazily, making the task of holding the sun's image steady on the horizon a difficult one. I braced myself, my feet planted wide, and brought the sextant to my eye. At the very moment that Captain Billings called out "Noon!", I caught the sun's lower limb just kissing the horizon, and locked the arm of the instrument. "Time!" I called out to Ohlsen, who noted the exact second on the ship's chronometer. A moment later, I heard Macdougall do the same.

I hurried below to the cabin to work out my calculations. The process was a familiar comfort to me: the page of logarithms, the careful sums, the corrections for dip, parallax, and refraction. Slowly, the figures on the paper began to resolve themselves into a coherent position on the vast, empty ocean. I worked through it twice to be certain, and finding no error, I transcribed the final latitude and longitude onto a clean slip of paper. Feeling a quiet satisfaction, I made my way back to the poop.

Macdougall was there before me, leaning against the rail with a look of ill-concealed smirk on his face. He had already handed his own slip to the captain, who was studying it with a frown.

"Well, Mister Leigh," the captain said, without looking up. "Let us see what you have made of it."

I handed him my paper. He held the two slips side by side, his frown deepening. The silence on the deck seemed to stretch for an eternity, broken only by the creak of the rudder and the gentle slap of water against the hull.

"This is a strange business," Captain Billings said at last, looking from me to the first mate and back again. "There is a discrepancy here. Your latitudes are near enough the same, a matter of a few cable-lengths. But your longitudes," he paused, tapping the papers, "your longitudes are apart by more than fifteen nautical miles! Mr Macdougall places us well to the east of the Leeward Shoals, while you, Mister Leigh, have us drifting directly towards them!"

I felt a cold shock, as if I had been doused with a bucket of sea-water. It was impossible! I had checked and re-checked my figures. I knew they were right. I looked at Macdougall, and the smirk I had seen earlier was now a full-blown leer of triumph.

"Och, Cap'en, the lad is young," he said, in a tone of false sympathy that was more insulting than any curse. "He's keen, I'll grant you, but he hasna' the experience. It takes years to get the proper feel for a sight, ye ken. A wee slip of the hand, a wee error in the sums... it's easily done. No doubt he'll learn in time. We'll just alter course to the west a bit, and we'll be clear of any danger."

He was trying to make me look like a fool, a "bumptious" apprentice who had endangered the ship through his own incompetence. My cheeks burned with indignation. "Captain," I said, my voice coming out louder than I intended. "I am certain of my calculation! I staked my life on it!"

"Certain, are ye?" sneered Macdougall. "As certain as ye are that two and two make five, I'll wager! The chronometer doesna' lie, lad, and my figures are ta'en from the chronometer!"

And then, in a flash, I understood. The chronometer! It was the heart of all longitude calculations, the one instrument upon which everything depended. An error of a few seconds could translate into miles of distance at the Equator. He had not interfered with me, or with my sextant; he had done something far more cunning.

"Jorrocks," I said, turning suddenly to the old sailor. "When was the last time the chronometer was wound?"

Jorrocks, who had been watching the whole affair with a look of grim concentration, started at my question. "Why, this morning, Mister Leigh, same as every morning. The skipper winds it himself at eight bells sharp."

"And has anyone been in the captain's cabin since then, besides the captain himself?" I pressed.

All eyes turned to Macdougall. It was his duty, as first mate, to have access to the charts and instruments in the cabin. His face, which had been so full of triumph, now showed the first flicker of unease.

"I had occasion to consult the chart an hour since," he said, defensively. "It is my duty, as ye well know."

"And did you happen to touch the chronometer, Mr Macdougall?" I asked, my voice steady.

"Touch it? Why would I touch it?" he blustered. "It's a delicate instrument! I wouldna' dream of it!"

"Then perhaps you would not mind," I said, addressing the captain now, "if we were to check its rate. We have the log of its daily gain or loss for the past month. If it has been tampered with, even slightly, a comparison with yesterday's rate will show a discrepancy. It is a simple matter to verify."

The trap was sprung. It was a long shot, I knew; a clever man might have made a minute adjustment that would be hard to prove. But I was counting on Macdougall's arrogance, on his belief that a mere 'prentice lad' like myself would never think to question the ship's clock.

The silence that followed was the most profound I have ever experienced. Macdougall's face went through a remarkable series of transformations, from red to white and back to red again, like a badly hoisted signal flag. He opened his mouth to speak, but no sound came out. He looked like a fish that had just been hauled on deck, gasping in the unfamiliar air.

Captain Billings looked at him, and his blue eyes, which could be so kind, were now as hard and cold as chips of ice. He did not need to wait for any verification. He had been at sea long enough to read a man's guilt in his face as easily as he could read a chart.

"Mister Ohlsen," the captain said, his voice dangerously quiet. "You will please escort Mr Macdougall to his cabin. He is unwell. He will remain there until I send for him. You, sir," he added, turning his icy gaze back to the first mate, "will consider yourself relieved of duty."

Macdougall stood frozen for a moment, his eyes wide with disbelief. Then, without another word, he turned and allowed the second mate to lead him away, a broken and defeated man.

The captain then turned to me, and the hardness in his face softened. "Mister Leigh," he said, and there was a new note of respect in his voice. "I believe your calculations are the correct ones. You will lay a course accordingly. You have the watch."

I could only stammer out, "Aye, aye, sir," my mind reeling. I had faced him down. I had beaten him at his own crooked game.

That evening, as I sat in my cabin, trying to make sense of the day's events, there was a soft knock at my door. It was Jorrocks, carrying a pannikin of coffee liberally laced with something much stronger, a gift, no doubt, from our friend Pat Doolan in the galley.

"I thought you might be needin' this, Mister Leigh," he said, setting it down on my small table. "That was a tricky bit o' sailing you did today, and I'm not talking about the ship."

"He nearly had me, Jorrocks," I confessed, the strain of the day finally catching up with me. "If he had been just a little more clever about it..."

"Ah, but he wasn't," Jorrocks interrupted, with a gravelly chuckle. "That's the way with men like him. They're so full of their own cleverness they can't imagine anyone else havin' any brains at all. He thought he was settin' a snare for a rabbit, and he found out he'd poked a bear. You showed him up, my lad, not with fists or with shouting, but with your wits. That's a lesson the hands won't forget in a hurry."

He took a thoughtful sip from his own mug. "The skipper's made Ohlsen acting first mate," he said, after a moment. "And he's rated you proper second. You'll be taking on Mr Ohlsen's watch by yourself now. The ship's papers are all to be put in order."

Second mate! The words sent a thrill through me that banished all my weariness. Second mate of the _Esmeralda_! It was a promotion that I had not dared to dream of for years to come.

"As for Macdougall," Jorrocks went on, his voice dropping lower, "the skipper's keepin' him confined. He'll be put ashore at the first port we make, Valparaiso most like, and sent home in disgrace. His career at sea is finished." He looked at me with his shrewd, kindly eyes. "You've not just won a battle today, Mister Leigh. You've won the whole bloomin' war."

Perhaps he was right. As I looked out of the porthole at the star-dusted velvet of the tropical night, I felt a sense of peace settle over me. The shark that had been circling was gone. The path ahead was clear. The long voyage to Callao still lay before us, with all its unknown challenges and adventures; but for the first time since stepping on board, I felt truly a part of the ship, and ready for whatever the sea might send our way.

I checked the course with the helmsman, a grizzled old salt named Evans; I scanned the set of the sails, from the towering main-skysail down to the broad mainsail; I peered into the gathering darkness ahead, my eyes searching for the distant glimmer of a passing ship. All was quiet; the _Esmeralda_ sailed on a calm sea under a canopy of stars that seemed close enough to touch, a gentle breeze filling her canvas and pushing her steadily south-westward at a comfortable six knots. As the hours of the dog watch crept by, I began to feel a greater sense of ease. The men, seeing my diligence, seemed to relax their scrutiny. When I gave an order to trim the yards to catch a slight shift in the wind, they jumped to it with an alacrity that was gratifying. Pat Doolan, emerging from his galley for a breath of air, gave me a broad wink and a thumbs-up gesture that was all the more welcome for its surreptitiousness. Even Jorrocks, who was in my watch, treated me with a new and formal respect, addressing me always as "Mister Leigh" and touching his forelock whenever I spoke to him, though the twinkle in his eye told me our friendship remained unchanged.

The next few days passed in a quiet routine that was a blessed relief after the high drama of Macdougall's downfall. Ohlsen proved to be a capable, if uninspired, first mate, content to follow the captain's orders and maintain the ship's discipline without the petty tyranny of his predecessor. The steward, having lost his powerful patron, became suddenly much more obliging, and the quality of the food served to the crew improved noticeably, a fact which did more to cement my popularity than any feat of navigation could have done. Macdougall himself remained a prisoner in his cabin, a silent, brooding presence that was felt but not seen. The captain had let it be known that he would be put on the first ship heading back to England from Valparaiso, his career at sea effectively over. I felt a pang of something almost like pity for the man, but it was quickly dispelled by the memory of his sneering face and his treacherous attempt to ruin me.

We had crossed the Equator with the usual ceremonies, in which King Neptune, played with boisterous good humour by Pat Doolan, had given me a particularly vigorous "shave" with a rusty iron hoop in honour of my new rank. The hot, sultry days of the doldrums had given way to the steady trade winds that now propelled us towards the Horn, and the _Esmeralda_ seemed to revel in the open sea, her bows slicing cleanly through the long, blue rollers of the South Atlantic. It was a happy ship, or as happy as any vessel on a long voyage can be. The change was palpable; the air of sullen resentment that had clung to the fo'c's'le under Macdougall's reign had vanished, replaced by a spirit of cheerful competence. Haxell, the carpenter, whom I had scarcely spoken to before, proved to be a mine of information on the lore of the sea, and would often join me on my watch to point out the subtle signs of the weather, his solemn face lighting up with a rare smile when I asked a particularly insightful question. Sails, the sailmaker, taught me the intricate art of the palm and needle, and my evenings were often spent in his little cubby-hole of a workshop, surrounded by the comforting scent of canvas and tar, learning to stitch a patch or splice a rope with a seaman's skill.

It was Jorrocks, however, who first sounded a note of warning. We were well south of the Rio de la Plata, and the weather had begun to change. The warm, blue skies had been replaced by a low, grey overcast, and the sea had taken on a hard, metallic sheen. A long, uneasy swell was running from the south-east, a "Cape Horn greybeard" as Jorrocks called it, that lifted the ship with a slow, ponderous motion and then dropped her into the trough with a sickening lurch. "Don't like the look of it, Mister Leigh," he said one morning, squinting at the horizon, where a line of dirty-looking clouds was massed like a distant mountain range. "There's a wickedness in the air, you mark my words. The glass is falling like a stone, and the old ship's groaning in her timbers. We're in for a real blow, or I'm a Dutchman."

His prophecy proved all too correct. By noon, the wind had freshened to a strong gale, and the sea was running in great, white-capped mountains that threatened to overwhelm us. Captain Billings was on deck, his face grim, his oilskins glistening with spray. "All hands on deck!" he roared, his voice carrying above the howl of the wind. "Shorten sail! Get the skysails and royals in! Furl the topgallant sails! We're going to fight for it, lads!" The crew, who had been waiting for the call, sprang into action with a disciplined urgency that was a credit to their training. Under the direction of Mr Ohlsen, they swarmed up the rigging, their figures looking like small, black spiders against the raging sky, and fought to subdue the flogging canvas. It was a desperate, dangerous business, with the ship rolling so violently that the yard-arms dipped into the sea, but they worked with a grim determination, clinging to the wildly bucking yards like grim death.

I was on the poop-deck with the captain, my hands raw from hauling on the braces, my face stung by the flying spray. The sheer power of the storm was terrifying, yet exhilarating. This was the true test of a sailor, the moment when all the book-learning and chart-work was stripped away, and all that mattered was courage, skill, and the will to survive. The captain, seeing the strain on my face, gave me a curt nod of encouragement. "Stay sharp, Mister Leigh!" he shouted. "We'll need all our wits about us tonight!" The sun, a pale, watery disc, vanished below the horizon, and the world was plunged into a roaring darkness, lit only by the phosphorescent glare of the breaking waves. The wind rose to a hurricane pitch, its voice a continuous, deafening scream in the rigging. The _Esmeralda_ was now running under bare poles, her masts stripped of all but the storm staysails, and still she was driven through the water at a terrifying speed, a mere chip of wood at the mercy of the elements.

It was sometime during the middle watch, what must have been two bells or thereabouts, that disaster struck. He was right. I found myself becoming a part of the ship's company in a way I had not thought possible. The men would come to me with their small grievances, knowing that I had the captain's ear, and I would do my best to represent them fairly. I spent many an evening in the fo'c's'le, listening to Sails's cheery songs and Pat Doolan's endless stream of jokes and stories. The Irishman, true to Jorrocks's prediction, had taken a particular shine to me, and I was never without a pannikin of his excellent coffee when I stood the morning watch.

"Begorra, Mister Leigh," he would say, his eyes twinkling, "a fine broth of a boy like yourself needs to be kept warm. 'Tis a cold and cruel sea, but a hot cup o' joe makes it a bit more friendly, eh?"

Even Mr Ohlsen, the easy-going second mate, seemed to thaw towards me. He had always been civil, but now there was a new warmth in his manner. He would often ask my opinion on matters of seamanship, and he listened with genuine interest when I explained the principles of Sumner's method, which I was still perfecting under the captain's tutelage.

"You have a good head for figures, Mister Leigh," he said one day, after I had helped him work out a particularly tricky sight. "The old man is right. You will be a fine navigator one day."

The only sour note in this otherwise harmonious chorus was, of course, Mr Macdougall. The first mate had not forgotten his public humiliation, and his resentment festered like an untended wound. He was too cowed by the captain to openly defy me, but he found a thousand small ways to make my life difficult. He would assign me the most menial and unpleasant tasks, from cleaning the pigsty to tarring the rigging in the hot sun. He would find fault with my work, no matter how carefully I performed it, and he would watch me with a cold, malevolent eye, waiting for me to make a mistake.

I bore it all with as much patience as I could muster, remembering Jorrocks's advice to "sail along without a chance of a squall." I did my duty, and I did it well, giving him no just cause for complaint. I was polite and respectful, addressing him as "sir" and obeying his orders without question. But I would not "truckle under to him," as Jorrocks had put it. I would not pretend to admire him or seek his favour. I met his coldness with a cool professionalism of my own, and I knew that this, more than anything, enraged him. He wanted me to fear him, to crawl at his feet, and my refusal to do so was a constant, galling reminder of his own powerlessness.

This state of armed neutrality might have continued indefinitely, had it not been for a change in the weather. After the storm, we had enjoyed a week of fair winds and clear skies, bowling along at a good ten knots towards the equator. But then, as we entered the region known to sailors as the doldrums, the wind died away completely. The sea became a vast, shimmering sheet of glass, the sun a merciless eye in a brassy, cloudless sky. The sails, which had been our wings, now hung limp and useless from the yards, their occasional slatting in the long, oily swell the only sound in a world of profound and unsettling silence.

At first, the calm was a welcome respite. The crew took the opportunity to make repairs, mend sails, and generally put the ship to rights after the storm. There was a holiday atmosphere on board, a sense of well-earned rest. But as the days turned into a week, and the week threatened to become a fortnight, the mood began to sour. The heat was relentless, the inactivity galling. The tar melted in the seams of the deck, and the pitch bubbled from the timbers. Tempers grew short, and small irritations festered. The ship, which had been our home and our world, now felt like a prison.

It was in this tense and oppressive atmosphere that Mr Macdougall chose to make his move. He had been brooding for days, his face a thundercloud in the bright, unforgiving sunlight. He saw his chance, I believe, in the captain's growing anxiety. We were making no progress, and our water supplies, though ample, were not infinite. Captain Billings was on deck at all hours, scanning the horizon for any sign of a breeze, his face etched with worry. He had less time for his "dominie's" duties, and I was left more and more to my own devices.

The crisis came, as I had long suspected it would, over the ship's log. It was the captain's standing order that I should keep my own independent reckoning, a check on the official log kept by the first mate. Every day at noon, after taking our sights, we would present our calculations to the captain. Hitherto, our positions had been in close agreement, a fact that had clearly irked Macdougall. But now, in the tricky and uncertain currents of the doldrums, our figures had begun to diverge.

"Mister Leigh," the captain said one day, after comparing our two slates, "you have us a full ten miles to the east of Mr Macdougall's position. How do you account for that?"

"I have allowed for a westerly set of the current, sir," I explained, pointing to my calculations. "The surface drift is almost imperceptible, but I have observed it by watching the floating weed. And I have used Sumner's method, which gives a more accurate line of position in these latitudes."

"Sumner's method!" Macdougall sneered, his voice dripping with contempt. "Hoot, mon, that's just fancy book-larnin'. It's no' for practical sailors. A good dead reckoning is what you need, no' all this fiddle-faddle wi' lines and angles."

"Your dead reckoning seems to have led you astray, Mr Macdougall," the captain said, his voice dangerously quiet. "We have been becalmed for ten days. A ten-mile error in our position is not insignificant. It could mean the difference between catching the trade winds and being stuck here for another week."

"It's the lad's fault, Cap'en," Macdougall retorted, his face flushing. "He's puttin' ideas in your head. He's trying to make me look bad. He's been at it since the day he came aboard, wi' his sextant and his fancy talk. He's no sailor, he's a..."

"That's enough, Mr Macdougall," the captain said, his voice like a whip-crack. "I will not have you speak of Mr Leigh in that manner. He is a capable and diligent officer, and his calculations, to my mind, are sound. I have checked them myself. It is your log, sir, that is in question. It is sloppy, it is full of errors, and it shows a shocking ignorance of the first principles of navigation. You are the first mate of this ship, sir, but you navigate like a cabin boy!"

It was a brutal, devastating indictment, delivered in the presence of half the crew, who had gathered at the break of the poop to witness the confrontation. Macdougall stood as if turned to stone, his face a mask of disbelief and fury. The blood drained from his cheeks, leaving them a pasty, unhealthy white. His lips moved, but no sound came out. He had been exposed, stripped of his authority, and publicly shamed by the one man he was supposed to serve.

"From this day forward," the captain continued, his voice ringing with an authority that no one dared to question, "Mister Leigh will be in charge of the ship's navigation. You, Mr Macdougall, will confine yourself to the duties of a boatswain. You will see to the working of the ship, and you will obey Mr Leigh's orders as if they were my own. Do I make myself clear?"

"But... but, Cap'en..." Macdougall stammered, finding his voice at last. "He's just a lad! An apprentice! Ye canna do this!"

"I can, and I will," the captain said, his eyes blazing. "I am the master of this ship, and my word is law. Now, get out of my sight."

Macdougall stumbled away, his shoulders slumped in defeat. He did not look at me, but I felt the heat of his hatred like a physical blow. The crew, who had been watching in silence, now began to mutter amongst themselves. There were a few grins, and I saw Jorrocks give Pat Doolan a triumphant nudge. But there were other looks, too: looks of apprehension, of unease. They had seen a first mate broken, a command structure overturned. It was an unnatural state of affairs, and it made them nervous.

I, for my part, felt no triumph, only a cold sense of dread. I had won the battle, but I knew, with a terrible certainty, that I had not won the war. Macdougall was a proud and vengeful man, and he would not forgive this humiliation. I had made an enemy for life, and we were trapped together on a small ship in the middle of a vast and empty ocean.

That evening, as I was working on my charts in the relative cool of the cabin, Mr Ohlsen came to me. He stood for a moment in the doorway, a silent, hulking figure, and then he spoke, his voice a low rumble.

"You were right about the current, Mister Leigh," he said. "I took a sounding myself, with a deep-sea lead. There's a westerly set, just as you said. The old man was right to trust you."

"Thank you, Mr Ohlsen," I said, surprised by his visit. "I appreciate that."

"He's a dangerous man, Macdougall," he went on, ignoring my thanks. "He won't forget this. You'll need to watch your back."

"I know," I said, my voice grim.

"The men are with you," he said, after a pause. "Most of them, anyway. They know a real sailor when they see one. But they're scared, too. They don't like to see the chain of command broken."

"It was the captain's decision," I said. "Not mine."

"Aye," he said. "But you're the one who'll have to live with it. Be careful, Mister Leigh. That's all I'm saying."

He turned and left as silently as he had come, leaving me alone with my charts and my thoughts. His words, though meant as a warning, were also a comfort. They were the first crack in his easy-going neutrality, a sign of a nascent alliance. I was not entirely alone, after all.

The next few days were the most difficult of my life. I was in a position of authority, yet I felt more vulnerable than ever before. I was the de facto first mate, responsible for the navigation and the daily running of the ship, yet I was still, in the ship's articles, a first-class apprentice. It was an anomaly, a contradiction, and it made for a tense and uneasy command.

Macdougall, true to form, was a model of sullen, resentful obedience. He did his work, but he did it with a black look and a muttered curse. He never spoke to me unless absolutely necessary, and then only in monosyllables. He was a constant, brooding presence on the deck, a reminder of the fragility of my new-found authority. His allies, the flabby Welsh steward and a handful of older hands who resented my youth and the captain's favour, followed his lead, creating a small but potent faction of opposition.

But the rest of the crew, led by Jorrocks, Pat Doolan, and the now openly supportive Mr Ohlsen, rallied around me. They obeyed my orders with a cheerful alacrity, they sought my opinion, and they treated me with a respect that went beyond the mere demands of duty. They were determined, I think, to make the captain's decision work, to prove that they could sail a ship without a bully and a tyrant on the poop deck.

And so we drifted, a small, divided kingdom on a sea of glass, waiting for the wind that would release us from our purgatory. And as we waited, I learned the true meaning of command. It was not about sextants and calculations, or about the right to dine at the captain's table. It was about earning the trust of the men you led, about holding a steady course in the face of resentment and doubt, and about watching your back, always, for the storm that was sure to come. I had faced the fury of the ocean and survived. Now, I had to face the fury of a broken man, and I knew, with a chilling certainty, that it would be the greater test.
