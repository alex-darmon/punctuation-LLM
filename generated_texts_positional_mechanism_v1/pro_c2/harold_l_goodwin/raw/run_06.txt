CHAPTER V
The Song of the Cove

Mr. Abernathy, the old harbormaster, had no trouble remembering the new fellow who'd been asking about the tides. He squinted out at the bay from his shack at the end of the pier, his face a roadmap of wrinkles from a life spent in the sun and salt spray.

"Calls himself a birdwatcher," the old man rasped, "but he keeps his binoculars pointed at the old light more than any gull."

"So there's a definite interest," Leo said, mostly to himself. He and Sam had come down to the docks after school, hoping for a lead. They got one. The strange newcomer, a man named Mr. Graves, was staying at the Sea Spray Motel, the only lodging in their small town.

Leo questioned the harbormaster about the eerie singing that sometimes echoed from the abandoned lighthouse in the cove. "You've heard it more than anyone, Mr. Abernathy. What's it really like?"

"Like a woman cryin', far away," the man said. "Heard it five or six times this month already. But it was strange, the night you boys were out there with your flashlights."

"What was strange?" Sam asked, leaning against a piling.

"It was just the one sound."

"It's always just one sound," Leo pointed out.

"Nope. Not always. Most nights, there's a clicking sound that goes with it. Comes right after the high note. Only heard it alone that once, the night you boys went scramblin' up the rocks."

This was a new piece of the puzzle. Leo and Sam exchanged a puzzled look.

"You've heard this clicking before?" Sam asked.

"Sure have. Heard it clear across the water. Like someone tappin' on glass. The song will go up, then right after, you hear it. Click, click. Like it's talkin' back."

"Where does the clicking come from?" Leo pressed.

"Couldn't say. The sound plays tricks out there. Me and strange noises, we keep a healthy distance."

Leo processed the information. "Does the singing happen at a set time?"

"Comes with the high tide, mostly. Just after dark."

Leo turned to Sam. "What do you think?"

"Nothing," Sam admitted. "Not a thing. A crying sound and now a clicking sound? What are we supposed to do with that?"

"It's another piece," Leo insisted. "The clicking is another piece. You think maybe the birdwatcher knows about it?"

"Oh, he knows about the song, all right," Abernathy put in, his statement deflating Leo’s line of thought. "I was down here two nights ago when he came askin' about it. He heard it plain as day, just like me." The harbormaster paused. "Said it was the wind goin' through the lantern housing. Sounded real sure of himself."

Leo looked at Sam, his frustration showing. "Thanks, Mr. Abernathy," he said glumly. "You've given us a lot to think about."

The boys walked back along the pier, the smell of brine and diesel thick in the air. There was no doubting what the old man had heard, only his way of seeing it. Clearly, Leo thought, there were two distinct sounds on most nights. But what were they, really?

"Don't get too down," Sam said. "Graves hearing the song doesn't prove anything. Maybe he was just establishing his cover story. Saying it's the wind is the perfect non answer."

"What about the clicking?" Leo wondered aloud.

"Maybe that's what we should be looking for. Not the source of the song, but the source of the click."

"We'll stick to our plan for tonight," Leo decided. "It's the only thing we have. If the sound happens at high tide, we need to be there before it starts."

By the time the sun had dropped below the horizon, painting the sky in streaks of orange and purple, the boys were in position. They had found a spot in the tall sea grass on the cliff opposite the lighthouse, a place that gave them a perfect view of the aging tower and the rocks below. A cool wind whipped in off the ocean, and they pulled their jackets tight. They lay flat on the ground, their binoculars ready.

The tide was still coming in, its waves crashing with a rhythmic roar that was the only sound in the growing dark. During a quiet moment between waves, Sam whispered, "You think he's already in there? We didn't see anyone go in."

"I don't know. He could have gone in this afternoon. But he'd need equipment, and we would have seen him carrying something big. There's no way he could hide a generator or a big speaker."

Leo had to agree. It made no sense to bring equipment in during broad daylight if you wanted to be secretive. The last of the light faded. It was just past eight thirty. High tide was in twenty minutes. Leo felt his theory about the source of the noise was the only logical explanation.

"Any sound has to have a source," he had explained to Sam on the walk over. "A disembodied voice isn't possible. It has to be a recording played on a speaker, or it has to be mechanical. Something caused by the wind or the water."

"A ghost machine," Sam had said. "But where is it?"

That, Leo knew, was the question that mattered. "We just have to watch. And listen for the click."

"Both ears," Sam corrected.

It was ten minutes to high tide when they saw it. Not a light in the lighthouse, but a flicker from the rocks near the water's edge, far below the tower's foundation. It was a tiny pinpoint of light, like from a small flashlight, and it blinked twice.

"On your toes," Leo whispered, his heart starting to pound. "That's not the song."

Sam trained his binoculars on the spot. "I see it. Someone's down there!"

Leo swallowed hard. In spite of his belief in a logical explanation, the thought of someone hiding in the dark on those treacherous rocks was unsettling. But, he told himself, Sam was right. This was a real clue, a tangible event they could investigate.

"Wait until the song starts," Leo whispered. "He's probably setting up."

A voice from the path behind them called out, "You boys shouldn't be here after dark! It isn't safe."

They turned in surprise, and in that instant, a low, mournful note began to drift from the lighthouse, seeming to rise out of the very stone of the tower.

Leo spun around, his heart leaping into his throat. A stooped figure stood on the path not ten feet away, a heavy wool coat pulled tight against the wind. In the gloom, Leo could just make out a weathered face and a shock of white hair. It was Mr. Albright, the old lighthouse keeper who now lived in a small cottage a mile down the shore.

"Mr. Albright," Leo said, his voice a mixture of relief and frustration.

"You know you shouldn't be up here," the old man said, his voice raspy but firm. He took a step closer, peering at them. "It's near high tide. That's when she sings."

As if on cue, the mournful sound intensified. It wasn't a song with words, but a single, resonant note that seemed to vibrate in their very bones. It rose and fell in a slow, sad cadence, a sound of immense loneliness that echoed the vast, empty ocean. Leo’s carefully constructed theories felt flimsy in the face of the sheer strangeness of the noise. It did seem to come from everywhere at once, from the stone of the tower, from the air, from the sea itself.

"Get on home now, boys," Albright urged. "This is no place for the living when she's calling for her lost sailor."

"We're just watching the tide," Sam said quickly, a poor excuse that sounded hollow even to his own ears.

Leo's mind was racing. He was torn between the need to placate the old man and the desperate urge to turn back to the rocks. The light. Had the person down there heard Mr. Albright too? Were they scrambling away in the dark? He had to look.

He turned his head, pretending to scan the ocean. He raised his binoculars, not to the tower, but down to the churning water at its base. The spot where they had seen the light was empty. There was nothing but black, jagged rocks and the white foam of crashing waves. A perfect opportunity, lost.

"It's a beautiful sound, in a way," Leo said, trying to keep the old keeper engaged while his mind processed the disappointment. "Where do you think it comes from?"

Albright shook his head, a deep sigh escaping his lips. "It doesn't come from anywhere you can point to, son. It comes from sorrow. From the night Captain Weymouth's ship went down in the great storm of '08. His bride was waiting for him up in that tower, watching. They say she still waits. That song is her crying for him."

The sound began to change, the single note splitting into a strange, discordant harmony before slowly, gradually, fading away into the roar of the surf. After a full minute of silence, the only sound was the wind and the waves. The performance was over.

"She's finished for tonight," Albright said with an air of finality. "Now, off with you. The cliffs are treacherous in the dark." He gave them a final, concerned look, then turned and began shuffling back down the path, a hunched silhouette against the faint starlight.

Leo and Sam waited until he was gone, the crunch of his boots on the gravel path fading into the distance.

"Well, that was helpful," Sam said, his voice laced with sarcasm.

"It was, actually," Leo countered, his mind already piecing things together. He lowered his binoculars. "Don't you see? He was a distraction."

"A distraction? He's an old man who believes in ghosts."

"I don't mean he was in on it," Leo explained, his excitement returning. "But his arrival was a perfect distraction. While we were talking to him, our friend on the rocks had plenty of time to pack up and slip away."

"If there was anyone there," Sam said dubiously. "Maybe we just saw a reflection."

"It blinked, Sam. Twice. That wasn't a reflection. Someone was down there, and they were signaling. They signaled, and a minute later the song started." Leo paused, letting the pieces fall into place in his mind. "It's a two man job. One person on the rocks to give the signal that the coast is clear, and one person inside the lighthouse to operate the machine."

"The ghost machine," Sam murmured. "Okay, I'll buy that. But the lighthouse is locked up tighter than a drum. And how did the guy on the rocks get away so fast? It's all sharp angles and slippery seaweed down there."

These were the questions that mattered. Leo stared at the dark, silent tower. "That's what we find out tomorrow. We'll come back at low tide. We're going to search those rocks from top to bottom. If someone was climbing around there, they must have left a sign."

"And what about the tower?"

"We'll worry about the tower after we've checked the rocks," Leo said. "One clue at a time."

They made their way back in silence, the old keeper's story about the captain's bride echoing in Leo's thoughts. It was a good story, the kind that grew over time to fit the mystery. But Leo didn't believe in crying ghosts. He believed in people. And people always had a reason for what they did. He was determined to find it.

The next morning dawned clear and bright. The tide was at its lowest point just after ten o'clock, exposing a wide skirt of rock and tidal pools around the base of the lighthouse that was usually hidden by the sea. The boys met at the start of the cliff path, their sneakers swapped for sturdy hiking boots.

"Ready to go rock hopping?" Sam asked, adjusting the small backpack he wore.

"Ready as I'll ever be," Leo replied. "Did you bring the chalk?"

Sam nodded, patting his pack. "And a camera. Just in case we find Bigfoot's footprint."

They scrambled down a steep, narrow trail that was more of a goat path than a walkway. At the bottom, the air was cool and smelled of salt and drying seaweed. The rocks were slick with green algae, and they had to pick their way carefully.

"The light was somewhere over there," Leo said, pointing to a jumble of large, dark boulders directly beneath the east face of the tower. "Just above the high tide line."

They spent the better part of an hour searching. They examined every crevice, peered under every overhang, and ran their hands over surfaces that seemed too smooth or oddly shaped. They found starfish, colonies of mussels, and a couple of angry crabs, but no sign of human activity.

"Maybe you were dreaming," Sam said, straightening up from his inspection of a deep fissure. "There's nothing here. No footprints, no dropped flashlight, nothing."

"People don't leave footprints on solid rock," Leo said stubbornly. "But they might leave something else. A scuff mark. A broken bit of gear. Anything." He stood on a high point, scanning the entire area. From this angle, the base of the lighthouse looked different. The masonry was old, made of massive granite blocks that had been weathered by more than a century of storms. He saw something that made him pause.

"Sam, come here."

Sam clambered over, following Leo's pointing finger. "What is it?"

"Look there," Leo said. "On that big, flat rock just below the foundation. See it?"

Sam squinted. "It's a metal ring. So what? Probably for tying up boats a hundred years ago."

"No," Leo said, his voice tight with excitement. "Look closer. It's not old and rusted like the other mooring rings we've seen down at the harbor. That thing is new. It's stainless steel. And it looks like a modern climbing anchor."

They carefully made their way over to the rock. The anchor was expertly placed, drilled deep into the granite and sealed with epoxy. It was clean and new, but set in a shadowed nook where it would be all but invisible unless you knew exactly where to look.

"You don't put one of these in to tie up a rowboat," Sam said, his skepticism finally melting away. "Someone's been climbing here. Recently."

"And not from the top down," Leo added, his gaze drifting upward along the stone wall of the tower. "They're coming from the water up. But why? Why not just use the door?"

"Because the door is locked and they don't have a key?" Sam suggested.

"Or because they don't want anyone to see them coming and going." Leo knelt and examined the rock around the anchor. He ran his fingers over the surface. "There's another one. A foothold, carved right into the stone." He pointed to a small, man-made indentation about four feet above the anchor. "This is a climbing route. Our man on the rocks wasn't signaling. He was the same guy who goes in the tower. He moors a boat, climbs up to this point, and waits for a signal, or waits until a certain time. Last night we saw his flashlight just before he started his climb up the wall."

"Up that wall?" Sam looked up the sheer, ninety-foot face of the lighthouse. "He'd have to be a spider."

"Or a very good climber," Leo corrected. "But where does he climb to? There are no windows for the first fifty feet."

They followed the invisible line of the climb with their eyes. About halfway up the tower, a narrow, decorative ledge of stone circled the structure. Above that, the first of the tower's small, square windows was visible.

"The ledge," Leo said. "He must climb to that ledge. From there, he can get to the window."

"That's insane," Sam breathed. "One slip and he's finished."

"It's risky," Leo agreed. "Which means whatever he's doing in there is very, very important to him." He stood up and brushed the grit from his knees. "Now we know how he gets in. The next question is, what is he doing?"

"And how do we get in to find out?" Sam asked.

That was the problem. They couldn't very well follow the same perilous climbing route. They needed a key, or another way in.

As they were discussing their options, a voice hailed them from the top of the cliff. "Findin' any treasures, boys?"

They looked up. Mr. Albright was standing on the path, his hands cupped around his mouth. With him was another man, taller and dressed in expensive looking outdoor gear. The man was holding a fancy camera with a long lens.

"Just exploring, Mr. Albright," Leo called back.

"Be careful on that seaweed!" the old man shouted. "Slicker than a greased pig!"

The two men on the cliff talked for another minute, then the stranger raised his camera and pointed it not at the ocean, but directly at the lighthouse, snapping a series of pictures of the tower's base. Right where Leo and Sam were standing.

"Who's that with him?" Sam asked in a low voice.

"I don't know," Leo said, feeling a prickle of suspicion. "But I don't like him taking our picture."

They decided to climb back up. When they reached the path, Mr. Albright was alone.

"Told you to be careful," he said, though his eyes were twinkling. "That was Mr. Blackwood. Silas Blackwood. He's a historian, writin' a book about the lighthouses of the Eastern Seaboard. Asks a lot of questions."

"He seemed interested in the tower," Leo said, trying to sound casual.

"Oh, he's fascinated by it," Albright confirmed. "Especially the legend. He's been all over town talking to folks about it. Paid me twenty dollars just to tell him the story of Captain Weymouth and his bride." The old keeper chuckled. "Seems a funny way to make a living, paying for stories everyone knows for free."

"Did he ask about getting inside?" Sam inquired.

Albright's expression sobered. "He did. Wanted to know if I still had the key. I told him the same thing I'll tell you. No one goes in that tower. The town council locked it up for good in '82 after some kids broke in and almost fell from the top. It's not safe. And besides," he lowered his voice, "it's her place now. Best to leave her be."

The old man bid them good day and continued on his way, leaving the boys alone with their thoughts.

"A historian," Sam said. "You think he's our climber?"

"He's fit enough," Leo mused. "And he's very interested in the lighthouse. It's possible. A historian wouldn't need a sound machine to research a book, though."

"Maybe the book is a cover story," Sam suggested. "Maybe he's a treasure hunter. The Siren's song is a warning to keep people away while he searches for Captain Weymouth's lost gold."

Leo shook his head. "The story is about a lost ship, not lost gold. And if Blackwood is our man, he's taking a big risk. People are starting to notice him. He's not being very secretive."

They started walking back toward town. Leo was quiet, his mind a whirl of anchors, climbing routes, historians, and ghost stories. There had to be a way into that tower. Mr. Albright had said the town council locked the main door. But lighthouses were old. They had secrets.

"Mr. Albright said he was the keeper," Leo said suddenly, stopping in the middle of the path. "He must have lived there, or spent most of his time there."

"Sure," Sam said. "For thirty years, he told my dad once."

"A keeper would know everything about his lighthouse," Leo continued, thinking aloud. "Every loose stone, every secret nook. The official entrance isn't the only way in and out of a place like that. There had to be other ways. An emergency exit. A supply tunnel from an old dock."

"A supply tunnel?" Sam's eyes lit up. "You think there's a secret tunnel?"

"It's a long shot," Leo admitted. "But old structures built on the coast often had ways to get supplies in during bad weather when the main dock was unusable. A lower entrance, maybe one that's hidden or has been sealed up for years. Mr. Albright wouldn't tell us about it because he wants to keep people out. But it might still be there."

A new plan began to form, a risky but exciting idea. They didn't need to climb the sheer face of the tower. They just needed to think like a lighthouse keeper from a hundred years ago.

"Tomorrow," Leo said, his voice firm with new resolve. "We're not searching the rocks for climbing anchors. We're searching them for a door."

It was exactly as he remembered it, a vaguely human shape that was not quite solid, its edges blurring into the night. It hovered, motionless, a silent sentinel of cold light. The sigh from the crowd had died, replaced by a deep, reverent hush. No one moved. No one spoke. They simply stared, a field of statues turned toward the impossible apparition.

Rick felt a surge of frustration. They had looked away for a mere second, and in that second, the ghost had materialized from nothing. His theory of a projector beam striking mist was still plausible, but to have missed the crucial moment of its activation was galling.

"Rick," Scotty whispered, his voice tight. "The plan."

Rick nodded, his jaw set. The plan was reckless. The plan was to charge right into it, to force the issue and see what happened when human flesh met spectral energy. It was a test of their core assumption that this was a trick.

"Now," Rick breathed.

They moved as one, breaking from their spot and dashing toward the pool.

"Hey! Are you boys crazy?" the same well-wishing voice called from behind them, this time filled with alarm.

They ignored it. Their sneakers crunched on the gravel path as they closed the distance. The shimmering blue figure grew larger, more imposing. It didn't react to their approach. It simply hung there, an object of ethereal beauty and menace. The air grew colder, and the familiar dampness of the mist began to cling to their skin.

Five more feet. Three.

Then they were on it, or rather, in it.

There was no impact. There was only the sudden, shocking plunge into a cloud of clammy, cold vapor. The blue light was all around them, a diffuse, sourceless glow that made the hairs on Rick's arms tingle. It was like running through a fogbank that had been electrified. He could see Scotty a pace to his left, a dark silhouette moving through the blue haze.

Then, as suddenly as it had appeared, the light winked out.

One moment they were inside a blue cloud, the next they were standing in simple darkness, the damp mist swirling around their knees, smelling faintly of the wet earth and something else, something sharp and chemical.

A collective gasp went up from the Sons of the Old Dominion. It was followed by a murmur of angry, confused voices.

"They scared it off!" someone shouted.

"What did they do?" another voice demanded.

Rick and Scotty stood their ground, panting slightly, turning to face the crowd. Flashlight beams now cut through the darkness, pinning them. Out of the group of men, one stepped forward. He was tall and silver-haired, with a stern but not unkind face. He wore a linen jacket that marked him as a man of some importance.

"What was the meaning of that, sons?" he asked, his voice calm but carrying an edge of authority. "You've interfered with something that you don't understand."

Rick's mind raced. He couldn't very well announce that they were trying to debunk their sacred local ghost. "We're sorry, sir," he said, trying to sound both respectful and a little foolish. "We just... we wanted to get a closer look. We didn't mean to make it go away."

The man studied them for a long moment, his eyes moving from Rick to Scotty and back again. "The spirit of the mine appears when it chooses, and leaves when it chooses," he said slowly. "But I would advise you not to tempt fate again. This is a place of sorrow, not a playground."

"Yes, sir," Scotty said meekly.

The man gave a curt nod and turned back to his fellows. The spell was broken. The annual feast and its ghostly finale were over. Men began talking in low tones, gathering their things and heading back toward their cars, their conversations a buzz of speculation about what the boys' actions had meant.

Rick and Scotty waited, making themselves as inconspicuous as possible. They watched as the last car pulled away, its red taillights disappearing down the dark road. Finally, they were alone with the chirping of the crickets and the gentle sigh of the wind in the trees. The mine entrance was a patch of deeper blackness in the night.

"Well," Scotty said, breaking the silence. "That was something."

"It was a bust," Rick said in frustration, pulling out his own powerful flashlight. He swept its beam over the area. "We proved it was an illusion we could run through, but we didn't see how it was made. And we've probably made the locals mad at us."

"We're not trying to win a popularity contest," Scotty reminded him. "We're trying to solve a mystery. Let's look around before our friend the ghost producer comes back to collect his equipment."

"My spook projector theory is looking a little weak," Rick admitted. He directed his light beam across the clearing, playing it over the opposite bank, the trees, the rock face above the mine. "There's no logical place to hide a projector big enough to create an image that size. Not without it being obvious."

They began a systematic search. Scotty took the area around the pool, while Rick carefully hoisted himself up onto the wide rock shelf directly over the mine entrance, the very spot where the apparition had hovered. The rock was cool and damp from the mist. He crawled on his hands and knees, his flashlight beam probing every crack and crevice.

It was just bare stone, worn smooth by centuries of weather. He was about to give up when his light caught a tiny glint of reflected metal.

Wedged deep in a narrow fissure was a small, flat disc, no bigger than a quarter. It was made of a dull gray metal. He worked it loose with the tip of his pocketknife. It was surprisingly heavy for its size. There were no markings on it.

"Scotty, come look at this," he called down.

As he stood up, a peculiar odor reached him. It was the same sharp, chemical smell he had noticed in the mist, but stronger here. It was a clean, almost sterile smell, like a hospital room after it had been zapped with a disinfecting lamp.

"Ozone," Rick muttered to himself. That meant a high-voltage electrical discharge.

Scotty scrambled up the rocks to join him. "What did you find?"

Rick held out the metal disc. "This. And there's a smell up here. Like ozone."

Scotty took the disc, turning it over in his fingers. "Doesn't look like much. Part of a flashlight?"

"Too heavy," Rick said. "And the smell. An optical projection wouldn't create ozone. This was something else. An electrical effect, maybe. Something that ionizes the air, or the water vapor." He took a deep breath. "The mist. It wasn't just a screen for a projection. The mist itself was part of the reaction."

This new idea shifted everything. If the ghost was an electrical phenomenon created in the mist, it would explain the tingling sensation and the ozone smell. It would also require some serious equipment. Equipment that needed to be brought to the site.

"The wagon," Rick said suddenly, his voice filled with excitement.

"What wagon?" Scotty asked, confused.

"Belsely's ghost wagon! He saw the ghost's 'men' pulling a cart. He thought they were collecting dead soldiers. They weren't! They were hauling gear!"

Scotty's eyes widened. "Of course! A generator, maybe. Batteries. Whatever machine makes this happen. They'd need a cart to move it through the fields without leaving tire tracks from a truck."

"And they didn't use the road tonight because they knew we were watching it," Rick continued, the pieces clicking into place. "They came through the fields, set up their equipment, put on the show, and right now they're probably packing it all up again."

"And what about the searching?" Scotty asked. "Belsely said they'd walk, then stop and bend over, like they were searching for something."

Rick pondered that as he carefully pocketed the metal disc. "I don't know. But the fields are the key. That's where the real activity is. The ghost at the mine is just a distraction. A brilliant one. It gets everyone looking in one direction while the real work is being done somewhere else."

"So what do we do?" Scotty asked. "Charge into the cornfield and hope we don't run into three guys and a ghost?"

"Not tonight," Rick decided. "We're not prepared for that. We'll go back at first light. We'll look for the tracks from their cart. We'll look for whatever they were searching for. There has to be some sign."

They climbed down from the rock shelf and started the long walk back up the road toward their airplane. The night was quiet again, giving no hint of the strange events that had transpired.

"The Frostola man still fits," Scotty said after a while. "He's the front. The scout. He moves around the area, selling ice cream, but he's really gathering information. Checking sight lines, seeing who's around, reporting back to his partners."

"And Belsely putting him at the girls' picnic proves he's interested," Rick agreed. "He wanted to see the show himself, to make sure the effect was working." Rick's frustration from earlier was gone, replaced by the familiar thrill of a coherent theory taking shape. "Our plan to watch the road was too simple. They're smarter than that."

"So, a new plan," Scotty stated.

"A new plan," Rick confirmed. "Two parts. First, at dawn, we search the cornfield on top of the mine hill. We look for any trace of their activity. Second, we get more direct with the Frostola man. No more waiting for him to come to us. We find him. We find out where he goes when he's not on his route."

By the time they reached the field where their plane was parked, the eastern sky was already showing the first pale gray wash of approaching dawn. They were bone-tired, but sleep was the last thing on their minds. Inside the cabin of the plane, Rick switched on a small lamp and carefully examined the metal disc under a magnifying glass. It remained an enigma, a simple piece of dense, unfamiliar metal alloy.

"Whatever it is, it's part of their machine," he said, wrapping it carefully in a handkerchief and stowing it in his gear. "And they might be missing it."

They spent the next hour preparing. They filled a canteen, packed their flashlights with fresh batteries, and grabbed a pair of binoculars. As the sun finally broke over the horizon, casting long shadows across the Virginia countryside, they set out for the mine hill.

They avoided the road, cutting across a dew-soaked pasture and approaching the hill from the rear. The corn was high, easily over their heads, creating a dense, rustling maze.

"This is going to be like looking for a needle in a haystack," Scotty commented, pushing a thick stalk aside.

"We're looking for a path, not a needle," Rick corrected him. "A cart and four men would leave a clear trail."

They entered the field at the edge farthest from the road and began walking in parallel lines, keeping about twenty feet apart. For nearly an hour, they found nothing but the uniform rows of corn and the soft earth between them. Rick was beginning to feel a familiar sense of discouragement when Scotty gave a low whistle.

Rick hurried over to his position. Scotty was pointing at the ground. There, nearly invisible unless you were looking for it, was a faint trail. It wasn't just a few broken stalks; it was a swath about four feet wide where the corn was subtly pushed aside and the ground was noticeably more compressed.

"The cart," Rick breathed. "This is it."

They began to follow the trail. It led them deeper into the field, winding its way toward the center of the hill, which Belsely had said was directly over the mine. The trail was not straight. It meandered, and at several points, it widened into areas where the ground was scuffed up, as though the cart had been stopped and several people had milled about.

"Look," Scotty said, pointing to one of these spots. "Just like Belsely said. They'd walk, then stop."

Rick knelt down, examining the ground closely. He could see numerous overlapping footprints. And something else. In the center of the scuffed area was a small, perfectly round hole, about an inch in diameter, driven deep into the earth. It looked like a hole made by a soil auger or a survey stake. He poked a twig into it. It went down at least two feet.

"They're not just searching," Rick said, his voice low. "They're taking samples. Or measurements."

They found three more such spots along the trail, each with its telltale hole. The trail finally ended near the crest of the hill, in a large, trampled clearing. The marks of the cart's wheels were clear here, as were the footprints of several men. It was obvious this was their staging area. From here, they would have a clear, though distant, view of the mine entrance below.

"They set up their gear here," Scotty surmised. "But why all the holes? Prospecting for gold?"

"The Spindrift mine was a copper mine, not gold," Rick said, thinking hard. "And it's been played out for a hundred years. No, they're looking for something else. Something specific. These aren't random holes. They're taking readings of some kind. Maybe magnetic. Or seismic." He stood up and scanned the area. "They're mapping what's under our feet. They're mapping the old mine tunnels."

The realization settled over them with the weight of cold fact. The Blue Ghost, the men in the fields, the cart, the ice-cream vendor, it was all an elaborate, spooky cover for a highly technical surveying operation.

"But why?" Scotty asked the million-dollar question. "Why map a worthless old mine?"

"Because maybe it isn't worthless," Rick replied. "Maybe there's something down there. Something the original miners didn't know about, or couldn't get to. And our 'ghosts' want it."

Their morning's work was done. They had the "what" and the "where." Now they needed the "who."

"Let's go find an ice-cream man," Rick said.

The boys hiked back to their plane, their minds buzzing with the new discoveries. The cornfield evidence had transformed the mystery from a simple haunting into a complex conspiracy. The ghost was a diversion, the fields a worksite.

"The question is, where do we find him?" Scotty asked as they reached the edge of the field where the Spindrift was parked. "He could be anywhere."

"Not anywhere," Rick corrected. "Belsely said he was renting a room from Jethro Collins. We start in town, at the real-estate office. Collins is the hub. Let's see if we can find one of the spokes."

They decided against using the plane. A flight into town would be too conspicuous, and they wanted the element of surprise. Instead, they took the dusty access road on foot until they reached the main highway, where they were lucky enough to catch a ride with a farmer heading to the feed store.

The town was a sleepy collection of brick buildings and white-painted storefronts, simmering in the late morning heat. Jethro Collins's real-estate office was easy to find, a neat, one-story building on the corner of Main and Oak. A sign in the window advertised "Properties for Sale and Rent."

There was no sign of the Frostola man's distinctive scooter.

"Maybe he's out selling ice cream," Scotty suggested.

"Maybe," Rick said, his eyes scanning the street. "Or maybe he's in his room. Let's see if we can talk to Collins first. Play it casual."

They walked in, the chime of a small bell announcing their arrival. The office was cool and smelled of old paper and furniture polish. A man in his late forties, with thinning hair slicked back and a neatly trimmed mustache, looked up from a large desk. He wore a crisp white shirt and a tie, despite the heat.

"Well, now. What can I do for you young gentlemen?" he asked, his voice smooth and professional. "Looking to buy a farm? A little early to be settling down, isn't it?" He chuckled at his own joke.

"No, sir," Rick said, approaching the desk. "We're just curious. We're staying out at the old Spindrift property."

Collins's smile tightened just a fraction. "Ah, yes. The boys with the airplane. I've heard about you. Causing quite a stir. I'm Jethro Collins." He extended a hand, and Rick and Scotty shook it in turn. His grip was firm and dry.

"Rick Brant, and this is my friend Scotty."

"A pleasure. So, what brings you to my humble establishment? Surely not the ghost." Again, the small, practiced chuckle.

"Actually, in a way, yes," Rick said, deciding a direct, if slightly misleading, approach was best. "We were talking to one of your tenants, the man who sells Frostola ice cream. He was telling us about the local legends."

Collins's eyes became watchful. "My tenant? Oh, you must mean Mr. Miller. A quiet fellow. Pays his rent on time. I wasn't aware he was an expert on local folklore."

"He's not," Scotty put in. "That's the thing. He didn't seem to know much at all, which we thought was funny for someone living here. We were hoping you could tell us where to find him. We had a few more questions."

"Questions about what?" The smoothness was still there, but an edge of steel had crept into his voice.

"About the mine," Rick said plainly. "He seemed interested in it, but didn't have any information. We thought, being a real-estate agent, you might have maps or geological surveys of the area. We're interested in the history."

It was a gamble, and Rick knew it. He was laying his cards on the table, face down.

Collins leaned back in his chair, tapping a perfectly manicured finger on his desk blotter. He studied them for a long moment. "Geological surveys? For a played-out copper mine? That's a rather specific hobby for two young men. Most boys your age are interested in baseball, or perhaps girls."

"We're interested in science," Rick stated.

"I see." Collins stood up and walked over to a large filing cabinet. "The Spindrift mine is worthless. Has been for a century. The tunnels are collapsed, flooded. It's a death trap. I advise everyone who asks to stay far away from it." He pulled open a drawer, making a show of shuffling through files. "As for Mr. Miller, he keeps to himself. He has a room in the back of my building. Access is around the side. If he's in, he's in. If he's not, he's probably out peddling his wares." He closed the drawer with a definitive click. "Now, as for surveys, I have nothing of the sort. Just property deeds and zoning maps. Nothing that would interest you."

His dismissal was polite but firm. He was showing them the door without asking them to leave.

"Thank you for your time, Mr. Collins," Rick said. "You've been very helpful."

"Any time, boys. And take my advice. Stick to flying your airplane and leave the ghost hunting to the professionals, whoever they may be." The smile was back, but it didn't reach his eyes.

Once outside in the glaring sun, Scotty let out a low whistle. "Wow. That guy is as slippery as a greased eel. You think he was telling the truth? About not having any maps?"

"Not a chance," Rick said, his gaze fixed on the side of the building where Collins had indicated the tenant's entrance was. "He was testing us, trying to find out how much we know. When I mentioned geological surveys, he froze. He knows exactly what's going on."

"So what now? Knock on Miller's door?"

"No," Rick decided. "That's what he expects us to do. And Miller will be ready for us. Let's do a little reconnaissance first."

They walked around the block, acting like tourists, eventually coming up the alley behind the real-estate office. A narrow flight of wooden stairs led up to a single door on the second floor. Below it, at ground level, was a small, grimy window that looked into what was probably a storage area. There was no sign of life.

"He's either not there, or he's the quietest man in Virginia," Scotty whispered.

"Let's wait," Rick said. "Across the street. We'll give it an hour."

They bought cold sodas at a general store and found a shady spot on a bench that gave them a clear view of both the front of Collins's office and the alley entrance. They talked about airplanes, about their home on Spindrift Island, about anything but the mystery, just in case they were being watched. The hour crawled by. Cars came and went. The town baker made a delivery. Nothing happened at the real-estate office.

Just as Rick was about to suggest giving up, a man emerged from the front door of Collins's office. It wasn't Collins. It was a burly man in work clothes. Rick sat up straight. It was one of the "mourners" from the fake funeral procession, the one who had been driving the hearse. He got into a dusty pickup truck parked down the street and drove off.

"Did you see that?" Scotty breathed.

"I saw him," Rick said, his mind racing. "Collins isn't just a real-estate agent with a suspicious tenant. He's holding meetings."

Ten minutes later, their patience was rewarded again. The Frostola man, Miller, came striding down the street, not on his scooter, but on foot. He was dressed in casual slacks and a polo shirt, not his vendor's uniform. He looked quickly up and down the street, his manner furtive, before ducking into the alley. A minute later, they heard the sound of the upstairs door closing.

"He's back," Scotty said. "Now's our chance."

"No. Still not right," Rick countered. "Let's change the plan. Collins is the key, not Miller. Miller's just the lookout. Collins is the coordinator. And his office is the headquarters. The information we want is in there."

"So we break in?" Scotty asked, his eyebrows raised.

"We don't have to," Rick said, a slow smile spreading across his face. "Collins gave me an idea. He said he advises people to stay away from the mine. He's established himself as the local authority on the property. So, we're going to ask for his professional help."

"I don't follow," Scotty admitted.

"We're two concerned young men who are worried about the safety of the mine on the property where our plane is parked. There are kids from a camp nearby. It's a public hazard. We want to ask the town's most prominent real-estate agent what can be done about officially sealing it off. We need to see the official property maps and records to determine who is responsible for it. We'll be so civic-minded he won't be able to refuse us."

They waited another twenty minutes for good measure, then walked back to the real-estate office, their expressions a careful blend of earnestness and concern. The bell chimed again.

Collins looked up, his annoyance barely concealed. "Boys. Back so soon? Did you get lost?"

"No, sir, Mr. Collins," Rick said respectfully. "We were thinking about what you said. About the mine being a death trap. And we agree."

Scotty picked up the thread. "There's a girls' camp just down the road. We've seen kids wandering near the mine entrance. If someone fell in that pool, or a rockslide happened..." he let the sentence hang.

"We feel it's our civic duty to see if something can be done," Rick finished. "Like putting up a permanent fence, or even using dynamite to seal the entrance for good."

Collins stared at them, his jaw tight. They had him. To refuse would be to seem utterly unconcerned with public safety, a bad look for a prominent citizen. To agree was to let them into the very records he had just denied existed.

"Dynamite?" he said faintly. "That seems a bit extreme."

"Maybe," Rick conceded. "But first, we'd need to know who legally owns the mine itself, and what the property lines are. We figured your office would have the official county plat maps. We'd be happy to do the research ourselves, if you'll just point us to the right file."

Checkmate. Collins knew it, and they knew he knew it. For a long moment, he seemed to weigh his options. Then, a slow, thin smile spread across his lips. It was not a pleasant sight.

"You boys are very... persistent," he said. "And you are correct. Public safety is paramount. Very well. The county records are in the back room. Come with me. Let's see what we can find about this 'worthless' old mine."

He led them through a door behind his desk into a large, cluttered room. It was filled with shelves groaning under the weight of binders, boxes of files, and rolled-up documents. In the center of the room was a large drafting table, and on it, unrolled and weighted down at the corners, was not a county plat map, but a highly detailed geological chart of the Spindrift Hill area. It was covered in notations and concentric lines, and a series of red X's formed a clear pattern leading from the cornfield down into the earth.

Collins saw them looking. The polite mask fell away completely. "You should have stuck to flying airplanes," he said, his voice flat and cold.

Before Rick or Scotty could react, the door to the alley swung open. The Frostola man, Miller, stood there. And behind him was the burly man from the pickup truck. They were not smiling.

"We have a problem," Collins said to them. "Our young scientists have taken their 'hobby' a little too far."

The burly man cracked his knuckles. "What do you want us to do with 'em, boss?"

Rick's mind raced, looking for an escape. There was none. The only way out was the door they'd come through, and Collins was standing near it. The back door was blocked by Miller and his large friend. The grimy window he had seen from the alley was their only hope, but it was small and blocked by a stack of old phone books.

"We'll have to hold them for a while," Collins said, more to himself than to the others. "Until tonight's work is finished. It's the final survey. After that, we won't need the ghost anymore." He looked at Rick and Scotty with something that might have been regret. "You wanted to see the geological survey. Here it is. The original copper vein was just a small offshoot. The main lode is deeper. A massive deposit of tungsten and other rare earth minerals, essential for electronics and guidance systems. Worth millions."

"So the ghost stories, the men in the fields, the cart... it was all to scare people away so you could conduct your survey in secret," Rick said, fitting the last piece of the puzzle into place.

"Precisely," Collins said. "The 'ghost' was a simple projection onto the mist, as I'm sure you figured out. The 'men in the fields' were my geologist and his assistant, taking core samples and magnetometer readings. The ice-cream man kept an eye on the comings and goings at the girls' camp. It was a perfect system. Until you two came along."

"You won't get away with this," Scotty said, his voice defiant.

Collins sighed. "The classic line. So disappointing. Lock them in the old cellar, Carl," he said to the burly man. "Give them some time to think about the foolishness of their curiosity. We'll decide what to do with them after we've retrieved the final data."

Carl grinned, a gap-toothed expression that was not reassuring. He and Miller advanced on the boys. Rick tensed, ready to fight, but knew it was hopeless. They were trapped. Carl grabbed Rick's arm in a grip of steel, while Miller moved toward Scotty. This was not a mystery anymore. It was a kidnapping. And as Carl shoved him toward a dark, descending staircase he hadn't noticed before, Rick knew their chances of seeing the sun again depended on something more than just scientific deduction. They were going to need a miracle.

Carl's grip was like a vise. He shoved Rick toward the dark opening in the floor, and Scotty was prodded along right behind him. The stairs were steep and wooden, groaning under their weight. A smell of damp earth and decay rose to meet them, a cold, dead air that had not been disturbed in years. At the bottom, a rough push sent Rick stumbling forward into blackness. Scotty landed beside him a moment later.

"Enjoy the quiet," Carl's voice echoed from the top of the stairs. The heavy wooden door slammed shut, the sound booming in the enclosed space. A moment later, the metallic scrape and thud of a heavy bar being dropped into place sealed their fate. The faint light from the office above was gone. They were in absolute, total darkness.

For a long moment, neither boy spoke. The only sound was their own breathing, harsh and loud in the suffocating silence. The cold of the dirt floor began to seep through Rick's jeans. This was no longer a game of clues and theories. This was real.

"Rick? You okay?" Scotty's voice was a low whisper, seeming to come from miles away.

"I'm here," Rick answered, his own voice tight. He forced himself to take a slow, steadying breath. Panic was an enemy they couldn't afford. "We're in a cellar. Probably under the main office."

"Some vacation," Scotty muttered. "Kidnapped by a phony ghost and a crooked real-estate agent. My folks are never going to let me leave the house again."

"We're not staying here long enough for them to find out," Rick said with more confidence than he felt. He began to move, shuffling his feet carefully, his hands outstretched. He touched a cold, rough stone wall. "First thing is to figure out the layout. Stay where you are so we don't bump into each other."

Rick followed the wall, his fingers tracing the gritty mortar between large, uneven fieldstones. The cellar was not large. He paced it off, his steps slow and deliberate. About fifteen feet by twenty, he estimated. The ceiling was low; he could touch the heavy wooden joists that supported the floor above by simply reaching up. He could hear the faint murmur of voices from the office, Collins and his men discussing their plans.

"It's a box," Rick reported after completing his circuit. "Four stone walls, dirt floor, wood ceiling. No windows that I can feel."

"So we wait for them to come back and decide what to do with us?" Scotty's tone was bleak. "I didn't like the look on that guy Carl's face. He doesn't seem like the 'let them go with a warning' type."

"No," Rick said firmly. "We don't wait. Collins said they were going back for the final survey tonight. That gives us a time limit. We have to get out before they come back."

He began a second, more thorough search of the walls, running his hands over every stone, every inch of mortar. He was looking for a weakness, a loose stone, anything. The construction was old but solid. Whoever had built this foundation had intended it to last.

"Anything?" Scotty asked from the center of the room.

"Nothing yet. It's solid as a rock. Literally." Rick's hands were scraped and covered in dirt. He moved to the next wall, his methodical search fueled by a growing sense of urgency. The muffled voices from above had stopped. He heard footsteps, then the sound of the office door opening and closing. They were leaving.

"They're gone," Scotty whispered. "They've gone to the mine."

"Then we have to hurry." Rick's fingers brushed against something different. Wood. He stopped and explored the area with his hands. It was a small, square section of the wall, framed with wood. A small door, or a boarded-up opening. It was low to the ground.

"Scotty, I've got something," he said, his voice alive with new energy. "Feels like a window, or maybe an old coal chute. It's been boarded up from the outside."

Scotty was beside him in an instant. "Can we get through it?"

Rick pushed against the boards. They were thick and held fast. "Not with our bare hands. The nails are probably rusted solid. We need a lever. Something to pry with."

The two of them dropped to their knees, sweeping their hands across the damp dirt of the floor. They searched in expanding circles, their fingers sifting through the loose earth, bits of rotted wood, and pebbles. It was a long shot, but old cellars were often used as dumping grounds for junk.

"Ow!" Scotty yelped quietly. "Found something sharp."

"What is it?"

"Hold on." There was a scraping sound, then the clink of metal against stone. "Feels like a piece of pipe. Or a crowbar. It's heavy." He grunted with effort. "It's stuck."

Rick joined him, and together they pulled. With a loud sucking sound, a long, slender object came free from the packed earth. It was about three feet long, cold and metallic. Rick's fingers identified a flattened, chisel-like end.

"It's an old pry bar," Rick said, a surge of triumph in his voice. "A lining bar. They use them for aligning railroad tracks or moving heavy stones. This is better than I could have hoped for."

"Let's hope it's strong enough," Scotty said.

They moved back to the boarded-up opening. Rick felt for a gap between the wooden frame and the first board. He worked the chisel end of the bar into the crack. "Okay," he whispered. "When I tell you, put all your weight on the end. We have to do it as quietly as we can. A sudden snap will make too much noise."

Scotty positioned himself. "Ready."

"Now."

They pushed down on the bar. The old iron groaned in protest. The wood creaked, a high, piercing sound that made them both freeze. They listened, their hearts pounding. Silence. There was no one in the building above. They were alone.

"Again," Rick breathed.

They heaved again. There was a sharp crack, and the sound of a rusted nail pulling free from the wood. It was working. They repeated the process on the other side of the board. It took them ten minutes of tense, sweating effort, their muscles straining in the darkness, but finally the first board came loose. A sliver of gray evening light sliced into the cellar, blinding them for a second. With the first board gone, the others were easier. They worked with a desperate, focused energy, prying and pulling until two more boards were torn away, creating an opening just large enough for them to squeeze through.

Rick peered out. They were looking into a narrow, weed-choked space between Collins's building and the one next to it. The opening was at ground level. Freedom was only a few feet away.

"I'll go first," Rick said. "If it's clear, I'll signal for you."

He wriggled his shoulders through the opening, scraping his back on the rough wood. He dropped the few inches to the ground outside, landing in a patch of stinging nettles. He ignored the burn, crouching low and peering around the edge of the building. The main street was visible, but it was nearly empty. The sun had set, and the sky was a deep twilight purple. There was no sign of Collins or his men.

He gave a low whistle. A moment later, Scotty squirmed through the opening, landing beside him.

"We did it," Scotty whispered, grinning in the dim light. "We actually did it."

"We're not done," Rick reminded him, his mind already racing ahead. "They went to the mine. Collins said it was the final survey. That means tonight's their last chance to operate in secret. And it's our last chance to stop them."

"How?" Scotty asked. "It's us against at least four of them, and one of them is the size of a small mountain. We go to the sheriff?"

"And say what?" Rick countered. "That the town's most prominent real-estate agent kidnapped us because he's using a fake ghost to cover up a secret mining survey? Collins would just deny it. He'd say we were trespassing and made the whole thing up. We have no proof. The survey map is in his office, and he has the projector."

Rick stood up, brushing the dirt and leaves from his clothes. He looked up the road, in the direction of the Belsely farm. An idea, desperate but logical, began to form in his mind.

"We need an ally," Rick said. "And I think I know who."

"Who?"

"Mr. Belsely."

Scotty looked skeptical. "The farmer who thinks ghosts are real? What's he going to do, scare them with a pitchfork?"

"He's the only other person who's seen Collins's men in the fields. He called them spirits, but he saw them. He described them searching the ground, pulling a cart. It all fits with a geological survey. And you saw him yourself. He's not a coward. He was scared, but he stayed and watched. He's got nerve."

"It's a long shot," Scotty said, but he didn't argue. He knew Rick was right. They had no other options.

They crept out of the narrow alleyway and, staying in the shadows, began to jog up the road toward the Belsely farm. The air was cool and carried the scent of mown hay and damp earth. Every passing headlight sent them diving for the ditch, their nerves raw. They didn't speak, saving their breath for the run.

It took them twenty minutes to reach the lane that led to Belsely's farmhouse. A single yellow light burned in a downstairs window. A dog barked from somewhere behind the house, a low, warning sound.

"This is it," Rick said, catching his breath. "Let me do the talking. We have to make him understand quickly."

They walked up the path and Rick knocked on the solid wood door. The barking grew more insistent. After a moment, they heard heavy footsteps inside. A bolt was drawn, and the door opened a few inches. Belsely's face, etched with suspicion, appeared in the gap. He was holding an old but well-cared-for shotgun.

"What do you boys want?" he asked, his voice a low rumble. "It's late."

"Mr. Belsely, we're in trouble," Rick said, speaking quickly. "We need your help. It's about the ghost."

Belsely's eyes narrowed. "I told you I don't mix with spirits."

"They're not spirits," Rick insisted. "They're men. You were right, they were looking for something in the fields. But it wasn't their dead. It was minerals. Tungsten. It's a scheme run by Jethro Collins."

The farmer stared at him, his expression unreadable. He looked from Rick's earnest, dirt-streaked face to Scotty's, then down at their torn and filthy clothes.

"You boys look like you've been pulled through a hedge backwards," he observed.

"We have, almost," Scotty put in. "They locked us in a cellar. We just escaped."

Rick quickly recounted everything: their discovery of the ice-cream man's connection to Collins, their search of the office, the survey map, and their capture. He explained how Belsely's own observations about the "spirits" in the field with a cart were the key. They weren't collecting the dead; they were collecting core samples.

When Rick finished, the farmer was silent for a long time, his gaze fixed on some distant point over Rick's shoulder. The shotgun was still held at the ready. Rick held his breath. Everything depended on this man's belief.

Finally, Belsely lowered the gun. "Collins," he said, the name tasting like something foul. "I never did trust that man. Too smooth. And he's been buying up mineral rights on worthless land all over the county for the past year. Said it was for a 'long-term development plan'. People thought he was crazy." He looked at Rick, and a new light of understanding, and anger, was in his eyes. "It wasn't crazy at all, was it? It was smart."

He pulled the door open wide. "Come in," he said. "We've got work to do."

The farmhouse kitchen was warm and smelled of coffee and pipe tobacco. Belsely gestured for them to sit at a sturdy oak table while he poured three mugs of coffee from a pot on the stove. He moved with a quiet, deliberate confidence that Rick found immensely reassuring.

"So Collins and his crew are at the mine now," Belsely said, sitting down across from them. "Finishin' their survey, you said. And they'll be usin' that Blue Ghost machine to keep folks away."

"That's right," Rick said. "The Sons of the Old Dominion are still there for their feast. The ghost is due to appear any minute now. Collins is counting on everyone being distracted."

Belsely took a sip of his coffee, his brow furrowed in thought. "The sheriff's a good man, but he's a friend of Collins. He won't move without proof you can hold in your hand. Your word against a man like Jethro Collins... it ain't enough."

"That's what I figured," Rick agreed. "We have to catch them in the act. With the projector and the survey equipment."

"And how do you propose we do that?" the farmer asked, his gaze steady. "You said yourself there are at least four of them."

"There are three of us," Scotty pointed out.

Belsely gave a short, dry chuckle. "Boy, I'm a farmer, not a lawman. But I don't like bein' made a fool of. And I don't like city slickers comin' here and trying to cheat my neighbors out of what's rightfully theirs." He stood up and walked over to a corner, where he picked up a heavy, coiled rope. "If we're going to do this, we're going to do it smart."

Rick's mind clicked into gear, energized by the coffee and the presence of a capable ally. "The projector," he said. "That's the key. When we were there before, I couldn't figure out where the beam was coming from. It has to be hidden somewhere near the mine entrance, with a clear line of sight to the mist over the pool."

"There's an old hunter's blind," Belsely said suddenly. "Up on the ridge, on the other side of the road from the mine entrance. Overlooks the whole area. It's covered in brush. No one's used it in years. Perfect place to hide a man and a machine."

"That has to be it," Rick exclaimed. "They have one man operating the projector from the blind, while Collins and the others get their last readings near the mine itself, under cover of the distraction."

A plan began to form, simple and daring. "Mr. Belsely, do you know a back way to that ridge? A way we can get there without being seen from the road?"

The farmer nodded. "There's a deer trail that runs up the back of the hill from my lower pasture. They'll never see us coming."

"Then here's the plan," Rick said, leaning forward. "Scotty and I will sneak up to the blind and take care of the projector operator. We need to disable the ghost. Mr. Belsely, you'll be our backup. But the most important thing is to get proof. Once we have the projector, we need to get it to the sheriff. And we need to catch Collins with his equipment."

Belsely considered it, his rugged face serious. "It's risky. If they catch you boys again..."

"They won't," Rick said, his voice hard. "This time, we know what we're up against."

The farmer gave a slow, deliberate nod. "All right. Let's go hunting."

He disappeared for a moment and came back with a powerful, heavy-beamed flashlight. He handed it to Scotty. "You might need this." To Rick, he handed the coil of rope. "Just in case."

As they slipped out the back door and into the deep darkness of the farm, Rick felt a renewed sense of hope. They weren't just two boys running for their lives anymore. They were a team, on their way to unmask a ghost and bring a clever criminal to justice. The final act was about to begin, right back where it all started, at the entrance to the old mine.

A collective gasp rose from the assembled Sons of the Old Dominion. The apparition drifted slowly, its indistinct form glowing with an unnatural blue light, a silent, terrifying sentinel.

Belsely nodded. "This way. Keep low and quiet. Sound carries on a still night like this."

He imagined Collins and his men down below, their heads snapping up, their ears trained on the dark ridge. But there was no answering shout, no sound of pursuit.
