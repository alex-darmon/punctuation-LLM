Abernathy was firm. "No. I won't have it. Two of you men," he said, gesturing to others in the crowd, "drive back to town and get Sheriff Miller. Tell him what's happened. Tell him Jethro Collins is mixed up in it. The rest of us will stand guard here and make sure they don't slip out."
It was a sensible plan, but Rick felt a familiar impatience. Every minute they waited was a minute Collins and his crew had to disappear into the labyrinth of tunnels. He caught Scotty's eye, and saw the same thought reflected there.
While the men organized themselves, sending a car speeding back toward town, the boys examined the situation more closely. The power cable was heavy-duty, insulated and thick. It ran from the wreckage of the projector straight into the mine, disappearing around the first bend.
"They must have a generator," Scotty whispered, keeping his voice low. "A big one, to power that projector."
"And lights," Rick added. "They wouldn't be working in the dark. That cable is our map. It has to lead right to their main base of operations."
They stood for a few minutes, watching the men post themselves near the entrance. They were brave enough, but they were townsmen, not trackers. They kept a respectful distance from the black opening, their conversation a low, excited murmur.
"They're not going to see anything from out here," Rick said decisively. "And the sheriff could take an hour. By then, Collins could be miles away underground, if there's another exit."
"What are you thinking?" Scotty asked, though he already knew.
"I'm thinking that cable is a road map, and we're the only ones willing to read it right now." Rick looked at his friend. "My arm is throbbing, but my legs work fine. How about you?"
"Ready when you are," Scotty said with a grim nod. "Just let me find something." He scanned the ledge with his flashlight and picked up the heavy length of pipe the thug had used to smash the projector. He hefted it. "This might come in handy."
They waited for a moment when the men's attention was focused on the road, listening for the sheriff's siren. Then, moving as one, the boys slipped past the outer ring of guards and into the chilling embrace of the mine.
The air changed instantly. The warm, humid night was replaced by a deep, damp cold that smelled of wet rock and centuries of stillness. The only sound was the drip, drip, drip of water from the ceiling, echoing in the profound silence. Their flashlight beams seemed weak, swallowed by the immense darkness just beyond their reach.
The floor was uneven, littered with loose rock and slick with mud in places. The heavy black cable was their only guide. They followed it cautiously, their sneakers making soft scraping sounds. Rick went first, his good hand holding the flashlight, his injured arm held protectively against his chest. Scotty followed close behind, the iron pipe held at the ready.
The tunnel twisted and turned. Several times, dark, narrow passages branched off into nothingness, but the cable always led them straight ahead. After about a hundred yards, they saw the first sign of the culprits. A discarded candy wrapper, the same brand the Frostola man sold from his cart, lay in the mud.
"Getting warmer," Scotty murmured, his voice sounding loud in the confined space.
A little farther on, the tunnel opened into a small cavern where three passages diverged. It was a potential dead end, but the cable was unerring. It snaked across the cavern floor and into the center tunnel, the largest of the three. Here, the signs of activity were more pronounced. The ground had been cleared of debris, and they could see the distinct tread of work boots in the damp earth.
Rick paused, listening. He thought he heard a faint, distant noise. A low hum.
"You hear that?" he whispered.
Scotty cocked his head. "Generator. Has to be. We're getting close."
They moved with even greater caution now, pressing themselves against the rock wall. The humming grew steadily louder, accompanied by another sound. Voices.
Rick motioned for Scotty to douse his light. He clicked his own off, and they were plunged into absolute, total blackness. For a moment, it was disorienting, but their eyes slowly adjusted, and a faint glow became visible from around the next bend in the tunnel.
The voices were clearer now, angry and frustrated.
"...should have just knocked them out and been done with it!" a rough voice growled. That must be the heavy, Rick thought.
"And have the whole town and the state police crawling all over us? Use your head, Lank," another voice snapped. It was Jethro Collins. "The whole point was to be discreet. Now the projector is smashed, and those boys saw us."
"The plan's shot, Jethro," a third voice whined. Rick recognized the slightly higher pitch of the ice-cream vendor. "The whole town knows. We should pack it in."
"Pack it in? With a quarter million dollars in gold coin almost in our grasp?" Collins retorted, his voice dripping with scorn. "I've spent two years on this. I found the diary, I bought the land, I researched the maps. We're not quitting now. Not when we're this close."
Rick and Scotty exchanged a look in the dim, reflected light. Gold coin! A quarter million dollars! The pieces suddenly fell into place. Belsely's stories about the ghosts on the battlefield, the wagon, the searching. They weren't looking for bodies. They were looking for a buried treasure.
"Close?" the vendor, Henderson, scoffed. "We've been digging and probing for three months, Jethro! We've turned over half that cornfield. Your map is worthless!"
"The map is not worthless!" Collins insisted. "The paymaster's diary said he buried the Union payroll chest 'within sight of the lone chimney stack, forty paces from the split rock.' We found the split rock. We've been searching in the wrong direction, that's all. We have to search on the other side."
"The sheriff will be here any minute," Lank grumbled. "We should be clearing out."
"Let them come," Collins said. "They won't find this chamber. And they'll never think to look for another way out. We'll wait them out, then go out the air shaft on the far side of the hill tomorrow night. Now, help me with this new survey grid."
Rick's mind was a whirlwind. A lost Civil War payroll. An alternate exit. They had to act now, before the sheriff arrived and Collins's crew slipped away.
He crept forward to the edge of the bend and peered around. The tunnel opened into a large, high-ceilinged cavern, brightly lit by a string of bare lightbulbs. In the center was a rough camp. Three cots, a table covered with topographical maps, and a chugging gasoline generator in the corner, its exhaust routed into a fissure in the rock. Collins, Henderson, and the big man named Lank were huddled over one of the maps.
Rick backed away slowly. "They have a whole setup in there," he whispered to Scotty. "And they're planning to leave through another exit."
"Three of them," Scotty said, gripping the iron pipe. "The odds aren't great."
"We don't have to fight them," Rick breathed, his brain working furiously. "We just have to stop them." His eyes scanned the tunnel around them. It was old and rough-hewn. Leaning against the wall were several rusted tools, a pickaxe, a couple of shovels, and a stack of rotting timbers. Beside them sat a derelict mine cart, its iron wheels fused to a short, disconnected section of track.
An idea, audacious and risky, began to form.
"The generator," Rick whispered. "It powers their lights. If we could cut the power..."
"They'd be in the dark," Scotty finished for him. "But so would we."
"We have flashlights. They probably do too, but it would take them a second. And in that second..." Rick pointed toward the mine cart. "That thing looks heavy."
Scotty's eyes followed his gesture. A slow grin spread across his face. "You want to block the tunnel."
"Exactly. They're in the main cavern. This is the only way back to the entrance. If we can get that cart across the opening while they're disoriented, we can trap them."
"How do we move it?" Scotty asked. "The wheels are rusted solid."
"We don't roll it. We tip it." Rick pointed to the pile of rotting timbers. "We can use one of those as a lever. It'll make a racket, so we'll need a diversion."
"I'll be the diversion," Scotty volunteered. "I can go back down the tunnel a little way and knock over those tools we saw. While they're looking that way, you get to the generator."
"It's too risky. They have Lank."
"He'll be expecting trouble from the mine entrance, not from inside. I'll be fast."
Rick hesitated. The plan was full of holes. But it was better than no plan at all. "Okay," he agreed. "Be careful. Give me thirty seconds after you make the noise. I'll cut the power cable to the lights. Don't cut the main cable from the generator, or we won't have any power to work with later if we need it."
They synchronized their watches. Scotty checked that his flashlight was working, then slipped back down the tunnel. Rick crept to the edge of the cavern entrance again, his heart pounding. He could see the wires running from the generator. A thick one, the main feed, and a smaller one that snaked up the wall to the string of lights. That was his target. He pulled a pocketknife from his jeans and opened the sharpest blade.
He counted the seconds. Ten. Fifteen. He saw Collins point to a spot on the map, Lank nodding. Twenty. Twenty-five.
Then came the crash. A tremendous, echoing clatter of metal on rock rang out from the tunnel behind them.
"What was that?" Henderson yelped, jumping back.
"It's the sheriff!" Lank growled, grabbing a heavy wrench from the table.
"Stay here!" Collins commanded. "It could be those kids again. Lank, go see."
The big man started toward the tunnel entrance, his boots crunching on the gravel. This was Rick's chance. He darted from his hiding place, keeping low. The generator chugged away, masking the sound of his footsteps. He reached the wall, located the thinner wire, and began sawing at it with his knife. The rubber was tough. He put all his weight into it.
Sparks flew as the blade cut through the copper. The string of lights flickered once, twice, and died. The cavern was plunged into absolute, impenetrable blackness.
A chorus of confused and angry shouts erupted from the three men. "The lights! What happened to the lights?"
"My flashlight!" Collins yelled. "Where did I put it?"
Rick didn't wait. He clicked on his own flashlight, its beam a lonely sword cutting through the dark. "Scotty! Now!" he yelled.
Scotty's light appeared at his side. "They're trying to find their lights!"
The two boys scrambled to the mine cart. It was heavy, made of solid oak and iron. "The timber!" Rick grunted, shoving one of the rotting beams under the cart's edge. "Together! On three! One, two, three!"
They threw their combined weight on the end of the timber. There was a groaning, splintering sound, and the rusted wheels screeched in protest. The cart lifted, tilted, and then crashed over onto its side with a deafening boom, completely blocking the narrowest part of the tunnel. Dust and grit filled the air.
From the cavern came a roar of fury. "They've blocked the tunnel! The other way! The air shaft!"
"We have to stop them!" Scotty said, but Rick held up a hand.
"No. Let them go," Rick said, breathing heavily. "We know where they're going. And they don't know that we know." He pointed his flashlight at the maps still spread out on the table inside the now-barricaded cavern. "Their escape route is on that map."
Just then, a new sound reached them from far down the main tunnel. A series of authoritative shouts.
"This is Sheriff Miller! Is anyone in there?"
Relief washed over Rick, so potent it almost buckled his knees. "In here, Sheriff!" he yelled back, his voice cracking. "We're in here! And we've got them trapped!"

Flashlight beams danced in the darkness of the main tunnel, growing stronger. Two uniformed deputies appeared behind Sheriff Miller, their expressions a mixture of grim purpose and surprise. The sheriff strode forward, his own powerful flashlight beam cutting a straight path to the overturned mine cart.

"What in thunder have you boys done?" he asked, his voice echoing off the rock walls. He wasn't angry, just deeply serious.

"We had to, Sheriff," Rick explained, his voice still shaky with adrenaline. "It's Collins, the real-estate agent. And the Frostola man, Henderson. There's another one with them, a big fellow. They're in that cavern."

The sheriff played his light over the barricade. The heavy oak cart was wedged tight, its iron-banded wheels jammed against the ceiling of the low tunnel. "They aren't getting through that anytime soon," he said with grudging admiration. "Good work, boys. Now, stand back." He turned to his deputies. "Jim, a little help. Let's see if we can move this thing."

As the two men put their shoulders to the cart, straining with no effect, a muffled shout came from behind the barrier. "When I get my hands on those kids!" The voice was a low growl, full of menace.

"That's the big one," Scotty whispered. "Lank."

"They have maps in there, Sheriff," Rick said urgently. "And a gasoline generator. We think they're looking for something in the mine."

"We'll get to that," the sheriff said, wiping a hand across his forehead. "First we get them out. Any other way out of that cavern, Rick?"

Rick's heart gave a lurch. This was the most important part. "Yes, sir. That's the problem. There's an air shaft. I saw it on their maps before we blocked the tunnel. They'll be heading for it right now."

Sheriff Miller stopped trying to move the cart and turned to face Rick squarely. His flashlight beam illuminated the boy's earnest, dirt-streaked face. "An air shaft? I've been all through the records on this mine. It was sealed tight thirty years ago. No other known exits."

"They found one," Scotty insisted. "Or made one. That's what Belsely saw, I bet. Them in the fields at night, searching the ground. They were looking for the shaft exit!"

The sheriff was silent for a long moment, his jaw set. He looked at the immovable cart, then back at the boys. From the cavern, the sounds had changed. There was no more shouting, only a faint scraping noise.

"They're moving equipment," Rick said, his mind racing. "They're making a run for it." He took a step closer to the sheriff, his voice pleading. "Please, sir. You have to believe us. I know where the shaft comes out. Or at least, I know the general area from the map. It's on top of the mine hill, near the old orchard."

Sheriff Miller looked at his deputy, Jim, who just shrugged. "The cart's not moving without a winch, Sheriff. And it'll take an hour to get one up here."

The sheriff made his decision. "All right," he said, his voice crisp. "I'm taking a chance on you, Rick. Jim, you stay here with Bill. Try to clear some of these smaller rocks. If you hear them coming back, you be ready. But I don't think they will." He turned back to the boys. "You two, with me. Show me where this supposed air shaft is. And you stick close. No more heroics. Is that understood?"

"Yes, sir!" the boys said in unison.

"Let's move, then," the sheriff commanded. "We'll take the service road up the back of the hill. It'll be faster."

They scrambled out of the mine entrance into the cool night air. The sounds of the Sons of the Old Dominion feast had died down completely. A few men were standing by the police car, their faces pale and worried in the flashing red light. The sheriff gave them a quick, reassuring wave. "Everything's under control! Just stay clear."

He led the way to his patrol car, grabbing a long, heavy flashlight and a coiled rope from the trunk. "No time to drive," he muttered, already starting up the steep, rutted track that led up the hill behind the mine.

The path was dark and treacherous. Rick and Scotty, younger and more nimble, had no trouble keeping up, but they stayed dutifully behind the sheriff as he crashed through the underbrush. The woods were alive with the chirps of crickets and the occasional hoot of an owl. High above, the moon played hide and seek with scattered clouds.

"It should be near the crest of the hill," Rick panted, trying to remember the crude drawing on the map. "Near a big stand of oak trees. Belsely called it the orchard, but it's all overgrown now."

"I know the place," the sheriff grunted. "Used to pick apples there as a boy."

They ran on in silence, the three flashlight beams bouncing and weaving through the trees. Rick's lungs burned. Beside him, Scotty was breathing in ragged gasps. But the thought of Collins and his men getting away was a spur that drove them onward. They had not come this far only to fail.

Finally, the ground leveled out. They burst from the dense woods into a clearing dotted with ancient, gnarled apple trees. This was the place. The air was still. Too still.

"We're too late," Scotty said, his voice full of disappointment.

"Don't be so sure," the sheriff said quietly. He swept his flashlight beam across the clearing, letting it rest on the ground. The grass was thick and matted. "Spread out. Look for anything that seems out of place. A pile of rocks, freshly turned earth, anything."

The boys began their search, moving in a careful grid pattern the way the sheriff had shown them. Rick's eyes scanned the ground, his mind going over Belsely's description. The farmer had seen them stop and bend over, searching. They had to be looking for the cover.

It was Scotty who found it. "Over here!" he called in a loud whisper. "Rick! Sheriff!"

Rick and the sheriff hurried over. Scotty was pointing his light at the base of the largest oak tree. At first, Rick saw nothing but a tangled mat of roots and a thick growth of honeysuckle. Then he looked closer. Hidden beneath the vines was a low, flat stone, about three feet square. It did not look like a natural rock. It was too regular, too smooth on top.

The sheriff knelt, running his hand along the edge. "This is it," he said grimly. "Granite slab. Not native to this part of the hill." He pushed aside the vines and pointed. Along one edge of the slab, the grass and dirt were scuffed and disturbed. "They've been here before. This is their door."

He put his ear to the stone. "I can hear something. Tapping."

Just then, the stone slab shuddered. A line of dirt trickled out from under one edge. From below came the sound of a muffled grunt.

"They're coming up," the sheriff said, drawing his service revolver. "Boys, get behind that tree. And stay there. That's an order."

Rick and Scotty scrambled for cover, their hearts hammering against their ribs. They peered out from behind the wide trunk of the old oak. The sheriff stood his ground, a solid, unmoving figure in the moonlight. He held the big flashlight in his left hand, aimed at the slab, and his revolver in his right.

Slowly, with a grinding scrape, the granite slab began to move. It was being pushed up from below. A gap appeared, and a sliver of yellow light from a lantern inside the shaft sliced through the darkness. The slab tilted, then was shoved aside with a heavy thud.

The first man to emerge was the hulking figure of Lank. He clambered out of the square black hole, blinking in the glare of the sheriff's light. He held a small crowbar in one hand.

"Police, Lank! Drop the bar and put your hands up!" the sheriff's voice boomed.

Lank was momentarily stunned. He squinted, trying to see past the blinding light. "It's the sheriff!" he growled back down the hole. "It's a trap!"

Instead of surrendering, he roared and charged. He was a foolhardy man, but not a coward. He swung the crowbar in a vicious arc aimed at the sheriff's head.

Sheriff Miller didn't flinch. He sidestepped the wild swing with surprising agility, and as Lank stumbled past, off-balance, the sheriff brought the heavy metal butt of his flashlight down hard on the man's wrist. There was a sickening crack and a yell of pain. The crowbar clattered on the ground. Before Lank could recover, the sheriff had him spun around, one arm twisted behind his back.

"I said drop it," the sheriff said calmly.

From the hole, a second figure emerged. It was Henderson, the Frostola man. He saw the sheriff and Lank, and his face went white with fear. He immediately threw his hands in the air. "Don't shoot! I give up! It was all his idea!" he squeaked, pointing a trembling finger back down the shaft.

The last man to appear was Jethro Collins. He climbed out, brushing the dust from his expensive suit. He held a canvas bag in one hand. He took in the scene with a cold, calculating gaze: Lank groaning in the sheriff's grip, Henderson surrendering meekly. He looked at the sheriff, then his eyes found the two boys peeking from behind the tree. His face tightened into a mask of pure hatred.

"You meddling kids," he snarled. "I should have dealt with you properly the first time."

"It's over, Collins," the sheriff said. "There's nowhere to run."

Collins gave a short, bitter laugh. "Over? It's never over." In a movement so fast it almost defied belief, he flung the canvas bag he was holding directly at the sheriff's face.

It was an old trick, but an effective one. The sheriff instinctively let go of Lank to block the bag, which burst on impact, showering him with dirt and rock samples. In that split second of confusion, Lank shoved the sheriff backwards and bolted for the dark woods. At the same time, Collins sprinted in the opposite direction, toward the steep slope of the hill.

"Stay with Henderson!" the sheriff yelled to the boys as he recovered his balance and took off after Lank.

Rick and Scotty looked at each other. Henderson was no threat; he was huddled on the ground, whimpering. But Collins, the mastermind, was getting away.

"Scotty, watch him!" Rick said, making a quick decision. He broke the sheriff's order without a second thought. He couldn't let Collins escape.

He sprinted after the real-estate agent. Collins was fast, but he was a city man, unused to the treacherous terrain. He slipped on a patch of wet leaves and stumbled, giving Rick the chance to close the distance. Rick was smaller, but he was wiry and full of righteous anger. He launched himself through the air in a flying tackle, just like he had practiced in school football.

He hit Collins hard around the knees. The man went down with a surprised grunt, his head hitting the ground with a solid thwack. He lay still.

Rick scrambled to his feet, breathing heavily, and stood over the fallen man. A minute later, Scotty ran up, his flashlight beam bobbing. "Is he...?"

"He's just knocked out," Rick said, prodding Collins with his toe. "I think."

Just then, a loud crashing sound came from the woods, followed by the sheriff's voice. "I've got him!"

A few minutes later, the sheriff emerged from the trees, marching a subdued and handcuffed Lank in front of him. He saw Rick and Scotty standing over the unconscious form of Jethro Collins. He shook his head, but a small smile played on his lips.

"I believe I gave you boys an order to stay put," he said, though his voice lacked any real anger.

"Sorry, Sheriff," Rick said. "He was getting away."

"I see that." The sheriff knelt and checked Collins's pulse. "He'll have a headache when he wakes up. Good job, Rick. Both of you." He pulled a second pair of handcuffs from his belt. "I have a feeling Mr. Collins is going to have a lot to tell us."

An hour later, back at the brightly lit sheriff's office, the whole story came tumbling out. Collins, his head bandaged and his arrogance gone, sat sullenly while Henderson eagerly told everything he knew, hoping for a lighter sentence.

The maps, recovered from the cavern, were the key. They were old geological surveys Collins had discovered in a forgotten county archive. They showed a rich, untapped vein of high grade quartz running directly through the old mine hill. The kind of quartz, the sheriff later learned, that was highly valuable for manufacturing electronics.

"The Blue Ghost was your idea, wasn't it, Collins?" the sheriff asked.

Collins stared at the floor. "The land was worthless. The mine was a public hazard. I was going to put it to good use."

"By scaring the locals half to death so you could buy their property for pennies on the dollar and mine it illegally?" the sheriff countered.

Rick listened, fascinated, as the pieces fell into place. The ghost projector, a powerful custom built device, was Collins's invention. He had set it up in the mine entrance, using the natural mist from the pool as a projection screen. The other "ghosts" Belsely had seen were Lank and Henderson, searching for the air shaft or hauling equipment. The "ghost wagon," as Belsely had called it, was a simple handcart they used to move bags of ore samples at night, when no one was around.

"What about the ice cream truck?" Scotty asked.

Henderson looked up, eager to be helpful. "That was my part! Mr. Collins set me up. It was the perfect cover. I could go anywhere, listen to what people were saying about the ghost, keep an eye on things. Nobody suspects the ice-cream man."

"And you were at the girls' camp picnic to check on the ghost," Rick concluded. "To see how it looked from a distance."

Henderson nodded miserably. "Mr. Collins wanted a report. He was a perfectionist."

The sheriff looked at the two boys, who were sitting on a bench, tired but triumphant. "Well," he said, "it's a good thing for this county that Mr. Collins's plan wasn't quite perfect. He didn't count on the curiosity of two boys with an airplane."

He stood up. "I'll see that you both get home. Your parents are probably worried sick." He paused at the door. "You know, we have a junior deputy program in the summer. You two might be interested."

Rick and Scotty looked at each other, their faces breaking into wide grins.

"We might be, Sheriff," Rick said. "We just might be."

As they walked out into the pale light of early morning, the whole adventure seemed like a dream. The Blue Ghost was gone, the mystery was solved, and the criminals were behind bars.

"A spook projector," Scotty said, shaking his head in disbelief. "All that trouble for some shiny rocks."

"It's more than that," Rick said thoughtfully, looking up at the mine hill, now just a dark silhouette against the rising sun. "It's about greed. Collins wanted something that wasn't his, and he was willing to hurt people to get it." He felt a sense of satisfaction that was deep and quiet. They had not just solved a puzzle. They had done something good.

"Well," Scotty said, clapping him on the back, "I'm just glad our ghost-hunting days are over. For a little while, at least."

Rick smiled. "For a little while," he agreed. But he knew, and Scotty knew, that for them, the next adventure was probably just around the corner.
