Then, with a sigh of straining metal, the heavy vehicle began to roll. It moved backward, slowly at first, then gathered momentum, its path taking it not back down the road they had come, but angling off the track and down the steep, brush-choked hillside opposite the valley with the mine. "Now!" Rick yelled, grabbing Scotty's arm and pulling him into the thicket of pines and undergrowth beside the road. They dove into the foliage just as the motor scooter skidded to a halt where the truck had been moments before. The Frostola man, his face a mask of fury, stared at the empty space on the road. Then his head snapped toward the sound of crashing brush and snapping branches as the old truck careened down the opposite slope. "Hey!" he shouted, his voice echoing in the sudden quiet. He jumped off his scooter and, without a moment's hesitation, plunged into the woods after the runaway truck. He was convinced his quarry was inside it. The boys held their breath, listening. A final, tremendous crash, a symphony of tortured metal and splintering wood, signaled the truck's journey was over. Then, silence. "He bought it," Scotty whispered, a note of awe in his voice. "He actually bought it." "It was the only thing he could think we'd do," Rick reasoned, already crawling deeper into the woods. "He thought we were trying to get away. Come on. We have to circle around." They moved as quietly as they could, parallel to the dirt road but deep in the cover of the trees. They passed the abandoned scooter, its chrome handlebars glinting in the late afternoon sun. The temptation to sabotage it was strong, but Rick shook his head. They couldn't afford the time or the risk of making noise. Their only goal was to put as much distance as possible between themselves and the hidden valley. For an hour they walked, pushing through tangled rhododendron and stepping carefully over mossy logs. The terrain was rough, and their pace was slow. Finally, Rick judged them far enough away to risk a rest. He collapsed at the base of a huge oak tree, pulling a canteen from his belt. Scotty sank down beside him, breathing heavily. "Okay, genius," Scotty said, after a long pull of water. "First, my compliments on the disappearing truck trick. Second, what in the world was all that back there?" "That," Rick said grimly, "was the answer to everything. Belsely wasn't seeing ghosts picking up invisible dead. He was seeing miners prospecting." "Prospecting?" "Think about it, Scotty. He said they walked the fields, stopping and bending over. 'Like they were searchin' the ground.' They weren't looking for lost heads, they were taking soil samples. The 'ghost wagon' he saw was a cart for carrying their tools and samples." "And the Blue Ghost?" Scotty prompted. "The perfect cover story. They create a terrifying local legend to scare everyone away from the mine area at night. It gives them free rein to work without being disturbed. The Sons of the Old Dominion feast was the perfect time for them to be active. Everyone local is in one place, accounted for." "But what are they mining?" Scotty asked, wiping his brow. "That wasn't a coal operation. I saw a sluice box." Rick nodded, his eyes distant. "Exactly. A sluice box is for separating heavy materials from sand and gravel by washing it with water. Gold, maybe. But the geology here isn't right for a major gold strike. There's something else, something even more valuable." He paused, letting the pieces click into place. "Alluvial diamonds." Scotty stared at him. "Diamonds? In Virginia? You're kidding." "It's not impossible," Rick insisted, his voice gaining excitement. "There have been isolated finds. A few kimberlite pipes, the volcanic formations where diamonds are formed, were discovered in this part of the Appalachians. If they eroded over millions of years, the diamonds could have washed down into streams and settled in gravel deposits. Like the one in that valley. It would be an incredibly rich find. Worth millions. Worth enough to set up an elaborate hoax and kill to protect." The implications settled over them, as heavy as the humid air. They had stumbled onto a secret diamond mine, and the miners knew it. "Our truck," Scotty said suddenly. "It's wrecked at the bottom of a ravine. That's our proof, right there. We just have to lead the sheriff to it." Rick shook his head. "It's not enough. A wrecked truck in the woods doesn't prove anything. We were trespassing on that logging road. They'll say we panicked and crashed. It's our word against theirs, and they've got a lot more at stake than we do. They'll have a story all ready." "So what do we do? We're on foot, miles from town, and a very angry ice-cream man is probably looking for us with his friends." "We get back to Belsely's," Rick said, getting to his feet. "We have to stay off the roads. We'll cut cross-country. And we have to get there before they start a systematic search for us." The journey was an ordeal. The sun began to set, casting long, eerie shadows through the forest. Insects descended in whining clouds, a constant torment despite the repellent they had used earlier. Twice they had to drop to the ground and lie motionless as they heard the sound of a vehicle on a nearby road. They didn't know if it was the miners or an innocent local, and they couldn't afford to find out. It was well after dark when they finally saw the familiar lights of Belsely's farmhouse. They circled around, approaching from the rear, moving from shadow to shadow like the very ghosts they had been chasing. Rick peered through a kitchen window. The farmer was sitting at his table, reading a newspaper under a single bare bulb. He looked solid, permanent. Rick tapped lightly on the glass. Belsely jumped, his chair scraping on the floor. He peered out into the darkness, saw the boys' faces, and his own face registered a mixture of alarm and relief. He quickly unlatched the back door and motioned them in. "Sakes alive, boys! Where's your truck? I heard you drive off hours ago." "We had some trouble, Mr. Belsely," Rick said, choosing his words carefully. "The truck broke down. A long way from here. We had to walk back." "Broke down?" Belsely looked at their scratched faces and torn clothes. "Looks more like you wrestled a bobcat. You hungry?" "Starving," Scotty admitted. The farmer, a man of few questions when hospitality was called for, silently cut thick slices of bread and ham and poured two tall glasses of milk. The boys ate like wolves. "That Frostola fella was by here," Belsely said casually, as he watched them eat. "Askin' about you." Rick and Scotty froze, their sandwiches halfway to their mouths. "What did he want?" Rick asked, trying to keep his voice steady. "Wanted to know if you boys came back. Said you'd made plans to go look at some old caves with him and never showed up. Seemed mighty put out about it." A cold lie, delivered smoothly. The ice-cream man was already building his alibi, painting them as unreliable kids. "What did you tell him?" Scotty asked. "Told him I hadn't seen you. Wasn't a lie, at the time," the farmer said with a slight twinkle in his eye. "Figured if you wanted him to know where you were, you'd have told him yourself. Didn't like his tone. Too pushy." Rick felt a surge of gratitude for the farmer's simple, sturdy intuition. "Mr. Belsely, you did exactly the right thing. Thank you." "Well," the farmer stood up, stretching. "You need a place to sleep, my barn's empty. And I'll drive you to town in the mornin' to fetch a tow truck for your vehicle." "We'd appreciate that," Rick said. "But we might not need the tow." After Belsely had bid them goodnight and retired to his room, Rick turned to Scotty. "He's buying time, Scotty. That story about the caves. He's trying to establish that we disappeared on our own. By tomorrow, they'll have moved everything out of that valley. They'll clear out the mine, hide the equipment, and our story will sound like a complete fantasy." "So we have to act tonight," Scotty concluded, his voice grim. "What's the plan? We can't go up against that whole crew." "We don't have to," Rick said, his eyes gleaming with a familiar intensity. "We're not going after the diamonds. We're going after the ghost." Scotty groaned. "I thought we were done with the ghost." "We're not. Think. The ghost is their biggest liability right now. It's the one piece of their whole operation that's designed to be seen. It's their public face, so to speak. If we can expose the ghost for the fraud it is, right now, tonight, their whole security system falls apart. It will discredit them and draw exactly the kind of attention to the area that they don't want." "And how do we do that?" "The projector," Rick said simply. "My theory was right. It has to be an optical projection on mist. We find the projector, we win." "We looked for it before, Rick. We didn't see anything." "We were looking in the wrong place at the wrong time. We were down with the crowd. The projector has to be in a hidden, elevated position, probably across from the mine entrance to get a straight-on image. And tonight, with the Frostola man out searching for us and the whole operation on high alert, they might get careless. They might even leave it unguarded." It was a long shot, a desperate gamble, but it was the only one they had. "All right," Scotty said with a sigh of resignation. "Let's go catch a ghost projector. What do we need?" "Flashlights. Belsely had a couple on the porch. And rope. There's a coil in his barn. We might need to climb." They slipped out of the house and raided the barn, their movements swift and silent in the moonlight. Armed with two heavy-duty flashlights and a hundred feet of stout rope, they set off once more into the night. They didn't take the road this time, but cut through the fields, using the terrain Belsely had described. They walked through the cornfield on top of the mine hill, the dry stalks rustling around them. This time, they weren't looking for spectral figures. They were looking for a projection booth. They reached the ridge overlooking the mine area. Below, the scene of the Sons of the Old Dominion feast was quiet. A few cars remained, but most of the members had gone home, disappointed by the ghost's failure to appear. "They must have called off the show," Scotty whispered. "The lookout never got back into position." "Good," Rick whispered back. "That means they think they're safe for the night. Let's find our spot." Rick scanned the opposite cliff face, a dark wall of rock and trees across the deep pool from the mine entrance. "The light would have to come from over there," he mused, pointing. "High enough to project down onto the mist rising from the pool, but concealed from the road. See that cluster of hemlocks, about fifty feet up? The one growing out from that wide ledge?" Scotty followed his gaze. "I see it. It's a perfect hiding place." "That's where I'd put it," Rick declared. "Let's go." Getting to the other side of the pool meant a long, treacherous hike down into the ravine and up the other side. They used their flashlights sparingly, shielding the beams with their hands. The climb up the far cliff was even harder. It wasn't a sheer rock face, but a steep, crumbling slope of earth and stone, studded with trees that offered precarious handholds. Near the top, they had to use the rope, with Rick scrambling up to secure it to a thick tree trunk so Scotty could follow. Finally, gasping for breath, they pulled themselves onto the wide ledge. It was even better than Rick had imagined. It was a natural balcony, twenty feet long and ten feet deep, completely hidden from below by the thick, drooping branches of the ancient hemlocks. And at the far end of the ledge, tucked back against the rock wall, was a large, weatherproof metal box. A thick, insulated cable ran from the box down the cliff face, disappearing into the darkness. "The spook projector," Scotty breathed, running his hand over the cold metal. "Bingo," Rick said with grim satisfaction. The box was locked with a heavy padlock. Rick examined it with his flashlight beam. "We can't break this without making a racket." "So what do we do? We can't just sit here with it," Scotty said. Rick was already examining the cable. "We follow this. It has to lead to a power source. A generator, most likely. If we can disable the generator, we've shut them down for good." They began to trace the cable. It led them away from the ledge and back into the woods, running along the ground, partially buried in the leaf litter. It was a clear trail leading them deeper into the enemy's territory. After a quarter of a mile, they smelled it before they saw it: the faint, acrid scent of diesel fuel. They doused their lights and crept forward. Ahead, in a small clearing, was a canvas tent. The generator was inside, its low, rhythmic thumping a steady heartbeat in the forest quiet. A single man sat on a crate outside the tent, a rifle across his lap. He was a silhouette against the faint light from a lantern inside the tent. He was a guard. They were no longer dealing with a simple hoax. This was a professional operation, with armed guards and a permanent power station. Rick and Scotty backed away slowly, their hearts hammering. Their simple plan to disable the projector had just become infinitely more complicated, and infinitely more dangerous. They were alone, on foot, and had found the nerve center of the entire conspiracy. And it was guarded. "Now what, genius?" Scotty whispered, for the second time that day. Rick didn't have an answer. He only knew that they couldn't turn back now. They had the criminals cornered, but in doing so, they had walked right back into the trap themselves.

Rick’s mind, which usually worked with the speed and precision of a calculating machine, was a blank. He stared at the dark shape of the guard, the glint of moonlight on the rifle barrel, and felt a cold knot form in his stomach. They had blundered from a boyish adventure into something deadly serious.

"Back up," he breathed, the words barely a puff of air. "Slowly. Don't step on anything."

Scotty needed no further encouragement. He was already moving, placing each foot with the care of a man walking on eggshells. They retreated the way they had come, melting back into the deeper shadows of the woods until the clearing, the tent, and the guard were swallowed by the night. They didn't stop until they were a hundred yards away, their backs pressed against the rough bark of a massive oak.

"An armed guard," Scotty said, his voice a strained whisper. "Rick, they have a man with a rifle. This isn't a spook show, it's an occupation."

"I know," Rick said. His own heart was still thumping a frantic rhythm against his ribs. "A professional one. Collins, the Frostola man, the guard, and Belsely's field walkers. It's a whole crew."

"And they're not just trying to scare people away from a mine," Scotty reasoned. "You don't need a rifle for that. They're protecting something. Or they're protecting themselves while they look for something."

"Belsely's story," Rick mused, the pieces clicking into place with sudden, frightening clarity. "He said they walked the fields, searching. Bending over, like they were looking for something on the ground. And they had a cart."

"To collect the invisible dead," Scotty added, a note of dark humor in his voice.

"To collect whatever they found," Rick corrected. "They're not searching for a lost wallet, Scotty. You use a cart when you expect to find something big, or a lot of something small. They're digging. The ghost is a diversion. It draws everyone's attention to the mine entrance at nine o'clock, and again at midnight, while the real work is happening somewhere else."

"Like in the cornfield on top of the mine hill," Scotty concluded. "But what are they looking for?"

Rick shook his head. "I don't know. Old treasure, maybe. This is Civil War country. Could be anything. But we can't find out with that guard sitting there. We can't go to the police, not yet. They'd want proof, and by the time we convinced them, our friends with the rifle would be long gone."

"So we're back to 'Now what, genius?'" Scotty asked, the familiar challenge in his voice a welcome return to normalcy.

A slow smile spread across Rick's face in the darkness. The fear was receding, replaced by the cold thrill of a problem to be solved. "Now we get creative," he said. "We can't take him on, so we have to move him. We need a diversion."

They spent the next half hour in a whispered conference, huddled at the base of the oak. They were too far from the road to lure the guard with a car horn, and a simple shout would give away their position. The solution, Rick decided, had to come from the woods themselves.

"There's a gully about fifty yards past the clearing," he explained, drawing a map in the dirt with a stick. "It's steep, and filled with loose rock. If we can get up on the ridge above it, on the opposite side from the tent…"

"We could start a rockslide," Scotty finished, catching on immediately. "Not a big one, just enough to make a racket. It would sound like someone falling, or a deer losing its footing."

"Exactly. It has to be a sound that's natural enough not to spook him into hiding, but alarming enough to make him go and investigate. He's a guard. It's his job to check out strange noises."

"And while he's checking it out?"

"You'll be my lookout," Rick said. "I'm going into that tent. I'll have maybe five minutes to disable that generator. It should be enough."

The plan was risky, and they both knew it. If the guard didn't take the bait, or if he came back too soon, Rick would be trapped. But it was the only plan they had. They moved out, circling wide around the clearing. The forest was alive with the sounds of the night, a symphony of crickets and rustling leaves that helped to cover their own noise.

Finding the gully was easy. It was a dark slash in the earth, smelling of damp soil and decay. They crept along its edge until they were well past the clearing, then scrambled up the steep bank on the far side. From their new vantage point on the ridge, they could just make out the faint glow of the lantern in the clearing through the trees.

"This is the spot," Rick whispered, pointing to a cluster of weathered stones, each the size of a man's head, perched precariously on the slope. "Give me a ten minute head start to get in position. Then let them have it."

"Be careful," Scotty said, his hand gripping Rick's shoulder for a moment.

"You too," Rick replied. "If he heads your way instead of down into the gully, get out of here. Don't wait for me."

Rick slipped away, moving back toward the generator tent like a shadow. The five minutes of waiting were the longest of Scotty's life. He kept his eyes glued to the distant light, his ears straining for any sound. He pictured Rick, creeping closer and closer to the armed man, and his hands grew slick with sweat. He counted to six hundred, then took a deep breath. It was time.

He chose a rock the size of a small pumpkin and gave it a mighty shove. It tumbled down the slope, dislodging a cascade of smaller stones and dirt. The noise was spectacular, a loud, crashing, scraping roar that echoed through the quiet woods. It sounded exactly like a clumsy animal, or an even clumsier man, taking a serious fall.

Scotty held his breath. For a long moment, there was no reaction from the clearing. Then he saw the silhouette of the guard stand up. The man listened, head cocked. He shouted something into the darkness, a gruff "Who's there?"

When no answer came, the guard hesitated. Then, with a muttered curse that was too far away for Scotty to hear, he picked up his rifle and a powerful flashlight. He clicked on the beam, a bright white cone that sliced through the woods, and started walking directly toward the sound. He was taking the bait.

Scotty let out a silent sigh of relief. He watched as the guard's light bobbed and weaved through the trees, moving farther and farther away from the tent. Now it was up to Rick.

Rick, hidden in a thicket of mountain laurel a stone's throw from the tent, watched the guard depart. The moment the flashlight beam was safely swallowed by the trees, he sprang into action. He covered the remaining distance in a low crouch and slipped through the canvas flap of the tent.

The air inside was hot and thick with the smell of diesel. The generator, a squat, greasy metal block, sat in the center of the dirt floor, thumping away with its steady, monotonous rhythm. A single bare bulb, powered by the machine, hung from the center pole, casting stark shadows. Against one wall was a cot with a rumpled sleeping bag. Against the other was a small folding table. On the table, next to a greasy paper plate and a stack of magazines, was a map.

Rick's eyes were drawn to it instantly. It was a topographical survey map of the area, the kind used by hikers and engineers. But this one was covered in markings. A large red circle was drawn around the cornfield on the mine hill. The circle was subdivided by a grid of blue pencil lines, and many of the small squares had been crossed out with an X. It was a search grid.

He wanted to study it, to memorize it, but there was no time. His primary mission was the generator. He knelt beside the thumping machine, his eyes scanning for the quickest way to kill it. Smashing it was out; too much noise. He saw the fuel line, a thin black rubber hose running from a red five gallon can to the engine block. He could pull it, but that would be messy and obvious.

Then he saw the spark plug wire. It was a thick, insulated cable leading to the top of the cylinder head. Perfect. It would be quiet, and with any luck, the guard wouldn't think to check it right away. Rick reached for it, his fingers brushing against the hot metal of the engine. He gave a firm tug. The wire popped off its terminal with a satisfying little snap.

The generator coughed once, sputtered, and died.

The sudden silence was deafening. The light bulb went out, plunging the tent into absolute blackness. Rick froze, every nerve screaming. He had done it. Now he had to get out.

He was about to grab the map when he heard a sound from outside. A low whistle. It was Scotty's signal, the one they had agreed on. It was the call of a screech owl, and it meant trouble.

Rick didn't hesitate. He forgot the map. He dove for the back of the tent, fumbling in his pocket for his knife. He sliced a long, vertical gash in the canvas, scrambled through, and came up running. He didn't look back.

He found Scotty twenty yards into the woods, his face pale in the gloom.

"Two of them!" Scotty hissed. "The ice cream man. He just drove up on his scooter, no lights. He's talking to the guard now. They're heading back to the tent."

Rick could hear their voices, angry and confused, growing closer. "This way!" he urged, pulling Scotty deeper into the woods. "Away from the gully!"

They ran blindly, crashing through underbrush, branches whipping at their faces. Behind them, they heard a shout of anger as the men discovered the dead generator and the slashed tent. A moment later, two powerful flashlight beams began to sweep through the woods. The hunt was on.

"They're coming!" Scotty panted, stumbling over a root.

"Keep moving," Rick ordered, his own lungs burning. "They don't know the woods like we do."

They were no longer just boys on an adventure. They were prey. The flashlights behind them split up, one moving to the left, one to the right, in a classic pincer movement. Their pursuers were experienced.

Rick risked a glance over his shoulder. One of the beams was getting closer. He veered to the right, heading for a familiar landmark. He and Scotty had explored this section of the woods thoroughly. He knew there was a steep, rocky drop off just ahead, leading down to the creek bed. It was dangerous in the dark, but it was their only chance.

"The creek!" he shouted to Scotty. "Follow me!"

They half ran, half slid down the treacherous slope, sending a shower of loose dirt and pebbles into the darkness below. They landed in a heap at the bottom, bruised but in one piece. The creek was shallow here, gurgling over smooth stones.

"Upstream!" Rick commanded. "In the water. It'll hide our tracks."

They scrambled into the frigid water, the shock of the cold taking their breath away. They waded upstream, staying close to the bank, using the overhanging bushes for cover. Above them on the ridge, the flashlight beams crisscrossed, searching fruitlessly. They heard the men calling to each other, their voices filled with frustration.

"They went this way!" one voice, the guard's, yelled. "Down the bank!"

"Find them!" the other voice, the Frostola man's, snarled. "They can't have gone far. No one gets away with this."

The beams played over the surface of the water, but Rick and Scotty were huddled under a thick curtain of willow branches, invisible. After a few tense minutes that felt like hours, the lights moved on, continuing their search downstream.

The boys waited until the voices and the lights had faded completely. Only then did they dare to climb out of the creek, shivering and soaked to the skin. They were safe, for now.

They made their way back toward their plane in a wide, cautious circle, every snap of a twig making them jump. The adrenaline was wearing off, replaced by a bone deep chill and exhaustion.

"That," Scotty said, his teeth chattering, "was entirely too close."

"Tell me about it," Rick agreed. He was thinking about the map. He had been inches away from a crucial piece of the puzzle, and he had been forced to leave it behind. "I had it, Scotty. A map. A search grid for the cornfield."

"A search grid? Then Belsely was right. They are looking for something up there."

"And now they know that we know," Rick said grimly. "We didn't just disable a projector tonight. We declared war."

They reached the field where the plane was parked. The familiar shape of the small aircraft was a comforting sight in the moonlight. They climbed into the cockpit, the familiar smells of oil and canvas a welcome relief from the damp woods.

Scotty wrapped a blanket around his shoulders. "So they'll probably fix the generator tomorrow. The ghost will be back in business by tomorrow night."

"Probably," Rick admitted. "We only bought ourselves some time." He stared out the canopy at the dark shape of the mine hill, a brooding presence against the starry sky. He had failed to get the map, but he had seen it. His mind, now calm and focused, began to reconstruct the image. The red circle. The blue grid lines.

"But we also got something more important than a broken generator," he said slowly, a new thought taking shape.

"A case of pneumonia?" Scotty asked wryly.

"A target," Rick said, turning to his friend, his eyes gleaming with renewed purpose. "They have a map, and they know what they're looking for. We don't. That gives them an advantage. But I saw that map, Scotty. I saw where they've already looked."

Scotty sat up, the blanket forgotten. "The X's in the squares."

"Exactly. They've been searching systematically. But they haven't found it yet, or they'd be gone. This means whatever they're looking for is in one of the squares they haven't crossed out yet." Rick's voice grew more confident. "We don't need their map. We just need to search the places they haven't gotten to yet. They're hunting for something in that cornfield. And tomorrow, before they do, so are we."

Scotty shivered again, despite the blanket. "Search for what, though? Belsely said they were collecting the invisible dead. It's not like we're going to find a pile of spectral bones."

"No," Rick agreed, his mind racing. He fumbled in the dark for the small notepad and pencil he always carried in his flight jacket. He switched on their tiny cockpit flashlight, shielding its beam with his hand. "But Belsely also said they stopped and gathered and looked at the ground. He said they acted like they were searching. The wagon was probably just part of the show, a prop to make it look spooky. The real work was the searching."

He clicked the pencil, the sound sharp in the quiet cockpit. "Let's see. The mine hill is on one side of the road, the field with the plane is on the other. Belsely saw them in both places." He began to sketch a rough diagram. "The cornfield was on top of the mine hill. He said they walked there, and in the field where our plane is. But the map I saw, the grid, it was definitely for the cornfield. That's their primary search area."

"Okay," Scotty said, leaning over to look. "So we focus on the cornfield. How many squares did the grid have?"

Rick closed his eyes, picturing the brief, illuminated glimpse he'd had of the paper. The image was surprisingly clear, burned into his memory by the adrenaline of the moment. "It was a simple grid. Maybe ten by ten. A hundred squares in all. The corn rows would make the lines."

"And the X's?"

"I saw a cluster of them, on the side of the field nearest the mine. Maybe two dozen, three dozen at most. They're working their way across. The red circle was on the far side, away from the mine, near the woods at the back of the hill."

"The side they haven't gotten to yet," Scotty concluded. "The circle must mark the spot they think it is."

"Exactly. They're searching the whole grid just to be sure, or maybe the circle is a general area, not a specific point. But it's our best lead. If we get there first, we can search that circled area before they do." Rick looked at his friend, the faint flashlight beam throwing their faces into sharp relief. "We have to go at first light. Collins and the Frostola man will be sleeping. They'll probably think we're miles away, licking our wounds."

"Our wet wounds," Scotty corrected, pulling the blanket tighter. "All right. First light. But if we find anything, we don't stick around to admire it. We grab it and go."

"Agreed," Rick said, clicking off the flashlight. He settled back in his seat, but sleep was a long way off. He kept seeing the map in his mind, the neat blue lines and the angry red circle, a target waiting in a field of green corn.

The first hint of gray in the eastern sky was their signal. They moved stiffly, their clothes still damp and their muscles sore from the previous night's exertions. They left the plane and cut across the fields, sticking to the cover of hedgerows, circling wide around Belsely's farmhouse. The tenant farmer was an early riser, and they had no desire to explain what two boys were doing sneaking onto the mine hill at dawn.

The air was cool and heavy with dew. Mist clung to the low places in the fields, looking ghostly in its own right. As they climbed the gentle slope of the mine hill, the rows of corn rose before them like a solid green wall, taller than their heads.

"This is going to be like looking for a needle in a haystack," Scotty whispered, pausing at the edge of the field.

"A hundred haystacks," Rick amended. He pulled out the page from his notepad. In the growing light, his reconstructed map looked crude, but the essentials were there. "According to my drawing, the circle should be about ninety rows in, and maybe twenty rows over from the far edge."

"Let's hope your memory is as good as you think it is," Scotty said. They plunged into the corn.

It was an eerie world. The thick leaves brushed against them, soaking their shirts with cold dew. The rising sun was invisible, its light filtering down in a diffuse green gloom. Every rustle of the leaves, every snap of a dry stalk underfoot, sounded like a cannon shot in the stillness. They walked down a long row, counting.

"Eighty-eight, eighty-nine, ninety," Rick counted softly. "This should be the row. Now we go across, twenty rows in from the woods."

They turned, forcing their way through the dense stalks. It was slow going. They had to move carefully to avoid making too much noise. The ground underfoot was soft and loamy.

"Nineteen, twenty," Scotty announced. "We're here. The center of your red circle. See anything?"

Rick scanned the ground. It looked exactly like every other square foot of the cornfield. Dirt, corn stalks, and a few scattered weeds. "Let's spread out a little. Search in a spiral. Look for anything out of place. A patch of disturbed earth, a stake, anything."

For the next half hour, they searched. They moved in expanding circles, their eyes glued to the ground. They found a rusty bolt, a broken piece of a glass bottle, and a bird's nest that had fallen from a stalk. Nothing.

Rick was beginning to feel the familiar sting of disappointment. "Maybe I remembered it wrong. Maybe the circle was just a coffee stain."

"You saw what you saw," Scotty said, his voice firm. He was on his hands and knees, pushing aside the low-hanging leaves of a large stalk. "We just haven't looked hard enough. Belsely said they bent over, like they were searching the ground. Maybe it's buried." He began patting the soil. "Feels a little loose here."

Rick knelt beside him. The earth did seem softer, less compacted than the surrounding ground. He pulled a multi-tool from his pocket and folded out the small shovel blade. He began to dig carefully.

An inch down, the blade struck something with a dull clink. Not a rock. It was a metallic sound.

The boys exchanged a wide-eyed glance. Working together now, they scraped away the dirt with their hands. An object began to emerge. It was a flat, tin box, about the size of a shoebox, rusted and stained with soil. It looked very, very old.

"This has to be it," Scotty breathed, his voice filled with awe.

Rick worked his fingers under one edge and pulled. With a sucking sound, the box came free of the earth. It was heavier than he expected. There was no lock, only a simple clasp on the front, fused shut with rust.

"We have to open it," Rick said, his heart pounding. "Here, let me try to pry the clasp."

He inserted the tip of his tool's screwdriver blade under the clasp. He was concentrating so intently that he didn't hear the sound at first.

It was Scotty who grabbed his arm. "Listen."

Rick froze. From the far side of the field, near the mine road, came the sound of voices. A man's gruff voice, then another, higher-pitched and angrier. Collins and the Frostola man. They were early.

"They're coming," Scotty whispered urgently. "They must have found the generator."

"Or they're just starting their search for the day," Rick whispered back. He shoved the tin box into Scotty's hands. "Go. Head for the woods at the back of the field. I'll cover our tracks and meet you there."

"We go together," Scotty insisted.

"No time to argue! They're working a grid. If they see our footprints leading straight to the woods from here, they'll know we found something. I'll walk backward in our tracks to the edge of the field, then circle around. It'll confuse them. Now go!"

Scotty hesitated for a fraction of a second, then nodded, clutching the precious box. He disappeared into the green maze of the corn.

Rick worked frantically. He pushed the dirt back into the hole, smoothed it over with his hands, and even scattered a few loose leaves on top. It wasn't perfect, but it might pass a casual glance. He began to walk backward, carefully placing his feet into the prints they had already made, using a broken corn leaf to brush away the scuff marks around the hole.

The voices were getting closer. He could hear them counting rows.

"Seventy-four," the Frostola man's voice called out. "Nothing. This is taking too long, Jethro. We should have just started in the target zone."

"Patience, my friend," Collins's smoother voice replied. "The general's instructions were explicit. Complete search. No shortcuts. He didn't survive all those campaigns by taking shortcuts."

The general? Rick's mind snagged on the word. What general?

He was nearly at the edge of the field where they had entered. He could hear the men moving down a row just a few dozen feet away. He risked a glance through the leaves. He could see them, two dark shapes moving methodically, their heads bent down.

Rick held his breath and took the last few backward steps out of the corn. He was back in the hedgerow. He turned and began to run, low to the ground, circling the long way around the mine hill toward the woods where Scotty would be waiting. He hoped.

He found Scotty near a huge old oak tree, deep inside the treeline that bordered the back of the cornfield. Scotty was pacing back and forth, the tin box tucked under his arm.

"I was about to come looking for you," Scotty said, his relief obvious. "Did they see you?"

"No. It was close. They're being very systematic about it." Rick took the box from him, turning it over in his hands. "Let's see what was worth all this trouble."

They sat on a fallen log, hidden from view. Rick took out his tool again. This time he used the pliers to grip the rusted clasp and bend it back and forth. For a long moment it resisted, then, with a rusty shriek, it snapped.

Rick took a deep breath. He lifted the lid.

The box did not contain gold or jewels. It was packed with oilcloth-wrapped bundles, nestled in what looked like dried grass. At the very top lay a single folded piece of paper. Rick picked it up carefully. The paper was old and brittle, the ink faded to a brownish color. It was a letter.

He unfolded it. The handwriting was elegant, looping script. "My Dearest Anna," it began.

"It's just old letters," Scotty said, a note of anticlimax in his voice.

"Wait," Rick said. He read aloud. "'My Dearest Anna, The campaign proves more trying than we had anticipated. The men's spirits are low, but mine remain fixed on the cause, and on the thought of my eventual return to you. I have taken the liberty of securing the regimental funds. In these uncertain times, with spies and scoundrels seemingly behind every tree, I could not entrust them to the quartermaster's wagon. I have buried them in a place known only to God and now, through this letter, to you. Should the worst befall me on the field of battle, you are to come to the place we spoke of, the old abandoned hollow, and follow the map I have drawn on the reverse of this page. It is our children's future, and yours. Do not let it fall into the hands of thieves or the government. Your devoted husband, Brigadier General Jedediah Thorne.'"

Rick flipped the letter over. On the back, drawn in the same faded ink, was a crude map. It showed a hill, a creek, and a mine entrance. An X was marked on top of the hill.

"Brigadier General Jedediah Thorne," Scotty whispered. "A Confederate general. I've read about him. He was killed at the Battle of Cedar Creek. They said he was a hero, but that a payroll he was carrying vanished with him."

"The regimental funds," Rick said, looking at the other bundles in the box. "Not just letters. This is the payroll." He carefully unwrapped the corner of the topmost bundle. Inside was not paper money, but the unmistakable gleam of gold coins.

"Gold," Scotty breathed. "Real Civil War gold."

Rick quickly rewrapped the bundle and closed the box. "And Collins mentioned a general giving instructions. Not Jedediah Thorne. A living general."

"His descendant," Scotty realized. "He must have found other letters, letters that described this one. He knows the payroll is here, but he doesn't have the exact map. That's what this is about. That's what they're looking for."

"They're looking for this box," Rick said, tapping the tin lid. "They're hunting for a lost Confederate payroll. And we just found it."

A twig snapped behind them.

The boys spun around, their hearts leaping into their throats. Standing not ten feet away, holding a gnarled walking stick, was Belsely, the tenant farmer. His face was a mixture of curiosity and suspicion.

"Mornin', boys," he said, his voice calm. "Bit early for a picnic, ain't it? Found what you was lookin' for?" His eyes were fixed on the tin box in Rick's hands.

Rick and Scotty were speechless. They were caught.

Belsely took a slow step forward. "I saw you sneak up the hill. Figured you were up to more than bird watchin'. I been watchin' them other fellas for weeks. The ice-cream man and that city slicker he rents from. Prowlin' around my corn at all hours. Tramplin' the stalks. Figured they was huntin' for somethin'. Looks like you found it first."

"Mr. Belsely," Rick began, his mind scrambling for an explanation. "It's not what you think."

"Isn't it?" the farmer said, his gaze shrewd. "I think you boys just stumbled into somethin' a whole lot bigger than a simple ghost scare. And I think them fellas," he nodded his head back toward the cornfield, "are gonna be mighty unhappy when they find that hole empty." He paused, chewing on a piece of grass he'd plucked. "Question is, what are you gonna do now?"

Before Rick could answer, a shout echoed from the cornfield. "Jethro! Over here! Look at this!" It was the Frostola man's voice, sharp with anger. "The ground's been disturbed! Someone's been here!"

Belsely's face hardened. "Reckon you've run out of time for thinkin'. Reckon it's time for runnin'. Get, you two. Go down the back of the hill, through the woods. It'll bring you out on the old logging road a mile from here. They don't know about that path."

"What about you?" Scotty asked.

"Me? I'm just a farmer inspectin' his property." Belsely gave them a slow wink. "Get a powerful urge to chase trespassers sometimes. Might just slow 'em down a bit. Now git!"

Clutching the box, Rick and Scotty scrambled down the wooded hillside, sliding on loose dirt and grabbing tree limbs for support. Behind them, they heard Belsely's voice, raised in a righteous yell.

"Hey! You there! What do you think you're doin' in my corn? Get off my land, you hear me?"

The boys didn't stop to see what happened next. They ran.

They could hear shouting behind them now, a different voice mixed with Belsely's. It was Collins, his tone sharp and angry. The farmer's diversion was working, but for how long? They crashed through a thicket of wild raspberry canes and found themselves on a gentler slope, the trees thinning slightly.

"The logging road," Scotty said, pointing ahead. Through a gap in the foliage they could see a narrow, overgrown track, more of a wide trail than a road. It was their path to safety. They half slid, half ran the last hundred feet and stumbled onto the path, panting for breath.

For a moment they just stood there, listening. The shouting from the hilltop had faded, replaced by the chirping of crickets and the rustle of wind in the leaves.

"Think he's all right?" Scotty asked, looking back the way they had come.

"Belsely? He can handle himself," Rick said, though he didn't feel as confident as he sounded. "He said he was just a farmer chasing trespassers. They can't do anything to him for that." He shifted the weight of the box. "The question is, what do we do?"

Scotty looked down the overgrown road. "He said this would bring us out a mile from here. Where's 'here'?"

"I don't know," Rick admitted. "Somewhere on the far side of the mine property. We're on foot, we've got this," he patted the box, "and those two know we have it. They'll be looking for us."

"They'll go to the plane first," Scotty reasoned. "They know it's ours. They'll probably figure we'd head right for it."

"Which means we can't go near it," Rick concluded. He set the box down and leaned against a tree, trying to think. His mind, usually clear and orderly, felt as jumbled as the forest floor. They had the treasure. They had found the lost Confederate payroll. It was the kind of discovery boys dreamed of. But right now, it felt more like a curse.

"We can't go to the police, either," Rick thought aloud. "Not yet. What would we say? 'We found a box of gold that some men were looking for while they were projecting a ghost to scare people away from a mine.' They'd think we were crazy."

"We need proof," Scotty agreed. "Proof about the ghost. If we can expose Collins and the ice-cream man, the rest of it will make sense."

A low rumble cut through the quiet. It was the sound of a motor, growing closer. The boys ducked instinctively behind a wall of overgrown honeysuckle. Through the leaves, they saw a flash of movement on the main road that ran parallel to their logging track, about two hundred yards away. It was a dusty sedan, Collins's car, kicking up gravel as it sped away from the mine area.

"There he goes," Scotty whispered. "He's not wasting any time."

"He's going to search for us," Rick said. "He'll check the road to town, then double back to the plane. The ice-cream man is probably on his scooter doing the same thing." He looked at the tin box sitting on the ground. "We can't carry this around with us. It's too heavy, and if they catch us with it, we're in real trouble."

"So what do we do? Bury it?"

Rick's eyes scanned the woods around them. "Burying it takes time, and we'd leave signs. We need to hide it. Somewhere they'd never look." His gaze fell on a massive oak tree a few yards off the path. Its trunk was thick and gnarled, and a great hollow, black and deep, scarred its base near the roots. "Like that."

They hurried over to the tree. The opening was big enough for a boy to crawl into. Inside, it smelled of damp earth and decay.

"Perfect," Scotty said. "Let's put it way in the back."

Rick hesitated, his hand on the box. "Wait. What if they see our tracks leading here from the path?"

"Who's going to be looking for tracks on an old logging road nobody uses?" Scotty argued. "Come on, Rick. We don't have time to be picky."

Scotty was right. Rick pushed the heavy box deep into the hollow, sliding it over rotting wood until it was lost in the shadows. He then grabbed a few pieces of fallen bark and some dead leaves, arranging them over the opening to make it look as natural and undisturbed as possible. It wasn't a perfect concealment, but it would have to do.

"Okay," Rick said, dusting his hands off. "Step one, complete. The gold is safe. Now for step two."

"Exposing the ghost," Scotty finished for him. "Which means we have to go back."

The idea of returning to the place they had just fled sent a chill down Rick's spine, but he knew it was the only way. "We don't go back to the mine, not directly. We find a new spot to watch from. Belsely said the other ghosts, the shadows, walked in the cornfield on top of the hill. And in the field where our plane is. Let's find a place where we can see both."

They made their way carefully back along the logging road, moving in the direction of the mine but staying deep within the cover of the woods. The sun was getting low, casting long, distorted shadows that made the forest seem alive and watchful. Every snapping twig sounded like a footstep.

They found a spot on the ridge that gave them a commanding view. Below and to their left was the mine entrance and the pool. To their right, the field where their plane sat, a silver shape in the fading light. Directly across the hollow was the mine hill, topped with Belsely's cornfield. They were well hidden in a cluster of dense pine trees, the ground thick with soft needles.

"If anyone comes to set up the spook projector," Rick whispered, "we should be able to see them from here."

"Assuming they haven't set it up already," Scotty countered.

"I don't think so. It's too risky to leave the equipment out. They'd bring it, set it up just before nine, and then take it with them."

They settled in to wait. The sky turned from orange to purple, and the first stars began to appear. The air grew cooler. From their perch, they saw a car's headlights sweep across the field by their plane. The car stopped, and two figures got out. Even from this distance, Rick recognized the stocky build of Collins and the lankier frame of the Frostola man.

"They're checking the plane," Scotty breathed.

The figures walked around the aircraft, their flashlight beams cutting sharp arcs in the gloom. After a few minutes, they got back in the car and drove off, heading back toward the main road.

"They're convinced we flew away," Rick said with relief. "Or at least that we're not at the plane."

"That buys us some time," Scotty said.

They waited. Eight o'clock came and went. The area around the mine was silent and empty. The Sons of the Old Dominion were clearly not holding their feast tonight. The ghost scare, and perhaps news of the daytime disturbance, had kept them away.

At eight thirty, a new light appeared. It wasn't a car. It was a single, bobbing point of light, moving through the trees on the far side of the hollow, coming from the direction of the main road.

"There's one of them now," Rick whispered, pointing.

The light moved steadily, not toward the mine entrance, but up the slope of the opposite hill. It paused near a rocky outcrop that jutted out from the hillside, about halfway up. The light clicked off. A dark figure was silhouetted against the sky for a moment, then it disappeared among the rocks.

"That's it," Rick said, his voice tight with excitement. "That's where the projector has to be. He's not going to the mine. He's going to the place with the best line of sight to the mine."

"So what's the plan?" Scotty asked. "We go over there now?"

"No. We wait. Let him set it up and leave. He'll want an alibi for nine o'clock. He'll turn it on and go somewhere he can be seen, like the diner in town."

They watched the rocky outcrop. A few minutes later, the figure reappeared, scrambled down the hill, and the bobbing flashlight beam retraced its path back toward the road.

"Now," Rick said. "Let's go. But be careful. He might have left a lookout."

"Or a booby trap," Scotty added grimly.

They slid down from their vantage point, crossed the dirt road to the mine, and began to climb the opposite hill, moving as silently as they could. The rocks were larger up close, a jumble of granite boulders that formed a small, natural cave. As they drew near, they heard a faint, low hum.

Rick peered around the edge of the largest boulder. There it was. Propped on a sturdy tripod was a device that looked like a modified slide projector, only much larger, with a powerful lens. Thick cables ran from it to a heavy-duty marine battery. A small, ticking box was attached to one of the cables.

"A timer," Scotty whispered, looking over Rick's shoulder. "He set it to go off at nine."

Rick studied the setup. "Spook projector, just like I said. It projects an image onto the mist that rises from the pool. Simple, but effective." He pointed at the lens. "All we have to do is block this. Put a rock in front of it, or throw a jacket over it. No projection."

"And then what?" Scotty asked. "They'll know someone was here. They'll come looking for us again."

"Exactly," Rick said. "But not right away. They'll come back later tonight, after the ghost show fails. They'll come back for their real work. The midnight search Belsely told us about. With their cart."

A new idea was forming in Rick's mind, a plan far more ambitious than just stopping the ghost. "We're not going to block the lens, Scotty."

"We're not?"

"No. We're going to let the ghost appear. And while it's on, we're going to steal their battery."

Scotty stared at him. "Steal the battery? Why?"

"Think about it. If the lens is just blocked, they'll come back, find a jacket over it, and know someone's onto them. They'll grab the rest of their equipment and disappear. But if the battery is gone, they might think it just died. The projector will go dark mid-show. They'll be confused. They'll have to come back to investigate what went wrong with their expensive machine."

"And when they do, we'll be waiting for them," Scotty caught on, a grin spreading across his face.

"Right. We'll hide and watch. They'll be too busy trying to figure out the projector to notice us. It gives us the element of surprise." Rick checked his watch. It was ten minutes to nine. "We've got to be quick. Wait until the show starts. Once that beam of light is on, it'll help hide us while we move. We'll unhook the cables, grab the battery, and get out of here. We can hide it in the woods and then get back to our lookout spot."

"It's heavy," Scotty warned.

"We'll carry it together," Rick said, his resolve hardening. This was their chance. Their chance to turn the tables on the ghost makers.

They flattened themselves behind the rocks and waited. The ticking of the timer seemed to grow louder in the silence, each click a countdown to action. At precisely one minute to nine, a soft sighing sound came from the valley below as the mist began to rise from the pool, just as it had the night before.

Then, with a sharp click from the timer, the projector whirred to life. A cone of pale, shimmering light shot out from the lens, hitting the rising mist. And there, suspended in the air above the dark water, the Blue Ghost was born. It wavered, solidified, and began its silent, spectral march.

"Now," Rick whispered. "Let's go."
