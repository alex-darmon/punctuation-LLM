CHAPTER V
The Wharf Watcher

Leo found Sam down at the town docks, skipping stones across the flat, grey water. "Anything from Mr. Sterling?" Sam asked without turning around.

"Nothing," Leo said, joining him at the water's edge. "I followed him from the post office to the diner, just like we planned. He just bought a newspaper and a cup of coffee. He's not our man."

"I don't know," Sam said slowly. "He's too interested in that old cannery for a man who says he's just on vacation. Why would a city businessman want to buy a derelict building?"

"Maybe he likes rust," Leo offered. "The bigger question is the humming. Did you talk to old Gable?"

"Just now. He was mending his nets. Said he heard it again last night, louder this time." Sam picked up a particularly flat piece of shale. "He had a new story to go with it, too."

"What now?" Leo asked. "More tales of Captain Hemlock's ghost guarding his treasure?"

"Worse. He says he saw the 'Sea Lights'."

Leo stopped looking for stones and stared at his friend. "You're kidding. No one's seen those in fifty years. They're just a legend."

"That's what I said. But he swears it. Saw them out on the long wharf, bobbing around the cannery doors. He said they were dancing."

This was a new complication. Sam and Leo exchanged a puzzled look.

"Dancing lights?" Leo repeated. "What did they look like?"

"Like fireflies, he said, only bigger and bluer. And they didn't blink. They just floated back and forth, sometimes one, sometimes three of 'em. Said they was lookin' for a way inside the cannery."

"How does he know they were looking for a way in?" Sam demanded.

"Because they'd stop at the doors and the windows. Just hover there for a minute, then move on. He got the notion they were spirits of sailors lost in the '48 storm, tryin' to find shelter."

Sam pondered this. He had no belief in sea spirits, but he did believe Mr. Gable had seen something. "Did he say what time this was?"

"Same time as the humming. A little after one in the morning."

"And the lights were silent?" Sam pressed.

"Not a peep out of 'em, he said. They just danced in the quiet, and the whole time the building was moanin' like it was in pain. Gave him the shivers, so he went inside. Can't say I blame him." A man with a healthy respect for his own skin, Sam thought. Believed in legends, but not enough to get a closer look.

"What do you make of it?" Leo asked.

"I think Mr. Gable saw flashlights," Sam replied firmly. "And our humming isn't a ghost, it's a generator."

"A generator? For what? The place has been without power for decades."

"That's the million dollar question," Sam said. "Someone is inside that cannery at night. They're using a generator for power, and they're using flashlights to get around outside without being seen. The humming is the generator, and the 'Sea Lights' are the flashlights."

"So they're not looking for a way in," Leo concluded. "They're already in, and they're patrolling the outside."

"Exactly. They're making sure no one else gets close while that generator is running." Sam’s eyes gleamed with the thrill of a theory clicking into place. "And I bet Mr. Sterling is one of them."

"His alibi last night was the diner," Leo pointed out. "I saw him myself."

Sam's face fell, his careful theory suddenly full of holes. "You're sure?"

"Positive. I was there until it closed at ten. He was still at the counter reading his paper." Leo paused. "Wait a minute. He didn't have his car. He said it was getting repaired over in Brighton."

Sam looked at Leo, a slow smile spreading across his face. "So he couldn't have driven over to the cannery."

"He would have had to walk," Leo finished. "And that's a forty minute walk, at least. He'd have plenty of time to get there by one in the morning."

"We've got to see for ourselves," Sam decided. "Tonight. We'll hide in the old lobster boat at the end of Gable's pier. It has a perfect view of the long wharf and the cannery doors."

"We'll need blankets," Leo said practically. "The wind off the water gets cold."

It was a few minutes before one when they saw it. A single pinprick of blueish light appeared near the far end of the wharf. It moved without a sound, a tiny, floating star against the black timbers of the cannery.

"There's one," Sam whispered, his heart thumping against his ribs.

Another light joined it, then a third. They moved in a strange, sweeping pattern, just as Gable had described. They paused at the main loading door, their beams playing over the rusted metal and padlock. Then, just as the first low hum began to vibrate through the pier beneath them, one of the lights detached from the group and started moving toward them.

"Down!" Leo hissed.

The boys ducked below the gunwale of the boat. The light grew steadily brighter, its beam cutting a swath across the dark water. Sam held his breath. He had been sure of his theory, sure that these were just men with flashlights. But as the silent, blue glow swept over their hiding place, a cold doubt began to creep into his mind.

A voice, low and rough, cut through the night. "Thought I saw something over here."

The boys froze, and in that instant, the humming from the cannery suddenly stopped.

Silence. The sudden absence of the low thrumming was more alarming than the sound itself. It left a ringing in Sam's ears. He pressed himself lower into the damp bottom of the lobster boat, the smell of old fish and saltwater sharp in his nostrils. The wooden gunwale was a scant few inches above his head.

The beam of the flashlight cut across the night, painting a brilliant, moving stripe on the black water beside the pier. It moved closer, sweeping over the pilings, then hesitated. Sam could hear the soft crunch of boots on the old wooden planks of the pier, just beyond their boat. He held his breath until his lungs ached.

Another voice, farther away, called from the direction of the cannery. "What is it, Cole?"

The man near them, Cole, grunted. "Not sure. Thought I saw a flicker out here. Probably just a reflection on the water." His light beam danced over the surface of the small cove, narrowly missing their boat again. It paused on the weathered hull of their hiding place. Sam's heart hammered. He could almost feel the light through the thin wooden planks.

"Well, get back here," the other voice commanded. "We're shutting down for now. Something's wrong with the feed."

"On my way." The footsteps receded. The flashlight beam swung away, pointing back toward the hulking shadow of the cannery.

Sam and Leo remained motionless for what felt like an eternity. They listened to the footsteps join the others. A low murmur of conversation drifted over the water, too faint to make out the words. Then came the squeak of a heavy, rusted hinge. Silence returned, deeper than before.

Leo let out his breath in a long, shaky sigh. "That was too close."

"Did you hear that?" Sam whispered, slowly pushing himself up. He peered over the edge of the boat. The three blueish lights were gone. The wharf was empty. "They said something was wrong with the 'feed'."

"And the man's name was Cole," Leo added, his voice still hushed. He rubbed his arms against the cold. "What kind of feed? Animal feed?"

"I don't think so," Sam said, his mind racing. "The generator stopped, and then they said there was a problem with the feed. It has to be related. Maybe the power feed. A short circuit, or a fuse blew."

"And that's why the generator stopped?"

"Or they stopped it because of the problem," Sam corrected. "It's a security measure. Something goes wrong, they shut everything down instantly. Including the lights. Did you see how fast they left?"

Leo nodded, looking toward the silent cannery. "They were organized. Like they'd practiced it." He shivered again, and this time it wasn't entirely from the cold. "This is bigger than we thought, Sam. These aren't just a few guys hiding out."

"No," Sam agreed. "They're professionals." He thought about the man named Cole. The voice was rough, but calm. Not panicked. He was a man doing a job. "And Mr. Sterling is still a suspect. He could be Cole, or the other man, or someone we didn't see."

"What do we do now?" Leo asked. "We can't just sit here all night."

"We go home," Sam said decisively. "And we come back tomorrow in the daylight. We need to get a look at that place up close. We'll look for what could have gone wrong with their 'feed'."

They waited another ten minutes, listening to the gentle lapping of the water against the hull. When they were certain no one was coming back, they slipped out of the boat as quietly as they had entered. They crept along the shadows of the pier until they reached the shore, then broke into a brisk walk, not slowing their pace until the lights of the town were visible ahead.

The next morning was bright and clear. The abandoned cannery looked almost peaceful in the sunlight, its weathered gray siding and rusted roof looking merely old, not sinister.

"Looks different in the day," Leo commented as they approached along the pebbled shoreline.

"Everything does," Sam said. "That's why they operate at night."

They had told their parents they were going to explore the tidal pools near the old wharf, which was true enough. Sam carried a small backpack with a bottle of water, a flashlight, and a pair of binoculars.

They started at the long wharf where the men had been walking. Sam knelt and examined the thick wooden planks. "Look here." He pointed to a series of scuff marks and a few faint, muddy footprints near the main loading door. They were tracks from heavy work boots.

"Could be from anyone," Leo said. "Fishermen, kids."

"Maybe. But look at the padlock." Sam pointed to the enormous, rust-pitted lock on the sliding door. "It's old, but the shackle is cleaner than the body. It's been oiled recently." He wiggled it. It was solid.

They walked the perimeter of the building. Most of the windows were boarded up from the inside, thick planks of wood nailed securely over the frames. A few on the second story were just grimy, unbroken panes of glass, too high to see through from the ground.

"No easy way in," Leo observed.

"They don't want any visitors," Sam agreed. He kept his eyes on the ground, scanning for anything out of the ordinary. They rounded the corner to the back of the building, the side that faced away from the water and toward a thick stand of pine trees.

Here, the ground was softer. And it was here that Sam found what he was looking for. "Leo, over here."

Partially hidden under a low-growing juniper bush was a thick, black cable. It was heavy, insulated, and looked brand new. It snaked out from a small, crudely drilled hole in the cannery's foundation wall and disappeared into the dense woods.

"The power feed," Leo breathed.

"Exactly," Sam said, his eyes gleaming. "This must be what they were talking about. And it doesn't go to a power pole. It goes into the woods."

"To the generator," Leo concluded. "They've hidden it back there so no one hears it as easily."

They followed the cable. It was expertly concealed, tucked under roots and covered with pine needles in places. It led them about a hundred yards into the woods to a small clearing. In the center of the clearing was a large, green tarpaulin covering a bulky shape. The smell of diesel fuel hung faintly in the air.

Sam carefully lifted a corner of the tarp. Underneath sat a powerful, modern-looking generator. It was bolted to a heavy wooden pallet. Beside it were several large, red cans of diesel fuel.

"This is a serious piece of equipment," Sam said, impressed despite himself. "This could power a whole house, maybe more."

Leo was looking at the cable where it connected to a junction box on the generator. He pointed. "Sam, look."

A section of the cable near the connection point was blackened, the thick rubber insulation melted away in one spot, exposing the copper wires within. A dead squirrel lay on the ground just beneath it.

"He must have chewed on it," Leo said, wrinkling his nose.

Sam nodded slowly. "And shorted it out. That's what went wrong with the feed. When the squirrel completed the circuit, it must have blown a fuse or tripped a breaker inside the cannery. That's why the generator stopped, and that's why they had to shut down for the night."

"So it was just an accident," Leo said, sounding a little disappointed.

"A lucky accident for us," Sam replied, letting the tarp fall back into place. "It proves our theory. And it gives us an idea of how sophisticated they are. They have a whole system set up."

"And now they have to fix it," Leo said. "They'll probably be back tonight to repair that cable."

A new thought struck Sam. "Which means tonight, the generator might not be running. At least not at first. They'll have to work on the cable out here."

The boys looked at each other. The same idea was in both their minds.

"If they're all out here in the woods," Leo began, "then no one will be on the wharf."

"And no one will be inside the cannery," Sam finished, a determined look on his face. "Tonight's our chance. We're going in."

"How?" Leo asked, his practical nature taking over. "The doors are locked and the windows are boarded."

"Not all of them," Sam said, his gaze turning back toward the hulking gray building. "The ones on the second floor aren't boarded. Just high." He started walking back toward the cannery, his mind already working on the problem. "There's a drainpipe on the west wall. It looks old, but it's fastened securely. I think I can climb it."

That evening, they put their plan into action. They waited until well after midnight, giving the town time to fall completely asleep. Dressed in dark clothing, they made their way back to the cannery, this time avoiding the pier and approaching from the woods at the rear.

They found a spot in the trees where they had a clear view of the clearing with the generator. They didn't have to wait long. At around twelve thirty, two figures emerged from the trees on the far side of the clearing. They were just silhouettes in the gloom, but they moved with purpose. One of them carried a toolbox.

"It's them," Leo whispered from their hiding spot behind a thicket of bushes.

The men didn't speak. They pulled back the tarp and one of them clicked on a small flashlight, keeping the beam pointed directly down at the damaged cable. They set to work immediately.

"That's our cue," Sam whispered back. "Come on. Stay low."

They circled wide around the clearing, moving as silently as ghosts through the undergrowth until they reached the west side of the cannery. The building loomed over them, a black wall against a sky full of stars.

"There it is," Sam said, pointing up. A thick, cast-iron drainpipe ran from the roof all the way to the ground, bracketed to the wall every ten

feet. It was a stout piece of metal, but its surface was slick with years of accumulated grime and sea mist.
"Are you sure about this?" Leo asked, his voice a low tremor of doubt. "It looks slippery. And we're a long way up."
"It's the only way," Sam said, his jaw set. He tested the pipe, pulling on it with his full weight. It groaned, a low metallic complaint, but the brackets held firm. "The men in the clearing are making enough noise with their tools. If I'm careful, they won't hear a thing."
He didn't wait for another argument. He spat on his hands, rubbed them together for grip, and started to climb. It was harder than he had anticipated. The pipe was wider than his grip, and he had to press himself flat against the wall, using the rusted bracket heads as precarious footholds. He moved slowly, methodically, his muscles protesting the strain. A few feet up, his foot slipped on a patch of damp moss. For a terrifying second he dangled by his hands, his boots scraping against the weathered siding.
Leo let out a stifled gasp from below.
Sam froze, listening. In the clearing, he heard the metallic rasp of a file, then a low curse. The men hadn't noticed. He took a deep breath, found his footing again, and continued upward. The world narrowed to the feel of cold iron, the rough texture of the wall, and the burn in his arms and shoulders.
Finally, his fingers brushed against the sill of the second-story window. It was a simple sash window, its glass opaque with dirt. He carefully pulled himself up until he was level with it, balancing precariously on the bracket below. He tried to push the window up. It didn't budge, sealed shut with age and paint.
He fumbled in his pocket for his multi-tool, his fingers clumsy with cold and exertion. He unfolded the small pry bar and worked it into the seam between the sash and the frame. He applied gentle pressure. A loud crack echoed in the night. Sam froze again, his heart hammering against his ribs. He held his breath, waiting. The sounds from the clearing continued, uninterrupted.
He let out the breath he was holding and applied pressure again, this time more steadily. With a long, groaning complaint of wood and old paint, the window gave way and slid upward a few inches. It was just enough. He pushed it the rest of the way up, the noise covered by a sudden clang of metal on metal from the woods.
Wasting no time, he slithered through the opening, landing in a crouch on the dusty floor inside. The air was thick and musty, heavy with the ancient smell of fish oil and decay. Moonlight streamed through the dirty glass, illuminating a space filled with ghostly, draped shapes.
He went back to the window and peered down. Leo was a small shadow looking up at him expectantly. "Now what?" Leo whispered up.
"Stay there. I'll find something."
Sam turned and began to explore the room, his feet shuffling through a thick carpet of dust. The shapes were old machinery, covered in canvas tarps. He found stacks of rotting wooden crates, empty barrels, and tangled piles of old netting. There was no rope. His eyes scanned the room, growing more accustomed to the gloom. He spotted a length of thick electrical conduit running along the ceiling. Hanging from it was a loose coil of heavy-gauge wiring, left over from some long-forgotten installation. It wasn't ideal, but it would have to do.
He dragged a heavy crate under the wire and climbed on top. The wire was stiff and coated in a brittle, cracking insulation, but it felt strong. He managed to unhook it and toss one end out the window.
"Catch!" he hissed.
Leo grabbed the wire. "This will hold?"
"It has to," Sam said.
With Sam bracing the wire from above, Leo made the climb. It was an easier ascent for him, and in less than a minute he was scrambling through the window to join Sam. They were both inside.
"Okay," Leo breathed, wiping his dusty hands on his jeans. "We're in. What are we looking for?"
"Anything that tells us what they're doing here," Sam replied, his eyes scanning the cavernous space. "Let's check this floor first, then head down."
The second floor was a disappointment. It was clearly just for storage. They moved between the shrouded machines, their hopes sinking with every step. It was all just debris from the cannery's past life. There were no signs of recent activity, aside from a few clear paths in the dust where someone had walked.
"Nothing up here," Leo said after fifteen minutes of fruitless searching. "Just junk."
"They must be using the main floor," Sam reasoned. He found a wide staircase at the far end of the room, leading down into blackness. "Careful. They could have left someone on watch."
They crept down the stairs, each step groaning under their weight. They paused after every sound, their ears straining for any sign they weren't alone. But the only sound was the distant, rhythmic scraping from the woods and the faint sigh of the wind outside.
When they reached the bottom, they found themselves on a vast, open floor. This was the cannery's main processing area, and it was here that the scene changed dramatically. The moonlight, filtering through a bank of large, grime-streaked windows facing the wharf, revealed a bizarre juxtaposition of old and new.
The long, stainless-steel gutting tables and conveyor belts were still in place, relics of a bygone industry. But arranged on and around them was an array of modern equipment that was completely out of place. There were workbenches set up with soldering stations, oscilloscopes, and spools of fine wire. Several high-end computers sat on a makeshift table, their screens dark. It looked like a modern electronics lab had been dropped into the middle of a museum.
"Wow," Leo breathed. "You were right. This is sophisticated."
They moved cautiously into the room, weaving between the tables. A faint, clean smell of ozone and solvent mingled with the heavier odor of the old building. They were careful not to touch anything, aware that they were trespassing in a place that was clearly not meant to be seen.
On one of the tables, Sam found a set of blueprints spread out, held down at the corners by heavy tools. He leaned in close, trying to make out the details in the dim light. The drawings were complex, showing a small, torpedo-shaped object. He could just make out some of the labels, printed in neat block letters.
"Leo, look at this," he whispered, his voice tight with excitement. He pointed to the paper. "It says 'autonomous underwater vehicle.' And here... 'acoustic transducer array.' And this section is labeled 'payload bay.'"
"Payload bay?" Leo repeated. "What kind of payload?"
"I don't know. But this isn't for fishing." Sam's mind was racing. An unmanned submarine, built in secret in an abandoned cannery. What for?
"Sam, over here." Leo was standing by another table, where several large marine charts were spread out. He pointed a trembling finger at one of them. It showed the local coastline and the bay. Several positions out in the deeper channel were marked with small red X's. And a route was drawn in pencil, leading from the cannery's wharf out to a point several miles offshore.
"They're planning to send that thing out into the shipping lane," Leo said.
"But why?" Sam murmured, staring at the chart. "What are they doing?"
Before Leo could answer, a new sound cut through the night. The scraping from the woods had stopped. It was replaced by the low murmur of voices, and then the sound of footsteps on the gravel outside.
"They're finished," Sam whispered, his blood running cold. "They're coming back. Hide!"
Panic seized them. They looked around wildly for a place to conceal themselves. The room was too open. A large canvas tarp was draped over a hulking piece of machinery nearby, a relic from the cannery's past. It was their only option.
"Under here!" Sam hissed.
They scrambled under the heavy, dusty canvas, pulling it down around them. They crouched in the dark, their hearts pounding in a frantic rhythm. The space was cramped and smelled of rust and old grease. Through a small gap in the fabric, Sam could just see a sliver of the room.
The footsteps grew louder, stopping right outside the main loading door. There was the sharp, metallic sound of a key sliding into a lock. The lock turned with a heavy clunk. The door creaked open.
Two figures stepped inside. One of them reached for a switch on the wall. A row of bare fluorescent bulbs overhead flickered to life, flooding the massive room with harsh, sterile light.
Sam and Leo froze, squeezed together under the tarp. Sam squinted through his peephole. He could see the men clearly now. One was a stranger, a tall, thin man with a gaunt face and dark, watchful eyes. The other man, the one who had unlocked the door, turned to face the room, and Sam's breath caught in his throat.
It was Mr. Henderson, the quiet, unassuming man who ran the town's hardware store. Sam had bought nails from him just last week. Henderson, who always had a friendly nod for everyone, was part of this.
"All right, let's get the power back on for the system," Henderson said, his voice stripped of its usual mildness. It was crisp and authoritative. "The squirrel chewed it up pretty good, but the new splice will hold."
"We lost a whole night," the thin man grumbled. He had a faint, unplaceable accent. "The deadline is Thursday. We can't afford any more accidents."
"It was bad luck, that's all," Henderson replied, walking over to one of the computer banks and flipping a series of switches. A low hum filled the room as the machines came to life. "We'll make up the time. We just need to finish the final systems check on the delivery vehicle and get it calibrated."
The thin man walked toward the center of the room, his gaze sweeping over the equipment. He stopped near the tarp where the boys were hiding. He was so close Sam could see the scuff marks on his work boots. The man paused, his head cocked as if listening. Sam held his breath, certain they had been discovered.
The man's eyes fell on a wrench lying on a crate next to their hiding spot. He grunted, picked it up, and tossed it into a toolbox. "Keep your tools organized, Henderson. A sloppy workspace leads to mistakes."
"I'm not your apprentice, Klaus," Henderson said without turning around from the computers.
Klaus grunted again and moved toward the main workbench where the blueprints were laid out. This gave Sam a clearer view of what they had been working on. There, sitting in a custom-built cradle, was the object from the blueprints. It was a sleek, dark-gray submersible, about six feet long. It looked less like a scientific instrument and more like a weapon.
"The main power supply is stable," Henderson announced. "Let's run the diagnostic on the transducer."
Klaus leaned over a console, his fingers flying across a keyboard. The boys were trapped. There was no way to get back to the stairs without being seen. They could only wait, praying the men would finish their work and leave.
But they showed no signs of leaving. They settled into a routine, their voices a low murmur of technical jargon as they worked on the submersible. Minutes stretched into an eternity. Leo's leg, cramped beneath him, began to tremble. Sam put a steadying hand on it.
After what felt like an hour, Henderson straightened up with a sigh. "Okay, the diagnostics are clean. We're back on schedule. I'm going to head home. My wife will wonder where I've been."
"I'll stay," Klaus said. "I want to re-check the guidance system programming. Go. I'll lock up."
Henderson nodded and walked toward the main door. This was their chance. If they could just wait until Henderson left and Klaus was distracted, they might be able to slip out.
But then Klaus said, "Wait. Did you hear that?"
Henderson stopped, his hand on the doorknob. "Hear what? It's just the wind."
"No. Upstairs," Klaus said, his voice sharp. "A floorboard. I heard it."
Under the tarp, Leo had shifted his weight, and a loose board under them had emitted a loud, unmistakable creak.
"It's an old building," Henderson said, though he now sounded uncertain.
"I don't like it," Klaus said. He started walking slowly toward the staircase. "I'm going to check."
It was now or never. "Go!" Sam whispered, shoving Leo. "Run for the stairs!"
They burst from under the tarp. For a split second, both men were too stunned to react.
"Hey! Stop!" Henderson shouted, finally finding his voice.
Klaus was closer. He lunged for them, but the boys were already past him, sprinting for the stairs. They scrambled up the steps, their feet thundering on the old wood. They could hear the men shouting and running after them.
They burst into the second-floor storage room and didn't slow down, racing for the faint rectangle of starry sky that was the open window. They didn't have time to be careful. Sam reached the window first, grabbing the heavy wire they had left dangling.
"Get down!" he yelled at Leo.
He didn't wait to see if Leo was following. He swung himself out the window and slid down the wire, the rough insulation scraping his palms raw. He landed hard on the ground below, stumbling but keeping his feet. He looked up just as Leo came sliding down after him.
Leo landed in a heap, and Sam hauled him to his feet. At the same moment, the main cannery door was thrown open and Henderson and Klaus ran out, their figures silhouetted against the light from within.
"There they are! Get them!" Klaus yelled.
The boys didn't need any more encouragement. They turned and fled, plunging into the darkness of the woods. Branches whipped at their faces as they ran blindly, fueled by adrenaline and terror. Behind them, they could hear the men crashing through the undergrowth in pursuit, their angry shouts growing fainter as the boys put more distance between them.
They didn't stop running until their lungs burned and their legs felt like lead. They finally collapsed behind a thick stand of pine trees, far from the cannery, gasping for breath.
For a long time, they just lay there, listening to the pounding of their own hearts. The sounds of pursuit had faded away. They were safe, for now.
"Did you see?" Leo finally panted. "It was Mr. Henderson."
"I saw," Sam said, his mind replaying the scene in the cannery. The lab, the charts, the sleek gray submersible. "This is bigger than we thought, Leo. Much bigger. They're not just smugglers. They're building some kind of underwater drone."
"And they saw us," Leo added, his voice grim. "They know someone was in there. Henderson knows us, Sam. He'll know it was us."
Sam pushed himself into a sitting position, ignoring the sting in his hands and the scratches on his face. Leo was right. Their secret investigation was over. They had stumbled into something dangerous, and now the people involved knew they had been seen. The town was no longer safe. They had the knowledge, but they also had the trouble that came with it. What they were going to do next, he had no idea. He only knew they couldn't do it alone.

It hung in the air above the dark pool, a shimmering, man-shaped figure of pale blue light. It was translucent; the boys could see the rocky face of the mine entrance right through it. The form was indistinct, its edges wavering as though it were made of luminous smoke, but the outline of a head, torso, and limbs was unmistakable. A collective gasp, part awe and part fear, had come from the assembled Sons of the Old Dominion.

"We missed the start," Scotty muttered, his eyes wide.

"Doesn't matter," Rick said, his own gaze fixed on the apparition. "It's here now. And it's exactly what I thought." The light was not coming from the figure itself. It was playing upon the mist that rose from the pool, just as he had theorized. A projected image.

The Blue Ghost drifted slowly from side to side, its form stretching and contracting with the eddies of vapor. It was a convincing, eerie performance. The crowd was utterly silent, mesmerized by the sight.

"Now," Rick whispered, his voice tight. "Let's go."

"Right behind you," Scotty affirmed, his own nervousness betrayed by the clenching of his fists.

They broke from the edge of the crowd and sprinted directly toward the pool. A few shouts of protest and warning followed them.

"Hey, boys, get back here!"

"You'll anger the spirit!"

They ignored the calls. Their feet pounded on the hard-packed dirt, closing the fifty yards to the mine entrance in seconds. They ran right up to the edge of the pool and plunged into the column of mist where the ghost flickered.

The effect was instantaneous and disorienting. The clammy vapor enveloped them, and for a moment the blue light was all around them, a diffuse, sourceless glow. Rick felt the same strange, cold dampness he'd felt before. He blinked, trying to see through the haze. The image of the ghost was gone, because they were inside its medium. Looking back, he could see the startled faces of the crowd, their features washed in the faint blue light reflecting from the mist that clung to the boys' own clothes and hair.

"There's nothing here!" Scotty yelled, his voice muffled by the vapor. He was swinging his arms through the air, finding nothing but cold dampness.

"Of course not," Rick called back. "The projector isn't here! It's somewhere out there!" He pointed back toward the crowd and the road. "We have to find the beam!"

They stepped out of the mist, shivering slightly. The Blue Ghost immediately reformed in the space they had just vacated, as silent and unconcerned as if they had never disturbed it. But their action had broken the spell. The crowd was stirring, murmuring. The boys began scanning the darkness beyond the picnic area, their eyes searching for the telltale cone of a projection beam.

"It's a trick!" someone yelled. "It's a picture!"

"The projector," Rick said, his voice filled with the thrill of discovery. He ignored the clamor from the crowd. "It has to be across the way. Look for the beam, Scotty!"

The ghost continued its silent glide, its performance uninterrupted save for the brief flicker. Its operator was either unaware of them or was determined to finish the show.

The boys' eyes swept the dark hillside opposite them. It was a black wall of trees and undergrowth. Finding a single beam of light in that mess would be next to impossible. The ghost itself was too bright, a source of glare that made looking past it difficult.

"I can't see a thing," Scotty said, squinting. "Just woods."

"It has to be there," Rick insisted. "The light is being projected from somewhere. It has to travel in a straight line. Shield your eyes and look along the edges of the ghost. Try to see the cone."

He cupped his hands around his eyes like blinders, trying to block out the ghostly glow. He scanned the opposite bank, starting from the ground and working his way up. Nothing. The operator must have chosen his position with extreme care. The projector would be hidden, camouflaged. But the light itself couldn't be hidden. It had to cross the space between the projector and the mist.

"There!" Scotty suddenly hissed, grabbing Rick's arm. "Look, up high. To the left of that big oak tree."

Rick followed his friend's pointing finger. At first, he saw nothing. Then he caught it. It was incredibly faint, almost invisible. A slight shimmer in the air, a place where the darkness wasn't quite as absolute. Dust motes and tiny particles of moisture, invisible on their own, were catching the powerful beam of light and revealing its path. It was a perfect, widening cone of light, its base creating the image of the Blue Ghost on the screen of mist. The source was a dark clump of bushes high on the slope.

"Got it," Rick said with grim satisfaction. "Let's go pay the ghost producer a visit."

They skirted the edge of the pool, moving quickly but quietly. The crowd was still buzzing, their attention divided between the spectral actor and the two boys who had defied it. No one seemed to notice them slip away into the trees that lined the road.

"We have to circle around," Rick whispered as they hit the dirt road. "If we go straight up, he'll see us coming."

They ran, their sneakers silent on the dusty surface. They passed the spot where the cars were parked, then plunged into the woods a hundred yards down, beginning a wide, looping climb up the steep hillside. The going was tough in the dark. Branches whipped at their faces, and unseen roots threatened to trip them with every step. They used their pocket flashlights sparingly, clicking them on for only a second at a time to get their bearings.

Finally, they were above and behind the spot Scotty had indicated. They moved with the caution of hunters, placing each foot deliberately. The faint hum of a generator grew louder as they approached, confirming their theory. A projector powerful enough to throw such a clear image would need a significant power source.

They crept up to a dense thicket of rhododendron. A small tent of dark, heavy canvas had been erected within the bushes, completely hiding it from view from below. A narrow slit was cut into the front of the canvas, aimed directly at the mine. Peeking through a gap in the leaves, Rick could see the back of a man's head. He was hunched over a large, metal contraption, his attention fixed completely on the show. The hum was louder here, coming from a small, muffled generator a few feet away.

Rick looked at Scotty and held up a hand, signaling him to wait. He wanted a better look. He eased himself around the side of the thicket. Through the open back flap of the small tent, he could see the operator in profile. The man was thin and wiry, with a nervous, birdlike way of moving his head. He was watching the ghost through a pair of binoculars, his hand resting on the side of the projector.

He wasn't Jethro Collins, and he wasn't the Frostola man. This was a third party. One of the "men" Belsely had seen in the fields.

Rick motioned for Scotty to circle around to the other side. Their plan was simple: get the drop on him from two directions.

Scotty had barely taken two steps when his foot came down on a dry twig. It snapped with a crack that sounded like a rifle shot in the quiet woods.

The operator's head whipped around. His eyes, wide with alarm, locked on Scotty. For a second he was paralyzed. Then he reacted with frantic speed. He shoved the heavy projector, trying to topple it, and scrambled out the back of the tent, away from the boys.

"Stop him!" Rick yelled. He lunged into the tent, not after the man, but to save the evidence. He caught the projector just as it was about to tip over its tripod. It was heavy, and hot to the touch.

Scotty was already in pursuit. The wiry man was surprisingly fast, darting through the trees like a frightened deer. Scotty, a natural athlete, was right on his heels. Rick heard them crashing through the underbrush, their progress marked by snapping branches and Scotty's occasional shout to "Halt!"

Rick steadied the projector, his mind racing. He reached over and flipped a switch on the side of the machine. Down below, the Blue Ghost winked out of existence. He could imagine the collective shout of surprise from the crowd. Then he turned and flicked another switch on the generator, silencing the hum. The woods were suddenly quiet again, except for the sounds of the chase.

He couldn't leave the equipment. It was the most important piece of evidence they had. But he couldn't let Scotty face the man alone. He pulled his powerful flashlight from his belt, aimed it in the direction of the fleeing footsteps, and thumbed it on. The beam cut a brilliant path through the dark woods.

"I'm coming, Scotty!" he yelled.

He heard a crash and a grunt from somewhere ahead. The sounds of running had stopped.

"I've got him!" Scotty called back, his voice strained. "But he's putting up a fight!"

Rick hesitated for only a moment. He couldn't risk the projector being stolen or damaged. The operator wasn't their only problem. The rest of the gang was still out there. He had to assume Collins or the ice-cream man might be nearby, waiting for their ghost-maker to finish his work.

Just then, new lights appeared, bobbing through the trees from the direction of the mine.

"What's going on up there?" a man's voice called. "Who turned out the ghost?"

It was some of the Sons of the Old Dominion, their curiosity having finally overcome their fear.

"Up here!" Rick shouted. "We need help! My friend has the man who was running the show!"

That was all the encouragement they needed. Four men, their faces grim in the flashlight beams, scrambled up the hill toward him. One of them was the man who had warned the boys away from the pool.

"A projector!" the man exclaimed, his eyes falling on the strange machine. "I'll be. You boys were right."

"Can you watch this?" Rick asked urgently. "My friend is just through those trees. He's caught the operator."

"You bet we will," another man, large and burly, said. "Go on. We'll make sure nobody touches this thing."

With the evidence secure, Rick plunged into the woods, following the beam of his own light. He found Scotty about fifty yards away, locked in a desperate struggle with the thin man. They were rolling on the ground, a tangle of limbs in a small clearing. The operator was wiry but strong, fighting with the desperation of a cornered animal.

Rick didn't hesitate. He threw himself into the fray, grabbing the man's flailing arms. Between the two of them, they quickly subdued him. Scotty sat on the man's chest while Rick held his arms pinned to the ground.

"Nice of you to drop in," Scotty panted, a grin spreading across his face despite his breathlessness. "He runs faster than he looks."

The operator glared at them, his chest heaving. "You meddling kids," he snarled. "You don't know what you've gotten yourselves into."

"I think we have a pretty good idea," Rick replied coolly. He looked up as the four members of the Sons of the Old Dominion arrived, their flashlights illuminating the scene.

"Well, I'll be," the first man said, looking down at the captured operator. "Who is he?"

"We don't know his name," Rick said. "But we know he's part of the gang that's been faking this ghost."

The capture was complete. With the help of the men, they tied the operator's hands securely with a belt. The burly man, who introduced himself as Mr. Abernathy, kept a firm grip on the prisoner's arm.

They marched him back to the projector. The crowd from below had started to drift up the hill, drawn by the commotion. They gathered around the thicket, their voices a low hum of questions and exclamations as they saw the projector and the captured operator. The ghost of Spindler's Mine was well and truly busted.

Rick and Scotty took a moment to examine their prize. The projector was a custom-built job. A powerful lamp housing was connected to a series of focusing lenses. A slot held what looked like a glass photographic plate. Rick carefully slid it out.

"Look," he said, holding it up for Scotty to see. It was a positive image of the Continental soldier. When light shone through it, it would project the soldier's image.

"A spook projector, just as you said," Scotty murmured, shaking his head in admiration. "But how did he get the image so clear? And what about the mist?"

"The mist is the key," Rick explained, his excitement growing as he put the pieces together. "The air coming out of the mine is cold and saturated with moisture. When it hits the warmer, humid night air over the pool, the water vapor condenses. It forms a perfect, natural projection screen. The operator just had to wait for the right atmospheric conditions." He pointed to the powerful lenses. "This is a high-quality, long-throw lens system. It keeps the image sharp even at this distance. It's a clever setup. Very clever."

Mr. Abernathy, still holding the sullen prisoner, pushed his way through the crowd. "The sheriff's on his way," he announced. "A few of the fellows went down to call him as soon as we saw what was happening. He'll want to talk to you boys."

"Good," Rick said. He looked at their prisoner. "Maybe he'll be more willing to talk to the sheriff."

The man just sneered at him.

Scotty leaned in close to Rick. "This solves the ghost part," he said in a low voice. "But what about the rest of it? Belsely's story about the men digging in the field with a cart. This isn't the whole story."

"Not by a long shot," Rick agreed. "This was just the cover. The ghost was a distraction, a way to scare people away from this area at night so they could do their real work."

"Searching for something," Scotty mused.

"Exactly. And I'll bet Jethro Collins and the Frostola man are in charge of that part of the operation," Rick said. "This man was just the technician."

They now had a prisoner and the machine he used. They had exposed the hoax that had held the community in a grip of fear. But as Rick looked out over the dark, silent fields stretching away from the mine, he knew their adventure was far from over. The Blue Ghost was gone, but the treasure it had been created to protect was still out there, waiting. And a cornered gang, their plans disrupted, was more dangerous than any phantom. The real hunt was just beginning.

The real hunt was just beginning.

A distant siren confirmed Mr. Abernathy's words. The wail grew steadily louder, cutting through the night and the excited murmur of the crowd. The captured man, still held firmly by the farmer, shot a venomous glare at Rick and Scotty, then turned his face away, his jaw set in a line of stubborn silence.

"Looks like your friend is about to have company," Scotty said to the man, but received only a grunt in reply.

A county sheriff's car, its single red light flashing, pulled up the road and stopped near the mine entrance. A tall, uniformed man with a weathered face and a slight paunch stepped out. He surveyed the scene, his eyes taking in the crowd, the projector in the thicket, and the prisoner held by Mr. Abernathy.

"What's all this, Ab?" the sheriff asked, his voice calm and authoritative.

"Evening, Sheriff Bodarian," Mr. Abernathy said. "Caught us a ghost, or rather, the fellow who was makin' it. These two boys here figured it all out."

Sheriff Bodarian walked over, his boots crunching on the gravelly soil. He gave Rick and Scotty a quick, appraising look before turning his attention to the projector. He nudged it with the toe of his boot. "So this is the spook machine."

"That's it, Sheriff," Rick said. "It's a projector. He was using the mist over the pool as a screen."

The sheriff knelt for a closer look, then stood and faced the prisoner. "All right, son. The show's over. Let's you and me have a talk." He took the man from Mr. Abernathy's grasp, his grip professional and inescapable. He led him toward the patrol car. The man didn't resist, but he didn't cooperate either, moving like a sack of dead weight.

While the sheriff put the man in the back of his car, Rick went back to the projector. He ran his hand over the casing, feeling for any identifying marks. "Scotty, bring a flashlight over here."

Scotty retrieved a light from their small pack and shone it where Rick pointed. There, on the side of the lamp housing, was a small, tarnished brass plate. It was stamped with a name and a series of numbers.

"Lambda Instruments, Arlington, VA," Rick read aloud. "Model 7 Long-Throw Optical Projector. Serial number 004."

"Think that means anything?" Scotty asked.

"It means it's not homemade," Rick replied. "And it means it might be traceable. There can't be too many of these in existence. This isn't something you buy at the corner hardware store."

Sheriff Bodarian returned, a small notebook in his hand. "All right, boys. I need your names and I need you to tell me everything that happened."

Rick and Scotty took turns explaining, starting with their suspicion of the Frostola man, the connection to Jethro Collins, and Belsely's information about the figures in the field. They finished with their theory about the projector and their plan to catch the operator in the act.

The sheriff listened patiently, his expression unreadable. He jotted down a few notes. "Jethro Collins, you say? And this new ice-cream fellow?"

"That's our theory, Sheriff," Rick said. "We think this whole ghost business was just a distraction."

"A distraction for what?"

"We don't know for sure," Scotty admitted. "But a farmer, Mr. Belsely, saw men walking in the fields at night. As many as four of them, including the man in the ghost costume. They were carrying a cart and searching the ground."

The sheriff frowned. "Belsely sees ghosts behind every stump. I've known him for thirty years. But he's an honest man. He tells what he sees, even if he gets the reason for it wrong." He looked from Rick to Scotty. "You think Collins is involved in that?"

"He's the one renting a room to the Frostola man, who we think is part of the gang," Rick explained. "And he's a real-estate agent. He'd know the value of land, and maybe what's under it."

Sheriff Bodarian rubbed his chin. "It's a fantastic story. A projection ghost to cover up a treasure hunt." He looked at the crowd, which was slowly beginning to disperse now that the main excitement was over. "But this projector is real enough. And my prisoner back there isn't talking. Clammed up tight. Won't even give me his name."

"We found a serial number on the projector," Rick offered, reading the information to the sheriff, who wrote it down carefully.

"Lambda Instruments," the sheriff repeated. "I'll have the state police run that down in the morning. A fella named Art Simpson just got a vendor's license for an ice-cream scooter a few weeks ago. Gave Collins's address. I'll have a talk with him. And with Jethro." He paused, his gaze sharp. "I appreciate your help, boys. You did a good thing here tonight, putting an end to this foolishness. But from here on out, this is official police business. I don't want you two getting any more bright ideas. These men, if you're right about them, are playing for keeps. They aren't just pranksters. You understand?"

"Yes, sir," Rick and Scotty said in unison.

"Good. Now get on home. It's late." The sheriff tipped his hat and walked back to his car. A few minutes later, its taillights disappeared down the road, taking the ghost operator with them.

The boys were left standing in the near-darkness, the sounds of the last few Sons of the Old Dominion heading to their cars echoing from the road.

"Well," Scotty said, breaking the silence. "He told us to stay out of it."

"He did," Rick agreed. A slow grin spread across his face. "He didn't say we had to stop thinking, though."

"I figured that's what you were going to say," Scotty sighed, but he was grinning too. "So what are you thinking?"

"I'm thinking about what Belsely saw. Men with a cart, searching the ground. He said they walked in the cornfield on the mine hill, and in the field where our plane is."

"Looking for something," Scotty mused.

"Exactly. But they weren't just wandering around. Belsely said they'd walk, then stop, and bend over. Systematically. Up and down, back and forth. What does that sound like to you?"

Scotty thought for a moment. "Sounds like they were using some kind of detector. Like a guy with a mine detector on a beach."

"My thought precisely," Rick said. "But what were they detecting? It's a big area. They must have been looking for something specific. Something that gives off a signal."

"Like metal?"

"Maybe," Rick said. "But a whole gang and this elaborate ghost hoax just for some metal? It has to be valuable. And the cart Belsely saw. That wasn't for collecting dead soldiers. It was for carrying their equipment. Probably a heavy power source and the detector itself. This was a professional operation."

They started walking back toward the road where they'd left their own gear. The night air was cool, carrying the scent of damp earth and crushed leaves.

"We need more information," Rick said, more to himself than to Scotty. "If we knew what they were looking for, we might be able to figure out where they'll look next."

"Where do we get that information? The prisoner isn't talking, and I doubt Jethro Collins is going to invite us in for a chat about his secret treasure hunt."

"No," Rick agreed. "But Belsely might know more than he thinks he knows. He's lived here his whole life. He knows the land."

"You want to talk to him again? Now?"

"No, it's too late. First thing in the morning. I want him to show us exactly where he saw them. Every single spot." Rick stopped and looked up at the star-filled sky. "This gang had a plan. They had a technician for the ghost, and a crew for the search. Now we've taken out their technician and exposed the ghost. They're not going to just give up and go home. They're going to get nervous. And desperate."

"Desperate men are dangerous men," Scotty quoted.

"Right. Which means we have to be careful. But it also means they might make a mistake."

By the time they reached their camp near the airstrip, they were both exhausted. But sleep didn't come easily. Rick lay awake, his mind racing, fitting together the pieces of the puzzle. The ghost, the projector, Collins, the Frostola man, the mysterious searchers in the night. It all revolved around one central, unknown object. A treasure. But what kind?

The next morning, they were up before the sun. After a quick breakfast of cold cereal and milk, they walked over to Belsely's farm. They found the tenant farmer mending a fence, his movements methodical and sure.

"Morning, Mr. Belsely," Rick called out.

Belsely looked up from his work and straightened, a pleased look on his face. "Heard the news in town already," he said, leaning on a fence post. "Heard you boys smoked out the spook. Knew it had to be somethin' like that. Spirits

don't need wagons," the farmer finished, a dry chuckle in his voice. "And they don't stop to wind a clock, neither. Figured it was men, but didn't know why. Never seen a man glow blue before."

"We think we know why," Rick said. "We captured the man who was running the projector. He's in the hands of the county sheriff now. But the projection wasn't the whole story. It was a cover up."

Belsely's eyes narrowed. "Cover for what?"

"For the men you saw in the fields," Scotty explained. "The dark shadows with the cart. We think they were searching for something."

"That's what I told you," the farmer nodded. "Walkin' and bendin', walkin' and bendin'. Had to be."

"Mr. Belsely, would you be willing to show us exactly where you saw them?" Rick asked. "It could be very important. We need to see the exact pattern of their search, as best as you can remember it."

A look of satisfaction crossed the farmer's weathered face. He was no longer just a curious neighbor who believed in spirits; he was a key witness in a real-life mystery. "Be glad to. Nothin' to do this mornin' that can't wait. Let me put these tools away."

He led them away from his neat farmhouse, past a weathered red barn, and up a gentle slope toward the hill that overlooked the abandoned mine. The corn stood high on either side of the path, rustling in the morning breeze.

"Now, the first few times I saw them," Belsely began, pointing, "it was up here. On top of the mine hill. They'd come out of the woods over yonder, near the old logging road. Never saw 'em on the main road. Always kept to the trees."

He stopped at the edge of the cornfield. "They worked this whole section. Started right here, by this big oak, and went clear over to that line of rocks." He swept his arm in a wide arc. "Back and forth, like plowin', but at midnight. The Blue Ghost would be down at the mine, doin' his nine o'clock show, and these fellas would be up here a few hours later."

Rick looked at the ground. It was just tilled earth between the rows of corn, showing no sign of recent disturbance beyond the usual farm work. "They were very careful," he murmured.

"Then a week or so ago, they moved," Belsely continued, leading them back down the hill and across a pasture. "They started workin' in the field where your plane is." He gestured toward the flat expanse where the boys had established their temporary airstrip. "Same thing. Came out of the woods, pulled that cart of theirs, and walked in rows. Up and down, back and forth. But they didn't cover the whole field. They stuck to the half closer to the woods."

"And they always had the cart?" Scotty asked.

"Every time I saw 'em in the fields. Never saw it down at the mine, though. Just when they was searchin'."

"And you never got a look at their faces?"

"Not a one. Kept my distance. But I can tell you this. They worked quiet. No talkin' that I could hear. Just the sound of that cart wheel squeakin' every now and again, and the sound of 'em movin' through the grass. Spooky, even when you know it's men."

Rick stood in the middle of the field, turning in a slow circle. "Mr. Belsely, do you know of any local stories about this land? Anything about something being buried here?"

The farmer scratched his chin, his gaze distant. "Well, this is all part of the old plantation. Site of a skirmish during the war. Not a big battle, mind you, but men died here. That's why the ghost story got started in the first place." He paused. "There's always been tales. Old Man Hemlock, who owned this land before the state took it, he used to tell a story his grandpappy told him. About a Confederate paymaster. Supposedly, he was carryin' a chest of gold coin to pay the troops over at Manassas. Word came that the Union cavalry was cuttin' across this way, so he buried the chest somewhere on this plantation to keep it safe. Planned to come back for it."

"What happened to him?" Scotty asked, his eyes wide.

"Got himself killed in the very skirmish he was tryin' to avoid," Belsely said with a shrug. "Or so the story goes. Died without tellin' anyone where he'd hid the payroll. People have looked for it for a hundred years. Never found a thing. Most folks figure it's just a story."

"But a good story," Rick said thoughtfully. "Good enough for a man like Jethro Collins to believe. A real-estate man would know all the local legends, especially ones involving treasure on property he might want to buy or sell."

"Reckon he would," Belsely agreed. "He's been mighty interested in this state land for a while now."

The boys thanked the farmer profusely for his help. As he walked back toward his house, Scotty turned to Rick. "A chest of gold coins! This is bigger than I thought."

"It fits," Rick said, his mind working rapidly. "It fits perfectly. Gold isn't magnetic, so a standard mine detector wouldn't be very effective. But a more sophisticated device, maybe an electromagnetic conductivity meter or a ground penetrating radar, could find a void in the earth, or a nonferrous metal mass like gold. That would require a lot of power and sensitive equipment. Equipment you'd need a cart to haul around."

"And a ghost to keep people away while you use it," Scotty added.

"Exactly. But look." Rick paced out the area Belsely had indicated. "They searched this whole western half of the field. Systematically. But they stopped right about here." He drew a line in the dirt with his shoe. "They didn't search the eastern half at all. Why not?"

"Maybe they found it," Scotty suggested.

"I doubt it. Collins and his men wouldn't still be hanging around if they had. The Frostola man would be long gone, not sitting in a jail cell. No, they were interrupted. Our arrival with the plane must have spooked them. They couldn't risk searching out in the open field with us right here."

"So the treasure, if it's here, is in the part they didn't search."

"It's the most logical place to look," Rick confirmed. "They must have had information that narrowed the search to this field. Maybe an old map. But their search is incomplete. We're going to complete it for them."

"With what?" Scotty asked. "We don't have any fancy ground penetrating radar in our back pockets."

Rick grinned. It was a familiar challenge, the kind he liked best. "No, but we have brains, and we have the parts for a BFO."

"A bee-fo? What's that?"

"Beat Frequency Oscillator," Rick explained as they walked back toward their plane. "It's a simple type of metal detector. You use two oscillators, set at nearly the same radio frequency. One is fixed, and the other has a search coil. When the coil passes over a metal object, it changes the inductance, which changes the frequency of its oscillator. The two frequencies then 'beat' against each other, creating an audible tone in a set of headphones. The bigger the object, the greater the change in pitch."

"And you think you can build one of these things?" Scotty asked, impressed.

"I know I can. We've got most of what we need. Wire, batteries. We can cannibalize the portable radio for some of the components. The hardest part will be winding the coils just right, but I think I can manage it."

The rest of the day was a blur of activity. Rick, in his element, spread his toolkit out on a blanket beside the plane. With Scotty as his assistant, he began to assemble the device. He carefully unwound wire from a spare ignition coil, painstakingly re-forming it into two identical loops around a plastic plate he'd cut from a spare container. He then mounted one coil on a long stick, creating the search head. The other he built into a small box containing the radio parts, batteries, and a headphone jack.

He talked as he worked, his fingers moving with practiced skill. "The trick is to get the two oscillator circuits tuned so they are just a few hundred cycles apart. That will give us a low hum in the headphones. Any change, and the hum will rise in pitch."

It was tedious work. Several times, the delicate soldering failed, and they had to start over. The sun was setting by the time Rick finally plugged in the headphones and switched the device on. A low, steady hum filled his ears. He passed a wrench near the search coil. The hum immediately shot up into a high pitched squeal.

"It works!" Scotty exclaimed.

"It works," Rick agreed, a tired but triumphant smile on his face. "It's crude, and it will probably pick up every buried nail and bottle cap in that field, but it's our best shot."

They ate a quick, cold dinner as darkness enveloped the Virginia countryside. The air grew still and heavy with the chirps of crickets.

"We go now," Rick said, his voice low. "Collins and his men might be desperate enough to come back. They might even be watching us. We have to move quietly and stay in the shadows."

"They're not going to be happy if they find us digging in their spot," Scotty noted, checking the flashlight.

"That's why we're not going to let them find us," Rick replied grimly.

They slipped away from the airstrip, carrying the homemade detector, a shovel, and a small pickaxe. They moved like shadows themselves, using the tree line for cover until they reached the edge of the field. The moon was a pale sliver, providing little light. The field was a vast, dark rectangle of emptiness.

"Okay," Rick whispered, putting on the headphones. "According to Belsely, their search stopped about here. We'll start at this edge and work our way east. Just like they did. Back and forth."

He switched on the detector and began to walk, sweeping the coil in slow, overlapping arcs just above the ground. The only sound was the faint swish of the coil through the long grass and the steady, low hum in Rick's ears. Scotty followed a few feet behind, carrying the tools and acting as lookout.

The work was nerve-racking. Every five or ten feet, the hum would jump in pitch, and they would stop. Scotty would dig carefully with the trowel, only to unearth a rusty bolt, a piece of old wire, or a tin can. The field had been part of a farm for a century and a half; it was littered with junk.

"Anything?" Scotty would whisper.

"Just another nail," Rick would reply in frustration, and they would move on.

They worked for over an hour, moving deeper into the unsearched portion of the field. Rick's arms began to ache from the weight of the awkward detector. The tension was making them both jumpy. A rustle in the cornfield sent them diving for the ground, hearts pounding, until a raccoon ambled out, gave them a disgusted look, and scurried on its way.

"This is getting us nowhere," Scotty whispered, wiping sweat from his forehead, though the night was cool. "Maybe the story was just a story after all."

"No," Rick insisted. "Collins is too smart to go to all this trouble for a fairy tale. He had better information. The gold is here somewhere. We just have to..."

He stopped. The hum in his ears had not just jumped, it had soared into a clear, solid, unwavering tone. It was completely different from the brief squeals they had gotten from the small pieces of scrap metal.

"Scotty," he said, his voice tight with excitement. "I've got something. Something big."

He moved the coil back and forth. The tone remained strong and steady over an area about three feet square. "It's right here. And it's not iron junk. The tone is too clean."

Scotty didn't need any more encouragement. He dropped to his knees with the shovel and began to dig. The turf was thick, the soil underneath packed hard. He worked quickly but quietly, placing the excavated dirt carefully on a small tarp they had brought to avoid leaving a conspicuous mound.

Rick kept the detector over the spot. "You're right on it. The signal is getting stronger."

A foot down, the shovel struck something with a dull, wooden thud.

"I've hit it!" Scotty whispered.

Working together, they cleared the dirt away. In the dim beam of their hooded flashlight, they saw the top of a heavy, wooden box, bound with dark, corroded bands of what looked like brass. It was about three feet long and two feet wide. It was ancient, the wood waterlogged and soft in places, but largely intact.

"The paymaster's chest," Rick breathed. "It has to be."

They worked the shovel and pickaxe around the sides, trying to pry it loose. It was heavy and settled deep in the clay soil. As Scotty gave a powerful heave with the shovel, a sudden sound cut through the night. It was the distinct sound of a car door slamming shut, not far away.

The boys froze, every muscle tensed. They listened. Voices. Low and angry. And then the sweep of a powerful flashlight beam cut a swath across the far end of the field.

"It's them," Scotty hissed. "Collins."

"Get down!" Rick ordered.

They scrambled away from the hole, dragging their tools and the tarp of dirt with them. They didn't dare run. They crawled on their bellies toward the deep shadows of the cornfield fifty yards away. They reached the first rows of corn and burrowed into the thick stalks, peering back through the leaves.

Two, no, three figures were walking into the field. One of them, a tall, heavyset man Rick recognized as Jethro Collins, was holding the flashlight. Another man was carrying a professional-looking metal detector, its circular head gleaming in the moonlight.

"I told you they'd be back," Rick whispered, his breath catching in his throat.

"I don't like this, Rick. We're trapped."

"Just stay quiet," Rick whispered back.

The men stopped not far from where the boys had started their own search.

"Spread out," Collins's voice carried clearly in the still night air. "The boss is done waiting. He says the map was clear. It has to be in this section. Start sweeping. And be quick about it."

The man with the detector switched it on. A quiet, rhythmic beeping began as he started his own methodical sweep of the ground, moving in the exact direction the boys had come from. Collins and the third man followed him, their eyes scanning the ground.

Rick's heart sank. Their own tracks, faint as they were, led directly from the edge of the woods to the treasure. The gang's professional detector was a hundred times more sensitive than his homemade one. It was only a matter of time.

They watched, helpless, as the man with the detector moved closer and closer to the partially excavated hole. He was less than twenty feet away. Then ten. Rick could see the faint outline of the disturbed earth from his hiding spot. Surely the men would see it.

The detector operator passed right over the spot. The rhythmic beeping suddenly erupted into a loud, continuous shriek.

"Here!" the man shouted, planting his feet. "Got it! Right here! A big one!"

Collins and the other man rushed to his side, their flashlight beam zeroing in on the ground.

"What is it?" Collins demanded.

"It's big, Mr. Collins. Real big. And deep."

Collins stared at the ground. Even from fifty yards away, Rick could see the greedy, triumphant look on his face in the spill of the light. "Well, don't just stand there," he snarled. "Dig!"

The third man grabbed a shovel from his back and plunged it into the earth, right where Scotty's shovel had been only minutes before. The boys could only watch from the darkness of the cornfield as the villains began to unearth the treasure they had just found.

Rick's stomach twisted into a knot. The metallic scrape of the shovel against the hard-packed earth was the sound of their discovery being stolen from them. He pressed his face against the damp soil between the corn stalks, the sweet, earthy scent filling his nostrils, a smell he knew he would forever associate with failure.

"They'll be done in minutes," Scotty whispered, his voice tight with panic. "We have to do something."

"Do what?" Rick hissed back. "Rush them? It's three against two, and they're not kids."

The third man, a wiry figure who moved with a nervous energy, grunted with effort as he drove the shovel into the ground again and again. Collins stood over him, holding the flashlight steady, a predator watching his kill. The man with the metal detector paced back and forth, sweeping the ground nearby as if expecting to find a whole field of treasure.

"It ain't just dirt," the digger said, his words punctuated by heavy breaths. "Hit something solid."

"Get on with it, then!" Collins barked. "I don't plan to be out here all night."

The shovel scraped against metal. A loud, grating sound that made Rick wince. The digger tossed the shovel aside and knelt, clawing at the dirt with his hands. Collins lowered the flashlight beam, illuminating the object.

Even from fifty yards away, they could see it. A dark, rectangular shape. The top of the iron box.

"That's it," Collins said, a low note of triumph in his voice. "That's the one. Get it out."

The two henchmen worked together, grunting and straining, to pry the heavy box from its resting place of nearly a hundred years. It came free with a sucking sound of earth releasing its grip. They heaved it onto the grass beside the hole. It was about two feet long and a foot and a half wide, its surface crusted with dirt and rust, bound with heavy iron straps.

"Open it," Collins commanded.

"Here, Mr. Collins? What if someone comes?" the wiry man asked, glancing nervously toward the dark road.

"Who's going to come?" Collins sneered. "The ghost? Open it. I want to see."

The man fumbled in his pockets and produced a small crowbar. He wedged it under the lid, near a large, corroded lock. He put his weight on it. The old metal groaned in protest. Then, with a loud crack, the lock broke and the lid sprang open a few inches.

Collins snatched the crowbar and threw the lid back himself. He shone the light inside. Rick and Scotty craned their necks, trying to see, but the angle was wrong. They could see the intent look on Collins's face, however, and the way the light seemed to be absorbed by the contents, not reflected.

"Paper," the detector man said, sounding disappointed. "It's just full of old paper."

"It's not just paper, you fool," Collins said, his voice reverent. "It's the regimental muster rolls. And the payroll ledgers. Everything the boss said would be here. This is worth more than gold to the right collector." He reached in and lifted a stack of what looked like brittle, yellowed documents. "Intact. They're all intact."

Scotty looked at Rick, his face a pale oval in the gloom. "All that work. For nothing."

Rick didn't answer. His mind was racing, connecting the pieces. The ghost story, the battlefield, the search. It wasn't about a lost spirit. It was a cover story. A way to scare people away from the area so this gang could search for historical documents. The ghost appearances were a diversion.

And a diversion, Rick thought with a sudden spark of an idea, was exactly what they needed.

"They're spooked," Rick whispered, his eyes fixed on the nervous henchmen. "Belsely told us everyone around here believes in the ghost. They might act tough, but they're still looking over their shoulders."

"So what? We can't just say 'boo'," Scotty retorted.

"No," Rick agreed. "But they don't know we're here. All they know is that this is a haunted field. We can use that. We just need to separate them."

"How?"

Rick thought fast. The cornfield was their only ally. "They're making a lot of noise. They won't hear us if we're careful. I want you to crawl, fast, about fifty yards that way," he pointed deeper into the field, away from the road. "When you get there, find a dry stalk and snap it. Just one sharp crack. Then wait."

"And what will you be doing?" Scotty asked, his skepticism plain.

"I'm going to be on the other side. When they look your way, I'm going to make a different noise. We'll play tennis with their nerves."

"Rick, this is crazy. If they find us..."

"They won't," Rick said with more confidence than he felt. "They'll be looking for a ghost, not for two boys in the corn. Go on. Now."

Scotty hesitated for a second, then gave a short nod. He turned and began to crawl away, melting into the deep rows of corn like a shadow. Rick watched him go, then turned his attention back to the men. Collins was carefully placing the documents into a leather satchel he had brought.

"All right, let's get the box," Collins said. "We'll take the whole thing."

The two men bent to lift the heavy iron chest. That was Rick's cue. He moved, crawling silently in the opposite direction from Scotty, putting another fifty yards of dark, rustling corn between himself and the center of the action. He found a spot, took a deep breath, and waited.

From across the field came a single, sharp SNAP.

It was louder than Rick had expected in the stillness of the night.

All three men at the hole froze instantly. "What was that?" the wiry man hissed, dropping his side of the box with a thud.

"Probably just a deer," Collins said, but he turned and swept the flashlight beam across the field in the direction of the sound. The powerful white light cut through the darkness, making the cornstalks look like the bars of a cage. It passed harmlessly over the spot where Scotty was hiding.

"Didn't sound like a deer," the other man muttered, his hand straying to his pocket.

"Just get the box," Collins ordered, his voice losing some of its bluster.

As they bent down again, Rick made his move. He didn't snap a stalk. Instead, he took a deep breath and let out a low, mournful moan. He pitched it low, letting the sound trail off into what could have been the wind whistling through the corn.

The effect was instantaneous.

The men shot upright. "Did you hear that?" the wiry one yelped, his voice cracking.

Collins swung the flashlight beam wildly toward Rick's position. "Who's there?" he shouted, his voice echoing in the open space. "Show yourself!"

The beam swept back and forth. Rick ducked low, his heart hammering against his ribs. The light passed right over him. He held his breath.

A new sound came, from Scotty's direction again. It wasn't a snap this time. It was a rhythmic rustling, the sound of something moving steadily through the corn, getting closer. Shh-shh-shh-shh. Scotty was running his hand along the dry leaves of the stalks as he moved.

"It's in the corn!" the detector man whispered, his eyes wide in the faint moonlight. "It's coming this way."

"It's the wind, you idiots," Collins snarled, but he took a step back from the cornfield's edge. He was trying to sound brave, but Rick could see the tension in his shoulders.

Rick decided to press their advantage. He picked up a small clod of dry earth and tossed it in a high arc. It landed with a soft thud in the grass about twenty feet to the left of the men.

The wiry man jumped. "It's circling us!"

"This is crazy," the other man said, backing toward the road. "I don't like it, Mr. Collins. They say he walks these fields. The Blue Ghost."

"There's no such thing as ghosts!" Collins roared, but his voice was drowned out by a new sound, the most brilliant part of Scotty's improvisation.

From deep in the field came a faint, metallic ringing. Then again. Clang. Pause. Clang.

Rick stared into the darkness, puzzled for a moment, then grinned. Scotty had found their discarded metal detector. He was tapping the aluminum tubing with his shovel. The sound was alien, unnatural. It wasn't the sound of an animal or the wind. It was the sound of something made of metal, moving in the dark.

That was the final straw.

"I'm not sticking around for this," the wiry man said, and he turned and scrambled up the slight embankment to the road.

"Me neither," the other henchman said, dropping his flashlight and running after him.

"Get back here, you cowards!" Collins bellowed. "It's a trick!"

But his men were gone. They heard the sound of a car door opening and slamming shut. Collins stood alone, his flashlight beam cutting a useless path through the corn. He glared at the iron box on the ground, then at the leather satchel in his hand. He looked back toward the road, then at the rustling cornfield. He was torn.

Rick held his breath. If Collins was brave enough, or greedy enough, he might try to drag the box himself.

Scotty's rustling sound started again, this time seeming to come from a different place, closer to the road, as if cutting off Collins's retreat. It was a masterful touch.

Collins made his decision. Cursing under his breath, he clutched the satchel of documents to his chest, turned his back on the heavy iron box, and scrambled up to the road. A moment later, a second car door slammed. An engine roared to life, tires squealed on the pavement, and a pair of red taillights sped off into the darkness, heading back toward town.

Silence descended on the field. The only sound was the chirping of crickets and the pounding of Rick's own heart.

He waited a full minute, listening, making sure they were truly gone. Then he stood up, his legs shaky. "Scotty?" he called out softly.

"Over here." Scotty's voice came from the darkness. A moment later, he emerged from the corn, carrying his shovel and the homemade metal detector. "Did you see them run? They ran like scared rabbits!"

"You were brilliant," Rick said, clapping him on the shoulder. "The tapping on the detector tubing was genius."

"I figured it would sound stranger than just snapping twigs," Scotty said, grinning. "Did we get them? Did they leave it?"

They walked together toward the hole. The beam from Rick's flashlight found the spot. The deep indentation in the grass was there. The freshly turned earth was there. But the iron box was gone.

Scotty's grin vanished. "What? But... I saw them. Collins was alone. He couldn't have carried it."

Rick shone the light around. Then he saw it. The crowbar, lying in the grass. And next to it, the heavy iron lid of the box. Collins hadn't left the whole thing. In his haste, he had done the next best thing. He had dumped the contents of the box into his satchel.

"He didn't take the box, Scotty," Rick said grimly, shining the light into the empty hole. "He took what was inside it. He took the muster rolls."

Their moment of triumph evaporated, replaced by the cold reality of their loss. They had scared the thieves away, but the thieves had gotten exactly what they came for.

"We failed," Scotty said, slumping down on the edge of the hole. "They won."

"No," Rick said, his mind already working, shifting from the panic of the moment to the problem that lay ahead. "They didn't win. They just scored a point." He walked over to the edge of the field, near the road, his eyes scanning the ground. He bent down. "They were in a hurry, Scotty. And people in a hurry get careless."

"What are you looking for?"

"Tire tracks," Rick said. He shone the light on a set of deep impressions in the soft shoulder of the road. A heavy car. The tread was distinctive, with a repeating V-pattern. "And this." He pointed the beam at a small puddle of dark liquid. He touched it carefully with his finger, then sniffed. "Oil. Looks like a fresh leak."

He stood up and walked back toward Scotty. "And I got a look at the car as it pulled away. It was a big, dark sedan. A Cadillac or maybe a Lincoln. Late model."

Scotty looked up, a glimmer of hope returning to his eyes. "You think we can find it?"

"A big, dark, fancy sedan leaking oil, with Jethro Collins inside it? In a town this size?" Rick knelt and started gathering their tools. "They think they got away clean. They think the only people who know about their little treasure hunt are their own men and a bunch of superstitious locals who'll blame it on a ghost."

He handed Scotty his shovel. "They're wrong. They have the documents, but we have something they don't know about."

"What's that?" Scotty asked, getting to his feet.

Rick looked from the empty hole to the dark road leading back to town. A determined set came to his jaw.

"We have the truth," Rick said. "And we know who they are. The hunt isn't over, Scotty. It's just getting started."

Rick stood, his silhouette framed by the faint starlight against the dark line of trees. He felt the familiar surge of energy that came not from victory, but from the sudden clarity of a well-defined problem. The empty hole in the ground was no longer a symbol of failure; it was the starting point of a new, more dangerous race.

"Let's go," he said, his voice low and tight. "We're burning moonlight."

They gathered their shovels and the crowbar, their movements quick and silent. The night was still, the only sounds the chirping of crickets and the distant hum of the highway. As they walked back toward the road where they had hidden their jeep, Scotty broke the silence.

"A Cadillac or a Lincoln, you said? Leaking oil? That's not much to go on, Rick. There must be dozens of cars like that, even in a small town."

"Not as many as you'd think," Rick replied, his mind already piecing together the next steps. "And not many are driven by a man who fits Jethro Collins's description, making late-night trips to a supposedly haunted mine. The oil leak is our trail. Like a string through a maze. We just have to follow it."

They reached the jeep, a rugged, mud-spattered vehicle that looked out of place on the quiet country road. They threw the tools in the back and climbed in, Rick taking the wheel. He started the engine, the sound unnaturally loud in the stillness, and turned onto the paved road, heading back toward the town of Spindrift.

"What do you figure is on those muster rolls?" Scotty asked, settling into the passenger seat. "Why go to all this trouble? A ghost, a projector, digging up a field in the middle of the night... for a list of names?"

"It has to be more than that," Rick reasoned, keeping his eyes on the road ahead. "Belsely said the ghosts were searching the ground. Collins and his man were digging. They're looking for something specific. My guess is the muster rolls are a map."

"A map? Written on a list of soldiers?"

"Or coded into it," Rick elaborated. "Think about it. This area was a hotbed of activity during the Civil War. John Mosby and his Rangers operated all through here. They were famous for lightning raids, capturing Union payrolls, supplies, intelligence. What if they captured something they couldn't carry, something they had to hide?"

Scotty whistled softly. "A treasure."

"Exactly. A Confederate treasure. Maybe gold, or important documents. Mosby was a clever man. He wouldn't draw a map with an X on it. He'd hide it in plain sight. What's more common for a military unit than a muster roll? He could have used names, dates, or service records to create a cipher. A code that only he or his trusted officers could read."

"And Collins figured it out," Scotty finished. "Or part of it. He knew the rolls were buried here. Now he has them, and he's going to find the treasure."

"Not if we find him first," Rick said grimly. He slowed the jeep as they entered the outskirts of Spindrift. The town was asleep. Streetlights cast long, lonely pools of yellow on empty sidewalks. "Now for the hard part. We need to find that oil trail."

He turned off the main street and began a slow, methodical search, cruising down one block and up the next. He drove with his door open, leaning out to scan the asphalt with his powerful flashlight beam. Scotty did the same on his side. It was tedious, frustrating work. For twenty minutes, they found nothing but the usual stains that mark any town street.

"Maybe he went the other way," Scotty suggested, his voice showing the strain of a long night.

"I don't think so. His real-estate office is in the center of town. His rooming house, where the Frostola man is staying, is nearby. It makes sense he'd come back here."

Rick turned onto a quiet side street lined with old Victorian houses and darkened storefronts. Halfway down the block, his flashlight beam caught a series of small, dark splotches on the gray pavement.

"Here!" he exclaimed, stopping the jeep. "Scotty, look."

Scotty leaned out, his own light joining Rick's. The drops were fresh, glistening under the beams. They were spaced a few feet apart, a clear trail leading down the street.

"You were right," Scotty said, a note of admiration in his voice. "A string through the maze."

Rick put the jeep in gear and followed the trail at a crawl. The oil spots led them past Collins's darkened real-estate office. The trail continued for another block, then made a sharp right turn into a narrow, unpaved alleyway that ran behind the buildings.

"He's trying to be cute," Rick muttered. "Doesn't want to park his big fancy car out front."

He killed the jeep's engine and headlights at the mouth of the alley. The two boys got out, taking their flashlights but being careful not to switch them on yet. Their eyes adjusted slowly to the deeper darkness of the alley, a canyon of brick and dumpsters.

The oil trail was harder to follow on the dirt and gravel, but it was still there. They moved forward step by step, their sneakers making almost no sound. The alley ended at a high wooden fence with a wide gate. The gate was closed but not locked. Just to the side of it, a single light burned over a door set into the back of a brick building.

Rick put a hand on Scotty's arm, signaling him to stop. They could hear voices from behind the fence. Muffled, but clearly agitated. Rick pointed to a stack of old wooden crates piled against the side of a neighboring building. Silently, they climbed up, until they could peer over the top of the fence into the yard beyond.

The yard was a gravel-covered space behind what looked like a mechanic's garage. Parked in the center of it was a long, black Lincoln sedan. A puddle of oil was already forming under its engine block. Three men stood near the car's open trunk, illuminated by the single bare bulb over the door.

One was Jethro Collins, the real-estate agent. He was dabbing his forehead with a handkerchief, his face pale and strained. The second man was big and burly, with a thick neck and a surly expression. He wore the white uniform of an ice-cream vendor. It was the Frostola man.

The third man was a surprise. He was thin and wiry, with nervous eyes and ink stains on his fingers. He was holding a sheaf of old, yellowed papers in his hands: the muster rolls. The boys had never seen him before.

"I tell you, it was him!" the thin man was saying, his voice high and jittery. "The ghost. The Blue Ghost. Just like Belsely described. It came right at me."

"Shut up, Miller," the Frostola man growled. His name, Rick guessed, must be Henderson, based on the name stitched over his pocket. "It wasn't a ghost. It was a trick. Those kids, probably. They were snooping around earlier."

"A trick?" Miller squeaked. "Did you feel that cold? Hear that sound? That wasn't a trick!"

"It doesn't matter what it was," Collins cut in, his voice sharp and commanding. "We have the rolls. That's all that matters. Now, did you find it? The key?"

Miller, the thin man who had apparently played the part of the ghost, nervously shuffled through the papers. "I'm looking, Mr. Collins. It's not easy. Mosby's clerk had terrible handwriting. It says here... let's see... the roster for Captain Montrose's company. There's a note in the margin about quartermaster stores."

"Forget the stores," Collins snapped. "Look for the notations. The ones we talked about. A reference to 'Pharaoh's Gold' or the 'Widow's Tithe'."

"Pharaoh's Gold," Scotty whispered to Rick, his eyes wide.

Rick nodded, his mind racing. It was a treasure. A big one, if the name was any indication.

"Here's something," Miller said, his finger tracing a line on one of the fragile pages. "Under the entry for a Private John Carter, service terminated October 1864. There's a notation. 'Sent to the widow's keeping'."

"That's it!" Collins said, stepping closer. "The Widow's Tithe. That's the one. What else does it say?"

"Just coordinates," Miller replied. "But they're not latitude and longitude. It's a series of numbers. 'Two hundred paces north from the weeping angel, then east to the surveyor's stone, one hundred paces.' It sounds like a riddle."

"Not a riddle," Collins said, a thin, greedy smile spreading across his face. "It's a map. The 'weeping angel' is a statue in the old Spindrift cemetery. I researched it weeks ago. It marks the grave of a Confederate general's wife. She was the 'widow'. The surveyor's stone is an old boundary marker on the edge of the cemetery property. We've got it, Henderson. We've finally got it."

Henderson, the Frostola man, clapped his big hands together. "So we go there now? Get the gold?"

"Not now, you fool," Collins hissed. "It's almost dawn. We'll be seen. We'll go tomorrow night. We'll take the tools and be discreet. No one will be in the cemetery at that hour. We'll get the gold and be in Richmond before anyone even knows it's gone." He took the muster rolls from Miller. "You did well, Miller, despite your nerves. The ghost projector was a fine piece of work."

"It's a standard rear-projection unit," Miller said, regaining some of his composure. "I just adapted it for a portable power source. The blue color came from a simple theatrical gel. The moaning sound was a looped recording I played through a directional speaker. Simple, really."

"Simple but effective," Collins said with satisfaction. He carefully placed the muster rolls into a leather briefcase. "It kept the locals terrified and gave us all the cover we needed. Now, both of you go home. Henderson, get that oil leak fixed first thing in the morning. I don't want you leaving a trail all over the county. Meet me back here tomorrow, after sunset."

Henderson and Miller nodded. Miller scurried out a back door of the garage, and Henderson popped the hood of the Lincoln to get a look at the engine. Collins snapped the briefcase shut, a look of pure triumph on his face.

Rick and Scotty looked at each other. They had everything. The who, the what, the where, and the when.

Slowly, carefully, Rick began to back down from the crates. His foot slipped on a loose board. The crate shifted with a loud, scraping groan.

Instantly, every head in the yard snapped toward the sound.

"What was that?" Collins snarled.

Henderson slammed the hood of the car. "I'll check."

"No time to be quiet now," Rick whispered urgently. "Go!"

They scrambled down from the crates and bolted down the alley. Behind them, they heard the rattle of the gate being thrown open and an angry shout.

"Hey! There's someone there!"

They didn't look back. They sprinted the length of the alley, their lungs burning, and piled into the jeep. Rick fumbled with the keys, his fingers suddenly clumsy. The engine turned over, coughed, and died.

"Come on, come on," Scotty breathed, staring back toward the mouth of the alley.

A heavy figure appeared, silhouetted against the distant streetlight. Henderson. He saw the jeep and started running toward them, a tire iron now gripped in his big fist.

Rick turned the key again. The engine roared to life. He slammed the jeep into gear, spun the wheel, and stomped on the accelerator. The tires shrieked on the pavement as the jeep shot away from the curb. Rick risked a glance in the rearview mirror. Henderson stood in the middle of the road, shaking the tire iron at them, his face a mask of fury.

They didn't slow down until they were several blocks away, their hearts pounding in their chests.

"That," Scotty said, letting out a long, shaky breath, "was entirely too close."

"Tell me about it," Rick agreed, his own hands tight on the steering wheel. He drove them out of town, taking a different road back toward the state park where they had set up their small, mobile laboratory in a secluded campsite. He didn't speak again until they were safely inside the canvas tent, with a gas lantern hissing softly on a folding table.

"Okay," Rick said, pacing the small space. "Let's review. Jethro Collins is the leader. Henderson, the Frostola man, is the muscle. And a man named Miller is their technical expert, the man who built and operated the ghost."

"And they're after Confederate gold," Scotty added, "hidden in the Spindrift cemetery. They're going for it tomorrow night."

"Right. The muster rolls weren't the treasure, they were the key. A coded map. 'Two hundred paces north from the weeping angel, then east to the surveyor's stone, one hundred paces.' We have to beat them to it."

Scotty looked skeptical. "How? We can't just go marching into the cemetery with shovels. The sheriff would be on us in a minute. And if we tell the sheriff what we know, what's our proof? A story about a ghost that isn't real and a conversation we overheard while trespassing? Collins would deny everything. He's a respected businessman. We're just a couple of kids on vacation."

Rick stopped pacing and leaned over a topographical map of the county that was spread on the table. He found the town of Spindrift, and the small square that marked the old cemetery.

"You're right," Rick admitted. "We can't go to the law. Not yet. And we can't let them get that gold. Collins isn't going to turn it over to a museum. He's going to melt it down and sell it. History will be lost forever."

"So we become treasure hunters ourselves," Scotty said. "Tonight."

Rick shook his head. "Too risky. They're on high alert now. They know someone was watching them. They might even post a guard. We need a better plan."

He tapped the map. "Their plan is to dig. That's slow, noisy, and obvious. We have an advantage they don't."

"What's that?"

"Science," Rick said, a familiar glint in his eye. "If there's a large amount of buried metal, we don't need to dig for it blindly. We can find it electronically." He walked over to a heavy footlocker in the corner of the tent and opened it. Inside, nestled in foam padding, was a collection of dials, wires, and carefully machined metal components. "We have my new experimental metal detector."

Scotty looked at the device. "I thought that thing was for hunting meteorites."

"It's a wide-range induction-balance detector," Rick corrected him. "It can be calibrated to ignore small, ferrous objects like nails or belt buckles and focus on large masses of non-ferrous metal. Like gold."

A slow grin spread across Scotty's face. "So while they're planning to dig up half the cemetery tomorrow night, we can pinpoint the exact spot."

"Precisely. We'll go in early tomorrow, before dark, when people are allowed to be in a cemetery. We'll look like we're just visiting. We can disguise the detector coil as... I don't know, maybe a wreath holder or something. We'll get a precise location. Then we can decide our next move. We'll know where the gold is, and they won't."

Rick began assembling the detector, his movements sure and efficient. The near-disaster in the alley was forgotten, replaced by the thrill of the new challenge. They were no longer just reacting to the thieves. They were taking control.

"They have the map," Rick said, tightening a connection with a small screwdriver. "But we have the science. The hunt isn't just about who gets there first anymore, Scotty."

"What's it about then?" Scotty asked, watching his friend work.

Rick looked up from the intricate device, his face illuminated by the lantern's glow. "It's about who's smarter."
