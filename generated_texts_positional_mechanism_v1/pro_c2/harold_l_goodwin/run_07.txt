"They'll be gone by morning." The tide lapped softly against the old pilings. Leo shivered, though the night air was mild. The thrill of their discovery was wearing off, replaced by the damp chill coming off the water. He leaned his head against a cool, iron bollard. Soon he was nodding.

A sharp nudge from Sam brought him awake. "I'm up," he mumbled, rubbing his eyes. "What is it? See anything?"

"It's past midnight. I was about to call it a night and head home." Sam's voice was a low rasp. "A truck just pulled up to the old cannery pier. Doused its headlights half a mile out. Something's not right."

"Let's get a look," Leo said, suddenly wide awake. He slid off the bollard, Sam right behind him. They crept along the wharf, using stacks of old lobster pots for cover. Leo felt a familiar knot of excitement and fear tighten in his stomach. Why would a truck come here, to the abandoned waterfront, in the dead of night? This could be it. They might finally have stumbled onto something real.

Sam pointed. "Through there." The younger boy darted across a patch of moonlight and into the deep shadow of the cannery's main building. They moved with the practiced stealth of boys who spent their summers exploring forbidden places. Their sneakers made no sound on the weathered planks. Sam was better at this, his movements fluid and certain. Leo followed, his heart thumping a nervous rhythm against his ribs.

Soon they were pressed against the cannery wall, the smell of rust and salt thick in the air. They edged along the building until they reached a grimy window. "Give me a boost," Sam whispered. Leo cupped his hands, and Sam scrambled up for a quick look before dropping silently back to the ground.

"Two of them," he reported. "They're moving crates from the truck into a boat tied up at the end of the pier. Can't see what's in them."

"We need to get closer," Leo insisted. He pointed to a dark doorway a few yards ahead. "In there. We can get to the loading bay on the other side."

Sam hesitated, then nodded. They slipped through the doorway into a vast, cavernous space. The air inside was heavy and still. Moonlight slanted through holes in the roof, illuminating ghostly shapes of machinery long silent. Leo led the way, stepping carefully around piles of debris. He put his foot down on what he thought was solid flooring.

There was a sharp crack, loud as a pistol shot in the silence. The rotten wood gave way. Leo flailed, his leg plunging through the floor up to his hip. Pain shot up his shin. From the pier came a sudden silence, then a low voice. "What was that?"

"Rats," another voice answered, but it lacked conviction.

"Get me out!" Leo hissed, struggling to pull his leg free. The wood held it fast.

Sam grabbed his arms. "Stop moving! You'll just get more stuck." He strained, pulling with all his might, but Leo was wedged tight. Footsteps crunched on the gravel outside the cannery. They were coming.

"The back way!" Sam whispered frantically, letting go of Leo. "I'll get help!"

But it was too late. A powerful beam from a heavy flashlight pinned Sam against a rusted conveyor belt. "Well, well. Look what we have here," a cold voice said. A tall, lean man stepped into the cannery, another right behind him. Sam stood frozen, like a deer caught in headlights.

The first man turned the light on Leo, still trapped in the floor. He chuckled without humor. "Looks like you've got a bit of a problem, kid." He walked over and grabbed Leo by the collar, hauling him free of the broken boards with a single, rough yank. Leo cried out as his leg scraped against the splintered wood. The man shoved him toward Sam.

"Just a couple of kids," the second man grunted. "What do we do with them?"

"Can't let them go," the first man said. "Not yet." He took a coil of rope from his belt. "They're about to get a lesson in minding their own business. The cold storage room."

They were marched to a heavy, insulated door and pushed inside. The room was small and smelled of stale brine. The first man was brutally efficient. He bound Leo's wrists and ankles with the rope, then did the same to Sam, leaving them back to back in the center of the concrete floor.

"They won't be going anywhere," the man said. "We'll be gone in twenty minutes. By the time someone finds them, we'll be a hundred miles away."

"What if no one finds them?" the other man asked, his voice nervous.

"The door doesn't lock from the outside. They'll get out. Eventually. Let's go." The heavy door swung shut, plunging them into absolute, suffocating darkness. The sound of the truck engine starting up was faint, then it faded completely.

"Leo? You okay?" Sam's voice was shaky.

"I've been better," Leo admitted, his leg throbbing. "This is a real mess."

"At least we didn't get thrown in the water," Sam said, trying for a joke.

Leo felt the rough floor with his fingers. "The knots are tight. I can't get any slack." He strained, pulling his arms until the rope bit into his wrists. "It's no good."

"Wait," Sam said. "My back is against some kind of pipe. I think there's a valve handle on it. It's got a sharp edge." There was a pause, then the sound of Sam squirming. "I'm trying to rub the rope against it."

Silence stretched in the cold dark, broken only by Sam's grunts of effort and the faint, rhythmic scrape of rope against metal. Leo's hopes began to sink. His muscles ached from his awkward position. Then, a new sound. A soft tearing noise.

"Did you get it?" Leo whispered, his heart leaping.

"Almost. The first strand just broke." There was a renewed burst of scraping. "It's working, Leo! It's working!" A moment later, there was a muffled snap. "I'm free," Sam breathed. "My hands are free."

Leo felt a surge of relief so strong it made him dizzy. In the darkness, he heard Sam fumbling with the knots on his ankles. Presently, Sam's hands were on the ropes that bound Leo's wrists.

"Hold still," Sam whispered, his fingers probing the complex knots. "This is going to take a minute."

"Don't worry," Leo said, a grin spreading across his face despite the pain in his leg. "We've got all night."

Sam worked his newly freed hands, rubbing his wrists to get the circulation back. In the pitch blackness, Leo could hear the soft chafing sound and Sam's quiet hiss of discomfort.
"Okay," Sam's voice came out of the dark. "Your turn. Don't move."
Leo held himself perfectly still, the cold of the concrete floor seeping through his jeans. He felt Sam shift position, moving until their backs were pressed together again. Then came the delicate, probing touch of Sam's fingers on the ropes binding Leo's wrists.
"These guys weren't kidding around," Sam muttered. "This is a sailor's knot. Or a fisherman's, I guess."
"Can you get it?" Leo asked. The hope that had surged through him was beginning to feel fragile. His leg was a dull, throbbing weight, and every minute spent in the cold seemed to drain more of his strength.
"I think so," Sam replied. His voice was tight with concentration. "It's like one of those puzzles. You just have to find the right part to pull."
Leo listened to Sam's breathing, the occasional grunt of effort. He could feel the slight tugging and pulling as his friend worked on the intricate knot. The air was thick with the smell of old fish and salt, a damp, penetrating odor that seemed to cling to the back of his throat. He tried to focus on something else, anything besides the cold and the pain. He thought about the men, their rough voices and the callous way they had been handled. Why here? What was so important in a derelict cannery?
"I feel a loop," Sam said, his voice a little breathless. "I'm trying to work some slack into it."
Leo felt a subtle loosening around his right wrist. It was barely perceptible, but it was there. His heart gave a little jump. "It's working," he whispered, a new energy flowing into him.
"Don't get excited yet," Sam warned. "I almost dropped it."
There was another long minute of tense silence, filled only by the tiny, scraping sounds of rope on rope. Leo imagined Sam's fingers, numb with cold, working by feel alone in the complete absence of light. It was an impossible task.
Then, with a sudden give, the pressure on his wrists lessened dramatically.
"Got it!" Sam exclaimed. "The main knot is undone."
It took another thirty seconds for Sam to unwrap the coils of rope. The moment his hands were free, Leo began rubbing them together, trying to force life back into his numb fingers.
"The ankles are next," Sam said, already moving down. The knots on Leo's ankles were simpler, and in less than a minute he was completely free.
"Thanks, Sam," Leo said, his voice thick with relief. "I owe you one."
"You can buy me a hot dog later," Sam said. "Let's get out of here first."
Leo pushed himself up, leaning on his good leg. When he tried to put weight on the other, a sharp, fiery pain shot up from his calf. He grunted and nearly fell.
"Easy," Sam said, grabbing his arm to steady him. "How bad is it?"
"It's not great," Leo admitted through gritted teeth. "But I can stand on it. I think." He tested it again, carefully. The pain was still there, but he found he could bear his weight on it for a second or two. It would have to be enough.
"Okay," Sam said, his voice low and serious. "The guy said the door doesn't lock from the outside. Let's see if he was telling the truth."
With Sam supporting most of his weight, they hobbled through the darkness. Leo held one hand out in front of him until his fingers brushed against the cold, metal surface of the insulated door. He fumbled for a handle. There wasn't one.
"It must be a push bar," Sam whispered.
Leo felt his way down the door. His hand found a long, flat metal bar. He gave it a tentative push. Nothing happened. He pushed harder, putting his shoulder into it. The bar moved with a rusty groan, and he heard the click of a latch. A thin line of gray light appeared at the bottom of the door.
"Let's go," Leo urged.
They pushed the heavy door open just enough to slip through, then eased it shut behind them. The sound of the latch clicking back into place seemed as loud as a gunshot in the silent building.
They were standing in a vast, cavernous room. Faint moonlight trickled through rows of high, grimy windows, illuminating a scene of industrial decay. Long conveyor belts snaked between hulking pieces of machinery, all of it silent and coated in a thick layer of dust and rust. The air here was still and cold, but it felt cleaner, fresher than the suffocating air of the storage room.
"They're gone," Sam whispered, stating the obvious. The silence was absolute. There was no sound of a truck engine, no voices. Just the faint sigh of the wind outside.
"Where do you think they went?" Leo asked, his voice barely audible. He leaned against a rusted metal vat, taking the weight off his leg.
"Away," Sam said. "And that's good enough for me. We need to get to our boat and call the sheriff."
"Wait," Leo said. "What were they doing here? Why go to all this trouble, tying us up and leaving us in that freezer?"
"I don't care, Leo," Sam said impatiently. "I just want to go home."
"But don't you want to know?" Leo persisted. "They were loading something onto that truck. They were in a hurry. People in a hurry make mistakes."
Sam hesitated. He looked around the huge, shadowy room, then back at Leo. He knew that look on his friend's face. It was the look that had gotten them into this mess in the first place. But it was also the look that promised an adventure.
"All right," Sam sighed. "But if we hear anything, we run. Deal?"
"Deal," Leo agreed. "Let's check the office. They must have been in there."
The office was a small, glass-walled room built on a raised platform overlooking the main cannery floor. Getting up the three short steps was an ordeal for Leo. Each step sent a jolt of pain through his leg, but he clenched his jaw and kept going, with Sam's help.
The office door was ajar. Inside, it was a wreck. A dusty metal desk was overturned. A filing cabinet stood open, its drawers pulled out and emptied. Papers were scattered across the floor like fallen leaves.
"Looks like they were looking for something," Sam observed, stepping carefully through the mess.
"Or they were packing something up and didn't care about being neat," Leo countered. He leaned against the doorframe, scanning the room. "Help me look. Be careful what you touch."
Sam knelt down and began sifting through the papers. Most of them were old invoices and time sheets, yellowed and brittle with age. They were damp from years of sitting in the humid, salt-laced air.
"Nothing here," Sam said after a few minutes. "Just history."
Leo wasn't so sure. He pushed himself away from the door and hobbled over to the overturned filing cabinet. The men had been in a hurry. They wouldn't have been careful. He knelt down on his good knee, ignoring the protest from his injured leg, and peered behind the cabinet. It was dark in the space between the cabinet and the wall. He ran his fingers along the dusty floorboards.
His fingertips brushed against a small, stiff piece of paper.
He pulled it out. It was a shipping label, torn from a larger box or crate. It had been peeled off something, but its adhesive back had picked up a layer of dust, rendering it useless. Under the faint moonlight filtering through the office window, he could just make out the printed words.
"What is it?" Sam asked, coming over to look over his shoulder.
Leo held it up to the light. "It's a shipping label. Part of one, anyway."
The label was addressed to a company he'd never heard of. "Vardaman Analytical," he read aloud. The address was incomplete. Only the city and state were fully visible. "Quantico, Virginia."
"What's Vardaman Analytical?" Sam asked.
"No idea," Leo said. "But look at this." He pointed to the "contents" line on the label. It was a long, complex chemical name. "Beryllium Oxide," he read slowly, stumbling over the pronunciation. "Hazard Class 6.1. Toxic."
A chill that had nothing to do with the cold night air ran down Leo's spine. "Sam, I don't think these guys were stealing old fish hooks."
"Toxic?" Sam repeated, his eyes wide. "What do we do with this?"
"We take it to the sheriff," Leo said firmly, carefully folding the label and putting it in his pocket. "Right now. Let's get to the boat."
They made their way out of the office and across the silent floor of the cannery. The building opened up onto a long, decaying wooden pier. The night air was sharp and clean. Out on the water, the moon cast a silver path across the gentle swells of the bay.
Their small, sixteen-foot motorboat was tied up right where they had left it, bobbing gently next to the pier.
"Home free," Sam said with a sigh of relief. He jumped nimbly from the pier into the boat and turned to help Leo.
Getting into the boat was a clumsy, painful process for Leo, but he managed it without falling into the water. He settled onto the seat near the stern while Sam untied the bow line.
"Okay," Sam said, moving to the outboard motor. "Let's fire this thing up and..." His voice trailed off.
"What's wrong?" Leo asked.
"The fuel line," Sam said, his voice flat. "It's been cut."
Leo twisted around. Sam was holding up the black rubber hose that ran from the portable gas tank to the engine. It was severed cleanly, as if with a very sharp knife.
"They didn't want us following them," Leo said grimly. "They thought of everything."
They sat in silence for a moment, the gentle rocking of the boat the only sound. They were stranded. The nearest town was a five mile walk along a deserted coastal road. For Sam, it would be a long trek. For Leo, with his injured leg, it was impossible.
"So what now?" Sam asked, finally breaking the silence. "We sit here and wait for morning?"
Leo's mind was racing. He refused to be beaten. There had to be a way. "Those men were professionals," he said, thinking aloud. "They were efficient. But they were hired help. People like that don't bring their own tools if they don't have to. They use what's on site."
"What are you talking about?" Sam asked.
"The cannery," Leo said, a spark of an idea taking hold. "It's been abandoned for years, but a place like this would have had a maintenance shed. With tools. Spare parts. Engine parts."
Sam caught on immediately. "You think we can find a piece of fuel line in there?"
"It's a chance," Leo said. "It's the only one we've got."
Getting back out of the boat and onto the pier was even harder than getting in, but desperation gave Leo strength. They found the maintenance shed at the far end of the pier, its door hanging off one hinge.
The inside was a treasure trove of junk. Rusted tools hung on pegboards, their shapes barely recognizable under a thick coat of grime. Shelves were piled high with old motors, coffee cans full of nuts and bolts, and coils of wire. It smelled of oil, rust, and decay.
"Start looking," Leo said, leaning heavily against the doorframe. "We need about two feet of rubber hose. And some kind of clamp."
They searched in the near darkness, their hands fumbling through years of accumulated debris. Sam rummaged through a pile of old hoses for a water pump, while Leo carefully opened greasy cardboard boxes. His fingers closed around something cold and metallic. He pulled it out.
"Hose clamps," he said, holding up two small, circular metal clamps. "Still in the package."
"I found something!" Sam called out from the back of the shed. "I don't know if it'll work."
He emerged holding a dusty coil of black hose. It was thicker than their fuel line, and it looked ancient. But it was flexible, and there were no cracks in it.
"It was for a bilge pump, I think," Sam said. "It might be too big for the fittings on the engine."
"We'll make it fit," Leo said with a confidence he didn't quite feel. "Let's go."
Back at the boat, they set to work by the faint light of the moon. The repair was frustrating. The new hose was indeed too wide for the brass fittings on the motor and the gas tank. It slipped right off.
"It's not going to work," Sam said, slumping in defeat.
"Wait," Leo said. He was thinking about a science class project he had done. He remembered how heat made things expand and cold made them contract. "We need to make the fittings bigger, or the hose smaller." He looked at the water. "We can't make the hose smaller."
He pointed to Sam's pocket. "Do you have your lighter?"
Sam pulled out a small butane lighter. "You're not going to set the boat on fire, are you?"
"Not if we're careful," Leo said. "Give it to me."
Leo took the lighter. He held the end of the severed fuel line that was still attached to the engine. "Okay, we need to get the brass fitting off this old piece of hose." It took several minutes of twisting and pulling, but he finally worked it free.
"Now for the tricky part," he said. He handed the fitting to Sam. "Hold the hose. I'm going to heat the fitting. When I say go, you push it onto the new hose as hard as you can."
Leo flicked the lighter. The tiny flame seemed incredibly bright in the darkness. He held the brass fitting with a rusty pair of pliers they had found in the shed, heating it in the flame. He had to be careful not to get it too hot, but it needed to be hot enough.
"Okay," he said, his voice tense. "Get ready." The brass began to glow a dull red. "Now!"
Sam shoved the end of the new hose onto the heated fitting. There was a sizzle and a faint smell of burning rubber. The hose slid over the barbs of the fitting and seated itself perfectly. As the brass cooled, it would create a seal that was almost as good as the original.
They repeated the process for the fitting on the gas tank. Finally, they used the new clamps to secure both ends. Leo prime the fuel bulb, pumping it until it was firm.
"That's it," he breathed. "It should hold. Try it."
Sam turned the key in the ignition. The engine coughed once, twice, then roared to life, shattering the night's silence. The sound was the most beautiful thing Leo had ever heard.
"It worked!" Sam yelled over the noise of the motor. "You did it!"
Leo just grinned, the pain in his leg momentarily forgotten. "Let's get out of here before our friends decide to come back."
Sam didn't need to be told twice. He put the boat in gear and they pulled away from the decaying pier, leaving the silent cannery behind them. They were a hundred yards out in the bay when Leo looked back.
A light had just flicked on in the office window.
"Sam," he said, his voice tight. "They're back."
Sam looked. He saw the light, and then he saw the dark shape of a larger boat pulling up to the pier. "They must have heard the engine!"
"Go!" Leo yelled. "Head for the shoals near Miller's Point. They can't follow us in there!"
Sam pushed the throttle forward as far as it would go. The little boat leaped ahead, its prow lifting out of the water. Behind them, they heard the roar of a much larger, more powerful engine. A searchlight beam suddenly cut through the darkness, sweeping across the water toward them.

The white beam sliced through the night, a brilliant, searching finger. It swept past their stern, illuminating their wake in a ghostly white froth, then swung back.

"They've got us!" Sam shouted. His knuckles were white on the tiller.

"Keep going!" Leo's voice was tight with pain and urgency. "Don't look back, just drive!"

The beam caught them, pinning the small boat in a circle of harsh, artificial daylight. In the glare, Leo could see every rock and patch of seaweed in the churning water around them. Ahead, the sea was a black wall, but he knew the way. He had explored these waters since he was a kid.

"See that dark shape on the right?" Leo pointed, his arm trembling. "That's the first of the channel markers for the shoals. Aim just to the left of it."

Sam obeyed without question, wrenching the tiller. The boat skidded sideways, spray flying over the gunwales, soaking them in cold salt water. The searchlight overshot them for a second before finding them again. Behind them, the deep-throated roar of the larger engine grew louder, closer. It was a sound of pure menace.

"They're gaining on us!" Sam yelled.

"They're faster in open water," Leo gritted out, his leg throbbing in time with the engine's frantic pulse. "But not in the narrows. Get ready."

The water ahead grew choppy and confused. Dark, jagged shapes began to appear, the tops of submerged rocks that could rip the hull out of a boat that strayed from the channel. Sam's eyes were wide, fixed on the treacherous path ahead. The searchlight was a curse and a blessing, blinding them one moment, revealing a deadly obstacle the next.

"Right!" Leo commanded. "Hard to the right! Around that black rock!"

Sam spun the tiller. The boat responded, banking sharply. For a heart-stopping moment, Leo thought the turn was too tight. The port side gunwale dipped, kissing the water. He threw his weight to the other side, and Sam corrected. The boat straightened out, missing a jagged tooth of granite by inches.

Behind them, the pursuit boat's engine noise changed. It dropped in pitch, a frustrated growl. Leo risked a glance back. The larger vessel had slowed, its searchlight now sweeping back and forth more cautiously. They had reached the edge of the shoals.

"They're stopping," Sam said, a note of disbelief and relief in his voice.

"They have to," Leo said. "Their draft is too deep. They'd tear their bottom out in here."

They navigated another hundred yards into the maze of rock and shallow water, then Leo told Sam to cut the engine. The sudden silence was overwhelming, broken only by the gentle lap of water against the hull and the distant, idling rumble of the other boat. The searchlight beam still held them, a silent accusation across the water.

"Now what?" Sam whispered, as if the men on the other boat could hear them.

"We wait," Leo replied, his eyes scanning the dark shoreline of Miller's Point. "They can't stay there forever."

But Leo was wrong. A minute passed, then another. The big boat didn't move. Then, they saw activity on its deck. Two figures were moving around a smaller object.

"Leo," Sam said, his voice barely audible. "They're lowering a dinghy."

A cold knot formed in Leo's stomach. Of course. A small inflatable boat with an outboard motor would be just as maneuverable as their own. The brief respite was over.

"Start the engine," Leo said quietly. "We have to make it to shore."

The engine coughed back to life. As they moved deeper into the shoals, they heard the high-pitched whine of an outboard motor start up behind them. The new chase had begun. This time, their pursuers were just as agile.

"Head for that little cove," Leo directed, pointing toward a sliver of sandy beach nestled between two rocky bluffs on the point. "Run us aground. It's our only chance."

The dinghy was faster than they had expected. The sound of its motor was closing in. Sam pushed their small boat as hard as it would go, weaving through the last of the channel markers. The cove grew larger. He could see the treeline just beyond the beach.

"Get ready to jump!" Sam yelled.

He aimed the prow straight for the center of the beach and didn't slow down. The boat hit the sand with a jarring scrape of fiberglass on stone. The impact threw them both forward. Leo cried out as his injured leg slammed against the forward seat.

"Come on!" Sam was already over the side, splashing through the shallow water. He turned and grabbed Leo's arm, half-dragging him out of the boat. "We have to move!"

Leo's leg buckled as soon as he put weight on it. The pain was a white-hot flare behind his eyes. "I can't," he gasped.

"You have to!" Sam hauled him up, slinging Leo's arm over his shoulders. "Lean on me!"

They staggered across the sand, a clumsy, two-man creature racing against time. They reached the edge of the trees just as the inflatable dinghy slid onto the beach behind them. Two dark figures jumped out. A third man stayed with the bigger boat, a distant silhouette.

"Into the woods!" Leo urged.

They plunged into the darkness under the trees, stumbling over roots and rocks. The ground sloped upward steeply. Every step was an agony for Leo. Behind them, they could hear the men crashing through the underbrush, their progress swift and confident.

"Can't go much farther," Sam panted, his breath coming in ragged bursts.

"Up," Leo said, forcing the word out. "Keep going up. To the top of the bluff."

They struggled onward, pulling themselves up by grabbing onto saplings and vines. The sounds of pursuit seemed to fall back slightly. Maybe the steep terrain was slowing their pursuers as well. After an eternity of struggle, the ground leveled out. They had reached the top. They were in a small clearing, and in the center of it, rising like a stone specter against the star-dusted sky, was the old Miller's Point lighthouse.

"The lighthouse," Sam breathed. "We can hide in there."

It was their only option. They hobbled across the clearing to the massive iron-strapped door at the base of the stone tower. Leo grabbed the heavy iron ring that served as a handle and pulled. It didn't budge.

"It's locked. Or rusted shut."

"Together," Sam said.

They both gripped the ring and pulled with all their remaining strength. There was a hideous shriek of protesting metal, a groan of ancient wood, and the door scraped inward a few inches. It was enough. They squeezed through the opening into the absolute blackness inside and pushed the door shut behind them. The scraping sound as it closed seemed deafeningly loud.

For a moment, they stood in the cool, musty air, their hearts hammering. The inside of the lighthouse smelled of damp stone, salt, and decay. A spiral staircase, a faint metal skeleton in the gloom, wound its way up into the darkness above.

"We need to find a place to watch them from," Leo whispered.

"Upstairs," Sam replied.

Supporting Leo, they began the slow, painful ascent. Each step sent a fresh jolt of pain up Leo's leg. They passed a landing with a small, grimy window. Leo paused, peering out. He could see the clearing below. The two men had just emerged from the trees. They stopped, looking around, then one of them pointed directly at the lighthouse.

"They know we're in here," Leo said grimly.

They continued their climb, higher and higher, the air growing colder. They finally reached the top, emerging into the lamp room. It was a circular space encased in glass panels, with a narrow catwalk running around the outside. In the center stood the heart of the lighthouse: a massive, multi-faceted Fresnel lens, as tall as a man, looking like a giant glass beehive. It was covered in a thick layer of dust.

"Let's see what they're doing," Sam said.

They moved to the glass, careful to stay in the shadows. Below, the two men were approaching the tower. One was big, a heavy-shouldered silhouette. The other was leaner, moving with a kind of predatory quickness. The big one carried something long and thin. A rifle.

The men reached the door. They saw that it was ajar. After a moment's hesitation, the lean man slipped inside, while the big man with the rifle took up a position a few yards away, watching the tower.

"He's inside," Sam whispered, his voice trembling.

They heard a footstep on the iron stairs below. A single, deliberate tread. Then another. He was coming up.

"What do we do?" Sam asked, panic creeping into his tone.

Leo's mind raced. They were trapped. His eyes darted around the lamp room, searching for a weapon, a tool, anything. He saw an old maintenance locker in the corner. He limped over to it, Sam at his heels, and pulled on the handle. It was stuck fast with rust.

"Help me," he hissed.

Together they wrenched it open. It flew back with a rusty clang that echoed in the small room. Below, the footsteps stopped.

"He heard that," Sam whispered.

They froze, listening. The silence stretched. Then the footsteps started again, faster this time.

Leo frantically rummaged inside the locker. His hands closed on something heavy and metal. A large, industrial-sized wrench. He pulled it out. It was at least two feet long, solid steel.

"Stay behind me," Leo whispered, positioning himself at the top of the stairwell, just out of sight. The wrench felt impossibly heavy.

The footsteps grew louder, ringing on the iron steps. A head and shoulders appeared in the stairwell opening. The man moved cautiously, his eyes trying to pierce the gloom of the lamp room.

This was their only chance. Leo put all his strength into it, swinging the heavy wrench in a downward arc. It wasn't a clean hit. The man must have sensed the movement at the last second, because he ducked. The wrench glanced off his shoulder with a sickening thud instead of hitting his head.

The man grunted in pain and surprise and stumbled backward down the stairs. Leo heard him fall, a clatter of limbs and metal.

"Go!" Leo yelled to Sam. "The door! We have to block the door!"

There was no time to see if the man was unconscious. They scrambled back down the spiral staircase, nearly tripping over each other in the dark. They found the lean man sprawled on the landing ten feet below the lamp room, groaning but already trying to get up. They clattered past him, not slowing down.

They reached the ground floor. "The door!" Leo shouted again.

There was nothing to barricade it with. The ground floor was bare stone. Sam threw his weight against the heavy door, trying to force it shut. It scraped a few inches and then stopped, blocked by whatever was jamming it.

From outside, a voice yelled, "What's going on, Marco?"

The man upstairs, Marco, yelled back, a voice thick with pain. "They're in here! The kid hit me!"

Footsteps crunched outside. The big man with the rifle was coming.

Leo's eyes swept the room again, desperately. His gaze fell on the staircase. It was made of cast iron, bolted to the wall. But the central support column...

"Sam, the stairs!"

Sam looked, uncomprehending. "What?"

"The main bolt! At the bottom!" Leo pointed to a large, rusted bolt head where the central pillar of the staircase was anchored to the stone floor. "If we can break it, the whole thing might come down!"

It was a crazy idea, born of desperation. The stairs wouldn't collapse completely, but maybe they would shift enough to block the doorway.

"The wrench!" Sam said, understanding dawning.

Leo still had it. He handed it to Sam. "Hit it! As hard as you can!"

It was like their approach to the ghost town all over again, but this time they were the ones who were hunted and exhausted. They moved in a low crouch, the scraping of bushes against their clothes sounding like thunder in the silence.

As they drew closer, they could make out the shape of a vehicle. It was a dark-colored pickup truck, parked directly in the middle of the road. The light they had seen was a single lantern, placed on the truck's hood. A man stood beside it, his back to them, looking north, the same direction the helicopter had gone.

Rick's hope turned to ice in his stomach. A search party from the base would have used a jeep, and they would have used powerful searchlights, sweeping the desert. This was something else. This was wrong.

He and Scotty dropped to the ground behind a large creosote bush, peering through its branches. The man was big, a bulky silhouette against the lantern light. He raised a small object to his mouth. A crackle of static broke the stillness, and the man spoke into a hand-held radio.

"Anything?" a tinny voice squawked from the device.

"Negative," the man by the truck said. His voice was a low rumble. "Sky is clear. They should have been here by now."

"They're on schedule," the radio voice replied. "The cargo transfer takes time. Just hold your position. They'll signal when they're inbound for pickup."

"And the other business? In town?" the man asked.

There was a short pause. "Contained. A couple of kids snooping around. They won't be a problem for a long, long time. Just sit tight, over."

"Roger. Over and out." The man lowered the radio.

Rick and Scotty looked at each other in the gloom, their eyes wide. "A couple of kids snooping around." There was no doubt who the man was talking about. This wasn't a search party. This was one of them. The third man from the hotel, the one who had gone to find their jeep. Or another one entirely. It didn't matter. He was the enemy.

"He's waiting for the helicopter," Scotty mouthed silently.

Rick nodded, his mind racing. This changed everything. Their desperate walk through the desert had led them straight into more trouble. They were trapped. They couldn't go forward, and they couldn't go back without this man seeing them. They were stuck, hiding behind a bush, miles from anywhere.

The man stamped his feet, blowing into his cupped hands. He seemed restless. He walked around the truck, kicking at the tires, then went back to his post, staring into the northern sky.

"What do we do?" Scotty whispered, his voice trembling slightly. "We just wait here until he leaves?"

"He's not leaving," Rick whispered back. "He's waiting for the others. They'll all be here. And then what? We can't stay here all night." He looked at the truck, then back at the man. An idea, desperate and reckless, began to form in his mind. "That truck is our only way out of here."

Scotty stared at him as if he'd lost his mind. "What are you talking about? You want to fight him?"

"We don't have a choice," Rick insisted, his voice low and urgent. "Think about it, Scotty. He's alone. He's not expecting us. We're two against one. If we can get that truck, we don't have to walk back to the base. We can drive."

"He's a full-grown man, Rick! And he's probably got a gun. They hit you, remember? They're not playing games."

"We'll be careful," Rick said, his determination hardening. "We'll surprise him. We can't just sit here and do nothing. This is our chance. Maybe our only chance."

Scotty hesitated, chewing on his lip. He looked from the man to Rick, then back again. He was scared. Rick was too, but his fear was being burned away by a hot, fierce resolve. They had come this far. He wasn't going to give up now.

"What's the plan, then, Lone Ranger?" Scotty finally sighed, the use of the old nickname a sign of his reluctant agreement.

Rick's mind worked quickly, assembling the pieces. "He's focused on the north. We circle around, get behind him. You'll stay on that side of the road. I'll get on this side. We need a distraction. Find a rock. When I give a signal, a bird call or something, you throw it far off to the side, away from us. When he turns to look, I'll move in."

"And do what?" Scotty asked. "Ask him politely for the keys?"

"I'll tackle him," Rick said simply. "When I do, you come in from the other side. We'll have to overpower him. Tie him up."

"With what? Our shoelaces?"

"There's got to be rope in the truck," Rick reasoned. "A truck like this, out here? There's always rope. We just have to get to it."

It was a flimsy plan, full of holes and possibilities for disaster. But it was better than no plan at all. It was better than freezing in the desert while they waited for the rest of the spies to arrive.

"Okay," Scotty breathed. "Okay. A bird call?"

"Like a quail," Rick whispered. He made a soft, three-note whistle. "Like that. When you hear it, throw the rock. And be ready to move."

"Ready as I'll ever be," Scotty muttered.

They separated, melting back into the shadows. Rick moved in a wide, slow arc, his eyes locked on the man by the truck. The man was humming now, a tuneless, off-key sound that grated on Rick's nerves. The cold air burned his lungs. His scraped hands throbbed with every movement. He found a position behind a cluster of yucca plants, directly across the road from where Scotty would be. He was about thirty feet from the back of the pickup. He waited, letting his breathing quiet down, letting his heart hammer against his ribs. He could see Scotty's dark form settle behind a low rise on the other side.

Rick took a deep, silent breath. He formed his lips and let out the soft quail call.

For a second, nothing happened. Then, a rock clattered in the darkness, a good fifty yards to the west of the truck. The sound was sharp and loud in the quiet night.

The man by the truck stopped humming. "What was that?" he muttered to himself. He turned, peering into the blackness where the sound had come from. He took a few steps away from the truck, his hand moving toward his belt.

It was the opening Rick needed. He burst from cover, sprinting across the open ground. His feet pounded on the hard-packed dirt, the sound seeming to echo in the stillness. The man heard him. He spun around, his eyes wide with surprise, his hand fumbling for something on his hip. He wasn't fumbling for a gun. It was the radio.

Rick didn't hesitate. He lowered his shoulder and drove into the man's midsection, just as Scotty had done in the hotel. The impact was like hitting a brick wall. The man let out a grunt of surprise and pain, stumbling backward. But he was heavy, solid. He didn't go down.

Instead, his big hands shot out and clamped onto Rick's jacket. "What the-?"

Scotty charged in from the other side, just as they had planned. He slammed into the man's legs. The combined attack worked. The man's balance gave way and all three of them went down in a heap on the dusty road.

Rick scrambled, trying to pin the man's arms. It was like wrestling with a bear. A powerful arm swung out, catching Rick on the side of the head. His ears rang, and spots danced in his vision. The man was trying to get up, trying to throw them off. He was shouting now, angry, garbled words.

"The truck!" Rick gasped at Scotty. "Rope!"

Scotty wriggled free and scrambled toward the back of the pickup. The man bucked, trying to throw Rick off. He got one hand free and grabbed a fistful of Rick's hair, yanking his head back. Pain shot down Rick's neck. He gritted his teeth, holding on with all his might, trying to keep the man's other arm pinned to the ground.

"Got it!" Scotty yelled. He came running back with a coil of rough nylon rope. "Roll him over!"

It was easier said than done. The man was fighting furiously now, sensing the danger. He twisted, getting a knee under him. Rick felt himself being lifted. He was losing his grip.

In that moment, Scotty acted. He didn't try to help wrestle. He took the end of the rope and, with a quick, desperate motion, looped it around the man's flailing ankles. He pulled hard. The man's feet were yanked out from under him and he crashed back down onto the road with a heavy thud. The back of his head hit the ground. His body went limp.

For a moment, there was silence, broken only by the boys' ragged gasps for air.

"Is he...?" Scotty whispered, his voice shaking.

Rick cautiously pushed himself off the man's chest. He was breathing. Unconscious, but breathing. "He's out," Rick said. "Quick. Let's tie him up before he wakes up."

Working together, their hands trembling with adrenaline, they quickly and efficiently tied the man's wrists behind his back. They tied his ankles together, too, pulling the knots as tight as their tired fingers could manage. They found a dirty rag in the truck bed and used it as a gag.

Only when he was completely secured did they stop. Rick leaned against the side of the truck, his whole body trembling. His cheek throbbed where the man had hit him. Scotty sat down hard on the road, running a shaky hand through his hair.

"We did it," Scotty said, a note of disbelief in his voice. "We actually did it."

"We did," Rick agreed. He pushed himself off the truck and went to the cab. He opened the driver's side door. The keys were in the ignition. A wave of profound relief washed over him. He slid into the driver's seat and his eyes fell on two plastic water bottles in the center console. "Scotty! Water!"

Scotty was at his side in an instant. Rick handed him a bottle. They twisted the caps off and drank, the cool liquid a miracle in their parched throats. They didn't stop until both bottles were empty.

"Now what?" Scotty asked, wiping his mouth with the back of his hand.

"Now we decide," Rick said. He clicked on the small dome light, bathing the cab in a weak glow. He pulled the folded map from his pocket and spread it out on the steering wheel. The circle around the box canyon seemed to glow. "South to the base? Or north to that canyon?"

"Rick, we have to go back," Scotty said immediately. "We got the truck. We have a prisoner. We won. Let's not push our luck."

"But they don't know," Rick argued, tapping the map. "The man on the radio thinks he's still waiting. The ones in the helicopter think we're tied up in an old jail. We have the element of surprise. No one has it but us." He looked at Scotty, his eyes intense. "If we go to the base, it'll take hours to explain, to get a party organized. By then, they'll have transferred the guidance system to a plane or another truck and they'll be gone. Forever. This is our only chance to find out where they're taking it."

"And do what? Fight all of them?" Scotty protested. "There are at least two more, maybe more. And they'll have the helicopter."

"We don't fight them," Rick said. "We do what we did before. We watch. We find out what's happening, we get a license plate, a description. We get real, solid information. Then we go back. With this truck, we can be at that canyon in twenty minutes. We watch, and we leave. It's a better plan than walking for eight hours."

Scotty was quiet for a long time, looking from the map to Rick's determined face. The logic was risky, but it was there. They were in a unique position.

"Just watch?" Scotty asked finally. "No heroics? No tackling anyone else?"

"Just watch," Rick promised. "From a safe distance. We're witnesses, not commandos."

Scotty let out a long, weary sigh. "All right," he said. "All right, Lone Ranger. Let's go see the rest of the show. But if we hear that helicopter, we are gone. Deal?"

"Deal," Rick said with a grin of relief.

They had one last problem. The man lying tied up on the road. They couldn't leave him there. He could die of exposure, or be found by his friends. With a great deal of effort, they managed to haul his dead weight into the bed of the pickup truck. They found a dusty tarp and covered him, hoping he wouldn't wake up and start struggling.

Rick slid back behind the wheel. His hands were still trembling, but his mind was clear. He turned the key. The truck's engine roared to life, shattering the desert's deep silence. Rick flicked off the dome light, shifted the truck into gear, and turned the wheel. He didn't turn south, toward safety. He turned the truck north, deeper into the dark, and pressed his foot on the accelerator. The canyon, and the spies, were waiting.

The truck bounced and rattled on the unpaved road, its headlights dark. A sliver of moon hung in the black sky, providing just enough light to turn the desert into a landscape of ghostly shapes and deep shadows. Rick gripped the steering wheel, his knuckles white, his eyes straining to pick out the track that was little more than two ruts in the sand and gravel. Beside him, Scotty kept his gaze fixed on the darkness ahead, scanning for any sign of movement.

"How far do you think it is?" Scotty asked, his voice barely a whisper over the engine's growl.

"The map showed the canyon about five miles north of the town," Rick answered. "We should be getting close. Keep an eye out for any rock formations that look like the entrance to a canyon."

The truck lurched as a wheel dropped into a deep rut. In the back, something shifted with a muffled thump, followed by a low groan. Both boys froze.

"He's waking up," Scotty hissed.

Rick eased his foot off the accelerator, letting the truck slow to a crawl. "We can't stop. Not out here. If he starts yelling..."

"What do we do? We can't just let him thrash around back there. He'll get loose."

Rick thought for a second. The plan was fragile enough without a conscious prisoner announcing their arrival. "We'll have to pull over as soon as we find some cover. There should be a wash or some boulders up ahead."

He scanned the landscape more urgently now. To their right, a dark mass of rock rose up, a small butte that cast a deep pool of shadow at its base. "There," Rick said, pointing. He turned the wheel, guiding the truck off the track and into the soft sand. The engine whined in protest, but the tires found purchase and pulled them into the darkness at the foot of the rock. Rick killed the engine. The sudden silence was immense, broken only by the chirping of a lone cricket and the sound of another groan from the truck bed.

"Stay here," Rick whispered, grabbing the pencil flashlight from the dashboard. He slipped out of the cab, his feet making no sound in the sand. Scotty followed, closing his door with a soft click.

They moved to the back of the truck. Rick pulled back a corner of the dusty tarp. The man was stirring, his head moving from side to side. His eyes were still closed, but he was no longer unconscious. The gag was still in place, but the ropes around his wrists seemed looser than before.

"He's been working at them," Scotty observed grimly.

Rick shone the thin beam of the flashlight on the man's hands. The knots were still there, but there was more slack in the rope. "We have to tighten them. And check the gag."

It was a clumsy, unpleasant business. The man flinched as Rick touched his face, his eyes fluttering open for a moment, wide with confusion and fear in the brief flash of light. Scotty held the man's shoulders down while Rick expertly re-tied the gag, making sure it was secure but not choking him. Then he turned his attention to the wrists, pulling the rope tight and adding a new, more complicated knot he'd learned in the Sea Scouts. The man struggled weakly, but he was still groggy from the blow to his head and the two boys were stronger than they looked. They checked his ankles, then pulled the tarp back over him.

"That should hold him for a while," Rick said, his breath coming a little faster. "Let's get going. We're wasting time."

They climbed back into the cab, and a few moments later the truck was bumping its way north again. The brief stop had sharpened their nerves. The reality of what they were doing, a captured spy lying trussed up in the back of their stolen truck, settled on them with new weight.

Another ten minutes of careful driving brought them to a place where the flat desert floor gave way to a jumble of rock and rising canyon walls. A narrow opening, barely wider than the truck, marked the entrance to a box canyon.

"This has to be it," Scotty whispered, pointing.

Rick pulled the truck to a stop a hundred yards from the entrance, tucking it behind a cluster of massive boulders that would hide it from a casual glance. He cut the engine. "Okay," he said, turning to Scotty. "This is as far as the truck goes. We go the rest of the way on foot."

"Just like the ghost town," Scotty said, a wry tone in his voice. "We're getting good at this."

"Let's hope we're good enough," Rick replied. "Remember the deal. We watch, we get information, and we leave. No heroics."

"You don't have to tell me twice," Scotty muttered.

They got out, making sure the prisoner was still quiet, and then began the approach. They moved with the same practiced silence they had used before, keeping low, using the scattered creosote bushes and rock outcroppings for cover. The entrance to the canyon was a narrow slot between two sheer cliffs. As they drew closer, a faint, rhythmic sound reached them.

"What's that?" Rick whispered, holding up a hand.

They listened. It was a low, steady hum. A generator.

"They have power," Scotty breathed. "This is more organized than I thought."

They reached the canyon mouth and flattened themselves against the rock wall. Rick peered cautiously around the edge. The canyon was not deep, maybe a hundred feet wide, and it ended a few hundred yards in, at a solid wall of rock. In the center of the canyon floor, a single-engine plane stood gleaming faintly in the moonlight. It was a small bush plane, with high wings and rugged landing gear, perfect for landing on rough terrain. Near the plane, a gasoline generator hummed away, powering a string of two bare light bulbs that cast a harsh, yellow glare over the scene.

A helicopter, the same one they had seen at the base, was parked a short distance from the plane. Two men were working by the light, transferring a large, heavy-looking crate from the helicopter's cargo bay to the open door of the plane. One was the pilot Rick had seen earlier. The other was a stocky man in a leather jacket. A third man stood near the plane's cockpit, holding a rifle and scanning the canyon rim. A sentry.

Rick's heart hammered against his ribs. This was it. The guidance system.

He pulled back, his mind racing. "The sentry is watching the rim," he whispered to Scotty. "We can't go up. We have to find a spot on this level."

Scotty nodded, his eyes wide. "There's a pile of fallen rock over there," he pointed. "About fifty yards in. If we can get there, we'll have a clear view and good cover."

It was their only option. Taking a deep breath, Rick gave the signal. They moved from the canyon entrance, darting across an open patch of ground and into the shadow of a large boulder. They paused, listening. The generator's hum and the scraping sound of the crate being moved covered any small noise they might have made. The sentry hadn't moved.

Their next dash took them to another cluster of rocks. Then another. Rick's eyes were fixed on the sentry. The man seemed relaxed, confident. He clearly wasn't expecting visitors. Finally, they scrambled the last few feet and slid into the jumble of fallen rock Scotty had spotted. It formed a perfect natural blind. They could see the entire scene clearly through the gaps between the boulders.

The two men grunted with effort as they finally shoved the crate into the plane's small cabin. The pilot wiped his brow. "That's the last of it," he said, his voice carrying clearly in the still air. "Let's get loaded up. I want to be over the border before sunrise."

The man in the leather jacket nodded. "Where's Janos? He should have reported in by now."

"He's probably on his way," the pilot said dismissively. "He said he would check out the town and meet us here. Don't worry about Janos. He can take care of himself."

Rick and Scotty exchanged a look. Janos. The man tied up in the back of their truck.

The sentry walked over from the cockpit. "Everything secure?"

"All set," the pilot replied. "You get the radio packed up. We'll get the generator. The sooner we're in the air, the better I'll feel. I don't like this place."

Rick pulled a small notebook and a pencil from his pocket, the same ones he'd used to copy the map. He held them up so Scotty could see, then pointed toward the plane. Scotty understood. While the men were busy packing their equipment, Rick focused on the tail of the plane. The numbers were painted in black against the silver fuselage. In the dim light, they were hard to read. He squinted, trying to make them out. N-4-7-7-B. He wrote it down carefully.

"I can't believe we're seeing this," Scotty whispered, his voice filled with awe. "The whole operation."

"Get a good look at their faces," Rick instructed. "We need descriptions."

The pilot was tall and lean, with a sharp, bird-like face. The man in the leather jacket was shorter, powerfully built, with dark, curly hair. The sentry was nondescript, of medium height and build, but he moved with an alertness that suggested he was dangerous.

The men worked quickly. The sentry dismantled a small radio set and stowed it in the plane. The other two disconnected the lights and wrestled the generator toward the helicopter. Suddenly, the pilot stopped and looked up at the canyon rim.

"Did you hear that?" he asked.

The other men froze, listening. "Hear what?" the stocky man asked. "I don't hear anything."

"A rock," the pilot said. "I thought I heard a rock slide. Up there." He pointed in their general direction.

Rick's blood went cold. He held his breath, not daring to move a muscle. Beside him, Scotty was as still as the stone they hid behind. The sentry raised his rifle, his eyes methodically sweeping the dark cliffs. The beam of his powerful flashlight cut through the darkness, moving slowly, deliberately, across the rock face.

The light beam crept closer and closer to their hiding place. Rick could see the individual dust motes dancing in its path. It was going to hit them. There was nowhere to run, nowhere to hide. He braced himself.

The beam touched the edge of their boulder pile, illuminating the rock just inches from Rick's face. It paused there for a long second that stretched into an eternity. Rick squeezed his eyes shut, waiting for the shout of discovery, the blast of the rifle.

Then, from the direction of the canyon mouth, came a faint, metallic scraping sound.

The sentry's head whipped around. The flashlight beam swung away from them, pointing back toward the entrance. "What was that?"

"It came from the road," the stocky man said, his voice low and tense.

They all stared into the darkness beyond the canyon. The pilot swore under his breath. "It's Janos. He must have had trouble with the truck. Come on."

Leaving the generator, the pilot and the stocky man trotted toward the canyon mouth. The sentry remained where he was, his rifle ready, but his attention was now divided between the cliffs and the road.

Rick let out a breath he didn't realize he'd been holding. His entire body was trembling. "The truck," he whispered, a sick feeling in his stomach. "Our prisoner. He got loose."

"He must have," Scotty agreed, his face pale in the gloom. "He's making noise to warn them. We have to get out of here. Now."

But they were pinned down. The sentry was still on alert, and the other two would be back any second. If they ran, they would be seen for sure.

The two men returned from the canyon entrance, their footsteps crunching on the gravel. "Nothing," the pilot reported, sounding annoyed. "No sign of him or the truck. Must have been an animal."

"I don't like it," the stocky man said. "First the rockfall, now this. Something's wrong."

"We're leaving," the pilot said, his voice decisive. He strode to the helicopter and climbed into the cockpit. "Get that generator loaded. Now."

The other two complied, heaving the heavy machine into the helicopter's cargo hold. The sentry climbed into the plane, and the stocky man followed him. A moment later, the helicopter's engine coughed to life, then settled into a low whir that quickly grew into a deafening roar. The rotor blades began to turn, slowly at first, then faster and faster, kicking up a whirlwind of dust and sand.

"This is our chance!" Rick yelled over the noise. "They won't hear us now! Go!"

They scrambled out of the rock pile and ran, bent low, back the way they had come. The helicopter's rotor wash blasted them with sand and small pebbles, stinging their faces and hands. They didn't dare look back. They reached the canyon mouth and sprinted into the open desert, not stopping until they were a hundred yards away, their lungs burning.

They threw themselves down behind a line of brush and looked back. The helicopter was lifting off, its landing lights now blazing, turning the canyon into a stage. It hovered for a moment, then tilted forward and climbed swiftly into the night sky, heading south.

On the ground, the plane's engine sputtered, then caught with a roar. It taxied down the short, makeshift runway, turned, and then accelerated back toward them. It lifted off just inside the canyon mouth, its wings seeming to skim the cliffs on either side. It climbed steeply, banked to the south, and followed the helicopter into the darkness.

Within a minute, they were gone. The only sound was the fading thrum of their engines and the frantic pounding of Rick's own heart. The canyon was dark and silent once more.

"They're gone," Scotty said, his voice shaky with relief. "They're all gone."

"And so is Janos," Rick added, getting to his feet and brushing the sand from his clothes. "Come on. We have to see what happened at the truck."

They ran back to the cluster of boulders where they had left the pickup. The truck was still there, but the driver's side door was open. The tarp was thrown aside in the truck bed. The ropes lay on the floor of the bed, cut and frayed.

"He's gone," Scotty said unnecessarily.

Rick shone his flashlight around. On the ground near the truck, he found a small, sharp-edged piece of metal. "From the truck bed," he guessed. "He must have worked an arm loose and found this. Used it to saw through the ropes."

"And the noise we heard?"

"Probably him stumbling around in the dark, or maybe knocking something over in the truck bed," Rick figured. "He must have heard the generator and headed for the canyon on foot, just like we did. When he got there, he made a noise to warn them we were here."

"He saved them," Scotty said. "And he almost got us caught."

Rick nodded, his expression grim. "He's out there in the desert somewhere, on foot. But the others are gone. They think we were captured or scared off. They don't know we saw everything. They don't know we have the plane's tail number." He tapped the notebook in his pocket. "This is what matters now. We have to get this to the base."

They were exhausted, bruised, and their nerves were frayed to the breaking point. But they had succeeded. They had what they came for.

"South, then?" Scotty asked, a weary grin touching his lips. "For real this time?"

"For real this time," Rick confirmed. He climbed into the driver's seat. The key was still in the ignition. He turned it, and the engine came to life with a comforting rumble. He switched on the headlights. The twin beams cut a brilliant path through the darkness, illuminating the road back toward civilization, toward safety.

Rick shifted into gear and swung the truck in a wide arc, pointing its nose south. The ghost town, the canyon, the spies, they were all behind them now. Ahead lay the long drive back to the air base, and the difficult task of making people believe the wild story of a couple of kids who went looking for trouble, and found more than they ever bargained for. Rick pressed down on the accelerator. The truck picked up speed, leaving a cloud of dust to settle in the silent, empty desert.

We need to go south. At least they won't be looking for us on the road." He climbed into the useless jeep and retrieved their canteens from the floor. They were still mostly full. He also found a compass and a flashlight that the saboteur had missed. "We've got water, and we know the direction," he said, trying to sound more confident than he felt. "How far is it?" Scotty asked. "I don't know. Thirty, maybe forty miles to the main highway." Scotty whistled softly. "That's a long walk." "We can do it," Rick insisted. "We'll follow the road. It's easier than trying to cross the open desert. We'll walk all night and find a place to rest when the sun gets too high." They took one last look at the crippled jeep, then started walking. The moon was high now, casting a pale, ethereal light over the desert. The dirt road was a faint ribbon stretching south into the darkness. They walked in silence for a long time, conserving their energy, their sneakers making soft scuffing sounds in the dust. The adrenaline of the chase and their escape had worn off, leaving behind a deep, bone-wearying fatigue. Rick's mind raced. They had the information, the vital tail number of the plane, but it was useless until they could deliver it. Every hour that passed gave the spies a bigger head start. Who were they? What had they taken from the hotel? Whatever it was, it had been important enough to risk coming here, important enough to capture two snooping kids, and important enough to make sure they stayed put. After what felt like an eternity, Scotty spoke. "Do you hear that?" Rick stopped, straining his ears. At first, he heard nothing but the wind and the chirping of crickets. Then he caught it. A low, distant rumbling. It wasn't the sedan. This sound was deeper, heavier. "A truck?" Scotty guessed. "It's coming from ahead of us. From the south." Hope surged through Rick. Someone was coming. A rancher, maybe. Or a prospector. Anyone with a working vehicle. The rumbling grew steadily louder. Then they saw it. A single headlight, bobbing and weaving in the distance. "Just one light," Rick noted, a knot of unease tightening in his chest. "Motorcycle?" "Or a truck with a broken headlight," Scotty said hopefully. They moved to the side of the road, ready to flag it down. The vehicle drew closer. It was a truck, an old, beat-up pickup, and it was moving fast. As it neared, Rick felt his hope curdle into fear. Something was wrong. The truck was weaving erratically from one side of the road to the other, kicking up huge plumes of dust. "He's driving like a maniac," Scotty muttered, taking a step back from the road. "Maybe he's drunk." The truck was almost upon them. Rick grabbed Scotty's arm and pulled him further back, behind a scraggly mesquite bush. Just as they took cover, the truck swerved violently in their direction. Its one headlight swept across them, momentarily blinding them. Rick caught a glimpse of the driver, a lone figure hunched over the wheel, his face a pale blur. The truck roared past their hiding spot, missing them by only a few feet. It careened down the road toward the ghost town, its engine screaming. "That was too close," Scotty breathed, his voice shaky. "What's his hurry?" "I don't know," Rick said, watching the truck's red taillight disappear into the darkness. "But he was headed straight for the town. The same place the spies were." A terrible thought struck him. "What if he's one of them? What if he was supposed to meet them there?" "But they already left," Scotty argued. "They said they were behind schedule." "Maybe he's the reason they were behind schedule," Rick countered. "Let's wait here a minute. See if he comes back." They crouched behind the bush, listening. They heard the truck's engine noise fade as it reached the town, then it was cut off abruptly. The desert was silent again. Minutes stretched by. "He's not coming back," Scotty whispered. "What do we do?" "We keep walking," Rick said, his jaw set. "But we stay off the road. If he is one of them, we can't let him see us." They left the relative safety of the road and struck out across the desert, paralleling the track from a distance. The going was much harder. They had to pick their way around spiny cactus and navigate through patches of loose rock, their progress slowed to a crawl. Rick kept glancing over his shoulder, toward the ghost town, but there was no sign of movement, no sound. The single headlight did not reappear. The moon began its slow descent toward the western horizon, and the temperature started to drop. Rick's arms, still sore from being tied, ached with a dull, persistent pain. Every muscle cried out for rest. "We need to rest," Scotty said, echoing Rick's thoughts. "Just for a little while." Rick knew he was right. They found a shallow depression, a small wash shielded from the wind by a low ridge, and slumped to the ground. Rick took a small sip of water from his canteen, swishing it around in his dry mouth before swallowing. "Do you think that guy in the truck found our jeep?" Scotty asked. "He must have," Rick said. "He'll know someone was here. He'll know the others ran into trouble." "So he might come looking for us." "He might." Rick scanned the dark landscape. It was vast and empty. A man on foot could be anywhere. Or he could be waiting for them up ahead. The thought sent a fresh chill down his spine. They rested for what Rick judged to be half an hour, though it felt like both a minute and a lifetime. The fatigue was a heavy weight, pulling at his eyelids. "Come on," he said, forcing himself to his feet. "We have to keep moving. The longer we're out here, the more danger we're in." They started walking again, the grim, silent march south. The landscape became more rugged, the flat desert giving way to rolling hills and rocky outcrops. It was harder to keep the road in sight. Rick relied on his compass, checking it every few minutes to make sure they were still on course. They had just crested a small, rocky hill when Scotty suddenly grabbed his arm and pulled him down. "Look," he whispered, pointing. Rick followed his gaze. Down below, perhaps a quarter of a mile ahead, was the road. And on the road was the truck. It was parked, its single headlight now extinguished. It was just a dark, silent shape in the moonlight. "Is he waiting for us?" Scotty breathed. "He must be," Rick said. "He knows we have to come this way." They were trapped. They couldn't go forward, and going back was pointless. They could try to make a wide circle around him, but in this terrain, in the dark, they would make noise. He would hear them. They were pinned down. Rick's mind worked furiously. The man in the truck had the advantage. He was armed, most likely, and he had a vehicle. All he had to do was wait. Rick and Scotty had only their wits and a rapidly dwindling supply of water. "We can't just sit here," Rick whispered. "When the sun comes up, he'll be able to see for miles. He'll spot us for sure." "So we have to get past him now, while it's still dark," Scotty finished. "But how?" Rick surveyed the land between them and the truck. It was a gentle, open slope, dotted with sparse brush. There was very little cover. To their right, however, the ground was rougher. A series of small, rocky gullies snaked their way down toward the road, ending in a deep wash that ran under the road through a concrete culvert. The culvert was a dark, uninviting hole, but it offered a way past the truck. "I have an idea," Rick said, his voice low and tense. "See that big wash? It goes under the road. If we can get to it without him hearing us, we can crawl through the culvert and come out on the other side of him." Scotty considered it. "It's risky. If he hears us in that culvert, we're like rats in a trap." "It's riskier to stay here," Rick countered. "It's our only chance." Scotty nodded slowly. "Okay. Let's do it." They began to move, crawling on their hands and knees, keeping the crest of the hill between them and the silent truck. They moved with agonizing slowness, testing each handhold and foothold before putting their weight on it. A single dislodged pebble could give them away. It took them nearly an hour to cover the few hundred yards to the edge of the deep wash. They peered over the edge. It was a drop of about fifteen feet, a steep wall of dirt and loose rock. "Now what?" Scotty whispered. "We can't climb down that without making a racket." Rick scanned the edge of the wash. A few yards away, the gnarled roots of a dead ironwood tree clung to the rim, hanging down into the darkness. "The roots," Rick pointed. "We can use them to climb down." He went first. He swung his legs over the edge and grasped the thick, woody roots. They were solid. He began to lower himself, his sneakers searching for purchase on the dirt wall. Dust and small pebbles pattered down into the wash below, the sound seeming impossibly loud in the stillness. Rick froze, listening. There was no sound from the direction of the truck. He continued his descent, finally dropping the last few feet to the sandy bottom of the wash. He looked up and gave a slight wave. Scotty followed, moving with a wiry strength that always surprised Rick. Soon he was standing beside him in the darkness of the wash. The air was cool and smelled of damp earth. "So far, so good," Scotty breathed. They were shielded from sight now. They stood up and began to walk, their feet sinking into the soft sand. The wash twisted and turned, and soon the open desert was hidden by its high, earthen walls. They followed it until they saw the road ahead, a bridge of dirt and asphalt crossing the wash. In the center was the black, yawning mouth of the culvert. It looked like the entrance to a tomb. "This is it," Rick said. They approached it cautiously. The culvert was a huge concrete pipe, maybe five feet in diameter. It was long, and utterly black inside. "I'll go first," Rick said, pulling his flashlight from his pocket. "You stay right behind me." He switched on the light. The beam cut a bright circle in the darkness, revealing a smooth concrete floor littered with debris and dried leaves. He ducked his head and stepped inside. The air was stale and close. Every sound was magnified. The scuff of his shoes, the sound of his own breathing, seemed to echo off the curved walls. He moved slowly, sweeping the flashlight beam ahead of him. The pipe was dry, thankfully. He could see the other end, a faint circle of lighter gray against the pitch black. He was halfway through when Scotty, a few feet behind him, stumbled. Scotty let out a small, sharp gasp as he caught his balance. He had kicked a loose rock. The sound was like a pistol shot in the enclosed space. Rick's blood ran cold. He immediately switched off the flashlight. They were plunged into absolute darkness. They stood frozen, listening. Above them, they heard a sound. A car door opening. Rick's heart hammered against his ribs. He heard footsteps on the road, right above their heads. The man from the truck was checking the noise. Rick held his breath. He could hear the man walking back and forth, his boots crunching on the gravel at the side of the road. He was directly overhead. A small pebble, dislodged by the man's weight, fell from the top of the culvert entrance and clattered onto the concrete floor near Rick's feet. Rick didn't dare move a muscle. An eternity seemed to pass. Then, he heard the footsteps move away. A car door slammed shut. Silence. Rick and Scotty waited, not daring to breathe. Had he gone? Or was he waiting, listening from inside his truck? After a long minute, Rick decided they couldn't wait any longer. He gestured in the dark for Scotty to follow and began to move again, this time with even greater caution, placing each foot as though he were walking on eggshells. They reached the end of the culvert and peered out. The desert spread before them, silver in the moonlight. The road was empty. The truck was gone. "He's gone," Scotty whispered in disbelief. "He must have thought it was just an animal." "Or he's trying to trick us," Rick said, not ready to relax. "He could be parked just down the road, out of sight, waiting for us to show ourselves." They climbed out of the wash on the south side of the road and took cover in a thicket of greasewood. They scanned the road in both directions. It was empty. The dark shape of the truck was nowhere to be seen. "I think he really left," Scotty said. "He probably figures we're still miles behind him. He's probably gone to set up another roadblock further south." "Maybe," Rick agreed. But he couldn't shake the feeling of being watched. They started walking again, sticking to the desert, keeping a hundred yards or so from the road. The eastern sky was beginning to lighten, the black turning to a deep, velvety blue. Dawn was not far off. With the coming of the light, their chances of being spotted would increase dramatically. They had to find a place to hide and wait out the day. They came to a region of badlands, a chaotic jumble of sun-baked clay hills and deep, eroded ravines. It was difficult terrain, but it offered plenty of cover. "In here," Rick said, pointing to a narrow, winding canyon. "We can find a spot in here and rest until tonight." They left the open desert and entered the maze of ravines. The ground was dry and cracked. They found a spot under a large overhang, a shallow cave that would shield them from the coming sun. They were utterly exhausted. Rick sat with his back against the cool rock wall and took a long drink from his canteen. The water was warm, but it was the best thing he had ever tasted. Scotty did the same, then slumped down and was asleep in moments. Rick knew he should sleep too, but his mind wouldn't shut off. He kept seeing the dark sedan, the helicopter, the man in the truck. He took the crumpled notebook from his pocket and looked at the tail number he had scribbled down. Everything depended on getting that number to the base. He put the notebook away and made himself more comfortable. He had to rest. They had another long night of walking ahead of them. He closed his eyes, and despite the tension and the fear, exhaustion finally claimed him. He slept.

A dull, throbbing heat pressed on Rick's eyelids. He stirred, his mouth dry and tasting of dust. He was lying on his side on hard-packed earth, his back against a wall of rock that was no longer cool. The sun had climbed high, and the overhang of the shallow cave offered little protection from the reflected glare. The air was still and scorching.

He sat up slowly, his muscles protesting. Every part of him ached from the night's exertions and the awkward way he had slept. Scotty was a crumpled heap a few feet away, his face flushed, breathing deeply. Rick was glad his friend was getting some rest. They would need all their strength.

He reached for his canteen. The metal was hot to the touch. He unscrewed the cap and took a small, careful sip. The water was lukewarm and tasted of metal and plastic, but it was life. He resisted the urge to gulp it down. They didn't have much. He sloshed the canteen gently. It felt less than half full.

He looked out from their hiding place. The badlands were a bleached and silent world. The sun hammered down on the tan and rust-colored clay, and the air above the ground danced in shimmering waves. There was no movement, no sound but the faint, high-pitched ringing of heat in his ears. It was a dead place.

Rick took the crumpled notebook from his pocket. The paper was soft with sweat. He smoothed it out on his knee and looked at the numbers and letters he had scrawled in the dim light of the helicopter's cockpit. N7708H. It seemed like a lifetime ago. What did it mean? It was a civil registration number, he knew that much. Probably for a helicopter owned by a private company. A company working with the men in the sedan.

His mind went back to the hotel. The voices. "We'll be out of here in fifteen minutes, if the others keep to schedule." "We can't use this place again." It was a rendezvous. A meeting. And they had been a part of it, a very unwelcome part. The men weren't just exploring a ghost town. They were professionals. The way they had tied him and Scotty, the way they had talked. It was cold and efficient.

He shivered, despite the heat. These men were dangerous. He and Scotty had been lucky. Lucky for a rattler and a rickety door. He had to grin at the thought. Some luck.

A groan from Scotty brought him back to the present. The dark-haired boy pushed himself into a sitting position, blinking against the fierce light.

"Man, it's hot," Scotty croaked, his voice thick with sleep. He ran a hand through his tangled hair. "What time do you think it is?"

Rick squinted at the sun's position. "Noon, maybe later. We slept for a few hours."

Scotty reached for his own canteen and took a drink, his throat working. He looked at Rick. "How much water have you got?"

"Less than half."

Scotty shook his canteen. "About the same here. It's going to be a long day." He leaned his head back against the rock, his eyes closed. "I keep thinking about that snake."

"Me too," Rick admitted. "Better a snake than those guys, though."

"I don't know," Scotty said grimly. "At least with a snake, you know what you're dealing with." He was quiet for a moment. "Who do you think they are, Rick? Spies?"

"Could be. Or smugglers. Something secret is going on. Something they don't want the base to know about. The car without lights, the helicopter meeting. It's all connected." Rick tapped the notebook in his pocket. "And this is the only real clue we have."

They fell silent, lost in their own thoughts. The heat was a physical presence, pressing down, making it hard to think, hard to breathe. The only thing to do was wait. Wait for the sun to lose its power. Wait for the relief of darkness.

Hours crawled by. They took tiny sips of water, making it last. They talked in low tones, rehashing their escape, speculating about their captors. The afternoon seemed to stretch into an eternity.

It was Scotty who heard it first. He sat bolt upright, his head cocked. "Listen."

Rick strained his ears. At first, he heard nothing but the ringing silence. Then he caught it. A distant, rhythmic chopping sound. It grew steadily louder.

"Helicopter," Rick whispered, his heart beginning to pound.

They scrambled deeper into the shadow of the overhang, pressing themselves against the back wall. The sound was much closer now, a loud, whirring beat that seemed to fill the sky. It wasn't the light aircraft from the night before. This sounded heavier, more powerful.

A shadow swept over their hiding place, and the roar was directly overhead. Dust and small pebbles rained down from the rock ledge above them. The wash from the rotors was a blast of hot, gritty wind. Rick squeezed his eyes shut, his body tensed, expecting a voice to boom from a loudspeaker, expecting a rope to drop from the sky.

The noise was deafening. It hovered for what felt like a long time, then slowly moved on, the sound receding as it swept farther down the canyon. They stayed frozen in place, listening until the chopping sound was just a faint buzz in the distance, and then gone.

Silence rushed back in, more profound than before.

Scotty let out a long, shaky breath. "That was close."

"Too close," Rick said. His shirt was stuck to his back with sweat. "They're searching the badlands. They know we came this way."

"They're not giving up," Scotty stated the obvious. "They must really want to find us."

The encounter left them shaken. The desert no longer felt empty. It felt like a trap, and they were the prey. They didn't talk much after that. They just waited, watching the shadows lengthen, praying for the sun to go down.

Finally, the relentless heat began to fade. The western sky turned from pale blue to orange and then a deep, bruised purple. A slight breeze stirred, cool on their sun-baked skin. The desert was coming alive again.

"It's time," Rick said, getting stiffly to his feet.

They finished the last of their water. The two empty canteens felt unnervingly light. Thirst was now their biggest enemy, a dull ache in the back of their throats.

"Which way?" Scotty asked, looking out at the darkening maze of ravines.

"Away from the road," Rick said decisively. "The helicopter was searching along it. They'll have men watching it tonight, for sure. We cut straight across country. The base is that way." He pointed south, to where the last glow of twilight lingered. "We'll use the North Star to keep our bearing."

Scotty nodded. "Okay. Straight across it is."

They climbed out of the ravine and set off, moving at a steady pace. The ground was uneven, a mix of gravel and hard-packed earth littered with rocks. The moon was rising, not yet full but bright enough to cast a pale, ghostly light over the landscape, creating deep, deceptive shadows.

They walked in silence for more than an hour, concentrating on placing their feet, conserving their energy. The initial coolness of the evening had worn off, and a thin sheen of sweat covered Rick's skin. His throat was painfully dry. He tried to swallow, but there was nothing to swallow.

They were crossing a wide, flat stretch of desert when Scotty, who was a few feet ahead, stopped suddenly. "Hold it," he whispered.

Rick came up beside him. "What is it?"

Scotty pointed at the ground. In the silvery moonlight, two parallel lines were clearly visible, pressed into the dirt. Tire tracks.

"A car," Rick breathed. "Out here?"

They crouched down to examine them. The tracks were fresh. They were from a car, not a truck or a jeep. The tread was for a street tire, not an off-road vehicle.

"The sedan," Scotty said. "It has to be."

"But what's it doing way out here?" Rick wondered aloud. The tracks didn't lead toward the main road they were avoiding. They cut west, deeper into the desolate wilderness. "They're going away from the base."

An idea began to form in Rick's mind. An idea born of thirst and desperation. "Scotty. What if they have a camp out here? A hideout."

"So? Let's get as far away from it as we can," Scotty urged.

"No, think," Rick insisted, his voice a dry rasp. "A camp would have supplies. It would have to. Water, Scotty. They'd have water."

Scotty was quiet for a long moment. Rick could see the conflict on his friend's face. The desire for water warring with the instinct for survival. "It's too risky, Rick. We'd be walking right to them."

"We'll be careful," Rick argued. "More careful than we've ever been. We'll follow the tracks from a distance. If it looks bad, we pull back. But we have to try. We won't make it to the base without water."

He was right, and they both knew it. Their thirst was becoming a serious problem. Another night of walking without a drink would leave them weak and stumbling by morning, easy to find.

"All right," Scotty agreed reluctantly. "But if I say we go, we go. No arguments."

"Deal," Rick said.

They turned west, their new goal pulling them forward. They didn't follow the tracks directly. Instead, they walked parallel to them, a hundred feet to the side, using the sparse cover of sagebrush and creosote bushes to break up their outlines.

The tracks led them toward a low ridge of dark rock that jutted from the desert floor like the spine of some massive, buried creature. The sedan had driven straight for it. As they got closer, Rick saw that the ridge was not a solid wall. It was broken by a series of narrow, shadowy canyons. The tire tracks disappeared into the mouth of one of them.

"This is it," Rick whispered. "They went in there."

They left the open ground and approached the canyon entrance, moving from one rock outcropping to the next. The air grew still and close within the narrow rock walls. They paused at the entrance, listening. There was no sound.

"Stay here," Scotty whispered, his voice barely audible. "I'll take a look."

Before Rick could object, Scotty had flattened himself against the rock wall and slipped around the corner into the darkness of the canyon. Rick's pulse hammered against his ribs. He waited, straining his ears for any sound, any sign of discovery. Every second stretched into a minute.

He was about to risk a look himself when Scotty's form materialized out of the shadows. He beckoned for Rick to follow.

Rick moved to his side. "What did you see?"

"The canyon opens up into a small basin," Scotty whispered back, his mouth close to Rick's ear. "The car's in there. It's covered with a tarp. And there's a light."

"A light?"

"Yeah. Faint. Behind some rocks. I think it's a campfire."

Water. The thought was overwhelming. Rick nodded, and together they moved into the canyon. It was short, no more than fifty feet long, and it twisted sharply before opening into a natural amphitheater, a circular basin walled in by sheer cliffs perhaps a hundred feet high. It was a perfect hideout.

On the far side of the basin, just as Scotty had described, was a dim, flickering orange glow. It was shielded by a cluster of boulders, the light thrown up against the rock face behind it. The sedan was parked near the canyon entrance, shrouded in canvas and further camouflaged with dead brush.

Two figures were silhouetted against the fire's glow. They were sitting on low rocks, talking in low, intermittent murmurs that carried across the basin in the still air.

Rick and Scotty dropped to their bellies, crawling behind a line of scrubby bushes that offered a sliver of cover. From here, they were about forty yards from the fire. They could see jerrycans stacked near the boulders. Water cans.

"We need to get closer," Rick breathed. "We need to hear what they're saying."

They began to crawl, moving an inch at a time, testing every patch of ground before putting their weight on it. The slow, painstaking process was agonizing. Finally, they reached the base of a rockpile that was only twenty yards from the campfire. They found a space between two boulders and peered through.

They could see the men clearly now. Neither was one of the men who had captured them in the hotel. One was thickset with a heavy beard. The other was tall and lean. They were drinking coffee from metal cups.

"...should have been back by now," the bearded man was saying. His voice was a low grumble.

"The schedule's shot," the lean one replied. He tossed the dregs of his coffee into the fire. "Ever since those kids got away. Now Johnson's got half the crew hunting for them instead of handling the pickup."

"It's a mess," the bearded man grumbled. "What happens if they don't find them?"

"They will. Can't get far out here. But it doesn't matter. The Spindrift isn't going to wait. The transfer is tonight, with or without Johnson. We just have to be ready."

Spindrift. Rick filed the name away. A transfer. A pickup. This was a staging area. He glanced at Scotty, whose eyes were wide in the faint, reflected light. They had stumbled right into the middle of the operation.

The lean man stood up and stretched. "I'm going to check the gear one more time." He walked away from the fire, disappearing into the shadows behind the boulders.

The bearded man remained by the fire, staring into the flames.

Rick saw his chance. The water cans were only a few yards from the fire. If he was quiet, if he was fast...

He looked at Scotty and pointed toward the cans, then at himself. Scotty's eyes widened and he shook his head emphatically. It was insane.

But the thirst was a fire in Rick's own throat. He had to try. He began to ease himself backward from the rockpile, intending to circle around in the deeper darkness. He moved with infinite care, his hands searching the ground before him. His fingers brushed against a loose stone, no bigger than his thumb. He froze. He didn't think he had moved it.

He waited, holding his breath. The bearded man at the fire didn't move. Rick let his breath out and began to shift his weight to his other hand.

It was a tiny sound. A scrape, and then a faint clatter as the small stone he had touched rolled a few inches and came to rest against another.

In the immense silence of the basin, it sounded as loud as a gunshot.

The bearded man's head snapped up. "What was that?" he called out sharply.

From the shadows, the lean man's voice answered. "What was what?"

"A noise. Over in the rocks."

The man stood up from the fire, his body tense. He peered into the darkness, directly toward the rockpile where Rick and Scotty were hiding.

"Probably just a coyote," the lean man said, his voice closer now as he walked back toward the fire.

"Maybe," the bearded man muttered, unconvinced. "But I don't like it. Not tonight."

Rick's heart was a cold lump in his chest. They were trapped. There was no way to retreat without making more noise. All they could do was lie perfectly still and pray the shadows would hide them.

The bearded man made a decision. "Get the big flashlight," he ordered. "Let's check it out."

Feet scuffed on the dirt. A moment later, a powerful beam of light cut through the darkness. It played over the basin floor, then began to sweep slowly, methodically, across the rock faces. The beam moved closer, illuminating the bushes just in front of them.

Rick flattened himself to the ground, trying to will himself into the earth. The beam crept upward, sliding over the face of their rockpile. Any second now, it would drop into the gap between the boulders. Any second now, they would be found.
