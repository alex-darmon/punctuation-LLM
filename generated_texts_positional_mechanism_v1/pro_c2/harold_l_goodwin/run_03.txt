"Should have been here an hour ago," Sam muttered, his breath fogging in the chill night air. Leo shivered, pulling the collar of his jacket tighter. The abandoned cannery complex was even spookier than he had imagined. "Maybe they're not coming." "They're coming," Sam insisted. His voice was a low rumble. "This is the third night in a row a boat's met someone here. No lights, no noise. Something's going on." Leo peered through a grimy window of the packing shed they were hiding in. Below them, the dark water of the inlet lapped against the rotting pilings of a long pier. He was starting to think this was a bad idea. Suddenly, Sam tensed beside him. "There." A long, low shape was gliding across the water, a black shadow against the slightly less black surface. It moved with an eerie silence, not even the chug of an engine to mark its progress. For the first time, he felt a surge of excitement. They were right about this! Sam led them along the cannery's rear wall, using the deep shadows as a shield. Door's solid steel. They aren't going anywhere." Leo felt himself being lifted and thrown over a shoulder like a sack of potatoes. He was carried through a doorway and into a colder, musty-smelling space. His captor dropped him unceremoniously onto the damp concrete floor. It wasn't much, but it was a start. "I think I'm loosening it." Hope, sharp and sudden, flared in his chest. He ignored the pain in his arms and the biting cold of the floor. He went back to work, scraping the rope back and forth against the small point of metal hidden in the dark. The rope bit into his wrists, but he kept working.

The rope bit into his wrists, but he kept working. The rough hemp fibers sawed back and forth across the small, sharp point of metal. It was agonizingly slow work. Sweat beaded on his forehead, cold in the chill air of the ice house. The pain in his shoulders was a dull, constant fire, and his fingers were clumsy with cold and numbness.

"Anything?" Sam whispered from the darkness. His voice was tight with strain.

"It's fraying," Leo grunted, putting all his energy into a hard, sawing motion. He felt a single strand pop. A small victory, but it sent a jolt of renewed hope through him. He twisted his body, trying to find a better angle. His shirt was soaked with sweat and the dampness of the concrete floor. "This is going to take a while."

"We've got nothing but time," Sam said, though his voice lacked conviction. "Just hurry before my hands fall off."

Leo didn't answer. He was concentrating completely on the task. Scrape, pull, scrape, pull. The rhythm was maddening. He could feel the individual strands of the rope giving way one by one. It was like trying to chew through a steak with a single tooth. He imagined the men outside, loading their boat, getting ready to leave. Every second he wasted was a second they were getting closer to their escape.

Then, with a sudden, satisfying snap, a major part of the cord gave way. The tension on his wrists lessened instantly. "I'm through!" he gasped, his voice hoarse. He flexed his hands, pulling them against the now-loosened bonds. The rope was still looped around, but there was enough slack. With a painful effort, he contorted his hands and managed to slip one free.

A wave of relief washed over him, so potent it almost made him dizzy. His freed hand was a clumsy, tingling club, but it was free. He fumbled with the knots on his other wrist, his numb fingers struggling with the tight, wet hemp. It took another full minute before he had both hands untied. He rubbed them together, trying to bring feeling back into them. Pins and needles shot up his arms.

He crawled on his hands and knees over to Sam, the rough concrete scraping his palms. "Stay still," he whispered.

"You don't have to tell me twice," Sam said, his voice shaky with relief. "Find the knots?"

"I'm trying." The darkness was absolute, a black, velvety curtain that swallowed all light. Leo's hands, still clumsy, fumbled over Sam's back, searching for the ropes. He found them, pulled taut and biting into Sam's jacket. The knots were even worse than his had been. The man who had tied them was an expert.

"He really cinched these down," Leo muttered, probing with his fingertips for a loose end or a loop he could work on. There was nothing. It was a solid mass of twisted rope. "I can't get a purchase on it."

"Try pulling," Sam suggested.

Leo grabbed the main rope between Sam's wrists and pulled. Sam let out a sharp hiss of pain. "Sorry," Leo whispered.

"It's okay. Just get me out of this."

Leo's fingers finally found the hard lump of the main knot. He began to pick at it, digging his fingernails into the tight fibers, trying to worry a loop free. It was like picking at a rock. His own circulation was slowly returning, bringing with it a dull, aching pain in his hands. He ignored it and kept working. Minutes stretched into an eternity in the cold, silent dark. The only sounds were their own ragged breathing and the soft scraping of Leo's fingers against the rope.

"I think I've got something," he said at last. He had managed to work a small loop out of the main knot. He hooked two fingers into it and pulled. The knot resisted, then grudgingly gave a fraction of an inch. He pulled again, harder. The rope loosened. He worked frantically now, pulling loops, loosening twists, until the entire knot collapsed. Sam's hands were free.

Sam groaned as he brought his arms around, rubbing his wrists. "Thanks. I thought I was going to be stuck like that forever."

"We're not out of the woods yet," Leo reminded him, getting to his feet and fumbling with the ropes on his own ankles. They came away easily. He moved to untie Sam's feet. "We're still locked in."

They stood together in the oppressive blackness, listening. There was no sound from outside. "Maybe they're gone," Sam suggested hopefully.

"I doubt it. That door sounds like a bank vault. They think we're secure." Leo took a cautious step, his hands outstretched. His fingers met a cold, slimy wall. "Let's feel our way around. There has to be another way out of here."

They moved slowly, each keeping a hand on the wall, their feet shuffling on the gritty floor. The room was larger than Leo had thought, and the walls were slick with a thin film of moisture and what felt like ancient grime. The air was heavy with the smell of mold and brine.

"This place gives me the creeps," Sam said, his voice echoing slightly in the confined space.

"Just keep moving," Leo urged. Their hands traced the rough concrete, moving past unseen corners and recesses. It was a featureless box. They completed a full circuit and met back near the door.

"Nothing," Sam said, his voice flat with disappointment. "It's a concrete tomb."

"No. Wait," Leo said. He pounded his fist against the heavy steel door. The sound was a dull, unsatisfying thud. "They wouldn't build a place like this without ventilation. Or a way to get the ice out."

"What are you thinking?"

"In old canneries, they used to load ice from above, through a chute," Leo reasoned, his mind racing. He tipped his head back, staring up into the perfect blackness. "Let's check the middle of the room. And look up."

It was a strange, disorienting experience, walking into the center of a room they couldn't see. They moved with arms outstretched, like blind men, until their hands bumped into each other. "Okay," Leo said. "Now, can you see anything at all? Any change in the darkness above?"

They both stared upwards, their eyes straining. At first, there was nothing. But as his eyes adjusted further, Leo thought he could detect a very faint difference. Not light, but a patch of black that was somehow less black than the rest. A square of near-black against a background of pure-black.

"There," he whispered, pointing. "Do you see it?"

"I think so," Sam breathed. "A square. Right above us."

"That's it. That has to be it. The loading chute." Hope surged through Leo again. "Now, how do we get up there?"

"It must be twenty feet up," Sam said doubtfully.

"There has to be a ladder. Or something." Leo began searching the floor around them again, this time with a new purpose. His foot hit something that clanged softly. He knelt down, his hands exploring. "Sam, I've got it! It's a ladder. An iron one, built into the wall."

He followed the rungs with his hands. They were cold, thin, and slick with rust and grime. He tested the lowest one. It seemed solid enough. "Okay," he said, his decision made. "I'm going up. Stay here. If I can get it open, I'll find a way to get you up."

"Be careful," Sam said. "That thing's probably as old as the hills."

Leo's hands were still sore and his shoulders ached, but adrenaline pushed the pain away. He gripped the cold iron rung and pulled himself up. The metal was unforgiving, biting into his palms. He went slowly, testing each rung before putting his full weight on it. The ladder shuddered and groaned, and flakes of rust rained down on Sam's head below.

"You okay up there?" Sam called quietly.

"So far," Leo grunted, his knuckles scraping against the concrete wall. The chute seemed to be getting closer. He could feel a faint draft of cooler, fresher air. He reached the top of the ladder and his outstretched hand touched the wooden frame of the chute. He clung to the ladder with one hand and explored the opening with the other. It was a square hatch, made of heavy wood, and it was swollen tight in its frame. He pushed. It didn't budge. He pushed harder, putting his shoulder into it. It groaned but held fast.

He was going to need more leverage. He positioned his feet carefully on the top rungs, braced his back against the opposite side of the narrow chute shaft, and pushed with his legs. The wood creaked loudly, a sound that seemed explosive in the silence. For a second, nothing happened. Then, with a scraping shriek of protest, the hatch moved. It lifted a few inches, letting in a sliver of gray, pre-dawn light.

Leo pushed again, with all his strength. The hatch gave way, swinging upwards on rusty hinges and thudding against the roof. Cool, salty air washed over his face. He pulled himself up through the opening and onto a flat, gravel-covered roof.

He was out.

He lay there for a moment, breathing in the fresh air, his heart hammering against his ribs. Below, the waters of the small cove lapped against the pilings of the old cannery. The sky was turning from black to a deep, bruised purple in the east. He could see the bad guys' truck parked near the pier, and the low shape of their boat tied to the dock. There was a dim light on in the boat's cabin.

He scrambled back to the opening. "Sam!" he hissed. "I'm on the roof. The coast is clear."

"How do I get up?"

That was a problem. There was no rope. Leo looked around frantically. His eyes fell on a coil of thick electrical conduit cable lying near a defunct generator. It was heavy, but it would have to do. He dragged it over, uncoiled an end, and lowered it down the chute. "Grab on!" he yelled down. "I'll pull you up."

He heard Sam grab the cable. Leo leaned back, wrapping the cable around his arm, and began to haul. Sam was not light, and the cable was slick. For a few agonizing seconds, Leo felt his grip slipping. He gritted his teeth, dug his heels into the gravel of the roof, and pulled with every ounce of strength he had. Slowly, hand over hand, he brought Sam up. Sam's head and shoulders appeared in the opening, and he clambered out onto the roof, panting.

"I owe you one," Sam said, looking around. "Wow. We made it."

"We're not done yet," Leo said, his eyes fixed on the scene below. Two of the men, the big one and the thin one, were on the dock, moving crates from a stack on the pier onto the boat. The third man was in the cabin, probably at the helm. "They're getting ready to leave."

"We should get out of here," Sam said. "Call the police."

"By the time they get here, these guys will be halfway to Mexico," Leo countered. He pointed. "They saw us. They know who we are. If they get away, they might come looking for us." A shiver went down his spine that had nothing to do with the morning chill. "We have to stop them. Or at least slow them down until help can get here."

"How?" Sam demanded. "We're two kids. They're three grown men with a boat."

Leo's gaze swept across the rooftop and the adjacent pier. The cannery was a graveyard of old machinery. Rusting winches, decaying conveyor belts, and a large, skeletal crane that loomed over the dock where the boat was tied. Its hook dangled a few feet above a massive, folded-up cargo net. An idea, crazy and dangerous, began to form in his mind.

"I have a plan," Leo said slowly, a grin spreading across his face for the first time in hours. "But it's going to be tricky." He pointed to the crane. "See that?"

Sam followed his gaze. "You're kidding, right? You want to operate that thing?"

"Why not? It's just levers and gears," Leo said with more confidence than he felt. "You see that cargo net? If we can get that crane working, we can swing the boom over the dock. When they're all on the boat, ready to cast off, we drop it on them."

Sam stared at the crane, then at Leo, his eyes wide. "That's the craziest, most brilliant plan I've ever heard. Let's do it."

"It looks simple enough," Leo whispered, his hand hovering over the controls. He glanced out the window. The men were loading the last of the crates. They were laughing about something.

"They're almost done," Sam hissed from the doorway. "What do we do about the net?"

"You'll have to get down there," Leo instructed. "The release for the hook should be right on the block. When I get it in position, you have to pull the pin so it drops."

"Got it." Sam nodded, his face grim. He slid down a maintenance ladder on the crane's leg, landing silently on the pier below. He darted from the shadow of one pile of crates to another, until he was concealed directly beneath the crane's hook, hidden from the men on the dock.

Leo took a deep breath. He wrapped his hand around the lever marked 'Swing.' He had no idea if the crane even had power. He pulled the lever gently toward him. For a heart-stopping moment, nothing happened. He pulled it harder. With a horrible groan and a shower of sparks from an ancient electrical box, the massive arm of the crane began to move.

The men on the dock froze. The big one looked up, his face a mask of confusion. "What was that?"

Leo held his breath, not moving. The crane had swung only a few feet.

"Probably just the wind," the thin man said, eager to finish. "Come on, let's get these last two aboard."

They turned back to their work. Leo let out his breath in a silent rush. He waited until they had their backs to him, then pulled the lever again, this time more smoothly. The crane's boom swung out over the water in a wide, silent arc. He pushed another lever, and the hook and net lowered with a quiet whir of cables, stopping just a few yards from the boat's stern. He had to swing it back over the boat itself. He nudged the 'Swing' lever again. The third man, the pilot, rushed out of the cabin and was immediately entangled with the others. The boat, its lines now cast off, drifted a few feet from the dock, the three men struggling helplessly under the weight of the net.

Sam froze, his victory jig cut short. The big man was thundering down the wooden pier, his face contorted in a mask of fury. For a split second Sam was a statue, a deer caught in the headlights of a freight train.

Leo fell to the ground, rolled, and was back on his feet in an instant. The big man was hopping on one foot, his face a mask of agony and rage. He turned his furious glare on Sam.

"Come on!" Leo shouted, grabbing Sam's arm. "Let's go!"

They didn't need a second invitation. They ran, side by side, leaving the hopping, cursing man behind them. They didn't stop until they had put two of the cannery's largest buildings between themselves and the docks. They found a narrow, dark space between a rusting water tank and a brick wall and squeezed into it, panting for breath.

"You okay?" Leo whispered, his chest heaving.

"I think so," Sam gasped. "Did you see his face?"

"I saw his hand on my jacket," Leo said, rubbing his shoulder. "Thanks. You saved me back there."

"You distracted him from me," Sam replied. "I guess that makes us even." He peeked cautiously around the edge of the water tank. The docks were not visible from here. All he could see was the peeling paint of the building opposite and the darkening sky above. "What do we do now?"

Leo tried to think. His mind was still racing from the chase. "Those men on the boat," he said. "They're still there. And that guy is still here. We can't go back that way."

"What about the other way?" Sam suggested. "Through the town?" The abandoned town sat on a small rise behind the cannery.

Leo shook his head. "We don't know who else is around. Those men in the boat, one of them said something about a schedule. About others."

A cold realization settled over them. This wasn't just three men on a boat and one angry guard. It was bigger. Organized. And they had just dropped a net on the whole operation.

"They saw our faces," Sam said, his voice barely a whisper.

"I know." Leo's mind was working furiously. "We need to get out of here. We need to get to Sheriff Brody."

"The town is a good ten miles away," Sam reminded him. "And our bikes are chained up near the old general store, on the other side of this whole mess."

They were trapped. Trapped between the smugglers on the dock and the long, dark road to safety. For a minute they were silent, the only sounds their own ragged breathing and the distant cry of a gull.

"They had a car," Leo said suddenly.

Sam looked at him. "The men on the boat?"

"No, the men in the other story," Leo said, then shook his head, confused. "Sorry, I mean, the men who chase people in stories like this always have a car. That big guy, the one who chased us. He came out of the main building. He didn't come by boat."

"So?"

"So his car has to be here somewhere," Leo reasoned. "Probably parked out back, away from the water. If we can find it..."

"What?" Sam asked. "Steal it? I don't know how to hotwire a car, do you?"

"No," Leo said. "But I know how to let the air out of tires. And I know what a distributor cap looks like. My dad showed me once."

A slow smile spread across Sam's face. "If they can't drive," he said, catching on, "they can't come after us."

"Exactly," Leo confirmed. "We disable their car, we get to our bikes, and we ride like the wind."

It was a risky plan. It meant going back into the heart of the cannery grounds, looking for a car they weren't even sure was there, all while avoiding the man who now had a very good reason to want to catch them. But it was a better plan than waiting to be found.

"Okay," Sam said, his voice firm. "Let's do it."

"We need to be quiet," Leo whispered. "Like ghosts."

They slipped out from behind the water tank, moving into the deepening shadows. The cannery, which had been their playground for years, now felt like enemy territory. Every creak of a loose board, every rustle of wind through a broken window, sounded like a threat. They moved in a low crouch, sticking close to the walls of the buildings, using every bit of cover they could find.

They worked their way around the back of the main processing building, the same one the big man had emerged from. The back of the building faced a wide, gravel-covered lot that was mostly used for storing old fishing equipment and broken-down machinery. And parked near a stack of corroded lobster pots was a dark green, slightly battered pickup truck.

"That has to be it," Leo breathed.

They froze behind a large, overturned dory, studying the scene. The lot was empty. There was no sign of the man who had chased them. From the direction of the docks, they could hear faint, angry shouts. The men in the net were still trying to get free.

"He must have gone back to help them," Sam guessed in a whisper.

"Good for us," Leo replied. "Come on. We have to be fast."

They darted from the cover of the dory and ran, low and silent, across the gravel to the truck. Leo immediately went to the front, fumbling with the unfamiliar latch for the hood. Sam went to the tires. He pulled a small multi-tool from his pocket, a birthday present from his uncle, and flipped out the small, sharp blade. He crouched by the rear tire and pushed the point against the valve stem. The hiss of escaping air was shockingly loud in the quiet evening.

Leo, meanwhile, had managed to get the hood open. It creaked loudly and he froze, his heart pounding. But the shouting from the dock continued, covering the sound. He peered into the greasy, complex world of the engine. He had only a vague idea of what he was looking for. A round cap with a bunch of thick black wires coming out of it. He spotted it near the back of the engine block. It was held down by two metal clips.

He reached in, his fingers brushing against the still-warm engine. He unclipped one side. It was trickier than he thought. His fingers were clumsy with adrenaline.

"Leo, hurry!" Sam hissed. He had finished the first tire and was starting on the second.

Leo fumbled with the second clip. It snapped open. He lifted the distributor cap, pulling the central wire free from the coil. He didn't want to just leave it there. He had to take it. He tucked the plastic cap and its trailing wires under his shirt. It felt greasy and awkward against his skin.

He gently lowered the hood, not latching it all the way, hoping it would look undisturbed at a glance. "Done," he whispered.

Sam had just finished the second tire and was moving to the front. "Two more to go," he said through gritted teeth. The hissing sound seemed to fill the entire world.

Suddenly, a new sound cut through the air. It was the low rumble of an engine, coming from the main road that led to the cannery. A pair of headlights cut a swath through the dusk, sweeping across the buildings.

The boys froze. The "others" from the schedule. They had to be.

"Probably went to help with whatever that noise is," the second man, Marco, replied. He patted the fender of the truck. "At least he made it here on time." He walked around the front of the truck to the driver's side.

"Fine," the first man grumbled. He turned and started walking toward the docks, following the path the first man had taken. Marco leaned against the driver's side door, crossed his arms, and waited, his back to the boys.

This was their chance. It was the only chance they were going to get. Leo looked across under the truck's chassis and met Sam's eyes. He gave a tiny, almost imperceptible nod. Now.

Slowly, carefully, they began to inch backward, sliding on their stomachs across the sharp gravel. It was agonizingly slow. Every scrape of stone sounded like an avalanche. The man, Marco, shifted his weight, and they both froze. But he was just getting comfortable. He didn't seem to have heard anything.

They reached the shadow of the overturned dory and paused. Marco was still waiting by the truck, his back to them. They scrambled behind the boat, their hearts hammering.

"Go, go, go," Leo whispered.

They moved with a speed and silence born of pure terror. Finally, they reached the edge of the cannery property, where the gravel and dirt gave way to overgrown grass and scrub brush. They were clear.

"The bikes," Sam gasped. "The general store."

They ran, heads down, through the tall grass, their legs pumping. They burst out onto the old, cracked asphalt of the main street of the ghost town. The general store was at the far end, its porch sagging, its windows boarded up. Their bikes were right where they had left them, locked to a wooden post that held up the porch roof.

The wind whistled in their ears, a welcome sound after the terrifying silence of their escape. The gravel road stretched out before them, a gray ribbon leading away from the cannery and into the safety of the night. Behind them, tucked under Leo's shirt, was a greasy distributor cap, and in their minds was a story that they knew no one would ever believe. But they had to tell it. They had to.

Hartson Brant's gaze fell on his son, taking in the dirt, the torn shirt, the piece of rock still clutched in his hand. He walked over and put a hand on Rick's shoulder.

"Are you boys alright?" he asked, his voice softer now.

Rick finally let himself relax. The tension drained out of him so fast he felt dizzy. He dropped the heavy piece of steel. It landed with a thud.

"We're fine, Dad," Rick said. "But we found your spies."
