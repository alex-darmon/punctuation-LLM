In the cracked sliver of mirror, a stranger's eyes stared back at him: hard, hollowed by sleeplessness, and lit with a feral gleam he had not seen in many months. It was the face of the Lone Wolf, and its familiarity was a grim comfort.

He inventoried his assets. The few francs and centimes were sufficient for a roll of bread and a cup of black coffee at a working-man's zinc bar, nothing more. His shabby raincoat was a disguise in itself, but his suit, though well-worn, was of a cut too fine for the character he must now assume. The pocket-knife was serviceable. The string was merely string. It would not do. He required capital, not for comfort but for operation. He required a base from which to think, to plan, to strike.

Dressed once more, he descended the rickety stairs, melting into the pre-dawn bustle of market porters and street-sweepers. Paris was waking, and Paris, for the Lone Wolf, had always been a treasure chest waiting to be opened. He did not seek a fat purse to pick or a strayed bauble to purloin; such measures were for common thieves and beneath his contempt. His art was more refined. He needed a small, untraceable sum, enough to grease the wheels of his immediate design.

His steps led him, as if by instinct, toward the warren of streets around the Sorbonne. Here, among the book-stalls and stationers, was a certain shop that dealt in antique maps and prints. Lanyard had cased it long ago, not for its meager cash-box, but for a specific portfolio of 17th-century anatomical drawings he knew were kept in a rear office. The proprietor, a fussy, myopic man named Blanchard, was a creature of unshakable habit. At precisely seven-thirty, he would leave his shop to take his morning chocolate at a nearby café, leaving his young, distractible nephew in charge.

Lanyard passed the shop once, a casual stroller. He saw the nephew within, nose-deep in a lurid novel. He saw the proprietor emerge, lock the door with a key he then placed under a loose stone in the doorway, and toddle off toward his café. It was an invitation, a testament to a provincial security of mind that had no place in this city. Lanyard felt a flicker of professional disdain. He waited five minutes in a neighbouring alley, then emerged, retrieved the key, and let himself into the quiet, dusty shop. The bell above the door gave a cheerful little tinkle.

He did not go to the rear office. He did not touch the anatomical drawings. His target today was smaller, his needs more modest. He moved directly to a glass case near the front, a case he had noted on his first pass. It contained old scientific instruments: brass sextants, ivory-handled scalpels, and a beautiful little pocket-telescope of inlaid silver and mother-of-pearl. It was a piece for a gentleman of leisure, exquisite and utterly useless. And therefore, he reasoned, valuable. The lock on the case was a simple tumbler, a child's puzzle. His pocket-knife, with a minute of delicate pressure, persuaded it to open. He slipped the telescope into his coat pocket, relocked the case, and paused. From the till, he took not a single sou. He was not a thief; he was an investor, raising capital. He let himself out, replacing the key beneath the stone, and was gone before the nephew had even turned a page.

Half an hour later, in a pawnshop on the rue Mouffetard whose proprietor asked no questions so long as the merchandise was good, the telescope became three hundred francs. It was more than enough. He purchased a cheap, durable workman's blouse, a coarse wool cap, a loaf of sausage-stuffed bread, and a flask of raw brandy. Thus fortified and transformed, he found a bench in the Luxembourg Gardens, now filling with nursemaids and their charges, and began the real work.

Where was "the usual place"? Popinot. Lanyard dissected the man in his mind. Popinot was not of the true Apache caste, the silent, knife-in-the-dark predators of the slums. He was a step above: a procurer, a blackmailer, a parasite who fed on the weaknesses of the rich. He aped their manners, coveted their luxuries. His vanity was his defining feature. Therefore, "the usual place" would not be some sordid back-alley dive. It would be a place of public resort, but one that afforded privacy. A place that flattered his sense of self-importance. A place where a man like him could meet a woman like Lucy Shannon without raising eyebrows.

Not a grand hotel; too many prying eyes, too much formal scrutiny. Not a public park; too open, too common. A café? Possibly, but which one? There were thousands. Lanyard's mind, a meticulous index of the city's hidden life, began to cross-reference. Popinot favoured the districts around the Grands Boulevards. He had a taste for good food and bad women. He enjoyed the theatre of his own life.

The answer, when it came, was so clear, so obvious, that Lanyard almost laughed aloud. Le Zéphyr. It was not the city's most fashionable restaurant, nor its most exclusive. It was, however, renowned for two things: a solid, unpretentious cuisine that attracted a loyal clientele of businessmen and politicians, and a series of exceedingly discreet cabinets particuliers on its second floor. These private dining rooms were a Parisian institution, places where deals were struck, affairs conducted, and reputations quietly ruined. They were served by their own staircase and a staff famous for its collective deafness, blindness, and amnesia. Popinot would feel like a king there, playing the man of affairs, secure in his temporary kingdom. And it was but a ten-minute walk from the Opéra, his preferred hunting ground. Yes. Le Zéphyr. Noon.

He arrived at half-past ten. The restaurant was on a corner, its ground-floor windows already gleaming. Lanyard did not approach the front. He circled the block, his eyes scanning the architecture. He found what he was looking for in the narrow service alley behind: a row of second-floor windows. One of them, belonging to a linen closet or a pantry, he guessed, was slightly ajar. Below it, a large refuse bin. It was almost too easy.

He retired to a small café across the street, ordered a coffee he did not drink, and watched. He saw the delivery of wine, of linens, of produce. He saw the kitchen staff arrive, smoking and joking in the alley. At eleven-fifteen, a portly, bald man in a fine but slightly-too-tight suit emerged from a taxicab. Popinot. Lanyard's jaw tightened. The man did not enter, but stood on the pavement, preening, checking his watch, a picture of smug anticipation. He was early. A sign of his eagerness.

Lanyard finished his brandy in one fiery gulp. It was time. He crossed the street, entered the service alley, and finding it momentarily deserted, mounted the refuse bin. The window was a tight squeeze, but his lithe frame slipped through with barely a sound. He found himself, as predicted, in a small, warm room smelling of soap and steam, stacked floor to ceiling with tablecloths and napkins. The door was unlocked.

He eased it open a crack. A corridor, carpeted and quiet. At one end, the main stairwell; at the other, a series of numbered doors. He heard the murmur of voices, the clink of silver. He slipped into the hallway, a ghost in workman's clothes, and flattened himself into the shadows of an alcove. He saw a waiter bustle past, a tray held aloft, disappearing into Number Four. A moment later, he heard Popinot's unctuous laugh from within.

Lanyard's heart was a cold, heavy stone in his chest. He needed to get closer, to hear. Number Three, next door, was dark and silent. He tried the handle. Locked. Of course. But the doors in these old buildings were often set in thin partitions. He pressed his ear to the wood paneling between the two rooms. Muffled sounds, nothing distinct. He needed a better way.

His eyes scanned the corridor. At the far end, near the service stair he had just bypassed, was a dumbwaiter, a relic of a more gracious age, used now only to send down dirty dishes. Its shaft would run the height of the building, a hollow core connecting the floors. And the wall of the shaft would be adjacent to the walls of the dining rooms. A listening tube.

He moved silently back toward the service area. The dumbwaiter door was a simple hook-and-eye latch. He opened it and peered into the greasy darkness. Below, the clatter of the kitchen; above, silence. The ropes and pulleys were thick with grime. He looked back toward the occupied room, Number Four. The dumbwaiter access panel for that room would be inside, of course. But the shaft itself was his conduit.

He returned to the alcove by Number Three. Directly opposite, on the other side of the hall, was Number Five. He tried its handle. It turned. The room was empty, the table unlaid. He slipped inside, locking the door behind him. Here too was a dumbwaiter panel. He worked it open. The dark shaft yawned before him. By leaning in, he could just see the corresponding panel for Number Four across the narrow abyss. It was closed tight. But sound would travel up the shaft. It was not ideal, but it would have to do.

He was settling in to wait, his ear near the opening, when he heard light footsteps in the hall. They paused outside Number Four. A soft knock. The door opened.

"Ah, my dear," came Popinot's oily voice, amplified by the hallway. "Punctual, as always. Come in, come in!"

Lanyard did not need to see. He knew the graceful, hesitant way she would move. He knew the scent of her perfume, a faint violet fragrance that now seemed a part of the betrayal. He squeezed his eyes shut. For a single, agonizing moment, he was back in the restaurant by Les Halles, his hand on hers, the future a sunlit path. Then he opened them. The wolf was back, and his gaze was fixed on the hunt.

He pressed his ear to the dumbwaiter shaft. The voices were clearer now, echoing in the enclosed space.

"...a glass of sherry? They have an excellent Amontillado." Popinot, playing the host.

"No, thank you, Monsieur. I would prefer to be brief." Her voice. It was low and strained, without the quiet confidence he remembered. It was the voice of someone walking a tightrope.

"As you wish, my dear Miss Shannon. Business before pleasure, eh?" A thick chuckle. "First, then: have you brought them?"

A pause. Lanyard held his breath.

"I have," she said. "But we must be clear on the terms. You have the letters?"

"Of course, of course! Popinot is a man of his word." A rustling of paper. "Three letters, as we discussed. All written in your own hand, all quite compromising, I'm afraid. One to the German attaché in '13, detailing your father's financial indiscretions. One to the Minister's wife, a rather... colourful account of her husband's private hobbies. And the best one, a very desperate-sounding plea to your former fiancé, the young Vicomte, begging him not to expose your little... arrangement. Tsk, tsk. A dangerous game you were playing, my dear."

Lanyard felt the blood drain from his face. This was not what he had expected. This was a past he had never conceived, a web of blackmail and desperation that predated him entirely. He had filled in the outlines of her history to his own taste, a simple story of a girl of good family. The reality was infinitely more complex, and infinitely more sordid.

Her voice, when it came, was brittle. "They are not for you to read. Do you have them, or not?"

"Right here," Popinot said soothingly. "And I also have what you asked for. Two thousand francs, and a second-class passage to Le Havre, on a freighter leaving tonight. From there, you can get to America, or wherever you like. A clean slate."

"And Lanyard?" she asked, her voice barely a whisper. "You will not go to the police? You will let him go?"

"My dear, what is Lanyard to me, without the ledgers? He is a ghost, a minor annoyance. Let him run. The police have bigger fish to fry, and frankly, so do I. Without those books, he has no leverage. He is just another broken man in a city full of them. This deal is for you and me. The ledgers, for your letters and your freedom. A fair exchange."

Lanyard leaned his head against the cool plaster of the wall. He felt a dizzying wave of... something. Not relief. Not joy. It was a vertiginous recalculation of his entire world. She had not been selling him out. She had been buying his freedom. She had come to Popinot not as a conspirator, but as a victim, trading the one thing she had—the location of the ledgers—for a chance to escape a past that had its claws in her. She was sacrificing her one hold over Lanyard to save him from the police and herself from a blackmailer. She was cleaning the slate for both of them, at her own expense.

The shame was a physical blow. The scene on the steps of the Sacré-Cœur replayed in his mind, but with new clarity. Her distress, her furtive glances, his own arrogant assumption of betrayal. He had been a fool. A stupid, arrogant, wounded fool. He had reverted to the Lone Wolf, indulging his own pain, when she had been in the midst of a desperate, courageous battle on his behalf.

"Very well," she said, her voice gathering a sliver of strength. "Give me the letters and the ticket. I will tell you where he has hidden the ledgers."

"After I have them in my hand," Popinot corrected smoothly. "I am not a fool, my dear. We will go together. You and I. We will retrieve them from this hiding place, and once I have verified them, I will give you this envelope. Not before."

"No," she said, a new, firm note in her voice. "That was not our agreement."

"Agreements change," Popinot said, his voice hardening. "You are in no position to bargain. You will take me to the ledgers now. Or I will burn these letters one by one in this ashtray, and then I will make a telephone call. The choice is yours."

Lanyard heard a small, sharp intake of breath. He heard the scrape of a chair.

He had heard enough.

He did not burst in. The Lone Wolf did not blunder. He stepped back from the dumbwaiter, his mind racing, cold and clear once more, but animated by a purpose entirely new. He was no longer hunting for control, but for redemption. His own.

He strode out of room Number Five, down the corridor, and without knocking, opened the door to Number Four.

The scene within was a tableau. Popinot, halfway out of his chair, a sneer of triumph on his face. Lucy Shannon, standing pale and defiant, her back to the door. On the table between them, a small stack of letters and a thick manila envelope.

They both turned as he entered. Popinot's jaw dropped, the sneer turning to a mask of slack-jawed shock. Lucy's eyes widened, first in terror, then in a complex flood of emotions he could not begin to name. "Michael!" she breathed.

Lanyard ignored them both. He walked to the table with the unhurried grace of a man who owned the room. His eyes swept over the letters, the envelope, the wine glasses, the ashtray.

"Popinot," he said, his voice quiet, almost conversational. "You have exceeded your brief. You are a bottom-feeder, a slug. You are not equipped to swim in these waters."

Popinot found his voice, a spluttering, indignant squeak. "Here now, Lanyard! You have no right—"

"I have every right," Lanyard cut in, his voice still soft, but with an edge of polished steel that silenced the other man instantly. He picked up one of the letters. "You traffic in shame, Popinot. It is a poor commodity. It spoils easily." He looked at Lucy, and for the first time, his eyes were not hard. They were filled with a profound and aching regret. "And you, Miss Shannon," he said softly. "You should have trusted me."

He then did a very curious thing. He picked up the heavy, silver-plated water pitcher from the centre of the table. With a swift, economical motion, he poured its entire contents over Popinot's head.

The man gasped, sputtered, and flailed, drenched and ridiculous, his carefully-oiled hair plastered to his scalp.

While Popinot was still blinking and dripping, Lanyard scooped up the letters and the manila envelope from the table. He turned to Lucy. "Is this everything?" he asked.

She could only nod, her eyes fixed on his face.

"Good." He tucked the envelope into his own pocket. Then he took the letters, her past, her shame, and with a single, sharp tear, ripped them in half, then in quarters, and let the pieces flutter down onto the sodden tablecloth. "The debt is paid," he said.

He grabbed Lucy's hand. "Come."

He pulled her toward the door, leaving Popinot dripping and speechless in his ruined sanctuary. "Wait!" the man finally managed to roar. "My ledgers! You can't—"

Lanyard paused in the doorway and looked back, a faint, wolfish smile touching his lips. "I can," he said. "And I will. I believe you know where to find me, should you wish to discuss terms. The usual place."

And then, hand-in-hand, they were gone, leaving the door swinging on its hinges, leaving Popinot with nothing but a lapful of ice water and the torn confetti of his failed little enterprise. The hunt was over. A new one was about to begin.

The air in the little cabinet particulier grew thick with unspoken things; with the ghost of a future he had so boldly sketched and the shadow of a past she so carefully concealed. The spell was broken by the discreet reappearance of their waiter, bearing the bill upon a silver plate. Lanyard settled the account with an air of magnanimity that his few remaining francs hardly justified, and a moment later they were descending to the street.

The mist had thickened into a fine, persistent drizzle that gave the gas-lamps a jaundiced and diffuse halo. The city seemed to have sunk into a weary quietude, the late-night traffic sparse and hurried. He drew her hand through the crook of his arm, a gesture both proprietary and protective, and for a time they walked in silence, their footfalls echoing softly in the deserted streets. He steered them not toward the nearest Métro station, but eastward, into the labyrinthine heart of old Paris.

"What you did back there," she began, her voice low and tight, "with that man... with Popinot...."

"Was a necessary piece of business," he finished for her, his tone level. "A bit of sanitation. The world is cleaner for it."

"You... you terrified him. You terrified me."

He glanced down at her, a faint smile playing on his lips. "The first was my intention; the second, I regret. But there was no other way. A creature like Popinot understands only one language: the credible threat of ruin. I spoke it fluently."

"And the envelope you took?" she pressed, her fingers tightening on his arm. "The one he called his ledgers?"

"His insurance policy," Lanyard corrected. "And now, it is mine. He kept meticulous records, that one. A list of all the poor souls caught in his web. They turned into a narrow, cobbled alley, so dark it seemed a mere cleft between the ancient, leaning buildings. She stopped, pulling him to a halt beneath the feeble light of a single, rust-streaked lantern. "So this is your new beginning?" she asked, and there was no judgment in her voice, only a profound, searching curiosity. "To become a blackmailer yourself?"

"Never," he stated with quiet force. "I do not trade in shame, Miss Shannon; I intend to abolish it. I told you I must prove myself. This is how. I cannot simply turn my back on the world I knew. My past is not a coat I can take off and hang in a closet. But I can use what I learned in that world, the skills I honed, to a different end. I will not steal from these people. She was silent for a long moment, studying his face in the gloom. He saw not fear in her eyes now, but a dawning comprehension, and a touch of awe. "The hunt is over," she murmured, echoing the thought that had been in his own mind. "A new one has begun."

"Precisely," he said. "And it will be a dangerous one. Which is why we cannot go back to your apartment, nor to any place we have been before. We need a new refuge. And I need to examine these ledgers with a clear head."

Without another word, he led her on, through a warren of streets that grew steadily less reputable, until they came to a small, grim hotel tucked away on a side street near the Porte Saint-Denis. Its sign, "Hôtel des Voyageurs", was faded, one of its letters missing. It was the sort of establishment that asked no questions and kept no register to speak of, a transient haven for those who wished to remain unseen. Lanyard spoke a few low words to the drowsing night porter, a corpulent man with a face like a dissipated bulldog, and pressed a bill into his hand. The man grunted, procured a key from a board, and gestured them toward a narrow, unlit staircase.

Their room was on the fourth floor, overlooking a central well of utter blackness. It was small, monastically bare, containing only an iron bedstead, a rickety wash-stand with a porcelain basin and pitcher, a single wooden chair, and a wardrobe. The air was stale with the ghosts of a thousand hurried sojourns. It was a place of profound anonymity; a place where one could simply cease to exist for a time. For Lanyard, it was perfect.

He bolted the door, then crossed to the window, peering cautiously through a slit in the grimy curtains down into the street. It was empty save for a stray cat. Satisfied, he turned back to the room, shrugging out of his damp raincoat. Lucy stood motionless in the centre of the floor, her Burberry still buttoned to her chin, looking small and out of place in the grim surroundings. Her eyes followed his every move.

"Not exactly the Ritz," he remarked, with a wry twist of his lips. "But it is safe. For tonight, at least."

He pulled the single chair to the small table, switched on the unshaded bulb that dangled from the ceiling, and drew the manila envelope from his pocket. The harsh light threw his face into sharp relief, all keen angles and deep-set shadows, his expression one of intense concentration. He slit the envelope open with his thumbnail and slid out a thick, leather-bound book.

He opened it. The pages were covered in a cramped, precise script. Names, addresses, dates, and cryptic notations filled column after neat column. He read for several minutes in absolute silence, the only sound in the room the faint drip of water from a faulty tap somewhere down the hall. Lucy watched him, her hands clasped tightly before her. She saw not the charming dinner companion, nor the ardent suitor, but the Lone Wolf in his element: cold, calculating, and utterly absorbed in the architecture of his new campaign. It was as if a mask had dropped, revealing the formidable machinery that worked behind his easy smile.

"Mon Dieu," he breathed at last, looking up from the page. His eyes were alight with a grim fire. "This is worse than I imagined. He looked at her, and his expression softened as he registered her pallor, the tension in her posture. "I'm sorry," he said, his voice changing, becoming gentle once more. "My endgame," he said slowly, "is to force Popinot into a final confrontation, on my terms. I will dismantle his empire piece by piece until he has nothing left but desperation. A desperate man makes mistakes. And when he makes his, I will be there. I will see him ruined or imprisoned, not just for what he did to you, but for all of them." He looked down at the ledger again, his jaw tight. "This is the task I have undertaken. This is my report, at the end of the year, that I will bring to you."

An immense wave of tenderness washed over him, momentarily eclipsing the cold strategist. "Lucy," he began, his voice rough with an emotion he could not quite name. "About what I said earlier, in the restaurant... about my feelings...."

"You must not say it again," she interrupted softly, placing a finger on his lips. "Not now. Not while we are... here. In the middle of this. Let us survive the night. Let us survive the tomorrow you have planned. There will be time for such words when the hunt is truly done."

He nodded, understanding her unspoken plea. To speak of love now would be to profane it with the grim reality of their situation. It was a promise for a cleaner, brighter time. He took her hand and raised it to his lips, kissing her fingers gently. "As you wish," he said. "But know this. Whatever happens, my first duty is to your safety. And so they settled in for the long hours until dawn, a strange tableau in the heart of sleeping Paris. Lanyard sat in the rickety chair, his pistol cleaned and lying on the table beside Popinot's ledger, his senses preternaturally alert. Lucy, refusing the bed, curled up in her coat at the foot of it, a makeshift pillow beneath her head. The light remained on, a solitary beacon in the encroaching darkness. For a time they spoke in low tones, of trivial things, of places they had seen, books they had read, deliberately avoiding the dangerous topic that lay between them on the table. But gradually, silence fell, and in the quiet, Lanyard felt the full weight of his resolution. He was no longer the Lone Wolf, adrift and accountable to no one. He had a purpose, a cause, and, most terrifying and exhilarating of all, someone to lose.

Hours crept by. The city outside sank into its deepest slumber. It must have been close to three in the morning when a sound, faint but distinct, broke the stillness. A soft, scraping noise from the hallway, near their door.

He backed away from the door, gesturing for Lucy to move to the far corner of the room, away from the line of fire. His eyes swept the room, assessing, planning. There was no escape. That he is the true man, and the one I have come to know is only a... a mask you wear for my benefit." A distant shout echoed down the warren of alleys, galvanizing him. This was no place for a debate on the metaphysics of his soul. "We can't stay here," he said, his tone once more brisk and practical. He took her arm. "We must find a place to rest. A hotel. Somewhere anonymous, where we can think." She allowed him to lead her away, but the warmth of their earlier intimacy was gone, replaced by a polite, sorrowful distance. He guided them through the twisting by-ways with unerring skill, his senses on high alert, scanning every shadow, every doorway. They emerged onto a wider, more populated street, a boulevard noisy with the rumble of late-night traffic and the chatter of people spilling out of brasseries. Here, Lanyard hailed a taxicab, a monstrous, coughing machine that reeked of stale tobacco. He gave the driver the address of a small, discreet hotel he knew near the Gare de l'Est, a place frequented by commercial travelers and those who had reason to desire anonymity. The journey was made in silence. Lanyard sat beside her, acutely aware of the space between them, a gulf wider than the worn leather seat. He looked at the bundle of clothes on his lap; the suit of a dead man, paid for with the promise of a future crime. It felt heavy, damning. The hotel was a tall, narrow building squeezed between a tobacconist and a closed bakery. The concierge, a sleepy man with a drooping moustache, barely glanced at them as Lanyard signed the register with a fictitious name and paid for a room in advance. The key he was given was attached to a heavy brass fob. Their room was on the fourth floor, reached by a rickety, protesting lift. It was small and sparsely furnished: a brass bed, a wardrobe, a small table with a single chair, and a washstand with a pitcher and basin. A single, unshaded bulb cast a harsh, dreary light over everything. It was a room for transients, a place of profound impermanence. Lanyard set the bundle on the bed and turned to face her. The door was closed. They were safe, for now, from the world outside. But the true danger was here, in this room, in the doubt that shadowed her eyes. "You must let me explain," he said. She crossed to the window and pulled back the thin, grey curtain, looking down at the glistening street below. "There is nothing to explain," she said, her voice weary. "You did what you felt you must. You said it yourself; it was an expedient." "But you don't approve." "How can I?" she asked, turning from the window. The stark light caught the distress in her face. "You speak of building an honest life, Michael. But the very first step you take, upon that new path, is to plan a burglary. You pay for our safety with the future misery of others. The foundations you spoke of... they are not just built on your past. You are laying them now, with fresh dishonesty." He ran a hand through his hair in a gesture of pure frustration. "You see it in such absolute terms! Black and white. Good and evil. The world I have lived in is not like that. It is a world of shades, of necessities. To escape that world, I must use its own rules against it, just this once." "Just this once," she repeated, and her voice held a note of infinite sadness. "That is what every man tells himself, is it not? The gambler who makes one last bet. The drunkard who takes one last drink. The thief who plans one last job." He felt a surge of anger, hot and defensive. "This is different! I am not doing it for myself, for the thrill or the profit. I am doing it for you! For us!" "No," she said, and her quiet denial cut through his anger like a shard of ice. "You are doing it for yourself. Because it is what you know. It is easier for you to conceive of a brilliant, intricate crime than it is to imagine earning an honest living. You told me yourself; you were a drudge at old Troyon's. You hated it. The Lone Wolf does not know how to be a drudge. He knows how to be a king in his own dark kingdom." He had no answer for that, for it was the truth. He opened the bundle and took out the suit Anatole had provided. It was a dark, well-made tweed, sober and respectable. As he shed his own worn raincoat and shabby jacket, he felt as if he were shedding a skin. But what lay beneath? He began to dress in the dead man's clothes. The irony was not lost on him; he was trying to build a new life for himself by stepping into the garments of a man whose life had ended. The suit fit him remarkably well. As he buttoned the waistcoat, his fingers brushed against a small, hard object in an inner pocket. Curious, he drew it out. It was a small, silver locket, tarnished with age, hanging from a delicate chain. It was not the sort of thing a man would typically carry. He pressed the catch. It sprang open, revealing two miniature, hand-tinted photographs. On one side was a woman with a gentle, smiling face, and on the other, a small child, a boy of no more than four or five, with serious, dark eyes. He stared at the tiny faces, and the reality of Anatole's callous phrase, "a client who... met with an accident," struck him with the force of a physical blow. This was not just a suit. It was a life. A life with a wife and a son. A life that had been extinguished, perhaps in the very world of crime and violence he was so desperate to escape. He felt Lucy's eyes on him. He looked up and saw that she had moved closer, her gaze on the locket in his hand. "Anatole's client," she whispered. A wave of revulsion washed over him. He thought of the deal he had struck, the information he had sold. He had used his knowledge of a man's weakness, his love for a woman, to set him up for ruin. And what of the watchman who drank? Did he too have a wife? Children? He saw now, with a clarity that was agony, the web of consequence that spread from his actions. It was not an abstract exercise in strategy; it was a chain of human suffering, and he was the one forging the links. "My God," he breathed, sinking onto the edge of the bed. He looked from the locket to her face, and his own was a mask of self-loathing. "You were right. You were right all along." He held out the locket. "This... this is what it means. This is the reality. Not a game of wits, not a clever evasion. It's this. A man is gone, and this is what is left. And I... I just used another man's love to buy my freedom." Her expression softened, the judgment in her eyes replaced by a profound, aching pity. She came and sat beside him on the bed, her shoulder just touching his. She did not take the locket, but looked down at it where it lay in his palm. "You see it now," she said softly. "I do," he admitted, his voice hoarse. "I saw it only as a problem to be solved, a move on a chessboard. I didn't see the people. I've spent a lifetime training myself not to." He closed his hand over the locket, its sharp edges digging into his palm. The smooth, self-confident shell of the Lone Wolf had cracked, and the man underneath was exposed, raw and uncertain. "What Anatole and I arranged... it cannot be undone. The information is given. The plan is in motion." "And the money?" she asked. He looked at the thick envelope lying on the bedspread. Five thousand francs. The price of a man's ruin. "It feels unclean," he said. He stood up and walked to the washstand, pouring water from the heavy ceramic pitcher into the basin. With a deliberate motion, he unbuttoned his new waistcoat and shirt cuffs, and began to wash his hands, scrubbing them as if he could wash away the stain of his bargain. He watched the grey, soapy water swirl in the basin. "This life," he said, more to himself than to her, "is a poison. Even when you try to use it for good, it corrupts everything it touches. You were right to be afraid. You should run from me, Lucy. As fast as you can." He turned, drying his hands on a thin towel, expecting to see fear or revulsion in her eyes. Instead, he saw a strange, new light; a measure of respect. "A moment ago," she said, her voice steady, "I was afraid of the man who made the bargain. But I am not afraid of the man who is standing before me now. The man who sees the truth and hates it... he is the man I believe in." He stared at her, uncomprehending. "But I did it. I am still responsible." "Yes," she agreed. "You are. The question is, what will you do now? Not six months from now. Now. With that money. With that knowledge." He looked back at the envelope. An idea, half-formed and audacious, began to take shape in his mind. It was a chimerical thought, an impulse born of shame and a desperate need for atonement. "The jeweler Anatole means to rob... his name is Marchand. On the rue de la Paix. The nephew, the weak link, is named Philippe. He's in love with one of the shopgirls. He's in debt." He spoke the facts aloud, as if laying evidence before a court. "I told Anatole how to use the boy, how to pressure him." "And?" she prompted. "And I think," he said slowly, a grim smile touching his lips for the first time, "that five thousand francs is more than enough to get a foolish young man out of debt, if it is given to him with a very stern warning." Her eyes widened. "You would... warn him? You would betray Anatole?" "Anatole's business is with the Lone Wolf," he said, a new and harder resolution in his voice. "And the Lone Wolf has left Paris. My business is with you. I made you a promise. It seems I am to have a chance to keep it sooner than I thought." He picked up the envelope of money. "I cannot build an honest house on a corrupt foundation," he said, echoing her own words back to her. "Perhaps it is time to dynamite the foundation and start again. Even if it leaves us with nothing." She rose and came to him, and this time, when she placed her hand on his arm, the warmth was back, a current of trust and renewed faith. "It will not leave us with nothing," she said softly. "It will leave us with everything that matters." He looked down at her, at the unwavering faith in her grave eyes, and felt a humility he had never known. The path ahead was no clearer; Popinot was still hunting them, their future was a blank page, and he had just declared war on his own past. But for the first time, he felt he was not walking that path alone. And for the first time, the man he wanted to be did not seem like an impossible, distant dream, but a choice he could make, here and now, in this dreary, anonymous room. He carefully placed the silver locket on the table, a small monument to a lesson learned in the hardest of ways. "We will need a new plan," he said. "A real one this time."
