"Then to the rue Jacob we go," Lanyard decided. "But we walk. No more cabs tonight. We will take the long way, through the alleys and courts. They will be searching the main thoroughfares."

For the next hour, they became ghosts in their own city, flitting through a labyrinth of back-streets and darkened passages that Lanyard knew as well as the lines on his own hand. He led her through the sleeping heart of Paris, across the Pont des Arts, its iron railings slick with moisture, and into the winding, ancient streets of the Left Bank. They spoke little, the silence between them a living thing, woven from shared danger and a new, unspoken understanding. His resolution to win her had been based on an illusion, on the idea of a simple, virtuous woman. The reality, he was discovering, was infinitely more compelling.

At last they came to the rue Jacob, a narrow street lined with the shuttered fronts of bookshops and art galleries. She led him to a small, dark storefront, its windows crammed with dusty volumes. The old man's eyes darted from her face to Lanyard's, and then to the street beyond. "Trouble follows your father's legacy like a shadow," he murmured, more to himself than to them. "Do not stand in the street. Come in, come in, both of you. And bolt the door behind you."

Lanyard let her speak. "I know a man," Lanyard said after a moment's thought. The name came to him from the depths of his past, a name he had not thought to utter again. "He is not a forger, not in the criminal sense. The room to which Monsieur Dubois conducted them was tucked away beneath the eaves, a space born of necessity rather than design. He watched her for a moment, her straight back, the proud set of her head. “And you?” he asked. “Which do you choose?”

She turned back to face him, and the tears had vanished, replaced by a fire he had seen once before, in the restaurant. “I am my father’s daughter,” she said simply.

In that moment, he knew he would have followed her into the very jaws of hell. And the thought did not frighten him in the least.

The next morning dawned grey and grudging, the light filtering weakly through the grimy windowpane. Lanyard had slept little, spending most of the night in the rickety chair, the weight of the pistol in his coat pocket a familiar comfort. He had insisted Lucy take the bed, and after a brief protest, she had acquiesced, falling into the deep sleep of pure exhaustion. He had watched her for a time, the rise and fall of her breathing, the way a stray lock of hair fell across her forehead, and a fierce, protective instinct had risen in him, an emotion more potent than any he had ever known. It was more than love; it was responsibility, a purpose that eclipsed the selfish ambitions of the Lone Wolf.

They breakfasted on stale bread and strong, hot coffee in the small kitchen behind the bookshop, the air fragrant with the competing aromas of roasting beans and ancient leather bindings. Monsieur Dubois was grim and preoccupied, but he had news.

“A friend of mine, a clerk at the Ministry of Justice, owed me a favour of some twenty years’ standing,” he announced, pouring a thin stream of milk into his coffee. “I called it in this morning. The official report on your father’s death, my dear Lucy, has been sealed. By order of the Minister himself, on recommendation from a certain committee on which the Baron de L’Isle sits. “It means he knows,” Lucy said, her face pale. “He knows we have the ledger.”

“He knows someone has it,” Lanyard corrected. “He suspects, perhaps, but he does not know. Sealing the file is a defensive move. He is covering his tracks, battening down the hatches. It gives us time.”

“Time you must use wisely,” Dubois said, fixing his gaze on Lanyard. “This artist of yours. Vachell. Where does he live?”

“In Montmartre. On a little street behind the Place du Tertre. It is not a place one simply walks into.”

Etienne Vachell lived, as Lanyard had said, in a narrow, crooked house wedged between a garish souvenir shop and a bistro that was already exuding a powerful aroma of onions and stale wine. She took a deep breath and nodded. "Ready."

Hand-in-hand, not like frightened children now but like sober conspirators, Monsieur and Madame Girard descended the rickety stairs of the tenement. They stepped out not into the sinuous rue d'Assas but into a bustling working-class street, loud with the cries of vendors and the rattle of commerce. They moved with the slow, unhurried pace of people with nowhere special to go, blending seamlessly into the river of humanity. Lanyard kept up a low patter of conversation about the price of textiles, the difficulty of finding reliable suppliers; a commonplace monologue for the benefit of any who might be listening.

They made their way to a large post office near the Gare de l'Est. The air was thick with the smells of coal smoke, sweat, and cheap perfume. Lanyard chose this place for its very anonymity, its chaotic crush of people coming and going. He held the envelope for a moment, a final, symbolic hesitation. It contained his life, his sins, his triumphs; it was the sum of all he had been. With a firm, decisive movement, he pushed it through the brass slot. The faint sound of its landing within the iron box was the tolling of a bell for the death of the Lone Wolf.

He turned back to her, a faint, rare smile touching his lips. "It is done," he said. "He is dead. Long live... Monsieur Girard."

For the first time that day, she smiled a genuine, radiant smile. "And Madame Girard," she added. "Though I think she will insist on a new wardrobe once we reach Lyons."

The mention of a destination, a future, seemed to make it real. The tension that had held them in its grip for days seemed to loosen its hold. They walked out into the sunlight, which now seemed brighter, cleaner. They had a thousand francs, a new set of names, and a world of possibilities before them. The most dangerous part, it seemed, was over.

It was precisely at this moment of burgeoning hope that Lanyard's finely honed instincts screamed a warning. It was nothing tangible; a flicker of movement in his peripheral vision, a figure detaching itself from the crowd, the indefinable sense of being watched by a professional. He did not turn his head; instead, his arm tightened around Lucie's, his pace unaltered. "To our left," he murmured, his voice devoid of all emotion, "by the newspaper kiosk. A man in a grey felt hat. Do not look."

She was a superb actress. Her steps did not falter; her head did not turn. "Popinot's?" she breathed.

"I do not know him. Which makes it worse. Popinot's men I know. This one... is different." The man was good; he was not a common street-corner mouchard. He kept his distance, using other pedestrians as a screen, moving with a lazy nonchalance that was utterly convincing to the untrained eye. But to Lanyard, it was a finely choreographed dance of predation.

"What do we do?" she asked, her voice steady.

"We continue our walk," Lanyard said calmly. "We are Monsieur and Madame Girard, newly arrived from the provinces, enjoying the sights of the capital before we catch our train. We are going to buy a hat for Madame. It is a very important transaction."

He guided her towards the Galeries Lafayette, a vast, overwhelming cathedral of commerce. If they were to be lost, this was the place to do it. The sheer density of the crowd, the maze of departments, the multiple exits offered a hundred avenues of escape. As they passed through the great gilded doors, Lanyard risked a fractional glance over his shoulder. The man in the grey hat was still there, fifty paces back, calmly lighting a cigarette, his eyes never leaving them.

"He has followed us inside," Lanyard reported. "Stay close. We will go to the millinery department on the third floor. We will take the escalator up, and the staircase down. Be ready to move quickly."

The ascent was a slow, agonizing torment. Lanyard could feel the man's eyes on his back, a physical pressure. He maintained his role, pointing out a display of silks to Lucie, his face a mask of placid domesticity. Lucie, for her part, played the dutiful wife, her expression one of mild interest. They reached the third floor and stepped off the moving staircase into a wonderland of feathers, ribbons, and felt. The air was heavy with the scent of perfume and money.

Lanyard did not hesitate. He led her directly through the department, past tables piled high with the latest creations. He did not look back. "Now," he whispered. He veered sharply, pulling her behind a tall display of hat stands, and pushed through a door marked 'Service'.

They were in a dim, dusty corridor, a stark contrast to the opulence they had just left. The sounds of the store were muffled. A staircase, meant for employees, spiralled downwards. They took the steps two at a time, their soft-soled shoes making little sound on the worn wood. Down one flight, then another. Lanyard gambled that their pursuer would lose precious seconds searching the crowded millinery floor before realizing his quarry had vanished.

They emerged on the ground floor, in a labyrinth of storerooms and delivery bays. The smell of cardboard and packing straw filled the air. Through a grimy window, Lanyard could see the street. Freedom was only a few yards away. He was about to lead her towards a side exit when a sound from the staircase above froze him in his tracks. The sound of a man descending, not with the hurried clatter of a guard, but with the quiet, deliberate tread of a hunter.

They were trapped. The way forward was blocked by deliverymen; the way back was cut off. Lanyard pushed Lucie into the deep shadow of a stack of crates. "Do not move," he hissed. He drew from his pocket not a pistol, for Monsieur Girard did not carry one, but a simple, heavy packing hook he had palmed from a nearby trolley. It was a crude weapon, but it would serve. He flattened himself against the wall, his heart hammering not with fear for himself, but with a cold, desperate terror for her.

The footsteps stopped at the bottom of the stairs. The man in the grey felt hat stepped into the corridor. He was not one of Popinot's thuggish brutes. He was tall, slender, and impeccably dressed despite his drab hat. He held a pistol, but he held it with the casual ease of a man to whom it was a familiar tool. He did not look for them. Instead, he spoke to the empty corridor, his voice calm, cultured, and laced with a faint, mocking amusement.

"It is an admirable effort, Lanyard," the man said. "The bourgeois disguise, the public postbox, the department store. All classic manoeuvres. But you teach an old dog new tricks, and then he uses them against you."

Lanyard remained motionless, silent. It was a name he had not expected to hear.

The man took a few steps into the corridor, his eyes scanning the shadows. "She is with you, of course. That was the one variable I could not predict. The woman. There is always a woman, is there not? The Achilles' heel of even the most solitary wolf." He paused. "Come now, Michael. Let us not be theatrical. This is beneath us. You have a choice. You can come out, and we can discuss this like civilized men. Or... I can start shooting into the crates. I am a patient man, but my employers are not."

Lucie let out a tiny, involuntary gasp. The man's head snapped towards the sound. A slow smile spread across his face. "Ah," he said. "There you are."

Lanyard knew the game was up. He could fight, perhaps even win, but the risk to Lucie was too great. He stepped out from the shadows, the packing hook held loosely at his side. He positioned himself between the man and where Lucie was hidden.

"Bourke," Lanyard said, his voice a low growl. "I should have known."

The man in the grey hat inclined his head in a mock bow. The mocking smile never left his lips. "You always were my most promising student, Michael," said Bourke. "It is a pity you chose to graduate without my permission. Now... be a good lad and introduce me to your new... colleague."
