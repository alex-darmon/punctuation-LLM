And in that instant, Lanyard knew a terror and a triumph greater than any he had ever felt before the most formidable of steel vaults. The terror of what he stood to lose; the triumph of what he had, for this one moment, won. He paid the bill with a flourish that belied the thinness of his wallet, and they stepped out again into the Parisian night, its chill a bracing shock after the warmth of the restaurant. The mist had thickened into a fine, persistent drizzle that beaded on her hair and the shoulders of his coat. He hailed a passing fiacre, an ancient, rickety conveyance drawn by a horse that seemed as weary as the night itself; it was a risk, but a lesser one than the Métro, and his ankle throbbed with a dull and insistent pain. They clopped and rattled through the sleeping heart of the city, a phantom carriage adrift in a sea of grey fog. The lamps cast hazy, aureate circles on the wet pavement, and the familiar landmarks loomed and receded like the ghosts of a life he was determined to leave behind. He felt her shiver, and tightened his arm around her. "Cold?" he murmured. "No," she answered, her voice low. "Just... thinking. My father... he will not stop. You do not know him. He is not like your Popinot, a creature of impulse and brute force. He is a man of ice and iron. He plans. He waits. And when he strikes..." She did not need to finish. He knew. He had seen the handiwork of such men before. "Then we must be quicker than his plans, and harder than his iron," Lanyard said, his tone more confident than he felt. He will not think to look for us under his very nose, in the oldest part of Paris." The fiacre slowed as it reached the bridge. He pressed a few of the last of his francs into the driver's hand. The river flowed on, indifferent to the small, desperate dramas played out upon its banks; it had seen everything, and remembered nothing. "You told me," she began, her gaze fixed on the black current, "that you were responsible for my trouble. That is not true. I came to you. I deceived you. This... all of this... is my fault." They say de Montalais has turned this city upside down looking for his daughter. And for the man who took her." He looked from Lanyard to Eve and back again, a slow dawning of comprehension on his face. "Mon Dieu. You fly high, Michael. Too high, perhaps." "Perhaps," Lanyard conceded. He showed them into a small chamber with a simple iron bedstead, a washstand, and a single window overlooking a dark inner courtyard. "It is not the Ritz," Fleury said, placing the lamp on a small table. "But no one will find you here. The walls are thick, and the only way in is through my shop. I will bring you food and wine." He paused at the door. "De Montalais has offered a fortune for your return, Mademoiselle. And a grave for the man who accompanies you." He looked at Lanyard. "Be careful, Michael. You were always a superb cracksman, the best I ever knew. But love... love is a lock for which there is no master key. It opens easily enough, but it is impossible to pick your way out again." She wrung out the towel and began to wrap it carefully around his ankle. "My mother," she said, her voice distant, "was an invalid for many years before she died. I was her nurse." Lanyard had spent his life opening things that were meant to stay shut, breaching the most intricate defences ever devised by man. It was a door he had opened himself, as Bourke had warned, and whatever followed in would not be evil or degrading. He reached out and touched her hair. She looked up, her grave eyes meeting his. The last of his reservations, the last vestige of the Lone Wolf's cynical solitude, dissolved and fell away. The emptiness he had feared was gone, filled to overflowing by her presence. He was a hunted man in a hostile city, with a powerful enemy on his trail and no resources but his wits and the pistol in his coat. By any sane measure, he was ruined. And yet, he had never felt more wealthy. "Eve," he said, his voice husky. She did not answer, but her fingers tightened on the damp cloth around his ankle, a silent acknowledgment. They were in a cage, a temporary sanctuary in a city that had become a hunting ground. But for the first time in his life, Michael Lanyard was not alone in the cage. And that, he knew with a sudden, blazing certainty, made all the difference.

He was already shrugging into his raincoat, his movements swift and economical despite the throbbing in his ankle. He checked the pistol, slipped it into his pocket, and picked up his flash-lamp. "The way we came in is blocked. We'll have to try the roof."

"The roof?" she whispered, her eyes wide.

"It is our only chance. These old houses in the Quarter are huddled together like frightened sheep. Their roofs are a veritable highway for one who knows the terrain. And I know it." He offered her a smile that was a mere ghost of his earlier confidence, a flash of bravado in the face of disaster. "Come. Our grand tour of Paris by night is not yet concluded."

He unlatched the door, not to the corridor, but to a narrow closet at the back of the room. Inside, a rickety wooden ladder, meant for maintenance, led up to a square trapdoor in the ceiling. Below, the city was a low growl, punctuated by the occasional sharp cry of a whistle.

"This way," he whispered, taking her hand. His grip was firm, reassuring. "Stay low and step only where I step."

He was leading her through a peril he had created; her life was in his hands because his past had caught up with them.

At one point, lying flat on the peak of a roof, they peered down into a side street. A dark automobile had pulled up to the curb, and from it emerged a man whose portly silhouette was unmistakable even in the gloom. Popinot himself. He stood conversing with two of his men, his gestures angry and impatient. They were casting their net wide.

"He is not a man to give up easily," Eve murmured, her breath warm against Lanyard's ear.

"Nor am I," he returned grimly. "He has made this personal. A fatal error in our line of work."

They pushed on, putting more and more distance between themselves and the compromised rooming house. He led them unerringly toward the north, toward the butte of Montmartre, which rose like a distant, holy mountain against the bruised sky. For a moment they leaned against a cold brick wall, gasping for breath, the driving mist clinging to their faces and clothes. Lanyard's leg was a torment of fire.

It was all on foot, a punishing trek up the steepening inclines of Montmartre. Lanyard leaned on her more and more, the Lone Wolf humbled, dependent. She took his weight without complaint, her slim frame possessed of a surprising strength. Her proximity, her scent, the steady warmth of her arm supporting him, was a strange and potent mixture of solace and agony.

At last, they saw it. Rising from the summit of the hill, the Basilica of the Sacred Heart of Paris loomed out of the mist, its white domes like immense, ghostly pearls. It seemed less a building than a celestial vision, a promise of peace in a war-torn world. They climbed the last of the great flights of steps, two weary pilgrims seeking sanctuary, and passed through the massive bronze doors into the cavernous nave.

The contrast with the world outside was absolute. Here, all was stillness and shadow, the air thick with the scent of cold stone and old incense. The sheer scale of the place was humbling; the high, vaulted ceilings soared into a darkness that seemed infinite. A handful of other souls were scattered throughout the pews: a black-clad widow, a hunched old man, a young woman weeping softly into her hands. He led her to a dark, secluded pew near the back, sinking onto the hard wood with a groan of relief that was almost a sob. The pain in his ankle had subsided to a dull, monstrous ache. Eve sat beside him, her shoulders slumped with a fatigue that she had refused to show until now. For several long minutes they sat in silence, simply breathing, the profound quiet of the church a balm on their frayed nerves.

Lanyard looked at her. In the flickering candlelight, her face was worn with care, but it held a beauty that was deeper, more resonant than mere prettiness. It was the beauty of character, of endurance. He thought of her on the roof, her courage and her quiet faith in him. He thought of her kneeling to tend his injury. He thought of her whispered confession about her mother. The questions he had, the doubts that had been sowed in the restaurant, seemed petty and unworthy in this sacred space.

"I asked you a question back there," she said at last, her voice barely disturbing the silence. "Before the whistles."

"You said it would take something damnable to shake your faith in me," she continued, her gaze fixed on the distant, glittering altar. "You said appearances would have to be black. That you would not believe I had deceived you."

A cold premonition stirred within him. "I meant it," he said slowly.

"You think you met me by chance at the Pré Catalan," she said, her voice flat, devoid of emotion, a recitation of a bitter lesson. "You think you rescued me from de Montalais, and that I have been your blameless companion in this flight. It is not true. None of it."

"He threatened you," Lanyard stated, a cold fury beginning to smoulder beneath his shock.

"He did more than threaten," she corrected softly. "He owned me. My job, my apartment... everything was by his grace, and could be taken away. Lanyard's mind reeled. The Montalans necklace... a string of flawless emeralds he had lifted from a corrupt financier's safe two weeks before his 'retirement,' a final act of the old life he now despised.

"Popinot wanted it," Eve went on. "He had a buyer. He knew you were in Paris. But he also knew you were unapproachable, a ghost. So he devised a trap. And I... I was the bait."

The words struck him with the physical force of a blow. The bait. The enchanting woman of mystery, the damsel in distress... it had all been a charade. A carefully constructed performance.

"The meeting at the Pré Catalan was no accident," she confessed, the words spilling out now in a torrent of misery. "Popinot's men followed you there. I was instructed to attract your attention, to seem vulnerable, to play upon your... chivalry. The scene with de Montalais... it was staged. He was one of Popinot's men, playing a part."

Lanyard felt a wave of nausea. His grand rescue, the moment he had felt himself a knight errant, had been a squalid piece of theatre. "The house in the impasse Stanislas," she whispered, "was the trap. I was to lead you there. I was to keep you there until Popinot arrived to... persuade you to give him the necklace. My part was to gain your trust, to make you feel safe." Her voice broke. "And God help me, I did it."

He stared at her, seeing not the woman he had fallen in love with, but a stranger, an actress, a tool in his enemy's hand. The entire foundation of his new life, his resolution to reform, which he had built upon his love for her, was a lie. He felt the old cynicism, the Lone Wolf's cold, bitter solitude, rush back to fill the void. He had been a fool, the worst kind of fool: a sentimental one.

"Why are you telling me this now?" he asked, his voice harsh, unrecognizable to his own ears.

"Because," she choked out, tears finally tracing paths down her pale cheeks, "when you turned out the lights... when you spoke of a rendezvous... when you took my hand in that tunnel... She shook her head, unable to continue. "I was supposed to signal them tonight, from the window of the rooming house. That's how they found us so quickly. I... I couldn't. I couldn't betray you again."

He looked away from her, his gaze sweeping over the silent, holy place. He felt Fleury's words echoing in his mind. *Love is a lock for which there is no master key.* The old detective had been right after all. He, Michael Lanyard, who could open any lock made by man, had been undone by a simple, cruel deception. The emptiness was back, a vertiginous chasm beneath his feet.

He thought of her question: *And you'd backslide?* He had not known the answer then. He felt the pull of it now, the allure of the easy way he had described to her. The way of the Lone Wolf. To trust no one, to care for nothing, to take what he wanted and disappear into the shadows. It would be so easy to leave her here, to melt back into the night, to save himself. It was the smart play. It was the only sane play.

He looked back at her. She was not looking at him, but staring at her hands, her shoulders bowed in shame. She was not the cool, calculating agent of his enemy. She was a broken, weeping girl in a church, confessing her sins. He saw again her quiet competence as she tended his wound. He saw her face, etched with fear and resolve, on the rooftop. He saw the genuine terror in her eyes when he had tried to confess his love in the restaurant, a terror not of him, but of the lie she was living.

He had not been entirely wrong. The chivalry may have been misdirected, but the woman he had glimpsed beneath the performance... the woman of quiet strength, of sorrow, of duty... she was real. She had been forced into a monstrous role, and she had, at the final, crucial moment, refused to play it. She had chosen him, and in doing so, had likely signed her own death warrant.

The rage and bitterness receded, replaced by a vast, aching pity, and something more. Something that felt, to his own astonishment, like a deeper, more painful, more profound love than he had felt before. He had fallen for a fantasy. But he was here, now, with the real, flawed, terrified, and courageous woman.

"So," he said, his voice quiet again, but with a new timbre of iron in it. "Popinot wants the Montalans necklace."

She looked up, startled by his tone. Her eyes were red-rimmed, her face slick with tears. "Yes."

"Yes. He is obsessed with it."

Lanyard leaned back in the pew, the pain in his ankle a distant, unimportant fact. The mind of the master strategist, the faculty that had lain dormant under the haze of infatuation, clicked back into place, sharp and cold and clear. But it was different now. It was not just for himself.

"Then we will give it to him," he said.

Eve stared at him, bewildered. "Give it to him? But... you don't..."

"Oh, but I do," he said, a faint, dangerous smile touching his lips. "Or rather, I know where it is. It is in a very safe place. A place that not even Popinot, with all his hounds, could get to." He leaned toward her, his voice dropping to a conspirator's whisper. "You were his bait. Now, you will be my accomplice. You know his habits, his safe houses, his organization. He reached out, not to imprison her hand as he had in the restaurant, but to gently cup her chin, tilting her face toward his. "You asked if I would backslide. If my faith would be shaken. It was. "You did," he agreed soberly. "And we are past it. The game has changed, Eve. And the board is reset. From now on, there will be no more lies between us. Agreed?"

In the ancient, shadowy nave of Saint-Étienne-du-Mont, Eve Shannon knelt in a pew near the back. She was not praying. Her eyes were fixed upon the flickering constellation of votive candles at the shrine of Saint Genevieve, patroness of Paris, but her thoughts were far away, in the dark and secret places beneath the city. Every creak of the church's old timbers, every cough from one of the few other late-night worshippers, sent a fresh tremor of fear through her.

She had been here for hours, a small, still figure lost in the vastness of the Gothic gloom. The minutes crawled by with an agonizing slowness. She felt a presence, a sense of being watched that was more than just the overwrought state of her nerves. She risked a glance over her shoulder. A man stood half-hidden by a massive stone pillar, his face obscured by shadow. He was not praying; he was simply standing there, watching the entrance. One of Popinot's men? Or one of the Crow's? She could not tell. She lowered her head again, her heart a frantic drum against her ribs.

A memory surfaced, unbidden; a fragment from that life she never spoke of. A party in a brightly lit salon in Geneva, the murmur of polite conversation, the clink of crystal. And a man's face, smiling, his eyes cold and watchful. The man by the pillar... was it him? No, it could not be. It was a trick of the light, of her fear. And yet, the feeling of recognition lingered, a chill that had nothing to do with the damp air of the church. She clutched the worn wood of the pew before her, her knuckles white. Who was she more afraid for? For Lanyard, in the clutches of Paris's criminal underworld, or for herself, with the ghosts of her own past rising up to meet her?

The sacristan began to move through the church, his keys jangling, preparing to lock the doors for the night. Panic seized her. It was time. He was not coming. The plan, their desperate, fragile plan, had failed at the first hurdle. She rose, her legs unsteady, and took a step toward the side door.

"Eve."

The voice was a low whisper, directly behind her. She gasped and spun around. It was Lanyard. He was pale, his clothes were smudged with dirt, and there was a new, hard light in his eyes, but he was alive. He was whole. A wave of relief so profound it was painful washed over her, and she nearly collapsed against him. He caught her, his arm a strong, steadying band around her waist.

"It is all right," he murmured, his lips close to her ear. "I am here. Let us go."

He guided her out into the night, which was now giving way to the first, grey hint of dawn. He did not speak again until they were several blocks away, walking quickly along the deserted quai beside the Seine.

"You saw him? The Crow?" she asked, her voice hushed.

"I saw him," he confirmed. "He has offered us a way out, Eve. A path to a new life."

She looked up at him, her heart lifting with a hope she had not dared to feel. "Truly? How?"

Lanyard's face was grim in the thin, morning light. He looked away from her, out at the dark, flowing water. "He wants me to do one last job for him," he said, his voice flat. "A theft. He wants me to be the Lone Wolf again, for one more night." He stopped and turned to face her, taking both of her hands in his. "It is the only way. To win our freedom, I must step back into the life I promised you I would leave behind. I had to agree. But I would not do it without telling you. Without you understanding."

She searched his eyes, seeing there the conflict, the self-loathing, the desperate hope. The question he had thrown at her in the restaurant echoed in her mind: "If you found I had deceived you--?" This was not a deception. It was a terrible, necessary honesty. She thought of the man in the church, of her own secrets, of the past that she, too, had run from. Who was she to judge the path he had to walk?

"I understand," she said, her voice quiet but firm. Her fingers tightened on his. "We will do what we must. Together."
