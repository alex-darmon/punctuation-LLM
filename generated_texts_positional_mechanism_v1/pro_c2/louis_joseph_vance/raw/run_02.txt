The temptation, insidious and sweet, whispered to him from the pocket of his raincoat. The diamonds. A king's ransom in glittering, flawless carbon, a key that could unlock any door save the one she had closed in his face. It was the "smooth and easy way" she had spoken of, the path of least resistance back to a life he understood; a life of luxury and larceny, of cleverness and contempt for the law. The Lone Wolf had never wanted for anything that money could buy or nimble fingers could procure. Why should Marcel, the drudge, the fool who had traded his kingdom for a woman's smile, now condemn himself to a life of want and struggle for a memory?

"And you'd backslide--?"

Her question, once a tender challenge, was now an indictment. He could almost hear her voice, tinged with that gentle, wistful sadness that had so captivated him. To backslide would be to prove her right; to prove that he was, indeed, the man she believed him to be: a creature of habit, incapable of change, a rogue whose reformation was a fleeting whim. It would be to admit that her desertion, cruel as it was, had been justified.

And that, Lanyard discovered with a kind of detached surprise, was a thing he could not, would not, do.

His pride, a formidable beast that had slumbered beneath the softer emotions of the past few days, awoke with a savage roar. It was one thing to be jilted; it was another entirely to be judged and found wanting by the very person who had inspired the verdict. No! He would not be the man she thought him to be. He would not fail. He would not backslide. If he had built his new life upon the foundation of her, and that foundation had proved to be sand, then he would build again upon the bedrock of his own implacable will. He would become an honest man out of sheer, bloody-minded spite if for no other reason.

With a sudden, convulsive movement, he turned from the window. The paralysis of despair was broken; in its place, the cold, clear logic of the strategist, the mind of the Lone Wolf, took command. Grief was a luxury he could not afford; action was the only anesthetic for the pain that threatened to consume him.

He smoothed the crumpled note on the nightstand, his eyes scanning the words not as a spurned lover but as a cryptographer searching for a hidden meaning. "I have done you a great wrong... You were mistaken in me." The phrases were a shield, meant to repel. But what were they protecting? Her, or him? "Do not, I beg of you, try to find me. It will bring you only ruin." A warning, or a plea? The more he read, the less it seemed like the confession of a faithless woman and the more it resembled the desperate gambit of a cornered one. Popinot... the cordon around the block... her fear. What if her flight was not an act of betrayal, but one of abnegation? What if the great wrong she had done him was not in leaving, but in involving him at all in some danger he did not comprehend?

The thought was a spark in the darkness. It did not warm him, not yet; but it provided a single point of light upon which to fix his gaze. He would not accept her verdict. He would conduct his own investigation, render his own judgment. He would find the truth, for her sake and for his own.

First, however, came the practicalities of survival. He was a fugitive in a hostile city, with enemies on both sides of the law. He had no money, no allies, and a single change of clothes. He retrieved his raincoat and felt the reassuring weight of the small, chamois bag. One of the smaller stones would have to be sacrificed. The prospect of re-engaging with the Parisian underworld, even for a simple transaction, left a sour taste in his mouth; it was a step, however small, back toward a world he had sworn to abandon. But necessity was a harsh mistress. He needed funds to eat, to live, to move freely. The irony was not lost on him: to finance his new life as an honest man, he must first commit one last dishonest act.

He washed his face in the cracked basin, the cold water a welcome shock. Staring into the mottled mirror, he saw a stranger's eyes looking back at him: hard, resolute, and shadowed by a pain too deep for tears. The romantic youth of the previous night was gone; in his place stood the man he had been, yet infused with a purpose he had never known.

There was one more stop he must make before he descended into the labyrinth of the city's underbelly. A promise he had to keep, even if its purpose was now rendered moot.

"The rendezvous is broken," she had written. "All rendezvous are broken."

Perhaps. But the Sacré-Cœur was more than a meeting place; it was a symbol of the vow he had made, the future he had glimpsed. He pocketed the diamonds, locked the door of the small, sad room behind him, and left the key on the concierge's unattended desk. Out in the street, the morning air was sharp and clean. Lanyard turned his face toward Montmartre, and began to walk.

The climb up the butte was a penance. With each step, the city unfolded below him, a grey and sprawling tapestry of slate roofs and chimney pots. He ignored the artists setting up their easels in the place du Tertre, the tourists already flocking to the vendors of cheap souvenirs. His gaze was fixed on the white, Byzantine domes of the basilica, stark and brilliant against the morning sky.

Inside, the air was cool and heavy with the scent of old stone and incense. Light filtered through the high windows, illuminating the vast, golden mosaic of Christ in Majesty that dominated the apse. Lanyard, who had entered more churches as a fugitive than as a believer, felt the familiar sense of being both an intruder and an observer. He found an empty pew in a side chapel and sank onto the hard wood, the exhaustion of the night and the morning's emotional cataclysm settling upon him like a physical weight.

He did not pray. He had no one to pray to, and no words for the appeal. Instead, he sat in the echoing silence and allowed his mind to work. He was Lanyard, the master thief, the Lone Wolf. He had outwitted the police of a dozen nations. He had planned and executed scores of impossible feats. And now he was faced with the most impossible feat of all: finding a woman who did not want to be found, who had vanished into the anonymity of a great city, leaving behind nothing but a name that was likely false and a heartache that was all too real.

Where to begin? He mentally retraced their steps, every moment from their first meeting. The hurried escape, the tense journey across Paris, the brief, idyllic interlude in the restaurant. He replayed their conversation, searching for a slip, a clue, an unguarded word. "A young woman of good family obliged to become self-supporting." That was his own romantic invention. What if the truth was the opposite? What if she was not from a good family at all? What if her past was something she had to be... self-supporting... to escape?

The thought sparked another. Her hands. He had held them. They were calm, capable hands. But in the restaurant, as she had traced patterns on the tablecloth, he had noticed... what? Not calluses. And her speech... her accent was perfect, her French as fluent as his own, but there was an underlying cadence, an occasional turn of phrase that he now, in retrospect, identified as subtly, unmistakably English. "Miss Shannon." An Irish name. A deliberate misdirection?

When they had paid the bill at the restaurant, a small, silver locket she wore had slipped from beneath the collar of her dress. A coronet, and beneath it, a heraldic beast... a lion, rampant, holding a fleur-de-lis.

It was not much. It was, in fact, next to nothing. A common enough charge. But it was something. A thread. And the Lone Wolf knew how to follow a thread.

His vigil was at an end. He rose, his joints stiff, and walked back out into the brilliant sunlight. His purpose, once a desperate act of will, was now honed to a fine point. He knew his next destination. He needed an expert, not in stolen gems, but in stolen lineage; a man who trafficked in the pedigrees of the great and the near-great, a specialist in the arcane science of heraldry. But first, money. The thought brought him back to the distasteful task at hand. He found the man he was looking for in the back room of a dingy bistro, a Belgian named van Deel who specialized in the discreet conversion of ill-gotten gains.

The negotiation was brief and brutal. Van Deel, a portly man with eyes like two chips of dirty ice, sensed Lanyard's desperation and tried to press his advantage. It was a mistake.

"You have been out of the game for a time, my friend," the Belgian said, pushing a jeweler's loupe back on his forehead. "The market is... soft. And this stone, it is a difficult size. I can offer you perhaps ten thousand francs."

Lanyard did not argue. He did not raise his voice. He simply leaned forward, his eyes locking with van Deel's, and smiled a smile that held no warmth at all. "Van Deel," he said softly, "the last man who tried to cheat me by sixty per cent is still wondering who paid his passage to Devil's Island. The market rate for a stone of this quality is forty thousand francs. You will give me thirty-five, for old times' sake. You will do it now. The Belgian's composure evaporated. He saw in Lanyard's eyes not the desperate fugitive he had imagined, but the implacable predator of legend. He began to sweat. He licked his lips. The Lone Wolf was back, and van Deel wanted no part of his wrath.

"Thirty-five," he stammered, fumbling for a key to his safe. "Of course, of course, thirty-five it is. A misunderstanding, my friend, purely a misunderstanding.... As for the girl... Popinot... Lanyard's blood ran cold. The pieces of the puzzle shifted, and a new, terrible picture began to form. She had not betrayed him. She had used him. Used him as a shield, as a means of escape. And the trouble she had brought upon him was not accidental; it was the entire point of the exercise.

He took the thick bundle of banknotes from van Deel's trembling hand without counting them. The money felt dirty, tainted. He had stepped back into the muck of his old life, and the first thing he found there was a truth more bitter than any lie. The anger that had sustained him, the pride that had driven him, now curdled into a cold, hard knot of fury in the pit of his stomach.

"Her name," he said, his voice dangerously quiet.

"I do not know it," van Deel squeaked, shrinking back in his chair. "I swear, Lanyard! I know nothing more!"

Lanyard held his gaze for a long moment, then turned and walked out of the bistro, leaving the Belgian to his terror. He strode out into the garish daylight of the boulevard de Clichy, a man doubly betrayed. Betrayed by the woman he loved, and, in a way, betrayed by himself, by the hope he had dared to entertain. The smiling future was a mirage. The dark pages had not been sealed; they were the only story there was. He felt the emptiness beneath his feet once more, a dizzying, terrifying void. Only this time, he was no longer sure which side he was on, or what he was fighting for. The only certainty was the hunt itself. He would find her. Not to save her, not to redeem her, but to understand the full measure of his own folly. He would find her, and he would have his answers.

He sank back into his chair, the self-confident, supremely alive young man of ten minutes prior completely gone, replaced by a hollow shell. She had not merely deceived him; she had expertly dissected him, used his own vanities and desires against him, and then discarded him with the cool indifference of a scientist finishing an experiment. He had boasted of his resolution, of the man he would become for her, and she had shown him that the entire premise was a farce. There was no one to become good for. The very foundation of his new world had not only been sand, but a deliberate mirage. For a long while he sat there, adrift in a sea of silent, incandescent rage and a humiliation so profound it was a physical sickness. He had been played. Not just played, but mastered, by a woman who saw through his Lone Wolf persona to the sentimental fool beneath. But as the minutes bled into an hour, the rage began to cool, crystallizing into something else, something harder and much more dangerous. The emptiness was still there, but it was no longer a void of despair. It was a vacuum, cold and absolute, into which his old self came rushing back, not as a retreat, but as an act of will. She had told him the Lone Wolf was a liability. She was wrong. The sentimental fool, Marcel, was the liability. The Lone Wolf was the solution. He stood up, the movements stiff at first, then fluid. But do not get too close." The chase led them through the heart of Paris, to the opulent district surrounding the place Vendôme. The Daimler pulled up before the Ritz. Sir Reginald Abercrombie, clearly a man of habit and little imagination, was going for a drink. Lanyard paid his driver and melted into the evening shadows, his mind already working on the next move. To approach Abercrombie here would be impossible. But Lanyard had no intention of speaking to him. He intended to listen. For Abercrombie would not be drinking alone. He would be meeting someone. The one person a man in his compromised position would have to report to: his superior. Lanyard entered the hotel through a service entrance he knew from a long-past, and far more lucrative, enterprise. The pantomime of their gestures, the grim set of their faces, told him everything. He was a witness to the aftermath of a disaster, the dressing-down of a man who had allowed vital state secrets to walk out the door on the arm of a pretty woman. And as he watched, he felt a strange, unwelcome sensation: a grudging respect for the woman known as Lucy Shannon. She had not merely fooled a sentimental thief; she had outmaneuvered the intelligence services of an empire. He had what he needed. He had confirmation. He had the shape and scope of the game. He slipped away as silently as he had come, his mind now fixed on a new, audacious plan. He could not fight her, and he could not fight them. But he could insert himself back into the equation. He knew what she had done, and he knew who she had done it to. That knowledge was a weapon. And the Lone Wolf, above all else, knew how to use the weapons that came to hand. The emptiness was still there, but it was an operative void now, a space in which to think, to plot, to become the fulcrum upon which this entire affair would turn. He would have his accounting, not by chasing her, but by making himself so central to the resolution of the crisis she had created that she would have no choice but to come to him. The hunt was over; the trap was about to be set.

He walked for hours, letting the damp chill of the Parisian night seep into him, a welcome counterpoint to the cold fire in his brain. His footsteps, echoing on the wet pavement of deserted boulevards, became a rhythm for his thoughts. She had called him a tool; very well, a tool could be turned against its wielder. She thought him a liability; he would become a necessity. She had closed the door on their shared story; he would write a new chapter in which he was the protagonist. His mind, which an hour ago had been a maelstrom of wounded pride and a hollow, aching sense of loss, was now a cold furnace of calculation; a place where emotion was burned away to leave only the hard, clean ash of purpose. He found what he sought in a dingy little hotel near the Gare du Nord, a place that asked no questions and offered no comforts. The room smelled of stale tobacco and regret, a fitting atmosphere for the work at hand. He did not sleep. Instead, he sat at a rickety table, the single dim bulb overhead casting long shadows, and began to assemble his strategy. The English had lost something vital. They were wounded, angry, and, most importantly, they would be desperate. They would be hunting her, of course, but they were a bureaucracy, an apparatus of state; they moved with power but without finesse. She, on the other hand, was a wraith, a creature of the shadows he knew so well. They would blunder; she would glide. He, however, understood both sides of the equation. He understood the mindset of the hunter and the tradecraft of the hunted. He was the only man in Paris who could think like her because, in his own way, he was her. This was the key. He would not offer them information; he would offer them a mirror, a reflection of their adversary’s own cunning. He would offer them the Lone Wolf. Before dawn, he was out again, his few remaining francs spent on a sheaf of high quality linen paper, a matching envelope, and a bottle of good black ink. He read it over one final time, a grim smile touching his lips. Shortly before noon, a junior attaché, a young man with an air of harried importance, emerged and walked briskly toward the Métro. Lanyard fell into step behind him. He did not notice the crisp, heavy envelope that had been slipped deftly into the outer pocket of his briefcase. Lanyard watched him board his train, a ghost of a smile on his face. The letter would be found, not upon the Ambassador’s desk, but amidst the man’s own papers when he returned. It would cause confusion, then alarm, and then it would find its way to the proper hands, its mysterious appearance only adding to its weight. The rest of the day he passed in a state of suspended animation, conserving his energy. He ate a sparse meal of bread and cheese, and walked the city, his senses alive, absorbing the life of the streets, feeling the hunter’s thrill he had not known for years. At a quarter to three the following day, Lanyard was in position. He was not on the Pont des Arts, but in the upper window of a dusty, vacant apartment in the Louvre itself, a window he had gained access to through means that would have horrified the museum’s curators. The window gave him a perfect, unobstructed view of the entire length of the bridge. He had a pair of powerful opera glasses, a relic from a past exploit. At precisely three o’clock, a man appeared, walking from the Left Bank. He was of middle height, dressed in a sober grey suit, and wore a white carnation in his lapel. Under his arm, he carried a folded copy of Le Temps. Lanyard studied him. The man’s posture was too rigid, his gaze too active as he scanned the crowds of artists and lovers on the bridge. He was a professional. Lanyard swept his glasses along the quays on either side of the river. He spotted them easily enough: two men trying to look like idle fishermen, another reading a book on a bench, a fourth watching from the entrance to the Square du Vert-Galant. It was a clumsy, predictable trap. They had come not to negotiate, but to capture. A flicker of contempt passed through him. This was the best the British Empire could do? He let their man wait. For fifteen minutes, the agent stood in the centre of the bridge, a monument to growing impatience. His casual air began to fray; he checked his watch twice. The watchers grew restless. Lanyard smiled. It was time for the second lesson. He had anticipated this. The previous evening, he had located a street urchin, a sharp-eyed boy of about ten named Jean-Pierre, and for the price of a few francs had engaged his services. The boy was now waiting, as instructed, near the bridge’s entrance on the Right Bank. Lanyard gave a low whistle, a prearranged signal. A moment later, he saw Jean-Pierre dart out onto the bridge, weaving through the pedestrians like a ferret. The boy ran straight up to the man in the grey suit. Lanyard watched, fascinated, through his glasses. The agent stared after the boy in frustrated surprise, then unfolded the note. His face, even from that distance, was a study in chagrin. Lanyard could well imagine the contents of the note he had so carefully penned:

Your tradecraft is appalling. The fisherman on the Quai du Louvre has not re-baited his hook in twenty minutes, and your man on the bench is holding his book upside down. He watched the agent, defeated, walk stiffly off the bridge. The watchers emerged from their positions and conferred with him briefly before melting back into the city. Lanyard remained in his perch for another half hour, ensuring he was not observed, before slipping out of the Louvre as silently as he had entered. He had seized the initiative. He had demonstrated his superiority, humiliated them on their own turf, and dictated the terms of the engagement. He was no longer the jilted lover, the cast-off tool. He was the Lone Wolf, and he had placed himself squarely in the centre of the web. The next move belonged to the British, but he knew, with an absolute and chilling certainty, what it would be. They had no choice. She had forced their hand; now, he had forced it further. And somewhere in this city, the woman who called herself Lucy Shannon would soon learn that the past she thought she had discarded had come back to haunt her, not as a ghost, but as a hunter. The thought brought him no joy, only a grim, consuming satisfaction. The game was afoot.

The days that followed were a blur of calculated patience. It was a bleak, anonymous cell, furnished with a narrow iron bed, a rickety table, and a single chair; its one window looked out upon a grimy air-shaft. Yet for Lanyard, it was a fortress. From here, he waged his silent war.

Each morning he would venture out, his appearance altered by a pair of spectacles and a workman's cap, and purchase a copy of Le Figaro from a kiosk several blocks away. He did not hurry; haste was the hallmark of the amateur, and he was a master playing for the highest stakes. He would return to his room, lock the door, and spread the newspaper on the table. On the third day, it was there. Tucked between an advertisement for a lost locket and a notice concerning a private sale of antiques, were the words: 'Wolf. A park was too open; a café too confined. The next day, fifteen minutes before the appointed hour, Lanyard was not on the ground floor, but on the first-floor balcony, leaning against the ornate iron railing, seemingly engrossed in the chaotic ballet of commerce below. He wore a perfectly tailored but unassuming tweed jacket and carried a copy of Le Monde. It was Davies. He did not look up. He knew his man would be watching from an unexpected quarter.

It was a tempting offer; the clean break he had yearned for. A chance to truly begin again, far from the shadows of his past. But there was a piece missing from the puzzle, the most important piece of all.

For the first time, a flicker of something unreadable crossed Davies's face. He seemed to choose his next words with immense care. "Miss Shannon," he began, "is a complication. A regrettable one."

"Suppose... suppose you were to find out... you'd been mistaken in me?" It had not been a hypothetical query; it had been a warning. And he, blinded by his own burgeoning sentiment, had dismissed it as a woman's gentle doubt.

How could he have been so stupid? He, the Lone Wolf, who had survived by trusting no one, by reading the subtle tells in a man's eyes or the tremor in his voice, had looked at this woman and seen only what he wished to see. He had built a cathedral of hope upon a foundation of lies, and now the entire edifice had come crashing down upon him, leaving him buried in the rubble of his own folly.

A cold drizzle began to fall, slicking the pavement and casting a halo around the gas-lamps. The city was emptying, the sounds of its life muting to a distant hum. It was time. He could not stand here all night, nursing his wounds like some spurned lover. He was Lanyard. And there was a reckoning to be had.

He straightened up, his face set. The romantic fool who had feasted at Les Halles was gone; in his place was the professional, the man who had stalked the capitals of Europe, a ghost in the world of law and order. His senses, dulled by a month of unaccustomed peace, sharpened to a preternatural acuity. He no longer saw the city as a backdrop for romance; he saw it as a tactical map, a terrain of angles, shadows, and escape routes.

He set off for Montmartre, but not by the most direct course. He was a creature of the by-ways now, more than ever. He slipped through the warren of streets behind Saint-Germain-des-Prés, his footfalls making no sound on the wet cobblestones. He crossed the river not by the grand Pont Neuf, but by the narrow, pedestrian Pont des Arts, a wraith among the shadows. He used the reflection in a darkened shop window to check for a tail, his eyes scanning the street behind him with an old, familiar patience. There was no one. Davies, it seemed, was honouring their venomous truce, at least for now.

The journey was long. He walked, conserving what little money he had left. The physical exertion was a welcome distraction, a counterpoint to the turmoil in his mind. With every step, he dismantled the woman he thought he knew. He recalled her reluctance to speak of herself, her moments of distraction when his talk of the future seemed to set off some "occult train of thought." It had not been maidenly reserve; it had been the caution of a secret agent. He remembered her trim Burberry, her calm efficiency, the way she had handled the initial escape from Popinot's men. He had seen it as fortitude; he should have recognized it as training.

The climb up the butte Montmartre was punishing. The steep streets and endless flights of stairs seemed designed to test the resolve of a pilgrim, and in a way, he was one. He was on a pilgrimage to a truth he did not want to find, to face a saint who was in fact a sinner. Or was she? The question still gnawed at him. Davies had called her a betrayer. Betrayed whom? For what reason? Perhaps she had played the British as she had played him. Perhaps she was a free agent, a lone wolf herself, and he had simply been a convenient tool, a strong arm and a quick wit to be used and then discarded.

He reached the final steps below the great white basilica. The Sacré-Cœur loomed above him, a ghostly leviathan against the bruised purple of the night sky. Its pearly luminescence seemed to mock the darkness of his purpose. This was the place he had chosen for their rendezvous, a symbol of hope and sanctity. Now it felt like a courtroom, a place of judgment.

He did not go directly to the entrance. He circled the perimeter, melting into the shadows of the small park, his eyes scanning the broad terrace before the church. Tourists were scarce at this hour; a few hardy souls huddled against the balustrade, looking out over the sleeping city. And there, near the centre, a solitary figure, she stood.

She was looking out over Paris, her back to him. The posture was the same; the line of her shoulders, the simple elegance of her form. He had last seen her in the restaurant, her face animated, her eyes indulgent. Now, she seemed smaller, more isolated, a lonely silhouette against the vast, glittering expanse of the city. He felt a sudden, unwelcome pang of his old protective instinct, a ghost of the man he had been only a few hours before. He crushed it ruthlessly. That man was a fool, and fools ended up dead or in prison.

For a long minute he simply watched her, the hunter observing his quarry before the final move. He studied her for any sign of treachery, any indication that this was a trap. Were there watchers in the shadows? Was Davies's promise of non-interference a lie? He saw nothing, felt nothing but the damp chill of the night and the heavy beat of his own heart. She was alone.

He began to walk toward her, his steps deliberate on the wet flagstones. The sound, though soft, was distinct in the quiet of the night. She heard him. Her shoulders stiffened slightly, but she did not turn. He stopped a few feet behind her. The scent of her perfume, the same faint scent of rain and flowers, reached him, a fragile bridge to a past that was now a foreign country.

"You're late," she said, her voice quiet, without inflection. It was not the greeting he had expected.

"I was detained," he replied, his own voice a stranger's, devoid of warmth.

She turned then, slowly, and her face was a pale oval in the gloom. The great, grave eyes he had found so beautiful were shadowed, her mouth a tight line. There was no surprise in her expression, only a deep, profound weariness. It was as if she had been expecting this very tone, this very distance between them.

"I saw the notice in Le Figaro," she said. "You saw Davies." It was not a question but a statement of fact.

"I did."

A silence fell between them, thick with unspoken accusations. The lights of Paris below seemed to hold their breath. Lanyard felt the careful structure of his anger, the cold resolve he had built on his long walk, begin to fracture under the weight of her steady, sorrowful gaze. He had come here for a confrontation, an interrogation, to tear the truth from her with the savage force of his betrayal. But looking at her now, he felt a sudden, sickening wave of doubt. This was not the face of a cold, calculating operative. This was the face of someone who had lost as much as he had.

"He told you about me," she said at last, her voice barely a whisper. "He told you... everything."

"He told me enough," Lanyard countered, his voice harder now, fighting to regain his fury. "He told me about Miss Thorne. He told me you were a fugitive from his government. He told me you betrayed them." He took a step closer, the space between them crackling with tension. He had planned to deliver the line like an executioner; instead, it came out ragged, half-plea, half-accusation. "He told me my 'Lucy Shannon' never existed."

She did not flinch. She did not look away. Her eyes held his, and in their depths he saw not guilt, but a pain that mirrored his own. Her lips parted, as if to speak, then closed again. A single tear traced a glistening path down her cheek, a betrayal her stoic expression could not contain.

"He was right about that," she said, and her voice broke on the final word. "She never did."

The confession, when it came, was not the victory he had imagined. It did not bring the satisfaction of righteousness or the cold comfort of being proven right. It was a simple, devastating blow that shattered the last vestiges of his hope. The emptiness he had described to her in jest, the feeling of nothing beneath his feet, was suddenly, terribly real.

"Then who," he began, the words tasting like ash in his mouth, "who are you?"

She looked away from him then, back out at the city spread at their feet, a kingdom she had seemingly conquered and lost. "My name is not important," she said, her voice regaining a sliver of composure, a brittle strength. "What I am... that is the question. And Davies only told you his side of it. He told you I betrayed them. He did not tell you why. He did not tell you what they asked me to do."

"Then tell me," Lanyard demanded, his fists clenched at his sides. "Tell me the truth. For once, the unvarnished truth."

She gave a small, bitter laugh. "The truth? You ask for the truth in a world of spies and shadows? The truth is a luxury, Mr. Lanyard. Like love. And trust. Luxuries neither of us can afford." She turned back to him, and for the first time, a spark of fire appeared in her eyes, a flash of the woman who could deceive governments. "You made a deal with Davies. You blackmailed him for our escape. You think you are in control. You are not. You have simply entered a new cage, one with a slightly wider view. They will let us go, yes. They will give us passports and a boat. And they will watch us. They will wait for me to make contact with the people I am supposed to have betrayed. They will follow your 'smiling future' every step of the way, and when the time is right, they will close the trap, with you in it."

"I can take care of myself," he said, the words an old, hollow refrain.

"Can you?" she challenged. "Can you take care of us both? You do not know who you are fighting, Mr. Lanyard. You do not know the stakes. The diary you hold... it is a trifle. A source of 'catastrophic embarrassment' for one man. The information I carry... it is a source of war."

He stared at her, the scale of his ignorance expanding with every word she spoke. He had thought this was about a stolen past and a salvaged future; it was about the fates of nations. He was a master thief caught in the middle of a game played by kings and ministers, and he had mistaken it for a sordid back-alley brawl.

"So everything," he said slowly, deliberately, forcing himself to voice the deepest wound. "Everything between us. The restaurant. Your... interest. It was all a lie. A performance to keep your tool sharp and willing."

She looked as though he had struck her. Her face paled further, and her hand went to her throat. "No," she whispered. "No. That is the one thing... the one thing that wasn't a lie. It was a mistake. A terrible, unprofessional, unforgivable mistake. I let myself... I let myself believe in your 'smiling future' too. I let myself forget who I was, and what I had to do." She took a shaky breath. "That is why you are in more danger now than you ever were with Popinot. Not because of what I am, but because, for a few short hours... I was Lucy Shannon. And that woman was a liability we can no longer afford."

He stood frozen, caught between two irreconcilable realities: the woman who had used him, and the woman who had, impossibly, cared for him. The two were one and the same. The rage he had nurtured was gone, replaced by a vast, aching confusion.

"So what now?" he asked, his voice low.

She met his gaze, and her expression was as hard and resolute as his had been when he first arrived. The brief moment of vulnerability was gone. Miss Thorne was back in control. "Now," she said, her voice crisp and clear, "we use the escape you have so capably arranged. We take the passports and the boat. We disappear. But not together. That was your plan. It is no longer viable. The moment we are clear of French waters, we separate. You will go your way, and I will go mine. Your association with me ends there. That is the only way you will ever be truly free. That is the only way you might survive."

She stated it as a final, non-negotiable term, a clinical severing of their fates. It was, he knew, the logical, sensible, professional thing to do. It was the only way out. And yet, he found he could not accept it.

"No," he said, the word a simple, solid fact.

A flicker of surprise crossed her features. "No? What do you mean, no? It is not your decision to make."

"Isn't it?" He stepped closer still, until he was standing directly before her, forcing her to look up at him. The cold anger was gone, replaced by something else, something far more dangerous: a reckless, defiant purpose. "I'm in this game now, Miss Thorne, whether I like it or not. You say I don't know the stakes? Then it is time I learned them. You say I am a tool? Very well. But this tool has a mind of its own. We will take the boat, yes. And we will do it together. You will not get rid of me that easily."

"You are a fool!" she breathed, a note of desperation in her voice. "They will kill you!"

"Many have tried," he retorted, a ghost of his old, arrogant smile touching his lips, though it did not reach his eyes. "You said my faith in you would need something damnable to be shaken. Well, consider it shaken. But my faith in myself... that is another matter. You may be Miss Thorne, secret agent, but I am still the Lone Wolf. And I do not abandon a... partner... in the middle of a hunt. Not until I have my answers. You owe me that." He leaned in, his voice dropping to a low, intense whisper. "You owe me the truth. All of it. And I will have it. You are my responsibility now, Miss Thorne. For better... or for worse."
