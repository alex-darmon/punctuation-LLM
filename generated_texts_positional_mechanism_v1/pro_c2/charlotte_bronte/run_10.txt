She paced the room, the moonlight striping the floor before her. "They are all at war," she murmured. "Helstone with his parish, Moore with his workmen, Farren with his poverty. And I? What is my part in this?"

She was a landowner, a captain in her own right. The people on her land were, in a sense, her people. Farren was one of them now. Her duty, as she saw it, was not Helstone's cold duty of suppression. It was something else, something warmer, more vital. To protect, to nurture, to stand as a bulwark not only for them, but with them.

"He called me a romantic," she said to the silent, listening room. "Perhaps I am. But his reality is a prison. I would rather live in a dream of justice than in his well-ordered dungeon."

Her thoughts turned to Caroline. Poor Lina, trapped in that dungeon, her gentle spirit subject to that iron will. She had seen the fear in Caroline's eyes, the way she shrank from her uncle's anger. Caroline had no army, no fortune, no grand philosophy to sustain her. Her only weapons were a kind heart and a quiet endurance. And tonight, they had been shown to be utterly powerless against the rector's cold authority. The desire which had surfaced earlier in the evening, Caroline's deep, aching wish for a mother, seemed to Shirley now not a mere girlish fancy, but a profound spiritual need; a cry for a different kind of world, one governed not by law and battle, but by love and sanctuary.

"She needs her mother," Shirley whispered. "She needs Nature, my mother Eve, to take her to her heart. But Helstone has locked the garden gate."

She went to the window and looked out over her own lands, the rolling park and the dark woods, all sleeping under the moon. This was her kingdom. Here, at least, she could make her own laws.

"He shall have wine and meat," she declared, her voice firm again. "And I will see to the wife myself. And if Mr. Helstone disapproves, let him. He is rector of the parish, but I am master of Fieldhead."

A new resolve settled in her. The evening had not been just a series of disconnected events: the vision of the Titan-Mother, the passage of the soldiers, the encounter with Farren, the clash with the rector. It was all one story, one grand, unfolding struggle. And she was no longer a mere spectator. She had been challenged, and her spirit rose to meet the challenge. The soft excitement of the summer evening had hardened into a warrior's resolve. She would not let the spirit of men like William Farren be broken, not if she could help it. She would stand for the human heart against the iron machinery of the world. It was a romantic notion, perhaps, but it was one for which Shirley Keeldar felt she could happily go to war.

William Farren’s rugged face, which had softened in the presence of the young ladies, hardened again into its customary lines of stoic endurance. “A man has his pride, miss,” he said, not to Caroline but to the churchyard path. “It is sometimes all he has. When work is gone, and the hearth is cold, his own good name is the last bit o’ property he holds. And I’ll not mortgage that, not for a loaf o’ bread.”

“A harsh creed, Farren,” a new voice cut in, cold and sharp as chipped flint.

All turned. Mr. Helstone had emerged from the porch, his prayer-book in his hand. The last of the congregation were trickling out behind him, casting curious glances at the group by the graves. The rector’s gaze was fixed on William Farren, and it contained no particle of the complacency he reserved for his social equals; it was the look of a magistrate appraising a vagrant.

“A creed that fills the workhouse and the debtor’s prison,” he continued, his voice ringing with clerical authority. “The Lord helps those who help themselves, it is true; but pride that refuses a brother’s offered hand is no Christian virtue. It is the sin of Lucifer. You would do well to remember that, my man.”

Farren stood up, his son still held in one arm. His face was a thundercloud. He touched his hat, a gesture of formality utterly devoid of respect. “And some hands are offered, sir, in a way that scolds a man for his poverty. A crust given with a sermon tastes bitter. I’ll bid you good-evening, sir; and you, young ladies.” He gave a nod, this time a genuinely respectful one, to Shirley and Caroline, and turning his back squarely on the rector, walked away down the lane, his little daughters trotting to keep up with his long, angry strides.

“Insolent,” muttered Mr. Helstone. He turned his severe eye upon his niece and her companion. “And you, young ladies; what keeps you loitering here, conversing with malcontents? Shirley, your fancies are growing notorious. You will bring yourself into disrepute. Caroline, go home at once. It is past supper-time.”

Shirley’s eyes flashed, a spark of the scorn William had noted. “Mr. Farren is a tenant on my land, sir. I shall converse with him when and where I please.”

“Your land will not protect you from foolishness, nor from the consequences of encouraging sedition. These are not times for landowners to be seen fraternizing with the disaffected mob.”

“He is not a mob, Mr. Helstone; he is a man, with a wife and children. And he is hungry.”

“Then let him apply to the parish for relief, in a proper and humble spirit,” the rector retorted. “That is what the rates are for. Come, Caroline.” He made a peremptory gesture.

Caroline, who had stood silent and pale throughout this exchange, cast a swift, apologetic glance at Shirley, a look that begged forgiveness for her desertion. She had no choice; her uncle’s will was law, his house her only shelter. She murmured, “Good-night, Shirley,” and obediently followed him towards the rectory gate.

Shirley watched them go, the tall, unbending black-coated figure and the slight, drooping one. The gate clanged shut behind them, a sound of finality, of imprisonment. She remained a moment longer, alone in the deepening twilight, her heart a battleground of pity for Caroline and indignation against the rector. The soft summer evening was over; its poetry had been trampled under the heel of cold, hard duty. The tramp of unseen soldiers, the cry of a hungry child, the clang of the rectory gate: these were the true sounds of the world she inhabited.

The morning broke, not with the fiery passion of the previous evening's vision, but with a serene and settled light that promised a day of steady heat. Shirley was awake with the first thrush’s song. The warrior’s resolve had not evaporated with the night’s mists; it had, rather, solidified, taken practical shape. She had declared war; now she must provision her army, an army which for the present consisted of one proud, unemployed cloth-dresser.

She descended to the breakfast-parlour, where she found Mrs. Gill, her housekeeper, already overseeing the placement of china and silver. Mrs. Gill was a woman of respectable age and comfortable proportions, whose placid countenance was the very picture of domestic order.

“Good-morning, Mrs. Gill,” said Shirley, her tone brisk. “I have an errand for you. I wish a basket to be prepared and sent to William Farren’s cottage this morning.”

“To Farren’s, Miss Keeldar? Yes, ma’am. A little soup, perhaps, and some bread?” Mrs. Gill’s idea of charity was of the proper, discreet, and somewhat watery kind.

“No, not soup,” Shirley replied, taking her seat. “Something more substantial. A joint of that cold roast beef from yesterday; a whole loaf of your best white bread, not the second-best; a round of Stilton, and a bottle of the port Mr. Yorke praised last week. See to it that the basket is a good, strong one, and lined with a clean napkin.”

Mrs. Gill’s hands, which were arranging a vase of roses, stilled. She looked at her mistress with an expression of faint alarm. “A whole joint, miss? And the best port? For William Farren?” The question was not merely about the expense; it was about the propriety, the sheer unprecedented scale of such a gift to such a man.

“Is he not a man, Mrs. Gill? Does he not have a mouth to eat and a stomach to fill, the same as Mr. Yorke? He is an honest workman fallen on hard times, and I will not insult him with watery soup and stale crusts. It is a gift, not an alms.”

“Yes, miss,” the housekeeper said, her placidity somewhat ruffled. “But Mr. Helstone… he does not hold with encouraging the idle.”

“Mr. Helstone is not the master of Fieldhead,” Shirley said, her voice quiet but unyielding. “I am. And I do not consider William Farren idle; I consider him wronged. The fault lies with the times, not with the man. Pray have the basket made ready within the hour. Miss Helstone and I will take it ourselves.”

“You will go yourself, ma’am?” This was a still greater departure from custom.

“I will. Farren has his pride, a quality which, though inconvenient, I respect. A gift delivered by a servant is charity; a gift brought by a friend is a different matter. Now, I will have my coffee.”

Mrs. Gill, seeing the subject was closed, curtsied and withdrew, her mind in a state of bewilderment. Her young mistress was more peculiar than ever; giving away good meat and wine as if it were nothing, and planning to walk into the heart of the village on such an errand, like any common farmer’s daughter.

An hour later, Shirley, attired in a plain morning-dress and a broad-brimmed straw hat, met Caroline at the gate. Caroline had come with a hesitant step; she looked anxious, her eyes still holding the shadow of her uncle’s displeasure from the night before.

“Are you sure about this, Shirley?” she asked in a low voice, as they set off down the drive, a footman following at a respectful distance with the heavy basket. “My uncle was furious. He said you were stirring up trouble.”

“And what is it to him if I do?” Shirley retorted, though not unkindly. “His trouble is a lack of feeling; mine is an excess of it. Which is the greater sin? Do not look so frightened, Lina. He cannot lock you in the tower for walking with me. We are merely taking the air, and performing a small act of neighbourly kindness.”

“It feels like more than that,” Caroline whispered. “It feels like defiance.”

“Then let it be defiance,” Shirley declared, her step firm on the dusty lane. “I will not have my life dictated by the fears of old men. Look, Lina!” She swept her arm towards the valley below them, where the sun glinted on the slate roofs and the green fields. “This is my land, our land. Is it not beautiful? And the people who live on it, they are our people. Are we to stand by and watch them starve while we feast on ‘dulcet creams’? It is not just. It is not natural.”

They walked on in silence for a time, descending from the pleasant heights of Fieldhead towards the cluster of cottages where the mill-workers lived. The air grew thicker here, tinged with the smell of coal-smoke and damp wool. The thrum of a distant water-wheel, Hollow’s Mill, was a constant, low accompaniment to their steps. It was a sound of industry, of life, but today it sounded to Caroline like a mournful groan.

They dismissed the footman at the head of the row of cottages, and Shirley took the basket herself. Its weight was considerable, but she bore it as if it were a trophy. William Farren’s dwelling was at the very end, small but distinguished from its neighbours by the scrupulous cleanliness of its doorstep and the determined brightness of the marigolds planted in its tiny patch of front garden.

Shirley knocked. After a moment, the door was opened not by William, but by his wife. She was a thin, worn-looking woman, whose face must once have been pretty, but was now etched with care. On seeing the two ladies on her step, she flushed and hastily wiped her hands on her apron.

“Miss Keeldar, Miss Helstone,” she stammered, curtsying. “I beg your pardon, the house is all at sixes and sevens.”

“Good-morning, Mrs. Farren,” said Shirley, with her frankest smile. “We have not come to inspect your housekeeping, but to bring you something. My mother Nature was feeling generous this morning and commanded me to share her bounty.” She held out the basket.

Mrs. Farren stared at it, then at Shirley, her eyes wide with incomprehension. “For us, miss?”

At that moment, William himself appeared behind her, drawn by the voices. His face on seeing them was a mixture of surprise and stiff-backed pride. He did not smile.

“We want no charity here,” he said, his voice low and gruff.

“It is not charity, William,” Shirley replied, her gaze steady and direct. “It is a toll. I find I have been trespassing. I was walking on Stilbro’ Moor last night, speaking with God, and I realized it was your mother’s house as much as mine. This is simply the rent I owe for the use of her roof.”

Farren looked utterly baffled by this fantastical explanation. He stared at Shirley’s earnest, handsome face, then at the basket, then back again. He knew want; he knew the gnawing ache of an empty stomach and the terror of having nothing for his children. But his pride was a raw, tender thing, and the rector’s words from the previous night still stung like a fresh wound.

His wife, however, was a more practical being. Her eyes had fallen on a corner of the white napkin, where a dark crust of roast beef was visible. Her face changed. The instinct of a mother, desperate to feed her young, overrode all other considerations.

“Oh, William,” she breathed, her hand going to his arm. “The little ones… they had but porridge this morning.”

That quiet plea did what Shirley’s grand speech could not. William’s rigid posture sagged. He looked from his wife’s pleading face to the open, friendly countenances of the two young women before him. He saw no condescension in their eyes, no pitying scorn. He saw only kindness. His own eyes grew misty.

“I… I cannot take it, miss,” he mumbled, but his voice had lost its conviction.

“You will do no such thing as refuse it,” Shirley said, stepping past him into the small, clean room and placing the heavy basket firmly on the table. “Now, Mrs. Farren, you will give us a glass of water, for our walk has made us thirsty, and then we will leave you to your dinner. Do not say another word, William. A man must eat to have the strength to be proud.”

She turned the full force of her radiant smile upon him, and the last of his resistance crumbled. He sank onto a wooden stool, burying his face in his hands. His wife, with tears streaming down her own cheeks, went to the cupboard for glasses, her movements full of a dazed, trembling gratitude. Caroline, her own eyes wet with sympathy, knelt by the stool and laid a gentle hand on the shoulder of one of the little girls, who was staring at the basket as if it were a miracle.

The walk back to Fieldhead was quieter. The raw emotion of the scene had left Caroline shaken, but also filled with a strange, fierce joy. She had been a party to a good and noble act. She had stood, for a moment, on Shirley’s side against the cold world of her uncle.

“You see, Lina,” Shirley said, as they neared the estate, “it is not so very difficult. A little beef, a little wine. It is a simple matter to make someone happy.”

“It was not the beef, Shirley,” Caroline replied softly. “It was the way you gave it. You gave him back his dignity.”

“Perhaps. It is what he deserved.”

They were about to turn into the main drive when the sound of a horse at a brisk trot made them pause. A rider was approaching along the lane from the direction of the mill. As he drew nearer, they recognized the straight-backed, commanding figure of Mr. Robert Moore.

He reined in his dark mare as he reached them, touching the brim of his hat. “Miss Keeldar, Miss Helstone. You are out early.”

His eyes, sharp and intelligent, took in the scene: the two ladies, unattended, their faces flushed from their walk, returning from the direction of the village. Moore was no fool; he could guess the nature of their errand.

“We enjoy the morning air, Mr. Moore,” Shirley replied coolly, meeting his gaze without flinching.

“So I see,” he said. His expression was unreadable, a mixture of business-like gravity and something more personal, a kind of ironic curiosity directed at Shirley. “I trust you are not straying too far. The roads are not as safe as they once were. There is… unrest.”

“We have seen your guards,” Shirley said, a pointed reference to the soldiers. “Six of them. They rode past the churchyard last night. Is your mill a fortress, then, that it requires such a garrison?”

“A man must protect his property, Miss Keeldar. My frames are my livelihood. They are the livelihood of this whole valley, if the men had the sense to see it. When madmen threaten to destroy them, I must take measures.”

“And what of the men whose livelihood you have already taken away by bringing in those very frames?” Caroline ventured to ask, her voice barely a whisper, yet clear in the still air.

Moore’s gaze shifted to her. It softened slightly. “Miss Helstone, you have a tender heart. But business is not a matter of heart; it is a matter of necessity. England must compete. We must have the new machinery, or we all perish together. The men will adapt, or they will be left behind. It is the law of nature.”

“It is the law of the iron machine!” Shirley burst out, her patience exhausted. “You and Mr. Helstone sing the same tune, from different hymn-books. He preaches submission to God’s law, you preach submission to the law of trade. Both are cold, cruel laws that grind men down and call it necessity. I have just been to visit a man, a good man, whom your ‘necessity’ has left to starve.”

A flicker of something, perhaps annoyance, crossed Moore’s face. “You mean Farren. I heard you spoke with him last night. You are unwise, Miss Keeldar. Your charity is misplaced. It will be seen as weakness. It will encourage others to idleness and complaint.”

“And your hardness, sir? What will that be seen as? What will it encourage? It has encouraged desperate men to plot violence. You are making your own enemies, Mr. Moore, and then you hire soldiers to shoot them. It is a foolish and a bloody way to conduct one’s affairs.”

They faced each other in the lane, the fiery heiress and the inflexible mill-owner; two sovereigns of this small valley, their philosophies in stark opposition. Moore looked at her, at the passion that lit her face and made her beautiful, and a reluctant admiration warred with his practical disapproval.

“You are a romantic, Miss Keeldar,” he said, his voice losing some of its edge. “You see the world as you wish it to be. I see it as it is. Take care that your romantic notions do not lead you and those you influence into real danger.”

“And you, Mr. Moore,” she retorted, “take care that your reality does not become a desert, with no living thing left in it but your cold, iron frames. Good-day to you.”

Without another word, she turned and swept through the gates of Fieldhead, her head held high. Caroline, with a small, troubled nod to Moore, hurried after her.

Robert Moore sat on his horse for a long moment, watching them go. He saw Shirley’s proud, defiant figure disappear up the drive, and a strange, complicated expression settled on his features. Then, with a curt shake of the reins, he turned his mare and trotted on towards the bleak reality of his mill. The war had more than one front.
