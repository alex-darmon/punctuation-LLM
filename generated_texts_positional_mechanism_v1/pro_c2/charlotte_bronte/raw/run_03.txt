She expected me to hold my own, to have opinions and to voice them, and I found that, after the stifling atmosphere of Finch Manor, my mind felt like a cramped limb being allowed to stretch.

I answered her questions, I offered my thoughts on the book she mentioned, which I had been fortunate enough to have read, and I found myself speaking with a freedom I had not known for years. She listened, her head tilted, her sharp eyes missing nothing. “You have a good mind, Miss Eyre,” she remarked at one point, as if commenting on the quality of a piece of cloth. “What do you intend to do with it in London? A mind is a poor tool if it is left to rust in a drawer.”

“I intend to seek a post as a governess,” I said. It was the only respectable path open to me, the only way I knew to earn my bread by my own labour.

“A governess,” she mused. “A thankless trade. You will be neither servant nor family, a ghost at the feast. Still, it is a way to maintain one’s independence. You will need references.”

At this, my heart sank. My only reference was my time at my uncle’s house, a place I wished to forget entirely. “I… I may have some difficulty in that regard.”

Mrs. Gable looked at me, a long, penetrating look. “Every story has its difficult chapters, Miss Eyre. The trick is not to let them become the whole book. We shall be in London for two days. When we arrive, you will be my guest until you find a suitable lodging. From there, we shall see what we shall see.” She then turned her attention to the passing landscape, indicating that the subject was, for the moment, closed.

That night, we stopped at an inn far grander than The Coach and Horses. I was shown to a small, clean chamber of my own, a luxury I had not anticipated. Before I slept, I took the paper from my dress. In the flickering candlelight, my father’s script seemed to pulse with a faint life of its own. *…for his daughter, Jane Eyre Finch…* I traced the name with my finger. I had shed the latter part of it, but the first two remained. Jane Eyre. It was a beginning. The document was not proof of a fortune I must claim, but a charter of identity, a reminder of a love that had existed before the hate began. My uncle had intended for me to use it as a weapon of revenge, to mire myself in his own bitterness. But I would not. I would use it as a foundation.

The following day, as the chaise rattled over the cobblestones of the London suburbs, the sheer scale of the city began to impose itself upon me. The air grew thick with coal smoke, the noise a constant, deafening roar of iron-shod wheels, street criers, and the unseen hum of a million lives being lived in close proximity. It was terrifying, and yet it was magnificent. It was a stone ocean of anonymity, a place where a single, small life might be swallowed whole, or might, with sufficient will, carve out its own small island.

Mrs. Gable’s house was in a quiet, respectable square, a tall, narrow edifice of brick that seemed to stand as a fortress of quiet dignity against the clamour of the city. Inside, all was order, polish, and silence, presided over by a severe-looking housekeeper who received me with the same dispassionate courtesy as her mistress. I was given a room at the top of the house; it was small and plainly furnished, but the window looked out over a row of gardens, a patch of determined green in the grey expanse. For the first time in what felt like a lifetime, I could breathe.

I stayed there for three days. Mrs. Gable was as good as her word. She made no demands upon me, but I made myself useful, reading to her in the evenings and assisting with her correspondence. On the third day, she summoned me to her drawing-room.

“Miss Eyre,” she began, without preamble, “I have been making some enquiries on your behalf. There is a lady of my acquaintance, a Mrs. Fairfax, who is the housekeeper of a country estate in Yorkshire. The place is called Thornfield Hall. She is in need of a governess for a young French girl, a ward of her employer. The salary is thirty pounds a year. The position is isolated. The master of the house, a Mr. Rochester, is rarely there. It seems to me a post that would suit a woman who wishes for a quiet life and a degree of independence.”

Thornfield Hall. The name was unknown to me. Yorkshire was a world away. But the offer was concrete; it was a future. It was a clean slate.

“You have done this for me?” I asked, my voice thick with emotion. “A stranger you met at an inn? Why?”

Mrs. Gable looked at me, and for the first time, I saw a flicker of something other than cool appraisal in her slate-grey eyes. It might have been sympathy; it might have been the memory of her own past struggles. “Because, Miss Eyre,” she said, her voice a fraction softer than usual, “I know what it is to have one’s circumstances ‘altered’. And because I dislike seeing a good mind go to waste. The world is hard enough on women of intelligence; we have no cause to make it harder for one another. Will you take the post?”

My heart swelled with a gratitude so immense it was painful. This woman, with her brisk manner and her transactional charity, had done more for me in three days than my own blood relatives had done in a lifetime. She had offered me not pity, but opportunity.

“Yes,” I said, and the word was a vow. “Yes, I will take it.”

And so, a week after walking out of the gates of Finch Manor with nothing but the clothes I stood in and a secret paper in my dress, I found myself once again on a northbound coach. This time, I was not fleeing a bitter past, but travelling towards an unknown future. I had a destination. I had a purpose. I had a name that felt like my own. The paper, my father’s last testament, remained folded in a secure pocket, no longer a source of torment or temptation, but a quiet, steadying presence. It was the foundation stone of a life I would now have to build for myself, piece by piece, on the distant, unknown ground of Thornfield. The road was long, and I did not know what awaited me at its end, but for the first time, I felt not fear, but a flicker of a feeling I had rarely known: a quiet, tremulous hope.

As we travelled further north, the landscape transformed itself. The gentle, rolling hills of the south gave way to a sterner, more dramatic aspect: the earth grew darker, the hillsides steeper, and great, brooding expanses of moorland stretched away to a horizon that seemed perpetually cloaked in grey mist. It was a country that did not offer a gentle welcome; it was stark and challenging, yet I found its wildness stirred something resonant in my own soul. This was a land that promised no easy comforts; a land where one would have to be strong to survive. I felt my spirit rise to meet the challenge.

We arrived in Millcote, a smoky, bustling town, late in the afternoon of the second day. From there, I was to take a hired conveyance to Thornfield Hall, which I was told lay some miles distant. As the small trap-and-pony carried me away from the noise of the town, a sense of profound isolation descended upon me. The lane grew narrower, the trees closing in overhead, their branches interlaced like the vaulting of some natural, solemn cathedral. At last, the driver pointed with his whip towards a pair of imposing stone gates. “Thornfield,” he said, and the single word fell into the deep silence with a kind of finality.

The gates opened onto a long avenue of ancient chestnut trees, at the end of which the house itself came into view. It was no elegant manor, airy and light, but a formidable edifice of dark stone, turreted and battlemented like a fortress. Three storeys high it rose, its roofline a jagged silhouette against the bruised purple of the evening sky. A flock of rooks, startled by our approach, rose from the topmost branches of a great oak and wheeled about the chimneys, their harsh cries the only sound to break the stillness. My heart, which had been so full of a determined, if sober, hope, contracted with a sudden chill. The place looked less like a home than a prison; a grand and stately prison, to be sure, but one whose very walls seemed saturated with secrets and melancholy.

My driver deposited me and my small trunk at the main entrance, a great oaken door studded with iron, and departed with a haste that I found vaguely disquieting. I pulled the bell-chain, and its clang echoed strangely in the vast quiet. After a long moment, I heard the drawing of bolts, and the door opened to reveal a woman. She was elderly, clad in a black silk dress with a snowy white apron and cap: a picture of respectable domesticity. Her face was kindly, if a little flustered, with spectacles perched on her nose and a placid expression in her light blue eyes.

“Are you Miss Eyre?” she asked, her voice soft and pleasant.

“I am.”

“I am Mrs. Fairfax. I am so pleased to see you, my dear. We have been expecting you. Do come in, come in out of the cold. The evenings draw in so quickly in these parts.”

She ushered me into a hall that was as vast and imposing as the exterior had promised. It was panelled in dark wood, and the evening shadows were already gathering in its distant corners. A wide, shallow staircase of polished oak swept upwards into the gloom of the upper storeys. The air was cool and still, carrying the faint, clean scents of beeswax and old stone. Despite its grandeur, it was not an unwelcoming place; there was a fire burning in a huge hearth at the far end, and its flickering light cast a warm, dancing glow on the walls.

“This is quite a house,” I remarked, my voice sounding small in the great space.

“It is indeed,” Mrs. Fairfax replied with a gentle sigh. “A very old house. It has been in the Rochester family for generations. But it is far too large for our little household now. It is just myself, and Leah and John the footman, and little Adèle, of course. Let me order you some tea and refreshment before I show you to your room. You have had a long journey.”

Her kindness was a balm to my spirit. This simple, motherly woman was so far from the imperious coldness of Mrs. Reed or the cool, appraising manner of Mrs. Gable that I felt my anxieties begin to recede. We sat by the fire, and she poured me tea from a silver pot, pressing sandwiches and cake upon me with an unfeigned solicitude for my comfort. She asked about my journey, and I answered her questions, feeling the hard knot of tension in my shoulders begin to dissolve.

“And you will be governess to Miss Adèle Varens,” she explained. “She is a French child, a ward of Mr. Rochester’s. A sweet little thing, though a trifle spoiled, perhaps. Her mother was a French opera-dancer, and Adèle has inherited something of her liveliness. I’m afraid her English is not yet perfect, but she is quick to learn. I do hope you will be comfortable here, Miss Eyre.”

“I am sure I shall, Mrs. Fairfax,” I said, and I meant it. The cavernous hall, the formidable exterior, the strange, absent master—all these things seemed less daunting in the warm circle of her simple kindness.

Later, she led me up the grand staircase. Our footsteps echoed on the polished wood. She showed me to my room, which was not in one of the grander wings of the house, but in a quieter, more secluded corridor. It was a modest chamber, but clean and comfortable, with a small bed, a wardrobe, a washstand, and a writing desk. A small fire had already been lit in the grate, and it filled the room with a cheerful, crackling warmth.

“This will be your own private space, my dear,” Mrs. Fairfax said. “The schoolroom is just next door. I have had it prepared for you. I hope you will find everything you need.”

After she had left me, I went to the window and looked out. It overlooked a quiet courtyard at the rear of the house. The moon had risen, and it silvered the stone flags below and the slate roofs of the outbuildings. The world was utterly still. Here I was, Jane Eyre, in this strange, vast house in the middle of Yorkshire, with a new life about to begin. I was no longer a dependant at Gateshead, nor a charity-child at Lowood, nor a fugitive on the road. I was Miss Eyre, the governess. I had a post; I had a salary; I had a room of my own. The feeling was so new, so strange, that it brought tears to my eyes: not tears of sorrow or self-pity, but tears of a deep, quiet, and overwhelming gratitude. I knelt by my bed, not to any image or idol, but to the great, unknown Providence that had seen fit to guide my steps to this strange sanctuary.

The next morning, I was introduced to my pupil. Adèle Varens was a small, elfin creature of perhaps seven or eight years, with large, dark eyes, a profusion of black curls, and a restless, bird-like energy. She was dressed in a frivolous little frock of pink muslin, and she chattered away in a mixture of French and broken English, her attention flitting from one object to the next like a butterfly.

“You are my new governess?” she asked, inspecting me with a frank curiosity. “Mademoiselle Eyre? You are not very big. My last governess, she was very tall, and very strict. I did not like her. Will you be strict?”

“I will be as strict as is necessary to help you learn,” I replied, trying to adopt a tone of gentle authority. “And I hope we shall be good friends.”

“Friends?” She considered this. “Will you give me presents? Mr. Rochester, he always brings me presents when he comes from his travels. A new doll, or a dress of silk.”

“My friendship will be the only present I have to offer, Adèle,” I said. “We shall have to see if that is enough.”

Our lessons began that day in the schoolroom. It was a pleasant, light-filled room, furnished with a large table, two chairs, a globe, and shelves of books. I soon discovered that Mrs. Fairfax’s assessment of the child was accurate: Adèle was quick-witted and eager to please, but her mind had been little cultivated. Her knowledge of history and geography was non-existent, her arithmetic was deplorable, and her chief interests appeared to be dancing, singing, and planning what new frock she would ask Mr. Rochester for on his next visit.

The days settled into a quiet routine. Mornings were for lessons. Afternoons, if the weather was fine, Adèle and I would walk in the extensive grounds of Thornfield. The gardens were somewhat neglected, but they possessed a wild, melancholy beauty that suited my own temperament. We would wander along the overgrown paths, Adèle chasing squirrels and gathering wildflowers, while I fell into my own solitary musings. Evenings I spent in Mrs. Fairfax’s parlour, a cosy, chintz-filled room where we would sit with our sewing, talking quietly by the fire.

I learned more about the absent master of the house from these conversations. Mr. Edward Rochester was, it seemed, a man of contradictions. According to Mrs. Fairfax, he was a gentleman of good family and great intellect, but he was possessed of a restless, moody temperament. He had travelled widely and kept company with all sorts of people, some of whom, Mrs. Fairfax hinted with a delicate cough, were not entirely respectable. He was capable of great kindness and generosity, but also of a harsh, cynical humour that could be quite alarming.

“He is a good master,” she would say, her knitting needles clicking in the silence. “But he is a troubled man, Miss Eyre. He has had a great deal of sorrow in his life. His father was a hard man, and his elder brother, Rowland, inherited the estate, leaving Mr. Edward with very little. It was only after Rowland’s death that Thornfield came to him. But I do not think the place has brought him much happiness. He is rarely here for more than a few weeks at a time before he is off again, to London or the continent, seeking distraction.”

His ward, Adèle, was another puzzle. Mrs. Fairfax spoke of her with an air of compassionate mystery. “Mr. Rochester brought her back with him from France some years ago,” she said. “He feels a responsibility for her. That is all I can say.”

The weeks turned into a month. The initial strangeness of Thornfield wore off, replaced by a sense of peaceful, if monotonous, seclusion. I was content. My duties were not arduous, my pupil was manageable, and my solitude was respected. I felt as though I had found a haven, a quiet port after a long and stormy voyage.

It was on a grey afternoon in late autumn that my sense of peace was first disturbed. Adèle had a slight cold, and was confined to the nursery. Having finished my own small tasks, I found myself with a free hour. A restless feeling took hold of me, a desire to explore the parts of the great house I had not yet seen. I had been up the main staircase many times, and the lesser servants’ stair, but I knew there was a third storey, a range of rooms beneath the attics that seemed to be unoccupied.

On a whim, I decided to investigate. I ascended the main staircase to the second floor, then found a narrower, steeper flight of stairs leading upwards. The air grew colder as I climbed. The third-floor corridor was long and dim, lit only by a single, narrow window at the far end. The doors leading off it were all closed. A profound silence reigned, the kind of deep, listening silence that inhabits deserted places. I walked slowly down the corridor, my footsteps muffled by a runner of faded carpet. I tried one of the doors; it was locked. I tried another, with the same result. The whole floor seemed to be shut up and disused.

I was about to turn back, feeling a little foolish for my trespass, when I heard a sound.

It was a laugh.

It was not a sound of mirth or happiness. It was a strange, chilling sound, low at first, then rising to a high, almost inhuman shriek of derision. It seemed to come from behind one of the locked doors further down the corridor. It was a wild, disquieting sound, full of a strange and terrible despair, and it ceased as abruptly as it had begun, leaving a silence that was more terrifying than the noise itself.

My blood ran cold. I stood frozen, my hand on the doorknob, my heart hammering against my ribs. Who was up here? I had been assured the household was a small one. The sound had been too guttural, too strange, to be Leah or one of the other maids indulging in some secret amusement. It was a sound that had no place in a well-ordered, respectable house.

I turned and fled, my feet making no sound on the thick carpet, my breath catching in my throat. I did not stop until I was back on the broad landing of the second floor, in the familiar, sunless light of the main house. I leaned against the banister, trying to calm the frantic beating of my heart.

That evening, as I sat with Mrs. Fairfax, I tried to approach the subject obliquely. “Mrs. Fairfax,” I began, my voice steadier than I felt, “I was exploring the house a little this afternoon. The third storey seems quite empty.”

She looked up from her sewing, her expression placid. “Yes, most of those rooms are unused. They are mainly for storage now.”

“I thought I heard something up there,” I ventured. “A sound. A sort of laugh.”

A subtle change came over her face. A shutter seemed to come down behind her kind eyes. “A laugh?” she repeated, her tone a little too casual. “Oh, you must have heard Grace Poole. She does her sewing up there sometimes. She is one of the servants. A very reliable, trustworthy woman, though perhaps a little eccentric. She sometimes talks to herself. And she has a strange, harsh laugh. I do apologise if it startled you.”

Grace Poole. I had seen the woman once or twice: a stout, grim-faced person of middle age, with a reserved, almost sullen manner. I had not thought her capable of producing such a sound. Mrs. Fairfax’s explanation seemed plausible, yet I was not entirely convinced. There was something in her manner, a slight hesitation, a refusal to meet my eye, that suggested she was not telling me the whole truth. The laugh I had heard was not the mutter of an eccentric seamstress; it was something wilder, something darker.

I did not press the matter. But as I went to my room that night, the sense of Thornfield as a safe, quiet haven was gone, replaced by a new and unsettling feeling. The house had secrets. And I, Jane Eyre, was no longer merely an observer of its quiet life, but a reluctant witness to its mysteries. The peace I had found was a fragile one, and I had a premonition, cold and sharp as the north wind that now rattled my window-pane, that it would not last.

The air in the room grew thick, unbreathable. The cruelty of the act, so deliberate, so absolute, was a physical blow. She had not merely harmed me; she had erased me. My childhood self, the girl who had been locked in the red-room, screamed within me.

Yet, I remained outwardly calm. My new-found strength, forged in the fires of more recent and more profound passions, held fast. I looked at her, this malevolent ruin of a woman, and I saw not a monster to be feared, but a pathetic creature to be mastered.

“And the letter?” I asked, my voice cold and even as ice. “The letter from my uncle, John Eyre. Do you still have it?”

“It may be in my letter-case,” she mumbled, her brief surge of energy spent. She sank back against the pillows, her eyes clouding over once more with the fog of illness and apathy. “In the top drawer of the bureau. I do not know. It is of no consequence now.”

“It is of every consequence,” I said, and my tone made her look at me again, a flicker of her old fear in her eyes. It was the look she had given me on the day I had finally turned on her, the day I had told her she was a murderess. I walked to the bureau, my movements measured and deliberate. I did not need to ask for a key; I knew she always left it in the lock of the top drawer. I turned it.

The drawer was a chaos of papers: old bills, receipts, invitations, dried flowers, a child’s drawing of a house with smoke curling from all its windows. And there, tucked into a small portfolio of watered silk, was the letter. The paper was yellowed, the ink faded, but the address was clear: ‘To Miss Jane Eyre, at Gateshead Hall’. The postmark was three years old. Beside it was the folded draft of her own reply, the lie written in her familiar, sharp, angular hand.

I took the letter. I did not read it then; I would not give her the satisfaction of seeing the full extent of the injury she had inflicted. I closed the drawer and turned back to the bed.

“You have done me a great and terrible wrong, Mrs. Reed,” I said, my voice low but resonant in the hushed room. “You have robbed me of a family, of a home, of a future. You have done this out of pure malice, and for that, God will judge you. But I—I will not. Your hate could not destroy me then, and it cannot touch me now. I have a life of my own, a life you know nothing of. I came here to offer you peace, to find reconciliation before you died. But that is not possible. Your heart is a stone. I forgive you for your cruelty; I pity you for the misery in which you have lived. May God have mercy on your soul.”

She stared at me, her mouth slightly agape, her eyes wide with a mixture of confusion and terror. Perhaps she had expected tears, reproaches, a storm of passion. My quiet, cold condemnation seemed to disarm and frighten her more than any outburst. She raised a trembling hand as if to ward me off.

“Go away,” she whispered. “You look like a spectre. Go.”

I did not need a second bidding. I turned and left the room, closing the door softly behind me. On the landing, Bessie was waiting, her face etched with anxiety. “How is she, Miss Jane?” she asked. “Did she know you?”

“She knew me,” I replied, my voice hoarse. “She is very ill, Bessie. Very ill indeed.” I showed her the letter in my hand. “And she is as wicked as she ever was.”

Bessie’s eyes widened at the sight of the letter, but she did not ask what it was. She knew her former mistress well enough to guess it was some relic of a past injustice. She simply shook her head, a sigh escaping her lips. “Ah, poor missis. She was never a happy woman. Her passions were always too strong for her own good.”

I went not to my own assigned chamber, but downstairs. I found Eliza and Georgiana in the drawing-room, engaged in their separate, silent pursuits. Eliza was poring over a thick account book, her lips a thin, calculating line. Georgiana was reclining on a chaise-longue, listlessly turning the pages of a fashion-plate journal. The air was thick with a bored, resentful silence.

I stood before them, the letter held in my hand. They both looked up, Eliza with annoyance, Georgiana with a languid curiosity.

“I have seen your mother,” I said. “She is not likely to last the night. She has made a confession to me.”

Eliza’s pen stopped its scratching. Georgiana sat up a little straighter.

“She has told me,” I continued, my gaze fixed upon them, “that three years ago, a letter arrived for me from my uncle, Mr. John Eyre of Madeira. He wished to adopt me and make me his heiress. Your mother intercepted this letter and wrote to him, telling him that I was dead. She did this so that I should not have a fortune while her own children, as she put it, ‘struggled’.”

A flush of angry red stained Georgiana’s pale cheeks. “Mama did that?” she exclaimed, her voice a mixture of shock and, I thought, a strange sort of vicarious shame. “How perfectly dreadful! To cheat you out of a fortune! It is wicked.”

Eliza’s reaction was different. Her face, which had at first shown a flicker of surprise, settled into a cold, impassive mask. She laid down her pen with deliberate care. “And what is your point in telling us this, Jane Eyre?” she asked, her voice as crisp as a winter frost. “Do you expect us to make restitution? The money, if it ever existed, is not ours to give. Mama’s affairs are, as you may have overheard, in a state of considerable disarray. This revelation is unfortunate, but it changes nothing.”

“I expect nothing from you,” I retorted, my own coldness a match for hers. “I do not want your pity, Georgiana, nor your pragmatism, Eliza. I tell you this so that you know the full measure of the woman who raised you. I tell you this so that when you think of me in the future, you remember that my poverty, my position as a governess, was not an accident of fate, but a circumstance engineered by your mother’s hand.”

I held up the letter. “I have the proof. I will not be silenced or ignored. You will treat me with civility for as long as I remain under this roof. I am here to see your mother through to the end, because my conscience dictates it, and because your dying father once begged it of me. I am not here as a poor relation to be scorned. Is that understood?”

For a long moment, there was silence. Georgiana looked from me to Eliza, her blue eyes wide and uncertain. Eliza’s gaze was hard, calculating. She was weighing the situation, I could see: the potential for scandal, the inconvenience of my presence, the sheer force of my will. I had always been a creature that shrank; now, I stood my ground, and the novelty of it seemed to impress even her frigid nature.

“As you wish,” she said at last, her tone clipped. “It is a matter of indifference to me. So long as you do not interfere with my arrangements, you may stay or go as you please.” She picked up her pen again and returned to her accounts, her dismissal absolute.

I had won, if it could be called a victory. I had asserted myself in the house of my tormentors. Yet the triumph was a hollow one, ashes in my mouth. I left the drawing-room and sought the solitude of my own chamber. There, by the light of a single candle, I finally opened and read the letter from the uncle I had never known.

It was a kind letter, written in a clear, masculine hand. It spoke of his late brother, my father, with affection. It explained that he, John Eyre, was unmarried and in failing health, and that he wished to do his duty by his only living relative. He asked me to come to him in Madeira, or, if that were not possible, to allow him to make a legal settlement that would ensure my future independence. He asked for a reply at my earliest convenience.

I folded the letter carefully. The words, so full of potential and kindness, were now just a record of a life I might have had. The path had been there, waiting for me, and Mrs. Reed had built a wall across it. And yet, as I sat there, a strange thought occurred to me. Had she not built that wall, I would have left England. I would never have gone to Thornfield. I would never have met Mr. Rochester.

The thought struck me with the force of a physical blow, sending a tumultuous wave of feeling through me. My life as a governess, which had seemed a destiny of servitude, was also the path that had led me to the one profound, exquisite joy I had ever known. The pain of Mrs. Reed’s treachery was immense, but the idea of a life without Mr. Rochester in it was an abyss of unimaginable blackness. My loss was also, in some strange, providential way, my gain.

My mind was a whirlwind. I felt a fierce, desperate yearning to be away from the suffocating gloom of Gateshead, to be back in the grey, brooding presence of Thornfield Hall, to see his dark eyes and hear his sardonic, challenging voice. He was my world now; this place of my childhood was nothing but a tomb of bitter memories.

A soft knock came at my door. It was Bessie, her face pale. “Miss Jane,” she said, her voice trembling. “The mistress is asking for you again. She is… she is not well. The doctor is with her.”

I followed her back to the death-chamber. The scene had changed. A doctor I did not know stood by the bed, his expression grave. Eliza and Georgiana were there now, standing near the foot of the bed like well-dressed, impatient sentinels. Mrs. Reed was thrashing weakly, muttering my name over and over, her eyes wide and staring.

“Jane Eyre,” she gasped as I approached. “Jane… forgive…”

But the sentence remained unfinished. A final, violent shudder wracked her frame. Her head fell back against the pillow, her jaw slackened, and the light in her hard eyes went out for ever. The doctor solemnly checked for a pulse and then drew the sheet over her face.

It was over. My aunt, my enemy, was dead. I looked at her still form, and I felt—nothing. No triumph, no sorrow, only a vast, empty peace. The accounts of my childhood were closed at last. I was free. And my only thought, my only desire, was to return to Thornfield, and to him.

The grievances against me, so large in her memory a moment before, were now eclipsed by the more pressing anxieties of money and the disappointment of her own children. I looked at her, the formidable tyrant of my youth, now reduced to this fretful, broken creature, and the last embers of my resentment died. She was not a monster of deliberate evil; she was a woman of narrow sympathies and a hardened heart, whose nature could not encompass anything beyond the sphere of her own pride and her own kin. Her hatred of me was not a grand passion, but a petty, persistent irritation, born of a deathbed promise she had begrudged.

“Aunt,” I said softly, my voice holding no trace of the storm that had passed through me. “Rest now. We can talk more in the morning.”

She turned her head on the pillow, her gaze vacant. “Is the light troubling you? It shines in my eyes.” I rose and moved the candle, so its glow was shielded by the bed-curtain. She gave a faint sigh, which might have been relief, and her eyelids fluttered and closed. I resumed my seat, watching the troubled landscape of her sleeping face. Bessie came in presently, treading softly, and looked from her mistress to me.

“She is asleep,” I whispered.

“Poor soul. She has been like this for days; her mind wanders from one thing to another. Did she know you, Miss Jane?”

“Yes, Bessie. She knew me.”

I did not elaborate on the substance of our conversation. What purpose would it serve to repeat her bitter words, to give them life outside that disordered mind? They were the ghosts of old sorrows, and I wished them to be laid to rest with her. I stayed for another hour, a silent vigil in the dimly lit room, until Bessie insisted I go to my own chamber and seek rest. “There is nothing more you can do to-night, miss,” she said, her kind face etched with weariness. “And you look as pale as a spectre yourself.”

I conceded, for in truth a profound exhaustion had settled upon me. The events of the day, the journey, the confrontations, the flood of resurrected emotions, had taken their toll. My own room, once a place of exile, now felt like a quiet harbour. I did not sleep at once; I lay awake for a long time, listening to the old house groaning around me, the wind sighing in the eaves. I thought of the letter from my uncle, John Eyre, and the life it had offered. A fortune, a new home in a sunlit land, the care of a kinsman. It was the sort of miraculous deliverance one reads of in fairy tales. And Mrs. Reed, in her spite, had stolen it from me. She had told him I was dead. The lie was so stark, so cruel, it took my breath away. She had not merely wronged me; she had attempted to erase my very existence from the world, to sever my last tie of blood.

And yet, as I had realised in that first shock of discovery, her deceit had forged another destiny for me. Had I received that letter a year ago, I should have answered its summons with a grateful heart. I should have been in Madeira now, heiress to my uncle’s wealth, mistress of his house. I would have been secure, independent, and utterly ignorant of Mr. Rochester. The thought was a cold hand gripping my heart. To have never known him; to have never heard his voice, at once harsh and tender; to have never seen his eyes, burning with a fire I had come to know as my own sun: it was a fate more terrible than any poverty. The wealth of the Indies could not weigh in the balance against a single hour of the life I had known at Thornfield. My aunt had, in her malice, been the unwitting agent of my soul’s greatest happiness. It was a strange and humbling thought, a thread of light in a tapestry of darkness. It did not absolve her, but it altered the nature of the injury. The wound was deep, but the scar it left was the map of my way to him. With this last, complex thought, I finally drifted into a deep and dreamless sleep.

The next few days passed in the strange, suspended time that precedes a funeral. A funereal hush fell upon Gateshead, a hush not of genuine sorrow, but of rigid propriety. The blinds were drawn, the servants walked on tiptoe and spoke in whispers, and callers left cards of condolence at the door. Georgiana, swathed in fashionable mourning, spent her days sighing on a chaise-longue, lamenting the interruption to her social ambitions. Eliza, ever practical, took charge of the household accounts and corresponded with the undertaker, her face a mask of cold composure. I kept to myself for the most part, a silent observer on the fringe of their remote grief. I was in the house, but not of it; a spectre from the past who had inconveniently appeared to witness the final scene of the drama.

I saw my aunt twice more. She was awake, but her mind was mostly gone, adrift on the foggy seas of memory and delusion. She did not know me again. Once she mistook me for a housemaid and querulously demanded a glass of water; another time she seemed to look through me, her eyes fixed on some point in the distant past, muttering my uncle Reed’s name. There was no more talk of hatred, no more confession; only the sad, disjointed ramblings of a spirit preparing for its release. Her animosity towards me required a strength of mind she no longer possessed.

The end came on the third night after my arrival. I was sitting with Bessie in the sick-room, for the doctor had warned that the close was near. Eliza was there also, seated bolt upright in a chair, her prayer-book in her hand. Georgiana had declared her nerves unequal to the task and retired to her room with a bottle of smelling-salts. Shortly after midnight, Mrs. Reed began to stir. A violent shudder ran through her body. Her eyes opened wide, but they were unseeing. She gasped my name, a single, choked utterance: “Jane…” It was not the “Jane Eyre” of her previous tirade, but just “Jane,” a sound less of accusation than of simple recognition, as if in that last moment she saw me not as the burden, the fiend, the incomprehensible child, but as the niece her husband had charged her to love. A moment later, a profound stillness settled over her features. The struggle was over.

The doctor, who had been dozing in a nearby chair, started up and came to the bed. He felt for a pulse, listened for a breath, and then gently drew the sheet over her face. “She is at peace,” he said, in the low, solemn tone reserved for such moments.

Eliza closed her book with a quiet snap. “May God have mercy on her soul,” she said, her voice as devoid of inflection as if she were commenting on the weather. She then looked at me, a flicker of something, perhaps calculation, in her cold eyes. “The funeral is arranged for Tuesday. I presume you will stay.”

It was a statement, not a question. I was a necessary piece of the funereal tableau, a testament to the family’s grudging performance of its duties. “Yes,” I said. “I will stay.”

The funeral was a grand and gloomy affair. The hearse with its sable plumes, the line of mourning-coaches, the gathering of neighbours and tenants in their blackest attire: all spoke of the importance of the Reed family in the district. I stood by the graveside, between Bessie and the housekeeper, a little apart from Eliza and Georgiana, who stood as chief mourners, their faces hidden behind thick veils. I listened to the hollow sound of earth falling on the coffin, the final, irrevocable word in the story of my aunt’s life. I felt no sorrow, no triumph, only a vast, quiet emptiness. The great creditor, Death, had claimed his due; the long account of my childhood, with all its debts of pain and bitterness, was settled at last. As the assembly began to disperse, I felt a hand on my arm. It was Bessie, her eyes red with weeping.

“She is gone, Miss Jane. For all her hard ways, I cannot help but be sad.”

“I know, Bessie,” I said, and I squeezed her hand, for her simple, honest grief was a relief in the midst of so much cold ceremony. “You were a true friend to her.”

The day after the funeral, the family lawyer came to read the will. We assembled in the drawing-room, the three of us, a strange trinity of disconnected souls. The document was brief and to the point. The house and the bulk of the remaining fortune were to be divided equally between Eliza and Georgiana. There were bequests to the senior servants, including Bessie. My name was not mentioned. I had expected as much; it was the final stone cast from a hand now powerless to wound. It struck me without bruising, a confirmation of a truth I had long accepted. I was not, and had never been, one of them.

That evening, as I was packing my small trunk, Eliza knocked and entered my room. She came directly to the point, as was her way.

“You are leaving to-morrow, I collect?”

“Yes, on the first coach.”

“It is for the best. Georgiana intends to go to London to live with our uncle Gibson as soon as the period of deep mourning is past. She will seek to marry well. I shall remain here only long enough to see the estate settled and the house let or sold. I have made my arrangements.”

“Arrangements?” I asked, pausing in my folding.

“I am to enter a convent. The establishment of the Sacré Cœur at Lisle. My portion will serve as my dowry to the church. I shall take the veil and devote my life to prayer and discipline. I find the prospect… satisfactory. It is a life of order, without passion and without disappointment.”

I looked at her: her rigid posture, her pale, emotionless face, her narrow, determined eyes. She was walling herself up, exchanging the prison of Gateshead for the prison of a convent, seeking not grace, but merely an escape from the untidiness of the world. There was a terrible sort of logic to it.

“And you,” she continued, her gaze assessing me with cool detachment. “You return to your situation as a governess?”

“I return to Thornfield Hall, yes.”

“To Mr. Rochester. Georgiana has prattled on about him. She met him in London, it seems, and found him distinguished, but excessively grim. Is he to be your future?”

The question was so direct, so devoid of delicacy, that it took me by surprise. My future. The word hung in the air, full of a terrifying, thrilling potential. My cheeks grew warm, but I met her gaze steadily.

“Mr. Rochester has been a very kind master to me,” I said, my voice quiet but firm. It was an evasion, but it was all the truth I could offer her.

She gave a slight, dismissive shrug. “Well, I wish you a more placid existence than our mother knew. Her life was a torment of vexations. Farewell, Jane Eyre. It is unlikely we shall meet again in this world.”

She did not offer her hand. She simply turned and walked out, closing the door quietly behind her. And so I parted from my cousin Eliza. A few minutes later, a rustle of silk announced Georgiana. She swept in, dabbing at her eyes with a lace-trimmed handkerchief, though I saw no tears.

“Oh, Jane, it is all so dreadful!” she began. “To be buried in the country at this time of year! And Eliza is so unfeelingly morbid, talking of convents! I shall die of ennui. I live for the day I can escape to Uncle Gibson’s in London. Do you think this mourning dress becomes me? I had it made with the subtlest of trimmings, for one must show respect, of course, but one need not look a complete fright.” She chattered on, a stream of vanity and shallow complaint, and I listened with a patience that would have been impossible for me a year ago. She was like a brightly coloured butterfly, trapped under a glass, fluttering her wings against an invisible barrier.

When her monologue ran its course, she asked, “You are truly going back to that dreary old Thornfield? You must be quite devoted to your little pupil.”

“I am,” I said, and let her believe that was the whole of it.

“Well, do give my regards to Mr. Rochester. He has a great fortune, you know, though he is so peculiar and plain. I wonder he has never married. A man of his position ought to have a wife to manage his establishment. But I daresay no one could bear his temper. Farewell, Jane. Try to find some happiness, if you can.”

She drifted out as she had drifted in, leaving a trail of expensive perfume. And that was my farewell to my cousin Georgiana. I had seen them for the last time. My business at Gateshead was well and truly finished. The ties of blood, which had been for me only bonds of servitude and pain, were now finally and completely severed. I was alone, but I was free.

The next morning, I was up before the dawn. Bessie was waiting for me in the kitchen with a hot cup of tea and a parcel of bread and cheese for my journey. Her honest face was my last and fondest link to the place.

“Oh, Miss Jane,” she said, her eyes filling with tears. “Must you go so soon?”

“My place is at Thornfield now, Bessie. But I will write to you. And you must write to me. You and your husband and little Bobby.”

“That I will, miss. And you will be happy there? This Mr. Rochester, he is a good man?”

A great wave of feeling rose in me, a certainty so profound it was like a religion. “He is a very good man, Bessie,” I said, my voice thick with emotion. “And I am happy.”

She embraced me then, a warm, motherly hug that spoke more than words. It was the only true affection I had ever received within those walls. I returned her embrace fiercely, then broke away, for the coach-horn was sounding from the gate. I hurried out, without a backward glance at the house. I did not wish to see its grey, forbidding façade one last time. My past was behind me; my future lay ahead, a hundred miles to the north-west.

The journey was a torment of impatience. Every turn of the wheel seemed too slow, every stop to change horses an insufferable delay. The world outside the coach window passed in a blur of green fields and rolling hills. I saw none of it. My inner eye was fixed on a single image: the dark, crenellated towers of Thornfield Hall. My heart outran the carriage, flying ahead to the place I now called home. Home. The word was a miracle on my tongue. After a lifetime of being an outcast, a dependant, a paid servant, I had a home. It was not a place of brick and mortar, but a place in another’s heart.

As we drew further north, the landscape began to change, becoming wilder and more familiar. My agitation grew with every mile. What would he say when he saw me? Would his face light up with that rare, brilliant smile that transformed his harsh features into something more beautiful than beauty? I lived the moment of our meeting a hundred times in my mind, each imagined scene more exquisitely joyful than the last. He had sent for me; he had wanted me back. That knowledge was a fire in my blood, warming me against the chill of the drizzling rain that had begun to fall.

The coach deposited me at the George Inn in Millcote late in the afternoon. The air was damp and heavy. A brooding stillness had settled over the land, a weight in the atmosphere that seemed to press upon my spirits even as they soared with anticipation. I dismissed the feeling as a product of my own overwrought nerves and hired a chaise for the final few miles to the Hall.

As we approached the gates, I leaned from the window, my heart hammering against my ribs. There it was. Thornfield. It stood solid and imposing against the bruised purple of the evening sky. Yet something was not right. A thin veil of smoke, thicker than I remembered from the kitchen chimneys, clung to one of the western battlements, and I fancied I could smell a faint, acrid odour on the damp air, like burnt wood. A tremor of nameless dread shot through me, cold and sharp. I told myself I was being foolish, that it was merely the mist playing tricks on my senses.

The chaise drew up before the great oak door. I paid the driver, my hands trembling slightly, and stepped out onto the wet gravel. The front of the house was dark; no welcoming light shone from the windows of the hall or Mrs. Fairfax’s parlour. All was silent and strangely still. I pulled the bell, and its clang echoed in the unnerving quiet. I waited. No one came. My anxiety mounted, turning from a vague tremor to a sickening certainty that something was terribly wrong. I rang again, pulling with more force, the sound seeming to mock the emptiness.

Finally, I heard the sound of a bolt being drawn, and the door creaked open a few inches. A man’s face, one I did not recognise, peered out. He was old, with a grim, sorrowful expression.

“What do you want?” he asked, his voice rough.

“I am the governess, Jane Eyre,” I said, my own voice sounding thin and strange to my ears. “I have been away. I have come back. Is Mr. Rochester at home?”

The old man stared at me, his eyes full of a strange pity. He opened the door wider, and gestured me inside with a weary hand. The great hall was dark and cold. The air within was thick with the same terrible, smoky smell.

“Mr. Rochester is not here,” he said slowly, his gaze not quite meeting mine.

“Not here?” I echoed, my blood turning to ice. “Where has he gone? When will he return? And where is Mrs. Fairfax? Where is everyone?”

The man shook his head, and a deep sigh escaped him. “There has been a great trouble, miss. A terrible, terrible trouble.” He finally looked at me, and in his eyes I saw the reflection of a catastrophe so immense I could not yet grasp its shape. “The Hall… Thornfield Hall is a ruin. There was a fire.”

A fire. The word fell into the profound silence of the hall, a small, hard stone dropped into a bottomless well. It did not splash; it simply sank, and I felt its descent through my whole being, a cold, heavy plummet of dread. I stared at him, my mind refusing the import of the sound; my own recent history with fire, that nocturnal horror in Mr. Rochester’s chamber, rushed back upon me, not as a memory but as a present and suffocating terror. My lips parted, but no sound came; my breath was a captive in my lungs.

“A fire?” I managed at last, the question a dry whisper. “When? How? Was anyone… was anyone hurt?”

The old man, who I now perceived was the landlord of the Rochester Arms from the nearby village, a man I had seen once or twice before, let out another heavy, rasping sigh. His face, etched with the lines of a hard life, was a mask of weary commiseration.

“It was about a month since, miss. In the deep of the night. A fearsome sight it was; the whole sky was red as blood, and you could see the flames for miles around, leaping up from the roof like devils dancing.”

He held up the lantern he carried, and its weak, yellow light did little to dispel the cavernous gloom. It served only to make the shadows deeper, the emptiness more vast. He gestured towards the interior, a motion of profound weariness: “Come, you had best see for yourself. There is little left to tell what it once was.”

I followed him, my feet moving as if in a dream, or rather a nightmare from which I could not wake. We crossed the great hall, my footsteps echoing on the smoke-stained marble floor where they had once been silenced by thick Turkish carpets. The air was cold, not with the chill of a grand, unoccupied house, but with the damp, sepulchral cold of a tomb. The acrid smell I had noticed outside was a hundred times stronger here: an inescapable miasma of soot, damp ash, and something else, a faint, lingering scent of utter desolation.

The great oak staircase, which I had so often ascended and descended with a heart full of warring emotions, was no longer there. A gaping black hole yawned in its place, rising up through the floors to the bruised and starless sky above. Charred beams jutted out from the void like the broken ribs of some colossal skeleton. It was as if the very heart of the house had been violently torn out.

“The fire started on the third floor,” the man said, his voice low and steady, a plainspoken anchor in the swirling chaos of my thoughts. “In the north wing. It spread like wildfire, as you might say. The old timbers, they went up like tinder.”

My questions tumbled out, a frantic torrent: “But the people? Mrs. Fairfax? And Sophie, and little Adèle? Mr. Rochester… where was he?”

“Patience, miss,” he said, not unkindly. “I will tell you all. First, you must understand the ruin.”

He led me past the gaping maw of the staircase and towards the library. The door was gone, burned from its very hinges. I stopped on the threshold, a cry of pure anguish catching in my throat. This room, this sanctuary of my soul, where I had first truly spoken with my master, where his mind had met mine, where I had felt the first stirrings of that profound and unconquerable love; this room was a hollow shell. The shelves were black, skeletal frames; the books, those thousands of volumes that had been his companions and mine, were now nothing but heaps of grey, sodden ash. The great window at the far end, through which the winter moon had so often shone, was a black and sightless socket, its intricate stonework scarred and broken; the gallery above, where I had once paced in an agony of suspense, was gone. Nothing remained. Here, in this very spot, he had stood; his presence had filled this room with a vitality that was almost a physical force. Now, that force was extinguished, and a crushing void had taken its place.

I felt a sob rise, but I forced it down; this was no time for the weakness of tears. I had to know. I had to face the truth, however terrible it might be.

“Tell me,” I said, my voice hard with the effort of control. “Tell me everything. Do not spare me.”

He seemed to understand, for he nodded gravely and his gaze became more direct. “The master was here, miss. He had not gone to London as he’d planned. He stayed on, quiet and brooding like, shut up in the library most days. Mrs. Fairfax said he was not himself at all.”

My heart contracted. He had stayed. For me? Or for some other, darker reason?

“That night,” the landlord continued, “it was the master himself who raised the alarm. He was woken by the smoke, he said. The first thing he did was get the servants out. He went from room to room, waking everyone, calm as you please, they said, while the roof was burning over his head. He got Mrs. Fairfax out, and Sophie, and the little French girl.”

A wave of relief washed over me, so potent it left me weak. Adèle was safe. Mrs. Fairfax was safe. I clutched the ruined doorframe for support.

“Thank God,” I breathed. “And they are well? Where are they?”

“Mrs. Fairfax has gone to live with some relations of hers, many miles from here. She left a letter for you, in case you ever came. I have it at the inn. The little girl, Miss Adèle, the master had already made arrangements for her to go to a good school, and she was sent there straight away, with her nurse.”

He paused, and his face grew grimmer still. “He got them all out. But then… then he went back in.”

“Back in?” I cried. “For what? For his papers? His valuables?”

The man shook his head slowly, his eyes fixed on the blackened void above us where the third floor had been. “No, miss. He went back for her. For the madwoman.”

The breath left my body in a rush. Bertha. Of course. The author of all this ruin. The secret at the heart of the house had finally consumed it.

“She was the one who set the fire,” he said, his voice dropping to an awed whisper. “She had got out of her room, you see. That woman who looked after her, the Grace Poole, she had taken to the drink again that night, and fell into a stupor. The creature got her key and crept out. She went to your old room first, miss.”

“My room?”

“Aye. She took the sheets from your bed and set them alight. Then she went to the master’s own chamber and did the same. It was her shrieking that folks heard first, a sound not of this earth. They say she was up on the roof, with her hair all loose and flying in the wind, lit up by the flames like a fiend from hell. She was shouting and laughing as the house burned beneath her.”

I pictured the scene: the red-streaked sky, the roaring inferno, and that terrible, tragic figure silhouetted against it. A shudder ran through me, a strange mix of horror and pity.

“Mr. Rochester went up after her,” the landlord went on. “Right up onto the roof. The fire was all around them. We could see him from the lawn below, trying to get to her, calling her name. He wanted to save her, you see. Even then. But she would not have it. She stood on the very edge of the battlements, and when he got near, she gave one last great shriek, and she jumped.”

He pointed a trembling finger towards the lawn, now a dark, rain-soaked patch of ground. “She fell there. They said she was dead before she even hit the ground.”

I stood frozen, the ghastly image searing itself into my mind. Dead. The obstacle, the great and terrible impediment to my happiness, was gone. But at what a cost. This ruin, this devastation, was the price. And what of the man who had paid it?

“And Mr. Rochester?” I asked, my voice barely audible. “What happened to him? Did he get down safely?”

The old man’s face, which had been turned away, now swiveled back to me. In the flickering lantern light, I saw that his eyes were glistening with unshed tears. The sight filled me with a new and more potent dread than any I had yet felt.

“He was coming back across the roof,” he said, his voice thick with emotion. “He was almost clear. But just as he reached the gable, the whole floor beneath him gave way. The main beam, the great timber that held up that part of the roof, it came crashing down.” He stopped, unable to continue, and passed a rough hand over his face.

“Go on,” I commanded, though my own heart was a block of ice. “For God’s sake, tell me.”

“He fell, miss. Not all the way to the ground, but into the fire and the wreckage. It took the men an hour to get him out. We all thought he was dead. When they carried him out, his face… his hair… it was terrible.”

“Is he dead?” I asked, the words stark and brutal. I needed to know the worst.

“No, miss. Not dead.” He took a deep, shuddering breath. “But some say it would have been a mercy if he had been. The fire… it took his sight.”

“His sight?” I echoed stupidly.

“Aye. Both his eyes. The surgeon from Millcote came, and a great man from London too. They said the optic nerves were perished by the heat and the smoke. There is no hope, they say. He is blind.”

Blind. The word was a blow, more final and more terrible than death. Mr. Rochester, my eagle, my falcon, whose keen, piercing gaze could read my very soul; whose eyes had flashed with wit, and darkened with passion, and softened with a love I had never thought to know. Those eyes now saw nothing. A universe of light and life extinguished, replaced by an endless, unyielding darkness. The thought was an agony so sharp, so exquisitely painful, that I bent double, a silent scream tearing through me.

The landlord waited for the paroxysm to pass, his silence a respectful testament to a grief he could not comprehend but could plainly see. When I straightened up, my face was wet with the tears I had forbidden, but my resolve was like iron.

“And what else?” I asked, my voice flat. “You said… you did not finish.”

He looked down at his own gnarled hands, as if reluctant to speak further. “The falling timbers, miss… they crushed his left hand. And the fire… it is a seared and useless thing now. And one side of him is much scarred. He is… a wreck, miss. A ruin, like his house.”

A blind, maimed, broken man. The proud, sardonic, powerful master of Thornfield Hall was gone, and in his place was a helpless dependent. The world, I knew, would see him as an object of pity, a tragic figure to be mourned and then forgotten. But the world’s view was nothing to me. In that moment of absolute horror, a new and different feeling began to dawn in my soul. It was not pity that I felt; no, my love was too fierce, too possessive for so weak an emotion. It was a fierce, surging tenderness, a yearning to find him, to serve him, to be his eyes and his hands. He was no longer a master to be looked up to; he was my own, my equal, a fellow-sufferer whom I could comfort and cherish. My path, which had seemed so lost and dark, was suddenly illuminated by a single, blazing purpose.

“Where is he?” I asked, my voice clear and strong. “He cannot be here. Where did they take him?”

“He would not go to a town, nor be with strangers,” the landlord replied. “When he was well enough to be moved, he had them take him to a little manor-house he owns, a place deep in the woods, a lonely, forgotten spot. It is called Ferndean.”

Ferndean. I had heard the name. A house some thirty miles distant, sequestered and remote, a place he kept for shooting parties and had not visited in years.

“He lives there now,” the man continued. “With two old servants, John and his wife. He sees no one. He wishes to see no one. He is shut away from the world with his grief.”

Shut away from the world. But not from me. Never from me. My journey was not over; it had only just found its true and final destination. The hundred miles I had travelled from Morton were nothing. The last thirty miles that separated me from him were all that mattered now.

“I must go to him,” I said, turning from the ruin of the library and facing the old man. My shock and sorrow had given way to a strange, calm energy. I was no longer the waif who had arrived at Thornfield a year ago, but a woman with a mission.

“But miss,” he stammered, taken aback by my sudden resolve. “He will not see you. He has given strict orders. He sees no one.”

“He will see me,” I stated, with a certainty that left no room for argument. “How far is it to Ferndean? Can I walk it?”

“Walk it? Thirty miles? No, miss, not at this hour, and in this weather. It is a wild road. You must stay the night at my inn. In the morning, I can find you a cart to take you at least part of the way.”

I considered his words. He was right. To arrive at Ferndean exhausted and soaked, in the dead of night, would serve no purpose. I needed my strength. I would need all my strength for what was to come.

“Very well,” I said. “I will stay at the inn. But we must leave at first light.”

I took one last look around the despoiled hall. I saw not the ruin, but the ghost of what had been: the fire in the great hearth, the portraits on the wall, the master descending the grand staircase, his eyes searching the shadows for me. A tear traced a cold path down my cheek, but it was not a tear of despair. It was a tear of farewell to the past, and of consecration to the future.

My master, I thought, my Edward. I am coming. You think you are alone, but you are not. You have been brought low, but I will lift you up. You are in darkness, but I will be your light. I turned my back on the ashes of Thornfield Hall and followed the landlord out into the damp, cleansing rain. My heart, which had been a vessel of dread, was now filled with a purpose so vast and holy it left no room for fear.

The inn to which the landlord led me was a humble affair, its rooms small but scrupulously clean; a place far removed from the grandeur I had known, yet offering a necessary and welcome sanctuary for my overwrought spirit. The good man showed me to a chamber whose window looked out upon the village churchyard, its grey stones slick with the persistent rain. A fire had been kindled in the small hearth, and its flickering light cast dancing shadows upon the whitewashed walls. I was grateful for the warmth, for a chill had settled deep in my bones that had little to do with the dampness of the evening.

He offered me supper, and I accepted, though I knew I should scarcely be able to eat. My mind was a vortex of images: the blackened shell of Thornfield, the kind, pitying face of the landlord, and, above all, the spectre of my master, blind and broken, hidden away in the gloom of Ferndean. When a simple meal of bread and cheese and ale was brought, I forced myself to partake; for I understood that the trial before me would demand not only fortitude of soul, but also endurance of body. I must not faint or fail now. Every ounce of my strength belonged to him.

The night was long. I did not undress, but sat in a wooden chair before the dwindling fire, my travelling cloak still wrapped about me. Sleep was an impossibility; my senses were wound to a pitch of extreme tension. I listened to the rain lashing against the windowpane and the wind sighing in the yew trees of the churchyard. It seemed the very elements were weeping for the desolation of my master’s fate. Desolation? No, I would not permit the word. Seclusion, sorrow, suffering, yes; but desolation implied a finality, an abandonment I was even now hastening to refute.

My thoughts, like restless birds, flew back over the months of my separation. I saw again the rigid, classical features of St. John Rivers; I heard his cold, compelling voice offering me a life of purpose and duty at his side. I had almost yielded. I had believed it was God’s will that I should mortify my heart’s affections and devote myself to a higher, colder calling. What a mistake that would have been! A profanation, a lie against my own soul. The call I had heard on the moor, that ghostly cry of “Jane! Jane! Jane!”, was not a temptation from the devil, but a summons from the truest part of my own nature, a divine sanction for the love I had tried so hard to deny. I had a purpose, a duty, far nearer and more sacred than the conversion of distant heathens. My mission was here, in England; my work was to bind the wounds of one solitary, suffering man.

“He will not see you,” the landlord had said. The words echoed in the quiet room, a challenge to my resolve. And what if he would not? What if his pride, wounded and festering in the dark, had built a wall around him that even my love could not breach? I remembered his dark moods at Thornfield, his abrupt changes of temper, his capacity for a harshness that bordered on cruelty. Grief and pain would not have softened such a nature; they would have hardened it, tempered it to a bitter, brittle steel. He might spurn me, curse me, drive me from his door as an unwelcome reminder of a past he wished only to forget.

I rose from my chair and went to the window, pressing my forehead against the cool, damp glass. The rain had lessened to a fine drizzle. A sliver of moon shone fitfully between scudding clouds, illuminating the ancient gravestones below. I felt a kinship with the silent dead; they had finished their struggles, while mine, it seemed, was only just beginning. And yet, I felt no fear. A strange, serene certainty had taken possession of me. The love I bore for Mr. Rochester was not a girl’s fleeting fancy; it had been tested by separation and jealousy, by poverty and temptation, and it had emerged not weakened, but purified and strengthened. It was no longer a passion that sought its own gratification, but a principle, as firm and unshakeable as the ground beneath my feet. If he rejected me, I would bear it. I would find some way to remain near him, to serve him unknown, to be a secret guardian of his welfare. But I would not leave him. My place was at his side, whether he willed it or not. With this resolution firm in my heart, a measure of peace descended, and lying down upon the bed, still clothed, I fell at last into a brief, dreamless sleep.

I awoke before the first hint of dawn, roused by an inner urgency. The inn was silent. I washed my face and hands in the cold water from the ewer, smoothed my hair and dress, and made myself ready. When I descended the stairs, the landlord was already awake, as he had promised, stirring the embers in the main room’s great hearth. He looked at me with a sort of compassionate curiosity.

“You are determined, then, miss?” he asked, his voice low.

“I am,” I replied. “The cart you spoke of, is it ready?”

“Joe is harnessing the horse now. He can take you as far as the Izon cross-roads; it is a good twenty miles. From there, you must walk the rest. The way to Ferndean is no true road, but a track through the woods, too rough for any cart. It is a lonely place.”

“I do not mind loneliness,” I said.

He insisted I take some breakfast, and I drank a cup of hot milk and ate a piece of dry toast, my eyes fixed on the grey light that was slowly filtering through the window. Soon, a sturdy-looking man with a weathered face and taciturn expression appeared at the door. It was Joe. The cart, a simple, two-wheeled affair, stood waiting in the courtyard, a stout pony between its shafts. I paid the landlord for my lodging, thanking him for his kindness with a sincerity that seemed to touch him.

“God go with you, miss,” he said, as he handed me up into the cart. “You have a good heart.”

The journey began. We travelled for hours through a landscape that grew progressively wilder and more sparsely populated. The neat fields and stone-walled pastures of the lowlands gave way to rolling, heathery hills and dark patches of woodland. The road deteriorated from a maintained turnpike to a rutted lane, jarring the simple cart and making conversation difficult, had my companion been inclined to it; which he was not. He spoke only to his pony, and I was content with the silence, for it allowed me to retreat into the fortress of my own thoughts.

With every mile that passed, my sense of anticipation grew more acute. I felt as if a cord, stretched taut between my heart and Ferndean, was slowly being wound in, drawing me inexorably towards my destination. The air grew cooler, smelling of damp earth and pine. We were climbing into a higher, more remote region. Now and then, we would pass a lonely farmhouse or a shepherd’s cottage, smoke curling from its chimney, but for the most part, we were alone with the wind and the sky.

At last, near midday, the driver pulled the pony to a halt at a place where four rough tracks met. A broken stone cross, covered in moss and lichen, stood sentinel at the intersection.

“This is Izon cross, miss,” he said, pointing with his whip down a path that was little more than a green tunnel plunging into a dense wood. “You must take that way. Keep to the main track for nigh on three miles, and you will come to the house. You cannot miss it; there is naught else there.”

I descended from the cart, my legs stiff from the long, jolting ride. The air here was still and heavy, the silence broken only by the drip of water from the overarching branches. The woods before me looked dark and forbidding, a sylvan labyrinth. I gave the man his payment and a little more, and he touched his cap, turned his pony, and rumbled away, leaving me quite alone.

For a moment, I stood motionless, gathering my courage. The solitude was absolute. I was a pilgrim at the final stage of my journey, and this dark wood was the last barrier I must pass. I took a deep breath, adjusted the small bundle I carried, and stepped onto the path.

The track was soft and damp underfoot, a carpet of fallen leaves and moss. Great oaks and beeches formed a canopy high above, filtering the daylight to a dim, greenish twilight. The atmosphere was one of profound and ancient melancholy. This was a place that had slept, undisturbed, for centuries. It was a fitting approach to the house of a man who wished to shut out the world. My heart beat a slow, heavy rhythm, a drum marking my steady advance. I did not hurry; my pace was measured, deliberate. I noted the ferns uncurling in the damp shadows, the flash of a startled bird, the gnarled roots of the trees that coiled across the path like sleeping serpents. My senses were preternaturally sharp, yet my mind was fixed on one single point: him.

After what seemed an age of walking, the trees began to thin, and I saw before me a clearing. And in the centre of the clearing stood the house. Ferndean. It was no grand hall, but a moderately-sized manor-house, built of dark, sweating stone. It was two storeys high, with a steep, moss-covered roof and mullioned windows, many of them shuttered. The whole aspect of the place was one of neglect and deep seclusion. It was not so much a house as a tomb, a place where a man might come to bury himself alive. A low wall, in a state of partial ruin, enclosed a small, overgrown yard in front. The gate hung crookedly on a single hinge.

I stopped at the gate, my hand resting on the cold, damp stone of the pillar. This was it. Within those walls was the man who held my entire heart in his keeping. I felt a tremor run through me, a wave of such intense emotion—a mingling of love, pity, and a kind of sacred terror—that for a second my strength faltered. I leaned against the stone, closed my eyes, and offered up a silent prayer; not a petition for success, but for the fortitude to accept whatever was to come.

Then, my resolve returned, stronger than before. I pushed open the groaning gate and walked up the weed-choked path to the front door. It was a heavy oak door, studded with iron nails. There was no bell or knocker that I could see, only a heavy iron ring for a handle. I lifted it, and let it fall. The sound was unnervingly loud in the profound silence.

I waited. Nothing. No sound from within. The silence seemed to press in on me, thick and suffocating. I knocked again, more forcefully this time. I heard a shuffling sound from within, the drawing of a bolt, and the door creaked open a few inches. A woman’s face peered out. She was elderly, with a clean cap and a worried, suspicious expression. This must be Mary, John’s wife.

“What do you want?” she asked. Her voice was sharp with the defensiveness of one who guards a precious, irritable secret.

“I should like to see the master of the house,” I said, keeping my own voice calm and low.

“You cannot. He sees no one. He is unwell.” She began to close the door.

I put my hand upon it to prevent her. “Please,” I said, my tone gaining an urgency I could not suppress. “I have come a very long way. It is a matter of great importance. I must see him.”

“It’s no use,” she insisted, her face grim. “He has given strict orders. Strictest orders. It’s more than my place is worth to disturb him. Now, be on your way, miss.”

From the dark interior behind her, a voice suddenly erupted, a voice that struck my heart like a physical blow. It was his voice, yet horribly changed—hoarse, raw, and freighted with an intolerable bitterness.

“Mary! Who is it? I told you I will see no one! Send them to the devil and bolt the door!”

The shock of hearing him, the pain in his tone, broke through my composure. Without another thought, I pushed past the startled woman and stepped into the hall.

It was dark and smelled of woodsmoke, damp, and disuse. A fire smouldered in a large, unadorned fireplace, giving off more smoke than heat. The furniture was sparse and heavy. And there, in a great armchair by the hearth, sat a figure. His back was partly to me, and he was hunched forward, his head sunk on his chest. Even in that posture of defeat, the proud set of his shoulders was unmistakable. It was him.

Mary gave a little cry of distress. “Oh, miss, you shouldn’t have! He will be so angry.”

He had heard me enter. He lifted his head and turned it in my direction, a terrible, sightless searching.

“Who is that?” he demanded, the words a low growl. “Is that you, John? I am not to be disturbed. Who is it, I say?”

My breath caught in my throat. I saw his face now, illuminated by a stray beam of light from the open door. One side was cruelly scarred, the skin drawn and discoloured. But it was his eyes that undid me. They were open, but they were fixed, vacant, seeing nothing. My glorious, flashing, commanding master was blind. A wave of anguish so profound washed over me that I thought I would fall. But I did not. I stood my ground.

Mary was stammering, “It is a young woman, sir… she insisted…”

He made an impatient, angry gesture with a bandaged hand. “A woman? What woman? There are no women here. Tell her I have nothing for her. Tell her to go.” He turned his head away again, a gesture of final, utter dismissal.

I took a step forward, then another. The floorboards creaked. He started, his head snapping back towards the sound. His whole body was tense, like a wounded animal sensing a presence in its lair.

“Who is that?” he whispered, and the anger was gone, replaced by a strange, listening dread. “That step… I know that step. But it cannot be.”

I could not speak. My name was a knot in my throat. I took another step, my hands clasped before me as if in prayer. I stood now only a few feet from his chair. He did not move, but I saw a muscle twitch in his scarred cheek. He was listening, straining with every fibre of his being to interpret the evidence of his ears against the conviction of his reason.

“Wraiths and ghosts,” he muttered to himself, his voice rough with unshed tears. “The mind plays tricks when the body is broken. It is a dream. Only a dream.”

I found my voice at last. It was no more than a breath, a whisper that seemed to fill the vast, silent room.

“Mr. Rochester?” I said. “It is I.”

He flinched as if I had struck him. A violent shudder ran through his powerful frame. He did not turn.

“Her voice,” he breathed. “The very tone. A trick of the ear. An echo from the grave.”

“No echo,” I said, my voice gaining a little strength, a little of its old firmness. “No ghost. I am here.” I moved closer still, until I stood directly before him, my skirts almost brushing his knees. “It is Jane Eyre, sir. I have come back to you.”

He lifted his hand, the uninjured one, and held it out in the space between us, a gesture of agonised uncertainty. His fingers trembled.

“Jane Eyre?” he repeated, his voice cracking. “Jane is in a foreign land. She has forgotten me. This is madness.”

I could bear it no longer. I knelt on the floor before him, took that searching, trembling hand in both of mine, and held it fast. “She has not forgotten you,” I said, my tears now flowing freely, wetting the hand I held. “She has never forgotten you. I am not in a foreign land. I am here, Edward. I am home.”

The contact, the living warmth of my hands, the indisputable reality of my touch, was the proof his tormented mind required. A great, ragged sob was torn from his chest. He clutched my hand with a desperate strength, his fingers closing over mine like a vice.

“Jane,” he cried out, and it was a sound of such disbelief, such agony, and such dawning, incredulous joy that my own heart felt near to bursting. “It is you. It is you in the flesh. My Jane.” He drew my hands to his chest, pressing them against his wildly beating heart, and bowed his head over them, his whole body shaking with the force of his emotion. And in that moment, kneeling on the cold floor of that desolate house, with my master’s tears falling upon my hands, I knew that my journey was truly over.

Her mind, unmoored from the present terror of my reappearance, had drifted into the well-worn channels of her daily anxieties; her grievances against her son, her failing fortune, her pride warring with her necessity. She had forgotten I was there. It was a strange and pitiable spectacle: the mind of the tyrannical Mrs. Reed, once a fortress of cold, hard certainty, now crumbling into a ruin of disconnected complaints. I waited for a moment, in silence, as one waits for the ramblings of a fever-patient to subside. Her eyes, which had for a moment flashed with the old Gibson hardness, grew vague again, wandering about the shadowed ceiling.

“Aunt,” I said softly, to draw her back.

She started, and her gaze fixed on me once more, a glimmer of recollection, and with it, the old animosity, returning to their clouded depths. “You,” she said, her voice dropping to a hostile whisper. “You are there still. I feel you there. I was dreaming, a horrid dream, that you had come back.”

“It is no dream,” I answered, my brief surge of ire now wholly extinguished by a profound and sorrowful pity. “I am here. You sent for me, and I have come.”

“Did I send for you?” she murmured, a frown of effort creasing her brow. “I do not recall. Why should I send for you? John is the one who should be here; he is my son. Not you.”

“Perhaps you will remember in the morning,” I said gently. “You should rest now.” I made to smooth the bedclothes which she had disarranged in her restless turnings, but she snatched the quilt away from my touch.

“Do not handle me,” she hissed, with a flash of her former spirit. “I will not be handled by you. Go to your room. Bessie will have shown you a room?”

“Yes, aunt. I will not disturb you further to-night.”

I rose from my chair. My heart, which had entered the room with such a high-minded resolve for reconciliation, was now heavy with the confirmation of an old and bitter truth: that some hatreds are not accidents of circumstance, but part of the very fabric of a soul, and cannot be unpicked. I had offered a kiss, and been met with ice; I had offered my hand, and it had been withdrawn. To remain was to subject myself to a fresh round of the old, meaningless cruelties, and for what? My purpose in coming had been to answer her summons and, I had hoped, to make peace. The first was accomplished; the second, I now saw, was an impossibility. Her enmity was a part of her illness, perhaps the very core of it.

I turned to leave, my spirit wearied, my magnanimous intentions turned to dust. As my hand touched the door, her voice, sharp and commanding as I had ever known it, arrested me.

“Jane Eyre.”

I stopped, but did not turn. “Yes, Mrs. Reed?”

“Come back. Do not leave. There is something… it was what I had to tell you.”

I returned to the bedside. The shaded lamplight cast deep hollows in her face, making a skull of her proud features. She seemed to be wrestling with some internal obstacle, her breath coming in short, difficult pants.

“Closer,” she commanded. I bent my head nearer to the pillows.

“Three years ago,” she began, her voice a low, conspiratorial rasp, “I received a letter. It was addressed to you.”

I felt a tremor of surprise. A letter for me, sent here to Gateshead? After my public disgrace and departure, who would imagine I was to be found under Mrs. Reed’s roof?

“It was from your uncle,” she continued, her eyes burning into mine with a feverish intensity. “Your father’s brother. A Mr. John Eyre of Madeira.”

John Eyre! The name struck a chord deep within me. It was a name I had heard but once, when, as a small and inquisitive child, I had pressed Bessie with questions about my parentage, questions which she was ill-equipped and forbidden to answer. He was a merchant, she had thought, in some far-off, warm country. I had thought him dead, or as good as dead to me.

“My uncle?” I breathed, my heart beginning a slow, heavy beat of suspense. “Is he in England?”

“He is dead,” she said, with a certain grim satisfaction. “He wrote to me because he had learned, through some channel of business, that his brother had left a penniless orphan. He had no family of his own; he was a bachelor, and his health was failing. He wished to adopt you, and to make you his heir.”

The room seemed to sway around me. A sensation of such profound and dizzying import rushed over me that I was forced to grip the bedpost for support. Adopt me. His heir. The words were simple, yet they contained a world; a world of security, of belonging, of independence, a world suddenly revealed and just as suddenly snatched away. A life of dreary servitude, of struggle and heartache, might have been exchanged for one of ease and affection. All the pain of Lowood, the years of earning my bread amongst strangers, the very predicament which had driven me from Thornfield—all might have been averted.

“He wrote to you,” I said, my voice hoarse, trying to piece together the sequence of this monstrous revelation. “He wrote to you, asking for me?”

“He did,” she confirmed, her gaze unwavering. “He asked for my character of you, and for your address.”

“And you gave it?” The question was a fool’s hope, and I knew it. I knew the answer by the look on her face, by the memory of a thousand calculated cruelties.

“I answered him,” she said, and a faint, ghastly smile touched her lips. “I told him what I believed to be the truth. I told him you were no longer among the living.”

“You told him I was dead?”

“I wrote that a fever had swept through the Lowood institution some years since, and that you had been amongst the children carried off by the infection. I had a right. You had tried to disgrace me. You had once risen up and cursed me in this very house.”

“And so for that,” I said, the blood roaring in my ears, “for a child’s fit of passion, you robbed me of a family, of a fortune, of a future? You condemned me to a life of poverty and toil, when I might have had a kinsman’s love and protection?”

My voice, which had begun in a whisper of disbelief, rose with each question. The old Jane, the rebellious, passionate child she had so feared and hated, was alive and well; I felt her surge within me, righteous and furious. But then I looked at the woman in the bed. I saw not the powerful tyrant of my youth, but a wasted, dying creature, tormented by her own venom, her crime now rising up to haunt her at the last. The fire of my indignation sank as swiftly as it had risen, quenched by a cold wave of understanding. This was her final confession; this was the burden she could no longer bear alone. Vengeance was a fool’s errand when death was already at the door.

“Why do you tell me this now?” I asked, my voice quiet again, weary and flat.

“Because it has weighed on me,” she muttered, turning her face to the pillow. “Especially since John began to waste his money. The thought of that fortune… your fortune… it tormented me. And I believe in signs. Your coming back, just now, when I have been thinking of it… it is a sign. I must clear my conscience.” She fumbled under her pillow. “Here. The letter. It came with his. I kept it. I do not know why.”

She drew out a folded, yellowed paper, and pushed it towards me. It was the original letter from John Eyre, addressed to his niece, Jane Eyre, at Gateshead Hall. My hands trembled as I took it.

“Now,” she said, her voice faint with exhaustion. “I have told you. You have the letter. Do with it what you will. Only leave me. I wish to sleep.”

Without another word, she closed her eyes. Her breathing settled into the shallow rhythm of a troubled slumber. I stood for a long while, the letter clutched in my hand, a testament to a life I had never lived. The great four-post bed, the amber hangings, the whole oppressive room seemed to hold its breath. I looked at the stern, sleeping face of my aunt, and I felt nothing. Not hatred, not forgiveness, only a vast and empty stillness. The account between us was settled at last; not by reconciliation, but by a final, irrevocable act of injury, confessed too late.

I quietly left the room, closing the door on the past. I went to my own chamber, and there, by the light of a single candle, I sat down to read my dead uncle’s words. The ink was faded, the handwriting that of a man of business, clear and without flourish. He wrote of his solitude, of his desire to right the wrong he felt had been done to his brother’s child. He offered me a home in the warm sunshine of Madeira, and at his death, all that he possessed. He asked only for a daughter’s affection in his declining years.

Tears, which I had refused to shed in Mrs. Reed’s presence, fell now upon the page, blurring the kind words of a man I should never know. I wept not for the lost fortune, for my wants were few and my nature was not avaricious. I wept for the lost possibility of love; for the kinsman who had sought me, for the father-figure I had always craved. St. John Rivers had offered me a name and a duty; this man, this stranger, had offered me a home and a heart. And Mrs. Reed, out of pure, unadulterated spite, had barred the door.

I did not sleep that night. I sat by the window, watching the moon traverse the sky, my uncle’s letter in my lap. A strange sense of liberation began to dawn in me. The truth, however painful, had set me free. I was not merely Jane Eyre, the governess, the penniless orphan. I was Jane Eyre, the intended heiress, the niece of a gentleman of substance. It changed nothing of my present circumstances, yet it changed everything in my perception of myself. I was not a creature of charity, a dependent tolerated by the grudging goodwill of others. I had a right to my own existence, a claim upon the world which had been denied but could not be erased.

I stayed at Gateshead for a fortnight. Mrs. Reed lingered on, mostly lost in a twilight state between delirium and sleep. She rarely spoke to me again, and when she did, it was with the confused peevishness of one who no longer distinguished between dream and reality. Eliza maintained her rigid, monastic routine, counting her money and planning her future convent life. Georgiana fretted over her complexion, bemoaned the lack of society, and tried, with a singular lack of success, to engage me in gossip about the local gentry. They were like figures in a play whose lines I already knew by heart. Their power over me was utterly broken. I moved amongst them, a quiet and self-possessed observer, attending to my aunt’s needs when I could, but my spirit was already far away.

When the end came for Mrs. Reed, it came in the dead of night. She passed away in her sleep, without another word or sign. I stood with my cousins beside her deathbed, and felt a solemn peace. My business at Gateshead was concluded.

After the funeral, a brief and chilly affair, I prepared for my own departure. Georgiana was to go to London, to an uncle of the Gibson family; Eliza had her arrangements made with a convent in France. The house was to be shut up. As I packed my small trunk, I felt a sense of finality. This chapter of my life, the chapter of childhood wrongs and youthful resentments, was now closed for ever.

I took my leave of Bessie with genuine affection, pressing into her hand a portion of my small savings.

“And where will you go now, Miss Jane?” she asked, her kind face full of concern.

“I am going to a new situation,” I said, the words feeling truer than she could possibly know. “To a friend.”

The journey back to the vicinity of Thornfield was one of sober reflection. I had left a poor, heartsore fugitive; I returned a woman of independent mind, with a secret knowledge of her own worth. I did not yet know what I should do, or what my reception would be. I knew only that I must see him again. The tidings I had received from the innkeeper, that Thornfield was a blackened ruin and its master a grievously injured recluse, had filled me with a terror that spurred me onward. Love, which had once been a source of sweet and secret joy, was now a fierce and urgent duty. My place was with him, whatever his state. If he was blind, I would be his eyes; if he was broken, I would be his strength.

The coach left me at the familiar crossroads. This time, I did not hesitate. I walked the two miles to the estate, my heart pounding a rhythm of dread and determination. The October air was crisp, and the leaves underfoot were a carpet of red and gold, but I saw nothing of the beauty around me. My eyes were fixed on the horizon, searching for the first glimpse of the great house. And then I saw it; or rather, what was left of it. A hollow shell. A gaunt, blackened skeleton against the grey sky. The sight smote me with a physical force; I stopped in the lane, my hand flying to my mouth. It was real. The rumour, the nightmare, was true.

I crept closer, like a ghost haunting the scene of its former life. The silence was profound, broken only by the cawing of rooks who had taken possession of the shattered battlements. The front of the house was a gaping ruin, the roof gone, the windows empty sockets. My portion of the building, the wing where my own dear little room had been, was utterly destroyed. It was there that the fire had been fiercest. And in my mind’s eye, I saw it all: the flames, the chaos, the madwoman on the roof, the fall. My master’s fall.

I could not bear to linger. I turned away from the ruin and enquired of a passing farm-worker the way to Ferndean Manor. It was, he told me, a remote and gloomy place, some miles distant, where Mr. Rochester now lived in seclusion with two old servants. The description did not deter me; it only strengthened my resolve. It was there I must go.

The walk was long, through tangled woods and over damp, un-drained fields. As evening drew in, I came upon the house, sequestered deep in a hollow, surrounded by a dark rampart of trees. It was as dreary a place as could be imagined; a squat, two-storey building of dark stone, damp and moss-covered, with a melancholy, forsaken air. A single light glimmered in one of the lower windows. For a moment, my courage faltered. What welcome could I expect? A man so afflicted, so sunk in despair, would he wish to be disturbed by a ghost from a happier past? Perhaps my coming would only inflict a fresh wound.

But the thought of him, alone in that desolate place, overmastered all my doubts. I must see him. I must know. I approached the house, my steps muffled by the overgrown lawn. Through the window, I saw a room, sparely furnished, lit by a single candle and the glow of a hearth. And I saw him. He sat in a high-backed chair, turned away from the window, his great dog, Pilot, lying at his feet. His posture was one of utter dejection, his head sunk upon his chest. He was not the proud, imperious master of Thornfield; he was a fallen eagle, chained to his rock. And in that instant, all my fear dissolved, replaced by a love so fierce and protective it felt like a pain in my own chest.

I went to the door and knocked. There was a long silence, then the sound of a bolt being drawn, and an old woman, whom I took to be the housekeeper, peered out.

“What do you want?” she asked, her tone suspicious.

“I should like to see Mr. Rochester,” I said.

“He sees no one,” was the curt reply.

“Please,” I said, “tell him it is a person on urgent business. Tell him… tell him it is Jane.”

She looked me up and down, but something in my face must have persuaded her. She left me standing on the damp step and shuffled back inside. I heard her voice, and then his; a deep, rough sound that sent a shock through my every nerve. It was his voice, but altered, roughened by pain and disuse. After a moment, the old woman returned.

“He will see you,” she said, without ceremony, and led me into the house. She gestured towards a door at the end of a dark passage. “In there.”

I pushed open the door and entered the room I had observed from without. The air was thick with the scent of woodsmoke and old sorrows. He had not moved from his chair by the fire. He was still turned away from me, a dark, hulking shape of misery.

“I was told a visitor had come,” he said to the fire, his voice hollow. “I know of no one who would seek me out in this place. State your business and be gone.”

His back was to me, but I could see the proud set of his head, the broad shoulders I knew so well. He was thinner, and his clothes, once so impeccably tailored, hung loosely on his frame. My heart contracted. I took a step into the room, and the floorboard creaked under my foot. He stiffened, his head lifting in a gesture of sharp attention, like a wounded animal sensing a presence in its lair. “Who is that?” he whispered, and the anger was gone, replaced by a strange, listening dread. “That step… I know that step. But it cannot be.”

I could not speak. My name was a knot in my throat. I took another step, my hands clasped before me as if in prayer. I stood now only a few feet from his chair. He did not move, but I saw a muscle twitch in his scarred cheek. He was listening, straining with every fibre of his being to interpret the evidence of his ears against the conviction of his reason.

“Wraiths and ghosts,” he muttered to himself, his voice rough with unshed tears. “The mind plays tricks when the body is broken. It is a dream. Only a dream.”

I found my voice at last. It was no more than a breath, a whisper that seemed to fill the vast, silent room.

“Mr. Rochester?” I said. “It is I.”

He flinched as if I had struck him. A violent shudder ran through his powerful frame. He did not turn.

“Her voice,” he breathed. “The very tone. A trick of the ear. An echo from the grave.”

“No echo,” I said, my voice gaining a little strength, a little of its old firmness. “No ghost. I am here.” I moved closer still, until I stood directly before him, my skirts almost brushing his knees. “It is Jane Eyre, sir. I have come back to you.”

He lifted his hand, the uninjured one, and held it out in the space between us, a gesture of agonised uncertainty. His fingers trembled.

“Jane Eyre?” he repeated, his voice cracking. “Jane is in a foreign land. She has forgotten me. This is madness.”

I could bear it no longer. I knelt on the floor before him, took that searching, trembling hand in both of mine, and held it fast. “She has not forgotten you,” I said, my tears now flowing freely, wetting the hand I held. “She has never forgotten you. I am not in a foreign land. I am here, Edward. I am home.”

The contact, the living warmth of my hands, the indisputable reality of my touch, was the proof his tormented mind required. A great, ragged sob was torn from his chest. He clutched my hand with a desperate strength, his fingers closing over mine like a vice. “Jane,” he cried out, and it was a sound of such disbelief, such agony, and such dawning, incredulous joy that my own heart felt near to bursting. “It is you. It is you in the flesh. My Jane.” He drew my hands to his chest, pressing them against his wildly beating heart, and bowed his head over them, his whole body shaking with the force of his emotion. And in that moment, kneeling on the cold floor of that desolate house, with my master’s tears falling upon my hands, I knew that my journey was truly over.

The days that followed, leading up to the funeral, were a strange and hollow time. A funereal quiet descended upon the house, broken only by the hushed movements of the servants and the occasional histrionic sigh from Georgiana, who had taken to reclining on various pieces of furniture in attitudes of picturesque grief. She was not mourning her mother; she was mourning the inconvenience of a death in the house, which precluded any possibility of fashionable callers or exciting diversions. Eliza, true to her nature, retreated into a regimen of rigorous devotion and account-keeping, her grief, if she felt any, locked away behind a ledger of piety and profit.

I, for my part, found myself in a peculiar position of authority. The servants, led by a proud and tearful Bessie, began to look to me for direction. It was I who conferred with the undertaker, I who chose the plain oak coffin, I who decided that the funeral should be a simple, private affair, with no ostentatious display. My cousins offered no objection; Georgiana was too absorbed in her own boredom, and Eliza seemed to approve of the economy of my arrangements. It was as if, in death, Mrs. Reed’s iron grip on the household had slackened and fallen, and the reins had passed, unbidden, into my hands.

During those days, I spent many hours in the library, a room I had been forbidden to enter as a child after the incident with the book. Now I sat in the very armchair from which John Reed had ordered me, surrounded by the scent of old paper and leather, and I read. I did not read for escape, as I once had; I read for understanding. I read histories and accounts of the world, of places I might now see, of lives I might now lead. The world, which had always seemed a vast and locked fortress, now appeared to me as a map, spread out and waiting, and I had but to choose my path.

My thoughts, however, were not always so clear and forward-looking. In the quiet of the night, when the old house groaned around me, my mind would inevitably turn to him: to Mr. Rochester. What would he think of this transformation? I, who had fled him a penniless, heartbroken fugitive, could now return to him a woman of fortune. I was his equal in station, or near enough. The great impediment of wealth and status which had stood between us like a mountain had been levelled to a plain. Could I now go to him? Could I stand before him and say, “I am independent, I am rich: will you have me now?”

A hot flush of shame would creep up my neck at the thought. No; that was not the way. My love for him had never been a matter of money, and to use my newfound wealth as a lure felt cheap and degrading. It was not his pride I wished to appease, but my own. I had to be certain that if I returned, I did so not merely as an heiress seeking a husband, but as Jane Eyre, complete in myself, needing nothing from him but the love I knew he bore me, a love that must be offered freely, without the shadow of obligation or dependence. I had left him to preserve my soul; I could not return until I was sure that same soul would remain intact, whole and self-possessed, in his presence. The question of Mr. Rochester, I decided, must wait. First, I had to settle the affairs of my past.

The day of the funeral was grey and damp, a fitting final curtain for a life lived without sunshine. I stood by the graveside between my two cousins, a figure in black, my face veiled. Georgiana wept decorously into a lace-trimmed handkerchief; Eliza stood as rigid as a statue, her face a mask of stern propriety. I shed no tears. My heart was a still, quiet pool, reflecting the sombre sky above. I felt a profound pity for the woman being lowered into the cold earth, a woman who had lived a life of such bitter resentment, who had nursed her hatreds like precious jewels until they had consumed her from within. I offered up a silent prayer, not for her soul’s rest, but for my own: a prayer of thanks for my deliverance.

When we returned to Gateshead, a carriage was waiting in the drive. A gentleman, stout and wearing spectacles, descended as we approached.

“However,” I continued, “you will not live here. I have no desire for this house. Its memories are not pleasant. But I will not see it sold to strangers. Bessie and her husband, Robert Leaven, shall live here as caretakers. This house was a prison to me; for them, it will be a home and a livelihood. As for you, Georgiana, I will not take you as a companion. We should not suit. But I will not see you cast out. I will settle a portion of my inheritance on you, an amount sufficient to grant you a comfortable independence. What you do with it will be your own affair. You may find your rich husband, or you may squander it on trinkets. The choice, for the first time, will be entirely yours.”

Georgiana stared, her mouth agape, a torrent of conflicting emotions—disbelief, gratitude, a hint of disappointment that she was not to have direct access to the entire fortune—playing across her face.

Eliza was the first to speak. “That is… a surprisingly sensible arrangement,” she said, a note of grudging respect in her voice. “A business transaction. I accept.”

“And I,” I said, turning my gaze to the window, to the grey, brooding sky beyond, “am leaving. I shall leave tomorrow.”

“But where will you go?” Georgiana asked, her voice now small and awed.

I thought of the road. I thought of the wide world. And I thought of a dark, brooding house in a distant part of England, a house that held the other half of my heart. But the time was not yet right. There was one more thing I had to do first. I had to find my own place, my own home, a place that was not Thornfield, not Gateshead, not Lowood, but a place of my very own. A place from which I could, in my own time and on my own terms, choose my future.

“I do not know yet,” I answered, and the words were the sweetest I had ever spoken. “I am going to find out.” I was no longer Jane Eyre, the orphan; I was Jane Eyre, the explorer. A new chapter was beginning, and I was its sole and sovereign author. The world was quiet, and I was listening for its call.
