A steward from the Vulture was hauled aboard and instantly the deckhands swarmed him. When his terrified confession was heard, a group of them charged toward the captain’s quarters. They found him in conference with the first mate.
“Hold your positions, men!” the captain roared at their approach.
“But sir, it’s him,, ”
“Silence! What is it!”
“Gold!”
“Aye, a sovereign for every man who sees him safe to port.”
“That changes things. He’s as safe as a babe in arms, Captain.”
“You’re right he is! Inform the rest of the crew.”
The men hurried off to spread the news. In a few moments every soul on the Vulture knew of the bargain, and the steward who had been bribed felt a profound sense of doom. The situation had developed exactly as he feared, but he was powerless to act against the will of the entire crew.
The villain, a scoundrel named Elias Thorne, was jubilant. He kept himself carefully hidden in his stateroom, well away from the deck. Professor Artemis Kane grew restless when it became clear the scoundrel was not being produced.
“Ahoy the Vulture!” he bellowed through his speaking trumpet.
“What is it you want?” came the captain’s gruff reply.
“The man we have pursued!”
“You shall not have him!”
“What! You conspire with that thief?”
“We do! And we will defend him to the last man!”
This reply was a shock to Professor Kane, for it told him plainly that Thorne had bought the loyalty of the ship and her company.
“We will send your vessel to the bottom of the sea with our hydro-torpedoes!” he shouted, his voice thick with anger. “You will regret this foolishness, captain.”
“Nonsense! We’ll not be threatened by a tin-pot fish!”
Kane withdrew from the conning tower rail and went for the main cabin. He had scarcely crossed the threshold, however, when a great shudder ran through the ship. The next moment the electric turbines whined to a halt and the submersible, the Nautilus II, began to list heavily to starboard. It was clear that some dire misfortune had occurred.

CHAPTER VII. THE PHANTOM OF THE DEEP.

By the time the Nautilus II settled into the waves of the Celebes Sea, the turbines were silent and the interior lights had failed. The great shudder Kane felt clearly indicated a catastrophic failure in the engine room. He seized a lantern and made his way below. The pride of his invention floated inertly, but the Vulture steamed away from her with great haste. The steamer was making nearly twenty knots, and her crew let out a cheer of derision when they saw the plight of the submersible. Now they were sure they could outrun their pursuer, and deliver Elias Thorne to Singapore without further trouble. That was a simple way to earn the gold he promised them for his protection. In a short time, the steamer was but a smudge on the horizon.
Kane shone his lantern about the engine room and soon discovered that the primary drive shaft had sheared in two. Its broken end had torn a hole in the ballast tank. The full power of the Nautilus II was nullified. She could not dive or run until the shaft was mended, and as Kane saw that it would take many hours to complete the work he knew the escape of the steamer was assured.
It was then ten o’clock in the morning. His young ward, Jack, came rushing in.
“Great Scott! what a terrible mishap!” he cried.
“Thorne will escape us for certain,” said Kane, despondently.
“Never have I seen a fellow with such infernal luck.”
“It cannot be helped. We must set to work immediately and repair the damage. The sooner we fix the shaft the sooner we can give chase.”
Jack and the chief engineer, a stout man named Briggs, were summoned. They worked like Trojans all day and into the night, and it was well past midnight before they had the shaft welded and the ballast tank patched and sealed.
Jack prepared a late supper, after which they took turns at the helm. Professor Kane took the first watch. He submerged the Nautilus II. The new weld on the drive shaft groaned under the strain, but it held fast and soon the turbines were humming properly once more.
Keeping a constant scan with the hydroscope, the professor guided the vessel toward the Strait of Malacca. Briggs relieved him at the controls. The Nautilus II was now bound for the Andaman Sea. Day broke over the ocean. Nothing had been seen of the Vulture yet. Kane made a new calculation after breakfast, and turning to his engineer he said:
“We should be closing on the steamer’s position by now.”
“You think so, sir?”
“Based on her speed and the course she was on, she must be somewhere in these waters, west of the Nicobar Islands.”
“Knowing we are after him, Thorne will surely have them take precautions.”
“Precisely what I am counting on.”
“Then we could sail right past her.”
“That is my fear.”
“To lose him now would be a disaster.”
“Thorne might make for land. If so, we may never find him. I am proceeding at a reduced speed.”
“Why are you keeping the Nautilus II so near the surface?”
“To avoid missing the steamer’s engine noise if she is nearby.”
Kane rose and put his eye to the hydroscope. It was a calm, sunlit day, and the waters were exceptionally clear. Briggs and Jack were at their stations. In the distance rose a small, uncharted island. Tucked behind a rocky outcrop Kane suddenly saw a trail of dark smoke and riveting his attention upon it he shouted:
“Briggs, stop the engines!”
“Aye, sor,” replied the engineer, obeying.
“I see smoke without a vessel.”
“Faith, an’ that’s a strange thing indeed.”
“Look, close to that island, there.”
A brief silence ensued. Then the engineer saw the smudge against the sky. He angled the submersible toward it and as they drew nearer he suddenly switched on the forward sonar pulse. A cry of triumph now escaped Kane, for he recognized the engine signature of the steamer to be the Vulture.
“That is her!” he cried.
“Shure, they’re hidin’ behind the rocks.”
“Are they not launching a longboat, Briggs?”
“They are that, an’ makin’ for the island. By the saints, there’s a man in that boat wearin’ a fine suit, who I’d swear on my life must be Elias Thorne!”
“Can he be fleeing ashore to hide in those caves? It appears so.”
The longboat reached the beach. Grating on the volcanic sand, she was pulled ashore. The man alluded to leaped out and scrambled up the beach, while the sailors prepared to push their boat off again. While they rowed back to the steamer, the man on the shore stood in full view, shaking his fist at the sea. The sonar pulse was pinged directly at him. He was thus made to know that he was discovered. Whirling about, he bolted for a great cave in the cliff face. Kane observed his every action.
“There he goes!” he cried.
“Begorra, that must be Thorne!” said Briggs. “After him!”
On sped the submersible toward the island. The longboat returned to the Vulture. When the Nautilus II passed the rocky outcrop, a deck gun on the steamer suddenly fired at her. It had been loaded with scrap iron and chain, and the wicked shot clanged against her hull. She was too well built to suffer damage from such a volley, however, and continued on her course unmolested.
“I wonder if the man on shore wasn’t a ruse to draw us out for an ambush?” muttered Kane.
“Professor Kane!” yelled Briggs, “will ye have us answer their salute, or shall we let them be?”
“Fire a sonic charge at the scoundrels!” replied the inventor.
The engineer and the boy obeyed the order by aiming the forward emitter at the sailors. A high pitched tone filled the air, and several shouts of pain were heard as the men clapped their hands to their ears.
Passing on, the Nautilus II left the steamer behind and reached the land where the fugitive had vanished. He had gone into a dark cave. The mouth of the cavern was wide, and the island seemed to be made of porous volcanic rock. If the fugitive were to attempt to leave by another exit, the men in the submersible might never know. Kane saw that it would be a fool’s errand to hunt the man in the dark, so he said to his companions:
“I am going to keep the Nautilus II positioned here before the cave until he is forced out. In the meantime, if you will watch the shore he cannot escape without being seen.”
The others agreed to this plan. At that moment the submersible floated just fifty feet from the shore. She was kept there. A watch was set. Nothing happened for several hours to disturb our friends, and as the sun began to set they grew weary. As Kane scanned the dark maw of the cave with his glasses he heard a strange, low scraping, like a rusty chain being dragged over stone, from deep within the island. It is with this grating sound that the giant cave crab announces its territory, and in the deep dark it scrapes its massive claws to sharpen them for the hunt. When it attacks, its charge is a silent, terrifying scuttling, and a single snap of its pincer can sever a steel cable which

was meant to hold a battleship at its moorings.

Kane heard the sound and held up a hand for silence. He tilted his head, his brow furrowed in concentration. The grating noise echoed from the dark maw of the cave, a chilling sound that spoke of immense size and stony indifference.

“Saints preserve us!” whispered Briggs. “What in creation makes a noise like that? It sounds as if the island itself is groaning in its sleep!”

“That is no groan, Briggs,” replied Kane, his voice low and serious. “That is a warning.” He retrieved his glasses from his pocket and polished them with a cloth, a habitual motion when he was deep in thought. “If my suppositions are correct, we are hearing the giant cave crab, a creature of legend among the native islanders of this region. It was thought to be extinct.”

“A crab!” cried Ned, the young cabin boy who had accompanied them. “But that sound! It is so large!”

“Indeed, boy,” said Kane. “Reports claim it can grow to the size of a small carriage, with claws that can snap a man in two as easily as a dry twig. It is a formidable predator.”

A sudden realization dawned on Ned’s face. “But Professor! Mr. Thorne! He is in that cave!”

Briggs snorted with contempt. “And a good riddance to him, I say! Let the beast have its dinner! It will save the hangman a job!”

“No, Briggs,” said the inventor firmly. “We cannot allow that. Our quarry is a scoundrel, it is true, but he is a man nonetheless. We came here to see him face the law, not to leave him to a fate too horrible to contemplate. We are going in.”

“Into the cave, sor?” the Irishman asked, his eyes wide. “Into the lair of that monster?”

“Precisely,” Kane affirmed. “Thorne is our responsibility. We will need the portable searchlight and the two electric lances. Ned, be quick about it. Briggs, prepare the inflatable skiff. There is no time to lose.”

The men hurried to obey. The Nautilus II was a marvel of modern engineering, equipped for any contingency. Within minutes, a small but sturdy rubber boat was inflated on the deck. Ned returned from the equipment locker, struggling under the weight of a heavy lamp and two long poles tipped with brass points, with insulated wires coiling from their handles.

Kane surveyed the equipment. “Excellent. We will power the light and the lances from the submersible’s main battery. Unspool a hundred yards of cable. That should give us sufficient range.”

Soon, all was in readiness. Kane, Briggs, and Ned lowered themselves into the skiff. A long, rubber coated cable connected them to the mighty dynamo of the Nautilus II, their only link to safety. With a few powerful strokes of the oars from Briggs, the skiff slid through the calm water and grated upon the black sand of the beach.

The mouth of the cave loomed before them like the entrance to the underworld. A foul, briny air wafted out, mingled with a dry, dusty scent of ancient stone and something else, a faint, musky odor of a predator’s den.

“Stay close,” Kane ordered in a whisper. “Briggs, you take the lead with the searchlight. Ned, you and I will handle the lances. Keep your wits about you.”

“Aye, aye, sor,” Briggs replied, his earlier bravado replaced by a grim determination. He hefted the powerful searchlight and pressed the switch. A brilliant cone of white light sliced through the oppressive darkness, illuminating a cavern of immense proportions.

The ceiling, lost in shadow far above, wept salty tears that had formed great pillars of stone, reaching from floor to roof like the columns of a grotesque cathedral. The floor was uneven, littered with rocks and shallow pools of brackeish water.

“Thorne!” Kane called out, his voice echoing strangely in the vast space. “Martin Thorne! Show yourself! We are here to help you!”

Only the dripping of water and the faint hum of the searchlight answered him.

“He must have gone deeper in,” said Ned, his voice trembling slightly.

“The fool,” muttered Briggs. “Running from the devil into the deep blue sea, or in this case, a cave full of crabs.”

They advanced cautiously, the electric cable trailing behind them like a long, black serpent. The searchlight’s beam danced from wall to wall, revealing glistening rock faces and dark crevices that seemed to watch them with unseen eyes. Suddenly, Briggs stopped.

“Professor, look!” he said, pointing the beam at the ground.

There, caught on a sharp outcropping of rock, was a shred of fine wool cloth, torn from a gentleman’s coat. A few feet beyond it lay a discarded leather glove.

“He came this way,” Kane confirmed. “And in a great hurry, it seems.”

At that moment, the grating, scraping sound returned, louder this time, and seemingly just ahead of them, around a bend in the cavern. It was accompanied by a series of sharp clicks, like a thousand castanets playing a song of death.

The three adventurers froze. Briggs tightened his grip on the searchlight, his knuckles white. Ned held his electric lance like a knight of old, his young face pale but set with resolve.

“Steady, men,” Kane said, his voice a calm anchor in the sea of their fear. “Whatever happens, we stand together.”

They rounded the bend slowly. The searchlight revealed a smaller, secondary chamber, a cul-de-sac in the larger cavern. And there, pressed against the far wall, was the man they sought.

Martin Thorne was a picture of abject terror. His expensive suit was torn and covered in grime, his face was ashen, and his eyes were wide with a madness born of fear. He saw the light and the men, and a choked sob escaped his lips.

“Help me!” he shrieked, his voice cracking. “For the love of God, help me! The beast! It cornered me here!”

Before Kane could reply, a shadow moved at the edge of the light. A vast, dark shape detached itself from the cavern wall, where it had been camouflaged perfectly. It was the crab.

It was larger than any of them had imagined. Its body was as wide as a farmer’s wagon, a great, flat carapace of mottled grey and black stone. Its legs, thick as a man’s thigh, were jointed and spidery, carrying its immense bulk with a horrifying, silent speed. But it was the claws that drew the eye and chilled the blood. The right claw was a massive, crushing pincer, thick and gnarled like an ancient tree trunk. The left was smaller, sharper, a serrated blade designed for tearing and shearing. Two long, segmented stalks rose from the front of its shell, at the ends of which swiveled two eyes, black and shining like polished obsidian, devoid of any emotion save a cold, ancient hunger.

The creature clicked its mandibles and took a step forward, the sound of its chitinous feet on the stone floor like the tapping of a blind man’s cane.

“Be ready!” Kane commanded.

The giant crab scuttled sideways, its stalked eyes tracking their every move. Thorne, seeing a momentary gap, made a foolish, desperate dash for the entrance to the chamber, hoping to use his pursuers as a shield.

“The coward!” roared Briggs. He was a moment too late to grab him.

Thorne’s flight seemed to trigger the creature. With a scuttling rush that belied its enormous size, the crab moved to intercept him, its great crushing claw raised.

“Thorne, get back!” Kane yelled.

But the villain was beyond reason. He saw only escape. He did not see the shallow pool of water that lay directly in his path. He slipped on the wet stone, fell with a cry, and landed hard.

The crab was upon him in an instant. It loomed over the fallen man, its shadow covering him completely.

“We can’t let it take him!” Ned cried.

“Fire!” ordered Kane.

He and Ned jabbed their electric lances forward. The brass tips struck the crab’s armored leg. There was a loud crackle and a flash of blue light. The air filled with the smell of ozone. The beast let out a strange, hissing sound, a noise like steam escaping a broken boiler, and took a stumbling step backward, shaking its leg as if in irritation. The attack had hurt it, but not enough.

It turned its attention from the prone form of Thorne to the new source of its annoyance. Its black eyes fixed upon Kane and his companions.

“It’s coming for us!” Briggs shouted, trying to keep the heavy searchlight beam focused on the monster’s eyes. The brilliant light seemed to bother it, causing it to lower its head and wave its claws defensively.

“The shell is too thick!” Kane analyzed aloud, his mind working furiously even in the face of mortal danger. “The electricity cannot penetrate it effectively! We need to find a weak spot!”

The crab charged. It was not a fast run, but an inexorable, terrifying advance. Briggs stumbled backward, tripping over the power cable. The searchlight fell from his hands, its beam now pointing uselessly at the cavern ceiling, plunging them into a world of terrifying, dancing shadows cast by the single bulb.

“Briggs!” cried Ned.

The Irishman scrambled to his feet, cursing. “I’m all right, lad!”

Kane saw his chance. The crab, momentarily confused by the shifting light, had paused. “Its underside! And its eyes! Those are the only vulnerabilities! We must force it to rear up!”

He lunged forward, not at the creature itself, but toward Martin Thorne, who was just now scrambling to his feet. Kane grabbed the villain by the collar.

“You will do as I say, or we all perish here!”

Thorne’s face was a mask of pure terror. He struggled in Kane’s iron grip, his eyes wide with a madness born of fear.

“Let go of me, Kane!” he shrieked, his voice thin and reedy in the vast cavern. “You’re insane! It will tear us to ribbons!”

“Silence, you cur!” Kane retorted, his voice echoing with command. “Your cowardice has endangered us long enough. Now, for once, you will make yourself useful!”

The monstrous crab, its stalked eyes swiveling from one man to the other, clicked its great mandibles together. The sound was like the snapping of dry bones, a hungry noise that sent a chill down even the bravest man’s spine.

“What’s the play, skipper?” yelled Briggs, who had managed to right the heavy searchlight and was now struggling to keep its brilliant beam trained on the creature’s face. “Me arms are gettin’ weary holdin’ this sunbeam!”

“When I give the word, you will blind it!” Kane called back, not taking his eyes off the monster. “Ned! Have your lance at the ready! We may only get one chance at this!”

“Aye, aye, sir!” the boy replied, his knuckles white around the grip of the electric weapon. His heart pounded like a drum against his ribs, but he held his ground.

Kane gave the trembling Thorne a violent shake. “You will run directly at the beast! Do you understand me? Straight at it!”

“No! I won’t do it! I won’t!” Thorne sobbed, all pretense of bravado gone, replaced by pathetic, groveling desperation.

“You will,” Kane said, his voice dropping to a deadly quiet that was more terrifying than any shout, “or I will throw you to it myself. The choice is yours, but make it quickly.”

For a moment, Thorne’s terrified eyes darted from Kane’s resolute face to the horrifying creature and back again. He saw no mercy in either direction, but with Kane, there was at least a chance of survival. With a gulp that was mostly a sob, he nodded his head jerkily.

“Good,” said Kane, releasing his collar. “Now, Briggs! On my mark!”

Kane took a step back, pulling Ned with him. “Ready!”

The crab took a hesitant, scuttling step forward.

“Now, Briggs! NOW!”

Briggs, with a mighty heave, jammed the searchlight’s full, concentrated beam directly into the creature’s stalked eyes. A strange, high pitched hissing noise escaped the monster. It reared back, waving its giant claws in confusion and agony, trying to shield its sensitive optics from the sudden, searing glare. Its entire front, the softer, paler shell of its underside, was lifted high in the air.

“That’s it, lad! Go!” Kane yelled to Thorne.

For a fatal second, Thorne froze. Then, with a scream of pure animal fear, he turned not toward the monster, but toward the cavern entrance.

“The coward!” roared Briggs. “He’s makin’ a run for it!”

“Let him go!” Kane shouted, seeing his true opportunity. “Ned, with me! Charge!”

The young leader and his faithful companion did not hesitate. They leaped forward, their electric lances held like jousting poles. The crab was still standing on its rearmost legs, a great, exposed target.

With a final, desperate push, the two men plunged the brass tips of their weapons deep into the creature’s vulnerable underbelly.

There was a tremendous crackle, a sound louder than any thunderclap. A blinding flash of blue and white light erupted from the point of contact, illuminating the entire cavern in a ghastly, strobing glare. The air filled with the acrid smell of ozone and something else, the scent of cooked meat on a gigantic scale.

The crab emitted a shriek that was unlike any earthly sound. It was a bubbling, steaming scream that seemed to tear itself from the very depths of the sea. The monster convulsed violently, its great legs thrashing. The electric lances were torn from Kane’s and Ned’s hands as the beast crashed back down onto the stone floor with the force of an avalanche.

“Stand back!” Kane yelled, pulling Ned away from the dying behemoth.

The crab was not yet finished. In its death throes, it spun in a mad circle, its claws scything through the air. One of the great pincers, sharp as a guillotine, struck a massive stone pillar that supported the cavern roof.

With a deep grinding sound, the pillar cracked. Then, with a deafening roar, it shattered. A section of the ceiling gave way, and tons of rock and debris came crashing down.

“Look out!” Briggs cried.

The men scrambled for safety, dodging the falling stones. A large boulder, the size of a carriage, smashed down directly upon the single, naked light bulb that had provided their only ambient light, plunging the cavern into a terrifying darkness, broken only by the single, wild beam of the searchlight which Briggs had dropped in his haste.

“Saints preserve us! The light!” the Irishman hollered.

The searchlight lay on its side, its beam cutting a frantic, lonely swath across the far wall. The only other light was the horrifying, intermittent blue glow that still sparked and sputtered from the dying crab’s body.

In the chaos and the gloom, Martin Thorne saw his chance. He had been cowering near the entrance, but the rockfall had cut off his escape. Now, with his enemies distracted, a wicked thought entered his craven mind. He saw the thick power cable snaking across the floor, the lifeline for their only remaining source of light. Nearby lay a sharp-edged piece of fallen rock.

If they could not have light, no one would.

Briggs, scrambling to his feet, saw the villain’s intent. The shadowy figure of Thorne was hunched over the power line.

“The divil!” Briggs bellowed. “He’s after the cable! Stop him!”

Without a second thought for his own safety, the brave Irishman abandoned the idea of retrieving the searchlight and charged headlong into the darkness toward Thorne. He tackled the scoundrel with the force of a bull, and the two men went down in a tangle of limbs, their grunts and curses echoing in the suddenly silent cavern. The crab had finally stopped twitching.

“Ned! Are you alright?” Kane called out, his voice sharp.

“Yes, sir! I think so!” came the boy’s reply from somewhere in the dark. “What happened?”

“Briggs has Thorne. The monster is finished. Find the searchlight! We need that light!”

Ned stumbled through the darkness, his hands outstretched, until his fingers brushed against the metal casing of the searchlight. With some effort, he wrestled it upright and swung its powerful beam around the cavern.

The light first fell upon the still form of the giant crab. It was a hideous sight, its great shell cracked and smoking, a bluish fluid leaking from its wounds onto the stone floor. It was most certainly dead.

Then the beam found Briggs. The Irishman was sitting atop the spluttering, defeated form of Martin Thorne, holding him by the scruff of the neck like a drowned rat.

“I’ve got the slimy eel, skipper!” Briggs announced, giving Thorne a shake. “He was tryin’ to cut the power, so he was! Leave us all in the dark, he would!”

“We’ll deal with him in a moment,” Kane said, walking over to inspect the dead monster. He prodded it with his boot. It was as still and solid as a rock. “A magnificent beast. It is a shame we had to destroy it.”

“Shame, is it?” cried Briggs. “Begorra, it was him or us, skipper! I’ll not be sheddin’ any tears for a bug that big!”

“Kane! Look!” Ned called out, his voice filled with excitement. He had pointed the searchlight beam at the wall behind where the crab had been.

Where the monster’s thrashing claw had smashed the stone pillar, it had also broken away a large section of the cavern wall. Behind the rubble, a dark, rectangular opening was now visible. It was no natural formation. The edges were too clean, too perfect. It was a doorway.

“A doorway!” Kane breathed, his weariness forgotten. He strode over to the opening, Ned lighting the way. “This is no mere cave. This is a structure. It was built!”

“Built by who, sir?” asked Ned, peering into the inky blackness of the passage.

“That is what we are about to find out,” Kane replied, his eyes gleaming with the thrill of discovery. “Briggs! Bring our prisoner. We are going in.”

“Right you are, skipper,” the Irishman grunted. He hauled the miserable Thorne to his feet. “On your feet, ye snake. You’re goin’ to have a look first, I’ll wager.”

Thorne whimpered. “No! Not in there! Who knows what other horrors await?”

“You should have thought of that before you tried to leave us in the dark,” Kane said sternly. “You have proven you cannot be trusted. From now on, you will walk in front of us, where we can see you.”

They took a few moments to prepare. They had lost their electric lances in the fray, but they still had their revolvers and a good supply of cartridges. Kane retrieved a coil of rope and a small geologist’s hammer from his pack. Ned, meanwhile, had cleverly jury-rigged the heavy searchlight onto a simple sledge made from a flat piece of rock and a length of the power cable, allowing him to pull it along behind them.

“Excellent work, Ned,” Kane said with a nod of approval. “Your ingenuity will serve us well.”

With Briggs prodding the reluctant Thorne from behind, and Ned pulling the all important searchlight, Kane led the way into the newly discovered passage.

The air that flowed out of the opening was cool and dry, with a faint, dusty smell of ages. The passage was about ten feet high and wide enough for two men to walk abreast. The walls, floor, and ceiling were made of a smooth, black stone that seemed to drink the light from their beam.

“I’ve never seen rock like this,” Ned remarked in a hushed tone. “It’s like polished glass.”

“It is not rock,” Kane said, running his hand along the wall. “It is some kind of manufactured substance. Stronger than steel, I’d wager. The people who built this place were masters of a technology far beyond our own.”

They proceeded for a hundred yards in silence. The only sound was the scraping of Ned’s sledge and the terrified, shallow breathing of Martin Thorne. The passage went on, straight as an arrow, into the bowels of the earth.

“Where are we going?” Thorne whined.

“To find the end of this corridor,” Kane replied curtly. “Now be quiet.”

Suddenly, the passage opened into a vast, circular chamber. The scale of it was breathtaking. Ned swung the searchlight beam upward, and it was lost in the immense darkness of the ceiling high above. In the center of the chamber stood a colossal structure.

It was a pyramid, but not like the pyramids of Egypt. This one was made of the same smooth, black material as the corridor, and it was inverted. Its broad base was somewhere up in the darkness of the ceiling, and it tapered down to a sharp point that hovered a mere three feet above the floor. The entire, massive structure floated in the air, held aloft by some unseen, silent force.

“By the saints,” Briggs whispered, his voice full of awe. “What manner of devilry is this?”

“Not devilry, Briggs,” Kane said, his face alight with intellectual fervor. “Science. An advanced science we cannot yet comprehend. Magnetism, perhaps, or a force we have not yet discovered.”

He took a step forward, his eyes scanning the chamber. All around the perimeter, at regular intervals, were more of the dark doorways, leading off into unknown passages.

“It is a hub,” Ned said. “Like the center of a wheel.”

“Precisely,” Kane agreed. “And that,” he said, pointing the beam of a small hand-torch at the floor directly beneath the pyramid’s hovering point, “must be the key.”

On the floor, set into the black stone, was a large, circular disk made of a pale, golden metal. It was covered in a complex pattern of intersecting lines and strange, unfamiliar symbols.

“A map?” Ned suggested.

“Or a control panel,” Kane countered. He knelt for a closer look, careful not to touch it. “These symbols… they are not like any known language. They are purely geometric.”

While Kane was engrossed in the mysterious disk, Thorne saw another chance. Briggs, equally captivated by the impossible floating pyramid, had loosened his grip on the prisoner’s arm. Thorne was a coward, but he was also an opportunist. With a sudden, desperate movement, he shoved the big Irishman with all his might.

Briggs, caught off balance, stumbled forward with a cry of surprise. “Whoa, there!”

Thorne did not wait to see the result. He sprinted away, not toward the entrance they had come from, but toward one of the other dark doorways on the far side of the chamber.

“He’s escaping again!” Ned yelled, swinging the searchlight around to follow him.

“Let him!” Kane snapped, not even looking up from the golden disk. “He cannot get far. Briggs, give me a hand here. This disk looks like it can be rotated. I believe if we can align these symbols correctly, we can activate this chamber!”

Briggs, recovering his balance, looked from the fleeing coward to his leader. “But skipper! The blaggard is gettin’away!”

“He is running into another maze, Briggs. He is trapping himself. Our goal is here!” Kane’s excitement was infectious. “This could be the greatest discovery in the history of mankind!”

Reluctantly, Briggs turned his back on the passage Thorne had disappeared into and joined Kane at the center of the room. “Shure, and if you say so, skipper. What do you need me to do?”

“This disk is heavy. Together, we will turn it. I will call out the symbols as they align with this marker here. We must be careful. The wrong combination could have disastrous consequences.”

Ned kept the searchlight beam fixed on the golden plate as Kane and Briggs put their shoulders to it. With a deep groan of metal on stone, the disk began to move. The fate of Martin Thorne was forgotten for the moment, lost in the greater mystery that now lay at their feet. The secrets of a lost civilization were waiting to be unlocked.

With a grunt of exertion, Briggs put his massive shoulder against the rim of the golden disk. Kane did likewise on the opposite side. Ned held the electric torch with both hands, its beam a stark white circle upon the strange, metallic plate.

“Now!” Kane commanded. “Together!”

The two men pushed with all their might. For a moment, nothing happened. The disk was as immovable as the mountain in which it was hidden.

“Heave!” cried Briggs, his face turning red with the strain. “Put your back into it, skipper!”

A deep, grinding noise, like the protest of ancient stone, echoed in the vast chamber. The disk moved. It was a slow, grudging rotation, but it moved nonetheless.

“It’s turning!” Ned exclaimed. “Keep going!”

“Watch the marker, Ned!” Kane panted, his eyes fixed on the engraved symbols sliding past a small, triangular mark on the floor. “Call them out! The spiral first!”

“Almost there… almost… now! The spiral is at the marker!”

As the symbol clicked into place, a low hum began to emanate from the floating pyramid above them. A soft, blue light pulsed from within its crystalline structure, bathing the chamber in an ethereal glow.

“Begorra, it’s working!” Briggs shouted, a grin splitting his face. “We’re doin’ it!”

“Do not stop!” Kane urged, his voice filled with a feverish intensity. “The next symbol is the circle with the three lines. Push, Briggs, push!”

Again they strained, and again the great disk turned, its groaning loud in the humming silence. The air grew thick with a strange energy, a palpable force that made the hair on their arms stand on end.

“The circle is coming around!” Ned called out. “A little more… stop!”

The hum from the pyramid grew in pitch. The blue light intensified, throwing their shadows into sharp relief against the far walls. They could now see that the black stone of the chamber was not entirely featureless; it was carved with intricate patterns that absorbed the light, making them seem like windows into a starless night.

“What next, skipper?” Briggs asked, breathing heavily.

Kane, his face alight with discovery, did not answer immediately. He was staring at the sequence of symbols remaining. “There is a branching pattern, like a lightning bolt. That must be the one!”

They set their shoulders to the task once more. The disk moved more easily now, as if some internal mechanism had been awakened. As the lightning bolt symbol approached the marker, the pyramid began to vibrate. The hum became a resonant, almost musical tone.

Suddenly, a jarring clank sounded from beneath the floor. The disk seized, refusing to move another inch. The light in the pyramid flickered and died, plunging the room back into the limited illumination of Ned’s torch. The hum ceased.

“What happened?” Ned asked, his voice betraying his alarm.

“Shure, and it felt like something broke,” Briggs muttered, rubbing his sore shoulder.

Kane knelt by the disk, running his hands over its cool surface. “We have done something wrong. A misalignment. We must have overshot the mark.” He looked at the marker. The lightning bolt symbol had gone a fraction of an inch too far. “We must reverse it. Push the other way!”

They tried, but the disk would not budge. It was locked fast.

“It’s no use,” Kane said, frustration in his voice. “It is jammed.”

“Then we’re stuck?” Ned asked. “Is that all?”

“No!” Kane declared. “This is a machine. It must have a failsafe, a release. Look around the room, both of you. Search the walls for any kind of switch or lever. Anything that looks out of place!”

Briggs and Ned began to search the perimeter of the great chamber, their lights dancing across the carved walls. While they were thus occupied, a new sound reached them. It was faint at first, a distant cry from the darkness of the passage into which Thorne had fled.

“Did you hear that?” Ned asked, freezing in place.

They all fell silent, listening. The sound came again, clearer this time. It was a scream of pure, unadulterated terror. It cut off abruptly, followed by a strange, metallic clattering.

“Saints preserve us, that’s Thorne!” Briggs exclaimed. “He’s found trouble!”

“Let him,” Kane said dismissively, his attention still on the puzzle of the disk. “He brought it on himself. We have more important work to do.”

“But skipper!” Briggs protested, his face a mask of concern. “We can’t just leave him! That wasn't a man who tripped and fell. That was a sound of mortal fear!”

“Briggs is right,” Ned added, turning to face Kane. “Whatever is in these tunnels, we led him here. We are responsible.”

Kane stood up, his impatience warring with his conscience. “He is a treacherous coward who tried to kill us! His fate is his own concern.”

Another sound echoed from the passage. This time it was not a scream, but a series of high pitched, rapid reports, like a strange kind of machine gun, followed by a loud clang, as if something heavy had been thrown against a metal wall.

Briggs took a step toward the dark corridor. “I’m not leavin’ a man to be torn apart by some devilry in the dark, skipper, not even a blackguard like Thorne. It ain’t right.”

Kane sighed, running a hand through his hair. He saw the resolution in the Irishman’s face and knew it was useless to argue. Besides, his scientific curiosity was piqued. What manner of device or creature could make such a sound?

“Very well,” he said at last. “You are right. We cannot abandon him. But we must be cautious. We have no idea what we are facing.” He drew a heavy revolver from his coat pocket, its nickel plating gleaming in the torchlight. “Briggs, you have your pistol?”

“Aye, skipper. And me fists, if it comes to that.”

“Ned, you keep the light. Stay behind us. We will proceed down that corridor. Walk slowly and make no sudden movements.”

With Kane in the lead and the burly Irishman close behind, the small party entered the dark passage from which the screams had come. The air inside was colder, and a strange, ozonic smell, like the air after a lightning strike, hung in the corridor.

The passage was hewn from the same black stone as the central chamber. It was about ten feet high and just as wide, and it twisted and turned like a serpent’s burrow. After a hundred feet, they came to a fork.

“Which way?” Ned whispered.

Kane knelt and examined the floor. “There are scuff marks here. He went to the right. He was running.”

They took the right-hand passage. It led them into a veritable labyrinth of intersecting tunnels. It was clear that Thorne had been running blindly, taking any turn that presented itself in his panic.

“He could be anywhere in here,” Ned said, his voice echoing slightly. “We could search for days.”

“No,” Kane replied, pointing his torch at the floor. “Look here.”

A small, shiny object lay on the stone floor. Briggs picked it up. It was a brass button from Thorne’s jacket.

“He’s shedding his skin like a snake,” the Irishman grumbled. “He came this way, all right.”

They pressed on, guided by Kane’s keen eye for tracks and the occasional sign of Thorne’s panicked flight. Suddenly, a low whirring sound came from the tunnel ahead. It grew steadily louder.

“Get back!” Kane ordered, pushing his companions against the wall. “Ned, kill the light!”

The corridor was plunged into absolute blackness. The whirring grew to a loud hum, and then, around a bend fifty yards ahead, a red light appeared. It bobbed and weaved in the darkness, moving silently toward them.

“What in tarnation is that?” Briggs whispered, his hand tightening on the grip of his pistol.

As the object drew closer, they could begin to make out its form. It was a perfect sphere of polished, dark metal, about the size of a man’s head. It floated three feet above the ground, moving without any visible means of propulsion. The single, glowing red lens on its front-facing side swept the tunnel from wall to wall, like the eye of some unholy predator.

“A sentry,” Kane breathed, his fear mixed with a thrill of scientific awe. “An automated guardian. Incredible!”

The sphere paused, its red eye fixing on the spot where the brass button lay. It emitted a series of soft, inquisitive clicks. Then its eye swiveled directly toward their hiding place. It had detected them.

“It sees us!” Ned hissed.

With a sharp, electronic chirp, the sphere shot forward. It moved with unnatural speed and silence.

“Fire!” Kane yelled.

Briggs and Kane both opened fire with their revolvers. The sound of the shots was deafening in the enclosed space. Their bullets struck the metallic sphere with loud pings, but they might as well have been throwing pebbles. The projectiles ricocheted wildly off its hardened shell, whining past their heads in the darkness.

The sphere did not slow. It was twenty yards away, then ten. A thin, articulated arm telescoped from its side, ending in a pincer-like claw.

“It’s no use!” Briggs shouted. “The bullets do nothing!”

“Stand your ground!” Kane commanded, firing his last two shots to no effect.

Just as the mechanical guardian was nearly upon them, Ned, in a moment of desperate inspiration, did the only thing he could. He switched on his electric torch, directing the powerful, concentrated beam directly into the sphere’s red lens.

The effect was instantaneous. The sphere let out a piercing electronic shriek, like metal being tortured. It swerved violently, its red eye flickering. It crashed into the stone wall of the corridor with a loud bang, then fell to the floor, where it spun in a tight circle, still shrieking.

“You’ve blinded it!” Kane cried in triumph. “Brilliant, Ned, brilliant!”

The sphere’s spinning slowed. It wobbled, and then with a final, pathetic whir, its red light went out and it became still. A wisp of smoke curled up from its lens.

Briggs gave the inert object a hefty kick. “Take that, you tin-pot divil!”

“Don’t damage it!” Kane snapped. “That is a priceless artifact! We must study it later. For now, we must find Thorne. It must have been this machine that he encountered.”

They continued down the corridor, moving with greater caution than before. A few turns later, they found him.

Martin Thorne was huddled in a dead-end alcove, his face pale and streaked with grime. He was shivering uncontrollably, his eyes wide with a terror that went beyond mere fear. At his feet lay a second metallic sphere, this one dented and scorched, clearly the one that had been hit by the clanging object they had heard. Thorne held a heavy, iron crowbar in his shaking hands.

“He fought back,” Ned observed, surprised.

“The courage of a cornered rat,” Kane muttered. He stepped forward, his revolver leveled at Thorne’s chest. “It is over, Thorne. Your flight is ended.”

Thorne did not seem to hear him. He was staring past them, back down the corridor. “There are more,” he whispered, his voice a hoarse croak. “I saw them. Dozens of them. They patrol the halls. We are all going to die in here.”

Briggs stepped forward and gently took the crowbar from Thorne’s unresisting hands. “Easy now, lad. You’re safe with us. We’ll get you out of this.”

Thorne looked at the big Irishman, and for the first time, a glimmer of sanity returned to his eyes. He seemed to shrink in on himself, the fight gone out of him completely.

“We must return to the central chamber,” Kane announced. “Sticking together is our only chance. Thorne, you will walk between me and Briggs. If you try to run again, I will not hesitate to shoot you in the leg. Do you understand?”

Thorne nodded numbly.

The journey back was tense. Every shadow seemed to hold another floating menace, and every echo sounded like the whir of an approaching sentry. But they encountered no more of the guardians, and soon they saw the faint blue glow spilling from the main chamber’s entrance.

When they stepped back into the great hall, they all stopped in amazement.

During their absence, the chamber had come to life. The pyramid was no longer just glowing; it was now projecting a vast, three dimensional image into the center of the room. It was a map, but a map of such complexity and scale that it boggled the mind. Luminous blue lines crisscrossed the air, showing not only the labyrinth of tunnels they were in, but a city that lay beneath it. There were massive caverns, intricate structures, and pathways of light that pulsed with energy.

“My word,” Ned breathed. “It is a whole world down here.”

Kane walked toward the hovering display, his earlier encounter with the sentry forgotten, his face once again alight with the fervor of a true explorer. “It’s not a map of a city, Ned,” he said, his voice trembling with excitement. “It is a schematic. A blueprint! This is the power system, these are the residential districts, this must be their data archive! The knowledge here… it could change everything!”

Briggs, holding Thorne by the arm, looked from the incredible map to his leader. “That’s all well and good, skipper, but what about gettin’ out of here? Those floatin’ cannonballs are still out there.”

Kane turned from the map, a wild look in his eyes. “Out? Briggs, why would we want to get out? We have just stumbled upon the greatest discovery in the history of human civilization! Thorne’s terror has given us a gift. We now know this place is not dead. It is dormant. It has defenses. It has power! We are not raiders in a tomb; we are visitors in a sleeping city.”

He pointed a finger at a section of the glowing map, where a large, spherical structure was depicted. “That is our next destination. A power station, I’d wager. If we can understand how to control it, we can control the entire complex. We can turn the lights on, disable the sentries, and unlock every secret this place holds.”

Thorne, who had been staring at the map with a look of dread, suddenly spoke. “You are mad,” he said, his voice flat. “This place is a tomb. We woke it up, and it will kill us all.”

“Silence, you fool!” Kane snapped. “You see only death. I see the dawn of a new age!” He turned back to his companions, his enthusiasm overriding all caution. “Briggs, secure our prisoner. Ned, find a way to make a copy of this map. We must not lose it. Our real adventure has only just begun. We are no longer just explorers. We are about to become masters of a lost world!”

The great chamber was silent save for the soft hum of the projector. The secrets of the ages floated before them in a river of blue light, guarded by the ghosts of a long dead people and their tireless, mechanical servants. For Kane, it was a dream come true. For the others, it was a beautiful and terrifying trap from which there might be no escape.

For a time, it seemed the simple plan would work. The light of the Rover dwindled until it was once more swallowed by the immensity of the sea.

“That should settle that,” Frank said, easing back on the throttle to conserve power.

But he had underestimated the obstinacy of the Rover’s skipper. An hour later, Tom Reynard spotted the light again, doggedly holding its position on the horizon.

“He is persistent, I will give him that,” the detective said with a frown.

“Persistent and foolish,” Frank corrected. “He is burning his coal for nothing. We can play this game all night. Let him waste his fuel.”

Frank decided on a new tactic. He would not run, but instead maintain a steady pace just out of the steamer’s reach. It was a contemptuous and confident maneuver, designed to show the ship’s captain the utter futility of his pursuit. The night wore on. The Pegasus flew on, a silent sentinel in the sky, while far below, the lone steamer followed like a frustrated shark trailing a whale it could not hope to catch.

The first sign of trouble came not from the pursuing ship, but from the heavens themselves. The stars, which had been so brilliant, began to disappear behind a high, thin veil of cloud. The air grew heavy and humid. A strange, greenish light began to tinge the eastern horizon, though the sun was still hours from rising.

“I do not like the look of that sky,” Frank said, his brow furrowed in concentration. He tapped the barometer in the deck-house. The needle had fallen sharply. “We are in for a blow, Tom. A bad one.”

“A typhoon?”

“It has all the signs of one. We are in the right season for it, and the right part of the world. We must gain altitude. Try to fly above the worst of it.”

Frank again pushed the levers for more power. The Pegasus began to climb, rising swiftly from a few hundred feet above the sea to several thousand. But the storm seemed to be building with unnatural speed. The sea below, visible in flashes of sudden, silent lightning, was no longer calm. It was a churning cauldron of black water, streaked with angry white foam. The wind began to rise, not as a steady gale, but in violent, unpredictable gusts that struck the airship like giant fists.

“Hold on!” Frank shouted, his knuckles white as he fought the wheel.

The Pegasus, for all her power, was tossed about frightfully. She rose and fell hundreds of feet at a time, yawing wildly from side to side. Rain began to fall, not in drops, but in solid sheets that hammered against the deck-house windows. The roar of the wind became a deafening shriek.

Down in the hold, the prisoners were being thrown about in their cages. The four sailors cried out in fear, but Martin Murdock remained silent, a strange, calculating gleam in his eyes. The storm was a terror, but it was also an opportunity.

The airship gave a particularly violent lurch, and one of the sailors was thrown hard against the bars of his enclosure. The iron lock, perhaps weakened in their previous struggles, shattered under the impact. The door swung open.

“I’m free!” the sailor, a burly man named Grimes, yelled above the storm’s howl.

“Then free us, you fool!” Murdock hissed from his own cell. “Now is our chance! They will be too busy with the storm to notice!”

Spurred on by the villain’s command, Grimes scrambled to the other cells. He had no key, but he was a strong, desperate man. He found a loose iron bar on the floor and began to work furiously at the locks of his comrades’ cells.

At that moment, Barney and Pomp, who had been awakened by the tempest, were making their way forward to see if Frank needed assistance.

“Saints preserve us, what a storm!” Barney cried, holding onto a stanchion for support. “The ould girl is takin’ a beatin’!”

“She’ll hold, Barney!” Pomp yelled back. “Masther Frank built her strong!”

It was then that Pomp heard a suspicious noise from the forward hold, a metallic clang that was not part of the storm’s chorus. He motioned to Barney, his eyes wide. The Irishman understood at once. Drawing the heavy revolvers they always carried, the two crept toward the prison hold.

Peering through the hatch, they saw a scene of mutiny. Grimes had just succeeded in breaking the lock on a second sailor’s cell. Murdock was shouting encouragement from his own cage.

“Begorra, the snakes are loose!” Barney roared. He did not hesitate. He threw open the hatch and leaped down into the hold. “Back in your cages, ye divils!”

The two freed sailors, startled, turned to face him. They were armed with nothing but their fists, but they were desperate. They charged at the Irishman. Pomp was right behind him, his own pistol leveled.

“You ain’t goin’ nowhere!” Pomp shouted.

A chaotic and brutal fight erupted in the swaying, lurching hold. Barney swung his pistol like a club, felling one sailor with a blow to the head. The other, Grimes, grappled with him, and the two men wrestled back and forth across the floor. Pomp, meanwhile, kept the other two prisoners, still in their cells, at bay, while also trying to aid his friend.

Up in the deck-house, Frank and Tom Reynard were oblivious to the mutiny below. They had their own desperate battle to fight. A brilliant fork of lightning lit up the sky, followed by a crack of thunder so loud it shook the very frame of the Pegasus.

At the same instant, the lights in the cabin flickered and died, plunging them into absolute darkness save for the ghostly flashes from outside. The steady hum of the engine faltered, becoming a strained, grinding noise.

“The dynamo!” Frank yelled. “The new wheel can’t take the strain!”

The Pegasus, deprived of half her power, gave a sickening plunge. It fell like a stone for a thousand feet before Frank, wrestling with the controls, could partially arrest the fall. The helices were still turning, but only just. They were no longer climbing, but sinking slowly toward the raging sea below.

“Tom, I need your help!” Frank shouted. “Keep her as level as you can! I have to get to the engine room!”

The detective, his face grim, took the wheel. It fought him like a living thing, but he held on with all his strength. Frank, grabbing an electric lantern, unfasted a panel in the floor and lowered himself into the heart of his machine.

The sight that met his eyes was alarming. The new dynamo wheel, the one they had worked all night to install, was glowing a dull red. Sparks flew from its bearings, and the whole assembly was vibrating dangerously. The immense and erratic power demands of fighting the storm were tearing it apart.

“It’s going to fail completely!” Frank muttered to himself. He had to think fast. There was no time for a proper repair. He needed a temporary solution, something to get them through the storm.

His eyes scanned the engine room, his brilliant mind racing. He saw the auxiliary power unit, a smaller dynamo used for the ship’s lights and secondary systems. It was not nearly powerful enough to drive the main helices on its own. But perhaps, if he could somehow link it to the main drive shaft, it could provide just enough assistance to keep the primary dynamo from self destructing.

It was a desperate, untested theory. A mistake could overload the entire system, paralyzing the Pegasus completely. But to do nothing was to guarantee a fatal plunge into the sea.

Working with a speed and precision that was astonishing to behold, Frank began to uncouple wires and reroute circuits. He was drenched in sweat, his hands burned by contact with the hot machinery. The airship lurched, and he was thrown against a bulkhead, but he scrambled back to his task, his concentration absolute.

Meanwhile, the battle in the hold reached its climax. Barney, with a mighty heave, threw the sailor Grimes from him. The man crashed into the far wall and lay stunned. The Irishman, breathing heavily, turned his attention to the second freed sailor, who was now menacing Pomp with an iron bar.

“Drop it, ye scut!” Barney bellowed, leveling his revolver.

The sailor hesitated, his eyes darting between the Irishman and the brave coon. Just then, the ship gave another violent roll. He lost his footing and stumbled. Pomp seized the moment. He lunged forward, not with his gun, but with his whole body, tackling the man around the knees. Both of them went down in a heap, and the iron bar clattered away. Before the sailor could recover, Barney was upon him, and the fight was over.

“Tie them up, Pomp!” Barney ordered, panting. “Tie them up good and tight this time!”

They used spare rope to bind the two recaptured sailors hand and foot. Murdock, who had watched the entire failed mutiny from his cell, slumped back against the bars, a look of utter despair on his face. His last chance was gone.

At that very moment, deep in the engine room, Frank Reade Jr. made the final connection. He threw a heavy switch. There was a terrifying shower of sparks, a loud hum, and then the groaning of the main dynamo eased. The red glow faded from the wheel. The vibrations lessened.

Up in the deck-house, the lights flickered back on. The helices, which had been barely turning, picked up speed. Tom Reynard gave a cry of relief as he felt power return to the controls. The Pegasus stopped her descent and began, slowly but surely, to climb once more.

Frank, exhausted but triumphant, climbed back into the deck-house.

“She’ll hold!” he announced. “The patch will hold until we can get through this!”

And as if on cue, the fury of the storm began to abate. The shriek of the wind lessened to a mournful moan. The rain eased. Through a break in the clouds, a single, watery moonbeam shone down upon the sea.

The typhoon had passed as quickly as it had arrived. The Pegasus, battered but intact, floated serenely in the now calm air. Frank, Tom, Barney, and Pomp stood together on the deck, looking down at the waters below.

The sea was littered with wreckage. Splintered planks, a broken mast, and the dark shapes of cargo crates bobbed on the subsiding swell. Of the steamer Rover, there was no sign.

“Holy smoke,” whispered Tom Reynard. “She foundered. The storm took her.”

“They followed us into a deathtrap,” Frank said, his voice somber. He felt no triumph, only a grim sense of fate’s inexorable justice. The greedy captain and his crew had met a watery grave, a direct result of their own foolish and vengeful pursuit.

Barney came forward. “Masther Frank, the prisoners tried to break loose. We’ve subdued them.”

“Good work, you two,” Frank said, clapping him on the shoulder. “It seems we have fought battles on two fronts tonight, and won them both.”

He walked over to the hatch leading to the hold and looked down. He could see Martin Murdock, sitting silently in his cell. The arch-rogue looked up, and his eyes met Frank’s. For the first time, the villain’s face was not filled with hate or cunning, but with a hollow, empty shock. He had seen his last allies, his final hope for rescue, swallowed by the sea. He was utterly, completely alone.

Frank closed the hatch.

“He will give us no more trouble, I think,” the young inventor said quietly.

He returned to the helm. The emergency patch on the dynamo would not last forever, and the ship had sustained other minor damages. They needed to find a safe place to land and make proper repairs before attempting the long trans-oceanic flight.

“Barney, take the wheel,” Frank commanded. “Set a course for the coast of Oman. We will find a secluded spot there to set down and put the Pegasus back in perfect order.”

“Yis, sor!” the Irishman replied, his usual cheerfulness returning.

As Barney took the controls, Frank stood with Pomp and Tom Reynard, watching the sun begin to rise. It painted the eastern sky in brilliant hues of gold and rose, promising a new day. The wreckage of the Rover was far behind them, a dark memory on the vast, empty sea. Their path was clear. The greatest dangers of the chase were behind them, and the final destination, San Francisco, seemed at last within their reach. But the world was a wide and dangerous place, and as Frank Reade Jr. knew, for the crew of the Pegasus, the next adventure was never truly far away.

The coast of Oman rose like a jagged brown wall from the turquoise sea. Its stark mountains, deeply shadowed in the morning light, promised the seclusion Frank so desperately needed. The Pegasus flew with a slight, almost imperceptible tremor, a constant reminder of the makeshift patch holding her vital dynamo together.

“She’s flying true, Masther Frank!” Barney called from the helm. “But she has a wee bit of a limp, like an ould man wid the gout!”

“She’ll hold, Barney,” Frank replied, his eyes scanning the coastline through a spyglass. “But I won’t risk a long flight over water until we’ve made her as good as new. We need a place to hide from the world for a day or two.”

Tom Reynard joined him at the rail. “Any port in a storm, eh, Frank? Or in this case, any canyon after one. It’s a desolate looking country.”

“All the better for us,” said Frank. “The fewer people who see the Pegasus on the ground, the fewer questions we have to answer. Look there!”

He pointed toward a narrow opening in the coastal cliffs. It was the mouth of a dry riverbed, a wadi that snaked its way deep into the rugged hills. It looked like a perfect natural hangar, concealed from the sea and from the arid plains that lay further inland.

“That’s our spot!” Frank declared. “Take her down, Barney. Easy now.”

“Easy it is, sor!”

The great airship descended with a gentle whisper of her mighty helices. She passed between the sheer rock walls of the canyon, her wings clearing the stone by a mere dozen feet on either side. Barney, with a practiced hand, brought the Pegasus to rest upon the sandy floor of the wadi. The moment the landing gear touched down, the hum of the engine ceased. A profound silence fell, broken only by the sighing of the wind through the canyon.

“All right, lads!” Frank’s voice was crisp and full of energy. “No time to waste! Pomp, you and Tom will stand guard at the canyon mouth. Keep a sharp lookout. Barney, you’re with me. We’re going to tear down that dynamo and replace the wheel properly.”

“As good as new it will be, Masther Frank!” the Irishman chirped, already grabbing a tool chest.

“I’ll get to it!” added Pomp, taking up his rifle.

Down in the hold, the air was still and close. Martin Murdock sat on the cot in his small steel cell. He had watched the descent through his tiny porthole, his mind a whirlwind of cold calculation. His allies were gone, swallowed by the sea. His bid for freedom had ended in this floating prison. But Martin Murdock was not a man to succumb to despair. He was a creature of opportunity.

He watched the crew disembark. He heard Frank’s orders. He noted their position, a deep and isolated ravine. A trap, perhaps, for them. But also, a cage. His sharp eyes scanned the rim of the canyon high above. He was a prisoner, yes, but his captors were now prisoners of this desolate land as well, at least until their repairs were complete. He listened, and he waited.

The work began in earnest. Frank, his sleeves rolled up, directed the delicate operation. With Barney’s powerful assistance, they unbolted the damaged dynamo casing. The broken wheel was a sorry sight, its metal warped and fractured.

“It’s a wonder she held together at all,” Frank muttered, examining the cracks. “Another hour of that typhoon and we would have been as helpless as the Rover.”

“Faith, an’ the luck of the Irish was wid us!” Barney declared.

Hours passed. The sun climbed high in the sky, beating down into the canyon and turning the metal skin of the Pegasus into a furnace. Tom and Pomp, sheltering in the shadow of a rock outcropping, kept their vigilant watch. The landscape was utterly still. Nothing moved but the heat haze shimmering above the rocks.

“See anything, Pomp?” Tom asked, wiping his brow.

“Nothin’ but rocks an’ sand, Mistah Tom,” the coon replied, his gaze unwavering. “But this place, it feels like it’s watchin’ us.”

Tom Reynard felt it too. A prickling sensation on the back of his neck. It was the instinct of a man who had spent his life hunting other men. He raised his own spyglass and swept the canyon rim. Rock, sand, and scrubby, thorny bushes. Nothing. And yet, the feeling persisted.

He was not wrong. High above them, lying flat upon his belly behind a sun-scorched boulder, a man in flowing white robes peered down at the strange, winged machine in the canyon below. His dark eyes widened in disbelief. He had been tracking a stray goat when he saw the great metal bird descend from the sky. He had never seen such a thing. It was a marvel, a miracle, or a devil’s machine. He did not know which. He only knew that his master, the powerful Sheikh Omar bin Talal, must be told of this incredible visitor. With the stealth of a desert cat, the scout crawled backward from the edge, rose to his feet, and ran, his bare feet making no sound on the rocky ground.

By late afternoon, the new dynamo wheel was in place. Frank and Barney were greasy and sweat-stained, but their work was nearly done.

“Just a few more connections to make, Barney,” Frank said, tightening a final bolt. “Then we can test the circuits. We should be ready to lift off by morning.”

“That’s good news, Masther Frank!” Barney grinned. “This heat is enough to bake a potato in yer pocket!”

It was then that Pomp’s powerful voice echoed down the canyon.

“Mistah Frank! Riders comin’!”

Frank and Barney scrambled out of the hold. Tom was already running back from his post, his rifle at the ready.

“How many, Tom?” Frank called out.

“A dozen, maybe more!” the detective shouted back. “Armed! They’re coming right down the wadi!”

Frank’s face was grim. “Get aboard and bar the hatches! Barney, Pomp, get your rifles! Don’t fire unless I give the word!”

The crew hastened to obey. They clambered inside the Pegasus and sealed the main door. Frank went to the pilot-house, peering through the thick plate glass window.

Down the canyon they came, a formidable procession. They rode magnificent Arabian horses that pranced and tossed their heads. The men were tall and fierce-looking, with dark, bearded faces and piercing eyes. They were armed with long rifles and curved scimitars that flashed in the sun. At their head rode a man of immense presence, his robes finer than the others, a golden cord binding his headdress. This was clearly their leader.

The horsemen reined in about fifty yards from the nose of the Pegasus. They stared in silent awe at the colossal airship. The leader, Sheikh Omar, raised a hand.

“Who are you that comes in such a vessel to my lands?” he called out. His voice was deep and resonant, and to Frank’s surprise, he spoke in clear, if heavily accented, English.

Frank opened a small speaking-port set into the hull. “We are peaceful travelers!” he called back. “My name is Frank Reade Jr. Our vessel was damaged in a storm. We mean no harm! We are only making repairs and will be on our way.”

The Sheikh’s dark eyes narrowed. He looked from Frank’s face in the port to the immense, silent machine. He saw it not as a traveler’s conveyance, but as an instrument of unimaginable power.

“A ship that sails the sky!” the Sheikh mused aloud, his gaze hungry. “Such a thing belongs to a king! Not to a boy. This wadi is my land. The sky above it is my sky. Therefore, this ship is my ship!”

“You are mistaken, sir!” Frank replied, his own voice hardening. “This airship is the product of my own invention! It belongs to me and my crew, and we will not surrender it!”

“Brave words!” the Sheikh sneered. “But you are four men, trapped in a metal shell. I am Sheikh Omar! My warriors are as numerous as the sands of this desert! You will give me the sky-ship, or my men will tear it apart to see how it works, and your bones will bleach in the sun!”

A tense silence followed. Frank’s mind raced. The dynamo was still disconnected. The Pegasus was a helpless, grounded bird. They were caught.

It was in that moment that a new voice, muffled but clear, shouted from within the Pegasus.

“Sheikh Omar! Great Sheikh! Do not listen to him! He is a liar and a thief!”

The Sheikh started, his eyes searching for the source of the sound. It came from a small, barred porthole on the underside of the airship’s hull.

It was Martin Murdock.

“Who speaks?” the Sheikh demanded, urging his horse a few steps closer.

“A friend! An enemy of this boy, Frank Reade!” Murdock yelled, his face pressed against the bars of his cell. “He stole this machine from me! It is mine by right! He holds me prisoner!”

Frank’s blood ran cold. “Murdock, you treacherous villain!” he hissed under his breath.

“What is this?” the Sheikh called, his interest piqued. “You are his prisoner?”

“Yes! A captive!” Murdock cried, playing his part with devilish skill. “He is a boy who plays with things he does not understand! I am an engineer! I know the secrets of this ship! Release me, oh great Sheikh, and I will deliver it into your hands! I will teach you its secrets! Together, we will be masters of the sky! You will be the most powerful ruler in all of Arabia!”

The Sheikh’s eyes lit up with a fire of avarice and ambition. Murdock’s words were like a potent poison, perfectly tailored to his desires. He looked from Murdock’s desperate face to Frank’s defiant one. A slow, cruel smile spread across his lips. This was a most interesting development.

“This changes things,” the Sheikh said, his voice smooth as silk. “It seems I have a choice of partners. Boy inventor! Your prisoner offers me a better bargain than you do. He offers me knowledge! He offers me power! You offer me only defiance. I will make you one final offer. Open your ship. Release the man who calls himself your prisoner. Surrender to my authority. Do this, and I may yet spare your lives.”

“Never!” Frank shouted back, his anger flaring. “That man is a criminal! A murderer and a scoundrel! If you side with him, you make an enemy of us and the justice he is fleeing!”

“Justice?” the Sheikh laughed, a harsh, grating sound. “I am the justice in this land! You have made your choice. And a foolish one it is!” He turned to his men. “Prepare to attack! We will take the sky-ship by force! The man in the cage has promised us the prize!”

A savage cheer went up from the warriors. They brandished their rifles and scimitars, their horses stamping impatiently.

“Barney! Pomp! Tom!” Frank yelled, turning from the port. “They’re going to attack! We have to hold them off!”

“But the engine, Masther Frank!” Barney cried, his face pale. “She has no power! We’re sittin’ ducks!”

“Then we have to buy ourselves time!” Frank retorted, grabbing his own rifle. “I need five more minutes, Barney! Can you make the final connections in five minutes?”

“Five minutes!” the Irishman exclaimed. “It’s a desperate race, sor, but I’ll do it or die tryin’!”

“Go!” Frank commanded.

Barney and Pomp scrambled back toward the engine room. Tom Reynard took up a position at a gun-port on the port side, Frank on the starboard.

Outside, Sheikh Omar raised his sword. “Forward!” he roared.

With wild yells, the desert warriors spurred their horses. They charged toward the stationary airship, a wave of white robes and flashing steel.

“Fire!” shouted Frank.

“They have the guns of demons!” one of the men cried.

“They are only men!” Sheikh Omar bellowed, enraged. “Fire back! Rush them! They cannot shoot all of us!”

The warriors, recovering their nerve, began to fire their own long rifles. Bullets whanged and ricocheted off the Pegasus’s tough aluminum-steel alloy hull. The noise was deafening.

Down in the hold, Barney worked with a frantic, desperate energy. His hands flew across the terminals and wires of the new dynamo. Pomp stood over him, rifle in hand, his eyes fixed on the hatch, ready to defend his friend from any who might try to board.

“How’s it comin’, Barney?” Pomp asked, his voice steady.

“Saints be praised, I think I’ve got it!” the Celt muttered, his brow beaded with sweat. “Just one more lead… there!”

For three days the journey was a triumph of mechanical perfection. The new dynamo hummed its steady tune of power, the helices whirred with reassuring strength, and the airship cleaved the blue vault of heaven at a tremendous pace.

“Faith, an’ it’s smooth sailin’ we’re havin’, Masther Frank!” Barney exclaimed from the pilot-house, his hands resting lightly on the wheel. “At this rate, we’ll see Ceylon before the week is out.”

Tom Reynard, the detective, joined them on the deck. He had been inspecting the makeshift cell in the hold where their prisoner was confined.

“He is as sullen and venomous as a cornered snake,” the detective reported. “He refuses all food and speaks only to utter threats. The man’s spirit is not broken, Frank. It is merely coiled, waiting for a chance to strike.”

“Golly, Marse Frank, dat Mistah Murdock am a stubborn mule,” Pomp declared, shaking his head. “He won’t eat a bite. Says he’d rather starve dan take food from our hands. He jes’ sits an’ glares, lookin’ mighty spiteful.”

Toward evening, however, Frank’s keen eyes noted a change in the atmosphere. The sky in the east had taken on a strange, coppery hue, and the sea below seemed to lose its sparkle, becoming a dull, leaden gray.

“Barney, take a look at that,” the young inventor said, pointing eastward. “I don’t like the look of that sky.”

The Irishman peered intently in the direction indicated.

“Bejabers, you’re right, sor,” he muttered. “It has a quare, greasy look to it. An’ feel the air! It’s as heavy as a wet blanket.”

Frank hurried to the instrument and saw that the detective was correct. The needle had dropped alarmingly. His face grew serious.

“We are in for a blow,” he announced. “A severe one, by the looks of it. We are entering the season for cyclones in this part of the world.”

“A cyclone!” cried Barney. “Saints preserve us! Can the Pegasus weather such a storm, Masther Frank?”

“She is the strongest airship ever built,” Frank replied, his voice calm and steady, though his eyes were filled with concern. “But a cyclone is the most powerful force in nature. We must make all preparations. Pomp, Barney, help me secure everything on deck. Tom, please double check the fastenings on the boats and the deck-house doors.”

The men sprang to their tasks with alacrity. Every loose object was lashed down. The great searchlight was covered with a waterproof canvas, and the gears of the helices were given a final inspection.

Down in the hold, Martin Murdock felt the change in the air and heard the hurried footsteps above. A flicker of savage hope ignited in his dark heart. A storm! A storm might be the very agent of chaos he needed.

By nightfall, the placid sea had been transformed into a heaving, agitated mass of black water. The wind began to moan through the rigging of the Pegasus like a thousand lost souls. The sky was completely blotted out by a ceiling of roiling, ugly clouds.

“Here it comes!” Frank shouted over the rising shriek of the gale. “Stand by the engines, Barney! We’ll try to rise above it!”

“Aye, aye, sor!” the Celt yelled back, his knuckles white as he gripped the controls.

Frank threw the levers that governed the vertical propellers. The engine roared in protest as it fought against the incredible pressure of the wind, but the Pegasus began to climb. She ascended into the black maelstrom, tossed about like a child’s kite. Rain began to fall, not in drops, but in solid sheets that hammered against the deck-house with the force of a fire hose.

Lightning tore the sky apart, illuminating a scene of terrifying grandeur. All around them were mountains of black vapor, churning and colliding. The airship groaned and shuddered, every plate and rivet strained to its utmost limit.

“She can’t make it, Masther Frank!” Barney cried, his face pale in the flickering light of the binnacle. “The wind is too strong! It’s pushin’ us down!”

It was true. Despite the engines working at full power, the Pegasus was slowly but surely being forced back toward the raging sea. Down, down they sank, into the very heart of the tempest.

Suddenly, a sound like a cannon shot echoed from the deck, even above the howl of the storm. It was followed by a horrific grinding noise.

“What was that?” yelled Tom Reynard.

Frank’s face was a mask of grim realization. “One of the port helices! The main shaft must have snapped under the strain!”

The effect was instantaneous. The Pegasus, her lifting power crippled, lurched violently to one side. A dizzying spin began, sending the occupants staggering. The airship was now completely at the mercy of the cyclone.

“We’re going down!” Pomp shrieked from where he was braced against a bulkhead.

“Hold on!” Frank commanded, his voice ringing with authority even in this desperate moment. He wrestled with the controls, fighting to check their terrifying descent. “There’s land to the west! I saw it in a lightning flash! An island! It’s our only chance!”

With masterful skill, the young inventor used their remaining helices and the rudder to guide the crippled airship. It was a desperate gamble. The waves below were mountainous, and to strike one meant certain destruction.

For what seemed an eternity, they were tossed and battered. Then, through a momentary lull in the driving rain, they saw it. A dark shape against the blackness, a jagged line of coast.

“There it is!” Frank cried. “I’m going to try and make that cove! It looks sheltered!”

The Pegasus plunged toward a narrow inlet between two towering cliffs. The wind howled through the gap, threatening to dash them against the rocks. With a final, heroic effort, Frank angled the ship, letting the gale push them through the opening. They passed into the relative calm of a small bay. But their momentum was too great.

With a sickening crunch, the undercarriage of the Pegasus struck the sandy shore. The airship skidded forward a hundred feet, tearing a great furrow in the beach before coming to rest at a sharp angle, its damaged port side half-buried in the sand.

The sudden stop threw the men to the deck. For a moment, there was only the sound of the receding storm outside and the panting of the exhausted crew.

They were alive. They had survived.

As dawn broke, it revealed a scene of devastation. The storm had passed, leaving behind a bruised sky and a still-choppy sea. The Pegasus lay wounded upon the beach of a small, tropical island. The port vertical helix was a twisted wreck, its great blades shattered.

Frank was the first to emerge, surveying the damage with a heavy heart.

“The main axle is sheared clean through,” he said to Tom and Barney, who had joined him. “And three of the blades are gone. It will take us days to forge a new axle and fashion new propeller blades.”

“Faith, an’ it’s a sorry sight,” Barney lamented, kicking at a piece of twisted metal. “But we’re alive, and that’s a miracle in itself.”

“Indeed,” agreed the detective. “But where are we, Frank? This island is not marked on any of my charts of the area near Ceylon.”

“It must be an uncharted isle,” Frank mused, scanning their surroundings. The cove was beautiful, a crescent of white sand fringed with lush, green jungle. A small stream of fresh water trickled down from the rocks into the bay. “At least we have water. And the jungle should provide food. Our first priority is to repair the Pegasus. Pomp, get the forge set up. Barney, we’ll need to clear the wreckage of the old helix.”

The men set to work, their spirits low but their resolve unbroken. While they were thus engaged, a more sinister event was taking place within the tilted hull of the airship.

The crash had thrown Martin Murdock violently against the door of his cabin. He was bruised, but unhurt. To his astonishment, he found that the impact had done what his own efforts could not. The locking mechanism, damaged in the collision, had sprung open.

For a moment, he stood frozen. Then, a slow, wolfish grin spread across his face. He pushed the door. It swung inward. He was free.

He crept silently from his cell. The hold was empty. He could hear the voices of Frank and his companions outside, busy with their repairs. Cautiously, he climbed the ladder to the main deck. He peered out of a shattered porthole. He saw the beach, the jungle, and his captors, their backs to him as they worked.

Escape. The thought burned in his mind. Not just escape, but revenge.

He slipped over the side of the airship, dropping softly onto the sand. Keeping the hull of the Pegasus between himself and the work party, he scurried toward the tree line. He did not run aimlessly. His sharp eyes had noticed something Frank’s crew had missed in their focus on repairs: a thin, almost imperceptible trail leading from the far side of the cove, disappearing into the dense jungle. A man-made path.

Where there was a path, there were people. And any people, he reasoned, were better than the company he had been keeping. With a last, hateful glance at the Pegasus, he plunged into the green wilderness.

An hour later, Frank called a halt to the work. “We need to scout the island,” he said. “We should know if we are alone here. Tom, will you come with me? Barney, you and Pomp stand guard. Fire a rifle shot if you see anything amiss.”

“Right you are, Masther Frank,” the Irishman agreed, picking up his rifle.

Frank and the detective armed themselves and began to walk the perimeter of the cove. It was then they discovered the path Murdock had used.

“Look here, Frank!” Tom called. “This is a well-trodden trail. We are not the first ones on this island.”

“And look at these footprints,” Frank added, kneeling down. “They are fresh. Someone came this way not long ago. And they were running.” He examined the tracks more closely. “Wait a moment. This boot pattern. I recognize it! It’s Murdock’s! He’s escaped!”

A cold dread filled the two men. Murdock, loose on an island with unknown inhabitants, was a danger beyond calculation.

“We have to go after him!” the detective exclaimed. “There’s no telling what mischief he’ll stir up.”

“Agreed,” said Frank grimly. “He has an hour’s head start. We must move quickly.”

They followed the trail, which wound its way up a steep ridge. The jungle was thick and alive with the sounds of strange birds and unseen creatures. After a grueling half hour of climbing, they reached the summit. From there, they could see the other side of the island.

What they saw made them gasp in astonishment.

Below them, nestled in a large, natural harbor, was a ramshackle settlement of wooden huts and tents. And anchored in the harbor were two ships. One was a grimy, modern-looking steam tramp. The other was a three-masted schooner, and from its mainmast fluttered a flag that sent a chill down their spines. It was black, and emblazoned upon it was a white skull and crossbones.

“Pirates!” Tom Reynard whispered, his voice hoarse. “By heaven, Frank, we’ve landed on a pirate stronghold!”

Frank’s face was pale. “And Murdock is heading straight for them.”

They watched, horrified, as they saw a figure emerge from the jungle below and walk boldly into the pirate camp. It was Martin Murdock. They saw him accosted by a group of swarthy, evil-looking men armed with cutlasses and pistols. Murdock gestured wildly, pointing back in the direction of the Pegasus. He was talking to them, pleading, bargaining.

Soon, a large, bearded man with a captain’s hat and a brace of pistols tucked into his sash came out of the largest hut. This was clearly the leader. Murdock spoke with him for a long time. The pirate captain seemed skeptical at first, then intrigued, and finally, a greedy smile spread across his face. He clapped Murdock on the back and shouted orders to his men.

A great commotion filled the pirate camp. Men began grabbing weapons, forming into a large, unruly mob.

“He’s done it,” Frank said, his voice barely a whisper. “He’s told them about the Pegasus. He’s promised them a share of the prize if they help him capture us.”

“And they’ve taken the bait,” the detective added. “They’re coming for the airship. We have to get back and warn Barney and Pomp!”

The two friends turned and raced back down the trail, their hearts pounding with a new and terrible urgency. They burst out of the jungle onto the beach, shouting a warning.

“Barney! Pomp! To arms! We’re about to be attacked!”

The Irishman and the coon needed no second telling. They grabbed their rifles and took up positions behind a pile of wreckage, their faces set and determined.

“Pirates, is it?” Barney growled. “Let the divils come! They’ll get a warmer welcome than they’re bargainin’ for!”

Frank and Tom joined them, checking the loads in their own repeating rifles. There was no time to continue repairs, no time to do anything but prepare for a desperate fight.

They did not have long to wait. A roar of brutal voices echoed from the jungle, and a moment later, the pirate horde burst onto the beach. There were at least fifty of them, a savage-looking crew from every corner of the globe, armed to the teeth. At their head, a triumphant, sneering look on his face, was Martin Murdock.

“There they are!” Murdock shrieked, pointing at the four defenders. “Seize them! Kill them if you must, but the flying ship is mine! It’s ours!”

The pirate captain, a giant of a man with a scarred face, drew a long cutlass.

“At them, my lads!” he bellowed. “A fortune for every man, and the devil’s own chariot for our prize! Charge!”

With a bloodcurdling yell, the pirates surged forward across the sand.

The crack of four repeating rifles split the air, a sound far sharper and more deadly than the pirates’ chaotic roar. The front rank of the charging horde faltered as if they had run into a solid wall. Half a dozen of the swarthy villains threw their hands in the air and pitched face-first into the white sand, their charge ending before it had truly begun.

“Give it to thim, b’ys!” Barney roared, working the lever of his rifle with practiced speed. “Here’s a taste of American lead for yez black-hearted scoundrels!”

Another volley rang out. More pirates fell. The mob, so confident a moment before, slowed to a confused halt just out of pistol range. They were armed with cutlasses, flintlocks, and a motley assortment of antique firearms, weapons no match for the rapid and accurate fire of the aeronauts.

Murdock, safely behind the main body of pirates, shrieked in a fury. “What are you stopping for, you fools? There are only four of them! Rush them! Overwhelm them!”

The giant pirate captain, whose name they later learned was Blackheart Bill, snarled at his men. “He’s right, ye lily-livered curs! Are ye afraid of a few pops from a rifle? There’s a king’s ransom on that flying machine! Forward!”

He shoved one of his own men stumbling into the open. Tom Reynard’s rifle cracked, and the man fell with a cry. This only enraged the pirate captain further.

“Fire, you dogs! Give them a volley!” he bellowed.

A ragged eruption of smoke and flame burst from the pirates’ line. An assortment of lead balls whizzed through the air. One struck the wreckage near Frank’s head, sending splinters flying. Another hummed past Pomp’s ear.

“Golly, dat was too close for comfort!” the brave coon exclaimed, calmly drawing a bead on a pirate reloading his pistol. He fired, and the man spun around and dropped his weapon.

“Good shootin’, Pomp, me lad!” cried Barney. “We’ll make a soldier out of ye yet!”

The pirates, encouraged by their own noise, began to advance again, this time spreading out, using the sparse clumps of dune grass for what little cover they could find.

Frank knew this was a battle they could not win by attrition. They had a limited supply of cartridges, while the pirates had a seemingly endless supply of men.

“Tom, we have to get the Pegasus in the air!” he said, firing another shot that sent a pirate scrambling back. “They’ll surround us if we stay here!”

“The repairs, Frank!” the detective shouted back over the din. “Are they finished?”

“Not entirely! The new cross-shaft for the auxiliary gyros isn’t seated! She’ll be unstable, but she might fly! I need to get to her!”

“It’s too risky! They’ll cut you down before you’re halfway there!”

“We have no choice!” Frank replied, his jaw set. “You, Barney, and Pomp give me covering fire! I’m going to make a run for it!”

“Masther Frank, no!” Pomp cried. “Let me go!”

“I’m the only one who can fix it, Pomp. Keep firing! Don’t let them get a clear shot at me!”

Without another word, the young inventor took a deep breath, crouched low, and burst from behind their barricade. He sprinted across the open sand, a desperate, lonely figure against the vastness of the beach.

“There goes one!” Murdock screeched, pointing. “Shoot him! Don’t let him reach the machine!”

A dozen muskets and pistols were leveled at the running youth.

“Not on my watch, ye divils!” Barney bellowed. He, Tom, and Pomp unleashed a furious fusillade, their rifles working like clockwork. The pirates who were taking aim at Frank suddenly found themselves under a hail of lead. They ducked and scattered, their shots going wild.

Frank scrambled up the boarding ladder of the Pegasus and threw himself over the rail, landing hard on the deck. He was safe for the moment. He didn’t waste a second, plunging through the door of the deck-house and down into the engine room.

Back on the beach, the fight grew more intense. Seeing their prey had reached the airship, the pirates redoubled their efforts to storm the remaining three defenders.

Blackheart Bill, enraged at the resilience of his quarry, roared an order. “Bring up the swivel gun from the longboat! And you lot, flank them! Get into the jungle and come at them from the side!”

Tom Reynard saw the danger at once. A group of a dozen pirates was breaking off from the main force and running toward the line of trees.

“Barney! Pomp! To your right!” he warned. “They’re trying to get behind us!”

“Let thim try!” the Irishman retorted, turning his attention to the new threat. “They’ll find more than coconuts in that jungle!”

Meanwhile, in the engine room of the Pegasus, Frank worked with a desperate haste. His hands flew over the machinery, his mind a whirlwind of calculations. The unseated cross-shaft was the problem; without it, the delicate balance of the airship’s gyroscopic stabilizers would fail, and she would handle like a wounded bird.

He grabbed a heavy wrench and a mallet. He had to hammer the shaft into its brace, a delicate operation that required both force and precision. Outside, the sounds of the battle seemed to grow louder, closer. He could hear the triumphant yells of the pirates and the defiant shouts of his friends.

Bang! Bang! Bang!

He hammered at the shaft, sweat pouring from his brow. The metal shrieked in protest. One more solid strike. He raised the mallet.

Just then, the entire airship shuddered violently. A heavy thud echoed from the hull.

“What was that?” he muttered.

He ran to a porthole and peered out. Down on the beach, he saw the cause. Several pirates had dragged a small cannon, a swivel gun, from one of their boats and were loading it with scrap metal and rocks. The first shot had struck the Pegasus low on the hull, leaving a noticeable dent.

“They’re going to tear us apart!” Frank gasped.

He had no more time for finesse. With a cry of exertion, he swung the mallet with all his might. There was a loud, satisfying clang as the cross-shaft seated itself firmly in the brace. It was a rough fix, but it would have to do.

He scrambled to the control panel, his heart pounding. His fingers flew across the levers and switches, diverting power from the damaged dynamo to the main helices.

“Come on, old girl,” he whispered. “Fly for me.”

Slowly, with a deep groan of protesting metal, the giant helices above the deck began to turn. They moved sluggishly at first, then picked up speed, their hum growing into a powerful roar.

On the beach, the sound was a thunderclap of doom to the pirates and a chorus of angels to the three defenders.

“He’s done it!” Tom Reynard cried with relief. “He’s got her started!”

“Hooray!” yelled Barney. “Up the rebels! Now we’ll show thim!”

The Pegasus shuddered and began to lift from the sand, slowly, unsteadily. It rose a foot, then five, then ten.

“Back to the ship!” Tom ordered. “Quickly, before they cut us off!”

The three friends broke from their cover and made a mad dash for the dangling boarding ladder. The pirates, seeing their ultimate prize about to escape, let out a collective howl of rage and surged forward in a final, desperate charge.

Blackheart Bill was at their head, his cutlass raised, his face a mask of fury. Murdock followed close behind, his features twisted with greed and frustration.

Tom Reynard was the first to reach the ladder, and he scrambled up quickly. Pomp was right behind him.

“Hurry, Barney!” Pomp shouted down.

The Irishman was last, turning to fire one last defiant shot into the charging mob. A pirate went down, but the delay was costly. As Barney grabbed the lowest rung of the ladder and the Pegasus continued to rise, Blackheart Bill reached him.

The giant captain swung his cutlass in a vicious arc.

“I’ll have one of ye, at least!” he roared.

Barney tried to swing his legs up, but he was not quick enough. The heavy blade sliced across his calf. The Irishman cried out in pain, his grip on the ladder faltering.

“Barney!” Tom and Pomp screamed from the deck above.

They grabbed Barney’s arms and hauled with all their strength. Below, the pirate captain tried to grab Barney’s legs, but the airship was rising too fast. He was forced to leap back, shaking his fist at the escaping vessel.

They dragged the wounded Irishman over the rail and onto the deck. Blood was pouring from a wicked gash on his leg.

“Bejabbers,” Barney groaned, clutching the wound. “The big divil got me.”

“Pomp, get the medicine kit!” Frank ordered, emerging from the deck-house. His face was grim as he saw his friend’s injury. The Pegasus was now a hundred feet in the air, but she was wobbling dangerously, the makeshift repair making her difficult to control.

Below, the pirates were in a state of chaos. They fired their pistols and muskets uselessly at the ascending airship. Murdock was running among them, gesticulating wildly, his face a picture of apoplectic rage.

“Don’t let them escape!” he shrieked. “The schooner! Get to the schooner! Use the cannons!”

At his urging, Blackheart Bill turned and bellowed orders. The pirates abandoned their attack on the now-empty beach and scrambled for their longboats, rowing furiously toward the three-masted schooner anchored in the cove.

“They mean to fight us with their ship’s guns,” Tom said, tearing a strip of cloth to make a tourniquet for Barney’s leg.

“They will find us to be a harder target than they think,” Frank replied, his eyes flashing with anger. He strode to the control pedestal on the open deck. “Pomp, tend to Barney. Tom, man the signal gun! Load it with a bomb!”

“A bomb, Frank?”

“Yes! The small incendiary one! I don’t intend to sink them. I just intend to teach them a lesson about attacking the Pegasus!”

The detective’s eyes lit up. He hurried to the small, brass cannon mounted near the bow and unlocked the ammunition locker.

The airship, now five hundred feet up, circled over the cove. Frank fought with the controls, the vessel lurching and dipping like a wild thing. Below, the pirates swarmed onto the deck of their schooner. They uncovered the hatches on two large cannons and began the frantic work of loading and aiming them.

“They’re almost ready to fire, Masther Frank!” Pomp called out, having finished bandaging Barney’s leg as best he could.

“Are you ready, Tom?” Frank shouted.

“Ready!” the detective called back, aiming the signal gun carefully.

“Wait for my mark! I’m going to take us in low. It will be a dangerous pass!”

Frank pushed the control levers forward. The Pegasus, which had been circling uncertainly, suddenly dove. It plunged toward the pirate schooner like a hawk upon a sparrow. The pirates on the deck below looked up, their faces filled with alarm at the terrifying sight of the immense machine rushing down upon them.

“Fire!” Blackheart Bill screamed.

Boom!

One of the ship’s cannons went off. A nine-pound ball whistled through the air, passing just beneath the hull of the Pegasus with a terrifying roar. The wind of its passage made the airship rock violently.

“Now, Tom! Fire!” Frank yelled.

Tom pulled the lanyard on the signal gun. With a sharp crack, a small, finned projectile shot from the muzzle. It flew in a perfect arc and landed squarely on the wooden deck of the schooner, near the mainmast.

For a moment, nothing happened. The pirates stared at the small, smoking object. Then, with a brilliant flash, it erupted. A shower of white-hot chemical fire sprayed across the deck, instantly igniting the dry wood, the tarred ropes, and the canvas of a nearby sail.

Screams of pain and terror rose from the schooner. The pirates beat at the flames with their hands, their jackets, anything they could find. The fire spread with unnatural speed.

“Direct hit!” Tom shouted in triumph.

“It’s not over yet!” Frank warned. “They have another cannon!”

He wrestled with the controls, trying to bring the unstable Pegasus about for another pass. As he did, the second pirate cannon fired. This time, their aim was better.

Crash!

The cannonball tore through one of the large, slowly spinning helices. There was a horrible sound of grinding, snapping metal. The entire airship lurched violently to one side, throwing everyone to the deck. Alarms blared from the engine room.

“We’re hit!” Frank cried, struggling back to the controls. “Helix four is gone! We’re losing altitude!”

The Pegasus began to sink, spinning in a sickening spiral. Below, the pirate ship was a scene of chaos, the fire now raging out of control and climbing the mainmast. But the pirates, seeing the airship crippled, let out a cheer.

Frank fought the controls, his knuckles white. He shut down the engine power to the broken helix, diverting everything to the remaining three. The spin slowed, but they were still falling.

“Lighten the ship!” he shouted. “Throw anything you can overboard!”

Tom and Pomp, clinging to the rail for support, began tossing spare coils of rope, crates of supplies, anything that wasn't bolted down, over the side.

“Where is Murdock?” Tom yelled, scanning the deck of the burning ship below.

Through the smoke, they saw him. The arch-villain, seeing the pirates’ vessel was doomed and the airship was crashing, had made for the far side of the schooner. He climbed the rail and dove into the clear water of the cove. He was abandoning his allies and making a swim for the shore.

“He’s getting away again!” Tom growled in frustration.

“We have bigger problems!” Frank grunted, sweat beading on his forehead. “I can’t hold her! We’re going to crash!”

The jungle-covered shoreline rushed up to meet them. Frank pulled back on the altitude lever with all his strength. The remaining helices screamed in protest, but their descent slowed from a fatal plunge to a terrifying, controlled crash.

With a tremendous series of cracking and snapping sounds, the Pegasus plowed into the upper canopy of the jungle. Giant trees broke and splintered under its weight. The mighty airship groaned and shuddered, and then, with one final, deafening crunch, came to rest, tangled in the treetops some fifty feet from the ground.

Silence fell, broken only by the drip of water from a ruptured tank and the distant, angry shouts from the burning ship in the cove.

For a long moment, no one moved. They were alive.

Frank was the first to pick himself up, his body aching. “Is everyone all right?”

“I think so,” Tom answered, helping Pomp to his feet.

“Me leg is throbbin’ something fierce,” Barney moaned from where he lay, “but I’m still in one piece, thanks to you lads.”

The Pegasus was canted at a precarious angle, its hull pierced in several places by thick branches. The engine was silent, the helices still. They were stranded, shipwrecked in the sky.

Frank surveyed the damage with a heavy heart. “She’s hurt badly. It will take weeks to repair her, even if we have the materials, which we don’t.”

“And what of the pirates?” Tom asked, peering through a break in the leaves.

In the cove, the schooner was now an inferno from bow to stern. The remaining pirates were leaping into the sea to escape the flames, swimming for the same shore as Murdock.

“They’re stranded here with us,” Frank said grimly. “And they will not be in a forgiving mood.”

“Nor will Murdock,” Tom added. “He knows we’re crippled. He’ll rally those cutthroats and come for us again as soon as they regroup.”

“Then we must make this wreck our fortress,” Frank declared, his inventor’s spirit rising to the new challenge. “They can’t climb up here easily. We have rifles, and we have the high ground. Pomp, see what food and water supplies survived the crash. Tom, help me rig some defensive positions. Barney, you stay put and act as our lookout.”

“Aye, Frank,” the Irishman said, his pain forgotten in the face of the new danger. “The divil himself won’t set foot on this ship while Barney O’Shea is on watch!”

As night began to fall, the castaways on the Pegasus worked tirelessly. They were trapped in the heart of a hostile jungle, on an island controlled by pirates, with their magnificent airship a broken wreck. Their enemy, Martin Murdock, was still at large, more desperate and dangerous than ever. Yet, as he looked at the determined faces of his friends, Frank felt not despair, but a renewed resolve. They had survived. And as long as they were together, the adventure was not over. The hunt for Martin Murdock would continue.
