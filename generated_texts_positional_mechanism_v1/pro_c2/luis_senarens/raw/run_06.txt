The captain’s only reply was a shouted order. A sailor with a lit torch touched it to the cannon’s fuse. There was a deafening boom, and a cannonball hurtled through the air.

Frank, however, was ready. He had anticipated the shot and had already sent the Pegasus rising swiftly. The cannonball passed harmlessly beneath them, splashing into the sea a hundred yards away.

“Too slow!” Frank muttered. He brought the airship around in a wide circle, well out of the cannon’s range of elevation. “They can’t aim high enough if we stay directly above them.”

He positioned the Pegasus directly over the center of the steamer. Looking down, he could see the frustrated sailors trying to reload their weapon.

“Now is our chance to end this!” Frank said. He turned to his friends. “They want a fight? We will give them one they will not soon forget. But I do not wish to sink their ship or cause needless loss of life. I have a better idea.”

He hurried into the deck-house and went to a large locker marked “SPECIAL ORDNANCE.” From it, he produced a number of glass globes filled with a thick, yellowish liquid.

“What are those, Masther Frank?” Barney asked, peering at the globes curiously.

“My latest invention, Barney,” Frank replied with a grim smile. “I call them ‘slumber bombs.’ They are filled with a potent but harmless anesthetic gas. Upon impact, the glass shatters and the gas is released. Anyone who breathes it will be rendered unconscious for about an hour.”

“Holy smoke! A bomb that puts folks to sleep!” said Tom. “That’s brilliant, Frank!”

“We shall see if it works as well in practice as it does in theory,” the young inventor said. “Pomp, you and Barney will drop these on the deck. Aim for the area around the cannon and the bridge. Tom, you will remain here and guard Murdock.”

“Yis, sor!”

“You got it, Marse Frank!”

Barney and Pomp each took several of the glass globes. They went to the rail, positioning themselves on either side of the airship’s deck.

“Ready?” Frank called.

“Ready!” they replied in unison.

“Then let them have it!”

Barney and Pomp hurled the slumber bombs downward. The glass globes plummeted through the air and shattered on the deck of the Rover below. Puffs of thick, yellow smoke instantly billowed up, enveloping the ship’s bow and bridge.

The effect was immediate and dramatic. The sailors who were reloading the cannon suddenly stopped, looked around in confusion, and then one by one, they slumped to the deck, fast asleep. On the bridge, the defiant captain staggered out of the wheelhouse, clutching at his throat. He took two steps, swayed, and then fell forward, unconscious.

Within a minute, the entire deck of the Rover was littered with the sleeping forms of its crew. The steamer, its wheel untended, began to drift aimlessly in the water.

“It worked!” Frank cried in triumph. “It worked perfectly!”

“Begorra, it’s like magic!” Barney cheered, slapping his thigh. “They’re all takin’ a nap!”

Frank brought the Pegasus down lower, to within fifty feet of the silent steamer. He picked up his speaking trumpet again, though he doubted anyone could hear him.

“Is there anyone still conscious aboard the Rover?” he called.

There was no reply, only the gentle lapping of the waves against the ship’s hull. The victory was total and complete.

“Now what, Frank?” Tom asked. “We can’t just leave them like this.”

“No, we can’t,” Frank agreed. “The gas will wear off in an hour. They will wake up with headaches, but they will be unharmed.” He thought for a moment. “We will wait. When they awaken, they will find their spirit for a fight is quite gone. We will leave them with a warning and be on our way.”

As Frank predicted, just under an hour later, the crew of the Rover began to stir. They sat up, groaning and holding their heads, looking around in bewilderment.

The captain was the first to regain his full senses. He looked at his sleeping crew, then at the still-hovering Pegasus, and his face paled. He knew he was beaten.

Frank’s voice boomed down once more.

“Captain! You have had a taste of our power! It is a power I used with restraint. Next time, I may not be so merciful. You will take your ship to the nearest port and turn yourselves over to the authorities. If you attempt to follow us or continue your voyage to San Francisco, I promise you, we will find you, and we will sink your vessel without a second thought. Do you understand me?”

The humbled captain looked up. The fight was gone from his eyes. “Aye!” he croaked, his voice hoarse. “We understand! We won’t trouble you again!”

“See that you don’t,” Frank said. With a final, warning glance, he turned the Pegasus away. He set a course for Ceylon, where they could hand their prisoner over to the British authorities.

Below decks, in his locked cabin, Martin Murdock had heard the entire battle. He had listened to the cannon shot, hoping it would bring his liberation. Instead, he had heard the shouts of triumph from Frank’s crew, and then a profound silence. He knew his last hope of escape was gone.

When Tom Reynard entered his cabin with a plate of food, Murdock looked up, his eyes full of bitter resentment.

“So, you’ve won,” he sneered. “You and your boy inventor with his flying toys.”

“That’s right, Murdock,” Tom said calmly. “It’s over. You should have known you could never outrun Frank Reade Jr.”

“Every man has his price, detective,” Murdock hissed, leaning forward. “Name yours. Ten thousand dollars. Twenty thousand. I can have it for you in gold. We can land this machine, and you and I can disappear. Reade will never find us.”

Tom Reynard simply laughed. “You still don’t get it, do you, Murdock? Some things aren’t for sale. Justice is one of them. Now eat your dinner. It’s a long flight to Ceylon, and a longer prison sentence after that.”

The detective left, locking the door securely behind him. Murdock stared at the food, his appetite gone. He slumped back on his bunk, a man truly and finally defeated.

Up on deck, the mood was jubilant. Barney and Pomp were singing a lively sea shanty, and even the normally serious Tom Reynard was smiling. The Pegasus flew smoothly through the clear evening sky, a silver marvel against the backdrop of a thousand stars. Frank stood at the wheel, his hand resting on the polished brass. He looked out at the peaceful sea below and the vast expanse of the heavens above. The villain was captured, his cronies defeated. The world, for the moment, was safe. The young inventor allowed himself a small, satisfied smile. Another adventure was over, but he knew, as the engine of the clouds carried them onward, that a new one was always just over the horizon.

CHAPTER XII.
A NEW FOE IN THE SKY.

The Pegasus flew on, a silver speck against the velvet blackness of the night sky. On her deck, a spirit of victory reigned. Pomp, his face split by a wide grin, had gone to the galley to prepare a celebratory feast of fried chicken and corn dodgers. Barney, his fiddle tucked under his chin, was scraping out a lively jig that had Tom Reynard tapping his foot, a rare smile on his usually stern face.

“Begorra, Masther Frank, but we gave thim pirates a taste of what for!” the Irishman chortled, pausing his tune. “They won’t be botherin’ honest folk agin in a hurry, I’ll be bound.”

“You’re right about that, Barney,” Frank replied from the pilot-house. He had set the airship on a steady course for Ceylon and was now monitoring the instruments. “The Rover was a formidable vessel, but no match for the Pegasus.”

“Thanks to your genius, my boy,” said Tom Reynard, stepping into the pilot-house. “That electric volley was a master stroke. It disabled them without sinking them, just as you intended.”

“It’s a terrible power, Tom,” Frank said, his expression turning serious. “One I hope to never use to its fullest extent. But against villains like Murdock and his hired thugs, we have little choice.”

“Speaking of Murdock,” the detective said, his voice lowering, “I checked on him. He’s in a black mood, but secure. He tried to bribe me, of course.”

“The scoundrel!” cried Frank. “He believes every man has a price.”

“He is learning a hard lesson, then,” Tom said with grim satisfaction. “His journey ends in a prison cell.”

The conversation was interrupted by Pomp, who appeared at the door balancing a tray laden with food.

“Dinner am served, Marse Frank!” he announced cheerfully. “Got some fine victuals to celebrate puttin’ dat scamp Murdock in his place!”

The crew gathered in the main cabin, the mood light and triumphant. They were safe, their enemy was captured, and a great wrong was about to be put right. As they ate, they recounted the highlights of the battle, laughing at the memory of the Rover’s crew scattering in panic.

An hour passed. The empty plates were cleared away and Pomp and Barney took the watch, their voices a low murmur on the deck. Frank retired to his workshop in the forward part of the deck-house, a space filled with whirring gears, glass tubes, and intricate brass mechanisms. He was not a man to rest on his laurels. He was already at work on a new invention, a device he called a ‘kinetoscope-recorder’, designed to capture moving pictures of their adventures.

Tom Reynard sat in the cabin, reviewing his notes on the Murdock case. All was quiet, save for the steady hum of the dynamo and the whisper of the wind past the hull. The Pegasus was an island of tranquility in the vast, starry ocean of the sky.

It was Barney who first noticed something was amiss. He was peering through a long brass telescope, scanning the horizon as was his habit.

“Saints presarve us!” he muttered, squinting. “What in tarnation is that?”

Pomp looked up from polishing the deck rail. “What you see, Barney?”

“I’m not shure,” the Celt replied, his voice filled with puzzlement. “It looks like a star, but it’s movin’ too fast. And it’s gettin’ bigger!”

He swung the telescope to follow the object. It was a pinprick of light, but it was indeed growing in size and intensity at an alarming rate. It was not a meteor, for its course was steady and purposeful.

“Pomp, go fetch Masther Frank!” Barney said, his voice now tight with alarm. “Hurry, lad! There’s somethin’ comin’ at us, and it’s comin’ like the divil himself is at the helm!”

Pomp, seeing the gravity in his friend’s expression, did not hesitate. He dropped his polishing cloth and dashed for the workshop. A moment later, Frank Reade Jr. emerged, wiping a spot of grease from his hands. Tom Reynard was right behind him.

“What is it, Barney?” Frank asked, striding to the rail.

“Look there, sor!” Barney pointed. “Off the port bow!”

The light was now visible to the naked eye, a brilliant white gleam that pulsed with an unnatural rhythm. It was closing the distance with incredible speed.

Frank grabbed the telescope from Barney’s hands and put it to his eye. What he saw made his blood run cold. It was not a star, nor any known type of heavenly body. It was an airship!

But it was like no airship he had ever seen or imagined. It was long and black, shaped like a monstrous bat, with vast, membranous wings that seemed to drink the moonlight. It had no visible helices or propellers, yet it cut through the air with a silent, deadly grace. A single, powerful searchlight, the source of the approaching gleam, shone from its prow like the eye of a predator.

“Great Scott!” Frank exclaimed, lowering the telescope. “Tom, Barney! To your stations! Arm yourselves!”

“What is it, Frank?” Tom Reynard asked, his hand already reaching for the rifle he always kept near.

“Another airship!” Frank replied, his voice tense. “And by the looks of it, she’s not on a friendly visit! Pomp, turn our searchlight on them!”

The powerful electric beam of the Pegasus sliced through the night, illuminating the approaching craft. The sight was even more menacing up close. The hull was made of a dull black metal, with sharp, cruel angles. There were no windows, only narrow slits that might be for observation or for firing weapons. It was a flying fortress, a machine built for war.

As the light of the Pegasus struck it, a hatch opened on the underside of the bat-winged ship. A large cone, like the bell of a gigantic gramophone, descended. From it, a voice boomed across the night air, amplified by some powerful electric device. The voice was cold, precise, and carried a distinct German accent.

“Attention, airship Pegasus! You are intercepted by the imperial sky-cruiser Fledermaus! I am Baron von Hess!”

Frank stepped forward to the rail, his face set like granite. “This is the American airship Pegasus, Frank Reade Jr. commanding! State your intentions, Baron!”

“My intentions are simple, Herr Reade!” the voice boomed back. “I am here for the fugitive, Martin Murdock. I am also here for your magnificent vessel. You will surrender both to me at once!”

Frank’s eyes flashed with anger. “You are mistaken, Baron! Murdock is our prisoner, and the Pegasus surrenders to no one! Turn back now, or you will face the consequences!”

A cold, metallic laugh echoed from the Fledermaus. “Folly! You are a clever boy, Reade, but you are a boy nonetheless. You cannot comprehend the power you face. This is your final warning. Surrender, or be destroyed!”

“Never!” Frank shouted back. “Barney, Pomp, Tom! Prepare to repel boarders! They mean to take us!”

“Bejabers, they’ll have a fight on their hands if they do!” Barney roared, cocking his rifle.

But the Baron had no intention of boarding. Not yet.

From the prow of the Fledermaus, a strange apparatus extended, a collection of brass spheres and copper coils that crackled with blue energy.

“Look out!” Frank yelled. “He’s got some kind of electric weapon!”

With a sound like the sky ripping in two, a bolt of pure white energy leaped from the Baron’s ship and struck the Pegasus. It was not a projectile, but a guided stream of lightning that enveloped the airship in a blinding corona of electrical fire.

The lights on the Pegasus flickered and died. The steady hum of the dynamo devolved into a tortured screeching, and the mighty helices, starved of power, began to slow. The airship shuddered violently and began to lose altitude.

“He’s overloaded the dynamo!” Frank cried, struggling to keep his footing on the tilting deck. “Tom, try to return fire!”

Tom Reynard raised his rifle and fired, the shot a pathetic pop in the face of the electrical onslaught. The bullet sparked harmlessly off the black hull of the Fledermaus.

“It’s no use!” the detective shouted over the roar. “The hull must be an inch thick of steel!”

“Pomp! Man the signal gun!” Frank ordered. “Load it with a bomb! Aim for his weapon!”

The brave coon scrambled toward the small cannon mounted on the foredeck. Barney, seeing his intent, rushed to help him. Together, they wrestled one of the powerful explosive bombs into the breech of the gun.

Another bolt of energy struck the Pegasus, this one even more powerful than the first. The deck plates groaned, and a shower of sparks erupted from the pilot-house. The helices slowed further, their rotation becoming erratic and labored. The Pegasus was falling faster now, listing heavily to one side.

“Fire, Pomp, fire!” Frank yelled.

With a tremendous roar, the signal gun discharged its deadly payload. The bomb flew true, a small dark speck against the night sky, headed directly for the crackling apparatus on the Fledermaus.

Baron von Hess, watching from his command center, allowed himself a thin, cruel smile. “Primitive,” he sneered to his lieutenant. “Activate the magnetic repulsor shield.”

A low hum emanated from the Fledermaus. Just as the bomb was about to strike its target, it suddenly veered off course as if slapped aside by an invisible hand. It exploded a hundred yards away in a harmless flash of light and smoke.

A cry of despair went up from the deck of the Pegasus.

“Saints forgive us, it didn’t work!” wailed Barney.

“He’s got some kind of defensive shield!” Frank realized with a sinking heart. Their technology was hopelessly outmatched.

Down below, in his locked cabin, Martin Murdock had been thrown from his bunk by the violent lurching of the airship. He heard the crackle of the energy bolts and the roar of the cannon. A wild, desperate hope ignited in his chest. Someone was attacking the Pegasus! Someone had come for him!

He threw his shoulder against the cabin door, again and again, shouting for release. “Let me out! I can help! Let me out!”

No one paid him any mind. On deck, the situation was growing more desperate by the second. A third energy bolt struck, and this time the dynamo gave out completely with a final, grinding shriek. The helices stopped turning. The lights went out for good, plunging the deck into near total darkness, lit only by the terrible glare of the Fledermaus’s searchlight.

The Pegasus was now in a freefall, plunging toward the dark waters of the Indian Ocean thousands of feet below.

“We’re done for!” Barney cried, crossing himself.

“Not yet!” Frank’s voice cut through the panic, sharp and commanding. “Pomp! Barney! To the ballast vents! Stand by to release everything! Tom, take the wheel! Try to use the rudders to guide our fall!”

Though they were plummeting like a stone, the young inventor’s mind was racing, calculating, searching for any chance, however slim. He saw only one.

“Baron von Hess wants the Pegasus intact!” he shouted, his plan forming even as he spoke it. “He’ll stop firing now that we’re helpless! He’ll follow us down to claim his prize! That’s our only hope!”

As if to prove his words, the energy bolts ceased. The Fledermaus, its searchlight still fixed on them, began a slow, arrogant descent, shadowing the falling Pegasus.

“Stand by the vents!” Frank repeated. “We’re going to try to make a water landing! The hull is watertight! It might just hold!”

The dark surface of the sea rushed up to meet them with terrifying speed. The wind screamed past the silent airship.

“Now, Tom! Hard to starboard!” Frank yelled. “Try to hit the water at an angle! Barney, Pomp! Release the ballast!”

Tom Reynard spun the wheel, and the big air rudders caught the wind, slewing the massive vessel sideways. At the same moment, Barney and Pomp threw open the levers for the emergency ballast vents. Tons of sand and lead shot poured from the belly of the Pegasus, a desperate attempt to slow its descent.

It was not enough.

The Pegasus struck the water not with a splash, but with a cataclysmic impact that felt as if it had hit solid rock. The sound was a deafening combination of tearing metal and shattering wood. The entire structure of the mighty airship shuddered, and the deck-house groaned in protest. Water surged over the rails.

For a moment, Frank thought the hull had been breached, that they would sink like a stone to the bottom of the sea. But his design, born of genius and crafted with care, held true. The watertight compartments, though battered, remained sealed. The Pegasus, grievously wounded and half-submerged, floated.

The Fledermaus hovered a thousand feet above, its searchlight painting a merciless circle of light around the crippled airship. From the amplified speaker, the Baron’s voice came again, dripping with condescending triumph.

“A valiant effort, Herr Reade. But ultimately, futile. My men will be boarding you momentarily to take possession of my new property. Do not resist. It will only postpone the inevitable.”

Frank staggered to his feet, bruised and drenched. He looked at his small, loyal crew. Pomp was helping a dazed Barney to his feet. Tom Reynard was checking his rifle, his face a grim mask. They were alive, but they were trapped.

“He’s won,” Tom said quietly, his voice heavy with defeat.

“No,” Frank said, his jaw set. He looked up at the hovering bat-ship, then at the dark line of a nearby island coast, barely visible a mile away. “He hasn’t won yet. Not while we’re still breathing.” His mind was already working, formulating a new, impossibly daring plan. “Pomp,” he said in a low, urgent voice. “Do we have any power at all? Is there anything left in the secondary batteries?”

“I think so, Marse Frank,” the coon replied. “Dey ain’t connected to de main dynamo. Dey just for de interior lights and de small machines.”

“Good!” Frank’s eyes gleamed with a sudden, wild hope. “Listen closely, all of you. We have one last chance. It’s a desperate one, but it’s the only one we’ve got.”

He gathered his friends close as the first of the Baron’s launches began to lower from the belly of the Fledermaus. In the circle of the searchlight, they could see the armed men preparing to claim their prize. But Frank Reade Jr. was not a man to admit defeat, and the engine of the clouds, though broken, still had one final trick up its sleeve. The battle for the Pegasus was not over yet.

The Baron’s mocking words hung in the damp sea air. Below, the launches, filled with armed thugs, were already halfway to the water. Frank Reade Jr.’s mind, a mechanism more marvelous than any he had ever built, worked with lightning rapidity.

“It’s the batteries, Pomp! The secondary storage cells!” Frank cried, his voice cutting through the despair. “They power the internal lights, but their potential is far greater! We can divert that current!”

“Golly, Marse Frank, what you mean?” asked Pomp, his eyes wide.

“The hull! It’s a solid-steel conductor! If we can connect the main terminals of the batteries to the outer plating, we can turn the entire Pegasus into a gigantic electric eel!”

Tom Reynard’s grim face broke into a flicker of a smile. “You mean to shock them?”

“Precisely! They expect a fight with bullets, not with volts! Pomp, you know the circuitry better than I do. Can you get to the secondary battery junction in the aft hold and run a heavy cable to the main frame of the ship?”

“I can do dat, Marse Frank!” the coon declared, his spirit reviving instantly. “It’ll take a few minutes, but I can make dat connection good and strong!”

“Go, then! And be quick about it!” Frank urged. “Barney, Tom! Take your positions behind the rail. Do not fire until I give the word. We must lure them into touching the ship. Make them think we are too stunned to resist.”

“Yis, sor!” the Celt replied, gripping his Winchester. “We’ll give thim divils a proper welcome.”

Pomp scrambled away toward the aft hatch and disappeared into the gloom of the water-logged lower decks. Frank joined Tom and Barney at the starboard rail, peering down at the approaching boats. There were two of them, each carrying a dozen men armed with rifles and grappling hooks. They rowed with a confident, practiced rhythm, their faces illuminated by the harsh glare of the Fledermaus’s searchlight.

“They’re getting close, Frank,” Tom Reynard said in a low voice.

“Let them,” Frank answered, his teeth gritted. “The more confident they are, the greater the surprise.”

From the hovering bat-ship, the Baron’s voice boomed once more. “You see, Herr Reade? It is hopeless. My men are boarders of the highest caliber. They have taken many a vessel. Surrender your rifles and you will be treated with a modicum of civility.”

Frank made no reply. He kept his eyes fixed on the lead boat. It was now only twenty yards away. A burly ruffian stood in the prow, a heavy, three-pronged grappling hook in his hands.

“Ready to throw the hook!” the man yelled to his companions.

“Hold your fire,” Frank whispered to his friends.

With a mighty heave, the man in the boat swung the grappling iron. It sailed through the air in a graceful arc and clanged loudly against the steel plating of the Pegasus, its sharp tines biting into the metal just below the rail. Another hook followed from the second boat, securing itself nearby.

“Now, up you go, lads!” a voice commanded from the launch. “The Baron wants this thing taken intact!”

The first man, the one who threw the hook, grabbed the thick rope and began to haul himself up, his heavy boots finding purchase on the side of the hull. Another man followed him on the same rope, while two more began ascending the second line.

Frank glanced anxiously toward the aft hatch. “Pomp! Are you ready?” he shouted.

A muffled cry came from below. “Just one more second, Marse Frank! De connection is almost——there! It’s done!”

The first boarder was now just feet from the rail. A cruel grin was on his face as he reached a hand up to grab the top of the bulwark.

“Now, Pomp!” Frank roared. “Throw the switch!”

Deep within the ship, there was a loud hum and the snap of a heavy-duty circuit breaker being closed. At that exact instant, the Pegasus came alive. A brilliant blue corona of St. Elmo’s fire erupted along every metal edge, and a crackling, sizzling sound filled the air.

The man touching the rail let out a shriek that was barely human. His body convulsed violently, his muscles locked in a terrifying spasm. For a moment he was frozen there, silhouetted against the night, a conduit for thousands of volts of raw electrical power. Then, with a final, choked gasp, he was thrown backward, tumbling through the air to land in his boat with a sickening thud.

The other three men climbing the ropes cried out in agony as the current surged through the wet ropes and the metal hooks. They lost their grip and plunged into the sea, thrashing and screaming. The men still in the boats stared in horror, unable to comprehend what had just happened.

“What devilry is this?” one of them screamed.

“It is electrified! The whole ship is electrified!” another yelled in panic. “Get us away from here! Cut the lines!”

A man in the first boat, fumbling with a knife, tried to saw at the thick rope attached to the grappling hook. But the current was traveling down the damp hemp, and as his blade touched it, he too was seized by a violent shock and thrown against his comrades. Chaos reigned. The organized boarding party had devolved into a panicked mob.

“Retreat! Retreat!” the leader in the second boat shrieked, ordering his men to row for their lives. They needed no encouragement. The oarsmen pulled frantically, churning the water as they fled from the crackling, buzzing airship.

Frank, Barney, and Tom watched, a grim satisfaction on their faces.

“Begorra, that put the fear of God into thim!” Barney chuckled. “They jumped like frogs on a hot skillet!”

From above, the Baron’s voice, now stripped of its condescending tone and filled with pure rage, thundered down. “Incompetent fools! What are you doing? I ordered you to take the ship, not to flee from it like frightened children!”

“The ship—it is cursed!” a voice yelled back from one of the retreating launches. “It is surrounded by lightning!”

“Silence!” the Baron bellowed. “It is a trick! An electrical trick! Return to the Fledermaus at once! I will deal with Herr Reade myself!”

The launches scurried back to the belly of the bat-ship like spiders retreating to their web. The massive searchlight on the Fledermaus remained fixed upon the Pegasus, pinning it in its glare.

“That bought us some time,” Tom Reynard said, reloading his rifle. “But what now? He knows our trick. They’ll come back with rubber gloves and wooden ladders next time.”

“They will,” Frank agreed. “And we won’t have enough power in the batteries for a second shock of that magnitude. We used nearly everything we had.” He looked toward the dark smudge of the island on the horizon. “That is our only hope. We must get the Pegasus to that shore.”

“But how, Marse Frank?” Pomp asked, emerging from the hatch, his face streaked with grease. “De helices are dead, and we have no sails.”

Frank’s eyes scanned the deck of his crippled ship. His gaze fell upon the valves for the compressed air tanks; the same tanks used to power the pneumatic rudder controls and to start the massive dynamo engine. An idea, bold and perilous, formed in his mind.

“The compressed air!” he exclaimed. “We have thousands of pounds of pressure stored in those tanks! Pomp, is there a way to bypass the regulators and vent the main tanks directly through the ballast outlet pipes below the waterline?”

Pomp’s eyes lit up with understanding. “You mean to make a jet! Push de ship with air bubblin’ out de back! Golly, Marse Frank, I never thought of dat! Yes, sor! It would be a job to rig it. I’d have to cross-connect de main air lines to de ballast conduits. It would empty de tanks in minutes, but it might just work!”

“Then that is what we must do!” Frank declared. “The Baron is planning his second assault. That is our window of opportunity. Get to work, Pomp! Barney, you and Tom keep a watch on the Fledermaus. Let me know the instant they begin to lower another boat.”

“Aye, aye, sor!” said Barney.

Pomp, wasting no time, gathered a wrench and a coil of flexible high-pressure tubing and once more descended into the hold. The sounds of clanging metal and hissing valves soon echoed up from below. Frank paced the deck like a caged lion, his mind racing. The plan was audacious. If the pressure was too great, it could rupture the ballast pipes. If it was too little, they would remain a sitting duck.

Ten minutes passed. Then fifteen. Barney suddenly pointed up at the Fledermaus.

“Frank, look! They’re at it again!”

A new platform was being lowered from the bat-ship. This time, it was not a simple boat. It was a large, flat-bottomed raft, and upon it were men equipped with long wooden poles, coils of dry rope, and what looked like a small, deck-mounted cannon.

“He’s not taking any more chances,” Tom muttered. “He means to blast us from a distance and then send his men over on non-conductive ropes.”

“Pomp! How much longer?” Frank yelled down the hatch.

“Almost there, Marse Frank! Dis last valve is mighty stubborn!” came the reply, followed by a loud grunt and the screech of protesting metal. “Got it! I’m ready when you are!”

“Stand by!” Frank shouted. He turned to the detective. “Tom, when we start moving, that raft will be right on our tail. They will try to stop us.”

“We’ll give them a proper dissuasion,” Reynard said, patting the stock of his rifle.

“Here they come, sor!” Barney announced. The raft had detached from the Fledermaus and was now being propelled toward them by a half dozen men using long sweeps. It was slow but steady.

“They’re trying to get behind us,” Frank observed. “Out of the direct line of fire from the deck-house. It’s time. Pomp! Open the port-side aft valve! Half pressure at first!”

“Opening it now, Marse Frank!”

A deep, groaning shudder ran through the Pegasus. Then, a tremendous hissing sound erupted from beneath the waves at the stern of the airship. The water behind them began to boil and churn as if a sea monster were waking from its slumber. The massive vessel, which had been sitting dead in the water, slowly, almost imperceptibly, began to move.

A cry of astonishment went up from the men on the raft. Above, the Baron’s voice, amplified and distorted with disbelief, echoed across the water. “What new sorcery is this? They are moving! Stop them!”

“Full pressure, Pomp!” Frank roared.

Pomp obeyed. The hissing became a deafening roar. A huge plume of white, frothing water erupted from the stern, and the Pegasus surged forward, its speed increasing to a surprising three, then four knots. It was no match for a steamship, but it was faster than the raft.

“Hooray!” yelled Barney, waving his hat in the air. “We’re sailin’ on a bubble! Who’d have thought it!”

The crippled airship plowed through the water, leaving a wide, churning wake behind it. It was a strange and wonderful sight: the magnificent engine of the clouds, its helices still and silent, transformed into a makeshift speedboat, fleeing toward the dark shore of the island.

The men on the raft, recovering from their shock, redoubled their efforts at the sweeps, but they were losing ground. The man at the small cannon was frantically trying to aim the weapon at the fleeing Pegasus.

“Barney! Tom! Discourage that gunner!” Frank ordered.

Two rifles cracked almost simultaneously. A chip of wood flew from the cannon’s mounting. The gunner ducked down with a yelp of fear. He tried to aim again, and another bullet from Tom Reynard’s rifle whizzed past his ear. The man abandoned his post and took cover behind his comrades.

They were making steady progress toward the island. The dark line of the coast was growing larger, the sound of surf on a sandy beach now faintly audible. But their pursuers were tenacious. The raft, though slower, continued its pursuit.

Suddenly, a new sound split the air. A sharp, powerful report from the Fledermaus itself. A shell screamed past them and exploded in the water fifty yards ahead, sending up a colossal waterspout.

“He’s using his main guns!” Tom cried. “He’s given up on taking the ship intact!”

“He’s a poor shot in the dark, and we’re a moving target!” Frank shouted back, his face wet with spray. “He’s afraid to get lower for fear of running aground himself! We just need a little more time!”

Another shell screeched overhead, this one closer. It struck the water just off their stern, and the concussion rocked the Pegasus violently.

“De air pressure is dropping fast, Marse Frank!” Pomp yelled from the hold. “We ain’t got but another minute, maybe two!”

The shore was now tantalizingly close. Frank could make out individual palm trees silhouetted against the moonlit sky. He could also see a line of white water that marked a treacherous reef or sandbar.

“Head for the reef, Frank!” Tom advised. “We might ground ourselves, but that raft won’t be able to follow!”

“My thought exactly!” Frank yelled. He strained to see through the gloom, trying to pick a path. “Pomp! Give me everything you’ve got! Vent all remaining tanks!”

The roar from the stern intensified for one final, glorious burst. The Pegasus leaped forward like a startled horse, racing toward the line of breaking waves. The raft fell farther behind. The Baron, in a fit of pique, fired one last shell. This one came terrifyingly close, clipping the very tip of the aft mast and sending splinters raining down on the deck.

Then, with a deep, grinding shudder that ran the length of the hull, the Pegasus came to a sudden, jarring halt. They had hit the sandbar. The forward momentum drove the ship’s bow high up onto the sand, tilting the deck at a precarious angle. The roaring from the stern died away into a final, pathetic hiss. The compressed air was spent.

They were aground.

The crew picked themselves up, bruised but safe. They were a mere hundred yards from the beach. The pursuing raft, seeing the Pegasus lodged firmly in the shallows where they could not go, gave up the chase and turned back toward the Fledermaus.

Frank walked to the rail and looked up. The great bat-ship hovered directly overhead, its searchlight still holding them captive. They were trapped on the island, their vessel broken, but they were alive and out of the Baron’s immediate reach.

The amplified voice came down one last time, no longer yelling, but speaking in a low, menacing tone that was far more frightening.

“Very clever, Herr Reade. Very clever indeed. You have won this little skirmish. You have found a temporary sanctuary. But do not celebrate your victory. That island is my private game preserve. And now, you and your crew are the most dangerous game of all. I will hunt you down. There is no escape. Enjoy your final hours of freedom.”

The searchlight clicked off, plunging them into the moonlit darkness. The hum of the Fledermaus’s engines faded as the dark shape moved away, disappearing over the jungle-clad hills of the island’s interior.

Silence descended, broken only by the gentle lapping of waves against the stranded hull and the whisper of the wind through the palm trees on the shore.

“Well, now,” Barney said, breaking the quiet. “That sounds like a promisin’ invitation, don’t it?”

“We’re marooned,” Tom stated flatly.

“Yes,” Frank said, his eyes scanning the dark, mysterious jungle that awaited them. “We are marooned. But we are armed, and we are together.” He turned to his loyal friends, his face set with the old, indomitable resolve. “The Baron thinks he is the hunter. We will show him that the inventor, the detective, the Irishman, and the coon can be hunters, too. Our first task is to get ashore and find cover. Then, we will find a way to repair the Pegasus. This battle is far from over.”

With a grim nod, Frank’s companions armed themselves. The first order of business was to get what they could from the wounded Pegasus. She was a beacon on the sandbar, a clear target should the Baron change his mind and return.

“We move now!” Frank commanded. “Tom, you and I will gather the essential instruments and my schematics. Barney, Pomp, secure all the rifles, ammunition, and as much preserved food as you can carry. We must travel light, but we must be prepared.”

“Right yez are, Masther Frank!” Barney said, hefting a crate of cartridges. “We’ll give that Baron a reception he’ll not soon be forgettin’!”

“Golly, I hope dis food ain’t too heavy,” Pomp muttered, already loading his arms with tins of salted beef and hardtack biscuits.

They worked with the desperate haste of men who knew their lives depended upon it. Frank and Tom Reynard carefully packed the delicate chronometers, sextants, and the priceless, leather-bound folio containing all the young inventor’s plans. Without these, repairing the Pegasus would be an impossibility. Frank paused, his hand on the great dynamo wheel that had replaced the one that had shattered. It was intact. The problem was the hull and the propulsion.

Within twenty minutes, they were ready. Each man was loaded with a heavy pack and carried a rifle. They clambered over the tilted rail of the airship and slid down into the warm, waist-deep water of the shallows. Wading ashore, they felt the soft sand of the beach give way to the firmer ground at the edge of the jungle.

The sight that greeted them was one of savage and imposing grandeur. A wall of green, so dense it seemed woven from a giant’s loom, rose up before them. Great trees draped with thick, twisting vines soared a hundred feet into the air, their tops forming a canopy that blocked out the moon. The air was thick, humid, and alive with the chirps, clicks, and rustles of a million unseen creatures. A sweet, cloying scent of unknown blossoms mixed with the smell of damp earth and decay.

“Saints preserve us,” Barney whispered, his usual bravado momentarily quelled by the primeval spectacle. “It’s like the world before man was ever thought of.”

“The Baron called it his game preserve,” Tom Reynard said, his detective’s eyes scanning the impenetrable darkness of the undergrowth. “That implies two things. First, there is game. Second, he is the hunter. We must assume we are now the quarry.”

“Then we must find a den,” Frank declared, his resolve unshaken. “A defensible position. A cave, or a rocky outcrop. We cannot stay in the open. The jungle will provide cover. Let’s move inland, parallel to the coast for now. Keep your eyes open and your rifles ready.”

With Frank in the lead, they plunged into the oppressive gloom of the jungle. The transition from the open beach was jarring. The air grew still and heavy, and the noise of the surf was quickly replaced by the cacophony of the jungle night. Pomp, with his keen senses, took the rear, his head constantly swiveling.

They had traveled for what seemed an hour, the moonlight barely penetrating the thick canopy above. Their progress was slow, hacking through tangled creepers and navigating around the massive buttress roots of ancient trees. Frank was just about to call a halt for a brief rest when Barney, who was just ahead of him, stopped dead.

“Whist!” the Irishman hissed, holding up a hand. “Do yez hear that?”

They all froze, straining their ears against the din of insects. At first there was nothing. Then they heard it. A low, guttural sound. A deep, resonant growl that seemed to vibrate in their very bones. It was not the sound of a small predator. It was the sound of something large, powerful, and very close.

“Masther Frank!” Pomp’s voice was a terrified whisper from the back of the line. “Look! Up in de tree!”

All eyes snapped upward. There, on a thick branch not thirty feet above them, was a sight that chilled the blood. A pair of enormous, yellow-green eyes glowed in the darkness. They belonged to a massive panther, its black coat a patch of absolute void against the gloom. Its muscles were bunched, its long tail twitched, and a low growl rumbled in its chest as it prepared to spring.

“Don’t shoot!” Frank commanded in a low, urgent tone. “A single shot will give away our position to anyone listening! Tom, the flash-powder!”

The detective, ever prepared, was already fumbling in his pack. He produced a small, sealed packet.

“On my mark!” Frank said, his eyes locked on the predator. The panther let out a snarling cough and gathered its powerful haunches. “Now, Tom! Now!”

Tom Reynard ripped open the packet and flung the contents toward the base of the tree. Frank, in the same instant, discharged a specially designed cartridge from a pistol he carried. It contained no bullet, only a percussive cap. The cap struck the cloud of powder and a brilliant, searing flash of white light erupted with a deafening WHOOMP!

The panther, its night-vision completely shattered and its senses assaulted by the thunderous report, let out a high-pitched scream of rage and confusion. It lost its footing on the branch and tumbled to the jungle floor with a heavy thud. It did not wait to recover. Blinded and terrified, it crashed off into the undergrowth, its panicked flight snapping branches and trampling bushes.

“Begorra, that was a close one!” Barney breathed, lowering his rifle. “Me heart’s poundin’ like a drum in a parade!”

“It was a warning,” Tom said grimly, peering into the darkness where the beast had fled. “The island is not welcoming.”

“All the more reason to find shelter,” Frank replied. “Let’s move. That noise was loud enough to wake the dead, or worse, the Baron’s men.”

They pressed on, their senses now heightened to a painful degree. Every rustle of a leaf, every snap of a twig, sent a jolt of adrenaline through them. After another half hour of grueling travel, the ground began to slope upwards. The dense jungle gave way to a rockier terrain, with large granite boulders littering the forest floor.

“This is more promising,” Frank said, pointing ahead. “This looks like the side of a hill or a small mountain. We are more likely to find caves here.”

He was right. A few hundred yards further, Pomp let out a cry. “Masther Frank! Over here! I tink I found somethin’!”

They hurried to his position. There, nestled between two colossal boulders and hidden behind a curtain of hanging vines, was a dark opening in the hillside. It was a cave.

“Excellent work, Pomp!” Frank exclaimed. “Barney, get a torch lit. Let’s see what we’ve found.”

The Irishman quickly produced a resin-soaked torch from his pack and, with a flint and steel, soon had it sputtering to life. The flickering light pushed back the darkness, and Frank took the lead, stepping cautiously into the opening.

The cave was larger than it appeared from the outside. The entrance was a narrow passage about ten feet long, which then opened up into a wide, high-ceilinged chamber. It was dry, and the air, while stale, was breathable.

“It’s perfect!” Frank said, his voice echoing slightly in the cavern. “The narrow entrance is easily defended. We can make our stand here if we must.”

As Barney brought the torch further into the chamber, the light fell upon something in the far corner. It was a pile of what looked like old rags and sticks. Tom Reynard, his detective’s curiosity piqued, walked over to inspect it. He prodded the pile with the barrel of his rifle.

A small, white object rolled out. It was a human skull.

A grim silence fell over the group. Tom knelt and examined the scene more closely. There were more bones, cracked and gnawed, mixed in with the tattered remains of what was once a fine gentleman’s suit. He picked up a tarnished silver flask. It was engraved with the initials ‘L. v. H.’

“Ludwig von Hessler,” the detective said quietly. “A German naturalist. He went missing in this part of the world two years ago. It was said he was on the trail of a rare orchid.”

“And found his way into the Baron’s game preserve instead,” Frank finished, his face a mask of cold fury. “It seems we are not the first to play this deadly game.”

“Faith, and what did him in?” Barney asked, looking around nervously at the dark corners of the cave.

Tom pointed to the bones. “The markings are consistent with a large predator. A big cat, most likely. He was probably cornered in here. Or perhaps he was wounded in the jungle and dragged himself here to die, only to have a scavenger find him.” He looked up at them. “Either way, he did not survive.”

“We will,” Frank said, his voice ringing with an iron certainty that bolstered the spirits of his companions. “We have each other, and we have our wits. Pomp, Barney, gather firewood. We’ll keep a blaze going at the entrance. It will keep the beasts at bay. Tom, help me unpack the instruments. We will set up a perimeter and take turns on watch.”

They set to their tasks with renewed purpose. The grim discovery of the unfortunate naturalist had served not to demoralize them, but to harden their resolve. They were not merely castaways; they were survivors in a contest of life and death, pitted against a ruthless madman and the savage nature he commanded.

Soon, a cheerful fire crackled just inside the mouth of the cave, its light and smoke a comfort against the oppressive darkness and a deterrent to any prowling creature. Pomp, despite the peril, managed to prepare a hot meal of boiled beef and biscuits, which did much to restore their strength.

After they had eaten, Frank unrolled his folio of schematics on a flat rock. “The Baron has made a critical error,” the young inventor began, his finger tracing the lines of the Pegasus. “He believes we are trapped. He thinks he holds all the cards. He is wrong.”

“What do you mean, Frank?” Tom asked.

“He left us with our most powerful weapon.”

“Our rifles?” Barney guessed.

“No,” Frank said, a glint in his eye. “He left us with my mind. And with the wreckage of the Pegasus. The hull is breached, and the compressed air engines are useless without a new pressure vessel, which I cannot build here. But the dynamo is intact. The electric motors for the helices are functional. The storage batteries can be recharged.”

“But how, sor? Without the main engine to turn the dynamo?”

“The island will provide the power,” Frank explained, a small smile playing on his lips. “Tomorrow, at first light, we will find a stream, a waterfall if we are lucky. We will construct a water wheel. It will be crude, but it will be enough to turn the dynamo. We will charge the batteries. We will have light, and we will have power for our tools. And,” he added, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper, “we will have power for our defense.”

“What are you thinking, Masther Frank?” Pomp asked, his eyes wide.

“I am thinking of an electric fence around this cave. I am thinking of arc-lights to turn night into day and blind our enemies. I am thinking of a way to send a message, a powerful distress signal that no ship for a hundred miles could ignore. The Baron thinks he is hunting us. He will soon discover that he has cornered an inventor. And that is a very dangerous thing to do.”

A new hope surged through the small group. They were no longer merely prey. They were a team, led by a genius, and they had a plan. The long night on the island of horrors suddenly seemed less daunting.

Barney took the first watch. He sat by the fire at the mouth of the cave, his rifle across his knees, staring out into the impenetrable blackness of the jungle. The firelight cast dancing shadows on the rock walls, and the sounds of the night seemed less menacing now. He thought of the mad Baron in his flying bat-ship, and of the poor soul whose bones lay in the back of the cave.

“Well, Baron, old boy,” the Irishman muttered to himself, checking the action on his rifle. “Ye’ve invited us to a hunt, is it? Shure, and we’ll come. But don’t be surprised if the foxes bring their own hounds.”

He settled in for the long watch, a determined smile on his face. The game was afoot.

The new day dawned reluctantly, its gray light filtering through the jungle canopy. Barney, stiff from his long vigil, was relieved by Tom Reynard. The detective found the Irishman wide awake and alert, his eyes scanning the impenetrable wall of green that surrounded their makeshift fortress.

“See anything, Barney?” Tom asked in a low voice.

“Nothin’ that walks on two legs, sor,” the Celt replied, stretching his cramped limbs. “But I heard it.”

“Heard what?”

“The Baron’s flyin’ divil-ship. High up, like a whisper on the wind. He was watchin’ us, I’ll be bound.”

Frank emerged from the cave just then, his face set with determination. He had slept little, his mind abuzz with schematics and calculations.

“Good morning,” he said briskly. “Pomp is preparing a quick meal. As soon as we have eaten, we begin our work. We have no time to lose.”

Breakfast was a hasty affair, but the hot coffee and fried bacon Pomp had salvaged from the galley stores put new heart into the men. Armed with their rifles and several hatchets from the emergency locker, they set out from the cave. Frank led the way, a compass in one hand and a long machete in the other.

“We need a stream with a decent fall,” the young inventor explained as he hacked at a thick vine blocking their path. “The greater the force of the water, the more power we can generate.”

The jungle was a sweltering, noisy place. Unseen birds screeched from the treetops, and the air was thick with the scent of damp earth and decaying vegetation. Pomp, bringing up the rear, kept a wary eye on their back trail. His years of experience in the wilds of Africa had given him a sixth sense for danger.

“Masther Frank!” he suddenly hissed, holding up a hand. “Stop!”

The party froze.

“What is it, Pomp?” Frank asked, his voice a whisper.

“Look up, sor. In dat banyan tree.”

They followed his gaze. Coiled around a thick branch directly over the path was a serpent of monstrous proportions. Its mottled green and brown skin blended perfectly with the foliage, but its massive head, as large as a dinner plate, was unmistakable. It lay perfectly still, its lidless eyes watching them with a cold, ancient hunger.

“Saints preserve us!” muttered Barney, slowly raising his rifle. “It’s a python big enough to swallow a horse!”

“Don’t shoot!” Frank commanded in a sharp undertone. “The sound will carry for miles. The Baron will know exactly where we are.”

“But, sor, we can’t go under that thing!”

“We won’t have to,” Pomp said grimly. He unslung the long, heavy bowie knife from his belt. “You all stand back. I will take care of dis big fella.”

Before Frank could object, the brave coon moved forward with a stealth that was astonishing in a man of his size. He did not approach the tree directly, but circled around to its far side, moving from trunk to trunk like a shadow. The great snake, its attention fixed on the group in the path, did not notice the new threat approaching from its rear.

With a final, cat-like bound, Pomp was at the base of the banyan tree. He scrambled up the gnarled trunk with the agility of a monkey. In a flash, he was on the same branch as the serpent, just behind its massive head. The reptile sensed the movement and began to uncoil, its head swiveling around with terrifying speed.

But Pomp was faster.

With a mighty cry, he lunged forward and drove the bowie knife downward with all his strength. The keen steel blade plunged deep into the back of the snake’s neck, severing its spinal cord. The monster’s body convulsed violently, thrashing with such force that it shook the entire tree. It whipped and coiled, a dying titan of muscle, nearly knocking Pomp from his perch. The brave fellow clung on for dear life until the death throes subsided and the great serpent hung limp over the branch.

“Golly!” cried Tom. “I’ve never seen anything like it!”

“Begorra, Pomp, ye’re a regular St. Patrick, chasin’ the snakes out of Ireland!” Barney shouted, his face beaming with admiration.

Pomp slid down the tree, breathing heavily but unharmed. He wiped his knife on a large leaf.

“Dat fella won’t be botherin’ nobody no more,” he said with a grin. “Now we can get on wid our business.”

Shaken but impressed, the party continued their trek. Not twenty minutes later, their ears were greeted by a welcome sound: the rush and tumble of falling water. They pushed through a final curtain of ferns and found themselves at the edge of a small clearing. Before them, a stream of clear, cold water cascaded down a twenty-foot rock face into a deep pool below.

“Perfect!” Frank exclaimed, his eyes lighting up with scientific fervor. “This is exactly what we need! The flow is strong and consistent. We can build our water wheel right here.”

The work began at once. It was a Herculean task. They had to return to the wreck of the Pegasus and begin the arduous process of salvaging the necessary parts. Barney and Pomp, with their great strength, did the heavy lifting. They dismantled sections of the airship’s light but sturdy aluminum frame, which Frank intended to use for the structure of the water wheel. They hauled the heavy dynamo, a marvel of copper wiring and steel, from the shattered engine room to the site of the waterfall.

Tom Reynard, surprisingly deft with tools, worked under Frank’s close direction. The young inventor was in his element. He seemed to see the finished product in his mind’s eye, and he directed his friends with a calm and confident authority.

“Barney, we need those flat sections of the hull for the paddles!” he would call out. “Tom, help me rig this axle from the landing gear! Pomp, we must have all the insulated copper wire you can strip from the lighting circuits!”

They labored without rest, driven by the urgency of their situation. Piece by piece, under Frank’s genius guidance, the crude but effective machine took shape. They constructed a large wheel, fitting it with paddles made from the airship’s skin. They mounted it on a sturdy axle and positioned it directly under the falling water. Then came the most delicate part: connecting the wheel’s axle to the dynamo with a makeshift belt fashioned from the leather seats of the cabin.

By late afternoon, the apparatus was complete. It was a strange sight, this product of advanced science and primitive engineering, gleaming in the green twilight of the jungle.

“All right,” Frank said, wiping sweat and grease from his brow. “Stand back, everyone. Let’s see if our labors have borne fruit.”

He took a long pole and used it to nudge a wooden lever they had constructed. The lever dislodged a chock, allowing the main sluice of water to pour directly onto the paddles of the wheel. For a moment, nothing happened. Then, with a groan of straining wood and metal, the great wheel began to turn. It moved slowly at first, then faster and faster as the full force of the cascade took hold. The leather belt tightened, and the armature of the dynamo began to spin.

A low hum filled the air, a sound that grew steadily in pitch and volume until it became a high, clear whine of pure power. Frank knelt by the dynamo, his eyes fixed on the voltmeter he had attached to the terminals. The needle on the gauge quivered, then climbed steadily.

“It’s working!” he cried, his voice filled with triumph. “It’s working! We have electricity!”

A great cheer went up from the others. Barney tossed his hat in the air.

“Hooray for Masther Frank!” bellowed Pomp. “He done it!”

Frank wasted no time in celebration. “Quickly! Bring the storage batteries!”

They connected the heavy banks of batteries they had salvaged from the Pegasus. The voltmeter confirmed that a powerful current was flowing into them, recharging their depleted cells. As the batteries drank in the electrical energy, Frank set about his next task. He took one of the powerful arc-lamps from the airship’s prow and wired it to the dynamo’s direct output.

“Cover your eyes!” he warned.

He threw a switch. There was a loud hiss and a brilliant, actinic flash. A miniature sun exploded into life in the jungle clearing, casting sharp, dancing shadows and banishing the gloomy twilight. The light was so intense it was painful to look at, a searing point of pure white-blue energy that illuminated every leaf and stone for a hundred yards.

“By the powers!” Barney gasped, shielding his eyes. “It’s brighter than the noonday sun!”

It was a moment of supreme triumph. They had taken the wreckage of their defeat and forged from it a weapon of science and hope. But their elation was to be short-lived.

As they stood marveling at the dazzling light, a new shadow fell over them. It was not a cloud, but something vast and silent that blotted out the sky above the canopy. They looked up.

Hovering directly above them was the Baron’s airship.

It was a terrifying and fantastic sight. Shaped like a colossal bat, its ribbed wings stretched a hundred feet from tip to tip. It had no propellers or noisy engines, but seemed to hang in the air by some unknown principle of silent levitation. In a transparent cockpit set into the head of the craft, they could just make out the figure of a man, staring down at them through binoculars.

“It’s him!” Tom Reynard said, his voice grim. “The Baron!”

The madman in the sky had clearly been watching their progress. The sudden appearance of the brilliant arc-light must have confirmed his worst fears. They were not helpless castaways after all.

Frank acted instantly. “Pomp! Douse that light! Barney, Tom, get your rifles! Take cover behind the rocks!”

Pomp threw the switch, and the clearing was plunged back into its gloomy twilight, the sudden darkness making them feel blind. The silent bat-ship descended slowly, ominously, until it was just above the level of the treetops. They could now see the Baron more clearly. He was a tall, gaunt man with a cruel, aristocratic face and a mad gleam in his eyes. He raised a speaking trumpet to his lips.

His voice, metallic and devoid of emotion, echoed through the clearing.

“So, the little mice have sharp teeth. I am impressed, Mr. Reade. Truly impressed. You have built a power station in my jungle.”

“You are a villain and a murderer, Baron!” Frank shouted back. “Your games are at an end! Surrender yourself!”

A dry, mirthless laugh was his only answer. “Surrender? My dear boy, the game has just begun. I cannot allow you to continue your clever work. It spoils the hunt.”

With that, the bat-ship drifted sideways. A small hatch opened in its belly. The Baron was not dropping a bomb, however. He was employing a more cunning strategy. A series of heavy, three-pronged grappling hooks, attached to strong cables, came tumbling down. The Baron was trying to snag their precious dynamo and water wheel, to tear their creation apart from the sky.

“He’s trying to destroy the dynamo!” cried Tom.

“He won’t succeed!” Frank retorted. “Barney, Pomp! Grab those axes! We have to cut the cables if they connect!”

The hooks crashed down into the clearing. One snagged on the frame of the water wheel, its prongs biting deep into the aluminum struts. Another clanged against the housing of the dynamo itself. The cables drew taut as the silent airship began to rise, pulling on the machinery.

The entire apparatus groaned and shifted. The water wheel was lifted half out of the stream, its motion faltering.

“Now!” yelled Frank.

Barney and Pomp sprang into action. With his great strength, the Irishman swung his axe like a woodsman, bringing it down on the taut cable connected to the water wheel. Sparks flew as steel met steel. The cable was thick, and the first blow only frayed it.

“Again, Barney!” shouted Frank.

Barney swung again, and this time the cable snapped with a loud crack, whipping through the air like a striking snake. At the same moment, Pomp, with a powerful, two-handed blow, severed the cable attached to the dynamo.

Freed from the strain, the machinery crashed back into place. The water wheel, though damaged, began to turn once more. The hum of the dynamo resumed.

From the cockpit of the bat-ship, the Baron let out a cry of rage. He had underestimated their speed and resourcefulness. Realizing he could not destroy their power source from a distance, he changed his tactics. The airship rose slightly and began to move toward the path that led back to their cave.

“He’s going to try and cut us off!” Tom realized. “Or attack the cave!”

“Then we must not give him the chance,” Frank said, his jaw set. “We have enough charge in the batteries for my next plan. We are turning our fortress into a trap. Everyone, grab a battery! We’re going back to the cave, now!”

Each man shouldered one of the heavy storage batteries. They abandoned the dynamo for the moment and raced back along the jungle path. They reached the cave just as the bat-ship came gliding silently over the ridge above them.

“He’s sending men down!” Barney yelled, pointing.

From the belly of the craft, two ropes were unspooling. Two figures, clad in dark uniforms and armed with rifles, began to descend with practiced speed. They were the Baron’s hired thugs, his hunting party.

“They will be here in minutes!” said Tom. “We must barricade the entrance!”

“We will do better than that,” replied Frank. He was already working, his hands flying as he uncoiled spools of the insulated copper wire they had brought. “We will give them a shocking welcome. Pomp, drive these metal spars from the wreckage into the ground, in a line across the front of the cave! Barney, run this wire between them!”

The men worked with frantic haste. Pomp used a heavy mallet to pound the metal stakes into the rocky soil, forming a low fence about thirty feet in front of the cave mouth. Barney, following close behind, strung the copper wire from stake to stake, creating a web of thin metal lines. It looked like a flimsy and pathetic defense.

The Baron’s two henchmen had reached the ground. They saw the small group working and, thinking them engaged in a desperate and futile attempt to build a barricade, they advanced with confident smirks, their rifles at the ready.

“It’s no use, you fools!” one of them shouted. “The Baron wants you alive, but we don’t mind roughing you up!”

“Just a moment more!” Frank muttered, connecting the ends of the wire web to the terminals of the four storage batteries he had linked together in series. “There!”

He threw a heavy copper switch he had salvaged from the main switchboard of the Pegasus. A faint, low hum emanated from the wires, but there was no other visible effect.

The two thugs were now only a few yards from the wire fence. The first man, a burly ruffian with a scar on his cheek, laughed.

“What’s this?” he sneered. “You think to stop us with a bit of string?”

He did not bother to step over the low wire, but strode right into it.

The result was instantaneous and spectacular.

A brilliant blue flash erupted where his body touched the wire. He let out a single, piercing shriek as thousands of volts of stored electrical power coursed through him. He was thrown violently backward, as if by a giant, invisible hand, and landed in a smoking, twitching heap on the ground.

His companion stopped dead in his tracks, his jaw dropping in stunned horror. He stared at his fallen comrade, then at the seemingly innocent wires.

“What in the devil——” he began.

“Come and find out, ye scut!” Barney taunted him from the cave entrance.

The man, though terrified, was clearly under strict orders. He raised his rifle to fire, but he was too slow. Tom Reynard, a crack shot, had already taken aim. He squeezed the trigger. His rifle cracked, and the thug cried out, clutching his shoulder as his own weapon clattered to the rocks. Wounded and terrified of the electric fence, he turned and fled back toward the ropes, scrambling away into the jungle.

From above, the Baron’s enraged shout echoed down. He had witnessed the entire debacle. He had sent two armed men against a “bit of string,” and they had been utterly and humiliatingly defeated by the power of science.

“Begorra, Masther Frank!” Barney roared with laughter. “Ye gave him a fine welcome, so ye did! He got quite a charge out of it!”

“Dey won’t be in such a hurry next time, dat am a fac’!” added Pomp, grinning from ear to ear.

Frank allowed himself a small, grim smile. “This will hold them for a while. But it will not last forever. The Baron is a madman, but he is not stupid. He will devise a new attack. We must use this time to complete the final phase of my plan.”

“What is that, Frank?” asked Tom.

The young inventor pointed toward the sky. “He has us trapped on this island. Our only hope is rescue. We have power, and I am going to use it to build a beacon. A distress signal so powerful that it will slice through the night sky like a sword. We will turn our arc-light into a colossal signal lamp. We will flash a message in Morse code that can be seen by any ship a hundred miles from this cursed island.”

He turned and strode back into the cave, his mind already designing the complex switching mechanism and the great parabolic reflector he would need. The others followed, their hearts filled with a renewed sense of purpose. They were no longer just the hunted. They were fighting back, and they had the greatest inventor in the world on their side. The game was far from over.

Baron von Ravn was beside himself with rage. His voice, a guttural roar, carried down the cliffside like the anger of a wounded beast. “Fools! Incompetents! You let a piece of wire and a farm boy defeat you! I will have you all flayed!”

The wounded thug who had fled back up the ropes was met not with sympathy, but with a savage kick from the Baron’s polished boot that sent him sprawling.

“Get up, you worthless dog!” the Baron snarled. “Tell me what you saw!”

The man, clutching his bleeding shoulder, stammered out the story of the blue flash and his comrade’s shocking demise.

The Baron listened, his fury slowly being replaced by a cold, calculating look. He was a man of science himself, though his passions were twisted toward destruction and conquest. He understood the principles at work.

“Electricity,” he muttered, stroking his pointed beard. “A simple but effective defense. He has turned his cage into a deathtrap. Very clever, Mr. Reade. But every defense has a weakness.”

He turned to his remaining men, a rough-looking crew of mercenaries and cutthroats drawn from the dregs of a dozen ports.

“He has a wire,” the Baron announced. “It carries a powerful current from a generator somewhere in that cave. We will not touch the wire. We will destroy it. Hans, you are the best marksman. From the ledge below, can you see the wire?”

A tall, grim-faced German stepped forward. “Ja, Herr Baron. It is thin, but I can see it glint in the sun.”

“Then you will shoot it,” the Baron commanded. “Sever it. Once the current is broken, the fence is useless. The rest of you, prepare to descend the moment the wire is cut. This time, there will be no failure. I want the inventor alive. The others are disposable.”

Down in the cave, Frank and his companions were hard at work. The young inventor was a whirlwind of focused energy, directing the efforts of his friends.

“Barney, I need that large brass sheet from the galley stores we salvaged. We will polish it to a mirror shine. It will be our reflector.”

“Yis, sor! A reflector it is!” the Irishman replied, disappearing into the back of the cave where they had piled their salvaged goods.

“Pomp, you have the strongest arms. I need you to rig a pulley system from that spar. We’ll need to hoist the finished beacon to the mouth of the cave.”

“Right away, Masther Frank!” the faithful coon declared, his muscles bunching as he hefted a thick coil of rope.

Tom Reynard stood guard at the entrance, his rifle at the ready. He had wedged himself into a crevice that gave him a clear view of the ropes dangling down the cliff face.

“They’re up to something, Frank,” the detective called out. “The Baron is giving orders. I see a man with a rifle taking up a position on a ledge about fifty yards down.”

Frank did not stop his work, his hands deftly twisting wires and adjusting screws on a complex-looking apparatus he was building from a telegraph key and a magnetic coil.

“He intends to shoot the wire, Tom,” Frank said without looking up. “It is the logical next step.”

“Can he do it?”

“It’s a difficult shot, but not impossible for a skilled marksman. How much time do you think we have?”

“Minutes, at best.”

“Then we must work faster!”

A frenzy of activity filled the cavern. The clang of Barney’s hammer as he flattened a dent in the brass sheet, the grating sound of sand as he and Pomp began to polish it, the quiet, precise clicks from Frank’s work station—all blended into a symphony of desperate industry. The air grew thick with the smell of ozone from the dynamo and the sweat of their labor.

Barney, scrubbing the brass sheet with a piece of burlap and sand, paused to wipe his brow. “Begorra, it’s a shame to be workin’ so hard an’ not have a drop of the good stuff to show for it.”

“Less talkin’ and more polishin’, ye Irish loafer,” Pomp grunted, pushing with all his might. “Dis here light has to save us, and it can’t do dat if it’s lookin’ all dull.”

Suddenly, a sharp crack echoed from the cliff face, followed by the whine of a ricochet.

“He’s starting!” Tom yelled.

Another shot rang out, and a spark flew from the rock just inches above the electric wire.

“He’s got the range!” Tom warned. “One more and he’ll have it!”

“Barney! Pomp! The reflector!” Frank shouted. “Is it ready?”

“It’s as shiny as a new gold coin, Masther Frank!” Barney cried, he and Pomp lifting the now gleaming three-foot brass disk.

“Excellent! Hoist it into position! Tom, give me thirty more seconds!”

Tom Reynard did not reply with words. He took careful aim at the ledge where the sniper was positioned. He could not see the man clearly, but he could see the puffs of smoke from his rifle. The detective squeezed his trigger. His own rifle barked, and a chip of rock exploded near the sniper’s position. It was not a hit, but it was enough to make the man pause.

In that brief moment, Frank connected the final wires. He, Barney, and Pomp wrestled the heavy arc-light mechanism—now attached to its massive reflector—onto the platform they had prepared at the mouth of the cave.

“He’s aiming again!” Tom shouted.

“Now!” cried Frank, throwing a large knife switch he had bolted to a wooden crate.

There was a loud hum as the dynamo strained. The carbon arc at the focal point of the reflector sputtered, then burst into life with a light so brilliant, so intense, it was like a piece of the sun had fallen into their cave. A colossal, solid beam of pure white light shot out from the cave mouth, aimed directly at the darkening sky.

“It works! Be ther powers, it works!” Barney roared, shielding his eyes from the glare.

Frank grabbed the modified telegraph key. “The whole world will see this! S.O.S. Pegasus!”

He began to tap out the message in Morse code. The giant beam of light flashed on and off, three short, three long, three short, a silent plea cutting through the twilight. Dot. Dot. Dot. Dash. Dash. Dash. Dot. Dot. Dot.

On the cliffside, the German sniper, who had been taking careful aim for the kill shot on the wire, was suddenly blinded. He cried out in pain and surprise, stumbling back from the ledge.

Above, the Baron stared, his mouth agape, at the incredible sight. The pulsating beam of light was a sword stabbing into the heavens, a declaration of defiance that could be seen for a hundred miles in every direction. His window of opportunity was closing rapidly. Any ship, any vessel seeing that signal would come to investigate.

“He signals for help!” the Baron bellowed, his voice laced with a new, frantic urgency. “He will not escape me! Not now!” His face was a mask of pure madness in the flashing light from below. “Bring the dynamite! If we cannot go in, we will blast them out!”

A shiver of fear ran through even his hardened mercenaries. This was a new level of insanity.

“But Herr Baron,” one of his lieutenants protested, “the explosives… we could bring the whole cliff down! On them, and on us!”

“I am willing to take that risk!” the Baron screamed. “Bring me the dynamite and the fuses! Now!”

Down in the cave, our friends felt a surge of triumph. They had a beacon. They had hope. The powerful beam pulsed rhythmically, a steady heartbeat in the vast darkness.

“Keep the signal going, Frank,” said Tom, his eyes still scanning the cliffs. “But I don’t think the Baron is the type to give up. He’s gone quiet. Too quiet.”

As if in answer, a new sound reached them. It was a scraping, dragging noise from directly above the cave entrance.

“What is that?” Pomp asked, his eyes wide.

Frank listened intently. “They’re on the cliff top, right over our heads. They’re moving something heavy.”

A moment later, a dark object was lowered into view on a rope. It swung gently back and forth, a sinister pendulum against the starlit sky. It was a wooden crate.

“What is it, Masther Frank?” Barney asked, his voice low.

Frank’s face was grim. He recognized the shape and the context. “It’s a crate of explosives,” he said, his voice deadly calm. “Dynamite.”

A collective gasp went through the small group. They were no longer in a fortress. They were in a tomb.

“He means to seal the entrance,” Tom said, “or blow us to kingdom come trying.”

A triumphant, maniacal laugh echoed from the cliff top. It was the Baron. “Your clever light cannot save you now, Mr. Reade! Surrender your secrets to me, or I will bury you and your pathetic friends under a thousand tons of rock!”

For a moment, silence reigned in the cave, broken only by the hum of the dynamo and the crackle of the arc light. They were trapped. The electric fence was useless against an attack from above. Their beacon, their great hope, had also served to sign their death warrant, pushing the Baron to this desperate, final act.

Frank’s mind, ever analytical, raced through the possibilities. They were dire. But the young inventor was not one to despair. His eyes darted around the cave, taking stock of their assets. The salvaged hull of the Pegasus, the ropes, the dynamo, the sea just a hundred feet away down a rocky slope. An idea, audacious and perilous, began to form in his mind.

“He has left us no choice,” Frank said, his voice ringing with renewed determination. “We cannot stay here. If we cannot go up, we must go down.”

“Down where, sor?” asked Barney. “Into the sea?”

“Precisely,” Frank replied. “The Pegasus floated, Barney. The hull is still watertight. It cannot fly, but it can sail. Or at least, it can float. We are going to launch it.”

“Launch it?” cried Tom. “Frank, that’s insane! We’d be crushed on the rocks! And they’re right above us!”

“It is our only chance,” Frank insisted. “Pomp, Barney, you and I will prepare the hull. We’ll use the remaining spars as rollers. Tom, you must give us covering fire. Keep them from lighting that fuse for as long as you can.”

“Be ther powers, it’s a mad plan!” Barney exclaimed. “But it’s better than bein’ buried alive! I’m wid ye, Masther Frank!”

“We’ll show dat Baron!” Pomp added, his fear turning to grim resolve.

Tom Reynard checked the magazine of his rifle. “It’s a long shot, Frank. But it’s the only shot we’ve got. I’ll buy you all the time I can.”

The Baron, growing impatient, shouted down again. “I am waiting, Mr. Reade! My man has a lit match! This is your final chance!”

From the mouth of the cave, Tom’s rifle cracked. The bullet whizzed past the dangling crate, making the man holding the fuse flinch and drop it. A furious scream from the Baron told them the shot had found its mark in delaying the inevitable.

That was the signal.

“Now!” Frank commanded. “To the hull!”

The three of them threw themselves at the great, wounded body of the Pegasus. They hacked away the vines and jungle growth that had already begun to reclaim it. They worked with the desperate strength of men for whom every second was a lifetime. They laid down logs as rollers, straining with every fiber of their being to push the massive, inert weight of the airship’s fuselage toward the cave opening and the steep, rocky slope that led to the sea.

Above, the battle of wits and marksmanship continued. Tom fired sparingly, precisely, making every shot count. Each time a man with a lit fuse or a firebrand appeared at the cliff’s edge, a bullet from the detective’s rifle would send them scrambling back. But he knew he could not hold them off forever. They had more men, and more rifles. Bullets began to chip away at the rock around his position.

“They’re getting bolder, Frank!” he yelled. “How much longer?”

“Just a little more!” Frank grunted, his shoulder pressed against the metal hull. “We have it on the slope! Now we need to guide it!”

With a final, heroic shove, the Pegasus hull tipped over the edge of the rocky incline. It began to slide, slowly at first, then gathering momentum, crashing and groaning its way down toward the surf. It was a controlled disaster, a chaotic avalanche of metal and hope.

The Baron saw it. He understood their plan in an instant.

“They are escaping!” he shrieked. “Light the fuse! Drop it now! Blow them all to hell!”

This time, there was no stopping it. A man leaned far over the edge and dropped the sputtering, lit crate of dynamite. It fell through the air, aimed directly at the cave entrance where Tom Reynard was now scrambling to join his friends.

The detective saw it coming. With no time to think, he dove headfirst down the rocky slope, tumbling and sliding after the escaping hull.

The world erupted in fire and thunder.

The explosion was immense, far greater than anyone had anticipated. The entire overhang of the cliff disintegrated in a blossom of smoke and rock. The cave entrance vanished. A torrent of stone and earth cascaded down the slope, a deadly landslide that pursued the fleeing heroes and their makeshift vessel.

Frank, Barney, and Pomp had just guided the hull into the churning surf when the blast hit. The shockwave threw them off their feet. The sea itself seemed to boil. Rocks rained down around them, splashing into the water with deadly force. A large boulder, the size of a carriage, smashed into the spot on the beach where they had been standing moments before.

Tom Reyrand, bruised and battered from his fall, splashed into the shallows beside them. “Did we make it?” he gasped.

“We did!” Frank cried, pulling him toward the hull. “Get aboard! Quickly!”

They clambered into the floating fuselage of the Pegasus, which bobbed like a great, wounded whale in the water. It was afloat, but they were not safe. The Baron and his men, silhouetted against the fire and dust on the cliff top, were not finished. Rifles cracked, and bullets began to zip past them, splashing into the water.

“They’re shooting at us!” Barney yelled, grabbing a spare oar. “The cowardly scoundrels! Let me at thim!”

“Stay down!” Frank ordered. “The current is pulling us out! Just stay down!”

They huddled inside the metal shell as the tide and current slowly dragged them away from the island of their torment. The rifle fire became less accurate as the distance grew. From the shore, they could hear the Baron’s voice, a thin screech of pure, impotent rage, echoing across the water. They had done it. They had escaped.

As the island receded into a dark shape against the horizon, Pomp pointed a trembling finger toward the sky. “Masther Frank! Look!”

Above the dissipating smoke of the explosion, something still pulsed in the night sky. The beacon. The blast had destroyed the cave, but the dynamo and the arc light, buried deep within the rockfall, had somehow survived for a few final moments. Its beam, now erratic and weak, flashed a few more times before sputtering and dying for good.

Dot. Dot. Dot. A final, desperate message sent into the void.

Frank Reade Jr. stood up in the floating wreck, his face illuminated by the moon. His clothes were torn, his body bruised, but his eyes burned with an unquenchable fire. They were alive. They were free from the Baron. But they were adrift in the Indian Ocean, in a powerless, damaged vessel, with no food, no water, and only the faintest hope that their signal had been seen.

“Well, lads,” Barney said, with a shaky attempt at his usual cheer. “It’s a fine night for a sail, is it not?”

Frank managed a tired smile. “The adventure is not over yet, my friends,” he said, looking out at the vast, empty expanse of the sea. “Not by a long shot.”

The vast, silent sea stretched away on all sides, a plate of liquid obsidian under a canopy of diamond-bright stars. The lapping of the waves against the metal hull of the Pegasus was the only sound, a lonely and persistent whisper.

“Not by a long shot,” Frank repeated, his voice low but firm. He turned his gaze from the endless water to the faces of his companions, illuminated by the cold moonlight.

Barney O’Shea, the indomitable Irishman, let the oar he held clatter against the floor of the fuselage. “Begorra, Masther Frank, it’s a grand sentiment, but a sentiment won’t put wind in our sails, seein’ as we have none!”

“And it won’t fill our bellies, either,” added Pomp, the young African’s voice a worried rumble. “We have no food, and no fresh water.”

Tom Reynard, the detective, who had been sitting quietly, finally spoke. “The situation is, to be concise, quite dire. We are adrift in a damaged vessel, hundreds, perhaps thousands of miles from any friendly port. Our prospects are grim.”

Frank Reade Jr. did not share their despondency. To the young inventor, a grim prospect was merely a complex problem awaiting a brilliant solution.

“Prospects are what we make of them, Mr. Reynard,” he replied, a spark of energy returning to him. “The hull is watertight. That is our first victory. We are afloat. Now, we must address our next challenges in order. Propulsion, water, and food.”

“An’ which o’ thim miracles will ye be performin’ first, Masther Frank?” Barney asked, his tone a mix of doubt and unwavering faith.

“Propulsion,” Frank declared. “We cannot hope for a rescue if we remain in one spot. We must make some headway, however slight. Pomp, Barney, help me look through the wreckage inside. There must be some spare canvas or metal sheeting we can fashion into a rudder and a sail.”

The next several hours were a hive of activity within the floating shell of the once-mighty Pegasus. Under Frank’s energetic direction, the men worked to salvage what they could. They tore strips of lining from the interior cabins and found a small roll of emergency canvas that had miraculously survived the crash and explosion.

By the time the first hint of dawn painted the eastern sky in shades of rose and violet, they had managed to lash together a crude but functional rudder from a section of a broken partition wall.

The sun rose with merciless speed, and with it came the heat. The calm, starry night gave way to a day of blinding glare and soaring temperature. The metal hull became an oven, and the men’s throats grew parched and raw. Thirst, a far more insidious enemy than the Baron, began its assault.

“Holy smoke, I’d give a hundred dollars for a glass of water!” Tom Reynard rasped, his face pale and beaded with sweat.

“Faith, I’d give me left leg for a sip from the River Liffey,” moaned Barney, staring at the endless, undrinkable blue that surrounded them.

Frank ignored the complaints, his mind focused wholly on the next task. “The sail, lads! We must get the sail up! It’s our only hope.”

Using a long, carbon-steel support rod they had wrenched from the engine housing as a makeshift mast, they began hoisting their patchwork sail. It was a pathetic affair, a jumble of canvas strips and cloth held together with wire and hope.

It was then that Pomp gave a sudden cry.

“Masther Frank! Look! In the water!”

He pointed a trembling finger over the side. Several dark, triangular fins were cutting through the water, circling their crippled vessel.

“Sharks!” Frank exclaimed. “And big ones, too!”

“Be ther powers!” Barney whispered, his eyes wide. “The divils are waitin’ for their dinner!”

The presence of the sharks added a new, terrifying urgency to their work. A slip, a fall into the water, would mean a swift and hideous end. One of the brutes, bolder than the rest, bumped its snout against the hull with a dull thud that vibrated through the entire structure, making the men jump.

“Pay them no mind!” Frank commanded, his voice tight. “Keep working! Get that line secured, Barney!”

The Irishman, muttering a prayer under his breath, leaned precariously over the edge to fasten the last corner of the sail. As he did, a shark surged forward, its gaping mouth and rows of teeth momentarily visible just beneath the surface. Barney scrambled back with a yell, nearly dropping his tools.

“The cowardly beast!” he shouted, his fear turning to anger. “Wants a piece o’ Barney O’Shea, does he? I’ll give him a piece o’ this!”

He grabbed a loose metal pipe and made to strike at the circling fin, but Frank stopped him.

“No, Barney! Don’t provoke them, and don’t risk falling in! Our energy is better spent on saving ourselves, not fighting fish.”

With a final heave, they pulled the sail taut. It flapped uselessly for a moment, and then a slight breeze caught it. The sail bellied out, and the hull of the Pegasus gave a gentle lurch. A cheer, weak and cracked from their dry throats, went up from the four men.

They were moving. It was a crawl, a snail’s pace, but it was movement. They were no longer entirely at the mercy of the currents.

The rest of the day was an agony of heat and thirst. The sun beat down upon them, and the salt spray stung their cracked lips. They drifted under their tiny sail, the sharks keeping a patient, unnerving escort. Night fell again, bringing blessed coolness but no other relief.

Another day dawned, and their situation grew more desperate. Tom Reynard was delirious, muttering about cases and clients in a dusty office. Pomp lay still, his eyes closed, conserving what little energy he had left. Even Barney’s endless well of spirit was running dry.

Frank, though suffering as much as the others, forced himself to stay alert. He scanned the horizon endlessly, his inventor’s mind refusing to accept defeat. He knew that without water, they had less than a day.

It was in the late afternoon, when the sun was a ball of fire sinking toward the west, that Barney, who had taken over the hopeless watch, gave a shout.

“Land! Or a ship! Masther Frank, I see somethin’!”

Frank struggled to his feet, his vision swimming. He shaded his eyes and stared in the direction Barney indicated. On the far horizon, there was a tiny, dark smudge. It was not land. It was a plume of smoke.

“A steamer!” Frank cried, his voice breaking with emotion. “It’s a steamer!”

The news roused the others. Pomp sat up. Tom Reynard’s eyes fluttered open, a glimmer of consciousness returning. Hope, more powerful than any medicine, surged through them.

“We must signal them!” Reynard rasped.

Frank was already on it. He grabbed a polished metal plate from the interior wreckage, a piece of a decorative panel. He angled it toward the setting sun, catching the light and sending a brilliant flash across the waves.

“Flash, you divil, flash!” Barney urged, as if his words could add power to the beam.

Frank worked the panel back and forth, sending a frantic, desperate signal. Dot, dot, dot. Dash, dash, dash. Dot, dot, dot. The universal call for help.

For an agonizing ten minutes, there was no change. The plume of smoke continued on its course. Despair began to creep back into their hearts.

“They haven’t seen us,” Pomp murmured.

“Keep trying, Masther Frank!” Barney pleaded.

Frank did. He continued flashing, his arm aching, until suddenly, Pomp yelled again.

“It’s turning! The smoke! It’s turning this way!”

It was true. The steamer had seen them. It was altering its course. They were saved! The men wept with relief, clapping each other on the back with what little strength they possessed.

As the vessel drew nearer, however, Frank’s initial elation was tempered with a growing sense of unease. This was no clean, proud passenger liner or naval vessel. It was a tramp steamer of the ugliest sort. Rust stained its hull in long, weeping streaks. Its paint was peeling, and a general air of neglect hung about it. It flew no flag that Frank could readily identify.

The steamer cut its engines and drifted to a stop a hundred feet from their hull. Men appeared at the rail, a rough, hard-bitten crew of various nationalities, their faces grimy and suspicious. A figure separated himself from the others, a man of immense girth with a thick, black beard and a captain’s hat perched on his head.

“Ahoy, the wreck!” he boomed, his voice a gravelly roar. “What’s the trouble with you?”

“We are shipwrecked!” Frank shouted back, his voice hoarse. “Survivors of the airship Pegasus! We are without food or water! We request assistance!”

The big captain stroked his beard, his small eyes scrutinizing them. “Airship, you say? Funny you should be here. Saw some strange lights in the sky a couple nights back. Flashing like mad. Was that you?”

“Yes!” Frank cried. “That was our beacon! Will you help us?”

The captain conferred with a lanky, sallow-faced man beside him for a moment. Then he shrugged his massive shoulders. “Ordinarily, Captain Mccabe don’t run a charity service. But I’m not one to leave Christian men to the sharks. We’ll take you aboard. Stand by for a line!”

A rope snaked through the air and slapped against the Pegasus’s hull. Soon, the four exhausted survivors were being hauled up the side of the tramp steamer, the Sea Serpent.

Once on deck, they were given water, which they drank as if it were the elixir of life itself. The ship’s cook, a greasy man with a sour expression, brought them some hardtack biscuits and a tureen of thin, salty stew. It was the most delicious meal they had ever eaten.

Captain Mccabe watched them, a strange, unreadable expression on his face. “Frank Reade Jr.,” he rumbled, having heard their names. “The famous inventor. I’ve heard of you. Read about your contraptions in the papers. Fancy seeing you in a fix like this.”

“We are in your debt, Captain,” Frank said, his strength slowly returning. “If you’ll take us to the nearest port, I can arrange for payment for our rescue and passage.”

“Payment,” Mccabe chuckled, a sound like rocks grinding together. “We’ll talk about payment later. For now, get some rest. You’re bound for Singapore with us. Should be there in a week or so.”

The four friends were shown to a small, stuffy cabin with four bunks. It was dirty and smelled of mildew and bilge water, but to them it was a palace. They collapsed into their bunks and fell into a deep, exhausted sleep.

Frank, however, awoke a few hours later. His mind, now clear of the fog of thirst and exhaustion, was already working. Something about Captain Mtacabe and his ship felt wrong. The evasiveness, the hard-eyed crew, the fact that a ship like this would be in this particular stretch of ocean.

Over the next two days, their suspicions only grew. The Sea Serpent was a vessel of secrets. The crew were close-mouthed and hostile, speaking in hushed tones and casting furtive glances at the rescued men. Barney, whose friendly overtures were usually met with cheer, was rebuffed with sullen grunts.

“There’s somethin’ foul about this ship, Masther Frank,” he said one evening as they stood at the stern rail. “The men are like a pack o’ wolves. They don’t talk, they just growl.”

Tom Reynard, his detective’s instincts on high alert, agreed. “The captain said he was bound for Singapore, but our course is too far south for that. And I have seen no cargo being loaded or unloaded. What is the purpose of this vessel?”

Pomp, who had been assigned to help the sour-faced cook, had the most disturbing news.

“Masther Frank,” he said, his voice low. “Down in the galley stores, I saw crates. They weren’t food. They were marked with strange symbols, the kind you see on bottles of acid and poison in a laboratory.”

Frank’s brow furrowed. An evasive captain, a brutish crew, a mysterious course, and a cargo of dangerous chemicals. The pieces were forming a sinister puzzle. The beacon that had saved them had also drawn them into a new and unknown danger.

“Mccabe is a liar,” Frank said grimly. “This is not a simple tramp steamer. We have been rescued by pirates, or smugglers, or something even worse. We must find out what is in the main cargo hold.”

The plan was simple, and dangerous. That night, after the ship had settled into its nocturnal rhythm and the watch had been set, Frank and Barney would make their way to the main cargo hatch, while Pomp and Reynard stood lookout.

The night was dark, with no moon to betray them. Frank and Barney crept along the shadowy deck, their bare feet making no sound on the wooden planks. They reached the large, tarpaulin-covered hatch in the center of the ship.

“The lock is a simple tumbler,” Frank whispered, pulling a set of fine metal picks from a hidden pocket in his jacket. The young inventor was always prepared. “I can have it open in a minute.”

“Hurry, Masther Frank,” Barney breathed, his eyes scanning the darkness of the bridge. “I’ve got a bad feelin’ in me bones.”

Frank’s skilled fingers worked quickly. There was a soft click, and the heavy padlock came open. Working together, they quietly rolled back a corner of the stiff tarpaulin and lifted the heavy wooden hatch cover just enough to peer into the darkness below.

A strange, metallic, and faintly sweet odor wafted up from the hold, an odor that made the hairs on Frank’s arms stand on end. He pulled a small bullseye lantern from another pocket and shielded its beam with his hand, directing a narrow sliver of light into the abyss.

His breath caught in his throat.

The hold was not filled with crates of chemicals, as Pomp had seen in the galley. It was something far stranger. Arranged in neat rows were dozens of large, iron-barred cages. That was not the shocking part.

The shocking part was what was inside the cages.

They were not animals.

They were men. But men the likes of which Frank had never seen. Their skin was a pale, almost luminous gray. They were hairless, with large, black, lidless eyes. They sat perfectly still in their cages, unmoving, as if in a trance. And from each of their necks, a series of fine, glowing wires ran to a complex silver apparatus mounted on the wall of the hold.

“Saints preserve us,” Barney whispered, his face a mask of horror. “What are they?”

Frank had no answer. He had never seen or read of such creatures. It was a sight that defied all known science, all logic. He stared, mesmerized and appalled.

He was so transfixed by the horrifying tableau below that he did not hear the soft footfalls on the deck behind him. He did not sense the approaching presence until it was too late.

A heavy hand, as strong as an iron clamp, fell upon his shoulder.

A low, gravelly voice, laced with cruel amusement, growled in his ear.

“Find the accommodations to your liking, Mr. Reade?”

Frank and Barney froze. They turned slowly, their hearts pounding in their chests. Standing over them, a massive silhouette against the stars, was Captain Mccabe. And in his hand, gleaming in the faint starlight, was a large, menacing pistol.
