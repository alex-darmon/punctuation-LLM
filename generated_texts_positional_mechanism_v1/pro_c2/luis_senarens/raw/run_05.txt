Professor Thorne had no idea that his former colleague, Baron Von Holtz, had followed him into the jungle, intent on stealing the sunstone for himself. Nor did Thorne know that the Baron had already suborned their local guide, a man named Rico, and that his daughter Amelia was on a steamboat chuffing up the very same river to warn him. The amphibious explorer, the *Vagabond*, was not hindered by the thick morass, for its treads were designed to handle far denser swamps. Silas and Tom, the engineer and cook respectively, were so pleased to be clear of the leech-infested waters that they brought out an accordion and a mouth organ, playing a jaunty tune as the machine churned through the jungle. The recent monsoon had carved a path of destruction, felling ancient trees, scouring the ground clean in some places and piling it high with debris in others.

“If the *Vagabond* had been caught in that squall,” remarked Jack, “we would have been torn to pieces.”

“Then it’s fortunate we found that cave to wait it out,” answered the professor. “Now, to find the lost city of Ixlan.”

“It’s on the far side of the Serpent’s Tooth mountain, is it not?”

“Yes,” answered Professor Thorne. “But why would the Baron head there directly?”

“My guess is that he means to bypass the river’s main patrols.”

“You think he wishes to avoid being seen?”

“It is my belief that he does not want the authorities to know he has the stolen map. In a remote ruin like Ixlan is said to be, he can excavate the sunstone, and no one will be the wiser for it. Do you understand my meaning?”

“That is precisely what I feared, Jack.”

At that moment, a magnificent jaguar, its spotted coat gleaming, leaped onto a fallen log directly in their path. The great cat fixed its burning yellow eyes upon the strange metal beast, let out a throaty snarl, and then bounded away into the undergrowth.

“What a splendid creature!” ejaculated Jack.

“And a dangerous one.”

“I think I could bring him down.”

“With a dart gun?”

“Yes; if I can get a clear shot.”

“Try it.”

“You take the helm.”

The professor took the controls, goosed the throttle, and Jack grabbed a tranquilizer rifle and went out onto the forward platform. The jaguar was a blur of motion through the ferns and vines, but the *Vagabond* crashed after it with a mighty roar from its engine. A loud clanking came from the treads and a great splashing from the boggy ground. As swift as the cat was, it was no match for the mechanical power of the explorer, which quickly closed the distance.

“The beast is running toward the cliff face,” said the professor. “It seems to be following a game trail.”

“Can you cut it off before it reaches the rocks?” asked Jack.

“Yes. That will bring us closer to the pass.”

“Do it, then. I must get a clear line of sight.”

The jaguar was indeed making for a high wall of rock; but, for some reason, it was weaving back and forth. Professor Thorne, instead of following its exact path, steered the *Vagabond* in a straight line to intercept it. They had scarcely covered half the distance when Jack raised his rifle and fired. The dart flew true, and the jaguar stumbled, slowed, and then collapsed in a heap.

“Capital shot! You’ve done it!” cried the professor. “Steer toward it, Jack.”

Jack was just about to retake the helm when the ground beneath them began to groan and shudder. Then it gave way. Down plunged the *Vagabond* as the earth opened into a chasm. It was a hidden cenote, a water-filled sinkhole concealed by a thin crust of soil and vegetation. The machine crashed through and dropped ten feet before hitting the dark water with a tremendous splash. The entire roof of the cenote had caved in, and great clods of earth and stone rained down around the *Vagabond*. It was to avoid this very sinkhole that the jaguar had been weaving so erratically.

A falling rock struck the railing of the platform, shearing it away and pitching Jack sideways into the cold, black water. Before he could even cry out, a mass of tangled roots and mud came down upon him, pushing him deep beneath the surface. His lungs screamed for air. For a moment Jack was so disoriented he thought his end had come; then he gathered his wits, and realizing his peril, he kicked powerfully and swam in the direction he hoped the explorer was. It was good fortune that he chose correctly, for he surfaced in the open water beside the machine’s hull. Had he gone the other way he surely would have drowned under the earthen ledge.

Clinging to a tread, he gasped for breath and then hauled himself onto the deck. No one had seen his plight until he staggered into the control cabin, where he found Silas and Tom with the professor.

“Good heavens! what happened to you?” asked Thorne.

“Pitched overboard by a falling rock.”

“Saints preserve us, lad, why didn’t you shout?” asked Silas.

“I had no time to draw a breath.”

“Ye’d best get into some dry clothes and have a spot o’ brandy,” advised Tom; “or, by Jove! it’s a tropical fever you’ll be catching.”

Jack managed a weak grin and went below. When he returned in a fresh safari suit, he seemed none the worse for his misadventure. He found his companions working on a plan to escape the watery pit into which the jaguar had unknowingly led them.

“The only thing for it is to engage the ballast tanks,” said Jack. “Let her rise to the surface and we’ll try to climb the far wall.”

This course of action was agreed upon. The machine rose until it was buoyant, and then, with its powerful treads clawing at the stone, it crawled up the steep side of the cenote and back onto solid ground. Once they were clear, they resumed their journey toward the mountain. The terrain grew steeper and more treacherous. They navigated narrow canyons, forded rushing streams, and ascended great rocky slopes where the jungle grew thin. Everything was wild, ancient, and utterly devoid of human life, though strange birds called from the canopy and monkeys chattered at their passing. How such creatures thrived was a wonder; but it was plain they did, for the jungle teemed with a vibrant, hidden life.

Mile after mile they traveled. Finally Jack consulted his chronometer, and looking at the map, he said to his companions in the cabin: “We cannot be far from the ruins of Ixlan now.”

“Faix, it’s naught but rock and trees have I seen for hours.”

“Nor I,” said the professor. “But our progress suggests we are very near,” insisted Jack.

Silas was about to make a reply when a shout came from Tom, who was on lookout in the observation bubble.

“Dar’s a camp smoke ahead!”

Everyone was on their feet in an instant. They rushed to the forward viewports. There, in a clearing at the base of a great stone pyramid, they saw the flicker of a fire.

As soon as Jack saw the camp’s layout he cried: “It is the Baron’s expedition!”

The camp, bustling with hired men, was situated in a wide plaza before the largest of the vine-choked stone structures, upon which the *Vagabond* had just come to a halt, concealed by the jungle’s edge.

CHAPTER V.

INTO THE DRAGON’S DEN.

The sight of Baron Von Holtz’s camp so near at hand sent a jolt of energy through the crew of the *Vagabond*. Not a sign of Amelia was to be seen among the men, and Jack at once had Silas reverse the explorer deeper into the shadows of the trees.

“Well,” said Professor Thorne, “there is the man we have been chasing. What is your plan now, Jack?”

“First, I must learn if he has found the sunstone.”

“And how do you propose to do that?”

“I am going down to the camp alone to have a look.”

“That is a fool’s errand, my boy.”

“Very true, sir; but I am not afraid of the risk.”

“Well, we will maintain a constant watch on your progress from here, and if you signal for help, we will be ready to come to your aid with all haste.”

“That is all I ask.”

And so saying, Jack armed himself with a revolver and a long knife, and leaving the *Vagabond*, he slipped away into the twilight. At some distance from the machine, he saw a dry waterway that would lead him down toward the edge of the clearing. The center of the plaza was brightly lit by the fire, but the edges near the great pyramids were cast in deep shadow, except for a path that led from the main tent to the jungle. Jack made his way down to the clearing.

He crept along the line of the ancient stonework, using the deep shadows cast by the overgrown buttresses as his cover. The main fire was a beacon in the center of the plaza, but the Baron’s tent, larger and set apart from the others, was lit from within by a single, steady lantern. It was toward this that Jack directed his steps. A pair of sentries, armed with modern repeating rifles, patrolled the perimeter of the camp, but their path was predictable, and their attention was more focused on the darkness of the jungle than the shadows within their own camp.

Jack flattened himself against the cold, damp stone of a lesser pyramid as the guards passed his position. Their voices carried on the still night air.

“The Baron is in a foul temper tonight,” said one, a burly man with a thick beard.

“Aye, and who can blame him,” the other replied. “The girl is giving him no end of trouble. I heard she struck him when he questioned her this morning.”

“She has spirit, that one. A pity it will be the death of her.”

The men chuckled grimly and continued on their way. Jack’s blood ran cold at their words, but his resolve hardened. Amelia was here, and she was in danger. He had to act.

He waited for the opportune moment. It came when the men paused at the far side of the plaza to speak with another of their company. Like a wraith, Jack darted across an open patch of ground and crouched low behind a stack of wooden supply crates near the Baron’s tent. From here, he could hear voices clearly through the canvas wall.

“It is of no use, Kurtz!” came the sharp, accented voice of Baron Von Holtz. “The girl is stubborn. She refuses to speak of the inscription, even under threat.”

“Perhaps a more… persuasive method is in order, Baron?” answered a second voice, this one smooth and menacing.

“No. Not yet. She is the last of her line. The legends say only one of the blood can interpret the final markings. If we damage her too greatly, she will be of no use to us. We have the sunstone, yes, but it is a locked box without its key. And that key is hidden in her mind.”

A shiver went down Jack’s spine. So that was the Baron’s game! He had not only found the sunstone, but he had captured Amelia to force its secrets from her.

“Then what is our next move?” asked Kurtz. “We cannot stay in this cursed jungle forever. The men grow restless, and our supplies are not infinite.”

“Patience, my dear Kurtz, patience. Tomorrow, we will take her into the central chamber of the ziggurat. The very air of that place is heavy with the power of her ancestors. Faced with the Altar of the Serpent God, her resolve will break. It must. And then, the secrets of Ixlan, and the power that comes with them, will be mine!”

The Baron laughed, a sound devoid of all mirth and filled with a chilling avarice. Jack had heard enough. He knew now where Amelia was being held, and he knew the Baron’s plan. He began to back away from the tent, his mind racing. As he moved, his boot came down upon a dry, brittle vine, which snapped with a crack that sounded as loud as a pistol shot in the quiet night.

“What was that?” came the Baron’s voice from within the tent.

“A jungle cat, perhaps,” said Kurtz.

“Go and see. I will not have my work disturbed.”

Jack’s heart pounded in his chest. The tent flap was thrown open, and a tall, lean figure emerged, a pistol in his hand. It was Kurtz. His cold eyes scanned the darkness around the supply crates. Jack held his breath, pressing himself into the shadows, his hand gripping the hilt of his knife. He knew that if he were discovered, a single gunshot would bring the entire camp down upon him.

Kurtz took a step toward the crates. Another. He raised his lantern, its beam cutting a swath through the gloom. The light played across the very spot where Jack was hiding, but the deep shadow of an overhanging crate kept him concealed. For a tense moment that seemed to stretch into an eternity, the man stood there, listening. A night bird called from the canopy above, and from the direction of the main fire, a burst of rough laughter rang out.

Kurtz seemed to relax. “It is nothing, Baron,” he called back to the tent. “Just the jungle making its noises.” He turned and went back inside.

Jack let out a breath he had not realized he was holding. He did not wait for a second chance. Moving with a speed and silence born of desperation, he retraced his steps, slipping past the sentries and melting back into the blackness of the jungle from which he came. He did not stop until he saw the dark, familiar shape of the *Vagabond* looming ahead.

He clambered aboard, his face grim. The others were waiting for him in the control cabin, their faces etched with worry.

“Well?” asked Professor Thorne, his voice a strained whisper.

“It’s as we feared, and worse,” Jack said, taking a moment to catch his breath. “The Baron has Amelia. He has her locked away somewhere in the camp, and he’s trying to force her to translate an inscription related to the sunstone.”

“Be the saints!” ejaculated Silas. “The poor girl! Trapped in that den of wolves!”

“Dat debbil Baron ain’t gwine to get away wid dis!” added Tom, his fists clenching at his sides. “We gotta do somethin’, Marse Jack!”

“He plans to take her into the main pyramid tomorrow,” Jack continued, his voice low and intense. “Into a place he called the central chamber, before an Altar of the Serpent God. He believes the location itself will force her to cooperate.”

“Then that is where we must strike,” said the professor, his eyes gleaming with scholarly fire. “The ziggurat itself! A place of untold archeological significance. But more importantly, it is where we can rescue Miss Amelia.”

“But how?” asked Silas. “The camp is swarming with his hired guns. A frontal assault would be suicide.”

“We will not use a frontal assault,” said Jack, a determined glint in his eye. “We will use the *Vagabond*. The Baron expects a threat from the ground, not from above. Professor, does your knowledge of these pyramids suggest another way in, besides the main entrance?”

Professor Thorne stroked his chin thoughtfully. “Why, yes. Most of these larger ceremonial structures had multiple points of entry. Priestly passages, ventilation shafts, and often an observation port at the very apex, for astronomical purposes. If we could reach the top of the pyramid undetected…”

“Then we can do just that,” Jack finished. “Silas, the *Vagabond* is equipped with climbing gear, is it not?”

“Aye, that it is, Jack! The magnetic grapples and the high-tensile winch cables. Designed for mountain exploration, but they’ll work on that pile o’ stones just as well, I’d wager.”

“Then that is our plan,” declared Jack. “We’ll circle around to the rear of the pyramid, under the cover of the jungle. Silas, you’ll pilot us up the face of the structure. Tom and I will go in through the top and make our way down. Professor, you will monitor our progress from the *Vagabond* and be ready to provide a quick escape.”

“A daring plan,” the professor mused. “But it may just be our only chance. The odds are against you, my boy.”

“I have faced worse odds,” Jack said simply. “And I have never had a better reason to succeed.”

There was no more discussion. The mood in the cabin was one of grim purpose. Silas went to the controls, his face set in a determined mask. With a low hum of its powerful electric engines, the *Vagabond* disengaged from the jungle floor and rose silently into the air. It moved like a great metal beetle through the dense canopy, its armored hull brushing aside thick vines and leaves, making no more noise than the whisper of the night wind.

They skirted the clearing, keeping the dense foliage between them and the Baron’s camp. Before them, the great pyramid loomed, a black mountain blotting out the stars. It was a monument to a forgotten age, a place of mystery and, now, of great peril.

Silas brought the *Vagabond* to a hovering halt near the base of the pyramid’s rear face, a sheer wall of stone blocks disappearing into the darkness above.

“Engaging the grapples,” he announced, his voice tight.

With a series of soft thuds, four powerful magnetic clamps shot out from the explorer’s chassis, biting into the ancient stone. The cables whined as they took the machine’s weight. Then, slowly, painstakingly, the *Vagabond* began its ascent, crawling up the face of the massive structure like an insect.

Inside, the cabin was tilted at a sharp angle. Jack and Tom checked their equipment: revolvers, knives, coils of rope, and a powerful electric lantern.

“We must be near the top,” Jack said, peering out a viewport. The ground was a dizzying distance below.

“Almost there,” Silas confirmed. “I see a large flat area just ahead. Looks like the summit platform the professor spoke of.”

A few moments later, the upward movement ceased. The *Vagabond* rested securely on the flat, windswept top of the great pyramid. Before them, exactly as the professor had predicted, was a square opening in the stone, like the mouth of a well, leading down into the blackness.

“This is it,” Jack said. “Tom, are you ready?”

“Ready as I’ll ever be, Marse Jack,” the big man replied, his hand resting on the butt of his pistol.

“Good. Professor, keep the engines warm. We may need to leave in a hurry.”

“Godspeed, boys,” said the professor, his voice filled with an emotion he rarely showed.

Jack secured a rope to the chassis of the *Vagabond* and tossed the other end into the opening. He went first, disappearing into the darkness. Tom followed without hesitation. They found themselves in a small, stone-lined chamber. A single passage led away from it, sloping steeply downward into the pyramid’s heart.

Jack switched on the electric lantern. Its powerful beam cut through the millennia of darkness, revealing walls covered in intricate carvings of feathered serpents and jaguar-headed gods. The air was cold and stale, thick with the smell of dust and stone.

“Stay sharp,” Jack whispered. “The Baron’s men could be anywhere.”

They proceeded cautiously down the passage. It twisted and turned, leading them ever deeper into the ancient structure. The only sound was the scuff of their boots on the stone floor and the frantic beating of their own hearts. After several minutes of descent, the passage opened into a wider corridor. It was here that Tom, his senses as keen as any jungle cat’s, suddenly held up a hand.

“Hold on, Marse Jack!” he whispered, his voice barely audible. “Look at de floor.”

Jack directed the lantern beam downward. There, almost invisible in the layer of dust, was a series of perfectly cut stone tiles that were just slightly different in color from the rest of the floor. A pressure plate.

“A trap,” Jack breathed. “The ancient priests of Ixlan were protecting their secrets.”

They carefully skirted the edge of the corridor, giving the treacherous tiles a wide berth. They had gone another twenty yards when Tom stopped them again. He pointed to a thin, almost invisible strand of vine stretched taught across the passage at ankle height.

“Another one,” Tom said grimly.

“This one looks newer,” Jack observed, kneeling to examine it. “The Baron’s work, I’ll wager. He’s protecting his back trail.”

He carefully snipped the vine with his knife. They continued on, their nerves stretched to the breaking point. Every shadow seemed to hold a threat, every echo a footstep. Suddenly, a low grinding sound echoed from the corridor ahead of them.

“What was that?” Tom whispered.

“I don’t know,” Jack replied, drawing his revolver. “But we’re about to find out.”

They crept forward to a bend in the passage and peered around the corner. The corridor opened into a vast, cavernous chamber. In the center, on a raised dais, was a hideous stone altar carved in the shape of a coiled serpent with its fanged mouth wide open. Standing before it were Baron Von Holtz, his lieutenant Kurtz, and two armed guards. And chained to the wall near the altar, pale but with her head held high and her eyes flashing with defiance, was Amelia Thorne.

“For the last time, girl!” the Baron’s voice boomed, echoing in the huge chamber. “Read the inscription on the sunstone, or by heavens, I will offer you to the god this altar represents!”

He held up the sunstone. It pulsed with a faint, inner light, illuminating Amelia’s defiant face.

“I will never help you, you monster,” she said, her voice clear and strong.

The Baron raised a hand to strike her.

Jack knew they could wait no longer. He raised his revolver, his eyes fixed on the man who threatened the woman he had crossed a continent to find.

CHAPTER VI. THE ALTAR OF THE SERPENT.

“Now!” Jack cried, his voice ringing like a clarion call in the vast chamber.

The Baron and his men whirled around, their faces a mask of shock and disbelief. In that split second of surprise, Jack fired his revolver. He did not aim for the Baron, but for the lantern one of the guards was holding. The crack of the shot was deafening, and was followed instantly by the tinkle of shattering glass as the lantern exploded, plunging half the chamber into disorienting shadow.

“Get the girl, Tom!” Jack yelled, firing again, this time at the second guard’s torch, extinguishing it as well.

Tom needed no second command. The big man was a force of nature unleashed. With a mighty roar, he charged across the chamber, not at the guards, but directly at the stone wall to which Amelia was chained. The ancient mortar, weakened by centuries of damp, stood no chance against his bull-like rush. He slammed his shoulder into the block holding the iron ring, and with a great cracking and groaning of stone, the entire section tore loose from the wall.

Amelia stumbled, the heavy block and chain still attached to her wrist. Tom did not hesitate. He scooped her up in his powerful arms as if she weighed no more than a child and began to retreat toward the passage.

The Baron, recovering his wits, let out a furious snarl. “Fools! Do not let them escape! Shoot them! Shoot them all!”

Kurtz and the two guards opened fire, their pistol shots echoing like a cannonade. Bullets ricocheted from the stone walls, whining past Jack’s head. He took cover behind a fallen pillar and returned fire, his shots carefully placed to keep the men pinned down.

“Go, Tom, go!” he shouted. “Get her to the *Vagabond*!”

“Not without you, Marse Jack!” came the reply from the darkness of the corridor.

“I’m right behind you! Now go!”

Under the cover of Jack’s fire, Tom and Amelia disappeared into the passage. Now that Amelia was safe, Jack’s objective changed. He had to delay the Baron and his men long enough for Tom to get clear. He fired his last two shots, forcing Kurtz to dive for cover, then turned and ran for the corridor.

He sprinted down the dark passage, his ears ringing. He could hear the enraged shouts of the Baron behind him. “After him! He must not escape! I want him alive!”

Jack risked a glance over his shoulder. He saw the beams of two electric torches bobbing as Kurtz and a guard entered the passage in pursuit. They were swift, and they were gaining on him. He put on a burst of speed, his lungs burning. He rounded a corner and saw, to his dismay, a dead end. A solid wall of stone blocked the passage.

He was trapped.

The pneumatic pistol hissed once, a sound no louder than a whisper. A tiny, anesthetic dart struck the sailor in the neck. The man’s eyes widened in surprise, he took one staggering step, and then collapsed to the deck in a heap, completely unconscious.

He made his way back to the ship’s rail, slung the keys around his neck, and slipped over the side. The return journey across the icy hawser was even more perilous, but he accomplished it without incident. Within minutes he was clambering back up the cleft in the ice.

He burst into the cabin of the Ranger, his face grim. Dr. Vaneyke, Barney and Pomp looked up in alarm.

“This’ll be just the thing to knock some sense into those scoundrels,” the Irishman remarked.

Together, the two brave men descended once more to the frozen shore of the bay. They crossed the open ice and reached the side of the silent whaler. With Barney giving him a powerful boost, Frank was able to grasp the rail and pull himself aboard. He then lowered a rope for his companion, and in a moment Barney’s massive form stood beside him on the deck.

They found the unconscious watchman still lying where Frank had left him. They made sure he was securely bound and gagged. Then, moving like shadows, they crept toward the after-hatch. The rest of the ship remained sunk in slumber.

Frank took out the ring of keys. His hand was steady as he selected the large iron key and inserted it into the heavy padlock. With a grating squeal that sounded monstrously loud in the silence, the lock sprang open. Frank held his breath, but no alarm was raised. He lifted the heavy hatch cover just enough for them to slip through.

“Stay here and keep watch, Barney,” Frank whispered. “I will go down and get the boy.”

He lowered himself into the darkness of the hold, landing on a pile of musty sails. The air was cold and damp, filled with the smell of bilge water and tar.

“Walter?” he called softly.

“I am here, sir!” came the boy’s eager whisper from a dark corner.

Frank made his way toward the sound and found the boy huddled under a thin, ragged blanket. Walter Grey was pale and thin, but his eyes shone with courage and relief.

“Come, Walter,” Frank said gently, putting a supportive arm around him. “We are getting you out of here.”

“Thank you, sir! I knew you would not fail me.”

Frank helped the boy to his feet and led him toward the square of pale light that marked the open hatch. He boosted Walter up, and Barney’s strong arms reached down to lift the boy easily onto the deck. Frank followed close behind.

They had done it. The boy was free. Now, only the escape remained.

“Back to the rope, and quickly,” Frank urged.

They were halfway across the deck when a cabin door flew open, and a figure stood silhouetted against the light from within. It was Captain Ben Bolt. He held a lantern in one hand and a heavy naval pistol in the other. His face, framed by a thick, unkempt beard, was a mask of fury.

“So!” he bellowed, his voice echoing across the bay. “Thieves in the night! I thought I heard a rat squeaking in my hold! To arms, men! To arms! We are boarded!”

In an instant, the ship erupted into chaos. Sailors, roused from their bunks, came pouring out onto the deck, armed with knives, clubs, and harpoons.

“Bejabers, Frank, we’re in for it now!” Barney exclaimed, brandishing his belaying pin. “Get the boy to safety! I’ll hold them off!”

The big Irishman was a whirlwind of motion. He waded into the press of sailors, his iron pin rising and falling with terrific force. Men went down like ninepins before his furious assault. But there were too many of them.

Frank saw that a direct escape was now impossible. He fired his revolver into the air. The sharp crack of the gunshot was the signal for Dr. Vaneyke.

“This way!” Frank cried, grabbing Walter’s hand. He pulled the boy toward the stern, seeking some temporary cover behind the wheelhouse. Ben Bolt and two of his men were closing in, their faces contorted with rage.

“You’ll not escape me, Reade!” the captain roared. “I’ll have your hide and the boy’s too!”

He raised his pistol and fired. The bullet whistled past Frank’s ear, splintering the wood of the wheelhouse. Frank shoved Walter behind him and returned fire with his own revolver, aiming not to kill but to drive them back. His shot sent one of the sailors scrambling for cover with a yelp of pain.

Suddenly, a new and terrifying sound filled the air; a loud, high-pitched buzzing that grew rapidly in volume. A brilliant beam of light, as bright as the sun, stabbed through the darkness, sweeping across the deck of the whaler and momentarily blinding the crew.

“What in thunder is that devil’s contraption?” screamed one of the sailors.

It was the Ranger.

The magnificent ice ship came sliding down from the eminence above, her steel runners crackling on the ice. She moved with an incredible, almost supernatural speed. Hitting the shore ice, she did not slow, but crashed through the twenty-yard barrier with a tremendous splintering and smashing of ice. She slid into the water of the bay, sending up a great wave, and her powerful propellers churned the water to foam as she bore down upon the Red Eric.

The whalers stood frozen, their mouths agape in terror and disbelief at the sight of this strange, luminous vessel that flew over the ice and swam in the sea.

“It is the Ranger!” cried Walter. “Hurrah!”

“Now, my friends! Jump for it!” came Dr. Vaneyke’s voice from the deck of the ice ship as it came alongside the whaler with a gentle bump.

“Go, Barney!” Frank yelled.

Barney, seeing his chance, broke free from the sailors he was fighting, scooped Walter up under one arm, and made a tremendous leap across the gap, landing safely on the Ranger’s deck.

Frank turned to follow, but Ben Bolt, recovering from his shock, lunged at him with a whaling knife. Frank sidestepped the wild thrust and brought the butt of his empty revolver down hard on the captain’s wrist. The knife clattered to the deck.

With a final glance of defiance at the infuriated captain, Frank sprang for the Ranger. He landed on the deck just as Pomp, at the controls, threw the propellers into reverse. The ice ship pulled away from the whaler.

“You’ll not get away!” Bolt shrieked in a frenzy of rage. He snatched up a rifle dropped by one of his men and took aim at the retreating vessel.

“Look out, Marse Frank!” cried Pomp.

Frank saw the captain aiming directly at the pilot-house where the doctor stood. Without a second’s thought, he raised his pneumatic pistol, took careful aim, and fired. The tiny dart flew true. It struck the stock of the rifle just as Bolt pulled the trigger. The shot went wide, and the rifle, jerked by the impact, flew from the captain’s grasp and spun into the dark water.

Ben Bolt stared at his empty hands, then at the rapidly departing ice ship, and let out a howl of pure, impotent fury.

The Ranger, her work done, reversed her course. She steamed back toward the shore, climbed out of the water, and onto the solid ice sheet as easily as an otter climbing onto a riverbank. Her mighty wheels began to buzz, and she shot away across the frozen wastes, leaving the Red Eric and its villainous crew far behind.

Inside the warm and brightly lit cabin, the adventure was over. Walter Grey, wrapped in a thick wool blanket and sipping a mug of hot beef broth prepared by Pomp, began to regain his color.

“I do not know how I can ever thank you,” he said, his eyes shining with gratitude. “You saved my life.”

“Nonsense, my boy,” said Frank with a warm smile. “We are only too glad to have been of service. You are safe now, and that is all that matters.”

“Begorra, it was a fine fight!” declared Barney, examining a new lump on his head with a certain pride. “But I wouldn’t want to be doin’ it every day.”

“You fought like a lion, Barney,” Frank said. “We all owe you our thanks.”

“An’ dat Ranger!” Pomp exclaimed from the pilot-house. “Did yo’ see her smash dat ice? Golly, she am de queen ob de arctic!”

Dr. Vaneyke laid a hand on Frank’s shoulder. “Another successful venture, my boy. You have snatched this lad from the very jaws of death.”

“We all did it together,” Frank replied. He looked at Walter, whose head was now nodding with exhaustion. “The boy needs rest. We can hear his full story on the morrow. For now, let him sleep in my bunk.”

They made Walter comfortable, and the rescued boy was soon fast asleep, a peaceful smile on his face for the first time in many weeks.

Frank took the wheel from the doctor, his heart light. The compass needle pointed south. Their perilous mission was accomplished. Before them lay the long journey home.

“Well, my friends,” said Frank, his voice ringing with cheerful confidence as the Ranger sped over the endless ice fields. “We have bearded the lion in his den and come away with the prize. And now for civilization and home!”

The morning found the ice ship many leagues to the south of Nordenskjold Bay. The sun, though low on the horizon, cast long, glittering rays across the endless plain of ice, making the world a dazzling spectacle of white and blue. Walter Grey, having slept the profound sleep of the utterly exhausted, awoke feeling like a new person. Pomp had a steaming breakfast of fried reindeer steaks and coffee ready for him, and the boy ate with an appetite he had not known for months.

After the meal, they all gathered in the main cabin, and Frank, with a kind and encouraging look, said to the boy:
“Now, Walter, if you feel up to it, perhaps you could tell us the whole of your unfortunate story. We know the generalities, but the details might be important.”
Walter Grey nodded, his young face suddenly grim as he recalled his ordeal.
“It began in New York,” he said, his voice low. “After my father’s death, Mr. Milburn, his lawyer, was put in charge of my affairs. He was always very kind to me, or so I thought. One day, he told me we were to take a trip, a sea voyage for my health. I was excited. We went to New Bedford, and there he took me aboard the Red Eric. Captain Ben Bolt was there, and the two of them spoke in low tones. Then Mr. Milburn patted my shoulder, told me to enjoy the adventure, and left.”

“The scoundrel!” muttered Dr. Vaneyke. “He delivered you right into the trap.”
“Yes, sir,” Walter continued. “As soon as he was gone, Captain Bolt’s whole manner changed. He locked me in a tiny, foul-smelling cabin and told me I was now a cabin boy. He said if I breathed a word of who I was to anyone, he would toss me overboard to the sharks. I was terrified. The life was brutal. The men were coarse and cruel, and the captain seemed to take a special delight in tormenting me. He told me that Mr. Milburn had paid him a great sum of money to make sure I never returned to America.”

“The murdering villain!” ejaculated Frank. “So he meant to kill you all along.”
“I am certain of it. When we reached the arctic regions, he kept me under close watch. His plan, I believe, was to wait until the whaling season was over and we were in some remote place, then to arrange an ‘accident.’ When you appeared in the Ranger, he became frantic. He knew his time was running out. He moved the Red Eric to the bay to hide from the other whalers, intending to finish his foul work there.”
“And he would have, too, if not for Marse Frank!” Pomp put in from the doorway.
“Indeed,” said Walter, his eyes filling with tears of gratitude as he looked at Frank. “You arrived in the very nick of time. I do not know how you found me.”
“It is a long story, my boy, which we will tell you in due course,” said Frank. “Suffice it to say that your mother, a very brave and resourceful woman, uncovered Milburn’s plot and sought our help.”
“My mother! Is she safe?”
“She is, and is anxiously awaiting your return,” answered the doctor kindly.
A look of profound relief washed over Walter’s face. The knowledge that his mother was safe and that he was finally rescued from his captors seemed to lift the last great weight from his shoulders.

The Ranger continued her swift journey south. The hours passed in cheerful conversation. Barney O’Shea, his fiddle in hand, played a series of merry Irish jigs that had even the serious Dr. Vaneyke tapping his foot. The ice ship performed magnificently, her powerful electric engines humming a steady song of progress. The wheels, shod in a special hardened steel alloy of Frank’s own invention, spun over the ice with a low, buzzing roar, leaving two perfectly straight lines stretching back to the northern horizon.
They were crossing a particularly vast and level sheet of ice when a sudden, deep rumble shook the vessel, far more profound than the mere cracking of the floes.
“What in thunder was that?” asked Frank, grabbing the wheel from the doctor.
“Felt like an earthquake, by Jove!” Vaneyke exclaimed, steadying himself against a bulkhead.
A second, more violent tremor followed, and a long, jagged crack appeared in the ice directly ahead of them, racing away into the distance like a black serpent.
“Look!” cried Walter, pointing to the north.
All eyes turned in that direction. The sky, which moments before had been a clear, pale blue, was now being blotted out by a colossal plume of black and grey smoke, rising miles into the air. At its base, a dull red glow pulsed ominously.
“It is a volcano!” gasped Frank. “A sub-glacial eruption! I have read of such phenomena, but never dreamed of witnessing one!”
“Be heavens, the whole world is comin’ to an end!” shouted Barney, his face pale.
“The ice sheet is breaking up!” yelled the doctor. “The heat from the eruption is melting it from below!”
His words were proven true in a terrifying instant. The vast plain of ice upon which they were traveling began to groan and heave. Cracks shot out in every direction, and with a sound like a continuous cannonade, the entire field disintegrated into a chaotic mass of churning, grinding ice cakes floating on a newly exposed, steaming sea.
“Start the gyroscopes!” Frank ordered, his voice ringing with authority above the din. “To the air, Pomp! It’s our only chance!”
Pomp, his eyes wide but his hands steady, threw the levers that engaged the mighty gyroscopic engines. A new, high-pitched whirring sound joined the mechanical chorus. The great wings on the Ranger’s sides, which had been folded, now extended, and the entire vessel began to lift from the churning chaos below.
She rose from the water-strewn ice with a lurch, ascending into the sky just as a huge section of the floe she had occupied tilted on its end and sank into the turbulent water.
“Golly! Dat was a close one!” Pomp breathed, wiping sweat from his brow.
From their new vantage point a thousand feet in the air, the scene below was one of apocalyptic grandeur. For miles in every direction, the solid ice cap was gone, replaced by a raging sea filled with bobbing, colliding bergs of all sizes. The volcano to the north was now in full eruption, spewing fire and rock into the darkening sky. A rain of fine, gritty ash began to patter against the windows of the pilot-house.
“We cannot go south,” said Dr. Vaneyke, studying a chart. “The breakup seems to extend for many leagues. We would not find solid ice on which to land and replenish our electric accumulators.”
“And we cannot stay in the air indefinitely,” Frank added, his face a mask of concentration. “The gyroscopes consume a tremendous amount of power. We must find a safe place to land.” He scanned the horizon with his binoculars. The world to the south, east, and west was a ruin of broken ice. To the north lay the volcano and certain destruction.
“Wait,” said Frank, focusing the lenses. “Over there, to the southeast. That looks like a high plateau, a mountain range. The ice there may be thick enough to have survived the initial shock.”
“It’s our only option,” agreed the doctor. “Let us proceed with all haste.”
Frank took control of the aerial navigation, steering the Ranger through the thickening ash cloud toward the distant, hazy peaks. The air grew acrid with the smell of sulfur, and the fine dust coated the ice ship, turning her from a gleaming white to a dirty grey. It was a grim and perilous flight. The air currents around the eruption were violent and unpredictable, tossing the Ranger about like a child’s kite.
Barney and Pomp worked tirelessly, monitoring the engines and clearing the vital air intakes of the choking ash. After a nerve-wracking hour, they cleared the worst of the fallout and saw their destination more clearly.
It was, as Frank had guessed, a massive highland of ancient rock, covered by a glacier that seemed, from this distance, to be intact. It rose from the surrounding chaos like a great island of stability.
“There’s a valley yonder,” said Frank, pointing. “It looks sheltered. We’ll try to land there.”
He skillfully guided the Ranger down. The valley was a strange and wondrous place. Protected by high, sheer cliffs on three sides, it had been shielded from the worst of the cataclysm. More remarkably, the floor of the valley was not entirely covered in ice. Geothermal heat, likely related to the same volcanic system that had just erupted, kept large patches of the ground clear and warmed the air. Wisps of steam rose from fissures in the dark, volcanic rock.
With a final hum, the Ranger settled to the ground on a flat, moss-covered clearing near a steaming hot spring.
Frank cut the power to the gyroscopes. The sudden silence was almost deafening.
“Well,” he said, breaking it. “Here we are. Safe, for the moment.”
“Faix, an’ it’s a quare place ye’ve found for us, Mister Frank,” said Barney, peering out a porthole. “It feels warmer here than in County Cork on a June day.”
“It is a geothermal oasis,” explained Dr. Vaneyke, his scientific curiosity overcoming his recent fear. “A pocket of life sustained by the earth’s inner heat. Fascinating!”
“First things first,” Frank declared. “We must inspect the Ranger for damage from the ash and make sure our accumulators are charged. Pomp, Barney, you see to the engines and the hull. Doctor, you and I will run a diagnostic on the electrical systems. Walter, you stay inside and rest.”
The crew set to work with their customary efficiency. The inspection revealed that while the Ranger was coated in a thick layer of grime, she had sustained no serious damage. Her robust construction had served them well.
As Frank and the doctor worked on the main dynamo, Pomp, who was cleaning ash from the forward runners, let out a sudden yell.
“Marse Frank! Doctor! Come quick! Yo’ ain’t gonna believe dis!”
Frank and Vaneyke rushed outside. Pomp was pointing at the ground a few yards from the ship. In the soft, damp earth beside one of the hot springs were a series of colossal, circular depressions. They were footprints, but of a size that beggared belief. Each was as large as a dinner table.
“Great heavens!” the doctor breathed, kneeling to examine one. “The stride, the depth of the impression… it would indicate a creature of immense size and weight!”
“Bigger than an elephant, by the look of it,” said Frank, his mind racing. “But what could live in a place like this?”
“There’s more, Marse Frank!” cried Pomp, pointing toward a grove of strange, stunted-looking conifers that grew near the valley wall. “Look at dem trees!”
The tops of several of the trees were broken and torn, and bark had been stripped away at a height of fifteen or twenty feet from the ground.
“It is a browser,” said the doctor, his voice trembling with excitement. “A massive herbivore. Frank, do you realize what this could mean? The mammoth bones we saw… the legends of the native peoples… what if…”
He was cut off by a sound that chilled them all to the bone. It was a deep, resonant trumpeting call that echoed off the high rock walls of the valley. It was a sound that had not been heard by human ears for ten thousand years.
From behind the grove of trees, a shape emerged. It was a mountain of shaggy, reddish-brown fur, moving with a slow, ponderous gait. Two immense, curving tusks, each at least twelve feet long, framed a long, prehensile trunk. Its small eyes, set deep in its massive head, regarded the strange, grey object that had invaded its valley with what looked like ancient and profound suspicion.
“A mammoth!” Frank whispered, his voice filled with awe. “A living, breathing mammoth!”
“It’s impossible!” stammered the doctor. “And yet… there it is!”
The creature took a few steps closer, its trunk questing toward them, sniffing the air. It let out another, louder trumpet, a clear challenge.
“Begorra, it’s the devil himself!” Barney yelled from the deck of the Ranger, where he and Walter had come to see what the commotion was about.
“Everyone get back inside the ship!” Frank commanded. “Slowly! Don’t make any sudden moves!”
They backed away carefully and scrambled into the safety of the Ranger’s steel hull, sealing the hatch behind them. Through the thick quartz windows of the pilot-house, they watched the prehistoric beast.
The mammoth, seemingly agitated by the strange scent of the humans and their machine, began to circle the Ranger. It was even larger than it had first appeared, standing a good fourteen feet at the shoulder. It was a relic of a bygone age, a magnificent and terrifying sight.
“It seems to view us as an intruder in its domain,” said Dr. Vaneyke.
“And I suppose we are,” Frank admitted. “What a discovery! We must not harm it if we can possibly avoid it. But if it attacks the ship, we will have to defend ourselves.”
As if understanding his words, the mammoth suddenly lowered its great head and charged. It moved with a speed that was shocking for a creature of its bulk. It struck the Ranger’s hull with a tremendous, shuddering impact that threw the men inside off their feet. The entire vessel groaned, but the hardened steel plates held firm.
The mammoth backed away, shook its head as if dazed, and then let out a roar of pure fury. It charged again, this time aiming its colossal tusks at the forward part of the ship.
“It will smash the forward wheels!” cried Frank. “We must stop it!”
He leaped to the control panel. “I have an idea! The dynamo is at full charge. I am going to electrify the hull!”
“Brilliant, Frank!” said the doctor.
Frank threw a heavy copper switch. A low hum filled the cabin, and a faint blue corona of St. Elmo’s fire began to dance along the outer skin of the Ranger.
The mammoth, its rage blinding it to all else, crashed into the ship for a third time.
The moment its tusks and hairy brow made contact with the electrified hull, the result was instantaneous and dramatic. The colossal beast let out a high-pitched squeal of shock and pain. Every muscle in its body convulsed, and it was thrown backward as if by an invisible giant. It landed on its side with a crash that shook the very ground, then scrambled to its feet, dazed and confused. It stared at the Ranger with a newfound fear, then turned and lumbered away as fast as it could, disappearing back into the strange, steamy forest.
“Hurrah!” cried Barney. “That sent the beastie packin’!”
“A non-lethal solution,” said Frank with satisfaction, cutting the power to the hull. “It will not be bothering us again, I think. It has learned a healthy respect for the Ranger.”

While Frank and his companions were congratulating themselves, another drama was unfolding miles away, on the edge of the broken ice field. The Red Eric, though moored, had been torn from the shore by the ice breakup and was now adrift and grievously damaged, her hull stove in by a massive iceberg. The whaler was sinking.
In the chaos, Captain Ben Bolt, his face a mask of demonic fury, had managed to get one of the large whaleboats into the water, along with a half-dozen of his most loyal and ruthless men.
“The ship is lost!” he snarled, watching the Red Eric list and go under. “But I’ll not be cheated of my prize or my revenge! That flying devil went to the southeast! I saw it! There is high ground there! He must land! We’ll follow him!”
Driven by a mad hatred, Bolt and his men began a desperate journey. They rowed through the treacherous maze of grinding icebergs, and when they reached the edge of the new shoreline, they began the herculean task of dragging the heavy whaleboat over the fractured, ash-covered ice, heading toward the plateau Frank had spotted from the air. It was an insane undertaking, but Ben Bolt was a man possessed.
For two days, he and his men toiled, driven by the captain’s promises of reward and his terrifying threats. They were near the point of collapse when they finally reached the foothills of the high plateau and saw the valley. And nestled within it, they saw the grey, unmistakable form of the Ranger.
“There she is!” Bolt hissed, his eyes glittering. “They think they are safe! They do not know Ben Bolt is on their trail! We will wait for nightfall, and then we will creep up and give them a surprise. Frank Reade will wish he had drowned in the sea before he crossed my path!”

Unaware of the new threat, the crew of the Ranger had spent the day making preparations for their departure. The accumulators were fully charged, the ship was clean, and all systems were ready. They planned to leave at dawn.
As dusk fell over the strange, primeval valley, a thick mist began to rise from the hot springs and the damp ground, blanketing the area in a dense, white fog.
“This fog is as thick as pea soup,” Frank remarked, peering out from the pilot-house. “We will have to post a watch tonight. There is no telling what other creatures might inhabit this lost world.”
“I’ll take the first watch, Marse Frank,” Pomp volunteered.
“Very good, Pomp,” said Frank. “Keep a sharp lookout.”
Frank and the others retired to the cabin for a final meal in their strange refuge. Pomp, armed with a pneumatic rifle, stood on the outer deck, his keen eyes trying to pierce the swirling vapors.
An hour passed. The only sounds were the gurgling of the hot springs and the drip of condensation from the Ranger’s hull. Then, Pomp’s sharp ears caught a new sound; the crunch of a boot on gravel.
He froze, listening intently. The sound came again, closer this time, from the direction of the valley entrance. It was not the tread of a four-footed beast. It was the sound of men.
Pomp slipped back inside the pilot-house and pressed the buzzer that sounded in the main cabin.
Frank was there in an instant. “What is it, Pomp?”
“Men, Marse Frank. I heard them in de fog. Dey are comin’ dis way.”
“Men?” Frank was incredulous. “Out here? It must be Ben Bolt! It’s the only explanation!”
“That devil!” cursed Dr. Vaneyke, who had followed Frank. “His malice is as boundless as the sea!”
“Quickly!” Frank ordered in a low, urgent whisper. “Arm yourselves, but do not show any lights! They mean to take us by surprise in the fog. We shall turn the tables on them!”
Barney, Walter, and the doctor armed themselves with pneumatic pistols. Frank grabbed a powerful electric lantern with a focused beam.
Through the forward porthole, they could now dimly make out figures moving in the mist. There were six or seven of them, creeping forward, rifles at the ready. At their head was the burly, unmistakable form of Captain Ben Bolt.
“They are walking right into our trap,” whispered Frank. “Wait for my signal.”
Bolt and his men, confident that their approach was undetected, crept to within twenty yards of the silent, dark ice ship.
“Now!” Bolt growRasped. “Fire a volley at the pilot-house! Then we rush them!”
But before his men could raise their rifles, Frank threw a lever.
Every exterior light on the Ranger blazed to life at once, flooding the clearing with a light as bright as day. Simultaneously, the ship’s powerful horn let out a deafening blast that echoed like the crack of doom.
Bolt and his men, momentarily blinded and deafened, cried out in shock and confusion.
“Now!” shouted Frank.
The side ports of the Ranger flew open, and Frank and his friends opened fire with their silent pneumatic pistols. The tiny, anesthetic-tipped darts flew through the air. Three of Bolt’s men crumpled to the ground without a sound, fast asleep. The others, terrified, fired their rifles wildly, the bullets ricocheting harmlessly off the Ranger’s armored hull.
But the sudden, tremendous noise had another, unforeseen consequence. From the forest at the far end of the valley came an answering roar of fury. The mammoth, its peaceful slumber disturbed by the horn, was now thoroughly enraged. It came crashing out of the trees, its eyes wild with anger, and charged directly toward the source of the noise.
It did not charge the Ranger this time. It charged directly at the small group of men caught in the brilliant glare of its lights.
Ben Bolt turned and saw the mountain of flesh and fury bearing down on him. His face, already contorted with rage, turned ashen with primal terror.
“Look out!” he screamed.
It was too late. The mammoth was upon them. Two of the whalers were trampled under its massive feet in an instant. Ben Bolt and his one remaining conscious sailor turned to flee, but the great beast was faster. It swung its colossal head, and one of its huge, curving tusks caught the captain around the waist, lifted him effortlessly into the air as if he were a rag doll, and flung him through the mist into the darkness. A single, terrible scream cut through the night, and then there was silence.
The mammoth, having scattered the intruders, paused, trumpeted one last time in triumph, and then, as if its anger was spent, it turned and trotted back toward the peace and quiet of its forest.
A stunned silence fell over the valley, broken only by the snores of the three unconscious whalers.
“Good heavens,” whispered Dr. Vaneyke. “The beast… it saved us.”
“It seems our noisy alarm clock served a dual purpose,” said Frank grimly. “It routed our enemies and called in a very unexpected ally.”
They waited until they were sure the mammoth was gone, then cautiously emerged from the Ranger. The two men who had been trampled were dead. Ben Bolt was nowhere to be seen. The remaining three whalers were unharmed, still deep in their anesthetic slumber.
“What shall we do with them, Frank?” asked the doctor.
“We are not murderers like Bolt,” Frank replied. “When they awake, we will leave them the whaleboat and enough provisions to reach the coast. What becomes of them after that is in the hands of fate. Their leader has met a fitting end for his villainy.”
They carried the sleeping men to their boat and left the supplies. Then, without another moment’s delay, they boarded the Ranger.
“Let us get away from this accursed valley,” Frank said. “It has been the scene of too much wonder and too much death.”
The gyroscopes whirred to life. The mighty ice ship lifted from the ground, rose above the misty valley, and turned her nose, at long last, to the south.
Below them, a prehistoric world, its brief and violent encounter with modern man concluded, returned to its eons-old slumber. Ahead of them lay the vast, open sea and the long-awaited journey home.
“Well, my friends,” said Frank, his hand steady on the controls as the Ranger soared into the clean, cold air of the arctic dawn. “I believe we can now say, with some certainty, that our troubles are finally behind us.”
And as the sun rose higher, the magnificent air and ice ship sped southward, a gleaming speck of civilization against the immense and lonely polar landscape, her perilous mission truly and finally accomplished.

Scarcely had these words of misplaced confidence left Frank’s lips than a low hum began to vibrate through the frame of the Ranger. It was a sound altogether different from the familiar whir of the gyroscopes or the buzz of the wheels. This was a deeper, more resonant thrum, as if a colossal tuning fork had been struck somewhere in the vastness of the arctic sky.

“What’s that?” asked Dr. Vaneyke, his brow furrowed with concern as he ceased his examination of the charts.

Barney and Pomp, who had just begun to tune their instruments for a celebratory jig, paused and looked about with apprehension.

“Be heavens, it sounds like a million bees wid a bellyache,” said Barney, his ear cocked toward the ceiling.

“Don’t like dat sound, Massa Frank,” Pomp added, his knuckles white where he gripped his banjo. “Don’t like it one bit.”

Frank’s face had grown serious. He quickly scanned the instrument panel before him. The barometer was behaving in a most alarming manner, its needle quivering and then plunging downward as if the weight of the very atmosphere had been suddenly spirited away.

“Something is wrong,” Frank said, his voice taut. “Very wrong. Look at the sky.”

They all rushed to the turret windows. The sun, which had been climbing in a clear, cold sky, was now being swallowed by a strange, greenish-yellow haze that was spreading from the southern horizon with unnatural speed. The air grew still and heavy, and the humming intensified, pressing in on them from all sides.

“I’ve never seen a meteorological phenomenon like it,” muttered the doctor. “The air pressure is dropping at a rate that defies all known science.”

“It’s no storm I’ve ever seen,” Frank agreed grimly. “It’s something else. Take the controls, Doctor. Barney, Pomp, secure everything that’s not tied down. We may be in for a rough ride.”

The words were barely out of his mouth when the Ranger was struck by a force so violent it felt as if it had run into a solid wall. The ship was tossed upward and then slammed down, flinging the occupants about the cabin like peas in a drum. A terrible shriek of tortured metal filled the air, followed by a series of cracking, explosive sounds.

“The port gyroscope!” cried Dr. Vaneyke over the din. “The housing has shattered!”

Frank scrambled back to the pilot’s station, his face a mask of concentration. He fought with the wheel, but the ship was no longer responding properly. With one of the main gyroscopic stabilizers gone, she began to list badly, falling into a sickening, corkscrew descent. Below them, the sea, which had been placid moments before, was now a churning maelstrom of white-capped waves, whipped into a fury by a wind that had appeared from nowhere.

“She’s going down!” shouted Frank. “Brace for impact with the water!”

There was a tremendous crash as the Ranger struck the surface of the ocean. A wave of monumental proportions washed completely over the vessel, smashing the thick quartz of the turret windows. Icy water poured into the cabin, shorting out the electrical panels in a shower of sparks and plunging them into a terrifying semi-darkness, lit only by the flickering of a few emergency lamps. The mighty air ship, her wings of flight broken, was now little more than a boat, tossed helplessly upon the angriest sea Frank had ever witnessed.

“Lord amassy, we’s gonna sink!” wailed Pomp, clinging to a support stanchion as water sloshed around his knees.

“Not while I’m at the pumps!” yelled Barney, already wading toward the engine room. “Faix, it’ll take more than a bit o’ water to sink the Ranger!”

“The hull is sound,” called Frank, his voice a beacon of calm in the chaos. “But the engines are dead and the gyroscopes are wrecked. We are adrift.”

For hours they were at the mercy of the tempest. The storm was like a living thing, a malevolent entity that seemed determined to tear the Ranger apart. They worked tirelessly, bailing water, securing damaged equipment, and doing what they could to keep the battered ship afloat. Dr. Vaneyke, his scientific curiosity momentarily forgotten in the face of raw survival, worked alongside Barney at the pumps, his face grim and set. Frank, though soaked to the bone and bruised from being thrown against a bulkhead, never left the helm, wrestling with the useless wheel as if by sheer force of will he could impose some order on the chaos.

At last, as if its fury was finally spent, the storm began to abate. The wind lessened its shriek to a mournful howl, and the mountainous waves subsided into a heavy, rolling swell. As a gray and cheerless dawn broke over the horizon, the exhausted crew of the Ranger could at last take stock of their situation.

It was dire. The port gyroscope was a twisted ruin of metal. The starboard one was damaged but perhaps reparable. The delicate electrical systems that powered the ship’s more advanced functions were flooded and useless. They were afloat, but that was all. They were a crippled vessel, adrift in an unknown part of the vast Arctic Ocean, with no means of propulsion save for a small emergency sail that would be of little use against the powerful currents.

“Well,” said Dr. Vaneyke, wiping brine and sweat from his brow. “I believe our troubles are no longer behind us, Frank. They appear to be all around us.”

Frank managed a weak smile. “You are correct, Doctor. But we are alive. While the Ranger floats, there is hope. Pomp, can you see anything from the turret?”

Pomp, who had scrambled back up to the observation post, let out a sudden cry. “Land! Massa Frank, I see land!”

Every eye turned to the west. Through the dissipating mists, they could make out a dark, jagged line on the horizon. It was not the familiar white expanse of the ice shelf, but the unmistakable shape of a rocky shore.

“It must be an island,” said the doctor, peering through his spectacles. “Not marked on any of our charts, I’ll wager.”

“Whatever it is, it is our only chance,” Frank declared. “The current is taking us toward it. With any luck, we can find a safe harbor and begin repairs.”

As the current drew them closer, the strange aspect of the island became more apparent. It was a place of stark and forbidding beauty, composed of black, volcanic rock that rose in sharp cliffs and pinnacles from the sea. No snow or ice was visible on its shores; instead, a thin, green fuzz of hardy-looking vegetation clung to the rocks in patches. Plumes of steam rose from fissures in the ground, hinting at the geothermal activity that kept the island free from the polar ice.

“A volcanic island,” mused Dr. Vaneyke. “Likely a part of the Mid-Atlantic Ridge that has pushed its way above the surface. Fascinating!”

“It may be fascinating, doctor, but is it safe?” asked Frank. “We must find a place to beach the Ranger before she is dashed against these cliffs.”

Fortune, which had so cruelly abandoned them, now saw fit to smile upon them once more. The current guided them toward a narrow inlet that opened into a calm, protected cove, whose beach was composed not of rock, but of fine black sand. With careful maneuvering, using long poles to push off rocks, they guided the wounded Ranger into the cove and ran her aground on the soft sand.

With a collective sigh of relief, the crew stepped out onto solid ground for the first time in what felt like an eternity. The air was cool but carried a faint, sulphurous scent from the steam vents. Twisted, hardy trees, unlike any they had ever seen, grew in groves further inland.

“Be heavens, it’s a quare sort of place,” Barney said, looking around. “Smells like a match factory that’s gone to blazes.”

“We’ll make camp here,” said Frank, his practical nature taking over. “Doctor, you and Barney assess the full extent of the damage to the gyroscopes and the engine. Pomp, you come with me. We need to find a source of fresh water and see what this island has to offer in the way of game. We must conserve our provisions.”

Frank armed himself with a rifle, and he and Pomp set off to explore the strange new land. They soon found a stream of clear, fresh water trickling down from the higher slopes, and not long after, Pomp pointed excitedly toward a flock of large, auk-like birds nesting on a cliff ledge.

“Looks like we’ll be havin’ roast bird for supper, Massa Frank!” Pomp said cheerfully.

Frank nodded, but his attention had been caught by something else. There, in the soft earth near the stream, was a set of footprints. They were clearly made by a human boot, and they were fresh.

“Pomp, look,” he said in a low voice.

Pomp’s cheerfulness vanished. “Golly, Massa Frank. You think we got company?”

“It would seem so,” Frank answered, his eyes scanning the surrounding terrain. “But what would anyone be doing on an uncharted volcanic island in the middle of the Arctic Ocean?”

They followed the tracks cautiously. The footprints led them away from the shore, up a winding path that snaked between steaming fissures and sharp volcanic rocks. The trail was well-worn, suggesting it was used frequently. As they rounded a tall spire of black rock, they suddenly heard voices.

Frank motioned for Pomp to get down, and the two of them crawled behind a screen of the tough, leathery bushes. Peering through the leaves, Frank saw a sight that made his blood run cold.

In a small, naturally formed amphitheater below them was a crude camp. A dozen or so rough-looking men were gathered around a fire. They were a motley crew, dressed in a mixture of tattered sailors’ clothes and animal skins, their faces bearded and their eyes holding a wild, desperate look. They were clearly shipwrecked sailors, castaways on this forgotten speck of land.

But it was not the sight of the castaways that so shocked Frank. It was the man who stood before them, addressing them with loud, imperious gestures. He was broad-shouldered and powerfully built, with a tangled black beard and a face that was a mask of brutal authority. Though his clothes were torn and his hair was wild, there was no mistaking him.

It was Captain Ben Bolt.

He had not been killed when the mammoth flung him into the darkness. He had, by some miracle of ill-fortune, survived the fall, and the storm that had crippled the Ranger had evidently deposited him and his whaleboat upon this very island.

“And so I tell you,” Bolt was roaring, his voice echoing off the rocks, “the spirits of the island have brought me to you! They have made me your leader! For years you have starved and squabbled on this rock, but I will lead you to glory! The storm that brought me here also brought a prize. A ship! A ship of wonders, filled with riches! It lies wrecked in the cove on the far side of the island. Its crew is weak, only four of them! We will take their ship, and with it, we will escape this prison and rule the seas!”

A ragged cheer went up from the assembled castaways. They had been lost and leaderless for so long that they were ready to follow anyone who promised them salvation, and the powerful, charismatic Ben Bolt was like a god sent to them from the sea.

“But what of the beast?” one of the sailors asked timidly. “The one that guards the high passes? It has taken three of our men already.”

Bolt spat on the ground. “The beast? I will hunt this beast and wear its hide for a cloak! We will attack at dawn. We will show them no mercy! The ship and everything on it will be ours!”

Frank and Pomp exchanged horrified glances. They had escaped one trap only to fall into another, far more insidious one. Their enemy was not dead. He had returned, and now he had an army of desperate men at his back.

Quietly, they backed away from their hiding place and hurried back to the beach, their hearts heavy with the terrible news they had to bear. They found Dr. Vaneyke and Barney staring disconsolately at the mangled remains of the port gyroscope.

“The news is not good, Frank,” the doctor said, shaking his head. “This is beyond our ability to repair. We would need a fully equipped machine shop. We are well and truly stranded.”

Frank looked from the wrecked machinery to the grim faces of his friends, and then back toward the dark, smoking hills where his nemesis was plotting their destruction. His jaw was set like granite.

“Our situation is worse than you imagine,” he said, and he quickly recounted what he and Pomp had seen and heard.

Barney let out a low whistle. “Saints preserve us! That divil Bolt is alive and has a gang of ruffians wid him!”

“And they mean to attack us at dawn,” added Pomp, his voice trembling.

A heavy silence fell over the small group. They were four men, on a hostile island, with a broken ship, and an enemy who outnumbered them and was bent on their annihilation. The situation seemed utterly hopeless.

It was Dr. Vaneyke who broke the silence. “Well, this certainly puts our mechanical difficulties into perspective. The question is, Frank, what are we to do?”

Frank looked at the Ranger, his beloved ice ship, lying wounded and helpless on the black sand. He looked at his loyal companions. A fire lit in his eyes, the same fire that had seen them through countless perils before. The hunted look on his face was replaced by one of grim determination.

“Do?” he repeated, a hard smile touching his lips. “We are not castaways, Doctor. We are the crew of the Ranger. Ben Bolt thinks he is the lion in his den, come to prey on the weak. He is about to learn that he has cornered the wrong animal.”

He strode over to the ship’s hull and began to unlatch a storage compartment.

“They want our ship,” Frank said, his voice ringing with renewed purpose. “Very well. They shall have it. We will prepare a reception for Captain Bolt and his new friends that they will not soon forget. Barney, Pomp, help me with the pneumatic cannons. Doctor, I want you to rig every spare voltaic cell we have into a single circuit. It seems we must once again beard the lion in his den.”

The little group stared at Frank, their expressions a mixture of desperation and dawning hope. The young inventor’s confidence was a palpable force, a spark in the oppressive gloom of their predicament. Barney O’Shea was the first to break the silence, a grin spreading across his broad, honest face.

“Faix, Frank, me boy!” he exclaimed, clapping his hands together. “It’s a mad plan, but a divil of a good one! Let’s give that blackguard Bolt a reception he’ll be tellin’ his grandchildren about, if he lives to have any!”

“Golly, Mistah Frank, you wants us to turn de Ranger into a big ol’ electric eel?” Pomp asked, his eyes wide.

“Precisely, Pomp,” Frank confirmed with a grim nod. “If they want to touch our ship, they’ll get a shock. Doctor, can it be done?”

Dr. Vaneyke pushed his spectacles up his nose, his mind already racing through calculations. “Theoretically, yes. We can reverse the polarity of the dynamo and channel the charge from the voltaic cells directly into the hull. The steel runners and the metallic skin of the ship will act as a massive conductor. The amperage should be sufficient to deliver a severe, non-lethal shock to anyone making direct contact.”

“That is what I am counting on,” said Frank. “We do not wish for a slaughter, but to capture our attackers and find out what has become of Walter Grey. Barney, Pomp, to the armory! Unship both pneumatic cannons and mount them behind the rocks here, overlooking the beach. We will create a crossfire.”

The two faithful companions needed no further bidding. They scrambled into the wounded vessel and soon emerged, grunting under the weight of the strange, tube-like weapons. These were not cannons that used powder and shot, but Frank’s own invention, capable of firing hardened rubber projectiles with the force of a battering ram, all powered by compressed air.

Meanwhile, Frank and the doctor worked with a feverish intensity inside the Ranger. They ripped out panels, rerouted thick copper cables, and carefully linked the dozen large voltaic cells that powered the ship’s interior lights and smaller systems. The doctor, a master of electrical science, directed the connections, his long fingers deftly twisting wires and tightening terminals.

“The main danger,” Vaneyke explained as he worked, “is creating a short circuit that could drain our cells before the attack. We need a master switch, something we can throw at the precise moment.”

“I’ve already thought of that,” Frank replied, pointing to the primary gyroscope activation lever. “We can reroute the final connection through this. When I pull this lever, the entire hull will become electrified.”

The work continued through the darkest hours of the night. The only sounds were the clink of tools, the grunts of exertion from Barney and Pomp as they dug their cannons into the black sand, and the ceaseless, mournful wash of the waves. They camouflaged the cannons with loose rocks and dark volcanic scree until they were all but invisible.

As the first faint hint of grey tinged the eastern sky, their preparations were complete. The Ranger sat silently on the beach, looking dark and derelict. From the outside, there was no sign of life. But hidden in the rocks, Frank, the doctor, Barney, and Pomp lay in wait, their hearts pounding in their chests. Each man clutched a weapon; Frank and the doctor with their pneumatic pistols, Barney and Pomp with loaded rifles in addition to the cannons they would man.

“Here they come,” Pomp whispered, his keen eyes having spotted the first movement in the pre-dawn gloom.

Frank peered over the rocks. A straggling line of dark figures was descending from the hills, moving with a rough, undisciplined gait. There were perhaps thirty of them, armed with a motley collection of cutlasses, old muskets, and knives. At their head strode the unmistakable, bear-like figure of Captain Ben Bolt, a long-barreled pistol in his hand.

“They look like a pack of wolves,” Dr. Vaneyke murmured, his scientific detachment momentarily replaced by a grim apprehension.

“And we are the shepherds,” Frank retorted softly. “Wait for my signal. Let them get to the ship.”

The mutinous sailors approached the Ranger with a cautious air. They fanned out, their eyes scanning the silent vessel.

“See! I told you!” Bolt’s rough voice carried clearly on the morning air. “They’re gone! Fled into the hills like the cowards they are! The ship is ours for the taking!”

A ragged cheer went up from his followers. They surged forward, their caution forgotten in a wave of greed. The first half-dozen men reached the Ranger and laid their hands upon the steel hull, preparing to hoist themselves aboard.

“Now, Doctor!” Frank hissed.

Dr. Vaneyke, positioned near the concealed entrance to the Ranger’s underbelly, reached in and slammed the heavy gyroscope lever into its final position.

The effect was instantaneous and dramatic. A low hum filled the air, and a faint blue corona of St. Elmo’s fire danced along the ship’s edges. The men touching the hull cried out in shock and pain, their bodies convulsing violently. They were thrown backward as if by an invisible hand, their muscles locked in spasm. They writhed on the black sand, groaning and stunned, their weapons fallen from their nerveless fingers.

The other attackers stopped dead in their tracks, their faces a mixture of confusion and superstitious terror.

“What devilry is this?” one of them yelled.

“It’s a trick!” roared Ben Bolt, his face contorted with rage. “Don’t touch the metal! Use the ropes! Get aboard, you fools, or I’ll shoot the first man who runs!”

Prodded by his threats, a few of the bolder sailors unslung coils of rope, intending to throw grappling hooks to the mast or railings.

“Now, Barney! Pomp!” cried Frank.

“Wid pleasure!” Barney roared.

From their hidden positions, the pneumatic cannons spoke. There was not a boom of gunpowder, but a deep, percussive *THUMP-THUMP* as they fired. The hardened rubber balls, the size of a man’s fist, flew with incredible speed. One struck a sailor in the chest, knocking him flat on his back with a cry of pain. Another shattered a musket a man was raising. A third hit a grappling hook in mid-air, sending it spinning away.

Chaos erupted among the attackers. They were being assailed by an invisible enemy with silent, powerful weapons, and the ship itself seemed to be alive and hostile. Several men broke and ran, scrambling back towards the hills.

“Stand and fight, you curs!” Bolt bellowed, firing his pistol into the air. He was no fool and had not touched the ship himself. His eyes swept the rocks, trying to locate the source of the attack.

“We have the advantage,” Frank said calmly, drawing a bead with his own pneumatic pistol. “Aim for their weapons, or their legs. We want prisoners, not corpses.”

A strange battle commenced on the volcanic beach. The crew of the Ranger, though outnumbered nearly eight to one, held all the cards. The crack of the rifles, the thud of the pneumatic cannons, and the surprised yelps of the sailors mingled with the roar of the surf. The mutineers, used to straightforward brawling and broadsides, were completely unnerved by this technological warfare. Their attack faltered and broke.

Enraged, Ben Bolt saw his prize and his authority slipping away. “The fat one!” he yelled, pointing toward the rocks where Barney’s cannon was positioned. “He’s there! Rush him!”

A group of the most desperate ruffians, galvanized by their leader, charged toward Barney’s emplacement.

“Bejabers, they’ve found me!” Barney muttered, swiveling his cannon. He fired, but the press of men was too great. Before he could reload, they were upon him, brandishing knives and clubs.

“Barney!” Frank shouted in alarm. He and the doctor broke cover, firing their pistols to drive the men back. Pomp, seeing his friend in peril, abandoned his cannon and charged into the fray with a roar, swinging his rifle like a club.

The battle dissolved into a desperate, hand-to-hand melee around the rocks. In that moment, with all attention focused on the brawl, no one at first noticed the new and far more terrible actor that had entered the scene.

A tremor shook the ground, not from the volcano, but from heavy footfalls. A shadow, vast and dark, fell over the combatants. A sound issued from the direction of the sea, a deep, guttural hiss that silenced the cries of men and the crash of waves.

Every man on the beach, friend and foe alike, froze and looked up. Out of the churning surf, a monstrous head had risen, attached to a long, serpentine neck. The head was as large as a rowboat, with cold, black eyes the size of dinner plates and a maw filled with teeth like daggers. It was the beast the sailors had spoken of, a great aquatic saurian from a forgotten age, drawn by the commotion and the scent of blood.

“The serpent!” a sailor screamed, his face a mask of pure terror. The fight was instantly forgotten. The mutineers scattered in every direction, their only thought to escape the dreadful apparition.

Ben Bolt stood as if turned to stone, his face ashen. The monster’s head swiveled, its dark eyes fixing upon the largest group of men. With a horrifying speed, the neck lunged forward, and the massive jaws snapped shut around two of the fleeing sailors. There was a sickening crunch, and the men vanished. The beast lifted its head and swallowed, its throat bulging.

A collective scream of horror went up from the survivors. Frank, the doctor, Barney, and Pomp scrambled back toward the relative safety of the Ranger’s hull.

“Saints preserve us!” Barney breathed, crossing himself. “What is that thing?”

“A plesiosaur, or something remarkably like it,” Dr. Vaneyke said, his voice trembling with a scientist’s awe and a man’s terror. “A creature thought to be extinct for sixty-five million years!”

The beast, having had its appetizer, now turned its attention to the largest object on the beach: the ice ship Ranger. It glided through the water with an unholy grace, its massive body now visible beneath the waves. It was an engine of primeval destruction.

“It’s coming for the ship!” Pomp yelled.

“The electricity!” Frank cried out, his mind racing. “Doctor, is the charge still active?”

“It should be, Frank! But will it be enough to stop… that?”

The creature reached the Ranger. It seemed to regard the silent vessel with a cold, alien intelligence. Then, it nudged the steel hull with the side of its enormous head, perhaps out of curiosity.

The moment its wet, leathery hide made contact with the electrified ship, the effect was cataclysmic. The beast let out a deafening, agonized roar that shook the very air. Its entire body, dozens of tons of prehistoric muscle, convulsed in a titanic spasm. The water around it boiled and churned into a white foam. The monster’s long neck thrashed wildly, smashing against the black sand with the force of a wrecking ball. Then, with a final, shuddering groan, it went limp, its great head slumping into the surf. The faint blue corona on the Ranger flickered and died, the immense drain having finally exhausted the voltaic cells.

Silence descended upon the beach, broken only by the panting of the four men and the whimpering of the few surviving sailors who cowered amongst the rocks.

Frank stared at the immense, unmoving form of the dead creature, then at his wounded ship. “Well,” he said, his voice hoarse. “That is one problem solved.”

Of Ben Bolt, there was no sign. He had vanished during the monster’s attack, though whether he had fled into the hills or been snatched by the beast, none could say. The dozen or so remaining sailors, their will to fight completely broken, threw down their weapons and surrendered meekly, their eyes still wide with the horror they had witnessed.

Frank and his friends rounded them up. They were a sorry lot, gaunt and desperate men who had been shipwrecked and brutalized by Bolt’s leadership.

“What are you going to do with us, sir?” one of them asked, a man with a weathered face who seemed to have more sense than the others.

“First,” Frank said, his gaze stern, “you will tell me everything you know about Captain Bolt and a boy named Walter Grey.”

The sailor, who gave his name as Higgins, explained that Bolt had found them stranded, survivors of a typhoon that had sunk their merchant vessel, the *Orion*. He had promised them rescue but had instead pressed them into his service, ruling by fear. As for Walter Grey, Higgins knew nothing. Bolt had kept his own counsel, and no boy had been seen among them.

This news was a blow to Frank, but he pushed his disappointment aside. The immediate problem was survival. “Is there a machinist or an engineer among you?” he asked.

The men looked at each other. Finally, a small, wiry man with oil-stained hands stepped forward. “I was a second engineer on the *Orion*, sir,” he said hesitantly. “Name’s Cogs. I know my way around a steam engine, and a bit about dynamos.”

Frank’s eyes lit up. He strode over to the man and clapped him on the shoulder. “Cogs, you may have just saved all our lives. Our ship has suffered damage to its port gyroscope. If you can help us repair it, you and your men will have your freedom and a share of our provisions.”

A murmur of hope went through the captured sailors. The promise of food and fair treatment was more than they had dared to expect.

“I’ll do my best, sir,” Cogs said, a spark of professional pride in his eyes. “Can I see the damage?”

Frank led him to the mangled gyroscope. Cogs inspected the twisted metal and shattered gears with a practiced eye. He whistled. “That’s a proper mess, sir. The main axle is sheared clean through, and the housing is cracked. We can’t forge a new axle here.”

The hope that had just bloomed threatened to wither. But Frank was not so easily defeated. “What about the creature?” he said, a wild idea forming in his mind. He pointed to the immense carcass on the beach.

Cogs looked from the broken machine to the dead monster, utterly bewildered. “The beast, sir?”

“Its bones!” Frank explained excitedly. “The doctor said it was sixty-five million years old. A fossilized bone of that density might be as hard as steel! Doctor, is it possible?”

Dr. Vaneyke considered it. “It is a radical theory, Frank! But the process of fossilization replaces organic material with minerals. The load-bearing bones of a creature that size, particularly the femur or a vertebra, might indeed possess the tensile strength we require! We would have to cut and shape it.”

“And we have the tools to do so,” Frank finished. “Barney, Pomp, fetch the saws and the grinders! Higgins, you and your men can help. We will butcher that beast not just for food, but for spare parts! We are going to rebuild the Ranger with the bones of a monster!”

A renewed sense of purpose electrified the small community on the beach. The former mutineers, now allies of a sort, worked with a will, eager to earn their passage away from the cursed island. Under the direction of Cogs and Dr. Vaneyke, they set about the colossal task of dissecting the saurian. Barney and Pomp, their good humor restored, sang a sea shanty as they worked the large bone-saws.

Frank stood on the deck of the wounded Ranger, watching the scene. They had survived, against all odds. Their enemy was vanquished, and they had found an improbable path to salvation. The hunt for Walter Grey was not over, but for now, it was enough that they were alive. The sound of saws grinding against prehistoric bone was the sound of hope, a strange and terrible music on the shores of the lost island.

The great bone saws, which had sliced through Arctic ice with such ease, grated and shrieked against the fossilized femur of the saurian. Sparks flew, not like wood or fresh bone, but like a blacksmith’s forge. The air filled with the acrid smell of superheated stone and the groans of exhausted men. For three hours the combined crews of the Ranger and the captured mutineers had labored, taking turns in teams of four to work the crosscut saw. Their progress was measured in mere inches.

“Bejabers, it’s loike trying to saw through the divil’s own skull,” panted Barney, wiping a cascade of sweat from his brow with a greasy forearm. He threw down his side of the saw in disgust. “Me arms feel loike a pair of wet noodles.”

“Golly, Barney, dis ain’t no skull,” said Pomp, examining the deep, polished groove they had managed to cut. “Dis here bone’s harder den a landlord’s heart. I’d have an easier time cutting up grief wid a butter knife.”

Cogs, the engineer, ran a calloused finger along the blade of the saw. The hardened steel teeth were worn down to smooth nubs. “It’s no use, Mr. Reade,” he said, turning to Frank with a troubled look. “We’ve ruined two blades and we’re not a quarter of the way through. The material is petrified to a density I’ve never seen; it’s more mineral than bone now. It’s like trying to cut granite with a timber saw.”

A wave of despair washed over the former mutineers. Their faces, already grimed with sweat and bone dust, fell. A large, brutish sailor with a scarred face, a man named Grice, spat on the ground. “Told you it was a fool’s errand,” he snarled to the man next to him. “We’re stuck here. We should have taken the ship when we had the chance.”

Frank overheard the remark but chose to ignore it for the moment. His mind was already racing, turning the problem over and over. Discouragement was a luxury he could not afford.

“If a saw cannot cut it, then we will use a different tool,” Frank announced, his voice ringing with a confidence he did not entirely feel. “The Ranger is a workshop as well as a vessel. Dr. Vaneyke, you said the bone was mineralized. What kind of mineral?”

The doctor, who had been chipping away at a fragment with a geologist’s hammer, looked up with academic excitement. “Primarily a silicate and carbonate composite, Frank, with significant iron deposits. In layman’s terms, it’s a natural ceramic of incredible hardness. Fascinating, simply fascinating!”

“Ceramic,” Frank repeated, a new idea taking hold. “Then a simple abrasive or cutting edge is inefficient. We need to vibrate it apart at a microscopic level, or apply a focused, intense heat. Pomp! Barney! Come with me to the machine shop. We’ll fetch the thermic lance.”

A new energy shot through the men as Frank and his two faithful companions disappeared into the belly of the wounded ice ship. The grumbling from Grice and his cohort subsided, replaced by watchful curiosity. Even Cogs, the practical engineer, looked on with a glimmer of hope.

They returned a few minutes later. Frank carried a device that looked like a futuristic rifle, with a thick, insulated cable connecting it to a heavy power cell which Pomp and Barney carried between them. It was the electric thermic lance, a tool of Frank’s own invention, designed for emergency repairs and for cutting through the hull of an ironclad if necessary.

“Stand back, everyone,” Frank commanded. He donned a pair of heavy welder’s goggles and aimed the nozzle of the lance at the groove in the bone. “Cogs, a little water on the cut, if you please. We need to manage the thermal shock.”

Cogs quickly fetched a bucket and trickled a small stream of water onto the fossil. Frank depressed a switch on the lance. A low hum emanated from the power cell, and the tip of the lance began to glow, first red, then orange, then a brilliant, blinding white. A needle thin jet of incandescent plasma, hotter than any forge, shot from the nozzle.

When the plasma touched the ancient bone, there was a sharp hiss and a plume of steam. Instead of the grating screech of the saw, there was a high frequency sizzle. The stone-hard material seemed to simply evaporate before the focused energy, leaving a clean, almost glassy cut in its wake. Slowly and steadily, Frank traced the line they had marked for the axle. The men watched in stunned silence, their faces illuminated by the eerie white light of the lance.

“Saints preserve us,” whispered Barney. “He’s cuttin’ it loike a block of cheese.”

The work was still arduous. The power cell had to be recharged twice, and the intense heat meant Frank could only work for a few minutes at a time. But it was progress. Inch by inch, the impossibly hard fossil yielded to the genius of the young inventor. Under the watchful eye of Cogs, they cut a great cylinder from the center of the femur, a piece nearly a foot in diameter and six feet long. The next task was to machine it down to the precise dimensions of the gyroscope axle.

This they did in the Ranger’s own machine shop. Using diamond-tipped grinders and lathes, powered by the ship’s secondary dynamos, Frank and Cogs worked tirelessly. The other men, organized by Dr. Vaneyke, continued the work of butchering the saurian’s fleshy remains for food, their spirits lifted by the renewed possibility of escape.

Only Grice and a few of his closest cronies held themselves apart, watching the proceedings with sullen, resentful eyes. They did not help, but they did not interfere, their intentions a dark cloud on the edge of the busy camp.

After two full days of relentless labor, the new axle was complete. It was a beautiful piece of work, a cylinder of sixty five million year old stone, polished to a glossy blackness, its ends threaded to accept the gyroscope’s mounting plates. It was heavier than the steel original, but Cogs and Frank had calculated that its immense strength would more than compensate.

“She’s a masterpiece, Mr. Reade,” Cogs said, wiping grease and bone dust from his hands. He looked at the strange, prehistoric part with an engineer’s reverence. “If it holds. I’ve never seen the like.”

“It will hold,” Frank said firmly. “Now for the difficult part; fitting it.”

The installation was a delicate and nerve-wracking operation. Using a block and tackle rigged from the Ranger’s mast, the men carefully hoisted the heavy bone axle into the damaged engine room. Cogs, with the deftness of a surgeon, directed its placement, calling out measurements and adjustments as they slowly lowered it into the shattered housing of the port gyroscope. Barney and Pomp, along with Higgins and the more trustworthy of the mutineers, strained at the ropes, their muscles burning.

Dr. Vaneyke was documenting the procedure in his journal when a sudden commotion from the beach caught his attention. He heard a sharp cry, followed by a heavy thud and the unmistakable sound of a scuffle.

“Trouble!” the doctor yelled.

Frank, his attention wholly absorbed by the final seating of the axle, looked up, startled. “What is it?”

Before the doctor could answer, Pomp appeared at the engine room hatch, his eyes wide with alarm. “Massa Frank! It’s dat Grice! He’s tryin’ to smash up de rudder controls!”

Leaving Cogs in charge of the final bolt tightening, Frank vaulted from the engine room, a heavy wrench still in his hand. He was followed closely by Barney. Two of the ex-mutineers were trying to restrain him, but he threw them off with bearish strength.

“If I can’t have the ship, no one will!” Grice roared, raising the hammer for another blow.

“Not so fast, ye blaggard!” cried Barney, leaping from the deck of the Ranger and landing in the soft sand with the agility of a cat.

He charged Grice, but the bigger man was ready. He dropped the hammer and met Barney’s rush, grappling him into a powerful bear hug. Barney, though strong, was no match for the man’s sheer size and desperation. The air was squeezed from his lungs in a painful grunt.

Just as it seemed Grice would crush the life from him, Frank landed beside them. He did not hesitate. He reversed his grip on the heavy steel wrench and brought it down in a short, sharp arc, striking Grice squarely on the side of the head. It was not a killing blow, but it was decisive. “Are you all right, Barney?” Frank asked, helping his friend to his feet.

They watched in silence as he was given his meager provisions and sent away, a lone, bitter figure trudging down the beach until he was lost to sight among the strange, jungle-like foliage.

The incident, though ugly, had a positive effect. It purged the last of the mutinous spirit from the remaining sailors. They saw in Frank a leader who was not only brilliant but also fair and decisive. Henceforth, their loyalty was not to their old captain, nor to their own selfish interests, but to the young commander of the Ranger.

With the crisis averted, they returned to their work. Cogs gave the final signal. “She’s in, Mr. Reade. Tight as a drum.”

A hush fell over the ship. All eyes were on Frank. This was the moment of truth. The fate of their entire expedition rested on a piece of fossilized bone and the ingenuity of their young leader.

“Back to your posts,” Frank ordered, his voice steady. “Doctor, please monitor the gyroscope housing for any signs of stress. Cogs, stand by the emergency disengage. Barney, Pomp, secure all loose gear. We are going to attempt a vertical lift.”

For a heart-stopping second, nothing happened. The Ranger groaned, and the crackling of sand and pebbles could be heard beneath its massive runners. Frank allowed himself a small smile of satisfaction. He circled the victorious crew on the beach and brought the Ranger to a gentle landing beside the remains of the saurian. “All aboard!” he shouted down. “We’re leaving this island behind!”

The ex-mutineers, now a loyal part of the crew, scrambled aboard, their faces shining with relief and gratitude. As soon as the last man was on deck, Frank lifted the Ranger into the sky once more. The two men looked at each other. The same thought was in both their minds. Grice had been sent in the opposite direction. There were no natives on this island, as far as they knew. Who, then, would be lighting a fire on that desolate plateau?

“It can’t be,” the doctor said, his voice a whisper.

“It is the only possibility,” Frank replied, a grim determination setting on his features. He brought the Ranger about, pointing its bow toward the distant smoke. “We thought Ben Bolt had escaped to the north. But what if we were wrong? What if he was wrecked on this island, just as we were?”

A thrill of renewed purpose shot through Frank. Their incredible repair, their miraculous escape from the beach, was not the end of their adventure. It was only the beginning of the next chapter.

“If Bolt is on this island,” Frank said, his eyes blazing, “then there is a chance, just a chance, that Walter Grey is with him. Cogs, give me full power. We have a fire to investigate.”

The bone-axle gyroscope whirred, a testament to prehistoric strength and modern ingenuity, as the Ranger surged forward, a vessel of vengeance and rescue, across the skies of the lost island. The hunt was on once more.

“I’ll take my chances!” the mutineer yelled back, pulling Walter toward a narrow ravine.

“He means to use the boy as a shield,” said Dr. Vaneyke anxiously.

“I won’t let him,” Frank said. He turned to his faithful companions. “Pomp, you are the best shot among us. Do you think you can disable him from this distance without harming the boy?”

“I kin try, Massa Frank,” Pomp said, his face set in concentration. He took up a special long-barreled rifle that fired a small, dart-like projectile filled with a powerful tranquilizer of the doctor’s own invention.

Pomp rested the rifle on the turret’s railing, took a deep breath, and squinted down the sights. Croft was still backing away, dragging the struggling Walter with him. The wind whipped snow across the ice, making the shot more difficult. For a long moment, there was silence. Then, a soft ‘phut’ from the rifle. The tiny dart was almost invisible as it flew through the air. Croft cried out and clapped a hand to his shoulder. He staggered for a moment, his eyes wide with surprise, then his pistol clattered to the ice. His knees buckled, and he collapsed into a heap in the snow.

“He’s down!” cried the doctor.

Walter, freed from the man’s grasp, did not hesitate. He turned and ran toward the Ranger as fast as his legs could carry him. Frank was already out on the ice to meet him. He swept the boy up in a great hug.

“Are you all right, Walter?” he asked, checking him for injuries.

“I am now, Frank!” the boy said, relief washing over his face. “They were awful men! They were going to make me dig for gold until I dropped!”

“Well, their digging days are over,” Frank said. He turned to Barney. “Barney, take some rope and go and round up the rest of those scoundrels. We’ll drop them at the nearest Danish outpost and let the authorities deal with them.”

“Aye, that I will, sir!” the Irishman said with relish.

With Walter safe and the mutineers captured, the immediate crisis was over. The crew of the Ranger gathered around the sled the sailors had abandoned. Upon it was a heavy, iron-bound chest.

“This must be what they were after,” said the doctor, examining the lock.

Frank pried it open with a crowbar. Instead of gold or jewels, the chest was filled with heavy, blackish rocks that glittered with veins of some yellow metal.

“Pyrite,” said the doctor with a chuckle. “Fool’s gold. They killed their captain and risked their lives for a chest full of worthless rock.”

“The Devil’s Gold indeed,” Frank mused, kicking a piece of the pyrite. “It seems their greed was their undoing.”

They left the mutineers’ treasure lying on the ice and returned to the Ranger. The journey back to the coast was a cheerful one. Pomp and Barney, celebrating another successful rescue, brought out their instruments. Soon the strange, desolate landscape of the Arctic echoed with the lively tune of a fiddle and banjo, a sound of civilization and triumph in the vast wilderness. As they neared the open sea, Frank stood on the deck, the crisp, cold air filling his lungs. Walter was safe, the villains were captured, and the Ranger had once again proven her worth. And yet, as he looked out at the endless expanse of water, a familiar feeling began to creep back into his heart. It was a restless feeling, a yearning for the next horizon, the next adventure. The world, he thought, was a vast and wonderful place, full of mysteries waiting to be solved. His rescue mission was complete, but he knew, with a certainty that thrilled him to his very core, that his journey was far from over.

As the ice ship glided from the frozen shelf into the dark waters of the Arctic Ocean, Frank ordered a southerly course, toward warmer climes and home. For a time, a comfortable routine settled over the ship. The doctor conducted his studies, Walter learned the principles of navigation from Frank, and Barney and Pomp kept the Ranger’s marvelous engines running in perfect order. Yet the spirit of adventure that drove Frank Reade Jr. could not be long suppressed by simple routine. After several days of uneventful travel, as they steamed through a vast, empty stretch of the North Atlantic, the lookout’s cry once again shattered the peace.

“Something in the water!” Pomp shouted from the turret. “Dead ahead!”

Frank grabbed his spyglass and rushed to the bow. There, bobbing gently in the waves, was a peculiar object. It was a small barrel, painted bright yellow, with a short mast from which a tattered flag hung limply.

“It’s a marker buoy,” said Dr. Vaneyke, who had joined him at the rail. “But for what? We are hundreds of miles from any shipping lane.”

“Let’s get a closer look,” Frank said, ordering the Ranger to slow its pace.

As they drew alongside the object, they could see that it was old, its wood weathered and covered in barnacles, but what was strange was the iron chain that snaked from it down into the green depths. “There is something attached to it,” Walter said, his face pressed against the glass. “It’s pulling the chain taut.”

“Indeed,” Frank mused. “Barney, prepare the grappling hook. I am going to bring it aboard.”

It was the work of a few moments for the powerful magnetic grappling arm of the Ranger to lock onto the chain and haul it up. As the strange object broke the surface, a collective gasp went through the crew. It was not a buoy or an anchor. It was a sphere of dark, greenish metal, about six feet in diameter, studded with thick glass portholes and strange, fin-like protuberances. A round hatch was visible on its top, sealed tight.

“Good heavens!” cried the doctor. “What is it?”

“It looks like some sort of diving bell,” Frank said, examining it closely. “But of a design I have never seen. It is incredibly advanced.”

He directed Barney to deposit the sphere on the forward deck. As soon as it was settled, Frank was upon it, running his hands over the smooth, cold metal. It was a strange alloy, hard as steel but surprisingly light. There was no sign of rust, despite what must have been a long immersion.

“Who could have built such a thing?” Walter asked in awe.

Frank pointed to a small, tarnished brass plate near the hatch. It was covered in intricate engravings, almost worn smooth by the sea. He took out a cloth and began to polish it. Slowly, letters began to appear. First an N. Then an A. Then a U, a T, a I, an L…

“The Nautilus,” Frank read aloud, his voice hushed with disbelief and excitement. “And below it… a name. Captain Nemo.”

The words hung in the air. The professor’s jaw dropped. Barney and Pomp looked on, uncomprehending but impressed by the gravity of the moment.

“Captain Nemo?” said Dr. Vaneyke. “But… that is impossible! He is a character from a work of fiction! A wonderful, imaginative tale by Monsieur Jules Verne, but a fiction nonetheless!”

“Is he, doctor?” Frank said, a strange light in his eyes. He tapped the solid metal of the sphere. “This feels very real to me. And it was chained to a marker barrel, as if its owner intended to retrieve it.” He looked from the mysterious submarine to the endless horizon, and a slow smile spread across his face. The restlessness was gone, replaced by a surge of pure, exhilarating curiosity. The world was not so small and dull after all. It was full of mysteries, just waiting to be found. “Well, my friends,” he said, turning to his crew, his voice ringing with a new sense of purpose. “It appears our journey home is to be postponed. We have a new puzzle to solve. First, how to open this hatch. And second, and far more importantly… where is the man who built it?”
