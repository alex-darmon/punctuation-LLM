“Dacoits!” Reynard exclaimed. “Indian bandits! Murdock is rich enough to hire a whole army of them.”

“Bejabers, the detective is right!” said Barney. “They could be tryin’ to lure us down!”

“We’ll proceed with caution,” Frank decided. “We won’t land, but we’ll have a closer look. Pomp, Barney, have your rifles ready. Mr. Reynard, you too. We are not going to be caught unprepared.”

He took the controls and sent the Pegasus gliding silently toward the source of the smoke. He kept the airship at an altitude of a thousand feet, high enough to be out of range of casual small arms fire. As they drew nearer, they could see that the smoke was rising from a natural amphitheater formed by a cluster of large, weathered boulders and cliffs. There was no sign of life.

“It’s very quiet down there,” Reynard observed. “Too quiet.”

“I agree,” said Frank. “I don’t like the look of it. Barney, take us up another five hundred feet. We’ll circle once more and then continue on our way.”

As the Irishman angled the airship upward, the trap was sprung.

From dozens of hiding places among the rocks, men suddenly appeared. They were fierce-looking individuals, armed with modern repeating rifles. A sharp command rang out, and a volley of shots cracked through the air.

Ping! Twang! Whizz!

Bullets struck the armored hull of the Pegasus, ricocheting with angry whines. One shot shattered a window in the main cabin, sending glass flying.

“The divils!” roared Barney. “They’re shootin’ at us!”

“Return fire!” Frank commanded, his voice cold as steel. “Aim for the rocks around them! Drive them back under cover!”

Reynard, Barney, and Pomp leaned over the rails and began firing their own rifles down at the ambushers. The dacoits were startled by this swift and accurate response from the sky. Several of them cried out as bullets chipped stone near their heads, sending sharp splinters flying.

“They are too scattered!” Reynard shouted over the din. “We can’t suppress them all!”

“Then we shall blind them!” Frank cried. He ran to a locker and pulled out a device of his own invention. It was a glass orb filled with a fine, white powder.

“What is that, Masther Frank?”

“A magnesium flash bomb! It will create a light brighter than the sun for a few seconds! Cover your eyes!”

Frank lit the fuse and, judging his moment, tossed the bomb over the side. It fell toward the center of the rocky amphitheater. When it was about two hundred feet from the ground, it exploded with a silent, instantaneous, and overwhelming flash of pure white light.

The effect was instantaneous. The men below cried out in pain and confusion, dropping their rifles and clutching at their eyes, their vision completely bleached by the dazzling glare. Their carefully laid ambush was in ruins.

“Now’s our chance!” Frank yelled. “Barney, full speed ahead! Let’s get out of here!”

The helices whirred to their maximum velocity, and the Pegasus surged forward, leaving the blinded and disorganized bandits far behind. In the brig, Martin Murdock, who had heard the gunfire, slammed his fist against the door in fury. His rescue attempt had failed.

“That confirms it,” Frank said, as they sped eastward. “His network is active, and they are hunting for us. We can’t afford any more delays or investigations. We make for Rajpore with all possible speed.”

The rest of the day’s journey was tense. Every shadow on the ground seemed to hold a potential threat. But their enemies had been left behind, and the vastness of the Indian plateau swallowed them up. They flew on, a solitary vessel in an endless sky.

As the sun began to sink toward the horizon, painting the clouds in hues of orange and purple, Tom Reynard called out from his post.

“Frank! Look ahead!”

Frank peered into the distance. There, rising from the plains, was a solitary, flat-topped mountain, a natural fortress. And on its summit, he could just make out the sharp, geometric lines of walls and battlements, with a flagpole at its center.

“That’s it,” Frank breathed, a feeling of profound relief washing over him. “That must be the fort of Rajpore.”

“Hooray!” yelled Barney. “We’ve made it! Begorra, I’ve never been so happy to see a British flag in all me life!”

The sight was a welcome one indeed. It promised safety, justice, and an end to their perilous flight with their cunning captive. Frank guided the Pegasus in a wide, slow circle around the mesa, a clear signal of peaceful intent. On the battlements below, they could see tiny figures of soldiers pointing up at them in astonishment.

“Prepare for landing,” Frank said, his voice steady once more, the calm and capable inventor in full command. “Let’s go down and meet Major Harding.”

As the Pegasus drew nearer, the figures on the ramparts grew more distinct. They were British soldiers, their red coats stark against the stone, their white helmets gleaming in the late afternoon sun. A bugle call cut through the air, sharp and clear, a note of alarm that quickly turned to one of inquiry.
Frank reduced the speed of the helices still further, allowing the great airship to descend with a stately and unthreatening grace toward a large, open parade ground in the center of the fort. Soldiers ran from the barracks, forming up in neat lines, their Martini-Henry rifles held at the ready. An officer with a bristling white mustache and a chest full of medals strode out from the main building, shielding his eyes to get a better look at the incredible machine descending from the heavens.
“Ahoy, the fort!” Frank called down through a speaking-trumpet.
“Identify yourselves!” the officer shouted back, his voice a powerful bark of command. “What is the meaning of this intrusion?”
“We are the crew of the American airship Pegasus!” Frank announced. “My name is Frank Reade, Jr.! We come in peace, and we seek the commanding officer, Major Harding!”
The officer visibly started at the name. He conferred for a moment with a younger lieutenant at his side, then looked back up.
“I am Major Harding! Land your vessel on the parade ground, sir! Make no sudden moves!”
“Aye, aye, sir!”
With consummate skill, Frank brought the Pegasus to rest as gently as a feather falling to earth. The landing gear absorbed the slight impact with a soft hiss of compressed air. For a moment, a stunned silence held the entire fort in its grip as the soldiers stared, dumbfounded, at the futuristic craft that now sat in their midst.
Frank, followed by Tom Reynard, descended the ladder. He instructed Barney and Pomp to remain on deck and keep a close watch on the brig.
“Keep your rifles handy, lads,” he said in a low voice. “But do not show them unless there is trouble.”
“Yis, sor,” Barney replied, his hand resting near the stock of his Winchester. “We’ll be ready for anything.”
Major Harding strode forward, his initial shock giving way to a stern, military curiosity. He was a man accustomed to being in command of his surroundings, and the arrival of this impossible machine had clearly unsettled him.
“Mr. Reade, is it?” the major said, his eyes scanning every detail of the Pegasus. “I must confess, your method of arrival is… unorthodox. We had no intelligence of your approach.”
“Our journey has been a hasty one, Major,” Frank said, extending a hand. “We come to you seeking the assistance of British justice.”
Major Harding shook his hand firmly, his gaze sharp and piercing. “Justice? A curious thing to seek from the sky. Explain yourself, sir.”
“We have a prisoner aboard,” Tom Reynard interjected, stepping forward. “A man wanted for a great many serious crimes, both in the United States and, we suspect, within the jurisdiction of the Crown. His name is Martin Murdock.”
The major’s eyebrows shot up. A flicker of recognition, or something like it, crossed his face before his professional mask snapped back into place.
“Martin Murdock,” he repeated slowly, tasting the name. “The financier? The speculator? I had heard he was traveling in this part of the world. I had not heard he was a wanted criminal.”
“He is that and more, sir,” Frank said. “A swindler, a kidnapper, and an accessory to murder. We have pursued him halfway around the world. We were advised that this outpost was the nearest seat of British law where he could be held securely.”
Major Harding stroked his mustache thoughtfully. “Indeed. This is most irregular. You have evidence of these claims, I presume?”
“We have the man himself,” Reynard said grimly. “And my own testimony. I am a detective from the United States Secret Service.”
The major’s demeanor shifted at once. The mention of a government agency gave their story a weight it had previously lacked.
“I see,” he said, his tone becoming more accommodating. “This is a serious business, then. Come with me to my office, gentlemen. We will discuss this in private. My men will see to it that your… vessel… is not disturbed.”
He turned to his lieutenant. “Lieutenant Davies, post a guard of honor around the airship. No one is to approach it without my express permission. See that the crewmen aboard are made comfortable.”
“Very good, sir!” the lieutenant replied, saluting smartly.
As Frank and Reynard followed the major toward the command building, Frank glanced back at the Pegasus. He saw Lieutenant Davies selecting a corporal and six men for the guard detail. They looked like fine, upstanding soldiers. A profound sense of relief washed over the young inventor. Their long and dangerous chase was finally over. Murdock was as good as in irons, and they could finally rest.
The major’s office was precisely what one would expect of a colonial officer. The walls were adorned with maps of India, regimental flags, and the mounted heads of a tiger and a water buffalo. Major Harding poured three glasses of whiskey.
“A trying journey, I have no doubt,” he said, pushing the glasses toward his guests. “Now, tell me everything. From the beginning.”
For the next hour, Frank and Tom Reynard recounted their tale. They spoke of Murdock’s initial crimes, the pursuit across America, the chase over the Pacific and through the jungles of India. They described the battle with the bandits in the rocky pass and their narrow escape.
Major Harding listened with rapt attention, nodding occasionally and asking sharp, pertinent questions. He seemed particularly interested in the resources Murdock had at his disposal.
“So this man has a network of agents,” the major mused. “And is free with his money. That combination can cause a great deal of trouble in this country.”
“He has proven to be as slippery as an eel and as venomous as a cobra,” Reynard said, taking a sip of his whiskey. “That is why we need to place him in the most secure custody possible until he can be extradited.”
“Of course, of course,” the major said, rising from his desk. “You have done the Crown a service, gentlemen. A man like that at large is a threat to civilized order. I will have him transferred to our military prison at once. It is carved from the solid rock of this mesa. Not even a rat could escape it.”
He stepped to the door and called for his lieutenant.
“Davies, assemble a full escort. Go to the airship and take charge of the prisoner, Martin Murdock. The American crewmen are to be relieved of their duty. Place the prisoner in high-security cell four.”
“Sir!” said the lieutenant, a faint, almost imperceptible smile on his lips before he saluted and departed.
“There,” said the major, turning back to Frank. “It is done. You and your men are my honored guests. I will have quarters prepared. You must join me for dinner tonight. We will celebrate the capture of this scoundrel.”
“Thank you, Major,” Frank said, feeling a weight lift from his shoulders. “Your hospitality and efficiency are greatly appreciated.”
“Think nothing of it,” Harding replied, beaming. “All in a day’s work for the Empire. Now, if you’ll excuse me, I must see to the prisoner’s processing myself. I will send an orderly to show you to your rooms.”
As the major departed, Tom Reynard stared down into his glass, a thoughtful frown on his face.
“Well, that was easier than I expected,” Frank said cheerfully.
“Almost too easy,” the detective murmured, his eyes narrowed.
“What do you mean, Tom?”
“I don’t know. Something isn’t right. A major in a remote post like this… he’s heard of Murdock the financier, but he accepts our story without demanding a single piece of paper, without sending a wire to Calcutta for confirmation?”
“He recognized your authority as a Secret Service agent,” Frank countered.
“Perhaps. But did you notice his ring?”
“His ring? No.”
“It was a gold signet ring. With a very particular design on it. A spider in the center of a web.”
Frank felt a sudden chill. “A spider? What of it?”
“It’s the symbol of a syndicate I’ve been investigating for years,” Reynard said, his voice low and urgent. “A shadowy international consortium of financiers and criminals. They call themselves the Arachnean Circle. And their leader, the one they call the ‘Master Weaver,’ is rumored to be none other than Martin Murdock.”
Frank’s blood ran cold. “Harding… is one of them?”
“It’s the only thing that makes sense! Murdock’s money got here before we did! Harding isn’t going to imprison him, Frank! He’s going to free him!”
The two men stared at each other for a horrifying second, the truth of their situation crashing down upon them. They had not delivered their captive to justice. They had delivered him to his most powerful ally.
“Barney and Pomp!” Frank cried. “They’re in danger!”
Without another word, they burst from the office and sprinted back toward the parade ground. As they rounded the corner of the barracks, their worst fears were confirmed.
Lieutenant Davies and his squad were not escorting a shackled Murdock from the ship. Instead, they had their rifles leveled at the deck of the Pegasus, where Barney and Pomp stood back to back, their own weapons raised. Martin Murdock was standing near the brig, a triumphant sneer on his face, while two soldiers worked at the lock with a set of iron tools.
“I knew yez were thraitors!” Barney was yelling. “Drop them tools or I’ll put a hole in yer gizzards!”
“Stand down, you Irish ape!” Lieutenant Davies commanded, his voice dripping with contempt. “You are prisoners of the Queen’s army!”
“The devil we are!” Pomp shouted back, his rifle steady. “You’re no soldiers! You’re just more of Murdock’s hired thugs!”
It was at that moment that Frank and Reynard arrived.
“Davies!” Frank roared. “Call off your men! You are committing treason!”
The lieutenant turned, his face a mask of surprise that quickly hardened into a cruel smirk.
“Reade! So you figured it out. A bit too late, I’m afraid. Major Harding sends his regards. Men, seize them!”
Two of the soldiers turned their rifles on Frank and Reynard. But the detective was faster. His own revolver was in his hand in a flash, and he fired twice. The two soldiers cried out and staggered back, clutching wounded shoulders.
That was the signal for all-out battle.
“Fire!” Davies shrieked.
The remaining soldiers opened fire on the Pegasus. Bullets whanged and ricocheted off the airship’s tough metal hull. Barney and Pomp returned fire from the deck, their repeating rifles cracking in the evening air.
“We’ve been fools, Tom!” Frank shouted over the din, taking cover behind a water trough. “We walked right into his web!”
“No time for regrets!” Reynard yelled back, firing again at a soldier who was trying to flank them. “We have to get back on the ship!”
A bugle sounded from the command post. It was the general alarm. In moments, the entire parade ground would be swarming with soldiers, all of them under Harding’s treacherous command.
On the deck of the Pegasus, Murdock saw his chance. While Barney and Pomp were occupied with the soldiers below, he lunged at the two men working on his cell. He snatched a heavy iron crowbar from one of them and swung it viciously, knocking the man senseless.
“To me!” Murdock bellowed, brandishing the crowbar. “Forget the ship! Get me out of this fort! The Major will reward you!”
Lieutenant Davies hesitated. His orders were to free Murdock, but also to secure the incredible airship. Greed and confusion warred on his face.
Frank saw his opening. “He’s trying to escape, Davies!” he taunted. “He’s going to cheat you out of your prize!”
The taunt worked. Davies turned his attention back to Murdock, shouting orders at his men. The brief distraction was all Frank needed.
“Now, Tom! To the ladder!”
The inventor and the detective broke cover and dashed for the Pegasus. Bullets kicked up dust at their feet. From the deck, Barney let out a wild Irish yell and laid down a covering fire that made two more soldiers dive for cover.
Frank scrambled up the ladder first, Reynard right behind him.
“Pomp, get to the engine room!” Frank commanded as his feet hit the deck. “Full power! Barney, cast off the moorings!”
“Right away, Masther Frank!” Pomp cried, vanishing into the deck-house.
Barney, meanwhile, was having trouble. Two of Davies’ men had rushed the ship and were trying to climb aboard. The Celt met them with the butt of his rifle, sending one tumbling back to the ground with a cracked skull. The other grabbed Barney’s leg, trying to haul him down.
At that moment, Murdock, seeing his escape with Davies’ men falling apart, decided on a new course of action. If he could not escape, he would seize the Pegasus for himself. He charged across the deck, the heavy crowbar raised high, aiming a killing blow at Frank’s head.
“Look out!” Reynard shouted.
Frank spun around just in time. He had no time to draw a weapon. He did the only thing he could. He ducked under the wild swing and tackled the larger man around the waist. The two of them crashed to the deck, rolling over and over in a desperate struggle.
The deep hum of the dynamo beginning to spin filled the air, and the great helices overhead began to turn, slowly at first, then with increasing speed.
“Let go o’ me, ye divil!” Barney roared, kicking himself free from the soldier and sending him sprawling. He rushed to the mooring lines and began frantically untying them.
Reynard leveled his revolver at the struggling form of Martin Murdock. “Hold still, Murdock, or this is your last day on earth!”
But Frank and Murdock were a tangled knot of limbs. A shot was too risky. Murdock was a strong man, maddened by his lust for freedom. He brought his knee up sharply into Frank’s ribs, forcing a pained grunt from the inventor. His fingers clawed for Frank’s eyes.
Down on the parade ground, more soldiers were pouring out of the barracks, rifles in hand. Major Harding himself stood on the steps of his office, his face purple with rage, shouting orders.
“Fire at will! Bring that airship down! I want them dead or alive!”
A volley of shots rang out, and bullets rained against the Pegasus. One tore through the canvas of the rudder, while another shattered a window in the deck-house.
The ship lurched as the first of the mooring lines was cast off. It began to rise unevenly, tilting at a dangerous angle.
“Hurry, Barney!” Frank gasped, barely blocking a blow from Murdock’s fist.
“I’m tryin’, sor! These knots are tied by a sailor!”
The ship tilted further. Reynard was thrown against the railing. Frank and Murdock rolled dangerously close to the edge of the deck. With a surge of desperate strength, Murdock shoved Frank away and scrambled to his feet. He saw the last mooring line straining, and with a wild cry, he swung the crowbar down upon it.
The thick rope snapped with a sound like a gunshot.
Freed from its last tether, the Pegasus leaped into the air. The sudden ascent threw everyone off balance. Murdock, caught mid-swing, stumbled backward with a cry of alarm. His heel caught on the low deck railing. For a heart-stopping second, he teetered on the brink, his arms windmilling wildly. Then, with a final, choked scream, he tumbled over the side.
“Murdock!” Frank cried, scrambling to the rail.
He was just in time to see the villain’s body plummet toward the fort below. Martin Murdock, the master criminal, fell like a stone and crashed onto the stone parade ground not fifty feet from where a horrified Major Harding stood. His reign of terror was over.
But their own troubles were just beginning.
“They’re still firing!” Barney yelled, ducking as a bullet whistled past his ear.
“Get us higher, Pomp!” Frank shouted down the hatch. “Maximum ascent!”
The hum of the engines rose to a powerful whine. The Pegasus shot upward, climbing rapidly out of rifle range. The fort of Rajpore shrank below them, the soldiers now just tiny red specks, their gunfire growing faint and then ceasing altogether.
Silence descended on the deck of the airship, broken only by the whir of the helices and the rush of the wind. Frank, Barney, and Reynard stared at each other, their chests heaving.
“Holy smoke,” Reynard breathed, holstering his revolver. “I thought we were done for.”
“Be ther powers,” Barney said, wiping sweat from his brow with a shaky hand. “He’s gone. The black-hearted divil is finally gone.”
Frank leaned on the railing, looking down into the deepening twilight where the fort had become just a dark shape on the plain. He felt no triumph, only a deep, weary sickness. A man was dead. A man they had fought so hard to bring to justice.
Pomp emerged from the deck-house, his face grim.
“He’s gone, then, Masther Frank?”
Frank nodded slowly. “He fell. We’re free of him, Pomp.”
“But we are not free of his friends,” Reynard added grimly. “We just escaped a fortress full of British soldiers who now want our heads. We have been branded as outlaws. We can’t land at any British port in India now. Or anywhere else, for that matter. Harding will have wired every outpost from here to Shanghai.”
The truth of his words sank in. They had won the battle, but in doing so, had made themselves fugitives. They were alone, in a stolen sky, with the world’s most powerful empire now counted among their enemies.
“Where can we go?” Barney asked, his voice losing its usual cheer. “What’ll we do now, Masther Frank?”
Frank stood up straight, the weariness in his face replaced by his old, familiar look of steely determination. He was the inventor, the leader, the man who found a way when there was no way.
He looked east, toward the night that was swallowing the subcontinent.
“Harding will expect us to run west, back the way we came,” he said, his voice quiet but firm. “He will expect us to try for the coast, to get back to American waters.”
“A logical assumption,” Reynard agreed.
“Which is exactly why we won’t do it,” Frank declared. He strode to the binnacle and took the wheel from the control lock. “We can’t go back. So we will go forward. We will cross the rest of India, fly over the Himalayas, and lose ourselves in the vast, uncharted lands of China.”
His crew stared at him, their faces a mixture of shock and dawning excitement. The Himalayas! China! It was a mad, impossible plan. It was a Frank Reade, Jr. plan.
“Begorra!” Barney exclaimed, a grin slowly spreading across his face. “Crossin’ ther roof o’ ther world! Shure, an’ it’s the grandest adventure of all!”
“They will never think to look for us there,” Reynard said, a spark of admiration in his eye. “It is a bold stroke, Frank. A very bold stroke.”
“We have no choice,” Frank said, his hands tightening on the wheel as he turned the nose of the great airship toward the distant, unseen peaks. “The Pegasus is our only home now. We’ll refuel and resupply in the wilderness, away from cities and soldiers. We are on our own.”
He looked at his loyal friends, their faces illuminated by the soft glow of the compass light. Barney, the stout-hearted Irishman. Pomp, the steady and capable engineer. Tom Reynard, the keen-witted detective. They were all that was left. A crew of four, on the most wonderful machine ever built, fugitives from a world that had betrayed them.
“Set the course due east, Barney,” Frank ordered. “Our chase is over. A new journey has just begun.”

THE END OF THE CHASE.

The Pegasus, a silent specter in the vast Indian night, turned her sharp prow eastward. Below, the jungle from which they had just escaped was a dark, tangled patch upon the sleeping earth. The Rover, the vessel of their long and bitter pursuit, was a fading memory, left behind with the ghost of Martin Murdock.

A new spirit filled the crew. The grim tension of the hunt was gone, replaced by the thrilling uncertainty of flight into the unknown.

“Begorra, an’ will yez look at that!” cried Barney from the port rail. “It’s like we have ther whole world to ourselves, Masther Frank!”

Frank stood at the great wheel in the deck-house, his eyes fixed on the softly glowing binnacle. The airship moved with a steady, reassuring hum, the powerful dynamo purring deep within its bowels.

“We do, Barney,” he replied, his voice ringing with confidence. “For now, at least. We are masters of our own destiny. Reynard, what is our altitude?”

The detective, who had been studying the aneroid barometer, looked up.

“We are ascending steadily, Frank. Just passing ten thousand feet. The air grows thin.”

“Good. We will need the height to clear the foothills. Pomp, how are the engines performing after the repairs?”

Pomp, ever the diligent engineer, came up from the engine room, wiping a smudge of grease from his cheek.

“She’s running as sweet as a song, Masther Frank,” he reported with a proud grin. “That new dynamo wheel has settled in proper. But golly, it’s getting cold down there! The metal is like ice to the touch.”

“Just as I expected,” said Frank. “The higher we go, the colder it will become. We are entering a new climate altogether. Engage the electric heaters in the engine room and the cabins. We must protect the machinery, and ourselves, from the frost.”

“Aye, aye, sor!” Pomp saluted smartly and vanished below.

Soon, a comfortable warmth began to permeate the living quarters and the vital heart of the airship. The Pegasus climbed higher and higher, a silver fish swimming up a vast, starry ocean. For two days they flew, a relentless push toward the east. The plains of India gave way to rolling hills, and then to the jagged, imposing teeth of the Himalayan foothills.

The crew was in high spirits. They had food, water, and the most advanced flying machine ever conceived by man. The threat of the British Empire seemed a distant problem, a world away from their lofty perch.

On the third morning, Barney, who was at the wheel, gave a sudden shout.

“Saints preserve us! Masther Frank, come quick!”

Frank and Reynard rushed to the deck-house. Before them, filling the entire horizon, was a sight that struck them all into a reverent silence.

The Himalayas.

It was not a chain of mountains, but a wall. A colossal, world-ending rampart of rock and ice, piercing the very heavens. Peaks of brilliant white, kissed by the morning sun, soared to heights so great they seemed to be a part of another, celestial world.

“By Jove,” murmured Reynard, his detective’s cool demeanor for once completely shattered. “I never imagined…”

“Ther roof o’ ther world!” Barney whispered in awe. “It’s grander than any picture!”

Frank’s heart swelled with the pride of an explorer. Before him lay the greatest natural barrier on earth, a place untrodden by all but the hardiest natives and a few lost souls. And he would conquer it. He would fly the Pegasus over its highest peaks.

“It is magnificent,” he said, his voice quiet. “But it will also be our greatest test. The winds in those passes are said to be terrible, and the air is too thin for a man to breathe for long. We must be prepared.”

He took the wheel himself, his face set in a look of intense concentration. “Pomp! Full power to the lifting helices! We must gain as much altitude as possible before we enter the passes!”

The hum of the dynamo deepened to a powerful roar. The Pegasus surged upward, climbing toward the dizzying heights. Below, the world fell away into a dizzying map of chasms and lower peaks. The cold became intense. Frost began to form on the outside of the deck-house windows.

“Altitude twenty thousand feet!” Reynard called out. “The air is dangerously thin, Frank! I feel a lightness in my head.”

“We all do,” Frank replied, his own breathing becoming more deliberate. “The engine feels it, too. The helices have less air to bite into. But we must go higher. There is a storm brewing ahead.”

He pointed. In the distance, between two immense peaks that formed a natural gateway, a dark mass of cloud was churning. It was not like a storm at sea. It was a solid, roiling wall of gray and white, shot through with flickers of lightning.

“Begorra, it’s a bad one!” Barney exclaimed, his face paling slightly. “Can we go around it, sor?”

“There is no around,” Frank said grimly. “Only through. Hold on, everyone! This is going to be rough!”

The Pegasus plunged into the storm.

The world outside vanished in a blinding swirl of snow and hail. The wind struck them not as a gale, but as a solid, physical blow. The mighty airship, which had sailed so serenely for weeks, was tossed about like a child’s kite.

“She can’t take much more of this!” yelled Reynard, clinging to a stanchion for support.

A terrific shrieking sound filled the air as the wind tore at the metal skin of the aerostat. The entire vessel groaned and shuddered.

“The helices are icing up!” Frank shouted over the din. “It’s adding weight and throwing them off balance! Pomp! Can you divert more power to the blade heaters?”

Pomp struggled up the ladder from below, his face a mask of concern.

“I’m giving them all she’s got, Masther Frank! The dynamo is screaming! The cold is unbelievable! It’s paralyzing the lubricants!”

A violent lurch threw them all against the walls of the cabin. A horrible grinding noise came from above.

“Ther liftin’ screws!” Barney cried. “They’re laborin’ somethin’ fierce!”

“The ice is too thick!” Frank roared, fighting the wheel with all his might. The ship was falling. Despite the helices spinning at their maximum, they were losing altitude at an alarming rate. “We are going down!”

Below them was nothing but a white abyss of churning snow. To be dashed against the unseen cliffs meant certain destruction. Frank’s mind raced, his inventor’s brain working furiously even in the face of doom.

“The forward propeller!” he yelled. “It’s our only chance! We must drive forward, try to break through the storm and find clearer air on the other side! Pomp, cut power to the helices and give everything to the thruster!”

It was a desperate gamble. Without the lift from the helices, they would fall even faster, but if they could gain enough forward momentum, they might be able to use the body of the Pegasus itself as a wing, gaining some aerodynamic lift and control.

“Aye, aye, sor!” Pomp scrambled back down to the engine room.

The deafening whir of the great helices overhead sputtered and died. The ship plunged sickeningly. For a heart-stopping moment, they were in a free fall. Then, with a surge that pressed them all back in their seats, the powerful forward propeller bit into the storm.

The Pegasus shot forward like an arrow. The controls barely responded, but Frank, with superhuman effort, managed to keep the nose pointed level. They were a projectile, fired blindly through the heart of the tempest.

How long they flew in this mad dash, none of them could say. It was an eternity of roaring wind, shuddering metal, and silent prayer.

Then, as suddenly as it had begun, they were through.

They burst out of the wall of cloud into a patch of eerily calm, clear air. But they were no longer high above the peaks. They were in a deep, enclosed valley, a vast bowl surrounded on all sides by sheer, unclimbable cliffs of ice and rock.

And they were still falling.

“The helices, Pomp!” Frank shouted. “Restart them now!”

The motors whined, but the great blades above remained sluggish. The ice was too heavy. They turned feebly, unable to arrest their descent.

Below them was a snow-covered expanse, a frozen lake or a flat valley floor. It was their only hope.

“Brace for impact!” Frank cried.

With a tremendous crunch and a long, groaning skid, the Pegasus struck the ground. It slid for several hundred yards across the snow, tearing a great furrow in the pristine white, before finally coming to a rest at a slight tilt.

Silence.

The roar of the storm was now a muffled sound, high above them. Down in the valley, an unearthly quiet reigned.

Frank slowly let go of the wheel, his knuckles white. He and his crew looked at each other, their faces drawn and pale in the dim light of the cabin.

“Is… is everyone alright?” he asked, his voice hoarse.

Barney, Pomp, and Reynard all gave shaky affirmations. They were bruised and battered, but alive.

“Holy smoke!” Tom Reynard said, peering out a window. “Where in the world are we?”

Frank looked out. They were trapped. The walls of the valley soared up thousands of feet, their tops lost in the swirling clouds of the storm they had just escaped. There was no way out.

“We’re alive,” Frank said, his resolve returning. “That is what matters. First, let’s assess the damage.”

A thorough inspection revealed the gravity of their situation. The landing gear was twisted and broken. The hull was dented in several places. But the most serious problem was with the main lifting gear.

“It’s the main shaft for the port helix, Masther Frank,” Pomp reported grimly after an hour’s work in the biting cold. “When she iced up and we hit the ground, the shock of it must have snapped the gearing clean in two. It’s a special kind of tempered steel. We can’t fix this with what we have on board. We need a forge. We need to recast the entire piece.”

A forge. In a desolate, frozen valley thousands of feet up in the Himalayas. The suggestion was absurd.

“Then we are stranded,” Reynard stated flatly.

“Begorra, we’re done for,” Barney added, his usual cheer gone. “We’ll freeze to death in this forsaken place.”

Frank refused to despair. He was Frank Reade, Jr., and his mind never accepted defeat.

“No,” he said firmly. “We are engineers. We are inventors. If we cannot find a forge, we will build one. We have the dynamo for power to create heat. We need fuel, and we need raw material. Let us search the valley. We cannot be the first living things to have ever been here.”

Leaving Pomp to begin the arduous task of dismantling the broken machinery, Frank, Barney, and Reynard armed themselves with rifles and snowshoes of Frank’s own quick design and set out to explore their prison.

The valley was perhaps five miles long and two miles wide, a perfect oval of white. The air was thin and bitingly cold, but breathable. For hours they trudged through the deep snow, their search yielding nothing but more rock and ice. Hope began to fade.

It was Barney who saw it first.

“Masther Frank! Look there! By ther cliffs!”

He was pointing to a dark patch against the far valley wall. It looked like a cave. As they drew closer, however, they saw it was no natural formation. Stone steps, cut with precision from the rock itself, led upward into the gloom. At the top of the steps was a massive, ornately carved wooden door.

“Man-made!” Reynard exclaimed. “Impossible! Who could live in such a place?”

“Someone who wishes to be left alone,” Frank said, his eyes gleaming with a mixture of caution and excitement. “And someone who may have the resources we need. Let’s see who is home.”

With their rifles at the ready, they ascended the slippery, ice-covered steps. The air grew colder as they climbed into the shadow of the cliff. The great door, made of a dark, heavy wood unknown to them, was closed fast. There was no bell or knocker.

Frank raised his fist and knocked loudly. The sound echoed in the vast silence.

For a long moment, nothing happened. Frank was about to knock again when a small sliding panel in the center of the door shot open. Two dark, piercing eyes stared out at them.

A voice spoke, not in English or any Indian dialect they knew, but in a strange, guttural tongue.

Barney, taken aback, raised his rifle. “What’d he say, sor?”

“Easy, Barney,” Frank commanded, holding up a hand in a universal gesture of peace. “He is as surprised to see us as we are to see him.”

Frank pointed to himself. “Frank,” he said clearly. Then he pointed to his friends. “Barney. Reynard.”

He then pointed down at the wounded Pegasus, a silver sliver far below on the valley floor, and made a gesture of falling, followed by a motion of breaking something.

The eyes in the panel narrowed. There was a long silence, then a series of clicks and bolts being drawn. With a low groan, the massive door swung inward.

Standing before them were three men. They were tall and powerfully built, dressed in thick robes of crimson and saffron. Their heads were shaved, and their faces were weathered and stern, like the mountains themselves. They carried no weapons, but held themselves with an air of absolute authority.

The man who had opened the panel, clearly the leader, looked from the strange visitors to the incredible machine in the valley below. His expression was one of profound, controlled astonishment.

He spoke again in his strange tongue, then gestured for them to enter.

Frank hesitated for only a second. They had no other choice. With a nod to his companions, he stepped across the threshold, out of the frozen valley and into the hidden monastery of the high Himalayas.

They were led down a long corridor lit by butter lamps, the air thick with the smell of incense and burning yak butter. The walls were covered in fantastic murals of gods and demons. It was a world out of time.

They entered a large, circular chamber. In the center, seated upon a raised platform, was an old man. He was ancient, his skin like wrinkled parchment, but his eyes were alive with a startling intelligence. He was clearly the High Lama, the leader of this strange community.

The monks who had led them in prostrated themselves, speaking rapidly. The High Lama listened, his gaze never leaving Frank. When his men had finished, he raised a withered hand and spoke. His voice was weak with age, but his words were in perfect, if archaic, English.

“You are from the outside world,” he said. It was a statement, not a question.

Frank and his friends started in amazement.

“You… you speak English!” Frank stammered.

“Many years ago,” the old man said, a faint smile touching his lips, “a man like you fell from the sky. Not in a silver ship, but in a basket of wicker and cloth, borne by hot air. He was a British explorer. He stayed with us for many years before he died. He taught me his tongue.”

He gestured to the floor. “Be seated. You are the first outsiders to find the valley of Zang-La in a generation. Tell me of your great silver bird, and why it has fallen.”

Frank, sensing a powerful and wise ally, explained their predicament. He told of their journey, of the great storm, and of the broken machinery that had stranded them. He explained, as simply as he could, the principles of the Pegasus and their need for a forge and tempered steel to affect a repair.

The High Lama listened patiently, his ancient eyes showing a deep understanding.

“A forge, we have,” he said when Frank was finished. “We work in iron and bronze. But steel of the kind you describe is beyond our art. However,” he added, his eyes twinkling, “the mountain provides. In the highest caves, there is a metal, a star-metal, which fell from the heavens long ago. It is harder than any rock. Perhaps it is what you seek.”

Hope surged in Frank’s chest. “A meteorite! It would be perfect! Can you show us where to find it?”

The Lama’s face grew grave. “It is not so simple. The caves are the hunting ground of the Yeh-Teh.”

“Ther what?” Barney asked.

“The creature you in the west might call the Abominable Snowman,” the Lama explained. “A great, white-furred beast, half-man, half-ape, of immense strength and cunning. It has taken many of our brothers over the years. We do not dare to venture into the high caves now. It is the demon of the mountain.”

Frank exchanged a look with Reynard. A plan was forming in his mind, a bold stroke born of necessity.

“Your holiness,” Frank said, standing up. “We are in your debt. You offer us the use of your forge and the location of this star-metal. In return, let us offer you something.”

He strode to the center of the room, his face alight with an idea.

“This beast, this Yeh-Teh,” he declared. “We will hunt it for you. We will destroy it or drive it from this valley forever. We have weapons it has never seen. Let us rid you of your demon. In return, you will aid us in the repair of our airship.”

A murmur went through the assembled monks. The High Lama stared at Frank, a new light of respect in his ancient eyes.

“You would face the demon for us?” he asked.

“We would,” Frank answered without hesitation. “We have faced men and tigers. We do not fear a beast of the snow.”

The High Lama was silent for a long time. The fate of the Pegasus, and its crew, hung in the balance.

Finally, he nodded slowly.

“It is a bargain,” he said. “Destroy the Yeh-Teh that plagues our valley, and my people will give you all the aid you require. The star-metal is yours. But be warned, stranger. Many have tried. All have failed. The mountain does not give up its demons easily.”

“And we are not easily defeated,” Frank replied, a grim smile on his face. “Tell us where to find this creature’s lair. The hunt begins at once!”

CHAPTER XII.
THE DEMON OF THE MOUNTAIN.

The High Lama nodded his assent to Frank’s bold proposition. He rose from his cushion, his movements slow and deliberate, and gestured for one of the monks. The man hurried away and returned a moment later with a rolled piece of yak-skin parchment.

“This is a map of the high passes,” the Lama said, his voice echoing softly in the great hall. “It shows the way to the caves of the Yeh-Teh. The star-metal you seek is within the beast’s lair. Our legends say it is the creature’s heart, though our scouts who have ventured near and lived to tell the tale say it is but a great dark rock the beast guards.”

Frank took the map and unrolled it. It was a crude but effective drawing, showing the twists and turns of the mountain paths leading upward from the monastery.

“We are in your debt, your holiness,” said Frank, bowing his head slightly. “We will not fail.”

“May the wisdom of the ages guide your hand,” the Lama replied gravely. “Go now, and prepare. The mountain is most treacherous after midday.”

There was no time to be lost. Frank, Reynard, Barney and Pomp hastened from the hall to the crippled Pegasus to gather their equipment.

“Begorra, a real live demon hunt!” exclaimed Barney, his eyes twinkling with excitement as he polished the barrel of his electric rifle. “It’s been a good while since we’ve had a proper scrap wid a wild baste!”

“Golly, I dunno, Marse Barney,” said Pomp, his eyes wide as he packed dried rations into a satchel. “Dis here Yeh-Teh sounds like a pow’ful bad customer. Half man, half ape, he says! Dat’s a combination dis chile ain’t eager to meet!”

“Nonsense, Pomp,” said Frank, checking the loads in a heavy-caliber revolver. “We have faced down worse than a simple beast. Our science gives us the advantage. Besides our rifles, I have brought along several of my new concussion grenades. If this creature is as tough as they say, one of these should give him a proper surprise.”

The young inventor held up a small, metallic sphere, no larger than a baseball. It gleamed under the dim light of the engine room.

“A stun grenade?” asked Reynard, inspecting his own sidearm.

“More than that,” Frank explained. “It releases a focused blast of compressed air and a high frequency sonic pulse. It is designed to disorient and incapacitate without destroying. It should be more than a match for any living creature.”

“A sound tactical option,” the detective agreed, nodding.

Soon they were ready. Clad in the warmest furs the monks could provide over their own clothing, and armed to the teeth, the four adventurers presented a formidable sight. They carried their electric rifles, coils of rope, and the provisions Pomp had packed. Frank had a special satchel for his grenades.

A silent monk, sworn to an oath of quietude, led them to the edge of the valley. He pointed up a steep, winding path that disappeared into the snow-swept rocks above. He bowed low to the party, and then retreated back toward the monastery, his saffron robes a stark splash of color against the white landscape.

“Well, lads, there’s no turning back now,” Frank said, his breath pluming in the frigid air. “The fate of the Pegasus is at the top of that mountain. Let’s go!”

With the young inventor in the lead, they began the perilous ascent. The path was narrow and slick with ice. A bitter wind howled down from the peaks, tearing at their clothes and stinging their faces. The air grew thin, and every step was a labor.

“Shure, and it’s a bit of a climb!” Barney panted after an hour of hard going. “Me lungs feel like they’re about to burst!”

“Keep moving,” urged Frank. “The higher we get, the less time we want to spend exposed on these ledges.”

They pushed on, the world around them a dizzying panorama of white peaks and blue ice. Far below, the hidden monastery was but a tiny speck. After another two hours of grueling effort, they reached a wide plateau, just as the map had indicated. Here, the wind was somewhat calmer, and they paused to catch their breath.

It was Reynard who saw them first.

“Frank, look!” the detective exclaimed, pointing to the snow at their feet.

Imprinted in the white powder were a series of enormous tracks. They were like those of a great, barefoot man, but easily twice the size, with long, claw-like toe marks gouged deep into the frozen ground.

“Bejabers!” whispered Barney, his eyes wide. “Look at the size of that footprint! It’s bigger than a dinner platter!”

“The Yeh-Teh,” Frank said grimly. “He is near. The tracks are fresh.”

A palpable tension fell over the group. The playful excitement of the hunt was replaced by the cold reality of the danger they faced. They followed the tracks, their rifles at the ready. The trail led them across the plateau to a dark cliff face, riddled with the mouths of numerous caves. A foul, musky odor, like that of a wet dog mixed with rotted meat, hung heavy in the air.

“He’s in one of these caves,” Reynard stated, his voice low.

“Which one, I wonder?” said Pomp, peering nervously into the dark openings.

Frank consulted the Lama’s map. He then studied the pattern of the tracks and the direction of the wind.

“This one,” he declared, pointing to the largest of the cave mouths, a gaping black maw from which the foul stench seemed to emanate most strongly. “The beast would choose the largest cave to command the entrance to the others, and the one most sheltered from the prevailing wind. Come on. And be ready for anything.”

Frank unhooked an electric lantern from his belt, and its powerful beam cut a brilliant swath through the oppressive darkness. He stepped cautiously into the cave, followed closely by the others.

The interior was cold and damp. The floor was littered with bones, cracked and split to get at the marrow. Most were from mountain goats and other large animals, but with a shudder, Frank saw what was unmistakably a human skull.

“Saints preserve us,” Barney muttered, crossing himself. “The poor souls.”

They proceeded deeper, their footsteps echoing in the vast, silent cavern. The beam of the lantern danced across walls of glittering ice and dark rock. The air grew colder, and the stench became almost overpowering. Suddenly, a low growl rumbled from the darkness ahead. It was a sound of pure, deep-chested malice that seemed to vibrate in their very bones.

“Golly!” Pomp whispered, his knuckles white on the stock of his rifle.

“Steady, men,” Frank commanded, his voice tight. “He knows we are here. Fan out. Pomp, you and Reynard take the left. Barney, with me.”

They advanced slowly into a vast central chamber. And then they saw it.

It was a nightmare given form. The Yeh-Teh was a creature of immense size and terrifying aspect, standing well over eight feet tall. Its body was covered in a coat of thick, shaggy white fur, matted with dirt and old blood. Its form was that of a great ape, but it stood upright like a man, on two massive, pillar-like legs. Its long arms, corded with inhuman muscle, ended in hands tipped with thick, black claws. But its face was the most terrible part. It was a semi-human visage, with a flat nose, a wide, lipless mouth filled with yellow fangs, and two small, intelligent eyes that burned with a hellish, red light.

With a roar that shook the very foundations of the mountain, the beast lunged.

It moved with a speed that belied its enormous bulk. Barney raised his rifle and fired. The bolt of electricity struck the creature square in the chest. Under normal circumstances, it would have dropped a charging bull. The Yeh-Teh merely flinched, roared again in fury, and kept coming.

“It didn’t work!” the Irishman cried in disbelief.

The monster swiped a great paw at him. Barney leaped aside just in time, the claws whistling past his head and gouging deep furrows in the rock wall behind him. Before he could recover, the beast backhanded him, sending him flying through the air to crash into a pile of rocks.

“Barney!” yelled Frank.

Pomp, seeing his friend in peril, let out a cry of rage and fired his own electric rifle. The charge struck the beast in the shoulder. It snarled and turned its malevolent red eyes upon him. At the same time, Reynard emptied his revolver at the creature’s head. The bullets might as well have been bee stings. They flattened against its thick skull with pathetic pings.

Now the beast’s full attention was on Pomp and the detective. It ignored Frank for the moment and charged them.

“This way, you ugly brute!” Reynard shouted, firing his last shot and drawing the creature away from Pomp, who was helping a dazed Barney to his feet.

This was the moment Frank had been waiting for. With the beast’s back turned to him, he saw his chance.

“Get clear!” he shouted.

He pulled one of the metallic spheres from his satchel, twisted a small dial on its surface, and hurled it with all his might. The grenade sailed through the air in a perfect arc, landing directly between the Yeh-Teh’s massive feet.

For a split second, nothing happened. The beast looked down, confused. Then the grenade detonated.

There was no loud explosion, but rather a blinding flash of white light and a sudden, violent CRUMP of displaced air. It was accompanied by a piercing, high frequency whine that was physically painful to hear.

The effect on the Yeh-Teh was instantaneous and dramatic. The mighty creature screamed, a high, thin sound of agony, and clutched its head with both giant paws. It staggered about like a drunken man, its red eyes rolling wildly. The sonic pulse had overloaded its senses, and the air blast had struck it like a physical blow.

“Now, lads!” Frank cried, raising his own rifle. “While it’s stunned! Aim for the eyes!”

His shot was true. The bolt of electricity struck the beast’s face. At the same time, Barney, Pomp, and Reynard, who had quickly reloaded, all fired as one. Four bolts of electric fire converged on the monster’s head.

The Yeh-Teh gave one last, horrific shriek. Blinded and mortally wounded, it stumbled backward. Its foot found no purchase. With a final, despairing cry, the great white beast toppled over the edge of a deep, black chasm that split the floor of the chamber. It fell for a long time, its scream fading into the depths, until a faint, final thud echoed up from the darkness below.

Silence descended upon the cave. For a long moment, no one spoke. They had done it. The demon of the mountain was no more.

“Begorra,” Barney said, breaking the silence as he nursed a bruised shoulder. “That was a close one.”

“You are telling me!” said Pomp, wiping a sheen of cold sweat from his brow.

Frank rushed to the back of the chamber, his lantern held high. There, embedded in the far wall, just as the High Lama had said, was a great mass of dark, lusterless rock. It was not a rock, however. As Frank’s light played over its surface, he saw the telltale Widmanstätten patterns of an iron meteorite.

“We’ve found it!” he cried in triumph. “The star-metal! Pomp, get the hammers and chisels from the bag. We have work to do!”

They labored for an hour, chipping away sizable chunks of the incredibly dense metal. It was arduous work, but their spirits were high. They had faced the monster and won. Now, they had the means to repair their beloved airship.

Laden with the heavy pieces of the meteorite, they made their way back out of the cave and began the descent. Their return to the monastery was met with joyous celebration. When the monks saw that they carried not only the star-metal but also a large, clawed hand of the Yeh-Teh as proof of their victory, their gratitude knew no bounds.

The High Lama met them in the main courtyard. “You have lifted a curse that has plagued our valley for generations,” he said, his ancient eyes filled with a new light of respect. “The monastery, and all its resources, are yours to command. My smiths will keep the forge burning for you day and night.”

And so they did. For the next two days, the clang of hammer on anvil rang out through the peaceful valley. Under Frank’s expert guidance, the monastery’s blacksmiths, aided by the mighty arms of Barney and Pomp, worked the strange, super-hard star-metal. It was a difficult process, for the metal resisted heat and reshaping, but Frank’s inventive genius found a way. Using a mixture of charcoal and a special oxygen-fed blast furnace of his own design, they were able to heat the meteorite fragments to a glowing, white-hot state.

Slowly, painstakingly, a new dynamo wheel began to take shape under the blows of the heavy forge hammers. It was a thing of beauty, stronger and more perfectly balanced than the one that had shattered. While the others worked, Tom Reynard stood guard, and Frank machined the final piece with files and polishing stones, working with a jeweler’s precision.

At last, the work was done.

The new wheel was installed in the heart of the Pegasus’s engine room. The connections were made, the circuits checked and rechecked.

“All right, men,” Frank said, his face smudged with soot but his eyes bright with anticipation. “This is the moment of truth. Stand clear!”

He stood before the main control panel. His hand hovered over the large, brass master switch. He took a deep breath, and threw it.

For a heart-stopping second, nothing happened. Then, a low hum began to emanate from the dynamo. The lights on the control panel flickered to life, one by one. The hum grew to a steady, powerful thrum. The lights in the cabin came on, brighter and clearer than ever before.

“The dynamo is working!” Frank shouted in triumph.

He threw another lever. Outside, a familiar whirring sound began. The great helices, which had been still for so long, began to turn. Slowly at first, then faster and faster, they spun until they were a blur, their motion creating a powerful downdraft that kicked up the snow around the airship.

The Pegasus trembled, and then, with a smooth and silent grace, it lifted from the ground.

A great cheer went up from the four friends inside the cabin. Below them, the assembled monks stared up in awe and wonder as the great silver bird ascended into the heavens.

Frank guided the Pegasus in a slow circle over the monastery, dipping the nose of the airship in a final salute to the High Lama and his people. Then, he turned the craft eastward.

They climbed high above the jagged peaks of the Himalayas, leaving the hidden valley of Shangri-La far behind. Frank stood at the new glass window of the pilot house, his hands on the wheel, a grim smile on his face. The Pegasus was not just repaired; it was improved, stronger and faster than before.

He turned to Tom Reynard, who stood beside him, gazing out at the endless blue sky.

“Our detour is over, Mr. Reynard,” the young inventor said, his voice firm with renewed purpose. “We have faced the demon of the mountain and we have won. Now, we resume the hunt for a different kind of monster.”

The detective nodded. “Where to now, Frank?”

“Back to the Arabian Sea,” Frank declared. “We will retrace our path. Murdock thinks he has escaped us. He thinks our airship is destroyed. He is in for a great surprise. We will find the Rover, and this time, Martin Murdock will not get away!”

The great airship, a gleaming speck of silver against the sun, sped onward, its crew ready for the next chapter in their incredible adventure.

Just as the tiger reached the apex of its leap, the detective fired.
The shot was true. The bullet struck the tiger in its powerful shoulder. It was not a fatal wound, but the shock and pain were immense. The beast let out a shriek of agony and its trajectory faltered. It fell short of its target, crashing heavily into the undergrowth and twisting to snap at its wounded limb.
Murdock did not need a second invitation. He grabbed the rope ladder and began to scramble up with the frantic energy of a madman. His fine city clothes were torn to shreds by the branches, and his hands were raw, but he climbed with astonishing speed.
Frank, Barney, and Pomp leaned over the rail, hauling on the ladder to help him. In a few moments, he flopped over the railing and collapsed onto the deck of the Pegasus, a gasping, trembling, wretched heap.
“Up, Barney! Get us up!” Frank commanded.
The Celt threw the levers. The helices bit into the air with a powerful roar, and the airship shot upward, leaving the enraged, wounded tiger and the dark jungle far below.
For a full minute, no one spoke. The only sound was the panting of Martin Murdock and the steady thrum of the engine. Pomp kept the searchlight trained on the jungle floor, where the tiger could be seen limping back into the shadows, its roars of pain and fury slowly fading.
Finally, Murdock pushed himself into a sitting position. His face was scratched, his clothes were in tatters, and his eyes burned with a strange fire. One would expect gratitude, or at least relief. Instead, his expression was one of pure, unadulterated hatred.
He glared at Frank, his voice a hoarse whisper.
“You should have left me,” he snarled.
Frank Reade Jr. looked down at the man he had just snatched from certain death. His own face was cold and impassive.
“We save men, Murdock, we do not leave them to be eaten by wild animals,” he said, his voice ringing with authority. “But make no mistake. We did not save you for your own sake. You have been saved to face the justice of the law.”
“Justice!” Murdock spat on the deck. “You will regret this, Reade! You and your whole crew of fools!”
“That is enough out of you,” Tom Reynard said, stepping forward. He held a pair of steel handcuffs. “You are my prisoner, Murdock. Wanted for conspiracy, theft, and attempted murder.”
Murdock’s eyes blazed, but he saw the detective’s determined face and the pistols in the belts of Barney and Pomp. The fight went out of him. He sullenly allowed Reynard to snap the cuffs on his wrists.
“Take him below,” Frank ordered. “Lock him in the spare supply cabin. Pomp, stand guard at his door for the first watch. Do not let him out of your sight for a moment.”
“Yessir, Masther Frank!” the coon replied, his rifle held at the ready.
As Pomp and the detective led the defiant prisoner away, Frank turned to the pilot-house.
“Barney, what of the Rover?”
The Irishman, who had been scanning the sea, shook his head.
“She’s gone, sor. While we were down in that divil’s garden, she must have weighed anchor and made a run for it. There’s not a light to be seen on the water.”
“They have abandoned him,” Frank mused. “As soon as they saw he was ashore and we were in pursuit, they cut their losses and fled. So much for their loyalty bought with a hundred dollars.”
“Good riddance to them,” Barney said. “A cowardly lot of curs.”
The Pegasus now hung high and safe in the clear night sky, the moon illuminating the vast expanse of the Indian Ocean before them. The immediate danger was past. They had their man.
Frank went to the chart room. He unrolled a large map of the world and began to make calculations with a pencil and compass. After a few minutes, Tom Reynard entered.
“He is secure,” the detective reported. “As angry as a hornet in a bottle, but secure. He does nothing but mutter threats.”
“Let him mutter,” Frank said without looking up. “His power to do harm is at an end. We must now set a course for home.”
“San Francisco is a long way off,” Reynard noted, looking at the map.
“Indeed it is. We will cross the Indian Ocean, round the Cape of Good Hope, then traverse the Atlantic. It will be the longest continuous voyage the Pegasus has ever attempted.”
“Think the airship is up to it, lad?”
Frank looked up from his charts, and a confident smile lit his face.
“Mr. Reynard, after rebuilding her engine in the heart of the Himalayas, I believe the Pegasus can fly to the moon and back if she has to. We will make for the coast of Africa first, to replenish our water supplies. Then, the long journey home begins.”
Later, after Pomp had prepared a late meal of salt beef and biscuits, the four friends gathered in the main cabin. The mood was one of quiet triumph.
“Well, bejabbers, we’ve got him,” Barney said, taking a large bite of a biscuit. “After chasin’ the blackguard halfway around the globe.”
“It was a near thing tonight,” Tom Reynard added, sipping his coffee. “If that tiger had been a second faster, or my aim a second slower…” He shook his head.
“All’s well that ends well,” Frank said. “The important thing is that he is in our custody. His days of creating misery and chaos are over.”
“Don’t be so sure, Masther Frank,” said Pomp, who had come in from his post. “That man is like a snake. Even in a cage, you cain’t turn your back on him.”
“Pomp is right,” Frank agreed. “We must all remain vigilant until we hand him over to the authorities in San Francisco. He claims to have friends everywhere. We cannot dismiss it as an idle threat.”
Frank decided to see the prisoner himself. Leaving the others, he went down the narrow corridor to the small, steel-walled cabin where Murdock was being held. Pomp stood guard outside, rifle in hand.
“Any trouble, Pomp?”
“No, sor. Just been sittin’ in there, quiet as a mouse for the last hour.”
Frank unlocked the door and stepped inside. The cabin was lit by a single, caged electric bulb. Martin Murdock sat on the edge of a narrow cot, his hands cuffed in front of him. He looked up as Frank entered, his eyes filled with a cold, calculating malice.
“To what do I owe the honor of this visit, inventor?” he sneered.
“I came to ask you one question, Murdock,” Frank said, his voice even. “Why? Why have you gone to such lengths to steal my inventions, to try and ruin me, even to kill me?”
Murdock let out a short, bitter laugh.
“Why? You, with your famous father and your flying machines, you ask me why? Because you have everything, Reade! Fame, fortune, genius! It all came so easily to you. Some of us have to fight and claw and take what we deserve in this world.”
“You deserve nothing but a prison cell,” Frank stated coldly.
“Perhaps,” Murdock said, leaning forward, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. “But this journey is not over. The world is a very large place, and the ocean is very deep. The Pegasus is a marvelous machine, I will grant you that. But is she truly infallible? Are you certain no accidents can befall her on such a long voyage?”
A chill went down Frank’s spine. The man’s tone was not one of idle threat, but of confident prophecy.
“What are you implying, Murdock?”
The villain leaned back with a cruel smile.
“Nothing at all. Merely making an observation. Enjoy your flight, Mr. Reade. I do hope you reach your destination. It would be a terrible shame if something… unexpected… were to happen.”
Frank stared at the man for a long moment, then turned and left the cabin, locking the door securely behind him. He returned to the pilot-house, his brow furrowed in thought.
Barney was at the wheel, steering a steady course southward under the brilliant stars.
“All quiet, lad?” the Irishman asked.
“For now,” Frank replied, his gaze turning to the vast, dark ocean below.
They had their prisoner. The chase was over. But as the Pegasus flew onward through the night, the young inventor could not shake the feeling that their greatest and most dangerous adventure was just beginning. Murdock was a captive, but his words were like a poison that had been released into the airship itself. The long voyage home was now filled with a new and unseen menace.

The young inventor’s words hung in the air of the pilot-house, heavy with a new kind of dread. The physical chase was done, but a battle of wits and unseen forces had just been declared. Tom Reynard, the detective, who had been quietly observing the compass, turned to Frank.

“His words have the ring of truth to them, Frank,” he said, his voice low and serious. “A man like Murdock doesn’t operate alone. He’s the head of a snake, not the whole creature.”

“I agree,” replied Frank. “He has proven too resourceful to be a simple lone wolf. While he is locked away, his influence may not be. We are a thousand miles from any friendly port.”

“Begorra, let his friends come!” declared Barney from the wheel. “We’ve handled iverything the divil has thrown at us so far, an’ we’ll handle the rest of ‘em too! A taste of the Pegasus’s medicine is what they’ll be gettin’.”

“I hope you are right, Barney,” said Frank, though his tone lacked the Irishman’s boisterous confidence. “But we must not be careless. From this moment on, we double the watch. One man at the wheel, and one on deck with a spy-glass at all times. Check the engine room every hour. Pomp, you and I will inspect every inch of the outer hull and machinery for any sign of tampering before we turn in.”

“Golly, Masther Frank, you think he could hab planted somethin’ on us?” asked Pomp, his eyes wide.

“I don’t know what to think, Pomp. But I know we cannot afford to take any chances. Murdock’s specialty is treachery.”

The orders were followed with sober diligence. Frank and Pomp, armed with electric lanterns, made a thorough inspection of the airship’s exterior. They clung to the safety rails, the vast, dark Pacific churning thousands of feet below them, its immensity a chilling reminder of their isolation. They found nothing amiss. The great helices spun with a steady rhythm, the steel hull was sound, and the machinery in the engine room hummed with its usual power.

For two days, the Pegasus flew onward, a silver marvel against the endless blue of the sky and sea. The sun rose and set in spectacular displays of color, and the stars at night were diamonds scattered on black velvet. The routine of the airship settled in. Barney and Pomp took their watches with unwavering focus, their eyes scanning the horizon. Tom Reynard spent his time cleaning his rifle and observing their prisoner during the brief moments the cabin door was opened for food.

Murdock remained a silent, brooding presence. He ate what was given him without complaint and spoke to no one, his face a mask of cold fury. Yet there was a strange patience about him, a coiled stillness that was more unnerving than any ranting or raving could have been.

On the third day after the capture, the mood aboard the Pegasus had begun to lighten. The constant, empty horizon had a lulling effect, and the perfect performance of the airship was a reassurance of its strength. They were now well into the eastern Pacific, with the coast of North America their far-distant goal.

Pomp was in the galley, preparing a stew that sent savory aromas throughout the vessel. Barney, who was off-duty, had brought out his fiddle and was playing a lively Irish jig in the main cabin, his foot tapping out the rhythm on the floor.

“It feels good to be headin’ home, eh, Mr. Reynard?” the Celt said with a grin, pausing his tune.

“It certainly does,” the detective agreed, looking up from a map he was studying with Frank. “Once we hand Murdock over to the federal marshals in San Francisco, I’ll consider this case well and truly closed.”

“I will not rest easy until he is behind bars,” said Frank, pointing to a spot on the chart. “According to my calculations, at our current speed, we should see the Farallon Islands in another forty-eight hours. Then, it’s a straight shot to the Golden Gate.”

“Hooray for that!” cried Barney. “The first thing I’ll be doin’ is findin’ a spot for a good square meal that doesn’t rock an’ roll!”

Just as he spoke, a shout came from the deck. It was Pomp, who had stepped out for a breath of fresh air.

“Masther Frank! Masther Frank, come quick!”

The music stopped abruptly. Frank and Reynard were on their feet in an instant and rushed out of the pilot-house, Barney close behind them. Pomp was standing at the port rail, pointing a trembling finger toward the west.

“Look dar, sor! Off on the horizon!”

Frank raised his powerful spy-glass to his eye. At first, he saw nothing but the meeting of sky and water. Then, a tiny speck appeared. It was not a bird, nor was it a cloud. It was dark, sharp, and growing in size with astonishing speed.

“It’s a vessel!” Frank exclaimed, his voice tight. “And it’s an airship!”

“An airship?” echoed Reynard, taking the glass. “Saints alive, you’re right! It’s coming this way like a bat out of Hades!”

“Is it a government craft?” asked Barney.

“I don’t think so,” Frank muttered, taking the glass back for another look. “It’s painted jet black. No markings. Its design is unfamiliar to me. It is long and narrow, more like a dart than my Pegasus.”

The strange craft closed the distance with incredible velocity. It was clear that its engines were of a formidable power, perhaps even rivaling those of the Pegasus. A sense of dread settled over the little crew. This could not be a coincidence.

“It must be Murdock’s friends!” Barney growled, his hands balling into fists. “The divil was tellin’ the truth!”

“To your stations!” Frank commanded, his mind racing. “Barney, to the pilot-house! Keep us steady! Pomp, Reynard, arm yourselves! They have not signaled us, and their approach is hostile. We must be ready for anything!”

The men scattered to obey. The festive air of moments before had vanished, replaced by the grim tension of impending battle. Frank remained on deck, his spy-glass fixed on the approaching menace. The black airship was now close enough to see its details. It was a sinister-looking machine, all sharp angles and unadorned steel plates. A single, large propeller at its stern pushed it through the air, and smaller fins on its sides seemed to control its direction.

A hatch opened on the top of the strange vessel, and a man appeared, holding a large brass speaking-trumpet.

“Ahoy the Pegasus!” the voice boomed across the gap, metallic and imperious.

Frank picked up his own trumpet. “Ahoy! Identify yourselves!”

“We are the Vulture!” came the reply. “And we have come for what is ours! You have a passenger, a Mr. Martin Murdock. Surrender him to us at once, and we will allow you to proceed unharmed!”

The demand confirmed Frank’s worst fears. Murdock’s network was not only real but possessed of technology to rival his own.

“Martin Murdock is a prisoner of the law!” Frank shouted back, his voice ringing with defiance. “He will be surrendered to the authorities in San Francisco, and nowhere else!”

“That is your final answer, inventor?”

“It is! We will not bow to pirates and thieves!”

“Then you have sealed your doom!” the voice from the Vulture roared. “Prepare to be sent to the bottom of the sea!”

The man disappeared back into the hatch. A moment later, a puff of smoke erupted from the side of the black airship. There was a high-pitched whistle, and something shot past the Pegasus, missing its forward deck by a few yards.

“Begorra, they’re firin’ on us!” yelled Barney from the pilot-house.

“Return fire!” Frank ordered. “Pomp, use the signal gun! Aim for their forward steering fins! Reynard, with me!”

Pomp, who had been trained by Frank in all the airship’s workings, rushed to the small cannon mounted on the bow. He loaded it with a canister of heavy shot and took careful aim. The gun boomed, and the shot peppered the side of the Vulture, but the steel plates seemed to deflect it with little effect.

From the deck of the enemy craft, a more fearsome weapon was revealed. A long tube was run out from a port, and with a strange, deep ‘thump,’ it launched a projectile. This was no simple cannonball. It was a shell that screamed through the air and exploded with a deafening crash just off the Pegasus’s stern, sending a shower of shrapnel against the hull.

“Golly, Masther Frank! Dat’s a pneumatic cannon!” Pomp cried out. “They’re firin’ explosive shells!”

“I see that!” Frank replied grimly. He and Reynard were crouched behind the steel bulwark of the deck-house, firing their rifles at the Vulture. The bullets sparked and whined off the enemy’s armored hull.

“Our rifles are useless against that armor!” shouted the detective over the din.

“We must get closer!” Frank decided. “Barney, bring us in! Try to get above them!”

“Yis, sor!” the Irishman responded, his face set with determination. He spun the wheel, and the Pegasus tilted, its powerful helices biting into the air as it began to climb.

The Vulture’s pilot was skillful, however, and matched their maneuver, keeping the distance between them. Another explosive shell was launched. This one found its mark. It struck the framework of the great starboard helix with a sickening crunch of metal.

The Pegasus shuddered violently, and a terrible grinding noise filled the air. The ship listed heavily to one side, its ascent suddenly halted.

“The starboard helix is damaged!” Barney yelled, fighting the wheel. “I can’t get her to climb! She’s pulling to the right!”

Frank’s heart sank. They were crippled. The Vulture, seeing its advantage, began to circle them like its namesake, preparing for another, more deadly strike.

“We can’t outrun them, and we can’t outgun them,” Reynard said, reloading his rifle with a grim expression. “What do we do, Frank?”

Frank’s mind was a whirlwind of calculations. Desperate situations called for desperate measures. He suddenly recalled a piece of experimental equipment he had stored in the hold, an apparatus he had designed for dispersing electrical storms.

“I have an idea!” he cried. “It’s a long shot, but it’s our only chance! Reynard, keep them busy! Pomp, come with me! Barney, do whatever you can to keep us level!”

Frank and Pomp scrambled down the ladder into the engine room. Amidst the throbbing machinery and humming dynamo, Frank pointed to a large, heavy chest in the corner.

“Help me with this!”

Together, they dragged the chest open. Inside, nestled in padding, was a strange device: a large coil of copper wire wrapped around an iron core, connected to a series of Leyden jars and a projecting dish of polished brass.

“It’s my atmospheric equalizer,” Frank explained quickly. “It can discharge a massive bolt of static electricity. If I can connect it directly to the main dynamo, it might be powerful enough to short out their electrical systems!”

“But how will we get it to hit dem?” Pomp asked, his eyes wide at the fantastic plan.

“We will have to get very close. And we’ll have to lure them into the right position. Pomp, get back to the deck and tell Barney my plan. He must execute a sudden dive, straight toward them, as soon as I give the signal. It will be our only moment to get a clear shot!”

Pomp nodded and scrambled back up the ladder. Frank worked with frantic speed. He bypassed safety circuits and ran heavy cables directly from the terminals of the main dynamo to the induction coil. The risk was immense. Overloading the system could cause the dynamo to burst, just as the smaller one had done before, paralyzing the Pegasus completely. It was a gamble for their very lives.

He dragged the heavy apparatus to the open cargo hatch on the underside of the airship and secured it, aiming the brass dish downward.

“Ready in the engine room!” he shouted up the speaking-tube to the pilot-house.

On deck, Pomp relayed the message. Barney’s voice came back, full of Irish grit. “Tell the lad I’m ready! We’ll give that black divil a surprise he’ll not be forgettin’!”

The Vulture was moving in for the kill. It was positioned almost directly below them, preparing to fire a shell into the vulnerable underbelly of the crippled Pegasus.

“Now, Barney! Now!” Frank yelled.

The Irishman spun the helm hard over. The Pegasus, already listing, responded with a terrifying lurch. It tipped its nose down and plunged from the sky in a steep, desperate dive, straight at its attacker.

The maneuver was so sudden and unexpected that it caught the crew of the Vulture completely by surprise. From their perspective, it must have looked as if the Pegasus was trying to ram them. They ceased their attack, their own pilot working to avoid a collision.

For a breathtaking moment, the two airships were separated by less than a hundred feet. Frank, braced in the open hatchway, had a clear view of the Vulture’s deck. He threw the master switch.

There was a tremendous crackle, and the air in the engine room filled with the sharp scent of ozone. A colossal bolt of blue-white lightning, thick as a man’s arm, leaped from the brass dish. It struck the Vulture squarely on its upper deck.

The effect was instantaneous and catastrophic. Every light on the enemy airship flickered and died. The hum of its machinery ceased, and the great propeller at its stern spun to a dead halt. A plume of black smoke erupted from its engine housing.

Deprived of all power, the Vulture became dead weight. It wobbled for a moment, then began to fall. It dropped past the Pegasus, tumbling end over end, a silent, dark shape plunging toward the vast, indifferent ocean below.

A few seconds later, a distant splash marked its end.

A cheer went up from the deck of the Pegasus. Barney, with a mighty effort, managed to pull the airship out of its dive, leveling it off just a few hundred feet above the waves. The grinding from the damaged helix was worse than ever, but they were flying. They were alive.

“Hooray!” yelled Barney. “We did it! We sent the blackguards to Davy Jones!”

“Golly, dat was a sight to see!” Pomp exclaimed, his face split by a wide grin.

Frank pulled himself up from the cargo hatch, his body trembling with adrenaline. He cut the power to the overloaded dynamo, praying it would hold. The immediate danger was past, but their situation was still perilous.

He made his way to the pilot-house. “Good work, Barney. You handled her beautifully.”

“Faith, an’ it was your own genius that saved us, Masther Frank,” the Irishman replied, wiping sweat from his brow. “But we’re in a bad way. This starboard helix won’t last much longer.”

Frank looked out at the damaged machinery. The frame was bent and twisted, and several of the great blades were shattered. “We’ll have to land on the water and make repairs. We can’t risk flying like this.”

With great care, Barney brought the Pegasus down until her buoyant, boat-shaped hull rested gently upon the surface of the Pacific. The waves lapped against her sides as the great helices finally spun to a halt. In the sudden silence, the isolation of their position felt more profound than ever.

They worked for the rest of the day and all through the night. It was a difficult and dangerous job, hanging from scaffolds over the water to cut away the ruined metal and fit the spare parts Frank had stored aboard. But with the combined efforts of Frank, Barney, and Pomp, the work was done. By dawn, a new helix frame was in place, and the machinery was declared sound.

After a hasty breakfast, Frank decided he must see his prisoner. The attack proved beyond all doubt that Murdock was more than just a thief; he was the leader of a dangerous and powerful organization.

He found the villain sitting on his cot, just as he had been before. The sounds of the battle must have reached him, but his face was an unreadable mask.

“Your friends failed,” Frank said, his voice cold. “Their ship and crew are at the bottom of the ocean.”

A flicker of rage, hot and pure, finally broke through Murdock’s composure. His eyes blazed with hatred. “So, the boy inventor has another trick up his sleeve. You were lucky, Reade. Do not mistake it for victory.”

“Your network is crumbling, Murdock. First the Rover, now the Vulture. By the time we reach San Francisco, there will be nothing left for you.”

Murdock let out a harsh laugh, a sound devoid of any mirth. “The Vulture was only one ship. One small piece of the puzzle. Do you truly think my influence ends at the water’s edge? I have friends in San Francisco. Powerful friends. They will be waiting for you. You may have won this battle, inventor, but you cannot win the war. You will never reach the shore with me as your prisoner. This I swear.”

Frank stared at him, a chill creeping into his heart despite the victory. Murdock’s confidence was absolute. It was the certainty of a man who held a final, unplayed card.

Without another word, Frank turned and left, locking the door behind him. He returned to the pilot-house, where the others were waiting.

“Start the engines, Barney,” he said, his voice heavy. “Let’s get back in the air.”

The Pegasus rose majestically from the water, leaving the scene of their desperate battle behind. They were flying east again, toward home. The California coast was now only a day away. But as the familiar shape of the Farallon Islands finally appeared on the horizon, no one on board felt the relief they had expected.

Murdock’s final threat echoed in Frank’s mind. “Powerful friends. They will be waiting for you.” The young inventor knew that Martin Murdock’s web of villainy was far wider than he had ever imagined, and that their arrival in San Francisco would not be an end, but the beginning of a new and more desperate struggle.
