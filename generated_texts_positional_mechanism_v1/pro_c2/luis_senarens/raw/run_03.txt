Captain Jack Sterling knew well that the fiendish Baron von Hess sought the lost geodes of Mount Garm, for he meant to use their power to fuel his dreadful war machine. Nor could Jack forget that the Baron had stolen the plans for his own vessel, the Mole, and still held the brilliant inventor, Professor Abernathy, a captive in his clutches. Jack’s mission was thus twofold: to secure the geodes for science and to rescue the professor from his terrible fate.

The vibrations of the forward drill did not interfere with the Mole’s stability, as her internal gyroscopes were designed to compensate for much greater turbulence. Silas and Pip were so relieved to have cleared the treacherous quartz fields that they were playing a game of checkers on a magnetic board. “Your move again, old salt,” said Pip, as the mighty drill-ship bored onward. The seismic tremor had left a great fissure in the rock strata, collapsing entire tunnels and twisting the solid granite into new and unfamiliar shapes.

“Had the Mole been ten minutes earlier,” said Jack, observing the chasm on the sonar screen, “we would have been crushed to so many scraps of metal.”

“Then it’s lucky we stopped to recalibrate the tertiary engine,” answered Finch, a young geologist and Jack’s trusted second in command. “Now to find a way around this mess.”

“The fault line seems to run northeast, does it not?”

“Yes,” answered Finch. “Why do you suppose the Baron’s machine went this way?”

“He is likely trying to avoid the magma tubes in the lower depths.”

“You believe he knows a safer route?”

“It is my belief that he is forcing Professor Abernathy to guide him, and the old man is leading him on a wild goose chase. In these unstable passages, the Baron can work his evil and no one will ever know what became of the professor. Don’t you see his gambit?”

“That is just what I was thinking, Jack.”

At that moment, a tremendous groaning sound echoed through the hull, and the whole vessel shuddered violently. A red light began to flash on the main console. The Mole lurched to one side and the lights flickered.

“Saints preserve us!” ejaculated Silas. “We’re caught fast!”

“What is it?”

“The forward drill is jammed!”

“Reverse the engines!”

“She won’t budge!”

Professor Finch was already at a secondary panel, his fingers flying across the dials. “The rock here is not granite! It is some sort of viscous, metallic resin! It is hardening around us!”

The groaning intensified, and the Mole’s steel plates began to creak and pop under the immense pressure. The ship was being held in a fatal grip. For a moment, it seemed their journey had come to a grim and sudden end; then Jack, his face set with determination, pulled a large brass lever.

“I am firing the forward torpedoes!” he cried. “It is a risk, but it is the only one we have!”

There was a muffled concussion, a great jolt that threw the men against the walls, and then a sudden, blessed release. The Mole shot forward into a vast, open cavern. The resin had been shattered. Scarcely had they caught their breath when Pip, from the observation port, let out a yell.

“Jack, look! By the heavens, look!”

Everyone rushed to the thick quartz windows. Before them lay a sight no man had seen for ten thousand years. The cavern was filled with a soft, phosphorescent light, which came from strange, glowing fungi clinging to the ceiling like stars. In the center of the cavern floor, nestled in a crystal basin, was a city of impossible architecture, its towers and spires carved from the very rock of the earth.

“The Sunken City of Xylos,” breathed Finch.

“We’ve found it!” cried Pip.

Jack Sterling’s eyes, however, were fixed on a dark shape moored near the city’s edge. It was a drilling machine, larger and more menacing than the Mole, with a cruel, wasp-like profile.

“The Iron Wasp!” said Jack, his voice low and dangerous. “The Baron is here.”

CHAPTER IV. THE GLOWING CITY.

The sight of Baron von Hess’s craft so near their prize sent a current of excitement through the crew of the Mole. No lights were visible on the enemy vessel, and Jack immediately ordered Pip to engage the sonic dampers, rendering their own ship invisible to detection.

“Well,” said Professor Finch, “there is the beast in its lair. What is our next move, Jack?”

“First we must determine if Professor Abernathy is still aboard.”

“How can we manage that?”

“I am going over there myself to find out.”

“That is a foolish risk, my boy!”

“Perhaps, sir; but it is a risk I must take.”

“Then we shall monitor your progress from the Mole, and if you signal for aid, we will come with every weapon we have.”

“That is all I ask.”

And so saying, Jack armed himself with a compressed air rifle and a magnetic grappling hook, and slipping out of the Mole’s primary airlock, he began to make his way across the silent, glowing cavern floor. The ground beneath his feet was soft, like moss, and made no sound. He crept along in the shadow of a giant crystalline spire, his eyes fixed on the dark, silent hull of the Iron Wasp. Jack made his way toward a large cargo bay door, which was hanging slightly ajar.

With the utmost caution, Jack slipped through the narrow opening. The massive door hung on a single damaged hinge, groaning softly with the imperceptible currents of air in the vast cavern. Once inside, he found himself in a cavernous cargo hold. The air was thick with the smell of ozone and hot metal. Great chains hung from the ceiling, and stacked against the far wall were crates of supplies and drilling equipment. The place was dark, save for the faint, otherworldly glow of the cavern’s fungi filtering in through the open door. It was silent as a tomb.

Jack drew his compressed air rifle, its polished brass stock cool in his hand. He knew the layout of these drilling machines; the Iron Wasp, for all its menacing additions, must follow the same general design as the Mole. The crew quarters and bridge would be forward, the engine room aft, and the brig somewhere in the lower decks. He moved like a shadow between stacks of crates, his boots making no sound on the grated steel floor. He found a companionway leading into the vessel’s interior and began his search.

The corridors were narrow and lit by a single, dim emergency light strip running along the floor. Every sound was magnified; the drip of condensation from a pipe, the hum of a distant transformer, the beat of his own heart. He had not gone far when he heard the murmur of voices from a cabin just ahead. Jack pressed himself flat against the cold steel bulkhead and listened.

“The old fool is stubborn,” said a gruff voice with a thick accent. “He will not talk.”

“He will, Otto, he will,” came a second voice, smoother and colder than the first. It was a voice Jack recognized instantly, and his hand tightened on his rifle. It was Baron von Hess. “Professor Abernathy is a man of science. He cannot resist a puzzle. The city’s power system is the greatest puzzle of his life. He simply needs the proper… motivation.”

“And if he does not find it?” asked Otto.

“Then we will provide it,” the Baron answered coolly. “We have detected another vessel in this cavern. Smaller than ours. They are no doubt the old man’s rescuers. When they show themselves, we will have our leverage. For now, let Abernathy contemplate his fate in the brig. See that the guard is alert.”

“Yes, Baron.”

Jack heard a door slide open and then shut. Heavy footsteps receded down the corridor in the opposite direction. The Baron was here, and he had the professor! More than that, he knew the Mole was nearby. There was no time to lose. Jack pushed off from the wall and continued down the passage, his mind racing. He had to get to the brig before the Baron could spring his trap.

He descended a steep ladder to a lower deck. This part of the ship was even darker and smelled of bilge water and decay. He passed the mess hall, a grim, empty room with bolted-down tables and benches, and finally came to a short, dead-end corridor with a single reinforced door. A hulking brute of a man sat on a stool beside it, cleaning a wicked-looking knife with an oily rag. The guard.

Jack ducked back behind the corner. A direct confrontation was out of the question; the man was twice his size and a single gunshot would bring the entire crew down upon him. He needed a distraction. His eyes scanned the corridor and fell upon a junction box on the opposite wall, a tangle of pipes and conduits leading from it. A small plaque identified it as the ‘Atmospheric Recirculation Control’. An idea sparked in his mind.

He unslung a small pouch from his belt and removed a thin, flexible periscope, no thicker than his thumb. He carefully extended it around the corner, getting a clear view of the guard and the control panel. He then took out a small, powerful hand-magnet. Taking careful aim, he tossed the magnet underhand. It flew in a perfect arc and clamped onto the side of the junction box with a loud clang.

The guard jumped to his feet, startled. “Who is there?” he growled, peering into the darkness.

Jack held his breath. He now withdrew a second device from his pouch: a small sonic resonator. He aimed it at the magnet and pressed a button. A high-pitched whine began to emanate from the junction box, growing louder and louder. The guard looked at the box, confused. Jack then overloaded the resonator. With a loud pop and a shower of sparks, a valve on one of the pipes burst, sending a jet of foul-smelling, harmless gas hissing down the corridor away from Jack’s position.

“Donnerwetter!” the guard cursed, dropping his knife. “The recyclers!” He lumbered off down the hall to find the source of the leak, his heavy boots clanging on the metal deck.

The moment the guard was out of sight, Jack sprang into action. He rushed to the brig door. The lock was a heavy, magnetic tumbler system, but it was a design he had studied. He produced a set of electronic picks and inserted them into the key slot. For a tense thirty seconds he worked, sweat beading on his brow as he listened for the guard’s return. At last, there was a soft click and the heavy door swung inward.

The cell was small and bare. A single figure lay on a narrow cot, his back to the door.

“Professor Abernathy?” Jack whispered.

The figure stirred and sat up. It was indeed the professor, though he was thinner and paler than Jack remembered. His clothes were ragged and his white hair was disheveled, but his eyes, when they focused on Jack, lit up with an unquenchable fire.

“Jack! Jack Sterling! By the stars, they said you were coming!” he cried, his voice a hoarse whisper.

“I’m here to get you out, professor,” said Jack, helping the old man to his feet. Abernathy was unsteady, but his grip was surprisingly strong.

“The Baron, Jack, he wants the Heart of Xylos,” the professor said urgently. “It is the city’s power source; a crystal of unimaginable energy. He means to use it for his weapons. I would not tell him how to find it, nor how to activate it.”

“You did the right thing, sir. Now we have to get you out of here.”

“He knows you are here,” Abernathy warned. “His sensors detected the Mole’s entry into the cavern. He is a cunning fiend; this is all a trap.”

“I know,” answered Jack. “That’s why we’re leaving right now.”

He helped the professor out of the cell and they began to retrace Jack’s steps, moving as quickly as the professor’s weakened state would allow. They had just reached the bottom of the ladder leading to the upper deck when their luck ran out.

A sudden, deafening klaxon began to blare throughout the ship, its screeching cry echoing in the narrow metal confines. Red lights flashed, bathing the corridor in a hellish glare. With a series of thunderous slams, heavy steel shutters dropped from the ceiling, sealing off the companionway ahead of them.

“It is the trap!” cried Abernathy.

Footsteps pounded down the corridor behind them. The brute of a guard had returned, and with him were two other men, all armed with heavy-caliber revolvers.

“There they are!” the guard bellowed, raising his weapon.

Jack shoved the professor behind him. “Get back, sir!” he yelled. He raised his compressed air rifle. It made no sound but a soft ‘pfft’ as it fired a tiny, anesthetic-laced dart. The lead guard gave a grunt of surprise, took one step, and then collapsed in a heap. The other two men, shocked by their companion’s sudden fall, hesitated for a fatal second. ‘Pfft! Pfft!’ Jack’s rifle spoke twice more, and the other two guards crumpled to the deck without a sound.

Just as Jack thought they were clear, a voice, amplified and dripping with scorn, boomed from a speaker on the wall. “Well done, Sterling. Very resourceful. But you did not think it would be so easy, did you? You are in the heart of my ship, and you have nowhere to run.”

As if to punctuate the Baron’s words, another shutter slammed down behind them, trapping them in the short section of corridor with the unconscious guards. They were completely boxed in.

“We are caught, my boy,” said Abernathy, his face grim in the flashing red light.

Jack’s eyes darted around the steel trap. The walls were solid, the doors impassable. But above them, crisscrossing the ceiling, was a network of pipes and conduits. And there, tucked away behind a thick power cable, was a small, square maintenance hatch, held shut by four simple bolts. It was their only chance.

“Not yet, professor,” said Jack, a grim smile on his face. “The Baron forgot about the service shafts.”

He unclipped the magnetic grappling hook from his belt. “Stand back, sir.” He swung the rope once and let it fly. The hook shot upward and clamped securely onto a thick steel pipe near the hatch. Jack tested the line; it was solid.

“Can you climb, professor?” he asked.

“With my life at stake? I will climb like a monkey in the Amazon!” the old scientist declared, his spirit reviving.

Jack went first, scrambling up the rope with an agility born of long practice. He braced himself against the ceiling and reached down for Abernathy. “Now, sir! Use the pipes on the wall as handholds!”

With a great deal of grunting and effort, the professor began to climb. He was surprisingly nimble for his age. Jack leaned down as far as he could, grasped the professor’s collar, and with a mighty heave, pulled him up the last few feet. While the professor caught his breath, Jack used a wrench from his kit to quickly undo the bolts on the hatch. He pushed it open into a dark, dusty space above.

“In you go, professor!”

They scrambled into the cramped, dark service conduit just as they heard the grinding of gears from below. The Baron was raising the shutters. They had escaped with only seconds to spare. They crawled on hands and knees through the tight, cobweb-filled shaft, following a bundle of thick cables. The air was stale and the darkness was absolute.

“Where does this lead?” whispered Abernathy.

“If I am right,” Jack whispered back, “it should pass directly over the engine room. We’re going to give the Baron something else to worry about.”

After a few minutes of crawling, a low, powerful hum vibrated through the floor of the conduit. A faint light glowed from a grated panel below them. Jack peered through. They were, as he had predicted, directly above the massive geo-thermal converters that powered the Iron Wasp. Two engineers were at a control station on the far side of the room, their backs to the main engine assembly.

“This will do nicely,” Jack muttered. He removed the grated panel and lowered himself silently into the engine room, landing softly behind the humming converter. He helped the professor down, and they crouched in the shadows of the mighty machine.

“What is your plan, Jack?” asked Abernathy.

“A little creative engineering,” Jack replied with a grin. He pointed to a series of crystal regulators pulsing with a soft blue light. “Those are the primary energy regulators. Without them, the converters will overload.” He slipped over to the control housing and, with a few deft movements of a specialized tool, pried open a panel. He carefully detached a single, vital-looking wire and re-routed it to a discharge node.

A small spark was the only immediate effect. “That should do it,” he said, hurrying back to the professor. “It will take about five minutes for the overload to build. By then, we should be long gone. Now, let’s find another way out.”

They crept along the wall of the engine room until they found what Jack was looking for: a small, circular door marked ‘Emergency Egress Port’. It was a secondary airlock, meant for engineers to perform external repairs. It was secured by a simple pressure wheel.

Working together, they managed to turn the stiff wheel. With a hiss of equalizing pressure, the outer door swung open, revealing the soft, phosphorescent glow of the cavern of Xylos. Freedom was in sight.

“Quickly, professor! To the Mole!”

They scrambled out onto the soft, mossy ground and began to run. The Mole was a dark shape a hundred yards away, nestled in the shadows of a crystal formation. Behind them, they heard a shout from the Iron Wasp. They had been spotted. Then, a new sound began, a low, groaning complaint from deep within the Baron’s vessel, followed by the flickering of its external lights. Jack’s sabotage was taking effect.

They were halfway to the Mole when a spotlight from the Iron Wasp’s bridge stabbed through the cavern’s gloom, pinning them in its bright glare. A siren began to wail.

“They see us!” panted Abernathy.

“Just a little farther!” Jack urged, practically dragging the exhausted professor along.

The outer airlock of the Mole slid open as they approached. The silhouette of Pip stood in the doorway, waving them on frantically. “Hurry, Jack! Hurry!”

They tumbled inside the airlock just as the air was split by a sharp crack and a puff of dust erupted from the ground where they had just been. A rifleman was shooting at them from the Iron Wasp. The door slid shut, sealing them in the welcome safety of their own ship.

They stumbled into the control room, where Professor Finch rushed to help Abernathy to a chair.

“Professor Abernathy! Thank heavens you are safe!” Finch exclaimed.

“No thanks to me, Finch,” Abernathy gasped, a smile spreading across his weary face. “It was all this young man here. He pulled me from the very jaws of the beast!”

“The Baron knows we’re here, and he’s not happy about it,” said Jack, moving to the main viewport. On the cavern floor, the lights of the Iron Wasp had stabilized. Its huge, forward-mounted drill began to whine, stirring from its slumber like an awakened predator. The great machine began to turn, its metallic bulk groaning as it brought its cruel profile to bear upon the Mole.

A thrill of anticipation ran through Jack Sterling. They had rescued the professor and crippled the enemy, but the fight was far from over. He gripped the control levers of the Mole, his eyes fixed on the menacing shape of the Iron Wasp.

“Pip, power up the sonic cannons!” he commanded, his voice ringing with authority. “Professor Finch, get our own drill spinning and reinforce the forward shields! The Baron wanted a fight; now he is going to get one!”

CHAPTER V.

THE BATTLE FOR XYLOS.

The two subterranean dreadnaughts faced each other across the glowing floor of the ancient cavern. Inside the Mole, the hum of power grew to a steady thrum. Professor Abernathy, now with a blanket around his shoulders and a cup of hot broth in his hands, watched the scene with a mixture of fear and excitement.

“His ship is larger, Jack,” Abernathy warned. “His drilling head is fortified with a carborundum alloy. He will try to ram us.”

“Let him try,” Jack said grimly. “We have a few tricks of our own. Pip, status on the sonic cannons?”

“Fully charged and ready to fire on your command, Jack!” Pip called out from his station. The young man’s face was pale, but his eyes were bright with determination.

“Good. Fire a warning shot across his bow. Let’s see how he reacts.”

Pip’s hands flew over his console. “Sonic cannon charged, Jack. Firing sequence initiated!”

A low hum filled the Mole’s control room, vibrating through the very deck plates. Outside, a pair of emitters on the ship’s forward mandibles began to glow with a pale blue light. With a sound like a giant tuning fork being struck, a shimmering wave of invisible force shot out from the Mole. It did not fly like a shell, but instead expanded in a cone of pure sonic energy. The wave passed just to the left of the Iron Wasp and struck the cavern wall with terrific force. There was no explosion, only a deep, resonant thrum that shook the entire chamber. The glowing fungi on the walls flickered wildly, and a shower of fine, crystalline dust rained down from the point of impact.

For a moment, all was silent. The crew of the Mole watched the opposing vessel, waiting for a sign of retreat or surrender.

“Did it work?” asked Professor Finch, his knuckles white where he gripped the back of a chair.

“I do not think so,” answered Abernathy, his voice grim. “The Baron is not a man to be frightened by loud noises. He is a creature of pure malice and ambition.”

As if in answer, the great drill on the front of the Iron Wasp, which had been turning slowly, began to spin with incredible speed. A high pitched whine filled the air, growing in intensity until it was a piercing shriek that set one’s teeth on edge. Then, a voice, amplified by some powerful machine, boomed from the Baron’s vessel and echoed off the cavern walls.

“So, the little mouse shows its teeth!” the voice of the Baron snarled. “You have been a thorn in my side for the last time, Jack Sterling! That miserable scientist you stole from me was the key to unlocking the secrets of this world. Now I shall take him back, after I grind your pathetic little craft into a pile of scrap!”

With this declaration, the Iron Wasp surged forward. The behemoth of a machine, twice the size of the Mole, charged across the cavern floor, its spinning carborundum drill aimed directly at them like the horn of some prehistoric monster.

“He’s coming right for us!” cried Pip.

“Steady, everyone!” Jack commanded, his hands tight on the control levers. “Professor Finch, shields to maximum! Pip, divert auxiliary power to the engines! He may be bigger, but we are faster!”

Jack threw the levers hard to starboard. The Mole, with a lurch that sent loose items skittering across the floor, darted to the side. The Iron Wasp thundered past, unable to turn quickly enough to follow the nimble craft. Its massive drill missed them by a scant twenty yards, its wake stirring up a cloud of glowing dust.

“A near thing!” gasped Finch. “His momentum is tremendous!”

“That is his weakness,” said Abernathy, pointing a trembling finger at the viewport. “His mass makes him powerful in a straight line, but clumsy in a turn. You must use your agility, Jack! Harass his flanks!”

“My thoughts exactly, professor,” Jack answered, a grim smile on his face. “Pip, target his port side treads! If we can cripple his movement, we can neutralize him!”

The Mole spun around and sped after the still-turning Iron Wasp. Pip, his face a mask of concentration, lined up his shot.

“Firing sonics!” he yelled.

Again the shimmering wave shot forth. This time it struck the Iron Wasp squarely on its left side, right where the massive chain-like treads met the armored body. There was a hideous screech of tortured metal. Several of the heavy tread plates shattered and flew off, clattering against the cavern floor. The Iron Wasp lurched violently to one side, its port treads grinding to a halt with a shower of sparks. The great machine slewed into a long, uncontrolled skid, finally coming to rest against a thick pillar of glowing crystal.

“A direct hit!” shouted Jack. “You’ve damaged his propulsion! Well done, Pip!”

“He ain’t goin’ far on that side now, Jack!” Pip said, a triumphant grin on his face.

Their victory was short lived. The Baron was far from finished. With its port treads useless, the Iron Wasp began to pivot on the spot, dragging its damaged side as it brought its terrible drill to bear upon them once more. The battle had taken a toll on the cavern itself. The sonic blasts and the impact of the Iron Wasp against the crystal pillar had destabilized the ancient rock formations. A deep groaning sound came from the ceiling high above.

“Look out!” cried Professor Finch.

From the roof of the cavern, a gigantic crystalline stalactite, as large as the Mole itself, broke free. It fell with terrifying speed, plunging downward like a dagger of glass. Jack reacted instantly, throwing the Mole’s engines into full reverse. The ship shot backwards just as the colossal crystal shard slammed into the floor where they had been a moment before. It shattered into a million pieces, sending razor sharp fragments flying in all directions. Several large pieces hammered against the Mole’s forward shields, causing them to flare brightly.

“Shields at eighty percent!” Finch reported, his voice strained. “That was too close!”

The Baron used the distraction to his advantage. While the Mole was recovering, a new sound came from the Iron Wasp. A series of hatches opened along its upper hull, and from them emerged a dozen smaller, rocket-propelled drills, each about the size of a man.

“What are those?” Pip asked, his eyes wide.

“Drill torpedoes!” ejaculated Abernathy. “A new wickedness of his! He must have developed them while I was his prisoner! They will home in on our heat signature!”

As he spoke, the drill torpedoes ignited their engines and shot out from the Iron Wasp, swarming towards the Mole like angry hornets.

“Evasive maneuvers!” Jack yelled, his face set like stone. “Pip, can you target them with the sonics?”

“There are too many, Jack! They’re too fast!”

Jack threw the Mole into a series of sharp, unpredictable turns, trying to shake the deadly projectiles. Two of the torpedoes were unable to follow the quick movements and slammed into the cavern wall, exploding in a shower of shrapnel. A third was destroyed when Jack expertly maneuvered the Mole so that a falling chunk of crystal fell in its path. But there were too many.

One of the torpedoes struck the Mole’s stern with a deafening crash. The entire ship shuddered from the impact, and the lights flickered violently.

“We’re hit!” Finch yelled. “Rear shield generator is offline! Hull breach in engineering!”

Another torpedo slammed into their starboard side, and this time the explosion was followed by a spray of sparks from Pip’s control console.

“I’ve lost the port sonic emitter!” he cried out in frustration. “Our firepower is cut in half!”

The Iron Wasp, seeing its advantage, began to advance again, dragging its damaged treads as it closed in for the kill. Its main drill spun with a hungry shriek. They were trapped, damaged, and outgunned.

“We cannot withstand another pass from his main drill,” said Finch, his face pale. “Our forward shields are weakening.”

“There must be a way,” Jack muttered, his eyes darting around the chaotic scene outside. His gaze fell upon the Baron’s colossal machine. It was a marvel of destructive engineering; but every machine, he knew, had a weakness.

“Professor Abernathy,” Jack said, his voice urgent. “You helped design parts of his engine, did you not? You said it was a geothermal power source. Is there a flaw? An exhaust port? Anything?”

Abernathy’s eyes, which had been glazed with fear, suddenly sharpened with recollection. He leaned forward, his expression intense.

“Yes!” he exclaimed. “Yes, there is! The geothermal converter requires massive heat exchangers to prevent a meltdown. The main exhaust vents for these exchangers are located on the roof of the ship, directly behind the command bridge. They are armored, of course; but the Baron was arrogant. He believed no one could ever get above him in a subterranean environment. The top armor is the weakest point of the entire vessel!”

“The top armor,” Jack repeated, a plan forming in his mind.

“If you could score a direct hit on those vents with a full power sonic blast,” Abernathy continued, his voice rising with excitement, “it would overload the cooling system. The geothermal core would go critical! It would not destroy the ship, perhaps, but it would cause a catastrophic power failure!”

“But our cannons are damaged,” said Pip glumly. “And how could we get above him?”

Jack looked at the chaos around them. The falling crystals, the unstable walls. The danger was also an opportunity.

“Professor Finch, what is the status of our main drill?” Jack asked.

“It is fully operational, Jack. Why?”

“We’re going to climb the walls,” Jack said simply.

The two professors and Pip stared at him as if he had lost his mind.

“Climb the walls?” Finch stammered. “That’s insane! The Mole was designed for horizontal drilling, not for scaling vertical surfaces!”

“She’s a sturdy ship,” Jack insisted. “Her grapplers and main drill should give us enough purchase. It is our only chance. Pip, I need you to re-route all power from the damaged port emitter to the starboard one. We need to put everything we have into a single blast.”

“I can do it, Jack,” Pip said, his hesitation gone, replaced by a steely resolve. “But it will take me a minute, and it will put a huge strain on the remaining systems.”

“You have one minute,” Jack said. “Professors, brace for a rough ride.”

Without another word, Jack spun the Mole around and aimed it not at the Iron Wasp, but at the cavern wall nearby. He pushed the throttle to full. The Mole surged forward and slammed its spinning drill head into the crystalline rock face. The ship groaned in protest, but the drill bit deep, anchoring them to the wall.

“Engaging vertical thrusters and magnetic grapplers!” Jack called out.

With a tremendous shudder, the Mole began to crawl upwards, its drill chewing a path up the side of the cavern. Below them, the Baron seemed confused by the maneuver. The Iron Wasp paused its advance, its remaining drill torpedoes circling uncertainly.

“He does not know what to make of it!” Abernathy cried.

“Let’s hope his confusion lasts,” Jack gritted out, his muscles straining as he fought the controls. The Mole was not designed for this, and every foot of upward progress was a battle. Alarms blared across the control room as various systems reported stress warnings.

“We’re almost at the ceiling!” Finch reported, watching their altitude on a display. “Another fifty feet!”

“Pip, are you ready?” Jack yelled over the noise.

“Almost, Jack! Power conduits are rerouted! The starboard emitter is charged to one hundred and fifty percent capacity! Any more and she’ll blow!”

“Get ready to fire on my mark!”

High above the cavern floor, the Mole reached the curved ceiling and began to crawl across it like some giant insect, its drill carving a deep scar in the ancient rock. Below them, the Iron Wasp was a perfect target. They were directly over it.

“He sees us!” Pip shouted. “He’s angling his torpedoes upward!”

“He’s too late!” Jack roared. “Now, Pip! Fire!”

Pip slammed his hand down on the firing stud.

From the Mole’s single functioning sonic emitter, a beam of brilliant blue energy, concentrated to a fine point, shot downwards. It was no longer a wave, but a focused lance of pure sonic power. It struck the top of the Iron Wasp, right where Professor Abernathy had indicated. The armor plating, thinner here than anywhere else, buckled and then shattered inward.

For a second, nothing happened. Then, a plume of black smoke and superheated steam erupted from the hole in the Iron Wasp’s roof. A series of internal explosions rocked the mighty vessel. Its lights flickered erratically and then went out, plunging the machine into darkness. The high pitched whine of its giant drill died away with a final, mournful groan. The remaining drill torpedoes, their guidance systems failing, flew off at random angles and exploded harmlessly against the cavern walls. The Iron Wasp was dead in the water, a dark, silent hulk.

On the ceiling, the Mole ceased its drilling. In the sudden silence, the crew could hear only the hum of their own ship and the drip of water somewhere in the vast cavern.

“You did it, Jack!” Pip cheered, breaking the silence. “You did it!”

“We did it, Pip,” Jack corrected, slumping back in his chair and wiping a sleeve across his sweat-beaded brow. “We all did.”

Professor Finch let out a long, shaky breath. “Incredible. A truly incredible piece of piloting and strategy, my boy.”

Slowly and carefully, Jack piloted the Mole back down the wall and onto the cavern floor. He brought the ship to a stop a safe distance from the crippled Iron Wasp. The battle was over.

The Mole proceeded cautiously down the strange, artificial corridor. The air, recycled through the ship’s purifiers, still seemed to carry a strange, electric tang, a scent of ozone and ancient dust. A few moments later, a massive underwater explosion rocked the area, as the ship’s boilers, flooded with freezing water, burst. A huge plume of steam and black smoke erupted from the place where the ship had been, and then there was only the churning water and the floating wreckage. “A fitting end,” Frank said grimly, watching the scene on the forward viewer, which had just cleared of snow, “is a fitting end for the villainous craft.” They had some trouble getting their prisoners up the cleft to the plateau, but with some clever use of ropes, the task was accomplished. Soon, they were all back in the warm, well-lit cabin of the magnificent ice ship. Ben Bolt and his men were locked securely in a storage compartment, their villainous careers at an end. Walter was once again wrapped in blankets and given a hot, nourishing broth by Pomp. As the Ranger began to move, gliding away from the bay of horrors, Dr. Vaneyke turned to Frank. “Your plan to rescue the boy was a complete success, Frank. But I must confess, my heart was in my throat when that ship exploded.” “It was a near thing,” admitted Frank. “Bolt is a more cunning and ruthless foe than I anticipated. But his own evil was his undoing.” “Faix, and it’s a good riddance to him and his tub,” declared Barney. “And now, are we headin’ for home, Captain Frank?” “Not directly, Barney,” said Frank, a thoughtful look on his face. He was studying a large chart spread on the table. “The explosion of the Red Eric will have been seen for miles. The authorities will send a ship to investigate. We will meet them in the strait and hand over our prisoners. But first, there is something else.” “What is it, Frank?” asked the doctor. Frank pointed to a spot on the chart, an unexplored region far to the north of their current position. “When I was on the roof of that ruin,” he said, “before the snow gave way, I had a clear view of the northern horizon. And I saw something. A light.” “A light?” “Yes. Not the aurora, but a steady, pulsing glow. It seemed to be coming from behind that great range of mountains marked here as the ‘Crystal Peaks’.” “What could it be?” wondered Walter, his own troubles forgotten in this new mystery. “I do not know,” answered Frank, his eyes gleaming with the fire of the true explorer. “It could be a volcano. It could be some strange atmospheric phenomenon. Or it could be something no man has ever seen before. The Ranger is in perfect condition. We have ample supplies. What do you say, my friends? Shall we go and see?” A chorus of assent was his answer. Dr. Vaneyke was eager for a new scientific discovery. Barney and Pomp were ready for any adventure their young master proposed. And Walter Grey, newly rescued, felt no fear, only the thrill of being part of this incredible crew. “Then it is settled,” said Frank with a smile. “Doctor, set a course for the Crystal Peaks. Our work here is done. A new adventure awaits!” The great ice ship turned her prow to the north, her steel runners hissing over the ice, and sped onward into the unknown, a moving marvel of science and courage in the vast, white heart of the polar world.

The days that followed were filled with a tense excitement. The Ranger pushed ever northward, leaving the familiar whaling grounds far behind and entering a realm of pristine, untouched desolation. The character of the ice changed. It became harder, more crystalline, and the formations more fantastic. Great castles of blue ice, carved by the relentless wind, rose up on all sides, their turrets and battlements sparkling in the perpetual twilight of the arctic sun. The temperature dropped to a point that would have frozen the life out of any ordinary vessel, but the Ranger’s insulated hull and powerful heaters kept the interior as comfortable as a drawing-room in New York. Pomp kept the galley stove roaring, producing a seemingly endless supply of hot coffee and savory stews, while Barney O’Shea passed the time by carving intricate designs into a piece of walrus tusk.

Walter Grey, his ordeal on the Red Eric fading into a bad dream, proved to be an apt pupil. He followed Frank and Dr. Vaneyke everywhere, his mind absorbing the wonders of the Ranger like a sponge. He learned to read the gyro-compass, to plot a course on the charts, and to identify the different types of ice. “You have the makings of a true explorer, Walter,” Frank said one evening, as they stood together in the glass-enclosed turret, watching the magnificent spectacle of the aurora borealis dancing across the sky. “I never imagined anything like this existed,” Walter said, his voice filled with awe. “The world is so much bigger and more wonderful than I ever knew.” “Indeed it is,” replied Frank. “And there is always something new to discover, just over the horizon.”

Their peaceful progress was interrupted on the third day by an unforeseen complication. They had been keeping their prisoners, the surviving members of Ben Bolt’s crew, locked in a reinforced storage compartment in the ship’s hold. They were provided with food and water, but otherwise left to contemplate their villainy in the dark. It was Pomp’s turn to deliver their meager rations. As he slid open the small slot in the door, one of the ruffians, a man named “Sharkey” Malone, feigned a sudden illness. “Water,” he croaked, clutching his throat. “For the love of mercy, water!” Pomp, whose heart was as soft as his master’s was brave, was moved by the man’s apparent suffering. “You just wait dar, I’ll get you a dipper,” he said, and, in a moment of carelessness, he unlatched the heavy door to better attend to the man. It was the opportunity the villains had been waiting for. The instant the door was unbolted, it was thrown open with violent force, knocking the astounded negro off his feet. Four desperate men, their eyes burning with hatred, poured out into the corridor. “Grab him!” snarled Malone. “He’s our ticket out of here!” They seized the struggling Pomp and dragged him up the ladder to the main cabin.

Frank, Dr. Vaneyke, and Walter were in the midst of a discussion about the geological formations of the region when the door burst open. They looked up in astonishment to see Pomp held fast by two of the sailors, a wicked-looking knife pressed to his throat by Malone. “Don’t nobody move!” the ruffian growled. “We’re taking over this ship.” For a moment, no one spoke. The tension in the cabin was so thick you could cut it with a knife. “You are making a grave mistake, Malone,” Frank said, his voice dangerously calm. “Let Pomp go, and I will see to it that your treatment is lenient.” Malone let out a harsh laugh. “I ain’t falling for any of your tricks, you fancy swell. We know what you’ve got in mind for us; a rope at the end of a yardarm. Well, we ain’t going to hang. Now, you’re going to turn this ice-boat around and take us to a neutral port.” “And if I refuse?” “Then your blackamoor friend here gets a smile in his throat,” Malone said, pressing the knife harder against Pomp’s skin. “Don’t you worry ‘bout me, Massa Frank!” Pomp cried out, his voice trembling but defiant. “Don’ you give ‘em the ship!”

Frank’s mind raced. To fight would be to endanger Pomp. To give in was unthinkable. He needed a distraction. His eyes darted around the cabin, and then a plan formed in his quick mind. “Very well, Malone,” he said, raising his hands in a gesture of surrender. “You win. But it will take some time to turn a vessel of this size. You must be patient.” “I’ll be as patient as I need to be,” sneered Malone. Frank nodded slowly and turned to the control panel, as if to begin the procedure for reversing their course. But his hand went not to the helm, but to a small, unmarked switch concealed beneath the console. He flicked it. Instantly, the cabin was plunged into absolute darkness, and a siren, with the ear-splitting shriek of a thousand banshees, erupted from hidden speakers. The effect on the mutineers was instantaneous. Accustomed to the dim light of their prison, the sudden blackness and the unholy noise threw them into a state of utter confusion. Malone let out a yell of surprise and pain, his grip on Pomp loosening for a fraction of a second. It was all the time the powerful negro needed. With a mighty heave, Pomp threw off his captor and launched himself into the darkness. At the same moment, Frank dove low, tackling another of the sailors and sending him crashing into a third. The fourth man, utterly disoriented, fired a pistol blindly into the dark, the flash of the shot momentarily illuminating a scene of wild chaos. Dr. Vaneyke, with a presence of mind that would have done credit to a military man, grabbed a heavy book from a shelf and brought it down on the head of the man nearest him.

When Frank restored the lights a moment later, the battle was over. The four mutineers lay on the floor, groaning and trussed up with rope that Barney, who had arrived from below to see what the commotion was about, had expertly tied. “Begorra, it’s a lively party you’re havin’ without me!” the Irishman declared. Pomp was rubbing his neck, but was otherwise unharmed. “That was a close one, Massa Frank,” he said. “You let your good heart get the better of your judgment, Pomp,” said Frank, though his tone was gentle. “But you redeemed yourself bravely. As for these men, their attempt to escape has forfeited any claim to lenient treatment. We will put them in the forward chain locker. I think they will find it less comfortable, and considerably more secure.” The prisoners were hauled away, and soon the Ranger was once more gliding peacefully through the arctic night. The incident, however, served as a stark reminder that danger could come not only from the elements, but from the treachery of men.

Two days later, they saw them. The Crystal Peaks. They rose from the endless ice plain like a vision from a fairy tale, a jagged range of mountains that seemed to be carved not from rock, but from pure, translucent crystal. They shimmered and glowed in the low light, catching and refracting the rays of the arctic sun into a million dazzling rainbows. “Magnificent!” breathed Dr. Vaneyke, peering through his binoculars. “I have never seen anything like it. It appears to be a form of quartz, on a scale that is geologically impossible.” “Impossible or not, there it is,” said Frank. “And behind it lies the source of the light we are seeking.” As they drew closer, they saw that the light was not a constant glow, but a slow, rhythmic pulse, like the beat of a giant heart. It emanated from behind the highest peak, bathing the surrounding sky in a soft, greenish luminescence. The terrain at the base of the mountains was treacherous, a maze of deep crevasses and razor-sharp ridges. The Ranger was forced to slow to a crawl, picking her way carefully through the labyrinth of ice.

It was during one of these slow maneuvers that Barney, who was on watch in the turret, let out a sudden cry. “Saints preserve us! What in the name of all that’s holy is that?” He pointed to a spot on the ice, not a quarter of a mile away. There, moving slowly and majestically out from behind a great pinnacle of ice, was a creature that should have been extinct for ten thousand years. It was a mammoth. Not the bones of a mammoth, but a living, breathing beast, immense and shaggy, its great curved tusks gleaming in the strange light. It was covered in a thick coat of reddish-brown hair and moved with a ponderous, shuffling gait, its huge, padded feet making no sound on the snow. The crew of the Ranger stared in stunned silence. “It cannot be,” whispered Dr. Vaneyke, his face pale with excitement. “Elephas primigenius. Here. Now. It is a biological miracle!” “It’s bigger than any elephant I ever saw,” said Walter, his eyes as big as saucers. The great beast seemed oblivious to their presence. It ambled over to a patch of ice that was strangely free of snow and began to strike it with its tusks. After a few blows, a section of the ice broke away, revealing a patch of what looked like green moss beneath. The mammoth then used its flexible trunk to gather up the moss and stuff it into its great mouth.

“It’s feeding!” exclaimed Frank. “There must be a source of geothermal heat under the ice to allow that moss to grow.” The mammoth, having finished its meal, turned and began to lumber away, not back the way it had come, but toward a narrow, almost invisible canyon that cut deep into the heart of the Crystal Peaks. “It knows where it is going!” cried Frank. “It is leading us to the secret of this place. Doctor, follow that mammoth!” The chase was on. The Ranger, for all her power, could not match the mammoth’s uncanny ability to navigate the treacherous terrain. The great beast seemed to know every crack and crevasse, every safe path through the icy maze. The ice ship followed at a respectful distance, her crew watching in fascination as their prehistoric guide led them deeper and deeper into the mountain range. The canyon walls rose up on either side, sheer cliffs of glittering crystal that soared thousands of feet into the air. The pulsing green light grew stronger with every mile, and, strangely, the air grew warmer. Patches of open water began to appear, steaming in the frigid air. The gyroscopes hummed as the Ranger lifted from her runners, her propellers now churning through a bizarre mixture of slush and water.

Finally, the mammoth disappeared around a sharp bend in the canyon. When the Ranger rounded the corner, her crew gasped in amazement. The canyon had opened up into a vast, hidden valley. The sky above was a dome of pulsating green light, a permanent aurora that seemed to be generated by the very air itself. The temperature was mild, almost tropical. The ground was covered not with ice and snow, but with a carpet of lush, green vegetation. Strange, brightly colored birds flitted through the air, and in the distance, a herd of mammoths could be seen grazing peacefully by the shores of a large, steaming lake. They had found it. A lost world, a prehistoric Eden hidden in the heart of the frozen north. At the far end of the valley, the source of the light and heat was visible; a colossal crystal, half-buried in the ground, that pulsed with an inner fire, sending its life-giving energy out into the hidden land. The Ranger floated silently over the water of the lake, her crew staring in silent wonder at the impossible scene before them. “Well, my friends,” Frank said at last, his voice filled with a quiet sense of triumph and awe, “it seems our adventure is just beginning.”

Flowers of impossible size and color bloomed in the perpetual twilight cast by the glowing sky. Strange insects buzzed in the warm, humid air, and the calls of unknown birds echoed from the canopy above. Out of the shadows stepped a creature from a nightmare. It was a great cat, larger than any tiger of the modern world, with a tawny, spotted coat. But its most terrifying feature was the pair of immense, curved teeth that descended from its upper jaw, great daggers of ivory that looked sharp enough to shear through bone.
“A smilodon!” gasped the doctor. “A saber-toothed cat! Frank, they have been extinct for ten thousand years!”
“This one did not get the message,” Frank said grimly, raising his pistol.
The beast crouched low, its tail twitching. Its yellow eyes were fixed upon them, burning with a cold, predatory intelligence. It was not like the animals of their time; this was a killer that had evolved to hunt mammoths, and two lone men were but a morsel.
With a deafening snarl, it sprang.
The creature’s speed was astonishing. It covered the twenty yards between them in what seemed like a single bound. But Frank Reade Jr. was not a man to be caught off guard. He did not aim for the charging beast’s head or body, for he knew its thick hide and powerful muscles might deflect the dart. Instead, he aimed for the one spot he knew would be vulnerable: its open mouth.
As the smilodon leaped, its great jaws agape to deliver a killing bite, Frank fired. The tiny dart, almost invisible in its flight, shot forward and buried itself in the soft flesh of the creature’s palate.
The effect was instantaneous. The great cat’s snarl choked in its throat. Its powerful leap faltered in mid-air. It landed not upon its prey, but in a clumsy, sprawling heap at their feet, its limbs twitching. A moment later, it was still, its great chest rising and falling in the deep, regular rhythm of drugged sleep.

They left the sleeping beast and pressed on with renewed caution. At last, they reached the edge of the woods that bordered the open ground around the great crystal. Peering through the foliage, they had a clear view of the whalers’ camp.
It was a rough affair. Six men, all of them hard-looking sailors with beards and worn clothing, were gathered around a fire, roasting a large joint of some unidentifiable meat. “Now, Barney!” cried Frank.
A second device was deployed from the ship. It was a powerful electromagnet, one Frank had designed for polar exploration. Barney, at the controls, directed its beam downward.
Ben Bolt, in his rage, had forgotten one crucial thing. In his belt was a large, steel buckle, a heavy flintlock pistol, and a sheathed marlinspike. The magnet caught them all.
The captain was jerked off his feet as if by an invisible hand. He was lifted, struggling and cursing, ten feet into the air, where he hung suspended and helpless in the magnetic field.
“Let me down, you devils!” he screamed, thrashing impotently.
The Ranger came to a hover beside the ledge where Frank stood. A rope ladder was thrown down.
“Up you come, Mister Frank!” yelled Barney.
Frank scrambled up the ladder and onto the deck. Dr. Vaneyke and Walter Grey were already there, wrapped in blankets and sipping hot broth Pomp had provided.
“Frank! You’re safe!” cried the doctor.
“Are you and the boy unharmed?” Frank asked, ignoring his own exhaustion.
“A few scratches, but we are well, thanks to you,” said the doctor. He looked over at the captain, still dangling and cursing in the air. “What shall we do with him?”
Frank looked from the captured captain to the men struggling in the net below. His mission had been to rescue Walter Grey, and that was done. But he could not leave these villains here to desecrate this lost world.
“Pomp,” he commanded, “lower the cage.”
A small, sturdy cage of steel bars was lowered from the Ranger’s cargo bay.
“We will take Captain Bolt with us,” Frank declared. “He has a trial to face back in the civilized world. As for his men, we will leave them here. Barney, release them from the net. They are disarmed, and without their leader, they are no threat. This valley will be their prison.”
The magnet was deactivated, and Captain Bolt dropped unceremoniously into the cage. His men, once freed, stared up at the impossible ship in terror, then scattered into the woods.
With their prisoner secure, Frank gave the order to ascend. The Ranger rose gracefully from the valley floor, up past the canopy of the giant ferns, and into the strange, glowing sky. Below them, the lost world stretched out, a secret, prehistoric Eden.
Walter Grey stood by the rail, looking down in wonder. “Mr. Reade,” he said, his voice quiet. “Thank you. You saved my life.”
“I was only doing my duty, Walter,” Frank said, placing a reassuring hand on the boy’s shoulder. “The nightmare is over. We’re taking you home.”
As the Ranger climbed toward the icy canyon that was its only exit from the hidden valley, Frank looked back one last time. He had found a world beyond imagination, fought a beast from the dawn of time, and rescued an innocent from the clutches of a villain.
“Doctor,” he said, a smile touching his lips. “I believe this has been a most successful expedition.”
“Indeed, Frank, indeed,” replied the doctor, already making fresh notes in his book. “Most successful. And I have a feeling the Royal Geographical Society will be very interested to hear about it.”
