Frank slipped through the opening into the hold. The space was immense, a dark cathedral of timbers stretching away into the gloom. Great vats for rendering blubber stood like silent sentinels, and the air was thick and heavy. A faint light filtered down from cracks in the deck above, just enough to make out the massive, shadowy forms. He knew that somewhere aboard this ship there must be a tool locker or a workshop. A whaler was a self-contained factory, and it would have everything needed for repairs; a crowbar, a sledgehammer, anything would do.

He moved silently through the hold, his senses on high alert. He could hear Bolt moving around on the deck above, humming a tuneless shanty. The captain was confident, sure of his victory. Frank reached the aft bulkhead of the hold and found another door. This one led to a small, cramped space filled with tools. Anvils, hammers, tongs, and files hung in neat rows. He had found the ship’s smithy. He quickly located a heavy sledgehammer and a cold chisel. With these, he could break any chain ever forged. He was just about to turn back when his eyes fell upon a collection of harpoons stacked in a corner. An idea, daring and dangerous, sparked in his mind. He grabbed not only the tools, but also a long, slender harpoon with a coil of rope attached. Armed for a new kind of hunt, he made his way back through the hold.

When he re-entered the forecastle, he found Walter waiting anxiously. “Did you find anything?” “More than I hoped,” Frank answered. He set to work on the chain. Placing the chisel against the thickest link of the chain, he motioned for Walter to cover his ears. Then, with a mighty swing, he brought the sledgehammer down upon it. CLANG! The sound was deafening in the enclosed space. “What in thunder was that?” came Bolt’s voice from the deck. CLANG! Frank struck again. He could hear the captain’s heavy boots stomping toward the hatch. CLANG! The link was beginning to give way. “Stop that noise down there!” Bolt roared, his voice muffled by the deck. Frank ignored him and delivered a fourth, powerful blow. With a sharp crack, the link snapped. Walter was free. “Hurrah!” the boy cried.

“Quiet!” Frank hissed. “Now for the next part of our plan.” He quickly explained his idea to the boy. He handed Walter the sledgehammer. “When I give the signal, I want you to start banging on the hull with this. Make as much noise as you can.” He then took the harpoon and positioned himself directly under the main hatch. He could hear Bolt fumbling with the bar. The captain was coming down to investigate. “Now, Walter!” Frank cried. The boy began to hammer on the ship’s timbers, producing a tremendous din. The bar was thrown back from the hatch, and it was flung open. Ben Bolt, a mountain of a man with a wild beard and a look of fury on his face, stood silhouetted against the sky. He held a lit lantern in one hand and a whaling spade in the other. “What devilry is this?” he bellowed, and started down the ladder.

That was the moment Frank had been waiting for. As Bolt’s legs descended past the opening, Frank lunged forward with the harpoon. He did not aim to kill; his target was the captain’s thick leather belt. The sharp point of the harpoon snagged the belt perfectly, and with a mighty heave, Frank pulled. The captain, caught completely by surprise and with his feet dangling in the air, let out a yell of astonishment as he was plucked from the ladder and sent tumbling into the forecastle. He landed with a heavy thud on a pile of old sails, the wind knocked out of him. The lantern flew from his grasp, rolled across the floor, and extinguished itself, plunging them once more into darkness. Before the burly captain could recover, Frank was upon him. He pressed the muzzle of his pneumatic pistol to the man’s temple. “The game is up, Bolt,” Frank said, his voice cold as the arctic ice. “Yield, or this is your last day on earth.”

The captain, dazed and out of breath, could only groan in response. Frank quickly relieved him of his own pistol and the knife in his belt. He also found the key to the padlock, just as Walter had said, on a string around the man’s neck. “Well, Walter,” Frank said. “I believe we have turned the tables on our host.” He bound the captain securely with a length of rope. Then, he and the boy climbed the ladder to the deck, emerging into the crisp, clean air. It had never smelled so sweet. As they stood on the deck, they saw a welcome sight. The Ranger, having seen the commotion, was beginning to move. Its great gyroscopes whirred to life, and the magnificent ice ship lifted from the eminence and began to float down toward the bay. Dr. Vaneyke, Barney, and Pomp were on the deck, waving frantically.

“Faix, we were just about to come down an’ level the whole bloomin’ ship!” Barney cried as the Ranger came to a smooth landing on the ice beside the Red Eric. “We saw the hatch close, and then heard all that banging!” “It was all part of the plan,” Frank said with a grin. He quickly explained what had happened, introducing them all to the rescued Walter Grey. The boy was overjoyed to be among friends again. “Lord amassy, chile, you look like you ain’t seen a square meal in a month,” said Pomp, his face full of concern. “We’ll get you back to the Ranger and fix you right up.” “And as for you, Mr. Bolt,” said Dr. Vaneyke, glaring at the captured captain whom Frank had now hauled up on deck, “you have much to answer for. Attempted murder, kidnapping; the law will deal with you harshly.” Ben Bolt simply glowered, saying nothing. He knew he was beaten.

They transferred the captive to the Ranger, locking him in a secure storage compartment. Walter Grey, after a hot meal and a change of clothes, told them his full story. He recounted how Ben Bolt, under the pay of the villainous lawyer Alfred Milburn, had shanghaied him from the docks and sailed for the most remote part of the arctic. The plan was to dispose of the boy in this desolate wasteland, so there would be no witnesses and no evidence. “He was going to leave me here to starve,” Walter said, his voice trembling at the memory. “He said the bears would finish the job.” “The scoundrel!” ejaculated the doctor. “It is a good thing we arrived when we did.” “Indeed it is,” said Frank. “Our mission to rescue the boy is a success. But our work is not yet done. Mrs. Grey is still waiting for news of her son, and justice has yet to be served on Milburn.” “What do we do with Bolt?” asked Barney.

“We will take him with us,” Frank declared. “He will be our star witness against the lawyer. Once we get back to civilization, his testimony will ensure that Milburn spends the rest of his life behind bars.” With their new passenger and their captive secure, the crew of the Ranger prepared to depart. The Red Eric was left behind in the bay, a ghost ship in a frozen world, a monument to greed and failure. The gyroscopes of the Ranger spun up to speed, the mighty vessel lifted from the ice, and with Frank at the wheel, they turned south. Their journey back would be as perilous as the one that brought them here; but their hearts were light. They had bearded the lion in his den and emerged victorious. As the ice ship sped across the frozen landscape, leaving Nordenskjold Bay far behind, Barney and Pomp, in their customary fashion, retrieved the fiddle and banjo. A lively tune soon filled the air, a melody of triumph and relief, echoing out over the vast, white wilderness. The Ranger, with its mission accomplished, was heading for home.

The music of the fiddle and banjo was a joyous sound, a stark contrast to the immense and silent wilderness through which the Ranger sped. For two days they traveled south, their spirits buoyed by success and the promise of home. Walter Grey, now fully recovered, proved to be a bright and inquisitive lad, his eyes wide with wonder at the marvels of the ice ship. He spent hours with Dr. Vaneyke, who was only too happy to explain the workings of the gyroscopes and the pneumatic engines.

Frank, ever vigilant, kept the Ranger on a steady course. The charts showed their way clear through the great strait that would lead them back to open water. Ben Bolt remained a silent and sullen prisoner in the aft storage compartment, a reinforced room that had been designed to hold scientific samples and was now proving its worth as a brig.

On the third day, Barney took the prisoner his meager meal of hardtack and water. As he unbolted the heavy door, he held a pneumatic pistol ready. Bolt sat on the floor in the corner, his ankles and wrists bound in chains, glaring at the Irishman.

“Here’s yer supper, ye blackguard,” said Barney, sliding the plate across the floor.

Bolt’s eyes glittered. “There’s no need for this, Irishman,” he rasped, his voice rough from disuse. “We can come to an arrangement. That boy’s fortune is vast. A portion of it, a handsome portion, could be yours. Enough to buy a hundred farms in the old country.”

“Be silent with yer devil’s talk!” Barney snapped. “It’s the end av a rope ye’ll be gettin’, not a fortune. And it’s what ye deserve for tryin’ to harm that fine lad.”

“Every man has a price,” Bolt sneered. “Yours is just higher than you know. Think on it. When we get to port, what will you have? A pat on the back? Frank will get the glory. You’ll just be the hired help. With me, you’d be a rich man.”

Barney slammed the door shut, the bolt shooting home with a loud clang. He stood there for a moment, his face flushed with anger. The captain’s words were poison, but they found no purchase in Barney’s loyal heart. Still, the incident left him unsettled, a grim reminder that the snake they carried was not yet defanged. He reported the conversation to Frank, who simply nodded.

“I expected as much,” Frank said. “He will try to turn us against each other. It is the only weapon he has left. Pay him no mind, Barney. But double check his bonds next time you go in.”

“Aye, that I will, Frank. I’ll see him trussed up like a holiday goose.”

Their progress, so swift at first, began to slow. The character of the ice changed. The broad, smooth plains gave way to a rugged, tortured landscape of pressure ridges and vast fields of broken pack ice, a result of the same cyclonic storm they had so narrowly avoided.

“The strait is choked,” Dr. Vaneyke announced, looking up from his charts and peering through the forward viewport. “Completely impassable. The ice is piled thirty feet high in places. We’d be crushed to splinters if we tried to force a passage.”

Frank studied the scene ahead. It was a veritable devil’s maze, a chaotic jumble of white and blue ice blocks the size of houses. “There is another way,” he said, pointing to the chart. “This channel, to the west. It is narrower, and the charts are older, but it should take us around this blockage.”

“It is little used by the whalers,” said the doctor. “It is said to be treacherous, with unpredictable currents beneath the ice.”

“All the more reason it is our best hope,” Frank replied. “A path unused is a path where we will not be expected. We have no choice. We take the western channel.”

The Ranger changed course, nosing its way into the narrow fjord. The walls of ice rose high on either side, casting the ship in a perpetual twilight. The silence was profound, broken only by the hum of the gyroscopes and the crackle of the runners on the frozen surface. An hour passed, then two. The tension in the cabin was thick. Pomp, perched in the lookout turret, scanned the horizon ceaselessly.

“Sail ho!” he suddenly cried out, his voice sharp with alarm. “Sail ho, off de starboard bow!”

Everyone rushed to the viewports. There, emerging from behind a massive iceberg, was not one ship, but three. They were whalers, their timbers dark and oil-stained, their rigging stark against the pale sky. They were proceeding in single file, blocking the channel ahead.

“Great Scott!” ejaculated Dr. Vaneyke. “A whole fleet of them!”

“They’re not flying any colors,” Frank observed, his face grim. He brought the Ranger to a halt. “We’ll wait here. Perhaps they will pass.”

But it was not to be. The lead ship, a grimy, brutish-looking barque with the name *Sea Serpent* barely visible on its bow, altered its course. It was heading directly for them. A signal flag was run up its mast.

“They wish to speak with us,” said the doctor.

“Faix, and I don’t like the looks of them one bit,” muttered Barney.

“Nor do I,” said Frank. “Pomp, stay at your post. Barney, stand by the engines. Doctor, you’re with me.”

The *Sea Serpent* drew alongside, its deck crowded with rough-looking men who stared at the strange ice ship with open curiosity and suspicion. A big, bearded man with a face like a slab of granite stood at the rail. He cupped his hands around his mouth.

“Ahoy the ice boat!” he bellowed, his voice echoing off the ice walls. “What vessel is that, and what is your business in these waters?”

“This is the exploration vessel Ranger!” Frank called back. “We are on a scientific expedition, and we are homeward bound!”

“Scientific expedition?” the big man laughed, a harsh, grating sound. “Never heard of such! We’re lookin’ for a friend of ours! Captain Ben Bolt of the Red Eric! Seen him?”

Frank’s heart went cold. This was a complication he had not foreseen. “We saw a whaler, the Red Eric, moored in Nordenskjold Bay!” he answered truthfully. “But there was no sign of its captain or crew!”

The bearded captain, who Frank guessed must be the notorious “Black” Larsen, a known associate of Bolt, narrowed his eyes. “Is that so? Empty, you say? Funny. He was to meet us here. He had valuable cargo. A boy.”

“We saw no boy,” Frank lied, his voice steady.

Larsen stared at the Ranger for a long, silent moment. He looked at its sleek lines, its enclosed turret, its powerful runners. It was clear he did not believe a word.

“I think you’re lyin’,” Larsen roared suddenly. “I think you know more than you’re sayin’! Heave to and prepare to be boarded!”

“We will do no such thing!” Frank retorted.

“Then we’ll make you!” Larsen bellowed. He turned and barked an order. On the deck of the *Sea Serpent*, a man ran to a small cannon mounted on the bow, a weapon used for firing explosive harpoons.

“Full speed, Barney!” Frank yelled. “Get us out of here!”

The Ranger leaped forward with a powerful hum. The whaler, though caught by surprise, was quick to react. With a great cloud of black smoke and a deafening roar, the cannon fired. A heavy, iron harpoon, trailing a thick rope, shot through the air. It missed the hull, but the rope snagged on one of the rear runner struts.

“We’re caught!” cried Dr. Vaneyke.

The rope went taut, and the Ranger was jerked violently, slewing sideways on the ice. The men on the whaler let out a great cheer and began to haul on the line, trying to drag the ice ship closer.

“They can’t hold us!” Frank shouted, his hands tight on the wheel. “The engines are too strong! Barney, give me more power!”

“I’m givin’ her all she’s got, Frank!” came the reply from the engine room.

The Ranger’s wheels spun furiously on the ice, throwing up a great cloud of snow and ice chips. The steel runners groaned under the strain. The thick rope stretched, humming like a giant guitar string. Slowly, inexorably, the Ranger began to pull the much heavier whaler across the ice.

Larsen’s jaw dropped. He had never seen such power. “Cut the line!” he screamed. “Cut it before he pulls us onto the rocks!”

A sailor with an axe ran forward, but it was too late. With a sound like a pistol shot, the massive rope snapped. The freed Ranger shot forward like a stone from a catapult. The *Sea Serpent*, its forward momentum suddenly gone, crashed its bow into a shelf of ice with a splintering crunch.

“Hurrah!” cried the doctor.

But their celebration was short-lived. The other two whalers, the *Orca* and the *Narwhal*, were now moving to block the channel ahead.

“They mean to trap us!” Frank said through gritted teeth. “We can’t go forward, and we can’t go back!”

“What about up?” asked Walter Grey, his voice small but clear.

Frank looked at the boy, then at the towering ice cliffs on either side of the fjord. He grinned. “The boy is a genius! Doctor, prepare the gyroscopes for vertical lift! Barney, stand by to divert power!”

The chase, however, had caused chaos not only outside the Ranger, but within. The violent jerk when the harpoon line caught had sent everything not bolted down flying. In the aft compartment, Ben Bolt was thrown hard against the far wall. The impact, combined with the immense strain on the ship’s frame, had done the impossible. One of the links in his chains, already weakened by his constant twisting and pulling, had snapped.

He was not free, but he had a length of heavy chain attached to one wrist. It was a weapon. The sounds of the cannon and the shouting on deck told him his friends were near. This was his chance. When Barney had last checked on him, he had noticed the lock was a simple, heavy-duty mechanism. With cunning and patience, he had worked a small piece of metal, a buckle from his boot, into the keyhole.

Now, as the ship shuddered again and the alarms for the vertical lift began to chime, he put his plan into action. He rattled the door violently.

“Help! Fire!” he roared. “There’s a fire in here! The wall is hot!”

Pomp, who had come down from the turret to assist, heard the cries. “Massa Frank! De prisoner says dere’s a fire!”

“It’s a trick, Pomp! Stay clear of that door!” Frank shouted back from the command cabin.

But Pomp’s good heart got the better of his caution. He saw a wisp of smoke curl from under the door; Bolt, in his cunning, had managed to set a piece of his own blanket smoldering with a flint and steel he had kept hidden. “Lord amassy, it’s true! He’s gonna roast in dere!”

Forgetting the order, Pomp threw the bolt.

The heavy door flew open and Ben Bolt burst out, a wild look in his eyes. He swung the length of broken chain like a mace. Pomp, taken completely by surprise, ducked just in time as the heavy links whistled past his head and smashed into the wall, leaving a deep dent in the metal.

“You ain’t goin’ nowhere, you villain!” Pomp cried, recovering his wits and grabbing a heavy wrench from a nearby toolkit.

Bolt sneered and advanced on him. “Get out of my way, you fool! I’m taking this ship!”

Meanwhile, in the command cabin, Frank was executing his daring maneuver. “Lift off! Now!” he commanded.

The hum of the engines changed to a high-pitched whine. The entire vessel shuddered, and then, with a lurch that sent a fresh wave of cracking through the ice below, the Ranger lifted into the air. It rose slowly, majestically, ten feet, twenty feet, fifty feet, above the frozen surface.

Below, the crews of the whalers could only stare in stunned disbelief as the incredible machine they had been chasing simply flew up and over their heads. Black Larsen stood on the deck of his crippled ship, shaking his fist in impotent rage.

Inside, the fight raged on. Bolt, powerful and desperate, forced Pomp back along the narrow corridor. He was not trying to escape the ship; he was heading for the engine room. Sabotage was on his mind.

Barney, hearing the commotion, emerged from the engine room. “Saints preserve us!” he yelled, seeing the scene.

The two of them were no match for the enraged captain, who fought with the fury of a cornered bear. He knocked Barney aside and lunged for the engine room controls. Just as he reached for the main steam valve, a small figure darted out from a side passage. It was Walter Grey. In his hands he held one of the fire extinguishers from the galley.

With a cry of determination, he aimed the nozzle and squeezed the handle. A thick cloud of cold, chemical foam blasted into Ben Bolt’s face. The captain roared in shock and pain, blinded and choking. He staggered back, slipping on the now slick floor, and crashed into a storage locker. Before he could recover, Pomp and Barney were on him, the big wrench and a stout Irish fist making short work of his resistance. In moments, they had him subdued, this time binding his hands and feet so securely that not even a Houdini could have escaped.

Frank, having cleared the ice cliffs, brought the Ranger gently down on a broad, clear ice plain miles away from the fjord. He set the ship down and hurried back to see what the commotion was. He found Pomp and Barney standing over the trussed and sputtering form of Ben Bolt, with a proud but trembling Walter Grey nearby.

“He tried to take over the ship, Frank,” said Barney. “If it weren’t for Pomp here, and the quick thinkin’ of young Walter, he might have done it.”

Frank looked at the boy, who had a smudge of grease on his cheek but whose eyes were shining. “You did well, Walter. Very well. You are a true member of the Ranger’s crew.” He turned to his other companions. “Pomp, Barney, you have my thanks. Let’s put this rubbish in the forward chain locker this time. He’ll find it less comfortable, and there’s nothing in there for him to break.”

They dragged the defeated captain away. The Ranger was safe, the enemy far behind. The vast, open sea was now only a day’s journey away. Frank returned to the controls, a thoughtful smile on his face. They had faced down the minions of the villain, outsmarted them, and proven the superiority of their magnificent vessel. The boy they rescued had, in turn, helped to rescue them.

“Set a course due south, doctor,” Frank said, his voice ringing with renewed confidence. “Next stop, civilization. We have a lawyer to visit.”

The doctor took the wheel, and with a smile, brought the Ranger’s speed up once more. The great steel runners hissed over the ice, leaving two parallel lines stretching back into the frozen wastes of the pole. Walter Grey, his adventure having reached a thrilling climax, was taken below by Pomp to be given a hot meal and a place to rest. He had been through a terrible ordeal, and though his spirit was high, his young body was weary.

“Well, Frank,” said Doctor Vaneyke, his eyes twinkling, “that was a close shave. Two villains foiled in as many days. I should say that is good work.”

“We have been fortunate, sir,” answered Frank modestly. “But the real villain is yet to be dealt with. Alfred Milburn awaits us in America.”

“And what of our prisoner?”

“Ben Bolt will be handed over to the proper authorities as soon as we make port. His testimony, along with Walter’s, will be more than enough to ensure that Milburn never sees the light of day again, even if he has somehow escaped his current predicament.”

“Faix, an’ it’s a good long sea voyage he’ll be takin’ to a prison rock,” said Barney, who had come up to the pilot house. “He’ll not be shanghaiing any more lads for a good long while.”

Pomp soon returned from below decks. “Dat boy Walter, he eatin’ like he ain’t seen food in a month, Marse Frank. Den he fall right asleep.”

“Let him rest, Pomp. He’s earned it.”

With their troubles seemingly behind them, a cheerful atmosphere descended upon the Ranger. Barney and Pomp, feeling the release of the prolonged tension, declared it was time for a celebration. Procuring their beloved instruments, they seated themselves in the main cabin and the lively strains of a reel soon filled the air. The banjo and fiddle sang a merry duet as the ice ship flew southward, each mile bringing them closer to the sea and farther from the desolate land of their conflict.

The landscape began to change. The vast, flat plains of ice gave way to a more rugged and broken country. Great pressure ridges, like the frozen waves of a forgotten sea, rose up before them, forcing the Ranger to weave a serpentine course.

“This is rough going, Frank,” commented the doctor, his hands firm on the wheel.

“It is,” agreed Frank, his eyes scanning the terrain ahead. “The ice here is older and has been subjected to immense forces. We must be cautious. A hidden crevasse could swallow us whole.”

He brought out his charts and began making calculations. “According to my reckoning, we should be approaching the coast. The open water cannot be more than fifty miles distant.”

“An’ then it’s a dip in the ocean for the Ranger, is it?” asked Barney, pausing his tune.

“Exactly. We will find a suitable spot, a deep channel leading out from the ice shelf, and transition her from an ice boat to a sea-going vessel. Her runners will retract, and the main propeller will be lowered into the water.”

The journey continued for several hours. The sun, which had circled endlessly in the polar sky, now began to dip toward the horizon, painting the ice in hues of orange and purple. It was Walter, now awake and refreshed, who first noticed something amiss. He was standing in the turret, gazing out at the magnificent but terrifying landscape with the powerful spyglass.

“Mr. Frank!” he called down, his voice sharp with alarm. “There are men on the ice!”

Frank was by his side in an instant, taking the glass from his hand. He trained it on a high ridge far off to their right. At first he saw nothing but snow and rock. Then he caught the movement Walter had seen. A line of dark figures, moving with surprising speed.

“Dog sleds,” Frank muttered, focusing the lens. “And men on skis. There are at least a dozen of them.”

“Who can they be?” asked Vaneyke, who had joined them.

“Eskimos?” suggested Walter.

Frank shook his head, a grim line forming on his lips. “No. They are not dressed like natives. They are whalers, or sailors of some sort. Wait.” He adjusted the focus again, his knuckles white on the brass tube of the spyglass. “I recognize one of the sleds. It is the one that belonged to Black Larsen.”

A chill went through the occupants of the turret that had nothing to do with the arctic air.

“Black Larsen!” ejaculated the doctor. “But his ship was crippled! How can he be pursuing us?”

“He is not pursuing us, doctor. He is trying to get ahead of us,” said Frank, his mind working with lightning speed. “He knows this coast better than we do. He is taking a shortcut across the ridges, a path the Ranger cannot follow. He means to head us off.”

“But what can he do?” asked Walter. “He has no ship to fight us with.”

“He doesn’t need one. Look at the chart.” Frank hurried back to the main cabin and spread the map on the table. He pointed with his finger. “Our present course will take us through this valley. It is the only clear path to the sea for a vessel of our size. See how the cliffs narrow here? It is a natural chokepoint. If Larsen and his men reach those cliffs above the valley before we pass through, they can set a trap.”

“An ambush!” cried Barney. “The cowardly divils!”

“What kind of a trap, Frank?” asked Vaneyke.

“An avalanche,” answered Frank grimly. “A few well-placed charges of dynamite in the glacier that hangs over that valley, and they could bury us under a million tons of ice and snow.”

“Golly, Marse Frank, we got to turn back!” said Pomp, his eyes wide.

“No,” said Frank, his jaw set. “Turning back is what he expects. We would be trapped in this frozen labyrinth with his men hunting us. There is only one way. We must go forward.”

“And spring his trap?” the doctor asked, aghast.

“No. We must beat him to the valley. We must increase our speed to the absolute maximum. It is a race, and the stakes are our very lives.”

Without another word, Frank took the wheel himself. He threw a series of levers, and a deep hum arose from the engine room. The electric motors whined as they poured power into the driving wheels. The Ranger leaped forward like a startled beast, the crackle of the ice beneath her runners rising to a continuous roar.

The landscape flew by in a blur of white and grey. Frank’s eyes were riveted on the path ahead, his hands expertly guiding the mighty craft around towering seracs and over frozen drifts. He pushed the Ranger to the very edge of her endurance, the frame of the vessel groaning under the strain.

From the turret, Vaneyke kept the spyglass trained on the distant ridge. “They are still ahead of us, Frank!” he called down. “But we are closing the gap! They see us now; they are driving their dogs mercilessly!”

“We need more speed!” Frank muttered, his face a mask of concentration.

“She can’t take much more, sor!” yelled Barney from the engine room hatch. “The motors are glowing red hot!”

“They will have to hold!” Frank shouted back.

The mouth of the valley was now visible ahead, a dark slash between two colossal cliffs of ice and rock. High above, on the eastern cliff, the glacier hung like a solid, frozen waterfall, its surface glittering menacingly. And on the crest of that same cliff, tiny black figures were now visible to the naked eye.

“They’ve reached the chokepoint, Frank!” cried Vaneyke. “They’re setting their charges!”

“We are not beaten yet,” said Frank through clenched teeth. “Doctor, Pomp, stand by the forward harpoon guns. Do not fire unless I give the order. Barney, stay in the engine room and give me every ounce of power you can.”

The Ranger shot toward the valley entrance. Frank could see Larsen’s men scrambling on the cliff edge, working frantically. A puff of smoke, followed an instant later by a dull boom, echoed from above. It was not the main charge, but a smaller one, designed to test the stability of the ice. A shower of snow and ice chunks tumbled down the cliff face, crashing onto the valley floor a quarter of a mile ahead of them.

“He is ranging us!” cried the doctor. “The next one will bring it all down!”

Frank’s eyes darted around the control panel. His gaze fell upon a set of shielded levers he had not yet had cause to use. It was a system he had designed for just such an emergency; a desperate measure for a desperate situation.

“Barney!” he yelled. “Divert all auxiliary power to the thermic projectors! Now!”

“The what, sor?” came the confused reply.

“The projectors on the forward bow! Do it!”

A heavy clanking sound was heard as Barney threw the massive switches. The hum of the motors changed pitch, becoming a high, piercing shriek. On the bow of the Ranger, just below the pilot house, two large, parabolic dishes swiveled into position, their polished surfaces aimed forward.

The Ranger was now only a few hundred yards from the area of greatest danger. Above them, Black Larsen himself could be seen, lighting a long fuse. He hurled the sputtering stick of dynamite into a crevice in the glacier’s face.

“Here it comes!” yelled Vaneyke.

With a deafening crack that sounded like the sky itself was being torn in two, a vast section of the glacier broke loose. It did not fall so much as collapse, a slow-motion cascade of millions of tons of ice, snow, and rock that began to pour down into the valley. A great white cloud of powdered snow billowed out before it, an unstoppable tide of white destruction.

At that precise moment, Frank slammed his fist down on a large red button on his console.

“Hold on!” he roared.

From the two dishes on the bow, invisible beams of intense energy shot forth. There was no sound, no flash of light, but the air in front of the Ranger began to shimmer and distort as if seen through a fire. Where the beams struck the oncoming avalanche, the effect was instantaneous and dramatic.

The wall of snow and ice did not simply melt; it vaporized. A tremendous cloud of steam erupted with a ferocious hiss, blotting out the view ahead. The snow turned to boiling slush, the ice chunks exploded into water and vapor. Frank steered the Ranger directly into the heart of the inferno he had created.

The ship plunged into the blinding white cloud of steam. The noise was deafening; the hissing of the projectors, the roar of the avalanche all around them, and the shriek of boiling water. The Ranger bucked and shuddered as it plowed through the super-heated slush, chunks of half-melted ice hammering against her hull. Inside, the crew was thrown about violently, clinging to whatever support they could find.

On the cliff top, Black Larsen and his men could only stare in stunned disbelief. They had expected to see the Ranger crushed and buried. Instead, the vessel had vanished into a self-made cloud of steam, and from the heart of that cloud they could hear the continued defiant roar of its engines. The main mass of the avalanche, deflected by the force of the vaporizing ice, crashed down behind the point where the Ranger had entered the cloud, filling the valley with a mountain of debris.

For what seemed like an eternity, the Ranger fought its way through the chaos. Then, as suddenly as it had begun, they were through. The ship burst out of the steam cloud into the clear, cold air of the valley’s far side. Behind them, the path was completely blocked by a new mountain of ice. Their escape was complete.

A ragged cheer went up from the crew. Barney, his face covered in soot, emerged from the engine room. “Saints be praised, Frank! What in heaven’s name was that?”

“Thermic projectors,” said Frank, his voice strained but triumphant. “Concentrated heat rays. I designed them to clear away ice jams, but I never imagined using them against an avalanche.”

“It’s a miracle!” cried Doctor Vaneyke, clapping Frank on the back. “A true miracle of science!”

Frank smiled wearily and cut power to the projectors. The ship slowed to a more sedate pace. They had won the race. The open sea was now just ahead.

An hour later, the terrain flattened out and the smell of salt was heavy in the air. Before them lay a vast, grey expanse of water, dotted with floating pans of ice. They had reached the Arctic Ocean. Frank guided the Ranger to the edge of a thick, stable ice floe that bordered a deep, clear channel.

“Well, gentlemen,” he said, “it is time for the Ranger to get her feet wet.”

He began the transformation sequence. With a series of whirs and clunks from hydraulic machinery, the great steel runners began to retract up into armored housings in the ship’s hull. At the same time, a panel opened at the stern, and a large, three-bladed propeller was lowered down into the freezing water. Finally, the geared driving wheels that had propelled them across the ice were drawn up and secured.

“All systems ready for marine operation,” Frank announced. “Cast off.”

Pomp and Barney went out on deck and released the ice anchors that had held them to the floe. Frank engaged the main propeller. The water at the stern began to churn, and slowly, majestically, the Ranger slid off the ice and into the sea. She settled in the water with perfect buoyancy, now looking less like an ice boat and more like a futuristic, armored yacht.

A fresh breeze was blowing from the north, and the Ranger picked up speed as she headed south into the open water. The last vestiges of the frozen continent faded behind them.

“Course set for San Francisco, doctor,” said Frank, turning the wheel over to Vaneyke.

“San Francisco it is,” the doctor replied. “And what of Mr. Bolt?”

“He remains in the chain locker. He will be our guest all the way to America.”

The next few days passed without incident. The Ranger proved to be as masterful a sea vessel as she was an ice ship. Her powerful engines drove her south at a steady twenty knots, and her gyroscopic stabilizers kept her perfectly level, even in the rolling swells of the North Pacific. Barney and Pomp resumed their musical interludes, Walter learned the rudiments of navigation from Doctor Vaneyke, and Frank spent his time poring over technical manuals and making minor repairs and adjustments after their ordeal.

They spoke often of the final confrontation to come.

“This lawyer, Milburn, must be a man of considerable influence to have organized all this,” the doctor mused one evening.

“He is a man of considerable greed,” said Frank. “And that is his weakness. He thought with Walter out of the way, the fortune was his. He did not count on us.”

“Do you think he knows of your father’s work, Frank? That he knows who you are?”

“It is unlikely,” Frank replied. “To him, I am just an unknown factor that interfered with his scheme. He sent Ben Bolt to eliminate Walter, and Black Larsen to eliminate the interference. He failed on both counts.”

“When we get to San Francisco, we must proceed with caution,” warned Vaneyke. “A man like that will have allies. He will not be taken easily.”

“I have a plan for that, sir,” said Frank with a confident smile. “We will not be the ones walking into a trap this time. We shall be the ones to spring it.”

One morning, nearly a week into their sea voyage, Pomp, who was on watch in the turret, gave a sudden cry.

“Sail ho! Ship off de starboard bow!”

Everyone rushed to the pilot house. Frank grabbed the spyglass and trained it on the distant vessel. It was a three-masted steamer, low in the water and trailing a thick plume of black smoke. It appeared to be on a course that would intercept their own.

“What do you make of her, Frank?” asked the doctor.

“She’s a commercial freighter by the look of her,” Frank said, squinting. “But she’s flying no flag. That’s odd.”

He watched the ship for a long time. It held its course, steadily closing the distance between them. There was something about its purposeful approach that set Frank’s nerves on edge.

“She is not altering her course,” he said slowly. “She is making directly for us. Keep our current speed, doctor, but be ready to make a hard turn.”

“Do you suspect trouble?”

“I always suspect trouble until proven otherwise,” Frank answered. “In our line of work, it is the only way to stay alive.”

The strange steamer drew closer. Now they could make out figures on her deck. As Frank kept the powerful lens of the spyglass focused on the approaching vessel, his blood ran cold. Standing on the bridge, next to the captain, was a man in a fine suit and top hat, a figure that looked utterly out of place on the deck of a grimy freighter. And he was looking directly back at them through a spyglass of his own.

Frank lowered his glass, a look of grim realization on his face.

“What is it, Frank?” cried Walter. “Who is it?”

Frank turned to his companions, his voice low and serious.

“It’s Alfred Milburn.”

The stunning revelation hung in the air of the pilot house for a long moment. Every eye was fixed upon Frank, whose face was a mask of grim determination.

“Milburn?” cried Doctor Vaneyke. “Are you certain, Frank?”

“As certain as I am that we are on this boat,” replied Frank, not taking his eyes from the approaching steamer. “He must have been released from prison, or perhaps he escaped. It makes no difference. He has found us.”

“Bejabers, the nerve av the man!” exclaimed Barney. “To come after us on the high seas!”

“He knows dat we hab Mastah Walter,” said Pomp, his knuckles white as he gripped a railing. “He won’t stop at nuffin’ to git him back.”

The steamer was now close enough that a man could have been heard shouting from its deck. It was an ugly, functional craft, its hull streaked with rust and its single smokestack belching a foul-smelling, sooty cloud that stained the clear blue sky. On its deck, a number of rough looking men in sailors’ garb had appeared, and they watched the Ranger with malevolent interest.

A figure on the bridge of the steamer raised a speaking trumpet to his lips. It was the lawyer, Alfred Milburn. His voice, amplified by the brass cone, boomed across the water with an air of triumphant arrogance.

“Ahoy, the Ranger!” he called. “Frank Reade Junior, I presume! I have come for what is mine.”

“He means Walter!” whispered the boy, shrinking back behind the doctor.

Frank stepped out onto the Ranger’s deck, his hands empty but his posture defiant.

“Your business is with me, Milburn!” Frank shouted back, his voice clear and strong without the aid of a trumpet. “The boy is under my protection. You have been exposed as a villain and a scoundrel. Turn your ship around and return to San Francisco to face justice.”

A harsh, grating laugh was Milburn’s answer.

“Justice?” he bellowed. “I make my own justice, you meddling boy! I have paid a great deal of money for this vessel and its crew. They are not the sort of men who are easily discouraged. And I have brought a little something to speed up our negotiations!”

As he spoke, a group of sailors on the freighter’s bow threw off a large tarpaulin. Beneath it was a long barreled deck gun, a piece of naval artillery that looked terribly out of place on a commercial vessel. Its black muzzle was aimed directly at the Ranger.

“Great Scott!” cried the doctor from the pilot house. “He has a cannon!”

“Surrender the boy, Reade!” Milburn’s voice boomed again. “And I may be inclined to let you and your companions live. Refuse, and I will blow your fancy toy out of the water!”

Frank did not flinch. He turned and calmly walked back into the pilot house.

“Well, Frank, what is the plan?” asked Vaneyke, his face pale. “We cannot outrun him, and we certainly cannot fight him. That gun will make short work of us.”

“We cannot outrun him on the water,” said Frank, a steely glint in his eye. “But he has forgotten that the Ranger has more than one way to travel. Doctor, take the wheel and prepare for a sharp turn. Barney, Pomp, go below and stand by the gyroscopes. Do not start them until I give the word.”

“Aye, sor!” said Barney.

“We’re on our way, Marse Frank!” added Pomp.

The two faithful companions scrambled down the ladder to the engine room.

“Walter, stay down and keep away from the windows,” Frank instructed. The boy nodded, his eyes wide with a mixture of fear and faith in his rescuer.

Frank returned his attention to the steamer. Milburn was growing impatient.

“I will not ask again, Reade!” the lawyer shouted. “This is your final chance!”

“My final answer is no!” Frank yelled back.

“So be it! Fire!” Milburn shrieked, his voice cracking with fury.

There was a deafening boom from the freighter, and a cloud of white smoke erupted from the cannon’s muzzle. A second later, a geyser of water shot twenty feet into the air, scarcely a dozen yards from the Ranger’s port side. The shockwave of the near miss rocked the little vessel violently.

“Heavens, that was close!” exclaimed Vaneyke, struggling to hold his course.

“He is testing his range,” said Frank grimly. “The next one will not miss. Hard to starboard, doctor! Now!”

Vaneyke spun the wheel, and the Ranger heeled over, beginning a wide turn away from the menacing steamer.

Another boom echoed across the waves. This time the shell screamed directly over their heads, so close they could feel the heat of its passage. It landed in the water far beyond them.

“His gunners are not accustomed to firing at a moving target,” Frank observed coolly. “But they will learn quickly. Stand by, doctor. Prepare for my signal.”

The freighter was turning as well, trying to bring its bow gun to bear once more. Milburn was on the bridge, waving his fists and screaming orders at his crew.

“Now, doctor!” cried Frank. “Full stop!”

Vaneyke threw the engine telegraph to “All Stop”. The Ranger’s forward momentum slowed. The gunners on the freighter, seeing their target steadying, took careful aim for what they surely believed would be the final, fatal shot.

“Barney! Pomp! Engage the gyroscopes! Full power!” Frank shouted down the speaking tube.

From the depths of the ship came a low hum that quickly rose in pitch to a high-pitched, powerful whine. The entire vessel began to vibrate with immense, contained energy. The deck beneath their feet trembled.

On the freighter, the gunner was just about to pull the lanyard. Milburn watched with a look of satanic glee on his face.

Then, to the utter astonishment of every man on the steamer, the Ranger lifted from the water.

Slowly at first, then with increasing speed, the marvelous craft rose vertically into the air. Water streamed from its steel runners and hull, glinting in the sun like a shower of diamonds. It cleared the waves by ten feet, then twenty, then fifty, climbing steadily into the sky on the power of its incredible gyroscopic engines.

The jaw of the gunner on the freighter dropped open. The men on deck stared, their faces a mixture of disbelief and superstitious terror. Alfred Milburn’s look of triumph curdled into one of apoplectic rage and utter shock.

“Fire! Fire, you fools!” he screamed. “Shoot it down!”

The gunner, shaken from his stupor, fired the cannon. But aiming a naval gun at a target in the sky was an impossible task. The shell flew harmlessly underneath the ascending Ranger, a hundred feet below its mark.

“He did not count on this!” said Doctor Vaneyke with a giddy laugh, his fear forgotten in the thrill of the moment. “Look at them, Frank! They are like ants!”

“Do not celebrate yet, sir,” said Frank. “We are not out of danger. He has a crew of desperate men and they will have rifles. Keep us climbing.”

The Ranger rose to an altitude of five hundred feet, well out of range of any small arms fire. It hovered there, its gyroscopes humming a song of victory. Below, the steamer looked like a child’s toy. They could see the tiny figures of the crew running about the deck in confusion.

“Well, Marse Frank, we showed dat villain a thing or two,” came Pomp’s voice up the tube.

“Bejabers, his eyes must be poppin’ out av his head loike boiled onions!” added Barney.

“Well done, both of you,” said Frank. “Keep the engines steady. Now, to end this matter.”

“What do you mean?” asked the doctor. “Surely we will just fly on to San Francisco. We have escaped.”

“If we leave him, he will simply repair his pride and come after us again,” Frank said firmly. “He is a menace that must be dealt with. As long as he is free, Walter is not safe. Nor are we. We are going to disable his ship and capture him.”

“Capture him? Frank, that is a freighter filled with ruffians!”

“And this is the Ranger,” Frank replied with confidence. He went to a polished mahogany locker and opened it, revealing a complex arrangement of brass levers and gauges. “It is time to test the pneumatic cannon.”

“I had almost forgotten we installed that!” exclaimed the doctor.

Frank sighted through a periscopic lens that protruded from the bottom of the hull. He made a few fine adjustments with a set of brass wheels.

“The smokestack is our best target,” he said. “Without steam, he is dead in the water. Stand by for a slight recoil.”

Frank grasped a lever and pulled it firmly. There was a loud, explosive hiss of compressed air, and the entire ship shuddered slightly. From a nozzle in the Ranger’s belly, a heavy, lead-cased projectile shot downward with incredible velocity.

Through the periscope, Frank watched it fall. His aim was perfect. The projectile struck the freighter’s smokestack just above its base. There was not a fiery explosion, but a tremendous clang of rending metal. The tall, soot-stained stack crumpled like a paper tube, toppling over with a great crash onto the deck and crushing a section of railing. A huge cloud of steam and smoke billowed out, obscuring the ship from view.

“A direct hit!” cried Frank.

When the steam cleared, they could see the full extent of the damage. The freighter was crippled, listing slightly and drifting without power. The crew was in disarray, some trying to fight the small fires that had started where hot coals had spilled, others simply staring up at the flying machine in terror.

“Now for phase two,” said Frank, his face set. “Doctor, bring us down. Hover fifty feet above their main deck. Pomp, Barney, arm yourselves. We are going aboard.”

“Aboard, sor?” stammered Barney. “It’s a hornet’s nest we’ll be droppin’ into!”

“We will be the hornets,” Frank said. He strapped on a belt that held two of his patent pneumatic pistols and a long knife. “These pistols fire pellets of concentrated anesthetic gas. They will not kill, but they will render a man unconscious for several minutes. Use them wisely. We want Milburn, alive.”

Barney and Pomp armed themselves similarly, their initial apprehension replaced by grim resolve.

The doctor skillfully maneuvered the Ranger until it hovered directly over the crippled steamer’s deck. The terrified sailors below scattered, unsure what to expect next. Ropes were thrown down from the air ship’s open hatch.

“I will go first,” said Frank. “Cover me from above. Follow as soon as I am on the deck. Let’s go.”

Without a moment’s hesitation, Frank grasped the rope and slid down, landing as lightly as a cat on the chaotic deck of the freighter. A burly sailor with a scarred face and a belaying pin charged at him. Frank raised his pneumatic pistol. There was a soft ‘phut’ sound, and a small pellet struck the man in the chest. His eyes widened in surprise, he took one more stumbling step, and then collapsed in a heap.

Seeing their comrade fall so mysteriously, the other sailors hesitated. That was all the time Barney and Pomp needed. The two stalwarts came sliding down the ropes and landed on either side of Frank.

“Come on, ye cowardly dogs!” roared Barney, brandishing his own pistol. “Who’s next for a nap?”

A few of the braver sailors, armed with knives and clubs, decided to rush them. Pomp met the charge, not with his pistol, but with his sheer strength. He grabbed one man by the collar and another by his belt and knocked their heads together with a resounding crack. They fell to the deck, dazed. Barney, meanwhile, sent two more to dreamland with well-aimed shots from his pneumatic weapon.

The fight was short and one-sided. The sailors, already demoralized by the supernatural appearance of the flying ship and the crippling of their vessel, had no stomach for a battle against these strange new weapons. Most threw down their arms and backed away, their hands raised in surrender.

“Secure them!” ordered Frank. “Pomp, find some rope and tie up anyone who looks like they might still cause trouble. Barney, you’re with me. We’re going to the bridge.”

Frank and Barney kicked open the door to the ship’s superstructure and charged up a narrow flight of stairs. They found the captain of the freighter in the wheelhouse, a hulking man with a tangled black beard. He made a move for a revolver in his belt, but a quick shot from Barney’s pistol put him to sleep before he could draw it.

“The divil’s gone nighty-night,” Barney chuckled. “Now where’s the head snake?”

“He must be in the chart room!” Frank deduced.

He kicked open the adjoining door. There, amidst maps and navigational instruments, stood Alfred Milburn. His fine suit was rumpled and his face was ashen with fury and disbelief. In his hand, he held a small, pearl-handled derringer, which he pointed with a shaking hand at Frank.

“Stay back, Reade!” he snarled, his voice a venomous whisper. “I’ll not be taken! I’ll see you in perdition first!”

“It’s over, Milburn,” Frank said calmly, his own pistol leveled at the lawyer’s chest. “You have lost. Your ship is crippled, your crew has surrendered. Give it up.”

“Never!” the lawyer shrieked, and he pulled the trigger.

But Frank had anticipated the move. Even as Milburn’s finger tightened, Frank fired his own weapon. The anesthetic pellet struck the lawyer’s gun hand. His fingers went numb instantly, and the derringer clattered uselessly to the floor. Before Milburn could even process what had happened, Frank had crossed the small room and pinned him against the chart table.

“Be heavens, that was a close shave, Frank!” Barney cried, rushing in.

“He is a desperate man,” Frank said, breathing a little heavily as he twisted Milburn’s arm behind his back. “Find something to tie him with, Barney. Tightly.”

A few minutes later, Alfred Milburn was trussed up like a holiday turkey and deposited unceremoniously on the floor of the chart room. His face was a mask of impotent fury.

“You will pay for this, Reade,” he hissed. “You and your brat and your whole cursed crew! I have powerful friends!”

“Your powerful friends can visit you in prison,” Frank retorted. He turned to Barney. “Let us return to the Ranger. We have what we came for.”

They brought their prisoner back to the main deck, where Pomp had finished tying up the most aggressive of the sailors. The rest of the crew were huddled by the broken mast, thoroughly cowed.

“What’ll we do wid this lot, Marse Frank?” Pomp asked.

“They are common sailors, led astray by Milburn’s money,” Frank said. “We will leave them. Their ship is adrift but not sinking. Another vessel will find them eventually. Our only concern is him.”

He gestured to the bound and gagged lawyer.

A rope harness was lowered from the Ranger. They strapped the sputtering lawyer into it and he was hoisted into the air like a side of beef, much to the delight of Barney and Pomp. Frank and his two companions followed, climbing the ropes back to the safety of their own vessel.

Once everyone was aboard, the hatch was sealed and the ropes were pulled in. Doctor Vaneyke, who had been watching the whole affair with breathless anxiety, clapped Frank on the back.

“Incredible, Frank! Absolutely incredible!” he said. “I have never seen anything like it.”

“It was a necessary risk, sir,” Frank replied. “Now, let us find a place to store our passenger.”

Milburn was taken below and locked securely in a small, empty storage cabin. He glared at them with eyes full of hatred, but said nothing more.

Back in the pilot house, a great sense of relief filled the air. Walter Grey looked at Frank with pure adoration.

“You saved us again, Frank,” he said.

“We all saved each other,” Frank corrected him with a smile. “Now, doctor, let’s get back on course for San Francisco. We have a villain to deliver to the authorities.”

The doctor took the controls, and the marvelous air ship, the Ranger, turned its nose toward the east and sped through the sky, leaving the crippled steamer and its bewildered crew bobbing helplessly on the vast, empty expanse of the Pacific Ocean. The final chapter of their long and perilous adventure was about to begin.

Frank piloted the marvelous craft with expert skill, navigating by the stars and the faint glow of the aurora borealis on the horizon. He brought the ice ship to a hover over the cliffs he had identified earlier, then slowly lowered her until the runners rested gently upon a flat ledge of rock and snow, a hundred feet above the hidden camp.

Not a light was to be seen from the hut below. The villains were either asleep or had doused their fires for the night. So much the better. Frank secured one end of his silk rope to a strong metal cleat on the Ranger’s deck and tossed the other end over the cliff’s edge. As Walter scrambled out, Frank turned to face the five furious men. The red bearded giant, who was clearly the leader, lunged at him with a wicked looking harpoon. Frank did not hesitate. He raised his pneumatic pistol and fired. There was a soft ‘phut’ sound, and a small pellet struck the man in the chest. A look of surprise crossed the giant’s face, his eyes went glassy, and he crumpled to the floor in a heap, fast asleep. It was Ben Bolt himself.

“The divil! He’s killed the captain!” one of the others yelled.

“It’s magic!” screamed another.

Their momentary shock gave Frank the opening he needed. He fired again, and a second man dropped. The remaining three, their courage faltering in the face of this silent, magical weapon, hesitated. Frank used that moment to dive out through the door and into the snow.

“Up the rope, Frank!” he heard Barney yell from the cliff top.

A shot rang out from the hut, and a lead ball whistled past Frank’s ear. The ruffians had recovered their wits and their firearms. Frank scrambled to his feet and sprinted for the cliff base. He could see Walter already a third of the way up the rope.

As Frank reached the rope and began to climb, the remaining three men burst from the hut, firing their muskets wildly. From the cliff top, there came an answering crackle of rifle fire. Pomp and Barney were laying down a covering fusillade. One of the villains cried out and clutched his arm. The other two, seeing their position was hopeless, retreated back into the relative safety of the hut.

Frank swarmed up the rope, his muscles straining. He reached the ledge just as Walter was being hauled onto the deck of the Ranger by Dr. Vaneyke. Barney and Pomp helped Frank over the edge.

“Begorra, that was a close one, Frank!” Barney exclaimed. “We saw the whole thing through the spy glass!”

“Is the boy alright?” Frank asked, panting.

“He is safe, Frank, thanks to you,” said the doctor, who was wrapping a warm blanket around the shivering Walter.

“Golly, Marse Frank, yo’ sure showed dem rascals!” said Pomp, reloading his rifle.

“There is no time to lose,” Frank said. “They will be after us as soon as they realize what has happened. We must be off.”

He hurried to the pilot house and threw the levers. The gyroscopes spun faster, and with a powerful hum, the Ranger lifted off the cliff and soared into the night sky, leaving the fuming, defeated villains far below.

They flew eastward through the remainder of the night. Walter Grey, after a hot meal and a cup of broth, told them his story. He confirmed Frank’s suspicions. Captain Bolt and his crew were indeed deserters and ivory hunters who had forced him to work for them under threat of death. The boy’s gratitude toward his rescuers was immense.

As dawn broke, they found themselves flying over a vast, open stretch of the Arctic Ocean, dotted with icebergs. The crisis seemed to be over. Dr. Vaneyke was at the wheel, while Barney and Pomp were in the galley preparing a victory breakfast. Frank and Walter stood in the forward observation dome, watching the magnificent scenery.

It was then that Pomp, who had gone up to the turret for a better view, gave a sudden cry of alarm.

“Marse Frank! Look behind us! Dar’s somethin’ comin’ fast!”

Frank grabbed his spy glass and spun around. His heart sank. Far astern, skimming low over the water and gaining on them rapidly, was another vessel. It was not an ice ship like the Ranger, nor a common whaler. It was long, sleek, and black, with no sails or visible means of propulsion. It was an air ship.

“Good Heaven!” exclaimed Dr. Vaneyke, who had joined them. “Who can that be? It is not a government vessel.”

Frank’s face was grim. “I do not know, sir. But I have a very bad feeling about this. Their design is unfamiliar, but their speed is tremendous. They are hostile, I am sure of it.”

“Be heavens, maybe it’s friends of the divils we left behind in the hut!” Barney suggested, running up from the galley.

“That is a distinct possibility, Barney,” Frank said. “A possibility we must prepare for. Doctor, give her all the speed you can. Barney, Pomp, man the pneumatic cannon. It seems our adventure is not yet over.”

The Ranger leaped forward as Dr. Vaneyke threw all the power of the gyroscopes into the propellers. The hum of the machinery rose to a high pitched scream, and the air ship, a marvel of modern invention, shot through the Arctic air at a speed that would have astonished any man of science. Yet, fast as she was, the black vessel astern was faster. It was a sinister looking craft, shaped like a long, sharp spindle, and it cut through the air with a silent, deadly grace. Frank kept the spy glass to his eye.

“They are gaining, doctor!” he cried. “I can make out figures on her deck now. They appear to be armed with strange rifles.”

“What can they want with us, Frank?” asked the doctor, his brow furrowed with worry.

“I believe Barney is right,” Frank answered grimly. “They must be confederates of Captain Bolt. Milburn must have had more resources than we imagined. Perhaps he hired this second vessel as an insurance.”

“Begorra, they’re a persistent pack of divils!” said Barney, standing by the breech of the pneumatic cannon. “Let them come within range, an’ I’ll give them a pill they won’t like.”

“Hold your fire, Barney,” ordered Frank. “Our pneumatic shells may be no match for their hull. We do not know what kind of armor she carries. Our best hope is to outmaneuver them.”

“Dar ain’t much room to maneuver in dis big empty sky, Marse Frank!” Pomp pointed out, his eyes wide as saucers.

Pomp’s observation was terribly true. Over the open sea, it was a simple race of speed, and the Ranger was losing. The black air ship was now less than a mile astern and closing the distance with frightful velocity. A flash of intense, blue-white light suddenly erupted from the bow of the pursuing vessel. It was not a shell, but a brilliant bolt of what looked like lightning, and it streaked through the air toward them.

“Down!” yelled Frank.

The bolt struck the aft rail of the Ranger with a deafening crack, like a clap of thunder. The metal glowed white hot and melted away as if it were wax. A shower of sparks flew into the air, and the whole ship shuddered from the impact.

“Good Heaven! What was that?” gasped Dr. Vaneyke.

“Some form of electrical weapon!” Frank ejaculated, his face pale but determined. “Far more advanced than anything I have ever conceived of. If one of those bolts hits our gyroscopes or the engine room, we are finished!”

“They’re firin’ again!” yelled Pomp.

Another bolt shot forth, this time passing just over the turret and singeing the flag that flew from its mast. Frank’s mind worked with lightning speed. They could not outrun their foe, and they could not fight it on these terms. Evasion was their only chance.

“Doctor!” he cried, pointing ahead. “Do you see that wall of ice ahead? That massive glacier?”

“Yes, Frank. What of it?”

“Steer for it! Head directly for the face of the cliff!”

“But Frank, that’s suicide!” the doctor protested. “We’ll be dashed to pieces!”

“Trust me, sir! It is our only hope!” Frank’s tone left no room for argument.

Dr. Vaneyke, having seen the boy inventor out of a dozen scrapes just as perilous, did not hesitate further. He spun the wheel, and the Ranger veered toward the colossal wall of blue and white ice that rose thousands of feet from the surface of the sea. Their pursuers, likely thinking their prey was choosing destruction over capture, let loose a third bolt, which went wide as the Ranger changed its course. From behind them, Frank could hear what sounded like triumphant shouts carried on the wind.

The glacier loomed before them, a terrifying spectacle of nature’s power. Great fissures and cracks marred its surface, and here and there, a cascade of water from some melting patch high above had frozen into a curtain of icicles, some as thick as the trunks of ancient trees.

“There!” Frank shouted, pointing to one such frozen waterfall. “Behind that curtain of ice! It looks like there’s a cavern!”

“It’s too narrow, Frank! We’ll never make it!”

“We have to! Pomp, Barney, secure everything that is not tied down! Prepare for a rough landing!”

The black air ship, seeing their intent, renewed its attack with vigor. Electrical bolts sizzled through the air around the Ranger, the heat of their passage causing the air to crackle. Frank stood beside the professor, his eyes fixed on the narrow opening behind the ice.

“A little to the port, doctor! Now hold her steady! Steady!”

With a tremendous grating sound, the starboard runner of the ice ship scraped against a massive icicle. The ice groaned in protest, but the steel of the Ranger held true. The ship slid through the gap, shattering a dozen smaller icicles, and then plunged into the relative darkness of the cavern behind the frozen falls. They were through. Dr. Vaneyke cut the power, and the Ranger settled onto a flat, solid floor of ancient ice, its forward momentum carrying it deep into the heart of the glacier. For a moment, the only sound was the drip of melting water and the heavy breathing of the crew.

“Be heavens, Frank,” Barney said, wiping his brow with a trembling hand. “I thought our goose was cooked for sure.”

“We are safe for the moment,” Frank said, peering back out through the curtain of ice. Through the gaps, he could see the black air ship hovering, its crew clearly baffled by the Ranger’s disappearance. “But they know we are in here somewhere. And we are damaged.”

A quick inspection revealed the truth of his words. The electrical bolt had melted a ten foot section of the railing and, more critically, had sent a terrific jolt through the ship’s frame. One of the secondary gyroscope stabilizers had been knocked out of alignment.

“Can you fix it, Frank?” asked Dr. Vaneyke, his voice grave.

“I believe so, sir,” Frank replied, already pulling a set of tools from a locker. “But it will take time. The main flywheel casing is warped. Barney, I’ll need your strength to help me bend it back into place. Pomp, keep a sharp lookout. Let us know the instant that ship makes a move.”

“Yes, Marse Frank!”

While Frank and Barney set to work on the delicate and arduous task of repairing the gyroscope, Dr. Vaneyke made Walter Grey comfortable and checked on their supplies. The cavern they had found was immense. It was a natural grotto of ice, a vast, glittering dome whose walls shimmered with captured light, casting blue and green reflections everywhere. It was like being inside a colossal geode. In the center of the cavern, a deep, dark pool of unfrozen seawater churned slowly, connected to the ocean by some sub-glacial channel.

An hour passed. Frank, his face smudged with grease, worked tirelessly with Barney. The groaning of tortured metal filled the cavern as they used a heavy winch and pry bars to force the warped casing back into shape. Pomp, meanwhile, remained glued to his post at the entrance.

“Marse Frank!” he called out suddenly, his voice an urgent whisper. “Dey’re lowerin’ a boat!”

Frank dropped his wrench and rushed to the opening. He peered through a crack in the ice curtain. The black air ship had descended to just above the sea. A small launch, containing half a dozen men, was being lowered to the water.

“They mean to search the base of the glacier,” Frank surmised. “They will find this opening sooner or later.”

“We can’t take off yet, Frank!” said Barney. “This machine is still in pieces!”

“I know that, Barney. We will not fight them with the Ranger. We will fight them with the cave itself.”

“What do you mean, Frank?” asked the doctor.

Frank’s eyes scanned the icy dome above them. His gaze settled on a gigantic mass of ice, a veritable mountain weighing many hundreds of tons, that hung precariously from the ceiling directly over the entrance to the cavern. It was attached by what seemed a surprisingly narrow column of ice.

“I have an idea,” Frank said, a flicker of excitement in his eyes. “A desperate one, but it may work. Pomp, bring me the longest coil of electrical wire we have. Doctor, I need the spare blasting detonator. Barney, come with me.”

Frank’s plan was as brilliant as it was audacious. He intended to rig the detonator to the narrowest point of the column supporting the massive block of ice overhead. When the invaders entered the cavern, he would trigger the charge, bringing tons of ice crashing down upon the entrance, sealing it forever and, with any luck, trapping their enemies beneath it.

It was a perilous climb up the slippery cavern wall, but with Barney’s help, Frank reached the spot. He carefully packed the small but powerful explosive charge against the ice column and ran the wire back down to a sheltered position behind a spur of ice near the Ranger.

They had not long to wait. The crunch of boots on the ice outside the entrance announced the arrival of the search party. A moment later, a figure stepped through the frozen waterfall, rifle at the ready. He was followed by five others. The last man to enter was a tall, imperious figure in a fine fur coat, whose face was contorted in a mask of vengeful fury.

Frank gasped. It was not Captain Bolt. It was the lawyer, Alfred Milburn.

“So, the rat has found a hole to hide in!” Milburn’s voice echoed through the vast cavern. He was no longer the picture of a respectable man of law; his eyes held a wild, manic gleam. “I should have known you would not be so easy to dispose of, Frank Reade. My associates on the Red Eric were clearly incompetent fools.”

“Milburn!” Frank cried, stepping out from behind the Ranger. “You have escaped prison, then.”

“Prison could not hold a man of my intellect and resources,” the lawyer sneered. “It was a simple matter to arrange my freedom and to purchase the services of a far more capable crew. This vessel, the ‘Avenger,’ and her electrical cannon were designed by a disgraced genius who owed me a great debt. A debt he has now repaid by giving me the means to hunt you down and recover what is mine!”

“The boy’s fortune is not yours, and never will be,” Frank retorted hotly.

“We shall see about that!” Milburn snarled. “Once I have disposed of you and your pathetic crew, and the boy, no one will be the wiser. Now, surrender the boy, and I may grant you a quick death!”

“Never!”

“So be it! Men, seize them! Shoot the inventor on sight!”

The five thugs raised their rifles. But just as they did, a lively tune struck up from the deck of the Ranger. It was Pomp, standing boldly in the open, furiously strumming his banjo. The sheer absurdity of the act gave the villains pause for a critical second.

“Now, Barney!” yelled Frank.

From the other side of the Ranger, Barney O’Shea let out a fearsome Irish war cry and hurled a heavy iron flywheel casing. The massive piece of metal spun through the air like a discus and crashed into two of the men, sending them sprawling to the ice with cries of pain.

“Fire, you fools! Fire!” screamed Milburn.

The remaining three men opened fire. But Frank had already ducked behind his icy bastion. The bullets ricocheted harmlessly off the walls.

“Come out and fight, you coward!” Milburn raged, stepping forward. He drew a polished revolver from his coat. He was now directly under the great mass of ice.

“It is you who should have stayed out, Milburn!” Frank shouted back. He held the two ends of the wire in his hands.

At that moment, Dr. Vaneyke, taking his cue, activated the Ranger’s powerful forward searchlight. A brilliant beam of pure white light shot forth, blinding Milburn and his men. They threw their hands up to their faces, stumbling in confusion.

“It’s now or never!” Frank muttered, and touched the wires together.

There was no loud explosion, only a sharp crack from high above. For a heart-stopping second, nothing happened. Then, a spiderweb of fissures appeared in the column of ice. A deep, grinding groan echoed through the cavern, a sound of ancient ice giving way.

“Look out!” one of the men screamed.

Milburn looked up, his face a mask of sudden, utter terror. The gigantic mass of ice was tilting. Slowly at first, then with gathering speed, it began to fall. It came down not with a crash, but with a ground-shaking roar that was beyond all sound. The air was filled with a hurricane of ice dust and pulverized fragments.

Frank and his companions could only press themselves against the far wall as the avalanche of ice struck the cavern floor where Milburn and his men had stood. The entire glacier seemed to shudder. The entrance to the cave was completely and irrevocably buried under a mountain of shattered ice. Of Alfred Milburn and his hired thugs, there was no sign. They were buried under a hundred thousand tons of ice, a fitting tomb for their greed.

The silence that followed was more profound than the noise that had preceded it.

“Lord amassy,” Pomp whispered, his banjo hanging limp in his hands. “Is it over?”

“It is over,” Frank said quietly, his voice strained. He got to his feet, brushing ice dust from his jacket. “Let us finish the repairs. We must get out of here.”

Two hours later, the gyroscope was humming smoothly once more. With no other exit, Frank’s only option was to go forward. He activated the Ranger’s powerful forward drills, devices he had installed for just such an emergency. With a grinding roar, the ice ship began to bore its way through the back of the cavern, creating a new tunnel through the living glacier. After a mile of slow, nerve-wracking progress, they broke through the other side of the glacier into the clear, cold light of the Arctic day.

Below them, on the sea, lay the wreckage of the ‘Avenger.’ The shockwave from the ice fall had evidently damaged the black air ship, sending it plummeting into the water. Its secrets, and its disgraced inventor, were now lost to the depths.

The journey home was swift and uneventful. They flew high and fast, leaving the desolate, white world of the Arctic behind them. Walter Grey, now safe and sound, proved to be a cheerful and intelligent companion, and he listened with wide-eyed wonder as Frank and the doctor explained the workings of the magnificent Ranger.

Their arrival in New York created a sensation. News of the rescue, and of the fate of the villainous lawyer Alfred Milburn, had been telegraphed ahead. A crowd had gathered to see the marvelous air ship land. Mrs. Grey was there, and her reunion with her son was a thing of tears and heartfelt gratitude.

“Mr. Reade,” she said to Frank, her voice choked with emotion. “There is no reward in this world that could ever be enough to thank you for what you have done. You have saved my son and brought a wicked man to justice. The fortune is secure, and you shall have any part of it you wish.”

“The safety of your son is all the reward I need, madam,” Frank replied with a modest smile. “I am just glad we were able to be of service.”

Later that evening, back in the comfortable cabin of the Ranger, the four companions sat relaxing after their long and perilous voyage. Barney was tuning his fiddle, Pomp was polishing his banjo, and Dr. Vaneyke was studying a large map of the world spread out on the table.

“Well, Frank,” said the doctor, looking up. “We have conquered the terrors of the frozen north. Where shall the Ranger take us next?”

Frank’s eyes lit up with their characteristic fire. He leaned over the map, his finger tracing a path down through the Americas to the great, green expanse of the Amazon basin.

“I have been reading a most peculiar account, sir,” he said. “A manuscript by an old Spanish explorer. It speaks of a hidden plateau deep in the Brazilian jungle, a place where creatures from a bygone age are said to still exist. It is called the Lost World.”

Barney stopped his tuning. “Begorra, Frank, you don’t mean dinosaurs?”

“That is precisely what the manuscript implies, Barney,” Frank said, his smile widening. “It sounds like a grand adventure, does it not?”

Dr. Vaneyke chuckled, Pomp’s eyes grew wide, and Barney let out a whoop of delight. The perils of the Arctic were already fading into memory, replaced by the thrilling promise of a new and even greater mystery to be solved. For Frank Reade and the crew of the Ranger, the world was full of wonders, and their journey was only just beginning.

CHAPTER I.
PREPARATIONS FOR A NEW WORLD.

“Dinosaurs, is it?” cried Barney, his eyes as wide as saucers. “Begorra, Frank, an’ do yez think it’s the truth that old bit o’ paper is tellin’?”

“It is my belief that the account is genuine,” said Frank, his finger still tracing the path to the remote plateau on the map. “The Spanish explorer, a man named Coronado, had no reason to invent such a tale. His descriptions of the geography are remarkably precise, which lends credibility to his more fantastic claims.”

“Golly, Mistah Frank, yo’ mean we’s gwine to see critters bigger dan a house?” asked Pomp, his banjo resting forgotten in his lap.

“If Coronado’s manuscript is to be believed, Pomp, yes,” Frank answered with a confident smile. “Creatures that have been extinct for millions of years elsewhere on the globe. Think of the scientific value of such a discovery!”

“I am thinking of the scientific value of our own hides,” said Dr. Vaneyke, though a twinkle in his eye betrayed his excitement. “If these beasts exist, they are likely not to be trifled with. What preparations must we make?”

“A great many,” Frank replied, his mind already racing. “The Ranger performed magnificently in the cold, but the tropics are another matter entirely. We must guard against humidity and heat. I will need to overhaul the ventilation systems and add a refrigeration unit to preserve our provisions.”

The next few days were a blur of activity. While New York City buzzed with tales of their Arctic rescue, the crew of the Ranger was hard at work. Frank Reade, with his seemingly endless genius, designed and installed a new atmospheric condenser, a device calculated to draw moisture from the humid jungle air and convert it into fresh, potable water. He also reinforced the viewports with a new type of crystallized glass of his own invention, which he declared was strong enough to resist the impact of a charging rhinoceros.

Barney and Pomp were tasked with restocking their supplies. They loaded crates of tinned meats, dried fruits, and ship’s biscuit, along with special bolts for their pneumatic rifles. Frank had designed a new type of projectile, one that delivered a powerful electric shock upon impact, hoping it would be more effective against giant fauna than a simple lead bullet.

“Faix, an’ it’s a queer sort of huntin’ trip this is,” Barney said to Pomp as they stowed a case of the electric bolts. “We’re not after skins or meat, but to look at the beasties an’ then run away.”

“Dat’s fine by me, Barney,” Pomp replied, securing the crate. “I ain’t got no desire to get too close to any critter what can swallow me whole. I’ll do my lookin’ from inside dis here ship, thank yo’ very much.”

At last, all was in readiness. On a clear, crisp morning, a week after their return, the Ranger stood gleaming on its private landing field. A small crowd of reporters and well wishers had gathered, but Frank allowed no one aboard. His work was his own.

“All systems are nominal, sir,” he said to Dr. Vaneyke, his hands flying over the control panel in the pilot turret. “The gyroscopes are spooling up to speed.”

A low hum filled the air, growing into a deep, powerful thrum. The mighty ice ship, now an air ship, lifted from the ground as smoothly as a bubble rising in water. It ascended a hundred feet, then five hundred, the people below shrinking to the size of ants.

“Set a course due south, doctor,” Frank commanded, his eyes on the horizon. “Next stop, the Amazon basin!”

“South it is,” the doctor replied, taking the wheel.

The Ranger banked gracefully and sped away from the city, a silver arrow aimed at the heart of a new and profound mystery.

CHAPTER II.
THE GREEN LABYRINTH.

The journey southward was a marvel of speed and comfort. The Ranger, a masterpiece of electrical and mechanical engineering, flew at a steady altitude of one thousand feet, its powerful dynamos humming a tune of progress. They crossed over the Carolinas, the sun-drenched coast of Florida, and the brilliant turquoise waters of the Caribbean Sea.

Barney and Pomp, their duties for the moment light, often sat on the deck, safely enclosed by a brass railing, playing their instruments as islands drifted by below like green jewels on a blue cloth.

“It’s a foine view, to be sure,” Barney said one afternoon, pausing his fiddle playing. “But it’s a powerful lot o’ water. It gives a man a thirst.”

“We’s got plenty o’ water now, thanks to Mistah Frank’s new contraption,” said Pomp. “Go on an’ play dat jig again.”

Frank and the doctor spent most of their time in the turret, checking their charts and calculations against the passing terrain. Frank had made a precise copy of the old Spanish map, overlaying it with modern charts to plot their course.

“According to Coronado’s log,” Frank said, pointing to a spot on the chart, “he followed this river, the Rio Negro, for two hundred leagues before he saw the escarpment. The river’s course has likely changed little.”

“And you believe this plateau has remained isolated for millions of years?” asked Dr. Vaneyke.

“It is the only explanation,” Frank asserted. “A geological upheaval must have raised it from the surrounding land long ago, creating a natural fortress. The cliffs are described as being sheer and unclimbable. Life on top would have been cut off from the evolution of the world below.”

Several days later, they reached the coast of South America. Below them, the mouth of the Amazon River was a stupendous sight, a muddy sea of fresh water pushing miles out into the Atlantic. They turned inland, and the true nature of their quest became apparent.

An endless, uniform carpet of green stretched to every horizon. The jungle was a solid canopy, broken only by the brown, winding ribbons of rivers. From their height, it looked peaceful, beautiful, and utterly impenetrable.

“Begorra, it’s like a big green blanket,” Barney observed from the turret doorway. “How are we to find a wee little plateau in all that?”

“By following the river and trusting our instruments,” said Frank calmly. “It will be slow going. I will need to take the Ranger down to a lower altitude to keep the river in sight.”

The air ship descended to two hundred feet, skimming just above the treetops. Now they could see the jungle in its awesome detail. Great trees draped in vines, impossibly large flowers of brilliant color, and the flash of iridescent wings as flocks of macaws took flight, startled by the Ranger’s passage. The air grew thick and heavy, and the sounds of the jungle, a cacophony of squawks, screeches, and insectile buzzing, rose up to meet them.

They followed the winding Rio Negro for two days. The monotony of the green canopy was hypnotic and disorienting. It was on the evening of the second day that they met their first true challenge of the jungle.

They had set the Ranger down in a rare clearing on the riverbank to allow the dynamos to cool and to make observations. Frank and Dr. Vaneyke were examining a strange, orchid-like plant when Pomp let out a terrified yell from the deck of the ship.

“Lord amassy! Look at dat snake! It’s a monster!”

They turned to see a horrifying sight. A gigantic anaconda, thicker than a man’s body and of a length Frank could scarcely estimate, was slithering from the river’s edge directly toward the Ranger. Its head was raised, its forked tongue flicking, and its lidless eyes were fixed upon the gleaming metal hull of the ship.

“To the ship! Quickly!” Frank yelled.

They scrambled up the boarding ladder just as the colossal reptile reached the vessel. It did not seem to attack so much as to investigate, its massive head weaving back and forth. Then, with incredible speed, it began to wrap its coils around the Ranger’s mid-section.

“It thinks we are some new kind of prey!” cried the doctor.

“Saints preserve us! It’ll crush us loike an egg!” howled Barney.

The steel hull groaned under the immense pressure. Frank, however, was already at the control panel. His face was set with grim determination.

“The serpent is a creature of muscle and water,” he said, more to himself than to the others. “It will be an excellent conductor.”

He threw a large switch on the panel marked ‘External Hull Defense.’ A deep hum vibrated through the ship, and a faint blue corona of St. Elmo’s fire danced along the metal plates.

The effect on the anaconda was instantaneous and dramatic. The moment the electric charge hit it, the creature’s mighty coils convulsed violently. It let out a great, explosive hiss that sounded like a steam engine letting off pressure, and threw itself backward, untangling from the ship in a single, spastic motion. It thrashed on the riverbank for a moment, then slid back into the dark water and was gone.

A silence fell over the crew inside the turret.

“Well,” said Dr. Vaneyke, breaking the silence. “That was a most effective demonstration of your new defense, Frank.”

“Golly,” whispered Pomp, peering out the viewport. “I think dat snake has learned his lesson about messin’ wit’ de Ranger.”

“Indeed,” said Frank, his composure unshaken. “Let this be a lesson to us as well. This jungle does not welcome visitors. From now on, we stay in the air until we reach the plateau.”

CHAPTER III.
THE GUARDIANS OF THE PLATEAU.

With the electric dynamos fully recharged and their nerves settled, the crew of the Ranger once more took to the sky. They maintained a low altitude, the better to follow the twisting course of the river. The jungle below continued to present a uniform and baffling face, but Frank’s faith in the old Spanish manuscript never wavered.

On the morning of the fourth day, Dr. Vaneyke, who was at the forward viewport with a spyglass, let out a sudden cry.

“Frank! Look there! To the west!”

Frank rushed to his side. There, on the far horizon, rising like a colossal wall from the green sea of the jungle, was a line of reddish cliffs. It was a plateau, exactly as Coronado had described it.

“We’ve found it!” Frank ejaculated, his voice filled with triumph. “Barney, Pomp, come and see!”

The two faithful companions hurried to the turret. As the Ranger sped onward, the plateau grew in the viewports until it filled their entire field of vision. It was a breathtaking and intimidating sight. The cliffs rose at least two thousand feet, sheer and vertical, their rock faces worn smooth by ages of wind and rain. Waterfalls tumbled from the summit in long, silvery threads, disappearing into the jungle below.

“Begorra, it’s a fortress made by God himself,” Barney said in an awed whisper. “How did that Spanish fella ever get up there?”

“He did not,” said Frank, consulting the manuscript again. “He wrote that he and his men tried for a month to find a way up and failed. They were forced to turn back. We, however, have the Ranger.”

Frank took the wheel and aimed the ship’s nose upward. He engaged the main gyroscopic lifters, and the air ship began to ascend steadily along the face of the massive cliff. The jungle fell away below them, and the scale of the rock wall became even more apparent.

They were about halfway to the summit when Pomp, who was looking down, suddenly pointed.

“Mistah Frank! Look down dar! People!”

Frank banked the Ranger slightly to get a better view. At the base of the cliff, nestled in a large clearing, was a village of primitive-looking huts. Dozens of small, dark-skinned figures were swarming out of the huts, pointing up at the Ranger and gesticulating wildly. They were armed with long spears and what looked like blowguns.

“A native tribe,” said Dr. Vaneyke. “Likely they have never seen a machine like this before. They must think we are some kind of spirit or god.”

Their assumption was quickly proven wrong. As the Ranger continued its ascent, a cloud of small darts suddenly rose from the village below. They flew with surprising speed and accuracy, but they were no match for the ship’s hull. The tiny projectiles pattered against the metal plates like a brief hailstorm and fell away harmlessly.

“Ha! They’re tryin’ to shoot us down with toothpicks!” Barney laughed.

“Do not underestimate them,” Frank warned. “Their darts are doubtless tipped with a powerful poison. It is fortunate for us that they cannot pierce our hull.”

They rose past the level of the attack, and soon they were soaring over the rim of the plateau. Frank brought the Ranger forward a few hundred yards before looking for a place to land.

The world they had entered was unlike anything they had ever seen. It was not the familiar jungle of the Amazon basin. Here, the vegetation was of a different order entirely. Giant ferns, like overgrown fiddleheads, stood thirty feet high. Trees with massive, pulpy trunks were topped with feathery leaves that looked like they belonged to a bygone era. The air was warm, humid, and filled with the scent of damp earth and strange, heavy blossoms.

“It is a different world,” Dr. Vaneyke breathed, his scientific curiosity overriding all other feelings. “It is the Mesozoic Era, preserved in a jar of stone.”

Frank found a relatively open space, a meadow of sorts covered in mossy ground and strange, low-lying red flowers. He carefully maneuvered the Ranger downward, the gyroscopes whining as they lowered the mighty ship. With a soft bump, the steel runners settled onto the alien soil.

Frank cut power to the main lifters, and a profound silence fell, broken only by the faint hum of the auxiliary dynamos and the strange, buzzing cries of unseen creatures.

“Well, gentlemen,” Frank said, a brilliant smile on his face. “We have arrived in the Lost World.”

CHAPTER IV.
A MEETING OF AGES.

For a long moment, no one spoke. They could only stare out of the thick crystal viewports at the primeval landscape that surrounded them. The very air seemed ancient and heavy with time.

“Let’s have a look outside,” Frank said, his excitement palpable. “Barney, Pomp, stay on watch. Dr. Vaneyke, will you accompany me? Bring your specimen case.”

“I would not miss it for the world, my boy,” the doctor replied, his eyes gleaming.

Frank armed himself with one of the new pneumatic rifles, and the doctor took another. After checking the exterior atmosphere for toxic gases, a precaution Frank’s genius had anticipated with an external sensor, they unsealed the main hatch and stepped out onto the plateau.

The ground was spongy under their feet. The scale of the plant life was incredible. A dragonfly with a wingspan of at least two feet buzzed past Frank’s head, and he instinctively ducked.

“Remarkable!” the doctor exclaimed, already stooping to examine a fern. “This is a species of Glossopteris! It has not been seen on Earth for two hundred and fifty million years!”

They had taken only a few steps from the safety of the Ranger when a sound reached them. It was a low, moaning call, and it was answered by another from further away. Then, through a grove of giant, tree-like cycads, a herd of creatures emerged.

They were colossal beasts, larger than any elephant, with long, sloping necks and tails, and a double row of great, bony plates running along their backs. Their small heads swung from side to side as they placidly tore leaves from the giant ferns.

“Stegosaurus!” Dr. Vaneyke whispered, his voice trembling with awe. “Frank, do you see? It is exactly as the paleontologists predicted!”

“They are magnificent,” Frank said softly, raising a small spyglass to his eye. “And they appear to be herbivores. They are paying us no mind.”

The herd, perhaps a dozen of the mighty creatures, ambled slowly across the meadow, their plated backs looking like a moving mountain range. The sight was so majestic, so utterly impossible, that the adventurers could only watch in stunned silence. This was the prize they had sought, proof that the age of reptiles had not entirely passed from the Earth.

Driven by the spirit of discovery, they decided to press on, moving cautiously away from the ship to explore a line of trees a few hundred yards distant. The doctor gathered leaf-samples and chipped off rock fragments, his scientific fervor making him almost forget the potential danger.

It was this very danger that found them.

They were nearing the edge of the treeline when a sudden, terrifying roar shattered the air. It was a sound of pure, savage power, a sound that chilled the blood. Out from behind a thicket of bizarre, fleshy trees burst a creature from a nightmare.

It was bipedal, standing nearly fifteen feet tall. Its massive hind legs were like pillars of muscle, and its smaller forelimbs were armed with wicked-looking claws. Its head was immense, a great block of bone and teeth, its gaping jaws revealing daggers of ivory six inches long. Its reptilian eyes, yellow and pitiless, fixed upon the two small figures in the open.

“Allosaurus!” screamed the doctor, his face pale with terror. “Run, Frank! Run for the ship!”

There was no need for the command. Frank was already sprinting, his heart pounding against his ribs. The monstrous carnivore let out another deafening roar and gave chase, its huge strides eating up the ground with horrifying speed.

Frank risked a glance over his shoulder. The beast was gaining on them. It was faster than any land animal he had ever imagined.

“Shoot at it!” Vaneyke panted, struggling to keep up.

Frank stopped, spun around, and raised the pneumatic rifle. He took a quick aim at the creature’s broad chest and fired. The electric bolt flew true and struck the dinosaur with a flash of blue light.

The Allosaurus jerked and let out a bellow of rage and surprise, but it did not fall. It shook its massive head as if annoyed by a fly, and then charged with renewed fury.

“It did not work!” cried Frank. “The hide is too thick! Run!”

They scrambled desperately toward the silver hull of the Ranger, which seemed impossibly far away. Pomp and Barney, alerted by the roars, were at the open hatch, their faces masks of horror.

“Hurry, Mistah Frank!” Pomp yelled. “He’s right behind yo’!”

The ground shook with the footfalls of the pursuing monster. Frank could feel its hot, foul breath on his neck. He and the doctor dove headfirst through the hatch just as the dinosaur’s massive jaws snapped shut where they had been a second before. The sound of teeth clicking on empty air was like the slamming of a steel trap.

Barney and Pomp slammed the heavy steel door and bolted it. A moment later, a tremendous impact shook the entire vessel, sending the men sprawling. The Allosaurus had thrown its full weight against the side of the Ranger.

A furious roaring and scratching commenced from outside. The beast was attacking the ship, clawing at the metal and ramming its head against the hull. The ship groaned and shuddered, but the plates held firm.

Frank scrambled to a viewport. The monster was in a frenzy, biting and tearing at the unyielding metal. Its fury was terrible to behold.

“Begorra, the divil will tear us to pieces!” cried Barney.

“Not while I have a say in it,” Frank said, his jaw set. He ran to the control panel and threw the same switch he had used against the anaconda.

The blue corona of electricity once more shimmered over the Ranger’s hull. The instant the Allosaurus touched the charged metal again, it let out a shriek of agony that was completely different from its roars of fury. It leaped back as if struck by lightning, its body convulsing. It stared at the gleaming ship with what looked like a glimmer of fear in its reptilian brain, then it turned and crashed back into the primeval forest, its retreat marked by the sound of splintering trees.

Silence returned to the small cabin, broken only by the heavy breathing of the four men. They were safe, but they were also prisoners.

Dr. Vaneyke sank onto a stool, wiping his brow. “Frank, my boy,” he said, his voice unsteady. “That was entirely too close. Your foresight in electrifying the hull has saved our lives twice over.”

Frank stood at the viewport, looking out at the alien world they had discovered. The sense of triumph was now mixed with a heavy dose of reality.

“We came here for adventure, and it appears we have found it in abundance,” he said, his characteristic confidence returning. “We are on the plateau, and it is everything the manuscript promised and more. But we are trapped in a world of monsters.”

Pomp peered out nervously. “What we gwine to do now, Mistah Frank?”

Frank turned from the viewport, and the old fire was back in his eyes. “Do?” he said with a grim smile. “We are going to study them, of course. We must devise a new plan. One that allows us to explore this incredible world, without ending up as an exhibit in a dinosaur’s digestive tract.”
