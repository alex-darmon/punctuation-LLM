By the time the Albatross settled upon the choppy waters of the Caribbean, the lifting-fans had ceased turning entirely and the electric lights on her deck went dark. The sharp report Arthur heard below was a sure sign of a disastrous breakdown. He rushed into the pilot-house and scrambled down the ladder. The vessel of the air floated well enough, but the steam-frigate was already pulling away from her.

The Harpy was making twelve knots an hour, and her crew let out a mocking howl when they saw the plight of the air-schooner. Now they would surely deliver Silas Blackwood to Jamaica and collect their handsome reward. In a matter of minutes, the steamer’s lights were a dim spark on the horizon.

Arthur struck a lucifer in the dark engine room and discovered the cause of the accident. The main drive chain for the fans had snapped. Its heavy links were scattered across the floor plates. All the lifting power of the Albatross was gone. She could not rise an inch until the chain was mended, and Arthur knew this would be a long and difficult task.

His chief engineer, a stout and capable man named Gus, came clambering down.

Jip and Horace, the other two members of the crew, were summoned. Horace cooked up a quick breakfast of salt pork and biscuits. After eating, the weary men took to their bunks.

Arthur took the first watch. He engaged the fans and the Albatross rose gracefully into the sky. The mended chain groaned and complained at first, but soon it settled into its rhythm and the engines ran smoothly once more. Keeping a sharp lookout for the steam-frigate, the young inventor set a course south toward Jamaica.

CHAPTER XII.
THE TRAIL OF SABOTAGE.

The Pegasus flew on, a silver speck against the vast blue canvas of the sky. Below, the coast of India slid by, a long, green ribbon of land fringed with white surf. The air was hot and humid, but at their altitude of two thousand feet, a steady breeze kept the decks of the airship comfortable.
Frank’s inspection of the engine room was thorough. He examined the new dynamo wheel, the shafts, the wiring, and the great electric engine that was the heart of the Pegasus. Everything appeared to be in perfect working order. There was no sign of tampering.
“He must not have had time to get down here before he fled the steamer,” Frank muttered to himself.
He then moved on, checking the supports for the great helices, the steering mechanism, and the integrity of the vessel’s aluminum hull. He found nothing amiss.
Pomp, having finished his duties securing the prisoner, found the inventor in one of the narrow corridors that ran the length of the ship.
“Masther Frank, suh, that man Murdock, he ain’t sayin’ a word. Just sittin’ in the dark.”
“Let him sit,” Frank replied. “As long as he stays there. Pomp, I want you to help me with something. We are going to institute a continuous watch, not just on deck, but below as well. One man must be in the engine room at all times.”
“Yis, suh. You think he’s gonna try somethin’?”
“I am certain of it. He is not the type to give up. He will look for any weakness, any opportunity.”
The day wore on without incident. The monotonous drone of the engine and the whir of the helices became a familiar background noise. Barney, Pomp, and Tom took turns at the helm and on watch. They served Murdock a simple meal of bread and water, which he accepted without a word, his eyes still burning with a cold fire.
As night fell once more, a brilliant canopy of stars emerged. Frank, feeling a bit more at ease, joined Tom Reynard in the main cabin for a late supper prepared by Pomp.
“At this speed, we should reach Bombay in another day and a half,” the inventor said, consulting a chart spread on the table.
“Good,” replied the detective. “The sooner we hand him over, the better I’ll feel. Having him on board is like carrying a live bomb.”
“I’ve taken every precaution I can think of,” Frank said. “The engine room is guarded, and his cabin is secure. There is little mischief he can do.”
He had scarcely spoken the words when a shrill, piercing alarm bell began to ring throughout the airship. It was an alarm Frank himself had designed, connected to the pressure gauges of the great gas-bags that gave the Pegasus its buoyancy.
“The gas-bags!” Frank cried, leaping to his feet. “We are losing pressure!”
He and Tom dashed out of the cabin and up a narrow ladder that led to the catwalks inside the vast, cavernous upper hull, where the multiple compartments of lifting gas were housed.
The air was thick with the strange, metallic scent of the hydrogen gas. The alarm was coming from the aft section. Armed with electric lanterns, they raced along the catwalk.
“Here!” Frank shouted, pointing his lantern’s beam upward.
On the silvery skin of the rearmost gas-bag, a dark, wet patch was spreading rapidly. From a tiny hole in its center, the lifting gas was hissing out.
“It’s been punctured!” Tom exclaimed. “But how?”
Frank scrambled up the maintenance ladder affixed to the bag’s support struts. He shone his light on the hole. Stuck to the fabric was a small, lead-lined disk.
“Look at this!” he cried. “It is a chemical device! Acid! It was designed to eat through the fabric after a set amount of time!”
“Murdock!” Tom snarled. “But how could he have placed it there? He has been locked up since he came aboard!”
“He must have done it when they brought him below!” Frank realized. “He was dragged right past the access hatch to this section. He must have slapped it on the wall in a split second while Barney and Pomp were manhandling him through the door. The scoundrel planned this from the very beginning!”
The Pegasus, losing lift at its stern, was already beginning to tilt downwards at a dangerous angle.
“No time to talk!” Frank yelled. “We have to patch it! Get the emergency kit!”
Tom scrambled to a nearby locker and returned with a box containing sealant patches and adhesive. The work was perilous. Clinging to the ladder with one hand, Frank had to clean the area around the hissing hole and apply the sticky, foul-smelling patch, while Tom held the light and handed him the tools. The escaping gas made them dizzy, and the tilting of the ship threatened to send them plunging to the catwalk below.
Meanwhile, in the pilot-house, Barney fought the controls.
“She’s not respondin’!” he yelled to Pomp, who was on watch with him. “The stern is dropping like a stone!”
“We’re falling, Masther Barney!” the coon cried, his eyes fixed on the altimeter. The needle was dropping steadily. Five hundred feet… four hundred… three hundred…
Below them, the dark, churning waters of the Arabian Sea seemed to rush up to meet them.
Frank finally managed to press the patch firmly over the hole. The hissing sound diminished, then stopped.
“It’s holding!” he gasped, his lungs burning.
But the damage was done. The Pegasus had lost a significant amount of its lifting gas. She was heavy in the air, and her stern still sagged.
“Masther Frank!” Barney’s voice came shouting up the voice tube. “I can’t get her nose up! And there’s a ship, dead ahead! She’s burnin’ lights!”
Frank and Tom scrambled back down to the main deck and rushed to the pilot-house. Frank grabbed his spyglass and peered out into the darkness.
A steamer was bearing down on them, its red and green running lights bright against the blackness. As he focused the glass, a cold dread filled him. He recognized the vessel’s silhouette.
“It’s the Rover!” he said, his voice grim.
“The Rover?” Tom exclaimed. “What are they doing all the way out here?”
“They must have seen us capture Murdock,” Frank deduced rapidly. “They saw us head northeast, and simply followed our course. And now they find us crippled and falling from the sky! They have come to take their passenger back!”
As if to confirm his words, a bright flash erupted from the bow of the approaching steamer. A moment later, the sharp report of a cannon shot echoed across the water. A shell whistled high over their heads.
“They’re firing on us!” Barney yelled.
“They mean to sink us and rescue Murdock!” Frank said, his mind racing. “We cannot fight them in this condition, and we cannot escape!”
The Rover fired again, this time the shot was closer, sending a spray of water up a hundred yards from the struggling airship.
“Begorra, we’re sittin’ ducks!” cried Barney.
Suddenly, a new sound was heard from below decks. It was a frantic, metallic banging.
Pomp’s eyes went wide.
“Masther Frank! That’s the door to Murdock’s cabin! He’s trying to break out!”
The villain, hearing the cannon fire and feeling the ship’s distress, had evidently concluded that his rescuers were at hand. He was trying to break free in the ensuing chaos.
“They are trying to disable us so they can board!” Frank realized. “And Murdock knows it!”
The Pegasus, now only a hundred feet above the waves, wallowed helplessly. The Rover was less than half a mile away and closing fast. On her deck, the crew could be seen preparing to fire again. Frank saw that their situation was desperate. They were out of options.
“There is only one thing to do,” he said, his face a mask of grim determination. “They want their man back? They shall have him.”
He turned to the detective. “Tom, go below. You and Pomp, bring Murdock up to the deck. And bring a rope.”
“What are you going to do?” Tom asked, baffled.
“I am going to make a trade,” Frank replied, his eyes flashing. “A prisoner for our survival!”

Tom Reynard and Pomp did not wait for a second command. They plunged down the companionway ladder into the hold of the airship. The frantic banging from within Martin Murdock’s prison had grown louder, a desperate, rhythmic pounding that echoed through the metallic hull of the Pegasus.

“He sounds like a wild animal in a cage!” the detective muttered, drawing his revolver.

“He is a wild animal, Mistah Tom!” Pomp replied, his own hands balling into powerful fists. “And we’s the keepers!”

They reached the stout metal door of the cabin that served as Murdock’s cell. The door was visibly dented outward from the force of the blows. The villain was likely using a piece of furniture as a battering ram.

“Stand aside, Pomp!” Tom ordered.

He did not bother with the lock. Bracing himself, he threw his full weight against the door, smashing it inward. The detective stumbled into the small cabin, catching himself before he fell. Martin Murdock, a great bear of a man with a wild look in his eyes, was holding the remnants of a metal stool. He had been about to strike the door again.

Seeing his captors, he let out a savage roar and lunged, not at Tom, but at the open doorway, seeking to escape onto the deck.

He had not counted on Pomp. The powerful coon met his charge with a low tackle that would have done a football player proud. The two men went down in a heap, Murdock cursing and flailing, Pomp grimly hanging on.

“Tie him up!” Tom Reynard cried, holstering his pistol and grabbing the length of rope they had brought.

It was a furious struggle. Murdock was a man possessed, his strength fueled by the proximity of his allies and the promise of freedom. He thrashed and kicked, but Pomp’s grip was like iron. Together, the detective and the brawny sailor managed to subdue the rascal, binding his arms tightly behind his back and looping the rope securely around his chest.

“My friends on the Rover will make you pay for this!” Murdock snarled, his chest heaving. “They’ll send you and this flying contraption to the bottom!”

“You talk too much,” Tom said coolly, pulling a knot tight. “Let’s get him topside.”

They half dragged, half carried the struggling prisoner up the ladder to the deck. The scene that greeted them was one of imminent peril. The Rover had closed to within a quarter of a mile. The dark water was churned white by its prow, and its decks were crowded with sailors, many of them holding rifles. The main cannon on its bow was being reloaded with grim efficiency.

The Pegasus, for her part, was a wounded bird. She floated barely a hundred feet above the waves, sagging pitifully at the stern where the patch had been applied. Her helices turned just enough to keep her from falling completely, but any real forward motion was impossible.

“Just in time!” Frank cried, his face pale but resolute in the glow of the deck lights. “Barney, turn the searchlight on him!”

“Aye, aye, sor!” the Irishman replied.

A powerful beam of white light shot out from the pilot-house, cutting through the night and illuminating the captive, Murdock. He blinked and cursed in the sudden glare, a perfect target for all to see.

Frank raised a speaking trumpet to his lips. His voice, clear and steady, carried across the water.

“Ahoy, the Rover! Cease your fire! Your passenger is on our deck!”

A voice, thick with anger and greed, bellowed back from the steamer. It was the captain.

“So he is! Hand him over, Reade, or we’ll sink you where you float!”

“You are in no position to make demands, captain!” Frank retorted. “You want your man? You shall have him. But on my terms! Call off your gunners and stand off a quarter mile. One more shot from your cannon, and Martin Murdock goes for a swim in the Arabian Sea! And I assure you, he will not float!”

There was a pause. On the deck of the Rover, the captain could be seen conferring with his men. They pointed toward the Pegasus, then back at the bound figure of Murdock. The promise of one hundred dollars a man was a powerful motivator, but a dead man could not pay his debts.

Murdock himself seemed to realize the terrible predicament he was in. His bravado vanished, replaced by a look of sheer terror.

“Don’t listen to him!” he screamed toward the ship. “He’s bluffing! He wouldn’t dare! Sink them! Sink them now!”

Frank ignored the prisoner’s frantic outburst. He turned to Barney. “Keep that searchlight steady on him, Barney. And have your rifle ready.”

To Tom, he said, “Get ready to lower him. But not yet.”

The captain of the Rover had apparently made his decision. His voice boomed across the water again, though this time it held a note of grudging compliance.

“Blast your eyes, you flying devil! All right! We have a deal! But if this is a trick, I’ll send every man on this ship to Davy Jones’s locker along with you!”

“There is no trick, captain,” Frank called back. “Simply a trade. Now, stand off as I commanded! And lower a boat!”

The Rover’s engines churned, and the steamer slowly began to back away, its wake washing against the wounded airship. Frank watched its position carefully through his spyglass. When it had reached a satisfactory distance, a boat was lowered from its davits, manned by four burly sailors.

“Now!” Frank commanded. “Let’s give them their prize.”

Tom and Pomp hauled the sputtering, cursing Murdock to the rail. The villain struggled mightily, his feet scrabbling for purchase on the deck.

“I’ll see you all hang for this, Reade!” he shrieked. “You and your whole crew!”

“Be quiet, you rogue!” Tom Reynard grunted, shoving him against the railing.

The boat from the Rover rowed cautiously toward the Pegasus. It was a perilous operation. The airship, barely stable, rose and fell on the air currents, while the boat below bobbed on the ocean swell.

“Easy now!” Frank coached. “Pay out the rope slowly. Pomp, you hold him steady. Barney, keep your rifle on that boat. If they so much as look at us wrong, you know what to do.”

“Trust me, Masther Frank,” the Irishman said grimly, his eye sighted down the barrel of his rifle. “They’ll be gettin’ more than a look back.”

With Pomp’s strong arms holding Murdock’s weight, Tom began to lower the villain over the side. Murdock, suspended in midair, let out a howl of fear and rage. He twisted and writhed on the end of the rope, trying to unbalance his captors, but Pomp’s grip was like a vise.

Down below, the sailors in the boat used their oars to keep their position directly under the dangling man.

“Lower away!” one of them shouted.

Slowly, carefully, foot by foot, Murdock was lowered toward the sea. The sailors reached up, grabbing his legs and torso, and hauled his bulk unceremoniously into their boat. The moment his weight was off the line, one of them cut the rope with a knife.

Murdock was free of the Pegasus.

He scrambled to his feet in the small boat, turning his face, contorted with hate, up toward the airship.

“You’ll regret this, Frank Reade!” he screamed. “I’ll hunt you to the ends of the earth! You’ll never be safe!”

“Row!” a sailor commanded, and the boat began to pull away, heading back toward the waiting steamer.

Frank did not wait to watch their progress. The instant Murdock was clear, he spun around.

“Barney!” he yelled. “To the pilot-house! Give the helices everything they’ve got! Get us altitude! Now!”

“On me way, sor!”

The Irishman scrambled for the controls. Frank knew the Rover’s captain was a treacherous man. A promise made to an enemy was a promise not meant to be kept.

His instincts proved correct.

The boat had barely reached the side of the Rover when a flash of orange lit up the steamer’s bow. The cannon roared once more!

But Frank had anticipated the betrayal. The helices of the Pegasus were already whining, clawing for purchase in the air. The sluggish airship lurched upward and to the side. A geyser of water erupted from the spot where they had been floating only seconds before, so close that the spray washed over the deck.

“They’re firing again!” Tom shouted, pointing.

The Rover’s crew, enraged at the escape of their prey, were firing their rifles now. Bullets whizzed past the deck-house, pinging and ricocheting off the metal hull.

“Go, Barney, go!” Frank urged, as if his will alone could make the ship fly faster.

The new dynamo wheel, still stiff from its recent installation, groaned under the strain, but it held. The helices bit into the air, slowly, painfully lifting the damaged ship. They were not rising fast, but they were rising. And they were moving forward.

The Rover, a vessel of the sea, could only watch. Her captain screamed impotent curses as the Pegasus, the miraculous engine of the clouds, limped away into the darkness of the pre-dawn sky. They fired the cannon one last time, but the shot fell far short, a final, angry splash in the dark waters.

For a long time, no one spoke. The only sounds were the hum of the machinery and the whistle of the wind. The Pegasus, though safe from the Rover, was still in grave danger. She flew heavily, losing altitude with every passing moment that the helices were not at full power.

Frank, Tom, Barney, and Pomp gathered in the pilot-house. The strain of the last hour was etched on their faces.

“Begorra, that was a close one, Masther Frank,” Barney said, wiping a sleeve across his sweaty brow. “I thought for shure we were goners.”

“We were,” Frank said grimly. “If we had stayed another ten seconds, that last shot would have been the end of us.”

“And we let Murdock get away,” Tom Reynard said, the frustration clear in his voice. “After all this, he’s free.”

“We had no choice, Tom,” Frank replied, placing a hand on the detective’s shoulder. “It was him or us. We saved the Pegasus, and we are all alive to tell the tale. That is a victory in itself.”

“The young inventor is right,” Pomp added, his voice a low rumble. “That man Murdock, he’s bad, but the Pegasus is our home. We live to fight another day.”

Frank nodded, his eyes already scanning the gauges and instruments that lined the pilot-house. “Pomp is right. But our first fight is with gravity. This patch on the gas-bag is only temporary, and we’ve lost too much hydrogen to stay aloft for long. We are too heavy. We must find a place to land, and soon.”

He unrolled a chart across the main console. The light of the binnacle illuminated the coast of Arabia and the vast, empty expanse of the sea.

“According to my calculations, we are here,” he said, his finger tapping a spot on the map. “There are no friendly ports for hundreds of miles. But…” His finger traced a line to the southeast. “My charts show a small group of islands here. The Kuria Muria Islands. They are mostly barren rock, uninhabited. But they are land. We can set down there, make proper repairs to the gas-bag, and generate new hydrogen with the electrolyzer.”

“An island, is it?” Barney said, his spirits seeming to lift at the prospect of solid ground. “Shure, a bit of rock is better than a bit of water to land on.”

“Then that is our course,” Frank declared. He took the wheel from Barney. “The rest of you should get some sleep. We have a long night and a long day ahead of us.”

The Pegasus flew on through the remaining hours of darkness. The first rays of dawn found the airship limping low over the waves, her shadow a fleeting specter on the blue water. Frank, with his inventor’s stamina, remained at the wheel, his eyes scanning the horizon.

Just after sunrise, a cry came from Pomp, who had climbed to the lookout post on the roof of the deck-house.

“Land ho! Land on the starboard bow!”

A thrill of relief ran through the weary crew. Frank grabbed his spyglass. On the horizon, a series of jagged, brown peaks rose from the sea, stark and unwelcoming under the morning sun. It was the Kuria Muria Islands.

“There it is, my friends,” Frank said. “Our sanctuary.”

He steered the Pegasus toward the largest of the islands. It was a desolate place, a mass of granite and sandstone, without a tree or a blade of grass to be seen. A high central peak was surrounded by cliffs that dropped precipitously to the sea. But on the leeward side, Frank spotted a small, sandy cove, a crescent of white beach protected from the wind.

“That will be our landing place,” he announced. “It’s sheltered and hidden from the open sea. We can work there without fear of being spotted.”

Bringing the crippled airship down was a task that required all of Frank’s skill. With so little lift, he had to use the helices to control their descent, feathering the power with a delicate touch. Barney and Tom stood ready with the grappling lines and anchors.

Slowly, the great machine settled toward the beach. With a final, gentle bump, the keel of the Pegasus touched the sand.

“We’re down!” Barney shouted in triumph. “Safe and sound on terra firma!”

The crew scrambled out onto the deck, their relief palpable. They had made it. For the first time in weeks, they stood on solid ground, the warm sun on their faces and the soft sand under their feet.

But their work was far from over. The first order of business was to inspect the damage. The hole in the gas-bag was worse than they thought. The temporary patch had held, but the fabric around it was torn and stressed.

“This will require a full day’s work, at least,” Frank said, after a thorough examination. “We will have to deflate the entire stern section, cut out the damaged panel, and sew in a new one from our reserve silk. Then, we must re-coat it for gas-proofing and begin the process of generating hydrogen to refill it.”

It was a daunting task, but the crew set to it with a will. They were alive, and they had their marvelous ship. Pomp, who was an excellent cook, went to work setting up a camp kitchen to prepare a hearty breakfast, while Frank, Tom, and Barney began the laborious process of repairing the Pegasus.

As they worked, Frank’s mind was already racing ahead.

“Murdock thinks he has won,” he said to Tom, as they measured a length of silk. “He is on his way to San Francisco, believing us to be sunk or lost. He will grow complacent.”

“And you think we can catch him?” the detective asked.

“I know we can,” Frank replied, his jaw set. “The Rover is a slow steamer. Once the Pegasus is repaired, she will be as good as new. We can cross the ocean in a fraction of the time it takes that ship. We will be waiting for him in San Francisco. The law has a long arm, Tom, but the arm of science is longer still, and much, much faster.”

A smile touched Tom Reynard’s lips. The young inventor’s confidence was infectious. The chase was not over. It had only entered a new, more exciting phase.

As the sun climbed high over the barren island, the sounds of hammering, sewing, and cheerful whistling filled the small cove, a testament to the indomitable spirit of Frank Reade Jr. and his loyal crew. Their enemy had escaped, but their resolve was unbroken. The engine of the clouds would fly again.

He had gone to his lair, as Frank supposed, and was probably sleeping soundly. The young inventor had a strange feeling as he was thus drawn through the silent jungle. It was like being in another world. The air was hot and steamy, and was filled with the odor of decaying vegetation. Great ferns and strange-looking flowers grew in profusion, and brilliantly colored birds flitted from tree to tree. It was a beautiful but treacherous place. After proceeding in this way for about half an hour, Frank saw something that made his heart beat faster. It was a footprint in the soft earth, the print of a man’s boot. It was a fresh track, and could have been made only a few hours before. “Murdock’s!” he muttered. “He must be near.” He followed the tracks, which led him deeper and deeper into the jungle. The Pegasus was moved along slowly and cautiously. Frank’s eyes were fixed on the ground, and he did not notice that the rope by which he was suspended was getting frayed. It had been rubbing against the sharp edge of a rock, and was now worn nearly through. Suddenly, there was a snap, and the rope parted. Frank fell to the ground, a distance of twenty feet. He was stunned by the fall, but not seriously injured. He picked himself up and looked around. The Pegasus was floating away, and he was alone in the jungle. His first thought was of the tiger. He clutched his rifle and listened. But there was no sound to break the stillness of the forest. The tiger was still asleep. Frank knew that he must get out of the jungle as quickly as possible. But which way should he go? He had lost his compass in the fall, and was completely bewildered. He looked up at the sun, but it was now directly overhead, and gave him no clue to the direction. He was lost. For a moment a feeling of despair came over him. But he was not one to give up easily. He was young and strong and resourceful. He would find a way out of the jungle, or perish in the attempt. He started to walk, he knew not whither. He pushed his way through the dense undergrowth, tearing his clothes and scratching his hands and face. He was tormented by insects, and the heat was almost unbearable. But he kept on, spurred by the thought of the terrible death that awaited him if he remained in the jungle after nightfall. Barney and Pomp, in the meantime, had been horrified to see the rope part and Frank fall to the ground. They had immediately stopped the Pegasus and lowered a new rope. But Frank was nowhere to be seen. He had disappeared in the dense undergrowth. They called his name, but there was no answer. They were in despair. They knew that it would be madness for them to go down into the jungle in search of him. They would only lose their own lives without being of any service to him. There was only one thing to do, and that was to cruise about over the jungle in the hope of catching a glimpse of him. This they did, going back and forth, and scanning the ground below with anxious eyes. But the jungle was like a great green sea, and gave up no sign of the lost inventor. The afternoon wore away, and night began to fall. The two faithful fellows were in despair. They had given up all hope of ever seeing their young master again. Suddenly, Pomp, who had the sharpest eyes, gave a cry. “Look, Massa Barney! Look dar!” He pointed to a spot in the jungle where a thin column of smoke was rising. “Bejabers, it’s a signal!” cried Barney. “It’s Misther Frank! He’s alive!” They turned the Pegasus in the direction of the smoke, and in a few minutes were hovering over the spot. They saw Frank standing in a small clearing, waving his arms. He had managed to start a fire with a sunglass which he always carried, and the smoke had guided his friends to the spot. A rope was quickly lowered, and the young inventor was soon safe on board the Pegasus. He was weak and exhausted from his terrible experience, but he was happy. He had escaped from the tiger’s jaws. His friends crowded around him, eager to hear his story. He told them of his fall, and of his wanderings in the jungle. “It was a close call,” he said, in conclusion. “If you hadn’t seen my signal I don’t know what would have become of me.” “Faith, an’ it was a lucky thing yez had that sunglass,” said Barney. “It was indeed,” replied Frank. “But we are not out of the woods yet. “What’ll we do, Masther Frank?” asked Barney. “The Rover is still out there. Shure, the rest of her crew must have heard the commotion.”
Frank pointed toward the coast. Through a break in the trees, they could see the steamer. Even as they watched, they saw a puff of white smoke from her funnel.
“They are getting up steam,” the young inventor said. “They are abandoning their shipmates and making a run for it. They know their cause is lost.”
“And Murdock?” asked Tom Reynard.
“He has two choices,” Frank replied. “He can stay in this jungle and starve, or risk meeting that tiger again. Or he can try to make a break for it across the open ground between here and the next patch of woods. I am betting he will choose the latter.”
“Then we will have him!” cried Barney.
“Exactly,” said Frank. “Raise the Pegasus to five hundred feet. We will give him a little time to think he has gotten away. We will let him get out into the open, where there is no place to hide. Then we will drop down on him like a hawk on a sparrow.”
The plan was put into action. The airship rose swiftly and silently, becoming a mere speck against the brilliant blue of the Indian sky. Below, the jungle lay quiet and seemingly peaceful once more.
For what seemed like an eternity, nothing happened. The sun beat down. The Rover steamed slowly away from the coast, heading for the open sea.
Then, a figure emerged cautiously from the edge of the jungle. It was Martin Murdock. He looked around wildly, saw the departing steamer, and seemed to curse. He looked up at the empty sky. Seeing nothing, he must have concluded that the Pegasus had given up the chase and followed the steamer.
A look of grim resolve came over his face. He bent low and began to run across the stretch of open, sandy ground that separated the jungle from a distant line of hills.
From his high perch, Frank Reade Jr. watched him through the spy-glass.
“There he goes,” he said softly. “Just as I predicted. He is ours now.”
He turned to his faithful crew.
“Barney, Pomp, Mr. Reynard,” he said, a smile of triumph touching his lips for the first time that day. “Let us go and collect our passenger.”
