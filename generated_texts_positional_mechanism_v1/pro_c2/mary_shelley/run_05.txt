My days were passed in rapt attention to the new inhabitants of the house, whose presence had become the sole measure of my time. The man, whose temples were silvered with care, I soon learned was named Alaric; his daughter, a creature of light and tender years, was called Elara. His voice, a deep and resonant timbre, would fill the parlour with tales not of empires or kings, but of the stars. He spoke of suns born from nebulae, of worlds of ice and fire, and of the vast, silent oceans of space that separated these celestial bodies. He explained the laws of gravity, by which a planet is held in its course, and the nature of light, which travels for an aeon only to fall upon the page of a book or the eye of a curious child. From these lessons, I, who had no memory of a life before this spectral existence, began to piece together a concept of the universe.

These cosmic histories engendered in me a profound and aching solitude. Was the world, then, so full of wonders, of innumerable spheres turning in a silent, magnificent dance, whilst I remained a prisoner in this one decaying shell of a house? I saw in my mind’s eye the beauty of which he spoke, the swirling colours of a nascent galaxy, the cold fire of a distant star; and yet, I could not feel the sun’s warmth nor the night’s chill. I was a consciousness untethered to a body, a witness to a life in which I could take no part.

While my mind expanded with these new and wondrous ideas, my own condition became a source of ever greater torment. I saw the gentle manner in which the father would place a shawl upon his daughter’s shoulders when a draught stirred the air; I observed the shared smiles that passed between them, a language of affection I could comprehend but never speak. They possessed a world of sensation, of touch and taste and scent, from which I was entirely excluded. What was the sweetness of the summer air, or the comfort of a warm hearth? These were but words to me, symbols of a reality I could only perceive through the veil of others’ experience.

The question of my own nature began to haunt my thoughts. I learned that mortals were beings of warm flesh and beating hearts, possessed of a voice to speak and hands to build. They were born, they lived, and they died, their existence a brief but vibrant flame. But what was I? I had no flesh to be wounded, no heart to be broken, though I felt a sorrow so deep it seemed to shake my very essence. I had no voice to ask the questions that plagued me. No father had taught me of the stars; no mother had wrapped me in a tender embrace. Was I, then, but a lingering echo, a memory trapped within these walls, less real than the most distant, dying star?

I cannot convey to you the misery these reflections caused me; I longed to dissolve, to cease this painful consciousness, but I had no means to do so. Oh, wretched is the mind that learns of warmth only to know its own eternal cold! Knowledge, I found, was a cruel mirror, showing me not what I was, but all that I could never be. I adored the goodness of Alaric and the bright spirit of Elara, but I knew I was forever barred from their society, a ghost in their home, more lonely than if I had been utterly alone. Their joys were not for me; their sorrows I could share in spirit, but I could offer no comfort. Miserable, unhallowed shade!

A deeper lesson was soon to be impressed upon me. I overheard them speaking of the past, of a wife and mother lost to a sudden fever, of the life they had been forced to leave behind. I saw that man was a being of a most strange and contradictory nature; that he was at once capable of the most sublime virtues and the most atrocious crimes. I admired the courage and the patriotism of the ancient Romans, but I detested the ambition which led them to enslave the world. I wept over the fate of the gentle Americans, but I felt my soul glow with indignation against their cruel and perfidious conquerors.

Every day I learned something new, and every new acquisition of knowledge was a fresh source of wonder and of pain. I learned that the human race was divided into nations and tribes, each with its own peculiar language, customs, and laws. I heard of the distinctions of rank and of the immense disparity of wealth which prevailed in every civilized country. I was told of the power of kings and nobles, of the pride of ancestry, and of the contempt with which the highborn and the wealthy regarded the poor and the obscure.

These discoveries produced in me a feeling of profound melancholy. I saw that I was a creature of a different order from all other beings. I was not a man, for I was destitute of all those qualities which are considered as the badges of humanity. I had no country, no kindred, no friends. I was alone in the world, a solitary and accursed being. My person was monstrous, my stature gigantic. What did this mean? Who was I? What was my origin? What was my destiny? These questions perpetually recurred to my mind, but I was unable to solve them.

The agony of these reflections was at times almost unbearable. I would fly from the society of men and seek for solitude in the deepest recesses of the forest. There I would give vent to my anguish in groans and lamentations which seemed to shake the very foundations of the earth. I cursed the day of my creation; I cursed the author of my being. I longed for death, but I knew not how to die.

One day, as I was wandering in the woods, I found a leathern portmanteau containing some books and clothes. Plutarch taught me to admire and to love the heroes of past ages. I imagined our meeting with a thousand different variations, each more terrible than the last. I would confront him, not as a supplicant, but as an avenging power. I would force him to acknowledge the wretch he had created and to account for the suffering he had so casually inflicted.

One day, as I was skirting a thick wood that bordered a swift-flowing river, I perceived a young girl, a child of perhaps four or five years, playing near the bank. She was chasing a butterfly, her laughter echoing like silver bells in the clear air. For a moment I paused, concealed amongst the trees, and a strange feeling, a ghost of my former self, stirred within me. She was a being of such innocence and beauty, a perfect flower of that humanity from which I was forever exiled. As I watched, her foot slipped on a wet stone at the river’s edge, and with a small cry, she tumbled into the rushing current. Without a moment’s thought, my limbs acted of their own accord. I sprang from my hiding place, dashed into the frigid water, and seized the child before she was swept away. With a few powerful strokes I reached the opposite bank and laid her gently on the grass. She was insensible, but I could feel the faint flutter of her heart, and I began to chafe her hands and limbs to restore her circulation.

At that instant, a man, doubtless the child’s father or some rustic labourer, rushed from the woods. Upon seeing me bending over the girl, he did not pause to comprehend the scene; he saw only my monstrous form, and his face contorted with terror and rage. He uttered a loud cry, and before I could make any gesture of peace or explanation, he raised a gun which he carried and fired. A sensation of searing, agonizing pain shot through my shoulder. I staggered back, a howl of surprise and misery escaping my lips. The man snatched up the child, who was now beginning to stir, and fled with her into the depths of the forest, casting back glances of unmitigated horror.

I fell to the ground, my whole being convulsed with a pain that was as much of the soul as of the body. I had saved a life! I had performed an act of selfless virtue, the very kind of act I had once admired in the histories read by Felix. And what was my reward? A bullet; a fresh wound to add to the countless wounds already inflicted upon my spirit. This, then, was the justice of mankind! This was their response to a being who, despite their cruelty, had yet found it within him to show compassion. I lay there for some time, the cold snow soaking my garments and the warm blood trickling from my wound, and I swore an oath of eternal hatred and vengeance against all mankind. The last vestiges of any gentle feeling were extinguished in my heart, replaced by a gall of bitterness and a craving for revenge that was now absolute and unquenchable. When I had recovered some strength, I tore a strip from my clothing to bind my wound and rose to my feet, my purpose clearer and more terrible than ever before. My creator was the ultimate source of my torment, and it was upon him, and those he held dear, that my vengeance would fall.

The pain of my wound, which festered and was slow to heal, added a physical torment to my mental anguish and served as a constant, burning reminder of my last, failed attempt to connect with humanity. My journey was now slower, my nights filled with feverish dreams and my days with a sullen, brooding fury. All thoughts of reasoning with my creator, of appealing to his sense of justice, were gone. I would not ask for his pity; I would command his terror. He would learn what it was to feel despair, to see that which he loved destroyed before his eyes.

After many more weeks of painful travel, I at last approached the environs of Geneva. The sight of the ordered fields, the scattered villas, and the distant gleam of the lake filled me with a dark and thrilling anticipation. This was the place of my origin; this was the nest of my persecutor. For several days I hid myself in the woods on the slopes of Mont Salêve, which overlooked the city, observing the comings and goings of the inhabitants. I was a serpent in their Eden, unseen and full of venom, waiting for the opportune moment to strike. My wound had at last begun to close, though the muscles of my shoulder remained stiff and sore, a perpetual memento of human gratitude.

One evening, as twilight was descending, I wandered through the fields closer to the city walls. My attention was caught by a child, a boy of exquisite beauty, with curling hair and a countenance of cherubic innocence. He was playing with a companion, and his joyful shouts seemed to pierce my heart with a pang of exquisite jealousy. Here was everything I was not: beautiful, loved, and happy, existing without a care in a world that had nothing but scorn for me. A thought, black and venomous, began to form in my mind. This child belonged to the race of my enemies. He was one of those who would have shrieked and fled at my sight. His very existence was an insult to my own.

As I watched from the shadows, the boy’s companion was called away, and he was left alone. He seemed to be searching for something he had lost amongst the grass. This was my moment. A fiendish inspiration seized me. I would take this child, this perfect specimen of humanity, and keep him with me. I would educate him to be my companion and my friend; he would be taught to look past my deformity and to love me for the qualities of my mind. His infant mind would be a blank slate, free from the prejudices of his elders.

With this wild and desperate plan in mind, I stepped from my concealment and approached him. At the sight of me, the boy did not flee, but stood his ground, a look of childish surprise upon his face. When I drew nearer, however, his expression changed to one of distaste. He covered his eyes with his hands and began to cry.

“Monster! Ugly wretch! Let me go! My papa is a Syndic, he is M. Frankenstein, he will punish you. You dare not keep me.”

Frankenstein! The name fell upon my ears like the thunderclap of doom. This was not merely one of my enemies; this was the kin of my arch-enemy, my creator, my tormentor! My blood ran cold, and then hot with a rage more potent and terrible than any I had yet experienced. The desperate hope of finding a companion was instantly annihilated, and in its place rose a monstrous and irresistible impulse.

“Frankenstein!” I cried, my voice a hoarse and terrible whisper. “You belong to him, do you? It is to him, then, that I will send my first message!”

The child struggled in my grasp, still screaming his threats and insults. His words were like salt in my wounds, reminding me of all the injustice, all the loathing I had endured. A hellish fury took possession of my faculties. I had meant only to seize him, but his struggles and the sound of that hated name drove me to a frenzy. My hands closed around his throat, not to silence him, but to extinguish the life that animated this vessel of my enemy’s blood. He writhed for a moment, and then his body went limp in my arms. I looked down at the beautiful, lifeless face, and a feeling of exultant horror filled my breast. I had done it. I had committed my first act of murder. The war I had declared was no longer a matter of words and feelings; it was now written in the blood of an innocent.

I gazed for a long moment at my victim, a strange calm descending upon me. The deed was done, and there was no remorse, only a grim sense of finality. I felt a power I had never known before, the power to create misery, to make others feel a fraction of the agony that was my constant companion. As I looked upon the boy’s still form, I noticed a locket hanging about his neck. I opened it and beheld the portrait of a most lovely woman. Her face was full of a gentle and benevolent sweetness that reminded me of all I had lost, of the goodness from which I was forever barred. A fresh wave of bitterness washed over me. Why should this inanimate object possess such beauty while I was a thing of horror? Why should others know the love of such a creature, while I was doomed to solitude and despair? A malicious thought entered my mind. This portrait would be the instrument of further mischief. I took the locket from the child’s neck and pocketed it. Then, I lifted the small, lifeless body and carried it to a thicket of trees, where I laid it upon a bed of damp leaves. Let my creator find him there. Let him gaze upon the work of the wretch he had brought into the world. The act of revenge had begun, and I knew, with a terrible and certain knowledge, that it would not end here. The taste of it was bitter, yet it was the only sustenance my starved and blackened soul could now accept.

I journeyed from that accursed spot with a speed that defied the uneven ground and the deepening twilight. My mind was a whirlwind of new and terrible sensations; the grim satisfaction of my act warred with the primal instinct to flee, not from the justice of men, for I knew that was a concept from which I was forever excluded, but from the scene of my own monstrousness. The darkness of the night descended, but it could not rival the blackness that had taken root in my soul. I did not rest, but pressed onward, my powerful limbs carrying me far from the environs of Geneva, towards the wild and untamed places where I might find a temporary refuge from the eyes of man. It was in my flight that I stumbled upon a barn, detached from any nearby dwelling, and sought its shelter more from a habit of concealment than from any true fatigue. The scent of hay and dry earth was a strange comfort, a reminder of the simple, peaceful existence of the cottagers, a life I had observed with such longing and from which I was now irrevocably severed by my own hand.

I climbed into the loft, burying myself in the deep, fragrant straw, and lay there, my senses on high alert. It was here, in the profound silence of the countryside, that I allowed myself to examine the locket I had taken. By the faint light of the moon that filtered through a crack in the wooden wall, I gazed again upon the miniature. The woman’s countenance was a portrait of maternal love and gentle virtue; her eyes seemed to look out from the tiny frame with a serene and loving expression that tore at my heart. This, I thought with a surge of venomous grief, was the face of a mother. This was the love that Frankenstein had known, the caress he had felt, the source from which his own being had sprung, nurtured and cherished. And what was my genesis? A convulsive spasm in a workshop of filthy creation, an assemblage of parts brought to a horrid life by a spark of stolen fire, only to be abandoned in disgust by the very hand that formed me. A bitter laugh, a sound more like a strangled sob, escaped my lips.

My dark reflections were interrupted by a sound from below. I froze, my body instantly rigid, my ears straining in the darkness. The barn door creaked open, and a soft light spilled upon the dusty floor. A figure entered, and I perceived it to be that of a young woman, holding a lantern. She appeared agitated, her face pale and streaked with tears. She moved about the barn, calling a name in a low, trembling voice. “William? William, where are you?” she cried, her words muffled by anguish. I recognized the name with a jolt that was almost electric. This must be a servant or relation, one of the search party whose desperate calls I had heard echoing in the distance as I made my escape. She sank down upon a pile of straw not far from the ladder to my loft, and, extinguishing her lantern, she gave herself over to a fit of weeping. The sound of her sorrow, so pure and so profound, was a torment to my ears.

I watched her for a long time as she slept, her exhausted frame having finally succumbed to its grief. Her features in repose were lovely and innocent, and a strange feeling, a shadow of the admiration I had felt for the sleeping cottagers, passed over me. Here was another of those beings I so desperately wished to join, another creature of grace and feeling from whom I was separated by an impassable gulf. For a moment, a pang of something akin to pity touched my cold heart. She was innocent of any crime against me; her only fault was her association with the world of man, the world that had declared me a monster.

But then, my eyes fell again upon the locket I still held in my hand, and the momentary softness in my breast was instantly consumed by a new and diabolical inspiration. The promptings of my earlier malice returned with tenfold force. I had sworn to visit misery upon my creator; I had shed the blood of his kin. But what if I could do more? What if I could turn his own world, with its cherished laws and its pretense of justice, into an instrument of his torture? This sleeping girl, this innocent, would be the means. Her beauty, her seeming virtue, would make her fall all the more terrible, and the injustice of her fate would be a poison poured directly into the heart of the Frankenstein family. They would see a monster where there was none, and in so doing, they would become monsters themselves.

With the stealth of a serpent, I descended from the loft. The floorboards did not creak beneath my weight. I approached the sleeping form, my shadow engulfing her like a shroud. Her breathing was soft and even, her face a mask of sorrowful peace. For an instant I hesitated, my hand hovering over her. Was there no other way? The question was a fool’s errand; my path was set. My desire for vengeance was an infernal fire that demanded fuel, and this girl’s life, her reputation, her very soul, would serve as the next offering. Carefully, so as not to wake her, I unclasped her dress at the neck and slipped the golden chain inside, nestling the locket into the folds of her bodice. It lay there against her warm skin, a glittering token of a crime she did not commit, a brand of guilt that would lead her to ignominy and death. I gazed upon my work, a chill and terrible satisfaction filling me. Let them find it. Let them draw their own conclusions and enact their blind, brutal justice. I had planted the seed of ruin, and I would watch from afar as it grew into a great and terrible tree, casting its shadow over all that my creator held dear. I retreated from the barn as silently as I had entered, leaving the sleeping girl to her doom, and turned my face once more towards the mountains.

My journey to the valley of Chamonix and the sublime glaciers that crowned its heights was a long and solitary pilgrimage. I travelled only by night, subsisting on berries and roots, my form hidden from the world by the cloak of darkness. The days I spent in caves or the deepest recesses of the forests, my mind a ceaseless torment of thought. The exultation of my first act of revenge had faded, leaving behind a cold, hard resolve. I was no longer the pitiable wretch who wept at the beauty of a spring morning; I was an agent of retribution, a self-appointed scourge. I had declared war upon my creator, and through him, upon all of mankind. Yet, this newfound purpose did not bring peace, only a more profound and desolate isolation. I was a single, thinking atom in a universe of beings from whom I was utterly estranged.

As I ascended into the higher altitudes, the landscape began to change, reflecting the frozen state of my own heart. The gentle green of the lowlands gave way to the stark and solemn pines, and then to the barren rock and eternal ice of the peaks. I stood upon the shores of the Mer de Glace, a vast and silent sea of frozen waves, and felt a sense of belonging I had never known. Here was a landscape as alienated from the warm, breathing world of life as I was. The jagged peaks, the deep crevasses, the unyielding ice; this was my kingdom, my only suitable habitation. The sublime horror of the scene was a mirror to my own soul. It did not mock my deformity or shrink from my presence. It was merely vast, powerful, and indifferent.

In this frozen wilderness, I meditated upon my plan. The murder of the boy and the framing of the girl were but the opening salvos of my campaign. They were acts designed to inflict pain, to show Frankenstein the consequences of his abandonment. But my ultimate goal was not simply to cause him misery; it was to force him to rectify his original crime. My loneliness was a physical agony, a perpetual sickness of the soul for which there was only one possible cure. He had created one such as me; he possessed the power, the profane and wondrous knowledge, to create another.

This was the demand that formed in my mind, a demand that grew from a desperate wish into an ironclad necessity. I must have a companion. I must have a female, one of the same species as myself, with whom I could share my existence, who would not flee from my hideousness but see in it a reflection of her own. With such a companion, I could quit the world of man forever. We would fly to the vast and uninhabited wilds of South America, a land I had learned of from Volney’s book. There, among the giant trees and savage beasts, we would live out our lives, harming no one, seen by no one. I would no longer be a monster, for I would no longer be alone. The very thought of it sent a shudder of longing through my frame, so potent and so painful that I cried out, my voice swallowed by the immense silence of the glacier.

My resolve was fixed. I would wait for Frankenstein. I knew, with an instinct born of our strange and terrible connection, that his grief would drive him here. He would seek solace in the grandeur of nature, just as I did, attempting to lose his personal sorrow in the vastness of the mountains. He would come to this very place, to the heart of this icy desolation, and I would be waiting for him. I would not approach him as a supplicant, but as a power equal to his own. I had demonstrated my capacity for destruction; now I would offer him a choice. He could grant my request and purchase peace for himself and his species, or he could refuse, and condemn himself to a war of extermination, a war in which I would relentlessly pursue him, tearing away every joy, every attachment, until he was as alone and as wretched as I.

Days turned into weeks. I learned the paths of the mountains, the secrets of the glaciers. I watched the tiny figures of tourists and travellers from inaccessible heights, my heart a knot of contempt and envy. I saw them point and marvel at the scenery, their faces animated with wonder and companionship, and then I would retreat to my ice cave, the contrast between their world and mine a fresh source of torment. My only nourishment was the hope of my confrontation, the anticipation of seeing my creator’s face when he at last stood before the being he had so carelessly brought into existence.

Then, one morning, I saw him. A solitary figure, making his way slowly up the path towards Montanvert. Even from a distance, I recognized his gait, the slump of his shoulders, the very air of despair that clung to him like a shroud. My heart hammered in my chest with a violence that was both fear and triumph. The moment had come. I descended from my lookout point with incredible speed, my form a blur against the ice and rock, and placed myself directly in his path.

As he rounded a precipice of ice, he saw me. He stopped, his face a mask of utter horror and disbelief. His jaw fell open, his eyes widened, and the colour drained from his cheeks, leaving them as pale as the surrounding snow. A gasp escaped his lips, a sound of pure, unadulterated terror. For a long moment, we stood in silence, the creator and his creation, face to face at last in the heart of a frozen wasteland.

He was the first to speak, his voice choked with a mixture of rage and loathing. “Devil!” he cried, his hand instinctively going to his breast, as if to ward off an evil spirit. “Do you dare approach me? And do you not fear the fierce vengeance of my arm wreaked on your miserable head? Begone, vile insect! or rather, stay, that I may trample you to dust! and, oh! that I could, with the extinction of your miserable existence, restore those victims whom you have so diabolically murdered!”

His words were poison, yet they were what I had expected. I did not move, but stood my ground, my immense stature casting a long shadow over him. “I expected this reception,” I said, my voice, though rough from disuse, was steady and clear. “All men hate the wretched; how, then, must I be hated, who am miserable beyond all living things! Yet you, my creator, detest and spurn me, thy creature, to whom thou art bound by ties only dissoluble by the annihilation of one of us. You purpose to kill me. How dare you sport thus with life? Do your duty towards me, and I will do mine towards you and the rest of mankind. If you will comply with my conditions, I will leave them and you at peace; but if you refuse, I will glut the maw of death, until it be satiated with the blood of your remaining friends.”

He looked upon me with an expression of abhorrence that would have crushed a lesser spirit. His frame trembled with fury. “Abhorred monster! fiend that thou art! The tortures of hell are too mild a vengeance for thy crimes. Wretched devil! you reproach me with your creation; come on, then, that I may extinguish the spark which I so negligently bestowed.”

He advanced towards me, but I easily eluded him. “Be calm!” I implored, my voice rising with the force of my long-suppressed agony. “I entreat you to hear me before you give vent to your hatred on my devoted head. Have I not suffered enough, that you seek to increase my misery? Life, although it may only be an accumulation of anguish, is dear to me, and I will defend it. Remember, thou hast made me more powerful than thyself; my height is superior to thine, my joints more supple. But I will not be tempted to set myself in opposition to thee. I am thy creature, and I will be even mild and docile to my natural lord and king if thou wilt also perform thy part, the which thou owest me. Oh, Frankenstein, be not equitable to every other and trample upon me alone, to whom thy justice, and even thy clemency and affection, is most due. Remember that I am thy creature; I ought to be thy Adam, but I am rather the fallen angel, whom thou drivest from joy for no misdeed. Everywhere I see bliss, from which I alone am irrevocably excluded. I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend. Make me happy, and I shall again be virtuous.”

His words, though spoken in a tone of supplication, struck me with the force of a command; my mind, which had for a moment been swayed by the strange and dreadful eloquence of his plea, recoiled into its former state of abhorrence and fury. “Virtuous!” I cried, my voice choked with a passion of disgust. “Dost thou, the murderer of my innocent brother, the vile author of my ceaseless torment, dare to speak of virtue? Thy words are but the empty justifications of a demon; dost thou presume to lecture me on happiness, whose very existence is a blight upon the face of the earth and a curse upon the soul of thy creator? Begone from my sight, fiend! Lest I repent of this momentary forbearance and in my rage tear thee limb from limb, and scatter thy accursed atoms upon these icy winds.”

My first impulse was to leap upon him and engage in a mortal combat, which must have terminated in the death of one of us; yet there was a something in his words, a horrid and compelling truth, that held me motionless. He did not retreat from my renewed threats, but rather seemed to draw a strange strength from my indignation. His countenance, which I could now behold more clearly in the stark and unforgiving light of the glacier, was a mask of profoundest sorrow, yet his eyes burned with an intelligence that was altogether human, and this, I believe, was the most terrible part of my torture.

“Still you revile me, and still you will not hear,” he said, and his voice, which before had been raised in agony, now settled into a low and melancholy cadence. “You call me murderer, and it is true; I have shed the blood of the innocent, and my soul is steeped in a guilt that can never be washed away. But was I born a killer? Was my first impulse to bring sorrow and destruction? No; a thousand times, no! I was born with a heart capable of love and a spirit yearning for sympathy; it was the unrelenting cruelty of mankind, and of you, my creator, above all others, that has warped my nature and turned my benevolence to gall. You curse my existence, but it was you who gave it to me; you spurn me for my crimes, but it was your abandonment that first set my feet upon this path of ruin.”

He paused, and for a long moment the only sound was the distant groaning of the ice and the sighing of the wind across the desolate expanse. I stood, my arms crossed, my features set in a mask of resolute hatred, yet I could not bring myself to depart. A dreadful curiosity, a feeling which I loathed even as I succumbed to it, began to stir within me. What had been the life of this being? What were the events that had led him from his first awakening to this terrible confrontation upon the summit of the world?

“I ask of you a single boon,” he continued, perceiving perhaps the change in my posture, the slight wavering of my absolute resolve. “It is a simple act of justice before you condemn me utterly. Hear my tale; listen to the chronicle of my days, the solitary and wretched hours which have shaped me into the being I now am. My story will be long and strange, and you will learn in it of sufferings such as you can scarcely imagine. When I have finished, when you have seen the world through my eyes and felt the sting of the sorrows that have been my only companions, then you may judge me. If you still believe that I deserve only death and execration, I will not resist you. I will deliver myself into your hands, and you may extinguish this miserable spark of life which you so regret having bestowed. But if, in the course of my narration, you should find in your heart a flicker of that pity, of that justice which you owe me, I will then make my demand. It is a demand which, if you grant it, will ensure my departure from Europe, and from the sight of any human being, for ever.”

I was silent. His proposition was as strange and monstrous as he was himself. To sit and listen to the confessions of this fiend, this creature of horror? The thought was repugnant to my very soul. And yet, what was the alternative? He had proven his power; he had threatened my remaining family, those beloved beings for whose safety I would willingly have sacrificed my own life and peace. His threats were potent; his reasoning, however perverse, was not without a certain dreadful logic. And above all, there was that terrible, gnawing curiosity. You, my creator, who were once so possessed by the ardour of discovery, can you feel no curiosity towards the workings of the life you have fashioned? His words echoed in my mind. You have studied the hidden laws of nature; will you now turn away from the study of the very soul you have yourself called into existence?

For a long time I deliberated, my mind a tempest of conflicting emotions. Rage, grief, and fear warred with a cold, calculating reason. To listen was to condescend to a parley with a demon; to refuse was to invite a war whose casualties I could not bear to contemplate. I looked upon him, my enemy, my creation, my eternal curse. The sun, breaking through the clouds, cast a momentary gleam upon the ice, and in that harsh light his features seemed to writhe with a thousand untold miseries. I saw, or fancied I saw, in his posture a profound dejection, the weight of an unutterable loneliness. It was not pity that I felt, for my heart was a stone within my breast, but a cold recognition of a shared doom. We were bound together, he and I, in a chain of misery that only death could sever.

“For what purpose should I listen?” I said at last, my voice hollow and strained. “What can you say that will wash the stain of William’s blood from your hands? Can your words restore the dead to life? Can they give me back my peace of mind?”

“They cannot,” he replied, with a somber gravity. “The past is immutable, and what is done cannot be undone. But my story can explain what led to that dreadful act; it can show you the fiend not as he is, but as he was made. It can show you the Adam you cast out of your sight, before misery turned him into the fallen angel you now behold. You owe me this. You owe it to yourself, that you may understand the full consequences of your own work. Hear me, and then decide. The power of life and death will still be yours.”

His argument was compelling, and I felt my resistance crumbling, not to compassion, but to a grim and awful necessity. “I consent,” I said, the words feeling like ashes in my mouth. “I will hear your story. But not here, not on this open plain of ice where the very air freezes the soul. There is a hut on the far side of this glacier; I have seen it from below. Let us go there. I will provide a fire, and you shall speak. But do not mistake this for compassion; do not for a moment believe that my hatred has abated, or that my purpose has changed. I listen only that I may know the full extent of your wickedness, and that I may be better armed to destroy you, should your tale fail to move me to any other course.”

He bowed his head, a gesture of acquiescence that seemed strangely formal and human. “I ask for no more,” he said. “Justice, not compassion, is all I seek. Lead on; I will follow.”

Without another word, I turned my back on him and began the arduous trek across the sea of ice. He followed, his immense strides easily keeping pace with my own, though he maintained a respectful distance, as if aware that his proximity was a source of torment to me. The journey was made in a silence more profound and terrible than any I had ever known. The ice groaned and cracked beneath our feet, and the wind whispered its mournful secrets from the crags above, but between my companion and myself, not a syllable was exchanged. The very mountains, in their silent and awful majesty, seemed to stand as witnesses to this unholy conference. My heart was a chaos of tumultuous feelings, but chief among them was a sense of fatality, a chilling certainty that my life had, on this desolate peak, reached its final, terrible turning point.

After an hour which seemed an eternity, we reached the small hut of which I had spoken. It was a rude shelter of rough-hewn logs, abandoned by some long-forgotten shepherds, and its interior was dark and filled with the damp chill of disuse. I entered first, my hand instinctively going to the pistol I kept concealed in my breast, a foolish gesture, for what was a pistol against a being of his strength? He followed me in and stood by the doorway, a colossal shadow against the glaring white of the world outside, patiently awaiting my next move.

With trembling hands, I gathered some dry wood that lay scattered in a corner and kindled a fire in the dilapidated hearth. The flames sprang to life, casting a flickering, uncertain light upon the squalid interior and upon the horrid form of my companion. I sat upon a broken stool on one side of the fire, and he, without invitation, seated himself upon the floor on the other. The firelight danced upon his features, accentuating their ghastly strangeness, throwing his yellow skin, his watery eyes, and his shrivelled complexion into a relief that was almost unendurable. For a long while we sat in silence, the crackling of the flames the only sound that broke the oppressive stillness. I was chilled to the bone, though my heart burned with a fever of rage and despair.

At last, I turned my gaze upon him and spoke, my voice devoid of all emotion. “Your story,” I said. “Begin.”

He drew a deep, shuddering breath, a sound that seemed to tear its way from the very depths of his being. The firelight flickered in his eyes, and for a moment I saw in them not malice, but a sorrow so profound, so ancient, that it seemed to belong to the world itself. He looked not at me, but into the heart of the fire, as if the story he was about to tell was written there in the shifting embers.

“It is with difficulty that I remember my earliest sensations,” he began, his voice low and guttural, yet possessed of a strange, compelling rhythm. “A strange multiplicity of sensations seized me, and I saw, felt, heard, and smelt at the same time; and it was, indeed, a long time before I learned to distinguish between the operations of my various senses. By degrees, I remember, a stronger light pressed upon my nerves, so that I was obliged to shut my eyes. Darkness then came over me and troubled me, but hardly had I felt this when, by opening my eyes, as I now suppose, the light poured in upon me again. I walked and, I believe, descended, but I presently found a great alteration in my sensations. Before, dark and opaque bodies had surrounded me, impervious to my touch or sight; but I now found that I could wander on at liberty, with no other obstacle than the inequality of the ground beneath my feet. The moon had disappeared from the night, and again, with a restless and fearful curiosity, I directed my steps towards the scene of my unknown despair. I guided myself by the mountains and the river, and in about two days reached the environs of Geneva.

“It was night when I arrived, and I retired to a hiding place in the deep woods that bordered the town. It was here that I first saw a human dwelling. The cottage was small, and its inhabitants were poor, but there was an air of peace and contentment about it that struck me with a feeling of wonder and longing. I observed the family for many days, my first study of the beings from whom I was so hideously different. But my tale properly begins before this, with the first dawning of my consciousness in the laboratory where you, my creator, so callously abandoned me to a world I did not understand. Listen, and do not turn away, for this is the truth you have so long fled. Listen, and learn what it is you have created.”

I held his gaze for a moment longer, a gaze which flickered between terror and a grudging curiosity, and then I turned my eyes back to the fire, the repository of my nascent memories.

“My first sensations are a confused and indistinct dream; a dream of oppressive light and a weight upon my limbs which I could not comprehend. A dazzling effulgence pierced my eyes, an agony so keen that I was forced to close them, and in that darkness I found a momentary, troubled peace. Yet the light was a persistent tormentor; it seemed to pour through my very eyelids, and I felt a strange compulsion to move, to flee from this unknown source of pain. With a convulsive shudder which wracked my entire frame, I arose. My limbs were stiff and clumsy; I tottered and fell, and the hard floor against my skin was but another new and startling sensation in a world of bewildering inputs.

“I know not how long I remained in that chamber of my birth, that charnel house you had endowed with motion; it may have been hours, or it may have been days. Time was a concept as foreign to me as the name of my own creator. I was driven only by instinct, by a series of inarticulate impulses. Hunger, a gnawing emptiness in my belly, first made itself known; I found some scraps left upon your worktable, some coarse bread and other victuals you had forgotten in your haste, and I devoured them without thought, my palate unable to distinguish pleasure from necessity. Thirst soon followed, a parching fire in my throat, which I slaked by dipping my hands into a vessel of water, a liquid cool and wondrous to my senses.

“One impulse, however, grew stronger than all others; a desperate need to escape the confines of that room and the insupportable glare of the single candle that burned there. I observed, with a faculty that was even then beginning to separate and analyze the chaos of my perceptions, that a draft of cold air emanated from beneath a heavy drapery. I pulled it aside, and before me was a door. My first attempts to manipulate the latch were clumsy, but my hands, though untutored, were strong, and soon the mechanism yielded. The door swung open upon a staircase, and a wave of cool, damp air washed over me, a welcome balm to my fevered skin.

“I descended, stumbling in the profound darkness, my hands brushing against cold stone. The silence of the house was absolute, broken only by my own clumsy movements and the sound of my ragged breathing. You, my maker, had fled; you had abandoned your monstrous progeny to navigate the labyrinth of your dwelling alone. I found another door, and then another, until at last I emerged from the house into the open air.

“And what an air it was! The night was cold, and a fine rain was falling, but to me it was a kind of liberation. I was enveloped not by walls, but by a vast, incomprehensible darkness, and above me, where a roof should have been, I perceived only a shifting canopy of black. I was a wretch, even then; I was naked and alone, a creature of mismatched parts thrust into a world for which I had no preparation. A profound melancholy took possession of my soul, and I sat down upon the wet ground and wept.

“My tears brought no relief, for I knew not what I was weeping for. I was a being of pure sensation, and my sorrow was as raw and unformed as my other perceptions. I wandered, aimlessly, for the remainder of the night, driven by a restless energy. When the dawn came, it brought with it a new species of torment. The glorious, rising sun, which I now know to be a source of joy for all other beings, was to me an instrument of torture. Its light was a thousand times more piercing than the candle in your laboratory; it painted the world in colours too vivid for my unprepared eyes, and I was forced to seek shelter from its oppressive splendour.

“I found refuge in a great forest which bordered the town of Ingolstadt. Here, for the first time, I felt a semblance of peace. The thick canopy of leaves filtered the harsh daylight, casting a gentle, green-hued twilight upon the ground. I walked upon a carpet of soft moss, and the air was filled with a thousand fragrant scents. I slaked my thirst at a clear brook, and its babbling sound was the first music I had ever heard. It was here, in the solitude of the woods, that I began the slow and painful process of learning.

“I learned to distinguish between the insects that buzzed around my head and the berries that grew on the bushes. Some of these berries were sweet and nourishing; others were bitter and caused me great pain, and thus I learned caution. I discovered the delight of sleep, a blessed oblivion that stole over me when my senses were overwrought. I would lie down upon a bed of dry leaves and surrender myself to a darkness more profound and more restful than any I had known.

“My greatest discovery, however, and my most profound teacher, was the moon. As the sun’s reign of fire gave way to the cool serenity of night, a pale orb would rise in the sky, casting a gentle, silvery radiance upon the world. Unlike the sun, its light did not pain me; rather, it soothed my spirit and filled me with a strange, contemplative sadness. I would sit for hours, gazing at its placid face, and a host of inchoate emotions would stir within me, feelings for which I had no words, but which felt like the stirrings of a soul.

“The birds, too, became my companions and my tutors. I listened to their cheerful songs, and though I could not understand them, I felt a yearning to imitate their melodious notes. My own voice, when I tried to use it, was a harsh and guttural croaking, a sound so monstrous that it startled even me, and I quickly fell silent, ashamed of the dissonance I had introduced into the forest’s symphony. Was I, then, so different from these other creatures? They had their mates, their songs, their nests; I had only my hideous form and my crushing solitude.

“One day, as I was wandering in search of food, I came across a strange phenomenon. A fire, left untended by some wandering beggars, was still smouldering in a clearing. I was drawn to it by its cheerful brightness and the warmth that radiated from it. In my ignorance, I thrust my hand into the living flames, and I cried out in agony as a new and terrible sensation, the sensation of being burned, shot through my arm. I quickly learned to respect this powerful element, but I also learned of its benefits. I discovered that the warmth could drive away the biting cold of the nights, which had grown more severe with the advancing season. I found that by placing my food near the fire, it became more palatable, and the berries and roots which had before been unpleasantly sour were rendered tender and sweet.

“I was overjoyed with this discovery and spent many days gathering wood to feed my precious flame. But a great storm arose, and the rain extinguished my fire, and I was once again left to the mercy of the cold. I wept bitterly at my loss, a loss that seemed to me then as grievous as any I could suffer. It was my first lesson in the transience of comfort and the relentless cruelty of the elements.

“Driven by a hunger more severe than any I had yet known, for the winter had stripped the trees of their berries and the ground of its roots, I ventured further than I had before. After a long and arduous journey, I spied a small hut, from which a thin plume of smoke was rising. My heart, if you can call it that, leaped with a strange mixture of hope and trepidation. Here, surely, was another being, perhaps one who could share with me the secret of creating and maintaining a fire.

“With trembling steps, I approached the hut and peered through a small window. Inside, an old man sat before a fire, preparing his morning meal. He was not like me; his stature was stooped, his skin was wrinkled, and his movements were slow. But he was a being, a living soul, and a desperate longing for communion seized me. I entered the hut.

“The old man looked up, and his face, which had been placid, was instantly transformed into a mask of terror. He let out a piercing shriek, and scrambling to his feet with a swiftness I would not have thought possible, he fled from the hut and ran across the fields, never once looking back. I was left alone, standing in the centre of the small room, more bewildered than I had ever been. I had not meant to cause him fear; I had sought only warmth and companionship. Why had he fled from me as if I were a demon from the pit of hell?

“I partook of the food he had left behind, a simple meal of bread and cheese, and the taste was a revelation to me. It was a pleasure far beyond the simple sustenance of berries and roots. But the pleasure was tainted by the bitterness of my reception. I left the hut, my heart heavy with a new and painful knowledge: the knowledge of my own monstrousness.

“My journey continued, though now it was tinged with a caution born of fear. I learned to travel only at night, hiding myself during the day in the deepest recesses of the woods or in abandoned barns. My solitude was now a prison, not a refuge, and the sound of a human voice, which I sometimes heard in the distance, would fill me with a mixture of terror and longing.

“It was in this state of mind that I arrived at a village. It was larger than any settlement I had yet seen, a cluster of neat cottages with smoke curling from their chimneys. It was daytime, but my hunger was a ravenous beast, and the memory of the bread and cheese spurred me on. I forgot my caution, my fear, my shame. I saw a young woman coming from one of the cottages, carrying a pail of milk. Surely, I thought, a being so fair and gentle would not recoil from me in horror.

“I approached her, my hands outstretched in a gesture of supplication, and I uttered some of the harsh, inarticulate sounds that were my only means of communication. Her response was not fear, but a scream of pure, unadulterated terror. She dropped her pail and fainted upon the ground. Her cries had alerted the other villagers, and soon I was surrounded by a mob. Men, women, and even children came rushing from their houses, their faces contorted with hatred and disgust.

“They shouted words I did not understand, but their meaning was clear enough. They hurled stones and other missiles at me, driving me from their village with a fury that was as incomprehensible as it was terrifying. I was struck many times; my body was bruised and bleeding, but the wounds to my flesh were as nothing compared to the wound that had been inflicted upon my spirit. I fled, the jeers and curses of the villagers ringing in my ears, and I did not stop running until I had collapsed from exhaustion in the depths of the forest.

“There, in the darkness, I lay and wept, not with the uncomprehending sorrow of my first days, but with the full, agonizing consciousness of my utter isolation. I was a monster. This was the truth that had been revealed to me, a truth more cruel and more absolute than any physical pain. I was an outcast, a thing of horror, a blot upon the face of the earth, an object of revulsion for the very beings I so desperately wished to join.

“It was this event, this baptism of hatred, that led me to the hovel which was to become my home, my university, and my torment for the next many months. I had found a small, dilapidated shed, a lean-to built against the wall of a much finer cottage. It was intended for storing wood or perhaps for sheltering pigs, but for me it was a palace. It was a refuge from the slings and arrows of a hostile world, a place where I could be unseen, a place where I could observe without being observed.

“And what I observed, my creator, was a vision of such domestic felicity, such gentle harmony, that it both soothed my wounded heart and inflamed my envy to a fever pitch. Through a small chink in the wall of my hovel, I could see into the cottage, and I became a silent, secret spectator to the lives of its inhabitants.

“They were a poor family, this I could discern from their simple clothes and meager meals, yet they possessed a wealth that I would have given my wretched existence to share. They possessed each other. There was an old man, blind, with a face of such benevolent sweetness that it seemed to radiate a gentle light. There was a young man, his son, whose countenance was often clouded with care, but who treated his father with a reverence and a tenderness that moved me to tears. And there was a young woman, his sister, a creature of such grace and kindness that the very air around her seemed to grow lighter and more pure.

“I watched their daily routines with an intensity that bordered on obsession. I saw the young woman, Agatha, perform her household chores with a quiet efficiency, always with a loving glance for her father or her brother. I saw the young man, Felix, work tirelessly in the garden or in the woods, his brow furrowed with effort, only to return at the end of the day with a smile for his family. I listened to the sounds of their voices, the gentle cadences of their speech, the music of their laughter. I did not understand their words, but I understood the love that bound them together, a love as palpable and as real as the walls of their cottage.

“It was from them that I first learned the name of emotions. I saw their faces light up with joy when Felix would play his guitar, or when the old man, De Lacey, would tell them a story. I saw their faces clouded with sorrow when their supply of food was low, or when the winter winds howled outside their door. And I, in my dark and lonely hovel, I shared their every emotion. I rejoiced in their joys, and I wept for their sorrows. They became my protectors, my friends, my world. I was a part of their family, a silent, unseen, and unloved member, but a member nonetheless. This was the fiction that I allowed myself to believe, the sweet, painful delusion that sustained me through the long and bitter winter. And as I watched and listened, a new and powerful desire took root in my soul: the desire to learn their language, to understand their words, to one day be able to speak to them and to tell them that I, too, was a creature of feeling, a creature capable of love, a creature who was not the monster they would surely believe me to be.”

Felix had accidentally been present at the trial; his horror and indignation were uncontrollable when he heard the decision of the court. He made, at that moment, a solemn vow to deliver him and then looked around for the means. After many fruitless attempts to gain admittance to the prison, he found a strongly grated window in an unguarded part of the building, which lighted the dungeon of the unfortunate Mahometan, who, loaded with chains, waited in despair the execution of the barbarous sentence. Felix visited the grate at night and made known to the prisoner his intentions in his favour. The Turk, amazed and delighted, endeavoured to kindle the zeal of his deliverer by promises of reward and wealth. Felix rejected his offers with contempt, yet when he saw the lovely Safie, who was permitted to visit her father and who by her gestures expressed her lively gratitude, the youth could not help owning to his own mind that the captive possessed a treasure which would fully reward his toil and hazard.

The Turk quickly perceived the impression that his daughter had made on the heart of Felix and endeavoured to secure him more entirely in his interests by the promise of her hand in marriage so soon as he should be conveyed to a place of safety. Felix was too delicate to accept this offer, yet he looked forward to the probability of that event as to the consummation of his happiness.

During the ensuing days, while the preparations were going forward for the escape of the merchant, the zeal of Felix was warmed by several letters that he received from this lovely girl, who found means to express her thoughts in the language of her lover by the aid of an old man, a servant of her father who understood French. She thanked him in the most ardent terms for his intended services towards her parent, and at the same time she gently deplored her own fate.

I have copies of these letters, for I found them later, and can relate their contents. She related that her mother was a Christian Arab, seized and made a slave by the Turks; recommended by her beauty, she had won the heart of the father of Safie, who married her. The young girl spoke in high and enthusiastic terms of her mother, who, born in freedom, spurned the bondage to which she was now reduced. She instructed her daughter in the tenets of her religion and taught her to aspire to higher powers of intellect and an independence of spirit forbidden to the female followers of Mahomet. This lady died, but her lessons were indelibly impressed on the mind of Safie, who sickened at the prospect of again returning to Asia and being immured within the walls of a harem, allowed only to occupy herself with infantile amusements, ill suited to the temper of her soul, now accustomed to grander ideas and a noble emulation for virtue. The prospect of marrying a Christian and remaining in a country where women were treated as rational beings was therefore doubly grateful to her.

The day for the execution of the Turk was fixed, but on the night previous to it he quitted his prison and before morning was distant many leagues from Paris. Felix had procured passports in the name of his father, sister, and himself. He had previously communicated his plan to the former, who aided the deceit by quitting his house under the pretence of a journey and concealing himself, with his daughter, in an obscure part of Paris.

Felix conducted the fugitive for several hundred miles in this manner. Safie, who had accompanied her father in his flight, was his constant companion. During the day they concealed themselves in the woods or remote cottages, and at night they continued their route. It was thus that they passed the frontiers of France and, after some weeks of travel, arrived on the coast of Italy, near Leghorn, where the merchant had decided to wait a favourable opportunity of passing into some part of the Turkish dominions.

When they were rested after the fatigues of their journey, and the prisoner had recovered his spirits, the prospect of Safie's union with a Christian filled him with abhorrence. He possessed a traitorous heart and hated the man who had saved him in a way that he could not forgive. He secretly arranged with the captain of a vessel to convey him to Constantinople; and having taught his daughter to expect that Felix would shortly join them in that city, he departed, leaving with the governor of Leghorn a paltry sum of money to settle his accounts with Felix.

The perfidy of this wretch, however, was not the only misfortune that our family was doomed to endure. The French government, enraged at the escape of their victim, spared no pains to discover and punish his deliverer. The plot of Felix was quickly discovered, and De Lacey and Agatha were thrown into prison. The news reached Felix and roused him from his dream of pleasure. His blind and aged father and his gentle sister lay in a noisome dungeon while he enjoyed the free air and the society of her whom he loved. This idea was torture to him. He hastened to the governor of Leghorn, cleared his accounts with him, and hastily returned to Paris, where he delivered himself up to the vengeance of the law, hoping to free De Lacey and Agatha by this surrender.

They were not liberated, however; and after a long trial, which brought to light all the circumstances of their assistance to the Turk, they were condemned to perpetual exile and the loss of all their fortune. They found a miserable asylum in the cottage in Germany where I discovered them. Felix soon learned that the treacherous Turk, for whom he and his family endured such unheard of oppression, on arriving at Constantinople, had betrayed his saviour and delivered him up to the very government from which he had been rescued. He had then, with his daughter, retired to a remote part of the Turkish empire.

But Safie, who had been taught to revere the name of Felix, and to look upon him as her future husband, could not be persuaded to accompany her father. With a spirit of resolution singular in a female of her country, she contrived to escape, attended only by a single servant, a native of Leghorn, who understood the languages of the country, and who was devoted to her. With a small collection of jewels which she had contrived to secure, and this faithful attendant, she departed from Turkey.

She arrived at Leghorn, but there she was received with the sad tidings that Felix had quitted the city and was gone with his family into exile. The servant, who had hitherto been faithful, now conceived a design of appropriating to himself the jewels of his mistress, and he abandoned her in an unknown country, where she was ignorant of the language, and of which he alone was the master. She fell sick, and it was some time before her health was restored. As soon as she was sufficiently recovered, she sold some of her jewels and with the money thus obtained, she proceeded on her journey. She was but ill acquainted with the name of the place to which Felix had retired, but she made her way towards Germany, trusting to chance for the rest.

Her journey was long and tedious. She was often bewildered and misled, and had it not been for the kindness of some peasants, who pitied her youth and apparent distress, she must have perished. At length she arrived at the cottage of my protectors, having by some happy chance learned that the exiled family resided there. Her joy at this discovery was extreme, as was that of Felix. Her arrival had diffused gladness through the humble dwelling, and it was this circumstance that had first drawn my attention to their history. Such, my creator, was the tale of these admirable people. It was filled with passages that excited my wonder and admiration. The gentle manners of the Arabian, and especially her love for Felix, softened and attracted me. For a moment I dared to fancy such joys for myself; I imagined that I also might obtain a companion who would overlook my deformity and love me for the excellent qualities of my mind; but this was a dream from which I was soon awakened. Who could ever look on me without horror? And what was there in my nature that could inspire affection?

My education was now complete, but my condition was more miserable than ever. I had learned to admire virtue, but I was shut out from the world; I had seen the beauty of human companionship, but I was condemned to eternal solitude. The more I saw of the peaceful cottage, the more I felt my own degradation. I saw the love that bound together the father, son, and sister; I saw the tender affection of Felix for Safie; and I asked myself, where were my friends? Where were the beings who would pity my sorrows and rejoice in my successes? I had none. I was a poor, helpless, miserable wretch; I knew, and could distinguish, nothing; but feeling pain invade me on all sides, I sat down and wept.

These reflections have carried me from my narrative, to which I must now return. The pleasant showers and genial warmth of spring greatly altered the aspect of the earth. I was soon able to stay abroad all night without feeling the cold, and my rambles were now my chief delight. The cottage of my protectors had been the only school in which I had studied human nature, but this book I had read with intense application. I had also, during the winter, found a leathern portmanteau containing several articles of dress and some books. I had eagerly seized the prize and returned with it to my hovel. Fortunately the books were written in the language, the elements of which I had acquired at the cottage; they were _Paradise Lost_, a volume of _Plutarch’s Lives_, and the _Sorrows of Werter_.

The possession of these treasures gave me extreme delight; I now continually studied and exercised my mind upon these histories, whilst my friends were employed in their ordinary occupations. I can hardly describe to you the effect of these books. They produced in me an infinity of new images and feelings, that sometimes raised me to ecstasy, but more frequently sunk me into the lowest dejection. In the _Sorrows of Werter_, besides the interest of its simple and affecting story, so many opinions are canvassed and so many lights thrown upon what had hitherto been to me obscure subjects that I found in it a never ending source of speculation and astonishment. The gentle and domestic manners it described, combined with lofty sentiments and feelings, which had for their object something out of self, accorded well with my experience among my protectors. But Werter is a being of sublime sensibilities; he is not, like me, a solitary monster. He has friends, he has a society which he can quit and which he can re-enter at his pleasure. But I was a poor, friendless outcast, a wretch for whom there was no sympathy, no communion.

_Plutarch’s Lives_ taught me high thoughts; he elevated me above the wretched sphere of my own reflections, to admire and love the heroes of past ages. Many things I read surpassed my understanding and experience. I was not master of the science of government and legislation, but I was deeply affected by the virtues of the great men of antiquity. I felt the greatest ardour for virtue rise within me, and abhorrence for vice, as far as I understood the signification of those terms, relative as they were, as I applied them, to pleasure and pain alone. Induced by these feelings, I was of course led to admire peaceable lawgivers, Numa, Solon, and Lycurgus, in preference to Romulus and Theseus. The patriarchal lives of my protectors caused these impressions to take a firm hold on my mind; perhaps the beings contained in these pages were physically more powerful than they, but they were not more virtuous.

But _Paradise Lost_ excited different and far deeper emotions. I read it, as I had read the other volumes which had fallen into my hands, as a true history. It moved every feeling of wonder and awe that the picture of an omnipotent God warring with his creatures was capable of exciting. I often referred the several situations, as their similarity struck me, to my own. Like Adam, I was apparently united by no link to any other being in existence; but his state was far different from mine in every other respect. He had come forth from the hands of God a perfect creature, happy and prosperous, guarded by the especial care of his Creator; he was allowed to converse with and acquire knowledge from beings of a superior nature, but I was wretched, helpless, and alone. Many times I considered Satan as the fitter emblem of my condition, for often, like him, when I viewed the bliss of my protectors, the bitter gall of envy rose within me.

Another circumstance strengthened and confirmed these feelings. Soon after my arrival in the hovel, I had discovered some papers in the pocket of the dress which I had taken from your laboratory. At first I had neglected them, but now that I was able to decipher the characters in which they were written, I began to study them with diligence. It was your journal of the four months that preceded my creation. You minutely described in these papers every step you took in the progress of your work; this history was mingled with accounts of domestic occurrences. You doubtless recollect these papers. Here they are. Every thing is related in them which bears reference to my accursed origin; the whole detail of that series of disgusting circumstances which produced it is set in view; the minutest description of my odious and loathsome person is given, in language which painted your own horrors and rendered mine indelible. I sickened as I read. ‘Hateful day when I received life!’ I exclaimed in agony. ‘Accursed creator! Why did you form a monster so hideous that even you turned from me in disgust? God, in pity, made man beautiful and alluring, after his own image; but my form is a filthy type of yours, more horrid even from the very resemblance. Satan had his companions, fellow devils, to admire and encourage him, but I am solitary and abhorred.’

These were the reflections of my hours of despondency and solitude; but when I contemplated the virtues of the cottagers, their amiable and benevolent dispositions, I persuaded myself that when they should become acquainted with my admiration of their virtues, they would compassionate me and overlook my personal deformity. Could they turn from a creature who adored and reverenced them? I resolved to make a trial of their virtue. I resolved, unknown to my protectors, to declare myself to the old man, De Lacey. As he was blind, I should have an opportunity of winning him to my cause before any prejudice against my external appearance could bias his judgment.

I waited for the day when Felix and the women would be absent. It arrived sooner than I had dared to hope. The young people were gone on a long country walk, and the old man, at his own desire, was left alone in the cottage. When I saw him thus, I trembled with agitation, yet I resolved to proceed. I knocked at the door. "Who is there?" said the old man. "Come in."

I entered. "Pardon this intrusion," said I; "I am a traveller in want of a little rest; you would greatly oblige me if you would allow me to remain a few minutes before the fire."

"Enter," said De Lacey, "and I will try in what manner I can relieve your wants; but, unfortunately, my children are from home, and as I am blind, I am afraid I shall find it difficult to procure food for you."

"Oh, do not trouble yourself, my kind host," I replied, "I have food; it is warmth and rest only that I need."

I sat down, and a silence ensued. I knew that every minute was precious to me, yet I remained irresolute in what manner to commence the interview, when the old man addressed me. "By your language, stranger, I suppose you are my countryman; are you French?"

"No," replied I, "but I was educated by a French family and understand that language only. I am now going to claim the protection of some friends, whom I sincerely love, and of whose favour I have some hopes."

"Are they Germans?"

"No, they are French. But let us change the subject. I am an unfortunate and deserted creature; I look around and I have no relation or friend upon earth. These amiable people to whom I go have never seen me and know little of me. I am full of fears, for if I fail there, I am an outcast in the world for ever."

"Do not despair. To be friendless is indeed to be unfortunate, but the hearts of men, when unprejudiced by any obvious self-interest, are full of brotherly love and charity. Rely, therefore, on your hopes; and if these friends are good and amiable, do not despair."

"They are kind, they are the most excellent creatures in the world; but, unfortunately, they are prejudiced against me. I have good dispositions; my life has been hitherto harmless and in some degree beneficial; but a fatal prejudice clouds their eyes, and where they ought to see a feeling and kind friend, they behold only a detestable monster."

"That is indeed unfortunate," replied the old man, "but if you are really blameless, cannot you undeceive them?"

"I am about to undertake that task; and it is on that account that I feel so many overwhelming terrors. I tenderly love these friends; I have, unknown to them, been for many months in the habits of daily kindness towards them; but they believe that I wish to injure them, and it is that prejudice which I wish to overcome."

"Where do these friends reside?"

"Near this spot."

The old man paused and then continued, "If you will unreservedly confide to me the particulars of your story, I dare venture to promise that I will mediate between you and your friends and endeavour to instil into them a kinder feeling for you. I am blind and cannot judge of your countenance, but there is something in your words which persuades me that you are sincere. I am poor and an exile, but it will afford me true pleasure to be in any way serviceable to a human creature."

"Excellent man!" I exclaimed. "I thank you and accept your generous offer. You raise me from the dust by this kindness; and I trust that, by your aid, I shall not be driven from the society and sympathy of your fellow creatures."

"Heaven forbid! even if you were really criminal, for that can only drive you to desperation, and not instil courage and virtue. I also am unfortunate; I and my family have been condemned, although innocent; judge, therefore, if I do not feel for your misfortunes."

"How can I thank you, my best and only benefactor? From your lips first have I heard the voice of kindness directed towards me; I shall be forever grateful; and your present humanity assures me of success with those friends whom I am on the point of meeting."

I paused. This was the decisive moment, which was to decide my fate. I struggled to suppress my emotions and answered, "They are the De Laceys, who reside in this cottage."

At that instant I heard the sound of footsteps approaching. I knew it was Felix, Safie, and Agatha. I had no time for thought, but, in a frenzy of despair, I cried, "Now is the time! Save and protect me! You and your family are the friends whom I seek. Do not you desert me in the hour of trial!"

"Great God!" exclaimed the old man. "Who are you?"

At that instant the cottage door was opened, and Felix, Safie, and Agatha entered. What a scene of terror ensued! Agatha, fainting, was carried out of the cottage by her brother. Safie, unable to attend to her friend, rushed out of the cottage. Felix darted forward, and with supernatural force tore me from his father, to whose knees I clung; in a transport of fury, he dashed me to the ground and struck me violently with a stick. I could have torn him limb from limb, as the lion rends the antelope. But my heart sunk within me as with bitter sickness, and I refrained. I saw him on the point of repeating his blow, when, overcome by pain and anguish, I quitted the cottage, and in the general tumult escaped unperceived to my hovel.

Crouched in the miserable darkness of my hovel, I was a creature newly born into a world of torment. The blow from the stick was a dull ache upon my shoulder, but it was a feather’s weight compared to the crushing agony that had seized my heart and soul. Through the chinks in the wall, I heard the confused and terrified sounds from within the cottage; a woman’s cry, the hurried and impassioned tones of Felix, and then, a dreadful silence that spoke more of terror than any noise. My protectors, my friends, my beloved cottagers were lost to me for ever. A howl of despair, like that of a wild beast caught in a hunter’s trap, rose in my throat, but I choked it back, lest it should bring them out again to wreak further vengeance upon my wretched frame.

For many hours I remained motionless, a statue of misery. The moon, which had so often been my sole companion and comfort, now seemed to mock my solitude, casting a cold and indifferent light upon the scene of my ruin. My mind, a tempest of conflicting emotions, replayed the events of the last hour with excruciating clarity. I saw again the benevolent face of the old man as he listened to my tale; I felt the nascent stirrings of hope as he promised his aid. That hope had been a fragile shoot, and the sudden, violent return of his children had been the brutal foot which had crushed it into the dust. I had been on the very precipice of salvation, only to be hurled into a deeper and more fathomless despair.

My thoughts turned upon Felix with a bitterness that was new to me. He, who had wept for the injustices done to his own family, who had spoken with such fire and eloquence of the rights of man, had been the first to raise his hand against me. He had not questioned, he had not paused; he had seen my form and in an instant condemned me. Was this, then, the justice of mankind? Was their virtue and compassion reserved only for those who wore a pleasing aspect? The lessons of Volney’s book returned to me, not as a history of distant empires, but as a prophecy of my own fate. I, like the original inhabitants of the newly discovered continent, was a hapless creature to be driven from the light of day, to be hunted and reviled without cause.

All night I kept a miserable vigil, my ear pressed to the cold earth, straining for any sound. A single candle burned late in the cottage, its lonely flame a sentinel to their fear. I pictured them within, huddled together, their faces pale with the horror I had inspired. The thought that I, who would have laid down my own life for their sakes, was now the object of their deepest terror, was a torment beyond the power of words to describe. My tears, which had once flowed from sorrow and delight, were now dried up by the fever of a growing rage. The milk of human kindness within me had curdled and turned to gall.

When the first grey light of dawn touched the eastern sky, I perceived a stir within the cottage. I saw Felix emerge, his face grim and determined. He did not go to his work in the woods, but instead began to converse earnestly with a man from the neighbouring village, who soon departed and returned with a cart. With a sinking heart, I understood their purpose. They were to fly from me, to abandon the home which had been the scene of their peaceful exile, because my presence had polluted it. I watched as they carried out their few possessions: the old man’s guitar, Agatha’s work-frame, the books from which I had learned of a world that would for ever exclude me. Each object was a dagger in my heart.

Safie and Agatha appeared, their faces veiled and stained with weeping. Last of all, the venerable De Lacey was led out by his son. The old man was trembling, and his sightless face, which had once been a picture of placid contentment, was now a mask of confusion and sorrow. He alone had not seen my deformity; he alone had listened to me with compassion. As Felix helped him into the cart, a groan of utter desolation escaped my lips. This was the last, the most bitter pang. With his departure, the final cord that connected me to any living being was severed.

They departed. I watched the cart recede along the winding road until it was but a speck in the distance, and then I was alone. The silence that descended was profound and absolute. The cottage, once the abode of every virtue and grace that I admired, stood empty and desolate. For a long time I did not move, but when the sun had risen high in the sky, a strange impulse guided my steps. I quit my hovel and, for the first time, entered the cottage, not as a secret benefactor, but as a being accursed.

I walked through the small rooms, my bare feet treading upon the floor they had so lately trod. I touched the simple furniture, still warm, it seemed, with the life that had departed. There, in the corner, was the stool where Safie had sat at the old man’s feet; here was the table where they had partaken of their simple meals. The air was still fragrant with the scent of the flowers Agatha had placed in a vase. An overwhelming sense of loss swept over me, and I threw myself upon the ground, weeping with a violence that shook my entire frame. I rose from the floor, my decision made. These beings, whom I had loved with a devotion that was almost worship, had declared war upon me. I would, in turn, wage a war against them, and against the whole of humanity. My first act would be to obliterate this monument to my crushed hopes. I went out into the woods and spent the day gathering great quantities of dry branches and brushwood. As night fell, I stood before my funeral pyre of hope. The moon had not yet risen, and the world was cloaked in a profound darkness that was a fitting emblem of my soul. With a cry that was a mixture of triumph and despair, I thrust a burning torch, which I had kindled with flint and dry moss, into the pile of wood. I watched as the fire consumed the roof, the walls, and everything within. When the last timber had fallen and the fire had sunk to a bed of glowing embers, a cold and lasting calm settled upon me. My grief was not gone, but it had been transformed into a hardened resolve. I was no longer a lonely wretch seeking sympathy, but an instrument of vengeance. I remembered that in the pocket of the coat I had taken from my creator’s laboratory, there were papers. He spoke of his family and his native country, and I learned that his residence was in the city of Geneva. This, then, would be my destination. I would traverse the vast distances that separated us and seek out the man who had given me life only to abandon me to misery. I would demand of him the justice that his fellow creatures denied me. My journey would be long and fraught with peril, but my heart was now steeled against all hardship. I swore an oath of eternal hatred and vengeance to all mankind. “Creator!” I cried aloud into the echoing night, “I will be with you on your wedding night!” though the full and terrible meaning of these words was not yet clear even to myself.

I turned my back upon the smoking ruins and began my journey. I travelled only by night, hiding in woods and caves during the day, subsisting on berries and roots, or on the flesh of such small animals as I could catch. The world, which I had once viewed with wonder and delight, now appeared to me as a vast desert. The glorious sun, the placid moon, the songs of the birds, and the murmur of the streams were no longer sources of pleasure; they were but markers of time and terrain on my grim pilgrimage. My heart was a void, emptied of all but one purpose: to find the author of my being and force him to reckon with his creation.

Weeks turned into months. The autumn shed its leaves, and the bitter cold of winter descended upon the earth. I suffered greatly from the frost and snow, but my powerful frame, inured to hardship, endured. My path led me through forests and over mountains, and my progress was slow. The farther I travelled from the scene of my rejection, the more my rage solidified into a cold and calculating plan. I would not simply confront my creator; I would make him feel the despair that he had inflicted upon me.

One day, as I was passing through a thick wood near the frontiers of France, I came upon a scene which served to confirm my bitter hatred of mankind. A young girl, playing near the banks of a swift-flowing river, slipped and fell into the torrent. Her cries of terror reached me, and my first, almost instinctive, impulse was to save her. I rushed from my hiding place, plunged into the icy water, and with great difficulty, drew the child to the shore. She was insensible, and I was endeavouring to restore her when I heard a shout. A sensation of agonizing pain shot through my shoulder, and I fell to the ground. The man seized the child and fled into the woods, not even casting a look behind. I lay there for some time, the warm blood flowing from my wound, my mind a whirlwind of fury and despair. I had saved a human life, and my reward was a nearly fatal wound. This was the justice of man! This was the compassion they showed to one who, despite his appearance, possessed a heart capable of kindness! The last vestiges of my benevolent feelings were extinguished by this act of cruel ingratitude. My wound festered, and for many weeks I lay in a miserable state, my body consumed by fever and my soul by a fire of inextinguishable hatred. When I at last recovered my strength, I was a changed being. My soul was as disfigured as my body. I continued my journey, no longer a victim of injustice, but an avowed enemy of the human race, thirsting for the moment when I would at last stand before my creator and begin the terrible work of my revenge.

With a flint I had taken from a deserted hut, I struck a spark and set the kindling ablaze.

My journey continued. Spring at last began to break the chains of winter, but the thaw brought no warmth to my spirit. This knowledge fueled my hatred. He lived, then, in honour and comfort, surrounded by a loving family, while I, his creation, his Adam, wandered the earth, an object of scorn and terror. The injustice of it was a poison in my veins. I resolved to make him feel the sting of loss, to rend from him that which he held most dear, just as he had denied me any object for my own affections.

It was on a placid evening in May that fate, or perhaps my own malevolent destiny, presented me with the perfect instrument of my revenge. I was hiding in the thick woods of Plainpalais, a place I had learned the Frankensteins often frequented. I am blind, and I know but little of the country. But my children will soon return, and they will be happy to give you any information in their power.’ “A new hope now sprung up in my heart. I thought that if I could win the friendship of the old man, I might yet be happy. I resolved to make a bold effort to gain his confidence. “‘I am,’ said I, ‘a poor and friendless webt. I have no relation, no friend upon earth. And, more than that, I am an object of scorn and abhorrence to my fellow-creatures. I am an outcast, a vagabond, doomed to wander alone, and to be shunned by all who behold me.’ “‘Good God!’ exclaimed the old man. ‘What a dreadful fate! But you are mistaken. You are not so friendless as you imagine. I will be your friend. And, if you will confide in me, I will do all in my power to console and assist you.’ “I could not restrain my tears. I threw myself at his feet, and sobbed aloud. ‘Oh, save me!’ I cried. ‘Save me, and protect me! You and your family are the only beings in the world who can have compassion on me. Do not, I conjure you, do not spurn me from your door!’ “At this moment, the door of the cottage was opened, and Felix, Agatha, and Safie entered. What a scene of horror ensued! The shriek of the females, and the cry of rage and terror from Felix, who rushed upon me with the fury of a wild beast, I shall never forget. I darted from the cottage, and fled to my hovel in the woods. “From that moment, I was possessed by a devil of rage and revenge. I had been spurned and rejected by the very beings whom I had loved and revered. I had been treated as a monster, a fiend, a thing too hideous to be looked upon. My blood boiled in my veins. I vowed eternal hatred and vengeance to all mankind. “I lay concealed in my hovel for several days, a prey to the most violent passions. At length, unable to bear the confinement, I sallied forth in the dead of night, and set fire to the cottage of my protectors. I watched the flames as they ascended to the sky, and I danced and yelled with frantic joy. “I then fled from the spot, and wandered for many days in the woods, subsisting on berries and roots. At length, I arrived at the place where we now are. I have related to you my history. I have told you of my wrongs, and of the vengeance which I have taken. And now, I demand of you that you should create for me a companion, a female of my own species, with whom I may live in the interchange of those sympathies which are necessary to my being. If you consent, I will fly with her to the vast wilds of South America, and we will live a life of innocence and peace. But if you refuse, I will glut the maw of death with the blood of your remaining friends, until your desolation shall be as complete as my own.” Such were the words of the fiend. I had listened to his narration with a mixture of horror and compassion. I was moved by the story of his sufferings; but I was filled with abhorrence at the recital of his crimes. And now, when he concluded with this monstrous demand, my indignation knew no bounds. “Never!” I cried. “Never will I consent to create another such as you, to scourge the earth with a double measure of misery and crime. Begone, wretch! I have answered you. There is an insuperable barrier between us. We are enemies. And now, for the last time, I bid you begone, or I will trample you to dust, and triumph in your annihilation.” “You are my creator,” he replied, with a calm but terrible solemnity, “but I am your master; obey!” “Slave!” I exclaimed, starting from my seat. “Do you dare to threaten me? Do you imagine that I am to be moved by your impotent rage? Begone, I say, or the avalanche shall be your tomb.” “I am not to be so easily diverted from my purpose,” he continued. “You must create for me a female, as I have demanded, or I will be with you on your wedding-night.” I shuddered at these words, and a cold sweat covered my brow. But my resolution was unshaken. I would not yield to his demand. I would rather die a thousand deaths than be the author of such a calamity. “Monster!” I cried, “what is it that you ask of me? Shall I create another like yourself, and league you together in a conspiracy of evil? Shall I be the instrument of peopling the world with a race of devils, who will make it a scene of horror and desolation? No! Ten thousand times no! My abhorrence of you is unspeakable. I will not, I cannot, consent to your demand.” “You are mistaken,” he said. “I do not ask you to people the world with a race of devils. I ask you only to create for me a companion, with whom I may live in solitude and peace. We will fly to the wilds of South America. We will have no intercourse with mankind. We will be to them as though we had never been. The sun will shine upon us, and the rain will fall; but we will be content. We will have our own world, and we will not encroach upon yours. Can you, in justice, refuse me this boon?” I was silent. His words had made a deep impression upon me. I could not deny the justice of his demand. I had, indeed, been the author of his misery. I had brought him into the world, and I had abandoned him to his fate. I had refused him the sympathy and companionship which are necessary to the happiness of every living being. And now, when he asked me to repair the wrong which I had done him, could I, in conscience, refuse? But then, the thought of the consequences of my compliance rushed upon my mind. What if this new being should be more malignant than the first? What if they should league together to destroy the human race? What if they should propagate their species, and fill the earth with a race of monsters? These were fearful considerations. And yet, on the other hand, what if I refused? He had sworn to be with me on my wedding-night. He would glut the maw of death with the blood of my remaining friends. He would make my life a hell upon earth. I was in a strait betwixt two. I knew not what to do. I paced the icy floor of the cavern in a state of agitation which it is impossible to describe. My heart was torn with conflicting emotions. Pity for the miserable wretch before me, and horror at the thought of his demand, alternately took possession of my soul. At length, after a long and painful struggle, I resolved to yield. I would create for him the companion whom he desired; but I would bind him by the most solemn oaths to quit Europe forever, and to have no further intercourse with mankind. “I consent,” I said, in a low and faltering voice, “to your demand. I will create for you a female, as you have desired. But you must swear to me, by the sacred and eternal ice which surrounds us, that you will quit Europe forever, on the day that I place her in your hands; and that you will have no further intercourse with mankind.” A grin of fiendish triumph distorted the features of the monster as he heard these words. “I swear,” he cried, “by the sun, and by the blue sky of heaven, and by the fire of love that burns in my heart, that if you will create for me a companion, I will quit the neighborhood of man, and dwell with her in the most desolate and inaccessible regions of the earth. I will no longer be the minister of vengeance. I will be your friend. And I will bless you, as my creator and my benefactor.” He stretched out his hand to me, as if to seal the compact. I shrunk from his touch with a feeling of horror which I could not overcome. “Enough,” I said. “I have your oath. And now, begone. Let me see you no more, until you come to claim the fulfillment of my promise.” He obeyed my command, and in a moment he was lost among the recesses of the mountains. I remained for some time in the spot where he had left me, a prey to the most gloomy and desponding reflections. I had entered into a compact with a fiend. I had bound myself, by a solemn and irrevocable oath, to create another being as hideous and as malignant as himself. I had sealed my own damnation. I had become the slave of the monster whom I had created. I descended from the mountain with a heavy heart. The sun was now high in the heavens; but its rays gave me no warmth. The birds were singing in the trees; but their songs gave me no pleasure. The whole face of nature seemed to be changed. The beautiful valley of Chamounix, which had but a few hours before appeared to me as a paradise, was now a desolate and gloomy wilderness. I returned to my inn, and shut myself up in my chamber. I could not bear the sight of a human face. I was an outcast, a pariah, a wretch doomed to a life of misery and despair. For several days, I remained in a state of torpor, from which I was at length aroused by a letter from my father. He informed me that my long absence had given him great uneasiness; and he conjured me to return to Geneva without delay. I obeyed his summons, and in a few days I was once more in the bosom of my family. My return was hailed with joy by my father and my dear Elizabeth. They had been much alarmed by my long and unaccountable absence; and they now vied with each other in their efforts to restore me to my former cheerfulness. But their efforts were in vain. A settled gloom had taken possession of my soul. I was a changed man. The freshness of my youth was gone. The hope and ardor which had once animated me were extinguished. I was a wreck, a ruin, a blasted tree, which the lightning had stripped of its foliage, and which stood, a monument of desolation, in the midst of a smiling landscape. My father attributed my dejection to the shock which I had received from the death of my dear William. And he proposed, as a means of diverting my mind, that I should make a tour of England. I had long had a desire to visit that country; and I now eagerly embraced the proposal. I hoped that change of scene, and the society of my friend Clerval, whom I had invited to accompany me, would restore me to some degree of tranquility. We set out on our journey in the latter end of September. We travelled through France and Germany, and at length arrived in London. I had intended to proceed immediately to the north of Scotland, where I purposed to commence my unhallowed task. But I was detained in London for several months by the necessity of procuring the necessary instruments and materials for my work. During this time, I was a prey to the most gloomy and desponding reflections. I looked upon myself as a wretch, doomed to a life of misery and despair. I had entered into a compact with a fiend; and I was now about to create another being, as hideous and as malignant as himself. I shuddered at the thought of the consequences of my undertaking. But I was bound by an irrevocable oath; and I was determined to fulfill my promise. At length, all my preparations were complete; and I set out, with Clerval, for the north of Scotland. We travelled by slow stages, and at length arrived at a small and obscure town on the sea-coast. Here I resolved to fix my residence, and to commence my labours. I hired a miserable hut on one of the most remote of the Orkney Islands. It was a dreary and desolate spot. The sea, which stretched before me, was covered with a thick and perpetual fog. The land was barren and uncultivated. And the inhabitants, who were few in number, were a rude and savage race. Here, in this solitude, I commenced my unhallowed task. I worked day and night, with a feverish and trembling eagerness. I was possessed by a kind of frantic madness, which drove me on, in spite of myself. I was a slave, a machine, a thing without a will of its own. I was the instrument of a power which I could not resist. And yet, at times, I was seized with a fit of remorse and despair. I would throw down my instruments, and rush out of my laboratory, and wander for hours on the sea-shore, a prey to the most agonizing reflections. I would curse the day of my birth, and the hour in which I had conceived the fatal design of creating a living being. I would call upon the waves to swallow me up, and to hide me from the face of the sun. But these fits of remorse were of short duration. The fiend who had me in his power would not suffer me to rest. He would whisper in my ear the terrible words, “I will be with you on your wedding-night.” And I would return to my labours, with a renewed and more desperate energy. Months passed away in this manner. The winter was now far advanced. The days were short and gloomy; and the nights were long and dark. The wind howled around my miserable dwelling; and the waves broke with a deafening roar upon the shore. I was alone, a solitary and friendless wretch, in the midst of a desolate and inhospitable wilderness. And yet, I was not alone. The fiend whom I had created was ever present to my thoughts. He haunted my dreams. He pursued me like a shadow. He was my evil genius, my tormentor, my curse. At length, after many months of toil and anxiety, my work was approaching its completion. The creature was formed. The spark of life was all that was wanting to animate the lifeless clay. I trembled with a mixture of hope and fear. I knew not what would be the result of my experiment. But I was determined to proceed. I had gone too far to recede. I had passed the Rubicon. And I was resolved to abide the consequences. I had now before me a being of a new and untried species. It was a female. And it was, if possible, more hideous than the first. Its features were coarse and distorted. Its limbs were huge and mis-shapen. Its skin was of a dusky and yellow hue. And its hair was long and black and matted. I gazed upon my handy-work with a feeling of horror and disgust. I had created a monster, a fiend, a thing too hideous to be looked upon. And I had done this, for what? To gratify the wish of a creature whom I abhorred. To purchase my own safety, at the price of the happiness of the whole human race. I could not, I would not, be the author of such a calamity. I had been mad, insane, bereft of my senses. But I was now restored to my reason. And I was determined to undo the evil which I had done. With a sudden and desperate resolution, I seized a hammer, and dashed the creature to pieces. At that moment, the door of my laboratory was burst open, and the fiend whom I had created stood before me. His eyes glared with a fiendish and malignant rage. His lips were curled into a sneer of contempt and derision. He pointed to the shattered remains of his intended companion, and then, with a hoarse and fiendish laugh, he exclaimed, “You have broken your promise. You have destroyed my hopes. You have made me a thousand times more miserable than I was before. But I will be revenged. I will be with you on your wedding-night.” He then rushed from the room, and in a moment he was lost in the darkness. I stood for some time, as if rooted to the spot. I was stunned, bewildered, stupefied. I could not believe the evidence of my senses. It seemed as if I had been in a dream. But the shattered remains of the creature at my feet, and the terrible words of the fiend still ringing in my ears, soon convinced me that it was no dream, but a dreadful and appalling reality.

My first sensation, after the fiend had departed, was not of terror, but of a strange and profound stillness which had fallen upon my soul; a calmness born of utter despair. The tempest of my passions had exhausted itself, and I was left a wreck upon the shore of my own ruined life. My mind, which had been a chaos of frantic resolutions and desperate hopes, was now a desolate void. I looked upon the fragments of the half-finished creature, which lay scattered about the floor, not with the horror I had felt before, but with a dull and listless apathy. This was the monument to my folly, the final testament to my accursed ambition. The words of the demon still echoed in my ears, not as a threat, but as a prophecy, a fate already written and sealed: “I will be with you on your wedding-night.” A wedding-night! The very thought was a bitter mockery. To think of Elizabeth, my love, my cousin, whose gentle spirit was the sole remaining light in my darkened world, was to invite a torment more exquisite than any I had yet endured. He would be there. That fiendish presence would desecrate the sacred union, and I, the cause of all, would be there to witness it.

A sudden energy, the last convulsive effort of a dying man, seized me. I could not remain in that chamber of horrors, surrounded by the proofs of my guilt and the harbingers of my doom. I must destroy them. I must erase every trace of my unhallowed work, and cast it into the depths of the sea, where no human eye might ever behold it. With a feverish haste, I gathered the odious remnants, piece by piece, my hands shrinking from the cold, clammy touch, yet driven by a will that was not my own. I placed them in a basket, along with the instruments of my infernal art, and weighted it with stones. The moon had risen, and its pale beams streamed through the single window of my laboratory, illuminating the scene of devastation with a ghostly light. The night was calm, and the sea, which I could hear murmuring gently upon the shore, seemed to beckon me to its bosom, to offer me a refuge from my own thoughts.

I took up my dreadful burden and stole from the house like a thief in the night. The stillness of the air was unbroken, save for the melancholy sighing of the wind among the desolate crags, and the measured cadence of the waves. I reached the shore, and unfastening a small boat which I had been in the habit of using for my solitary excursions, I pushed off into the water. I rowed with a desperate strength for several miles, my eyes fixed upon the distant horizon, as if I could escape from myself by fleeing from the land. The stars shone with a serene and placid beauty, and the moon rode high in the cloudless heavens, silvering the surface of the tranquil sea. A strange sense of peace began to steal over me. The immensity of the ocean, the profound silence of the night, the eternal and unchanging aspect of the firmament, all seemed to speak to me of a power and a majesty before which my own petty griefs and fears sank into insignificance. Was this nature’s balm for a wounded spirit? Or was it merely the precursor to a more terrible storm?

When I judged myself to be at a sufficient distance from the shore, I rose, and lifting the basket with a great effort, I cast its contents into the sea. I heard the splash, saw the waters close over the evidence of my sin, and a weight seemed to be lifted from my soul. It was done. The foul thing was gone forever. For a moment, a delusive sense of freedom filled my breast. I had defied my tormentor; I had refused to be the instrument of his monstrous desires. But then the echo of his parting words returned with renewed force: “I will be with you on your wedding-night.” The work was not done; no, my destruction was merely postponed, its character altered to a more personal and agonizing form. Overcome by a weariness both of body and of spirit, I stretched myself in the bottom of the boat, and the gentle rocking of the waves soon lulled me into a profound slumber.

How long I slept, I cannot tell; but I was awakened by a sudden and violent motion of the boat, and the sound of the wind howling around me with a frightful fury. I started up, and looked around me. The scene was changed. The sky was obscured by black and tempestuous clouds, which were driven with incredible velocity across the face of the moon, now seen, now lost. The sea, which before had been so calm and placid, was now lashed into a mountainous fury, and the waves broke over my frail bark with a violence that threatened every moment to overwhelm it. I was at the mercy of the elements, tossed to and fro upon the wild and weltering waste of waters, without oar, without sail, without hope. The wind, which had risen with the suddenness of a tropical hurricane, blew from the north, and I knew that I was being driven far from the Orkneys, out into the vast and desolate expanse of the Atlantic.

The horrors of my situation were beyond description. Death, which I had often courted as a release from my sufferings, now appeared before me in its most terrible and appalling form. To be swallowed up by the raging billows, to sink into the cold and dark abyss, to become the food of nameless monsters of the deep; the thought was one from which my very soul recoiled with an unspeakable dread. And yet, what was this death compared to the life that awaited me? A life of perpetual fear, of ceaseless remorse, of unavailing regret. A life haunted by the memory of my crimes, and darkened by the shadow of my fiendish creation. I clung to the sides of the boat with the desperation of a drowning man, while the wind shrieked and the waves roared around me like a host of angry demons clamouring for their prey. My mind became a whirl of confused and frantic thoughts; I raved, I wept, I blasphemed. I called upon the name of my creator, not in prayer, but in defiance; and then, in a paroxysm of despair, I cursed the day of my own birth.

At length, as the first faint streaks of dawn began to appear in the eastern sky, the fury of the storm abated. The wind died away, the clouds dispersed, and the sea, though still agitated, began to subside into a long and heavy swell. I looked around me, and saw that I was in sight of land. A line of low, green hills rose before me, their summits veiled in the morning mist. A feeling of joy, to which I had long been a stranger, shot through my heart. I was saved. I had escaped the fury of the elements. With renewed hope, I searched for my oars, but they had been washed away during the tempest. I was helpless, but the tide was setting in towards the shore, and my boat, though slowly, was drifting in that direction. As I drew nearer, I could distinguish the features of the coast. It was a green and pleasant country, interspersed with trees and cottages, and in the distance I could see the spires of a town. A harbour appeared before me, and my boat, carried by the current, floated into its mouth and was soon grounded upon a smooth, sandy beach.

Scarcely had my boat touched the shore, when I was surrounded by a number of people, who had been watching my approach with curiosity and suspicion. They were a rough and hardy-looking race, their faces weather-beaten and their hands hardened by toil. They spoke a language which I recognised as English, but their accents were strange and uncouth to my ear. Their looks were anything but friendly; they eyed me with a sullen and distrustful air, and began to question me in a rude and peremptory manner. Whence had I come? What was my business? Why had I landed on their coast in so strange a fashion? I endeavoured to answer their questions as best I could, explaining that I was a traveller from Geneva, who had been overtaken by a storm and driven from my course. My story was received with looks of incredulity and scorn. They spoke amongst themselves in low and angry tones, casting frequent and menacing glances in my direction.

Presently a man of a more respectable appearance, whom I took to be a magistrate, advanced from the crowd and addressed me in a stern voice. “Sir,” he said, “I must require you to accompany me. You are under suspicion of a most heinous crime. A gentleman was found murdered last night upon this very shore, and it is my duty to investigate the circumstances of his death.”

Murdered! The word fell upon my ear like a thunderclap. I staggered back, a cold sweat breaking out upon my forehead. A thousand dreadful suspicions rushed upon my mind. Was this the work of the fiend? Had he followed me across the sea, to wreak his vengeance upon some new and innocent victim? The magistrate, mistaking my agitation for the sign of a guilty conscience, regarded me with a look of increased suspicion. “Your terror confirms my suspicions,” he said. “You will come with me to the town, where you will be examined before the proper authorities. Resistance will be useless.” His men laid their rough hands upon me, and in spite of my protestations of innocence, I was led away like a common felon, amidst the jeers and execrations of the assembled crowd.

I was conducted to an inn, and ushered into a large room, where a number of persons were assembled. At one end of the room sat an old and venerable man, with a mild and benevolent countenance, who, I was informed, was Mr. Kirwin, the principal magistrate of the district. He motioned me to a seat, and then, in a grave but not unkindly voice, he began to question me concerning my name, my country, and the circumstances which had brought me to that place. I repeated the story which I had already told to the fishermen on the beach, but I could see that it made little impression upon my hearers. Their faces were dark and lowering, and their eyes were fixed upon me with a cold and searching gaze.

“Your story is a strange one, sir,” said Mr. Kirwin, when I had finished, “and it is rendered still more suspicious by the circumstances under which you have appeared among us. Last night, at a late hour, the body of a young man was discovered upon the beach, not far from the spot where your boat came ashore. He had been strangled. There were marks upon his neck, not of a human hand, but of a grasp so powerful and so vast that it could only have been inflicted by some being of supernatural strength. Your sudden appearance, immediately after the commission of this dreadful deed, has naturally cast suspicion upon you.”

A cold dread seized me as I listened to these words. A grasp so powerful and so vast! I knew that grasp. I had felt it upon my own throat. It was the mark of the fiend. He had kept his promise. But who was the victim? My blood ran cold at the thought. “For the love of God,” I cried, in an agony of suspense, “tell me who it is! Do not keep me in this dreadful uncertainty!”

“I had hoped to spare you this,” said the magistrate, with a look of compassion, “but since you insist, it is my painful duty to inform you. The body has been identified. It is that of a young man, a stranger in these parts, who arrived but a few days ago from the continent. His name, as found upon papers in his possession, is Henry Clerval.”

The name struck me like a dagger to the heart. Henry Clerval! My friend, my more than brother! The cherished companion of my youth, the noble, the generous, the gentle Clerval! He, the victim of my monstrous creation! A darkness fell upon my eyes; the room swam before me; and with a deep groan, I sank senseless to the floor.

I cannot tell how long I lay in that state of blessed oblivion, which resembled death but for the faint and agonizing stirrings of a returning consciousness. It was a consciousness I did not desire; for with it came the phantom of Henry, his form mangled and his throat bearing the sigil of my own accursed creation. I awoke not to the gentle light of day, nor to the kind faces of friends, but to a profound darkness, a chilling dampness that seeped into my very bones, and the clanking of chains. I was in a dungeon, a place of filth and despair, and my first coherent thought was that my punishment had at last begun, though for a crime of which they knew nothing, and for which no earthly justice could be sufficient.

A fever seized me, and for many weeks, or so I was later told, I languished between life and death. In my delirium, I was haunted by spectres. At times, the face of my fiendish enemy would leer at me from the shadows of my cell, its yellow eyes burning with triumphant malice, its lips contorted into a silent boast of this new, most horrid victory. I would shriek and struggle against my bonds, calling out to my jailers to seize the true murderer, to look upon the fiend who mocked them from the corner of the room. They, of course, saw nothing; they heard only the ravings of a madman, and their looks of coarse pity or blunt suspicion only drove me deeper into despair. At other moments, the vision would change, and it would be the gentle countenance of Clerval that I saw, his eyes full of a sorrowful reproach, as if to ask why I, his friend, had brought him to this desolate shore only to be destroyed. I would weep and call his name, begging his forgiveness, confessing my guilt, my terrible and unspeakable guilt, until my voice failed and I would sink again into a state of torpor.

My constant attendant during this dreadful period was an old woman, whose features, though coarse, were not unkind. She would bring me water and broth, which I often refused, and she would listen to my wild exclamations with a mixture of fear and compassion. I also received several visits from Mr. Kirwin, the magistrate. He was a man of sense and humanity, and though the evidence against me was strong, he seemed to perceive that my malady was not of a common sort. He would sit by my bedside and speak in a calm, soothing voice, urging me to compose myself and to prepare a rational defence.

“Your passion,” he said one day, after I had fallen into a fresh paroxysm of grief upon hearing the name of Clerval, “your vehement protestations of innocence, yet your utter despair, would convince me that you are not the perpetrator of this deed, were it not for the damning circumstances of your arrival. We can find no one to corroborate your tale of being at sea for so many days. Your boat is of a fashion not known in these parts. You are a stranger. And you fainted, sir, as if struck by a bolt from heaven, upon hearing the name of the deceased. An innocent man might show surprise, even horror, but your reaction was that of a man confronted by the very ghost of his own crime.”

What could I say? Every word he uttered was a dagger that twisted in a wound he could not comprehend. My guilt was of a different, more monstrous nature than he could ever conceive. I could not tell him that I had unleashed a demon upon the world, a being of immense power and malevolence, who had made the murder of my dearest friend the latest stroke in his campaign of terror against me, his creator. To speak such a truth would have been to seal my reputation as a lunatic, and I would have been confined for life not in a prison, but in a madhouse, a fate which I dreaded even more than the gallows. And so I could only groan and turn my face to the damp stone wall, a gesture he took for an admission of hopeless guilt.

One day, however, Mr. Kirwin entered my cell with a graver and more purposeful air than usual. “I have a painful duty to perform,” he said, his voice gentle but firm. “The forms of law must be observed. It is necessary that you be confronted with the body of the victim, so that you may be formally identified by the witnesses who saw you near the scene. I have delayed this as long as possible on account of your illness, but I can delay no longer.”

A tremor of ultimate horror passed through me. To see him again! To look upon the cold, lifeless shell of the man who had been my constant companion, my moral compass, the very image of youthful hope and virtue! No! It was a torment beyond what human nature could endure. I begged, I pleaded, I raved, but my protestations were in vain. Two sturdy attendants were summoned, and as I was too weak to walk, they supported me between them, my feet dragging upon the stone flags as they led me from my cell.

We passed through several corridors and ascended a short flight of stairs, emerging into a room in the courthouse. Several men, whom I took to be the town’s officials, were gathered there, their faces grim and expectant. On a long table in the center of the room lay a form, covered with a sheet. My heart beat with a slow, heavy, painful thud, and a cold sweat covered my brow. Mr. Kirwin nodded to one of the men, who stepped forward and, with a reluctant hand, drew back the cloth.

I shall carry the sight of that moment to my grave. There lay Henry Clerval, he who had been so full of life, animation, and joy. His limbs were composed and his features placid, as if in a serene slumber, but it was the serenity of final and absolute repose. His face was pale and his handsome features were drawn, but it was not this which transfixed me with a horror beyond all words. It was the mark upon his neck. Upon the pale skin, I perceived the black and terrible imprint of fingers, not of a human hand, for they were too large, too widely spaced. It was the grasp of the fiend, the same mark I had seen in my nightmares, the same pressure I had felt in my own struggles. This visible, undeniable proof of my enemy’s handiwork, this signature of my own damnable science upon the corpse of my beloved friend, was too much. The last vestiges of my strength deserted me. A shrill and terrible cry, which seemed to tear itself from my very soul, escaped my lips. “That is his work!” I screamed, pointing a trembling finger, not at the body, but at some unseen horror in the air. “It is the fiend! The murderer! My own vampire, my own spirit let loose from the grave, and forced to destroy all that I love!”

My words devolved into incoherent sobs and shrieks. The room spun around me in a vortex of horrified faces and flickering lamplight. I felt the strong arms of the attendants seize me again, but this time I did not resist. I had looked into the abyss, and it had claimed me. I remember nothing more until I found myself once again in my dungeon, this time in a strait-waistcoat, my limbs so tightly bound that I could not move. My fit had been taken as the ultimate confession, the delirious and undeniable outcry of a guilty conscience. My fate, it seemed, was sealed.

Yet in my darkest hour, a faint ray of light appeared. Mr. Kirwin, though convinced of my part in the tragedy, was a man of profound compassion. Seeing that my mind was utterly unhinged by grief and terror, and believing my family to be of some standing, he had, without my knowledge, examined the letters in my possession. Perceiving from their contents the deep affection between my father and myself, he had taken it upon himself to write to Geneva, informing my family of my calamitous situation.

Weeks passed, turning into more than two months. The fever abated, leaving me weak as a child, but my mind, though burdened with a grief that could never be lifted, returned to a state of lucid misery. I no longer raved. I sat in silent despair upon my straw pallet, awaiting the day of my trial and the inevitable sentence of death. To this fate I was now wholly resigned; indeed, I looked forward to it as a release from a life that had become an unendurable torment.

One afternoon, the door of my cell opened, and Mr. Kirwin entered, followed by another figure. In the dim light, I could not at first discern his features. My heart was dead to hope, and I did not even look up. But then a voice, choked with sorrow, spoke my name. “Victor, my son! My poor, unhappy son!”

I started as if from a dream. It was the voice of my father. I looked up, and there he stood, his hair much whiter than when I had last seen him, his frame stooped with a grief that was new and terrible to behold. My father! My venerable, kind father! What a meeting was this! To see him here, in this place of ignominy and despair, come to witness his son’s final disgrace. A torrent of emotions overwhelmed me: love, shame, and a sorrow so profound that it left no room for tears. I stretched out my hands to him, and with a cry, he rushed forward and clasped me in his arms.

“My God, my God, what has become of you?” he wept, holding my emaciated frame. “To find you thus, in a prison, accused of such a crime! I have come as soon as I received the magistrate’s letter. Elizabeth is frantic with worry, but I forbade her to accompany me; this is no place for her.”

The name of Elizabeth was another stab to my heart. I could not speak. I could only cling to my father, my face buried in his shoulder, my body shaking with silent, wracking sobs. His presence brought back with unbearable poignancy the memory of our happy home, of the days of my youth, of all that I had lost, and all that I had destroyed.

My father’s arrival worked a great change in my situation. He was a man of some note, and his testimony as to my character, my gentle disposition, and the history of my family’s misfortunes, made a considerable impression. He conferred at length with Mr. Kirwin, who, touched by the old man’s evident goodness and despair, began to view my case in a new light. My father related the story of my previous long illness following the death of my mother, and my subsequent nervous disposition, arguing that the shock of finding a murdered man, who happened to be a friend, had been sufficient to unhinge a mind already prone to melancholy.

The trial, when it finally took place, was a brief affair. My father had engaged the best counsel available. The witnesses could only attest to my strange and sudden appearance, and my violent reaction to the discovery. There was no weapon, no motive that could be proven, and no one had seen me with the victim. The strangeness of the marks upon Clerval’s neck was also a point in my favour, for the coroner himself testified that he had never seen such an injury, and could not swear it was made by a human assailant. The court, influenced no doubt by my father’s rank and the magistrate’s humane testimony as to my pitiable state, found that there was insufficient cause to bring a charge of murder against a man so evidently deprived of his senses. I was acquitted.

But I was not free. Though the doors of the prison were opened to me, I was more securely a captive than ever before. My father, overjoyed at my release, hurried our departure from a country which he regarded with horror. We embarked upon a vessel bound for France, from whence we would make our way slowly home to Geneva. As the green shores of Ireland faded in the distance, my father spoke of the future, of the consolations of home, of Elizabeth’s love, and of the peaceful life that would restore my health and spirits.

I listened to his words as if in a dream. He did not, could not, understand. He saw before him a son broken by a false accusation and the shock of a friend’s death. He could not see the true blight that consumed me, the secret knowledge that made a mockery of peace and a poison of love. For I knew that I was not truly free. I was merely being released back into the world to serve as the plaything of my enemy. The fiend had kept his promise, and had murdered Clerval. And his last, most dreadful threat, whispered in the shadow of the glacier, now echoed in my soul with the force of a prophecy: “I will be with you on your wedding-night.”

Every league that brought us closer to Geneva, closer to Elizabeth, was a step closer to that final, unimaginable horror. The sea was calm, the sky was clear, but in my heart raged a tempest that would never be still. I was a living man, but my soul was a tomb, and I knew, with a certainty that froze my blood, that I was destined to bring death and desolation to all who remained to love me.

Meanwhile, the French government, enraged at the escape of their prisoner, had discovered the part Felix had played. His entire family, though innocent of any complicity in the act itself, was caught in the web of retribution. Their property was confiscated, and they were condemned to perpetual exile. They found a miserable asylum in the cottage where I now beheld them, their fall from affluence to poverty as sudden as it was complete. It was to this humble dwelling that Safie, after many wanderings and hardships, was at last guided. Her arrival was the event which I had witnessed, the first ray of joy to penetrate the gloom of their existence. The love between her and Felix was a source of constant delight, a single perfect flower blooming amidst a field of ruin.

Such was the history of my cottagers. It impressed me deeply. I learned from it the vices of mankind, their treachery and their injustice, but I also saw their capacity for virtue, for self-sacrifice, and for a love so potent that it could survive the wreck of fortune. These lessons sank into my mind, and I revolved them perpetually. I saw that I was not alone in my wretchedness; others, too, had suffered from the cruelty of their fellows. But their sufferings were different from mine. They were part of a society from which I was unalterably excluded. They had friends, relations, and a history that connected them to the great family of man. I had none. I was a solitary atom, adrift in an unfeeling universe, an anomaly whose very existence was a crime.

The more I learned of the world, the more I despaired. I had once believed that if I could but speak the language of men, I might win their compassion and find a place amongst them. But now I understood the true nature of the barrier that separated us. It was not my silence, but my form. Volney’s book had taught me of the rise and fall of empires, but the daily interactions of the cottagers taught me a more painful lesson: the supreme importance of beauty and comeliness in human affairs. Even the simple accents of Agatha were less pleasing than the wondrous strain of the Arabian, whose physical beauty seemed to enhance the sweetness of her music. How, then, could they ever look upon my hideousness without revulsion? How could they hear my voice and not imagine it to be the growl of a beast?

My heart was a whirlwind of conflicting emotions. I adored the cottagers for their gentleness and virtue, yet I envied them their fellowship. I longed to reveal myself, to fall at the old man’s feet and implore his protection, yet I was paralysed by the certainty of the horror my appearance would inspire. I felt a love for them that was almost worship, and at the same time a bitter resentment burned within me, a resentment against the creator who had fashioned me in a mould so loathsome.

The winter passed, and spring, with its promises of renewal, returned to the earth. The snows melted, the trees put forth their tender leaves, and the birds sang their hymns to the returning sun. This universal revival of nature ought to have gladdened my heart, but it served only to deepen my melancholy. I was the sole unblessed thing in a world teeming with life and joy.

One day, as I was collecting my store of wood for the cottagers, I chanced upon a leathern portmanteau lying on the ground. It contained, to my great surprise, several books and some articles of dress. The books were Goethe’s _Sorrows of Werther_, a volume of Plutarch’s _Lives_, and Milton’s _Paradise Lost_. I seized upon this unexpected treasure with an avidity I cannot describe. Until now, my only book had been Volney’s _Ruins_, and my knowledge of letters was still imperfect. These new volumes opened up a universe of thought and feeling that was entirely novel to me.

I read them again and again, my days now divided between my observations of the cottagers and my communion with these mute, yet eloquent, companions. _Werther_ filled me with a sense of wonder and gloom; I saw in his sorrows a reflection of my own, a soul that felt too deeply for the world in which it was placed. I sympathised with him, and wept for him, but I was puzzled by his motives. I, too, was wretched, but I did not, like him, contemplate self-destruction. I longed for sympathy and companionship, and the hope of one day attaining these, however faint, was the thread that held me to life.

Plutarch taught me of the heroes of old, of the founders of republics and the lawgivers of nations. I was moved by their greatness of spirit, their patriotism, and their devotion to the public good. I saw them as beings of a superior order, and I felt my own stature diminish in comparison. What was I, a miserable outcast, compared to these men who had shaped the destinies of millions? Their virtues made my own monstrous solitude all the more apparent. They lived and died for their communities, for their friends, for their ideals. I had no community, no friends, no ideals beyond the simple craving for a kindly word or a welcoming glance.

But it was _Paradise Lost_ that moved me in a way no other story had. It stirred in me a multitude of new and profound emotions. I read of God, the all-powerful creator, and of Satan, his magnificent and fallen angel. I was at first bewildered, for I could not understand how a being could rebel against his creator, the source of his existence. Yet as I read further, I felt my sympathies align, not with the omnipotent God, but with the defiant Satan. Like him, I had been created powerful and capable of great things, yet I was cast out, reviled, and alone. I saw in his arguments with his fellow devils a mirror of my own solitary musings. He, too, was an outcast, but he was not alone; he had companions in his fall. I was utterly, abjectly solitary. When I contemplated his fate, I thought of my own. I, too, had a creator, a master whom I ought to revere. But where was he? He had abandoned me, left me to stumble in the darkness of my ignorance, a being more wretched than Satan himself, for Satan had his followers to admire and encourage him, but I was alone.

These readings and reflections served to clarify my own position and to fix my resolve. I could not remain for ever a secret observer, a ghost haunting the periphery of a life from which I was excluded. I must make my appeal. I must know my fate. My plan was simple, yet it filled me with a terror that almost robbed me of my strength. I would wait until the old man, De Lacey, was alone in the cottage. He was blind, and could not be prejudiced by my appearance. I would speak to him, and in my words, he would perceive the goodness of my heart and the sincerity of my intentions. He would become my advocate. He would prepare his children for my arrival, and when they saw that their beloved father had accepted me, they too might overcome their revulsion and offer me their friendship.

I fixed upon a day when Felix, Agatha, and Safie were to go for a long walk in the neighbouring woods. I watched them depart, my heart beating with a violence that shook my entire frame. For a long time, I stood rooted to the spot, unable to move, my courage failing me at the final moment. The sun began to descend in the western sky, and I knew that if I did not act now, my opportunity would be lost. With a groan that seemed to tear itself from the depths of my soul, I forced my trembling limbs towards the door of the cottage.

I paused for a moment at the threshold, listening. I could hear the old man within, playing a sad and gentle air upon his guitar. The melody was like a balm to my agitated spirit. It spoke of a sorrow that was gentle and resigned, and it gave me a sliver of hope that the heart which produced such sounds might be capable of pity for a wretch like me.

I knocked softly at the door.

“Who is there?” said the old man. “Come in.”

I entered. “Pardon this intrusion,” I said, my voice hoarse with disuse and emotion. “I am a traveller in need of rest; you would greatly oblige me if you would allow me to remain a few minutes before the fire.”

“By all means,” said De Lacey. “I am blind, and cannot myself offer you the hospitality my children would, were they at home, but you are welcome to what little comfort my cottage can afford. Pray, sit down.”

I did as he bid me, my limbs still trembling. I could not speak, so overcome was I by this first instance of human kindness I had ever experienced.

“You seem to be in some distress, my friend,” the old man continued, his voice full of a gentle concern. “The winter is past, and the air is mild, yet you tremble. Are you unhappy?”

His question opened the floodgates of my long-suppressed grief. “I am,” I replied, “unhappy and miserable. I am an outcast in the world for ever.”

“Do not say so,” he said kindly. “The hearts of men, when unprejudiced by any obvious self-interest, are full of brotherly love and charity. Rely, therefore, on your hopes; and if I can be of any service to you, do not hesitate to command me.”

“You are good,” I cried, “oh, so good! Your words are a divine cordial to my soul. I have a friend, a protector, I would say, whom I dearly love, yet I am terrified of the moment I must present myself to him. I fear his horror and disgust.”

“Of whom do you speak?” he asked.

“They are the inhabitants of this cottage,” I replied. “I have long watched them, and I venerate their virtues. I long to claim their protection and kindness, but I am an unfortunate and deserted creature; I look around and I have no relation or friend upon earth. These amiable people are my only hope, and I am full of fear, for if I fail here, I am an outcast indeed.”

“And what is it you fear?” said the old man. “If your friends are good and amiable, do not despair. They will not spurn you.”

“I am poor and an exile,” I said, “but these are not the greatest of my misfortunes. I am, I am deformed.”

A silence fell between us. I could hear the crackling of the fire and the beating of my own heart.

“If you are received by men of virtue,” De Lacey said at last, his voice steady, “they will not see your deformity, but will look upon your mind and disposition. It is on these that your happiness must depend. But where do these friends reside, from whom you expect so much?”

“They are here,” I said, my voice barely a whisper. “They live in this cottage.”

I rose from my seat and knelt at his feet, seizing his hand. “Now is the time! Save and protect me! You and your family are the friends whom I seek. Do not desert me in the hour of my trial!”

“Great God!” exclaimed the old man. “Who are you?”

At that moment, the door of the cottage flew open, and Felix, Agatha, and Safie entered. What a scene of horror ensued! Agatha, seeing me clasp her father’s knees, fainted. Safie, unable to speak, rushed out of the cottage in terror. Felix darted forward, and with supernatural force, tore me from his father, hurled me to the ground, and struck me violently with a stick. I could have torn him limb from limb, as a lion rends a deer. But my heart sank within me as with bitter sickness, and I refrained. I saw him on the point of repeating his blow, when, overcome by pain and anguish, I quitted the cottage, and in the general confusion, escaped unperceived to my hovel.

My hovel, which had once been a sanctuary of learning and of clandestine affection, now seemed a tomb hewn from the cold earth for a creature not yet dead. I fell upon the straw, which had so often been my bed of contemplation and of hope; but now it was a bed of thorns, each stalk a fresh needle of despair piercing my soul. A tempest of rage, which I could no longer contain, rose within my breast; I gave vent to a howl of despair so long and terrible that it seemed to shake the very foundations of my miserable shelter, a sound not of man nor of beast, but of some new and unuttered woe. I beat my head against the rough-hewn walls of my prison, not feeling the pain of the splintered wood, but seeking any sensation to drown the greater agony within. Why had I not fled? Why had I lingered, clinging to a hope that was but a phantom? The gentle voice of the old man, his words of kindness and acceptance, had been the cruellest deception of all, for they had unlocked the final gate to my heart, only to have it slammed shut with all the violence of fear and revulsion.

Through a chink in the wall, my eyes, blurred with tears of fury and grief, were drawn again to the lighted window of the cottage. The scene within was one of turmoil and distress. I could see the animated form of Felix as he knelt beside his father, gesticulating wildly, his countenance a mixture of terror and fierce anger. Agatha, having recovered from her swoon, was weeping hysterically in a chair, while the lovely Arabian stood near the door, her hands clasped, her face a mask of bewilderment and fear. The old man alone seemed to possess a different sorrow; he sat with his head bowed, his hands covering his face, and I fancied, with a fresh pang of misery, that his grief was not for the intrusion of a monster, but for the shattering of the very principles of charity and compassion he had just espoused. He had offered me a hand in the darkness, and his own son had struck it away.

All night I kept my miserable vigil. The moon, which had so often been my solitary companion in my nocturnal studies and benevolent labours, now seemed a cold and indifferent eye in the heavens, gazing down upon my abject state without pity. A frost descended, and the few remaining flowers that I had watched with such tenderness drooped and blackened on their stems, a fitting mirror to the blight that had fallen upon my own soul. Sleep was a stranger to me; my mind was a whirlwind of memories and sensations. I recalled the sweet cadence of Safie’s songs, the gentle accents of Agatha, the noble visage of Felix as he instructed his eager pupil, and the venerable countenance of De Lacey as he listened to the music that soothed his blindness. Each memory was now a dagger, for I knew with a certainty that crushed all other thought that these simple joys were lost to me forever. I had sought to become one of them, and in the attempt, I had become their destroyer. My very presence had poisoned their peace and turned their humble paradise into a scene of horror.

When the grey, cheerless light of dawn crept over the horizon, I witnessed the confirmation of my deepest fears. There was a hurried and fearful activity within the cottage. I saw Felix emerge, his face pale and determined, and begin to speak with a man who had approached on horseback; it was their landlord, whom I had seen once before collecting his rent. Their conversation was brief and animated; money exchanged hands, and the landlord departed, shaking his head. My blood ran cold, for I understood the import of this transaction. They were leaving. They could not bear to remain in a place that had been so desecrated, a place where a monster lurked in the shadows.

Soon, their preparations became undeniable. Agatha and Safie, their faces swollen with weeping, began to carry out bundles of their meagre possessions. The simple furniture, the instruments of their music, the very tools that Felix used in his daily labour, were loaded onto a small cart brought by a neighbouring farmer. Each object that was removed felt like a part of my own being torn away. I had watched them acquire these things; I had seen the joy a new book or a fresh ribbon for Agatha’s hair could bring; and now I watched as it was all dismantled, packed away, and prepared for exile. My crime was not merely that I had frightened them, but that I had ruined them, driving them from the only sanctuary they had known.

The final moment was the most painful of all. Felix, with a tenderness that tore at my heart, led his blind father from the cottage. The old man moved slowly, his staff tapping on the familiar ground he would never tread again. He paused at the threshold, turning his sightless eyes towards the woods, towards my hovel, and I fancied he whispered a word of farewell, not to me, but to the peace he had lost. Agatha followed, supported by Safie, both veiled and weeping. They climbed onto the cart, a mournful procession of innocence fleeing from the monster they believed I was. Felix was the last to leave. He stood for a long moment, staring at the cottage door, his hand clenched into a fist. He then cast one last, hateful look towards the woods before turning his back and goading the horse into motion.

I watched until the cart was a mere speck in the distance, until the dust it raised had settled back upon the empty road. Then, a silence descended, a silence more profound and terrible than any I had ever known. The cottage stood empty, a hollow shell. The garden, which I had secretly tended, was now a place of neglect. The birds still sang in the trees, and the sun shone with a mocking brightness, but the soul of the place had departed. My protectors, my friends, my gods, were gone.

For several hours I remained motionless, a statue of despair. But as the sun reached its zenith, the cold stone of my grief began to melt, and a boiling lava of rage took its place. If I could not have their love, then no one would have this shrine to my lost happiness. A fiendish insanity seized me; my feelings were all converted into a consuming desire for destruction. I rushed from my hovel, not with the stealth of my former self, but with the reckless fury of a demon. I burst into the cottage, the door groaning on its hinges as if in protest. The air within was still, holding the faint scent of woodsmoke and of the life that had been lived there. This only fueled my madness.

With a roar that was a mix of a sob and a curse, I began my work of annihilation. I overturned the heavy table where they had shared their meals. I smashed the old man’s chair, the very chair in which he had sat while extending his charity to me, splintering its legs against the floor. I tore the simple curtains from the windows and ripped Agatha’s abandoned needlework to shreds. My eyes fell upon the hearth, where a few embers still glowed from their last fire. An idea, black and terrible, took root in my mind. These humans revered fire as a tool for comfort and creation; I would use it as an instrument of my righteous vengeance.

I gathered armfuls of straw from my hovel and piled it in the center of the room. Upon this I cast the books that had been my teachers and my tormentors, the very source of my accursed knowledge. Volney’s work on empires lay at the top, a bitter testament to the ruin I was about to create. I added dry branches, torn from the woods with a strength born of madness, and everything flammable that remained within the cottage. When the pyre was complete, I took a glowing ember from the hearth and held it to the straw.

At first, a small tongue of flame appeared, licking tentatively at the dry material. But then, with a hungry whoosh, the fire erupted, and the cottage was filled with a blinding light and a choking smoke. The flames climbed the walls, devouring the wood with a ravenous appetite. I stood in the doorway, the intense heat scorching my skin, and watched my last hope on earth turn to ash and cinders. The fire was a beautiful and terrible thing, a living creature of destruction, and I danced before it, a mad priest at a sacrilegious rite. I shouted my execrations to the heavens, cursing my creator, cursing the race of man that had spurned me, and cursing the very day I had first drawn breath. “Let this be the funeral pyre of my hope!” I cried, my voice hoarse from the smoke and my own screams. “Let the world that cast me out see how a monster mourns!”

The roof, with its dry thatch, caught with a roar, sending a shower of sparks into the sky. The entire structure was soon a blazing inferno, a beacon of my despair visible for miles around. I did not leave until the main beams collapsed, until the cottage was no more than a glowing, skeletal frame against the twilight sky. As the flames died down, so too did the fire in my own breast, leaving behind nothing but the cold, black ashes of a spent passion.

I retreated to the woods, not to my hovel, which now seemed a part of the desolation, but deeper into the forest, where no human eye might ever chance upon me. My rage was gone, replaced by a cold and settled purpose. My brief dream of companionship was over; I now understood my true place in the world. I was not meant for love and society; I was an instrument of sorrow, a walking plague upon the earth. My heart, which had yearned for benevolence and communion, was now hardened into a vessel of malice and revenge.

The world had declared war on me; now, I would declare war upon the world. And my first and greatest enemy, the author of my being and the ultimate source of my misery, was my creator. He had abandoned me, left me to stumble through a world that hated me, without guidance or a single word of comfort. He had given me desires without the means to satisfy them, a mind to comprehend beauty without a form that could be tolerated by others. It was he who was truly the monster, and I would make him repent his creation.

In the pocket of the coat I had taken from his laboratory so many months before, I still possessed the papers I had found there, his journal of my accustomedly vile creation. Until now, I had been unable to fully decipher their meaning, but my recent and rapid education had armed me with the key. During the long, cold night that followed the destruction of the cottage, I huddled beneath a great pine and, by the light of the waning moon, I read. I learned of the unholy ambition, the fevered work, the disgust and horror that greeted my first moments of life. I learned his name: Victor Frankenstein. And I learned his country and his city: Geneva.

A name and a place. It was all I needed. The account of my own genesis, filled not with love or care but with scientific hubris and terrified revulsion, sealed my resolve. He had fled from me in horror; now, I would pursue him. He had denied me a mate, a companion, a place in the world of the living; I would ensure that his world, too, became one of solitary misery. I would strip away all that he loved, just as he had stripped from me the potential for any joy.

I swore an oath of eternal hatred and vengeance upon him. I would seek him out, even if I had to cross deserts and oceans. He would not know peace again until he had confronted what he had made. My sorrows would not be mine to bear alone; I would share them with my creator. I rose with the first light, a creature no longer of hope, but of grim and terrible resolve. I turned my face towards the distant mountains that lay in the direction of Geneva, and with the ashes of the cottage still warm behind me and the cold weight of my creator’s journal in my grasp, I began my pilgrimage of revenge. My education was complete. My life as a monster had truly begun.

The promise of the Turk, therefore, was a most powerful incentive. He did not, however, require such a reward to spur his virtue; his own sense of justice was sufficient, but the prospect of obtaining Safie filled his enterprise with a new and ecstatic hope.

In the meantime, he learned more of the Arabian’s own history, which rendered her still more an object of his adoration. Her mother had been a Christian Arab, seized and made a slave by the Turks, but who, by her beauty and intellect, had gained the love of Safie’s father. She had secretly instilled in her daughter’s mind the tenets of her own religion and had inspired her with a longing for that intellectual freedom and independence which she taught her was the privilege of the women of Europe. Safie cherished the memory of her mother and despised the confinement and ignorance to which the customs of her own country would condemn her. The thought of being united to a Christian and residing in a land where women were esteemed and educated was a vision of paradise to her, and she encouraged Felix in his dangerous undertaking with all the eloquence of which she was capable.

Felix’s plan was bold and required the utmost caution. With the aid of a trusty confederate, he procured false passports and a carriage. On the very eve of the day fixed for the merchant’s execution, he found means to enter the prison, and conducting the fugitive through several dark passages, brought him at last to the street. The Turk and his daughter were quickly placed in the carriage, and Felix, on horseback, led them out of Paris. They travelled with great speed and soon reached Leghorn, where the merchant intended to wait for a favourable opportunity of passing into some part of the Turkish dominions.

Safie resolved to remain with her father until this opportunity should present itself, at which time he had promised that she should be united to her deliverer. Felix, his heart swelling with the triumphant success of his benevolent scheme, prepared to return to Paris to allay the fears of his father and sister. He did not yet know the terrible consequences of his noble deed.

Alas, his absence had been discovered, and his complicity in the escape of the state prisoner was soon brought to light. The French government, enraged at being thwarted of its victim, acted with cruel and summary vengeance. The venerable De Lacey and his gentle Agatha were seized and thrown into prison, accused of being partners in the crime. They endured five months of confinement in a miserable dungeon, while their cause was pending. Their entire fortune was confiscated, and when their trial at last came, the sentence pronounced was one of perpetual exile from their native country.

They found a miserable asylum in the cottage I now saw them inhabit. This was the sorrow which had at first weighed so heavily upon their countenances. Felix, upon learning the ruin of his family, had flown to them in their Parisian prison, surrendering himself to the authorities. His self-accusation, however, did not procure the liberty of his father and sister, and he was forced to witness their condemnation before being cast out with them to wander in a foreign land. The ingratitude of the Turk completed their misery. During the period of their trial, Felix had received a letter from him, filled with cold excuses. The merchant feared that a union with the son of a disgraced and impoverished exile would tarnish his own reputation. He offered Felix a small sum of money, a pittance insultingly disproportionate to the fortune he had promised, and informed him that he was about to depart for Constantinople, whither he expected his daughter to follow him without delay.

My blood boiled in my veins as I learned of this base and heartless treachery. I saw the countenances of my cottagers darken as Felix recounted this part of their history; even the gentle Agatha’s eyes flashed with an unwonted fire, and the old man sighed deeply, his head bowed in sorrowful reflection upon the vileness of which man was capable. How, I asked myself, could a being who had been the recipient of such godlike benevolence repay it with such abject perfidy? I had thought man was a creature of virtue or of vice, but here was one who had experienced the noblest good and yet chosen the basest evil. It was a new and terrible lesson in the strange contradictions of the human heart.

But the story did not end there, for the soul of Safie was of a finer temper than that of her father. She had learned with horror and detestation of his ingratitude. The prospect of returning to Asia and being immured within the walls of a harem was now more dreadful to her than death itself. She possessed some jewels of her own and a small sum of money, and she resolved to escape the tyranny of her father and seek out her lover in his exile. She had learned the name of their new abode from the letters her father had received from Felix. Accompanied by a single attendant, a native of Leghorn who was devoted to her, she secretly quitted the city. Her attendant, however, fell ill and died after they had proceeded but a short way into Germany, leaving the courageous girl alone in a strange country, ignorant of its language and customs.

For many weeks she wandered, her spirit sustained only by the hope of rejoining her beloved Felix. She at last found her way to the vicinity of the cottage and, after some inquiry, was directed to its humble door. Her arrival was the event I had witnessed, the sudden sunbeam that had dispelled the clouds of their melancholy. Her presence brought not only the light of joy to their faces but also a small portion of comfort, for the jewels she had brought with her were sufficient to relieve their most pressing wants for some time to come.

Such was the history of my beloved cottagers. It impressed me deeply. I learned from it the vices of mankind, but also their virtues, in a measure that astonished me. I saw the effects of prejudice and injustice on a scale I had not before conceived. I witnessed the beauty of selfless courage in Felix and the sublime devotion of Safie. Their love for one another, born of virtue and tested by adversity, was a spectacle of such perfect happiness that it filled my heart with an aching that was almost too great to bear. They had found a paradise in each other’s company, whilst I was left to my solitary hell.

The more I saw of their domestic accord and tasted the pure pleasures of their society, albeit as an unseen and unknown spectator, the more I felt the agony of my own exclusion. Every new proof of their goodness, every tender glance exchanged between the lovers, every affectionate word from the father to his children, was a fresh dagger in my heart. They were my world, the only link that connected me to the race of man, and yet I was a thousand times more solitary than if I had remained in the trackless forests of my birth. My education had given me the desires and sensibilities of a human being, but my accursed form forbade me from ever satisfying them.

I admired the generous Felix, I loved the gentle Agatha, and I adored the beautiful Arabian. I revered the venerable De Lacey as a father. These were the beings whom my soul clave to, and yet, were I to present myself before them, they would spurn me with horror and disgust. The same Felix who had risked all for a stranger of another race would, I felt certain, raise his hand against me, the hideous monster who dared to aspire to their fellowship. This conviction was a perpetual torment.

I began to form a desperate plan. The old man, De Lacey, was blind. He could not see my loathsome figure. His heart was benevolent, and his manners were gentle. I conceived the idea that if I could gain his ear, if I could speak to him when he was alone, I might be able to win his sympathy and friendship by the words I should utter. My voice was not harsh, but rather mild, and my language, I flattered myself, was now as fluent and correct as that of the cottagers themselves. If I could but persuade him of my gentle disposition, of my admiration for their virtues and my longing for kindness; if I could move his heart before his eyes, or the eyes of his children, could behold my deformity, he might then become my advocate. He might prepare his children for my appearance and plead my cause when I was not there to be judged by my outward form alone.

This thought took possession of my mind and became my sole focus. It was a faint and desperate glimmer of hope in the overwhelming darkness of my existence. I trembled at the audacity of the scheme, yet it was the only one that offered even the smallest chance of alleviating my insupportable loneliness. I resolved to wait for an opportunity when the old man should be by himself in the cottage. I watched their movements with an attention more keen and anxious than ever before. I would note when Felix, Agatha, and Safie went forth together, whether to walk in the forest or to labour in their garden, leaving the patriarch to his meditations by the fireside.

Several days passed in this state of feverish anticipation. My heart beat tumultuously at every sign that the moment I longed for might be approaching. At last, one afternoon, I observed Felix take up some tools, and Safie and Agatha, equipping themselves with baskets, prepared to accompany him into the woods, doubtless to gather fuel or wild berries. My whole frame shook. The moment was at hand. I saw them depart, their cheerful voices fading as they walked deeper among the trees. The old man was alone.

I waited a short while, striving to calm the tempest of emotion that raged within me. My limbs trembled, and a cold sweat covered my brow. This single interview would decide my fate. Success would mean my admission into the human family, a joy too great to contemplate without tears; failure would mean my final and utter banishment, a confirmation that I was indeed the wretch whom all mankind must disown. I felt that if I were to be rejected by this blind and benevolent old man, there could be no hope for me anywhere upon the earth.

Taking a deep breath, I crept from my hovel and, with hesitating steps, approached the door of the cottage. My hand was raised to knock, but it fell again to my side, my courage failing me. What if my voice, trembling with my agitation, should betray me? What if some unforeseen interruption should ruin my attempt? I crouched for a moment by the wall, listening. I could hear the old man softly strumming his guitar within; it was a simple, melancholy air, and it seemed to soothe my agitated spirits. I thought of his kindness, of the mild wisdom in his voice when he spoke to his children. He was my last, my only refuge.

With a renewed and desperate resolve, I rose to my feet. I would not, could not, turn back now. The alternative was a solitude and despair that I was no longer able to endure. My future hung upon the next few moments. Steeling my heart against the terror that threatened to overwhelm me, I once more lifted my hand, and this time, with a tremor that shook my entire body, I knocked at the door of the cottage.

A silence followed, a void in which my own frantic heartbeat seemed to echo from the very walls of the world. For a moment I believed I had not been heard, that my timid summons had been lost to the wind. Despair began to creep back into my soul, when I heard a slight stirring from within, and then a voice, aged yet clear, issued from the other side of the portal.

“Come in,” he said.

I was so startled by the simple invitation that for a second I remained rooted to the spot. “Come in,” he repeated, a note of gentle inquiry in his tone. “If you are a traveller, do not hesitate; the evening air grows chill, and my fire has warmth to spare.”

His words, so full of that benevolence I had so often witnessed from my hiding place, lent me a portion of courage. With a hand that still trembled, I lifted the latch. The door creaked upon its hinges as I pushed it open, and I stood upon the threshold of that sacred place, the abode of my protectors. The interior was lit by the cheerful blaze of the hearth, which cast dancing shadows upon the humble furniture and the whitewashed walls. The old man sat in his customary chair, his back partially to the door, his head inclined as if listening. He was as I had pictured him, a figure of patriarchal dignity and calm.

I closed the door softly behind me and stood, unable to move farther, my breath caught in my throat. I was at last in their presence, a guest under their roof, yet I was an intruder, a monster whose very sight would have blasted them with horror.

“You are welcome, my friend,” said De Lacey, not turning his head. His sightless eyes were, I knew, fixed upon the fire. “Pray, come and sit. You seem agitated. Has some misfortune befallen you?”

His kindness was a dagger to my heart. How could I reply? What could I say that would not at once betray the horrid secret of my form? I took a hesitant step forward, my heavy boots sounding unnaturally loud on the stone floor.

“I am a traveller,” I said, my voice hoarse and strange even to my own ears, for I had not used it for speech in so long a time. I strove to modulate its tone, to soften its rough edges into something resembling human speech. “I am weary and have lost my way. I saw the light of your cottage and was bold enough to seek a moment’s rest.”

“You are most welcome to it,” he returned warmly. “There is a stool near the fire. Pray, draw it close and warm yourself. I regret that my children are from home, but they will return anon, and we can offer you such supper as we have. You shall not go hungry from our door.”

My children! The words sent a fresh pang of terror and longing through me. Their return was the very thing I dreaded most, the event that would shatter this fragile peace. I must make my case, and make it quickly. I moved to the stool he had indicated and sat, keeping my face in the shadows as much as the flickering firelight would allow. I was now but a few feet from him. I could see the fine lines upon his noble face, the placid expression of his lips, the silver of his hair. Oh, that I could have thrown myself at his feet and called him father!

“You are too kind, sir,” I began, my voice gaining some measure of steadiness. “I am a poor, miserable wretch, an outcast from the world. I have no friends.”

He turned his face more fully towards me, a look of profound compassion spreading over his features. “No friends, you say? That is a sad state indeed for any man. A stranger in a strange land, perhaps?”

“Yes,” I answered eagerly, seizing upon the word. “A stranger. I have come from a far country and I find myself utterly alone. I am shunned by all, and for a reason that I cannot help. Men judge me before they know me; they see my person and they turn away in disgust.”

The old man sighed deeply. “Ah, my friend, that is the great injustice of mankind. How often do we form our opinions based on fleeting appearances, on the outward shell, without ever seeking to discover the jewel of worth that may lie within? I, who am blind, have perhaps learned a lesson that my sighted brethren too often forget. It is the heart, the disposition, the goodness of a soul that matters, not the countenance or the stature.”

His words were a balm to my tormented spirit. Hope, which I had thought extinguished for ever, flared again in my breast, a bright and painful flame. He understood! He, of all beings, understood the cruelty of prejudice and the value of inner virtue.

“Oh, what you say is a divine truth!” I exclaimed, leaning forward in my earnestness, my voice trembling with an emotion I could no longer suppress. “If all men thought as you do, the world would be a paradise. But they do not. I am judged, and I am condemned, without a trial. I have love in my heart, a love for my fellow beings that is immense, yet I am met only with hatred and scorn. Tell me, good sir, if you had a person such as I have described, one who is gentle in spirit and longs for kindness, but whose appearance is off-putting to others; if such a one came to you for aid, would you spurn him?”

“Spurn him?” De Lacey cried, a noble indignation in his voice. “Heaven forbid! I should receive him as a brother. It would be my duty, and my greatest pleasure, to offer him what comfort I could, to shield him from the unkindness of the world, and to prove to him that true fellowship does not depend upon the trifles of external form. The unfortunate and the outcast have a special claim upon the compassion of their fellow men.”

Tears, hot and unrestrained, welled in my eyes and streamed down my monstrous cheeks. This was more than I had dared to hope for. This benevolent, wise old man had, in his innocence, accepted me. He had opened the door not only to his cottage, but to his heart. The moment had come. I must reveal myself, not in my horrid form, but in my true relation to him and his family.

“Then you will not cast me out!” I sobbed, my voice thick with unshed grief and newfound joy. “I have a secret to confess. The friends I long to gain, the protectors I seek, are not far away. Indeed, they live in this very cottage.”

The old man started, a look of confusion on his face. “In this cottage? What mean you, my friend? My children are good and kind, but we are humble folk; we have little influence to help any one.”

“It is not your influence I seek,” I cried, my agitation mounting to a fever pitch. “It is your acceptance! Your love! I have watched you for months. I have learned from you. I have come to adore your virtues. You are the friends I have sought! It is your family I wish to join! I am the outcast I described, and I throw myself upon your mercy!”

As I spoke, I fell to my knees and reached out, intending to take his hand, to seal this pact of compassion with a human touch. My words, however, had created a different effect than I had intended. A look of alarm, even of fear, began to dawn on his face. He drew back slightly.

“What is this?” he said, his voice now laced with a tremor of apprehension. “Your speech is wild. Who are you? What is the meaning of this strange confession? My children...”

He was interrupted by the sound of the latch lifting. The door to the cottage swung open. My blood ran cold. In the doorway, framed against the fading light of the afternoon, stood Felix, Agatha, and Safie.

For a single, eternal second, no one moved. Their eyes, accustomed to the dim light of the forest, widened as they took in the scene before them: their blind father, a look of confusion and fear on his face, and before him, on its knees, a monstrous figure, a creature from a nightmare, its grotesque features illuminated by the firelight, its face wet with tears.

The silence was broken by a shrill scream. It was Agatha. Her hand flew to her mouth, her eyes rolled back in her head, and she collapsed in a faint upon the threshold. Safie, her face a mask of utter terror, was unable even to scream; she turned and fled from the cottage, her flight as swift and silent as that of a startled deer.

But Felix’s reaction was the one that sealed my fate. His handsome face, which I had so often seen animated by love for his family and gentle joy, was now contorted into a mask of rage and horror. His eyes were fixed upon me, not with the curiosity one might show to a strange beast, but with the violent revulsion one feels for a thing of pure evil.

“Fiend! Demon!” he roared, his voice raw with fury. “What are you doing to my father?”

He did not wait for an answer. Snatching up a heavy piece of firewood from the pile near the door, he rushed at me. I was still on my knees, paralyzed by despair. The transition from the heights of hope to the abyss of rejection was so sudden, so total, that my mind could not comprehend it. I saw the blow coming, but I did not move to avoid it. I wished for it to kill me, to end the insupportable agony of my existence.

“No, Felix!” cried the old man, who had risen to his feet in alarm, his hands outstretched. “Do not harm him! He is a traveller, a poor unfortunate...”

His pleas were lost in the fury of his son. The wood descended upon my shoulder with a sickening crack. The pain was immense, but it was nothing, absolutely nothing, compared to the anguish that tore through my soul. He had struck me. Felix, my protector, my teacher, whose gentle conversations had been my greatest delight, had raised his hand in violence against me.

The first blow was followed by a second, and a third. I scrambled to my feet, not to defend myself, but to escape. A wild, inarticulate cry of pure agony was torn from my throat. It was not a sound of anger or of threat, but the howl of a mortally wounded animal. The sight of me, my colossal stature now fully revealed as I stood, my horrid face twisted in pain and sorrow, seemed only to inflame his rage. He drove me backwards, towards the door, with a volley of blows.

I could have torn him limb from limb. My strength was to his as an oak is to a reed. But my heart was broken. The spirit to fight, or even to live, had deserted me. I stumbled out of the door, out of that paradise I had so briefly inhabited, and into the cold darkness of the world. I cast one last, despairing look back at the cottage. I saw Felix standing over the prostrate form of his sister, his weapon still raised, his face a picture of righteous fury and terror. The old man was being led to a chair by Safie, who had, it seemed, found the courage to return. Their world was shattered, and I was the cause.

With a final groan that seemed to tear my very being apart, I turned and fled. I ran, I knew not where, my flight as aimless and as frantic as that of a hunted beast. The branches of the trees whipped at my face, but I did not feel them. The uneven ground threw me to my knees, but I rose and ran on. The physical pain in my shoulder was a dull ache compared to the fire of desolation that consumed my soul.

I found my way back to my hovel, the miserable kennel that had been my only home. I threw myself upon the heap of straw and leaves and gave vent to my grief in a paroxysm of howling and rage. I tore at my hair and beat my chest. I cursed the day of my creation. I cursed my creator, that false and cruel god who had given me aspirations and passions, only to cast me into a world that would for ever deny them.

For hours I lay in this state of abject misery. The sun set, and the woods were plunged into darkness, but the darkness in my heart was a thousand times more profound. My hopes were not merely dashed; they were annihilated. The gentle and benevolent De Lacey, my last refuge, had been the unwitting instrument of my final damnation. His children’s reaction had proved what I had long feared: that my hideousness was a barrier that no virtue, no gentleness, no love could ever surmount. I was not a man; I was a monster, a blot upon the earth, a thing to be feared and destroyed.

As the night wore on, my frantic grief subsided into a cold and terrible calm. A change had been wrought within me. The creature that had sought love and companionship was dead, slain by the hand of Felix in the sacred precincts of the cottage. In its place, a new being was stirring, one animated by a hatred as vast as the love it had once cherished. If I could not have kindness, I would have revenge. If I could not inspire love, I would inspire fear. My war against the species, against my creator who had abandoned me, had now truly begun.

I crept from my hovel one last time and made my way to a position where I could observe the cottage. A light still burned within. I listened, and I could hear their voices, though I could not distinguish the words. They were sounds of agitation, of fear, and of sorrow. I had brought ruin to this peaceful family. I had destroyed the one beautiful thing I had ever known. A bitter and malicious joy mingled with my despair. Let them suffer. They had taught me what it was to feel, and now they would feel the consequences of their own lessons in cruelty.

I remained at my post throughout the night and into the following day. I saw Felix and Agatha emerge, their faces pale and drawn. They did not work in the garden or walk in the woods. They spoke in low, anxious tones, constantly casting fearful glances towards the forest, as if expecting me to reappear at any moment. In the afternoon, I saw Felix speaking with a man, a stranger from the nearby village. Money changed hands. The man departed, and Felix returned to the cottage. My heart, a cold, dead thing in my chest, knew what this meant. They were leaving. They were abandoning their home, the place of their solace, because of me.

The next morning, my fears were confirmed. I watched as they packed their few belongings into a small cart. De Lacey was led out, his face a mask of sorrow and bewilderment. Agatha, her eyes red from weeping, supported him on one side, while Felix, his face grim and set, walked on the other. Safie followed, her usual gaiety replaced by a silent, fearful watchfulness. They did not look back. They walked away down the path, and a turn in the woods hid them from my sight for ever.

They were gone. My protectors, my gods, the only link I had to the world of men, were gone. The cottage stood empty and silent. A profound stillness descended upon the forest, the silence of a tomb. I was alone. Utterly, completely, and irrevocably alone.

For a long time I stood there, watching the empty doorway. The sun rose higher in the sky, its warmth a mockery of the ice in my veins. A feeling of rage, black and boundless, rose within me, choking me. I had loved them. I had venerated them. And they had cast me out. They had destroyed my soul, and now I would destroy the temple of my false worship.

That night, when the moon, a pale and sickly witness, had risen over the trees, I returned to the deserted cottage. I gathered dry branches and brushwood, my movements feverish and purposeful. I piled them against the walls, stuffed them through the broken window, and heaped them against the door through which I had entered in hope and fled in despair. Then, taking a burning brand from the embers of my own fire, I approached the pyre.

“And now, my watch is over,” I whispered to the unhearing night. I danced around the cottage in a mad frenzy, a fiendish glee taking possession of me. I applied the torch. The dry wood caught at once, and in an instant, a spire of flame shot up into the sky. The fire roared and crackled, its light turning the peaceful glade into a vision of hell. It consumed the humble dwelling, the scene of my brief education and my eternal damnation. It was a funeral pyre, not for them, but for my own hopes, for the gentle creature I had once been. As the flames devoured the roof and the walls fell in upon themselves, sending a shower of sparks towards the indifferent stars, I turned my back upon the smoking ruins and faced the world. My heart was a vortex of misery and rage. I was a wretch, and I would make mankind suffer for my wretchedness. My travels would now have a new purpose: I would find my creator, and I would have my revenge.
