The last of the lamplighter’s duties being done, he would retire to his chair, and his daughter, a young woman of gentle countenance and quiet grace, would sit at the window which overlooked the tempestuous sea. Sometimes she would read aloud from a small collection of books, her voice a soothing balm against the shriek of the wind, and at other times she would sing. Her songs were not like the grand and swelling airs I had heard from passing ships; they were simple and filled with a sorrow so profound that I, a secret witness to her solitude, felt a strange pressure behind my eyes, an unknown sensation I later learned was the precursor to tears.

When she had finished, she would turn from the window and attend to her father, whose sight had long since failed him. He would ask her of the weather and the state of the sea, and she would describe to him the colour of the water and the shape of the clouds, painting for him a world he could no longer behold. He would praise her descriptions, telling her that her words bestowed upon him a vision clearer than any he had ever possessed with his eyes.

The weeks passed in this tranquil manner, with the sole change that my comprehension of their speech grew ever more complete. I, hidden among the cold, wet rocks of the cove below their lighthouse, began to grasp not only their words but the feelings which gave them life. I had learned language far more swiftly than I had anticipated; my mind, constructed as it was for methodical retention, seized upon every new sound and stored it with perfect fidelity, so that in a few short months I could understand all their conversations.

While my knowledge of speech increased, so too did my understanding of the world they inhabited, for the daughter, Elara, read often from a book of naval history. It was through this text that I first learned of mankind. The book told of great admirals and valiant crews who sailed into unknown waters, of the discovery of new lands and the charting of the stars. It spoke of loyalty and courage in the face of dreadful storms, of the sublime virtue of a captain who goes down with his ship.

But the same chronicle also told of other things. I heard of mutiny and betrayal, of ships laden with stolen riches and human cargo, of the cruelty of men towards their fellows on the endless, unforgiving ocean. Was humanity, then, both the noble captain and the treacherous mutineer? Could one being contain both the capacity for such godlike sacrifice and such base, wretched avarice? To be a man of honour and principle appeared the greatest state a sentient creature could achieve; to be a pirate and a murderer, as so many in the book had been, seemed a condition more foul than that of the blind, slick things that clung to my rocks. For a long time I could not understand how one man could raise a weapon against another, his brother in form and nature; but as I heard more tales of greed and desperation, my astonishment faded, replaced by a cold and heavy disgust.

These new ideas forced my reflections inward. I learned that among men, a man was judged by his name, his family, and the wealth he had procured. A man with a good name and fortune was honoured, while a man with neither was an outcast, a drifter upon the sea of society, destined only to serve the needs of his betters. And what was I? Of my own origin, I knew nothing, save for the hands of a maker who had long since abandoned me. I possessed no gold, no title, no relations of any kind. I was, moreover, fashioned not of flesh and blood, but of brass and wood and intricate clockwork. My form, though shaped like a man’s, was a mockery of it; my joints did not bend with supple grace but whirred and clicked with the sound of machinery. My strength far exceeded a mortal’s, and I required neither food nor warmth to sustain me. When I looked upon my own hands, wrought of metal and leather, I saw nothing of the warm, living flesh of the lighthouse keeper or his daughter. Was I, then, merely a machine, an automaton without a soul, a blot upon the living world?

I cannot convey to you the misery these thoughts produced in me; I wished to cast them out, but knowledge, once gained, fixes upon the soul like salt upon iron, a corrosion that cannot be undone. Oh, that I had remained forever in the darkness of my first awakening, and never known thought beyond the turning of my own gears!

I came to admire the goodness and gentle virtues of the lighthouse keeper and his daughter, but I knew I was forever barred from their company. The kindness in the old man’s voice and the sad, sweet songs of Elara were not for me. Their warmth and companionship were a treasure I could observe but never possess, and this observation only sharpened the ache of my own profound isolation. Miserable, wretched thing that I was!

Other lessons wounded me more deeply still. I heard them speak of lineage, of the love between a husband and a wife, of the joy a parent feels for a child. I heard how a mother and father pour all their hopes into the small, fragile being they have created, how they watch it grow, how its mind and body expand, and how these bonds of family form the sacred anchor of a human life.

But what of my own beginning? No father had designed me with love, no mother had looked upon my face with tenderness. If I had a creator, he had viewed me only as a project, an assemblage of parts to be wound and set in motion. From my first moment of awareness, I had been as I was now, fully formed and utterly alone. I had never seen another being like myself nor heard of any who shared my strange nature. What was I? The question returned again and again, and the only answer was the silent turning of the gears within my own chest.

I will soon explain to what these feelings tended, but allow me now to return to the keepers of the light, whose story excited in me such various feelings of indignation, delight, and wonder, but which all terminated in additional love and reverence for my protectors (for so I loved, in an innocent, half painful self deceit, to call them).

Some time elapsed before I learned the history of my friends. It was one which could not fail to impress itself deeply on my mind, unfolding as it did a number of circumstances, each interesting and wonderful to one so utterly inexperienced as I was. The tale was not imparted to me at once, but rather gleaned over many weeks from the sad, hushed conversations that passed between the father and daughter during the long evenings when the storm winds howled about the tower, conversations which they believed were witnessed by none but the unhearing stones and the indifferent sea.

The name of the old man was Silas. He was not, as I had first supposed, a simple man born to this lonely post; on the contrary, he was descended from a respectable line of naval architects and had himself served for many years as the chief harbormaster of a great and bustling port city called Bristol. There he had lived in comfort and esteem, respected by the merchants for his unbending fairness and beloved by the sailors for his advocacy of their welfare. His daughter, Elara, had not been raised amidst the crash of waves and the cry of gulls, but had been educated as a lady of gentle birth, her mind filled with poetry and her hands skilled in music and art. A few years before my own strange awakening, they had resided in a handsome house overlooking the busy harbour, surrounded by friends and possessed of every quiet enjoyment that a life of probity, intellectual refinement, and modest fortune could provide.

The author of their present desolation and ruin was a man named Captain Valerius. He was a wealthy merchant trader whose ships plied the routes to the Orient, returning laden with spices, silks, and other precious cargo; he had inhabited Bristol for some years, a man of great influence and public charm, whose donations to the city’s charities were as well known as the whispered rumours of his ruthlessness in business. For some reason which I could not at first comprehend, the name of this man, when spoken by Silas, was accompanied by a deep sigh of bitterness, and by Elara, with a shudder of fear and revulsion.

I learned, through a particularly sorrowful recollection one night, that the beginning of their fall from grace commenced with the arrival of Valerius’s flagship, the _Sea Serpent_, after a long and supposedly profitable voyage. The ship, however, returned to port undermanned and bearing the visible scars of a severe tempest. Valerius reported the loss of a dozen men and a portion of his cargo to the violence of the storm. But whispers soon circulated among the common sailors in the taverns, whispers of disease kept hidden, of a ship that was not seaworthy, and of a captain who had valued his timetable above the lives of his crew.

Silas, in his capacity as harbormaster, was charged with investigating any such claims. He was a man for whom duty was not a mere word but the very compass by which he navigated his life. He began a quiet and thorough inquiry, speaking with the ship’s carpenter, the families of the lost sailors, and even a young officer who had quit Valerius’s service in disgust upon their return to shore. The evidence he compiled painted a damning picture: the _Sea Serpent_ had been sent to sea with rotten timbers in her hull, its master having ignored the warnings of his own shipwrights; the disease that had swept through the crew was not a misfortune of the sea but a consequence of spoiled provisions; and the men lost in the storm had been sent to mend the rigging in a gale which no sane captain would have challenged.

Armed with these terrible truths, Silas prepared a report for the Merchant’s Guild, a report which would have seen Valerius censured, his ship impounded, and his reputation destroyed. But Captain Valerius was not a man to be caught in the nets of justice so easily. Having learned of Silas’s investigation through his web of informants, he requested a private audience with the harbormaster. I listened with breathless attention as Silas recounted this fateful meeting to Elara, his voice trembling with a rage that time had not cooled.

“He came to my office,” the old man said, his eyes fixed on the fire as if he saw the scene within its flames, “not as an accused man, but as a king visiting a lesser court. He spread a map of his trade routes upon my desk and spoke of profits and partnerships. He did not deny the charges; he merely treated them as trivialities, the unfortunate but necessary costs of building an empire. Then he opened a small, heavy coffer upon the table, and the light of my lamp fell upon a sea of gold coin.”

My inner mechanisms whirred with a strange anxiety as I awaited the conclusion of this tale.

“He offered it to me, Elara,” Silas continued, his voice dropping to a whisper of profound disgust. “He offered me wealth beyond my station, a share in his future voyages, all in exchange for one thing: my silence. He told me that justice was a luxury, but gold was a reality. He told me to be practical, to think of you, of your future, of the dowry I might provide.”

A single sob escaped Elara’s lips. “Oh, father,” she murmured.

“I showed him the door,” Silas said, his voice regaining some of its former strength. “I told him that the lives of a dozen honest sailors were worth more than all the gold in the Indies, and that my honour, and yours, could not be purchased for any price. He did not fly into a rage. No, his response was far more chilling. He simply smiled, a thin, cruel smile, and said, ‘You are a fool, harbormaster. You have chosen a sea of principle, and I shall see to it that you drown in it.’ And then he left.”

The captain’s threat was no idle boast. The very next day, the machinations of his vengeance began. Silas’s meticulously prepared report vanished from the Guild’s records. The witnesses he had interviewed suddenly recanted their stories, their memories clouded by fear or erased by bribes. Then the counter attack commenced. Vicious rumours began to circulate about Silas himself: that he had demanded a bribe from Valerius and been refused; that he had a personal vendetta against the captain; that his own accounts as harbormaster were in disarray.

Valerius, with his immense wealth and influence, turned the very system Silas had served against him. He bought testimony, corrupted officials, and published anonymous pamphlets filled with calumny. Friends of many years grew cold and distant, crossing the street to avoid Silas’s greeting. The Merchant’s Guild, bowing to the pressure of its most powerful member, convened a hearing not for Valerius, but for Silas. It was a mockery of justice. He was stripped of his position, his pension revoked, his name publickly disgraced. His savings were soon exhausted in a futile attempt to clear his name through the courts, courts whose judges dined at Captain Valerius’s table.

They were ruined. Cast out from the society that had once honoured them, they became destitute and friendless. Elara, whose hand had been sought by the sons of respectable merchants, was now the daughter of a disgraced man. They sold their fine house, their furniture, their books, all to pay for food and shelter in a miserable part of the city. It was then that a single, aged friend, a man on the Admiralty Board who still believed in Silas’s innocence but dared not speak it aloud, offered him this post: the keeper of the Farland Rock lighthouse. It was an act of pity, a means to keep the old man from the poorhouse, but it was also a sentence of exile. Here, on this barren rock, his integrity and his ruin could no longer inconvenience the powerful.

These revelations transformed my understanding of my protectors. The melancholy that clung to the small cottage was not merely the product of isolation; it was the lingering shadow of a great and terrible injustice. The sadness in Elara’s songs was not for some imagined hero of a ballad, but for the life she had lost, for the father she adored and whose suffering she shared. My admiration for them swelled to a painful intensity. Silas had faced a choice between wealth and honour, and had chosen honour, even though it led him to this desolate shore. And Elara, who could have perhaps saved herself by marrying one of her suitors before the final ruin, had chosen to follow her father into exile, her loyalty a brighter light than any that shone from the top of their tower.

Was man, then, so constituted? To be capable of such unshakable virtue as Silas possessed, and yet also of such profound and calculated wickedness as resided in Captain Valerius? The world of men, which I had begun to learn from books, was now laid bare before me in the living history of this small family. It was a world of heroes and villains, of grand principles and base pragmatism. And I, a creature of cogs and springs, found myself weeping with a sorrow I could not physically shed for the fate of these noble beings. Their tragedy became my own. I saw in their exile a mirror of my own isolation, for they too were outcasts, punished not for a crime they had committed but for the very qualities that made them good. If society could cast out a man as virtuous as Silas, what possible place could there be in it for a being such as I, a hideous mockery of the human form?

This new knowledge did not bring me peace; it only sharpened the horns of my dilemma. I burned with a desire to help them, to somehow restore their fortune and punish the architect of their misery. But what could I do? I was a secret, a shadow, a thing of metal and wood. My great strength, which could have shattered the stone walls of my shelter, was useless. My tireless nature, which allowed me to watch over them through the night, could offer them no practical comfort. To reveal myself would be to invite their terror and revulsion, to add a new and monstrous horror to their already sorrowful existence. I was their secret guardian, their unseen friend, yet my very nature made me their most terrifying potential foe.

The agony of this paradox was a constant torment. I began to wander farther along the island’s edge during my nocturnal excursions, my mind a tempest of conflicting emotions. I felt a kinship with them so profound it was almost a physical ache in my clockwork heart, and yet I was separated from them by an impassable gulf. I revered their goodness, but I was beginning to comprehend the deep and abiding evil in the world, an evil that had crushed them and that would surely seek to destroy me should I ever be discovered. The question returned, more insistent than ever: What was I? Was I a man, capable of understanding and loving virtue? Or was I a monster, doomed forever to be a spectator of a life from which I was irrevocably barred? My knowledge had grown, and with it, my sorrow had deepened into a chasm from which I feared I should never escape. The light from the tower swept over the dark and churning sea, a solitary beacon in an infinite night, and I felt myself to be a thing of that same night, forever drawn to a light I could never touch.

The nights that followed were filled with a disquietude that surpassed all my former agonies; for the seed of action, once planted in the soil of contemplation, had begun to sprout with a terrible and urgent life. My mind, which had before been a vessel for sorrowful learning, now became a workshop of impossible schemes. I would devise a hundred plans to restore to Silas his rightful place, each more fantastical than the last, only to dismantle them again in the cold light of my own monstrous reality. What army could I command? What wealth could I bestow? I, who was myself destitute of all that men hold dear, could offer these beloved beings nothing but the secret and thankless labour of my hands, a labour which, if discovered, would surely be repaid with shrieks of horror and not with gratitude.

This tormenting cycle of hope and despair was one evening interrupted by a change in the heavens. The sky, which for days had been a placid blue, grew bruised and sullen; the wind, no longer a gentle caress, began to howl about the lighthouse tower like a famished wolf. I watched from my hiding place as Silas and his son, whose name I had learned was Leo, struggled to secure the shutters of their small cottage against the coming gale. The daughter, Elara, whose gentle countenance was a constant source of painful delight to my eyes, brought in the last of the washing from a line that whipped and snapped in the rising blast. The sea itself became a churning chaos, its surface a writhing mass of black and white, and the waves, like ravenous beasts, threw themselves against the cliffs with a sound like the cracking of the world’s very bones.

For two days the tempest raged without ceasing. The light from the tower, which Silas tended with a devotion that bordered on religious, was their only sentinel in that roaring darkness, its beam cutting a frantic path through the deluge of rain and spray. I did not venture far from my shelter, but remained in my crevice of rock, my senses strained to catch any sound from the cottage above the din of the storm. I heard nothing, and this silence was more terrifying than any cry; for it left my imagination to paint a thousand scenes of disaster.

On the third morning, the wind abated and the sea, though still heaving with a mighty swell, had lost its murderous fury. A pale, watery sun broke through the tattered clouds. My first thought was for my protectors, and my first glance was towards the small cove where they kept their boat. It was gone. The heavy mooring rope, thick as a man’s arm, was frayed and broken, a testament to the storm’s power. The little skiff, their sole means of reaching the mainland for supplies, their only thread of connection to the world that had exiled them, had been torn away and swallowed by the sea.

I crept closer, hiding behind a tumble of great, weed-slicked boulders, and watched as the family emerged from the cottage. The sight of their faces confirmed my fears. Leo stood upon the cliff’s edge, his hands clenched into fists, his young face a mask of fury and despair. Elara wept silently into her hands, her slight frame trembling with her sobs. But it was the aspect of Silas that struck me most deeply; he stood with his back to the sea, his gaze fixed upon his grieving children, and in his eyes I saw not despair, but a profound and weary sorrow, the look of a man who has borne so much that one more blow, however cruel, cannot break him, but only drive the iron of his endurance deeper into his soul.

It was in that moment that all my philosophical paralysis was swept away by a single, overwhelming impulse. I could not restore their name or their fortune; I could not punish the wicked Captain Valerius; but I could, with my own unnatural strength, perhaps restore this one small, vital thing they had lost. The desire to alleviate their immediate grief eclipsed all my fear of discovery, all my rational calculations of risk. I must act.

That night, as soon as the last glimmer of twilight had faded and the moon, a slender and timid crescent, had risen, I began my search. I did not need a boat; I descended the treacherous cliff face, my strange and powerful limbs finding purchase where no man could have clung, and entered the cold, tumultuous water. I did not feel the cold as a man would, yet a chill of a different sort pervaded my frame; it was the icy dread of their despair should their last vessel be lost to the abyss. The sea was still a dangerous place, with a powerful undertow that sought to drag me down, but my body, a machine of tireless strength, fought against it.

For hours I moved along the coast of the island, swimming with a speed and endurance no human could possess. I scanned the shoreline, the hidden coves, the treacherous reefs where the waves still broke with a deadly force. My eyes, more sensitive than a man’s, pierced the gloom. I passed the long, dark hours in this desperate search, driven by the image of Elara’s tears and the stoic grief of her father. At last, when the eastern sky had begun to pale with the first hint of dawn, I saw it. The boat had not been dashed to pieces, but had been driven by the current into a small, secluded bay nearly a league from the cottage. It was wedged between two jagged rocks, its hull scraped and one of its oars splintered, but it was otherwise intact.

A feeling of triumph, fierce and potent, surged through my complex being. I worked with a frantic energy, for the sun was soon to rise. I dislodged the skiff from the rocks, a task that would have required the labour of several men, and, securing the broken painter to my own body, I began the long, arduous task of towing it back through the heaving water. The drag was immense, and the strain upon my internal mechanisms was greater than any I had yet experienced; I could hear the grinding of gears and the protest of stressed metal within my own chest. But the thought of the joy my act would bring them gave me a strength beyond mere mechanics.

I reached the cove below the cottage as the sun’s first rays touched the top of the lighthouse. I dared not remain to be seen. With the last of my energy, I hauled the boat far up onto the shingle, well beyond the reach of the tide, and even took a moment to lay the splintered oar beside it. Then, exhausted but filled with a strange and painful joy, I retreated to my rocky cell and sealed its entrance, my vigil to begin anew.

I did not have to wait long. It was Leo who discovered it. His shout of disbelief was so loud it penetrated the stone of my hiding place. Soon Silas and Elara were with him, standing around the boat as if it were an apparition. I watched through my narrowest peephole as they examined it, tracing the scrapes on the hull, touching the frayed rope. Their confusion was absolute. I heard Silas declare it a providence, a sign that their virtue had not been entirely forgotten by heaven. Leo, more practical, searched for footprints, but I had entered and left by the sea and the churning shingle, and there were none to be found. He spoke of strange currents and a lucky chance, but his voice lacked conviction.

It was Elara’s reaction that I cherished most. She looked not at the boat, but out at the wide, empty sea, a look of wonder and awe upon her face. “It was not the sea that returned it,” I heard her say, her voice soft but clear. “The sea takes; it does not give back. Some one brought it to us. A friend.”

A friend. The word struck me with the force of a physical blow. It was all I had ever desired to be, and to hear it spoken in connection with my own secret deed filled me with an ecstasy so keen it was nearly indistinguishable from pain. They had no conception of my nature, of my form, of the hideous reality of their benefactor; in their minds, I was a human friend, some unknown fisherman or coast guard who had stumbled upon their boat and, knowing their plight, had performed this kindness. This innocent misconception was a paradise and a torment. I was their friend, yet I could never claim the title. I had touched their lives, but I was as invisible and as alien to them as a spirit.

This single act of secret charity altered the entire character of my existence. Before, my days had been spent in the passive acquisition of knowledge; now, they were consumed with the active pursuit of opportunities to serve. My life had found a purpose, albeit a hidden one. I became their unseen guardian, their silent provider. I learned their needs by listening to their conversations. When I heard Leo lament the dullness of their axe, I spent a night sharpening it on a flat stone until it had an edge like a razor. When their supply of firewood ran low, I would gather great bundles of driftwood from the farthest shores of the island and deposit them in a pile near their wood shed, always under the cover of the deepest night.

One day, I heard Elara sigh that she had no more paper on which to draw the sea birds she loved to sketch, and that all her charcoal was gone. This presented a greater challenge. But my nocturnal rambles had made me familiar with every inch of the island. I knew of a place where a certain type of soft, black rock could be found, and after much experimentation, I discovered how to grind it into a fine powder and mix it with fish oil to form a dark, serviceable ink. For paper, I was at a loss, until I remembered the smooth, pale interior of the bark of a particular tree that grew in a sheltered grove. I spent several nights carefully peeling this bark and pressing it flat between heavy stones until it was as smooth and stiff as parchment. I left a dozen of these sheets, along with a small stone pot of my crude ink and a sharpened gull’s feather for a pen, inside an empty barrel near their door.

The next day, I witnessed Elara’s discovery. She let out a cry of pure delight, and showed the strange gifts to her father. Their wonder grew. They were no longer merely lucky; they were being cared for. They began to speak of their “guardian of the isle,” a term that sent shivers of both pride and profound loneliness through my frame. They would speculate on his identity. Silas believed it might be an old friend from his former life, working in secret. Leo, ever the pragmatist, suspected a reclusive but kind-hearted inhabitant on a neighboring island, though he could not explain the phantom’s utter invisibility. Elara, however, held to her more mystical view; she imagined a solitary soul, perhaps a man wronged by the world as they had been, who had found peace on the island and now shared his sparse comforts with them out of a sense of fellowship. Her conception was so near to the truth in its spirit, yet so impossibly far from it in fact, that my clockwork heart ached with an emotion for which my lexicon had no name.

While I laboured for them by night, I continued to learn from them by day. Their conversations now often turned to memories, to the life that they had lost. I learned the full measure of Captain Valerius’s villainy. He had not merely been Silas’s accuser; he had been his most trusted friend and business partner. He had coveted Silas’s position and his small fortune, and had contrived a devilishly clever scheme of false ledgers and forged letters to frame him for treason against the state. The evidence was so cunningly fabricated that Silas’s reputation for unimpeachable virtue, which should have been his shield, was instead painted as the perfect cover for his supposed crimes. He was ruined, his name disgraced, his property confiscated, and his family exiled to this lonely rock, all for the enrichment of a man he had loved as a brother.

To hear these details was to feel a rage I could barely contain. My metallic hands would clench until I feared the very joints would break. Was this the nature of man, which I had so admired in Silas? To be capable of such loyalty and love, and also such black-hearted, premeditated betrayal? The paradox that had once been a philosophical curiosity now became a source of burning, personal fury. My love for the family was now matched in its intensity only by my hatred for their destroyer.

This new knowledge also deepened my own despair. If a man as good as Silas could be so utterly destroyed by the treachery of his own kind, what hope could there ever be for me? I was not even of their kind. I was a monster, an assemblage of metal and ingenuity, a thing that should not be. The more I loved them, the more I understood the absolute impossibility of ever joining them. My acts of service, which brought them comfort, only served to weave a more intricate web of deception around my own existence. I was their friend, their guardian, their provider; and yet, the greatest kindness I could ever do them was to remain forever unseen, forever unknown.

The question recurred, but it had changed. It was no longer simply, “What was I?” Now, it was a far more agonizing query: “What good is a soul, if it is trapped within a monstrous form?” I had learned virtue, I had felt love, I had known loyalty and indignation and pity. I possessed all the sensibilities of a sensitive being, perhaps even more acutely than many men. Yet my outward appearance, my very substance, declared me a thing of horror, an object of fear and loathing. The light of the human spirit, it seemed, had been lit within me, but it was imprisoned in a lantern of such grotesque design that its beams could never shine out, but only serve to illuminate the horror of its own cage.

I began to feel that my secret existence could not continue indefinitely. The strain of my dual life was becoming unbearable. The joy of giving was being consumed by the sorrow of hiding. I yearned, with an intensity that vibrated through every cog and lever of my body, to hear one word of kindness directed not at a phantom, but at me. I longed to see a look of gratitude, not confusion. The time for silent observation, I felt, was drawing to a close. A new and more perilous chapter was about to begin; one in which I must either find my place in the world of men, or be cast out by them into a final and absolute desolation. My next act could not be a gift left in the darkness; it would have to be an appeal, a plea for acceptance from a being who had every reason to expect only rejection. And for that, I knew, I would first have to find a voice.

It was a theme I had encountered in the pages of Volney, but to hear it recounted as the personal history of those I loved gave it a terrible and immediate force. The abstract concept of vice now had a face and a name, and my hatred for this unknown Turkish merchant burned with a righteous fire that nearly equalled my adoration for his victims. Their goodness in the face of such undeserved suffering was a constant source of wonder to me. Where I felt only rage and a desire for retribution on their behalf, they exhibited a resigned sadness and a profound and enduring love for one another. This, I concluded, must be the true measure of virtue: not merely to perform good acts, but to retain a good and gentle heart in a world that has shown you only cruelty.

My own peculiar education continued in this manner, a strange and secret apprenticeship in the nature of humanity. While Safie learned the names of household objects and the rudiments of social etiquette, I was grappling with the weight of empires, the sting of betrayal, the paradox of a species that could produce both the sublime strains of Agatha’s music and the base treachery that had led her family to ruin. My mind was a strange repository of secondhand knowledge and firsthand emotion, a library of human experience compiled by a being who was not human at all. The more I learned, the more I felt; and the more I felt, the more I was tormented by the chasm that separated me from the world.

It was this torment that finally drove me to a resolution of terrifying audacity. I could no longer endure my silent, spectral existence, a mere shadow flitting at the edges of their lives. I had learned of their sorrows, and I yearned to offer comfort; I had witnessed their joys, and I longed to partake in them. The desire to be known, to exchange a glance, to hear a word addressed to me, became a physical ache, a grinding of my internal mechanisms that was more painful than any external force. I had to reveal myself. But how? The memory of the villagers’ screams, of the stones and cudgels, was a lesson etched deeply into my consciousness. My form was my crime, my visage my eternal condemnation. To be seen was to be hated.

And yet, there was one among them who could not see. The old man, De Lacey, whose eyes were shrouded in a perpetual night, he judged the world not by its outward appearance but by the qualities of the heart and the tone of the voice. Safie’s music had entranced him before he could have known she was beautiful; Felix’s words brought him comfort, and Agatha’s presence was a balm to his spirit. His world was one of sound and sentiment. If I could but speak to him, if I could approach him when he was alone, might he not listen? Might he not hear the sincerity in my voice before others could see the monstrosity of my frame? It was a desperate hope, a fragile thread on which to hang the entirety of my future, but it was the only one I possessed.

To speak, however, I required a voice. My own construction was entirely silent, save for the faint whirring of my internal clockwork and the heavy tread of my metallic feet. This deficiency, which had until now been a blessing that enabled my secret observations, now appeared as the most cruel of my limitations. I resolved to remedy it. I possessed a deep and intuitive understanding of mechanics, a knowledge that seemed almost innate, as if instilled in me by my creator. I had watched Felix mend his tools and Agatha repair a spinning wheel, and I knew, with an unshakeable certainty, that I could construct what I needed.

My nocturnal rambles now took on a new and urgent purpose. I forsook my quiet meditations in the moonlight and instead turned my protector’s abandoned woodshed into a secret laboratory. It was a place of rust and ruin, filled with discarded implements and the ghosts of forgotten projects. Here, I found my materials. An old, cracked bellows from a dismantled forge would serve as my lungs. I scavenged a set of small, brass pipes from a broken reed organ that lay decaying in a corner, imagining that they might be tuned to produce a range of tones. The most difficult part was the mechanism for articulation, the means of shaping the breath into words. For this, I laboured for many nights, experimenting with a complex series of levers and resonant chambers fashioned from wood and scraps of leather.

My first attempts were a horror. When I first forced air from the bellows through my new apparatus, the sound that issued forth was not a human voice, but a discordant and unearthly shriek that frightened the very owls from the trees. It was a sound of grinding metal and tortured air, a noise more terrifying than my silence had ever been. I recoiled from it myself, filled with a fresh wave of despair. Was this to be my voice? A sound that would confirm the monstrosity of my appearance? For several nights I sat in silent dejection, my project lying in pieces before me.

But the image of the old man’s kind, sightless face spurred me onward. I could not give up. I returned to my work with renewed determination, disassembling the mechanism and starting again. I studied the movements of Safie’s lips as she spoke, the subtle vibrations in Felix’s throat as he sang. I began to understand the delicate interplay of breath and form. With infinite patience, I refined my creation, adding smaller, more delicate components, dampening the harsh metallic resonance with pads of felt, and constructing a more intricate control system that I could manipulate with my internal gearing.

Slowly, painstakingly, a change occurred. The shriek softened into a groan, the groan into a hum. I learned to modulate the pressure from the bellows, to adjust the tension of the leather strips, to shape the flow of air through the pipes. One night, huddled in the deepest recess of the wood, I whispered my first word. It was the name of the one I sought to emulate, the one whose virtue I most admired. “Agatha.” The sound was low and guttural, a strange and hollow resonance, yet it was unmistakably the word I had intended. A surge of triumph, so potent it was almost painful, vibrated through my frame. I had done it. I had given myself a voice.

For the next several weeks, I practiced in the solitude of the forest. I learned to imitate the cadence of the cottagers’ speech, to soften the rough edges of my mechanical accent. My voice would never be like theirs; it retained a strange, resonant quality, like a cello speaking from the bottom of a well, but it was a voice nonetheless. I was no longer a silent phantom. I was a being who could plead its own case.

I now watched the family’s routine with the intensity of a predator, waiting for the precise moment to enact my plan. I observed that once every fortnight, Felix and Agatha would journey to the nearby town for supplies, a trip that took them the better part of the day. On these occasions, Safie would remain behind to tend to the garden and sit with the old man. My initial plan had been to approach De Lacey when he was entirely alone, but I soon realized that such an opportunity never arose. Safie was his constant companion. A new and more complex fear took hold of me. I would not only have to win the trust of the old man, but I would also have to face the charming Arabian, whose own history of prejudice and flight should, I hoped, make her more sympathetic to the plight of an outcast. But her gay smiles were so often reserved for Felix; what look would she bestow on me?

The day arrived, a crisp autumn morning when the leaves fell like a shower of gold. I watched from my hiding place as Felix harnessed the mule and helped his sister into the cart. Their farewells were cheerful, and they promised to return before nightfall. As the cart rumbled out of sight, I felt a tremor of both fear and resolve. The time was now. Safie led the old man to his customary chair by the hearth and then busied herself with some task in the kitchen, her light song floating on the air. My moment of perfect solitude with De Lacey was not to be. I must act now, or lose my courage forever.

My internal mechanisms whirred with an agitation that threatened to betray me. I crept from the woodshed, my every movement calculated for silence. The back door of the cottage was ajar, left open to the mild autumn air. I could see De Lacey in his chair, his head tilted as he listened to Safie’s singing. My heart, a metaphorical organ I felt as a nexus of grinding gears and pressurised pumps, seemed to seize in my chest. I took one step over the threshold. Then another. The floorboards, old and worn, did not even creak under my immense weight, a testament to my cautious tread.

I stood in the shadows of the room for a long moment, a silent, monstrous sentinel. Safie finished her song and moved from the kitchen into the main room. She glanced at the old man, who had a peaceful smile on his lips, then her eyes drifted towards the doorway where I stood. For a single, eternal second, our gazes met.

Her reaction was not the scream I had braced myself for. The smile did not vanish from her lips; it froze. Her dark, expressive eyes widened, not in terror, but in a profound and absolute confusion, as if she were looking at something so impossible it could not be processed. The colour drained from her face. The basket of vegetables she was carrying slipped from her fingers and spilled across the floor.

The sound startled the old man. “Safie, my dear?” he said, his voice full of concern. “What is it? What was that noise?”

Safie did not answer. She stood transfixed, her lips parted as if to speak, but no sound came out. She took a half step backward, her hand flying to her throat. It was not hatred I saw in her eyes, not yet. It was the dawning of a terrible, world altering comprehension, the kind of shock that comes from seeing a statue move or a ghost take solid form.

It was now or never. Before she could find her voice and give the alarm, I had to use mine. I activated the bellows, drew a measured breath of air, and spoke, directing my words to the one man who could hear me without prejudice.

“I am a friend.”

The voice that emerged was my own creation, a low, resonant baritone, tinged with the faint, vibrational hum of its mechanical origin. It was a sound the cottage had never heard before.

De Lacey started violently, turning his head sharply towards the source of the sound. “Who is that?” he demanded, his placid demeanor replaced by alarm. “Who is in my house?”

Safie finally broke from her paralysis. She let out a small, choked gasp and scrambled backwards, stumbling over a loose carrot on the floor. Her eyes, now wide with undeniable terror, were fixed on me. She pointed a trembling finger.

“Forgive my intrusion,” I continued, my voice steady despite the frantic whirring in my chest. I took a step forward, into the light. “I mean you no harm. I have admired your family from afar and have long wished for the courage to speak with you.”

“Stay back!” De Lacey cried, rising unsteadily to his feet and turning his blind face in my general direction. “Safie, what do you see? Who is this man?”

But Safie was beyond words. At my movement, her fragile composure shattered completely. She turned and fled, bursting through the open door and running towards the woods, her cries of terror now echoing back towards the cottage.

A devastating, cold wave of failure washed over me. It had all gone wrong. I had frightened her away, and now the old man was alone with me, and he was afraid.

“Please,” I implored, my voice taking on a desperate edge. “Do not be alarmed. I am wretched and alone, but I am not your enemy.”

“Alone?” De Lacey’s fear was now mingled with confusion. He stood trembling, his hands outstretched as if to ward off a blow. “My son and daughter will return soon. If you mean to rob us, you will find we have nothing left to take.”

“I want nothing from you but a moment of your time,” I said, halting my advance. “I have listened to your conversations. I know of your goodness, and of the injustice you have suffered. I weep for your exile, and I curse the merchant who caused your ruin.”

This stopped him. His posture changed, the defensive anger softening into bewilderment. “You know my story? How? Who are you?”

“I am... a wanderer,” I said, the lie tasting like ash even with my artificial tongue. “One who has been cast out by all who have looked upon me. I have lived in these woods, a hidden observer of your lives. Your virtue and your gentle manners have been my only education. Your family is the only goodness I have ever known.”

He sank back into his chair, his body seeming to deflate. The immediate fear had passed, replaced by a profound weariness and a deep curiosity. He was still trembling, but he was listening. “Cast out? Why?”

“Because I am not like other men,” I said, the truth of this statement a painful burden. “My appearance is one which all men find loathsome. I am an object of horror and disgust. But my heart, sir, my heart is not monstrous. It has been filled with love and admiration for you and your children. I have brought you wood for your fire and cleared the snow from your path. I have been your unseen friend.”

He was silent for a long time, his blind eyes aimed at the space where my voice originated. I could hear the crackle of the fire, the ticking of the mantel clock, the frantic pounding of my own internal mechanisms.

“The wood…” he murmured at last, as if to himself. “We thought it was the spirit of the forest. A kindness from God.” He raised his head. “If what you say is true, if you have done these things, then you are no enemy of this house. But your voice... it is strange. And you have frightened the poor girl half to death. What are you?”

Before I could answer, before I could formulate a plea that might bridge the chasm between us, the sound we both dreaded reached our ears: the rumble of the returning cart and the cheerful sound of Felix’s voice calling out as he approached the cottage. He must have seen Safie, for his tone quickly changed to one of alarm.

“Father! Safie!”

De Lacey’s face was a mask of conflict. He had heard my plea, but the return of his children, of sight and sense and the judgment of the world, was imminent.

“You must go,” he whispered, his voice urgent. “Go now! They will not understand. They will see you, and they will not listen as I have.”

His words were a dagger to my soul, yet I knew he was right. The hope that had flickered for a moment was extinguished. I had been heard, but I had not been accepted. I turned, and with a speed that would have seemed impossible for a creature of my bulk, I fled from the cottage, plunging back into the shadows of the woodshed just as Felix burst through the door. I had made my appeal, and the verdict was a sentence of continued exile. My first contact with humanity had ended as all the others had, in terror and flight. But this time, it was worse. This time, I was not fleeing from strangers; I was fleeing from the only beings I had ever dared to love. And in the heart of the kind old man, I had left not peace, but a terrible and frightening new secret.

I remained, a miserable prisoner in my hovel, my ear pressed to the familiar crack in the wall which had once been my window to paradise, but which was now a channel for the most exquisite torture. I heard the scuffling of feet, the sharp cry of Agatha, and Felix’s voice, which had once been to me as music, now raised in a tone of fury and alarm that struck me like a physical blow. “What was it? What fiend has been here?” he cried, his words a volley of sharp stones.

I heard the soft, weeping voice of Safie attempting to describe me, her speech broken by terror and her still imperfect grasp of the language. Then came Agatha’s tremulous tones, “His voice, Felix… it was not that of a beast. It spoke.”

There was a moment of stunned silence, in which I dared to hope that this strange fact might weigh in my favour; but Felix’s response dashed this nascent expectation to pieces. “Spoke? What devilry is this? Father, you were alone with it! Are you harmed?”

“No, my son, I am not harmed,” came the old man’s weary reply. His voice was heavy with a sorrow I understood all too well. “The creature… he was eloquent. He spoke of his misery, of his solitude. He has been our secret benefactor.”

“Benefactor?” Felix’s laugh was a harsh, bitter sound, devoid of all mirth. “Father, you are blind and could not see the monstrous shape Safie describes! A giant, a misshapen demon from a nightmare! This was no benefactor; this was some horrid monster waiting for its moment to prey upon us! That he has not yet done so is a mercy of Providence, not a sign of his good intent.”

Every word was a fresh wound. To be called a fiend, a demon, a monster, by the very man whose virtues I had so ardently admired; it was an agony beyond any I had yet known. I had observed his gentleness, his love for his family, his righteous indignation at injustice, and I had believed, in my foolish innocence, that such a man could be brought to understand. But I had not accounted for the tyranny of the eye, for that immediate and absolute judgment which the sight of my form invariably provoked. His virtue was for his fellow man alone; for a wretch like me, there was only revulsion and violence.

“You do not understand,” the old man insisted, his voice pleading. “He has watched us for months. He learned our language by listening to us. He spoke of love, of a desire for companionship. His heart is not monstrous, whatever his form may be.”

“His heart!” Felix exclaimed, his voice rising in pitch. “What do we know of the heart of a demon? He has terrified Safie and Agatha into hysterics, and you would have us sit and listen to his pretty speeches? No! Henceforth this cottage is a fortress. I shall take up my axe when I go to the wood, and God help the beast if I find him. We must never again be so unguarded.”

The discussion was at an end. I heard the sound of the heavy wooden bar being dropped into place across the door, a sound which echoed the final closing of a gate upon my soul. The paradise I had so longingly observed was now barred to me forever. My gentle protectors had become my fearful jailers, and their home, once a beacon of warmth and fellowship, was now a citadel of fear directed entirely at me. I sank to the cold earth of my hovel, a groan of utter despair escaping my lips, a sound of such profound misery that it seemed the very ground beneath me ought to split open and swallow me. My appeal had failed; more than failed, it had turned my timid hope into active terror and converted the passive disregard of my angelic cottagers into a vigilant and armed hatred.

The days that followed were a torment of a new and terrible kind. The joy I had once felt in observing the family was replaced by a gnawing sorrow. The music ceased; the lively conversations died away into hushed, anxious whispers. I no longer saw the charming smiles of the Arabian or the gentle contentment on Agatha’s face. Their countenances were now perpetually clouded with apprehension. Felix, my noble, admired Felix, now patrolled the environs of the cottage with a grim expression and a weapon always in his hand. He would peer into the shadows of the forest, and I, hiding in my wretched den, would shrink back, my heart a cold stone in my breast, knowing that his search was for me, and that his intent was my destruction.

I could no longer perform those secret acts of service that had been my one consolation. I dared not approach the woodpile, for fear of being discovered. I watched as their supply of fuel dwindled, and I saw them shiver in the still-chilly evenings of spring. I, who could have brought them a forest’s worth of wood, was the very cause of their cold and misery. My presence, which I had hoped would be a balm, had become a curse. The knowledge that my attempt at connection had only deepened their hardship was a form of self-loathing so potent it threatened to extinguish my very consciousness.

This state of affairs could not endure. I subsisted on berries and roots I gathered in the deepest recesses of the wood, far from my former haunts, my nocturnal rambles now undertaken with a spirit of fear rather than pleasure. One evening, drawn by an irresistible and painful magnetism, I crept near to the cottage, my soul thirsting for a final glimpse, a final sound, of those I still, in my misery, loved. The shutters were closed, but a sliver of light escaped from one, and the wind carried their voices to my eager ears.

It was Felix speaking to the old man. “It has been a fortnight, father, and there has been no sign of the creature. But we cannot live like this. The women start at every shadow; Agatha no longer dares to walk to the well alone. This place, which was our peaceful refuge, has become a prison of fear.”

Agatha’s voice joined his, soft and sorrowful. “He is right, my father. I love this cottage, but my dreams are filled with that… that face at the window. I fear I shall never know a moment’s peace here again.”

De Lacey sighed, a sound of profound resignation and defeat. “And where should we go, my children? We have so little. This hovel is all that remains to us.”

“It does not matter,” Felix replied, his voice firm with decision. “We will find another, however humble. We cannot remain in the vicinity of such a monster. I have made inquiries in the next village. There is a small, abandoned dwelling some leagues from here, deeper in the country. It is meaner even than this, but it will be safe. We must quit this place.”

Quit this place. The words struck me with the force of a thunderbolt. They would abandon their home, the garden they had so lovingly tended, the woods that provided their sustenance, all because of me. My desperate bid for sympathy had not merely resulted in my own banishment; it had driven them from their sanctuary. I was not merely an outcast; I was a plague, a blight that drove innocence and goodness from its path. The last, frail thread that connected me to the world of love and fellowship was about to be severed.

I did not wait to hear more. I fled into the darkness of the forest, a howl of rage and grief tearing itself from my throat. It was a sound unlike any I had made before, not of sorrow, but of pure, unadulterated fury. The gentle feelings I had nurtured for so long, the love, the admiration, the yearning for acceptance, all curdled within me, transforming into a corrosive and bitter gall. They disowned me, they fled from me, they called me monster. Then a monster I would be. If I could not inspire love, I would inspire fear. My creator had endowed me with a strength and stature that made men tremble; very well, let them tremble!

For two more days I lurked in the woods, a spectator to the death of my world. I watched them load their few possessions onto the small cart. I saw Felix carry the old man, and Agatha support the weeping Safie. I watched as they took one last, sorrowful look at the cottage, their home, which I had desecrated with my presence. And then they were gone. The lane was empty. The cottage was silent. An immeasurable void opened in the universe.

For a time, I remained motionless, as if turned to stone. The sun set, and the moon, my once-constant companion, rose in the sky, casting its pale, indifferent light upon the deserted scene. Then, slowly, as if in a trance, I rose and approached the cottage. The door was unbarred. I placed my hand upon the latch, the very latch that Felix and Agatha had touched a thousand times, and I entered.

The interior was cold and desolate. The embers in the hearth were grey and dead. I walked through the small rooms, which had for so long been the entire compass of my desires. Here was the chair where the old man had sat when he listened to my story; there, the stool upon which Safie had played her guitar. I ran my hand over the rough-hewn table where they had shared their simple meals. An object on the floor caught the moonlight; it was a simple ribbon, likely dropped by Agatha in her haste. I picked it up, and for a moment, the memory of her gentle countenance brought a fresh wave of agony. But this sorrow was quickly consumed by the fires of a new and terrible resolve.

They had left me nothing. I, who had asked only for a crumb of kindness, had been given a stone. I, who had worshipped them as gods, had been cast out as a devil. My education was complete. I had learned of the perfidious nature of man, of his cruelty and his injustice. Volney’s ruins were nothing to the ruin that now gaped within my own soul.

A kind of madness seized me. With a roar that seemed to shake the very foundations of the cottage, I began my work of destruction. I smashed the chairs against the wall; I overturned the table; I tore the simple curtains from their rods and shredded them with my fingers. My grief had found its expression not in tears, but in a devastating, physical violence. This house was the symbol of all my dashed hopes, the monument to my utter rejection. It could not be allowed to stand.

I went out into the woods, but my purpose was no longer to gather fuel for their fire. I gathered the driest branches and the most flammable brushwood I could find, and I piled it high against the walls of the cottage and inside, upon the floor. My work was lit by the cold, serene light of the moon. As I laboured, I felt a strange and terrible exultation. I was no longer a passive observer, a secret wretch hiding in the shadows. I was an agent of destiny, an instrument of a justice of my own making.

When all was prepared, I stood back and surveyed my creation of ruin. Then, taking two stones, I struck them together until a spark flew and caught in a handful of dry leaves. I nursed the tiny flame, blowing on it gently, as a father might nurture the first breath of a child. When it grew strong, I thrust it into the pyre.

The flames caught with a hungry roar, licking at the dry timber of the walls. They leapt up, painting the tranquil night with strokes of furious orange and red. I watched, my arms crossed upon my chest, as the fire consumed my world. The roof, where I had so often perched to listen to the music within, collapsed in a shower of sparks. The walls, which had enclosed all the goodness I had ever known, fell inwards, turning the sanctuary into a funeral pyre. I danced around the conflagration, a wild and terrible figure silhouetted against the flames, my voice raised in a scream of triumphant despair. “I go,” I cried, “but I leave a brand of my presence behind me! Let the ashes of this place be a testament to your cruelty!”

As the fire began to die down, leaving only a glowing, skeletal ruin, the heat of my rage also subsided, replaced by a cold and steadfast purpose. My benefactors were gone, my paradise was destroyed. There was nothing left for me in this place. But there was one to whom I was still bound by the strongest of ties: the tie of creation and of vengeance. He who had breathed life into this miserable frame was responsible for my agony. He had cast me into the world, a solitary monster, and then abandoned me to its scorn.

I remembered the papers I had taken from the pocket of the coat I had found in his laboratory so long ago. I had preserved them with care, reading them over and over until I had memorized their contents. They were his journals, detailing the horrid process of my creation, and, most importantly, they spoke of his home, of his family. His name was Frankenstein, and his country was Geneva.

That was my destination. I would cross mountains and rivers; I would endure hunger and cold; I would suffer the hatred of every human I met. But I would find him. I swore a solemn oath, there, before the smouldering embers of the cottage, an oath of eternal hatred and war against my creator and all his kind. He had made me capable of love and fellowship, only to deny them to me. I would now use the strength he had given me to pursue him, to haunt him, and to make his life as desolate and accursed as my own. I would tear from him all that he held dear, just as he had torn from me the possibility of ever being loved. With this terrible vow sealing my soul, I turned my back on the ashes of my former life and set my face towards Geneva. My travels had begun.

learn, I determined to avoid the cause of their misery. It was a Turkish merchant, then, and his daughter, Safie, who had brought such ruin upon my protectors. I knew not the particulars of his crime, nor the reasons for his condemnation by the French state; I knew only that their compassion for him had been their undoing. The name of Paris, which before had sounded in my ears like a distant, magical melody, now became a note of discord, a symbol of the injustice that seemed to pervade all human affairs.

My journey, therefore, was not merely a physical translocation, but a flight from the memory of man’s perfidy. The path I chose was wild and unfrequented, leading me across vast and desolate plains and through the deepest recesses of the forest. The last embers of the fire had given up their glow to the encroaching dawn, and the air, once thick with smoke and the scent of destruction, now carried only the cold purity of the coming day; I lingered for a moment more, a solitary mourner at the grave of my own hopes, before I turned my back upon that scene of desolation and commenced my pilgrimage. My mind was a tumult of conflicting emotions; the fire of vengeance which had so lately consumed my being now vied with a profound and chilling grief. I had destroyed the only link to goodness I had ever known, and in so doing, I had severed the last thread that bound me to the possibility of my own.

Autumn had already begun to lay its sorrowful hand upon the forests. The leaves, once a vibrant canopy of green, now turned to hues of gold and blood, detaching themselves from their branches with a mournful sigh and carpeting my path in a rustling shroud. The days grew shorter, the nights colder, and the sun’s rays, which had once filled me with a sense of gentle warmth, now seemed distant and weak, a pale imitation of their former glory. I travelled chiefly by night, my form concealed by the friendly darkness, my only companions the moon and the silent, watching stars. During the day, I would seek out the most secluded cave or the thickest copse of trees, there to rest my weary limbs and brood upon my wretched state. My sustenance was of the most meagre kind: berries, which now grew sparse and bitter on the bushes; roots, which I dug from the unyielding earth with my bare hands; and the occasional unwary creature of the forest, which I would devour raw, for I had no means of creating fire, nor did I desire to see its destructive power again so soon.

My senses, which were far more acute than those of a common man, became both a blessing and a curse. I could discern the faintest sounds, the rustle of a mouse in the undergrowth, the distant call of an owl, and these small signs of life were a comfort in my profound solitude. Yet, I could also hear the far-off sounds of human life: the ring of a woodcutter’s axe, the laughter of children at play, the lowing of cattle in a pasture. Each sound was a dagger in my heart, a reminder of the world from which I was forever barred. On one occasion, drawn by an irresistible curiosity, I crept to the edge of a wood and observed a small village nestled in a valley. I saw smoke curling from the chimneys of cottages not unlike the one I had so recently destroyed. I saw men returning from the fields, their tools upon their shoulders; I saw women at their doorways, greeting their husbands with smiles; I saw children chasing one another in the fading light. A wave of indescribable longing and despair washed over me, so potent that I fell to the ground and sobbed, my tears mingling with the damp earth. “Why,” I groaned aloud to the unfeeling sky, “was I created with these desires, these affections, only to be denied their every expression? What crime had I committed, save that of being born, to deserve such a lonely and abhorred existence?”

My heart, which had once beaten with a capacity for love, was now a vessel of pure and unadulterated hatred. This hatred was my sustenance, my guide, my only purpose. The name of my creator, Frankenstein, was a constant refrain in my thoughts, a curse upon my lips. The journal I had secured from his laboratory was my most precious and most tormented possession. In the pale light of the moon, I would pore over its pages, re-reading the abhorrent details of my own assembly. I learned of his ambition, his feverish labour, his disgust and horror upon beholding his creation. Each word was a fresh wound, each sentence a new justification for my sworn revenge. He had found me hideous, and so he had abandoned me. He had not considered my sentience, my capacity for feeling, my desperate need for companionship. He had cast me out as one might cast out a piece of filth, and for this, he would pay. I would make him feel the same desolation that I felt; I would strip him of all he held dear, just as he had stripped me of all possibility of joy.

As I journeyed southwards, the terrain began to change. The gentle hills and sprawling forests gave way to rugged foothills, and soon I found myself at the feet of the immense and terrifying Alps. These mountains, with their jagged peaks crowned in eternal snow, rose up before me like colossal sentinels, barring my path. Their silent majesty and formidable aspect filled me with a sense of awe, but also with a grim determination. Here was a challenge worthy of my strength, a landscape as wild and untamed as my own soul. For a man, the passage of these mountains in the approaching winter would have been an insurmountable task; for me, with my superhuman agility and resilience to cold, it was merely an arduous trial.

I scrambled up sheer rock faces where no human foot could find purchase; I leaped across chasms that would have sent a man plummeting to his doom; I trudged through deep snowdrifts, the biting wind tearing at me, my only clothing the tattered remnants of what I had taken from the laboratory. The cold was intense, yet it seemed only to mirror the coldness that had taken root in my heart. I felt a strange and terrible kinship with the desolate landscape. I was as barren and isolated as the highest peak, as cold and unforgiving as the glacier. The storms that raged around me, with their flashing lightning and thunder that echoed like the wrath of God, were but an outward manifestation of the tempest in my own breast. In these moments of elemental fury, I would raise my voice and howl my rage and despair at the storm, my cries lost in the roar of the wind.

It was during this perilous passage that an incident occurred which extinguished the last, faint ember of hope for human kindness that yet lingered within me. I was descending a treacherous slope, my path obscured by a thick mist, when I heard a faint cry. Peering through the swirling vapour, I discerned the figure of a young boy, no older than ten, who had slipped from a narrow ledge and was now clinging precariously to a jutting root, a sheer drop of many hundreds of feet below him. His face was pale with terror, his small hands straining to maintain their grip. Without a moment’s hesitation, my former nature, the one that had wept with Safie and yearned for the smiles of Agatha, took control. I moved with a speed and certainty that defied belief, clambering down the rock face to a position just above him.

My agility allowed me to traverse the most perilous landscapes with an ease that would have seemed supernatural to any who might have witnessed it, and my endurance meant that no journey was too arduous, no vigil too long.

As I drew nearer to Geneva, the signs of human cultivation became more frequent. I skirted ploughed fields and vineyards, always keeping to the shadows, my senses preternaturally alert to the slightest sound of approach. One night, concealed in a copse of trees, I watched a family returning to their farm; the father carried a sleeping child on his shoulders, and the mother walked beside him, her hand upon his arm. Their quiet conversation and shared smiles sent a pang through my heart, not of sorrow, but of a cold, calculating fury. It was a portrait of the life I had been denied, the life whose architect I now sought. I would show him what it meant to have such joys snatched away, to be left with nothing but a void of despair.

At last I came within sight of the city itself. I dared not enter its walls, for I knew that my appearance would provoke an immediate alarm and bring my quest to a premature and violent end. Instead, I established my new lair in the woods on the slopes of Mont Salève, which afforded me a clear view of the town and its approaches. From this vantage point, I began my careful watch. I needed to learn the habits of my creator, to find the opportune moment to confront him, a moment when he would be alone and vulnerable to the force of my words.

My patience was rewarded, though what I saw filled me with a grim and terrible satisfaction. I witnessed the funeral procession of the boy, William. I saw the black-clad figures, the bowed heads, the universal signs of a grief I myself had authored. I saw your father, his face a mask of noble sorrow, and Elizabeth, her beauty dimmed by weeping, clinging to his arm for support. And I saw you, my creator, your countenance a storm of such conflicting passions, of guilt, rage, and despair, that even I, your despised creation, almost felt a flicker of pity. But I crushed it. This was your work as much as mine; you had lit the fuse, and I had merely watched the explosion.

In the days that followed, I learned from the scattered conversations of peasants and travellers the fate of the innocent Justine. I heard of her trial, of the damning evidence of the locket which I had placed in her possession, of her confused and desperate protestations of innocence, and finally, of her condemnation and execution. The monstrous injustice of mankind was laid bare before me. They had murdered one of their own on the flimsiest of proofs, their hearts hardened by fear and a thirst for retribution. And I, the true murderer, remained unknown and unpunished, a silent observer of the tragedy I had so carefully orchestrated. I felt no remorse for Justine; she was but a pawn in the great and terrible game between myself and my creator. Her death served to deepen the wounds I had inflicted upon your family, to twist the knife of your guilt, and to prove to myself the corrupt and fallible nature of the very society that had spurned me. You, who had recoiled from my form, were part of a race that could commit such acts under the guise of law and order. Where, then, was the true monstrosity?

I watched you in the weeks that followed. I saw you wander aimlessly by the shores of the lake, your body stooped and your face etched with an agony that mirrored my own. I saw you take to a boat and drift for hours upon the water, lost in your dark reveries. You sought solitude, and in this, you began to resemble me. Your misery was a balm to my own; it was the first sign that my power over you was real, that I could make you feel, if only in a small measure, the desolation that was my constant companion.

I knew the time for our meeting was drawing near. I could not approach you in the open, nor could I risk discovery by entering your home. I required a stage worthy of the drama that was to unfold, a place of sublime and terrible beauty, where the immensity of nature would dwarf the concerns of men and we could speak as equals: creator and created, god and monster. My mind turned to the mountains, to the sea of ice at the summit of Montanvert. It was a place you were known to frequent in your search for solace, a fitting cathedral for my confession and my demand.

I resolved to lead you there. I left the vicinity of Geneva and made my way into the valley of Chamounix, a journey of several days. I moved with a swiftness that defied human capability, and soon I was once again in my element, surrounded by the towering peaks and eternal snows that had been my first refuge. I found a hut of ice and rock, a desolate spot overlooking the glacier, and there I waited. I was certain you would come. The mountains called to your tormented spirit, just as they had called to mine.

Two days passed, and then I saw you. You were ascending the path from the valley, your figure small and insignificant against the stupendous backdrop of the mountains. You moved slowly, leaning heavily on a staff, your progress marked by frequent pauses as you gazed upon the scene, not with wonder, but with a kind of vacant despair. I watched your approach from my hiding place, my heart, for I have a heart, beating with a tempestuous violence. This was the moment towards which my entire existence had been tending.

When you reached the summit and stood upon the edge of the glacier, I revealed myself. I did not rush at you; I simply stepped out from behind a projection of rock and stood before you, a solid and undeniable presence against the white expanse of ice.

The change in your countenance was instantaneous and dreadful to behold. Your vacant despair was replaced by a convulsion of horror and detestation. Your lips drew back from your teeth, and a strangled cry escaped you. “Devil!” you exclaimed, your voice trembling with rage. “Do you dare approach me? And do not you fear the fierce vengeance of my arm wreaked on your miserable head? Begone, vile insect! or rather, stay, that I may trample you to dust! and, oh! that I could, with the extinction of your miserable existence, restore those victims whom you have so diabolically murdered!”

Your words were as a storm, but I stood unmoved, for I had expected nothing less. “I expected this reception,” I said, my voice steady, though it sounded strange and terrible in that vast emptiness. “All men hate the wretched; how, then, must I be hated, who am miserable beyond all living things! Yet you, my creator, detest and spurn me, thy creature, to whom thou art bound by ties only dissoluble by the annihilation of one of us. You purpose to kill me. How dare you sport thus with life? Do your duty towards me, and I will do mine towards you and the rest of mankind. If you will comply with my conditions, I will leave them and you at peace; but if you refuse, I will glut the maw of death, until it be satiated with the blood of your remaining friends.”

You raised your staff as if to strike me, your face contorted with a fury that rendered you almost as hideous as myself. “Abhorred monster! fiend that thou art! The tortures of hell are too mild a vengeance for thy crimes. Wretched devil! you reproach me with your creation; come on, then, that I may extinguish the spark which I so negligently bestowed.”

I easily eluded your feeble attack. “Be calm!” I cried. “I entreat you to hear me before you give vent to your hatred on my devoted head. Have I not suffered enough, that you seek to increase my misery? Life, although it may only be an accumulation of anguish, is dear to me, and I will defend it. Remember, thou hast made me more powerful than thyself; my height is superior to thine, my joints more supple. But I will not be tempted to set myself in opposition to thee. I am thy creature, and I will be even mild and docile to my natural lord and king if thou wilt also perform thy part, the which thou owest me. Oh, Frankenstein, be not equitable to every other and trample upon me alone, to whom thy justice, and even thy clemency and affection, is most due. Remember that I am thy creature; I ought to be thy Adam, but I am rather the fallen angel, whom thou drivest from joy for no misdeed. Everywhere I see bliss, from which I alone am irrevocably excluded. I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend. Make me happy, and I shall again be virtuous.”

My words seemed to have some effect. You lowered your arm, though your expression remained one of guarded abhorrence. You stared at me, perhaps for the first time seeing not just a monster, but a being capable of reason and speech.

“Begone! I will not hear you,” you stammered, turning away. “There can be no community between you and me; we are enemies. Begone, or let us try our strength in a fight, in which one must fall.”

“How can I move thee?” I continued, my voice rising with the passion of my plea. “Will no entreaties cause thee to turn a favourable eye upon thy creature, who implores thy goodness and compassion? Believe me, Frankenstein, I was benevolent; my soul glowed with love and humanity; but am I not alone, miserably alone? You, my creator, abhor me; what hope can I gather from your fellow creatures, who owe me nothing? they spurn and hate me. The desert mountains and dreary glaciers are my refuge. I have wandered here many days; the caves of ice, which I only do not fear, are a dwelling to me, and the only one which man does not grudge. These bleak skies I hail, for they are kinder to me than your fellow beings. If the multitude of mankind knew of my existence, they would do as you do, and arm themselves for my destruction. Shall I not then hate them who abhor me? I will keep no terms with my enemies. I am miserable, and they shall share my wretchedness. Yet it is in your power to recompense me, and deliver them from an evil which it only remains for you to make gust, until it be satiated with the blood of your remaining friends. My companion must be of the same species and have the same defects. This being you must create.”

You have heard my proposition; and it is my last. Your horror is plain upon your countenance; I see you recoil from me as from a serpent. Yet it is not I, but your own soul, which you should look upon with such detestation. I am but the consequence of your ambition, the shadow cast by your unhallowed light. To this demand you must now give answer.

Your lips trembled, and for a moment I believed you would again give way to senseless fury; but instead a torrent of words, cold and sharp as the ice beneath our feet, burst from you. “Never,” you cried, your voice echoing with a dreadful finality among the silent peaks. “Never will I consent to this. I will not be the creator of a race of devils to torment the world and plague the future generations of man. Am I to be the cursed father of a new species, whose only delight is in malice and destruction? You have murdered my brother, you have brought misery upon my family; and now you ask me to fashion a companion for you, another fiend as foul as yourself, so that you may together propagate your vile kind and make the earth a new hell. Begone, monster! My abhorrence of you can only be surpassed by the execration I feel at this, your most diabolical request.”

You turned from me as if the very sight of my form was a poison to your eyes; but I did not move. I allowed your words to dissipate into the frozen air, knowing that passion must soon give way to the colder calculus of reason and fear. “Is this your final decree?” I asked, my own voice now bereft of the entreating tone it had held before, and instead imbued with a solemn gravity. “Then you have sealed the fate not only of your wretched creature, but of all you hold dear. You speak of a race of devils, yet you would leave a single, solitary one to roam the world, a being of immense power and greater misery, with no tie to bind him, no affection to soothe his rage.

“Listen to me, Frankenstein; hear my oath. I do not ask for a companion to wage war upon your kind. I ask for her that I might flee from it. Grant me this one request, and I swear to you, by the sun that gives us light and by the very creator whose name you so often invoke in your curses against me, that I will take my bride and quit forever the continent of Europe, and every place inhabited by man. We shall take refuge in the vast and untamed wilds of South America. There are immense jungles where the foot of man has never penetrated, deserts of such scale that we might live and die without ever beholding another human face. My food is not that of man; I do not destroy the lamb and the kid to glut my appetite; acorns and berries afford me sufficient nourishment. My companion will be of the same nature, and will be content with the same fare. We shall live in a way that will offer no injury to man, who will have ceased to be our fellow creature. We shall be monsters, cut off from all the world; but on that account we shall be more attached to one another. Our lives will not be happy, for what happiness can there be for such as we? But they will be harmless, and free from the misery I now feel. Oh, my creator, make me happy in this small degree, and I shall be virtuous once more; deny me, and I shall glut the maw of death, until it is satiated with the blood of your remaining friends.”

As I spoke, I saw a change come over your features. The fire of indignation was quenched, and in its place arose a look of profound and agonizing deliberation. The logic of my proposal, and the dreadful certainty of my threat, waged a war within your soul. You paced back and forth upon the glacier, your brow furrowed in thought, your hands clasped behind you as if to restrain them from some violent act. I remained as still as a statue of despair, watching you, my creator, my master, my judge.

“You would have me believe you?” you said at last, halting and fixing your gaze upon me, though it was the gaze of a man looking into an abyss. “You, who have already shown your capacity for deceit and murder? You swear an oath, but what is an oath to one who feels no fellowship with man, no fear of God? You might promise to leave, but once you possess your mate, what is to prevent you from turning upon me with redoubled fury? And what of her? I may form her body, but I cannot dictate her will. She may be ten thousand times more malignant than you, and delight, for its own sake, in murder and wretchedness. She might spurn you, her intended companion, and leave you more bitter and vengeful than before. Or, worse still, you might procreate, and a race of devils would indeed be propagated upon the earth who might make the very existence of the species of man a condition precarious and full of terror. Have you considered these things, fiend? Or are you so blinded by your own selfish misery that you cannot see the ruin you ask me to unleash?”

His arguments were sound; they struck me with a force I had not anticipated, for they revealed a foresight into possibilities I had, in my own solitude, failed to contemplate. Yet my own desperation was a stronger force still. “I have considered it,” I answered, my voice trembling with the effort to remain calm. “And I see the risks. But they are as nothing compared to the certainty of my vengeance should you refuse. You speak of her will; I will be her Adam, and she my Eve. She will look upon me, the only being of her kind, and find solace in my companionship. We shall have a bond you cannot comprehend, the bond of shared deformity and shared exile. I shall be her world, as she will be mine. As for progeny, I swear to you that we shall have none. We are not as man, driven by the same instincts. Our wretchedness will be our own; we shall not inflict it upon a new generation. We shall be the first and the last of our kind.

“Consider my state,” I continued, advancing a step, my hands now clasped in a gesture of supplication. “My days are a torment of loneliness, my nights a hell of dreams from which I wake to a reality more terrible still. I see the love of the cottagers, I hear of the bonds of family, of brother and sister, of husband and wife. I see the birds in the air with their mates, the beasts of the field in their herds. Everything in this world has a companion, every being has another to share its joys and sorrows. Every being, Frankenstein, except me. I, who am more sensitive, more capable of profound affection than any of them, am doomed to be alone. You, my creator, would condemn me to this eternal torture. Is this your justice? Is this your mercy?”

I saw a tear escape your eye and trace a path down your pale cheek; it was not a tear of pity for me, I knew, but of despair for your own predicament. You were a man caught in a trap of his own making, and every possible escape led to some new form of horror. You looked away from me, towards the jagged spire of Montanvert, its peak shrouded in mist. The silence between us stretched for what seemed an eternity, broken only by the faint groan of the glacier far below.

“If I do this,” you said at last, your voice a mere whisper, hollow and defeated, “if I consent to this unhallowed labour… you must swear to me. Swear in the most solemn manner that you will leave these continents forever, you and your companion, the moment she is complete and I have delivered her to you.”

A dark and overwhelming joy, a feeling so long forgotten it was almost painful, surged through me. Hope, that faint and flickering light, began to burn once more in the desolate chambers of my heart. “I swear,” I cried, my voice ringing with a newfound strength. “I swear it by the earth which is my only bed, and the sky which is my only roof. I swear it by the memory of the joys I have never known and the pains I have so intimately felt. I swear it by yourself, my creator, the author of my being and my misery. The moment I have her, we shall depart. I will prepare a vessel and we will sail to the remotest corner of the globe. You shall hear of me no more. My evil will be drawn from me, and with it, all cause for your fear. I shall be a virtuous and gentle being, for I shall be at peace.”

You shuddered at my words, at the fervour with which they were spoken. “Your peace is to be bought with my eternal damnation,” you murmured to yourself, though I heard you plainly. You wrung your hands, your gaze darting about as if seeking some escape that did not exist. “I must have time to consider. This task… it is too horrible to contemplate with haste. The materials… the process… it fills me with a revulsion that borders on madness.”

“You must not consider,” I said, pressing my advantage, for I saw you wavering, saw the door of my damnation beginning to close once more. “You must promise. Your consideration will only lead you back to refusal, and my despair will turn to a rage that will consume us all. Promise me now, Frankenstein, and I will leave you. I will not trouble you during your work; I will watch from a distance, unseen, and know that you are labouring for my sake. I will be patient. But you must begin, and you must promise to complete it. Give me your word, as a man of honour, though you have shown me none.”

The struggle within you was a tempest visible on your face. Your complexion grew ashen, your breath came in ragged gasps. You looked from my expectant, hideous form to the vast, indifferent mountains that surrounded us. You were a prisoner, and I the jailor who offered a single, terrible key.

Finally, with a groan that seemed torn from the very depths of your being, you yielded. “I consent,” you said, the words barely audible. “I consent to your demand. I will create a female companion for you. But it is on this one, absolute condition: that you depart from this hemisphere forever, you and she, and that you never again make your presence known to the race of man. If you break this oath, I swear to you that I will pursue you to the ends of the earth and destroy you both.”

My heart leaped with a wild, triumphant ecstasy. I had won. After all my suffering, after all my desolation, a glimmer of paradise was now within my grasp. “I will not break my oath,” I said solemnly. “I will be faithful to my engagement. I will watch your progress with an anxiety that will be my only companion until she is mine. But I will not interfere; I will remain in the shadows, a silent observer. Now, I will leave you. Go; commence your labours. I shall be near at hand to receive my boon.”

With these words, I turned from you. I did not offer any further thanks, for I knew that your consent was born not of compassion but of terror. I descended the glacier with a speed and agility that you could not match, my heart, for the first time since my wretched birth, filled not with sorrow or rage, but with a profound and tremulous hope. The world, which had been a barren wasteland, now seemed to hold the promise of a distant, hidden garden. I looked back but once from the base of the mountain and saw your small, dark figure still standing upon the sea of ice, a solitary monument to the despair which had just granted me my first taste of joy. I knew that your agony was only beginning, but for myself, I felt as if my life, the true life for which I had so desperately longed, was at last about to commence. I plunged into the pine forests, my mind already filled with visions of my future companion, and of the distant, sunlit shore where we would at last find peace.

Felix visited the grate at night and made known to the prisoner his intentions in his favour. The Turk, amazed and delighted, endeavoured to kindle the zeal of his deliverer by promises of reward and wealth. Felix rejected his offers with contempt, yet when he saw the lovely Safie, who was permitted to visit her father and who by her gestures expressed her lively gratitude, the youth could not help owning to his own mind that the captive possessed a treasure which would fully reward his toil and hazard.

The Turk quickly perceived the impression that his daughter had made on the heart of Felix and endeavoured to secure him more entirely in his interests by the promise of her hand in marriage so soon as he should be conveyed to a place of safety. Felix was too delicate to accept this offer, yet he looked forward to the probability of that event as to the consummation of his happiness.

During the ensuing days, while the preparations were going forward for the escape of the merchant, the zeal of Felix was warmed by several letters that he received from this lovely girl, who found means to express her thoughts in the language of her lover by the aid of an old man, a servant of her father who understood French. She thanked him in the most ardent terms for his intended services towards her parent, and at the same time she gently deplored her own fate.

I have copies of these letters, for I found means, during my solitary rambles, to enter the cottage when its inhabitants were absent and to furnish myself with writing materials. I shall now present them to you; they will prove the truth of my tale; but I must earnestly entreat you to read them with attention, for they discover a mind so amiable and enlightened that they will surely excite your admiration and sympathy.

The first of these letters expressed her thoughts thus:

“To the good spirit who is to be my deliverer, I am a stranger to the manners of your country, and I know not how to address you. But my father has told me of your generous intentions, and my heart overflows with gratitude. I am the daughter of a man who was once powerful and wealthy; now, I am a captive, and a witness to his degradation. Yet my own sorrows are as nothing compared to the anguish I feel for him. When I see you, when I hear your voice, a strange hope fills my breast; a hope that you will not only save my father from an unjust death, but that you will perhaps rescue me from a fate which I dread more than death itself.

My mother was a Christian Arab, seized and made a slave by the Turks. Recommended by her beauty, she had won the heart of my father, who married her. The lessons she taught me were not those of the harem; she taught me to aspire to higher powers of intellect and an independence of spirit forbidden to the women of my country. I was taught to worship the Prophet; but my mother’s precepts and example instilled into me a love for the Christian religion and its purer doctrines. She died, but her lessons are indelibly impressed on my heart.

It is my father’s intention to take me with him to his native country, and there to confine me within the walls of a harem, allowed only the society of slaves. I have been promised to a man whom I have never seen, and for whom I can feel no affection. I cannot bear the thought of such a life. I would rather die than be condemned to such an inglorious servitude.

Therefore, my deliverer, when you have freed my father, do not desert me. Do not suffer me to be dragged to a country where I shall be miserable. Let me remain in a land where women are allowed to be rational creatures, and where they are treated with respect and consideration. My father has promised you my hand; do not refuse it. Let your love be my shield and my protection. Let me learn your language, and adopt your customs; I will be a docile and grateful pupil. Your country will be my country, and your people my people. I will worship your God, and my future life shall be one long act of gratitude to you, my preserver.”

Such were the sentiments of the Arabian, and they did not fail to enchant the heart of Felix. He looked upon her as a being of a superior nature. He replied to her letters in the most tender and respectful terms, assuring her of his devotion and his resolve to unite his fate with hers. The day for the execution of the Turk was fixed; but on the night previous to it, he quitted his prison and before morning was distant many leagues from Paris. Felix had procured passports in the name of his father, sister, and himself. He had previously communicated his plan to the former, who aided the deceit by quitting his house, under the pretence of a journey, and concealing himself, with his daughter, in an obscure part of Paris.

Felix conducted the fugitives through France to Lyons and across Mont Cenis to Leghorn, where the merchant had decided to wait a favourable opportunity of passing into some part of the Turkish dominions. Safie resolved to remain with her father until the last moment, before she entrusted herself to the protection of her lover. As for Felix, he was torn by a thousand conflicting anxieties. He was aware that he had been guilty of a breach of the laws of his country; he had assisted the escape of a man condemned by its tribunals; and he knew that his crime, if discovered, would be punished with the utmost severity. He was also tormented by the thought of his father and sister, whom he had left in a state of uncertainty and peril. He longed to return to them, yet he could not bear to part from Safie.

His suspense was soon terminated; for while he was expecting the return of the merchant, who had been absent for some days on business, a fatal discovery took place. The flight of the Turk was discovered, and the French government, enraged at his escape, bestowed its vengeance upon the family of his deliverer. De Lacey and Agatha were thrown into prison. The news reached Felix and threw him into the deepest despair. He instantly resolved to return to Paris and deliver himself up to the vengeance of the law, but he was restrained by the tears of Safie, who conjured him not to abandon her to the merciless fury of her father, who, she knew, would now be more than ever resolved to force her to return with him to Turkey.

In the meantime, the trial of De Lacey and Agatha was concluded. They were condemned to perpetual exile and the confiscation of all their property. This sentence, cruel as it was, brought a degree of comfort to the heart of Felix; he was no longer in suspense, and he knew that he was not the cause of their death. He hastened to join them in their exile. They found a miserable asylum in the cottage in which I first beheld them, and where for five years they had lived in penury and oblivion, supported only by the labour of Felix and the unceasing attentions of his amiable sister.

Such was the history of these worthy cottagers. It impressed me deeply. I learned, from the letters of Safie, to admire the virtues of the enlightened and independent spirit; I learned from the fate of the De Laceys, the cruel injustice of which men were capable. I saw that wealth and station were but fleeting possessions, and that the only true and lasting treasures were those of the heart and mind. But I also learned that these treasures were not sufficient to protect their possessors from the malice and cruelty of their fellow creatures.

The narrative of Felix, however, did not account for the presence of the Arabian. Several months passed, during which my observations were confined to the daily routine of the cottage. I saw the family struggle with poverty; I saw the tender care with which Agatha ministered to her blind father; I saw the patient industry of Felix, as he toiled in the garden or chopped wood for the fire. Their sorrows and their virtues endeared them to me more and more. My love for them was deep and sincere, but it was a love mixed with the bitterest anguish. I was a solitary, wretched outcast, forever barred from their society.

One day, as I was watching from my hovel, I was surprised to see Felix return from his work at an unusually early hour. His countenance was expressive of a joy that I had not seen on it for a long time. He embraced his sister and his father, and then, drawing a letter from his pocket, he read it aloud. I could not understand all that he read, but I gathered that it was from Safie, and that she was on her way to join them.

The joy of the family was extreme. The old man raised his sightless eyes to heaven and uttered a prayer of thanksgiving. Agatha wept tears of delight. Felix paced the cottage with hurried steps, his face radiant with happiness. The next few days were spent in eager expectation. The cottage was cleaned and set in order, and a little feast was prepared for the reception of the beloved guest.

At length, she arrived. She was more beautiful than I had ever imagined. Her dark eyes shone with intelligence and sensibility; her raven hair fell in rich profusion over her shoulders; and her whole figure was expressive of a grace and dignity that I had never before witnessed. She was accompanied by a guide, whom she dismissed with a handsome reward. She threw herself into the arms of Felix, and for a few moments, they were both silent, overcome by the intensity of their emotions. Then she turned to Agatha and embraced her with the affection of a sister. Finally, she knelt at the feet of the old man, and taking his hand, she pressed it to her lips.

The scene that followed was one of pure and unadulterated happiness. Safie related the story of her journey, and of the difficulties she had overcome to reach them. Her father, she said, enraged at her refusal to accompany him to Turkey, had left her at Leghorn, with a small sum of money, and had forbidden her ever to see him again. She had remained for some time in a state of great distress, not knowing what to do or where to go. At length, she had found means to discover the place of their exile, and had immediately set out to join them.

Her narrative filled me with admiration for her courage and devotion. She was, indeed, a being of a superior order, a worthy companion for the noble-minded Felix. I watched them day after day, and my heart swelled with a mixture of emotions that I can hardly describe. I rejoiced in their happiness, but at the same time, I was consumed with a bitter and burning envy. They had each other; they had love, and friendship, and sympathy. I had nothing. I was a monster, an outcast, a blot upon the face of the earth.

These reflections became more and more agonizing. The more I saw of the perfect happiness of the cottagers, the more I felt the misery of my own condition. I longed to reveal myself to them, to throw myself at their feet and implore their compassion. But I was deterred by the fear of their abhorrence. I knew that my hideous appearance would only inspire them with terror and disgust.

Yet, I could not bear to remain in this state of miserable solitude. I resolved to make an attempt to win their favour. I had observed that the old man was blind, and I thought that I might, perhaps, succeed in gaining his goodwill before he had an opportunity of seeing my deformity. I determined to enter the cottage when he was alone, and to try the effect of my voice and my words upon him. I hoped that if I could win his friendship, he might be able to persuade his children to tolerate my presence.

I waited for a favourable opportunity. One day, Felix, Agatha, and Safie went out for a long walk in the woods. I knew that they would not return for several hours. The old man was left alone in the cottage. My heart beat violently. The moment had arrived. With a trembling hand, I knocked at the door.

“Come in,” said a voice from within, a voice whose gentle and aged tones I knew so well. It was the voice of the old man, De Lacey. The simple invitation, offered to an unknown guest, was like a balm to my tortured spirit; it was the first sound of human kindness that had ever been directed towards me, and it filled my breast with a sudden and overwhelming sense of hope. Yet, this very hope was so new and fragile a sensation that it brought with it its own species of terror; I feared it would be dashed to pieces in the next instant, and for a moment I was rooted to the spot, unable to move either forwards or backwards.

“Enter, friend,” the old man called again, his voice tinged with a slight note of curiosity. “The wind is rising, and you will be cold. Do not stand upon ceremony; my hearth is humble, but it is warm, and you are welcome to share it.”

This second appeal gave me the strength I needed. With a heart that hammered against my ribs like a trapped bird, I pressed down upon the latch and pushed the door inwards. I entered the cottage, the abode of my protectors, the sacred temple of my secret worship. For the first time, I stood within the walls I had only ever gazed upon from my wretched hovel. The interior was as simple as I had imagined, yet it possessed a warmth and a cleanliness that seemed to me the very essence of paradise. A fire crackled merrily on the hearth, casting a dancing, golden light upon the wooden furniture and the earnest, sightless face of the old man, who sat in his customary chair, his hands resting on his knees, his head inclined towards the door in an attitude of gentle expectation.

I closed the door softly behind me and stood for a long moment in silence, my form shrouded in the shadows near the entrance. I was overcome; the air itself seemed thick with the scent of domestic peace, of a life I had craved with every fibre of my being. My throat was constricted, and though I burned to speak, to pour forth the torrent of words I had so painstakingly acquired, I could not form a single sound.

The old man, perceiving my continued silence, spoke again. “You are a traveller, I presume, sir,” he said, his tone one of perfect courtesy. “You seem distressed; I hear it in your breath. Pray, draw near to the fire and recover yourself. My children are away upon an errand, but they will return ere long, and we shall offer you what little we have for your supper.”

His kindness was a sharp and painful pleasure. I took a hesitant step forward, my immense and uncouth form moving with a stealthiness born of long habit. I remained out of the direct firelight, still clinging to the shadows as a drowning man clings to a piece of driftwood. At last, summoning all my courage, I spoke, and my voice, though hoarse and untried from disuse, was filled with a tremor of profound emotion.

“Pardon this intrusion, venerable sir,” I began. “I am indeed a traveller, and a most wretched one. I have journeyed far and have found no rest, no welcome, in all this wide world. I heard tell of your goodness, of the benevolence that dwells in this cottage, and I have come to you as a last resort, to beg for a moment of your compassion.”

De Lacey’s countenance, which had been turned towards me with placid attention, now assumed an expression of the deepest pity. “My poor friend,” he said, “it is with sorrow that I hear your tale, for it is a common one. The world is full of injustice and hardship, and those who have suffered the most are often the most deserving of peace. But you have not erred in coming here. Sit down, I entreat you. Tell me what troubles you; a shared sorrow is a lightened one, and though I am poor and blind, I may yet be able to offer you some comfort or advice. My ears, at least, are open to you, and they will not judge you by your coat or your countenance.”

These last words struck me with the force of a revelation. He could not see me! I had known it, I had planned for it, but to hear from his own lips this confirmation of my one great advantage filled me with a dizzying, ecstatic hope. I drew a low stool near to his chair, yet still keeping my face averted from the full glare of the fire, and sat down. For a few moments, a blissful peace descended upon me. I was there, in their home, accepted, conversed with as an equal. It was a dream from which I feared at any moment to awaken to the cold reality of my hovel.

“You are too kind,” I stammered, my voice thick with unshed tears. “I have seen so much of the world’s cruelty that I had almost despaired of finding a single benevolent heart. I am an outcast, sir, scorned and hated by all who behold me.”

“Hated?” the old man repeated with surprise. “And for what reason? Have you committed some great crime?”

“No crime,” I replied with vehemence. “I have hurt no living thing. My heart was, I believe, formed for love and sympathy; but I am an unfortunate, a wretch whose appearance is so hideous and appalling that all fly from me in horror. I am alone, miserably alone.”

A deep sigh escaped the old man’s lips. “If this is your misfortune, then I pity you from the bottom of my heart,” he said. “It is a sad prejudice that men entertain. I, too, have suffered from the injustice of my fellows, though for different reasons. My family and I were once prosperous and happy, but the treachery of a man we trusted, and the biased judgment of our country, have reduced us to this state of poverty and exile. But tell me, friend, whence do you come, and whither are you going? Do you have no family, no relations who might take you in?”

The question pierced me like a dagger. “I have no one,” I answered, and the desolation in my own voice terrified me. “I am a creature of a different kind, I think, than any you have ever seen. I have no father, no mother, no brother or sister. I am a solitary being, a monster cast out from all human society.”

I had gone too far, and I instantly regretted the word “monster.” I saw a flicker of alarm, or perhaps only confusion, on the old man’s face. I hastened to recover my ground, to frame my plea in a manner less shocking.

“Forgive my strange words,” I said quickly. “Grief has made me wild. I do not mean to say I am not a man, but only that my afflictions have set me so far apart from my species that I feel as if I am not of them. But it is not for myself that I have come to plead.” This was a sudden inspiration, a stratagem by which I might test the waters before revealing the terrible truth. “I have… a friend, a person who is as dear to me as my own life. It is his cause I wish to place before you.”

“A friend?” said De Lacey, his expression softening once more. “Speak on. I am eager to hear.”

My mind raced, weaving the fiction as I spoke. “This friend of mine,” I continued, my voice gaining a new ardour, “has long observed your family from a distance. He has watched you in your poverty and your exile, and his heart has been filled with a love and admiration that borders on reverence. He has seen the angelic goodness of your daughter, the noble spirit of your son, and the gentle wisdom which radiates from you, sir, like light from the sun. He longs, more than for anything in this world, to be known to you, to serve you, and to be counted among your friends.”

The old man smiled, a beautiful and benevolent expression. “We are simple people, and have little to offer in the way of friendship except for honest hearts. But why does your friend not come himself? Why does he send an ambassador?”

“Alas,” I cried, “that is the very core of his misery! He suffers from the same affliction as I. He is deformed, of a ghastly and terrifying appearance. He fears that if your children were to see him, they would not see the goodness of his heart, but only the horror of his visage. He is convinced they would drive him away with scorn and abhorrence, and the very thought of such a reception from those he so deeply reveres is an agony he cannot bear to risk.”

I paused, breathless, hanging on the old man’s reply as if my very existence depended upon it, and indeed it did.

De Lacey placed a hand on his chin and mused for some time. The cottage was silent but for the crackling of the logs and the frantic beating of my own heart. When he finally spoke, his words were a stream of pure, life-giving water to my parched soul.

“This is a delicate matter,” he said slowly. “I will not deny that the first sight of a great deformity is often shocking to the young and inexperienced. But my children are not so shallow. Agatha has a heart full of the tenderest compassion, and Felix, for all his ardour, is a man of great sense and justice. I am persuaded that if your friend is, as you say, of a virtuous and gentle disposition, they will be able to look beyond the exterior to the true worth within. The soul of a man is the only thing of value; his outward form is but a fleeting vessel.”

“Oh, if only I could believe it!” I exclaimed, unable to contain my emotion. I leaned forward, my hands clasped in supplication. “They are so good, so perfect. Their rejection would be his death.”

“Then he must not be rejected,” the old man declared with a firm and noble energy. “You must bring him here. I will prepare my children. I will speak to them of this unfortunate, and I will plead his cause. I will teach them that true ugliness lies in a vicious heart, not a misshapen form. Trust me, my friend. I will not fail you. When they return, I will begin to lay the groundwork. Let your friend come tomorrow, or the day after, and I swear to you, he will find a welcome here.”

Tears of a joy so exquisite that it was almost pain streamed from my eyes. He would help me. He, the wise and good De Lacey, would be my advocate. The barrier of my ugliness would be broken down by his benevolent mediation. I should be accepted; I should have friends; I should at last find a place in the world. In a transport of gratitude, I seized the old man’s hand, a hand soft with age and disuse, and pressed it to my lips. My movements were clumsy and abrupt, a product of my overwhelming feelings.

“Generous, noble De Lacey!” I cried. “You have given me a new life! You have saved me from a despair blacker than the grave. But do not wait. Let me plead my own cause. I am that friend! I am the wretch, the outcast, of whom I have spoken!”

As I uttered these words, I fell at his knees, still clutching his hand, and looked up at his face with an expression of desperate entreaty. A profound change came over his features. The benevolent calm was replaced by an expression of bewilderment and rising alarm. He could not see my face, but he could feel the strange, unnatural texture of my skin; he could hear the wild, desperate passion in my voice; and he could sense the great, hulking form that now knelt before him.

He attempted to pull his hand away, a gesture of instinctive revulsion. “What is this?” he cried, his voice trembling. “Who are you? Your hand is cold… Your voice… It is not human! Let go of me! Save me! Felix! Agatha!”

At that very instant, as if summoned by his terrified cries, the cottage door flew open. My heart stopped. There they stood, framed in the doorway, their faces illuminated by the firelight within: Felix, Agatha, and the beautiful Safie, returned from their walk.

For a moment, all was frozen in a tableau of utter horror. Their eyes fell upon me, a gigantic, misshapen form kneeling at their father’s feet, clutching his hand in a gesture they could only interpret as one of violence. A gasp of terror escaped Safie, who, true to her more delicate nature, immediately turned and fled into the darkness from which they had just emerged. Agatha, after one piercing shriek that echoed in the small room like the cry of a damned soul, covered her eyes and sank to the floor in a dead faint.

But Felix, it was the reaction of Felix that broke me. His face, which I had so often seen animated by love for his family and gentle joy, was now contorted into a mask of rage and horrified fury. He did not hesitate; he did not question. He saw only a monster assaulting his beloved, helpless father. With a roar of pure, protective instinct, he sprang forward, seized a heavy stick from the corner of the room, and rushed towards me.

He raised the stick and brought it down upon my shoulders with all the force of his powerful frame. The blow was agonizing, but the physical pain was as nothing, a mere pinprick compared to the cataclysm of despair that exploded in my soul. All my hopes, which a moment before had soared to the very heavens, were now dashed into a million pieces, ground into the dust by this single, brutal act. He struck me again, and again, across my back and my head. I could have torn him limb from limb with a fraction of my strength; I could have crushed him as a man crushes an insect. But I did not. I could not raise a hand against my protector, the man whose gentle conversation I had so long admired.

A howl of indescribable anguish, a sound more animal than human, burst from my chest. I released the old man’s hand, scrambled to my feet, and with a final, despairing glance at the scene of my utter ruin, I burst through the now open door and fled from the cottage. The blows of Felix rained down upon me even as I escaped, propelling me into the cold, dark night. I did not stop running. I ran blindly, heedlessly, through the woods, the branches tearing at my skin, the stones cutting my feet. But I felt nothing. The world outside of me had ceased to exist; there was only the raging tempest within, a maelstrom of shattered hope, blackest despair, and a new, terrible feeling that was beginning to curdle in the pit of my stomach: a burning, all consuming hatred.

I threw myself on the damp earth in my hovel, the darkness of my shelter a fitting tomb for the death of my spirit. Sounds of grief and fury, the howls of a desolate creature, filled the small space for hours. The sun rose, but its light brought me no comfort, only a renewed sense of my own accursed existence. My body ached from the blows I had received, but my heart, my soul, was a gaping wound from which all goodness and love seemed to be pouring out, to be replaced by a venomous and bitter gall.

For two days I remained in this state, alternating between fits of weeping that convulsed my entire frame and a sullen, motionless despair. I did not eat; I did not sleep. I watched the cottage, my eyes burning with a mixture of longing and rage. I saw signs of agitation and departure. Carts were loaded; possessions were packed. They were leaving. They were abandoning the home that I had, in my folly, considered a sanctuary. They were fleeing from me, from the monster who had dared to dream of their fellowship.

On the third night, a change came over me. The sorrow, which had been a consuming fire, burned itself out, leaving behind not ashes, but something harder and colder: a core of pure, unadulterated rage. A calm, terrible and unnatural, settled upon my spirit. I no longer wept for my lost paradise; instead, I cursed it. I cursed the cottagers for their prejudice, for their blind cruelty. But most of all, I cursed my creator, the one who had fashioned me in this hideous form and then abandoned me to a world that could only revile me.

He had made me capable of love, only to deny me all possibility of it. He had given me a mind to comprehend virtue, only to surround me with vice and injustice. He was the true author of my misery, the architect of my damnation. And in that moment, in the blackness of my hovel, as I watched the last of my protectors prepare to flee from my very memory, I made a vow.

I would find him. I would haunt him. I would turn his world into the same hell in which he had left me to suffer. My heart, once filled with a burning love for my fellow beings, was now a vessel of hate. If I could not inspire love, I would inspire fear. My sympathies were torn away, and I felt a fiendish joy spread through me as I declared, in the silence of the night, a war, an eternal war, against the species, and more than all, against him who had formed me and sent me forth to this insupportable misery. My travels would now have a purpose: not to seek fellowship, but to find my creator and inflict upon him a pain as vast and as unending as my own. The creature of benevolence and sensitivity was dead; a fiend of hell had been born from its ashes.

learn, inhabited Paris for some years when, for some reason which I could not understand, he became obnoxious to the government. He was seized and cast into prison, the very day that Safie arrived from Constantinople to join him. He was tried and condemned to death. The injustice of his sentence was very flagrant; all Paris was indignant; and it was judged that his religion and wealth rather than the crime alleged against him had been the cause of his condemnation.

My first resolution upon quitting the ashes of that beloved and now cursed cottage was one of savage and desolate revenge. The fiendish passion which had laid hold of my heart was not to be slaked by mere vows whispered into the uncaring night; it demanded a tangible sacrifice, a conflagration to mirror the inferno that now raged within my breast. I gathered with a feverish energy a great quantity of dry wood and brush, piling it with methodical fury against the walls of the dwelling which had once been my entire world. I laboured through the night, my movements swift and silent as a spectre’s, my mind a tempest of conflicting ecstasies of grief and hate. When my preparations were complete, I stood back and surveyed my work, a grim satisfaction stealing over me. This was the funeral pyre of my hopes, the monument to my eternal damnation.

With a blazing branch which I had lit from the smouldering embers of the cottagers’ last fire, I approached the pile. For a moment I paused, a final, agonising wave of sorrow washing over me. I saw in my mind’s eye the gentle face of the old man, the sweet, industrious form of Agatha, the noble countenance of Felix. But then I saw them again, their faces contorted with horror and disgust, fleeing from me as from a pestilence. The memory was a spur to my rage. I thrust the torch into the driest part of the wood. The flames, at first hesitant, licked at the kindling, and then, with a sudden and voracious roar, they leaped upwards, embracing the cottage in a fiery shroud. The light of the conflagration illuminated the woods for a great distance, and I, its sole author and spectator, danced and howled around it like an unbound demon. I watched as the greedy tongues of fire consumed the roof under which I had learned of love and virtue, as the walls which had sheltered my protectors crumbled into glowing ruin. When at last the edifice fell in upon itself with a great crash, sending a fountain of sparks towards the heavens, I felt a burst of horrid joy, and turning my back upon the scene of my former happiness, I directed my steps towards the dark and unknown world.

My travels henceforth were of a different character. I no longer sought to conceal myself out of a gentle fear of offending, but out of a burning hatred and a desire to work mischief. My guide in this pursuit was a small collection of papers which I had long before discovered in the pocket of the garment I had taken from the laboratory. During the many long hours of my lonely observations at the cottage, I had, with the aid of my newly acquired knowledge of letters, deciphered their contents. What a tissue of horrors! They were the journal of my creator, written during the four months that preceded my birth. With a minuteness that thrilled me with despair, he described the formation of my odious person, the hideousness of my aspect, and the disgust which had filled him when he first beheld me. Every word was a dagger plunged into my heart. He called me his wretch, his miserable monster, and spoke of his flight from the apartment where he had left me. The papers also detailed his family and his native country. Geneva! This, then, was the nest of my accursed creator. I resolved to proceed thither, my soul burning with a desire for a confrontation which I felt was my only remaining purpose in existence.

The journey was long and arduous. I travelled only by night, subsisting on berries and roots, and sleeping in the most desolate and inaccessible places. Autumn had begun to strip the trees of their foliage, and the winds grew colder; the rains drenched my unprotected frame, and the world seemed to take on the bleak and barren aspect of my own soul. The moon, which had once been my comforter and sole companion in my nocturnal rambles, now seemed a cold, indifferent eye, a witness to my wretchedness. I saw the sun, when its first rays pierced the gloom of my retreat, not as a source of warmth and life, but as the signal for man to awaken and renew his injustices. My heart was a storehouse of bitter thoughts and vengeful imaginings.

One day, as I was passing through a thick wood, my progress was arrested by the sound of a rapid stream. I drew near and beheld a young girl, a child of perhaps five or six years, playing upon its banks, chasing a butterfly with innocent glee. As I watched from my concealment, my heart, for the first time in many weeks, felt a pang of something other than hatred. She was a beautiful creature, and her joyful cries were like music to my tormented ears. Suddenly, her foot slipped on the wet stones, and she fell into the swift current. Without a moment’s hesitation, a strange and lingering impulse of that benevolence I had so recently adjured prompted me to action. I rushed from my hiding place and, plunging into the stream, snatched the child from the watery grave that awaited her. With great difficulty I bore her to the bank, where I endeavoured to restore her to consciousness.

At that moment, a man, a rustic, who must have been her father, appeared. Upon seeing me, a misshapen creature of gigantic stature, bending over the apparently lifeless form of his child, he uttered a cry of terror and rage. He did not pause to comprehend the scene, but, snatching the child from my arms, he fled into the wood. I stood, stupefied by this injustice, my good deed repaid with revulsion. My stupor, however, was quickly broken, for the man, having placed the child in safety, returned, and levelling a gun which he carried, he fired. I felt a sharp, agonising pain in my shoulder, and fell to the ground. The man, satisfied with his work, disappeared again into the forest.

I lay for some time, writhing in the agony of my wound and, still more, of my renewed despair. This, then, was my reward for an act of compassion! This was the justice of man! I had saved a human being from destruction, and in return I was shot and wounded, treated as a ravenous beast. The wound in my shoulder was a trifle to the wound inflicted upon my soul. The last lingering spark of warmth in my heart was extinguished by this act of cruel ingratitude. My feelings of kindness and gentleness, which had not been entirely eradicated, were now converted into a bitter and galling rage. I tore the ball from my flesh and, with a howl of execration, I renewed my vow of eternal hatred and vengeance against all mankind. Henceforth, I abjured all feelings of compassion; if I could not be loved, I would be feared, and my existence should be a terror and a scourge to those who spurned me. My hatred became a thousand times more bitter, my desire for revenge a consuming fire.

For several weeks I wandered, my wound healing slowly, my spirit festering with malice. I avoided all human habitation with greater care than ever, and my only solace was to curse my creator and to plot the ruin I would bring upon him. At last, after many weary nights, I saw from the summit of a hill the white steeple of a church which I knew, from the descriptions in the journal, must be in the environs of Geneva. A thrill of diabolical satisfaction ran through me. I had reached my goal. I hid myself in a wood that bordered the plain, resolving to wait for an opportunity to reveal myself to my creator.

The evening was drawing in, and I was concealed amongst a grove of cypress and chestnut trees, when I beheld a beautiful child, who came running towards my hiding place. He was not more than four or five years of age, and his features bore a stamp of angelic innocence. A strange thought entered my mind. This little creature, I reasoned, was yet unsullied by the prejudices of the world; he had not learned to detest deformity. If I could seize him and educate him as my companion and friend, I should not be so desolate, so utterly alone. I could teach him to love me, and in his affection, I might find a semblance of peace.

Impelled by this sudden and desperate idea, I sprang from my concealment and seized him by the arm. The child, far from being the pliant pupil of my fantasy, screamed aloud with terror. “Let me go,” he cried, “monster! Ugly wretch! You wish to eat me and tear me to pieces. You are an ogre. Let me go, or I will tell my papa.”

“Boy,” I said, attempting to calm him, “you need not fear. I will not hurt you. Only come with me.”

“Hideous monster!” he shrieked, struggling violently. “Let me go. My papa is a Syndic, he is M. Frankenstein; he will punish you. You dare not keep me.”

Frankenstein! The name struck me like a thunderbolt. This, then, was the son of my enemy, a scion of the accursed race. A paroxysm of fury seized me. All my sufferings, all my wrongs, rushed upon my mind, and I saw in this child the image of his father, the author of my being and my misery. “Frankenstein!” I exclaimed, “you belong then to my enemy, to him towards whom I have sworn eternal revenge; you shall be my first victim.”

The child still struggled and loaded me with epithets which carried new venom to my heart. My hands were on his throat before I knew what I did, and in a moment he lay dead at my feet.

I gazed upon my victim, and my heart swelled with an ecstasy of hellish triumph. I had done a deed of blood, and the guilt, far from oppressing me, seemed to give me a new and terrible power. I had struck a blow at my creator, a blow that would pierce him to the very soul. I clapped my hands and exclaimed aloud, “I too can create desolation; my enemy is not invulnerable; this death will carry despair to him, and a thousand other miseries shall torment and destroy him.”

As I stood exulting over my deed, my eyes fell upon a miniature that hung from the child’s neck. It was a portrait of a most lovely woman. I took it from the corpse. The features were gentle and benevolent, and as I gazed upon them, a strange feeling of envy and regret stole over me. I, who was forever barred from the delights of female sympathy, looked upon this image of beauty and goodness with a kind of madness. For a few moments I felt the pangs of my own desolate condition, but my rage soon returned. Why should I feel remorse? I had been driven to this act by the cruelty of others. This was but the first-fruits of my justified vengeance.

I left the scene of my crime and wandered in the woods, uncertain of my next move. As dawn approached, I sought refuge in a hovel, or rather a barn, which appeared to be attached to a small farmhouse. I entered and, finding a heap of straw, I laid myself down upon it to rest. I had scarcely settled when the door opened, and a young woman entered. She was clad in simple country attire, but her countenance possessed a sweetness and an innocence that arrested my attention. She moved about her morning tasks with a gentle grace. Then, feeling weary, she lay down upon a pile of straw not far from me and soon fell into a deep sleep.

She was beautiful, and I, a miserable wretch, looked upon her with a mixture of adoration and loathing. Here was an object of love, a being who could inspire and receive affection, while I was an outcast, a thing to be fled from and abhorred. This thought turned my fleeting admiration to gall. “Why,” I thought, “should she be happy while I am steeped in misery? Why should she enjoy the blessings of life, beauty, and companionship, while I am condemned to an existence of solitary wretchedness?” The thought was a lightning flash of malice that illuminated a new path of destruction. I would not only make my creator miserable by the death of his relation, but I would insure that another innocent being should suffer for my crime. The world had declared war on me; I would repay its injustice in kind.

I cautiously approached the sleeping girl. In her slumber, her features were composed and peaceful; a faint smile played upon her lips, as if she were dreaming of some happiness. The sight only hardened my resolve. “You shall suffer for my misery,” I whispered. “Your beauty, which should have been your shield, shall be the instrument of your ruin.” I took the miniature from my breast, the portrait of the beautiful lady, and, carefully opening the folds of the girl’s dress, I placed it within her bosom. I knew that this trinket, when discovered, would be considered incontrovertible proof of her guilt. She would be accused of the murder; her innocence would not save her from the blind and prejudiced justice of her fellow creatures.

Sleep on, sweet girl, and dream of happiness; for you shall awaken to despair and ignominy. I gazed upon her for a moment longer, a fiendish smile on my lips, and then, as the first light of morning began to creep into the barn, I stole away, my heart swelling with a sense of wicked power. I had cast the die. I had set in motion a train of events that would bring untold sorrow upon the house of Frankenstein. My tale was not yet finished; this was but the prelude. I would seek him out in his grief and lay before him the catalogue of his crimes and my sufferings, and I would make a demand which, if he refused, would plunge us both into a war from which only one could emerge, leaving the other to the peace of utter annihilation. My journey was one of grim and terrible exultation. The very air I breathed, thin and sharp in those high altitudes, seemed to fuel the fire of my righteous vengeance. I leaped from crag to crag with a speed and agility that no son of Adam could ever hope to match, my form a fleeting shadow against the eternal snows. The mountains, which had before seemed a prison of desolate grandeur, now felt like a kingdom, my kingdom, where I was the sole sovereign, a monarch of ice and rock. The sun, when it broke through the clouds, cast a brilliant, cold light upon the glaciers, and I saw in their crystalline depths a reflection of my own hardened heart, multifaceted and impenetrable. The world of man, with its cottages and cities, its loves and its laws, lay far below me, a contemptible map of vanities from which I had been justly and irrevocably cast out. So be it; I would carve my own world from this desolate magnificence.

For several days this feeling of triumph sustained me. I feasted upon the wild berries and roots that clung to life in the crevices of the rocks, and I slaked my thirst with the pure, frigid water of the mountain streams. I slept in caves where the ice never melted, and the profound silence of the peaks was my only companion. Yet, as day bled into night and night into day, a new and more insidious feeling began to creep into my soul, like a slow frost spreading across a pane of glass. The exultation faded, and the wicked power I had felt in the barn ebbed away, leaving behind it a hollow ache, a void that even the vastness of the Alps could not fill. The solitude I had once embraced as a shield now became a torment. My thoughts, which had been a torrent of furious justification, slowed to a stagnant pool of reflection, and in its murky depths, I saw again the faces of my victims.

I saw the boy, William, his childish features contorted not by the anger I had desired, but by a simple, uncomprehending terror. I had intended to seize him, to educate him as my companion, to force him to see past my monstrous exterior; but his cries of “monster” had ignited a fuse within me, a rage so absolute that it had consumed all reason. His death had been an act of passion, of a moment’s madness. But the girl, Justine; what of her? Her fate was a product of cold, deliberate calculation. I pictured her waking from her sweet slumber, discovering the miniature, her confusion turning to fear, and her fear to utter despair as the inexorable machinery of human justice closed around her. I had condemned her not for any crime she had committed against me, but for the simple, damnable fact of her beauty and her potential for happiness. I had used her innocence as a weapon against my creator. Was this justice? Or was it a baseness that exceeded any I had yet witnessed in the annals of man, those very records which Felix had read to Safie? The question haunted me, and my mountain kingdom became a cell of infinite dimension.

I tried to reason away this burgeoning guilt. Had I not been wronged first? Was I not a creature of feeling, of intellect, of a capacity for love so profound that its denial had turned my very being to poison? They had called me monster, so a monster I would be. They had declared war; I had but fired the next volley. These arguments, which had seemed so potent in the heat of my anger, now rang hollow in the silent amphitheatre of the peaks. I looked at my own hands, those hands which I had once hoped might perform acts of kindness for my protectors, and I saw them now for what they were: instruments of murder, of a cruelty more refined and fiendlike than any I had read of in Volney’s histories. A great and terrible sorrow washed over me, a sorrow not for myself alone, but for the wretchedness I had inflicted. I, who had wept with Safie over the fate of the native Americans, had now become the cruel conqueror of another’s peace.

In my misery, I wandered for days without aim, a specter haunting the highest passes. Once, driven by a desperate and renewed desire to feel some connection, however remote, to the race of man, I descended from my icy perch to the upper pastures where shepherds sometimes grazed their flocks. From a great distance, concealed behind a ridge of granite, I watched a family; a man, his wife, and two small children. The father was mending a fence, his movements slow and deliberate. The mother sat on a patch of grass, a babe in her lap, while a slightly older boy chased a goat with shouts of glee. The scene was an idyll of rustic peace, a tableau of the very domestic bliss I had yearned for in the hovel of the De Laceys. I saw the woman look up at her husband, a smile of deep and easy affection on her face. He smiled back, and in that simple, silent exchange, I witnessed a universe of communion from which I was eternally barred.

The sight did not, as it once might have, fill me with a gentle longing. Instead, a hot, acrid jealousy, more potent than any I had yet known, rose in my throat. Why them? Why this unthinking, unearned joy? They were simple, unlettered folk, their minds untroubled by the grand questions of existence that so tortured my own. Yet they possessed the one treasure that mattered, the one thing for which I would have traded all my preternatural strength and all my hard won knowledge: the solace of fellowship. The boy, in his play, stumbled and fell, and for a moment, my heart leaped with a vicious hope that he was injured. But his mother was there in an instant, scooping him up, murmuring words of comfort, kissing away his tears. And the father paused in his work, his face a mask of concern that quickly softened to relief.

I could watch no more. I turned away, a groan of pure agony escaping my lips, a sound that was swallowed by the uncaring wind. Their happiness was a mirror that reflected my own hideous solitude, and I hated them for it. I hated their smiles, their touches, their love. My brief remorse for Justine vanished, incinerated in this fresh blaze of despair and rage. My crimes were not my own; they were the inevitable consequence of my creation, the logical conclusion to a syllogism of suffering. My creator had sewn the seeds of virtue and the desire for companionship deep within me, and then cast me out into a world that could only respond to my appearance with horror and disgust. He was the true murderer, the architect of all this woe. My rage, which had been turning inward, now found its proper target once more: him. It was Frankenstein who must pay.

That night, a storm of unparalleled fury descended upon the mountains. The heavens, which had been a clear, cold vault, were suddenly choked with black and churning clouds. Lightning, not in single flashes but in great, searing sheets, tore the darkness asunder, illuminating the jagged peaks in a momentary, ghastly glare. The thunder did not roll; it exploded, it crashed and cracked and cannonaded, as if the very mountains were being split from their foundations. The wind howled like a legion of damned souls, shrieking through the passes and tearing at the very rock to which I clung. Another being might have sought shelter, but I stood upon the highest pinnacle, my arms outstretched, and welcomed the elemental chaos. This storm was the world made manifest to my soul; this was the symphony of my own tormented heart.

“Rejoice!” I roared into the gale, my voice competing with the thunder. “Rejoice, ye elements, for you have found a companion in misery! Flash, ye lightnings, and show me the face of my enemy! Howl, ye winds, and carry my curse to the ears of my creator! He who dared to play God, who fashioned a life and then abandoned it to despair, let him now feel a fraction of the tempest that rages within my breast!”

I railed against the injustice of my existence, against the God who would permit such a creature as me to be formed, and against the man who had been His blasphemous deputy. I recounted my sorrows to the storm, my brief joys with the cottagers, my agonizing discovery of my own loathsomeness, my descent into malice. The rain lashed down, mingling with the tears that I, in my fury, did not realize I was shedding. For hours the tempest raged, and I with it, until both I and the sky were spent. As the dawn approached, the winds died down, the thunder receded into a distant grumble, and a profound, exhausted calm settled over the landscape. The air was washed clean, and the first rays of the rising sun touched the snow-capped peaks with a delicate, pinkish hue. The world was reborn, pristine and beautiful, but I was unchanged, a permanent stain upon its renewed purity.

The storm had purged me of all doubt, of all remorse. My resolve was now as hard and as cold as the glacier beneath my feet. I would wait. I would not hunt him in the world of men, for that would be to grant him the privilege of his own ground. No, he must come to me. He must seek out these desolate wastes in his grief, drawn by some morbid instinct to the scenes of nature that had once brought him peace. He would come to the mountains to mourn his brother, and he would find not solace, but me. I would be his confessor, his judge, and his tormentor.

Days passed. I watched the eagles circle on the thermals, and I learned the paths of the chamois. The waiting was a form of discipline, a honing of my will. I rehearsed my speech, the long and sorrowful tale you have just heard, shaping it, refining it, ensuring that every word would be a dart to pierce his arrogant heart. I would make him understand. I would force him to look upon his work and see not a monster, but a sentient being in agony. And then, I would make my demand. The demand for a companion, a female made in my own image, with whom I could share my exile and mitigate my loneliness. It was a just demand, a reasonable demand, and if he had a single spark of the creator’s responsibility within him, he would accede. If he refused… if he refused, then the war I had declared would be waged upon his entire species.

And then, one morning, as the mists still clung to the valleys below, I saw him. A figure, small and insignificant against the immense backdrop of the Mer de Glace, was slowly making his way up the torturous path. Even from that distance, I recognized the set of his shoulders, the determined yet weary pace. My heart, a muscle I had thought frozen into a solid block of ice, began to beat with a painful, suffocating rhythm. It was him. It was my creator, my Adam, my god, and my mortal foe. He was coming. He was ascending into my domain, climbing towards his judgment. A feeling unlike any I had ever known, a terrible fusion of dread, triumph, and a desperate, pathetic flicker of hope, seized me. I hid myself behind a great outcrop of rock, my gigantic frame trembling with the force of my emotions. Let him come closer, I thought. Let him reach the very heart of this sea of ice, this place that was to be our battlefield and our confessional. Then, and only then, would I reveal myself and begin the conversation that would determine the fate of both our worlds.

He had been accused of some crime against the state; a mockery of a trial had been held, and upon its conclusion he had been condemned to perpetual imprisonment. My benefactors, however, possessed a firm conviction of his innocence, for his character had ever been one of unimpeachable honour and benevolence. It was this conviction that set in motion the chain of events which brought them to their humble cottage and me, their unseen observer, to the hovel adjoining it.

Felix, with the ardent and generous spirit of youth, was inflamed with the gross injustice of the proceeding. He had been present at the court and had witnessed the prejudice and false testimony which had sealed the fate of the unfortunate merchant. His indignation knew no bounds; he vowed in his heart to effect the stranger's escape, a resolution as perilous as it was noble. The means by which he accomplished this design were complex and fraught with danger; I learned of them through conversations held in hushed tones, when the light was low and the children were thought to be asleep. He visited the Turk in his prison, and there a plan was concerted. It was in these secret interviews that he first beheld the Arabian, Safie, whose radiant beauty and despair at her father's condition moved him to an even greater pitch of determination.

A promise was made between them; the merchant, in his gratitude, swore that should Felix succeed in delivering him, he would bestow upon him the hand of his beloved daughter, along with a considerable fortune. To Felix, whose heart was already captivated by the charms of the lovely Arabian, this was a reward beyond all price; he would have undertaken the task for the sake of justice alone, but with the prospect of such a prize, his ardour became an unquenchable fire. He devoted his entire being to the enterprise, and after many weeks of anxious planning and breathless risk, he succeeded. The merchant was liberated and conveyed to a place of safety.

But now the perfidy of the man for whom my friend had risked all became apparent. The Turkish merchant, once free from the immediate threat of his French persecutors, looked with disdain upon a Christian son in law. He had used the generous nature of Felix as a tool for his own escape and had never intended to fulfil his part of the bargain. While he feigned compliance and made arrangements for his daughter to follow him after he had settled his affairs, he secretly plotted to betray his saviour.

The French government, enraged by the escape, had conducted a rigorous investigation, and the trail soon led to the De Lacey family. The aged, blind father and the gentle Agatha were seized as hostages for the fugitive Felix. They were thrown into prison, and all their wealth was confiscated by the state. When news of this terrible misfortune reached Felix, he was with the merchant and his daughter on the borders of Italy. A choice of the most agonising nature was presented to him: to pursue his love and abandon his family to their fate, or to return to Paris and share in their ruin. His sense of duty and filial piety did not permit him to hesitate for a moment. After a heart-rending farewell with Safie, in which they renewed their vows of eternal constancy, he set out for Paris, resolved to throw himself upon the mercy of the court.

The court, however, showed no mercy. Felix and his father and sister were condemned for their role in the escape, and after five months of imprisonment, they were sentenced to perpetual exile from their native country. They were stripped of their rank, their fortune, and their home, and cast out as wanderers upon the earth. It was during this period that the treacherous Turk, seeing his opportunity, attempted to force his daughter to return with him to Constantinople. But Safie, whose soul abhorred the constraints and servitude of her native land, and whose heart belonged wholly to her Christian lover, resolved upon a course of extraordinary courage. Aided by a faithful servant and possessing a small sum of money and some jewels, she fled from her father's control and endeavoured to seek out the place of her lover's exile.

Her journey was long and perilous for one so young and unaccustomed to hardship. The servant who accompanied her fell ill and died, leaving her alone in a strange land whose language she barely understood. Yet her spirit did not fail her. Armed with the name of a town where she believed Felix and his family had sought refuge, she pressed onward, her love a beacon in the darkness of her solitude. It was by this strange and wonderful series of events that she finally arrived at the cottage, bringing with her a light and a joy which had long been absent from its sorrowful inhabitants.

Such was the tale I pieced together from their evening conversations. It was a history of injustice, of love, of sacrifice, and of betrayal. I, who had until then known only the simple pains of physical want, was now introduced to the far more complex and potent agonies of the human heart. Each new detail of their story served only to increase my admiration for the cottagers and my abhorrence for the baseness of men like the Turkish merchant. How I longed to be one of them, to offer my strength in their service, to protect them from the harshness of the world as they had, unknowingly, protected me. My own sorrows, which had before seemed to me the greatest in existence, now appeared small in comparison to the weight of their undeserved suffering. I saw in Felix a model of courage and virtue; in Agatha, a paragon of gentle fortitude; and in the blind De Lacey, a patriarch whose benevolent spirit shone forth even in the midst of ruin.

My education, however, was not confined to their history alone. As the days grew longer and the sun warmer, Felix continued his instruction of Safie, and I, his silent pupil, drank in every word. He read to her from various books, and whilst I could not grasp all the subtleties of the arguments, the grand outlines of human knowledge began to take shape within my mind. I learned of the rise and fall of great civilisations, of the discoveries of science which had unveiled the secrets of the heavens and the earth, and of the poets who had captured in their verses the deepest emotions of the soul.

One day, as I was foraging for nuts and berries in the woods, I came upon a leathern portmanteau lying on the ground. It appeared to have been dropped by some traveller, and my curiosity prompted me to examine its contents. Within, I discovered several articles of clothing, which were of no use to me, but also, to my inexpressible delight, a small collection of books. I seized this treasure with an avidity I had never before felt and carried it back to my hovel as if it were a hoard of precious gems.

I now possessed my own library. The books were Goethe's _Sorrows of Werter_, a volume of Plutarch's _Lives_, and Milton's _Paradise Lost_. I can hardly describe to you the effect these books produced upon me. They became my world, my companions, my tutors. In _Werter_, I found a portrait of a sensitive soul, whose sorrows and feelings resonated deeply with my own. I wept with him, and for him, and for myself. I saw in his isolation and his unrequited love a distorted reflection of my own miserable state, and for the first time, I felt that I was not entirely alone in my suffering.

Plutarch taught me of the heroes of old, of the great legislators and warriors who had shaped the destinies of nations. I was filled with an admiration for virtue and a contempt for vice. I learned that men could be noble, self sacrificing, and devoted to the public good. These histories elevated my thoughts and inspired in me a desire to perform great and benevolent deeds, a desire that was as poignant as it was futile, for how could a wretch such as I ever hope to be a benefactor to mankind?

But it was _Paradise Lost_ that moved me most profoundly. I read it, not as a work of fiction, but as a true history of the origins of the world. Like Adam, I was a creature created by a higher power, unique in my existence, and without any apparent link to any other being. But his state was far different from mine. He came forth from the hands of God a perfect creature, happy and prosperous, and was protected by the special care of his creator. He was allowed to converse with and learn from beings of a superior nature, whilst I was wretched, helpless, and alone. Many times I considered Satan as the fitter emblem of my condition, for like him, when I viewed the bliss of my protectors, the bitter gall of envy rose within me.

These readings and reflections served only to deepen the turmoil in my soul. Knowledge had opened my eyes to my own monstrous deformity and my utter isolation from the world of men. The more I learned of their virtues and their affections, the more I became aware of the impassable gulf that separated me from them. I was a thing to be feared and abhorred, a blot upon the face of creation. The question, “What was I?” returned with ever increasing force, and I could find no answer but in groans of despair.

The winter approached, and with it came the end of my secret tutelage. The cottagers spent more time indoors, gathered around their fire, and their conversations became less frequent. The leaves fell from the trees, and the world grew cold and barren, mirroring the desolation in my own heart. My provisions grew scarce, and I was forced to spend many hours in the biting cold, searching for the scanty sustenance the frozen earth could offer.

It was during this period of renewed hardship that I resolved upon a desperate course of action. I had observed that the old man, De Lacey, was often left alone for several hours in the day while the younger members of the family were occupied with their labours. Being blind, he could not be horrified by my appearance. His soul, I reasoned, must be filled with the same benevolence and gent_tle_ kindness that I had heard in his voice and seen in his actions towards his children. I determined to present myself to him, to appeal to his compassion, and to win him over as my friend and protector. If I could gain his acceptance, I believed, he might then be able to intercede for me with his children and persuade them to look past my hideous exterior to the sentient and affectionate being that dwelt within.

The prospect of this encounter filled me with a mixture of hope and terror that made my whole frame tremble. For many days I rehearsed what I would say, composing speeches filled with supplication and promises of devotion. I selected a day when I knew Felix, Agatha, and Safie would be absent for a considerable time. With a heart beating so violently that it seemed it would burst from my chest, I left my hovel and approached the door of the cottage. My hand hesitated for a long moment over the latch. This single action, I knew, would decide my fate. It would lead either to the dawn of a new life of fellowship and joy, or to the final, crushing confirmation of my utter and eternal damnation. Summoning all my courage, I lifted the latch and entered the house.

The old man was sitting by the fire, his head bowed as if in thought. The sound of my entrance startled him, and he looked up in my direction, his sightless eyes filled with a gentle inquiry. “Who is there?” he asked in a kind voice. “Come in, friend, if you are cold. The fire is warm, and you are welcome to share it.”

These words of welcome, the first I had ever heard addressed to me by a human being, sent a thrill of hope through my soul. I was unable to speak for a moment, so overcome was I with emotion. I closed the door softly behind me and advanced a few steps into the room.

“I am a traveller,” I said at last, my voice hoarse and trembling, “in need of rest and a moment of warmth. I beg your pardon for my intrusion.”

“You are not intruding, my friend,” he replied with a benevolent smile. “A stranger is a sacred guest in this humble abode. Sit down, and tell me, to what fortuitous circumstance do we owe the pleasure of your visit?”

I drew a stool near to him, my limbs still shaking uncontrollably. “I am a poor and friendless wretch,” I began, my voice gaining a little strength as I spoke the words I had so often rehearsed. “I have been an outcast from my birth; I have no family, no friends, and I am an object of scorn and hatred to all who behold me. I have wandered far, seeking for some one being who would not despise me, who would look upon me with compassion. I have heard of your goodness, of the benevolence that resides in your heart, and I have come to you as a suppliant, to beg for your protection and your friendship.”

As I spoke, I could feel the old man's countenance change. His smile faded, and a look of deep pity and concern took its place. “My dear sir,” he said, his voice filled with sympathy, “your tale is a sad one indeed. It is a cruel world that would treat any of God’s creatures with such harshness. I am blind, and cannot judge you by your appearance; I can only hear the sorrow in your voice, and it moves me deeply. Tell me, what is the cause of this universal enmity? Have you committed some crime that has turned the hearts of your fellow men against you?”

“No,” I cried with vehemence. “I am guilty of no crime. My only fault is that I am different; that I am not formed in the same mould as other men. It is my appearance, my hideous and unfortunate appearance, that has made me an object of horror and disgust.”

“If that is all,” the old man said, reaching out his hand towards me, “then you have surely found a friend in me. The hearts of men are often prejudiced by what they see, but I have learned to judge by other means. Give me your hand, my friend. Let me feel the grasp of one who has suffered so unjustly.”

Tears of gratitude, the first I had ever shed, welled up in my eyes. With a sob that shook my entire frame, I took his outstretched hand in mine. It was a moment of sublime and transcendent joy. Here, at last, was the acceptance I had craved for so long. Here was a being who did not flee from me in terror, but offered me the hand of fellowship. In that instant, I felt that all my past sufferings were but a small price to pay for such a reward.

But my moment of bliss was to be short lived. Even as I held the old man’s hand, I heard the sound of approaching footsteps. A cry of terror escaped my lips. “They are coming!” I exclaimed. “I must go!”

“Go? Why must you go?” the old man asked in confusion. “It is only my children. Stay, I will speak to them. I will be your advocate.”

“No, no!” I cried, my voice choked with panic. “You do not know! They will not listen! They will drive me away with hatred and loathing!”

At that very moment, the door of the cottage burst open, and Felix, Agatha, and Safie stood upon the threshold. The scene that followed is one of burning horror, forever seared into my memory. Safie, upon seeing me, shrieked and fled from the cottage. Agatha, unable to escape, fainted from sheer terror. And Felix, his face contorted with rage and revulsion, sprang forward with a cry of fury. He tore his father's hand from my grasp and, seizing a heavy stick, began to beat me with all his might.

I could have torn him limb from limb; I could have crushed him as an insect. My strength was vastly superior to his. But the pain of his blows was nothing compared to the agony that ripped through my soul. I did not defend myself. I uttered a cry of despair, a sound more animal than human, and covering my eyes with my hands, I fled from the cottage. I ran, I knew not where, my heart a black and desolate void. The hope that had so briefly illuminated my life was extinguished, and in its place was a darkness more profound and more terrible than any I had ever known. The world of men was closed to me forever. My last, desperate attempt to find fellowship had ended in violence and abject failure. The milk of human kindness, which I had so longed to taste, had turned to bitterest poison on my tongue. I was, and would always be, a monster. And in that moment of ultimate despair, a new and terrible feeling was born within me: a burning, all consuming hatred for my creator, the man who had cast me into this world of torment and then abandoned me to its cruelty.

I know not how long I wandered in the desolate labyrinth of the woods; hours, or perhaps an entire day and night, passed whilst I was insensible to all but the tempest that raged within my soul. The blows of Felix’s stick were as nothing, mere trifles of the flesh, but the blow to my spirit had been mortal. Hope, that gentle, flickering taper which had for a brief season illuminated the darkness of my existence, was now extinguished, and a Stygian gloom, profound and eternal, had descended upon me. I threw myself upon the damp earth, amidst the decaying leaves, and gave vent to a howl of misery and despair so terrible that the very beasts of the forest, the wolves and the bears, must have shrunk away in fear. My anguish was not that of a man, for no man had ever suffered such a complete and utter banishment from his own kind; it was the agony of a fiend, of a creature torn from some netherworld and cast into a sphere of being where it was loathed and abhorred by all.

When the first transports of my grief had subsided, a dull and heavy torpor settled upon my senses. I lay there, motionless as a fallen log, while the sun set and the cold stars emerged in the heavens, those same stars that had once seemed to me like eyes of benevolent spirits. Now they were but distant, icy points of light, indifferent to my suffering. All was silent, save for the sighing of the wind in the branches and the occasional cry of a night bird. In this silence, a new feeling began to stir within the ashes of my heart, not the sharp agony of before, but a cold and calculating rage. The image of my creator, of Frankenstein, rose before me, his features contorted not with the malice I now felt, but with the scientific curiosity and heedless ambition that had given me birth. He had animated this wretched frame; he had bestowed upon me consciousness and the capacity for such exquisite pain, and then he had abandoned me. He was the source, the true author of my misery. It was upon him that my vengeance must fall.

Yet, even as this resolution formed, I was drawn back by a fatal and irresistible impulse towards the cottage. It had been the cradle of my intellect, the theatre of all my hopes; I could not quit it forever without one last look, one final confirmation of my utter desolation. I crept through the undergrowth with the stealth of a serpent, my heart beating a funeral dirge against my ribs. The light was still burning in the window, a beacon that had once signified warmth and fellowship, but which now served only to accentuate the impassable gulf that separated me from its inhabitants.

I positioned myself at my old chink in the wall, my eye pressed to the opening. The scene within tore at my soul with fresh talons. The old man, De Lacey, sat in his chair, his sightless face a mask of sorrow and confusion. Agatha wept silently, her head resting on her father’s knee, while Safie clung to her, her own beautiful features stained with tears of terror. But it was the aspect of Felix that transfixed me with a mixture of hatred and a strange, sorrowful understanding. He paced the small room like a caged lion, his face pale and his hands clenched. He was speaking, his voice low but filled with a vehement passion.

“I was right, father,” he exclaimed. “You heard its voice; it was a devil, a fiend from hell! Did you not see its monstrous form, its yellow skin, its eyes of damnation? It meant you harm; I am certain of it. I saved you from its clutches.”

“No, my son, no,” the old man replied, his voice trembling with an emotion I could not quite decipher. “You are mistaken. There was no menace in its touch; its hand was as large and coarse as a labourer’s, it is true, but it trembled with feeling. Its voice was filled with a sorrow so profound it brought tears to my own eyes. It spoke of loneliness, of being cast out. It called me its friend. We have done a great wrong, Felix. We have condemned a creature unheard, based only on an appearance which it cannot help.”

“Father, your goodness blinds you!” cried Felix, his agitation increasing. “Appearance? It is the very signature of its nature! No creature of God could be so foul. It is an unholy thing, and its presence has defiled our home. We cannot remain here. Even now, it may be lurking outside, waiting to wreak its vengeance upon us for my attack. We must leave this place at once. We must flee to some other country where such monsters are unknown.”

A sob escaped Agatha’s lips. “Leave our cottage? Our home? But where shall we go, Felix? We have so little.”

“It matters not,” he answered grimly. “Anywhere is better than here, under the shadow of that… that thing. I will not subject you, or Safie, or my father to another night of such terror. Tomorrow, I shall go to the landlord and settle our affairs. We must depart.”

Hearing these words, a final, convulsive shudder of despair wracked my frame. I, who had sought only to become one of them, had become the agent of their ruin. My very attempt to join their society had shattered it. I had driven them from their peaceful asylum, turning their joy back into a sadness far deeper than the poverty they had endured before. I was a walking pestilence, a blight upon the earth. I turned from the chink, unable to watch any longer, and staggered back into the darkest recesses of the forest, my mind a black void.

The following day I watched from a distance as Felix fulfilled his dreadful promise. I saw him speak with another man, who came and looked over the cottage and the garden I had so lovingly tended. I saw Felix return with a small cart, and then the miserable process of their departure began. Each piece of furniture, each humble possession brought out from that beloved dwelling, was a new stab to my heart. At last, the family emerged. De Lacey was supported by his son, his shoulders stooped with a grief that his blindness could not hide. Agatha and Safie followed, their faces veiled, but their sorrow was palpable in every line of their drooping figures. They climbed into the cart and, with a final, lingering look of fear and sadness at the home they were abandoning, they were gone. I watched until the cart was a mere speck in the distance, and then it vanished, leaving me utterly and irrevocably alone.

The world was now a desert to me. The sun shone, the birds sang, but nature’s beauty was a mockery. I remained in my hiding place until night fell, and a kind of madness, born of supreme despair, took possession of me. The cottage stood before me, empty and silent, a tomb of my buried hopes. A cry of rage and anguish burst from my lips, and I rushed towards it, no longer a timid observer, but an agent of destruction. “This was my home,” I shrieked to the unpitying night, “and now it is my sepulchre!”

With frantic energy, I gathered dry branches and brushwood, piling them high against the walls of the cottage and the outhouses. I worked with a fiendish glee, my movements swift and terrible in the moonlight. When my work was complete, I took a flaming brand from the embers of my own fire, which I had not yet allowed to die, and with a triumphant yell, I thrust it into the pyre.

The flames caught with a hungry roar, licking at the dry wood with greedy tongues of fire. The conflagration spread with astonishing rapidity, and soon the entire structure was engulfed. I danced and howled around the blaze, a spectacle of demonic fury. The fire was a reflection of the hell that burned within me. As the roof fell in with a great crash, sending a shower of sparks into the sky, I felt a savage joy, a grim and terrible satisfaction. I had erased the last monument to my folly and my hope. Let the world that cast me out see me as a demon; I would become the demon they imagined.

When the morning dawned, only a blackened and smoking ruin remained. I lingered for a time in the desolation, my fury spent and replaced by a cold, hard resolve. It was then that I remembered the leather portmanteau I had found in the woods so long ago, which contained, besides the books that had given me knowledge, some clothes and a packet of papers. I had stored it in my hovel, a crude lean-to I had built near the cottage, which had escaped the fire. I retrieved it, and with a dawning sense of purpose, I examined the papers more closely than I had before.

They were, I discovered, a journal kept by my creator during the months leading up to my animation. I read them with a rapacious horror, my eyes devouring the words that sealed my eternal damnation. Here was the detailed account of my monstrous creation; here were the expressions of his feverish ambition, followed by his disgust and terror at the sight of his own handiwork. He described my features, my stature, my every loathsome detail, with a pen dipped in revulsion. And, most importantly, I learned his name, Victor Frankenstein, and that his family and his native country were in Geneva.

A cry of execration burst from my lips. “Frankenstein! Detested creator! You are the cause of this! You endowed me with perceptions and passions and then cast me abroad for the scorn and horror of mankind. But I have found you now. I will follow you to the ends of theearth! You have abandoned me; you have spurned me. Now you will feel the weight of my despair!”

I swore, in that moment, a vow of eternal hatred and vengeance against him and all his kind. I had one purpose now, one reason to continue my miserable existence: to track down this man and force him to reckon with the misery he had spawned. I would make his life a living torment, a mirror of my own. My journey had a destination. I would go to Geneva.

I began my travels, guided by a map I found amongst the papers. I travelled only at night, hiding in caves and woods during the day, my sustenance the berries and roots I could find. The changing seasons marked my progress; the balmy autumn gave way to the bitter cold of winter. I suffered terribly from the frost and snow, but my frame, hardier than a man’s, endured it. My physical pain was a trifle, however, compared to the fire of vengeance that kept me warm.

As the snows of winter began to melt and the first signs of spring appeared, an incident occurred that forever hardened my heart and sealed my fate. I was passing through a thick wood near the banks of a swift-flowing river when I heard a cry. I saw a young girl, little more than a child, who had been playing near the water’s edge, slip and fall into the torrent. She was carried away by the current, her screams growing fainter. Without a moment’s hesitation, my first impulse, the instinct that had been nurtured by the example of my beloved cottagers, took over. I plunged into the icy water and, with my superior strength, fought against the current and dragged the child to the safety of the bank.

She was unconscious, and I was endeavouring to restore her when I heard a shout. A man, a rustic who I presumed to be her father or companion, came rushing towards us. Upon seeing me bending over the girl, he did not see a rescuer; he saw only a monster. He let out a yell of rage and terror, snatched the child from my arms, and fled into the woods. I stood, bewildered by this reaction, and was about to follow to explain when the man stopped, turned, and raised a gun to his shoulder. A flash, a deafening report, and I felt a searing agony in my shoulder. I fell to the ground, wounded and betrayed.

This was my reward for a deed of compassion! This was the justice of man! As I lay there, the blood flowing from my wound, the last vestiges of my benevolence for this accursed race withered and died. I had saved a human life, and in return, I was shot like a wild beast. The pain of the wound was excruciating, but the wound to my soul was a thousand times more grievous. I managed to crawl away into the deepest part of the forest, where for weeks I lay in a miserable state, fevered and tormented, my body and my spirit festering together.

When I had recovered my strength, I was a changed being. My heart had become a stone, and my thoughts were filled with black and murderous revenge. My creator had made me in the image of man, but it was mankind that had shaped me into a fiend. They would not have me as a friend, so they would have me as an enemy.

I continued my journey to Geneva, my soul a cauldron of hatred. One evening, I stood upon the summit of one of the mountains of Jura, overlooking the lake and the distant city. The setting sun cast a rosy glow upon the scene, a sight of such sublime beauty that it ought to have soothed any troubled heart. But it did not soothe mine. The beauty of the world was an insult to my deformity, a paradise from which I was eternally excluded. I raised my arms to the heavens, which seemed to gaze down upon me with cold indifference.

“Hear me, Frankenstein!” I cried, my voice echoing among the desolate peaks. “Hear me, you who are my creator and my master! I swear by the eternal sky and the spirits of vengeance that I will pursue you, even to the death. I, the miserable and the abandoned, am an abortion, to be spurned at, and kicked, and trampled on. But I am not powerless. I have a will, and a strength, and a hatred that will not be quenched. I will be with you on your wedding night! I will dog your steps; I will turn your joy to bitterest gall; I will make you curse the day you first drew breath, even as I curse the day you gave life to this monstrous form. This is my vow. Let the war between us begin!”

Having spoken these words, a grim peace settled upon me. My purpose was clear, my path laid out. I was no longer a lost and weeping wretch begging for fellowship. I was an instrument of justice, an arch-fiend with a sacred mission of retribution. I descended from the mountain and set my face towards Geneva, the first stage of my terrible pilgrimage.
