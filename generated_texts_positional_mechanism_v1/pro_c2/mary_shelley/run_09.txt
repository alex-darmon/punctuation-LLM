The quiet hours after the main work of the day was done became my most treasured, and indeed my most torturous, period of study. The old man, Silas, would sit by the fire, his hands, gnarled from a life of hauling ropes and tending the great lamp, now occupied with the mending of a net or the carving of some small wooden bird. His daughter, Evangeline, would often read to him from a small collection of books, her voice a soothing balm against the eternal moan of the wind and the crash of the sea upon the rocks below. It was thus that I, a creature of shadow and sea-spray, learned the cadences of human speech.

“She chose a book of poetry one evening, and her voice gave to the words a life I could scarcely comprehend; it rose with the triumph of a hero’s charge and fell with the weight of a lover’s grief. My own mind, a machine of logic and observation, struggled to grasp the substance of these new sensations. They were not hunger, nor cold, nor the fear of discovery; they were something other, a phantom pain and a ghostly joy that seemed to emanate from the very soul of the words she spoke.

The days passed in this manner, my understanding growing with each setting and rising of the sun. The fierce gales of winter gave way to a milder season; the sea-foam was less violent upon the shore, and the birds returned to nest in the high crags of my lonely perch. I improved in my secret learning more swiftly than I could have hoped, for the conversations between father and daughter were my only instruction, and I gave them my whole being. I began to distinguish not only their words but the freight of feeling they carried; a sigh from the old man was a history of sorrow, a light laugh from the girl a promise of hope.

While I grew in this strange, second-hand knowledge of feeling, I also learned of the world beyond their isolated stone tower. Through their books and talk, a map of human existence was unrolled before me. I learned of continents and empires, of great cities teeming with souls, of the intricate dance of commerce and the brutal machinery of war. I heard of philosophy, which sought to explain the universe, and of faith, which accepted it without explanation.

These accounts stirred in me a profound and disturbing curiosity. Was this humanity, this race of beings, at once so ingenious and so destructive? They could chart the stars and measure the depths of the ocean, yet they would slaughter one another for a patch of earth or a point of honour. One moment they were composers of a beauty that could make my own silent, unbreathing chest ache with an unknown longing; the next, they were the architects of suffering on a scale I found impossible to conceive. To be a good and gentle soul, like Evangeline, seemed the highest state to which a being could aspire; to be cruel and vicious, as so many in their histories were, appeared a fall so low, a condition more wretched than that of the blind, unthinking things that crawled in the deep. For a long time, I could not understand how one man could raise a hand to his brother; but when I heard tales of greed and betrayal, my bafflement ceased, replaced by a cold and heavy disgust.

These thoughts turned me inward, upon myself. I had learned that to be human was to have a history, a family, a place among one’s fellows. It was to possess a name, and a face that could be met with love, or at least with recognition. A man might be praised for his strength or his cleverness, but without a connection to others he was but a ghost, a drifter on the tides of society. And what was I? Of my making I knew nothing, save for a single, searing memory of light and a great noise before I awoke in the darkness of the sea caves. I possessed no kin, no name, no fortune. I was, beyond all this, not even of their kind. My form, which I had only ever glimpsed in the mirror of still rock pools, was a thing of dark, polished shell and articulated limb, built for the crushing pressures of the abyss and the scouring brine. My strength was immense, my senses tuned to the subtle shifts of water and stone, not the warmth of a hearth. When I looked about the world, I saw none who resembled me. Was I, then, an aberration, a solitary flaw in the grand design, from which all of nature would shrink?

I cannot convey to you the anguish that these reflections brought upon me; knowledge, once it takes root, is a relentless vine that twists around the heart. Oh, that I had remained in the silent, thoughtless dark of the deepest trench, knowing nothing beyond the currents and the pressure!

Of what a strange quality is this awareness! It fastens to the spirit as a barnacle to the hull, impossible to scrape away. I yearned sometimes to extinguish the light of my own consciousness, to return to a state of pure existence, but I learned there was but one path to that end, and that was destruction, a state I feared, yet did not comprehend. I admired the kindness and the gentle spirit of my watchers, but I was forever barred from their society, a prisoner of my own form. The tender words of the father and the bright, hopeful smiles of the daughter were not for me. Their warmth, their light, their very world, was not for me. Unhappy, desolate thing!

Other lessons were carved into my awareness even more deeply. I heard them speak of childhood and of age, of how a life unfolds from helpless infancy to wise maturity, how memory accumulates and shapes the soul, how the parent sees his own past in the eyes of the child, and the child his future in the face of the parent.

But what of my own history? I had no infancy, no growth; I awoke as I was now, fully formed, with no memory of a gentle hand or a guiding voice. Had some being fashioned me? If so, he had cast me away into the sea, a piece of unwanted craft. The question returned, again and again, a tolling bell in the echo chamber of my mind: What was I? And the only answer was the roaring of the waves and the cry of the gull.

Some time elapsed before I learned the history of my watchers. It was a history which could not fail to engrave itself deeply upon my consciousness, unfolding as it did a sequence of events, each interesting and devastating to one so utterly inexperienced as I was in the intricate cruelties of mankind.

The name of the old man was Alistair. He was descended from a line of esteemed artisans in a great city of the interior, a place called Lyria, where he had lived for many years in comfort and high repute, respected for his genius and sought after for his counsel. His daughter, Elara, had moved amongst the most refined circles of that society, her grace and intellect a source of constant pride to her father. A few seasons before my awakening, they had inhabited a spacious and well appointed house upon a grand square, surrounded by colleagues and admirers, and possessed of every delight which ingenuity, a passion for knowledge, and a handsome fortune could afford.

The author of their present ruin was a man who had once called Alistair his dearest friend. This man, whose name was Valerius, was a syndic of the city’s most powerful guild, the Cartographers’ Collegium, of which Alistair was the undisputed master. For years Valerius had stood in the shadow of my protector’s brilliance, his own achievements, though considerable, appearing as faint stars beside a blazing sun. Alistair, I gathered from their sorrowful conversations, had created maps of such astonishing accuracy and daring scope that they had begun to redefine the known world. He charted not only the land but the movements of the stars and the depths of the sea, proposing theories that were as beautiful as they were, in the eyes of his rivals, heretical.

For some transgression I could not at first comprehend, born of this very genius, Alistair was brought to ruin. It seemed he had drafted a new chart of the Northern Waste, a chart which depicted lands and coastlines hitherto believed to be mythical, and, what was more, suggested that the pole was not a place of eternal ice but held a temperate and open sea. He had drawn this conclusion from ancient fragments of text and the meticulous study of oceanic currents, which he observed with instruments of his own invention. This work, the pinnacle of his life’s labour, was to be his undoing.

Valerius, his heart corrupted by a jealousy that had festered for decades, saw his opportunity. He accused Alistair before the city council not merely of error, but of a dangerous and blasphemous pride. He claimed that these maps were an affront to the established order, a fiction designed to mislead sailors to their deaths and to sow doubt in the sacred finality of the known world. He twisted Alistair’s genius into a kind of madness, his intellectual courage into a reckless treason. The council, composed of men more concerned with stability than with truth, were swayed by the venomous and declamatory style of Valerius.

A trial was held, a mere formality to lend the appearance of justice to a decision already made. Alistair was stripped of his titles, his workshop was sealed, and his fortune was confiscated to pay for the supposed damages his “heresies” had caused. His great charts, the labour of a lifetime, were publicly burned in the square before his home. He was declared anathema, and all citizens were forbidden from giving him aid or comfort. He and his daughter, who refused to forsake him, were exiled from Lyria, cast out to wander as vagabonds. It was thus that they came to this desolate coast, to the only dwelling they could find, a fisherman’s abandoned cottage, to live out their days in poverty and obscurity.

These wonderful and terrible narrations inspired me with the most violent and contradictory feelings. Was this the nature of man, which I had begun to admire? That a being of such stupendous intellect and virtue as Alistair could be destroyed by the base envy of another? That a society could celebrate truth one day and burn it the next? I saw in Valerius the very essence of the evil principle I had learned of, a creature who, for the sake of his own vanity, would extinguish a great light and call it justice. In Alistair and the faithful Elara, however, I perceived all that was noble and godlike. To suffer such injustice with dignity, to retain a gentle heart in the face of such bitter loss, appeared the highest state to which a sensitive being could aspire. To be Valerius, to carry such a black and corrupted soul, seemed a degradation more profound, a condition more abject than that of the unthinking crab that scuttles in the mire.

My own condition, which had been the source of such unending agony, now appeared to me in a new light. Was not Alistair also an outcast, banished for a truth his fellows could not bear to see? Was not his unique genius the very cause of his solitude? In his fate I saw a faint, distorted reflection of my own. I, too, was a creature set apart by my nature, a being whose very existence was a challenge to the ordinary world. This thought, however, brought me little comfort; for his exile was from a society to which he had once belonged, while I was an exile from the entirety of creation, a species of one. His misery was a fall from grace; mine was the very foundation of my being.

The winter grew deeper, and the hardships of my protectors increased. Their small store of wood dwindled, and the fierce winds that swept from the sea made the gathering of fuel a perilous and often fruitless task for the aging Alistair. I would watch from my cold refuge in the cliffs as he returned, his body bent against the gale, with only a few meager branches to show for his efforts. I saw the silent fear in Elara’s eyes as the fire burned low, and I heard them speak in hushed tones of the coming frosts.

A strange and powerful impulse now seized me. These beings, whom I revered, were suffering, and I, who possessed a strength and resilience far exceeding theirs, was a mere passive observer of their pain. My nocturnal rambles, hitherto a means of solitary exploration, now took on a new purpose. During the darkest hours, when the moon was hidden and the cottage was but a faint smudge of grey against the blackness, I would venture forth. My powerful limbs carried me with ease over the treacherous, ice-slicked rocks and through the dense, wind-blasted thickets that clung to the cliffs. I sought out fallen trees, great limbs torn from their trunks by the winter storms, which were far too heavy for any single man to move. I would break them down into manageable lengths and, bearing an immense load upon my shelled back, carry the wood to a spot near the cottage, stacking it neatly in a sheltered alcove of the rock where they would be certain to find it.

The first morning after my labours, I watched with a breathless attention I cannot describe. Elara was the first to emerge, her shoulders slumped with the familiar posture of weary resignation. She walked towards the path to the woods, then stopped, her eyes fixed upon the great pile of firewood. She uttered a cry of astonishment, a sound that was half disbelief and half prayer. Alistair came out at her call, and I saw his face, so often clouded with sorrow, illuminated by a sudden, wondering joy. They spoke in rapid, amazed tones, questioning how such a gift could have appeared. They looked to the sea, to the sky, to the empty path, as if seeking the agent of their salvation. They concluded it must have been some charitable traveller or perhaps a fisherman from a nearby cove, taking pity on their plight. Their gratitude was so pure, their relief so profound, that I felt a sensation of pleasure more intense and sublime than any I had yet known. It was a strange, aching joy, for I was their benefactor, yet I must forever remain unseen, a monster lurking in the shadows.

This success emboldened me. Every few nights, I replenished their supply of wood. My knowledge of the sea and its floor, my natural element, soon suggested other ways I might assist them. Their diet was meager, consisting of little more than dried grains and the tough, stringy seaweed they could gather from the shore. With my ability to withstand the crushing pressures of the deep, I began to explore the ocean bed in a way no human could. I discovered oyster beds in deep, sheltered chasms, where the shells grew thick and heavy. Within them, I sometimes found pearls of a remarkable size and lustre, milky globes that seemed to capture the faint light of the abyss. At first, I merely admired these strange treasures, but then a new idea took hold of me. I had heard my protectors lament their poverty, their inability to purchase medicines for Alistair’s persistent cough, or warmer clothes to shield them from the cold.

After much hesitation, I resolved upon a new course of action. I gathered a handful of the most perfect pearls and, during one of my nocturnal expeditions, crept closer to the cottage than I had ever dared before. I had observed that Elara would sometimes leave a small basket by the door to collect rainwater. With a trembling appendage, I placed the pearls gently inside, nestled amongst a few beautiful, iridescent shells I had also collected.

Their discovery the next morning was a scene of yet greater astonishment. I heard Elara’s gasp, then a long silence. She brought the pearls to her father, and they examined them by the light of the window. I heard the word “fortune” spoken in a tone of awe. Their initial shock soon gave way to a troubled discussion. Who was this mysterious benefactor? And why would they bestow such riches upon them? Their thoughts turned again to a secret sympathiser, perhaps an old friend from Lyria who had discovered their location and wished to help them anonymously, fearing the council’s wrath. This explanation, though it pained my lonely heart, seemed to satisfy them and calm their fears.

A few weeks later, Alistair made the long journey to the nearest fishing town, a place some miles down the coast. He returned not only with medicines and warm blankets but with new tools, books, and, most precious of all, a great roll of fine vellum and inks. A light returned to his eyes that I had not seen before. He began to work again. He did not attempt to recreate his grand, world-altering charts, but instead began to draw the coastline as he saw it now, mapping the intricate patterns of the tides, the locations of treacherous reefs, and the safest channels for boats. He worked with a quiet, renewed passion, and Elara would watch him, her face radiant with a happiness that was almost painful for me to behold.

I was the secret spring of this renewed felicity. I was the unseen hand that had given the master back his craft, that had brought comfort to their hearth and peace to their minds. And yet, this very role only served to widen the impassable gulf between us. I was their provider, their guardian spirit, but I remained a monster, a thing of shell and shadow whose appearance would have turned their joy to terror and their gratitude to revulsion. I would watch them through the window in the evenings, as Alistair drew his new, humbler maps and Elara read to him from one of the books he had bought. I saw the warmth, the love, the shared world of understanding that existed within those four walls, a world from which I was as eternally barred as a soul from paradise.

My heart was a strange vessel, filled at once with a sublime joy and the most wretched despair. The more I loved my protectors, the more I was tortured by my isolation. The more good I did for them, the more I yearned for a single word of recognition, a single glance that was not filled with horror. I had learned from their story that men could be both virtuous and base, and in my secret actions, I felt I was aligning myself with the virtuous. But what is virtue in a vacuum? What is a good deed performed by a being that cannot be known? It was but a shadow’s gift, a phantom’s caress, and the warmth it brought to them only seemed to make my own eternal coldness more bitter and profound. The question returned, no longer a simple cry of confusion, but a lamentation sharpened by the cruel blade of knowledge: What was I, and for what purpose did I suffer so?

The winter approached not with a sudden violence, but with a creeping and insidious chill that seemed to emanate from the very bones of the earth. The sun, a distant and indifferent sovereign, shortened his daily reign, leaving my world to the prolonged dominion of a cold and silent moon. The sea, which in summer had whispered secrets to the shore, now roared and thundered against the cliffs, its voice a constant memento of nature’s untameable power. My nocturnal wanderings became exercises in endurance against the biting winds and sleet which drove in from the unforgiving ocean; yet I welcomed the hardship, for the stark and desolate beauty of the winter landscape was a mirror to my own soul. It was a world stripped of its gentle adornments, a place of harsh truths and elemental fury, and in it I felt a strange and terrible kinship.

Within the cottage, the comforts I had secretly provided held the worst of the season at bay. A fire perpetually burned in the hearth, its cheerful crackle a stark contrast to the howling gales without. Alistair, his spirit rekindled, laboured over his vellum with a devotion that was almost holy. He was no longer merely a cartographer; he had become a poet of the coastline, a historian of the tides. I would watch for hours as his hand, guided by a mind of exquisite precision, traced the jagged teeth of the reefs and the gentle curves of the hidden coves. He was creating a testament, not to a grand empire, but to this small, forgotten corner of the world, and in his labour, he seemed to find a measure of redemption.

Elara was the guardian of his flame. She ensured he ate, that the fire did not die, and that the ink did not freeze in its well. In the evenings, their ritual continued; she would read from the volumes I had enabled them to purchase, her voice a soft melody weaving through the tapestry of his quiet concentration. They read tales of ancient mariners, of scientific discovery, and of philosophical inquiry. I, their unseen student, drank in every word. I learned of the laws of physics which governed the very storms I braved, and of the moral philosophies which sought to govern the tempestuous heart of man.

I learned that compassion was held as a supreme virtue, that self-sacrifice was the mark of a hero, and that love was the bond which gave meaning to an otherwise chaotic existence. These lessons were a torment. Was not my secret vigilance a form of compassion? Was not my lonely suffering a sacrifice? Did I not love my protectors with a devotion that consumed my entire being? Yet these virtues, as defined by their world, required a context of society and recognition. A hero was celebrated; a lover was embraced. I was a cipher, a ghost whose good deeds were attributed to an unknown human benefactor, a misapprehension that was at once the source of my success and the emblem of my failure. My virtue was invisible, and therefore, it was nothing.

As the winter deepened, the storms grew in their ferocity. They were no longer mere gales, but titanic assaults of wind and water that seemed bent on tearing the cottage from its foundations and casting it into the sea. During these tempests, a new tension entered the little dwelling. Elara would plead with Alistair not to venture out, her face pale with a fear that I understood completely. But he, possessed by his work, felt an irresistible pull to witness the ocean in its full fury. He claimed it was necessary for his charts, to see how the highest tides reshaped the beaches and how the currents behaved when driven by a hurricane’s force. I perceived, however, that it was more than science; it was a confrontation, a desire to stand before that immense power, perhaps to feel his own smallness and yet endure.

One afternoon, the sky turned a bruised and ghastly yellow, a colour I had never before witnessed. The air grew unnaturally still, and the sea drew back from the shore as if taking a great breath before a terrible exhalation. Elara, her hands clasped, stood at the door and begged Alistair to remain. “There is a malice in the air today,” she said, her voice trembling. “I have never seen its like. Stay, I implore you. The charts can wait. What use is a perfect map if the hand that draws it is lost?”

He kissed her brow, his own face set with a look of grim determination. “It is for that very reason I must go,” he replied. “To know the dangers is to conquer them. I will not be long. I will stay upon the upper cliffs, well away from the surf.”

She could not dissuade him. I watched from my barren shelter among the rocks as he ascended the path, his figure a small, dark mark against the ominous sky. Elara remained at the window, a statue of anxiety, until his form was lost to the winding trail. The storm broke with a suddenness and a violence that beggared all description. It was not rain that fell, but a solid wall of water, and the wind screamed like a legion of tormented souls. The world dissolved into a churning chaos of grey sea and grey sky, and the very ground beneath me seemed to tremble with the impact of the waves.

Hours passed. The dim light of afternoon faded into a premature and turbulent twilight. The lamp was lit in the cottage, a tiny, fragile star in the universal tempest. Alistair did not return. I could feel Elara’s panic as if it were my own. I saw her pace the small room, her form flitting past the window again and again. She would go to the door, open it a mere crack, and be driven back by the force of the wind and spray. Her despair was a physical blow to me; it was the suffering of the one I loved most, caused by the absence of the other. The structure of their world, the perfect, warm sphere I so adored, was threatening to shatter.

And I, who possessed a strength beyond human measure, who was impervious to the cold that would kill a mortal man in minutes, I stood by and did nothing. I was a god in power, yet a slave to my own loathsome form. But as I watched Elara sink into a chair, her face buried in her hands, her shoulders shaking with sobs that were lost to the storm’s howl, a new resolution formed within me. It was a thought born not of reason or philosophy, but from the raw, undeniable agony of my heart. What was the purpose of my strength if not to protect them? What was the meaning of my love if it did not compel me to act? I would be a monster in their eyes, yes, but I would not be a coward in my own.

Casting aside all fear for my own secrecy, I plunged into the storm. The wind, which had seemed a terrible force, was but an inconvenience to my powerful frame. The lashing water felt like a mere shower upon my thick skin. My eyes, accustomed to the deepest gloom, could discern the faint outline of the cliff path where a human eye would see nothing but a black, driving chaos. I moved with a speed and certainty that was not of mankind, my large feet finding purchase on the slippery, treacherous rock. I called his name, my voice a grotesque roar that was swallowed by the gale, yet I did not need a reply. My sense of smell, more akin to that of a beast, could detect the faint, alien scent of human presence even through the overwhelming odour of salt and wet earth.

I followed the trail of his passage to the highest point of the headland, to a place where the cliff fell away in a sheer drop to the churning cauldron below. There, my search at first revealed nothing. My heart, a leaden weight in my chest, began to sink into a despair deeper than any I had yet known. Had the sea claimed him? Had he been swept from the precipice by a gust of wind? As I cast my gaze about in a frenzy, I saw it: a patch of darker cloth snagged upon a thorn bush near the cliff’s edge. Below it, on a narrow ledge some twenty feet down the rock face, lay a dark shape.

With the agility of a mountain goat, I descended the treacherous cliff. The wind tore at me, seeking to pluck me from my hold and cast me into the abyss, but I held fast. There, on the ledge, I found him. Alistair was unconscious, his head bloodied from a severe wound, his clothes soaked through. One of his legs was twisted at an unnatural angle, clearly broken. He was terribly pale, and when I laid my hand upon his cheek, his skin was as cold as the stone upon which he lay. He was alive, but only just; the spark of his life was a flickering ember which this merciless storm would extinguish at any moment.

I did not hesitate. With a gentleness that belied my monstrous strength, I gathered his limp form into my arms. He was lighter than I had imagined, so fragile and breakable. The contrast between his delicate frame and my own immense and rugged body was a cruel testament to the gulf that separated our natures. I held in my arms the architect of my intellectual world, the man whose quiet labours had given my empty existence a semblance of purpose, and my heart swelled with a feeling so potent and painful I could not give it a name. It was the fierce protectiveness of a father, the selfless devotion of a disciple, and the agonised love of a hopeless outcast.

The ascent was more difficult than the descent. I had but one arm to steady myself, the other cradling Alistair’s body against my chest, shielding him as best I could from the fury of the storm. Once I reached the top of the cliff, I did not pause. I ran. I moved through the tempest not as a man would, but as a creature of the wild, my powerful legs devouring the path, my mind fixed on a single point: the small, warm light of the cottage.

As I drew near, the most terrible dilemma I had ever faced seized me. I had saved him, but what was I to do now? If I carried him to the door and knocked, Elara would see me. She would see the monster of her nightmares holding the broken body of her beloved. Her terror, her screams, would be the reward for my heroic deed. The shock might be so great as to cause her irreparable harm. She would not see a saviour; she would see a demon, a murderer who had perhaps caused this very injury. My good act would be irrevocably tainted by my appearance.

But the alternative was to leave him here, in the cold and the dark, mere yards from his salvation. His life was draining away with every passing second. To delay was to sentence him to death. My mind, which had so eagerly devoured the science and philosophy of men, was useless here. This was a problem of the heart, of the soul, and its logic was one of pain. I chose the path that would grant them life, and grant me only torment.

I crept to the very threshold of the cottage, my movements as silent as the shadows from which I was formed. I gently, so gently, laid Alistair’s body on the doorstep, propping him against the wooden frame so that he would be immediately visible. I took one last look at his pale, still face, a face I had studied for months with a love bordering on worship. Then, I retreated a few paces into the swirling darkness, raised my great fist, and struck the door three times, the sound like the crack of doom against the howl of the storm.

Before the echo had faded, I fled. I did not wait to see the door open. I could not bear to witness the inevitable shriek of horror, even if it was followed by a cry of relief. I scrambled back to my wretched hovel, the freezing rain and wind now seeming to pierce my skin and enter my very soul. I collapsed onto my bed of straw, a vessel of misery, and listened.

Even over the din of the gale, I heard it. It was not a scream of terror, but a sharp, broken cry of his name: “Alistair!” It was a sound of such anguish and love that it was like a physical dagger in my heart. I had succeeded. I had delivered him from the jaws of death and returned him to the arms of love. I had acted with the virtue of the heroes in their books. I had performed a deed of pure, selfless compassion.

And as I lay there in the cold and the dark, shaking with a sorrow more profound than any I had ever known, I understood the final, cruelest lesson of all. My virtue was my curse. My love was my damnation. To be good, I had to remain invisible. To protect them, I had to accept my eternal exile. I was the unseen foundation of their happiness, a happiness in which I could never, ever share. The warmth of their hearth was for them alone; for me, there was only the cold of the storm, a tempest without that was but a feeble imitation of the one that now raged forever within my own wretched breast. The question returned, no longer a plea for understanding, but a final, despairing condemnation: What was I? I was a monster whose only function was to suffer.

He was consumed by a double anguish: the ruin of his family, and the treachery of the man for whom he had sacrificed everything. It was this bitter disappointment that had etched such lines of sorrow upon his youthful countenance when I first beheld him. “Such was the tale to which I listened, and which filled me with a fresh tide of emotions. I had learned of the duplicity of man, but here was an instance of his virtue, his capacity for selfless love and heroic sacrifice. I adored my cottagers more than ever; I saw them as beings of a superior order, and their misfortunes seemed to me the blackest injustice. The name of the treacherous Turk filled me with execration; I loathed his deceit and his bigotry. And Safie, the lovely Safie! Her story was yet to be told, but I could already guess that she had not been a willing party to her father’s perfidy. “Indeed, she had not. When she discovered her father’s intention to betray his promise to Felix, her gentle heart was filled with indignation. She had been educated by a Christian Arab woman, a slave in her father’s household, who had secretly instilled in her a love of independence and a contempt for the restrictive customs of her own people. Her mother had been a Christian Arab, seized and enslaved by the Turks, and from her Safie had inherited a spirit that could not brook the confinement of a harem. The prospect of returning to Asia, to be immured for life and treated as a mere piece of property, was more dreadful to her than death. “She resolved, therefore, to escape. With the few jewels and a small sum of money she possessed, she fled from her father’s new home in Leghorn, accompanied only by the aged attendant who had been her confidante. She knew the name of the town to which the De Laceys had been exiled, and she journeyed towards Germany, her heart sustained by the hope of being reunited with her lover. Alas, her attendant fell ill and died on the journey, leaving the hapless Safie alone in a strange land, ignorant of its language and customs. For a time she was in despair, but her native courage and her love for Felix gave her strength. By showing the name of De Lacey, written upon a piece of paper, she was at last guided by a kindly peasant woman to the very cottage which I now observed. “Her arrival, as I have related, was a source of unexpected joy. Her presence banished the gloom that had settled upon the little household. Her gaiety was infectious; even the sorrowful Felix would sometimes smile at her charming attempts to master his language. Her love was a healing balm to his wounded spirit, and in her devotion he found a recompense for all that he had lost. “These histories, unfolding day by day, were a constant source of wonder and instruction to me. They were a supplement to the lessons of Volney; they gave a human face to the grand, impersonal sweep of history. I saw in the fate of my cottagers the story of countless families, caught in the gears of politics and religion, their private happiness sacrificed to the ambitions of kings and the dogmas of priests. I saw the power of love to transcend creed and country, and the bitterness of betrayal that could poison the purest heart. “My own heart was a whirlpool of these new and powerful feelings. I felt a profound sympathy for the exiled family, and a burning hatred for the injustice that had caused their ruin. When I heard of Felix’s selfless courage, my breast swelled with a vicarious pride; when I learned of Murad Ali’s treachery, I gnashed my teeth in fury. I wept with Safie for her mother, and I rejoiced with her in her escape. I was no longer a mere spectator; I was a participant in their drama, my emotions rising and falling with the tide of their fortunes. “And yet, this very participation served only to emphasize my own dreadful isolation. I was a part of their world, yet utterly alien to it. I felt with them, but I could not feel among them. My sympathy was a ghost at their feast, my love an unseen shadow in their light. The more I revered their virtues, the more I loathed my own monstrous form, which seemed a blasphemy against the beauty I had come to adore. The more I understood the bonds of family and fellowship, the more agonizing became the conviction that I was for ever excluded from them. “One day, a new circumstance occurred which was to have a profound and terrible effect upon my destiny. Felix, in his instructions to Safie, had often spoken of the duties of a citizen, of the importance of contributing to the good of the community. He spoke of the simple labours of the peasant, the skilled work of the artisan, the intellectual pursuits of the scholar, and the martial virtues of the soldier. He described how each man, in his own way, could serve his fellows and find a place in the great fabric of society. “These words sank deep into my soul. I, too, longed to serve. I yearned to prove that, despite my hideous exterior, I possessed a heart capable of gratitude and benevolence. I had, as you know, been secretly assisting the family for some time, gathering wood for their fire and clearing the paths of snow. But these were small, hidden services; I longed to perform some great and visible good, some act that would prove my worthiness and, perhaps, win me their acceptance. “The opportunity, I thought, had arrived. The spring thaw had been unusually swift, and the river that flowed at the bottom of the valley was swollen to a raging torrent. The bridge that had once spanned it, a flimsy wooden structure, had been swept away in the floods, and the villagers on the other side were cut off from the main road. I heard Felix lamenting this fact, for he had business with a farmer on the far bank and was unable to reach him. “Here, then, was my chance. I was stronger than any man; I could lift rocks and logs that would require the combined efforts of a dozen villagers. All that night, I laboured with a feverish intensity. I toppled great pines with my bare hands, I heaved massive boulders from the riverbed, and I constructed a new bridge, a rough but sturdy causeway of stone and timber. It was a work of immense toil, and by the time the first pale light of dawn touched the eastern sky, I was exhausted but triumphant. I had built a bridge, not only across the river, but, I hoped, across the gulf that separated me from my fellow beings. “I concealed myself in my usual hiding place and waited with a beating heart. I saw Felix emerge from the cottage, his axe on his shoulder, prepared for a day’s work in the forest. He walked towards the river, then stopped, his mouth agape in astonishment. He called to Agatha and Safie, who came running out, and their cries of wonder and delight were the sweetest music I had ever heard. They examined the bridge from every angle, marveling at its strength and the incredible speed with which it had been constructed. “‘It is a miracle!’ cried Agatha. ‘The good Lord has sent an angel to help us.’ “‘Or perhaps a giant from the old tales,’ said Felix, laughing, but with a note of awe in his voice. He looked around, into the woods, as if searching for the author of this prodigious feat. My heart leaped into my throat; for a moment I thought he saw me, and I shrank back into the shadows, trembling. “They spent the rest of the day in a state of joyful excitement. Felix crossed the bridge and conducted his business, and in the evening, the whole family gathered at the riverbank, admiring the work and speculating on its mysterious origin. They were convinced that some supernatural agency was at work, some benevolent spirit of the woods who had taken them under its protection. They offered up prayers of thanks, not to me, their true benefactor, but to a phantom of their own imagining. “And I, the lonely, wretched author of their joy, was forced to listen to my own praises bestowed upon a being that did not exist. It was a torment more subtle and more cruel than any I had yet endured. I had performed a godlike act of creation, and my reward was to be effaced, to be rendered invisible, to have my very existence denied. I had given them a gift, and in their acceptance of it, they had unwittingly confirmed my utter damnation. “For what hope could I have, if even my virtues were monstrous? My strength, which I had used for their benefit, was so far beyond the human scale that they could only attribute it to a supernatural being. My act of love was so great that it had rendered me inhuman. I had sought to join their world, but had only succeeded in proving that I could never belong to it. “That night, as I lay in my hovel, a cold and terrible clarity descended upon me. I saw that all my efforts were in vain. I could learn their language, I could adopt their manners, I could revere their virtues, but I could never, ever be one of them. My form was my destiny, and my destiny was to be alone. The bridge I had built was a monument not to my hope, but to my despair. It connected the two banks of the river, but the chasm between myself and humanity remained as deep and as impassable as ever. “A new and dreadful feeling began to stir within me. It was not sorrow, for sorrow I had known intimately. It was not loneliness, for loneliness was the very air I breathed. It was something colder, harder, a feeling for which I had no name, but which I now recognize as the first, faint whisper of revenge. If my good deeds were to be misconstrued, if my love was to be met with unwitting scorn, then what was the use of being good? If I was to be a monster in their eyes, regardless of the purity of my heart, then perhaps it was time to become the monster they imagined me to be. “The thought terrified me, and I thrust it away. I clung to the image of my cottagers, to the memory of their gentle manners and their amiable qualities. I reminded myself of the old man’s kindness, of Agatha’s sweetness, of Felix’s noble heart, of Safie’s innocent charm. I could not, I would not, allow myself to hate them. They were my world, my only link to the goodness I so admired. “And so I resolved to make one last, desperate attempt to bridge the chasm. My strength had failed me; my secret labours had only deepened my isolation. But I had another tool at my disposal, a tool I had so painstakingly acquired: language. I could speak. I could reason. I could plead my cause. “I had observed that the old man, De Lacey, was blind. His children often left him alone for hours at a time while they worked in the forest or the garden. Here, then, was my final, desperate hope. The others, I knew, would be horrified by my appearance; their judgment would be instantaneous and absolute. But the old man, he could not see me. He would hear only my voice. He would judge me by my words, by the sentiments I expressed. I would pour out my whole story to him, my loneliness, my suffering, my admiration for his family. I would convince him of my benevolence, of my gentle disposition. And he, in his wisdom and kindness, would surely pity me. He would see the man within the monster. He would become my advocate; he would prepare his children for my appearance, and persuade them to look past my deformities and see the heart that beat within. “This was my plan, my last throw of the dice. The risks were immense; if I failed, all would be lost. But the alternative was a slow descent into a madness of solitude and despair, a life so utterly devoid of hope that it was no life at all. I would watch for an opportunity, a time when the old man was alone. I would gather all my courage, all my eloquence, and I would present myself to him, not as a monster, but as a supplicant, a fellow being pleading for a crumb of the compassion that makes men human. My fate, I knew, rested upon the success of this one, single encounter. And as I waited, my whole being was a knot of terror and of hope, a fragile vessel on a stormy sea, praying for the sight of a friendly shore.

The days that followed were a torment of a kind I had not yet known; for whereas my previous suffering had been born of a steady and unchanging despair, this new agony was a ceaseless oscillation between the dizziest heights of hope and the most abysmal troughs of terror. I would watch the sun rise, and its golden rays, illuminating the simple beauty of the cottage, would fill me with an ardour that felt like certainty; I would tell myself that today, this very day, would be the one to decide my fate for ever. I rehearsed my speech in a thousand different forms, sometimes beginning with a direct and frank appeal, at other times with a more circuitous and philosophical discourse on the nature of prejudice. My mind laboured to find the perfect sequence of words, the key that would unlock the old man’s heart and, through him, the hearts of all the world.

But then, as the hours wore on, and the opportunity failed to present itself, my courage would falter and my spirits would sink into a gloom more profound than any I had before experienced. The sun would seem to mock me with its indifferent warmth, and the songs of the birds sounded like a dirge for my dying hopes. What if my voice, which I had trained to imitate the gentle cadences of my protectors, should betray me with some monstrous, guttural tone? What if the old man, though blind, should possess some finer sense, some instinct which would reveal to him the hideousness of the creature in his presence? These fears would assail me with such force that I would retreat to the darkest corner of my hovel, covering my ears as if to shut out the sound of my own frantic thoughts, and I would resolve to abandon my desperate plan, to resign myself to my eternal solitude. Yet with the next dawn, hope, that resilient and torturing companion, would again stir within me, and the cycle of agitation would begin anew.

Several weeks passed in this miserable state of indecision. I observed that Felix and Agatha now often went together to gather wood or to work for a neighbouring farmer, leaving the old man alone for two or three hours at a time. Safie would sometimes remain to read to him, but on other occasions she would accompany her friends, eager to behold more of the country in which she was now a resident. It was on one of these latter occasions that my chance at last arrived.

It was a clear, cold day in the decline of autumn; the leaves had fallen from the trees, and the ground was crisp with a light frost. I had watched from my hiding place as the three younger cottagers, their faces bright with health and cheerfulness, had set out on a walk of some considerable length, for Felix carried a basket which I guessed to contain their midday meal. My heart beat with a violence that threatened to rob me of breath; my limbs trembled as with a palsy. This was the moment. If I delayed now, my resolve might evaporate for ever, and I would be lost.

With a silent groan that was half a prayer and half a curse, I crept from my hovel. The short journey to the cottage door, a distance I had covered a thousand times in the dead of night, now seemed an immense and perilous voyage. The frozen earth crunched under my feet, and the sound struck me as loud as a thunderclap. I expected at every instant to see a face appear at the window, to hear a scream of terror that would signal the end of all things. But all was quiet. I reached the door, that simple wooden barrier which separated my world of wretchedness from all that I held dear and sacred. For a full minute I stood there, unable to move, my hand hovering in the air, my courage deserting me at the final precipice. But then I thought of the long, desolate winter that was approaching, of the endless days and nights of solitude, of the gnawing hunger for companionship that was eating away at my soul. This agony gave me a strength born of desperation. I raised my hand and knocked.

The sound was feeble, yet it seemed to echo in the stillness with a terrible resonance. I held my breath, listening. I heard a shuffling sound from within, and then the voice of the old man, gentle and inquiring. “Who is there?” he called.

I cleared my throat, which had become painfully dry, and strove to command my voice. “A friend,” I replied, and the word, so long a stranger to my lips, came out in a tone lower and more tremulous than I had intended. “A poor traveller, in need of rest and a little warmth.”

“Come in, then,” said the benevolent old man. “The door is not barred. Do not stand in the cold; my fire is a humble one, but it is at the service of any who are in need.”

My whole frame shook with a new and overpowering emotion as I lifted the latch and stepped inside. It was the first time I had ever entered a human dwelling, and for a moment, I was dizzy with the strangeness of it. The air was filled with the scent of woodsmoke and dried herbs, and the small room, though poor, was clean and filled with a sense of peace. I saw the chair where Agatha sat with her mending, the shelf which held Felix’s few books, the guitar that Safie played with such wondrous skill. My eyes, accustomed to the gloom of my hovel, drank in these simple details with a thirsty and painful delight. The old man sat in his armchair by the hearth, his sightless eyes turned towards the door, a placid and welcoming smile upon his face.

“You are welcome, stranger,” he said. “Pray, come and sit by the fire. You must be chilled from your journey.”

I did as he bid me, moving with a caution that was born of terror, lest I should stumble or make some clumsy movement that would betray my unnatural size and strength. I chose a small stool at the furthest corner of the hearth, keeping myself as much as possible in the shadows.

“You are very kind, sir,” I began, my voice growing steadier as I spoke. “I have travelled a great distance, and I have found little welcome in the world.”

“The world can be a harsh and prejudiced place,” he answered with a sigh. “I know it but too well. Men are too often judged by their outward circumstances, by their fortune or their station, and not by the true merit of their hearts. But tell me, friend, from whence do you come, and whither are you bound?”

Here was the opening I had sought. “I am an outcast,” I said, my words gathering force and passion as the floodgates of my long-suppressed misery were opened. “I am friendless and alone. There is one family in this world, and one only, whom I admire and love above all things. I have observed them from afar; I have learned to esteem their virtues, to weep at their sorrows, and to rejoice in their happiness. But I am an object of scorn and horror to all who behold me, and I fear that if I were to present myself to these good people, they too would spurn me, as all others have done.”

The old man listened with an expression of the deepest sympathy. “That is a sad tale, indeed,” he said. “A prejudice so deeply rooted is a difficult thing to overcome. But if this family is as good and as virtuous as you believe them to be, surely they would be capable of looking beyond any outward deformity to the goodness of the heart within.”

“Oh, if I could but believe it!” I cried, leaning forward in my eagerness. “Their kindness is the light of my dreary life. The father is a man of learning and benevolence; the son is noble and brave; the daughter is an angel of sweetness and compassion. To be admitted to their society, to hear their gentle voices addressed to me, to be allowed to perform the smallest service for them; for this I would give up all the world!”

“You speak with a strange ardour,” said De Lacey, a slight puzzlement in his tone. “You must know them very well, to feel so strongly.”

“I know them as I know my own wretched heart!” I exclaimed. “I have been their unseen friend, their secret servant. But they do not know of my existence, and I am terrified that the sight of me would turn their love into loathing.” I paused, my agitation almost choking me, and then I plunged onward to the heart of my plea. “It is for this reason that I have come to you. I am a stranger to you, but you seem to me a man of wisdom and compassion. I have a wild, desperate hope that you might be willing to act as my advocate. I wish to reveal myself first to you, who cannot be prejudiced by my appearance, and if I can win your pity, your friendship, perhaps you might then prepare your family to receive me. Perhaps you could teach them to see me not as a monster, but as a sensitive and feeling creature who craves their affection.”

For a moment, there was silence. The only sound was the crackling of the fire on the hearth. The old man’s face was a study of deep and troubled thought. My fate hung suspended in that awful silence. When he spoke again, his voice was filled with a profound and gentle gravity.

“My friend,” he said, “I will not deceive you. What you ask is a great and difficult thing. The prejudices of mankind are not so easily dislodged. But your words have moved me very deeply. There is in your voice an accent of sincerity and of suffering that cannot be feigned. I, too, have suffered from injustice; my family and I are exiles, driven from our home by the cruelty and falsehood of others. I cannot, I will not, refuse to help one who appears to be even more wretched than myself. Be assured, poor, unhappy stranger, I will be your friend. I will speak to my children; I will do all in my power to persuade them to receive you. Tell me, where does this family reside? Are they near at hand?”

A cry of joy, of inexpressible, delirious transport, escaped my lips. I had succeeded! My last, desperate gamble had paid off! He would be my protector; he would mediate for me! The long night of my solitude was over; the dawn was at hand! In an ecstasy of gratitude, I fell at his feet and seized his hand, pressing it to my lips. “You are my saviour! My creator!” I sobbed. “Oh, benevolent, godlike man! Now, now I can dare to hope for happiness! The family I speak of… they are your own children! It is you, you and your beloved family, whom I have adored from afar!”

“What?” he exclaimed, starting back in his chair, his face a picture of astonishment. “My children? Felix? Agatha?”

Even as he spoke the words, and as I, still kneeling, looked up at his bewildered face, the cottage door was thrown open. I turned my head, and my momentary paradise was transformed in an instant into a scene from the deepest circle of hell. Felix, Agatha, and Safie stood upon the threshold.

Their return was a calamity for which I was utterly unprepared. A shriek of terror burst from Agatha’s lips, and she sank to the floor in a faint. Safie, her face a mask of speechless horror, did not even possess the strength to scream; with a gasp of pure animal fear, she tore her eyes from my face and fled from the cottage, vanishing into the woods.

But it was the countenance of Felix that sealed my doom. His initial shock gave way in a heartbeat to a fury of protective rage. He saw not a supplicant holding his father’s hand in gratitude; he saw a loathsome monster, a hellish fiend, in the act of assaulting the blind and helpless old man. With a roar of execration, he sprang forward, and seizing a heavy stick from the corner of the room, he fell upon me with a fury I could not have conceived.

The blows rained down upon my shoulders and my back, but I felt no physical pain. The agony in my soul was so immense, so all-consuming, that it rendered my body insensible. I loosed the old man’s hand. I could have torn my assailant limb from limb, for my strength was to his as the tempest is to a summer breeze. But my heart was frozen with a grief and a despair that left me powerless. All I could do was to utter a cry of anguish, a howl of such inhuman misery that it seemed to tear my very being apart. Then, turning from the scene of my ruined hopes, I bounded from the cottage and fled with a speed that no human eye could follow.

I did not stop until I had reached the sanctuary of my hovel, where I threw myself upon the cold earth, my mind a vortex of indescribable torment. The change in my feelings was as swift and as total as the catastrophe which had produced it. The ardent love I had felt for my cottagers curdled in an instant into a bitter and burning hatred. My admiration for their virtues was transformed into a profound and scathing contempt for their blind, ignorant prejudice. They had judged me. They had condemned me without a hearing. They had answered my love with violence and my supplication with abhorrence. In that moment, the last vestiges of my gentle and benevolent nature were extinguished, and a fiend rose from the ashes, a creature of malice and revenge. I gnashed my teeth, and a vow of eternal hatred and war against all mankind escaped my lips in a hoarse and broken whisper.

I lay in a state of torpor for the remainder of the day and through the long, terrible night. My mind replayed the scene of my rejection a thousand and a thousand times, each repetition a fresh stab in my already mortally wounded spirit. I heard again Agatha’s scream, saw again the horror on Safie’s face, felt again the blows from Felix’s stick. Most terribly of all, I heard the echo of my own cry of despair, a sound so monstrous that it seemed to justify their hatred, to prove that I was indeed the demon they imagined me to be.

As morning approached, I heard sounds of commotion from the cottage. Peering through a chink in the wall of my hovel, I saw a sight that drove the final nail into the coffin of my former self. Felix was in conversation with another man, a stranger, whom I recognized as the owner of the cottage. Money was exchanged. Soon after, I saw Agatha and Safie emerge, their faces pale and tear-stained, carrying bundles of their few possessions. The old man was led out by his son, his head bowed in what I imagined was sorrow and confusion. They loaded their meagre belongings onto a cart which the stranger had brought, and without a single backward glance at the place which had been their home, the place which had been my entire world, they departed.

I watched until the cart had dwindled to a speck in the distance and disappeared. They were gone. They had abandoned the cottage, driven away by the horror of my presence. The one fragile link that had connected me to the world of feeling and of love was broken for ever. I was alone, truly and absolutely alone, in a universe that seemed to me nothing but a vast and empty desert. A black and icy calm settled over my soul, a quietude not of peace, but of utter, bottomless despair. And in that dreadful calm, a single, clear purpose began to form. They had cast me out; they had declared me to be their enemy. So be it. An enemy I would be. But my vengeance would not be wreaked upon these poor, deluded cottagers. My rage, my suffering, my entire being, now had a new and more fitting target. I would seek out the one who had given me life, the one who had endowed me with sensations and passions only to cast me abroad in utter disgust. I would find my creator, and he, my arch-enemy, would feel the full weight of the misery he had inflicted upon his wretched and abandoned creation.

With this vow burning in my breast, a fire more potent and destructive than any earthly flame, I rose from the damp earth of my hovel. My limbs, which had been locked in a stupor of grief, now moved with a terrible and newfound alacrity. The world, which had been a place of potential wonder, was now merely a stage for my revenge; and I, its most miserable actor, had at last been given my part. My first act was one of pure, unadulterated fury. I burst from my hiding place, no longer caring for stealth or secrecy, and stood before the empty cottage. It was a tombstone marking the grave of my hopes, a silent monument to my eternal despair. A scream, not of sorrow but of unholy rage, tore itself from my throat, a sound so discordant and monstrous that it startled the very birds from the trees.

With the strength of a tempest, I fell upon the little garden that Agatha had so lovingly tended. I tore the rose bushes from the earth, their thorns ripping my flesh to no effect, for the pain in my soul was so great as to render all physical sensation meaningless. I trampled the sweet-scented flowers, crushing their vibrant heads into the black soil until they were nothing but a coloured stain upon the ground. I shattered the neat wooden fence that Felix had built, splintering the posts with my bare hands and casting the pieces aside like so many broken twigs. I was a whirlwind of destruction, a demon let loose upon this fragile paradise, and I took a grim and terrible pleasure in the desecration.

My rage then carried me to the cottage itself. I smashed the window through which I had so often peered, watching the family in their domestic bliss. The sound of the shattering glass was music to my ears. I entered the main room, the sacred space from which I had been so violently expelled. The hearth was cold, the chairs were empty. On the floor lay a single ribbon, dropped perhaps by Agatha in her hasty departure. I snatched it up, and for a moment a wave of the old sorrow washed over me; but I crushed it, as I had crushed the flowers, and cast the ribbon into the cold ashes. My eyes fell upon the guitar left leaning against the wall, the very instrument that had drawn tears of joy from my eyes. I seized it, and with a roar of anguish and hatred, I brought it down against the stone fireplace, shattering the delicate wood into a hundred pieces. The silence that followed was more profound than any I had ever known.

But silence was not enough. This place had to be erased, its memory burned from the face of the earth. I gathered dried brush and the broken remnants of the fence, piling them high within the cottage and against its outer walls. I retrieved a flint and steel which I had observed Felix using, and with a grim deliberation, I struck a spark. A small flame flickered to life, and I fed it tenderly, as a mother might feed her child. I watched as it licked at the dry wood, grew bolder, and began to consume the structure that had been my school, my church, my entire world. I stood outside as the flames leaped towards the sky, a roaring conflagration that painted the night with hues of orange and red. The cottage became my own funeral pyre, the pyre of the gentle creature I once had been. As the roof finally collapsed in a shower of sparks and embers, I turned my back on the inferno and, without a single look behind, strode into the darkness of the forest. My past was now ashes, and before me lay only the long, dark road to my creator.

Before I set my course, I returned for one last time to the squalid hovel that had been my only shelter. I had left it untouched in my fury, and now I searched its confines, not with any hope of finding comfort, but with a methodical coldness, seeking any tool or remnant that might aid my journey. It was then, tucked away in a dark corner and covered by a pile of straw I had never before disturbed, that I found a leather satchel. It was not mine, nor did it belong to the cottagers. With a jolt, I realised it must have been left by my creator on that first dreadful night of my existence, abandoned in his haste to flee from his own creation. My fingers, trembling with a mixture of eagerness and dread, fumbled with the clasp.

Within, I found not food or money, but a change of clothes, some scientific instruments of a kind I did not understand, and a small, leather-bound book. It was a journal. I opened it, and though the script was hasty and at times difficult to decipher, my recently acquired knowledge of letters was sufficient. I sat upon the floor of my wretched den, the distant glow of the burning cottage my only light, and I began to read the story of my own conception.

The words I read chilled me to the very marrow of my bones. I learned of the man who had given me life; his name, Victor Frankenstein, was seared into my brain. I read of his family in Geneva, of his youth, of his consuming ambition that drove him to spurn all human connection in the pursuit of a terrible and solitary goal. He wrote of his labours with a kind of feverish pride, detailing the long months spent in charnel houses and dissecting rooms, violating the sanctity of the grave to assemble the materials for his unhallowed work. He called his quest noble, a gift to be bestowed upon mankind; but I, his monstrous progeny, saw in it nothing but the vilest and most selfish arrogance, a blasphemous desire to usurp the power of God himself.

And then I came to the account of my birth. I read of the night he infused the spark of life into my inanimate frame, and his words were not those of a proud father, but of a horrified artist recoiling from a work of unexpected ugliness. “His yellow eye was open,” he wrote, “but he breathed hard, and a convulsive motion agitated his limbs.” Disgust! Horror! These were the first sentiments I had inspired in my creator. He had fled from me, leaving me, a newborn, more helpless than any human infant, to awaken alone in a world I did not understand. He had abandoned me, disowned me, cast me out as a thing of filth and terror.

The journal continued, detailing his subsequent illness, his reunion with his friend Clerval, and his utter neglect and forgetfulness of the miserable being he had brought into the world. He spoke of me as his “curse,” his “demonic creation,” a secret shame to be hidden away and never acknowledged. Not once did he express a shred of concern for my fate, a flicker of responsibility for my suffering. I was a mistake, an abomination to be erased from his memory. As I read these passages, the last vestiges of any possible compassion for my creator were extinguished. My journey continued, but my heart was now a block of ice. The wound in my shoulder healed, leaving a scar that was a constant reminder of my folly. This was my promise, my vow, sworn in the blood of an innocent child and sealed with the destruction of an innocent woman. You are my creator, but I am your master; obey! Your misery is my dominion, and I will not rest until you have shared every last drop of the bitter cup you have forced me to drink. It was this resolve that led me to seek you out here, in this sea of ice, to tell you my story, to make you understand the depth of the wrong you have done me, and to lay before you my one, ultimate demand. Without it, the tale of our shared damnation is but half told, and the horrors to come shall make these first acts of my vengeance seem but childish pranks. You must listen, and you must obey.

I paused, allowing the weight of my confession to settle upon you; I let the silence of the glacier and the vast, indifferent peaks be the only witnesses to your dawning horror. You recoiled from me, your countenance a mask of mingled fury and despair. Your hand, which had been resting upon a ledge of ice, clenched until the knuckles were white as bone. I saw in your eyes not a flicker of the pity I had once so desperately craved, but only the blackest abhorrence. This was well; it was from your hatred, not your compassion, that I would wring my salvation.

“You have heard my tale,” I continued, my voice losing the tremor of passion and assuming a tone of cold, deliberate reason. “You have seen how I, who was born for benevolence and love, have been twisted into a fiend by the misery of my solitude. I am malicious because I am miserable; am I not shunned and hated by all mankind? You, my creator, would tear me to pieces and triumph; remember that, and tell me why I should pity man more than he pities me? You would not call it murder if you could precipitate me into one of those ice-rifts and destroy my frame, the work of your own hands. Shall I respect man when he contemns me? Let him live with me in the interchange of kindness, and instead of injury I will bestow every benefit upon him with tears of gratitude at his acceptance. But that cannot be; the human senses are insurmountable barriers to our union. Yet mine shall not be the submission of abject slavery. I will revenge my injuries; if I cannot inspire love, I will cause fear, and chiefly towards you my arch-enemy, because my creator, do I swear inextinguishable hatred. Have a care; I will work at your destruction, nor finish until I desolate your heart, so that you shall curse the hour of your birth.”

A groan of exquisite anguish escaped your lips, and you covered your face with your hands. “Devil!” you exclaimed. “Cease; and do not poison the air with these sounds of malice. I have declared my resolution to you, and I am no coward to bend beneath words. Leave me; I am inexorable.”

“It is well,” I said. “I go; but remember, I shall be with you on your wedding-night.”

I had started to turn away, but at your sudden, sharp intake of breath, I paused. You had lowered your hands, and your face, pale as the surrounding snows, was a study in profound terror. I had found the wound, the place where my threats could inflict the most grievous pain. I turned back, and my voice, which had been harsh with menace, now softened into a semblance of persuasion, a tool more subtle and perhaps more cruel than any overt threat.

“That is my curse,” I said, “and it shall be fulfilled. Yet it need not be so. You have heard my sufferings; you have seen that my capacity for feeling is as great, nay, greater than your own, for it has not been blunted by society or companionship. My demand, the one for which I have sought you out across these frozen wastes, is simple, yet it is the sum of all my desires, the sole condition upon which I will renounce my campaign of vengeance and quit the world of man for ever. You must create a female for me, with whom I can live in the interchange of those sympathies necessary for my being. This you alone can do. I demand it of you as a right which you must not refuse to concede.”

Your horror seemed to find its voice at last, and you burst forth in a torrent of furious refusal. “I will not!” you cried. “Never! I will not create another such as you, to join with you in the joy of destruction, to spread despair and murder across the earth. Shall I create another, more malignant than yourself, who might delight, for its own sake, in scenes of blood and misery? Begone, vile insect! Or rather, stay, that I may trample you to dust! And oh, that I could, with the extinction of your miserable existence, restore those victims whom you have so diabolically murdered!”

“I expected this reception,” I said calmly, though a tempest of rage and disappointment was gathering within me. “It is what I have learned to anticipate from all of humanity. But you, Frankenstein, you are my creator; you are my Adam, or rather, my fallen angel whom you have driven from joy for no misdeed. Everywhere I see bliss, from which I alone am irrevocably excluded. I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend. Make me happy, and I shall again be virtuous. My virtues will be a consequence of my happiness; my vices are the fruit of a solitude that you, and you alone, have imposed upon me. Can you, in the face of reason, deny this?”

I drew closer, my immense stature casting a shadow over you as you leaned, exhausted, against the rock. “Consider my proposition,” I urged, my voice a low and earnest murmur. “I ask for a companion of my own species, as deformed and horrible as myself; the gratification is small, but it is all that I can receive, and it shall content me. We shall seek out the vast and uninhabited wilds of South America. I shall no longer feel the sting of man’s scorn, for I shall have a companion who will not deny herself to me. My food is not that of man; I do not destroy the lamb and the kid to glut my appetite; acorns and berries afford me sufficient nourishment. My companion will be of the same nature. We shall make our bed of dried leaves; the sun will shine on us as on man and will ripen our food. The picture I present to you is peaceful and human, and you must feel that you could deny it only in the wantonness of power and cruelty. Pitiful, indeed, is the being who asks for so little, and yet is met with such utter repulsion.”

I saw you waver. Your brow, which had been furrowed with rage, now bore the lines of deep and troubled thought. Although the sun had now climbed high above the jagged peaks, casting a brilliant and painful glare upon the endless fields of ice, a chill deeper than that of the glacier entered my heart; for in his words I perceived not the ravings of a madman, but the cold and calculated logic of a being whose misery had sharpened his intellect to a terrifying point. You were moved, and I pressed my advantage.

“If you consent,” I continued, “neither you nor any other human being shall ever see us again. I will go to the vast wilds of South America. My evil passions will have fled, for I shall meet with sympathy; my life will flow quietly away, and in my dying moments I shall not curse my destroyer. But if you refuse, I will glut the maw of death, until it be satiated with the blood of your remaining friends. I will watch you, and when you believe yourself safe, I shall be there to strike. Your father, your last remaining brother, your bride; all shall perish until you are as alone as I am.”

“No, no!” you gasped, your voice hoarse with dread. “Do not speak of her! You would not, you could not!”

“I can and I will,” I answered, my tone now as cold and hard as the ice beneath my feet. “You are the arbiter of their fates. Grant my prayer, and I will be gone for ever. Deny it, and your hours of joy will be but a prelude to the deepest despair. Think of the peace you will purchase for them, for yourself. You will be free of me; my existence will be but a dreadful memory, a shadow that has passed. You will be able to return to your life, to your studies, to the arms of your family, and know that you have saved them by one act of justice. Is their safety not worth this one final labour? Is the happiness of your Elizabeth not worth this concession to your own creation?”

You were silent for a long time. The wind howled through the crevices of the glacier, a mournful and desolate sound. You turned your face away from me, towards the distant line of the horizon, as if seeking an answer in the empty sky. I could see the conflict that tore at your soul. On the one hand was your profound, instinctual loathing of me and of the act I was demanding; on the other, the stark and undeniable truth of my threats. You were a man of science, a man of reason, and my arguments, however monstrous their source, were not without a certain terrible logic. The scales of your judgement were trembling in the balance.

“You swear,” you said at last, your voice barely a whisper, “you swear to quit Europe for ever, and every other place in the neighbourhood of man, as soon as I deliver into your hands a female who will accompany you in your exile?”

“I swear it,” I replied, and in my eagerness I fell to my knees upon the ice. “By the sun, and by the blue sky of Heaven, and by the fire of love that burns my heart, I swear it. If you grant my prayer, while they exist you shall never behold me again. Depart to your home and commence your labours; I shall watch their progress with an anxiety that may be conceived, and when you have completed your work, I will appear and claim my reward.”

You shuddered, as though the very thought of my watching you was an unbearable violation. “And this… this companion,” you stammered, “must she be like you? In form? In strength?”

“She must,” I said firmly. “She must be of my species, in all its particulars. Else how could she be a true companion? How could she feel for me the sympathy for which I long? She must be able to endure as I endure, to live as I live. It is the only way.”

You rose to your feet, your decision made. The agony on your face was terrible to behold; it was the look of a man who has chosen his own damnation to forestall a greater evil. “I consent to your demand,” you said, and the words seemed to be torn from the very depths of your being. “On your solemn oath to quit Europe for ever, and every other place in the neighbourhood of man, I will create a companion for you. But first, leave me. My heart is full, and I desire solitude. Return to your hovel, and await my progress. I shall begin my work, and when it is done, you shall have your reward.”

A cry of triumphant joy, which sounded hideous and unnatural in that silent place, escaped my lips. “You have made me happy!” I exclaimed. “This one act of justice will go far to redeem the wrongs you have committed. I shall now depart; but I will be near. I shall watch you, Frankenstein; I shall know of your progress. Do not think to deceive me. My life, and the lives of all you hold dear, depend upon your fidelity.”

With these words, I turned and left you. I did not look back, but I felt your eyes upon me, burning with a hatred that was now mixed with the cold dread of a promise unwillingly given. I descended the mountain with a speed and agility that no human could have matched. My heart, for the first time since my creation, felt a sensation akin to hope. I was no longer a solitary wanderer; I was a being with a future, a purpose. The prospect of a companion, of a being who would not flee from my sight, who would share my exile and understand my sorrows, filled me with a rapture that bordered on madness.

I did not return to the hovel by the cottage of the De Laceys; that place was now tainted with the memory of my deepest despair. Instead, I made my way into the deepest recesses of the mountains, finding a cave in a remote and inaccessible glen where I could wait and watch. From the high peaks, I could sometimes see the distant glimmer of the lights of Geneva, and I imagined you there, in the bosom of your family, your heart heavy with the terrible secret you now carried.

My days were spent in a state of feverish anticipation. I watched the changing seasons, not with the former sadness of an outcast, but with the impatience of one who awaits a great and promised joy. The spring came, and the ice on the lower slopes began to melt; the summer followed, and the valleys were filled with the scent of pine and wild flowers. And all the while, I thought of you, my creator, my slave. I pictured you in your laboratory, surrounded by the same instruments that had given me life, now working to create my Eve. I trusted you, not to your honour, for I knew you had none where I was concerned, but to your fear. The image of your Elizabeth, of the terror I had planted in your heart for her, was my firmest security.

Yet as the months wore on, a new anxiety began to creep into my thoughts. You were slow, so very slow. My impatience grew with each passing day. Had you repented of your promise? Were you even now plotting some treachery, some way to evade your solemn oath? The thought filled me with a rage that shook my very frame. I would climb to the highest crags that overlooked the plains of Switzerland and watch for hours, my eyes straining for any sign of you. I would not be cheated of my reward. The happiness of one being, a happiness you yourself had made possible and then denied, depended upon your labour. And if you failed, if you faltered, I would unleash a storm of vengeance that would make my previous deeds seem like the harmless mischief of a child. You had made your promise on the sea of ice; I would hold you to it, though it cost you your soul. The world of man had cast me out, but you, Frankenstein, you were bound to me, and I would not release you until you had fashioned for me my bride.

He was noted for his great wealth, and it was this, rather than any proven crime, which marked him for destruction. He was seized one night, condemned upon the barest of accusations, and thrown into prison. The injustice of this proceeding was manifest and caused a great stir in Paris. Felix, in particular, was horrified by the tyranny of the court, and he publicly declared his indignation at the sentence which condemned the unfortunate merchant to death.

These declarations reached the ears of the Turk himself, who, languishing in his dungeon, conceived a desperate plan. He had a daughter, the beautiful and accomplished Safie, who had been the sole solace of his life. He found means to communicate with Felix and offered him his daughter’s hand, along with a considerable fortune, if he could effect his escape. Felix, whose heart was fired not by the promise of wealth but by a sense of justice and a romantic admiration for the beautiful Arabian, whose letters he had chanced to see, eagerly accepted the proposition. He laboured night and day, and at length, through a combination of daring and stratagem, he succeeded in liberating the merchant from his prison. They fled from Paris together, but their triumph was short lived.

The French government, enraged at the escape of their prisoner, discovered the part Felix had played. In their fury, they seized the innocent De Lacey and his daughter Agatha, casting them into a dungeon and confiscating their entire fortune. When this news reached Felix, he was torn between his duty to his family and his love for the Arabian, who he had escorted to safety in Leghorn. After a period of agonising indecision, he resolved to return to Paris and surrender himself to the authorities, hoping thereby to secure the release of his father and sister. Safie, distraught at the thought of being separated from her lover and terrified of being returned to the tyranny of her father, who wished her to live in a Turkish harem, resolved to follow him. But before she could put her plan into effect, De Lacey and Agatha were condemned, after a mockery of a trial, to perpetual exile from their native country. Felix, who had already begun his journey back, met them on the road, and together the ruined and heartbroken family sought a miserable asylum in the cottage in Germany where I first beheld them.

Safie, meanwhile, was left in Leghorn under the care of a servant, with a small sum of money and a few jewels. Her father, the treacherous Turk, hearing of the ruin of his benefactor’s family, forgot his promises and his gratitude and secretly embarked for his native land, leaving a cruel letter for his daughter in which he commanded her to follow. But the soul of Safie was formed for nobler things; she abhorred the idea of returning to a country where women were treated as slaves, and her heart yearned for her Felix and the freedom of thought which he had taught her to value. Taking what little property she possessed, and with the aid of an attendant who knew the German language, she made her way across the continent, and after many hardships and delays, she at last found the humble dwelling of her beloved.

Such was the history of my cottagers. It impressed me deeply. I learned from it the vices and virtues of mankind. I saw that a man could be driven to ruin by the malice of his fellows, and I felt my heart swell with indignation. I saw, too, the power of love and devotion, which could lead a gentle spirit like Safie to cross half a world for the sake of her beloved. These lessons sank deep into my mind, but they always led me back to one agonising question: where did I fit into this complex tapestry of human existence? I had no family, no history, no country. I was a thing apart, a monster.

The autumn passed into winter; the ground became hard and unyielding, and the trees were stripped of their leaves, standing like gaunt skeletons against a grey and unforgiving sky. The winds howled around my miserable hovel, and the snow fell in thick, silent drifts, burying the world in a shroud of white. My protectors were now confined almost entirely to their cottage, and their stores of food, I observed, began to dwindle. The sorrow which had been lifted by the arrival of Safie began to creep back into their countenances. Felix would often sit for hours with his head in his hands, a picture of despair, while Agatha wept silently by his side.

My own sufferings from the cold were intense, but they were nothing compared to the pain I felt at witnessing the distress of my friends. My nocturnal rambles were now dedicated entirely to their service. I would gather wood in quantities far greater than Felix could have managed in a week and pile it by their door under the cover of darkness. I cleared the path to the well of snow, and when I saw their supply of vegetables failing, I would venture to distant farms and pilfer potatoes and cabbages from their stores, feeling a strange mixture of guilt and satisfaction in my crimes. These small services were the only joy of my existence; to see a momentary smile on Agatha’s face or to hear the old man bless their unknown benefactor was a reward so exquisite that it brought tears to my eyes.

It was during one of these expeditions that a new idea took root in my mind. The old man, De Lacey, was blind. He could not see my odious form; he would be able to judge me only by the tone of my voice and the sentiments I expressed. If I could gain his confidence and friendship, might he not then intercede for me with his children? Might he not prepare them for my appearance and persuade them to look upon me with compassion rather than horror? The thought filled me with a tumultuous mixture of hope and fear. For days I debated with myself, my heart beating with a violent and painful agitation whenever I contemplated the attempt. I practiced speaking in a gentle and persuasive tone; I rehearsed the words I would use to tell my story, a story I would craft to elicit pity, omitting the more terrifying details of my origin.

At last, I summoned my courage. I waited for a day when I knew Felix, Agatha, and Safie would be absent on a long walk. As I saw them disappear into the woods, my limbs trembled and my breath came in short, ragged gasps. I approached the cottage door, my hand hovering over the latch for a full five minutes before I could master the tremor that shook my frame. I knocked. “Who is there?” said the old man. “Come in.”

I entered. “Pardon this intrusion,” I said, my voice faltering despite all my practice. “I am a traveller in need of a little rest; you would greatly oblige me if you would allow me to sit by your fire for a moment.”

“By all means,” said De Lacey. “Come in and warm yourself. It is a cold day, and I am glad to have a companion. My children are out, and I am alone. Pray, sit down.”

I drew a chair towards the hearth and sat in silence, my heart too full for speech. The old man’s benevolent countenance and gentle manner gave me a courage I had not thought possible. I felt as if I were in the presence of a father, a feeling so new and so overwhelming that it was almost painful.

“You seem to be a stranger to these parts,” he continued. “Are you French?”

“No,” I replied, “but I was educated by a French family and I have a great love for your countrymen.”

“Ah, that is a blessing,” said the old man. “I and my family are exiles from our native land. But we are not unhappy; we have learned to find contentment in simple pleasures and in the affection we bear one another.”

His words were like a dagger in my heart. “You are a happy man,” I said, my voice thick with unshed tears. “You have a family to love and who love you in return. But I, I am an unfortunate and deserted creature. I look around and I have no relation or friend upon earth. These gentle words of yours are the first I have ever heard from a human voice that were not curses or expressions of terror.”

“Your words touch me deeply,” said the old man with kindness. “If you are so unhappy, it is your duty to seek companionship and not to despair. There are kind hearts in the world who will not be turned away by misfortune.”

“Alas,” I cried, “my misfortunes are of a peculiar and terrible nature. I am an outcast, a wretch whom all men disown. I have a friend, a family that I love with an ardour you cannot imagine, but they are prejudiced against me. They have never seen me, but they have been taught to loathe the very idea of my existence. If they were to see me, they would spurn me and drive me away.”

“Do not say so,” replied De Lacey. “The hearts of men, when unprejudiced by any obvious self interest, are full of brotherly love and charity. Rely, therefore, on your hopes; and if your friends are good and amiable, do not despair.”

“They are the most excellent creatures in the world,” I exclaimed, “but, alas, my appearance is hideous and deformed. It is this which they cannot bear.”

“If you will unreservedly confide in me,” said the old man, “it is possible that I may be of service to you. I am blind, and cannot judge of your countenance, but there is something in your words which persuades me that you are sincere. Where do these friends reside?”

“Near this very spot,” I replied.

A terrible suspense held me. I was on the verge of revealing myself. My whole future, my very reason for being, hung upon the next few moments. I could hear the sound of footsteps approaching the cottage. It was Felix, Agatha, and Safie returning. “Now is the time!” I cried. “Save me! Protect me! You and your family are the friends whom I seek. Do not desert me in this hour of trial!”

I threw myself at the feet of the old man and clung to his knees. “Great God!” he exclaimed. “Who are you?”

At that instant, the cottage door was thrown open, and Felix, Safie, and Agatha entered. What a scene of horror ensued! Agatha, seeing a monstrous form grasping her father, fainted. Safie, unable to speak, rushed out of the cottage in terror. Felix darted forward, and with supernatural force, tore me from his father’s grasp. He struck me violently with a heavy stick, and I, maddened by pain and the bitterest disappointment, could have torn him limb from limb. But my heart sank within me as with bitter sickness, and I refrained. I saw him raise the stick to strike again, and with a howl of despair and agony, I bounded from the cottage and fled, faster than any human eye could follow, to the sanctuary of my hovel.

My rage was without bounds; it was a tempest, a hurricane, that threatened to overwhelm my reason. I cursed my creator, who had sent me into the world a detestable wretch, and I cursed myself for my foolish hope. For a time, I lay on the cold earth, groaning and gnashing my teeth. But as the first paroxysm of my fury subsided, a deep and soul-chilling despair took its place. All was over. I had been spurned by the very beings I had loved and served. My last, my only hope for human companionship was extinguished for ever.

That night, I did not watch the cottage. I remained in my hovel, a prey to the most miserable reflections. The next morning, I saw Felix in conversation with another man, a stranger. I watched as Felix, with an agitated and sorrowful air, paid the man some money. Soon after, a waggon arrived, and I saw my protectors loading their few possessions onto it. They were leaving. They were abandoning the cottage where they had found a brief and fragile peace, driven away by the horror of my presence. I watched them until they were out of sight, and then a cry of utter desolation broke from my lips. I was alone again, more utterly alone than ever before, for now even the illusion of their companionship was gone.

A kind of madness seized me. I declared war against the whole of mankind, and, above all, against him who had formed me and sent me forth to this insupportable misery. I returned to the cottage, now silent and empty. I looked at the little garden which I had so often tended, at the hearth which had been the centre of their domestic happiness, and my heart was filled with a bitterness that was almost sweet in its intensity. With a lighted torch, I set fire to the building. I danced and howled with delight as the flames leaped up, consuming the last vestiges of my hope. The wind fanned the blaze, and soon the cottage, the trees, and the outhouses were one roaring conflagration. As the dawn broke, I turned my back on the smoking ruins and directed my course towards Geneva, my heart swelling with a new and terrible purpose: revenge.

You were my creator; you were my target. I swore an oath of eternal hatred and vengeance to all mankind, but my deadliest venom I reserved for you. I travelled only by night, subsisting on berries and roots, my body inured to hardship but my soul a cauldron of seething rage. The journey was long, and my thoughts were my only companions. I brooded over the injustice of my fate, over the love I had felt and the hatred I had received. I replayed the scene in the cottage a thousand times, and each time my fury grew. I remembered the words I had learned from Volney’s book, of the vices and virtues of man, and I laughed aloud in the silent woods. Virtue? It was a mask for hypocrisy. Love? It was a privilege for the beautiful. I was a monster, and I would play the part.

It was in the environs of Geneva that my dark destiny began to unfold. One evening, as I was lurking in the woods near the town, I saw a beautiful child, a boy of about five years, playing by himself. He had a cherubic countenance and flaxen hair, and an air of innocence that might have moved any heart but mine. But my heart was stone. I thought, “This boy is one of them, one of the race that has spurned me. He is beautiful, and I am hideous. He is loved, and I am loathed.” A diabolical thought entered my mind. “This little creature,” I mused, “is too young to have learned the prejudices of his elders. He will not be horrified by my appearance. He can be my companion, my friend. I will seize him and educate him as my own.”

Filled with this new and desperate hope, I darted from my hiding place and seized the child. But as he looked up and saw my face, he shrieked in terror and covered his eyes with his hands. “Let me go!” he cried. “Hideous monster! Let me go! My papa is a Syndic, he is M. Frankenstein, he will punish you!”

Frankenstein! The name was a thunderclap. My long sought, my hated creator! This boy was your relation, your brother! A fury such as I had never before experienced took possession of me. “Frankenstein!” I exclaimed. “You belong then to my enemy, to him towards whom I have sworn eternal revenge; you shall be my first victim.” The child struggled and cried, but my grasp was iron. I placed my hands on his throat, and in a moment, he was silent and still.

I gazed upon my work, and a hellish triumph filled my soul. I had struck a blow against my enemy. I looked at the lifeless form, and felt a surge of power. I was no longer a helpless, weeping wretch; I was a being of might and terror. As I stood over the body, I noticed a miniature portrait hanging from his neck. I took it. It was a picture of a lovely woman, your mother I later learned, but at the time I believed it to be of Caroline Beaufort. I gazed upon the beautiful features, and a fresh wave of rage and envy swept over me. “This is the creature men adore,” I thought, “while I am a thing to be shunned and hated.” I would have destroyed the portrait, but a new and more fiendish idea occurred to me. I remembered seeing a young woman sleeping in a barn a few nights before. I had been moved by her beauty and had retreated, unwilling to disturb her. Now, I saw a way to turn her innocence into an instrument of my revenge. I crept to the barn and found her still sleeping. I placed the miniature in the folds of her dress and then fled, my heart exulting in its own wickedness.

I had tasted blood, and I found that it did not sicken me; it exhilarated me. This was the true nature of my being, the destiny you had crafted for me. For months I wandered, a terror in the night, my existence a blight upon the world. But my revenge was incomplete. The death of your brother and the unjust execution of an innocent girl were but a prelude. I needed more. I needed to confront you, to make you feel the full weight of your creation. I followed you, a shadow at your heels, on your journey of despair. I saw you in the valley of Chamounix, your face a mask of grief and guilt. And there, on that sea of ice, I revealed myself to you. I told you my story, I laid bare my soul, I showed you the being you had so carelessly cast into the world. And I made my demand: a companion, a female as monstrous as myself, with whom I could share my exile.

You recoiled in horror, but I held you to your purpose. I threatened you, I reasoned with you, I appealed to the last vestiges of justice in your heart. And you, in your weakness and your fear, you agreed. You gave me your solemn promise to create for me an Eve, a partner in my desolation. It was on the strength of this promise that I departed, my heart filled with a new and desperate hope. And so I came to these mountains, to wait.

The first months of my vigil were filled with a feverish anticipation. I believed in your promise, not because I trusted your honour, but because I knew the power of the threat I held over you. The image of Elizabeth, her life hanging in the balance, was my guarantee. I watched the seasons turn, the snows of winter giving way to the tender green of spring, and the vibrant life of summer. Each sunrise was a promise, each sunset a disappointment. My impatience grew into a gnawing anxiety. You were in Geneva, surrounded by the love and comfort of your family, while I languished in my desolate solitude. Did you think of me? Did you labour at your task? Or did you, in your selfish ease, forget your oath?

The suspicion, once planted, grew like a poison vine, choking the fragile flower of my hope. I could bear the uncertainty no longer. I descended from my mountain fastness, and under the cloak of night, I made my way towards your city. I moved with a stealth and silence that defied human senses, a spectre haunting the moonlit fields. I came to your family’s house at Belrive. I hid myself in the garden, among the thick foliage, and I watched. I saw the lights in the windows, I heard the faint strains of music, the murmur of conversation. I saw the venerable form of your father, his white hair a halo in the lamplight. I saw your younger brother, his face bright with youthful energy. And I saw her. I saw Elizabeth. Her countenance was a vision of celestial beauty, but it was clouded with a sorrow that I knew was of my own making. She looked towards the lake, her eyes filled with an anxious melancholy, and I knew she was thinking of you, of the secret grief that was consuming you.

To see this scene of domestic affection, from which I was eternally barred, was a torment beyond description. Every smile, every gentle word I overheard, was a fresh wound to my soul. But I did not come for this. I came for you. For several nights, I watched the house in vain. You were not there. Had you already departed? Had you gone to some remote corner of the earth to fulfill your promise, or to escape it? My rage began to boil anew. I was on the point of committing some new and terrible act, of making my presence known in a way that would leave no doubt of my power, when I at last discovered your hiding place.

It was a small, isolated building on the shore of the lake, some distance from the main house. I had at first mistaken it for a boathouse or a fisherman’s hut. But one night, I saw a faint, flickering light within. I drew near, my heart pounding in my chest. I crept to a small, grimy window and looked in. And there you were. You were not in the bosom of your family; you were here, in this squalid workshop, surrounded by the ghastly implements of your trade. I saw the chemical apparatus, the dissecting tools, the half-finished limbs of some horrid creation lying on the table. And I saw your face. It was not the face of an eager creator, fired with the passion of discovery. It was the face of a man in hell. Your brow was furrowed with anguish, your eyes were hollow with sleepless nights, and your hand, as it moved among the vile materials, trembled with a revulsion that you could not conceal.

I watched you for hours, a silent and unseen witness to your torment. I saw you take up a piece of the new creation, a hand formed with exquisite, if unnatural, skill, and I saw you fling it down with a curse of loathing. You paced the floor like a caged animal, your hands clenched, your lips moving in a silent, agonised debate. You were not fulfilling your promise; you were fighting it. Every fibre of your being recoiled from the task. You hated the work, and by extension, you hated the being for whom you worked. You hated me.

This realization, which should have been obvious, struck me with the force of a physical blow. I had known your revulsion at my own birth, but I had hoped, in my foolishness, that time and pity might have tempered it. I had hoped that in creating my companion, you might feel some measure of a creator’s responsibility, if not affection. But what I saw in that hut was a hatred more profound, more absolute, than any I had yet witnessed. You were not building a solace for my sorrows; you were cobbling together a monument to your own disgust.

My brief flicker of hope died, and in its place, a cold, hard resolve settled in my heart. You would not cheat me. You would not delay. You would complete your work, not out of justice, but out of terror. And I would be the instrument of that terror.

I waited until the moon was obscured by clouds, until the night was at its blackest. The light in your laboratory was extinguished, but I knew you were still within, likely fallen into a fitful, nightmare-ridden slumber. I moved to the door. It was old and ill-fitting. With a slight pressure, the lock gave way, and I slipped inside, as silent as the grave. The stench of death and chemicals assailed my nostrils, the same odours that had presided over my own nativity. I stood in the darkness, my eyes accustomed to the gloom, and I could just make out your form, slumped in a chair by the cold hearth.

I did not touch you. I did not need to. I let the silence stretch, filling the small room with a palpable sense of dread. Then, I spoke, my voice a low whisper that seemed to emanate from the very shadows themselves.

“You are slow,” I said. “Very slow. Did you think I would not notice? Did you imagine I would wait for ever in my mountain tomb while you dallied and delayed?”

You started in your chair, a gasp of pure terror escaping your lips. You sprang to your feet, your eyes wide, searching the impenetrable darkness. “Who is there?” you cried, your voice a choked and trembling thing.

“You know who is there,” I continued, my voice gaining a harder edge. “I am your master. I am the shadow that walks behind you, the voice that whispers in your dreams. I have seen your work. I have seen your hesitation. I have seen the loathing in your eyes as you handle the flesh that is to be my companion. You find the task distasteful, do you? It is nothing to the disgust I feel for a world that has only ever shown me its hatred.”

You shrank back, stumbling against your workbench, a clatter of glass breaking the stillness. “Wretch!” you stammered. “Demon! How did you find me?”

“I am bound to you,” I hissed, taking a single, heavy step forward, my form still lost in the gloom. “Where you go, I can follow. There is no corner of the earth so remote that you can hide from me. I am here to remind you of your oath. My happiness, the only happiness I may ever know, rests in your trembling hands. But do not think it is my happiness alone that is at stake. Remember our bargain. Remember the sea of ice. Remember Elizabeth.”

At her name, a low moan broke from you. It was a sound of such exquisite agony that it was music to my ears.

“Yes, remember her,” I whispered, my voice dropping to a tone of deadly intimacy. “Picture her. Her gentle smile. Her loving eyes. All that beauty, all that virtue, all that love which you possess and I am for ever denied. It all depends upon your diligence. Finish my bride. Fashion her with all the speed you can muster. For I will be watching. Every night, I will be near. Every hesitation, every delay, will be noted. And if you fail me, if you try to deceive me, I will leave you. I will go to Geneva. And I will not be as gentle with your beloved as I was with your brother. I will be with you on your wedding night. Fulfill your promise, and I will take my Eve and vanish into the vast wilds of South America, and you shall never see me again. Break it, and I will turn your world into a charnel house.”

I gave you no time to reply. As silently as I had entered, I slipped back out into the night, leaving you alone with your terror and my words echoing in the suffocating darkness of your workshop. I returned to the mountains, not with hope, but with the grim certainty of a hangman

who awaits the dawn. My part in the grim theatre was concluded; I had spoken my lines and laid down the terms of my continued existence, or, failing that, of your and my mutual and absolute destruction. Now I had only to wait for the final act, which you, my creator, my slave, were compelled to write.

I retreated to the highest and most desolate crags of the island, a place where the sea-birds themselves seemed to fear to tread. There, in a shallow cave carved by the ceaseless battery of the wind and waves, I made my new and miserable abode. The salt spray was my constant companion, coating the rock with a perpetual dampness and chilling me to the bone, a sensation I had grown almost to welcome, for it reminded me that I was a creature of the raw and unforgiving elements, not of the warm and gentle hearths from which I was for ever excluded. Here I sat, day after day, my gaze fixed upon the dreary expanse of the northern sea, my thoughts a tempest more furious than any that churned the grey waters below.

My mind, once a vessel for new and wondrous knowledge, was now a prison of obsessive thought. My every waking moment was consumed by the image of my promised companion, the Eve who was to be fashioned for this miserable Adam. When I had listened to the story of the first man, I had felt a strange kinship; yet he was created perfect, in the image of a benevolent God, and given a paradise to inhabit. I, a vile mockery of that divine creation, was formed in a workshop of filth and horror, and cast out into a world that was for me a veritable hell. But now, now I was to have my Eve! And in my solitude, I began to dream of her.

I pictured her not as a mere creature of flesh, but as the repository of all my hopes. In my imaginings, she did not possess the frail sensibilities of a human female; she would not swoon at my appearance, nor shrink from my touch. Having been made of the same coarse elements as myself, she would understand my nature. Her eyes, I dreamed, would not reflect the horror and disgust I saw in every other face, but a placid recognition, a deep and silent communion of shared being. We would not need the intricate and often deceptive language of men to understand one another; a glance, a gesture, the mere fact of our shared existence in a world that abhorred us, would be a bond more powerful than any vow or promise.

Oh, what sanguine dreams I wove in the darkness of my sea-girt cavern! I would not be her master, as Felix had been to Safie, condescending to bestow knowledge upon a grateful inferior. No, we would be equals in our exile. I would lead her to the vast and untrodden wilds of South America, a continent whose existence I had learned of with such wonder. There, far from the judgmental eyes of humankind, we would make our own Eden. I would build a shelter for her with my own powerful hands. I would find the sweetest fruits and the most wholesome roots for her sustenance. I would be her protector, her companion, her only friend. And she, in turn, would be mine. The sound of her voice, even if it were as strange and uncouth as my own, would be the sweetest music. Her presence would fill the void that echoed in my soul, a void so vast and so profound that I often felt it might swallow the very world. In her, my reflection would not be a monster’s, but simply a male of our species. I would no longer be a singularity, a hideous anomaly of nature; I would be one of a pair.

But as the days bled one into the other, a chilling and venomous doubt began to creep into my heart, like a serpent slithering into a garden. I had commanded you to make her, but I had dictated only the form, the physical substance. What of the spirit? What of the consciousness that you would ignite within that frame? You, my creator, detested me. You looked upon your work with an abhorrence that was plain in your every gesture. Was it not possible, was it not probable, that you would instill in her that same hatred? That you would fashion her with a soul that would despise me on sight?

The thought was an agony more potent than any I had yet endured. What if, upon the moment of her awakening, she should open her eyes, look upon my face, and scream? What if she, my last and only hope for solace, should turn from me with the same loathing as all the others? The idea was a torment that drove me to the brink of madness. To have my one chance at happiness, the very being for whom I had bartered away my vengeance, reject me would be a cruelty so exquisite that it could only have been devised by a fiend. And in that moment, I knew you, my creator, were capable of just such a refinement of torture. My desire for her was so immense that its potential failure became a terror of equal proportion. I had built a paradise in my mind, and now I saw that its gates might be guarded by an angel of despair, her face a mirror of my own deformity, her voice a chorus of every insult I had ever borne.

This new fear became an unbearable goad. I could no longer remain passive in my distant cave, a mere spectator to my own fate. My promise to remain absent while you worked was forgotten, swept away by a tide of desperate anxiety. I had to see. I had to know. And so, under the cloak of the darkest nights, when the moon was a prisoner of the clouds and the wind a moaning accomplice, I began to steal down from my crag. I became a shade, a whisper of movement among the rocks and heather, my prodigious agility allowing me to traverse the broken landscape with a silence and speed that would have defied any human pursuit.

My destination was the miserable hovel you had chosen for your laboratory. I would approach it with the caution of a wolf stalking a sheepfold, my senses straining in the darkness. I would creep to the very window, a single, uncurtained pane that overlooked your workbench, and there I would watch, my form concealed by the impenetrable blackness of the Scottish night.

The first time I looked in upon you, I saw you at your vile task. The room was lit by a single, guttering candle, which threw long, dancing shadows that made the scene appear even more grotesque. And you, my creator, were a sight to behold. Your face was gaunt and pale, your hair lank and wild, your eyes burning with a feverish light that was not of passion but of madness and despair. You handled the component parts of my future bride with a revulsion that was clear even in the flickering light. You would pick up a piece of flesh, hold it for a moment as if it were a venomous snake, and then, with a shudder that convulsed your entire frame, you would set about stitching it to the rest.

On the table before you lay her form, partially assembled. It was a sight that filled me with a dizzying mixture of hope and horror. There lay the vessel of my future life, the answer to all my agonies, and yet it was a thing of desecrated graves and butcher’s work, a hideous parody of the human form divine. I saw the delicate curve of a limb, the pale gleam of skin, and I felt a surge of possessive longing so fierce it nearly drove me to smash the window and claim her then and there. But I also saw the crude seams, the discoloured flesh, and I was reminded of my own monstrous birth. Did you look upon me with such loathing as you pieced me together? Did my own unformed features inspire in you such paroxysms of disgust?

I saw you attach the limbs, shape the torso, and finally, begin the terrible and intricate work upon her head. Your progress was agonisingly slow. There were nights when you would do nothing but stare at your creation, your face a mask of such misery that even I, who had every reason to delight in your suffering, felt a flicker of something akin to pity. Then you would spring up and pace the room, running your hands through your hair and muttering to yourself like a madman. Other times, you would work with a frantic, desperate energy, as if you wished only to be done with the task, to expel this new horror from your life as you had once expelled me.

My impatience grew with every passing night. Each delay was a new turn of the screw in my own torment. I began to watch the local fishermen as they went about their simple lives, a habit I had formed at the cottage of the De Laceys. I saw them mending their nets upon the shore, their faces weathered by the sun and sea. I saw their wives calling to them from the doorways of their stone cottages, and I heard the laughter of their children playing amongst the rocks. I saw a father lift his small son onto his shoulders, the child’s gleeful shrieks echoing in the clean air. And every one of these simple, common acts of human affection was a dagger in my heart. It was a world of connection, of family, of love, from which I was utterly and eternally shut out. These sights inflamed my desire for my companion, transforming it from a longing into a ravenous, all consuming need. She was not a mere wish; she was my only means of survival, my only shield against a world that would crush me.

One night, a great storm rose up from the sea. The wind howled like a legion of damned souls, and the waves, grown to monstrous size, crashed against the cliffs with the sound of cannon fire. Rain and hail lashed down, and the sky was rent by jagged spears of lightning. I felt a wild exultation in the heart of this tempest. Here was a power that matched the turmoil in my own breast! I stood upon the highest point of my cliff, unprotected, and roared back at the howling gale, my voice lost in the elemental chaos. The storm was my brother; its destructive fury was my own.

But even in my wild communion with the storm, my anxiety for your work possessed me. I feared the flimsy roof of your hovel might be torn away, that the sea might rise and inundate your workshop, destroying the fruit of your labour, the vessel of my hope. Driven by this new terror, I made my way through the teeth of the gale, the wind threatening to tear me from the rock face. I reached the window and peered in. You were not working. You were standing by the window, staring out into the maelstrom, your face a pale spectre in the intermittent flashes of lightning. For a moment, our eyes seemed to meet across the tempestuous darkness. I saw in your face not fear of the storm, but a profound, internal horror, as if you saw in the warring elements a reflection of the monstrousness you had unleashed upon the world. A grim smile touched my lips. You could not escape me, not even in the heart of a hurricane. I was the storm that would never break for you.

When the tempest finally subsided, a strange and unnerving calm fell upon the island. The sea, though still swollen, ceased its violent assault, and the moon, a pale and watery disc, appeared through the tattered clouds. I returned to your window, my heart pounding with a mixture of dread and anticipation. And I saw that she was finished.

There she lay upon the table, her form complete, her features settled into a semblance of life. Her hair was long and black, like that of the Arabian, but her skin had a pallor that spoke of the grave. She was beautiful, yet it was the awful beauty of a marble statue, cold and lifeless. All that remained was for you to infuse the spark of being into this perfectly formed clay, to animate this body and give me my Eve. My entire existence was balanced upon that single moment. The waiting was over. The time for your final act of creation, or my final act of destruction, was at hand. I watched you approach the table, your instruments in hand, and I held my breath, a silent, monstrous god awaiting the birth of his world.

I was, in my own eyes, a monster, and I longed to be known and loved by these gentle creatures; I yearned for a word of sympathy, a look of kindness, from one single being. These thoughts were my constant companions, my solace and my torment. One night, during my accustomed ramble, my spirits were oppressed by a sensation of utter desolation. Suddenly, the door was thrown open, and a female figure appeared. It was Safie. Her face was pale, and her dark eyes were filled with tears, but a smile of ineffable joy played upon her lips. She threw herself at the feet of the old man, and in a transport of delight, kissed his hands. Felix rushed towards her, and clasped her in his arms. Agatha, with a cry of joy, embraced her friend. I stood at the window, a silent and unseen spectator of this moving scene. My heart swelled with a strange mixture of pleasure and pain. I rejoiced in the happiness of my friends, but I felt, with a poignancy that I had never before experienced, the bitterness of my own solitary condition. “They have each other,” I cried, “but I am alone!” The tears streamed from my eyes, and I rushed away from the cottage, my soul filled with a sense of unutterable despair.

I passed the night in the woods, my mind a chaos of conflicting emotions. When morning dawned, I returned to my hovel, my heart heavy with a grief that seemed too great to be borne. The sun rose, and the birds sang, but their cheerful notes grated upon my ear. All nature seemed to rejoice, but I was an outcast, a thing of horror and detestation. My past life appeared to me as a dream, a frightful vision from which I had just awakened to a reality of misery. I thought of my creator, and a feeling of bitter indignation rose in my breast. “Why,” I exclaimed, “did you form a being so hideous that even you turned from me in disgust? God, in pity, made man beautiful and alluring, after his own image; but my form is a filthy type of yours, more horrid even from the very resemblance. Satan had his companions, fellow devils, to admire and encourage him, but I am solitary and abhorred.”

These were wild and vengeful thoughts, but I could not repress them. My creator had abandoned me; what hope could I have from his creatures, who were my equals in nature, but my superiors in rank and fortune? I had learned, from the history of the cottagers, that wealth and good descent were the possessions most esteemed by mankind. I had neither. I was a poor, helpless, miserable wretch; I was an abortion, to be spurned at, and kicked, and trampled on.

Even the ensuing days, which should have been filled with a reflection of the cottagers' newfound joy, only served to deepen my wretchedness. I saw their happiness, a perfect and self contained circle of affection from which I was eternally excluded. I watched as Felix and Safie walked hand in hand among the emerging flowers of spring; I heard their mingled laughter, a sound that was music to them but a dagger to my heart. He would point out the delicate workings of nature, the budding leaves and the industrious insects, and she would listen with an enraptured countenance, her eyes fixed on his as if he were the sole author of all that was beautiful in the world. At night, when they gathered around the hearth, their conversation was animated and gay. Safie, with her imperfect but charming accents, would relate the story of her journey, her trials, and her unwavering devotion to Felix; and he, in turn, would gaze upon her with an expression of such profound love and admiration that it seemed to light the humble cottage with a celestial radiance.

Agatha and the old man shared in this felicity. The lines of care on Agatha's brow were smoothed away, replaced by a gentle serenity. She moved about her tasks with a lighter step, often pausing to cast a fond glance at the two lovers, her heart overflowing with sympathy for their happiness. De Lacey himself, though blind, appeared to see their joy with an inner eye. He would sit for hours, a contented smile upon his lips, listening to the murmur of their voices, his spirit calmed and gratified by the restoration of his son's peace of mind.

And where was I in this picture of domestic bliss? I was the shadow that lurked without, the unseen blot upon their perfect world. Every smile they shared, every tender word they spoke, was a fresh reminder of my own grotesque solitude. My education, which I had pursued with such ardour, now seemed a curse. For what purpose had I learned the science of letters and the complexities of human society? It was only to understand more fully the depth of my own degradation. I had learned of love, only to know that I could never be loved. I had learned of companionship, only to feel the crushing weight of my own isolation. I had learned of beauty, only to be made excruciatingly aware of my own monstrous deformity.

My reflections became darker and more desperate. I began to contemplate the nature of my existence with a kind of morbid philosophy. Was I not, in truth, more human than those who reviled me? I felt the pangs of loneliness, the desire for sympathy, the capacity for love, with an intensity that, I believed, few of them could comprehend. My mind was capable of lofty thoughts; my heart was susceptible to the most refined emotions. Yet because my form was hideous, I was condemned to be a thing of scorn and terror. I saw that the world was governed by a cruel and arbitrary justice, a justice of the eye. A man might be a villain in his soul, a monster of selfishness and cruelty, but if his countenance was fair, he would be received with smiles and approbation. But I, whose only crime was my appearance, an appearance for which I was not responsible, was to be hunted from the light of day, an object of universal abhorrence.

A new and terrible thought began to take root in my mind. If I could not inspire love, I would inspire fear. If I could not have the sympathy of one living soul, I would have their curses and their terror. My creator had endowed me with a strength and an agility far superior to that of ordinary men. He had made me capable of enduring the extremes of climate, of subsisting upon the coarsest fare. He had, in effect, forged me as a weapon, and then cast me aside. Perhaps it was now time for that weapon to be used.

This idea, once it had taken possession of my mind, grew with a frightful rapidity. It was a dark and poisonous weed, choking out the last vestiges of my former gentle disposition. The image of my creator, which had once been a source of awe and a vague, filial yearning, now became an object of intense and burning hatred. He was the author of my being, and therefore the author of my misery. It was to him that I owed this unbearable solitude, this life of torment and despair. My rage, which had hitherto been a form of impotent grief, now began to coalesce into a definite and terrible purpose. I would find him. I would track him to the ends of the earth. And when I found him, I would force him to acknowledge his creation. I would demand of him the justice that was my due.

What was this justice? At first, the thought was vague, a formless desire for retribution. But as I watched Felix and Safie, a more specific and desperate plan began to form in my mind. I saw their companionship, the perfect sympathy that existed between them, and I thought, “He has his love, his companion. Why should I be denied mine?” If my creator could make me, he could make another like me. He could fashion a female, as hideous as myself, but who would not, for that reason, turn from me in disgust. She would be my companion, my solace, my entire world. With her, I would fly from the society of men. We would seek out the vast and uninhabited wilds of South America, a land I had learned of from Volney's book. There, far from the prying eyes and the cruel prejudices of mankind, we would live a life of simple, harmless contentment. I would no longer be alone. I would have one being who would not flee from me, one soul who would share my sorrows and my joys. The thought was intoxicating. It was a ray of light in the profound darkness of my despair. It was the one hope to which I could cling, the one purpose that could give meaning to my wretched existence.

With this new resolve, my demeanour changed. I was no longer a passive and mournful observer of the cottagers' happiness. I was a being with a plan, a purpose. I continued to watch them, but now my observations had a different object. I was studying the nature of the bond between man and woman, the subtle interplay of affection and dependence that I hoped to one day share with a being of my own. I watched as Felix would perform some small service for Safie, and how she would reward him with a smile of such gratitude and tenderness that it seemed to cheapen all the treasures of the earth. I listened to the soft modulations of their voices as they spoke to one another, the language of love that is universal and yet unique to every pair of lovers. I tried to imagine myself in Felix's place, to feel the pride and the joy of having such a being devote her life to me.

But these observations were not without their attendant pain. For every moment of hopeful anticipation, there were a hundred moments of agonising doubt. What if my creator refused my demand? What if he, in his revulsion, would not or could not perform this one act of justice? The thought threw me into a paroxysm of rage and despair. If he denied me this one, this only, solace, then I swore, by the eternal justice of heaven and the everlasting tortures of hell, that I would wreak such a vengeance as would make the world tremble. I would not be the only one to suffer. I would turn my hand against all that he held dear. I would make him curse the day he first conceived the impious idea of mocking his Creator by fashioning a living being from the dust of the charnel house.

My days were now a strange alternation between these two states of mind: a feverish hope that bordered on ecstasy, and a black despair that verged on madness. The beauty of the advancing spring, which had once been a source of such simple pleasure, now only served to heighten my emotional turmoil. The gentle warmth of the sun, the sweet scent of the flowers, the joyous song of the birds; all seemed to mock my unnatural condition. I was a discord in the universal harmony, a monstrous anomaly in the fair face of creation.

The time for my departure was drawing near. I had learned all that I could from my unwitting protectors. I could now speak and understand their language with fluency, and my knowledge of the world, though derived entirely from books and conversations overheard, was considerable. I had also, by a fortunate chance, discovered a valise containing some books and clothes in the nearby woods. The books, which included Plutarch's _Lives_, the _Sorrows of Werter_, and Milton's _Paradise Lost_, became my constant study. They opened up new worlds of thought and feeling to me, but they also served to increase my sense of alienation. I read of the noble deeds of the heroes of antiquity, of the passionate love and despair of Werter, of the sublime ambition and terrible fall of Satan. I sympathised with them all, but most of all with Satan. Like him, I had been created powerful and good, but had been cast out from the light. I too was an outcast, a fallen angel, but one who had committed no crime. My heart ached with a sense of cosmic injustice.

I resolved to make one final attempt to connect with the world of men before I set out on my quest. I would reveal myself to the old man, De Lacey. He was blind; he could not be prejudiced by my appearance. I would speak to him in the language I had learned. I would tell him my story, my sorrows, my hopes. He was benevolent and kind; surely he would listen with compassion. If he would accept me, if he would offer me his friendship and protection, then perhaps there was still hope. Perhaps I could find a place in the world without resorting to threats and violence.

I waited for an opportunity when the old man would be alone. It came one day when Felix, Agatha, and Safie went for a long walk in the woods. My heart beat so violently that I could scarcely stand. I approached the cottage, my limbs trembling. I knocked at the door.

“Who is there?” said the old man.

I summoned all my courage. “A friend,” I replied, “a traveller in need of rest.”

His voice was kind. “Come in,” he said. “I am blind, and cannot offer you much, but you are welcome to what I have.”

I entered, and sat down. For a few moments, I was unable to speak, so great was my emotion. The old man, perceiving my agitation, spoke again.

“You seem unhappy, my friend. Can I do anything to relieve your sorrow?”

His gentle words were like a balm to my wounded spirit. The tears started to my eyes. “I am an unfortunate and an outcast,” I said, my voice choked with sobs. “I have no friends, no family. I am utterly alone.”

“Do not despair,” he replied. “The world is wide, and there are many kind hearts in it. You are young; you have yet time to find happiness.”

“Alas,” I cried, “I am not as others are. There is a prejudice against me, an insurmountable barrier that separates me from my fellow men.”

“You speak in riddles,” said the old man. “But I will not press you for your secret. I am a poor and exiled man myself, and I can sympathise with misfortune. If you will confide in me, you will find me a true and sincere friend.”

My heart swelled with a joy that was almost painful. “You are good, you are kind!” I exclaimed. “If you knew all, you would not desert me!”

At that moment, I heard the sound of footsteps approaching the cottage. It was Felix, Agatha, and Safie, returning from their walk. A new and terrible fear seized me.

“Now is the time!” I cried. “Save me, protect me! You and your family are the friends whom I seek. Do not desert me in this moment of trial!”

The old man was bewildered. “What does this mean?” he asked.

But before I could reply, the door was thrown open, and Felix stood upon the threshold. His face, which had been smiling, was instantly transformed by an expression of horror and disgust. Agatha, who was behind him, fainted. Safie, with a shriek, fled from the cottage. Felix, with a cry of rage, rushed towards me and, with a strength I had not expected, tore me away from his father, and struck me to the ground with a heavy stick.

I could have torn him limb from limb. I could have crushed him as an elephant crushes a serpent. But my heart was filled with a bitterness and a grief that left no room for rage. I rose, and with a groan of despair, I fled from the cottage, and from the last, faint glimmer of hope that had remained to me in the world.

I fled, not from the stick which Felix had wielded with such unnatural fury, but from the hideousness of my own reflection in their eyes of terror. The forest received me into its dark embrace, its branches lashing at me as if they too were part of the universal conspiracy of hate. I ran without thought or direction, my mind a howling wasteland where once a garden of timid hopes had begun to bloom. A cry, more of a beast than of any being that knows language, tore itself from my throat, and I fell upon the damp earth, my face buried in the decaying leaves, and wept such tears as I had never known; they were not the sweet tears of sorrow and delight that the Arabian’s music had once drawn from me, but the bitter, scalding drops of absolute and eternal despair. For many hours I lay there, my limbs insensible to the cold, my mind lost in a vortex of misery. The moon, my once-kind companion, shone down through the canopy, but her light was no longer a gentle solace; it was a cold, accusatory glare that seemed to expose my loathsome form for all the universe to scorn. All the lessons of virtue, of kindness, of the gentle bonds of society which I had so eagerly absorbed, now returned to mock me. Was this the reward for my secret benevolence? For the firewood I gathered, for the paths I cleared, for the silent adoration I bestowed upon them from my wretched hovel? I had loved them; oh, I had loved them with a purity and a fervour that felt godlike in its intensity, and they had answered with revulsion and violence. The gentle countenance of the blind De Lacey, his voice promising friendship and solace, had been my last anchor to the world of men; but even that had been torn from me by the precipitous judgment of his son. I thought of Felix, whose face I had once studied as a model of manly beauty and feeling; now I could see only the contorted mask of hatred as he struck me to the ground. I remembered the swoon of Agatha, the shriek of Safie, and a new and terrible feeling began to curdle within me, displacing the grief that had held me prostrate. It was rage, a burning, sulphurous rage that filled my veins with fire and my mind with thoughts of such a black and ruinous nature that I myself was terrified by them. “I could have torn him limb from limb,” I muttered to the unhearing night, and the words tasted of a strange power. The desire for vengeance, a concept I had learned from Volney’s book as a vice of fallen empires, now presented itself to me as the only logical response to my condition. If I was to be a monster, then I would be a monster indeed. I would not be a harmless worm to be trodden upon, but a serpent whose fangs carried a venom proportionate to the injury received. My agony was infinite; so, then, should be my revenge. I passed the night in this tumultuous state, and with the first grey light of dawn, a cold and dreadful calm settled upon me. My decision was made. I would not flee this land; not yet. I must know the fate of my protectors, my persecutors. Some last, foolish remnant of my former self, some ghost of the hope I had so cherished, drew me back towards the cottage. I crept through the undergrowth with the stealth of a wild beast, my heart a cold stone in my breast. I took up my old position in my hovel, peering through the chink in the wall that had for so long been my window into paradise. Now, it was a peephole into hell. The cottage, once a scene of peaceful industry and gentle happiness, was in a state of utter disarray. I saw Agatha weeping in the arms of her brother, whose face was set in grim, pale lines. The old man, De Lacey, sat with his head bowed, an expression of profound sorrow and confusion on his features. Safie, the vibrant, joyous Arabian, was nowhere to be seen at first, but then I perceived her cowering in a corner, her face hidden, trembling violently. I listened, my whole being concentrated in the act of hearing. “An evil spirit!” Felix exclaimed, his voice shaking with a mixture of fear and rage. “It has haunted this place, and now it has shown its accursed form. We cannot remain here, father. We are no longer safe.” The old man raised his head. “But his voice, Felix, his voice was one of woe and misery, not of malice. He called me his friend, his protector. He was an unfortunate, an outcast. Perhaps we have done a great wrong.” “A wrong?” cried Felix. “Did you not hear the screams? Did you not see the terror of Agatha and Safie? Did you not feel the cottage shake with its monstrous presence? No, father. Your good heart deceives you. It was a fiend, a devil sent to destroy us. We must leave this place at once, before it returns to finish its work.” For a long time, the old man was silent, and I dared to hope he might argue, that his innate goodness would see through the prejudice of his son. But he was old and broken, and the united terror of his children was too much for him to withstand. He sighed deeply, a sound that seemed to carry the weight of all his misfortunes. “As you will, my son,” he said in a voice barely above a whisper. “We will go.” Those words were my death sentence. They confirmed my utter and complete separation from the human species. Even the best of them, the kindest, the most gentle, could not endure my presence. There was no hope. None. I watched for the remainder of that day and the next as they made their hurried preparations. I saw Felix go into the nearest town and return with a man to whom he spoke of settling his rent. I heard him tell this stranger that he and his family must quit the cottage immediately, as they had received an inheritance that required their presence in their native land. He lied to protect his pride, to hide the fact that they were fleeing in terror from a wretch like me. The lie was more painful than the blow from his stick; it codified my existence as a thing so shameful it could not even be spoken of. On the third morning, they departed. I watched them go, a small, sad procession. Agatha and Safie walked with their heads down, their forms shrouded in cloaks. Felix guided the horse upon which his blind father was seated. De Lacey’s face was turned back towards the cottage, as if he were trying to see what he had lost, and his sightless eyes seemed to look directly at my hiding place. For a moment, a wild impulse seized me to rush out, to fall at his feet, to beg for a word, a single word of pardon or understanding. But I remained rooted to the spot, a silent, unseen spectre of their ruin. I watched until their forms disappeared over the rise of a distant hill. Then, and only then, did I feel the full extent of my desolation. I was alone, more alone than a castaway on a deserted island, for he may yet hope for a ship to appear on the horizon. My world, the whole of my sentient existence, had been contained within the sphere of that humble cottage and its inhabitants; and now, it was empty. That night, a storm broke over the mountains, and its fury was a pale imitation of the tempest in my soul. The wind howled through the trees like a chorus of damned spirits, and the rain fell in torrents, but I felt nothing. A cold, burning purpose had taken root in my heart. When the last of the light had faded from the sky, I crept from my helpless shelter and approached the cottage. It stood dark and silent, a tombstone marking the grave of my happiness. I looked upon the little garden that I had so carefully tended in secret, where the flowers now drooped, beaten by the rain. I had helped to create this small patch of beauty, and for what? I tore the plants from the earth, shredding them with my hands and trampling them into the mud. I laid waste to every tender shoot, every blossoming flower, until the ground was a churned and barren ruin. My rage was not sated. I broke down the door of the cottage, the same door through which I had entered with such trembling hope only a few days before. The interior was dark and hollow. The humble furniture, the books, the guitar, all were gone. Nothing remained but the cold hearth and the lingering scent of the lives that had fled from me. An insane fury seized me. I gathered the driest wood I could find, heaping it in the centre of the room, and with the aid of my flint and steel, I set it ablaze. The flames leaped up, catching the dry timber of the walls and ceiling. I stood within the growing inferno, my arms raised in a gesture of triumphant despair. “I will have my revenge!” I cried, my voice mingling with the roar of the fire. The flames cast a hellish light upon my features, and I felt a surge of exultation. Let this be a funeral pyre, not for me, but for the gentle, loving creature I had once been. That creature was dead, burned away with the last vestiges of my hope. As the roof began to collapse, I rushed out into the night and stood at a distance, watching the cottage be consumed. The fire was a beacon of my hatred, a signal to the heavens that a new power had been unleashed upon the world. As the flames licked the sky, I danced a wild and terrible dance of liberation. I was free from the shackles of love and hope; henceforth, my companions would be hatred and revenge. When the fire had died down to a bed of glowing embers, and the cottage was nothing but a blackened skeleton against the stormy sky, my frenzy subsided, leaving behind a cold and resolute calm. My work here was done. But where was I to go? What was I to do? The world was a vast and empty place, and I had no purpose but the vague and burning desire for vengeance. It was then that I remembered the coat I had taken from the laboratory on the night of my animation. In my flight, I had preserved it, and in the pocket of this coat I had found some papers which, in my ignorance, I had not yet been able to decipher. Now, with the knowledge of letters that I had gained from my unwitting protectors, I retrieved them from my hiding place. There was a journal, written in a small, neat hand. I sat amongst the ashes of my former world and began to read. The journal was that of my creator, Victor Frankenstein. With a mixture of horror and fascination, I read of the months of his labour, of his unhallowed obsession. He described his disgust with the very being he had so painstakingly assembled; he detailed his flight from the room, his abandonment of me, his creature, on the very night of my birth. He called me his demon, a wretch, a miserable monster. Every word was a fresh stab to my already wounded soul. Here was the truth of my origin. I was not born of love or of nature, but of a profane and arrogant ambition that had recoiled in horror from its own success. The pages documented his subsequent illness and his return to his family in a city called Geneva. A name! A destination! A focus for the burning hatred that consumed me! Here was the author of my miseries, the man who had cast me into a world that reviled me and then abandoned me to my fate. He, my creator, my father, owed me a duty which he had shamefully neglected. If I could not have the love of my fellow creatures, then I would demand justice from him who had given me life. I would seek him out. I would find this Victor Frankenstein, and he would hear my story. He would know the depths of the suffering he had inflicted. And he, he would remedy my solitude. He must. A wild thought, a new and desperate hope, took root in the wasteland of my mind. He had created me; could he not create another? A female, as hideous and deformed as myself, with whom I could share my exile, from whom I would not be met with scorn and loathing. Yes! This would be my demand. He would create a companion for me, and we would flee to the vast wildernesses of South America, far from the sight of man, to live out our lives in a solitude that would then be a comfort, not a curse. With this new purpose burning in my heart, I rose from the ashes. I looked toward the south, toward the distant mountains behind which lay Switzerland and my creator. I swore a solemn oath, not of blind revenge, but of a terrible and inexorable justice. “I will find you, Frankenstein!” I vowed to the cold stars. “I will traverse the ice of the north and the sands of the desert to reach you. I will be with you on your wedding night! You have made me a fiend. I will act the part. But first, you will hear my tale, and you will do your duty by me. You will grant me my Eve, or I will work such destruction that the very memory of your name, your family, and your happiness will be erased from the face of the earth!” My journey began. I travelled only by night, hiding in forests and caves during the day, subsisting on berries and roots, and occasionally on the flesh of wild animals I caught and devoured raw. The change of seasons came and went. The warmth of summer gave way to the crisp air of autumn, and soon the world was covered in a blanket of snow. The cold, which had once been a minor inconvenience, was now a torment, but it could not cool the fire of the resolution that burned within me. My path was guided by the sun and stars and by the maps I had studied in the cottage. I learned to read the signs of men without approaching them, to discern the direction of towns and the passage of roads. The language I had learned with such joy I now used to curse the race of men as I overheard their distant conversations. Every sign of human happiness, a lighted window, the sound of laughter, the sight of a family gathered together, was a new source of torment, a new log on the fire of my hatred. I grew more savage in my habits, more monstrous in my feelings. The gentle creature who had wept at the beauty of Safie’s song was gone forever, replaced by a being of pure will and incandescent rage. And as I journeyed, my thoughts were ever on Geneva, and on the reckoning that was to come. I was no longer a blot upon the earth, a miserable accident. I was a scourge, a nemesis, and my creator would soon learn the terrible consequences of playing God.

learn, I directed my steps towards the south west, through the vast forests of Germany. I traversed the country of France, a land whose language was now my own, yet I felt a stranger there more profoundly than in any wilderness. Every village I skirted, every distant peal of a church bell, every lamp that glimmered in a cottage window, was a fresh wound to my soul, a testament to a world of fellowship and warmth from which I was eternally cast out. The gentle emotions that had once blossomed in my heart when I observed the love of the De Laceys were now inverted, transformed into a bitter and corroding acid. The sight of two lovers walking hand in hand would fill me with a paroxysm of rage; the sound of a mother singing to her child was a discordant shriek in the ears of my spirit. I was a solitary atom in the universe, a being whose existence was an error, and the happiness of others served only to illuminate the depth of my own desolation.

My sustenance was the wild bounty of the earth, but my appetite had grown more coarse. The berries and roots that once satisfied me now seemed a meager fare, and I found myself hunting the smaller animals of the forest with a ferocity that would have horrified my former self. I would tear them limb from limb and consume their flesh, my hunger a gnawing beast that mirrored the emptiness within. My physical powers, already great, seemed to increase with my misery. I could leap vast chasms, scale precipitous cliffs, and travel for many hours without rest, my body a machine of tireless despair. The elements themselves became my only companions; I exulted in the fury of the tempest, and the crashing thunder seemed a language I could understand, a voice that echoed the tumult in my own breast. In the freezing embrace of winter, I felt a grim satisfaction, for the world had become as cold and unforgiving as my own fate.

After many months of weary travel, whose exact duration I did not count, I at last crossed the Jura mountains and beheld, from a lofty eminence, the placid blue expanse of Lake Geneva. Below me lay the city, a jewel set between the water and the mountains. There, in that hive of humanity, lived my creator, my tormentor, my god. A tremor of mingled anticipation and dread passed through me. The hour of reckoning was at hand. I descended from the heights and took refuge in the woods and thickets that bordered the city, my heart a cold, heavy stone in my chest. For several days I lurked in the shadows, venturing closer to the outskirts under the cloak of night, gathering my resolve and seeking to learn the whereabouts of the Frankenstein family.

It was during one of these nocturnal explorations that fortune, or perhaps a more malevolent destiny, guided my steps. I had concealed myself in a copse of trees near a country lane when I overheard the conversation of two men returning from the city. They spoke of local affairs, of politics and commerce, matters which held no interest for me, until one uttered the name that was seared into my brain: Frankenstein. I pressed myself closer to the earth, my ears straining to catch every word. They spoke of the old Syndic, of his excellent reputation, and of his son, Victor, a young man of brilliant genius who had been absent for some years at the university of Ingolstadt but was expected to return soon. They mentioned also a younger son, a beautiful boy named William, the darling of the family. They pointed towards a handsome dwelling, whose lights shone through a grove of trees not a quarter of a league distant, and identified it as the Frankenstein estate.

Now I had my mark. A fiendish joy, cold and sharp as an icicle, pierced my gloom. I was no longer a lost wanderer; I was an avenging spirit with a destination. I retreated to my hiding place and began to watch the house, as I had once watched the cottage of the De Laceys. But oh, what a difference in the feelings which this surveillance inspired! Then, I had been an eager pupil, drinking in lessons of virtue and love. Now, I was a predator, my gaze filled with a malignant hatred as I observed the scenes of domestic bliss within. I saw the venerable, white haired father; I saw the lovely Elizabeth, for I soon learned her name, her face a portrait of angelic sweetness; and I saw the boy, William, his laughter echoing in the gardens, a sound that was like a knife in my heart. I saw the love that bound them together, a bond I could never share, and my resolution hardened into something terrible and adamantine. I would not merely confront my creator; I would first make him feel a fraction of the despair he had inflicted upon me. I would tear a hole in the fabric of his happiness, so that when I came to him with my demand, he would understand the depths of the misery from which it sprang.

One evening, as the shadows lengthened and the family was preparing to return indoors from their walk, I saw my opportunity. The boy William, in a spirit of playful mischief, ran ahead of the others, darting into a small wood where I lay concealed. My heart hammered against my ribs, a wild drumbeat of fate. This was the moment. My first impulse was not one of pure malice; a strange thought seized me, that I might take the child, not to harm him, but to keep him for my own. He was young, his mind not yet poisoned by prejudice. Could I not raise him as my companion, teach him to love me, and in his affection find a solace for my loneliness? I was a fool even to imagine it.

I stepped from behind the trunk of a great oak. The child stopped his play and looked up at me. For a moment he was merely curious, his bright eyes wide with surprise. Then, as the full hideousness of my form registered upon his senses, his face contorted in terror. A scream, shrill and piercing, tore from his throat. “Monster! Ugly wretch! Let me go!” he cried, for I had instinctively reached out a hand towards him.

“Boy, you must be quiet,” I said, my voice a hoarse growl that only frightened him more. “I will not hurt you, if you will only come with me.”

He struggled, his small fists beating against my leg. “Let me go! You are a monster! My papa is a Syndic; he is M. Frankenstein, he will punish you!”

Frankenstein! The name was a spark thrown into a powder magazine. My brain, my very soul, exploded in a cataclysm of rage. All my months of suffering, the cold, the hunger, the crushing loneliness, the universal scorn, all coalesced into a single, unbearable point of agony and were unleashed upon this small being who bore the name of my tormentor. A howl of fury, not human, escaped my lips. This was his creature, his darling, his joy. I would extinguish it. I closed my hands around his throat. He struggled for a moment, his eyes bulging with a final, horrified understanding, and then he was still.

I stood over the small, crumpled body, my breath coming in ragged gasps. The fury subsided, replaced by a cold, exultant horror. I had done it. I had struck my first blow. I felt a surge of power, a sense of dominion over the creator who had abandoned me. I looked at the lifeless form, and I did not weep; I felt a grim satisfaction. Now, he would know pain. Now, he would have a reason to listen to the wretch he had spawned. I noticed a miniature portrait hanging from a delicate chain around the boy’s neck. I took it. It was the picture of a beautiful woman, whose countenance radiated a gentle and maternal love. It was Caroline Frankenstein, his mother. I gazed at it, and a fresh wave of bitterness washed over me. Such a mother had smiled upon him, while I had been born into a world without a single look of kindness. I thrust the locket into the folds of my garment. It would be a useful tool.

My dark work was not yet complete. To kill was an act of brutal passion, but I had learned from Volney and the cottagers of the more subtle cruelties of man’s justice. I would not only bring sorrow to the Frankenstein family; I would bring discord and injustice, turning their own world against them. I fled the scene of my crime and sought shelter in the countryside. As dawn approached, I found a barn, and creeping inside, I buried myself in the straw to escape the hated light of day.

I must have slept, for I was awakened some time later by the sound of a door opening. I peered through a crack in the boards of the loft where I lay. A young woman entered. She was lovely, with a face of simple goodness, though it was now clouded with care and fatigue. She carried a lantern, and as she set it down, she knelt as if to pray, though no words came from her lips. I recognised her as a servant I had seen at the Frankenstein house. Her name, I later learned, was Justine.

As I watched her sleep, a thought of diabolical ingenuity entered my mind. She was an innocent. She was loved by the family. Here was a perfect instrument to compound their misery. They would lose not only their beloved William, but also this guiltless girl, and their grief would be poisoned by the agony of a wrongful conviction. I was a fiend, was I not? I would act the part to perfection. While she slept, I crept down from the loft, my movements as silent as the falling dust. With infinite care, I opened the bodice of her dress and placed the miniature portrait within its folds. Her sleep was deep and untroubled. The irony was exquisite; she slept the sleep of the innocent, while I, the true murderer, planted the seed of her destruction. She would awaken to a world of accusation and horror, a world much like the one I inhabited every day.

I slipped from the barn and fled back to the mountains, my task accomplished. I felt no remorse, only a cold and purposeful resolve. I had declared war on my creator and, through him, on all of mankind. These first acts were but a prelude, a declaration of my intent. Now, I would wait. I knew that the grief and horror of these events would drive Frankenstein from the solace of his family. He would seek solitude, as I did; he would be drawn to the untamed grandeur of the mountains, to a landscape that matched the desolation of his soul. And there, I would find him.

I made my new abode in a hut of ice on the summit of Montanvert. I was in my element, surrounded by the sublime and terrifying majesty of the Alps. The dazzling white of the glaciers, the stark black of the rock, the eternal silence broken only by the roar of the avalanche, this was a world fit for a monster. I watched the seasons turn again. I saw the sun rise and set over the jagged peaks, painting the snows in hues of blood and fire. I observed the storms gather and unleash their fury upon the valleys below. My rage cooled from a burning fire to a mass of glowing embers, a concentrated core of purpose. I had tasted power, and I had a demand to make. I rehearsed my speech, the tale of my sorrowful existence, the plea for a companion. He would not, he could not, refuse me now. He owed me this. Justice demanded it.

Days turned into weeks. I sustained myself on the sparse vegetation that clung to the rocks and the occasional chamois that strayed into my path. I was patient. I was as patient as the mountains themselves. And then, one day, I saw him. A lone figure, small and insignificant against the vastness of the glacier, was making his slow and arduous ascent. I knew it was he. My heart, a dead thing for so long, began to beat with a strange, fierce life. He was coming. My creator was coming to face his creation. I watched his approach, my form concealed behind a pinnacle of rock. He looked broken, his shoulders slumped, his face a mask of grief. Good. It was as I had intended. Let him suffer. Let him feel the despair that had been my only companion. When he had reached the summit, when he stood alone amidst the desolation, I would reveal myself. I would descend upon him not as a supplicant, but as a power, a destiny he could no longer escape. The time for hiding was over. The time for speaking had begun.

Shall I not then hate them who abhor me?” My words, which were wrung from the depths of my suffering, seemed only to inflame his fury. With a cry of incoherent rage, he rushed towards me. I saw the wild desperation in his eyes, the mad intent to grapple with me, to destroy me with his bare hands. It was a pitiable sight. I, with my superior strength and agility, could have torn him limb from limb with a single motion. Yet I did not. As he came at me, his hands outstretched like claws, I simply caught him by the arms. His struggles were like those of an infant in the grasp of a giant. He strained and cursed, his face contorted, but his efforts were utterly useless against my power. I held him fast, not with anger, but with a sorrowful and profound sense of the chasm that lay between us. “You are my creator, but I am your master; obey!” I said, my voice low and stern. He ceased his struggles, his chest heaving, his eyes still burning with a hate that could find no physical outlet. He was powerless, and he knew it. I released him, and he staggered back, a look of humiliation now mixed with his horror. “Listen to me, Frankenstein,” I continued, my calm returning. “You accuse me of murder, and it is true that my hands are stained with blood; but it is you who are the true author of these crimes. You cast me into the world, a creature of such sensibility and affection, and then abandoned me to scorn and hatred. My heart was made for love and sympathy; and when I was denied these, it was filled with despair and enraged to deeds of mischief and revenge. Hear my tale; it is long and strange, and the temperature of this place is not fitting to your fine sensations. Come with me to the hut upon the mountain. The sun is yet high in the heavens; before it descends, you shall have heard my story and can decide my fate. On your decision rests my own, for if I cannot inspire love, I will not cease to cause fear. If you refuse my conditions, I will continue my work of devastation until your heart is as desolate as mine.” I pointed towards the summit, where a small curl of smoke, invisible from the valley below, rose from the chimney of my rude dwelling. He followed my gesture with his eyes, and a shudder ran through his frame. He looked from the distant hut back to me, his mind evidently wrestling with a terrible conflict. He loathed me with every fibre of his being, yet my threat was not an idle one. He had seen the proof of my capacity for destruction in the death of his brother and the ruin of an innocent girl. Curiosity, too, must have played its part; how had I, the abandoned wretch, learned to speak and to reason with such eloquence? He was a man of science, and the desire to understand a phenomenon, even one he abhorred, was a powerful instinct. For a long time he was silent, his gaze fixed on the ground. The only sound was the sighing of the wind across the ice and the distant crack of a shifting serac. I waited with a patience that was as vast and as cold as the landscape around us. I knew that he had no choice. To refuse was to condemn his remaining loved ones to a life of perpetual terror, to a series of calamities from which he could not protect them. “For the first time,” he said at last, his voice hollow and strained, “I feel what the duties of a creator towards his creature are, and I ought to render him happy before I complain of his wickedness. I will consent to your demand. I will accompany you to your hut, and I will hear your story. But I make no promise. When I have heard all, I will make my determination. Until then, lead on. I will follow.” I inclined my head in acknowledgement, a gesture which felt strange and formal in that wild place. A flicker of hope, the first I had known in many months, ignited within my breast. It was a fragile flame in the midst of a vast darkness, yet it was enough. He would listen. He would understand. And perhaps, perhaps, he would consent. Without another word, I turned and began to ascend the glacier. My strides were long and swift, and I moved over the treacherous ice with an ease and confidence that was born of long familiarity. He followed behind me, his progress slow and laborious. He slipped often upon the glassy surface, and his breathing came in harsh, painful gasps. I did not offer him my hand; I did not look back. An impassable gulf of silence and mistrust separated us. We were two solitary beings, a creator and his creation, linked by a chain of misery and traversing a landscape that was the very emblem of our shared desolation. The ascent was long and arduous for him. We passed over immense crevasses, dark fissures that plunged into the blue-green heart of the glacier, their depths seeming to whisper of an eternal, frozen oblivion. We navigated fields of sharp ice-pinnacles, which rose from the surface like the teeth of some great, dead monster. The sun, though bright, offered no warmth, its light reflecting from the endless white with a painful, sterile glare. All the while, I was conscious of his eyes upon my back, burning with a hatred that was almost a physical force. He was studying me, his scientific mind perhaps cataloguing the unnatural strength of my limbs, the hideous deformity of my shape. What thoughts passed through his mind during that terrible journey? Did he feel a flicker of remorse? A pang of the responsibility he had so long denied? Or was his heart filled only with plans of vengeance, with schemes for my destruction once my tale was told? I could not know. We reached at last the small, level space near the summit where I had constructed my habitation. It was a miserable hut, built of stones and roofed with slabs of slate, with moss and mud crammed into the interstices to keep out the piercing wind. To a human being it would have seemed the most wretched of dwellings, but to me it was a palace. It was the only place on earth that was my own, the only shelter that did not reject me. I pushed aside the rough wooden plank that served as a door and entered. He hesitated on the threshold, as if unwilling to cross into a place so intimately associated with me. “Enter,” I said. “The fire is lit, and it will warm you.” He stooped and came inside, his eyes darting about the small, dark space. A fire of pine logs burned in a crude hearth, casting a flickering, ruddy light upon the rough stone walls. A bed of dried leaves and heather lay in one corner, and a simple wooden stool stood near the fire. This was the sum of my possessions. It was a scene of the utmost poverty and solitude. I motioned for him to sit on the stool, whilst I myself sat upon the floor. We remained for some minutes in silence. The fire crackled and hissed, and the wind howled about the corners of the hut like a tormented spirit. He had removed his hat, and in the firelight I could see the lines of care that were etched so deeply upon his brow. He looked older, wearier, and more broken than I could have imagined. “You have promised to hear my tale,” I began, breaking the heavy silence. “I ask for your attention for that time alone. I have suffered grievously, and the story of my woes will not be brief. It is with considerable difficulty that I remember the first moments of my being; all the events of that period appear confused and indistinct. A strange multiplicity of sensations seized me, and I saw, felt, heard, and smelt at the same time; and it was, indeed, a long time before I learned to distinguish between the operations of my various senses. By degrees, I remember, a stronger light pressed upon my nerves, so that I was obliged to shut my eyes. Darkness then came over me and troubled me, but hardly had I felt this when, by opening my eyes, as I now suppose, the light poured in upon me again. I walked and, I believe, descended, but I presently found a great alteration in my sensations. Before, dark and opaque bodies had surrounded me, impervious to my touch or sight; but I now found that I could wander on at liberty, with no obstacle which I could not either surmount or avoid. The light became more and more oppressive to me, and the heat wearing me as I walked, I sought a place where I could receive shade. This was the forest near Ingolstadt; and I slunk into it, and laid me down upon the damp grass, glad to find a screen from the intolerable light of day.”
