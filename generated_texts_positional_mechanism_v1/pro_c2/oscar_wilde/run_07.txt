He felt no remorse, only a terrible curiosity. He had started upon a new road, a dark and wonderful path, and he was eager to see where it would lead. The yellow book lay where he had left it. He picked it up and smiled. Lord Henry was a wonderful guide. He had given him the map to a new world. And somewhere, in the darkness at the top of the house, the price of the journey was already being paid.

A deep sense of foreboding filled him, a feeling that some dreadful chasm had opened up between them, a chasm which he would never be able to cross. But he said nothing. He walked over to the grand piano and, with heavy fingers, began to play a nocturne. The melancholy notes filled the room, a sad counterpoint to Dorian’s serene, untroubled beauty as he stood, lily in hand, a faint, secret smile upon his lips.

In the weeks that followed, Dorian Gray devoted himself to a new and fascinating study: the study of his own life, not as a participant, but as a spectator. Lord Henry’s philosophy, amplified and elaborated by the prose of the French decadent, became the guiding principle of his existence. He saw that the senses, and the senses alone, could cure the soul, just as the soul alone could cure the senses. He determined to have a life of new sensations, to allow no theory or creed to arrest the spiritual development of his nature.

He began by exploring the world of perfumes. He studied the strange effects of odours, the way that ambergris could stir the passions, and spikenard corrupt the imagination. He learned that violet had the power to evoke the memory of dead romances, and that musk could trouble the brain. He would spend whole days in his laboratory, distilling heavy oils and burning exotic gums from the East, seeking to create a new science of the senses, in which the mood of a man might be summoned or altered by the mere inhalation of a particular scent.

From perfumes he passed to music. He had a private box at the Opera, and he took a strange delight in the works of Wagner, with their monstrous passions and their depiction of a world of giants and gods. But he also found pleasure in the savage, primitive rhythms of travelling gypsy musicians, and in the plaintive, monotonous chants of Arab players he would bring back to his house. He collected the most curious instruments from all corners of the globe: the long clarions of the Mexican Indians, the hollow gourds of the Africans, the lacquered horns of Asia. He saw in their varied tones the whole history of human sorrow and human joy.

Jewels, too, possessed a unique fascination for him. He would spend hours with his collection, laying out the green fire of the emerald, the cold flame of the ruby, the shifting rainbow of the opal. He would tell the stories of the famous gems: the Moonstone that had been the eye of an Indian god, the Borgia pearl that was said to hold a subtle poison, the sapphire that was a cure for madness. In their soulless, eternal beauty, he found a sedative for his occasional moments of fear. They were things that could not be stained by sin or touched by time. They were as perfect as he was.

He filled his house with exquisite tapestries and priceless brocades. He became a connoisseur of ecclesiastical vestments, drawn by their symbolic richness and the heavy magnificence of their gold-thread and seed-pearl embroidery. It gave him a perverse pleasure to handle these sacred objects, to trail the cope of a priest or the chasuble of a bishop across his knees, knowing the terrible secret that was locked away in the room at the top of his house. It was a form of blasphemy, and blasphemy had a charm of its own.

Society, observing him, saw only a new and more brilliant dandy. They saw a young man of immense wealth and impeccable taste, who was attempting to recreate the glories of the Renaissance in modern London. His dinners were legendary, his concerts the talk of the season. Young men sought to imitate his dress, his mannerisms, his very way of speaking. He became a leader of fashion, an arbiter of elegance. No one suspected the dark duality of his life. No one saw the other side, the side that began to seek out stranger and more dangerous pleasures.

For as the months turned into years, the pursuit of beauty alone was not enough. The spectator grew weary of the play. He began to crave a more potent stimulus. He found himself drawn to the lowest quarters of London, to the sordid opium dens of the East End, where Chinese sailors and haggard wastrels lay stupefied on filthy bunks. In a shabby coat and a muffled scarf, he would slip out of his great house and make his way through the fog-wreathed alleys to these dens of iniquity. There, amidst the foul air and the groaning bodies, he felt a thrill of degradation. The contrast between his secret life and his public one was a source of endless delight. To be a paragon of virtue in the West End and a haunter of dives in the East gave his existence a wonderful, terrible complexity.

He began to surround himself with the most infamous and degraded of men. He was seen in the company of card-sharps, thieves, and forgers. His name began to be whispered in the clubs, at first with disbelief, and then with a sort of horrified certainty. Stories circulated about him, dark rumours of ruined lives and corrupted youths. Young men who had once been his friends were seen to turn pale and avoid him when he entered a room. The hostesses who had once fought for his presence now hesitated to send him an invitation. Yet his incredible beauty and his immense fortune still kept him within the bounds of society. People might whisper, but they did not dare to ostracize him completely. He was a fascinating monster, and London, for all its propriety, loves its monsters.

One cold November evening, as he was returning from some obscure and dreadful adventure, he passed Basil Hallward on the street. It was the first time they had met in almost two years. Dorian tried to walk on without acknowledgement, but the painter caught him by the arm.

“Dorian! I have been looking for you. I called at your house, but your servant said you were out.”

Basil looked older. His hair was flecked with grey, and his face was lined with a deep and settled sadness. Dorian felt a flicker of annoyance. He hated to be reminded of the passage of time.

“I am on my way to Paris, Basil,” Dorian said coldly, trying to pull his arm away. “I am catching the midnight train.”

“You must spare me a few minutes,” Basil insisted, his grip tightening. “The things they are saying about you are dreadful. I cannot believe them. But I must hear you deny them. Tell me they are not true, Dorian. Tell me you are still the boy I knew, the boy I painted.”

“The things they say are their own affair,” Dorian replied with a sneer. “I do not concern myself with the gossip of fools. And as for being the boy you painted, I hope I am not. I should be very sorry to have remained so undeveloped. Now, if you will excuse me…”

“I will not excuse you,” Basil said, his voice trembling with emotion. “I am going to your house with you, and I will not leave until you have answered me. “You have talked enough about corruption,” he said, his voice unnaturally calm. “Now you are going to see it.”

“Years ago, when I was a boy,” said Dorian, crushing his handkerchief in his hand, “you met me, flattered me, and taught me to be vain of my good looks. Basil stared at him in disbelief. “You are mad, Dorian. It is impossible.”

“Ah, but it is true,” Dorian said, a sigh of weariness escaping him. “It is the secret of my life. I have spared nothing. And the portrait has spared me nothing. Look at it. Your work, Basil! What do you think of it now?”

The painter stepped closer, peering at the canvas. He could see his own brushwork in it, his own style. It was a hideous parody of his art. “I cannot believe it,” he whispered. “But if this is true… if this vile thing is your soul… then you are more wicked than any man I have ever known. You must repent. You must pray for forgiveness. The thing can still be destroyed. We can save your soul.”

A sudden, uncontrollable wave of hatred for Basil Hallward swept over Dorian. He loathed the man’s moralizing, his conventional piety, his offer of salvation. It was Basil who had painted the picture. It was Basil who had started it all. The man was a witness. As long as Basil lived, his secret was not entirely his own. The sight of the loathsome face on the canvas, juxtaposed with the painter’s horrified expression, seemed to stir some dormant, bestial instinct within him.

He looked around the room. On top of an old carved chest lay a knife. He had seen it there before. It was a long, thin blade of foreign design. Without thinking, he seized it. He turned on Basil, his face a mask of fury.

“It is too late for prayers, Basil,” he snarled. He rushed at the painter and dug the knife into the vein that pulsed in his neck, again and again.

Basil Hallward cried out, a choked, gurgling sound, and stumbled back against the easel. He clawed at the air for a moment, then crashed to the floor. There was a dreadful, heavy sound, and then silence.

Dorian Gray stood over him, the knife still in his hand, breathing heavily. He watched the red blood spread slowly from beneath the body, staining the dusty floorboards. He felt no remorse, only a surge of curiosity. He felt as though he had been watching a play. He went over to the portrait and looked at it. There was a new change. A smear of red, like a glistening dew, had appeared on one of the hands. The hand that held the knife. He had murdered the painter, and the portrait had recorded the act. He had murdered his past, and he was free. He locked the door behind him, leaving the dead man and the terrible canvas together in the dark. The house was very quiet. It was time to go to Paris.

He went to his own bedroom and began to wash his hands. He watched the water swirl in the porcelain basin, and for a moment he fancied that it ran red, but it was only a trick of the light from a stray sunbeam piercing the damask curtains. His hands were quite clean. He held them up, slender, white, and aristocratic. It was impossible that they could have done such a thing. He felt a sense of complete unreality. It was as if he had been reading a gruesome story in some penny dreadful, and now the book was closed. Basil Hallward had left. That was all. He had gone away on a journey, perhaps back to Paris.

He changed his clothes, selecting a dark blue silk smoking jacket with braided frogs. He touched his hair with a little perfume from a silver-topped bottle. He looked at his reflection in the oval mirror. No, there was no change. The horror of the act had not marred his features. The portrait was doing its work. It was bearing the burden. A smile of triumph touched his lips, and he felt a curious thrill of pleasure. He had committed a sin that would have marked another man for life, and he was as fair as ever.

But the practical difficulties remained. The hat and coat. The body. He could not leave them up there forever. Sooner or later the house would have to be aired, servants would have to clean. The body would be found. And Basil would have been missed. People would ask questions. His name would be mentioned. The man’s ulster and bag were at the club. No, they were not; he remembered Basil saying he had sent them on to Victoria station. But what if he had not? What if the servants at the club remembered his leaving? He lit a cigarette, his hand trembling slightly for the first time. The details of life, its sordid, tedious details, were beginning to close in upon him.

He went back to the door and listened. Silence. The dead are terribly silent. He unlocked the door, and the faint, coppery tang of blood met him. It was a sickening smell. He took Basil’s hat and his heavy coat. He went downstairs to the library and threw them into the great wood fire. The felt of the hat shrivelled and stank; the thick wool of the coat smouldered for a while, filling the room with an acrid smoke, before it too caught alight and was consumed by the flames. He watched until they were nothing but a heap of black, feathery ash. That was one part of the problem solved. But the greater part remained.

He sat down and tried to think. To bury the body was impossible. To dismember it was a task too hideous to contemplate. He shuddered. His own finely-wrought sensibilities recoiled from the thought. It was work for a butcher, not for him. He needed help. But from whom? He ran through a list of his acquaintances. There were men who gambled, men who drank, men who lived scandalous lives of pleasure. But murder? That was a different matter entirely. That was a thing for the lower classes, a vulgar and clumsy affair. He felt a sudden, sharp contempt for what he had done, not for its sinfulness, but for its lack of style.

Then he remembered a name. Alan Campbell. He had not seen Alan for years, not since their strange friendship had come to its abrupt and dreadful end. Alan had been his most intimate friend, for a time. They had been inseparable. There was a period when they were always seen together, at the opera, in drawing rooms, in the little, discreet restaurants of Soho. Then, suddenly, it had all stopped. Alan had severed the connection completely. People had wondered why, but Dorian and Alan had kept their secret. It was a terrible secret, one that had shadowed Alan Campbell’s life ever since. He was a scientist, a brilliant young chemist, but the joy had gone out of his life. He found his solace not in art or in pleasure, but in the cold, hard facts of biology, in the immutable laws of matter. Dorian knew the power he held over him. Yes, Alan Campbell was the man. He was the only person who could save him. He had the knowledge, and Dorian had the means to compel him to use it.

He went over to his desk, a beautiful piece of Louis Quinze furniture, and wrote a note. He addressed it to Alan Campbell and sent his valet to deliver it with instructions to wait for an answer. As he waited, he felt a strange calm descend upon him. He was no longer the frightened boy; he was the master of a grim situation. He went to the piano and began to play a nocturne by Chopin. The music filled the quiet house, its sad, sweet melody a curious contrast to the silent horror locked away in the room upstairs.

An hour later, his servant returned with the news that Mr. Campbell was here. Dorian smiled. He told the servant to show him in.

Alan Campbell entered the room. He was a tall, pale man, with lank black hair and intense, brooding eyes. He looked older than his years, his face a mask of weary intelligence. He stood by the door, refusing to come any further into the room.

“You sent for me, Dorian,” he said, his voice cold and toneless. “I did not wish to come. You know that I never wish to see you again.”

“Ah, my dear Alan, that is so unfriendly of you,” said Dorian, rising from the piano stool and coming towards him with a charming smile. “I thought we had forgiven each other everything. The past is the past. And I am in trouble. I need your help.”

“I do not traffic in your kind of trouble,” Campbell answered, his lips a thin, hard line. “Your life and mine are utterly separate. What is it you want? Money? If so, write to my solicitors. I will not deal with you personally.”

“Money! How absurdly you talk, Alan. I have more money than I know what to do with. I want something far more precious than money. I want your genius. I want your knowledge.”

“My knowledge is not for sale.”

“It is not a question of a sale, Alan. It is a question of a gift. A favour, to an old friend.”

A look of disgust passed over Campbell’s face. “Friend? You dare to use that word? You know what you made of our friendship. You ruined my life.”

“I gave it a new direction,” said Dorian, lightly. “Every experience is of value. You devoted yourself to science. You have made a name for yourself. You should be grateful to me.”

“Grateful! My God! Dorian, what do you want? Say it and let me go. I cannot bear to be in the same room with you.”

Dorian’s expression hardened. The mask of pleasantry fell away, and something cruel and dangerous looked out of his eyes. “Very well, Alan. I shall be direct. There is a man upstairs. He is dead. He has been dead for about ten hours now.”

Campbell stared at him, his face turning even paler than before. “Dead? You are mad. What have I to do with that?”

“You have everything to do with it. This man is Basil Hallward. You remember him. He painted that portrait of me you used to admire so much.”

“Basil Hallward! Good heavens! Is he dead? What happened? A sudden illness?”

“I killed him,” said Dorian Gray. He spoke the words slowly, deliberately, watching their effect upon the man in front of him. Campbell recoiled as if he had been struck.

“You killed him! You are joking. It is some monstrous, hideous joke.”

“It is the truth. I killed him. And his body is in the locked room at the top of the house. I want you to destroy it. You are a chemist. You know how to do these things. You know how to get rid of it, to reduce it to its elements, so that not a trace of it remains. I have heard you talk of such things.”

Campbell sank into a chair, covering his face with his hands. “Destroy it! My God, Dorian, you don’t know what you are asking. It is murder you have done. Do you think I am going to be an accomplice to your crime? I will have nothing to do with it. I will not even listen to you. You are insane.”

“You must, Alan. There is no one else. If you do not help me, I am ruined. We shall both be ruined.”

“I will not do it,” Campbell cried. “I refuse. I will go to the police. It is my duty.”

“You will do no such thing.” Dorian walked over to his writing desk and took out a sheet of paper and a pen. He wrote something on the paper, folded it, and came back to where Campbell was sitting. “I am sorry you force me to this, Alan. I had hoped you would be more reasonable. But you were always so fond of high principles. You must choose. Either you do as I ask, or I send this letter. If I send this letter, you know what will happen.”

He held the folded paper out to him. Campbell looked at it as if it were a snake. He did not take it.

“I do not need to read it,” he whispered. “I know what is in it.”

“Then you know what you must do,” said Dorian, his voice like ice.

A long, shuddering sigh broke from Campbell’s lips. He seemed to collapse in on himself. All the strength, all the moral indignation, went out of him. He was a broken man. “And what if I do this for you?” he asked, his voice barely audible. “What becomes of me then?”

“What becomes of you is your own affair. I make no promises. But you will have saved yourself from a public disgrace that would be far worse than anything else. Now, are you going to help me?”

Campbell did not answer. He simply bowed his head in a gesture of utter defeat.

“That is better,” Dorian said coolly. “Now, what do you require? We must get to work at once.”

Campbell looked up, his eyes glazed with a dull horror. “I shall need things from my laboratory. I cannot go myself. You must send your servant.”

He took a pencil and a sheet of paper and wrote out a long list. It was a strange list of chemicals and apparatus, of acids and furnaces and metal clamps. Dorian read it over. “And you will be able to do it with these things?” he asked.

“Yes,” said Campbell. “I will be able to do it. Now, for God’s sake, get them. And do not let your servant see me. I will wait here.”

Dorian rang the bell and gave the list to his valet, with instructions to fetch a hansom and bring the items back as quickly as possible. Then he sat down and waited with his unwilling guest. Neither man spoke. The silence in the room was heavy, oppressive. Dorian could hear the ticking of the clock on the mantelpiece, and the frantic beating of his own heart. Alan Campbell sat motionless, his head in his hands, a figure of abject misery.

When the servant returned, the things were brought in. There were two large, heavy carboys, and a long, black wooden box filled with scientific instruments. Dorian dismissed the servant for the night.

“Now,” he said to Campbell, “I will take you upstairs.”

He picked up one of the carboys. It was immensely heavy. Campbell took the other, and together they carried them up the stairs. Dorian unlocked the schoolroom door and they went in. The body of Basil Hallward lay on the floor, a dark, huddled mass. A foul, sweetish odour hung in the air. Campbell gave a stifled cry and turned his face away.

“Leave me now,” he said, his voice trembling. “Do not come back until I call you. And do not speak to me.”

Dorian went out, locking the door behind him. He went downstairs and into the library. For a long time, he paced up and down the room, listening. He could hear a strange, rhythmic stamping sound from above, and then the sound of something heavy being dragged across the floor. He lit a cigarette and sat down with a volume of Gautier’s _Émaux et Camées_. He tried to read, but the words swam before his eyes. The house was filled with a new and dreadful silence.

After what seemed an eternity, he heard footsteps on the stairs. Alan Campbell entered the library. He was white as a ghost. His eyes were wide with a terror that Dorian had never seen in any human face.

“It is done,” he said. He swayed, and caught at the back of a chair to steady himself. “I have done what you asked.”

“Are you quite finished?” asked Dorian calmly.

“I am finished. There is nothing left of him. Nothing but what is in that carboy. And a heap of ash in the furnace.”

“You have done well, Alan,” said Dorian.

Campbell laughed, a dry, mirthless sound. “Done well? I have sealed my own damnation. Do not speak to me. I never want to see your face again. I never want to hear your voice again. We are finished.”

He walked to the door. On the threshold, he turned. “You have destroyed my life. I hope you are satisfied.” Then he was gone.

Dorian went upstairs. A hideous, almost unbearable smell of nitric acid filled the air. It was a smell that clung to the curtains and the furniture, a smell that seemed to have penetrated the very walls. But the room was empty. The thing that had been Basil Hallward had vanished. He went over to the portrait and pulled away the screen. There was no new mark upon it. The scarlet stain on the hand was no brighter. The murder of the man’s body had not been recorded. It was only the murder of the soul that the canvas registered.

He opened the window and breathed in the cool night air. The city hummed below him, a vast, indifferent beast. He felt a sudden surge of freedom. He was safe. The past was annihilated. He put the screen back in front of the portrait, locked the schoolroom door, and went to his room to dress for dinner. He had promised to go to Lady Narborough’s. He could not disappoint her. He had to be seen, to be witty, to be charming. He had a role to play, and he would play it to the end. The memory of the smell in the attic made him feel faint for a moment, but he brushed it aside. He would have to burn some scented pastilles when he got home. That was all. There was nothing more to be said.

He dressed with a meticulous care that bordered on the fanatical, each fold of his cravat a small victory against the chaos that threatened to overwhelm him. The choice of which pearl stud to wear, which ring to place upon his finger, became an act of profound importance, a ritual to restore order to a world that had tilted on its axis. He looked at his own reflection in the cheval-glass. The face that looked back was as serene and untroubled as ever, the mouth still curved with the innocent promise of joy, the eyes clear and deep. There was no trace of the day’s horror upon it. He was playing a part, and his face was the most exquisite of masks.

At Lady Narborough’s, the air was heavy with the scent of tuberoses and the murmur of discreet conversation. The light from a dozen shaded candelabra fell upon gleaming silver and the brilliant facets of jewels. It was a world of curated beauty, a world he had made his own, and as he entered, he felt a measure of his composure return.

“Ah, Dorian! We had quite given you up,” cried his hostess, a woman whose tragedies were her coiffure and whose triumphs were her chefs. “You are terribly late and terribly handsome, so we must forgive you. Harry has been insufferable, as usual, and we have needed you to distract us.”

Lord Henry was lounging in a gilt armchair, a green carnation in his buttonhole, observing the scene with the lazy amusement of a Roman emperor at a particularly tedious set of games. “My dear Dorian,” he said, his voice a low, musical drawl, “punctuality is the thief of time, and you are far too wealthy in hours to be robbed. Where have you been? Reforming a sinner? Or creating one? I trust it is the latter. It is so much more rewarding.”

Dorian smiled, a flash of white teeth in his perfect face. “I have been engaged on a matter of chemistry, Harry. A most fascinating experiment.”

“Ah, yes. The transformation of matter,” Lord Henry mused, taking a salted almond from a silver dish. “A dull business, unless one is transforming it into gold. I prefer the transformation of souls. It is the one truly divine art left to us. You, my dear boy, are my grandest creation. Tell me, has your soul been behaving itself to-day?”

A cold dread, sharp as a sliver of ice, touched Dorian’s heart. He felt the eyes of the room upon him, though no one was looking. He took a glass of champagne from a passing footman, the delicate stem cool against his suddenly feverish skin. “My soul and I are on the best of terms,” he answered lightly. “It gives me no trouble. It knows its place.”

“And where is that?” inquired Lady Narborough, who had a literal mind.

“In the dark, my dear lady,” said Lord Henry, before Dorian could reply. “That is where all interesting things are kept. Secrets, sins, and the English sense of humour. By the way, Dorian, what has become of your artist friend? The one with the earnest face and the deplorable neck-ties. Basil Hallward. I have not seen him in an age.”

Dorian’s hand tightened on his glass. The bubbles rose in a frantic, silent cascade. “I saw him to-day, as it happens,” he said, his voice astonishingly steady. “He is going to Paris. For a very long time.”

“How sensible of him,” sighed Lord Henry. “Paris is the only place for an artist who cannot sell his pictures. It gives his failure an intellectual gloss. The French will forgive anything, except a man who speaks their language perfectly. Did he say when he was leaving?”

“To-night, I believe,” Dorian said, taking a careful sip of wine. He could feel the lie spreading through him, a subtle poison. “He was in a great hurry. Something about a commission.”

The conversation drifted to other topics, to a scandalous elopement, to the latest political folly, to the quality of the ortolans that were being served. Dorian found himself talking, laughing, weaving the intricate tapestry of social intercourse with all his old skill. He was brilliant, he was charming, he was adored. And all the while, a single image burned behind his eyes: a figure slumped in a chair, a hideous red smile at the base of his neck, and a pool of something dark and thick matting the Aubusson rug. He felt a sudden, violent nausea, and for a moment the faces around him seemed to morph into leering, accusatory masks.

“Are you quite well, Dorian?” asked Lord Henry, his keen eyes missing nothing. “You have gone as pale as one of my wife’s cousins. It is not a becoming look for you.”

“It is the heat of the room,” Dorian murmured, pressing a hand to his forehead. The skin was damp. “And Lady Narborough’s flowers. They are a little overpowering.”

“Nonsense,” said Lord Henry. “There is no such thing as an overpowering flower, only an underpowered nose. What you need is a different kind of air. You are living your life too beautifully, my boy. You must explore the gutters, if only to appreciate the stars.”

The phrase struck Dorian with the force of a revelation. That was it. He needed a different air. He needed to plunge into something so ugly that it would scour the memory of the day’s ugliness from his mind. He needed to escape himself.

He made his excuses shortly after dinner, pleading a sudden headache. Lady Narborough was full of theatrical sympathy, and Lord Henry simply smiled his knowing, enigmatic smile. “Do not do anything I would not do, Dorian,” he called after him as he left. “It leaves you with so few options.”

Dorian did not go home. The thought of the silent house, of the locked door at the top of the stairs, of the faint, lingering chemical smell that no amount of scented pastilles could ever truly erase, was unendurable. He dismissed his hansom at the corner of Piccadilly and, pulling the collar of his opera cloak up to hide his white tie and tails, he walked eastward.

The city changed around him. The grand, stuccoed facades of the West End gave way to the grim, soot-blackened brick of the less prosperous quarters. The air grew thick with the smells of coal smoke and fried fish and unwashed humanity. The gas lamps cast long, distorted shadows that seemed to writhe and clutch at him as he passed. He was a creature of light wandering into the abyss, and he felt a strange, perverse thrill.

He found what he was looking for in a grimy lane near the docks, a squalid little house with greasy, curtainless windows. There was no sign, but a sickly sweet smell, acrid and cloying, seeped from beneath the door. It was a smell he knew well. He pushed the door open and stepped inside.

The opium den was a study in browns and greys. A low, long room, choked with smoke that coiled and eddied in the foul air. On tiers of shabby bunks that lined the walls, long, skeletal figures lay in various states of stupor. Some moaned softly; others lay as still as corpses, their faces vacant, their eyes like burnt-out coals. A wizened Malay sailor presided over the pipes, his face a network of a thousand wrinkles.

Dorian took a pipe, paid the man with a gold sovereign that glittered obscenely in the gloom, and found an empty bunk in a dark corner. He lay down, the rough blanket scratching at his cheek. He put the pipe to his lips and inhaled deeply. The smoke was bitter, but it promised oblivion. It promised to build a wall between himself and his memory. He closed his eyes, and for a moment, he felt a loosening of the terrible knot of fear inside him. The face of Basil Hallward began to recede, to blur at the edges. The sound of Alan Campbell’s broken sob grew faint.

He did not know how long he had been lying there when a hand fell upon his shoulder. He started, his nerves screaming. He had been so intent on escaping his own mind that he had forgotten the world outside it.

A woman was leaning over him, her face a pale oval in the gloom. She was no longer young. Her hair was a tangled mess, and her dress was cheap and torn, but there was a phantom of a long-lost beauty in the lines of her face. Her eyes, wide and dark, were fixed on him with a kind of desperate intensity.

“I knew it was you,” she whispered, her voice hoarse from the pipe. “I would know you anywhere.”

Dorian stared at her, his heart hammering against his ribs. He did not know her. She was just another lost soul in this gallery of the damned. “You are mistaken,” he said coldly, trying to pull away.

“Oh, no, I’m not mistaken,” she said, and a terrible, broken little laugh escaped her lips. “Not about you. The others, they change. They get old and ugly. But not you. Never you. You are still the same. Still my Prince Charming.”

The name struck him like a physical blow. Prince Charming. It was Sibyl’s name for him. The name she had whispered to her curious friends at the theatre. The name he had thought buried with her in an unmarked grave.

“Who are you?” he demanded, his voice a strangled whisper.

“You don’t remember me? I was her friend. Hetty Merton. I was with her the night she… the night she died. She used to talk of you. Always of you. Prince Charming, who would carry her away. And then you broke her heart. You killed her, as sure as if you’d put a knife in her.”

Her voice had risen, and one or two of the listless figures on the bunks stirred. A large, powerfully built sailor, who had been lying on the bunk opposite, slowly sat up. He had the brutish, flattened face of a prizefighter, and small, suspicious eyes. He looked from the woman to Dorian.

“What’s that you say, Hetty?” he grunted, his voice thick and menacing. “What’s that about killing?”

“It’s him!” she cried, pointing a trembling finger at Dorian. “It’s the man who drove Sibyl Vane to her death! The one she called Prince Charming!”

The sailor’s eyes widened. He lumbered to his feet and came towards them, his shadow swallowing Dorian whole. “Sibyl Vane?” he said, his voice a low growl. “I am her brother. I swore on her grave I would find the man who wronged her. I have been looking for him for eighteen years.” He loomed over Dorian, his huge hands clenching and unclenching. “And you say his name is Prince Charming?”

The woman, terrified by what she had unleashed, began to stammer. “Yes, but… it was so long ago… let him be, Jim…”

But the sailor, whose name was James Vane, was not listening. He grabbed Dorian by the collar of his fine cloak and hauled him to his feet as easily as if he were a doll. The stench of stale rum and sweat washed over Dorian in a suffocating wave.

“Eighteen years,” Vane snarled, his face inches from Dorian’s. “I’ve searched for a gentleman, someone with money. But she never told me his name. Only that. Prince Charming. Now, you will tell me. Is that what you call yourself?”

Dorian’s mind, which had been clouded with opium, was now terrifyingly clear. He was looking at his own death. This brute would snap his neck and think nothing of it. But in the extremity of his terror, a strange cunning came to him. He remembered the face he had seen in the mirror only a few hours before.

He did not struggle. He simply looked up at James Vane and let out a small, sad, and very youthful laugh. “My dear man,” he said, his voice as clear and untroubled as a choirboy’s. “You must be mad. Look at me. How old do you think I am? Eighteen years ago, I was a child in the nursery. You have got the wrong person.”

He let his head fall back, so that the flickering light from the oil lamp fell full upon his face. It was the face of a boy of twenty, fresh and unlined, the lips still parted in a look of innocent surprise.

James Vane stared. A look of confusion passed over his coarse features. The hand at Dorian’s collar loosened its grip. It was impossible. The man Hetty had pointed out was no older than he had been himself when Sibyl died.

“It’s true,” the woman, Hetty, whispered from the shadows. “My God, it’s true. He is not the one. The man I knew then would be nearly forty now. This is a boy. I am sorry. The opium makes me see things.”

Vane shoved Dorian away from him in disgust. “Get out of here,” he growled, shame and anger warring in his voice. “Get out before I change my mind.”

Dorian did not need to be told twice. He stumbled out of the den and into the cold, damp air of the lane, gasping for breath. He ran, blindly, through the labyrinth of dark streets, his heart a frantic drum against his ribs. He had been saved by his infernal youth, by the mocking perfection of the face that was his curse and his shield.

When at last he reached his own house, he was trembling uncontrollably. He let himself in with his latchkey and bolted the door behind him, leaning against it as if to hold back the entire world. The house was silent, but it was a menacing silence now. He looked at the luxurious objects around him, the silken curtains, the priceless tapestries, the gleaming bronzes. They seemed alien to him, part of a life that was no longer his.

He lit a candle and, driven by an impulse he could not resist, he went upstairs. He unlocked the door to the old schoolroom and went inside, holding the candle aloft. The hideous smell of nitric acid seemed to have faded, replaced by the musty scent of dust and decay. He walked over to the corner and, with a shaking hand, pulled back the screen.

He let out a strangled cry and the candle fell from his grasp, plunging the room into darkness. For in that brief, flickering moment, he had seen it. The change. The face on the canvas was still twisted with cruelty, the hand still dripped with scarlet. But there was something new. The lips, once set in a firm, sneering line, were now parted slightly, slack with a craven, abject terror. The eyes, which had been filled with a cold malice, were now wide with the horror of the hunted. It was the face of a man who had looked into the eyes of his own doom and had been saved only by a devil’s miracle. It was a face that was, for the first time, afraid. And it was his own.

He stood there in the crushing dark, his senses reeling, the acrid smell of the snuffed candle mingling with the ancient dust. The silence of the room was no longer empty; it was filled with the presence of that new, ghastly thing on the canvas. Fear. It was a sensation he had read of, had seen mimicked upon the stage, had even, in a curious, intellectual way, appreciated in the faces of others. But he had never known it. It was an emotion for the common, for those who were bound by the ludicrous tragedies of life. He had thought himself above it, a spectator at the play. Now, it had him by the throat, and the spectator had been dragged onto the stage to be devoured.

He fumbled for the wall, his hands slick with a cold sweat, and made his way out of the room, not daring to look back. He did not stop to lock the door. What was the use of a lock when the true horror was inside him, a part of him? He stumbled down the grand staircase, his hand trailing along the cool marble of the balustrade. In the hall, a single gas lamp burned low, casting long, dancing shadows that seemed to writhe and twist like living things. Every fold in the heavy portières, every glint on the polished armour of the Florentine knight in the alcove, seemed to hold a threat. He saw the face of James Vane in the dark grain of the oak panelling, in the dim reflection of a silver salver.

He poured himself a glass of brandy, his hand shaking so violently that the spirit slopped over the rim of the crystal glass, staining the white tablecloth. He drank it down in one raw, burning gulp, and then another. The alcohol did little to numb the terror; it only seemed to sharpen its edges, to give it a manic, glittering intensity. He paced the length of the library, a caged animal in a silken prison. To be saved by a mask! To owe his life to a lie that was truer than truth, a youth that was a mockery of time. And the price was that his own soul, locked away upstairs, had learned to be afraid. The portrait was no longer a passive record of sin; it had become an active participant in his dread. It felt what he felt. Its terror was his own, and the sight of it was more awful than the sailor’s knife at his throat.

Sleep was impossible. He watched the dawn creep into the sky, a delicate, pearly grey that sickened him with its purity. London began to stir, the distant rumble of carts a prelude to the symphony of the day. He felt a desperate, aching need for companionship, for the sound of a human voice that was not his own conscience screaming at him. He needed an antidote, a philosophy, a clever phrase that would rearrange the world and put him back in his golden throne. There was only one person in London who could provide such a tonic.

He dressed with his usual meticulous care, though his fingers felt clumsy and strange. The selection of a tie, the placing of a gardenia in his buttonhole, were acts of defiance against the chaos that had invaded his life. He found his hansom and directed the driver to Curzon Street.

Lord Henry was in his library, reclining on a divan and dissecting a pomegranate with a small silver knife. The room was heavy with the scent of his Egyptian cigarettes and the faint, dusty perfume of old books. He looked up as Dorian entered, a slow, welcoming smile playing on his lips.

“My dear boy,” he said, in his languid, musical voice. “You are an evangelist of the dawn. I had no idea you rose before noon. Is it a new affectation? It is charming, but I pray you do not make a habit of it. The early morning is for the grossly optimistic. It is the time of day when stockbrokers and dairymaids are at their most triumphant. It does not suit you.”

Dorian did not return the smile. He sank into a deep armchair, running a hand through his golden hair. “I have not slept, Harry. I have had a terrible night.”

“Ah, a terrible night,” Lord Henry murmured, holding a blood-red seed to the light. “That has a certain promise. Was she very plain, or too importunate? The worst nights are always the consequence of an error in aesthetic judgment. One should be as careful in choosing one’s pleasures as a chemist is in mixing his poisons. What was it? An ill-chosen word? A clashing colour? A sigh that lasted a moment too long?”

“It was nothing like that,” said Dorian, his voice strained. “It was a matter of life and death.”

Lord Henry raised a delicate eyebrow. “Life and death? My dear Dorian, you are becoming melodramatic. Those are subjects for the suburbs, where people have nothing else to talk about. In our world, there are only beautiful things and ugly things. What you describe sounds exceedingly ugly. You should have left at once.”

“I could not leave,” Dorian cried, leaning forward. “Harry, I have been in the most dreadful danger. A man tried to kill me.”

For a moment, Lord Henry’s amused placidity wavered. He put down the pomegranate and stared at Dorian. “Tried to kill you? How very impertinent of him. A burglar, I suppose? They are the one class of people who have a true, if misguided, appreciation for the property of others.”

“Not a burglar. A man. A sailor. It was Sibyl Vane’s brother.”

The name hung in the air between them. Lord Henry’s face became a mask of studied indifference, but his eyes were sharp and watchful. “Ah, yes. The young lady who thought that art was more important than life, and died to prove it. I had forgotten her. And she had a brother? How very complete. The lower orders are so terribly sentimental about family. They cling to each other like limpets. And what did this nautical person want?”

“He wanted revenge. He believed I was the man who drove his sister to her death. He had been hunting me for years. He found me last night, in some foul den by the river. He had a pistol to my head, Harry. I saw my own death in his eyes.”

Dorian’s voice broke. The memory was too vivid, too raw. He covered his face with his hands.

Lord Henry lit a fresh cigarette, the tiny flare of the match illuminating his cynical, handsome features. He blew a cloud of fragrant smoke towards the ceiling. “And yet, you are here,” he said softly. “You are not a shade, come to haunt me for my bad advice. You are quite solid, and looking, if I may say so, tragically handsome in your distress. So, what happened? Did you reason with him? Did you quote him some poetry? I have always believed that a well-chosen line from Baudelaire could disarm any assassin.”

“He let me go,” Dorian whispered. “He let me go because of my face.”

“Ah,” said Lord Henry. He leaned back, a genuine smile of intellectual pleasure spreading across his face. “Now, that is fascinating. That is a truly wonderful touch. A paradox of the most delightful sort. Your beauty, which has been the cause of so much trouble, has at last been its own salvation. It is a triumph of aesthetics over ethics. He saw you, and he could not believe that so divine a creature could be the wicked fiend of his melodrama. Tell me everything.”

Dorian recounted the scene in the opium den, the coarse hand on his collar, the woman’s intervention, the look of baffled disbelief on James Vane’s face. Lord Henry listened with the rapt attention of a connoisseur examining a rare gem.

“It is perfect,” he declared when Dorian had finished. “It is a scene from some Jacobean tragedy, rewritten by a celestial wit. The brute, armed with his crude morality, is confounded by the visible manifestation of eternal youth. He came to find a monster and found a god, and his simple brain could not contain the contradiction. You have proved my point, Dorian. It is better to be beautiful than to be good. But one is not always so lucky. You must be more careful. These forays into the sordid underworld are amusing, I have no doubt, but they are like dining with one’s solicitor: eventually, you will be presented with a bill. Perhaps it is time you cultivated a new hobby. Collecting cameos, perhaps? Or studying the heresies of the early Church? Something that can be done indoors.”

“But Harry, he is still out there!” Dorian insisted, his panic rising again, unsoothed by the cynical balm of Lord Henry’s words. “That woman, the one who saved me, she might have told him the truth by now. She said I would be nearly forty. He will realize his mistake. He will find me.”

“My dear boy, fear is a terrible vulgarity. It is the tribute mediocrity pays to genius. You must not indulge in it. This sailor is a ghost from a past you have wisely forgotten. He has had his dramatic moment. He will now, in all probability, get horribly drunk and be press-ganged onto a ship bound for some dreadful, fly-blown colony, and will trouble you no more. Forget him.”

“I cannot forget him,” Dorian said, standing up and beginning to pace again. “Every shadow, every face in the street, it will be him. I cannot live like this.”

“Then do not live like this,” said Lord Henry, standing as well and placing a hand on Dorian’s shoulder. “Change the scenery. Life is a question of nerves, and fibres, and slowly built-up cells in which thought lodges and passion has its dreams. You are overwrought. You have had a shock. What you need is not philosophy, but distraction. My wife’s cousin, the Duchess of Monmouth, is having a party at her place at Selby Royal next week. We are all going down. The country air, the tedious conversations about sport, the charming inanity of it all, will be the perfect cure. You will forget all about your vengeful sailor.”

The idea of Selby Royal, his own country estate, bloomed in his mind. It was a place of green lawns and cool, quiet woods, a sanctuary far from the grimy labyrinth of the docks. He could surround himself with pleasant, shallow people, with music, with laughter. He could hide.

“Yes,” he said, a look of relief on his face. “Yes, I will go to Selby. I shall leave London at once.”

“Excellent,” Lord Henry approved. “Flee from a single emotion as you would from a plague. Now, come and look at this new engraving I have acquired. It is by an Italian master, and it is a study in exquisite agony. It will make you feel that your own troubles are quite commonplace.”

But for once, art held no consolation. Dorian left Lord Henry’s house feeling more alone than ever. Harry’s words were like exquisite fireworks, brilliant and startling, but they vanished into the darkness, leaving no warmth behind. The terror was a cold, hard thing, and it had taken root in him.

He spent the next few days in a fever of paranoia. He dared not walk the streets. He travelled everywhere in a closed carriage, peering out through the curtains at the crowds, his heart lurching at the sight of any broad-shouldered man with a dark beard. The pleasures of London had turned to poison. The rich food at his clubs tasted of ash, the music at the opera seemed discordant and shrill, the faces of his friends were grinning masks. He was haunted. James Vane was not a man; he was an idea, a specter of retribution that followed him into the most luxurious and well-guarded rooms.

The journey to Selby Royal was a blessed escape. As the train sped through the green English countryside, he felt the tight knot of fear in his chest begin to loosen. Here, under the vast, open sky, amid the ancient trees of his own lands, surely he would be safe.

Selby Royal was a beautiful Jacobean house of rose-red brick, with mullioned windows and a forest of tall, twisted chimneys. The party was in full swing when he arrived. The air was filled with the sound of laughter, the clink of glasses, and the light, formal music of a string quartet playing in the great hall. The Duchess of Monmouth, a woman with a face like a tired angel and a wit almost as sharp as Lord Henry’s, greeted him with theatrical delight.

“Dorian! You have come at last! We had all but given you up. Harry told us you were pursuing some new form of martyrdom in London. I am so glad you have chosen our form of salvation instead. It is much more amusing and involves far better champagne.”

He was drawn into the glittering circle. He talked, he smiled, he paid extravagant compliments. He played the part of the charming, impossibly handsome Dorian Gray, the role he had perfected over the years. Lord Henry was there, of course, holding a small court of his own by the fireplace, dispensing paradoxes like a pagan priest dispensing blessings. Sir Geoffrey Clouston was loudly recounting a story about a hunting expedition, and the ladies listened with expressions of polite, feigned interest. It was all so normal, so wonderfully, beautifully superficial. For a few hours, Dorian felt the terror recede. He was safe in his gilded cage.

That evening, after dinner, he found himself in the conservatory with the Duchess. The air was warm and heavy with the scent of orchids and gardenias. Moonlight streamed through the great glass panes, turning the exotic foliage to silver and black.

“You are quiet to-night, Dorian,” the Duchess said, fanning herself slowly with a fan of painted silk. “Are you bored with us already?”

“Never with you, Gladys,” he murmured, taking her fan and moving it for her with a graceful turn of his wrist. “I was merely thinking how life is a disappointment.”

“Ah, do not say that,” she sighed. “It is the one true thing, so it should never be said. What has disappointed you? Love?”

“I have given it up,” he said.

“I have not,” she answered, looking at him with her curious, violet eyes. “But I have not found it, either. Perhaps it is like a fire, something one is always either running towards or running away from. What do you think?”

“I think it is an illusion, like conscience. And I have had enough of both.” He felt a sudden chill, despite the hothouse warmth. He looked past her, towards the rain-streaked glass of the conservatory wall.

And then he saw it.

Pressed against the pane, a white face, staring in at him. The face of a common man, with a rough beard and eyes that burned with a cold, murderous hatred. It was the face from the opium den. The face of James Vane.

A strangled cry broke from Dorian’s lips. He dropped the fan. He felt the blood drain from his cheeks, felt a coldness so profound it seemed to stop his heart. The world narrowed to that single, dreadful face, a phantom of vengeance come to claim him in his own sanctuary. He swayed on his feet and fell to the floor in a dead faint.

The great glass panes of the conservatory rattled, not from any wind, but from the sudden, sharp tremor that had seized the house itself. The Duchess’s little cry was a thin, silvery note against the sudden cessation of music from the hall. Servants appeared as if summoned from the very shadows, their faces masks of disciplined concern. The fan, a splash of painted silk and ivory, lay upon the cool tiles like a broken butterfly, its brief, tragic life at an end.

Lord Henry strolled in from the card-room, a gardenia in his buttonhole, his expression one of mild, scientific curiosity. “My dear Gladys,” he said, his voice cutting through the rising murmur of alarm, “what new sensation have you contrived for us? Fainting is a charmingly Victorian accomplishment. I had no idea you were encouraging its revival.”

“Harry, for heaven’s sake, be serious!” she cried, kneeling beside Dorian’s still form. “He has collapsed. His face is like marble. Ring for a doctor! Someone fetch brandy!”

“Nonsense, my dear,” Lord Henry replied, bending down not to assist, but to observe. He touched one of Dorian’s hands, which lay limp and pale upon the dark floor. “His pulse is merely a little agitated. It is the effect of an over-wrought soul upon a too-exquisite temperament. Passion is always a little declamatory. Dorian has merely mistaken the conservatory for a stage, and the climax of the third act has proved too much for him. Let us carry him to his room. The proper cure for a theatrical swoon is a perfectly appointed bedroom and an audience of one.”

There was something so calmly authoritative in his tone that the others obeyed without question. Two footmen, with the practiced ease of men accustomed to moving precious and fragile objects, lifted Dorian Gray. His head fell back, the moonlight catching the perfect curve of his throat, the dark gold of his hair. He looked like a fallen Endymion, a sleeping god struck down by a mortal malady. The little procession moved through the now-silent throng of guests, who parted as if for a funeral cortège. The Duchess followed, her own face pale, twisting a lace handkerchief in her hands. Lord Henry lingered for a moment, picking up the fallen fan. He smoothed its silk wing with a delicate finger. “What a tragedy,” he murmured to himself. “Not Dorian’s, of course. His are always so interesting. But the fan’s. It will never be quite the same again.”

Dorian awoke to the muffled scent of lavender and the soft hiss of a gas lamp turned low. His own room. He felt the reassuring weight of a silk quilt over him, the cool smoothness of linen pillows beneath his head. For a moment, there was only a profound weariness, a sense of having journeyed a very long way. Then, memory returned, not as a thought, but as a physical blow. The face. The white, staring face pressed against the glass, framed by the dripping leaves and the blackness of the night. It was a face from a nightmare, a face from the filthy recesses of a dockside den, yet here it had been, at Selby Royal, staring into the very heart of his sanctuary.

A cold dread, far colder than the chill he had felt in the conservatory, crept over him. He sat bolt upright, his heart hammering against his ribs like a frantic bird beating against the bars of its cage. He scanned the room, his eyes wide with terror. The familiar, beautiful objects seemed to mock him. A silver-gilt toilet set gleamed on the dressing table. A volume of Huysmans, bound in yellow leather, lay beside his bed. A bowl of pale, tea-scented roses filled the air with their funereal perfume. It was all a lie. The luxury, the beauty, the carefully curated perfection, it was a thin veneer, and something had just broken through.

He leaped from the bed, his bare feet cold on the Aubusson carpet, and rushed to the window. He tore back the heavy brocade curtain and stared out into the night. There was nothing but the rain-swept darkness, the black, tossing shapes of the yews, the faint, silver gleam of the lake in the distance. Nothing. Of course there was nothing. What had he expected? That the phantom would wait for him?

It could not be; it was impossible; a trick of the moonlight and a guilty fancy. He had been thinking of Sibyl Vane, and the mind, that terrible artist, had fashioned a monster from a shadow, a spectre from a memory. James Vane was in some distant sea, on some forgotten ship. He was dead, surely. He must be. Years had passed. People died. Sailors were drowned. It was a mere coincidence of nerves and atmosphere. He repeated the thought to himself, over and over, as a kind of desperate prayer.

A soft knock came at the door. Dorian started, a choked cry catching in his throat.

“Who is it?” he called out, his voice thin and strange.

“It is only I, Dorian,” came the languid, musical reply. “May I come in? I have brought you some hock and seltzer. I always find it a wonderful restorative for shattered nerves.”

Lord Henry entered without waiting for a reply, carrying a small silver tray. He placed it on a table near the fire, which a servant had evidently just lit. “I have dismissed your man,” he said. “Sympathy from the lower orders is always so badly expressed. And I have told the Duchess that you are suffering from a sudden and picturesque malzdy which I have christened ‘aesthetic shock’. She was quite fascinated. I believe she is thinking of acquiring it herself for the end of the season.”

He poured the wine, his movements precise and unhurried. “Now, Dorian, you must tell me what it was. Was it the particular shade of green on that orchid? I have often felt that Nature’s colours are sometimes in very bad taste. Or was it something Gladys said? Her compliments can be as startling as an unexpected telegram.”

Dorian watched him, a feeling of utter helplessness washing over him. How could he explain the unutterable horror of that face to this man, this connoisseur of exquisite sensations? For Harry, murder and vengeance were merely words in a book, interesting concepts for a paradox. For Dorian, they were a man’s face at a window.

“I am not well, Harry,” he said, sinking into a chair. “I think I should have a doctor.”

“A doctor!” Lord Henry exclaimed, holding the glass up to the light. “My dear boy, the only thing a doctor could do for you is to certify you are alive, and we already have sufficient, if rather dramatic, proof of that. Doctors are for the prosaic diseases of the middle classes. Your ailments are, I trust, entirely of the soul. Tell me, what did you see in the conservatory?”

“I saw nothing,” Dorian said, too quickly. “The heat... the scent of the flowers... I was tired.”

“You saw something,” Lord Henry insisted, his grey eyes narrowing. “You looked past Gladys as if you were seeing a Gorgon. And your face, for a moment, was quite fascinating. It lost all its colour. It was a study in pure terror. I have never seen you look so alive. Suffering has done you a great service, Dorian. It has made you new.”

“Don’t talk like that, Harry. I am afraid.”

“Of course you are afraid. That is the point. Fear is a sensation, like any other. One should experience it, analyse it, and then move on. To dwell on it is to become a sentimentalist. Was it a ghost? I hope so. I have always wanted to meet a ghost. The aristocracy has given up all its privileges, it might at least retain its family phantoms. Who was it? Some lace-ruffled ancestor with a grievance?”

“It was no one,” Dorian muttered, turning his face away. “It was a madness.”

Lord Henry took a sip of his own drink. “Ah, madness. That is more interesting still. But I do not believe you. You are hiding something from me, Dorian. It is the first secret you have ever kept from me, and it is vexing. But I shall not press you. Secrets are the only things that can make modern life mysterious or marvellous. Keep yours. For now. Only, I beg you, do not faint in public again. It is a social error. It puts one’s hostess in a terribly difficult position.” He smiled, placed Dorian’s glass on the table beside him, and glided from the room, leaving behind him the faint scent of his cigarette and a profound sense of isolation.

For two days, Dorian did not leave his apartments. He claimed a lingering weakness, a nervous exhaustion. He could hear the life of the great house continuing without him: the laughter of the ladies on the lawn, the sharp crack of billiard balls, the distant music of the quartet in the evenings. These sounds, once the pleasant backdrop to his existence, were now instruments of torture. They were the sounds of a world from which he was excluded, a world in which a man with a common, bearded face and murderous eyes was not waiting for him in the shadows.

He paced the floor of his room like a caged animal. He could not read. The elegant prose of his favourite French poets seemed hollow and meaningless. He could not eat. The sight of food brought to him on delicate porcelain was revolting. He drank, pouring glass after glass of wine from the decanters that were always kept full, but the alcohol did not numb his fear. It only sharpened it, giving his fancies a more grotesque and vivid shape. Every shadow in the corner of the room seemed to hold a lurking figure. Every creak of the old house was the sound of a footstep on the stair. The life of a spectator had become impossible. He was the most wretched and terrified of actors, trapped in a play he could no longer control.

On the third morning, the sun shone. It streamed into his room, a great, golden flood of light that seemed to mock the darkness in his soul. Outside, the world was washed clean, sparkling and fresh after the rain. He heard the sounds of the shooting-party assembling on the gravel drive below his window: the cheerful shouts of men, the barking of dogs, the clatter of guns. Sir Geoffrey Clouston’s booming laugh was unmistakable.

A sudden, desperate resolve seized him. He could not remain a prisoner to his own terror. That way lay madness. The thing he had seen was an illusion, a phantom bred of a guilty conscience and the shifting play of light and shadow. He had to prove it to himself. He had to go out into the light, to breathe the free air, to rejoin the world of the living. To hide in his room was to admit defeat, to confess his guilt to the very air he breathed.

He dressed with frantic haste, choosing a Norfolk jacket and thick tweed trousers. He looked at his reflection in the mirror. He was pale, certainly, and there were dark circles under his eyes, but the face that stared back at him was still untouched by the horror that gnared at his soul. The cruel contrast gave him a strange flicker of courage. He was Dorian Gray. He was invincible. He would not be hunted down by a ghost.

He joined the party just as they were about to set off for the woods. His appearance was greeted with cheers.

“Dorian!” boomed Sir Geoffrey. “The sleeping prince awakens! We thought you had abandoned us for the world of dreams.”

“I found it less amusing than I had hoped,” Dorian said, forcing a smile.

The Duchess, who was there to see them off, looked at him with real concern. “Are you sure you are well enough, Dorian? You look so pale.”

“I am perfectly well,” he insisted. “The fresh air will do me good.”

Lord Henry, who was leaning against a stone urn and declining to participate in what he called “the unspeakable in pursuit of the uneatable,” watched him with an amused smile. “Be careful, Dorian,” he called out as they walked away. “The woods are full of nymphs. And nymphs are so terribly provincial.”

Dorian took a gun from a gamekeeper, the cold weight of it unfamiliar but somehow reassuring in his hand. He walked beside Sir Geoffrey, trying to immerse himself in the mindless chatter of the sport. They spoke of cartridges and shot, of the best dogs and the worst poachers. It was a world of simple, brutal facts, and for a little while, Dorian felt the terror recede. He was surrounded by men, by the sounds of their boots crunching on fallen leaves, by the earthy smell of the damp woods. No phantom could touch him here.

They spread out in a line at the edge of a copse. The beaters began their work at the far end, their cries and the tapping of their sticks echoing through the trees. A rabbit broke from the undergrowth. Dorian raised his gun, but his hands were trembling. He could not shoot. Another shot rang out from further down the line.

“A hit, Clouston!” someone shouted.

The morning wore on. The sun climbed higher, dappling the forest floor with light. Dorian began to feel a sense of calm returning. The air was crisp, the sky a perfect, cloudless blue. He had been a fool to be so afraid. It was all a trick of his own mind. He felt a surge of his old self-confidence.

Suddenly, a hare darted from a thicket of ferns directly in front of him. It was a perfect shot. The creature, mesmerized by fear, paused for a fraction of a second, its ears pricked, its body tense. Dorian raised his gun to his shoulder, took aim, and as he did so, he heard the most piteous cry of agony he had ever heard in his life. It was not the cry of an animal.

He lowered his gun, his heart standing still. The other sportsmen were shouting. Sir Geoffrey ran towards him, his face red and agitated.

“What is it? What have you shot, Dorian?” he cried.

But he had not fired.

“My God!” came another shout from the ferns. “Someone is hurt!”

They pushed their way through the tangled undergrowth. A man lay on the ground, his body twisted at a grotesque angle. He was dressed in the rough clothes of a beater, but he had not been with their party. A thin stream of blood trickled from a wound in his chest, staining his coarse shirt a dark, terrible red. He had been shot through the heart.

Sir Geoffrey knelt beside him. “Damnation!” he swore, his voice shaking. “Some idiot has shot one of the beaters. It was my shot. I fired at the hare just as it broke cover.” He looked up, his face a mask of horror and self-reproach. “I have killed him.”

Dorian stared down at the body, a feeling of nausea rising in his throat. The casual brutality of it, the sudden, senseless extinction of a life. And then he saw the man’s face.

It was half-hidden by the ferns, but it was not the face of a stranger. It was a common face, with a rough beard matted with blood. But it was younger than the face in his memory, and the eyes, now glazed and sightless, held no hatred. They were merely empty.

“Who is he?” Dorian whispered, his voice hoarse.

The head gamekeeper came forward, twisting his cap in his hands. “We don’t know, Mr. Gray. He’s not one of our men. Must be a sailor from the town, come up here looking for work. We warned them to stay clear of the woods while the guns were out.”

Dorian felt a wave of dizziness. He stumbled back, leaning against a tree for support. A sailor. It could not be. It was a ghastly coincidence.

“Let’s carry him to the old steading,” Sir Geoffrey commanded, his voice thick with shock. “We must send for the police. My God, what a dreadful business.”

As they lifted the body, one of the dead man’s hands, clenched in the final spasm of death, fell open. Something fell from it, something small and metallic. Dorian saw it glint in the sunlight. He moved forward, as if in a trance, and picked it up. It was a cheap, poorly made revolver.

The head gamekeeper saw the look on his face. “Looks like he was a poacher after all, sir. Or something worse. What would a man like that be doing with a pistol in the woods?”

Dorian did not answer. He stared at the dead face, the face of his would-be executioner, the man he believed to be James Vane. And a wild, terrible, exultant joy surged through him. He was safe. The dreadful phantom that had haunted his steps had been destroyed, not by his own hand, not through any action of his, but by a blind, meaningless accident. Fate, or chance, had intervened. He had been saved. He looked away from the dead man, up at the clear blue sky, and for the first time in three days, he breathed freely. The world was beautiful again.

The horror of the moment had passed from him, had dissolved like a miasma in the brilliant winter sunlight, and in its place had blossomed a flower of monstrous and exquisite relief. The world was indeed beautiful again, painted in colours he had not seen for an eternity of three days. The sky was a flawless turquoise, the frost on the fallen leaves glittered like a carpet of crushed diamonds, and the very air, which moments before had seemed thick with menace, was now thin, crisp, and intoxicating. He felt as though a great weight had been lifted from his soul, a weight so immense he had not realized its full gravity until its sudden, miraculous absence.

He played his part, of course. One must always play one’s part; it is the first duty of the civilized. He affected a delicate pallor and a slight tremor of the hands. He murmured words of consolation to Sir Geoffrey, whose robust country face had crumpled into a mask of genuine agony.

“You must not blame yourself, Sir Geoffrey,” Dorian said, his voice a carefully modulated whisper of sympathy. “It was a tragic misadventure. Fate has a terrible artistry, does it not? To weave such a pattern of catastrophe from a single, thoughtless moment.”

“My God, Dorian, can you imagine it? To have that on one’s conscience,” the baronet groaned, burying his face in his hands. “To have taken a human life. The poor fellow.”

Dorian placed a hand on his shoulder. The gesture felt theatrical, but it was expected. “Life is a terrible thing, my dear friend. And death is a terrible thing. But the combination of the two is simply a bore. The man is past all suffering now. It is you who are the victim in this little drama.”

Sir Geoffrey looked up, his eyes wide with incomprehension. “Me? But I shot him!”

“And you will suffer for it,” Dorian said, with a sad, charming smile. “You will suffer far more than he did. His pain was but for an instant. Yours will be a companion for weeks. That is the injustice of it all. The person who commits the error is invariably the one who pays the highest emotional price. It is one of nature’s less witty paradoxes.”

The other men, having overcome their initial shock, were now practical. They fashioned a makeshift litter from branches and their own coats, and the body was carried with a grim reverence back towards the house. Dorian walked beside the Duchess of Monmouth, who had come out to see what had delayed the shoot. She held a little lace handkerchief to her nose, as if to ward off the vulgarity of the scene.

“How perfectly dreadful, Mr. Gray,” she murmured. “It has quite spoiled the day. Geoffrey will be impossible for a week, I expect. He takes these things so dreadfully to heart. It is the curse of the English country gentleman; they have convictions instead of ideas.”

“It is a melancholy business,” Dorian agreed, his eyes straying to the still, shrouded form being borne ahead of them. “And yet, is there not something almost Greek about it? A life for a life. A hunter, himself hunted and slain by a blind arrow of destiny.”

The Duchess looked at him, her brow furrowed. “I think it is simply clumsy, Mr. Gray. And rather inconsiderate of the man to get himself shot. It shows a certain lack of social foresight. One really ought to know where the guns are. Now the whole party is ruined, and we shall have to speak in hushed tones and pretend to be miserable until dinner. I detest morbid sympathy. It is so insincere.”

Dorian smiled. “You are a true philosopher, Duchess. You see things as they are.”

“I see them as they affect me,” she corrected him. “It is the only honest perspective. Ah, here is Harry. No doubt he will find the whole thing wonderfully picturesque.”

Lord Henry had sauntered down from the terrace, a cigarette between his fingers, an expression of amused inquiry on his face. He surveyed the solemn little procession with the air of a critic at a rather provincial play.

“My dear Dorian,” he began, his voice a lazy drawl. “What is all this? A tableau vivant? It is a little too realistic for my taste. Geoffrey looks positively stricken. Has he missed a pheasant?”

“He has shot a man, Harry,” said Dorian, his tone grave.

“Has he, by Jove? How extraordinarily careless. And who was the fortunate recipient of his aim?”

“Some poor fellow. A beater, they think. Or a poacher.”

Lord Henry raised his eyebrows. “A poacher? Then Geoffrey is a public benefactor. He has been protecting his property. I see no cause for this extravagant remorse. In fact, he ought to be congratulated. It is not often that one’s sport and one’s duty coincide so perfectly. It gives the whole affair a moral dimension, which is, of course, a dreadful artistic flaw, but it will be a great comfort to Geoffrey’s middle class conscience.” He turned to Dorian and lowered his voice. “You, however, seem quite unruffled, my dear boy. I came out here expecting to find you in a state of nervous collapse. You have a new-found stoicism. It is most becoming. Calamity suits you.”

“I have developed, Harry. As I told you.”

“So I see. You have learned that the sorrows of others are theatrical properties. One can be moved by them, but one must never allow them to clutter one’s own stage. Come, let us leave the good Samaritans to their work. The police will be summoned, there will be an inquest, and the whole sordid business will be published in the county papers. It is a triumph of modern squalor. I want to hear nothing more about it until it has become an anecdote. Tell me, did you see the colour of the blood on the man’s shirt? I have always thought that the vermilion used by the old masters was the only true shade. Nature is so often disappointing in her chromatics.”

They strolled back towards the great house, leaving the tragedy behind them in the cold winter air. Dorian felt a delicious sense of freedom. He was with Harry again, back in the world of epigrams and cynical philosophies, the world where a man’s death was merely a flaw in the day’s aesthetic. The phantom was gone, slain by a chance shot in a frosty wood. He was safe. He was absolutely and unequivocally safe.

The rest of the day passed in a haze of hushed conversations and artificial solemnity. Sir Geoffrey had locked himself in his library, and his wife fluttered about, complaining of a migraine. Dinner was a sombre affair, the conversation circling politely around the tragedy without ever quite touching upon it, like insects buzzing over a foul pond. Dorian, however, felt a strange, intoxicating gaiety. He talked brilliantly, his wit sharper than ever before. He told stories that made even the most grief-stricken guests smile. He was charming, fascinating, irresistible. He felt a wild desire to laugh, to sing, to tell them all that he was saved, that the shadow had passed from his life forever.

Lord Henry watched him with a curious, attentive smile. “You are in wonderful form to-night, Dorian,” he said, as they sat in the smoking-room after the ladies had retired. “One would think you had just received the best of news, rather than witnessed a commonplace tragedy.”

“Perhaps I have, Harry,” Dorian replied, leaning back in his leather armchair and inhaling the fragrance of his cigarette. “Perhaps I have discovered that life is infinitely more delightful when one ceases to be afraid of it.”

“Ah, fear! The basis of all religion and most morality. You have shed it? Then you are truly free. I always knew you had it in you. But what was it you were afraid of?”

Dorian hesitated for a moment. He looked into the fire, at the glowing logs that crumbled into fantastic shapes. “The past, I suppose,” he said slowly. “One’s own actions. They have a dreadful habit of following one about. I thought… I thought I was being followed.”

“And now you are not?”

“Now I am not. The ghost has been laid.”

The next day, the coroner arrived from the nearby town, a bustling, important little man who seemed to relish the grim nature of his task. The inquest was held in the great hall of the steading where the body had been laid. Dorian, out of a morbid curiosity he could not repress, attended it, standing at the back of the small crowd of servants and farmhands.

The head gamekeeper gave his evidence clearly. He described finding the man. He identified the revolver that had been found in the dead man’s hand. He stated that the man was not known to anyone on the estate. Sir Geoffrey, pale and trembling, then gave his own account. He admitted to firing the shot. He spoke of his horror and his undying regret. His voice broke, and he had to be led from the room.

Then a new witness was called, a rough-looking man in the uniform of a merchant navy captain. He had come from the port, having heard of a sailor found dead. He identified the body without hesitation.

“His name was James Vane,” the captain said, his voice gruff. “He was a seaman on my vessel. Signed on in London for a voyage to Australia. He was a strange one. Quiet. Kept to himself. Seemed to have a fire in him, though. A hatred for someone. He told me once, when he was in his cups, that he was hunting a man. A man who had wronged his sister years ago. He said he would know him by the name the man had given his sister: ‘Prince Charming’.”

A cold dread, sharp and sudden as an icicle to the heart, pierced Dorian’s exultant mood. He felt the blood drain from his face.

The coroner, a man with no sense of poetry, merely frowned. “Prince Charming? Very irregular. And did he say anything else about this man?”

“Only that he’d made a vow on his sister’s grave to kill him,” the captain answered. “He deserted the ship at the last port. Said he had a lead. I suppose he followed his man here. Poor devil. Looks like his hunt is over.”

The verdict was, of course, death by misadventure. The coroner added a rider, censuring the deceased for being in the woods with a firearm during a private shoot. It was all very neat, very tidy. The law had done its work.

Dorian slipped out of the hall and walked into the cold, clean air. He was trembling. So it was true. It was him. It was the face from the opium den, the face that had haunted his every step. And he was dead. The vow of vengeance had been broken by a random pellet of lead. He wanted to laugh, but the sound caught in his throat. He leaned against the cold stone wall of the steading, his heart hammering against his ribs. It was too perfect, too complete. The gods, or the devil, had been extraordinarily kind to him.

For the next week, he stayed at Selby, lapped in luxury and a profound sense of peace. The memory of the dead sailor, of James Vane, became a distant, almost pleasant thing, the final chord in a dark symphony that had now concluded. The fear was gone, and in its place was a new and daring idea. He had been spared, and for what? Surely not merely to continue his life of idle sensation. Perhaps this was a sign. Perhaps the power that governed his life, the strange magic of the portrait, was offering him a chance.

He began to think about goodness. It was a new concept for him, a new sensation to be explored. He had explored sin to its depths, and it had led him to a state of perpetual anxiety. Now, with that anxiety lifted, could he not experiment with its opposite? Could he not perform a good deed? And if he did, a truly selfless act, would it not be reflected in the portrait? Would not the cruel lines soften, the sneer of cynicism fade? Might he not begin to reverse the dreadful corruption, to win back the beauty of his own soul?

The thought excited him more than any sin had for years. It was the ultimate aesthetic experiment. To reform oneself not for moral reasons, but for artistic ones. To purify the soul in order to restore beauty to a painted canvas.

His chance came a few days later. He had taken to long, solitary walks, exploring the countryside around Selby. In a quiet lane, near a small, forgotten hamlet, he came across a girl. She was standing by a stile, a basket of eggs on her arm, her head bowed as if in thought. She was little more than a child, perhaps seventeen, with the kind of simple, peasant beauty that artists love to paint. Her hair was the colour of ripe corn, and her eyes, when she lifted them to look at him, were as blue and innocent as the summer sky.

Her name, he learned, was Hetty Merton. She was the daughter of a local farmer. She was artless, uneducated, and utterly guileless. She looked at him with an expression of frank and simple adoration, as though he were a being from another world. Her innocence was a perfect, untouched thing, like a wild rose in a hidden garden. And Dorian, the connoisseur of sensations, found himself utterly captivated.

He met her again the next day, and the day after that. He talked to her not of art or philosophy, but of simple things; of the birds in the trees and the flowers in the fields. He was gentle, he was charming, he was her “Prince Charming” in truth, a gentleman from the great house who had condescended to notice her. He saw the love dawning in her eyes, a pure, selfless, and total devotion.

And he knew what he could do. He could take her. It would be easy. A few more whispered words, a secret meeting, a promise of love. He could pluck this perfect rose, enjoy its fragrance for a day, and then discard it. It was a familiar pattern. But this time, he would resist. He would perform his great act of renunciation. He would leave her as he had found her: pure and untouched. He would spare her.

The thought of his own magnanimity thrilled him. He saw himself as a romantic hero, sacrificing his own pleasure for the sake of another’s virtue. He arranged to meet her one last time, in a small copse at the edge of her father’s farm. The evening was closing in, the sky a delicate wash of rose and grey.

She came to him, her face glowing with trust and love. “You are going away?” she whispered, her eyes filling with tears.

“I must, Hetty,” he said, his voice soft and sad. “I am not the man you think I am. I have brought ruin to everyone I have ever loved. I cannot bring ruin to you. You are too good, too pure. I am leaving you, for your own sake. Forget me. Go back to your simple life and be happy.”

He did not kiss her. He merely touched her hand, then turned and walked away, not looking back. He felt a virtuous glow suffuse his entire being. He had done it. He had sacrificed a new and exquisite sensation. He had been good.

He could not bear to stay at Selby another night. He left a note for Lord Henry, full of vague apologies and talk of urgent business, and took the late train back to London. All through the journey, his heart beat with a feverish excitement. He could think of nothing but the portrait. He pictured it already changing, the cruel mouth softening into a gentle curve, the hard glitter in the eyes melting into something kinder. He had performed his act of goodness. Now he would reap his artistic reward.

He arrived at his house in the early hours of the morning. He let himself in with his key and went straight upstairs, a lamp in his hand. His heart was pounding so loudly he felt it might burst from his chest. He unlocked the door to the old schoolroom, the air within striking him as stale and heavy with the scent of dust and corruption.

He walked over to the corner where the screen stood. His hand trembled as he set the lamp down on a small table. For a moment, he hesitated. What if nothing had changed? But no, it must have. He had been noble. He had sacrificed. The magic must obey the laws of this new, moral game.

With a swift, decisive movement, he pulled the great purple cloth away from the portrait. He held the lamp high and stared.

And then a cry of pain and horror broke from his lips, a wailing, animal sound of utter despair.

The picture had not improved. It had grown more hideous, more loathsome than ever before. The change was not one of decay, not another line of age or wrinkle of sin. It was something new, something worse. Around the scarlet, dripping mouth, a new expression had been carved. A line of cunning. A sneer of hypocrisy. The look of a man playing a part, of a vanity so profound that even its attempts at virtue were merely a new and more subtle form of selfishness.

He saw it all in an instant. The portrait had not been deceived. It knew. It knew that his “renunciation” was not born of goodness, but of a monstrous egotism. He had spared Hetty not for her sake, but for his. It was an experiment. She was a pawn in his game. He had left her not with her innocence intact, but with a broken heart, with the memory of a love that was a lie. He had not saved her; he had merely spoiled the new chapter of his own life with a more disgusting form of sin.

He stared at the jeering, hypocritical face on the canvas, and he understood at last. There was no escape. There was no reversing the pact. His soul was a foul, irredeemable thing, and every action he took, whether for good or for evil, only added a new and more terrible detail to its painted record. The picture was the diary of his life, and this last entry was the most damning of all.

The lamp fell from his hand and shattered upon the floorboards, its little flame sputtering for a moment like a dying soul before being extinguished utterly. He was plunged into a sudden, profound darkness, a blackness that seemed to rush in from the corners of the room to swallow him whole. Yet it was not a complete void. A single shaft of moonlight, as cold and as sharp as a blade, cut through the grime of the windowpane and fell directly upon the portrait. It illuminated the horror. It picked out the fresh, vile curve of the mouth, the glint of insufferable cunning in the painted eyes. The hypocrisy was now the most dominant feature on that face; it was a mask of virtue worn by vice, and it was a thousand times more repellent than the frank brutality of sin.

He stumbled back, his hands outstretched as if to ward off a physical blow. The thing on the canvas was a part of him, his very essence, and it was laughing at him. It was mocking his paltry attempt at goodness. It knew. It knew that his sacrifice had been a theatrical gesture, a new sensation to be explored. He had played at being a saint, just as he had played at being a sinner, and the portrait, his terrible, silent confessor, had recorded the falseness of the performance. The scarlet stain upon the hand seemed to glow with a more vivid vermilion, and the sneer was that of an actor who, having delivered a line about repentance, winks at the audience to let them know he does not mean a word of it.

All the past sins, the cruelty to Sibyl, the murder of Basil, the slow corruption of the young men who had called him friend, all these seemed almost clean, almost honest, in comparison to this new leprous spot of sanctimony. To be evil was one thing; but to pretend to be good for the sake of a new thrill, to use a girl’s heart as a stepping stone in an experiment of the soul, that was a refinement of degradation that even he had not known he was capable of. The portrait had known, however. It had been waiting.

A terrible, desolate loneliness swept over him. He was utterly alone with this foul homunculus that was his own spirit. He could not destroy it with goodness; he had just proved that. Every act, every thought, was simply fresh material for the painter of his ruin. He thought of Lord Henry, with his cool, poisonous phrases and his cynical wisdom. It was Harry who had first taught him to be afraid of nothing but ugliness, to worship his own youth. It was Harry who had given him the fatal book that had been a map to his own damnation. He could almost hear his voice now, languid and musical, turning this entire catastrophe into an epigram. “To be good is to be in harmony with oneself,” Harry would say. “Discord is to be forced to be in harmony with others. You tried to be in harmony with some rustic ideal, my dear Dorian, and naturally, you have produced a cacophony.”

And Basil. Poor, earnest Basil. He remembered the painter’s frantic pleading in this very room. He remembered the look of adoration that had once been in his eyes, an adoration that had soured into terror. Basil had created this thing. It was his genius that had given it life, his worship that had fixed Dorian’s soul to the canvas. In a twisted, spiralling logic of despair, Dorian began to blame the artist. If Basil had never painted the picture, he would have grown old like other men. His sins would have been written on his face; they would have dragged him down, and perhaps, in the end, he might have found some common, human path to repentance. But Basil, with his love and his art, had given him a terrible gift: a mask of eternal youth, behind which his soul could fester and rot unseen. Yes, it was Basil’s fault for creating it; it was Harry’s fault for telling him what to do with it; it was his own fault for being so beautiful that they could not leave him alone. The blame was a carousel, and he was sick from its endless spinning.

He moved through the dark room, his feet crunching on the shards of broken glass. His own face, pale in the moonlight, caught his eye in a fragment of a mirror that hung upon the wall. The beauty was still there. The crisp, golden hair; the clear, blue eyes; the finely chiselled lips. It was a lie. A beautiful, perfect lie. He looked from his own reflection to the monstrous thing behind the screen’s empty space, and a wave of nausea rose in his throat. One of them had to be destroyed. The hideous connection between the living, beautiful man and the aging, hideous painting had become a torment beyond endurance.

For years, the portrait had been a secret pleasure. It had been the spectator that Wilde had spoken of, allowing him to watch the drama of his own life with a detached, artistic interest. He had taken a morbid delight in the contrast, in the fact that he could sin and remain so fair. But now, that pleasure was gone. The spectator had become the judge. The record had become the sentence. He could no longer bear to live in the same world with it. He must end it.

He had killed the painter. Why not kill the painter’s work? A wild, desperate hope surged through him. If he destroyed the canvas, would the spell be broken? Would he be free? Perhaps his own face would suddenly wither, and the sins of his life would finally claim their rightful place upon his features. That would be a just punishment. He would accept it. To be old and ugly and hated seemed a paradise compared to this hourly terror, this communion with a monstrous other self. Or perhaps, in destroying the picture, he would destroy the record of his sins, and he would be pure again. The past would be annihilated. He could start anew. It did not matter which. All that mattered was the act of destruction. It was the only act left to him.

His mind was made up. A strange calm, the calm that precedes the final, irrevocable act, settled upon him. He knew what he had to do. He knew where the instrument was. He had not seen it since that dreadful night, but he knew it was there, tucked away in a carved chest where he kept things he wished to forget. He moved from the schoolroom, leaving the door ajar, the moonlight a solitary guardian over the jeering canvas. He walked down the silent corridor, his footsteps making no sound on the thick carpets. The house was asleep. The world was asleep. Only he and his soul were awake, and they were at war.

He found the chest and opened it. There it lay, on a bed of faded silk. The knife he had used to murder Basil Hallward. It was a beautiful object, with a long, thin blade and a handle of chased silver and ivory. He had cleaned it meticulously, but in his fevered imagination, he could see a faint, dark tarnish upon the steel, a stain that no amount of polishing could ever remove. It felt cold and heavy in his hand. It felt right.

He went back to the schoolroom. The air was thick with the scent of spilled oil from the lamp, a smell like incense in some foul temple. He walked directly to the portrait, the knife held firmly in his grasp. He did not look at his own face, but stared into the painted eyes. They met his gaze with their look of cunning and ancient evil. The scarlet lips seemed to part, as if to whisper some new and more terrible temptation.

“I shall kill you,” he whispered, his voice a dry rasp. “You are the past. You are my conscience. And I will not have a conscience.”

He thought of the moment Basil had looked upon his own masterpiece for the last time, the horror dawning in his eyes. Now, Dorian would give the painting its own final look. He would stab the evil in the heart, the way he had stabbed the good. There was a dreadful symmetry to it that appealed to his diseased sense of the dramatic. It would be an artistic ending.

He raised the knife high. The moonlight glinted on the blade. For a single, frozen instant, he saw the figure in the painting as it was, a record of a beautiful young man, full of promise, whom he had known once, and murdered long ago. A pang of something that was almost pity, almost grief, shot through him. It was a lament for his own lost innocence, for the boy Basil had loved.

Then the sneering, hypocritical mouth twisted again before his eyes, and his resolve hardened into a diamond point of hatred. He would kill this mocking witness. He would murder the soul that had tormented him. He would be free.

With a cry that was a mingling of triumph and despair, he lunged forward and plunged the knife into the heart of the painted figure.

There was a sound as of a canvas tearing, but it was followed instantly by a cry so dreadful in its agony that the sleeping servants started awake in their beds down below. It was a cry that seemed to come from no human throat, a shriek of pure, unadulterated pain that echoed through the silent, shuttered square. Then came a crash, a heavy, sickening thud, as of a body falling to the floor.

Silence.

Downstairs, the butler, Francis, and the two footmen listened, their faces pale in the gloom. They whispered to each other, their hands trembling, not daring to move. The cry had been too terrible. After a quarter of an hour of listening to the profound silence, Francis, his courage returning, decided they must investigate. He took one of the footmen with him. They crept up the grand staircase, their candles throwing long, dancing shadows on the walls. They tried the door to the old schoolroom, but it was locked from the inside. The cry had come from there. They called out. There was no answer.

They went down to the street and shouted up to the window, but it remained dark and silent. Finally, after fetching a constable from the corner, they made their way onto the roof of the adjoining house and, with some difficulty, clambered across to the balcony of the schoolroom. The window was unlocked. They pushed it open and stepped inside.

When they entered, they found, hanging upon the wall, a splendid portrait of their master as they had last seen him, in all the wonder of his exquisite youth and beauty. Lying on the floor was a dead man in evening dress, with a knife in his heart. He was withered, wrinkled, and loathsome of visage. It was not until they had examined the rings that they recognized who it was.

Dorian Gray was silent for a long moment. A feeling of immense relief had washed over him, so profound that it was almost a physical sensation. The secret was not about the painting’s miraculous change; it was merely about Basil’s foolish, romantic heart. The danger had passed. He felt a sharp, sudden pang of pity for the painter, for his earnest, clumsy confession, for the raw emotion in his face. It was the sort of pity one might feel for a bird with a broken wing. It was touching, but it was also a little contemptible. He had feared a judge, and he had found a supplicant. The sense of power that this gave him was intoxicating. He knew exactly what he must do.

He stepped forward, his own face a mask of beautiful, troubled sympathy. “Like you less, Basil?” he said, his voice a low caress. “My dear friend, how can you say such a thing? I am more moved than I can possibly say. To think that I have been all this to you... that my friendship has meant so much to your art. I never dreamed...”

He let his voice trail off, as if overcome with emotion. He laid a hand on the painter’s arm. “And now,” he continued, his tone deepening with a manufactured gravity, “you must let me tell you my secret. It is a strange counterpart to your own. You say you will not exhibit the picture because it reveals too much of you. My reason for hiding it is that it reveals too much of me. But not in the way you think.”

He paused, letting Basil’s expectant gaze rest upon him. “When you were painting it, Basil, I remember the things Harry used to say to me. He taught me to be vain of my good looks. He told me that my youth was the one thing worth having, and that this picture would mock me one day when I was old and wrinkled and horrible. He filled my head with a kind of poison. A wish that the picture could bear the burden of my passions and my sins, that it could grow old while I remained young. It was a wicked, foolish prayer, born of a moment of vanity.

“And then, after what happened with Sibyl... after I behaved so cruelly to her... I looked at the picture, and I fancied it had changed. There was a touch of cruelty about the mouth that had not been there before. It was my own imagination, of course. My guilt, my conscience, playing tricks on me. But I could not bear to look at it. It was a visible symbol of my own wickedness. It was like having one’s conscience outside of oneself, to mock and reproach one. That is why I covered it, Basil. I hid it because I could not bear to face the accusation I saw in its eyes. My secret is not one of magic, but of shame. I was hiding it from myself. And I was hiding it to protect you, too. I could not let the world see that beautiful work of yours marred, even in my own fevered imagination, by the shadow of my own cruelty. It was your masterpiece. I wanted to keep it pure.”

It was a performance of absolute genius, and Dorian knew it. He had taken the painter’s heartfelt confession and twisted it into a shield for his own monstrous secret, recasting his own selfish terror as a noble, sensitive act of friendship. He watched Basil’s face, and saw the confusion and doubt clear away, replaced by a look of profound, almost tearful gratitude.

“Dorian! My dear boy!” Hallward exclaimed, seizing both his hands. “I knew it! I knew there was something good and noble in you that I could not see. I have been so unjust to you. To think that you were suffering so, and that you did this to protect my work! Forgive me. I have been a brute. I should have understood. Of course, you felt it. The guilt must have been terrible. But you must not blame yourself so harshly. What you did was cruel, yes, but you are not wicked. It was a moment’s madness.”

“It has passed now,” Dorian said, lowering his eyes gracefully. “As I told you, I cannot repeat an emotion. But the memory, and the effect on the portrait... it is still too vivid. You must let me keep it hidden, Basil. At least for a time.”

“Hidden? Of course, my dear fellow! Keep it hidden forever, if you wish! I shall never ask to see it again. And as for exhibiting it, the idea is preposterous. I shall write to Georges Petit this very evening and tell him that it is impossible. The picture is yours. It has your secret in it, as well as my own. It must never be seen.” Hallward was beaming now, his kind, homely face alight with the joy of reconciliation. The horrible tension was gone. Dorian was his friend again, a sensitive, suffering, noble soul whom he had misjudged.

“You are too good to me, Basil,” Dorian murmured. He felt a flicker of genuine affection for the man he was deceiving so profoundly. It was so easy, so pleasant, to be liked, to be thought well of.

“And now,” said Hallward, his voice returning to its normal, cheerful key, “we will not speak of these dreadful things again. Tell me, what will you do with yourself this evening? Will you dine with me? We could go to the club afterwards.”

“I think not, Basil, thank you,” Dorian replied, a faint smile playing on his lips. “I have promised to go to the opera with Harry. He is determined to educate me in Wagner. He says that the prelude to _Lohengrin_ is the most perfect thing in modern art. I must see for myself if he is right.”

“Harry again,” Basil said, with a slight sigh, but there was no real malice in it. “Well, I suppose he amuses you. Do not let him spoil you, Dorian. There is so much in you that he could never understand.”

“Oh, Harry is not so bad. He simply says the things that other people only dare to think. There is a great deal of honesty in his cynicism. And besides, he makes me forget. You teach me to feel, Basil, and Harry teaches me to forget. One needs both, I think, to live a complete life.”

“Perhaps,” the painter said sadly. He picked up his hat and stick. “Well, I must be going. I am glad we have had this talk, Dorian. It has cleared the air between us. Remember, if you are ever in trouble, you must come to me.”

For several minutes after the door had closed, Dorian Gray did not move. He stood in the centre of the room, a slim, elegant figure, listening to the painter’s retreating footsteps on the staircase. A curious smile touched his lips. It was the smile of a chess player who has just checkmated his opponent in a difficult and brilliant game. He had been in mortal peril, and he had escaped not merely unharmed, but victorious. He had not only protected his secret, he had enhanced his reputation. Basil now saw him as a creature of exquisite, sorrowful sensitivity. The irony was delicious.

He felt no remorse for the lies he had told. They had been necessary. They had been, in their own way, a form of art. He had taken the raw, clumsy material of Basil’s confession and his own fear and moulded it into a beautiful, believable fiction. It was a triumph of the will. Harry would have been proud of him.

He walked slowly towards the screen, his heart beating with a new and strange excitement. He was no longer afraid of what lay behind it. He was curious. He wanted to see the result of his latest action. He wanted to see how his soul had recorded the afternoon’s work. With a steady hand, he took hold of the corner of the heavy brocade and pulled it aside.

He drew in his breath sharply. The change was unmistakable. The thing on the canvas was looking at him with a new expression. The faint trace of cruelty around the mouth was still there, but it had been overlaid with something else, something subtler and more terrible. It was a look of cunning, a sly, triumphant gleam of deceit in the eyes. The painted face was flushed with the pride of a successful lie. It was as if the portrait had been a silent observer of the entire scene with Basil, and had savoured every moment of the cruel deception. It was a loathsome, beautiful mockery of his own victory.

He stared at it, fascinated. The horror he had once felt was gone, replaced by a dark and thrilling curiosity. This portrait was more than a conscience; it was a diary. It was a record of his life, written in visible signs of passion and sin. It would absorb all the hideousness, all the degradation, and leave him untouched, forever young, forever beautiful. The bargain he had made in a fit of pique was real. He had a secret partner in his existence, a silent accomplice who would bear all the ugliness of his deeds.

A sense of absolute freedom surged through him. He was beyond judgment. He could explore every passion, taste every forbidden fruit, and the cost would be paid by another, by this painted image of himself. He laughed, a low, musical laugh. The figure on the canvas seemed to sneer back at him in silent complicity.

He must hide it, of course. Not out of fear, but out of prudence. It was too valuable, too dangerous, to be left here where a servant, or even Harry, might stumble upon it. He thought of the old schoolroom at the top of the house. It had been unused for years. No one ever went there. The key was on his own ring. It was the perfect place. There, he could lock it away and visit it in secret, to watch the strange, terrible progress of his soul.

He felt a sudden rush of energy. He would move it to-night. After the opera, when the house was asleep, he would carry his burden upstairs. He threw the cover back over the frame, his movements swift and decisive. The room, once more, looked as it always had, a picture of refined and elegant taste. But everything had changed. He had changed. He had played with fire and had not been burned. He had looked into the abyss and found it exhilarating.

He went to his dressing-table and began to arrange his tie for the evening. He looked at his own reflection in the mirror. The face that looked back at him was as flawless and innocent as ever. There was no trace of the afternoon’s lies, no shadow of the monstrous bargain he had just sealed with his own soul. He smiled. Prince Charming, Sibyl Vane had called him. The name seemed to him now wonderfully, terribly appropriate. He was a creature from a fairy tale, living a charmed life, while in a locked room at the top of a silent house, a magical portrait withered and aged and grew hideous with sin.

The curtain rose on the first act of the opera, but Dorian paid it little heed. The music was a pleasant background to the far more interesting drama of his own thoughts. He watched the people in the theatre, the flushed faces, the earnest expressions, the little gestures of boredom or delight. How remote they all seemed! They felt things, they suffered, they grew old and lined and ugly with their petty emotions. He alone was exempt. He felt a thrill of pride, of godlike isolation. He had made a pact with destiny, and he had won.

He thought of the picture hidden behind the screen in his library. It was waiting for him. It was his other self, the self that would bear the scars of his experiences. While he sat here, in the perfumed, glittering theatre, perhaps a new line was etching itself around the painted mouth, a shadow of cruelty was darkening the painted eyes. The thought sent a shiver of delicious horror through him.

When the opera was over, they went to the club. Dorian drank more than he usually did, his conversation brilliant with a new, hard recklessness. He repeated many of Lord Henry’s own paradoxes, but gave them a personal, more passionate twist. He felt exhilarated, on fire with a sense of infinite possibility.

It was very late when he returned home. He dismissed his sleepy-eyed valet and went at once to the library. The house was profoundly silent. He locked the door and pulled the screen away from the portrait. In the dim light of the single lamp he had lit, the face seemed to have changed. It was not his imagination. The curve of the mouth was different; there was a touch of cruelty in it, a subtle, almost imperceptible sneer. The beauty was still there, but it was a beauty that was beginning to curdle. He stared at it, fascinated. The thing was a living record of his own soul.

He felt a sudden, urgent need to hide it, to put it away where no human eye but his could ever look upon it again. The schoolroom. Yes, that was the place. He covered the picture again with the heavy purple pall, the same one that had draped his grandfather’s coffin. The rich, sombre fabric seemed horribly appropriate.

The portrait was large and heavy, framed in ornate, gilded wood. It was a difficult burden. He struggled with it, his breath coming in short gasps. The physical effort was strangely grounding after the feverish excitement of the evening. He carried it out of the library and began the slow, arduous journey up the great oak staircase. The house was full of shadows. The moonlight, slanting through the tall hall window, cast a ghostly pattern on the polished floor. His own shadow, distorted and monstrous with its burden, seemed to creep up the wall beside him like a guilty thing.

Each creak of the floorboards sounded like a cannon shot in the stillness. He imagined the servants waking, a door opening, a curious face peering out. A cold sweat broke out on his forehead. He was not afraid of being discovered, not in the way a common thief would be. He was afraid of being questioned. How could he explain this midnight pilgrimage, this furtive hiding of his own likeness?

He reached the top floor and made his way down a long, narrow corridor he had not traversed in years. At the very end was the door to the old schoolroom. He set the picture down, leaning it against the wall, and fumbled in his pocket for his key-ring. The key was old and rusted. It grated in the lock with a coarse, final sound that echoed in the profound stillness.

He pushed the door open. A wave of cool, musty air flowed out to meet him, thick with the scent of dust and decay, the ghosts of forgotten lessons. He lit a candle he had brought with him, and its small flame threw vast, dancing shadows across the room. It was just as he remembered it, but shabbier, sadder. A battered rocking-horse with one eye missing stood in a corner. A globe of the world, its colours faded and its surface scratched, was perched precariously on a bookshelf. Beside it was a row of tattered schoolbooks, their bindings broken: a Latin grammar, a history of England, a book of fairy tales his mother used to read to him. For a moment, a wave of poignant nostalgia swept over him. He remembered himself as a boy in this room, tracing the voyages of famous explorers on the globe, dreaming of a life of adventure. His eyes filled with tears. How innocent he had been then. What had happened to that boy?

The sight of the purple-draped canvas leaning against the door-jamb brought him back to the present with a jolt. That boy was gone forever. He had been sacrificed, and the thing on the canvas was the altar upon which the sacrifice had been made. The mood of tenderness vanished, replaced by a cold, practical resolve.

He dragged the heavy portrait into the room and leaned it against the far wall. Then he looked around for something to cover it with. An old Indian shawl, its pattern of faded paisleys eaten away by moths, was draped over a broken chair. He took it and carefully spread it over the picture, tucking the edges in so that not an inch of the frame was visible.

There. It was done. His secret was safe. His soul was locked away in this forgotten room, and he was free. He felt an immense sense of relief, a wild surge of triumph. He locked the door behind him and put the key back in his pocket. He would visit it from time to time, he thought, to watch the hideous progress of his own decay. It would be a new and terrible kind of pleasure.

As he walked back to his own luxurious rooms, he felt as though he had passed through some great crisis. The air in the rest of the house seemed fresher, the carpets softer under his feet. He had put the ugliness of life away from him.

On a small table by his bed, a package was waiting for him. It had been delivered by a messenger from Lord Henry’s house. Dorian picked it up. It was a book, bound in yellow paper, its cover undecorated. There was a note attached.

“My dear boy,” it ran, “I am sending you a book I think you will find fascinating. It has poisoned many a young man’s mind, and I am curious to see what it will do to yours. It is a psychological study of a certain young Parisian who spent his life trying to realize in the nineteenth century all the passions and modes of thought that belonged to every century except his own. I am sure you will like it. I see you in every line of it. Yours, in this world and the next, Harry.”

Dorian sat down and began to read. He forgot the time, forgot his weariness, forgot everything but the strange, decadent world that was unfolding before him. The sentences were like jewels, exquisitely wrought and full of a strange, sick-sweet perfume. The sins of the world seemed to pass in review before the book’s hero, but with the sound of flutes and the scent of ambergris. It seemed to Dorian that in exquisite prose the book was telling the story of his own life before he had even lived it. It was a poison, a delightful, slow-acting poison that clouded the mind with strange reveries and filled the soul with a wicked curiosity for all the things it had been taught to fear.

He read on, and on, until the dawn crept into the room and touched his pale face and the gilded pages of the book. The birds began to sing in the garden, but he did not hear them. He had found a new bible. Lord Henry had given him a philosophy of life; this book gave him a programme. The world was his to explore, and he had a map. He looked at his own reflection in the mirror opposite. The face that looked back was still flawless, still pure. And in the locked room at the top of the house, the canvas was waiting to pay the price. A smile of pleasure lingered on his lips. The future was a wonderful, limitless thing.
