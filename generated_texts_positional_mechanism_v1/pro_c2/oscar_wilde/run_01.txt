Of course I am wretched. But my wretchedness is not a thing for public display, a common grief to be shared and diluted amongst sympathizers. I remember finding a slim volume of French poetry in your library, bound in green leather, and seeing the phrase _la poésie de la perte_. I adore beautiful sensations that one can absorb. Eleanor felt a strange pang of pity. The young man was a vital part of her world, and his intellect had been a constant delight. She could not bring herself to censure him further. It was the mark of his own cruelty. His cold, dismissive words to Eleanor about the death of Sibyl, the girl whose love he had cultivated and then carelessly destroyed, had left their stain. This was his secret. This was the monstrous bargain he had made on the day he had first played this violin, when he had poured all his anguish and desire into its strings and wished, with all the fervour of his being, that the instrument might bear the burdens of his life, while he retained the flawless purity of his youth and his art. It was a thrill of scientific curiosity, a flicker of artistic delight in the sheer horror of it all. He was to be the spectator of his own life, and here was the tangible record of its spiritual cost, displayed for his eyes only. The violin was no longer just an instrument; it was his diary, his conscience, the visible picture of his soul. To destroy it would be to destroy the most interesting part of his own experiment. He stood for a moment in the hall, listening, his heart beating with a slow, triumphant rhythm. He had won. He had faced down the two greatest threats to his secret life: the exposure of the portrait, and the exposure of his own soul. He had manipulated Basil’s profoundest love into a shield for his own monstrous secret. He had played upon the man’s heartstrings as if they were the strings of a violin, and the music had been entirely to his own liking.

He went back into the library. The room felt different, larger, more silent. He was the sole occupant now, the master of the stage. He walked slowly, deliberately, over to the corner where the great screen stood. He felt no fear now, only an intense, burning curiosity. His lie to Basil, his story of morbid vanity, had been a work of art. But the truth was so much more fantastic, so much more terrible and wonderful.

With a steady hand, he took hold of the edge of the screen and pulled it aside.

The portrait was there, glowing with a rich, internal light under the silk coverlet he had thrown over it. He drew the cover away. And then he saw it.

It was no longer a matter of imagination, no longer a subtle hint that only his own guilty conscience could perceive. The change was definite. It was unmistakable. Around the beautifully painted mouth, the line of cruelty had deepened. It was as if the lips were pulled back in a permanent, silent sneer. And in the eyes, those marvellous blue eyes that Basil had captured with such genius, there was a new look. A glimmer of cunning. It was the face of a man who had just committed a foul and subtle sin of the spirit, a man who had taken a pure and sincere love and had twisted it for his own selfish ends.

He looked from the sneering mouth on the canvas to his own reflection in a nearby mirror. His own lips were curved in a faint smile, as innocent as a child’s. His own eyes were clear and untroubled. The picture had taken the stain of his action, had absorbed the ugliness of his soul.

A thrill of pure, unadulterated joy shot through him. It was true. It was all true. His mad wish had been granted. He was safe. He could sin, and his sins would leave no mark upon him. They would mar the canvas instead. They would write their terrible diary upon the painted face, while he, the real Dorian, would remain forever young, forever beautiful, a thing for the world to marvel at.

He thought of his conversation with Basil. Basil’s idolatry. Basil’s confession. It had been a beautiful thing, in its way. But it was a thing of the past now. He had moved beyond it. Basil was a part of the life he was leaving behind, the life of simple emotions and moral anxieties. He was entering a new world, the world Harry had spoken of, a world of beautiful sensations and exquisite sins, where the soul was a thing to be observed, not a thing to be cared for.

The portrait was an artist’s confession of love. Now it was the diary of a soul’s damnation. And he, Dorian Gray, was the author of both.

He stood before it for a long time, drinking in the horror and the beauty of it. This was his true self. This was the reality. The flawless young man who walked the streets of London was the illusion. He felt a sudden desire to add to the diary, to give the painter on the canvas something new to record. He thought of the opera, of the people he would see there tonight. He would go. He would sit in his box, and smile, and be adored. He would be the perfect gentleman, the glass of fashion. And all the while, he would know that here, in this locked room, his silent accomplice was growing a little more hideous, a little more corrupt. The thought gave him a feeling of exquisite pleasure. It was a secret that made all of life a thrilling, dangerous adventure. He carefully replaced the silk coverlet and pulled the screen back into its place, hiding the terrible miracle from the world. Then he went to his room to dress for the evening. He was looking forward to the night. He had a new passion, a new art form to practice: the art of living. And he had a feeling he was going to be a master of it.

Dorian Gray watched him without a flicker of pity. He felt a thrill of power, a sense of immense and terrible freedom. Basil’s secret was a weapon, and he knew, instinctively, how to use it. He had wanted a strange quarter of an hour, and he had got it. He had played his card, and he had won.

When the door had closed behind the painter, Dorian Gray stood for a moment in the centre of the room, a triumphant look in his eyes. He had been magnificent. He had played upon Basil’s deepest feelings as if they were the keys of a musical instrument. He had taken the raw, messy passion of his friend and had turned it to his own advantage, using it as a shield for his own far darker secret. It was a kind of artistry in itself.

He walked over to the screen and drew it aside. He had to see what effect his cruelty had had upon the canvas. For it had been cruel, he knew that. To take a man’s sincere and profound affection and to use it so coldly, so deliberately, was a sin against the spirit. He looked at the picture, and a gasp of pleasure escaped his lips.

The change was subtle, yet unmistakable. Around the beautiful, scarlet lips there was a new line, a line of hypocrisy. In the depths of the wonderful blue eyes, a faint, almost imperceptible glint of cunning had appeared. The whole expression of the face had shifted, as if the soul behind it were learning to wear a mask. It was fascinating, utterly fascinating. The picture was absorbing the moral stains of his life, leaving him physically pure and untouched. It was his silent accomplice, his partner in a new and thrilling game.

He felt a sudden, sharp pang of contempt for Basil. Poor Basil! To suffer so, to be so torn by his emotions. How much better to be a spectator of one’s own life, as Harry had said. To treat one’s soul as a subject for study, not as a thing to be wrestled with. Basil was still chained to the old world of right and wrong, of conscience and remorse. Dorian had broken those chains. He was free.

He covered the picture again, carefully, reverently. It was his most precious possession now. It was the diary of his soul, and he looked forward to filling its pages. He went to his dressing room, feeling a sense of delicious anticipation. The world was spread before him like a banquet, and he was ready to taste every dish. He chose his evening clothes with particular care: a white tie, a perfectly starched shirt, a gardenia in his buttonhole. He was a work of art himself, a flawless thing of beauty. And as he looked at his own reflection in the mirror, at the unspoiled innocence of his face, he smiled. The night was young, and he had a rendezvous with sin.

The hansom cab dropped him at the doors of the Opera. It was a gala night, and the entrance was thronged with the glittering, chattering world of London society. Diamonds flashed, silks rustled, and the air was heavy with the scent of perfume and flowers. Dorian moved through the crowd with his accustomed grace, acknowledging greetings with a slight inclination of his head, a charming, boyish smile playing on his lips. He felt a sense of exhilaration, of being at the very centre of life.

In his box, he found Lord Henry, lounging in a gilt chair and examining the audience through his opera glasses.

“Ah, Dorian,” he said, without turning. “You are late. I have been terribly bored. The music is mediocre, and the soprano has a profile that would justify a homicidal impulse. But the audience is quite good. Full of wonderfully ugly people. It is a great comfort to see that one is not the only one who has made a mistake in choosing one’s parents.”

Dorian laughed. “I have been detained, Harry. I have had a scene with Basil.”

Lord Henry put down his opera glasses and looked at Dorian with a new interest. “Did he indeed? How very interesting. And how very like Basil. He has all the passion of a poet, and all the cowardice of a man who is afraid of his own poetry. So you have discovered your power, Dorian. The tragedy of Sibyl Vane, the grief of Basil Hallward, seemed like things that had happened to someone else, in another life. They were simply material for an epigram, a source of aesthetic sensation.

During the interval, their box was filled with visitors. Dorian was the centre of attention. Young men with brilliant futures and old men with scandalous pasts vied for his favour. Women of the highest rank and the most dubious reputation watched him with hungry eyes. He was witty, charming, and utterly heartless. He talked of art, of fashion, of the latest society scandal, of everything and of nothing. He played the part of the beautiful, idle young man to perfection.

He found himself talking to Lady Narborough, a woman with a sharp tongue and an insatiable curiosity. “Mr. Gray,” she said, tapping him with her fan, “I hear you have taken up a new protégé. A young actress at some dreadful little theatre in the East End.”

Dorian felt a flicker of annoyance, but his face remained serene. “One must have an interest in the arts, Lady Narborough. It is the only thing that makes modern life tolerable.”

A cold anger took possession of Dorian. He looked at the foolish, painted woman before him, and he felt a desire to wound her, to say something that would pierce her thick armour of self-satisfaction. But he checked himself. That would be a mistake. It would be an emotion, and emotions were for the audience, not for the actor. He smiled his most charming smile.

“It was a great tragedy, Lady Narborough,” he said, his voice full of a beautiful, artificial sadness. “She was a true artist. She had played Juliet, and she had played Imogen, and she had played Rosalind. She had known all the great heroines of the stage. But she had never known love. When it came to her, it was too overwhelming. She could not bear the reality, after having lived for so long in the world of illusion. So she chose to die. It was her last, and her greatest, performance. A tribute to the art of which she was a priestess. One should not pity her. One should envy her. She has achieved a perfect and artistic death.”

There was a moment of silence. Lady Narborough stared at him, a little taken aback. Lord Henry watched, an amused smile on his lips. Dorian felt a thrill of triumph. He had turned the death of Sibyl Vane into an aesthetic theory, a beautiful, cruel paradox. He had taken his own guilt and had transformed it into a work of art.

“My dear Dorian,” said Lord Henry, as Lady Narborough moved away, looking rather bewildered, “that was a masterpiece. You are becoming a true cynic. I congratulate you.”

“I was merely stating the facts, Harry,” said Dorian, with a shrug. “It is for you to draw the moral.”

He left the opera before the end, pleading a headache. He could not bear the noise and the chatter any longer. He was filled with a feverish excitement, a desperate need to be alone. He walked home through the deserted streets, the cool night air on his face. He felt as if he were walking in a dream.

When he reached his house, he went at once to the library. He locked the door and, taking a lamp, he went behind the screen. He pulled back the coverlet with a trembling hand.

He cried out in horror and in joy. The thing on the canvas was monstrous. The line of hypocrisy around the mouth had deepened into a hideous sneer. The glint of cunning in the eyes had become a flame of malevolent intelligence. But there was something more. From the pure, white brow, a faint wrinkle seemed to be starting, the mark of a weary and sated cynicism. The whole face was coarsening, hardening. The beauty was still there, but it was the beauty of a fallen angel, the beauty of a thing that had once been divine, and was now damned.

He stood before it for a long time, drinking in every terrible detail. This was his true self. This was the record of his life. Each sin, each selfish act, each moment of cruelty, would be written here, in this secret diary of the soul, while he, the beautiful Dorian Gray, would walk the world, untouched, unspoiled, eternally young.

A feeling of immense power, of godlike freedom, surged through him. He was the master of his own fate. He could drain the cup of life to the dregs, and it would be another who would pay the price. He laughed, a low, triumphant laugh that echoed in the silent room. The world was his. And he would make it his kingdom. He had found the secret of eternal youth, and he would use it to explore every secret of sin. The portrait was his passport to a new and wonderful world, a world of endless sensation and exquisite corruption. And he was ready to begin his journey.

He delivered the lines with a perfect pathos. He was playing a part, and he knew he was playing it well. He watched Basil’s face, and saw the confusion and amazement there turn to a deep, sorrowful pity.

“Dorian, my dear boy,” Hallward stammered, deeply moved. “If I had known, if I had only had an inkling of this! I would not have done it. Forgive me. I see now that my art has caused you pain. It was a foolish vanity on my part to want to exhibit it. Of course, it shall not go to Paris. I shall write to Georges Petit to-night. “Of course I understand,” replied the painter earnestly. “Art should not be a source of suffering. And now, I must go. This has all been very trying. But we are friends still, Dorian, are we not?”

“Always, Basil,” said Dorian, with a charming smile. He walked with him to the door, a sense of profound relief washing over him. The danger was passed. His secret was safe, for the moment.

When the door had closed behind the painter, Dorian Gray returned to the library, a look of triumph on his face. He locked the door. His first act was to go to the large Florentine cabinet where he kept his letters and papers, and from one of its secret drawers, he took out a key. It was a key to a room at the very top of the house, a room that had not been entered for nearly five years. It had been his childhood playroom, and later his schoolroom. He remembered it with a curious distaste. It was a long, high-ceilinged chamber, with two large grated windows that looked out over the leads. In one corner stood his old Italian cassone, with its fantastically painted panels and its tarnished gilt mouldings, in which he had so often hidden as a boy. The walls were covered with a faded Flemish tapestry, where a company of dim figures hunted a crowned king through a dusky forest. The books of his boyhood were still there, their pages thumbed and ink-stained.

This was the place. Here his secret would be safe. The portrait could be locked away, hidden from every eye but his own. The screen was not enough; a servant might move it, a guest might glance behind it. But a locked room, a room to which only he held the key, that was security. That was freedom.

He rang the bell for his valet. When the man entered, Dorian gave him a note to take at once to a certain frame-maker in the city. Then he wrote a second letter, this one to Lord Henry, telling him that he was sorry for his abrupt departure from the opera, and that he would join him for dinner the following evening. He felt a sudden, sharp pang of loneliness. Basil was good and noble, but he was tedious. It was Harry, with his dangerous philosophies and his exquisite cynicism, that he craved. It was Harry who would understand, not the crime, but the beauty of his new position.

The next morning, before his valet was awake, Dorian dressed and slipped out of the house. He went to the address he had given, and waited for the frame-maker’s men. He had hired two of them to come to his home. He wanted no one from his own household to be involved. He walked back with them, and led them up the great oak staircase, and through the corridors, to the library.

“I want you to move this picture for me,” he said, pointing to the large, screen-covered object. “Take it to the room at the top of the house. Be very careful with it. It is extremely valuable.”

The men were stout and accustomed to such work. They removed the screen, revealing the painting still shrouded in its heavy cloth. Dorian held his breath, but they handled it with a professional indifference, seeing only a large, covered canvas. They hoisted it upon their shoulders and began the slow ascent. Dorian followed, the key clutched in his hand. He felt a grotesque sense of parody, as if he were in a funeral procession, carrying his own soul to its grave.

Up the stairs they went, their heavy boots echoing on the polished wood. They passed the portraits of his ancestors, the men and women of the house of Gray, with their cold, aristocratic faces. They seemed to watch him with stony disapproval. At last they reached the top landing and the door to the old schoolroom. Dorian unlocked it. A wave of dusty, stale air met them. The room was just as he remembered it, a place of ghosts and forgotten joys. The sunlight, struggling through the grimy panes of the grated windows, fell in pale bars across the floor, illuminating dancing motes of dust.

“Place it there,” he said, pointing to the far corner, beside the great cassone.

When the men had set the picture down and departed, Dorian locked the door behind them. He was alone with his secret at last. With a trembling hand, he approached the corner where the painting stood. He needed a pall for it, something to cover its shame. He remembered the cassone. He crossed the room and lifted the heavy lid. Inside, among a litter of old school prizes and discarded toys, lay a large piece of silk brocade, a magnificent cover that his grandfather had brought back from Venice. It was a rich, imperial purple, heavily embroidered with gold thread, the work of some sixteenth-century artist. It was the sort of cloth that priests use for the draping of an altar, or that kings might use for a funeral canopy. It was perfect.

He took out the splendid coverlet and carried it over to the portrait. As he was about to throw it over the canvas, a sudden impulse of curiosity seized him. He had not looked at it since the previous evening. Had it changed again? He had to see. He pulled back the shroud he had placed over it himself, and a cry of mingled horror and delight broke from his lips.

The thing on the canvas was even more dreadful than before. The cruelty in the mouth had become more pronounced, a line of infinite contempt. The eyes held a look of cunning satisfaction. It was as if the portrait were gloating over the death of Sibyl Vane, and over the cleverness with which he, Dorian, had acquitted himself in his conversation with Basil. The face was the face of a satyr. He could see the stain of his own hypocrisy writ large upon that beautiful visage, marring its perfection.

He stared at it, fascinated. This loathsome thing was the reality. He himself was the illusion. What a wonderful life awaited him! He could sin, and it would be the canvas that bore the burden of his shame. He could pursue every pleasure, taste every forbidden fruit, and he would remain forever as Basil had painted him, a creature of exquisite and unblemished youth.

He threw the magnificent purple and gold pall over the picture. It was like covering a corpse. The rich fabric fell in graceful folds, hiding the monstrous face from view. He felt a sudden sense of liberation. The horror was hidden away. He stepped back and surveyed his work. The corner of the dusty room was now transformed by a patch of glorious colour, a strange and splendid stain on the grey dimness.

He turned and left the room, locking the door carefully behind him. He went down the stairs, the key now safely in his pocket. He felt happier than he had for days. The sense of being watched, of being followed by his own conscience, was gone. The portrait was his secret diary, and it was locked away. He could now begin to live.

In the library, a parcel was waiting for him. It was a book that Lord Henry had sent over, bound in yellow paper, its pages uncut. He listlessly picked it up. He found a paper knife, a slender dagger of dull steel with a handle of jade, and began to cut the leaves. It was a strange and poisonous novel. It told the story of a certain young Parisian who had spent his life trying to realize in the nineteenth century all the passions and modes of thought that belonged to every century but his own; to sum up, as it were, in himself the various moods through which the world-spirit had ever passed, loving for their own sake the sacred and the profane, the beautiful and the grotesque, the just and the unjust. The style in which it was written was that curious jewelled style, vivid and obscure at once, full of argot and of archaisms, of technical terms and of elaborate paraphrases, that is the characteristic of the works of some of the finest artists of the French school of _décadents_. There were in it metaphors as monstrous as orchids and as subtle in colour. The life of the senses was described in the terms of mystical philosophy. One hardly knew at times whether one was reading the spiritual ecstasies of some mediaeval saint or the morbid confessions of a modern sinner. It was a poisonous book. The heavy odour of incense seemed to cling to its pages and to trouble the brain. The mere cadence of the sentences, the subtle monotony of their music, so full as it was of complex refrains and movements elaborately repeated, produced in the mind of the lad, as he passed from chapter to chapter, a form of reverie, a malady of dreaming, that made him unconscious of the falling day and the creeping shadows.

He read on, and the hours passed unnoticed. Clouds of opium seemed to hang about his senses. The world outside, the world of duty and of simple feeling, seemed to fade away, to become a distant, meaningless echo. The weight on his back seemed to increase with every step, a physical manifestation of the spiritual burden he had now shouldered for all time.

He was in the old schoolroom. It was just as he remembered it, only sadder and more decayed. Dust lay thick as velvet on every surface. He felt a sudden, sharp pang of something that was almost regret. This room was the tomb of his boyhood. It was a place of innocence, a place of simple sorrows and simple joys. The diary of his life would write its terrible entries in this locked and forgotten chamber, and no eye but his would ever look upon its degradation.

He felt a great sense of relief, a profound and weary peace. He locked the door of the schoolroom and put the key in his pocket. Then, without a backward glance, he made his way downstairs. The house was still silent. In the library, he found the sandwiches and the decanter of hock that Victor had left for him. He poured a glass of the pale, amber wine and drank it down in one draught. The physical exertion had left him exhausted, but his mind was clear and sharp.

He caught sight of himself in the great rococo mirror above the fireplace. His hair was dishevelled, his face was pale, and there was a faint smudge of dust on his cheek. But his beauty was undiminished. He looked like a young Greek martyr, some pale saint who had just passed through a night of spiritual terror. The image pleased his vanity.

I knew that I was putting something into the canvas that was more than just paint, more than just skill. I was putting my own self into it, my own worship, my own… adoration. It is all there, Dorian. Every line and curve of it speaks of my folly. I felt that I had revealed too much. I had shown the world the secret of my own soul. And that is why I would not exhibit it. I could not have callous, indifferent eyes gazing upon the evidence of my idolatry. I could not have the world mock my art for being too personal, or, worse still, understand it. It would have been like posting my own heart in the marketplace.”

He fell silent, his face pale and strained. He had said it. The terrible secret was out, and he looked at Dorian with an expression of desperate appeal, like a prisoner awaiting his sentence.

For a moment, Dorian felt a flicker of pity for the man who stood before him, so utterly exposed. But it was only a flicker. It was drowned in a great, rising wave of triumph. The fear that had gripped him only moments before vanished completely. He was no longer the one at a disadvantage. He had Basil’s secret, and it was a weapon, a shield, a key. He saw in an instant how he could use it. The last piece of the puzzle of his new life clicked into place.

“Basil,” he said, and his voice was cold and clear as ice. “So, that is your reason. An artist’s strange worship. It is a curious confession.” He smiled, a slow, calculating smile that did not reach his eyes. “It seems we both have our secrets, as I said. You have put too much of yourself into your work, and you are afraid the world will see it. Well, perhaps something similar has happened to me. Perhaps the portrait has a secret of its own now. A secret that I must keep.”

Hallward stared at him, bewildered by the sudden change in his tone. The warmth, the pleading, the boyish charm; it was all gone. In its place was a chilling self-possession. “A secret of its own? What do you mean, Dorian? It is a painting. It is ink and canvas. What secret can it have, except the one I put there?”

“Ah, that is my affair,” Dorian answered lightly, turning away and picking up a lily from a vase on the table. He inhaled its scent with a theatrical air of pleasure. “Let us just say that I have my own reasons for not wishing you, or anyone else, to look at it. Reasons as compelling as your own. We are in the same position, my dear Basil. We have both created something that reveals too much. Let this be our pact. Your secret is safe with me. I shall never breathe a word of your… idolatry. And in return, you must never again ask to see my portrait. You must never touch that screen. Is it a bargain?”

The painter felt a dreadful coldness creep into his heart. He had offered up his soul, and Dorian had received it not with sympathy, or even with horror, but as a bargaining chip. The lad he had known, the innocent, beautiful boy, was gone. A stranger stood in his place, a stranger with his face but with eyes that held a new and terrible knowledge. “A bargain?” he repeated, his voice hollow. “You speak of it as if it were a matter of commerce.”

“Everything is a matter of commerce,” Dorian replied, echoing one of Lord Henry’s aphorisms without even realizing it. “We are all trading something. You have traded your peace of mind for your art. I am merely trading my silence for my privacy. It is perfectly fair. So, we are agreed? You will not mention this again. And you will not attempt to look behind that screen. Ever.”

There was an absolute finality in his tone. Hallward felt utterly defeated. He had lost something precious, something he could never regain. The simple, beautiful friendship was over. It had been replaced by this strange, dark contract. “As you wish, Dorian,” he said, his shoulders slumping. “I will not ask again. I have no right.”

“Excellent,” said Dorian, his good humour seemingly restored. “Now, let us not be so dreadfully serious. The world is made for pleasure, not for morbid introspection. You will stay for lunch, of course? My man has found some quite remarkable ortolans.”

“No,” said Hallward, shaking his head. He picked up his hat and gloves from the chair where he had thrown them. “No, I cannot stay. I must be going. I… I have work to do.” He walked towards the door, his steps heavy. At the threshold, he paused and looked back. Dorian was still standing by the table, the lily in his hand, a figure of perfect, untroubled grace. A wave of the old affection, mixed with a new and profound sorrow, washed over the painter. “Good-bye, Dorian,” he said softly. “Try to be good.”

“I will try to be happy, Basil,” Dorian called after him with a light laugh. “That is much more important. Do give my regards to the Royal Academy. I am sure they miss your genius.”

The door closed, and Dorian was alone.

The silence in the great room was absolute. For a moment, he did not move. He stood listening to the retreating footsteps of the painter on the stairs, and then the sound of the front door closing. He was gone. The last link to his old life, the last voice that might have called him back, had been severed.

An immense sense of liberation surged through him. He had won. He had faced down Basil, defended his secret, and asserted his own will. He felt a thrill of power he had never known before. He had played a scene from life as if it were a scene from a play, and he had been the master of it. He was the director, the protagonist, the critic, all in one. To become the spectator of one’s own life. Harry had been right. It was the only way to escape the suffering.

He dropped the lily on the lacquered tabletop. Its petals bruised against the hard surface. He walked slowly, deliberately, towards the corner of the room. His heart was not pounding with fear now, but with a strange, exhilarating curiosity. He placed his hand upon the rich, brocaded fabric of the screen. He paused, savouring the moment, the exquisite tension of it. Then, with a slow, steady movement, he pulled the screen aside.

He drew in his breath sharply.

The portrait was there, on the easel, just as Basil had left it. But it was not the same. It was hideously, horribly changed. It was not merely the faint touch of cruelty about the mouth that he had noticed before. Now, it was a definite sneer. A line of hypocrisy seemed to have deepened the curve of the lips. The eyes, once so clear and full of wonder, now held a glint of cunning, of a sly, secret knowledge. It was as if some foul disease of the soul were beginning to fester and rot upon the painted canvas. Every beautiful lineament was subtly altered, coarsened, degraded. The exquisite harmony of the face was marred by a new and jarring note of ugliness.

He turned and walked over to a large, oval mirror that was set in the wall. He looked at his own reflection. There he was, the same Dorian Gray whose beauty was the wonder of his time. The crisp, gold hair curled upon his forehead. The frank, blue eyes met his own. The finely-carved lips were as yet untouched by any sign of vice or age. He was flawless, perfect. He was a thing of immortal youth and beauty.

He looked back at the portrait. The contrast was appalling. That grotesque and leering face on the canvas was the true mirror of his soul. That was what he was becoming. All the ugly thoughts, the selfish impulses, the callous cruelty he had shown to Sibyl, the cold manipulation he had just used against Basil, they were all there, recorded in sickening detail upon the painted face, while his own remained a pure and perfect mask.

A shudder of horror passed through him, but it was followed almost at once by a feeling of intense, perverse joy. This was the magic he had wished for! This was the fulfilment of his wild prayer. He could be young forever, beautiful forever, while this silent, painted thing bore the burden of his passions and his sins. He could drain the cup of life to the dregs, and his own lips would never be stained. He could pursue every pleasure, explore every vice, and his own face would never betray him. The portrait would be the record of his degradation. He, himself, would remain untouched.

He felt a sudden, wild love for the picture. It was his truest self. It was his diary, his conscience, his visible soul. He must protect it, guard it, keep it from all prying eyes. No one must ever see it again. Not even himself, perhaps, except on certain, special occasions when he wished to study the progress of his own decay.

The thought of Basil exhibiting it in Paris now seemed a profanity. The thought of anyone seeing it was an impossibility. It must be hidden away, somewhere safe, somewhere secret. He thought of the old schoolroom at the very top of the house. It had been unused for years. No one ever went there. It had a heavy door and a strong lock. It was the perfect place. He would take it there himself, that very night. He would lock it away and keep the key. It would be his most treasured, his most terrible possession.

He felt a new sense of purpose. The day was no longer empty. He had a task to perform. He carefully drew the screen back into its place, hiding the dreadful face from view. The room looked normal again, peaceful and luxurious. He glanced at the clock on the mantelpiece. It was past three. The afternoon was waning.

A footman entered silently, bearing a small silver tray. On it was a note. Dorian took it. The handwriting was familiar, a strange, spidery script he had come to know well over the past few weeks. It was from Lord Henry. He broke the seal.

“My dearest boy,” it ran, “what a tedious afternoon. The world is utterly given over to virtue. I have just been to a meeting of a society for the suppression of everything interesting. I feel I need a dose of sin to restore my faith in humanity. Let us dine together tonight. There is a new chef at the Café Royal who is said to be a poet, and his salads are sonnets. Then we can go on to the Alhambra. I have heard there is a Spanish dancer there who has all the magnificent insolence of a fallen angel. Do not mope. The only thing to do with a tragedy is to forget it, and the only way to forget it is to replace it with a sensation. I shall expect you at eight. Yours, as ever, Harry.”

Dorian smiled. Harry was always right. Sensation. That was what he needed. Not thought, not memory, not guilt. Just the thrill of the new. He would go. He would dine with Harry. He would watch the Spanish dancer. He would plunge himself into the vibrant, colourful, godless life of the city. He would begin his new existence tonight.

He went to his writing-desk and penned a quick reply, accepting the invitation. Then he went upstairs to his room to dress for the evening. As he stood before his mirror, selecting a cravat, he looked at his own face with a new sense of ownership. It was a mask, a beautiful, perfect mask, and he could do what he pleased behind it. The tragedy of Sibyl Vane, the sorrow of Basil Hallward, they were but shadows, passing moments in the great drama of his life. He felt wonderfully, terribly free. He had sold his soul, and he was determined to get a very good price for it. The future stretched before him, a vast, uncharted sea of pleasure, and he set sail upon it without a single backward glance.

This was the key to Basil’s strange moods, his passionate rebukes, his devoted friendship. It was not friendship at all, but something far more potent, far more dangerous. It was a weakness. And Dorian, in that moment, realized with a cold clarity that the weaknesses of others were the greatest source of his own strength. He had power over Basil now, a power absolute and undeniable. The confession had not made him like the painter less; it had simply made the painter less important. He was no longer a mentor or a friend, but a subject, a worshipper at a shrine of which Dorian himself was the deity.

He walked over to the weeping artist and placed a hand gently on his shoulder. The gesture was graceful, perfect in its feigned compassion. “Despise you?” he said, his voice low and rich with a tone of tender reproach. “My dear, dear Basil, how little you know me. Hallward looked up, his eyes moist and full of a pathetic gratitude. “Dorian… you mean that? You are not horrified?”

“Horrified? My dearest fellow, I am flattered. I am touched. And now,” Dorian continued, his tone shifting subtly, becoming more business-like, more commanding, “now that we have shared our secrets, you can understand mine. You see why I cannot let you look at the picture. You have put your soul into its creation, but it has taken mine in exchange. It is changing, Basil, while I remain the same. He spoke with such profound conviction, with such a weight of tragic seriousness, that Hallward was utterly convinced. The supernatural explanation seemed, in the heightened emotional atmosphere of the room, no more strange than his own passionate confession. He felt as if he and Dorian were characters in some grand, dark romance, their fates inextricably intertwined by this terrible, beautiful masterpiece.

“I see,” he whispered, his voice hoarse. “I see it all. Oh, Dorian, what a dreadful thing! To have this curse upon you. Forgive me for pressing you. I will never speak of it again. Hallward rose to his feet, unsteady, as if he had just survived a long illness. “I should go,” he said. “I feel… I am overwrought. I need to be alone.” He walked towards the door, then hesitated. “And Dorian… this other matter… the girl… Be careful. One tragedy is enough for a lifetime.”

“It has passed, Basil. It is already a memory. I cannot repeat an emotion,” Dorian said, echoing the very words he had used an hour before, but this time they were for him alone, a new mantra for his new life.

When the door had closed behind the painter, Dorian Gray did not move for a full minute. He listened to the footsteps descending the stairs, the sound of the front door opening and closing. He was alone. A slow smile spread across his face, a smile of pure, unadulterated triumph. He had won. He had faced down Basil’s morality and his own terror, and he had emerged victorious. He had bent the truth to his will, weaving a tapestry of lies and half-truths so compelling that even he almost believed it.

He went to the screen and, with a new sense of ownership, pulled it aside. The portrait was there, and the face in it seemed to have changed yet again. It was not merely the sneer of cruelty about the mouth that he had seen before. Now, in the subtle lines around the eyes, there was a new look, a flicker of cunning, of deceit. The hypocrisy of the scene he had just enacted was already written upon the canvas. It was a visible record of his own corruption, a diary of his soul.

He stared at it, fascinated. The horror was still there, but it was mingled with a strange sense of pride. This was his creation, as much as it was Basil’s. He was the author of this monstrous beauty. He felt a sudden, urgent need to protect it, to hide it away from all eyes, not from fear of discovery, but as a miser hides his gold. It was too precious, too terrible, to be shared.

He remembered the old schoolroom at the top of the house. It had not been used since his grandfather’s death. It was a large, desolate chamber, full of the ghosts of a lonely boyhood. The windows were grimed with dust, the furniture shrouded in white cloths like a row of silent spectres. It was the perfect place. No one ever went there. It was a tomb within the house, and in it he would entomb his soul.

He knew he could not move the large, heavy painting by himself. The frame alone was a magnificent, weighty piece of Italian craftsmanship. He would need help. But who could he trust? Not his own servants; they were too curious, they gossiped in the mews and the public-houses. He thought for a moment. Then he remembered a man named Ashton, a frame-maker in a little street off the Euston Road, whom Basil had sometimes employed. The man was discreet and accustomed to the odd whims of artists and their patrons.

He specified that they were needed for a simple task of moving a large object, and that their discretion would be rewarded. Having dealt with the practicalities, he felt a new sense of calm settle over him. The day, which had begun in a whirlwind of terror and grief, real or feigned, was ending with a feeling of purpose. He was taking control. He was shaping his own destiny.

Just as he was addressing the envelope, a footman entered the room, moving with the silent tread that is the highest art of the well-trained servant. Art was no longer a mirror to life, but a shield from it. The future stretched before him, a vast, uncharted sea of pleasure, and he set sail upon it without a single backward glance, the memory of a dead girl and a heartbroken friend already fading like the last notes of a sad, sweet song, drowned out by the promise of a louder, more thrilling symphony. But Dorian, whose soul was now intertwined with the pigments on the canvas, saw the truth.

He shuddered, not with fear, but with a thrill of excitement. The game was afoot. Life was about to become infinitely more interesting. He had a spectator for his sins after all: the man in the picture. And he was determined to give him a great deal to watch. He thought of Lord Henry’s words, of the new Hedonism, of the life that was to be its own aim. He thought of the endless procession of sensations that awaited him, of the passions that would scar the soul on the canvas and leave his own face a perfect, lovely mask. He paid the driver and walked up the shallow steps, feeling as though he were an actor ascending to a stage. Every moment, he felt, was now a performance.

Lord Henry was waiting for him in a small, private dining-room that overlooked the Green Park. The room was panelled in dark wood, the table laid for two with a profusion of silver and crystal that gleamed like captured moonlight in the soft glow of the shaded lamps. A single, perfect rose lay beside Dorian’s plate. Lord Henry himself was lounging in a deep armchair, a cigarette poised delicately between his long, tapering fingers.

“My dear boy,” he said, without rising. “Punctuality is the thief of time, but you, at least, have an eternity to squander. You look perfectly wonderful. Tragedy becomes you. It lends you a certain romantic pallor. Or perhaps it is merely the effect of my admirable taste in lighting.”

Dorian laughed and took the seat opposite him. “Tragedy is an art form I am just beginning to appreciate, Harry. From a distance.”

“Ah, from a distance! That is the only way to appreciate it,” murmured Lord Henry, motioning for the waiter to pour the wine, a pale, golden Chablis that shimmered in the glasses. “The moment it comes too close, it becomes a mere inconvenience. Like a relative, or a moral obligation. Your little actress, I confess, had a certain genius for it. To die for love! It is the last refuge of the unimaginative. But she did it with a certain style. One must grant her that. It has all the lurid charm of a Jacobean drama. You were quite right to treat it as a work of art.”

“You think I was right?” Dorian asked, leaning forward. He was hungry for justification, for the final intellectual seal of approval on his own hardening heart.

“My dear Dorian, to be right is to be commonplace. To be wrong is to be interesting. But in this case, you have managed to be both. You have recognised that the first duty in life is to be as artificial as possible. What the second duty is, no one has as yet discovered. You have suffered, I have no doubt. One must always suffer for a few hours. It is a form of spiritual calisthenics. It prevents the soul from becoming sluggish. But you have moved on. You have refused to be the hero of a bourgeois drama. Instead, you have become its critic. That is a far more civilised role.”

The dinner that followed was a masterpiece of its kind. Course followed course, each more delicate and surprising than the last. Lord Henry talked, and his words seemed to weave a spell over the room. He spoke of the absurdity of grief, the vulgarity of emotion, the supreme necessity of cultivating one’s sensations. He dissected life with the cool precision of a surgeon, laying bare its follies and its passions, and then dismissing them with a perfectly turned epigram.

“People talk about the secret of life,” he remarked, as he dissected a quail with his fork. “There is no secret. The mystery of life is personality. Everything else is a form of panic. We panic into love, we panic into marriage, we panic into parenthood. We even panic into death. The great virtue is to remain calm, to watch the pageant from a private box, and to applaud only when the scenery is beautiful or the dialogue is witty.”

“And what if one is on the stage, Harry?” Dorian asked, his eyes bright with wine and excitement.

“Then one must have the best lines,” said Lord Henry. “And one must never, ever, miss one’s exit. Your little Sibyl, for all her faults, understood that. Her exit was flawless. She will be remembered, for a week or two, as a pathetic heroine. Had she lived, she would have been remembered, if at all, as a rather bad actress who married above her station.”

Dorian felt a chill pass through him, but it was the pleasant, thrilling chill of intellectual cruelty. Harry was taking the ugly facts of the past few days and transforming them into a series of brilliant, glittering paradoxes. He was polishing the grubby tragedy until it shone like a jewel.

“She called me Prince Charming,” Dorian said, a little dreamily.

“Of course she did,” replied Lord Henry. “It was her role. It is what the populace always calls the beautiful people they cannot understand. They give them fairytale names to rob them of their reality. They are afraid of reality. It is the one thing that has not been idealised. But you, my dear boy, you are not a fairytale. You are a fact. A wonderful, beautiful, and dangerous fact. You must learn to live up to your own legend.”

After dinner, they drove to the opera. Lord Henry had a box, and as they entered, a ripple of interest passed through the house. All eyes were drawn to Dorian Gray. He stood for a moment at the front of the box, bathed in the brilliant light, conscious of the hundreds of lorgnettes that were being raised to look at him. His beauty seemed to have taken on a new, more dazzling quality. It was no longer the mere beauty of youth; it was the beauty of one who has a secret, a beauty that was both an invitation and a warning. He was aware of the whispers, the murmurs of admiration. He saw dowagers leaning across to their daughters, and old clubmen pointing him out to their friends. He felt a thrill of power, of absolute command. This was his world now, this world of artifice and pomp and beautiful, meaningless ritual.

The opera was Wagner, and the music, with its long, surging tides of passion, its cries of love and death, seemed to be the perfect accompaniment to his own mood. It was all sensation, pure and overwhelming. He did not follow the story; he simply allowed the waves of sound to wash over him, to carry him away. He looked at the stage, at the painted scenery and the gesticulating singers, and he saw it all as Harry did, as a spectacle. He looked at the audience, at the rows of pale, serious faces, and he saw them, too, as part of the performance. Life was a vast opera, and he had the best seat in the house.

During the interval, their box was filled with visitors. The Duchess of Harley, a woman famous for her six marriages and her collection of amethysts, tapped him on the arm with her fan. “You are more beautiful than ever, Mr. Gray,” she purred. “London has been so dull without you. You must come to my reception on Thursday. Everyone will be there.”

Sir Thomas Burdon, a grizzled diplomat with a reputation for scandal, drew Lord Henry aside. “Who is your friend, Harry?” he asked in a low voice. “He has the face of a Greek god who has just heard some amusingly bad news. I must know him.”

Dorian moved through it all with a new-found grace and confidence. He said little, but his smile, his gestures, the way he inclined his head to listen, were more eloquent than words. He was a perfect work of art, and he knew it. He saw the envy in the eyes of the young men, the adoration in the eyes of the women. He was no longer Basil’s shy model. He was Lord Henry’s creation, and he had surpassed his creator.

When the last of the visitors had departed, Lord Henry leaned back with a sigh of satisfaction. “You have had a triumph, my dear boy,” he said. “You have conquered society. And to conquer society, one must only scandalise it a little and charm it a great deal. You have the gift for both.”

Dorian did not reply. He was looking out at the glittering horseshoe of the theatre, his thoughts far away. The music, the admiration, the wine, the intoxicating philosophy of his friend; it had all combined to produce in him a state of lucid exaltation. He felt he could do anything, be anything. The portrait in the locked room was a distant, fantastic memory. It was the price he had paid for this, for this glorious, vibrant present. And the price, he thought, was laughably small.

They left before the final act. “One should always leave a little before the end,” Lord Henry remarked as they descended the grand staircase. “The applause is always so vulgar, and the sight of people struggling for their carriages is a disillusionment. The end of a pleasure is not a pleasure.”

In the cool night air, under a sky powdered with a faint dusting of stars, Dorian felt a sudden, sharp pang of something he could not name. It was not guilt. It was not remorse. It was a kind of aesthetic hunger. The evening had been perfect, but it was over. He needed more. He needed a new sensation, something sharper, something darker.

“Where are you going now, Harry?” he asked.

Lord Henry hailed a passing hansom. “Home, my dear boy. I have exhausted my capacity for epigrams for one evening. I am going home to read a little Gautier and to mourn the fact that I was not born in the eighteenth century.” He gave the driver his address. “Good night, Dorian. Remember what I said. The only way to get rid of a temptation is to yield to it. Resist it, and your soul grows sick with longing for the things it has forbidden to itself.”

The cab rattled off, leaving Dorian alone on the pavement. He stood for a moment, undecided. The night was full of possibilities. He could go home to his silent house, to the locked room and the secret it contained. Or he could follow the new path that had opened up before him, the path of experience and sensation that Lord Henry had so eloquently described. A sudden craving for the grotesque, for the strange, bitter flavours of life that lay hidden in the city’s dark places, took hold of him. He wanted to see the other side of the beautiful mask he presented to the world. He wanted to explore the labyrinth of his own soul, and he knew that the map was not to be found in the fashionable drawing-rooms of Mayfair.

He began to walk, turning eastward, away from the polite, well-lit squares, towards the tangled network of streets where the shadows were deeper and the gas-lamps cast a more lurid glow. He was on his way to find a new pleasure, and he had never felt so afraid, or so alive.
