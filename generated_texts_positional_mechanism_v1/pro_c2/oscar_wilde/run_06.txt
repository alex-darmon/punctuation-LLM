Clara’s heart went out to the phantasm, whose form shimmered like heat on a summer’s road. ‘You speak of the Great Silence,’ she murmured, her own voice barely a whisper. ‘Yes, Silence. It must be a wondrous thing. To be enfolded in the cool, dark loam, with the roots of the wild rose for company. To hear nothing, to feel nothing. She felt she was the unwilling audience to some ancient play. Then the Spirit spoke again, and his voice was the sound of a snapped violin string. ‘Have you not seen the inscription on the old piano forte?’ ‘Many times,’ cried the young girl, her courage returning; ‘I have it by heart. Suddenly she rose, her face determined, and with a curious resolve in her grey eyes. ‘I am not afraid,’ she said, quite simply, ‘and I will play the song for you.’ He drifted towards her with a sigh that might have been joy, and gesturing to her hand, he bowed. His touch was like a winter frost, and his presence smelled of dust and dried lavender, but Clara did not recoil as he guided her from the library. In the long gallery, the portraits of her ancestors seemed to frown down upon her. Their painted eyes followed her, and from their stiff lips they seemed to mutter, ‘Turn back! little Clara,’ they warned, ‘turn back!’ but the Spirit clutched her sleeve more urgently, and she lowered her gaze from them. Grotesque griffins with marble claws, carved into the posts of the grand staircase, seemed to flex in the gloom, and whispered ‘Beware! little Clara, beware! you will be lost to us,’ but the Spirit moved on more quickly, and Clara would not hear them. When they came to the music room he paused, and gestured to the piano forte. She lifted the heavy lid, and saw the keys, yellowed like old teeth, gleam in the faint moonlight. ‘Quick, quick,’ whispered the Spirit, ‘before the house is roused,’ and, in a moment, the heavy oak door had swung shut behind them, and the long gallery was empty.

Lord Canterville was a very sensible man. He listened to the Duke’s story with great attention, pulled at his white moustache, and then said, ‘My dear Duke, I cannot possibly take these jewels. In the first place, the ghost was a member of my family, and I feel a certain responsibility for his misconduct. It seems to me that your wife, in her great compassion, has absolved a family stain that has persisted for centuries. I owe her a debt of gratitude, not a legal claim. Secondly, I should not know what to do with them. They are far too grand for my wife, whose only ornaments are the jet brooches she inherited from her grandmother. And thirdly, I am quite convinced that if I were to take them, the old fellow’s spirit would be out of his grave in a fortnight, and would make my life a perfect misery. No, no, Duke, the jewels belong to your wife, and to her alone. She is a most remarkable young lady, and I hope you will bring her to visit us some day.’

The Duke was much relieved by this interview, and on his return to London, he told Virginia what had passed. She was very pleased, and kissed him for his trouble, and the next morning they went together to Bond Street and ordered the great Canterville ruby to be re-set as a pendant.

Washington Otis, for his part, became a celebrated diplomat, and gained a great reputation for his skill at the card-table. The twins, having been sent to Eton and then to Oxford, settled down in a most respectable manner, and were a constant source of amusement to all who knew them, though they never again attempted to terrorize a ghost with a patent lubricator or a pea-shooter. Mrs. Otis, who had a great fondness for titles, became a leading figure in London society, and was famous for her magnificent parties.

As for Canterville Chase itself, it was never again troubled by any supernatural visitations. The blood-stain on the library floor, which had so delighted the tourists and so annoyed the Otis family, faded away completely. The chains and armour in the hall no longer clanked and rattled in the dead of night. The strange, sepulchral groans that used to echo through the corridors ceased for ever, and the house became as quiet and peaceful as any other country seat in England.

Many years later, when Virginia was a stately Duchess and the mother of several handsome children, she and her husband happened to be driving through the county, and they decided to pay a visit to the old churchyard. It was a lovely summer evening, and the sky was a pale, delicate blue. They walked hand in hand to the quiet corner under the yew-tree, and stood for a while by the simple grassy mound that marked Sir Simon’s grave.

‘You know, Cecil,’ said the Duchess, after a long silence, ‘I have never told anyone, not even you, the full story of what happened when I was with him.’

‘I have never asked, my love,’ he replied gently. ‘I knew that you had a secret, and I respected it.’

‘He made me see what life meant, and what Death signifies, and why Love is stronger than both,’ she said, looking down at the grave. ‘He showed me terrible things, shapes of wickedness and despair, but he also showed me the beauty of repentance. It was a frightening journey, but I was not afraid, because I knew that what I was doing was right. He had suffered so much, and for so long, and all he wanted was for someone to weep for his sins, because he had no tears of his own.’

Her eyes grew dim, and she laid her hand on her husband’s arm. ‘You must not mock me, Cecil,’ she whispered, ‘but I think I owe Sir Simon a great deal. He taught me the value of pity.’

The Duke put his arm around her waist, and they stood there in the quiet twilight, surrounded by the sweet scent of the hayfields. A little farther off, they could see the lights of Canterville Chase twinkling through the trees, a symbol of the peace and happiness that had come to them all. And as they turned to leave, a little breeze stirred in the leaves of the old yew-tree, and it sounded, to Virginia’s ears, exactly like a sigh of contentment.

When they had gone, and the last of the footmen had retired below stairs, a profound quiet descended upon the house. It was a stillness of a kind that had not been known within those ancient walls for three hundred years; not the expectant hush before a storm, or the dead quiet of a house in mourning, but a new and almost unnerving peace. Mr. Otis, who was a practical man and had a great dislike of anything that could not be explained by modern science or Wall Street, declared that the house had finally ‘settled down’, and that it was high time, too. Mrs. Otis, finding that the mysterious blood-stain no longer reappeared upon the library hearthrug, had the entire room re-papered in a charming pattern of silver chrysanthemums on a dove-grey ground, and felt that she had at last triumphed over the more gloomy aspects of English aristocratic life. The twins, having exhausted the possibilities of pea-shooters and patent lubricants upon their spectral foe, found that life without a ghost to torment was exceedingly dull, and were soon packed off to school, where their energies were channelled into cricket and the composition of rather bad Latin verse. And so, for a time, it seemed that the prophecy had been fulfilled in its entirety, and that peace had indeed come to Canterville.

But Virginia, who alone knew the true cost of that peace, felt the change in the house more deeply than any of them. In the weeks that followed her strange adventure, she became quiet and thoughtful, and would often leave the cheerful family circle to wander by herself through the long corridors of the Chase. She would pause in the Tapestry Chamber, and run her fingers over the faded green fabric where the little huntsmen had warned her to go back, their tiny horns for ever silenced, their tasselled caps for ever still. She would stand in the library, not the bright, modern room of her mother’s design, but the shadowed space she remembered, and recall the sound of a voice like the sighing of the wind, and the touch of fingers as cold as ice. She had a secret, a terrible and beautiful secret, and it set her apart from the others. She had looked into the Garden of Death, and had returned, and the world of tennis parties and afternoon teas seemed a very shallow and unsubstantial thing afterwards.

It was during one of these solitary rambles that she came upon the little room at the top of the north turret. It had been Sir Simon’s favourite retreat, a small, circular chamber with a vaulted ceiling and a single lancet window that looked out over the slumbering park. No one had entered it for centuries, for the ghost had guarded it jealously, and the door was of heavy oak, studded with rusty nails. But now, when Virginia laid her hand upon the great iron ring, the door swung inwards with a low groan, as if sighing in its sleep. The room was thick with the dust of ages, and a single ray of sunlight, slanting in from the narrow window, illuminated a swirling galaxy of golden motes. In the centre of the room stood a small writing-desk of black oak, and on it lay a single sheet of vellum, a quill, and an ink-pot, long since dried to a black crust. Virginia crossed the room, her footsteps muffled by the carpet of dust, and picked up the vellum. On it, in the same curious black letters as the prophecy on the library window, were written two lines of verse:

Where Pity walks, and weeping is fordone,
A lonely shadow falls beneath the sun.

Virginia read the words over and over, and a cold shudder, like the one she had felt in the Tapestry Chamber, ran through her. She did not understand what they meant, but she felt that they were a message, a warning left for her and for her alone. She looked around the little room, at the dust and the decay, at the single beam of light struggling against the gloom, and she felt a sudden, overwhelming sense of loss. It was not her own loss, but the loss of the house itself. The great, ancient house, which had been alive for so long with a passion of sorrow and remorse, was now merely empty. It had traded its soul for peace, and the bargain had left it hollow. The ghost was gone, but the ghost’s loneliness remained, a faint, cold echo in the silent rooms.

That evening, as the family sat at dinner, Virginia was unusually quiet. The little Duke of Cheshire, who was staying with them for the week and was more in love with her than ever, tried his best to amuse her with stories of his life at Eton, which seemed to consist principally of getting into scrapes and being awfully sorry about it afterwards.
‘They tried to make me learn political economy, Virginia,’ he said, with a doleful look on his handsome face, ‘and it was the most dreadful thing you can imagine. All about supply and demand, you know. I told them that I had a plentiful supply of demerits, and absolutely no demand for them whatsoever, but the old beak didn’t seem to think it was funny.’
Ordinarily, Virginia would have laughed, but tonight she only gave him a faint, distracted smile. Her eyes kept wandering to the long windows, where the last light of the evening was fading from the sky, and the great yew-trees were turning into black, brooding shapes against the twilight.
‘Is anything the matter, my dear?’ asked her mother, noticing her abstraction. ‘You have hardly eaten a thing.’
‘I am just a little tired, Mamma,’ Virginia replied, pushing a pea around her plate with her fork.
‘It’s the house,’ said one of the twins, whose name was Stars, or possibly Stripes; no one could ever quite be sure. ‘It’s dreadfully dull now. I say, Papa, couldn’t we get another ghost? I saw a castle for sale in Scotland that was advertised as having three, one of which was said to play the bagpipes. Think of the fun we could have with that!’
‘We will do nothing of the sort,’ said Mr. Otis firmly. ‘This family has had quite enough experience with the supernatural to last it a lifetime. The house is quiet, and I intend for it to stay that way.’
But it was not quiet, not really. Later that night, as a storm gathered over the park and the wind began to moan around the gables of the old house, Virginia lay awake in her bed and listened. The wind sighed in the chimneys, and rattled the casements, and in its voice she heard a new note, a sound she had never heard before. It was not the theatrical groaning of Sir Simon, or the angry roaring of a winter gale. It was a thin, high, desolate sound, like the cry of a lost child. It seemed to come from the very stones of the house, a lament for the vibrant, tormented soul that had been torn from it. The house was weeping.

The following days were filled with a strange, creeping melancholy. A grey and listless mood seemed to settle over the Chase, seeping into the rooms like a damp mist. The servants, a superstitious lot for the most part, began to complain of cold spots in the corridors, and of a feeling of being watched by unseen eyes. The old housekeeper, who had been at the Chase for forty years, gave notice, declaring that the new silence was more terrifying than the old noises had ever been, and that she would rather face a hundred rattling skeletons than the dreadful emptiness that had fallen upon the place. Mrs. Otis’s silver chrysanthemums began to look tarnished and sad, and the whole house took on a dejected, mournful air. Even the twins were subdued, and their attempts at practical jokes lacked their usual creative malice.
The little Duke of Cheshire, who had extended his stay in the hopes of cheering Virginia up, was completely baffled by the change in her. She had become distant, and would spend hours by herself, saying that she needed to think.
‘But what is there to think about in the country?’ he asked her one afternoon, as he found her sitting on a stone bench in the formal gardens, staring at a sundial as if she expected it to tell her more than the time. ‘One doesn’t come to the country to think, one comes to shoot things, and to forget about all the beastly thinking one has to do in London. What is the matter, Virginia? If you are unhappy, I shall challenge the cause of your unhappiness to a duel, even if it is the weather, though I’m not quite sure how one goes about challenging the weather.’
Virginia looked at him, and her heart was touched by his absurd and charming devotion. ‘It is nothing you can fight, Cecil,’ she said softly. ‘It is a sadness. Can’t you feel it? The whole house is sad.’
‘Well, it is a very old house,’ said the Duke, reasonably. ‘It’s probably full of draughts. That’s what makes people sad, you know. Draughts, and bad sherry. My Uncle Augustus is always perfectly miserable, and my mother says it is because he will insist on drinking the sherry at his club. Shall we go for a ride? That always cheers me up.’
But Virginia did not want to go for a ride. She felt compelled to stay, to listen, to understand the new sorrow that had taken root in her home. She felt that the couplet she had found in the turret room was the key, but its meaning eluded her. ‘A lonely shadow falls beneath the sun.’ What could it mean? Sir Simon was at peace, in the Garden of Death where the sun never shone.
The crisis came a few nights later. The family had retired for the night, but a great restlessness was upon Virginia. The thin, weeping sound of the wind was louder than ever, and it seemed to be calling her name. She rose from her bed, and, wrapping a shawl around her shoulders, stole out of her room. The moonlight, filtering through the great oriel window at the end of the hall, cast long, distorted shadows on the floor, which seemed to twist and writhe like living things. Drawn by an impulse she could not explain, she made her way towards the library.
As she approached, she heard a sound from within, a soft, rhythmic thudding. Her heart hammered in her chest. Was it possible that he had returned? That the Angel of Death had been unmerciful after all? She pushed the heavy door open a crack and peered inside.
The room was bathed in an eerie, phosphorescent glow. It was not coming from any lamp, but seemed to emanate from the walls themselves, a faint, sickly light that made the familiar furniture look strange and menacing. And in the centre of the room, on the very spot where the blood-stain had once appeared, something was happening. The floorboards seemed to be sweating, oozing a dark, viscous liquid that pooled on the polished wood. As Virginia watched, frozen with a mixture of terror and fascination, the pool began to grow, spreading outwards like a stain of black ink. The thudding sound grew louder, and she realised it was the sound of a heartbeat, slow and laboured, like the heart of a dying man.
But it was not a man who was dying. It was the house. The heart of the house was breaking.
Suddenly, a figure appeared at her side, and a hand grasped her arm. She cried out, but it was only the little Duke, his face pale in the spectral light, his dressing-gown clutched around him.
‘Virginia! What in God’s name is that?’ he whispered, his eyes wide with a terror that was not in the least bit charming or amusing.
‘It is the house’s sorrow,’ she whispered back, her own fear momentarily forgotten in a flash of absolute certainty. ‘He is gone, and the house is alone for the first time, and it is dying of loneliness.’
The black pool on the floor had stopped spreading, and now it began to rise, forming a low, shapeless mound. It had no face, no features, but Virginia felt an overwhelming sense of despair emanating from it, a cold and bottomless grief that threatened to suck all the warmth and life from the room. It was the shadow of the ghost, the negative image of his great passion, a void of pure misery.
‘We must do something,’ gasped the Duke. ‘Shall I fetch your father’s pistol?’
‘No,’ said Virginia, her voice clear and firm. ‘You cannot shoot a shadow.’
She remembered the words of the ghost: ‘You must weep for me for my sins, because I have no tears, and pray with me for my soul, because I have no faith.’ Sir Simon had needed pity and prayer to be free. But what did this new sorrow need? It had no sins to be forgiven, no soul to be saved. It was simply an absence, a hollowness.
Then she understood. You cannot fill a void with pity. You must fill it with something else.
She stepped forward into the room, pulling the Duke with her. The air was bitingly cold, and the despair washed over them in waves.
‘Virginia, no!’ cried the Duke, trying to pull her back.
‘It is all right, Cecil,’ she said, her hand tightening on his. ‘Love is stronger than Death, he said. Perhaps it is stronger than the shadow of Death, too.’
She walked steadily towards the shapeless, weeping mass in the centre of the room. She did not pray, and she did not weep. Instead, she began to speak. She spoke of the future, not of the past. She spoke of the children that would be born in the house, of the laughter that would echo in its halls. She spoke of music, and dancing, and summer parties on the lawn. She described the life that would fill the old rooms, the warmth and the noise and the glorious, untidy business of living. She was not banishing the sadness; she was promising to overwhelm it. She was offering the house a new soul, not of one tormented man, but of a whole, happy family.
As she spoke, a strange thing began to happen. The unholy light in the room began to fade, and the cold receded. The dark shape on the floor quivered, and then, slowly, it began to sink back into the floorboards, not violently, but gently, as if it were being soothed to sleep. The laboured heartbeat grew fainter and fainter, until at last it was gone.
A profound silence fell, but it was a different kind of silence. It was not empty, but full of peace. The first rays of the dawn were creeping through the windows, and the room was filled with a soft, rosy light. The floor was clean and dry. There was no trace of the darkness that had been there only moments before.
The little Duke stared, utterly dumbfounded. He looked at the floor, and then at Virginia. Her face was pale, but her eyes were shining with a strange and beautiful light.
‘Virginia,’ he breathed. ‘You are the most extraordinary person I have ever met.’
He had said it before, many times, but this time it sounded different. This time, it sounded like the truth.
From that day on, the melancholy of Canterville Chase was lifted. Virginia, having kept her secret for so long, told the whole story to her father and mother, and to the little Duke. Mr. Otis, being a practical man, did not quite understand it, but he saw that his daughter was happy again, and that was enough for him. Mrs. Otis declared that it was all dreadfully romantic, and immediately began planning a grand ball to ‘air the house out properly’. The little Duke simply looked at Virginia with an expression of complete adoration, and asked her, for the seventeenth time, to marry him. This time, she said yes.
And so the prophecy, which Virginia had found in the turret, was fulfilled in a way she had not expected. The lonely shadow did not last, for the house was no longer in the sun of a single, great passion, but was filled instead with the diffuse and gentle light of everyday life and love. The Chase became famous not for its ghosts, but for its hospitality. It was a house full of noise and children, and the patter of dogs’ paws, and the sound of music drifting out into the gardens on long summer evenings. And sometimes, when the wind sighed in the old yew-trees, it no longer sounded like weeping, or even like a sigh of contentment, but very much like the sound of distant, happy laughter.

Each stroke fell upon their strained nerves like a hammer-blow, a sombre tolling for their lost hope. As the twelfth and final chime faded into the oppressive quiet, it was followed by a sound so unexpected, so entirely out of place, that every person at the table started in their seat. It was not a ghostly moan, nor the clanking of chains which they had all, in spite of their modern sensibilities, come to half expect. It was a tremendous, grinding crash from the direction of the great oak staircase, a sound of splintering wood and protesting metal, followed by a peal of thunder that seemed to shake the very foundations of the house. Yet, when Washington sprang to the window and pulled back the heavy velvet curtains, he saw a sky serene and brilliant with stars, with not a cloud to be seen.

‘Good heavens, what was that?’ cried Mrs. Otis, clutching at her husband’s arm, her face a mask of terror. ‘Hiram, the house is coming down!’

Mr. Otis, though his own face was ashen, rose with a show of his usual practicality. ‘Nonsense, Lucretia. It was probably just some old timber giving way. Washington, take a candle and go see. Twins, you stay right here.’

But before Washington could take more than a step, they all froze. A new sound had reached them, the faint, high-pitched creak of a turning hinge, coming from the top of the hall. All eyes were fixed on the head of the staircase, where the shadows thrown by the hall-lantern danced and writhed. And then, out of the darkness, a figure appeared.

It was Virginia.

She was very pale, but her eyes held a strange, luminous quality, as if she were gazing at them from a great distance. She wore the same simple frock she had been wearing when she disappeared, but it was now strangely rumpled, and there was a faint dusting of what looked like grey powder on her golden hair. In her hands, she carried a small, oblong box, made of some dark, heavily carved wood and bound with tarnished silver. She took a step forward, and then another, descending the staircase with a slow, almost dreamlike grace.

A collective gasp went through the room. Mrs. Otis gave a choked cry of ‘Virginia!’ and would have rushed forward, but she seemed to have lost the power of her limbs. The little Duke of Cheshire was the first to move. He leapt from his chair, knocking it over with a clatter that no one noticed, and in three bounds he was at the foot of the stairs, his handsome young face a picture of frantic relief and utter adoration.

‘Virginia!’ he breathed, his voice trembling. ‘You’ve come back! We thought you were lost forever!’

Virginia gave him a small, tired smile as she reached the bottom step. She did not seem surprised to see him. ‘I am all right, Cecil,’ she said, and her voice was quiet and clear, though it held a note of profound weariness. ‘I am quite all right. But he is not.’

‘He?’ exclaimed Mr. Otis, finding his voice at last and striding forward. ‘Who is he? Virginia, where on earth have you been? We have had the whole county out looking for you! We dragged the carp-pond! I have sent telegrams to every police inspector from here to London!’

Virginia looked at her father, and her eyes filled with a deep and gentle pity. ‘I am so sorry to have caused you all such anxiety,’ she said softly. ‘I could not help it. I had a promise to keep. I have been with the Ghost.’

There was a moment of absolute, stunned silence. The twins’ jaws dropped in unison. Mrs. Otis let out a faint moan and sank back into her chair, fanning herself weakly with a napkin. Mr. Otis stared at his daughter as if she had suddenly begun speaking in Greek.

‘The Ghost?’ he repeated, his practical mind struggling to grasp the enormity of the statement. ‘You mean Sir Simon? Virginia, this is no time for jokes. A band of gypsies has been detained on your account!’

‘It was not the gypsies, Papa,’ she said, her gaze unwavering. ‘It was Sir Simon. He asked for my help. And I gave it.’ She looked down at the box in her hands. ‘He is at peace now. He will not trouble this house any more.’

She held out the ancient casket. On its lid was a tiny coat of arms surmounted by a coronet, all intricately worked in dull silver. Mr. Otis took it from her cautiously, as if it might explode. It was surprisingly heavy.

‘What is this?’ he asked, his voice full of suspicion.

‘It is a gift,’ Virginia replied. ‘From him. For what I did.’

The twins, their awe finally giving way to their insatiable curiosity, had crept forward. ‘What is it? What’s inside?’ they whispered, tugging at Virginia’s sleeve. ‘Is it pirate treasure?’

Virginia shook her head, a faint smile touching her lips. ‘No, not pirate treasure. They are the Canterville jewels.’

At this, Mrs. Otis, who had been on the verge of a full swoon, sat bolt upright. ‘The Canterville jewels?’ she echoed, her eyes wide. ‘The famous ones? The ones Lady Clementina lost at cards to Mr. Boria? My dear child, you must be dreaming!’

‘I am not dreaming, Mama,’ said Virginia. She swayed a little on her feet, and the little Duke, who had not taken his eyes from her face for a second, immediately put a supportive arm around her waist. ‘I am just very, very tired.’

Seeing the genuine exhaustion in his daughter’s face, Mr. Otis’s fatherly concern finally overwhelmed his bewilderment. ‘Of course you are, my dear,’ he said, his tone softening. ‘We can talk about all this in the morning. Jewels and ghosts and gypsies. You need to rest.’ He turned to the housekeeper, who had been hovering by the door with the other terrified servants. ‘Mrs. Umney, prepare a hot bath for Miss Virginia, and some warm milk. And for heaven’s sake, stop looking like you’ve seen a ghost.’

The old woman curtsied, though the irony of the Minister’s command was evidently lost on her. As the little Duke, with the tenderness of a knight-errant, began to lead Virginia from the room, she stopped and turned back.

‘Papa,’ she said, ‘in the morning, I must show you something. In the wall by the tapestry.’ She gestured vaguely towards the library. ‘That is where he is. We must give him a proper funeral. I promised.’

And with that, she allowed the Duke to lead her away, leaving the rest of the family in the hall, staring at the mysterious casket and at each other in a state of the most profound and complete mystification. Mr. Otis looked down at the box, then back at the staircase where his daughter had vanished. He shook his head slowly, a dazed expression on his face.

‘I have,’ he said to the room at large, ‘marketed the world’s most advanced and celebrated products, from the Rising Sun Lubricator to the Demosthenes Dentifrice. But I am beginning to believe that there is something in this old country for which the Pinkerton Detective Agency itself would be utterly at a loss.’

The following morning, after a deep and dreamless sleep, Virginia felt much restored. She came down to breakfast to find that the news of her mysterious return, and the even more mysterious casket, had thrown the entire household into a state of barely suppressed excitement. The story had been embellished in the servants’ hall to such a degree that it was now believed Miss Virginia had single-handedly fought a legion of demons in a subterranean cavern and returned with the lost treasure of the Plantagenets.

After a silent breakfast, during which Mr. Otis did little but stare at his daughter with a mixture of pride and utter confusion, Virginia rose from the table. ‘If you will all come with me now,’ she said quietly, ‘I will show you.’

She led the way, not to the library as they had supposed, but to the Tapestry Chamber, the scene of her disappearance. The little Duke, who had arrived at the crack of dawn, was at her side at once. Mr. and Mrs. Otis followed, with Washington and the twins bringing up the rear, their faces alight with ghoulish anticipation.

Virginia walked to the wall at the far end of the room, the place where the Ghost had vanished with her. The tapestry of the little huntsmen looked just as it always had, faded and serene. ‘It is here,’ she said, and placed her hand flat against the wall, just to the left of the fireplace. At her touch, there was a soft click, and then, with a low groan of ancient, unoiled hinges, a section of the wainscoting swung inwards, revealing a narrow, dark passage.

A musty, cold air issued from the opening, smelling of damp earth and centuries of disuse. Mrs. Otis gave a little shriek and declared she was not going a step further, as the place was undoubtedly full of mice. Mr. Otis, however, having lit a candelabrum from the mantelpiece, squared his shoulders and prepared to follow his daughter.

‘I am not afraid,’ Virginia said, taking the candles from him. ‘I have been here before.’

She stepped into the darkness, and the others, their curiosity overcoming their trepidation, followed her. The passage was stone-lined and so narrow they had to walk in single file. It twisted and turned for some fifty feet before ending at a heavy, iron-studded oak door. It was bolted on the outside.

‘He couldn’t get out,’ Virginia whispered, more to herself than to the others. ‘They left him here to die.’

With some effort, Washington and Mr. Otis drew back the heavy, rusted bolts. The door swung open with a dreadful screech, revealing a tiny, windowless cell. As Mr. Otis held the candelabrum aloft, the flickering light fell upon a truly pitiable sight. Chained by a great iron ring to the wall was a gaunt skeleton, which stretched out its bony fingers as if to grasp a small, earthenware jug and platter that had been placed just out of its reach. The skeleton was that of Sir Simon de Canterville. On the stone floor beside him, a small bush had grown from a crack in the flagstones; it was withered and dead, but on its branches they could just make out the shape of a few dried, silvery blossoms.

‘The barren almond bears,’ Virginia murmured, tears shining in her eyes. ‘He was so thirsty. And they starved him to death.’

The twins, who had been peering excitedly over Washington’s shoulder, suddenly fell silent. The sight of the pathetic, chained skeleton was far more terrible and real than any game of ghosts they had ever imagined. Mrs. Otis hid her face in her handkerchief and began to sob quietly. Even Mr. Otis, the practical American minister, was visibly moved. He took off his hat with a simple, unconscious gesture of respect.

‘This is a terrible crime,’ he said in a low, stern voice. ‘A very terrible crime indeed.’ He looked at the skeleton, and then at his daughter. ‘So this is his secret. And you, my dear, have given him peace.’

He placed a hand on Virginia’s shoulder. At that moment, he understood everything. He understood her tears, her compassion, and the strange, holy light that had been in her eyes.

A proper funeral was arranged for Sir Simon. Mr. Otis wrote to the present Lord Canterville, a grand-nephew of the ghost who lived in Wales, informing him of the discovery of the remains and of his intention to give them a Christian burial in the consecrated ground of the local churchyard. Lord Canterville wrote back at once, expressing his immense gratitude and stating that he felt Virginia and her family had a far greater right to decide the ghost’s final fate than he did. He also politely, but firmly, refused to accept the casket of jewels. They had, he wrote, been a source of nothing but trouble for his family, and since they had been given to Virginia in gratitude, they rightfully belonged to her as part of her dowry.

Mr. Otis at first expostulated strongly against this, declaring that his daughter was an American citizen and, as such, was above the accepted need for dowries. He argued that the jewels were legally part of the Canterville estate. However, when the little Duke of Cheshire pointed out, with considerable tact, that to refuse the gift would be to dishonour the spirit of the giver and to wound Virginia’s feelings deeply, the Minister was obliged to give way. Mrs. Otis was secretly delighted, and spent a whole afternoon trying on a magnificent ruby necklace before declaring that it was far too ostentatious for a minister’s wife to wear, and would look much better on Virginia when she was a Duchess.

The funeral itself was a simple but deeply impressive affair. The hearse was drawn by four black horses, and the coffin was covered with a rich purple pall on which was embroidered in gold the Canterville coat of arms. By the side of the open grave stood Virginia, dressed in white, holding a large cross made of white and pink almond-blossoms. As the coffin was lowered into the earth, she stepped forward and laid the cross upon it. At that moment, the sun broke through the clouds, and a flock of linnets burst into song from a nearby thicket. The old yew trees seemed to sigh in contentment, and the whole churchyard was filled with a sense of profound peace.

That evening, as they walked home, the little Duke slipped his hand into Virginia’s. ‘You have no idea how much I love you,’ he said. ‘You are good and gentle and true. You were not afraid of Death, and you taught a lost soul how to find peace. When I am with you, I feel as if all the evil of the world could not touch me.’

Virginia smiled, and a faint blush coloured her cheeks. ‘That is because Love is stronger than Death,’ she said simply, echoing the words the Ghost himself had spoken.

The Duke stopped, and turning her to face him, he looked at her with an expression of the most earnest devotion. ‘Virginia,’ he said, ‘will you be my wife?’

‘I thought you would never ask,’ she replied, laughing, though there were tears in her eyes. ‘Of course I will, Cecil. I believe I have loved you since the moment you tore your trousers trying to climb the cedar tree.’

And so, the strange and gothic history of Canterville Chase found its happy conclusion. The wedding was the event of the season, and when Virginia, now the Duchess of Cheshire, returned to the Chase, the old bloodstain in the library, which had so vexed Mrs. Otis, was found to have vanished completely and for ever. The twins grew up and went to Eton, where they distinguished themselves chiefly by rigging the Headmaster’s bell to play ‘Yankee Doodle’ at inappropriate moments. Washington devoted himself to politics and became a noted diplomat, though he always maintained that the most difficult negotiation he ever faced was convincing Mrs. Otis that it was acceptable to serve pumpkin pie at an English garden party.

The Chase itself was never again troubled by any supernatural phenomena. It became a happy, bustling house, full of life and laughter, and the patter of many small feet. Virginia never spoke of her secret adventure with the ghost to anyone but her husband, but she often went and sat by the quiet grave in the churchyard. She never forgot what Sir Simon had taught her about life, and death, and the extraordinary power of pity. And though the ghost himself was gone, his memory lingered, not as a terror, but as the strange, sad, and wonderful beginning of a great and lasting love. The old house was at peace, and so, at last, were all who dwelt within it.

The house was never again troubled by any phenomena, supernatural or otherwise. It had, it seemed, learned its new story. The visit, it soon became clear, was not a social call but a siege. Citing a sudden and convenient illness on the part of their horse, Lady Honoria and Algernon installed themselves in the finest guest rooms and made it plain that they had no intention of leaving. They immediately declared a private war on Mr. Algernon Canterville. ‘This house is not right,’ he declared, his voice trembling slightly. ‘There is a presence here. I feel it. A guardian.’

The twins exchanged a look of pure, unholy glee. ‘A guardian?’ said Stripes, his face a perfect mask of innocence. ‘Whatever do you mean, Mr. Canterville?’

‘A spirit,’ Algernon whispered, his eyes wide. ‘Protecting its hoard. The old legends speak of it. Sir Simon was not the only spectre of the Chase. There is another, a Grey Lady who walks the Tapestry Chamber, protecting the family’s true inheritance from outsiders.’

This was, of course, a complete fabrication, but the twins seized upon it with an artist’s enthusiasm. That very night, they set to work. Procuring an old grey bedsheet from the linen closet and a generous amount of flour from the kitchen, they prepared to give Mr. Canterville the haunting he so richly deserved.

Their plan was simple. They would wait until Algernon was on one of his midnight prowls, which had become a regular occurrence, and then Stripes, draped in the sheet and dusted with flour, would glide menacingly across the far end of the Tapestry Chamber, moaning softly.

‘The Grey Lady!’ he breathed. ‘It is true! You have come to guide me! To show me the way to the Canterville fortune!’

Stripes stopped gliding, utterly baffled. He had not accounted for this reaction. He stood motionless in the middle of the room, a flour-dusted teenager in a bedsheet, while Algernon began to address him with passionate reverence.

‘Oh, noble spirit, I am your kinsman, Algernon, last of the true Canterville blood! These usurpers, these American merchants, have defiled our home. But you remember. You know where the treasure lies. Show me, I implore you! Show me the secret!’

From behind the cabinet, Stars smothered a giggle. Stripes, realizing his ghostly authority was on the line, decided to improvise. He pointed a floury finger dramatically towards the great carven chimney-piece, a place he knew to be perfectly solid and devoid of any secret passages, and then, with a final, wavering moan, he glided swiftly out of the nearest door.

For the next few days, Algernon was a man possessed. He spent hours tapping on the chimney-piece, running his hands over the carvings, and muttering to himself. He borrowed books on ciphers and heraldry from the library, convinced that the blinking, goggle-eyed animals carved into the wood held some cryptic message.

Virginia watched him with growing pity. His greed was so naked, so pathetic. She saw in his desperate, haunted eyes a different kind of imprisonment from Sir Simon’s, but an imprisonment nonetheless. He was chained not by a guilty past, but by a grasping, impoverished present.

The climax arrived on a dark and stormy night, the kind of night that seemed to have been designed expressly for melodrama. The wind howled around the gables of the Chase, and rain lashed against the windowpanes. Lady Honoria had retired early with a ‘headache,’ leaving the rest of the family in the hall, where a great fire roared in the hearth. They all rushed to the room. They found Algernon standing before the fireplace, a small crowbar in his hand and a frantic, wild look in his eyes. At that moment, the twins, overcome with a rare pang of guilt, chose to make their confession.

‘It wasn’t a spirit, Mr. Canterville,’ said Stars, shuffling his feet.

‘It was me,’ added Stripes, looking thoroughly ashamed. ‘In a bedsheet.’

Algernon stared at them, his mind slow to grasp the humiliating truth. His quest, his spiritual communion, his great secret, had been nothing more than a prank by two American schoolboys. He let out a choked, wretched sob and sank to the floor, burying his face in his hands.

She knelt beside him, her expression gentle. ‘Mr. Canterville,’ she said softly, her voice clear and calm above the storm. ‘The jewels you are looking for will not bring you peace.’

He looked up at her, his face streaked with tears and dust. ‘What would you know of it?’ he spat. ‘You, who have everything. Wealth, a title, this house… You cannot understand what it is to have nothing, to be the last ragged remnant of a noble line.’

She paused, and looked at him with her clear, honest eyes. ‘The jewels were his last burden. Their only value was in the act of mercy that set him free.’

She stood up and went to the wall where, years ago, a ghostly portal had opened. She pressed her hand against the green tapestry, where the little huntsmen still blew their silent horns.

‘Peace is not found in a strongbox,’ she said, her voice barely a whisper. ‘It is found when you forgive life for what it has not given you. I cannot give you the Canterville jewels, for they are not mine to give. They are a ghost’s memory. But my husband and I can help you. We can give you a chance to make a new life, one that is not haunted by the past.’

There was a long silence, broken only by the sighing of the wind. Algernon stared at her, at the strange, beautiful light in her eyes, the same light that had once promised salvation to a tormented spirit. The hard, grasping lines on his face seemed to soften. For the first time, he saw not an American usurper, but a woman of extraordinary grace.

Slowly, painfully, he got to his feet. He did not look at the broken chimney-piece or the dust of his shattered dreams. He looked at Virginia. ‘I… I am sorry,’ he stammered. ‘I have been… a fool.’

The next morning, the decrepit carriage was brought around once more. Lady Honoria, who had been informed of the night’s events by a contrite Algernon and subsequently had a very practical discussion with Mr. Otis involving a chequebook, looked almost pleasant. She opened the box. Inside, nestled on the velvet, was not the great hoard of jewels, but a single, exquisite diamond brooch in the shape of a star. It was a piece from her own collection, not from Sir Simon’s treasure.

‘To remember your visit,’ Virginia said with a small smile. ‘And as a token of new beginnings.’

Lady Honoria took the box, her hawk-like face for once unreadable. She looked from the glittering brooch to Virginia, and for a moment, it seemed she might refuse. Then, she gave a stiff, formal nod. ‘You are a very unusual young woman, Your Grace,’ she said. That afternoon, Virginia and Cecil walked in the garden, to the quiet churchyard beyond the wall. The wild roses on Sir Simon’s unmarked grave were in full, glorious bloom.

‘You did the right thing,’ Cecil said, putting his arm around her. ‘You have a remarkable talent for dealing with hauntings, my love, both spectral and familial.’

Virginia smiled and leaned her head against his shoulder. ‘He taught me how,’ she said softly. ‘He taught me that the heaviest chains are the ones we forge for ourselves. The legacy of the ghost was not a chest of priceless jewels, but a lesson in compassion that had taken root, like the barren almond tree, and blossomed in the heart of a golden girl.

‘The Tapestry Chamber,’ he said, his voice hoarse. ‘She was last seen in the Tapestry Chamber.’

He led the way, the others following close behind, their footsteps echoing in the vast, silent hall. They reached the door and found it slightly ajar. Mr. Otis pushed it open and they entered. The room was dark and cold, and the little huntsmen on the tapestry seemed to peer down at them with mocking, silent faces. The moonlight, filtering through the mullioned window, fell upon the floor, revealing nothing but dust and shadows. There was no sign of a struggle, no clue, nothing.

They stood there for what felt like an eternity, the last vestiges of hope draining away from them. Mrs. Otis finally broke the silence with a low, heart-rending sob and buried her face in her husband’s shoulder. It was over. Virginia was gone.

And then, it happened.

From somewhere deep within the foundations of the house, a low, solemn peal of thunder seemed to sound, though the night outside remained still and misty. It was not a noise so much as a vibration, a resonant chord that made the very air tremble. At the same moment, the old, withered almond-tree outside the library window, which had not borne blossom for as long as anyone could remember, suddenly burst into a cloud of delicate white flowers, their petals glowing with an ethereal luminescence in the moonlight.

‘Look!’ cried the little Duke, who was standing nearest the window. ‘The almond-tree! The barren almond bears!’

A great wave of excitement and awe swept through the family. They crowded to the window, staring at the miraculous bloom. ‘Then it is true,’ whispered Washington. ‘The prophecy is fulfilled.’

As he spoke, a brilliant, unearthly light flared behind them, and they turned as one. The section of the wall where the great tapestry hung was glowing with a soft, golden radiance. The threads of the tapestry itself seemed to dissolve, the little huntsmen and their horns fading like a dream, and the solid oak panelling behind it grew transparent, shimmering like a heat-haze. For a moment, they could see through it into a darkness that was not of this world, a place of swirling, formless shapes and silent, beckoning shadows.

Then, as suddenly as it had appeared, the light contracted, the wall solidified, and standing before them, where a moment before there had been only solid wood and ancient thread, was Virginia.

She was very pale, and there was a curious serenity in her gaze, as of one who had looked upon great mysteries and was no longer troubled by them. In her hands, she carried a small, oblong casket, made of dark wood and bound with tarnished silver.

For a moment, everyone was too stunned to move or speak. Then Mrs. Otis gave a wild cry of ‘Virginia!’ and rushed forward, folding her daughter in a frantic embrace. Mr. Otis, his face working with emotion, followed, and soon she was surrounded by her entire family, all talking at once, laughing and crying with relief. The little Duke stood back, his heart beating so fast he thought it might burst, his eyes fixed on her face, not daring to approach lest she prove to be a vision and fade away.

When the first storm of emotion had subsided, Mr. Otis held his daughter at arm’s length. ‘My child, where have you been? We have been out of our minds with worry! What is the meaning of all this?’

Virginia smiled, a faint, gentle smile. ‘I have been with the Ghost,’ she said simply. ‘He is at peace now. He is in the Garden of Death.’

She then told them what had happened. She did not dwell on the fearful shapes or the wicked whispers, but spoke of the great sorrow of Sir Simon, and how she had wept for his sins and prayed for his soul. She told them how the Angel of Death had come, a beautiful and silent figure, and had led the tired spirit away.

‘He asked me to guide him to the portal,’ she concluded, ‘and before he left, he gave me this.’ She held out the casket.

Mr. Otis took it and examined it. It was very old and curiously wrought. With some difficulty, he found the secret spring and opened it. Inside, lying on a bed of faded purple velvet, was a magnificent collection of jewels. There were ropes of pearls, great fiery rubies, sapphires like bits of the summer sky, and emeralds of a wonderful green. They glittered and flashed with a light that seemed almost alive.

The whole family cried out in wonder.

‘My dear girl,’ said the Minister, who was the first to recover himself, ‘these are very pretty, of course, but you must not mistake theatrical properties for genuine articles. I trust that your friend the Ghost was not so inconsiderate as to give you paste. I shall have them valued as soon as we get to London, and if they are worth anything, I shall place the sum to your credit with Messrs. Peabody and Co. As for the Ghost himself, I must say that while I object to his earlier habit of stealing your mother’s paints to refurbish that ridiculous blood-stain, I cannot but approve of his giving you these jewels as a reward for your very charming and sympathetic behaviour.’

‘They are for me, Papa,’ said Virginia quietly. ‘He told me so. They are my wedding present.’

‘Your wedding present?’ exclaimed Mrs. Otis, looking from her daughter to the Duke.

At this, the little Duke of Cheshire finally found his voice and his courage. He stepped forward, and taking Virginia’s hand, he knelt before her. ‘Virginia,’ he said, his voice thick with emotion, ‘I thought I had lost you forever. Your father would not let me be engaged to you last year, but perhaps he will now? You have shown more courage than any man I know, and I love you more than words can say.’

‘Of course he will,’ said Mr. Otis, who was a good deal touched by the boy’s obvious devotion, and also, it must be admitted, rather impressed by the casket of jewels. He patted the Duke kindly on the head. ‘You have won her fairly, my boy, and I can tell you that if you keep my daughter safe, you will be getting a prize worth more than all the gems in that box, though I daresay they are worth a pretty penny all the same.’

And so, when Virginia and the young Duke were married some months later, the famous Canterville jewels were her dowry. The wedding was a grand affair, celebrated at St. George’s, Hanover Square, and the bride was the wonder of all who saw her. After the honeymoon, the Duke and Duchess returned to Canterville Chase, which was their wedding gift from Mr. Otis. They were not at all afraid of the house; the air was now light and sweet, the blood-stain had vanished forever from the library floor, and the silence that fell at night was one of profound and gentle peace.

A few days after their return, they walked together down the lime-tree avenue to a lonely corner of the churchyard. Virginia had ordered a simple headstone to be placed there, inscribed with only the initials ‘S. de C.’ and the lines of the prophecy from the library window. She laid a wreath of white roses on the grass.

‘You know, Cecil,’ she said, as they turned to leave, ‘I owe him a great deal.’

‘My darling, you owe him nothing,’ said the Duke, kissing her. ‘He nearly frightened you and your family out of the country.’

‘No,’ she insisted. ‘He taught me what Life is, and what Death signifies, and why Love is stronger than both.’

The Duke drew his wife’s arm through his, and they walked back towards the house. As they passed the lawn, they saw that the old almond-tree was still covered in its glorious white blossoms, a testament to a soul at rest and a love that had conquered all. Virginia looked at her husband and smiled, and in that moment, she knew that the last and most important part of the prophecy had also been fulfilled; for peace had, at long last, come to Canterville, and to her own happy heart.

The first year of their marriage was an idyll painted in the softest colours of an English spring. The Duke, entirely devoted, found that his love for Virginia grew with each passing day, a fact that surprised him, for he had thought his heart quite full on their wedding day. Virginia, for her part, blossomed in her new role as Duchess, carrying her title with a gentle grace that charmed the county and silenced even the most formidable of London’s society hostesses. The twins, now young men who were meant to be studying at Eton, spent a great deal of their time at the Chase, declaring that the air was more conducive to learning, though in truth it was more conducive to fishing in the carp-pond and inventing new and ever more elaborate games of chance.

Canterville Chase itself seemed to breathe a sigh of relief. The servants no longer whispered in shadowed corners, the ancient armour in the hall ceased its inexplicable rattling, and the strange, spectral stains that had once plagued the household linen were now a thing of the distant past. Mrs. Umney, the old housekeeper, who had at first been quite terrified of her American employers, now presided over the domestic staff with a benevolent tyranny, often remarking that while the Otis family had certainly brought some peculiar habits with them, they had also, it seemed, brought a lasting and most welcome peace.

This tranquil state of affairs, however, was not destined to continue uninterrupted. English country life, like the English weather, is prone to sudden and unexpected squalls. The first sign of the coming storm arrived one crisp autumn afternoon in the form of a letter, delivered on heavy, cream-coloured stationery bearing a crest that was a faded, more elaborate version of the Canterville arms. It was addressed to the Duke of Cheshire, but its contents concerned his wife.

The author was a certain Lord Augustus de Canterville, a name hitherto unknown to any member of the Otis family. He was, the letter explained in a prose style both florid and faintly menacing, a distant cousin of the late Sir Simon, and the last surviving male of the direct Canterville line. He had been living abroad, he wrote, for his health and for reasons of a ‘delicate financial nature,’ and had only recently returned to England to learn that the ancestral seat had been sold and, what was more, that the legendary Canterville jewels had been given away. He expressed his profound shock and dismay, and concluded by announcing his intention to visit the Chase on the following Tuesday to discuss this ‘most irregular state of affairs.’

‘Well, I’ll be,’ said Mr. Otis, who was visiting with his wife for the shooting season, having read the letter over Cecil’s shoulder. ‘It seems we have a claimant. What does he want, do you suppose? To challenge me to a duel for selling a house I bought fair and square?’

‘I do not like the tone of it, Papa,’ said Virginia quietly. ‘It sounds bitter.’

‘Of course it’s bitter,’ said the Duke, his handsome face flushed with indignation. ‘The man is an impertinent scoundrel! To suggest there is anything ‘irregular’ about my wife’s property! I shall write back and tell him he is not welcome here.’

‘Now, Cecil, let’s not be hasty,’ counselled Mr. Otis, ever the diplomat. ‘In my experience, it is always better to meet a problem head-on. Let the man come. We’ll hear what he has to say. It is probably just a matter of a small financial settlement. Most English grievances, I have found, can be soothed with a well-timed cheque.’

And so, on the following Tuesday, a fly from the Ascot station deposited Lord Augustus de Canterville at the door. He was a man of perhaps fifty years, though ill-health and a perpetual sense of grievance had etched a decade more upon his features, and he carried himself with the rigid posture of one who fears his spine might at any moment collapse, along with his fortunes. His clothes were of a fine cut but noticeably frayed at the cuffs, and he looked upon the grand entrance hall, now bright and airy and adorned with bowls of Virginia’s chrysanthemums, with an expression of profound disapproval.

He was shown into the library, where the family was assembled. He bowed stiffly to the Duchess, gave a curt nod to the Duke and Mr. Otis, and ignored the twins completely, a slight which they did not fail to notice.

‘I have come,’ he began, his voice thin and reedy, ‘to rectify a grave injustice. This house, this sacred repository of my family’s history, has been treated like a common commodity. But that is a wound I must bear. The matter of the jewels, however, is another question entirely.’ He fixed his watery eyes upon Virginia. ‘Those gems, madam, are not personal trinkets. They are the Canterville heirlooms. They belong, by right of blood, to the head of the Canterville family. They belong to me.’

‘I’m afraid you are mistaken, my lord,’ said Virginia, her voice calm but firm. ‘The jewels were a gift to me.’

‘A gift? From whom, pray tell? This American father of yours, who purchased our heritage as he might a side of beef?’

‘No,’ said Virginia. ‘They were a gift from Sir Simon de Canterville himself.’

Lord Augustus stared at her as if she had suddenly gone mad. A strange, choked sound, half laugh and half gasp, escaped his lips. ‘From... from the Ghost? You expect me to believe such a preposterous tale? A ghost cannot bequeath property, madam! The very idea is a legal and spiritual absurdity! It is a fantasy, a schoolgirl’s dream concocted to lay claim to what is not yours.’

‘It is the truth,’ said the Duke, stepping forward. ‘And I will not have you speak to my wife in such a manner in her own home.’

‘It is my home!’ retorted Lord Augustus, his voice rising to a querulous squeak. ‘Or it ought to be! I am the last of the blood! For three hundred years my ancestor haunted these halls, protecting our legacy, and in the end it is stolen away by a colonial child with a pretty face and a fanciful imagination!’

‘Sir,’ said Mr. Otis, stepping between them, his expression one of calm, businesslike resolution. ‘I hold the deed to this property, purchased in good faith and for a handsome sum from the previous Lord Canterville. My daughter received the jewels as a personal gift for a service she rendered. If you believe you have a legal claim, I suggest you consult a solicitor. I can recommend a very good one in London. Until then, this discussion is at an end.’

Lord Augustus’s face went pale, then a blotchy, unpleasant red. He saw that he would get nowhere with these resolute, practical people. ‘This is not over,’ he hissed, grabbing his hat. ‘The spirit of my family will not stand for this desecration. You may have the deed, but the very stones of this house cry out against you! You will see. Canterville Chase does not suffer outsiders gladly.’

With that, he turned and stormed out of the room, leaving behind a silence that was far heavier and more troubled than any the house had known for a year. That night, for the first time since the Ghost’s departure, a floorboard creaked ominously in the corridor, a cold draught seemed to follow Virginia from room to room, and the great stained-glass window on the landing rattled as if in a gale, though the night air was perfectly still. The peace of Canterville was broken.

The following weeks were most unpleasant. Lord Augustus, unable to afford a solicitor, took his case to the court of public opinion. He wrote letters to The Times, he gave interviews to society papers that delighted in scandal, and he spread his tale of woe throughout the London clubs. The story of the upstart Americans, the stolen heirlooms, and the ‘fanciful’ ghost story became the talk of the season. Some sided with the young Duke and his charming American bride, but many, particularly those of the older aristocracy, sympathised with Lord Augustus. He was, after all, one of them, and the idea of a family’s treasures being given away by a spectre was an affront to all established notions of inheritance.

The Duke was furious, and spoke of horsewhipping and calling the man out, but was restrained by his wife and his father-in-law. Mr. Otis, for his part, hired a private investigator who soon discovered that Lord Augustus’s life abroad had been a shabby affair of gambling debts and failed business ventures. The man was, in short, a desperate and impecunious rogue.

Virginia, however, was not worried about the gossip, nor about the dubious claims of a desperate man. What troubled her was the house itself. The atmosphere had soured. The air grew heavy, flowers seemed to wilt faster in their vases, and a faint, musty smell, the very scent of decay and grievance, began to permeate the ancient corridors. It was as if Lord Augustus’s bitterness had infected the Chase, reawakening some dormant misery within its walls. She felt Sir Simon’s hard-won peace slipping away, and it broke her heart.

It was the twins, of course, who decided to take matters into their own hands. It was an opportunity too splendid to be ignored, a veritable call to arms for the inventive spirits of Washington and his younger brothers, who, though now approaching an age of supposed maturity, had lost none of their republican zeal for discomfiting the pompous. They had listened to Lord Augustus’s threats with great interest.

‘He says the spirit of his family won’t stand for it, you know,’ said one twin to the other, as they oiled a cricket bat on the lawn.

‘And that the stones of the house cry out,’ added his brother, polishing a new sort of camera he had imported from America.

‘It seems a pity to disappoint him,’ they said in unison, a familiar, wicked gleam in their eyes.

Their plan was formulated with military precision. They learned from a disgruntled footman that Lord Augustus, having been refused entry to the house, had taken to walking the grounds in the evening, staring mournfully at the grand façade. The twins made their preparations. Their arsenal included Washington’s new camera, which came with a device for creating a brilliant flash of magnesium powder, and, most importantly, a phonograph that Mr. Otis had brought over from the States, complete with a large brass horn and several wax cylinders.

One evening, as dusk was settling its grey cloak over the park, Lord Augustus began his customary melancholy patrol. He paused near the lime-tree avenue, sighing theatrically as he gazed upon the house that should have been his. As he stood there, a low, moaning sound began to emanate from the ancient yew hedge behind him.

‘Woe... woe... woe...’

Lord Augustus froze. The sound was unearthly, a hollow groan that seemed to rise from the very soil.

‘Who is there?’ he called out, his voice trembling slightly.

The moaning grew louder, closer. ‘Who dares disturb this house of sorrow? Who dares to bring avarice and spite where there is peace?’

Lord Augustus’s face, pale in the twilight, went a shade paler. This was it. The family spirit, roused to his defence. ‘It is I!’ he cried, his confidence returning. ‘Augustus de Canterville! Your kinsman! I have come to reclaim our birthright!’

‘Birthright?’ the voice boomed, now seeming to come from the other side of him. ‘The only birthright of a Canterville is sin, and the only inheritance is repentance! You trade on a name you have disgraced! You seek gold you have not earned!’

‘I am the last of the line!’ he stammered, turning in a circle. ‘The jewels... they are mine!’

‘The jewels were the price of a soul!’ the voice thundered, and at that exact moment, a blinding, supernatural flash of white light erupted from the branches of a nearby oak tree, illuminating Lord Augustus in a terrifying, spectral glare. At the same instant, the camera’s shutter clicked.

The effect on Lord Augustus was instantaneous and total. He gave a single, high-pitched shriek of pure terror, and then, without a second’s hesitation, he turned and fled. He ran as no Canterville had likely ever run before, his coattails flying, his dignity completely abandoned. He did not stop until he reached the Ascot road, where he flagged down a passing farmer’s cart and begged for a ride to the station, babbling about vengeful spirits and infernal light.

Behind the yew hedge, the twins collapsed in silent, helpless laughter, carefully packing away the phonograph. From the oak tree, Washington scrambled down, clutching his precious photographic plate.

The next morning, Mr. Otis received a telegram. It was from a frantic Lord Augustus, who had sent it from London. It read: ‘HAVE BEEN WARNED OFF BY ANCESTRAL SPIRIT. RELINQUISH ALL CLAIMS. BEG YOU TO PROPITIATE IT. DEEPEST APOLOGIES.’

In the library, the family gathered around the evidence. The twins, with immense pride, recounted their adventure. Cecil roared with laughter, and even Mrs. Otis, who disapproved of such pranks on principle, could not help but smile. Mr. Otis looked at his sons with a mixture of exasperation and admiration.

‘Well, boys,’ he said, ‘I cannot approve of your methods. Deceiving a man like that is not... entirely honourable. However,’ he added, a twinkle in his eye, ‘I must admit the result is highly satisfactory. You have saved me a great deal in legal fees.’

Washington then produced his masterpiece: a photograph, developed that morning, showing Lord Augustus de Canterville, his face a mask of pure horror, his hair standing on end, illuminated in a ghostly white light. It was a triumph of the photographic art.

It was Virginia who did not laugh. She looked at the photograph, at the pathetic, terrified face of the man, and felt not triumph, but a profound pity. She remembered Sir Simon’s despair, his centuries of loneliness and guilt. Lord Augustus was a fool and a rogue, but he was also a sad, broken man, clinging to a past that had brought his family nothing but misery.

That afternoon, she sat down and wrote a letter. She did not send the photograph. Instead, she enclosed a cheque for a modest but not insignificant sum. ‘My lord,’ she wrote, ‘peace, I have learned, is not a thing to be inherited like a house or a title, nor is it found in a casket of jewels. It must be earned. Sir Simon earned his through repentance, and I was but the instrument of his peace. These jewels are not a trophy of lineage; they are the price of a soul’s redemption, and as such, their worth is not something that can be measured or claimed. I hope this enclosure will assist you in finding a new start, and in time, a peace of your own. For I believe that is what the spirit of Canterville would truly wish for you.’

She sent the letter, and they never heard from Lord Augustus de Canterville again, though some months later, Cecil heard a rumour that a man matching his description had been seen running a small but respectable tea-importing business in Cheltenham.

As Virginia sealed the envelope, she felt the house change once more. A light breeze stirred the curtains, carrying the sweet scent of roses from the garden. The oppressive weight lifted from the air, and the faint, musty smell of grievance vanished as if it had never been. The silence that fell upon the Chase that evening was not the tense silence of a broken truce, but the deep, abiding peace she remembered, the peace of a soul at rest.

Walking in the garden with Cecil as the sun set, she saw that a single, premature white blossom had appeared on the old almond-tree, out of season but perfect in its form. She smiled, and took her husband’s arm, knowing that the lesson Sir Simon had taught her was one she would carry for life: that the greatest strength lies not in claiming one’s rights, but in forgiveness, and that true love is always, and without exception, stronger than pride. The house was still, and peace had returned to Canterville, this time, she felt, for good.

For several months, life at Canterville Chase settled into a rhythm of such profound and uninterrupted tranquility that even the twins, who had once found their chief delight in manufacturing chaos, seemed to have been tamed by the prevailing atmosphere of contentment, preferring the simple pleasures of fishing in the carp-pond to the more elaborate excitements of their past campaigns. The great house itself seemed to breathe more easily; its ancient timbers no longer groaned with the weight of old sorrows, and the sunlight which streamed through the oriel windows of the great library seemed to linger longer than it ought, as if reluctant to leave a place that had finally learned the secret of peace. Mrs Otis declared the air was better for one’s constitution; Washington found his photographic subjects decidedly less dramatic, but far more cooperative, and spent his afternoons capturing the play of light on the yew-tree walk and the placid surface of the ornamental lake. The little Duke of Cheshire, now a permanent and cherished fixture of the household, found that his love for Virginia deepened in the quiet of the Chase, a love no longer fraught with the anxiety of her mysterious disappearances, but enriched by the shared memory of the strange and solemn duty she had performed.

It was into this domestic idyll that the postman, one bright Tuesday morning, delivered a letter bearing a Boston postmark. It was addressed to Mr. Otis, and written in a hand so precise and angular it seemed to have been formed by a scientific instrument rather than a human being. Mr. Otis read it over his breakfast, a thoughtful frown gathering between his brows, before passing it across the table to his wife.

‘Well, my dear,’ he said, in that frank and open manner of his, ‘it appears our little spectral adventure has gained some notoriety in academic circles. It seems we are to be the subject of a scientific monograph.’

The letter was from a certain Mr. Alistair Finch, a Fellow of the Boston Society for Psychical Research. Mr. Finch wrote with great and tedious formality, explaining that he had become aware, through certain transatlantic correspondences, of the ‘well-attested, though hitherto un-catalogued, series of phenomena’ that had occurred at Canterville Chase. He expressed his profound regret that the ‘primary causal agent,’ by which he meant Sir Simon, was no longer available for study, but posited that any location subjected to such a prolonged ‘psychic disturbance’ would undoubtedly retain significant residual energy. He therefore proposed, with the Minister’s permission, to visit the Chase for a period of one or two weeks, in order to conduct a series of experiments designed to measure and document this energy. He would, he assured them, bring his own delicate instrumentation and require nothing more than simple board and the freedom to investigate the premises at his leisure.

Mrs. Otis read the letter and sniffed delicately. ‘A psychical researcher? Hiram, I do hope he does not intend to bring frogs or electrical wires into the house. The housekeeper has only just recovered from the twins’ last experiment with the garden hose.’

Washington, however, was immediately intrigued. ‘A proper scientific inquiry!’ he exclaimed, adjusting his spectacles. ‘This is precisely what the situation has always called for. We have a wealth of anecdotal evidence, but no quantifiable data. I should be very interested to see his equipment. I wonder if he has considered the potential of spectrographic analysis on the ectoplasmic residue.’

‘Oh, what fun!’ cried the twins in unison. ‘Can we help him? We know all the best places for phenomena!’

It was Virginia who felt a cold unease settle over the cheerful breakfast-table. She looked at her father, her large blue eyes filled with a sudden, anxious gravity. ‘Papa,’ she said softly, ‘I do not think that is a good idea.’

‘And why not, my dear?’ asked Mr. Otis, surprised. ‘It is for the advancement of knowledge. If our experience can contribute to the sum of human understanding, it is our duty to allow it. We Americans are not afraid of progress, even when it comes in a strange shape.’

‘But it is not about progress,’ Virginia insisted, her voice trembling slightly. ‘It was about a poor, unhappy soul. It was a secret, a very sad secret between him and me. It does not feel right to have a stranger come and… measure it. It seems a kind of violation. Cecil, you understand, do you not?’

The young Duke, who had been listening with an expression of aristocratic distaste, placed his hand gently over hers. ‘Entirely, my love,’ he said, directing a cool gaze at his father-in-law. ‘Some things, sir, are matters of the spirit, not of science. To turn the Chase into a laboratory would be in the most dreadful taste. It would be like asking a botanist to dissect a memorial wreath.’

Mr. Otis, however, was a man of his principles, and his principles were eminently practical. ‘Now, Cecil,’ he said kindly, ‘that is a very poetic and English way of looking at things, and I respect it. But in my country, we believe in facing facts and analysing them. This Mr. Finch is a man of learning. He is not a showman. To deny him would seem superstitious, and I will not have it said that the family of the United States Minister is superstitious. The letter is decided. Mr. Finch shall be welcome at Canterville.’

And so, despite the joint protests of his daughter and son-in-law, the invitation was dispatched. A fortnight later, Mr. Alistair Finch arrived at the railway station, accompanied by two large trunks that appeared to be constructed more of brass and leather strapping than of wood. He was a thin, dry man of middle years, with pale, watchful eyes and a habit of pressing the tips of his fingers together when he spoke, as if continually weighing evidence. He greeted the family with a stiff, formal bow and presented Mrs. Otis with a small, unsmiling dissertation on the meteorological conditions of the Atlantic crossing.

His presence immediately cast a pall over the house. The easy peace of the preceding months evaporated, replaced by an atmosphere of clinical observation. Mr. Finch spoke in long, academic sentences, referring to the house not as a home but as the ‘locus of the disturbance,’ and to the family’s experiences as ‘subjective testimony.’ He spent his first day unpacking his equipment in the Tapestry Chamber, which he had selected as his base of operations. The room was soon filled with a bewildering array of polished brass and mahogany instruments: a self-registering thermometer for detecting sudden drops in temperature, a highly sensitive barometer, a device for collecting air samples in sealed glass tubes, and a formidable camera fitted with a series of coloured lenses which, he explained, might capture light on spectra invisible to the naked eye.

The twins were at first delighted, following him about with a notebook and pretending to take very serious notes, but their enthusiasm waned when Mr. Finch explained that the primary requirement for a successful investigation was absolute silence and a lack of ‘external contamination,’ which he clearly defined as them. Washington attempted to engage him in a technical discussion about photographic emulsions, but was met with a patronising lecture on the fundamental differences between amateur portraiture and true scientific actinometry. Mr. and Mrs. Otis treated him with the unfailing politeness of good hosts, though Mrs. Otis was heard to confide in the housekeeper that she was quite sure Mr. Finch’s instruments were gathering more dust than data.

It was Virginia who suffered most. She felt as if the ghost of Sir Simon had been resurrected, not as a tormented soul, but as a pinned butterfly in a collector’s case. The house, her friend, felt alien and unhappy under the researcher’s cold gaze. The little huntsmen on the tapestry seemed to have lowered their horns, their embroidered faces turned away in silent protest. The library, her sanctuary, felt like a specimen room. She took to spending her days out of doors with Cecil, walking for miles through the park, trying to recapture the feeling of peace that had been so unceremoniously evicted.

‘It is insufferable,’ Cecil declared, flicking a fallen leaf from his coat with a walking stick. ‘The man is a ghoul of a different sort. Sir Simon at least had the good grace to be tragic. This fellow is merely tedious. He asked me this morning if I had ever personally experienced an “auditory hallucination of a percussive or metallic nature.” I told him I had, frequently, at the opera.’

Virginia gave a small, sad smile. ‘Papa does not see it. He thinks it is his duty to science. But Sir Simon did not ask for science. He asked for prayers. He asked for tears.’

The investigation, meanwhile, was yielding nothing. Mr. Finch’s thermometer refused to register any dramatic fluctuations; his barometer remained stubbornly steady. The air of Canterville Chase, when analysed, proved to be composed of the usual, unsurprising mixture of nitrogen, oxygen, and a faint, lingering trace of Mrs. Otis’s eau-de-cologne. After a week of fruitless observation, Mr. Finch began to look discouraged, and his evening disquisitions grew even longer and more convoluted, filled with theories of ‘psychic latency’ and ‘dormant energy patterns.’

It was at this point that the twins decided the cause of science was in need of a little assistance. Their sense of mischief, long suppressed by the household’s newfound peace, reasserted itself with a vengeance.

Their first contribution took place late one evening, as Mr. Finch sat alone in the Tapestry Chamber, watching his needles. A low, mournful moan echoed down the corridor, a sound of such profound and theatrical misery that Mr. Finch leapt from his chair, grabbing a notepad. He followed the sound to the end of the hall, where it seemed to emanate from a large suit of armour. As he approached, the moan rose in pitch, culminating in a rattling clang as the helmet of the armour slowly tilted forward and fell to the floor with a tremendous crash. Mr. Finch scribbled furiously. ‘Phenomenon 7b: Spontaneous kinetic event concurrent with auditory manifestation,’ he muttered. He did not notice the fine, almost invisible piece of black thread leading from the helmet’s gorget, up the wall, and through a small crack in the ceiling, where two small, stifled giggles could just be heard.

The next night, as Mr. Finch was taking air samples near the library window where the prophecy had been written, the letters on the pane began to glow with a faint, greenish light. It was an impressive effect, and Mr. Finch spent nearly an hour attempting to photograph it, his face a mask of intense concentration. He was quite satisfied with the results, and did not question why the strange luminescence smelled so distinctly of the phosphorous paste Washington used in his darkroom, a small pot of which was now missing.

Their masterpiece, however, was reserved for the end of the second week. Having learned from Washington that Mr. Finch was particularly interested in ‘olfactory manifestations,’ they set to work. That night, the entire ground floor of the Chase became filled with a sequence of the most extraordinary and unpleasant odours. It began with the smell of an open tomb, a scent they achieved by boiling old cabbage leaves with a cup of mud from the carp-pond. This was followed by the sulphurous stench of brimstone, courtesy of a large box of matches ignited in the fireplace, and culminated in what they described to each other as ‘the aroma of a guilty conscience,’ which apparently smelled very much like a pair of Cecil’s riding boots that had been left too close to a block of decaying cheese.

Mr. Finch was beside himself with a kind of grim, scientific ecstasy. He rushed from room to room with his air-sampling device, bottling the dreadful smells and labelling them with trembling hands. He declared it a breakthrough, a clear case of ‘sequential, thematically-linked psychic imprinting.’

The family, descending for breakfast the next morning, was met by an atmosphere that was, to put it mildly, offensive. Mr. Otis threw open the windows, while Mrs. Otis pressed a scented handkerchief to her nose and declared that the drains must be seen to at once. It was then that Cecil, his nose for mischief being nearly as sharp as his nose for claret, noticed a faint smear of cheese on the leg of the twins’ trousers. He said nothing, but a slow smile spread across his face.

That evening, Mr. Finch announced his intention to hold a final, conclusive séance in the Tapestry Chamber. He requested the attendance of the entire family, explaining that the collective ‘psychic battery’ of the household might be sufficient to trigger a more direct and observable event. Reluctantly, they all gathered. The twins looked immensely pleased with themselves, Washington appeared clinically detached, and Mr. and Mrs. Otis bore the expressions of martyrs to the cause of progress. Virginia sat beside Cecil, her hands clasped tightly in her lap, her face pale and unhappy.

Mr. Finch dimmed the lamps and arranged them all in a circle. He began to speak in a low monotone, asking any ‘residual consciousness’ to make itself known. For a long time, there was only silence, broken by the ticking of the grandfather clock in the hall. Mr. Finch’s voice grew more insistent. ‘We are here not as intruders, but as seekers of truth,’ he intoned. ‘We offer a medium for communication, a vessel for manifestation.’

It was at that moment that Virginia, unable to bear the charade any longer, stood up. Her movement was so sudden that everyone started. In the dim light, her face was luminous with an indignation that was almost holy.

‘You will find nothing,’ she said, her voice clear and resonant in the quiet room. It held none of its usual timidity; it was the voice of a girl who had walked in the Garden of Death and was not afraid. ‘You will find nothing because you are looking in the wrong way, and for the wrong thing.’

Mr. Finch looked up, startled and annoyed. ‘Miss Otis,’ he began, ‘the conditions for observation must not be broken…’

‘You speak of seeking truth,’ she continued, ignoring him, her gaze fixed on the space in the centre of the circle. ‘But the truth of this house is not in your instruments. The truth was in a sinner’s despair, and in the pity that could wash it clean. You are looking for energy, for phenomena, for things you can write in your book. But Sir Simon was not a phenomenon. He was a man who had forgotten how to weep, and who had lost his faith to pray. He did not need to be measured. He needed to be forgiven.’

She took a step forward, her eyes filling with tears, not for herself, but for the memory of the sad, shackled spirit. ‘He asked me to weep for his sins, because he had no tears. He asked me to pray for his soul, because he had no faith. He showed me the terror of darkness and the bitterness of a life without peace. And I learned that love is stronger than death. That is the only truth of Canterville Chase. It is not a thing you can capture in a glass tube or see through a coloured lens. It is something that can only be felt, in here.’ She pressed a hand to her heart. ‘And your science, sir, your cold, curious science, is frightening it away. You are not honouring his memory; you are desecrating his peace.’

As she spoke, a remarkable thing occurred. It was not a moan, nor a rattling chain, nor a phantom smell. It was something far stranger. A soft, gentle breeze stirred the air in the sealed room, carrying with it the unmistakable, achingly sweet scent of a thousand fresh-blooming roses. The heavy, faded green tapestry on the wall, the one on which the little huntsmen were broidered, seemed to brighten, its colours momentarily restored to their original vibrancy, as if lit from within. Through the window, though it was a dark and moonless night, the old almond-tree became visible, and upon its barren branch, the single, perfect white blossom glowed with a soft, pearlescent light of its own.

No instrument recorded it. No needle jumped. But everyone in the room felt it: a wave of profound, unassailable peace, a feeling of gratitude and gentle benediction that was so powerful it brought tears to Mrs. Otis’s eyes.

The twins stared, their mouths agape, all thoughts of pranks forgotten. Washington slowly took off his spectacles and polished them, as if he could not believe what he was seeing, or rather, feeling. Cecil put his arm around Virginia, his cynical smile replaced by an expression of pure awe.

Mr. Alistair Finch sat frozen in his chair. His notepad lay forgotten on the floor. He looked from the glowing blossom, to the strangely vivid tapestry, to the tear-streaked, radiant face of Virginia. He opened his mouth to speak, to offer a theory of mass hypnosis or olfactory suggestion, but the words would not come. The truth he had been seeking was present in the room, and it had no scientific name. It was a truth of the heart, and his mind had no way to process it.

Slowly, silently, he began to gather his things. He did not pack his instruments away with his usual precision, but bundled them hastily into their cases. The next morning, he announced his departure.

At the door, he turned to Virginia. ‘My monograph,’ he said, and for the first time his voice lacked its confident, academic edge, ‘will be on the limitations of empirical evidence in the study of deeply personal human experience. I came here to find a ghost, Miss Otis. Instead, I believe I have found something far more inexplicable: the evidence of a soul.’

He gave a short, stiff bow, and without another word, he was gone.

As the carriage drove away down the avenue, a great weight seemed to lift from the house for the second time. The sunlight, streaming in, felt warm and genuine again. Virginia looked at Cecil and smiled, a real smile of relief and happiness. The twins, chastened and awestruck, went quietly out into the garden to check on the almond-tree. The single blossom was still there, a tiny, perfect star against the dark wood.

That evening, as the family sat together in the library, the silence that fell was not the sterile silence of a laboratory, nor the tense silence of an unquiet grave. It was the deep, living peace of a home that had learned its lesson not once, but twice; the peace that must be earned through forgiveness, and then protected with love. The house was still, and peace had come back to Canterville, defended not against the sorrows of the past, but against the heartless curiosity of the future. And Virginia knew, with a certainty that no science could ever shake, that it was there to stay.

When they had finished their supper, and Mrs. Otis had been persuaded to take a little wine and toast, a feeling of profound exhaustion settled upon the occupants of Canterville Chase. The twins, for the first time in their memorable career, went to bed without a word of protest, their small faces pale and their usual fire quite extinguished. Washington paced the library for a time, muttering to himself about the inefficiency of county police forces, before he too retired, leaving Mr. Otis and the little Duke of Cheshire alone by the dying embers of the fire. ‘She will be found, Cecil,’ said the Minister, placing a kindly hand on the boy’s shoulder, for his own heart was heavy as lead, and he found some small comfort in attempting to give it to another. ‘Virginia is a sensible girl. There is no mischief she could have fallen into from which she cannot extricate herself.’ ‘But the Ghost, Mr. Otis, the Ghost!’ the boy cried, looking up with eyes that were wild with a sorrow too great for his years. ‘She went with him! I know it! He spoke of the Garden of Death, and she, in her goodness, she went with him!’ Mr. Otis sighed. He was a practical man, a man of stocks and shares, of political parties and tangible results, and he had no language for this spectral dread. He had accepted the Ghost as a regrettable fixture of the property, a sort of permanent, spiritual dry-rot, but he had never for a moment considered him capable of actual, physical abduction. ‘There is no such place, my boy,’ he said, though his voice lacked its usual firm conviction. ‘It is a poetical fancy, a piece of old-world folderol. We will have the house searched again from attic to cellar. There is a rational explanation for this. There is always a rational explanation.’ But as he spoke, a curious stillness fell upon the room. The wind, which had been rattling the casements, seemed to hold its breath. The old clock on the mantelpiece, which had ticked away the centuries with relentless monotony, suddenly fell silent. Both Mr. Otis and the Duke felt a strange tremor in the air, a faint, almost imperceptible vibration, as if a great chord had been struck at an impossible distance. At that very moment, a panel of the wainscoting to the left of the great chimney-piece creaked, groaned, and then swung slowly inwards, revealing not a dark cavern, but the familiar, dusty passage of a priest’s hole, unused for generations. And from it, pale and trembling, but with a wondrous light in her eyes, stepped Virginia. She was holding in her hand a small casket of antique workmanship, fashioned from dark wood and bound with tarnished silver. For a moment, she stood blinking in the light of the lamps, and then, seeing her father and the Duke, she gave a little cry and ran towards them. ‘Virginia!’ exclaimed Mr. Otis, catching her in his arms. ‘My dear child, where have you been? We have been out of our minds with worry! We have had the gypsies arrested, we have dragged the pond, I have sent telegrams to every police station in the county!’ ‘I have been with the Ghost,’ she said very quietly, her head resting against his shoulder. ‘He is free now. He is at peace. Sir Simon has been forgiven.’ The little Duke had crept to her side, and now took her other hand, pressing it to his lips as if she were a holy relic. ‘You went to the Garden of Death,’ he whispered in awe. Virginia looked at him, and her expression was very grave and sweet. ‘Yes,’ she said. ‘But do not call it that. It is the Garden of Sleep. It is very beautiful.’ She led them back to the secret panel. ‘For three hundred years,’ she said, ‘he has wandered and wept. He told me all his story. His wife’s brothers starved him to death in this very room because he had killed her, though he said it was in a fit of passion, and that she was a dreadful housekeeper. He was chained to the wall, and they left him with only a jug of water and a crust of bread. But before he died, his wife’s ghost appeared to him and forgave him, and she gave him this.’ She held up the little casket. ‘She promised him that when a maiden had wept for his sins and prayed for his soul, the barren almond-tree would blossom, and he would be allowed to sleep. I wept with him, and I prayed, though I have so little practice, and then he took me to his garden.’ Mr. Otis and the Duke peered into the dark passage. At the far end, lying on the stone floor, was a skeleton, chained by its wrists and ankles to a great iron ring in the wall. It was a gruesome sight, but it seemed to hold no terror. A faint, dry scent, like pot-pourri, emanated from the little chamber, and a strange calm pervaded it. The little Duke shuddered, but Virginia put her hand on his arm. ‘Do not be sad,’ she said. ‘Look.’ She pointed, and they saw that clustered around the skeleton, growing from the dust and decay, were a mass of strange, dark flowers, of a kind they had never seen before, and that the old almond-tree outside the library window, which had been barren for as long as any could remember, was now covered in a profusion of delicate, white blossoms. It was a miracle of grace in the heart of a winter’s night. ‘He gave me this, before he vanished,’ Virginia said, and she opened the little casket. Inside, resting on a bed of faded violet silk, was a magnificent necklace of rubies, set in the Venetian style, that glittered and burned like frozen drops of blood in the lamplight. ‘Good heavens!’ cried the Minister, ‘what a treasure! My dear child, you must not accept it. This is a family heirloom and may be worth a fortune. I shall have to write to Lord Canterville and insist that he takes it back. It is clearly entailed with the estate.’ ‘But Papa,’ said Virginia, ‘it is a gift. It is for me. Sir Simon said it was the least he could do for my trouble, and that it would look very well with the frock I am to wear to the next court ball. You would not have me be rude and refuse it, would you?’ Mr. Otis looked from his daughter’s earnest face to the glittering jewels, and for once in his life, he was at a loss for a practical response. The whole affair was entirely outside the bounds of his experience. He could negotiate a trade deal with the Germans and outwit the most cunning of Tammany Hall politicians, but he had no precedent for dealing with three-hundred-year-old ghosts who gave away priceless heirlooms. He was saved from having to formulate a policy on the matter by the arrival of Mrs. Otis and the twins, who, having been roused by the sounds from downstairs, now appeared in a flurry of dressing-gowns and alarm. The story had to be told all over again, to a running commentary of gasps from Mrs. Otis, who immediately declared that the draft from the secret passage would give Virginia a fatal chill and prescribed a large dose of her celebrated ‘Tammany Rising Sun’ tincture, and of incredulous whoops from the twins, who were bitterly disappointed that they had missed the final, and most exciting, act of the Ghost’s career. The following morning, the whole family, including the little Duke, went in solemn procession to the nearby churchyard for the funeral of Sir Simon de Canterville. Virginia laid the dark, strange flowers she had found in the secret chamber upon the pall, and as the coffin was lowered into the earth, which had been consecrated for the purpose, the sun broke through the clouds and flooded the little churchyard with liquid gold. It was a simple and beautiful ceremony, and as they turned to leave, Virginia thought she saw a faint, shimmering figure by the old yew tree, waving a grateful farewell, but when she blinked, it was gone. Life at Canterville Chase, after these remarkable events, settled into a period of almost unearthly tranquillity. The blood-stain in the library, which Washington had so diligently attempted to remove, vanished of its own accord and never reappeared. The strange noises ceased, the suits of armour remained firmly on their pedestals, and Mrs. Umney, the old housekeeper, was never again troubled by phantom hands playing tricks with her knitting needles. The Otis family found, to their surprise, that they had grown rather fond of the old house, now that it was no longer haunted, and they spoke of the Ghost with a sort of affectionate regret, as one might speak of a difficult but memorable house-guest who had finally departed on a long journey. The little Duke of Cheshire remained with them, his devotion to Virginia having become a fixed and unalterable fact of life at the Chase, like the sunrise or the ringing of the dinner-bell. He followed her everywhere, carried her sketching-books, praised her water-colours with an ardour that was entirely sincere, and quarrelled fiercely with the twins if they dared to aim their pea-shooters in her direction. It was, therefore, a matter of no surprise to anyone when, some months later, on Virginia’s seventeenth birthday, he formally asked Mr. Otis for her hand in marriage. The Minister was in his study at the time, going over the accounts for a new brand of patent stain-remover he was considering launching in the English market, and he looked up at the handsome, earnest young man with an expression of amused benevolence. ‘Well, Cecil,’ he said, laying down his pen, ‘in my country, a young lady makes her own mind up about these things. I have no objection, provided Virginia is of the same opinion. However,’ and here his expression became more serious, ‘you are the Duke of Cheshire. Your guardians will have to be consulted. Your family is one of the oldest in England, and we, as you know, are simple Americans. They may not view the match with favour.’ ‘I don’t care a fig for my guardians!’ cried the boy, his face flushing. ‘It is my life, and I shall marry whom I please! Virginia is worth more than all the duchesses in Debrett’s Peerage put together!’ ‘That may be true,’ said Mr. Otis with a dry smile, ‘but unfortunately, Debrett’s Peerage does not agree. Your principal guardian, I believe, is your great-aunt, the Dowager Duchess of Dorking. I suggest you write to her, and we shall invite her to the Chase. It is always best to face these matters head-on.’ The Dowager Duchess of Dorking was a formidable personage, a relic from a sterner and more unforgiving age, who regarded all things modern, and particularly all things American, with a deep and abiding horror. She lived in a vast, gloomy house in Belgravia, where the blinds were perpetually drawn and the air smelled of beeswax and disapproval. Upon receiving her great-nephew’s letter, she was thrown into a state of what she herself termed ‘profound constitutional shock,’ and immediately ordered her carriage, her maid, and a dozen trunks, and set off for the wilds of Berkshire to put a stop to this appalling mésalliance. Her arrival at Canterville Chase was like the descent of a glacier. She was a tall, angular woman with a face like a Roman bust and eyes as sharp and cold as splintered ice. She surveyed the cheerful, comfortable chaos of the Otis household through a lorgnette, as if it were a particularly unhygienic foreign zoo. She subjected Virginia to a scrutiny so intense and prolonged that the poor girl felt as if she were being assessed for faults by a judge at a dog show. ‘So,’ the Duchess said, her voice a reedy whisper that cut through the air like a razor, ‘you are the young person who has so bewitched my nephew.’ ‘I am Virginia Otis,’ said Virginia simply, though her heart was beating very fast. ‘And Cecil and I are very much in love.’ ‘Love,’ said the Duchess, with a small, contemptuous sniff, ‘is a matter for dairymaids and poets. A marriage in our family is a matter of state. It is an alliance of blood, of property, of history. Your father, I am given to understand, is in trade?’ ‘He is the American Minister,’ said Virginia, with a flash of spirit. ‘He was formerly the proprietor of Otis’s Celebrated Stomach Bitters,’ said the Duchess, consulting a small, leather-bound notebook. ‘And, before that, a variety of products including a patent pig-wormer and a self-acting carpet cleaner. The blood of the Cheshires cannot, and will not, be mingled with carpet cleaner.’ That evening, she cornered Mr. Otis in the library and delivered her ultimatum. The engagement was to be broken off at once. Cecil would be sent on a Grand Tour of the Continent, accompanied by a suitable tutor, until he had forgotten this childish infatuation. ‘Madam,’ said Mr. Otis, who was not in the least intimidated by her, ‘in my country, we believe that the pursuit of happiness is an inalienable right. My daughter’s happiness is my primary concern. If she wishes to marry your nephew, and he her, then they shall do so. As for the matter of trade, I can assure you that the profits from Otis’s Stomach Bitters are more than sufficient to restore the roof at Cheshire House, which, I am told, is in a lamentable state of disrepair.’ The Duchess turned a shade of pale mauve. ‘You are an impertinent vulgarian, sir,’ she hissed. ‘You are a snob, madam,’ he replied calmly, and, bowing politely, he left her alone with her fury. For two days, a chill descended upon the house that was colder than any Sir Simon had ever produced. The Duchess spoke to no one, communicating her desires through a series of sharp gestures and intimidating glares. The twins attempted to rig a bucket of water over her bedroom door, but she anticipated them with a sort of sixth sense and had the footman remove it, boxing their ears soundly into the bargain, an action which won her their grudging and immediate respect. Mrs. Otis tried to placate her with a soothing herbal remedy, which the Duchess promptly threw out of the window, declaring it to be poison. The situation seemed hopeless. Cecil was in despair, and Virginia, for all her quiet courage, felt her spirits beginning to fail. On the third evening, Virginia was sitting alone in the library, looking out at the old almond-tree, which was still covered in its ghostly white blossoms. The little casket of jewels lay in her lap, but she took no pleasure in it. She heard the door open and, turning, saw the Dowager Duchess standing there, her face set like stone. ‘I have come to say my final word, Miss Otis,’ the Duchess began. ‘I leave in the morning. I forbid this match. Absolutely. Cecil is a boy. He does not know his own mind. He is being led astray by a pretty face and a fortune that smells of the shop.’ ‘It is not his fortune I love,’ said Virginia, her voice trembling slightly. ‘And I do not think it is my face he loves, either. He loves me. And I love him. Is that not enough?’ ‘Enough?’ the Duchess scoffed. ‘My dear girl, it is barely the beginning. You do not understand what it means to be a Duchess of Cheshire. It is a sacred trust. Our history is written in the stone of our houses, in the portraits on our walls, in the earth of our churchyards. What can you, a child from a country with no history, possibly know of such things?’ Virginia stood up, and the strange light that had been in her eyes when she had faced the Ghost returned. ‘I know more than you think, Your Grace,’ she said, her voice suddenly clear and strong. ‘I have held the hand of a man who died in this house three hundred years ago. I have seen the sorrow in his soul and I have prayed for it. I have walked in a garden that is not on any map of this world. This house has a history, and it has shared it with me. And its history is one of sin, and sorrow, and, finally, of forgiveness. And it was love that made that forgiveness possible. Love, which you say is for dairymaids, was strong enough to bring peace to a tortured soul. I think that makes it quite strong enough to be the foundation of a marriage, even the marriage of a Duke.’ As she spoke, the most extraordinary thing happened. A soft, pearlescent light began to emanate from the almond-tree outside the window, causing the blossoms to glow with an unearthly radiance. The light streamed into the room, illuminating Virginia’s face and falling upon the ancient tapestry, where the little huntsmen seemed to lower their horns in a gesture of respect. The room grew very still, filled with that same profound, unassailable peace that the family had felt on the night the Ghost had vanished. The Dowager Duchess stood frozen, her lorgnette dropping from her nerveless fingers. She stared at Virginia, then at the glowing tree, and a look of utter astonishment, mingled with a profound and unfamiliar awe, came over her face. She was a woman of iron will and unbending prejudice, but she was also, in her own way, a true daughter of her ancient house, and she recognised a power and a mystery that was older and deeper than her own. She saw in Virginia not the daughter of an American tradesman, but a girl who had communed with the very spirit of the place she held so sacred. ‘What is this… magic?’ she whispered, her voice barely audible. ‘It is not magic,’ said Virginia softly. ‘It is the proof of what I have told you. Sir Simon gave me this in thanks.’ She opened the casket, and the rubies blazed in the strange, soft light, looking not like jewels, but like the heart’s blood of the old house itself. ‘He told me that love is stronger than death. Surely, then, it is stronger than pride.’ For a long time, the Duchess was silent. She looked from the jewels to Virginia’s radiant, tear-streaked face, and then back to the miraculously blossoming tree. A great and terrible battle was being fought in her soul between the prejudices of a lifetime and the undeniable evidence of a miracle. At last, she drew a long, shuddering breath. ‘The rubies,’ she said, her voice hoarse, ‘are the famous Cheshire jewels. They were a wedding gift from the Doge of Venice to Duchess Eleanor in 1575. They were thought to be lost forever when she was… when she disappeared.’ She looked at Virginia, and for the first time, her icy eyes softened with a flicker of human feeling. ‘It seems,’ she said, with a sigh that seemed to echo through the centuries, ‘that the House of Canterville has chosen its new mistress. And the House of Cheshire must, I suppose, concur.’ And so, Virginia Otis became the Duchess of Cheshire, and everyone agreed that it was a most brilliant and successful match. The wedding was a grand affair, and the old Dowager, to everyone’s astonishment, not only attended but presented Virginia with a magnificent tiara of diamonds, remarking that while American sentiment was all very well, a Duchess must have the proper stones. Mr. Otis walked his daughter down the aisle, looking prouder than if he had just secured a monopoly on global shipping, and the twins acted as page-boys, managing to get through the entire ceremony without loosing a single frog or firing a single pea. When Virginia and Cecil returned to Canterville Chase after their honeymoon, the first thing she did was to walk out into the garden. The old almond-tree was bare once more, its branches dark and twisted against the sky. Its purpose had been served. The house was finally, and completely, at peace, its sorrows laid to rest, its future secured not by bloodlines or by bargains, but by the gentle courage of a little girl from America. And as she stood there, with her husband’s arm around her, Virginia knew that the best gift the Ghost had given her was not the casket of jewels, but the understanding that true love is always, in the end, the strongest magic of all.

They abandoned the dining-room and trooped, a small, miserable procession, back to the Tapestry Chamber. Nothing had changed. The room was cold and silent, the air thick with the scent of dust and something else, something ancient and sorrowful. The little huntsmen on the faded green tapestry were perfectly still, their tasselled horns forever silent, their tiny hands giving no clue. The goggle-eyed monsters on the chimney-piece stared blankly, their carved warnings having died on their lips.

Washington tapped the wainscoting where Virginia had vanished. It was solid oak. He knocked on the wall; the sound was dull and unyielding. ‘It’s just a wall, father,’ he said, with a note of desperate finality.

‘It was not just a wall a few hours ago,’ murmured the Duke, who was on his knees, pressing his palms against the cold surface as if he could feel Virginia’s presence through the stone and plaster.

The night wore on, an eternity of waiting. Every creak of the old house made them start; every sigh of the wind in the chimney sounded like a ghostly whisper. Mrs. Otis had been persuaded to lie down on a sofa, where the old housekeeper, Mrs. Umney, who had reappeared looking more wraith-like than the Ghost himself, attended to her with smelling salts and quiet, pessimistic observations about the doom of the Cantervilles. Mr. Otis paced the length of the great hall, his hands clasped behind his back, his mind a battlefield where logic and terror fought a dreadful, losing war. Washington and the twins sat huddled together on the bottom stair, their usual boisterous energy entirely extinguished, replaced by a grim, fearful watchfulness.

Eleven o’clock passed, and then the great clock in the hall, which had not struck the midnight hour for as long as the oldest servant could recall, began to chime. It was not a loud, strident sound, but a deep, resonant tolling that seemed to vibrate not in the air, but in the very bones of the house. One, two, three... it counted out the twelve strokes of midnight with a solemnity that was profoundly unnerving.

As the last chime faded into a humming silence, a change began to occur. It started not with a sound, but with a light. A faint, silvery luminescence began to gather in the Tapestry Chamber, a light that did not come from the moon or the lanterns, but seemed to emanate from the very wall itself. The family, drawn by an unspoken instinct, gathered at the doorway, hardly daring to breathe.

The little Duke was the first to move. He walked slowly into the room, his eyes fixed on the section of wall beside the chimney-piece. ‘It is happening,’ he whispered. ‘She is coming back.’

The solid oak of the wainscoting seemed to lose its substance, turning soft and grey like a morning mist. The wall above it grew translucent, and for a terrifying moment they thought they could see shapes moving within it, vast and fearful forms swirling in a black, windy abyss. Mrs. Otis gave a little scream and hid her face. Even Mr. Otis felt a cold dread creep up his spine, a feeling utterly foreign to his nature.

Then, as suddenly as it had begun, the vision of darkness vanished. The wall shimmered like a heat haze, and then, with no sound at all, a section of it swung inwards, revealing not a dark cavern, but Virginia, standing on the threshold.

She was very pale, and there was a strange light in her eyes, as if she had looked upon things not meant for mortal sight, but she was unharmed. In her hands, she carried a small, ornate casket of dark wood, inlaid with silver.

For a moment, everyone was frozen, unable to believe what they were seeing. Then Mrs. Otis gave a great cry of ‘Virginia!’ and rushed forward, enfolding her daughter in her arms. Mr. Otis followed, his face working with emotions he could not name, and Washington and the twins crowded around, touching her arm, her dress, as if to assure themselves that she was real. The little Duke simply stood where he was, his heart beating so fast he thought it might burst, tears of relief streaming unashamedly down his face.

‘Oh, my darling, my child, where have you been?’ sobbed Mrs. Otis. ‘We thought we had lost you forever!’

‘I am all right, Mother,’ Virginia said, her voice quiet but steady. She gently disengaged herself from her mother’s embrace and looked around at her anxious family. ‘I am perfectly all right.’

‘Where did you go?’ demanded Mr. Otis, finding his voice at last. ‘What happened? What is that box?’

‘I went with Sir Simon,’ she said simply.

‘The Ghost?’ gasped Washington.

Virginia nodded. ‘He asked me to help him. The prophecy… it was all true. I had to weep for his sins, and pray for his soul. I had to lead him to the Garden of Death.’

A collective shudder ran through the assembled group. ‘You went to the Garden of Death?’ whispered the Duke, his voice full of awe.

‘Yes,’ said Virginia. ‘The Angel was waiting. Sir Simon was so afraid, but I held his hand. I told the Angel how wicked he had been, but also how much he had suffered. And because a little child was there to plead for him, the Angel smiled, and led him away into a beautiful garden. The almond-tree blossomed, and the house was filled with a wonderful music. He is at peace now. He will never trouble any one again.’

Her story was so strange, so impossible, and yet she told it with such simple, earnest conviction that no one could bring themselves to doubt her. They could only stare, bewildered, from her serene face to the solid wall that had, moments before, been a portal to another world.

Mr. Otis, ever the pragmatist, pointed a trembling finger at the casket in her hands. ‘And that? What is in that box, Virginia? Did the Ghost give it to you?’

‘Yes,’ she said. ‘It is a gift. He told me to keep it, in memory of him.’

She placed the casket on a small table and, with a little silver key that was tied to it with a faded ribbon, she unlocked it. As she raised the lid, a gasp of astonishment went through the room. Lying on a bed of faded purple velvet was a magnificent rope of jewels. There were rubies as red as blood, emeralds the colour of a deep sea, and sapphires like the night sky, all interspersed with pearls of extraordinary size and lustre. They blazed and glittered in the lantern-light, throwing out sparks of coloured fire, a treasure worthy of a king, or a pirate.

The twins let out a low whistle of appreciation. Washington’s eyes widened, calculating the immense value of what he was seeing. Mrs. Otis merely stared, overwhelmed. Mr. Otis reached out a hand and picked up a large, pigeon’s-blood ruby, holding it up to the light.

‘Good heavens,’ he muttered, his business instincts momentarily overriding his paternal relief. ‘This is a fortune. This is a considerable fortune.’

Virginia smiled, a gentle, distant smile. ‘He said they were all he had left to give. He said I had saved his soul, and that love was the only thing that mattered.’ She looked at the little Duke, who was still gazing at her with an expression of pure, unadulterated worship. ‘He was right, you know,’ she said softly.

And as the Otis family stood there in the ancient Tapestry Chamber, surrounded by the evidence of a miracle they could not comprehend, they all felt that the strange and terrible haunting of Canterville Chase had finally come to an end. A peace had descended upon the house, a stillness that was not empty, but full; a silence not of absence, but of resolution. Virginia was safe, the Ghost was gone, and in the heart of the practical American family, a little bit of old-world magic had taken root, and was about to blossom in the most unexpected of ways.

The great black cavern had vanished as if it had never been; the wall was solid, the wainscoting firm, and the little huntsmen on the tapestry seemed frozen in their attitudes of warning, their tasselled horns forever silent. The only proof that the night’s strange events had not been a collective hallucination was the small, ornate casket that still rested upon the table, and the serene, otherworldly calm upon Virginia’s lovely face.

When they had somewhat recovered from the initial shock of the evening’s events, a debate, as courteous as it was firm, arose concerning the fate of the Canterville jewels. Mr. Otis, having regained his composure, viewed the matter with the clear, unsentimental eye of a modern man of affairs.

‘My dear,’ he said, turning to his wife, but directing his words more pointedly at Virginia, ‘these are items of prodigious historical and, I must admit, monetary value. It would be irresponsible not to have them professionally appraised. I know a most reliable fellow at Messrs. Streeter & Co. on Bond Street. We should have them catalogued and secured in a bank vault forthwith.’

‘Oh, Hiram, I’m not sure,’ began Mrs. Otis, fanning herself weakly. ‘There is something so very… personal about them. To put them in a bank! It seems so cold. But then, to have such a fortune in the house, it is a great responsibility. What would the New York papers say if they found out? Or worse, the society pages!’

Washington, who had been examining a particularly fine emerald through his spectacles, offered his own practical opinion. ‘Father is right,’ he declared. ‘Think of the interest alone. Properly invested, the principal could found a hospital or, at the very least, a public library. To leave it lying in a box is a flagrant disregard for the principles of sound economic management.’

Only the twins were in favour of keeping them, for reasons that were entirely their own. ‘We could use the rubies for eyes in our catapult targets!’ suggested one. ‘And the pearls would make excellent marbles!’ added the other, before a stern look from their mother silenced them.

Through all this, Virginia remained quiet, her hand resting protectively on the lid of the casket. Finally, she looked at her father, her grey eyes serious and full of a light he had never seen there before. ‘They are not for selling,’ she said, her voice soft but unshakeable. ‘They are not for a bank, or for a hospital. They were a gift. They were given for a soul, and they are a memory.’

‘A memory, my dear child?’ Mr. Otis remonstrated gently. ‘A memory does not need to be kept under lock and key. The value of these…’

‘Their value is that he had nothing else to give,’ Virginia interrupted, a faint blush rising on her cheeks. ‘He gave them to me because I was not afraid, and because I wept for him. You cannot put a price on that, Father. It would be a betrayal.’

The little Duke of Cheshire, who had been listening with rapt attention, sprang to his feet. ‘Virginia is right!’ he cried, his handsome face flushed with emotion. ‘Absolutely right! It would be a beastly, shabby thing to do. Those jewels are not a commodity; they are a badge of honour! They are proof that chivalry is not dead, and that an English ghost has more decency than some living men. To sell them would be sacrilege!’

Mr. Otis looked from his daughter’s resolute face to the young Duke’s impassioned one and sighed. He was outnumbered by forces he did not entirely comprehend: the steadfast loyalty of a young girl and the romantic idealism of the British aristocracy. ‘Very well, Virginia,’ he said, surrendering. ‘We shall not speak of it again for now. The jewels are yours, to do with as you see fit.’

Virginia gave him a grateful smile and then, taking up the casket, she carried it from the room, the little Duke following her like a faithful page.

The days that followed were marked by a profound and almost unnerving tranquillity. The famous Canterville bloodstain, which had so stubbornly resisted Mrs. Otis’s Paragon Detergent, had vanished overnight and did not return. The spectral clanking of chains was heard no more in the corridors, and the twins, much to their disgust, found that their carefully laid booby-traps remained disappointingly unsprung. The servants, who had been in a constant state of nervous agitation, became placid and cheerful, and the old housekeeper declared that the house had not felt so peaceful in the fifty years she had known it.

Mr. Otis wrote a long and detailed letter to Lord Canterville, informing him that the ancestral ghost had, it appeared, permanently retired from his duties. He received a polite, if somewhat incredulous, reply, in which the noble lord expressed his thanks and his profound relief that his children would now be able to visit the Chase without fear of being turned into black cats or being made to stand in the corner of the great hall.

Amid this newfound peace, the romance between Virginia and the young Duke blossomed like the miraculously flowering almond-tree in the garden. They spent long hours together, walking in the park or sitting in the library, where Virginia would read to him from the very poets the Ghost had once admired. Cecil, for his part, was utterly captivated. Virginia was no longer simply the pretty American girl he had taken a fancy to; she was a heroine, a saint, a figure from legend who had walked into the Garden of Death and returned unharmed. His devotion was absolute and, one sunny afternoon, as they stood by the carp-pond, he formally asked for her hand in marriage.

Virginia, who had grown very fond of the handsome, earnest young man, did not refuse him, but she did not immediately accept either. ‘I must think, Cecil,’ she said, looking not at him, but at her own reflection in the still water. ‘Everything is so different now. I am different.’

It was this very difference that soon brought a new and formidable presence to Canterville Chase. News of the Duke of Cheshire’s extended stay and his increasingly obvious intentions towards the American Minister’s daughter had reached the ears of his guardian, the formidable Dowager Duchess of Bilton. The Duchess was a lady of the old school, a woman whose blood was as blue as the sapphires in Virginia’s casket, and whose opinions were as unbending as granite. She regarded Americans in general as a loud, common, and regrettably wealthy race, and the idea of her grandson, the premier Duke in the peerage, marrying one of them was, to her mind, a catastrophe of the highest order. When wild rumours of ghosts, disappearances, and a mysterious treasure began to circulate, she decided that direct intervention was required.

She arrived one afternoon in a magnificent carriage drawn by four perfectly matched bays, a vision of black silk, jet beads, and stern disapproval. She descended upon the Otis family, who had assembled in the hall to greet her, like a bird of prey upon a flock of unsuspecting doves.

‘Mr. Otis, I presume,’ she said, extending two fingers as cold and stiff as icicles. Her gaze swept over Mrs. Otis’s fashionable Parisian gown, Washington’s democratic necktie, and the twins’ unruly hair with an expression of deep misgiving. ‘I am the Duchess of Bilton. I have come to retrieve my grandson.’

Mr. Otis, ever the gracious host, bowed. ‘Your Grace is most welcome at Canterville Chase. We were not expecting the pleasure. Cecil is in the garden with our daughter, Virginia.’

‘So I have been given to understand,’ the Duchess remarked coolly. ‘It is this very matter I wish to discuss. I shall take a cup of tea in the library. Alone, with you and your wife.’

The interview that followed was a trial for all concerned. The Duchess sat bolt upright in a high-backed chair, her lorgnette held at the ready, and subjected Mr. and Mrs. Otis to a cross-examination that would have done credit to a seasoned barrister. She enquired into their ancestry, which was regrettably short; their politics, which were regrettably Republican; and their fortune, which was regrettably robust and derived from trade.

‘And now,’ she said, having exhausted the topic of their unsuitability, ‘we come to this… fantastical tale. This ghost. This disappearance. And these so-called jewels. My grandson, who is young and impressionable, writes to me of things that belong in a penny dreadful, not in the correspondence of a Duke. I must insist you tell me the truth of the matter. I suspect a hoax, Mr. Otis, a rather elaborate and vulgar theatrical.’

Mr. Otis’s face hardened. ‘Your Grace,’ he said, his voice dangerously calm, ‘I am the United States Minister to the Court of St. James’s. I do not engage in vulgar theatricals. What my daughter experienced was, I assure you, quite real.’

‘Real?’ the Duchess sniffed. ‘A girl vanishes into a wall and reappears with a pirate’s treasure, and you call that real? It is a story for children, or for the feeble-minded.’

At that moment, the library door opened and Virginia entered, followed by Cecil, who looked pale and anxious. Virginia, however, was the picture of composure. She had heard the raised voices and had guessed the nature of the visit.

‘Forgive me for interrupting,’ she said, her voice clear and steady. She curtsied gracefully to the Duchess. ‘You must be the Duchess of Bilton. I am Virginia Otis.’

The Duchess fixed her sharp, bird-like eyes upon the young girl. She had been expecting a hoyden, a brash and forward young woman. She saw instead a girl of singular beauty and grace, with an expression of such sweet solemnity that she was momentarily taken aback.

‘So you are the girl who has filled my grandson’s head with nonsense,’ she said, recovering herself.

‘I have told him only what is true,’ Virginia replied simply.

‘Truth?’ scoffed the Duchess. ‘And what is this truth? That you converse with spirits and accept priceless gems from them? Let me see these famous jewels. I have some small knowledge of such things. I daresay they are nothing more than paste, part of the stage-dressing for this absurd play.’

Cecil made to speak, to leap to Virginia’s defence, but Virginia laid a gentle hand on his arm. ‘Of course, Your Grace,’ she said. ‘I will fetch them for you.’

She left the room and returned a few moments later carrying the casket. She placed it on the table before the Duchess and, using the small silver key, unlocked it. She lifted the lid.

Even in the subdued afternoon light of the library, the effect was breathtaking. The gems blazed with an inner fire, a riot of crimson, green, and midnight blue, nestled amongst the milky perfection of the pearls. An audible gasp came from Mrs. Otis. Mr. Otis looked on with renewed respect for the Ghost’s financial standing. The Duchess, however, did not move. She stared into the box, her face rigid, her lorgnette forgotten in her lap.

Slowly, as if in a trance, she reached out a gloved hand and lifted a heavy necklace of rubies and diamonds from its velvet bed. She held it up, her expression unreadable.

‘These,’ she whispered, her voice a dry rasp, ‘are the Canterville rubies. They were a gift to Eleanor de Canterville from the Black Prince himself. They were lost, utterly lost, after the… unfortunate incident with Sir Simon.’

Her gaze fell upon a great sapphire, cut in a curious, antique fashion, that hung from a chain of Venetian gold. ‘And this… this is the Star of Padua. It was said to have been taken from the body of a Medici cardinal. My own great-grandmother spoke of it. It has been a legend in the county for three hundred years.’

She looked from the jewels to Virginia, and in her eyes a battle was being fought between a lifetime of cynical disbelief and the undeniable evidence before her. The jewels were not merely real; they were historical artifacts of immense significance, pieces she knew from portraits and from family lore. No American parvenu could have fabricated such a collection.

‘You say the Ghost… Sir Simon… he gave them to you?’ the Duchess asked, her tone stripped of its earlier arrogance.

‘Yes, Your Grace,’ Virginia said. ‘He said I had saved his soul. He asked me to pray for him, because he had no faith, and to weep for him, because he had no tears.’

The Duchess stared at Virginia, at her clear, innocent eyes, at the simple sincerity of her face. She saw not a schemer, but a child who had performed an act of immense and mysterious courage. The old woman, who had not wept since her husband’s death thirty years before, felt a strange and unwelcome prickling behind her eyelids. She saw the union of her ancient, titled house with this strange, vital new world in a different light. It was not a diminishment, but a renewal. This girl, who could command the pity of the damned and receive the treasures of kings, was no commoner. She was something more.

She carefully placed the ruby necklace back in its box and closed the lid. She then rose to her feet and, to the astonishment of every one present, she took Virginia’s hand.

‘My dear child,’ she said, and her voice had a new and tremulous quality. ‘You are a very remarkable young woman. Any family in England, no, in all of Europe, would be honoured to welcome you. You have done what no one else could. You have brought peace to this house, and to a very wretched soul. My grandson,’ she added, turning to the beaming Duke, ‘has shown more wisdom than I gave him credit for. I give you both my blessing.’

And with that, the Dowager Duchess of Bilton, the last bastion of aristocratic skepticism, bent down and kissed Virginia on the forehead. It was a seal of approval, a capitulation of the old world to the new, not in defeat, but in a strange and wonderful alliance. The hauntings of Canterville Chase were over, but its capacity for producing miracles, it seemed, was far from exhausted. A wedding was announced, a wedding that would unite not just a boy and a girl, but two continents and two ages, with a dowry provided by a repentant ghost.
