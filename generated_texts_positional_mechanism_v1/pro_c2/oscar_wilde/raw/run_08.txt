She walked to the wall by the fireplace, the one covered in the faded green tapestry of the little huntsmen.

‘It was here,’ she said, and placed her hand flat against the broidered scene. For a moment, nothing happened. Washington looked at his father with an expression that said, ‘I told you so.’ But then, with the same low, grinding sound they had heard earlier, a section of the wainscoting receded, and the tapestry swung inwards, revealing a black and narrow opening.

A chill, damp air flowed out, carrying with it the smell of dust and ancient decay. Mr. Otis took the candelabrum from Virginia and held it high, peering into the darkness. The rest of the family crowded behind him. The passage was barely wide enough for one person, and it sloped steeply downwards. After a moment’s hesitation, with Virginia leading the way, they entered.

They found themselves in a small, low-vaulted cell, with a tiny, barred window high up in the wall. Chained to a great iron ring in this wall was a gaunt and terrible skeleton, which stretched out its bony fingers as if to grasp a small, earthenware water-jug and a platter that lay just beyond its reach. It was clear that the prisoner had been deliberately starved to death. On the floor beside the skeleton lay a small, open prayer-book and a few withered blossoms, still faintly fragrant. Virginia went and knelt beside the skeleton, and the others watched in silent, horrified reverence.

‘This is Sir Simon,’ she said quietly. ‘His wife’s brothers starved him to death here, in revenge for what he had done. He could not rest while his bones lay unburied in an unhallowed place.’

She bowed her head, and for a few moments there was a profound quiet in the little cell, broken only by a soft sob from Mrs. Otis. Mr. Otis stared at the tragic scene, and all his American skepticism melted away in the face of this stark, three-hundred-year-old sorrow. He understood at last.

‘The prophecy,’ he murmured, looking at his daughter’s bowed golden head. ‘When a golden girl can win Prayer from out the lips of sin... And a little child gives away its tears...’ He looked at the withered blossoms, which must have come from the almond-tree, and at the proof of his daughter’s prayer. ‘Then shall all the house be still, and peace come to Canterville.’

He placed a hand on Virginia’s shoulder. ‘You have done a very brave and a very good thing, my dear,’ he said. ‘You have taught us all a great lesson.’

A few days later, a simple funeral was held. The Otis family, Lord Canterville, who had come down from Wales at once on hearing the news, and the little Duke of Cheshire followed the small leaden coffin to a quiet corner of the churchyard. The coffin was laid in a deep grave that had been dug under a large yew-tree, and Virginia herself placed a large cross made of white and pink almond-blossoms upon it. As she did so, the moon came out from behind a cloud and flooded the little churchyard with silent silver, and from a distant copse a nightingale began to sing. Virginia thought of the Ghost’s description of the Garden of Death, and she knew that he was at peace at last.

The following spring, she was married to the little Duke of Cheshire, who had by then come into his title. It was a splendid wedding, and Mr. Otis gave the bride away. Afterwards, her husband insisted that she must wear the Canterville jewels, but Virginia would only ever wear the ruby necklace and the simple diamond rings. The other pieces she kept locked away in the beautiful casket, and when she grew old, she would tell her children the strange, romantic story of her adventure with the Ghost.

She and her husband were wonderfully happy, and his devotion to her was a constant source of delight to all who knew them. And though they often visited Canterville Chase, which was now blessedly and permanently quiet, Sir Simon de Canterville was never seen again. The bloodstain on the library floor faded away completely, and the house, for the first time in its long and troubled history, was finally at peace, its redemption having been purchased by the simple purity of a little child.

When they had done with supper, and a kind of melancholy order had been restored to the household, the little Duke, who had refused to touch a morsel of food, took Virginia’s hand and held it as though he would never let it go. ‘You must tell me everything, Virginia,’ he whispered, when the others had dispersed to their rooms, exhausted by the night’s alarms. ‘You must tell me where you went, and what you saw.’ Virginia looked at the handsome boy, whose face was still pale with the anxieties of the evening, and a faint blush touched her cheeks. ‘I cannot, Cecil,’ she said softly. ‘It is a secret. Sir Simon’s secret. He has suffered so much, and now he is at peace. We must let him be.’ The Duke looked disappointed for a moment, but then his eyes, which were a very beautiful shade of hazel, filled with a deep and earnest admiration. ‘You are an angel, Virginia,’ he declared, pressing her hand to his lips. ‘You have always been an angel, and that is why I have always loved you.’ Virginia did not chide him for his forwardness; she felt that after the strange and terrible events of the day, a little simple human affection was a very welcome thing indeed.

The burial of Sir Simon de Canterville’s remains, though a simple affair, was conducted with all the gravity befitting the occasion. Lord Canterville, a grave, elderly gentleman with a distinct resemblance to the ghost’s portrait in the long gallery, expressed his profound gratitude to the Otis family, and to Virginia in particular. ‘My family owes you a debt that can never be repaid, Miss Otis,’ he said, his voice thick with emotion. ‘You have lifted a three-hundred-year-old curse from my house and from my name. Canterville Chase is yours, of course, for as long as you wish it, but more than that, you have the unending thanks of a very old and weary family.’ Mr. Otis, who was never one to be outdone in matters of formal exchange, replied with a grandiloquent speech about the bonds of friendship between the old world and the new, and insisted that the pleasure was all theirs, an assertion which caused Mrs. Otis to have a fresh attack of her nerves and require the immediate application of her smelling salts.

The affair, however, could not be kept as quiet as the family might have wished. The initial telegrams sent by Mr. Otis concerning a kidnapped child had set the entire constabulary of the county into a frenzy, and the subsequent explanation, involving a repentant spectre and a hidden skeleton, was met with a degree of official skepticism that even Mr. Otis found irritating. The story, in a garbled and highly sensational form, was leaked to the London papers. ‘AMERICAN HEIRESS IN GHOSTLY ABDUCTION DRAMA,’ screamed one headline; ‘SPIRIT OF CANTERVILLE FINDS PEACE THROUGH PURITAN MAID,’ announced another, with a sublime disregard for Virginia’s Episcopalian upbringing. For a fortnight, Canterville Chase was besieged by journalists, psychic researchers, and enterprising tourists, all hoping to catch a glimpse of the ‘Golden Girl’ or, failing that, a newly pacified phantom. Mr. Otis dealt with them with brusque American efficiency, having the drive barricaded and threatening the more persistent intruders with prosecution for trespass, while Washington and the twins found a great deal of amusement in frightening them away with white sheets and hollowed-out turnips, a stratagem which proved remarkably effective.

It was during this period of unwelcome fame that the little Duke of Cheshire, whose devotion had only intensified, renewed his proposal of marriage. He did so not with the frantic, boyish pleas of the previous year, but with a quiet, solemn earnestness that touched Virginia’s heart. ‘I know I am not worthy of you, Virginia,’ he said, as they stood by the carp-pond one afternoon, watching the great, lazy fish drift through the water-lilies. ‘After what you have done, you seem to belong to a higher world, a world of saints and angels. But I love you more than life, and I would spend all my days trying to make you happy, and protecting you from… well, from all this ugliness.’ He gestured vaguely towards the gate, where a man with a large camera was being politely but firmly escorted away by one of the footmen. Virginia, who had found the public attention a far more trying ordeal than anything the Ghost had subjected her to, looked at his sincere, handsome face and knew that her answer was clear. She gave him her hand, and Mr. Otis, when informed of the engagement, gave his blessing with a satisfaction that was only slightly diminished by the knowledge that he would be losing his only daughter to the British aristocracy, an institution for which he still held a certain democratic contempt.

The months leading up to the wedding were a whirlwind of activity, and it was a great relief to all when the family removed to London for the season, leaving the Chase to its newfound and profound silence. The bloodstain, which had reappeared with tiresome regularity for three centuries, was now completely and utterly gone. The armour in the hall no longer crashed to the floor at inconvenient moments, and the chains that had once echoed in the corridors were heard no more. The house slept, a deep and dreamless sleep, and the servants, for the first time, went about their duties without casting fearful glances over their shoulders.

Virginia, now the young Duchess of Cheshire, found London society to be a far more complex and treacherous realm than the haunted halls of Canterville. Her story had preceded her, and she was regarded as a creature of immense curiosity. At soirées and balls, she would be cornered by stout dowagers and inquisitive young fops, all demanding to know the details of her spiritual adventure. ‘Is it quite true, Duchess,’ a particularly formidable society hostess, Lady Agatha Carlisle, inquired of her one evening, tapping her fan against Virginia’s arm, ‘that you spent an entire night in the company of a ghost? How perfectly thrilling, and how very American! You colonists do have the most peculiar experiences.’

‘I merely helped a soul in torment find its rest, Lady Agatha,’ Virginia replied quietly, her dislike of the woman’s condescending tone making her feel rather cold.

‘A soul in torment! How wonderfully dramatic,’ Lady Agatha trilled. ‘And did it rattle its chains? I am told all the best-bred ghosts rattle their chains. And what of the famous bloodstain? My nephew informs me it has vanished. I suppose your father had it chemically treated by some clever American process?’

‘My father’s stain remover was not successful,’ said the Duke, appearing at Virginia’s side and fixing Lady Agatha with a cool stare. ‘The stain was removed by an act of forgiveness, a concept with which you may not be entirely familiar. Now, if you will excuse us, my wife has promised me this waltz.’ He swept Virginia away onto the dance floor, leaving Lady Agatha looking rather as if she had swallowed a wasp.

‘I cannot bear them,’ Cecil muttered, as they glided across the polished floor. ‘They speak of Sir Simon’s suffering as if it were a diverting piece of theatre, and they look at you as if you were some sort of travelling show.’

‘They do not understand,’ Virginia said, resting her head for a moment on his shoulder. ‘And perhaps it is better that they do not. It was not for them.’

The greatest trial, however, was yet to come. A certain Professor Alistair Finch, a man of considerable academic standing and a leading member of a society dedicated to the rational explanation of psychical phenomena, had taken a particular interest in the Canterville case. He had written several long and rather condescending articles in scholarly journals, in which he proposed that the entire affair was a case of ‘collective hysteria, precipitated by a suggestible young American lady and amplified by a pre-existing local superstition.’ He dismissed the discovery of the skeleton as a coincidence, and the vanishing of the bloodstain as a chemical anomaly yet to be understood. He concluded by publicly challenging the Duke of Cheshire to allow him to conduct a thorough scientific investigation of Canterville Chase, to once and for all ‘put to rest these fanciful and retrograde notions.’

The letter caused a considerable stir within the family. Mr. Otis, whose own skepticism had been so thoroughly routed, was indignant. ‘The man is a charlatan!’ he declared, pacing the length of the morning-room in their London house. ‘A pompous academic fraud! To suggest that Virginia is hysterical! I have a good mind to sue him for libel. We should not give him the time of day.’

Washington, who was now a rising star in the diplomatic service, was more measured. ‘To refuse him, Father, might be seen as an admission that we have something to hide. It would only fuel the speculation. Perhaps we should allow him to come. Let him bring his instruments and his theories. The house is quiet. He will find nothing, and his failure will be the best possible proof.’

Mrs. Otis shuddered. ‘Oh, I could not bear it! To have strange men poking about the house, searching for… for vibrations! It seems a desecration, after that poor ghost has finally found his peace.’

All eyes turned to Virginia. She had been silent throughout the discussion, twisting the simple ruby necklace at her throat, the very one Sir Simon had given her. ‘I do not want it,’ she said at last, her voice low but firm. ‘Sir Simon’s rest is sacred. It was a compact between him and me and God. It is not something to be proven or disproven with barometers and thermometers. His peace should not be disturbed.’

The Duke, who had been watching her with an expression of fierce loyalty, stood up. ‘That settles it, then,’ he announced. ‘I shall write to this Professor Finch and tell him that Canterville Chase is a private residence, not a public laboratory, and that his request is denied.’

But the press, having caught wind of the Professor’s challenge, would not let the matter rest. They painted the Duke’s refusal as an act of aristocratic obscurantism, a fear of modern science. The narrative they spun was a compelling one: the plucky, rational man of science being blocked by the superstitious, secretive nobility. The pressure mounted, until one morning Virginia, having read a particularly unpleasant editorial that questioned not only her sanity but her husband’s courage, turned to him with weary eyes. ‘Perhaps Washington is right, Cecil,’ she said. ‘Perhaps we must let him come. Let him see the silence for himself. It is the only way this will ever end.’

And so it was arranged. Professor Finch, accompanied by two junior assistants and a great many polished brass and mahogany cases, arrived at Canterville Chase on a grey afternoon in October. He was a tall, thin man with a reedy voice and spectacles that seemed to magnify his air of intellectual superiority. He greeted the assembled family, which included the Otises, who had come down from London, with a thin, bloodless smile.

‘Your Grace, Duchess,’ he began, without any preamble, ‘I am grateful for this opportunity to apply the clear light of reason to this… fascinating local mythos. I assure you we shall be as unobtrusive as possible in our investigations.’

‘You will find nothing to investigate, Professor,’ said the Duke coldly. ‘The house is at peace.’

‘Peace, Your Grace, is merely the absence of measurable disturbance,’ the Professor retorted smoothly. ‘It is that absence we are here to quantify.’

For two days, the Professor and his team turned the historic house upside down. They set up thermometers in the corridors to detect anomalous cold spots. They placed sensitive microphones in the library to listen for subsonic vibrations. They took scrapings from the floor where the bloodstain had been, and samples of the air in the secret chamber where the skeleton was found. They tapped the walls, measured magnetic fields, and photographed every room with plates sensitive to ultraviolet light. The twins, who had at first viewed the proceedings as a grand new game, soon grew bored with the Professor’s humourless efficiency and went off to terrorise the stable-lads instead. Mr. Otis followed the Professor about, cross-examining him on his methods with a lawyerly precision that clearly irritated the man of science, while Mrs. Otis remained in her room with a sick headache.

Virginia and her husband watched the whole affair with a feeling of deep sadness. It felt like a violation. They saw the Professor in the Tapestry Chamber, the very room where Virginia had made her fateful choice, and watched him pointing a strange, metallic device at the wall through which she and the Ghost had passed. ‘The wainscoting shows signs of a well-oiled counterweight mechanism, almost certainly of sixteenth-century German design,’ he announced to his assistant, who dutifully scribbled in a notebook. ‘The entire “disappearing wall” is a clever, if primitive, piece of stagecraft.’

Virginia felt a lump rise in her throat. To hear that moment of terror and pity and resolve reduced to a discussion of counterweights was almost more than she could bear. Cecil put his arm around her. ‘It will be over soon, my love,’ he murmured.

On the third morning, Professor Finch requested a final audience with the family in the library. He stood before the great fireplace, a sheaf of notes in his hand, a look of triumphant vindication on his face.

‘As I predicted,’ he began, ‘our investigation has yielded entirely rational and, I must say, rather mundane results. We have found no evidence whatsoever of any supernatural phenomena. The cold spots are attributable to simple drafts from ill-fitting window frames. The so-called “spectral groans” recorded by my equipment are nothing more than the house’s old timbers settling, combined with the acoustic resonance of the pipes in your new American plumbing system, Mr. Otis. The famous bloodstain, or rather its absence, is most likely due to a slow-acting chemical reaction with the particular type of oak used in the flooring, perhaps accelerated by a change in humidity.’

He paused, peering at them over his spectacles. ‘As for the skeleton in the priest’s hole, it is indeed a tragic curiosity, but its presence proves nothing. Such grim discoveries are not uncommon in houses of this antiquity. The entire legend, I am afraid, is a classic example of confirmation bias, a tapestry woven from suggestion, coincidence, and a lack of scientific rigour.’ He gave a small, dry cough. ‘The house is not haunted, Duchess. It is merely old.’

A heavy silence fell upon the room. Mr. Otis looked as if he were about to explode. The Duke’s hands were clenched into fists. But it was Virginia who spoke.

‘And the almond-tree, Professor?’ she asked, her voice clear and calm. ‘The one outside the library window. The barren one.’

The Professor looked momentarily confused. ‘The tree? Prunus dulcis. Yes, we took a sample. It is quite old, and as you say, appears to be sterile. A common enough condition in aged flora.’

‘It bloomed, Professor,’ said Virginia. ‘On the night Sir Simon found his peace. It was covered in blossoms. That is where I got the flowers for his coffin. It bloomed in the dead of winter, out of season, after three hundred years of being barren. Can your science explain that?’

Professor Finch opened his mouth, then closed it again. He shuffled his notes. ‘An unseasonal warming spell… a final, anomalous burst of life from a dying organism… there are several possible botanical explanations…’ he stammered, but his usual confidence had deserted him. He could quantify the silence of the house, but he could not quantify the miracle of the blossoms. The inexplicable flowering of a dead tree was a piece of poetry that broke his prose.

‘I think your work here is done, Professor,’ said the Duke, his voice like ice.

The Professor, for the first time, seemed to know that he was beaten. He gathered his notes and his dignity, gave a stiff, formal bow, and departed with his assistants and his polished brass cases, leaving behind him a silence that was now not just peaceful, but triumphant.

That evening, as twilight fell, Virginia went into the library alone. She stood before the great window and looked out at the old almond-tree. Its branches were bare and twisted against the purple sky, just as they had always been. But in her memory, and in her heart, it was forever covered in a cloud of pink and white blossom. She thought of Sir Simon, of his desperate sorrow and his beautiful description of the Garden of Death. Her act had not been for science, or for history, or for the gossips in London. It had been an act of love, for a lost and lonely soul.

She went to a small writing-desk and unlocked the beautiful casket that held the Canterville jewels. She looked at the ropes of pearls, the glittering tiaras, the pendants that flashed like fire. They were a king’s ransom. Then she picked up the simple ruby necklace and closed her hand around it. She knew what she had to do. The world did not need more proof of ghosts; it needed more of the compassion that had set one free.

A few months later, the society pages were filled with news of the new charitable foundation established by the Duchess of Cheshire, funded by the sale of a portion of her remarkable jewels. It was dedicated to the relief of the poor and suffering, to those who, in their own way, were as chained and tormented as Sir Simon had been. Lady Agatha Carlisle declared it a most eccentric and sentimental gesture, but for once, nobody listened to her. Virginia’s quiet act of charity captured the public imagination far more than her ghostly adventure ever had. She never spoke of the Ghost again to anyone but her husband, and in time, her own children, but the lesson he had taught her she shared with the world. The true legacy of the Canterville Ghost was not the silence he left behind in the old house, but the kindness he had awakened in the heart of a young girl, a kindness that, like the impossible blossoms on the almond-tree, bloomed brightly in a cold and cynical world. And in this, the redemption of Sir Simon de Canterville was made truly and eternally complete.

‘There is no use in our simply sitting here,’ he said, his voice lacking its customary boom. ‘We must organise a second, more thorough search of the house. There may be a loose panel, a forgotten priest’s hole… this is an old and illogical building.’

‘I searched every room myself, father,’ said Washington, his face grim. ‘I even tapped on all the walls in the Long Gallery.’

‘And we looked up all the chimneys!’ added one of the twins, with a flicker of his old mischief, which was immediately extinguished by a despairing sob from his mother.

‘Oh, Hiram, my child, my poor little Virginia!’ she wailed, burying her face in a lace handkerchief. ‘Those dreadful gypsies have taken her, I know it! They will sell her to a circus, or make her walk on a tightrope! She has no head for heights!’

‘Now, Lucretia, be calm,’ said Mr. Otis, though his own hand trembled as he poured a glass of water. ‘The gypsies have been cleared. Inspector Rance of Scotland Yard is on his way down by special train. We must place our faith in modern police methods.’

The little Duke, who had refused all food and had been staring blankly into the fire, suddenly sprang to his feet. ‘It was not the gypsies,’ he declared, his young voice ringing with a strange conviction. ‘It was the Ghost.’

Mr. Otis stared at him. ‘Cecil, my boy, this is hardly the time for… for ghost stories. We are dealing with a real and terrible crisis.’

‘But it is real!’ the Duke insisted, his eyes flashing. ‘You all saw him. We have all heard him. He spoke to her, in the hall, just before she vanished. I heard their voices. He was speaking of Death, and she… she was not afraid.’ His voice broke on the last words, and he turned away to hide the tears that he was too proud to show.

A profound and chilling silence fell upon the room. The practical, republican Americans, who had treated the Phantom of Canterville Chase as a mild domestic nuisance, were for the first time confronted with the terrible possibility that the supernatural was not merely a matter of clanking chains and inconvenient bloodstains, but a power that could steal a child from their midst. The ticking of the great clock in the hall, which had for weeks been a source of domestic irritation to Mrs. Otis on account of its ponderous and rather mournful chime, now seemed to be ticking with a funereal solemnity, each second falling into the vast, anxious silence like a clod of earth upon a coffin.

It was just as the clock began to strike the hour of midnight that a faint sound was heard from the far end of the Tapestry Chamber, which adjoined the library. It was not a creak, nor a groan, nor any of the usual noises of the house. It was a soft, grinding noise, like stone moving upon stone.

Every head turned. Mrs. Otis gave a little scream and clutched her husband’s arm. The twins, who had been drooping with exhaustion, were suddenly wide-eyed and alert. Mr. Otis picked up a heavy iron poker from the fireplace, his jaw set.

‘Stay here,’ he commanded, and strode towards the door of the chamber, followed closely by Washington and the little Duke.

They pushed open the door and peered into the gloom. The room was just as they had left it hours before, dusty and still, the moonlight silvering the faded figures on the walls. The little huntsmen were frozen in their eternal chase, their tasselled horns silent. The grotesque monsters on the chimney-piece stared out with their unblinking goggle eyes. For a moment, nothing happened. Then, before their very eyes, the section of the wall beside the great window seemed to grow thin, to shimmer and dissolve like a heat haze.

From out of the solid oak panelling, Virginia stepped forth into the room.

She was very pale, but her eyes shone with an extraordinary light, and in her hands she carried a small, ornate casket, intricately carved and inlaid with silver. She did not seem startled or afraid, but possessed of a strange and solemn calm. She looked at her father, at her brother, and at the Duke, and a faint, sad smile touched her lips.

‘Virginia!’ cried the Duke, rushing forward, but he stopped short a few feet from her, as if an invisible barrier stood between them.

‘My child! You are safe!’ Mr. Otis gasped, lowering the poker. He hurried to her side, his relief so profound that it left him breathless. ‘Where have you been? We have been out of our minds with worry! Are you hurt? Did someone take you?’

Virginia shook her head gently. ‘I am perfectly well, Papa,’ she said, her voice soft and clear as a little bell. ‘You must not worry.’

‘Not worry?’ exclaimed Mrs. Otis, who had come running from the library, followed by the twins. ‘Not worry? Virginia, I have imagined every possible horror! I had visions of you sold to brigands in Italy! I have aged ten years tonight!’ She enveloped her daughter in a frantic embrace, weeping and laughing at once.

When the first storm of familial relief had subsided, they all drew back and looked at her, and the questions began anew.

‘But where on earth were you, Ginny?’ demanded Washington. ‘We searched this room half a dozen times. The wall was solid.’

‘And what is that box?’ asked one of the twins, pointing a curious finger at the casket. ‘Is it treasure?’

Virginia looked down at the casket in her hands. ‘It is a gift,’ she said simply. ‘From Sir Simon.’

A collective gasp went through the little group.

‘From the Ghost?’ said Mr. Otis, his brow furrowing in utter bewilderment. ‘Virginia, what is the meaning of this? You will tell us everything, at once.’

Virginia looked at the anxious faces surrounding her and her own expression grew infinitely sorrowful. ‘I cannot,’ she said. ‘It is a secret between him and me. But you need not be afraid of him any more. He will never trouble this house again. He is at peace.’ She glanced towards the library window, through which the gnarled branches of the old almond-tree were visible. ‘He is in the Garden of Death.’

No one knew what to say to this. It was a statement so far removed from their ordinary experience that it seemed to defy all rational response. Mrs. Otis began to look faint again and murmured something about smelling-salts. Mr. Otis, however, a practical man even in the face of the impossible, focused on the one tangible object.

‘Let me see that casket, Virginia,’ he said.

She handed it to him. It was surprisingly heavy. He lifted the lid, and even in the dim moonlight of the Tapestry Chamber, the contents blazed with a spectacular fire. Ropes of perfect pearls, rings set with luminous emeralds, a great sunburst of diamonds, and, lying on a bed of faded purple velvet, a magnificent ruby necklace, each stone like a drop of frozen blood.

‘Good heavens,’ whispered Mr. Otis. ‘These are… these are the Canterville jewels.’

He had heard the story from Lord Canterville himself; a famous collection of Renaissance jewellery that had vanished with Lady Eleanor de Canterville in 1584 and had never been seen since. Their value was legendary, almost mythical. And here they were, in his daughter’s hands, a gift from a three-hundred-year-old ghost.

‘We must return them to Lord Canterville at once,’ he declared, snapping the lid shut as if their glitter were somehow dangerous. ‘This is a matter for lawyers. It is most irregular.’

‘No, Papa,’ said Virginia firmly. ‘They are for me. He gave them to me. He said I was to have them because I had wept for him, and prayed for him.’

The Duke of Cheshire, who had been silent all this time, gazing at Virginia with an expression of absolute adoration, now stepped forward. ‘Mr. Otis,’ he said, his voice trembling slightly, ‘I think we must believe her. Virginia has done something wonderful tonight, something none of us could do. She has saved a soul. These jewels are her reward. It is a matter not for lawyers, but for poets.’

Mr. Otis looked from the earnest young Duke to his daughter’s serene face, and for the first time in his life, he felt that the principles of the Republican party and the logic of the modern world were entirely inadequate. He ran a hand through his hair in a gesture of profound confusion. ‘I think,’ he said slowly, ‘that we all need to go to bed. We will speak of this in the morning.’

The next morning, the sun rose on a house transformed. The oppressive anxiety of the previous night had lifted, replaced by a sense of hushed wonder. The family gathered for a late breakfast, but no one had much of an appetite. They spoke in low tones, constantly glancing at Virginia as if she might vanish again.

It was Washington who made the first discovery. After breakfast, he took his bottle of ‘Pinkerton’s Champion Stain Remover and Paragon Detergent’ and went, as was his morning ritual, to make another attempt on the famous Canterville bloodstain by the library fireplace. He knelt down, uncorked his bottle, and then stopped, staring.

He called for the others. They came running and looked down at the spot where the ghastly stain had reappeared each morning for centuries, a source of endless fascination and frustration. The floorboards were clean. There was not a trace, not a shadow, not the faintest discoloration to be seen.

‘It’s gone,’ Washington said, in a tone of complete disbelief. He dabbed at the spot with his cleaning-fluid, but there was nothing to remove. ‘It’s completely gone.’

It was the final, irrefutable proof. The Ghost was at rest. The curse was lifted.

Later that morning, Virginia led them out into the garden. She walked with a quiet purpose, past the manicured lawns and the neat flowerbeds, to a dark corner of the Chase, overshadowed by a great yew-tree. Here, the wall was old and crumbling, and in it was a small, iron-ringed door they had never noticed before. It was rusted and seemed as if it had not been opened for an age.

‘He is in there,’ she said.

It took the combined efforts of Mr. Otis and Washington to pull the door open. It groaned on its hinges, revealing a narrow, stone-flagged passage, dark and cold, and smelling of damp earth. At the end of the passage was a small, windowless cell. And there, chained by the wrist and ankle to a great iron ring set in the wall, was a skeleton. It lay huddled on the floor, its bony fingers stretched out towards a simple wooden trencher and an earthenware ewer, which lay just beyond its reach, showing how the wretched Sir Simon had been starved to death by his wife’s brothers.

Mrs. Otis gave a cry and turned away, but Virginia walked forward and knelt beside the pitiful remains. She bowed her head, and her tears, the tears she had promised to give, fell upon the dusty bones. The little Duke knelt beside her, his own eyes dim, and placed a gentle hand on her shoulder.

That afternoon, a simple funeral was held. At Virginia’s insistence, the skeleton of Sir Simon de Canterville was laid to rest in the corner of the churchyard, just beneath the yew-tree. The Otis family, the Duke of Cheshire, and the old housekeeper, Mrs. Umney, who cried silently into her apron, were the only mourners. As the Reverend Augustus Dampier was reciting the last prayers, a strange and beautiful thing happened. The old, barren almond-tree, which stood just outside the churchyard wall, suddenly burst into a cloud of delicate pink-and-white blossoms.

‘A miracle!’ gasped the Reverend, dropping his prayer-book.

The twins thought it was a splendid conjuring trick, and Mr. Otis began to theorize about a sudden change in meteorological conditions, but Virginia just smiled, for she knew it was the Ghost’s way of saying farewell.

A few days later, Lord Canterville arrived at the Chase, having received a rather bewildered telegram from Mr. Otis. He was a pleasant, elderly gentleman with a weary expression, who was far more interested in the quality of Mr. Otis’s cigars than in the fate of his spectral ancestor.

‘My dear sir,’ he said, when Mr. Otis had finished a heavily edited account of the events, omitting the more fantastical details and simply stating that his daughter had discovered a secret chamber, ‘I am immensely grateful to you, and to your charming daughter. The ghost was, to be frank, a dreadful inconvenience. He frightened away three of my wife’s maids, and my great-aunt, the Dowager Duchess of Bolton, swore she would never set foot in the house again after he dropped a skeleton hand on her during dinner. His being gone will increase the value of the property enormously.’

Mr. Otis then, with some trepidation, brought out the jewel casket. Lord Canterville’s eyes widened. He examined the rubies with a jeweller’s loupe he produced from his waistcoat pocket.

‘Yes,’ he said, ‘these are they. Exquisite, are they not? A fine example of Cellini’s work, I believe. My ancestress, Lady Eleanor, was, I am afraid, a rather plain woman, and she refused to wear them in public. Sir Simon, being a man of some vanity, was greatly offended, which led to the unfortunate… ah… domestic disagreement.’ He sighed. ‘Well, they are legally mine, of course, as heirlooms of the estate.’

Mr. Otis’s heart sank. ‘Of course, my lord. I shall have them sent to your bank in London at once.’

‘Papa, no!’ cried Virginia, who had been listening quietly. ‘He gave them to me!’

Lord Canterville looked at the young American girl, at her earnest, pleading face and her eyes that still seemed to hold a strange light. He saw the little Duke of Cheshire place a protective arm around her. He sighed again, a long, weary sigh of a man burdened by titles and traditions.

‘My dear Miss Virginia,’ he said with a gentle smile. ‘Your courage and your compassion have done for my family what no one has been able to do for three hundred years. You have given peace to a troubled spirit and, more importantly, you have made this house habitable for people who are not psychical researchers. If you were to accept these baubles as a small token of my family’s gratitude, it would be I who was in your debt.’

And so it was settled. That evening, after Lord Canterville had departed, the little Duke of Cheshire sought out Mr. Otis in the library.

‘Mr. Otis,’ he began, twisting his hat in his hands with a nervousness that was quite at odds with his ducal title, ‘I asked you once before for Virginia’s hand, and you told me to wait. I have waited. I have seen her face down a ghost, comfort a lost soul, and look upon Death without fear. I do not think I can live without her. I love her more than I can say.’

Mr. Otis looked at the handsome, flushed face of the young man. He thought of his devotion during the terrible search, of his immediate understanding of Virginia’s strange quest, and of the fortune in jewels that his daughter now possessed, which did not harm the young man’s case in the slightest.

He smiled, a broad, warm, American smile. ‘Well, Cecil,’ he said, clapping him on the shoulder, ‘I used to think that the nobility of England was a spent force. But I see now that you have some good material left. You have my permission, provided, of course, that Virginia agrees.’

Virginia, who had been standing in the doorway, did agree, and her happiness was so radiant that it seemed to light up the whole room.

The Canterville Ghost was never seen again, but his memory was not forgotten. The bloodstain never reappeared, the clanking of chains was heard no more, and the twins were forced to find new, less supernatural forms of amusement. Canterville Chase became known not for its phantom, but for its hospitality and for the beauty of its young mistress, the Duchess of Cheshire. Virginia wore the ruby necklace on her wedding day, and many said she was the loveliest bride that England had ever seen. She and Cecil were deliriously happy, and their love was a testament to the old prophecy on the library window, for it was a love that had proven itself, in the end, to be far stronger than Death.

The wedding itself was a spectacular affair, talked of for months in the society papers on both sides of the Atlantic. It took place not at Canterville Chase, which was deemed a little too rustic, and perhaps a little too notorious, for such a high-profile union, but in London, at a cathedral so grand that even Mr. Otis was momentarily silenced by its ancient splendour. The bride, of course, was Virginia, who looked so ethereally lovely in white satin and lace, with the Canterville rubies glowing at her throat like a chain of captured sunsets, that a collective sigh went through the congregation. The Otis family, naturally, brought their own unique flavour to the proceedings. Mrs. Otis, though secretly thrilled, maintained an air of practical concern, remarking to a neighbouring Countess that the sheer quantity of flowers seemed a terrible extravagance and that she hoped they would be distributed to the local hospitals afterwards. Washington, his hair pomaded to a brilliant sheen, discussed American stock prices with a bewildered bishop, while the twins, dressed in matching velvet suits that they loathed, were a source of constant, low-level anxiety for the entire household. They had, thankfully, been persuaded that releasing a cage of white mice at the altar would be an international incident, but they still managed to swap the salt and sugar at the wedding breakfast, a prank which caused the French ambassador to splutter into his champagne with a most undiplomatic expression.

And so, Virginia Otis became Her Grace, the Duchess of Cheshire. She and Cecil spent their honeymoon in Italy, where they wandered through sun-drenched ruins and drifted in gondolas under starry Venetian skies, and for a time, the world seemed a perfect, blissful dream, uninterrupted by prophecies or phantoms. When they returned to London, they took up residence in Cheshire House, a vast and somewhat gloomy mansion in Belgravia that made Canterville Chase look like a cheerful country cottage. It was a house of echoing marble halls, of portraits of stern-faced ancestors, and of footmen who moved with such spectral quietness that Virginia sometimes wondered if Sir Simon had sent a few relatives to keep an eye on her.

It was here that Virginia’s real trial began, a trial far more subtle and, in its own way, more terrifying than her encounter with the Canterville Ghost. For the ghost of Sir Simon had been, in the end, a creature of passion and sorrow, a soul who could be understood and pitied. The society of late-Victorian London, however, was a far more formidable and less forgiving entity. At its head sat a cabal of imperious dowagers and gatekeepers of etiquette, chief among them the Dowager Marchioness of Dumbleton, a woman whose posture had all the flexibility of a poker and whose heart, it was rumoured, had been mislaid in her youth and replaced with a small, cold stone.

The Marchioness had not approved of the match from the beginning. An American Duchess was, in her opinion, a contradiction in terms, a colonial absurdity like a pineapple in a pine forest. She observed Virginia with the keen, merciless eye of a naturalist examining a strange new insect. Virginia’s honesty, she called naivete; her cheerful friendliness, she called a lack of proper dignity; her compassion, which had saved a 300-year-old soul, she dismissed as morbid sentimentality.

‘They say, my dear,’ she would confide to her circle of cronies, her voice a dry rustle of silk and disapproval, ‘that the girl consorted with the ghost. Held conversations with it. A most unhealthy preoccupation for a young woman. And those jewels! So very large. So very red. Almost theatrical, are they not? One suspects they are not entirely, you know, real. Like the girl herself.’

The story of the ghost, which had been a nine-days’ wonder, was now twisted into something sinister. It was whispered over tea-cups and behind fans that the new Duchess of Cheshire had a morbid streak, that she was perhaps not entirely stable, and that her influence over the poor, infatuated Duke was a cause for grave concern. Virginia, who had faced down the powers of Hell in a darkened chamber, found herself bewildered and hurt by the whispered malice of a drawing-room. The shapes in the darkness had not harmed her, but the sideways glances and the faint, cold smiles of these women chilled her to the bone.

Cecil, bless his devoted heart, was largely oblivious at first. He saw only that his wife was the most beautiful and wonderful creature in the world, and he assumed everyone else must see it too. When he did notice a slight coldness or a barbed comment, he dismissed it as feminine jealousy. ‘They’re just envious, my darling,’ he would say, kissing her. ‘There isn’t a woman in London who wouldn’t wish to be in your shoes.’

But it was not her shoes they coveted so much as her place, and they were determined to show her she was not fit to fill it. They intended to give the Marchioness a taste of a real American-style haunting.

They bribed one of her footmen, a young man with more debts than sense, and gained access to her ladyship’s house one evening when she was hosting a small, exclusive soirée. Their equipment consisted of a hollowed-out turnip carved into a grotesque face, a bedsheet, and several packets of ‘Rising Sun All-Purpose Cleaner,’ a product of Mr. Otis’s own company which, when mixed with a little water, produced a quite astonishing amount of thick, green foam. Their goal was to stage a spectral appearance in the conservatory during dessert.

The affair, however, went dreadfully awry. The twins, creeping through the darkened corridors, mistook the library for the conservatory. The Marchioness’s prized pug, which was supposed to be asleep in its basket, was instead sniffing around for fallen cake crumbs and met the boys with a volley of furious, asthmatic yaps. The guests, alarmed, rushed into the hall to find one young American boy entangled in a suit of armour and another desperately trying to prevent a small, furious dog from licking a puddle of foaming green cleaner.

The scandal was immense. The twins were sent home in disgrace, and Mr. Otis had to write a cheque to the Marchioness so large that it made him question the benefits of a hereditary aristocracy all over again. But the true victim was Virginia. The incident was laid squarely at her door. It was seen as proof of the vulgarity of her entire family. The new Duchess, it was said, not only communed with spirits but her brothers attempted to impersonate them with kitchen vegetables and household soap. Cheshire House became an object of pity and scorn. Invitations dwindled. Callers ceased to call. Virginia found herself living in a state of splendid isolation, the silence of her grand new home more oppressive than any silence she had known in the Garden of Death.

She grew pale and quiet. The light in her eyes, which had so captivated the Canterville Ghost, began to dim. Cecil was frantic with worry. He argued with his friends, challenged one young lord to a duel for making a slighting remark (an affair which Mr. Otis managed to prevent with a mixture of stern lecturing and an invitation to invest in a new telegraph company), and generally made himself miserable.

One evening, he found Virginia standing by a window in the long gallery, looking out at the grey London rain. Her ruby necklace was locked away in a safe; she now wore only simple dresses, as if trying to make herself smaller, less visible.
‘Virginia,’ he said softly, putting his arms around her. ‘What is it? What can I do? We could leave London. We could go back to the Chase, or travel again.’

Virginia turned in his arms, and he saw that her eyes were full of tears, the very tears she had once given away to save a soul. ‘Oh, Cecil,’ she whispered, her voice trembling. ‘It’s not London I wish to leave. It’s myself. They look at me and they see a loud, strange American girl who doesn’t belong. They think I am common and foolish. Perhaps they are right. I saved Sir Simon because I understood his sadness, but I don’t understand this. This coldness, this cruelty that smiles at you while it stabs you. I don’t know how to fight it.’

‘You don’t have to fight it,’ he said fiercely. ‘I will fight it for you. I will tell them all to go to the devil.’

‘No,’ she said, a flicker of her old firmness returning. ‘That is what they expect. It will only prove them right. There must be another way. Sir Simon told me that against the purity of a little child, the powers of Hell cannot prevail. But I am not a little child anymore, and this is not Hell. It is something much more complicated.’

She was quiet for a long time after that, and a plan began to form in her mind, a plan born not of anger or revenge, but of the same deep well of compassion that had always been her truest strength. She had learned from the ghost that the greatest pains are those that are unseen, the deepest sorrows those that are borne in silence. And she knew that beyond the glittering world of Belgravia and Mayfair, there was another London, a city of real darkness and real despair.

She summoned her father the next day. Mr. Otis found her in the morning room, looking more like her old self than she had in weeks.
‘Papa,’ she said, ‘I need your help. I want you to find for me the poorest, most neglected orphanage in the East End of London.’
Mr. Otis looked at her, a shrewd glint in his eye. ‘And what do you intend to do with it, my dear? Buy it and re-sell it for a profit?’
‘No, Papa,’ she said with a small smile. ‘I intend to fix it.’

The place he found was called the St. Giles’ Home for Unwanted Children. It was a grim, soot-stained building in a lane that smelled of poverty and coal smoke, a place forgotten by God and completely unknown to the Dowager Marchioness of Dumbleton, who was, ironically, the chairwoman of the ‘Society for the Amelioration of the Condition of the Deserving Poor,’ a charity known for its lavish fundraising balls and its almost total lack of actual amelioration.

Virginia went to St. Giles’ herself, dressed not as a Duchess but in a simple, practical dress. What she saw there broke her heart. She saw children with old, tired faces, in clothes that were little more than rags. She saw cracked walls, broken windows, and a larder that was almost bare. She saw a matron, a woman with a kind but exhausted face, who was trying to feed fifty children on a budget that would not have paid for the flowers at one of the Marchioness’s dinner parties.

Virginia did not weep. She rolled up her sleeves. With Cecil’s bewildered but full support, and with her father’s formidable organizational skills, she set to work. She did not simply write a cheque. She brought in workmen to repair the building, she ordered cartloads of food and warm clothing, and she spent her days there, reading to the children, playing with them, and listening to their stories. She taught them some of the new American games the twins had taught her, and for the first time in a long time, the grim corridors of St. Giles’ were filled with the sound of laughter.

News of the Duchess’s strange new hobby soon reached the ears of the Marchioness of Dumbleton, who sniffed with disdain. ‘Playing Lady Bountiful in the slums,’ she declared. ‘So theatrical. So American. She seeks to buy favour with charity, a most vulgar transaction.’

But Virginia was not seeking favour. She was creating it. One day, a journalist from a popular paper got wind of the story and, smelling a human-interest piece, visited St. Giles’. He had expected to find a duchess posing for a few minutes with a photogenic orphan before retreating to her carriage. Instead, he found her on her knees, scrubbing a floor, with a smudge of dirt on her cheek and three small children clinging to her skirts. His article, when it appeared, was a sensation. It did not speak of the American Duchess, but of an ‘Angel of the East End.’

The culmination of her plan was a masterstroke of Otis family ingenuity. Virginia announced she would be hosting a charity event for St. Giles’. The invitations, which went out to every important name in London, were for an ‘American-style Thanksgiving Fair’ to be held on the grounds of Cheshire House itself.

Curiosity, a force more powerful even than snobbery, compelled them to attend. They arrived to find the formal gardens transformed. There were stalls selling apple pies and pumpkin tarts, there were games of skill and chance, and in the centre of it all, a stage had been erected. The Marchioness of Dumbleton arrived late, intending to make a brief, condescending appearance. She found herself swept up in a crowd that was, to her astonishment, actually enjoying itself.

The main event was a presentation on the stage. The matron of St. Giles’, her face shining with gratitude, spoke of the home’s transformation. Then Virginia herself stepped forward, looking radiant and confident. She did not speak of herself, but of the children, and of the work that still needed to be done. Then, to everyone’s surprise, Mr. Otis took the stage.

He announced that he was holding an auction. Not for fine art or French wines, but for what he called ‘Opportunities.’ He auctioned off the chance to name a new dormitory, to sponsor a child’s education, to provide a week’s worth of hot meals. His brash, energetic style was infectious, and soon a spirit of competitive philanthropy, a concept utterly alien to the Marchioness, had seized the crowd. Lords were outbidding each other for the honour of buying a new roof, their wives pledging fortunes for new beds and blankets.

The final item for auction was announced by Virginia. It was a private performance by the world-renowned soprano, Madame de la Roche, who had been persuaded by Virginia to donate her time. The bidding was fierce. Just as it was about to close, the Dowager Marchioness, caught in a pincer movement of social pressure and a desperate need to re-assert her pre-eminence, found herself placing the winning bid for a staggering sum.

It is a fine thing indeed when the old world’s sense of duty and the new world’s sense of enterprise can come together for such a noble cause.’

The Marchioness was trapped. She could not deny it without looking foolish and uncharitable. She was forced to smile, a rictus of a smile, as the crowd applauded her generosity. She had been defeated, not by malice or intrigue, but by sheer, overwhelming goodness, wielded with a healthy dose of American showmanship.

That night, after the last guest had departed and a truly astonishing sum of money had been counted, Cecil found Virginia in the library. The fire was lit, casting a warm glow on the room. She was wearing the ruby necklace again, and it seemed to glow with an inner fire of its own.

‘You did it,’ he said, his voice full of awe. ‘You beat them all. You didn’t become one of them; you made them come to you.’
Virginia smiled, a real smile this time, full of light and life. ‘I didn’t beat them, Cecil. But I have learned that Compassion is stronger than Pride, and Joy is stronger than Malice. And a little bit of American enterprise doesn’t hurt, either.’

They stood together by the fire, the Duke and his American Duchess, and the silence in the great house was no longer oppressive, but peaceful. The ghosts of Cheshire House, the stern ancestors and the whispering footmen, had been well and truly exorcised, replaced by a spirit of warmth and life that had travelled all the way from across the sea. And in the heart of London, their love, which had once conquered the sorrows of the dead, had now proven itself more than a match for the follies of the living.

London, which had at first seemed a fortress of impenetrable social customs, now appeared to Virginia as a charmingly eccentric old city, its peculiarities something to be navigated with humour rather than apprehension. The engagement of the young Duke of Cheshire to the lovely American heiress was the talk of the season, and their story, with its hints of gothic romance and its triumphant conclusion at the charity fête, had captured the public imagination. Invitations arrived in such a deluge that Washington, in his capacity as family secretary, was obliged to purchase a second and much larger silver tray for the hall table.

Cecil was in a state of perpetual, boyish delight. He showered her with gifts: extravagant bouquets of flowers, a small, mischievous-looking terrier, and a first edition of sonnets bound in white vellum. To Virginia, however, the greatest gift was his unwavering adoration and the simple joy of his company. Her love for him, which had been born in a moment of shared, supernatural sorrow, now blossomed in the bright sunshine of everyday life.

Their wedding was set for the autumn, and Cheshire House was to be their principal residence. Virginia found herself poring over catalogues of fabrics and furniture, a task she undertook with the same practical earnestness she had applied to reforming the London charitable scene. She had plans for a new library, a proper American-style bathroom with unlimited hot water, and a nursery, a prospect she considered with a quiet, happy blush. But the poison, once injected, began its slow work. A week later, Virginia noticed a distinct chill at a reception at the French Embassy. Young Lord Merton, who had been a tireless suitor before her engagement, looked at her with an expression of pity that was far more insulting than any open hostility. The whispers followed her, quiet but persistent, like the rustling of dry leaves.

The Dowager Marchioness of Dumbleton had chosen her weapon with care. She had not started a rumour; she had merely unearthed an old one and given it a new and malicious spin. The story of Virginia’s disappearance at Canterville Chase, so alarming at the time, had been quickly supplanted in the family’s memory by the joy of her safe return and the astonishing events that followed. To the outside world, a vague and reassuring explanation had been given. Now, in the cruel light of society speculation, that vagueness became a canvas onto which any number of sordid pictures could be painted. A secret elopement, a clandestine meeting, a fit of madness; all were hinted at, and all served the same purpose: to render Virginia Otis unsuitable as the future Duchess of Cheshire.

The blow landed hardest, as it was intended to, upon Cecil. He was summoned one afternoon to the London residence of his great-uncle, Lord Dumbleton, the Duke’s sole surviving trustee and a man whose spine was composed entirely of precedent. ‘Cecil,’ he began, without any preamble, ‘this business concerning your fiancée will not do. It will not do at all.’

‘What business, Uncle?’ Cecil asked, though he knew perfectly well.

‘This talk,’ Lord Dumbleton said, waving a brittle hand. ‘It is everywhere. They are saying she is unstable. They are saying she ran away with gypsies. The honour of your name, Cecil! The Duchess of Cheshire cannot be a woman with a past full of… of unexplained lacunae. It is one thing for an American to be eccentric; it is quite another for her to be compromised.’

‘Virginia is the purest, most wonderful girl in the world!’ Cecil cried, his face flushing with anger. ‘These stories are vile, contemptible lies!’

‘Are they?’ his uncle enquired coolly. ‘Then provide an explanation. You were there. You were, I am told, “perfectly frantic.” Where did she go? Who was she with? The family has a right to know. Our name is to be joined with hers. We cannot have it joined to a scandal.’

Cecil fell silent. He could not, of course, tell his uncle the truth. To speak of a four-hundred-year-old ghost, a garden of death, and a prophecy on a library window would not mend the situation; it would merely confirm the suspicions of his fiancée’s, and indeed his own, insanity. He was bound by a promise to Virginia, and by the sheer impossibility of the tale. He could only say, ‘I trust her completely. That is all the explanation I need, and it should be all that you need, too.’

Lord Dumbleton sighed, a thin, dusty sound. ‘Love is a charming sentiment in a young man, Cecil, but it is a poor foundation for a dynasty. I must urge you to reconsider this engagement. Postpone the wedding, at the very least, until this unfortunate gossip dies down.’

‘I will not,’ Cecil said, his jaw set. ‘I would sooner give up my title, my house, everything, than give up Virginia.’

He stormed out of the house, leaving his uncle to shake his head at the lamentable and romantic folly of youth. But his bravado faltered as he walked through the London streets. The Marchioness had done her work well. She had created a situation where any denial was an admission of guilt, and the only possible truth was too fantastical to be believed.

That evening, the Otis family convened in the drawing-room of their house in Belgravia. It was not a happy gathering. Mrs. Otis had been weeping, and was dabbing her eyes with a lace handkerchief. The twins, for once, were not planning any mischief; they sat quietly, looking with dismay at their sister’s pale face.

‘It’s that old cat, the Marchioness,’ said Washington, pacing the floor. ‘She was humiliated at the fête, and this is her revenge. It’s a typically aristocratic mode of warfare; no declared battles, just poisoned wells and back-stabbing in the dark.’

‘I shall go to her house and demand a public apology!’ Mr. Otis declared, striking a fist into his palm. ‘In America, if someone slanders your daughter, you can hold them to account!’

‘This isn’t America, Father,’ Virginia said, her voice barely a whisper. She was sitting in a large armchair, looking small and fragile. The ruby necklace lay on a table beside her, its fiery light seeming to mock the gloom in the room. ‘You cannot hold a whisper to account. How can you fight something that is everywhere and nowhere at once?’

For the first time since coming to England, her bright, indomitable spirit seemed to be failing. She remembered the Ghost’s longing for peace, for the silence of the Garden of Death where there was no yesterday and no to-morrow. The world of society, with its cruel memories and its malicious future-planning, suddenly seemed infinitely more terrifying than any phantom.

Cecil, who had been standing by the window, came and knelt by her chair. He took her hand, which was cold as ice. ‘We will fight it together, my darling,’ he said, his voice thick with emotion. ‘I don’t care what they say. I don’t care about any of them. We can go away. We can go back to America. We can live anywhere you like, as long as we are together.’

Virginia looked at his handsome, earnest face, and her heart ached. To flee would be to admit defeat. It would mean the Marchioness had won. It would mean that Pride and Malice were, after all, stronger than Love and Compassion. She thought of the little huntsmen on the tapestry at Canterville, waving her back, and the goggle-eyed monsters on the chimney-piece, warning her away. She had not faltered then, and she would not falter now.

A strange light, the very same light that had filled her eyes when she had agreed to help the Ghost, returned. She sat up a little straighter. ‘No,’ she said, her voice regaining its firmness. ‘We will not run away. This is our home now, Cecil. We will not be frightened out of it by whispers.’

‘But what can we do, sis?’ asked one of the twins. ‘You can’t put a rumour in a sling-shot.’

Virginia looked from the concerned faces of her family to the devoted face of her fiancé. A plan began to form in her mind, a plan so audacious, so theatrical, and so uniquely American in its conception that it made Mr. Otis’s idea of suing for libel seem positively timid.

‘The Marchioness wants a story,’ Virginia said slowly, a smile beginning to play on her lips. ‘She has offered the world a penny dreadful. I think we ought to give them a great romance instead.’

Three nights later was the Duchess of Paisley’s annual ball, the glittering apex of the London season. All of society was there, buzzing with speculation. Would the young Duke of Cheshire attend? And if he did, would he be alone? The prevailing opinion was that the engagement was, to all intents and purposes, over.

The Dowager Marchioness of Dumbleton was holding court near the grand staircase, accepting the sympathetic condolences of her friends with an expression of grave sorrow that barely concealed her triumph. It had been a most unfortunate affair, she agreed, but entirely predictable. One could not expect, after all, to import a bride as one might import bacon, without first checking her provenance.

At that moment, the majordomo’s voice boomed through the ballroom. ‘His Grace, the Duke of Cheshire, and Miss Virginia Otis.’

A hush fell over the assembly. Every head turned towards the entrance. There they stood. Cecil, in immaculate evening dress, his head held high, looking more ducal than anyone had ever seen him. And on his arm, Virginia. She was dressed in a gown of shimmering white satin, as if she were already a bride. Around her neck, blazing like a captured star, was the Canterville ruby necklace. It pulsed with deep, red light, a defiant slash of colour against the virginal white of her dress. She did not look pale or frightened. She looked radiant, serene, and utterly magnificent.

They walked into the ballroom not like defendants entering a courtroom, but like monarchs entering their hall. The whispers died on the air. The crowd parted before them as if by magic. They walked directly, and with great purpose, towards the spot where the Dowager Marchioness stood, her face a mask of astonishment and fury.

Virginia smiled, a dazzling, confident smile. ‘Your Ladyship,’ she said, her clear voice carrying in the sudden silence. ‘I have been so looking forward to seeing you.’

The Marchioness, momentarily speechless, could only incline her head.

‘I must thank you,’ Virginia continued brightly. ‘In fact, we both must thank you.’ She gestured to include Cecil, who looked down at her with pure adoration. ‘Your… interest in my past has given us the most wonderful idea. You see, society has been hearing a rather dull and sordid version of my little adventure at Canterville. We have decided it is time they heard the true story.’

She paused, letting the suspense build. The entire ballroom was now hanging on her every word.

‘It is a story of a lonely, four-hundred-year-old soul, a terrible curse, and an ancient prophecy. It is a ghost story, you see. A true one. It is the story of how a young girl’s compassion, and a young man’s love, helped to bring peace to a tormented spirit and to a haunted house.’ She looked at Cecil, her eyes shining. ‘It is, we think, the true founding story of our life together.’

Cecil spoke then, his voice ringing with pride. ‘My fiancée,’ he announced to the astonished crowd, ‘is an authoress. In collaboration with myself, she is writing an account of the events. We have decided to call it “The Phantom of the Chase; or, The Puritan’s Pardon.” It will be published privately and sent as a gift to all those who will be attending our wedding in the autumn.’

A collective gasp went through the room. It was an act of social genius. They had not denied the rumour; they had seized it, transformed it, and turned it into a romantic legend with themselves as the heroes. To question their story now would be to admit to having a soul of impenetrable dullness, to prefer a grubby piece of gossip to a magnificent tale of the supernatural.

Virginia turned back to the Marchioness, her expression one of perfect, sweet innocence. ‘We should like to send you the very first copy, Your Ladyship,’ she said. ‘As a small token of our gratitude for the inspiration. After all, it is not every day that one’s love story is mistaken for a scandal, is it?’

The Dowager Marchioness of Dumbleton stood frozen, her fan clutched in her hand. She had been outmanoeuvred, outflanked, and utterly defeated. She had tried to wound them with a whisper, and they had answered with a symphony. She saw in Virginia’s clear, calm eyes not the triumph of a rival, but something far more galling: the pity of a superior.

That night, the Otis family did not need to light the fire in the drawing-room; the warmth of their victory was enough. Mr. Otis poured champagne, declaring that his daughter was a better strategist than General Grant. The twins demanded to know if they could illustrate the book with pictures of the ghost, and Mrs. Otis was already redesigning the wedding invitations to include a tasteful spectral motif.

Virginia and Cecil stood apart by the window, looking out at the London night. ‘“The Phantom of the Chase,”’ Cecil murmured, taking her hand. ‘You are brilliant. Utterly, impossibly brilliant.’

‘The Ghost taught me that Love is stronger than Death,’ Virginia said softly, leaning her head on his shoulder. ‘And London has taught me something else. The best way to silence a rumour is to tell a better story.’

They stood together, the Duke and his American fiancée, who was soon to be a Duchess and was already an authoress. The ruby on her breast glowed with a deep and steady fire, no longer a relic of a sorrowful past, but a beacon for a bright and fearless future. They had faced the ghosts of the old world and the malice of the new, and had discovered that the most powerful magic of all was not to be found in ancient prophecies or haunted galleries, but in a combination of courage, compassion, and a healthy dose of American ingenuity. The house of Cheshire, it was clear, was in very good hands indeed.

The autumn wedding, however, was predicated on one rather significant condition: the approval of the Dowager Duchess of Cheshire, Cecil’s mother. This formidable personage, who resided for most of the year at the principal family seat, Cheshire Court, in a state of magnificent and frosty isolation, had yet to meet her prospective American daughter-in-law. Her correspondence on the matter had been civil, yet so exquisitely devoid of warmth that it could have been used to chill wine. She had, she wrote, been informed of Cecil’s ‘rather sudden romantic attachment’, and supposed that she would have to inspect the young lady before the season was out. The verb ‘to inspect’ was underlined three times in her spidery, aristocratic hand, a fact which Cecil had conveniently omitted to mention to Virginia.

It was therefore decided that the Otis family, along with the little Duke, should spend a fortnight at Cheshire Court, so that the two great houses, as it were, could take each other’s measure. Mr. Otis, who believed that any situation could be improved by a firm handshake and a direct question, was entirely unconcerned; Mrs. Otis, however, spent a full week consulting transatlantic society pages for any mention of the Dowager’s preferences in tea-gowns, and the twins, having heard that Cheshire Court possessed a genuine moat, immediately began devising new and terrifying experiments involving model battleships and large quantities of gunpowder.

Their arrival was a study in contrasts. The Otis family, vibrant and talkative, disembarked from their carriage like a cheerful invasion, while the Duchess received them in the Great Hall, a chamber so vast and ancient that it seemed to absorb all sound and light. She was a tall, thin woman, dressed in a severe grey silk that rustled like dead leaves, and she wore her disapproval like a family jewel.

‘So you are the young lady who has captured my son’s heart,’ she said, her voice a delicate icicle. She did not extend a hand, but merely examined Virginia through a lorgnette, as if she were a curious, and possibly flawed, piece of porcelain.

‘I am Virginia Otis,’ Virginia replied, with a curtsy that was perfectly executed, if a trifle democratic. ‘And I am very happy to have captured his heart, for he has certainly captured mine.’

The Duchess’s thin lips tightened almost imperceptibly. ‘Indeed. We are a family that values… restraint, Miss Otis. In all things.’ Her gaze flickered for a moment to the rather exuberant ruby that pulsed on Virginia’s breast.

The first few days were a quiet torment. The Duchess instituted a routine of such rigid and monotonous correctness that it made life at Canterville Chase seem like a bohemian carnival. Meals were silent, mournful affairs, presided over by a butler who looked older than the house itself and moved with the silent, inexorable dread of a glacier. Afternoons were for ‘improving’ walks along gravel paths, during which the Duchess would point out various species of lichen and offer chilly monologues on the duties of a duchess, all of which seemed to involve opening bazaars and avoiding any display of genuine emotion.

It was during this trying period that Virginia and Cecil attempted to begin their great literary collaboration. They set up a small writing-desk in a corner of the morning-room, a space which the Duchess had grudgingly allotted them. The partnership, however, proved to be more challenging than either had anticipated.

‘But Sir Simon never had a “soulful, sable-plumed helmet”,’ Virginia remonstrated gently, looking up from the page on which Cecil was scrawling with a great deal of flourish. ‘He told me his armour was dreadfully uncomfortable, and that he mostly wore a dressing-gown after the first century.’

‘My dearest, darling girl,’ Cecil explained, with the patient air of a great artist explaining his vision, ‘we are not writing history; we are creating a legend. The public does not want a ghost in a dressing-gown; they want romance, tragedy, a hint of noble despair! The sable-plumed helmet is not a fact, it is a spiritual truth.’

‘It seems a great deal like a lie,’ Virginia said, her American pragmatism rising to the surface. ‘He was a very unhappy old man who murdered his wife and was starved to death by her brothers. It is a sad story, not a romantic one.’

‘Precisely! And we shall make it romantic!’ Cecil cried, seizing her hand. ‘We shall transform his vulgar crime into a crime of passion! His starvation will become a noble, self-imposed penance! It will be magnificent.’

Virginia sighed. It seemed that the English aristocracy applied the same principles to storytelling as they did to their landscaping: any untidy bits of reality were to be ruthlessly pruned away in favour of a more pleasing, if entirely artificial, effect.

Their literary disputes became a small, secret rebellion against the oppressive quiet of the house. They would argue in hushed, laughing whispers over the character of Lady Eleanor de Canterville, whom Cecil wished to portray as a faithless Florentine intrigante, and Virginia insisted was a plain but kind woman who had simply been a very bad cook. They debated the exact nature of the Garden of Death, with Cecil favouring a description full of marble angels and weeping willows, while Virginia remembered only the soft brown earth and the sound of silence.

Unbeknownst to them, they had an audience. The Dowager Duchess, who possessed a supernatural ability to move about her own house without making a sound, had taken to observing them from behind tapestries and through slightly ajar doors. She did not understand the nature of their whispered conferences, but the sight of them, their heads bent close together, their faces animated with a shared secret, filled her with a cold and unfamiliar fury. She saw not love, but conspiracy. She saw this bright, confident American girl remaking her son, filling his head with absurd notions, turning the ancient and honourable name of Cheshire into a public spectacle.

Her campaign against Virginia intensified, though it was waged with such exquisite subtlety that only its target was truly aware of the attack. At dinner, she would solicit Virginia’s opinions on complex matters of European politics, only to smile a thin, pitying smile at her straightforward answers. She would ask her to play the pianoforte, knowing full well that Virginia’s repertoire consisted of simple folk songs rather than the intricate sonatas favoured by the daughters of the aristocracy. She spoke constantly of Cecil’s former fiancées, imaginary creatures of impossible grace and breeding, who had all, it seemed, been tragically lost to them through an excess of delicate feeling.

‘Lady Clementina was so very sensitive,’ she would sigh, dabbing a non-existent tear from her eye with a lace handkerchief. ‘The slightest draft, a coarse word, and she would simply… wilt. She was not made for this rough world. A true aristocrat of the soul.’

Through it all, Virginia remained resolutely polite and cheerful, a fact that seemed to infuriate the Duchess more than any outburst would have. Mrs. Otis saw only that the Duchess was a rather dull and formal woman, and Mr. Otis concluded that she was a ‘first-rate specimen of a fossilized society’, but Virginia knew that she was in a battle for her future. She felt like a character in one of Mr. Henry James’s novels, an innocent from the New World lost in a labyrinth of ancient European malice.

The crisis came, as it was bound to, over the book. The Duchess, in one of her silent glides through the house, discovered a half-finished manuscript page that had fluttered to the floor. It contained one of Cecil’s more florid descriptions of Sir Simon’s ghostly torment, ending with the line, ‘And so the spectral sinner rattled his chains, a sound that was at once a lament for his lost love and a protest against the injustice of the grave!’

That evening, as the family gathered for their pre-dinner ordeal in the drawing-room, the Duchess held the page between her thumb and forefinger as if it were a contaminated object.

‘I believe this belongs to you,’ she said, her voice dropping the temperature in the room by several degrees. She did not look at Cecil, but fixed her cold, reptilian gaze upon Virginia.

‘Oh, how wonderful!’ cried Cecil, entirely missing the tone. ‘You have had a sneak preview, Mother. What do you think? I am particularly proud of the “protest against the injustice of the grave”. Has a certain ring to it, does it not?’

‘What I think,’ the Duchess said slowly, ‘is that I am at a loss to comprehend the vulgarity of it all. You propose to take a sordid family tragedy, a matter of ghosts and graveyards, and print it for the delectation of the common public? You wish to turn the spiritual history of a noble house into a… a penny dreadful?’

‘It is not a penny dreadful!’ Cecil protested, his cheeks flushing. ‘It is a romance! A tribute!’

‘A tribute to what?’ the Duchess snapped, her composure finally cracking. ‘To this modern American fashion for laying bare one’s soul in public? For having no secrets, no dignity, no sense of what is private? We are the Cheshires. Our sorrows are our own. We do not sell them for shillings at a book-stall, bound in cloth, to be pawed at by grocers and factory-hands!’

A dreadful silence fell upon the room. Mr. Otis looked as if he were about to make a sharp comment about free markets, but was silenced by a stern look from his wife. The twins, for the first time in their lives, looked genuinely frightened.

‘You have brought this circus with you, Miss Otis,’ the Duchess continued, her voice trembling with rage as she turned fully to Virginia. ‘This loud, garish world of headlines and gossip. You have encouraged my son in this… this literary abomination. You are not one of us. You do not understand our ways. You think our history is a commodity to be bought and sold, like your father’s tinned goods.’

Virginia had gone very pale, but her eyes, when she raised them to meet the Duchess’s, were clear and steady. She did not look to Cecil for support. She stood up and walked towards the old woman, her small figure radiating a strange and unexpected authority.

‘Your Grace,’ she began, her voice low but firm, ‘you are mistaken. I do not think your history is a commodity. I think it is a treasure. But a treasure that is buried and forgotten is no treasure at all. It is just dust. The story of Sir Simon is not one of vulgarity; it is one of sin, and suffering, and, finally, of redemption. It is a story about how a little bit of love can heal a very old wound.’

She paused, and glanced at Cecil, who was watching her with an expression of pure adoration.

‘Your son,’ she went on, turning back to the Duchess, ‘has a poet’s soul. He sees the beauty and the romance in that story. I have a practical mind. I see the lesson in it. Together, we hoped to write something that was both beautiful and good. We did not mean to sell your sorrows. We meant to honour the peace that was finally won. The peace that I myself helped to bring to a lost soul.’

She reached out and, to the astonishment of everyone present, took the Duchess’s cold, rigid hand in her own. ‘The ghost taught me that Love is stronger than Death. It is a lesson that is too important to keep locked away in a haunted gallery. It is a story that belongs to everyone who has ever felt hopeless, or lost, or in need of forgiveness. It is not just your family’s story, Your Grace. It is a human story.’

The Dowager Duchess stood frozen, staring at the American girl who was holding her hand. She looked from Virginia’s earnest face to her son’s, and saw there not the foolishness of a boy in love, but the steady resolve of a man. She looked at the manuscript page, still clutched in her other hand, and the words ‘protest against the injustice of the grave’ seemed to take on a new and deeper meaning. She had spent her life protesting that injustice, the slow, silent grave that time made of all good families, of all noble traditions. She had fought it with hauteur, with silence, with an iron-willed adherence to the past. And she had felt herself losing.

This girl, this strange, impossible girl, was proposing another way. To fight the grave not with silence, but with a story. To achieve immortality not by resisting the world, but by charming it.

A long, shuddering sigh escaped her lips, the sound of a winter’s ice breaking up in the spring. Her hand, in Virginia’s, relaxed.

‘A human story,’ she repeated, her voice no longer an icicle, but the barest whisper of a thaw. ‘You say he had no faith, and you prayed for him?’

‘Yes,’ Virginia said softly.

‘And he had no tears, and you wept for him?’

‘Yes, Your Grace.’

The Duchess looked at Virginia then, truly looked at her, without the lorgnette, without the prejudice, without the fear. She saw the purity that the powers of Hell could not prevail against, and she saw, with the dawning and painful recognition of an old woman, that this same purity was more than a match for the powers of a Dowager Duchess.

‘Well,’ she said, her voice still shaky, but possessing a new and unfamiliar note of resignation, perhaps even of grudging admiration. ‘If you are to be a duchess, I suppose you had better learn to tell a good story. It is, after all, the only thing that lasts.’

And with that, she turned and swept from the room, leaving behind her a silence that was no longer oppressive, but filled with a sense of profound and utter astonishment. The battle for Cheshire Court was over, and victory, once again, belonged to the gentle heart and the indomitable spirit of Miss Virginia E. Otis. The wedding, it was clear, would proceed in the autumn as planned.

You gave a little, you held your ground, and you appealed to the other party’s deeper interests. I couldn’t have done it better myself. Though,’ he added, stroking his chin thoughtfully, ‘I would probably have offered her a small percentage of the book royalties. It is always wise to sweeten the pot.’

‘Hiram, for heaven’s sake,’ said Mrs. Otis, who had been hovering near the doorway, torn between maternal pride and sheer terror. ‘This is not a stock market transaction. This is our daughter’s future. Oh, Virginia, my dear, you were so brave!’ She enveloped her daughter in a warm hug, a gesture of straightforward American affection that still seemed a novelty within the ancient, drafty walls of Cheshire Court.

And so, the great domestic crisis was averted. The engagement was formally, and this time irrevocably, sanctioned, and the conversation turned, as it inevitably must, to the practical matter of the wedding. It was here that a new and perhaps more formidable challenge arose, a challenge not of spectral prophecies or ducal pride, but of something far more entrenched: the immutable laws of English high society.

Mrs. Otis, being a woman of excellent common sense and considerable organisational skill, immediately proposed the formation of a committee. ‘We shall need a subcommittee for flowers, one for the catering, and one for the guest list,’ she announced one morning at breakfast, producing a handsome leather-bound notebook and a newly sharpened pencil. ‘I have already drafted a preliminary project timeline. If we are to be ready by autumn, we must be efficient.’

The Dowager Duchess, who was now a regular, if generally silent, presence at their meals, looked up from her toast. She did not say anything, but she slowly raised her lorgnette and peered at Mrs. Otis’s notebook as if it were some strange and possibly dangerous insect.

Washington Otis, ever the dutiful son, supported his mother’s initiative. ‘An excellent idea, Mother. Proper management is the key to any successful enterprise. We can use a system of cross-referenced ledgers to track invitations and RSVPs. I shall design one this afternoon.’

The twins, who had been remarkably subdued since Virginia’s disappearance and reappearance, saw in the wedding preparations a grand new theatre for their particular talents. ‘Can we be on the pyrotechnics committee?’ asked one. ‘I have a new formula for pink smoke,’ added the other, ‘which would be a splendid tribute to Virginia’s American heritage.’

It was at this juncture that the Dowager Duchess finally spoke. ‘There will be no committees,’ she said, her voice having regained some, though not all, of its former frost. ‘And there will most certainly be no pyrotechnics. A Cheshire wedding is not an enterprise. It is a sacrament. The arrangements have been the same for the last four hundred years. They do not require a timeline. They require adherence to tradition.’

‘But how will anyone know what to do?’ asked Mrs. Otis, genuinely perplexed.

‘They will know,’ the Duchess said simply, ‘because it has always been done. The vicar knows his part. The tenants know theirs. The cook knows precisely what to prepare for the wedding breakfast. It is a matter of historical record.’

This, for Mrs. Otis, was a deeply unsatisfactory state of affairs. To her mind, an event without a plan was simply a catastrophe waiting to happen. The conflict simmered for several days, a polite but palpable tension between American efficiency and English precedent. Mr. Otis attempted to mediate by proposing a ‘merger’ of the two approaches, a suggestion that was met with a blank stare from the Duchess.

The impasse was broken by the arrival of the Dowager Duchess’s elder sister, Lady Agatha Thornton, a woman who made the Duchess herself seem, by comparison, a reckless bohemian. Lady Agatha was a tall, thin woman with a face like a forgotten Roman coin, whose chief occupation in life was the preservation of aristocratic dignity against the vulgar encroachments of the modern world. She had come, she announced, to oversee the preparations and ensure that the wedding of her nephew was not turned into a ‘circus sideshow.’

‘Lucretia, you are far too lenient with these people,’ she told her sister in a carrying whisper that was clearly intended for the entire household to hear. ‘A project timeline! Ledgers! It is a wedding, not the construction of a railway. And the girl, while pretty enough, I suppose, has no notion of what is required of her. I asked her yesterday what she thought of the Chartres lace for her veil, and she told me it was ‘lovely,’ as if she were describing a field of daisies.’

The first major battle was fought over the wedding breakfast menu. Mrs. Otis, in a spirit of helpfulness, had suggested a few of her favourite dishes from home. ‘A Waldorf salad is so refreshing,’ she had said, ‘and perhaps some Boston baked beans for the gentlemen. Simple, hearty fare.’

Lady Agatha, who was presiding over the discussion, very nearly dropped her teacup. ‘Beans?’ she repeated, her voice a horrified squeak. ‘Madam, we are not provisioning a whaling ship. The Duke of Cheshire’s wedding breakfast has consisted of nine courses since the time of Queen Anne. It begins with chilled consommé, proceeds through poached turbot, lamb cutlets, and roasted quail, and concludes with a rum-flavoured Savoy cake. It is not open to negotiation.’

‘But nine courses seems so excessive,’ Mrs. Otis ventured. ‘And so time-consuming. We have found in America that a buffet-style arrangement allows for a more fluid social dynamic.’

Lady Agatha’s face became a mask of aristocratic horror. ‘A buffet,’ she whispered, as if the word itself were a profanity. ‘Lucretia, did you hear? She suggests we have the guests stand in a line and serve themselves, like miners at a soup kitchen. The shame of it!’

It was into this fraught atmosphere that Virginia moved with her usual quiet grace. She listened patiently to Lady Agatha’s pronouncements on the correct placement of fork tines and the ancient lineage of the family’s silversmiths; she listened with equal patience to her mother’s detailed plans for streamlining the floral arrangements. She felt herself to be a bridge between two worlds, and she knew that a bridge must be strong, yet flexible.

While the great ladies debated menus, Virginia and Cecil would slip away for long walks in the Chase. The autumn air was crisp, and the leaves were beginning to turn, painting the landscape in glorious shades of gold and crimson. It was during one of these walks that Cecil brought up the matter of the ghost’s story.

‘My mother told me what you said,’ he began, ‘about it being a human story, and not just ours. I think you are right. But what did you mean to do? My great-aunt is terrified you are going to sell it to some dreadful penny-press journalist.’

Virginia smiled. ‘I would never do that. The ghost asked for peace, not for fame. His story is not for entertainment.’ She paused, looking out over the placid waters of the carp-pond. ‘I thought I would write it down, just as it happened. All of it. The prophecy, my fear, his sadness, and the Garden of Death. And I would have it printed, just a few copies, in a beautiful book bound in dark leather. We would give one to your mother, and keep one for ourselves, and perhaps place one in the library, next to the window with the prophecy. It would be a record, not a sensation. A memorial.’

Cecil looked at her, his heart swelling with a love so profound it was almost painful. It was this quality in her, this perfect instinct for what was right and true and beautiful, that had captured him from the very beginning. It was the same quality that had won the forgiveness of a centuries-old ghost and the surrender of a formidable duchess. ‘That,’ he said softly, ‘is a perfect idea. It is exactly what he would have wanted.’

Meanwhile, the twins had grown tired of the endless talk of lace and invitations. They missed the ghost. The house, for all its newfound peace, felt decidedly dull. They decided to remedy this. Their first project was an attempt to ‘cheer up’ the rather grim-looking portraits of the Canterville ancestors that lined the great hall. Using some of Mrs. Otis’s imported laundry bluing and a pot of mustard, they gave Sir Simon’s scowling predecessor a rather jaunty blue moustache and a pair of startling yellow eyebrows. Their work was discovered by a horrified footman, and Lady Agatha declared that she was on the verge of a nervous collapse.

‘The children require discipline!’ she shrieked at Mr. Otis. ‘They are running wild! They have defaced a priceless work of art!’

‘Now, now, Lady Agatha,’ said Mr. Otis calmly, inspecting the portrait. ‘I wouldn’t say priceless. The frame is quite nice, but the subject is, if you’ll forgive my saying so, a rather sour-looking individual. I think the boys have, in fact, improved his general expression. He looks almost cheerful. In any case, I shall have the cost of the damages deducted from their allowance. It’s a simple matter of accounting.’

This practical response so infuriated Lady Agatha that she retired to her room with a bottle of smelling salts and refused to emerge for the rest of the day.

The final confrontation occurred a week before the wedding. Lady Agatha, having recovered her strength, summoned a family council. She had discovered Virginia’s plan for the privately printed book about the ghost.

‘This is the final indignity!’ she announced, waving a letter from her informant, a gossip-loving cousin. ‘She intends to publish a history of the family’s shame! A sordid tale of murder and haunting, to be circulated for all to read! It will make us a laughing-stock. Lucretia, you must forbid it. Cecil, you must put a stop to this nonsense at once.’

Cecil stepped forward, placing a protective arm around Virginia. ‘It is not a sordid tale, Aunt Agatha. It is a tale of sin and redemption. And Virginia does not intend to circulate it. It is a private memorial.’

‘Private!’ scoffed Lady Agatha. ‘Nothing is private with these Americans! Everything is for public consumption! First, it is a book for friends, then it is an article in a magazine, and before we know it, there will be guided tours of the Tapestry Chamber and souvenir plates with the ghost’s face on them!’

It was at this point that the Dowager Duchess, who had been sitting silently in her high-backed chair, rose to her feet. All eyes turned to her. She looked at her sister, and her face was weary, but resolute.

‘Agatha, you are a fool,’ she said, her voice cutting through the tension like a shard of ice. ‘You have been here for a month, and you have learned nothing. You see an enemy where there is only an ally. You see a threat where there is only hope.’

Lady Agatha stared at her sister, aghast. ‘Lucretia, have you taken leave of your senses?’

‘On the contrary,’ the Duchess replied, ‘I believe I am only just now coming to them. For years, I have done as you do. I have fought to keep our world just as it was. I have worshipped at the altar of tradition, and I have treated every change as a sacrilege. And what has it brought us? Isolation. A slow, graceful decay. We were becoming a museum piece, Agatha. A beautiful, dead thing in a glass case.’

She turned her gaze upon Virginia, and for the first time, there was something akin to warmth in her eyes. ‘This girl, with her strange American ways and her complete disregard for precedent, has done what generations of Cheshires could not. She has brought peace to this house. She has not done it by following our rules. She has done it by following her heart. Her plan for the book is not an insult; it is an act of grace. It honours the past without being imprisoned by it.’

She took a step closer to her sister, her voice dropping to a low, intense whisper. ‘The world is changing, Agatha. We can either stand on the shore, screaming at the tide, until we are swept away, or we can learn to build a new kind of ship. Virginia is not sinking the family. She is our ship. And if you are not prepared to sail with her, then I suggest you disembark at once.’

There was a profound silence. Lady Agatha stood, her mouth slightly agape, looking as if one of the Canterville ancestors had just stepped down from its frame and slapped her. Without another word, she turned and swept from the room, defeated not by an argument, but by a revelation.

The wedding day dawned bright and clear, a perfect jewel of an autumn morning. Canterville Chase was a hive of activity, but it was a happy, harmonious activity. The wedding was a beautiful compromise, a perfect fusion of two worlds. The service in the ancient village church was steeped in tradition, the words of the Book of Common Prayer echoing in the rafters as they had for centuries. But the bride, walking down the aisle on her father’s arm, was a vision of modern, radiant beauty, her smile not one of demure compliance, but of genuine, unalloyed joy. The little Duke of Cheshire, waiting for her at the altar, looked as though he had been granted a glimpse of heaven.

The wedding breakfast, held in the great hall, featured not nine courses, nor a buffet, but a carefully curated menu that included both roasted quail and a very elegant Waldorf salad. The twins, resplendent in velvet suits, served as pages and managed, miraculously, to cause no explosions. Mr. Otis made a short, heartfelt speech about the union of two great nations, and Mrs. Otis shed a few happy, discreet tears into a fine lace handkerchief.

Later that evening, Virginia, now the Duchess of Cheshire, stood by the library window, the same window where the prophecy had been written. She was wearing the string of famous Canterville jewels, and they glowed with a soft, internal fire against the warm skin of her throat. The house was quiet, filled with the gentle hum of sleeping guests and the lingering scent of woodsmoke and champagne. Cecil came and stood beside her, slipping his arm around her waist.

‘Happy, my love?’ he whispered.

‘More than I can say,’ she answered, leaning her head against his shoulder.

Her eyes drifted past the window, out towards the dark, silent park. She thought of the ghost, of the soft brown earth of the Garden of Death, of the peace he had so longed for. He had taught her that Love was stronger than Death, and looking at her husband, at the peaceful house, at the future stretching before her, she knew it was true. The ghost of Canterville was gone, but his last, greatest gift remained. He had not just found peace for himself; he had left it behind for them, a legacy of forgiveness that had healed a family, united two worlds, and turned a haunted old house into a home. And Virginia knew, with a certainty that settled deep in her soul, that this was the story that truly mattered, the one she would carry with her for all her days.

When they had finished their supper, a proceeding which had been more a pantomime of nourishment than a true meal, the sorrowful party repaired to the library. The great logs burning in the hearth did little to dispel the chill that had settled not in the room, but in the hearts of its occupants. Mrs. Otis, having exhausted the efficacy of eau-de-cologne, lay prostrate upon a crimson sofa, her face a mask of pale anxiety, attended by the housekeeper who hovered with a fresh bottle as if it were some sacred elixir against despair. He was trying to chart the geography of a miracle, and was finding the task singularly difficult. Washington and the twins were huddled near the window, their faces pressed against the cold glass, peering into a night so profoundly black it seemed to have swallowed the world. For the first time in their lives, the twins were silent, an unnatural state that was, in its own way, more alarming than Virginia’s absence. ‘It is my fault,’ the Duke said suddenly, his voice thick with unshed tears. ‘I should have insisted. I should have ridden over the groom and followed you to Bexley. A scuffle! I should have welcomed a scuffle! What is a scuffle when Virginia is lost?’

Mr. Otis turned from his map, his expression softening for a moment. ‘Now, Cecil, it is no one’s fault, and least of all yours. We have done everything a man can do. We have alerted the constabulary, we have dragged the pond, we have scoured the district. Logic dictates she must be somewhere.’

‘And what, may I ask, is in the box?’ he enquired.

‘His last gift,’ said Virginia. ‘A token of his gratitude.’ With delicate fingers, she unfasted the silver clasp. The lid sprang open, and she tilted the box so that the contents spilled out onto her lap, cascading in a river of fire and light.

The Otis family had seen a great deal of wealth in their time, but they had never seen anything like this. It was the Dowager Duchess who first gave voice to their shared, unspoken fear. She arrived one afternoon, unannounced, having motored down from London with the grim determination of a general inspecting a failing front line. She found Virginia sitting by a window in the great drawing-room, the famous string of rubies lying in her lap, their facets glittering like drops of fresh blood against the pale silk of her dress.

‘So,’ the old lady said, her voice devoid of any warmth. ‘It has begun.’

Cecil, who had followed his grandmother into the room, rushed to his wife’s side. ‘Virginia, my love, what are you doing with those? You know you dislike wearing them.’

Virginia looked up, and for a moment her eyes were not her own. They held a weariness that was centuries old, a chilling echo of the despair she had once seen in the eyes of the Ghost. ‘They are so sad,’ she whispered, her fingers tracing the outline of the largest ruby. ‘They cannot rest.’

‘Nonsense,’ the Duchess snapped, though a flicker of uncertainty crossed her features. ‘They are stones. They cannot feel. It is the legacy of that dreadful house you have brought with you. I knew it. That place is a blight, and its poison has seeped into you.’ She turned to her grandson. ‘Cecil, you must be firm. The girl is unwell. She is suffering from a malady of the nerves, no doubt brought on by her unfortunate American constitution, which is not bred for the rigours of a proper life. The jewels must be locked away, and she must see Sir Alfred Pucey, the specialist in hysterical afflictions.’

‘I am not hysterical,’ Virginia said, her voice regaining some of its former firmness. She stood up, letting the rubies slide from her lap onto a velvet cushion. ‘And I am not unwell. It is the jewels. They… they remember.’

Before Cecil could reply, the door was flung open and Mr. Otis was announced. He had arrived on the afternoon train, having received a frantic telegram from his son-in-law. He strode into the room, a whirlwind of American practicality and paternal concern.

‘Virginia, my dear girl! Cecil tells me you’re feeling a little under the weather,’ he boomed, taking in the scene with a sharp, assessing glance. ‘Now, what’s all this? The Duchess, a pleasure as always. I see you’re admiring the family heirlooms. Fine stones, very fine, though I dare say Tiffany could have given them a better setting.’

The Duchess drew herself up to her full, formidable height. ‘Mr. Otis,’ she said, in a voice cold enough to freeze mercury, ‘your daughter is in the grip of a morbid fantasy, a direct result of her association with your haunted house. I am recommending a course of treatment with Sir Alfred Pucey.’

‘Pucey?’ said Mr. Otis. ‘Never heard of him. If she needs a doctor, we’ll telegraph for the best man in Boston. But I suspect this isn’t a matter for pills and potions. This is a Canterville matter, and Otis family business is best handled by the Otis family.’ He turned to his daughter, his expression softening. ‘Tell me what’s wrong, Ginny.’

And so, Virginia told him. She spoke of the encroaching coldness, the sorrow that was not her own, the terrible dreams of a man in a velvet doublet, his hands stained with blood, his face a mask of rage and regret. She spoke of the jewels, and how they seemed to whisper to her of a crime that could not be forgotten and a penance that had not been fully paid.

Mr. Otis listened with a gravity that none of the Cheshires had ever seen in him before. He did not dismiss her fears or patronise her concerns. When she had finished, there was a long silence, broken only by the ticking of a French clock on the mantelpiece.

‘I see,’ he said at last. ‘It would appear that Sir Simon’s affairs were not as tidily concluded as we had believed. The Ghost is gone, but the ghost of the crime remains.’ He looked at the glittering rubies on the cushion. ‘These are not just jewels, are they? They’re exhibits in a case that was never closed.’

‘This is preposterous,’ the Duchess declared. ‘You are encouraging her delusion.’

‘Ma’am,’ said Mr. Otis, with a calm authority that silenced her, ‘I have seen a ghost phase through a wall and watched my daughter vanish into thin air for an entire evening. My capacity for believing in the preposterous is, at this point, quite well developed. This is a problem, and problems have solutions. We simply haven’t found the right one yet.’

That evening, a family council was held. Washington arrived, full of theories about psychical residue and the mnemonic properties of crystalline structures. Mrs. Otis came armed with a bottle of Dr. Dobell’s Tincture and the firm belief that what Virginia needed was a long sea voyage. The twins, now gangling youths of seventeen, suggested a scientific approach involving electricity and a large kite, which was quickly vetoed.

It was a melancholy gathering. Virginia sat pale and withdrawn, and Cecil watched her with an expression of helpless misery. The debate raged around her, a mixture of American pragmatism and British exasperation, but no solution presented itself. The jewels lay in their open case on a nearby table, and in the firelight they seemed to watch the proceedings with a cold, malevolent intelligence.

Suddenly, Virginia stood up. A strange light had come into her eyes, the same light of purpose Cecil remembered from the day she had faced the Ghost in the Tapestry Chamber.

‘I know what must be done,’ she said, her voice clear and strong. ‘I have been selfish. I thought that because I had helped him, the story was over. But it was not just his story. It was his wife’s, too. It was Eleanor de Canterville’s.’

Everyone stared at her. The name of Sir Simon’s murdered wife had scarcely been spoken since they had learned the grim history.

‘The jewels were hers,’ Virginia continued, her gaze fixed on the rubies. ‘He killed her, and in his guilt and pride, he kept them. They were passed down, stained with his crime. When he was laid to rest, his soul found peace, but the memory of the wrong he did is still trapped in them. The prophecy said that prayer must be won from the lips of sin. I prayed for Sir Simon, the sinner. But no one ever prayed for his victim.’

She walked to the table and picked up the heavy necklace of rubies and diamonds. They did not feel cold to her now. They just felt heavy, weighted with an ancient grief.

‘These do not belong to the Cheshires, or to the Cantervilles,’ she said softly. ‘They do not belong to anyone. They belong to a memory. And the only way to free them, and to free me, is to give them back.’

‘Give them back?’ Cecil exclaimed. ‘But how? To whom?’

‘To the earth,’ Virginia replied. ‘Where she lies. We must go to Canterville.’

The journey back to the Chase was a strange and solemn pilgrimage. The entire Otis clan came, as did Cecil and, to everyone’s astonishment, the Dowager Duchess, who announced that if the family was determined to engage in this theatrical nonsense, it was her duty to see that it was done with a modicum of dignity.

Canterville Chase was quiet and still. The air no longer held a chill of supernatural dread, but was filled instead with the ordinary, gentle melancholy of a house that had stood empty for too long. Mrs. Umney, the old housekeeper, greeted them with tears in her eyes, overjoyed to see ‘her’ little Virginia again, though she looked at the Duchess with considerable trepidation.

Virginia led them not into the house, but through the overgrown gardens towards the churchyard. She walked with a confident step, as if guided by an unseen hand. She passed the yew-tree where Sir Simon’s bones were buried and went to the far side of the little church, to a section of the yard that was neglected and forgotten. There, half-hidden by ivy and weeds, was a single, ancient slab of stone, its inscription almost entirely erased by centuries of wind and rain.

‘This is it,’ Virginia said, her voice barely a whisper. ‘There is no name left, but this is her grave.’

The family gathered around in a silent circle. The sun was beginning to set, casting long, dramatic shadows across the grass. Mr. Otis took off his hat. The twins, for once, were utterly subdued, their usual high spirits quenched by the solemnity of the moment.

Virginia knelt on the damp earth before the stone. She held the casket of jewels in her hands. It was not the large casket of all the Canterville gems, but the smaller, ornate box that held the famous ruby necklace. She opened the lid. The stones blazed with a final, defiant glory in the fading light.

‘Eleanor de Canterville,’ Virginia said, her voice ringing out in the stillness. ‘We have not forgotten you. He for whom you suffered is at peace. And we have come to ask your forgiveness for him, and to return what was always yours.’

She took a breath, and her eyes filled with tears, not of sorrow for herself, but of pity for the woman who had lain there, forgotten, for so long. They were tears freely given, just as she had wept for Sir Simon. ‘Weep for my sins, because I have no tears,’ the Ghost had told her. Now, she wept for the sins committed against another.

Then, she did something that shocked them all. She did not bury the casket. Instead, she took out the magnificent necklace and, with a firm and steady hand, she began to break it apart. She twisted the golden links, her small fingers surprisingly strong, and one by one, she prised the gems from their settings. The great rubies, the priceless diamonds. She took the largest ruby, the one they called ‘The Heart of Canterville,’ and laid it gently on the stone slab. Then she took another, and another, arranging them in a simple, shining cross upon the weathered stone.

A gasp went through the assembled group. The Dowager Duchess looked as though she might faint. To see a fortune, a legendary piece of history, dismantled and offered up to an unmarked grave was an act of such profound, such American, disregard for convention that it bordered on madness.

When Virginia had finished, the ancient grave slab was adorned with a glittering cross of red and white fire. She bowed her head for a moment in silent prayer, and as she did so, a faint breeze rustled through the leaves of the old yew-tree, sounding for all the world like a gentle sigh of release.

She stood up, and turned to face her husband. The shadows were gone from her eyes. The paleness was gone from her cheeks. She was Virginia again, radiant and alive, and the smile she gave him was filled with all the love and light he had feared he had lost forever.

Cecil rushed to her, taking her in his arms. ‘It is over,’ he murmured into her hair. ‘You are back.’

‘Yes,’ she said, her voice full of peace. ‘It is over. To forget time, to forgive life… it is a lesson for the living, too.’

The Dowager Duchess stared at the grave, at the sacrifice of jewels glittering in the twilight. She looked at her grandson holding the American girl as if she were the most precious thing in the world. She looked at Mr. Otis, who was watching his daughter with an expression of immense pride. Something within the old woman, a wall of cynical, aristocratic stone built up over a lifetime, finally crumbled.

‘Well,’ she said, her voice raspy with an emotion she would never have admitted to. ‘I must concede, it was a gesture of… significant style.’ She turned to Mr. Otis. ‘You have a most unusual daughter, sir.’

‘I know it,’ said Mr. Otis, with a slow smile. ‘She is a true pioneer. She has a way of settling old accounts.’

Peace, a deeper and more lasting peace, had finally come to all who were touched by the legacy of Canterville. The remaining jewels were eventually given to a museum, as Virginia declared she had no more use for them. The world, which had been so charmed by the story of the girl who tamed a ghost, never learned the full tale of the jewels that grieved. But Virginia knew. And as she and her husband left the quiet churchyard, hand in hand, she knew with a certainty that went deeper than words that some treasures are too heavy to be worn, and that the greatest act of possession is, sometimes, to let go. For she had learned again what Life is, and what Death signifies, and why Love, in its infinite capacity for forgiveness, is indeed stronger than both.

When they rose from the table, it was not to the usual clatter of boyish enthusiasm and parental sighs, but to a heavy, shared silence, a silence more profound than any the Ghost had managed to conjure in three centuries of professional haunting. The twins, Washington and Star, who had once found such sport in the spectral torments of Sir Simon, now moved about the great house with a reverence that was entirely new to them; they peered into shadowy corners not in search of a prank, but as if expecting to find some lingering trace of a sorrow they were only now beginning to comprehend. Mrs. Otis, having been coaxed into taking a small glass of sherry, had retired to her room with a bottle of Dr. Dobell’s Tincture, her nerves, as she declared, being quite shattered by this final, dramatic chapter in their English adventure.

It was Mr. Otis who finally broke the quiet, clapping the little Duke on the back with a heartiness that was perhaps a trifle too loud for the occasion. ‘Well, Cecil,’ he said, his voice echoing in the cavernous hall, ‘it seems our residency at Canterville Chase has come to a satisfactory conclusion. The property is now free of all supernatural encumbrances, which I am sure will have a positive effect on its eventual resale value.’

The little Duke, who was gazing adoringly at Virginia as she stood by the great hearth, started at the remark. ‘Resale, Mr. Otis? But surely, you do not intend to leave?’

‘Of course we must leave,’ said Mr. Otis practically. ‘The lease is nearly up, and my business requires me in New York. Besides,’ he added, with a twinkle in his eye, ‘I believe my daughter has another English residence to attend to now.’

A blush crept up Virginia’s neck, a lovely sight that made the Duke’s heart beat a rather frantic tattoo against his ribs. The past few hours had been a maelstrom of terror, despair, and finally, a miraculous and solemn peace. He had thought he had lost her first to gypsies, then to some unknown and dreadful fate, and for a moment, in the Tapestry Chamber, he had feared he had lost her to a ghost. To see her now, safe and whole, made him feel as if he himself had been given a reprieve from some terrible sentence.

‘Indeed, sir,’ he said, taking Virginia’s hand, which was no longer cold but warm and alive in his. ‘Cheshire House awaits its Duchess.’

And so it was settled. The packing began the very next day, a whirlwind of American efficiency that left the long suffering servants of Canterville Chase in a state of perpetual bewilderment. Trunks were filled, crates were nailed shut, and the Stars and Stripes, which had flown so defiantly from the battlements, was lowered for the last time. Virginia’s final act before departing was to visit the small churchyard once more. She laid a simple wreath of white roses upon the unmarked slab where the almond tree, now miraculously covered in delicate blossoms, cast its gentle shade. She did not weep this time, for her tears had already been given away, and in their place was a quiet understanding that filled her with a strange and mature grace.

Their arrival in London was an affair of considerable notice. The papers had been full of the strange disappearance and reappearance of the American heiress at Canterville Chase, and though the full story of the ghost was known only to the family, the incident had lent Virginia an aura of mystery and romance that the London season found utterly irresistible. She was not merely a wealthy American, of which there were a good many; she was the girl who had vanished into thin air and returned with a look in her eyes that spoke of secrets the living were not meant to know.

Cheshire House, a vast and imposing mansion on Belgrave Square, was a world away from the charming, haunted disrepair of Canterville. Here, the ghosts were of a different sort entirely. They were the ghosts of etiquette, of protocol, of a thousand unspoken rules that governed the lives of the English aristocracy. They lurked in the stiff posture of the butler, in the precise arrangement of the forks at dinner, and in the icy smiles of the ladies who came to call.

‘It is a different kind of darkness,’ Virginia confessed to Cecil one afternoon, as they sat in a drawing room so grand and perfect that she was afraid to touch anything. ‘At Canterville, the shadows were old and sad. Here, they are… polite.’

Cecil laughed and kissed her forehead. ‘They are, my love, and you are the sunshine that will drive them away. You must not be afraid of them.’

‘I am not afraid,’ Virginia replied, a familiar firmness in her voice. ‘But I do not understand them. Yesterday, Lady Atherton spent a full twenty minutes telling me about the lamentable quality of this year’s strawberries, as if the fate of the Empire depended upon it. I told her about the new fruit canning process being developed in California, and she looked at me as if I had suggested she should dance a jig on the dinner table.’

‘Well, you practically did, my darling,’ said Cecil, trying and failing to suppress a smile. ‘One does not speak of… processes… in a drawing room. One alludes, one hints, one suffers in style. It is the English way.’

Virginia’s principal challenge came in the formidable shape of the Dowager Duchess, Cecil’s grandmother, a woman whose opinions were as rigidly corseted as her figure. Having been grudgingly impressed by Virginia’s final act at Canterville, she had now taken it upon herself to mould the girl into a proper Duchess, a task she approached with the grim determination of a sculptor facing a particularly stubborn block of granite.

‘My dear Virginia,’ she would say, her lorgnette aimed like a weapon, ‘a Duchess does not “help” a footman who has dropped a tray of glasses. She expresses her regal displeasure at the noise and allows the housekeeper to deal with the subsequent dismissal. It is what maintains order.’

Or, on another occasion, ‘No, no, child. When one encounters a bore at a garden party, one does not tell him that his conversation is as dull as a rainy Tuesday. One murmurs something about a sudden headache and glides away. It is a question of aesthetics.’

Virginia listened to all this with a patient sweetness that both exasperated and intrigued the old woman. She tried, for Cecil’s sake, to learn the intricate dance of London society, but her nature, so honest and direct, was ill suited to the task. Her compassion, which had been the key to saving Sir Simon’s soul, was seen as a strange and rather embarrassing weakness in a world that valued appearances above all else. She was forever asking the servants about their families, complimenting rivals on their gowns with a sincerity that was deeply unsettling, and speaking her mind on subjects that ladies were supposed to know nothing about.

The whispers began, as they always do, over tea cups and behind fans. They called her the ‘Ghost Duchess,’ and it was not always meant kindly. The story of Canterville became distorted in the telling, twisted into a narrative of American hysteria and morbid obsessions. The more charitable suggested she was merely eccentric; the less charitable, led by a certain Lady Wexcombe, a woman with the face of a pug and the disposition of a hornet, hinted that the new Duchess was not entirely stable.

‘They say she communed with spirits,’ Lady Wexcombe would murmur, to a captivated audience at some glittering ball. ‘That the poor Duke, in his infatuation, has married a girl whose mind is perpetually wandering in the graveyard. So dreadfully morbid. So… American.’

Virginia was not deaf to these whispers. She felt the chill in the air when she entered a room, saw the quickly averted glances, the sly smiles. She felt more alone in the crowded ballrooms of London than she ever had in the empty, echoing halls of Canterville. She was in a terrible dream, once again, but this one was peopled with smiling assassins and suffocating perfumes. She saw Cecil’s worried face, his constant efforts to shield her, and she felt a pang of guilt that she was the cause of his anxiety.

One evening, after a particularly trying dinner party during which Virginia had engaged the French Ambassador in a spirited debate about penal reform, a topic which caused the poor man to spill wine on his starched white shirt, she retreated to her rooms in a state of quiet despair. She sat by the window, looking out at the gaslit street, and for the first time since leaving Canterville, she felt the sting of tears in her eyes. The prophecy on the library window came back to her: *When a golden girl can win Prayer from out the lips of sin…* She had wept for Sir Simon’s sins, but who would weep for hers? For the sin of being too earnest, too honest, too alive for this pale and powdered world?

There was a soft knock at the door, and Cecil entered. He did not speak, but simply came and sat beside her, taking her hand.

‘They do not like me,’ she said softly, not a question but a statement of fact.

‘They do not know you,’ he corrected gently. ‘They see a Duchess, and they expect a certain… performance. They do not see Virginia.’

‘But I am the Duchess now,’ she whispered. ‘Perhaps I should try harder to be the person they expect.’

‘Never,’ he said, with a sudden fierceness that surprised her. ‘I did not fall in love with a performance. I fell in love with the girl who was not afraid of a ghost, the girl who saw a lonely old man where everyone else saw a monster. If London society cannot appreciate that, then it is London society that is at fault, not you.’ He lifted her hand and kissed it. ‘I would rather be exiled to a desert island with the real Virginia than preside over all of England with a Duchess who is merely a copy of all the others.’

His words were a balm to her wounded spirit. She leaned her head on his shoulder, and the tears that fell were not of sorrow, but of gratitude. In his unwavering love, she found a strength that the whispers of the London season could not touch.

The turning point came, as such things often do, at the social event of the year: the Marchioness of Dumbleton’s annual costume ball. It was a crush of epic proportions, a glittering, sweltering sea of Dukes disguised as pirates, Countesses as milkmaids, and politicians as Roman senators. The Dowager Duchess, declaring the entire affair to be a vulgar pantomime, had refused to attend, but had insisted that Virginia and Cecil go to represent the family.

‘You will go as… something simple, Virginia,’ she had advised. ‘Marie Antoinette, perhaps. It requires very little acting.’

But Virginia had another idea. When she descended the grand staircase of Cheshire House that evening, Cecil, who was dressed rather uncomfortably as Sir Walter Raleigh, simply stared. Virginia was not Marie Antoinette. She was not a milkmaid or a goddess. She was dressed in a simple gown of shimmering silver, the colour of moonlight. Her golden hair was unadorned, save for a single, withered almond blossom she had preserved from the tree at Canterville.

‘Virginia,’ Cecil breathed. ‘Who are you supposed to be?’

‘I am the Angel of Death,’ she said, with a small, secret smile. ‘A friend of mine described her to me once. He said she was very beautiful.’

There was a strange light in her eyes, the same light Cecil had seen when she had stood up to the Ghost. ‘You are magnificent,’ he said, and he meant it.

At the ball, her costume was a source of much confusion. It was too simple, too severe, too… strange. It lacked the theatricality of the other outfits, and yet, it drew every eye. And then, Lady Wexcombe, dressed as a particularly gaudy Queen Elizabeth I, complete with a red wig that sat slightly askew, saw her chance.

Surrounded by her coterie of sycophants, she approached Virginia, her voice carrying across a momentary lull in the music. ‘My dear Duchess,’ she boomed, ‘what an… interesting choice of attire. Are you meant to be a ghost? I hear you have some experience in that area.’

A ripple of cruel laughter went through her group. The circle widened as others stopped to watch the unfolding drama. Cecil stepped forward, his face a mask of thunder, but Virginia put a gentle hand on his arm, stopping him. She looked at Lady Wexcombe, and there was no fear or anger in her eyes, only a deep and profound pity.

‘No, Lady Wexcombe,’ she said, her clear, quiet voice cutting through the noise of the ballroom. ‘I am not a ghost. I am a friend to them. I have learned that ghosts are not always the ones who rattle chains and moan in corridors.’

She took a step closer, and the crowd held its breath. ‘Some ghosts,’ she continued, her voice gaining strength, ‘are the echoes of unkind words. They are the shadows of old griefs we refuse to forgive. They are the spectres of loneliness that haunt even the most crowded of rooms, feeding on whispers and suspicion. Those are the most terrible ghosts of all, for they seek to steal the joy from the living.’

She paused, and her gaze swept over the stunned faces in the crowd before returning to Lady Wexcombe, who seemed, for the first time in her life, to be at a loss for words.

‘The friend I knew,’ Virginia said, her voice softening, ‘was trapped for three hundred years by his own sins, because he had no tears and no faith. He needed someone to weep for him, and to pray for him. I think perhaps these other ghosts, the ghosts of the drawing room, are much the same. They are trapped by their own bitterness. And I think they too must be forgiven, for it is a sad and lonely thing to have a heart that has forgotten how to be kind.’

She touched the withered blossom in her hair. ‘He told me that Death must be so beautiful. To have no yesterday, and no to-morrow. To forget time, to forgive life, to be at peace. It is a lesson for all of us, I think. Not just for ghosts.’

A profound silence fell over the ballroom. In that moment, Virginia was not an American heiress or a strange, eccentric Duchess. She was a figure of luminous grace and wisdom. She had not scolded or accused; she had simply spoken a truth so deep and simple that it disarmed all malice.

Lady Wexcombe’s face was a mottled purple. She opened her mouth, but no sound came out. She had been prepared for an argument, for tears, for anger. She had not been prepared for a sermon on forgiveness from a girl dressed as the Angel of Death. Without another word, she turned and pushed her way through the crowd, her red wig now noticeably tilted over one eye.

It was the little Duke who moved first. He strode to his wife’s side, took her hand, and raised it to his lips in full view of the entire London society. ‘My Duchess,’ he said, his voice ringing with a pride so immense it brought tears to her own eyes. And then, a wonderful thing happened. Someone began to clap. It was an old, gout-ridden Earl who had been on the verge of falling asleep, but he was clapping with genuine enthusiasm. Then another joined in, and another, until the entire ballroom, save for a few of Lady Wexcombe’s most loyal acolytes, erupted in a spontaneous and heartfelt ovation.

They had come expecting to mock the Ghost Duchess, but they had been humbled by her purity. For against the simple goodness of a loving heart, the powers of polite hell could not prevail.

As she and Cecil left the ball, hand in hand, she knew she had not conquered London, and had no wish to. She had simply made a small space in it where she could be herself. The peace of Canterville was no longer confined to a lonely grave in a country churchyard; she had brought it with her. She had learned that to forgive life was not a single act, but a daily practice, and that Love, which had been strong enough to open the portals of Death’s house, was more than strong enough to quiet the whispers of a London ballroom.

For the first time, the house itself seemed to be at peace, as if the very timbers and stones, long saturated with an ancient sorrow, had finally exhaled a sigh of relief. The wind that moaned in the corridors now sounded less like a tormented soul and more like the gentle breathing of a slumbering giant. Even the bloodstain in the library, which had so vexed Mrs. Otis and amused the twins, seemed to have faded to a dull, unassuming brown, as if ashamed of its former theatricality.

The party returned to London a week later, not to the fashionable hotel they had first occupied, but to a grand, if somewhat gloomy, town house in Belgrave Square which the Duke had inherited along with his title and a great many debts. It was here that Virginia, now the Duchess of Cheshire, began her new life, a life that proved to be quite as strange and challenging as her brief but memorable acquaintance with Sir Simon de Canterville.

London society, having been momentarily stunned by her performance at Lady Farnsworth’s ball, quickly recovered its natural state of malicious curiosity. The story of the ‘Ghost Duchess’ and her bizarre sermon on forgiveness became the chief topic of conversation at every afternoon tea and evening soiree. Opinions were, as is so often the case in high society, sharply divided. A certain set, composed mostly of romantic young ladies and sentimental old gentlemen, declared her to be a saint, a creature of exquisite purity and profound spiritual insight. Others, led by the still-fuming Lady Wexcombe, pronounced her to be a dangerously eccentric and possibly mad American, whose theatrical displays were an affront to the hallowed dignity of the British aristocracy.

‘To speak of Death at a ball!’ Lady Wexcombe would exclaim, fanning herself vigorously, to a nodding chorus of her companions. ‘It is not merely in poor taste; it is positively morbid. And all this talk of forgiveness for a ghost! The girl should be in a sanatorium, not in the House of Lords.’

Virginia was, for the most part, blissfully unaware of the tempest she had caused. She spent her days trying to bring a little of her own American sunshine into the cavernous, shadowy rooms of Cheshire House. She filled the vases with fresh flowers, much to the astonishment of the housekeeper, who was accustomed to letting them stand empty from one season to the next. She insisted on opening the heavy velvet curtains to let in the light, and she even persuaded Cecil to have a new invention called a ‘telephone’ installed, an act which the butler regarded as a direct insult to the time-honoured practice of sending messages by footman.

Cecil, for his part, was utterly enchanted by his wife. He had always loved her, but now he regarded her with a kind of holy awe. He saw how the world, with its petty cruelties and cynical whispers, tried to tarnish her, and how she remained, through it all, as pure and untroubled as a star. He took to calling her his ‘golden girl,’ a reference to the old prophecy which had once seemed so mysterious and was now the cornerstone of their shared history.

One afternoon, about a month after their return to London, a visitor was announced. It was the Dowager Duchess of Cheshire, Cecil’s mother, a woman renowned throughout England for her imperious manner and her magnificent collection of diamonds. She was a tall, formidable lady with a Roman nose and an expression of perpetual disapproval, as if she had just detected a bad smell. She had been wintering in Menton for her health, or so she claimed, and had not attended the wedding, an omission for which Virginia was privately rather grateful.

‘Cecil, my dear boy,’ she said, presenting a powdered cheek for her son to kiss. ‘And this, I presume, is Virginia. You must forgive me for not calling sooner, but the channel crossing was simply dreadful, and my nerves have been in a state of utter collapse.’ She appraised Virginia from head to toe, her cold grey eyes missing nothing, from the simplicity of her dress to the American frankness of her gaze.

‘We are so pleased you have come, Your Grace,’ Virginia said, with a quiet courtesy that seemed to disarm the Dowager, if only for a moment.

‘I have been hearing the most extraordinary stories about you, my dear,’ the Dowager continued, settling herself into a stiff-backed armchair. ‘They are saying in London that you converse with spirits and deliver sermons at balls. I trust these are merely the exaggerations of a jealous society.’

‘Virginia was wonderful,’ Cecil said hotly, stepping forward. ‘She silenced the most awful woman, Lady Wexcombe, and everyone applauded her. I have never been prouder.’

‘Applause,’ the Dowager said, her voice dripping with ice, ‘is for the theatre, Cecil. The aristocracy does not seek applause; it commands respect. And respect is not earned by making a spectacle of oneself.’ She turned her gaze back to Virginia. ‘You must understand, child, that you are a Duchess now. The name of Cheshire is one of the oldest and most respected in the kingdom. It does not lend itself to… American novelty.’

Virginia felt a blush creep into her cheeks, but her voice was steady when she replied. ‘I only spoke of what I believe to be true, Your Grace. That it is better to forgive than to condemn.’

The Dowager Duchess raised a perfectly sculpted eyebrow. ‘A noble sentiment, to be sure, but one better suited to the pulpit than the ballroom. The world, my dear Virginia, is not a church. It is a battlefield, and our family is presently losing the war.’ A shadow passed over her severe features. ‘Cecil, I must speak with you. Alone.’

Cecil looked at Virginia, his face a mixture of apology and distress, and led his mother into the adjoining library. Virginia remained in her chair, her hands clasped in her lap, feeling for the first time since her marriage a chill that had nothing to do with the English climate. She could hear their voices through the thick oak door, the Dowager’s low and insistent, Cecil’s rising in disbelief and despair.

After what seemed an eternity, the door opened and Cecil emerged, his face pale and drawn. His mother followed, her expression more severe than ever.

‘I am awfully sorry, my love,’ he began, his voice trembling slightly. ‘I should have told you before. I did not wish to burden you.’

‘Told me what, Cecil?’ Virginia asked, her heart sinking.

It was the Dowager who answered. ‘My son, in his romantic folly, has neglected to inform you of our circumstances. To put it plainly, we are ruined. Utterly and completely ruined.’

Virginia stared at her, uncomprehending. ‘Ruined? But this house… the Chase… you are the Duke and Duchess of Cheshire.’

‘Titles do not pay bills, child,’ the Dowager said sharply. ‘My late husband, the Duke, was a man of great taste and even greater appetites. He gambled away a fortune. My son has inherited a mountain of debt so vast that it threatens to swallow us whole. The Chase is mortgaged to the hilt. This house is leased. The Cheshire diamonds, the ones I am not presently wearing, are paste copies lying in a bank vault. The originals were sold years ago.’

Cecil sank onto a sofa and buried his face in his hands. ‘We were hoping I might marry an heiress,’ he mumbled, his voice thick with shame. ‘It is what everyone does. And then I met you, and I fell in love, and I could not bring myself to treat you like a… a business transaction. I thought I could find some other way. But there is no other way.’

‘The creditors are closing in,’ the Dowager said, her voice flat and devoid of emotion. ‘We have until the end of the year. Then, the name of Cheshire, which has stood for seven hundred years, will be dragged through the mud of the bankruptcy courts. That, my dear Virginia, is the reality that awaits you. Your little sermons on forgiveness will not pay the butcher, I assure you.’

A terrible silence filled the room, broken only by Cecil’s muffled sobs. Virginia felt as if the floor had fallen away from beneath her. She looked at the handsome young man she had married, now broken with shame, and at the proud, cold woman who was watching her with an almost cruel intensity, as if testing her. For a moment, she was back in the Tapestry Chamber at Canterville, a cold wind sweeping around her, a great black cavern opening before her. This, too, was a fearful shape in the darkness.

Then she thought of Sir Simon. She thought of his long, lonely vigil, trapped by sins far older and deeper than gambling debts. She remembered his yearning for peace, for the quiet of the soft brown earth. ‘To forgive life,’ he had said. And what was this, if not life in all its brutal, messy reality?

Slowly, she rose to her feet. ‘You are wrong, Your Grace,’ she said, her voice so quiet that both Cecil and his mother looked up in surprise. ‘I am an heiress. My father is not a man of title, but he is a man of considerable means.’

The Dowager gave a short, humourless laugh. ‘And you think your father, the American minister, will simply hand over his fortune to pay the debts of a decadent British aristocracy? He seems a most sensible man. I cannot imagine he would approve of such a thing.’

‘No,’ Virginia agreed. ‘He would not. But I am not my father.’

She walked over to the small writing desk in the corner of the room and took out a pen and a sheet of paper. Her hand did not tremble as she wrote. She addressed the note to Mr. Hiram B. Otis, c/o The United States Embassy, London.

It was very brief. It said: ‘Dearest Papa, I find myself in a position to acquire a great deal of history at a very reasonable price. Please come to Belgrave Square at your earliest convenience. We must discuss the salvation of the House of Cheshire. Your loving daughter, Virginia.’

She sealed the envelope and handed it to the butler, who had been hovering discreetly in the hall. ‘Please have this delivered at once.’

Then she turned back to her husband and her mother in law. ‘My father is a businessman,’ she said, a strange light in her eyes, the same light that had been there when she had stood up to the ghost. ‘He does not believe in charity for the undeserving. But he does believe in a sound investment. And I believe the Duke of Cheshire is a very sound investment indeed.’

The Dowager Duchess stared at her, for the first time in her life utterly speechless. She had expected tears, hysterics, recriminations. She had not expected a business proposal.

Mr. Otis arrived within the hour, looking a little bewildered but thoroughly practical. He listened patiently as the situation was explained to him, first by a shamefaced Cecil and then by a surprisingly blunt Dowager, who had decided that if dignity was to be lost, it might as well be lost efficiently.

When they had finished, Mr. Otis pursed his lips and tapped his fingers on the arm of his chair. ‘So let me get this straight,’ he said, in his clear, nasal voice. ‘The late Duke spent all the money, and you two have been trying to keep up appearances by borrowing more money, which you now cannot pay back. Is that the long and short of it?’

‘That is the indelicate summary of our position, yes,’ said the Dowager, stiffly.

‘Hmph,’ said Mr. Otis. ‘In my country, we call that a poorly managed enterprise. However,’ he continued, a twinkle appearing in his eye as he looked at his daughter, ‘Virginia here seems to think it’s an investment opportunity. Now, I am not in the habit of throwing good money after bad, particularly not after a family that seems to have the financial sense of a flock of peacocks. No offense intended.’

‘None taken,’ the Dowager said, through gritted teeth. ‘I believe I used the term ‘ruined’ myself.’

‘But,’ Mr. Otis went on, ‘I am in the habit of supporting my daughter. And my daughter is now the Duchess of Cheshire. That makes the House of Cheshire a family concern. And the Otises do not allow their family concerns to fail.’ He stood up and began to pace the room. ‘I will not simply pay off your debts. That would be foolish. But I will establish a trust, managed by my own lawyers. The trust will settle the debts and provide an operating budget for the estates. In return, I will take a controlling interest in the management of all Cheshire properties. We will run the farms on a profitable basis. We will lease the Scottish hunting lodge. We might even, if the public shows an interest, open Canterville Chase for guided tours on Tuesdays and Thursdays. Two shillings a head, children half price.’

The Dowager looked as if she were about to faint. ‘Guided tours?’ she whispered, horrified. ‘Of Canterville?’

‘It’s a house with a genuine, authenticated ghost, ma’am,’ said Mr. Otis cheerfully. ‘Or at least, it was. That’s a prime attraction. We could sell little souvenir skeleton bones. The twins would love that.’

It was at this moment that Virginia intervened. ‘No, Papa,’ she said firmly. ‘No tours. No souvenir bones. The peace of Canterville was bought at a great price, and it is not for sale.’

Mr. Otis looked at his daughter, and his expression softened. ‘Alright, Ginny. No tours. But the rest of the deal stands. We run it like a business, or there’s no deal at all.’

‘I think,’ said Virginia, turning to the Dowager, ‘that it is a very fair offer. It is a chance not merely to survive, but to build something new. A partnership, between the old world and the new.’

The Dowager Duchess looked from the relentlessly practical American, to her own despairing son, and finally to her strange, quiet daughter in law. She saw the strength in Virginia’s face, a strength that had nothing to do with money or titles. It was the strength of a loving heart that had faced down the powers of Hell in a dusty chamber and was not about to be intimidated by a few London creditors.

For a long, tense moment, she was silent. Then, with an almost imperceptible nod, she gave her assent. ‘Very well, Mr. Otis,’ she said, her voice barely a whisper. ‘You may save us.’

That night, as Virginia lay in bed, Cecil took her hand. His fingers were no longer as cold as ice, but warm and full of life. ‘You saved me,’ he said. ‘Just as you saved Sir Simon.’

‘No, Cecil,’ she replied softly, hiding her face against his shoulder. ‘It was not me. It was Love. Papa loves me, and I love you. The prophecy was not just for the ghost, you see. It is for all of us. Love is always with us, and Love is stronger than Death is. And,’ she added, a small smile playing on her lips, ‘it appears to be stronger than bad financial management, too.’

Cecil laughed, a real, heartfelt laugh for the first time that day. As he held his golden girl in his arms, he knew, with a certainty that filled his entire being, that the old prophecy had been fulfilled in more ways than one. Peace had come not only to Canterville, but to the troubled House of Cheshire as well. And it had been brought by a little American girl who knew the true value of a tear, a prayer, and a sound business plan.

When they had all finally retired, and a profound and weary silence fell over the great house, Mr. Otis, who never allowed sentiment to interfere with a good night’s sleep, was snoring soundly in a matter of moments. Mrs. Otis, however, lay awake for a long while, her mind a turbulent sea of gypsies and ghosts and the terrifying thought of what might have been. She finally drifted into an uneasy slumber, her dreams haunted by the image of her daughter being led away by a spectre whose lips burned like fire. It was a most unsettling prospect for a mother from New York.

The days that followed were a whirlwind of activity, for Mr. Otis was not a man to let grass grow under his feet, especially when that grass was part of an estate he was now financially responsible for. He arrived at Canterville Chase a week after the wedding, not with congratulatory bouquets, but with three large leather-bound ledgers and a sharp-pencilled clerk from his London office. The Dowager Duchess, who had come to regard her new American relations with a sort of bewildered fondness, found her affection severely tested.

‘My dear Mr. Otis,’ she said, fanning herself weakly as he spread vast architectural plans across the library table, ‘what, may I ask, is the meaning of this intrusion? Cecil and Virginia have only just returned from their honeymoon. Surely the estate’s accounts can wait.’

‘Ma’am,’ replied Mr. Otis, tapping a long column of figures with his pen, ‘time is money, and from the look of these books, the House of Cheshire has been wasting both for the better part of two centuries. We have, for instance, a Hereditary Grand Butler, a post which has been vacant since 1783, yet to which we are still paying a stipend, sent quarterly to a very surprised tobacconist in Bristol who happens to be the man’s great-great-grandnephew.’

The Dowager looked as though she might swoon. ‘The stipend of the Grand Butler is a matter of honour, Mr. Otis!’

‘It is a matter of three hundred pounds a year, ma’am, and the honour is all on the tobacconist’s side,’ he returned briskly. ‘And then there is the matter of the east wing. According to this survey, its foundations are perilously close to collapse. I propose we shore them up and convert the rooms into a modern dairy. There is a fortune to be made in Cheshire cheese, if properly marketed.’

The Dowager clutched her heart. ‘A dairy? In the east wing? But that is where King James I slept! We cannot have cows where a monarch has slumbered!’

‘If King James were alive today, he’d be the first to invest, I assure you,’ said Mr. Otis with unassailable confidence. ‘A king who can’t appreciate a good return is no king at all.’

And so it went on. A melancholy cavalcade of dismissed footmen, under-gardeners, and keepers of non-existent hounds made its way down the avenue, each clutching a generous severance package from Mr. Otis that left them more confused than aggrieved. The carp-pond was drained, not for any sign of Virginia, but to be restocked with a more commercially viable species of trout. Washington Otis was put in charge of what Mr. Otis termed ‘agricultural logistics,’ a role he accepted with the grave seriousness of a young man who has found his purpose, even if that purpose involved manure.

The twins, now entering that awkward age between childhood mischief and adolescent invention, were a problem of a different sort. Denied the simple pleasure of tormenting a phantom, they had turned their formidable energies to science. Their rooms at the top of the house became a terrifying laboratory of wires, batteries, and evil-smelling chemical concoctions. They had nearly electrocuted the new butler, a man hired for his efficiency rather than his lineage, and had caused a small but memorable explosion in the gun-room while attempting to create a ‘horseless firework.’

Virginia, now the Duchess of Cheshire, found herself in the delicate position of being a bridge between two worlds that seemed determined to misunderstand each other. She would spend her mornings with Cecil, walking through the grounds and listening to his plans for reintroducing native species of wildflowers to the meadows, and her afternoons with her father, nodding patiently as he explained the virtues of crop rotation and cost-benefit analysis. In the evenings, she would sit with the Dowager, listening to long, sad stories about the glories of the past, and assuring her that, no, Papa did not intend to replace the family portraits with advertisements for soap.

‘He is a good man, your father,’ the Dowager would sigh, dabbing her eyes with a lace handkerchief, ‘but he has no soul for history, Virginia. He sees a priceless tapestry and wonders about its thread count.’

‘He sees a leaking roof and wishes to fix it, so the tapestry does not get ruined,’ Virginia would reply gently. ‘It is a different kind of care, but it is care all the same.’

It was Cecil who suggested they take a house in London for the Season. ‘It would do us all good,’ he said, looking at his mother’s pale face and his wife’s weary one. ‘And besides, it is expected of us. You must be presented at Court, my dear, and we must show society that the House of Cheshire is not, as they are all no doubt whispering, entirely defunct.’

Mr. Otis thought it a capital idea. ‘A London presence is excellent for business,’ he declared, immediately calculating the potential for influential dinner parties. And so, a grand house was taken in Belgrave Square, and the family, including a protesting Mrs. Otis who was certain the London fog would be the death of her, was transplanted from the country to the city.

It was there that Virginia encountered a new kind of foe, one far more subtle and, in its own way, more terrifying than a chain-rattling ghost. This was the London society, an entity ruled by a cabal of hawk-eyed dowagers and acid-tongued wits, for whom a new, rich, and particularly an American, duchess was an object of intense and merciless scrutiny. The undisputed queen of this realm was the Marchioness of Dumbleton, a woman whose family tree was as ancient as her diamonds and whose heart was a good deal harder.

They met at a crushingly tedious ball given by the Foreign Secretary. The Marchioness surveyed Virginia from head to toe, her lorgnette poised like a weapon. ‘Ah, the new Duchess of Cheshire,’ she said, her voice carrying across the chatter with piercing clarity. ‘The little American. We heard such stories. Something about a ghost and gypsies? It all sounded dreadfully colonial.’

Virginia felt a blush creep up her neck, but she met the woman’s cold gaze. ‘The ghost was a very old friend of the family, ma’am. He had simply been waiting for a proper introduction.’

A ripple of cruel amusement went through the Marchioness’s circle. ‘How charmingly quaint. And your father, I hear he is in… trade?’ The word was pronounced as if it were a communicable disease.

‘My father is in many things,’ Virginia said, her voice steady. ‘He believes that a man should leave the world a more profitable and well-managed place than he found it.’

‘A truly American philosophy,’ the Marchioness sneered. ‘We in England prefer to leave the world as it is, with its ancient beauties and, of course, its ancient families, intact.’ She turned her shoulder, a gesture of dismissal as absolute as a slammed door, and left Virginia standing alone, the sudden focus of a hundred pitying or malicious eyes.

Cecil, who had been trapped in conversation with a portly ambassador, hurried to her side. ‘Don’t mind her, darling,’ he whispered, his face tight with anger. ‘She is a dreadful old harpy. Her own husband sold a Rembrandt to pay his gambling debts last year.’

‘It isn’t her,’ Virginia said, her eyes shining with unshed tears. ‘It is all of them. They look at me as if I am an oddity in a museum. They whisper about Papa’s money and my accent. They do not see me at all.’

The Season proved to be a trial by fire. Virginia’s quiet nature was mistaken for simplicity, her kindness for a lack of breeding. Her dresses, though from the finest Parisian couturiers, were declared ‘too modern,’ her conversation ‘too earnest.’ She was an alien creature in this world of intricate rules and unspoken snobberies, and for the first time since she had faced the Angel of Death, she felt a tremor of real fear. She was failing. She was failing Cecil, and the ancient name she now carried.

It was Mr. Otis, of all people, who provided the solution, though he was entirely unaware of doing so. ‘The London experiment is a bust,’ he announced one morning at breakfast, scanning a newspaper report that described his daughter as ‘the bucolic Duchess.’ ‘We’re losing the public relations war. We need a new strategy, a home-ground advantage. We will host a grand ball, a fête champêtre, at Canterville Chase itself. We will show them the new Cheshire, restored, vibrant, and solvent.’

The Dowager was appalled. ‘A party? Now? With the paint on the new dairy scarcely dry? It is unseemly!’

‘It is necessary,’ said Mr. Otis. ‘We will invite everyone. The whole of London. Especially,’ he added, with a glint in his eye, ‘that Marchioness of Dumbleton. We will show her what American management and a bit of capital can do for a crumbling old estate.’

And so, preparations began on a scale that Canterville had not seen for a century. Armies of decorators descended upon the house. The gardens were coaxed into a state of impossible perfection. An orchestra was hired from Vienna. The twins were strictly forbidden from any and all scientific pursuits and were promised a trip to America if they behaved, a bribe so magnificent that it rendered them temporarily docile.

Virginia moved through the preparations in a daze of apprehension. The ball felt like a final examination, a last chance to prove her worth. The night of the party arrived, a perfect, balmy evening in late July. The Chase was a blaze of light, lanterns hanging from the ancient oaks and the great house glowing from every window. Carriages streamed up the avenue, disgorging a river of silk, satin, and diamonds into the great hall.

The Marchioness of Dumbleton arrived late, as was her custom, making an entrance designed to disrupt and dominate. She swept in, a vision in purple velvet, her eyes immediately beginning their cold search for flaws. She found Mr. Otis in the hall, beaming like a proud proprietor.

‘A remarkable transformation, Mr. Otis,’ she said, her tone dripping with false sweetness. ‘One would scarcely recognise the old place. So much… light. And is that new paint I smell?’

‘That, ma’am, is the smell of progress,’ Mr. Otis replied cheerfully. ‘And solvency.’

The Marchioness gave a brittle laugh and moved on, her sights set on Virginia, who was standing with Cecil near the entrance to the ballroom, greeting their guests. Virginia wore a gown of simple white silk, with no jewellery save for the string of pearls that had been Cecil’s wedding gift, the very pearls that had once belonged to Sir Simon’s wife. Her golden hair was simply dressed, and her face was pale but composed.

‘My dear Duchess,’ the Marchioness purred, ‘how very brave of you to attempt an entertainment on such a scale. It is so easy to get things wrong. One relies so much on, well, inherited taste.’

Before Virginia could reply, there was a sudden and peculiar flicker in the lights. A low hum, like a thousand angry bees, seemed to emanate from the very walls of the house. The Viennese orchestra faltered, a violinist producing a startled squeak.

Cecil looked alarmed. ‘What on earth was that?’

Mr. Otis frowned. ‘Must be a problem with the new generator. I’ll have my man look at it.’

But the hum grew louder, more insistent. A strange, bluish light began to pulse from the corridor that led towards the twins’ rooms. Several of the more nervous ladies screamed. The Marchioness of Dumbleton looked both terrified and triumphant. ‘Good heavens,’ she exclaimed, ‘it is the ghost! I hear the house is still dreadfully haunted!’

Suddenly, with a sound like a thunderclap and a great flash of ozone-scented blue light, every lamp and lantern in Canterville Chase went out. The house was plunged into an absolute, profound, and terrifying darkness.

Panic erupted. There were shrieks, gasps, the sound of a champagne flute shattering. In the blackness, ancient fears bloomed. The whispered stories of the bloody hand, the spectral monk, the terrible fate of Sir Simon, all came rushing back into the minds of the terrified guests.

‘We are trapped!’ someone wailed. ‘The ghost has us!’

The Marchioness of Dumbleton, for all her iron composure, gave a little cry of genuine terror and clung to the arm of the nearest man, who happened to be the new, efficient butler.

Mr. Otis was shouting for lanterns, for his clerk, for someone to check the blasted generator. Cecil was trying to force his way through the frightened crowd towards Virginia.

But Virginia did not move. She stood perfectly still in the suffocating dark, and she was not afraid. This darkness was nothing. It was a comfortable, familiar blanket compared to the terrible void she had glimpsed through the portal of Death’s house. The whispers of the frightened society ladies were like nursery rhymes compared to the wicked voices she had heard in that other place.

She took a deep breath, and her voice, clear and calm, cut through the chaos. ‘Please, everyone, be still. There is nothing to be afraid of. It is only a loss of power.’

There was a moment of silence, as the sheer, unruffled authority in her voice registered.

‘The servants will bring candles in a moment,’ she continued, her voice gaining strength. ‘There is no ghost. The ghost of Canterville has found his peace. This is merely… a technical difficulty.’

From the top of the stairs, two small, soot-stained figures appeared, holding a single, guttering candle. It was the twins. ‘We’re awfully sorry, Ginny,’ one of them said, his voice trembling slightly. ‘We were just trying to build a new system for atmospheric illumination based on ionized particles.’

‘It was meant to be a surprise,’ added the other, mournfully.

A wave of angry muttering went through the crowd, but Virginia silenced it. ‘Washington,’ she called out into the darkness, ‘please find Papa and help him. And would the orchestra be so good as to play something? A waltz, perhaps. Something gentle.’

Hesitantly, guided only by memory, the strings began to play. It was a slow, beautiful melody that filled the echoing darkness. Servants began to appear with candelabras and oil lamps, their small flames creating islands of warm, dancing light in the vast rooms. The panic subsided, replaced by a strange sense of wonder. The house, stripped of its modern glare, looked ancient and mysterious and beautiful. The shadows thrown by the candlelight made the portraits seem alive, their painted eyes full of secrets.

Virginia, her white dress luminous in the gloom, moved among her guests with a grace and confidence no one had ever seen in her before. She calmed the frightened, found a chair for a lady who felt faint, and directed the serving of wine by candlelight. She turned a catastrophe into an adventure.

The Marchioness of Dumbleton found herself seated on a divan, a glass of sherry in her hand, watching the young Duchess. She saw not a gauche American girl, but a woman of extraordinary poise, a commander in the face of chaos. She saw a true mistress of her house.

Later, as the guests began to depart, their mood not of outrage but of having been part of a uniquely memorable event, the Marchioness approached Virginia. ‘Duchess,’ she said, and her voice held a new and unfamiliar note of respect. ‘Your ball was… unforgettable. You have the soul of a true Canterville, after all.’ And with a stiff, almost imperceptible nod, she was gone.

When the last carriage had rolled down the avenue, and the house was quiet once more, save for the tired sighing of the staff and the distant scolding Mr. Otis was administering to his sons, Cecil came and took Virginia’s hand. He led her out onto the terrace, where the moon had risen, bathing the garden in silver light.

‘You were magnificent,’ he said, his voice full of awe. ‘You saved us all tonight.’

‘It was nothing,’ she said, leaning her head on his shoulder. ‘I simply remembered what it is like to be truly afraid, and this was not it. The darkness holds no power, you see, not when you know how to find the light.’

They stood in silence for a long time, looking out at the peaceful park. The old Dowager came to the door of the terrace, wrapped in a shawl. She did not speak, but simply looked at her daughter in law, and in her eyes, for the first time, was a look of complete and utter acceptance. She saw the girl who had not only faced down a ghost and London society, but who had tamed the wild, modern spirit of the Otises and woven it into the ancient tapestry of the Chase.

‘The prophecy was fulfilled once,’ Virginia said softly to Cecil. ‘And I think, perhaps, it must be fulfilled again and again. Not with prayer and tears, but with patience, and with kindness. To keep the peace at Canterville.’

Cecil kissed her forehead. ‘And perhaps,’ he added, with a smile, ‘with a good electrician on retainer.’

Virginia laughed, a sound as clear and bright as a silver bell in the quiet night. In the great, silent house, it seemed as if the very walls were listening, and were content. The business of the House of Cheshire was now firmly in hand, and so, it appeared, was its heart.
