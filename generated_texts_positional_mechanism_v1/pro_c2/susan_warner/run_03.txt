He paused in the doorway of their small parlour, his hand still on the latch. The lamp had not been lit, and in the gloaming her form was but a shadow against the tall window that looked out upon the damp brick of the opposite wall. "Martha," he said, his voice low so as not to startle her. "Are you not cold, sister?"

She turned her head slowly, her silhouette breaking its stark outline. "I hadn't taken notice, Jacob. The chill feels of a piece with the evening."

"That aint a good reason to catch an ague," he replied, stepping inside and closing the door, which shut out the noise of a distant cart and the babble from the floor below. He went to the mantel, striking a lucifer match whose brief, sulfurous flare illuminated her pale face and the weary set of her mouth. He touched the flame to the lamp's wick. "There," he said, setting the glass chimney back in its place, "that's a little more like cheer."

Martha said nothing, but turned back to the window.

"What is it, dear?" he asked, coming to stand beside her.

"Nothin' new, Jacob. Just the same old thoughts turning over and over." She sighed, a small, tired sound. "I was thinking of father, and the farm, and how the lilac bush must be now. I can almost smell it, if I try very hard."

"The city has smells of its own," Jacob said, with a poor attempt at a smile. "Though I'll grant they are not so sweet." He put his hand on her shoulder. "Don't dwell on it, Martha. 'Taint good for you. 'Sorrow is a rust of the soul,' as the preacher says."

"My soul must be very rusted then," she whispered. "It feels heavy as old iron. I am a burden to you, Jacob, I know it."

"Hush," he said, his voice firm but not unkind. "You'll not say that. You are all the family I have in this world. What else should I do but care for my own sister?"

He left her side and began to busy himself with laying a fire in the little grate, though the season was nearly past for it. He broke the kindling with a careful, steady hand. Martha watched him, her hands clasped in her lap. The little noises of his work were a comfort in the quiet room; the snap of a twig, the rustle of paper, the scrape of the small coal shovel.

"Jacob," she said, so softly he barely heard.

"Yes, dear?"

"Mrs. Reed stopped me on the stair to-day. She said she had a bit of sewing, if my eyes were up to it."

Jacob paused, his back to her. "What did you tell her?"

"I said I would ask you."

He turned then, his face serious in the growing lamplight. "Her sewing is fine work, Martha. It's hard on the eyes, and she pays little for it. You need rest."

"We need the money, Jacob," she said, her voice gaining a sliver of its old strength. "Your clerking don't stretch to cover all. I know it don't. I'm not a child. Let me do what I can."

He looked at her for a long moment; at the thinness of her wrists and the dark circles that seemed to be permanently etched beneath her eyes. He knew she was right. His wages were barely enough for rent and plain food, and his own coat was worn thin at the elbows. He had hoped to spare her this, to give her time to recover her strength and spirits, but hope was a luxury, much like a steady fire in the grate.

"Very well," he said at last, turning back to his task. "But only if you promise to stop when you are tired. You must promise me that, Martha."

"I promise," she said. A log caught, and a small, cheerful flame began to lick up the side of the grate, casting dancing shadows on the wall. "You make a good fire, Jacob. Better than anyone."

"It is a small skill," he said, "but a useful one." He stood up, brushing the dust from his trousers. The next morning, Jacob rose before the first grey light touched the soot-stained windows of their small room, a habit ingrained from years of needing to wring every possible moment from the day; but this morning his waking was not prompted by the distant chime of a city clock, but by a stirring of care within his own breast. He moved with practiced quietness, washing his face in the basin of cold water and dressing in the clothes he had laid out the night before. Martha was still asleep, a faint, troubled line between her brows even in slumber. He looked at her for a moment, his heart a mixture of tenderness and a dull, constant ache of anxiety. She was so frail; the city seemed to leech the very life from her, which all his care could not quite replenish.

His own breakfast was a piece of dry bread and a cup of water, but he took care to set aside a slightly larger piece for her, and to make sure the little kettle was ready to be set on the fire she would make when she woke. These small offices were the language of his affection, the only one he knew how to speak with constancy. His resolve of the night before had not lessened with the coming of the day. Before he began his long walk to the counting-house where he worked, he would call on Mrs. Reed.

The lodging house was still mostly quiet as he descended the narrow, creaking stairs. Mrs. Reed's door was on the floor below theirs, set at the front of the house where it commanded a view of the street and all its comings and goings. Jacob knocked softly.

The door opened a crack, and Mrs. Reed's sharp face appeared. "Mr. Carver," she said, her voice a low rasp. "You are about early."

"Good morning, Mrs. Reed. I beg your pardon for the hour. My sister mentioned you had spoken to her of some sewing."

Mrs. Reed opened the door wider, though she did not invite him in. She was a woman made of sharp angles and practicality; her charity did not extend beyond a fair price for a fair job, and even then her definition of 'fair' was known to be a narrow one. "I did. A lady I know has some fine shirtwaists that need finishing. Hems and cuffs. It's particular work." Her eyes, small and bright as a bird's, scanned his face. "Is your sister's health sufficient for it? I'll not have the material spoiled."

"Her health is improving, thank you," Jacob said, his tone even and firm. "She is an excellent needlewoman. But as you say, it is particular work, and her eyes are not as strong as they once were. I came to ask what the work is, precisely, and what the pay would be."

A flicker of annoyance crossed Mrs. Reed's face; she was not accustomed to having her arrangements questioned, least of all by a young clerk. But there was a quiet determination in Jacob's bearing that gave her pause. "The pay is five cents a shirtwaist. There are a dozen of them."

"Five cents," Jacob repeated, not as a question but as a statement of fact. He did some quick, bleak arithmetic in his head. Sixty cents. For what he knew would be hours upon hours of painstaking labour. It was a pittance. But it was also sixty cents they did not have. "May I see the material, ma'am?"

With an impatient sigh, Mrs. Reed turned and retrieved a neat bundle wrapped in brown paper. She unfolded a corner for his inspection. It was a fine, delicate batiste, the colour of cream. The thread that accompanied it was of a nearly identical shade. To make a stitch that would be invisible upon such fabric would require good light and a very steady hand. He thought of Martha's tired eyes, and his heart sank.

"It is very fine," he said, his voice betraying none of his misgivings. "Martha will take the greatest care. But the light in our room is poor, especially in the afternoon. She will not be able to work as quickly as she might wish."

"That is your affair," Mrs. Reed said, beginning to wrap the bundle again. "The lady requires them by the end of next week. If it cannot be done, I can find another girl."

"It can be done," Jacob said quickly. "She will do it. But Mrs. Reed, seven cents a shirtwaist would be a fairer price for such delicate work. It would allow me to purchase a better lamp oil, so she might work without straining her sight."

Mrs. Reed gave a short, dry laugh. "And where would the extra two cents come from, Mr. Carver? My pocket? The lady has set the price."

Jacob met her gaze without flinching. "I am sure the lady is a person of quality, who would wish for quality work. Poor light and haste make for poor stitches. Martha will do her best work if she is not worried about the cost of an extra hour of lamplight."

There was a long silence. He was not begging; he was negotiating, and this unexpected turn seemed to command a grudging respect from the landlady. She looked from his earnest, tired face to the bundle in her hands. Perhaps she considered the trouble of finding another girl, or perhaps a long-buried sliver of fairness stirred within her.

"Very well," she said at last, her voice sharp. "Six cents. And not a penny more. And they must be perfect, mind you. Not a slip, not a pucker."

"Thank you, Mrs. Reed," he said, the small victory feeling both vital and deeply insufficient. "They will be perfect."

He took the bundle from her, the fabric feeling impossibly light and fragile in his hands. It felt like the embodiment of their circumstances: delicate, easily spoiled, and of little worth to anyone but themselves.

When Jacob returned that evening, the fire was burning low in the grate and a single candle cast a brave but lonely circle of light in their small room. Martha was sitting on the low stool, her hands idle in her lap, her gaze fixed on the flickering flame. She looked up as he entered, her face brightening with a genuine smile that made his heart lift.

"Jacob! You are late."

"The streets were crowded," he said, setting his worn satchel down and placing the paper-wrapped parcel on the little table. "I have something for you."

Her eyes went to the parcel, and a look of apprehension mingled with her hope. "Is that... is that the sewing?"

"It is." He came and stood before her, beginning to unwrap it carefully. "I spoke with Mrs. Reed this morning." He spread one of the unfinished shirtwaists across the table, the fine material seeming to glow in the candlelight. "The work is hems and cuffs. The fabric is delicate, Martha, so you must not tax yourself. I told her it would be done by the end of next week."

She reached out a trembling hand and touched the fabric. "Oh, Jacob, it is so fine. Like a baby's gown." She looked up at him, her eyes wide with worry. "Can I do it? What if I make a mistake?"

"You will not make a mistake," he said with a confidence he did not entirely feel. "You have the best eye for a needle of anyone I have ever known. And I have made an arrangement. It is to be six cents for each one."

Her eyes widened further, this time in surprise. "Six cents! But I heard Jenny downstairs say Mrs. Reed never pays more than five."

"We had a discussion on the matter," he said simply, folding the garment back up. "Now, you must not begin to-night. Your eyes are tired. To-morrow, in the morning light, you can make a start."

"Oh, Jacob," she whispered, and taking his hand, she pressed it to her cheek. His hand was rough from his work and the city grime, but she held it there as if it were the most precious thing in the world. "You are so good to me. I will be so careful. I will make you proud."

"I am already proud, Martha," he said softly. "Now, let us have our supper."

The following days took on a new rhythm. In the mornings, when the light was best, Martha would sit by the window, her back straight and her head bent in concentration over her work. She spread a clean white cloth over her lap to protect the delicate batiste, and her needle flew with a swiftness and precision that seemed a relic of her former, healthier self. Jacob would watch her for a moment before he left for work, his heart tight with a mixture of pride and fear. He saw the diligence, but he also saw the way she rubbed her eyes when she thought he was not looking, the faint tremor in her fingers as she threaded the needle.

He began bringing home small, simple surprises, purchased with pennies he could ill afford to spare. An apple, a small bunch of wilting flowers from a street vendor, once even a small pot of jam. They were offerings laid at the altar of her well-being, small acts of love to fortify her against the unremitting labour.

She worked with a quiet, fierce determination. The pile of finished shirtwaists grew slowly on the corner of the table, each one perfectly hemmed, the stitches so small and even they were nearly invisible. The task seemed to give her a renewed sense of purpose. Her talk was less of the rust on her soul and more of the progress she was making.

"Only four more to do, Jacob," she would say as they sat together in the evenings. "The light was very good to-day. I finished two before noon."

"That is good, Martha," he would answer, while his eyes traced the new lines of fatigue around her mouth. "But did you rest? You promised you would rest."

"Oh, I rested," she would say, though they both knew her rests were brief, guilt-ridden pauses. "It feels good to be useful again, Jacob. It feels like I am helping."

Then, one afternoon, the small disaster he had been dreading occurred. The sky had grown overcast, and the light in the room was a murky grey. Martha, eager to finish one more cuff before the light failed completely, leaned closer to her work. Her hand, grown tired and less certain, slipped. The needle, instead of taking a tiny bite of the fabric, jabbed through and tore a small, jagged hole, no bigger than a lentil, but on the pristine surface of the cuff it looked like a gaping wound.

A small, strangled cry escaped her lips. She dropped the shirtwaist as if it had burned her. For a moment, she just stared at it, at the tiny, irreparable flaw. Then, the tears that were always so close to the surface welled up and spilled over, silent tears of pure despair. All her work, all her care, undone by one clumsy movement. She thought of Mrs. Reed's sharp warning: "Not a slip, not a pucker." She thought of the sixty cents, now an impossible dream, and the debt she would now owe for the ruined material. The weight of it all fell upon her, and she bowed her head into her hands and sobbed, her thin shoulders shaking.

She was still crying when Jacob came home. He found her huddled on the stool, the ruined shirtwaist on the floor beside her.

"Martha? What is it? What has happened?" he asked, his voice sharp with alarm.

She could not speak, only gestured with a trembling hand towards the piece of fabric on the floor. He picked it up, his brow furrowed with concern, and saw the tiny, dark tear. He understood at once. He looked from the tear to his sister's weeping form, and his heart, instead of filling with anger or disappointment, was flooded with a deep and sorrowful compassion.

He knelt beside her and put his arm around her shoulders. "Hush now, Martha," he said gently. "Hush, dear. It is nothing."

"It is ruined!" she choked out, her voice muffled by her hands. "She will make us pay for the whole bolt of cloth! I have ruined us, Jacob, with my foolishness and my weak eyes!"

"You have done no such thing," he said, his voice a low, steady murmur. "Let me see. Let me look at it properly."

He gently pulled her hands away from her face and wiped her tears with his own handkerchief. He led her to the chair and sat her down, then he took the shirtwaist to the table, lighting the better lamp he had bought with the first of her earnings. He spread the cuff out under the light and examined the tear closely. It was small, but it was undeniable. A simple mend would show; it would be a pucker, a flaw.

Martha watched him, her breath catching in her throat with every movement he made. She expected a sigh of resignation, a word of gentle blame. But instead, he sat down, his face a study in concentration.

"The thread is very fine," he said, almost to himself. "And the weave is close."

From his own satchel, he took out a small case containing pens and a sharp penknife he used for his work at the counting-house. With the utmost delicacy, using the point of the knife, he began to work at the tear, gently teasing the broken threads of the fabric, trying to align them back into the weave. It was work that required a surgeon's patience, a watchmaker's steadiness. His large hands, accustomed to ledgers and ink pots, moved with a surprising and innate grace.

Martha watched, mesmerized. She had never seen him do anything like this. His focus was absolute. He took one of her own needles, threaded it with a single, gossamer strand pulled from a scrap of the batiste, and began to work. He did not sew. He rewove. He passed the thread through the existing weave, following the pattern of the cloth itself, drawing the torn edges together not with a seam, but by recreating the fabric over the tiny gap. It took a long time. The only sounds in the room were the ticking of the clock on the mantel and the soft whisper of his own breathing.

And as he worked, a thought, quiet and unbidden, began to form in his mind: a wonder at the steadiness of his own fingers, at the patience he possessed for such a minute task. His clerking work was all columns of figures, blunt and unartistic. He had never considered that his hands might be capable of such fine, creative repair. He had always thought of himself as a plodder, a provider, the sturdy, unlovely root that fed the delicate flower of his sister. But here, in this moment, bent over this tiny, impossible task, he felt a flicker of something else: a spark of the artist.

At last, he was finished. He held the cuff up to the light. The tear was gone. In its place was a small section, barely noticeable, where the weave was a fraction thicker than the rest. It was not perfect, but it was not a flaw. It would pass inspection.

"There," he said, his voice tired but triumphant. "What did I tell you, Martha? It is not ruined."

He brought it to her. She took it with hands that still trembled and held it close to her eyes. She could barely find the spot. She looked from the cuff to her brother's face, and her eyes filled with a new kind of tears, tears of gratitude and awe.

"Jacob," she whispered. "How did you do that? I have never seen such a thing."

"I do not know," he said, a faint smile touching his lips. "I just looked at it, and saw how it must be done."

He went back to the table and began to carefully pack away the rest of the sewing, placing the mended shirtwaist on the very bottom of the pile. The crisis had passed. But something had shifted in the small, dim room. Martha's despair had been replaced by a profound, wondering love for the brother she thought she knew so well. And Jacob, as he looked at his own hands, saw in them not just the tools of a weary clerk, but the promise of some other, finer possibility. The resolve he felt now was not just to endure, but to find a better way. A way to build a life for them not of mended tears and careful savings, but of something stronger, something made with the full measure of his own quiet and surprising skill. He would see it through. He would find a way.

His father's words came back to him with a fresh and heavy weight. "I don't see but I shall have to sell the farm." The mortgage, the unpaid interest; it was a millstone around all their necks. He was the sturdy tree, as Winnie had said; but the tree itself was being shaken by a storm it had no power to withstand. He made his purchases with a careful hand; a loaf of bread, a small piece of cheese, a jug of milk, and a bit of salt pork to be cooked with some beans for their supper. As he was turning to leave the grocer's, his eye was caught by a small handbill tacked to the wall by the door. It was an advertisement, printed in a neat, clear type. Inquire of Mr. Alistair Finch, at his offices, No. 12 Broad Street." Winthrop read it once, and then again. A draughtsman. He had, in his youth, a good skill with a pencil. He had once drawn a likeness of his mother that she had treasured, and had filled a sketchbook with drawings of the trees and fields around Shahweetah. It was a talent he had long since put aside, as having no practical use in the world of accounts and ledgers he now inhabited. But the words "a neat hand" seemed to leap out at him. His handwriting was his one pride in his work for Mr. Grueby, the merchant who employed him. It was clear, and regular, and without a single blot or flourish. He stood for a long moment, staring at the paper, a strange new current of thought moving through him. What if there were other work for him in this city than adding up columns of figures? What if his "neat hand" could be turned to some other purpose? The idea was so novel, so far outside the narrow track his life had been following, that it almost frightened him. It seemed a kind of disloyalty to the path he had so painstakingly carved out. But then he thought of Winnie's pale face, and of his father's sigh, and of the dwindling coins in his pocket. The path he was on led only to a lifetime of scrimping and saving, of barely keeping their heads above water. Perhaps Providence had placed this notice in his way. The thought was a presumptuous one, and he checked it; but it would not be entirely banished. He turned away from the grocer's shop with his parcels, the words of the advertisement echoing in his mind. No. 12 Broad Street. It was not far from his own place of employment. He could, perhaps, make an inquiry on his dinner hour the next day. There could be no harm in that. He walked back to Little South St. with a quicker step, his mind less burdened than it had been. It was only a possibility, a very faint one; but it was a crack of light in a sky that had seemed entirely overcast. He found Winnie awake, and looking more rested. She had unpacked the small bag she had brought with her, and had arranged her few possessions with a touching attempt to make the room her own. A well-worn Bible lay on the little table, next to the vase with its sprig of willow. "I feel better, Governor," she said, managing a small smile. "The bed is very comfortable." "I am glad of it," he said, his heart lighter for her smile. "And I have brought us a feast. We shall have to ask Mrs. Nettley's leave to use her stove, but I think she will not refuse us." Mrs. Nettley, a woman whose heart was considerably larger than her small house, did not refuse. She bustled about, making room for their pot of beans on her stove, and declaring that it was a pleasure to have a young lady in the house, and that she hoped Miss Winnie would feel quite at home. The warmth of her welcome, combined with the warmth of the fire and the simple, hot meal, did much to restore Winnie's spirits. She began to ask questions about the house, and about George, Mrs. Nettley's artist son, whose room was filled with the mysterious paraphernalia of his craft. After supper, Winthrop read to her from the Bible, as their father had always done at home; a chapter from Isaiah, full of promises of comfort and restoration. His voice, steady and calm, was a balm to Winnie's frayed nerves. When he had finished, she leaned her head against his shoulder. "It almost feels like home now, Winthrop," she whispered. He saw her to her room, and made sure she was warm and comfortable before returning to his own. He sat for a long time by the fire, his mind not on the ledger books he had brought home to work on, but on the advertisement for a draughtsman. He rose, at last, and went to his trunk. From the very bottom, beneath his spare set of clothes, he pulled out a small, leather-bound sketchbook. He had not opened it in years. He sat down at his table and, by the light of the fire and a single candle, began to sketch. He did not draw the view from his window, the grim and angular rooftops of the city. He drew from memory: the great weeping willow that stood by the creek at Shahweetah; the curve of the hill behind the house; the face of his sister, asleep on the cot. His hand, at first, was stiff and unpractised. But as he worked, the old facility began to return. The lines flowed more easily, the shapes took form upon the page. The intelligence from Mr. Darrow's office, which had seemed so promising, now felt like a drop in an ocean of need. The figures swam before his eyes; -- the cost of milk and eggs, of the beef for the broth the doctor had recommended, of the extra fuel that would be needed to keep the room at a constant, gentle warmth. He thought of the mortgage on the farm, a debt he felt as his own, and of his father's weary face. Every avenue seemed to lead to a blank wall of impossibility.

He got up and paced the small room, from the window that looked out on a forest of chimneys to the door of Winnie's little chamber. He pushed it open a crack. In the faint moonlight, he could see her small form under the blankets, one hand lying on the pillow, thin and white. She was his charge, given to him by God. He could not fail her. Despair was a luxury he could not afford; it was a betrayal.

He came back to the table and drew his own account book towards him, a little ledger in which he noted every expenditure down to the last half-penny. He went over it again and again, trying to find some place to cut, some corner to shave. But there were no corners. His life was already pared down to the barest of necessities. To give Winnie what she needed, he could not simply economize further; he must earn more.

But how? His position at Mr. Darrow's office was a good one, but the salary was fixed, and he was too new, too junior, to ask for an advance or a raise. The city was full of men looking for work; extra employment was not a thing to be had for the asking.

He sat with his pen in his hand, staring at the blank page opposite his neat columns of figures. His mind, trained to solve problems of stress and structure, of leverage and support, turned this new problem over and over. He needed to apply a new force, to find a new source of income. It was an engineering problem of a different sort.

His eyes fell upon his drawing instruments, lying in their worn velvet case. They were his tools, the key to his profession. He was a good draftsman; Mr. Darrow had commended his neatness and precision. Perhaps there was work for such skills outside of the office. Other engineers, architects, inventors; -- they must need drawings made, plans copied, calculations checked. Such work could be done at night, by the light of his own lamp, in his own room.

The thought, once it came, took hold of him with a firm grip. It would mean sacrificing his own rest, his only hours of leisure. It would mean long nights of painstaking work after long days of the same. But what was that, if it could buy milk for Winnie? What was his own weariness, if it could purchase the warmth and food that were her only chance? The path was a hard one, but it was a path. It was not a wall.

His resolution was taken. But where to find such work? He knew no one in the vast, impersonal city, save for the Nettleys. He could not go from door to door like a common pedlar. The idea was daunting, but his will was hardening.

Then a new thought, a bolder one, entered his mind. Mr. Darrow. His own employer. The man who had said, "Gratitude does not build bridges, Mr. Landholm. A clear head does." A man who valued work and results above all else. To ask for charity would be impossible, an offence. But to ask for more work? To offer his time and his skill for fair compensation? That was a transaction, a matter of business. It was a risk. Mr. Darrow might think him presumptuous, or be annoyed at the request. But he might also respect the industry, the desire to work. He had shown a moment of kindness that afternoon; perhaps there was more of it behind his stern exterior than the world knew.

Winthrop pulled a clean sheet of paper towards him. He would not do it now, in the heat of the moment. He would compose the letter carefully, tomorrow, when his head was clear. He would state his request plainly and respectfully. He would offer to do any sort of drafting or copying, no matter how menial, that the office might have. He would propose to do it at his lodgings, in the evenings, for a piece-rate to be determined by Mr. Darrow himself.

He folded the blank sheet of paper and put it aside, a tangible representation of his new plan. He felt a quiet calm settle over him, the calm that follows a great and terrible storm. The fear and helplessness had not vanished, but they had been beaten back by the force of a determined will. He had a direction. He had something to do.

He went to the window and looked out. The flurry of snow had stopped, and the moon shone clear in a cold, dark sky, casting sharp black shadows on the neighbouring roofs. It was a vast, indifferent world. But in it, he had his own small post to defend, his own battle to fight. He thought of the verse from Chaucer that had prefaced the chapter of his life at Shahweetah; "Here is no home, here is but wildernesse." It was true. But even in the wilderness, a man could build a shelter, and keep a fire burning for the one he loved. He looked back at the closed door of Winnie's room. For her, he would do it. He would not fail.

To Mr. Darrow,
Sir,
I trust you will pardon the liberty I take in addressing you on a personal matter. Circumstances have lately changed for me, requiring an increase in my income beyond what my current wages afford. It is on this account that I venture to inquire if there might be any supplementary work available from the office which I could perform.
I am a proficient draftsman and my handwriting is, I believe, clear and regular. I propose to do this work in the evenings, at my own lodgings, so that it would not interfere with my duties at the office. I would be content with whatever piece-rate you would deem fair and appropriate for such labour.
I would not make this request if the need were not pressing. I ask for no charity, sir, only for the opportunity to exchange my free time for honest work.

He read it over twice. It seemed to him to strike the right tone. It was plain, it was a business proposition, and it betrayed nothing of the desperate anxiety that fuelled it. He folded it carefully and placed it in an envelope, which he addressed with a steady hand.
When it was time for him to leave, he found Winnie had fallen into a light sleep on the settee, her cheek pillowed on her hand. The firelight flickered on her pale face, and for a moment she looked so much like the little sister of his memory, before sickness had begun its cruel work, that his throat tightened. He tucked the old cloak more securely about her, placed a plate with a piece of bread and an apple on the table beside her, and with a last look, went quietly out.
On the landing below, he met Mrs. Nettley, who was coming up with a small covered bowl in her hands.
"Ah, Mr. Landholm," she said in a hushed, kindly voice. "I was just bringing a little something for your sister. It's just a bit of broth, but it's strengthening. I thought it might do her good."
"That is very good of you, Mrs. Nettley," Winthrop said, truly grateful. "Her appetite is not what it should be."
"The poor dear," said the good woman, her face full of sympathy. "It's a hard thing, to be sick in a great city. You tell her if she needs a thing, a single thing, she's only to knock on the floor and I'll be up in a trice. George and I, we'd do anything."
"You have done a great deal already," said Winthrop. "I don't know how to thank you."
"Pshaw," she said, waving a hand. "What are neighbours for? Now, you get along to your work, or you'll be late. Don't you worry about her. I'll look in on her."
Winthrop went on his way with a heart a little lightened by her kindness, but the weight of his own errand soon settled upon him again. The office was the same as ever; a large, rather dim room filled with the rustling of papers and the low murmur of clerks at their desks. Winthrop took his place and set to his own work, his mind preoccupied. The letter was a heavy weight in his pocket. He watched Mr. Darrow's closed door for an opportunity. The morning wore on. Men came and went from the inner office; there was no moment when Winthrop felt he could approach without being conspicuous.
At last, near midday, Mr. Darrow emerged and spoke to the head clerk for a moment before preparing to go out. It was now or never. With his heart beating a steady, heavy rhythm against his ribs, Winthrop rose from his stool and approached his employer.
"Mr. Darrow, sir," he said, his voice lower than he intended.
The man turned, his expression impatient. "What is it, Landholm?"
"I have a letter for you, sir. If you would have a moment to read it at your convenience."
He held out the envelope. Mr. Darrow took it, his sharp eyes glancing from the envelope to Winthrop's face and back again, as if to decipher its contents from his clerk's expression.
"Very well," he said curtly. He did not put it in his pocket, but held it in his hand as he put on his hat and coat. He left the office without another word.
Winthrop returned to his stool, feeling as though he had just jumped from a great height. The thing was done. The rest was out of his hands. The afternoon was one of the longest of his life. Every time the door to the street opened, he looked up, expecting to see Mr. Darrow return. Every time the door to the inner office creaked, he tensed, expecting a summons. But nothing happened. The slow hours passed in a kind of feverish dream. He drew lines and calculated figures with his usual precision, but his mind was elsewhere; it was in that letter, it was with Winnie by the fire, it was with his father at the threatened farm.
The day drew to a close. The other clerks began to put away their things, to stretch and talk in low tones. Winthrop remained at his desk, his work finished, but unwilling to leave until he had some answer. He felt that to go home in this state of suspense would be unendurable. Finally, only he and the head clerk remained.
Just as he had given up hope and was reaching for his coat, the door of the inner office opened and Mr. Darrow stood there. He had returned some time before, unnoticed by Winthrop.
"Landholm," he said. His voice was devoid of any expression. "Come into my office."
Winthrop's mouth went dry. He rose and followed his employer into the room, which felt even more imposing and severe at this late hour. The great desk of polished mahogany was clear save for a few neat stacks of papers and, in the very centre, Winthrop's letter.

Winthrop was silent, astonished at the man's methods, and a little resentful of the intrusion, yet also strangely impressed by it.

"I understand that, sir," Winthrop said, his heart sinking. "I am not asking for charity."
"You are asking for an opportunity," Mr. Darrow said, turning back towards him. "Very well. I will give it to you. On a trial basis."
Relief washed over Winthrop so powerfully that he felt light-headed.
"There is a set of engineering specifications for the new canal lock," Mr. Darrow went on, his tone as crisp as if he were discussing the weather. "They must be copied in triplicate. The work is precise and allows for no errors. One of my senior draftsmen was to do it, but he is overburdened. You may have the work. I will pay you two cents per page. Nine dollars. The sum was a fortune. It was more than a week's wages.
"The first copy must be on my desk one week from today. The other two, the week following. If the work is satisfactory, there may be more. If it is not, this experiment ends. Is that clear?"

"There is a fruiterer on the corner of the next street," he said, his voice clipped. "He sometimes has oranges from the West Indies. They are said to be good for invalids."

Winthrop stood for a second, utterly confounded. Then he murmured, "Yes, sir," to the man's back, and went out, closing the door softly behind him.
He walked home through the cold, bustling streets in a state of quiet exaltation. The wilderness was still a wilderness, but he had found a spring of water in it. He stopped at the fruiterer's Mr. Darrow had mentioned, and with a small part of the advance he had received, he bought a single, perfect orange. It was a wild extravagance, but his heart felt light and reckless.
