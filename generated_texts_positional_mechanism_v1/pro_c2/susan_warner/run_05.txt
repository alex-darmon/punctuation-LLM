He was a formidable man, with a face that looked as if it had been carved from granite and a reputation for being as hard as the gold he counted. He would not have his father bear the burden of knowing how hard he had laboured. It was a son's duty, and a son's privilege.

The next morning, before he left for the counting house, he sat at the small table with pen and paper. Winnie, already dressed, was perched on her bed, her sewing basket in her lap, pretending to sort threads but in reality watching his every move. The scratching of his pen was the only sound.

"My dear Father," he wrote, in his clear, precise hand. "I trust this letter finds you and Asahel in good health. I have by the grace of Providence been able to secure an advance on my wages, a sum sufficient to meet the interest owed to Mr. Haye. I enclose a bank draft for the amount. He folded the letter, sealed it with a wafer, and addressed it to his father's hand. He felt a profound sense of relief as he placed it in his coat pocket, alongside the precious bank draft he would purchase. He turned to his sister.

"I must go now, Winnie. I will lock the door. Do not open it for anyone but me or Mrs. Nettley."

"I will not, Governor," she promised. "I will be very good."

He stooped to kiss her forehead. Her skin was cool, and her eyes followed him with a look of such utter reliance that it both strengthened and burdened him. As he walked through the noisy, crowded streets toward the bank, he felt the weight of her dependence, of his family's hopes, resting squarely on his shoulders. But his step was firm. He was no longer a boy, but a man with a man's work to do.

For Winnie, the hours after he left were the longest of the day. The little attic room was her whole world. She would stand at the window, which looked out not upon fields and trees but upon a forest of grimy brick chimneys and a tangled web of clotheslines. Below, the narrow street was a river of ceaseless activity, of carts and horses and people whose haste and clamour made her head ache. She missed the quiet of Shahweetah, the sound of the wind in the pines, the lowing of the cattle in the evening. Here, the only sounds were harsh and strange.

Her enforced idleness wore upon her. She was not strong enough for heavy work, but her fingers longed for occupation. Her eyes fell upon the mending basket. An idea, timid at first, began to take root in her mind. Winthrop worked so hard for them both; was there nothing she could do, no small service she could render to ease his load? George, Mrs. Nettley's son, was a painter and notoriously careless with his clothes. And Mrs. Nettley herself, with her endless round of cooking and cleaning, surely had little time for darning and patching.

Gathering her courage as one might gather a cloak against the cold, Winnie took her basket and went softly out of the room and down the narrow stairs. The smell of boiling cabbage and soap grew stronger as she descended. She paused at the kitchen door, her heart beating a little faster. Mrs. Nettley was at the great table, her arms white with flour, kneading a mountain of dough. She looked up, her expression one of surprise.

"Well, child. What is it? You look like a mouse peeping out of its hole."

"If you please, Mrs. Nettley," Winnie began, her voice scarcely a whisper. "I was wondering, that is, I have a great deal of time, and I am not very sick to-day, and I wondered if, perhaps, you had any mending I might do for you. Or for Mr. George."

Mrs. Nettley stopped her kneading and wiped her hands on her apron, regarding Winnie with sharp, not unkindly eyes. "Mending, is it? George has stockings that are more hole than stocking, that's for certain. But are you sure you're well enough for it, child? Your fingers look like little white spindles."

"Oh yes, ma'am," Winnie said eagerly. "I am a very neat sewer. My mother taught me. It would make me so happy to be of some use."

A softer look came into Mrs. Nettley's face. "Your mother's own daughter, are you? Well, there's a pile in the basket by the hearth that would frighten a tailor. You may take what you like. "Thank you, Mrs. Nettley! Oh, thank you," Winnie said, her face alight.

She carried a small pile of socks and a torn shirt back up to her attic refuge, her heart lighter than it had been in weeks. She had a purpose. She sat in the low chair by the window and set to work, her needle flying with unaccustomed energy. The city's noise seemed to fade as she concentrated on the careful, even stitches, a small act of order against the chaos of the world.

Meanwhile, at the offices of Gresham & Co., Winthrop was finding his own world expanding. After dispatching his letter and the draft, he had returned to his high desk, expecting another mountain of figures to conquer. But in the mid-morning, Mr. Gresham had summoned him to his private office.

"Landholm," the merchant said, without preamble, looking up from a letter he was reading. "Your penmanship is clear and your English is tolerable. I have a new task for you. These are letters to clients whose accounts are in arrears. I require you to draft responses. You will state the sum outstanding, the date it was due, and request immediate settlement. You will be firm, but you will not be uncivil. Bring the drafts to me for approval before you make the fair copies."

He pushed a small stack of papers across the desk. It was a promotion of sorts, a move from the pure mechanics of accounting to the more delicate art of business communication. It was a sign of trust.

"Yes, sir," Winthrop said, his mind already racing. "I will do my best."

"See that you do," Gresham returned, and looked back down at his letter, a clear dismissal.

Winthrop spent the rest of the day composing the letters. It was more difficult than he had imagined. Each one required a slightly different tone. Some accounts were only slightly overdue; others had been delinquent for months. He had to be precise, firm, and yet maintain the goodwill of the firm's clientele. He wrote and rewrote, striving for the perfect balance of authority and courtesy. When he finally presented the drafts to Mr. Gresham late in the afternoon, he braced himself for criticism.

The merchant read through them slowly, making a small mark with a pencil here and there. He said nothing until he had finished the last one.

"This one is too lenient," he said, tapping a finger on a letter to a notoriously slow payer. "Strengthen the closing. For the others, they will suffice. You may proceed with the copies."

Winthrop's heart gave a leap. "They will suffice." It was, from Mr. Gresham, a veritable chorus of praise. He returned to his desk with a renewed sense of energy, feeling that he was at last beginning to make his way, to prove his worth in this hard, demanding city.

When he climbed the stairs to their rooms that evening, he was weary in body, but his mind was alert and full of a quiet satisfaction. He opened the door to find the lamp lit, the fire glowing, and a cloth laid on their little table with two plates and a loaf of bread. Winnie was sitting by the fire, her sewing in her lap. She looked up as he entered, and the smile she gave him was worth all the day's toil.

"Welcome home, Governor," she said.

"This looks very cozy, Winnie," he said, taking off his coat. "Have you been busy?"

"A little," she said, and with a shy pride, she held up a man's gray woolen sock. The heel, which had been a gaping hole, was now a neatly woven patch of gray yarn. "I have been mending for Mrs. Nettley. She said I might."

Winthrop took the sock and examined it. The darning was exquisite, the stitches tiny and almost invisible. He looked at his sister, at her flushed cheeks and shining eyes. The poor little vine was putting out a new leaf, trying to climb on its own.

"Why, Winnie," he said gently, "this is done beautifully. As well as mother could have done it."

Tears sprang to her eyes at the praise, and at the mention of their mother. "Do you truly think so?"

"I do," he said, handing it back to her. "You have been a very good and useful little sister to-day."

They sat down to their simple supper of bread and cheese, with a cup of milk for Winnie. He told her of his new work, of drafting letters for Mr. Gresham, and she listened with rapt attention, as if he were recounting heroic deeds. And she told him of her interview with Mrs. Nettley, and how good it felt to hold a needle again. For the first time since they had come to Mannahatta, the little attic felt less like a perch of exile and more like a home, a small kingdom of two which they ruled together with love and mutual industry.

Later, when Winnie had gone to her own small room, Winthrop remained by the dwindling fire. The day's small triumphs warmed him. The money was on its way to save the farm. He had earned a measure of his employer's trust. Winnie had found a small happiness, a way to feel she was contributing. Yet, as he stared into the embers, a familiar shadow of care crept back into his heart. The mortgage was a beast only temporarily placated, not slain. Mr. Gresham's trust meant greater responsibility, and greater chances for failure. And as he sat there in the quiet, he heard it again from the next room, a small, dry cough, a sound as persistent and worrying as the ticking of a clock that measures out a limited time. The city was a wilderness still, and the path through it was steep and strewn with stones. He bowed his head for a moment, his silent prayer a mixture of gratitude for the day's light, and a plea for strength for the days to come.

The fire fell into embers, and the embers to gray ash, and still he sat, his head bowed in his hands. The city's distant murmur was the only sound, a low and constant growl, as of a beast that never slept. He thought of the farm at Wut-a-qut-o, of the profound stillness of the fields under the stars, a silence that healed rather than threatened. Here, the quiet was only an absence of clamor, not a presence of peace. At last, chilled and weary, he rose and made his way to his own narrow bed in the corner, but sleep was a shy bird that would not come to his call. His thoughts were a troubled sea, tossing between the day's small gains and the great, looming perils of the future. The cough from Winnie's room, however faint, was the signal of a storm he had no power to still.

The morning, however, broke with a deceptive brightness, the sun slanting through their single attic window and laying a cheerful yellow bar across the floor. Winthrop was up first, as was his custom, rekindling the fire with a practiced hand. Soon after, Winnie's door opened, and she appeared, looking pale but with a serene light in her eyes. She had dressed herself with unusual care in her best brown merino, her hair combed smooth and tied with a ribbon.

"You are an early riser, little sister," Winthrop said, smiling at her.

"The sun would not let me lie abed," she answered, coming to his side. "And I thought I might help you. Is there water to be fetched?"

"I have fetched it already. But you can set the plates, if you will. Mrs. Nettley left a little pitcher of milk for us on the stairs."

Her face brightened at the charge. To be useful, to have a part in their small household, was the very medicine her spirit craved. As she moved about the room, setting their two tin plates and mugs upon the small table, Winthrop watched her, his heart filled with a mixture of love and pain. The vine was indeed trying to climb, but the trellis was so very fragile.

After their breakfast of oatmeal porridge, a store of which Winthrop had laid in, he prepared to leave for his day's work. Winnie followed him to the door, her hands full of mending for Mrs. Nettley, which she had eagerly solicited.

"Will you be gone all the long day, Governor?" she asked, her voice tinged with the old note of dependence.

"Till the sun is low, Winnie. Mr. Gresham has much for me to do. You will be a busy little bee here, I know." He touched her cheek. "And you will not be lonely?"

"Not while my hands are full," she said bravely, though her eyes told a different story. "And Mrs. Nettley said I might sit with her in the kitchen for a spell, if I liked. She is a very kind woman, Winthrop."

"That she is," he agreed. "Providence has been good to place us under her roof."

With a final look at his sister, poised in the doorway like a timid sparrow on the edge of a great, roaring thoroughfare, he descended the narrow stairs and went out into the world.

Mr. Gresham's counting house was a hive of activity, the scratching of pens a constant undersong to the city's noise. Winthrop took his place at a tall desk near a window, the very one where he had penned the letter that had saved the farm, and applied himself to his tasks. His penmanship was clear and strong, his figures exact, and his quiet diligence had not gone unnoticed by his employer. Mid-morning, Mr. Gresham himself came to Winthrop's desk.

"Mr. Landholm," he said, his keen eyes resting on the neat ledger before him, "a word with you in my office."

Winthrop followed him into the small, glass-walled room, a space of quiet authority amidst the general hum.

"Your work is satisfactory," Mr. Gresham began, without preamble. "Very satisfactory. You have a head for figures and a hand for a fair copy. I have a matter that requires discretion as well as diligence. I believe you possess both."

"I will endeavor to prove worthy of your trust, sir," Winthrop replied, his heart beating a little faster.

"I have no doubt of it," Mr. Gresham said, a rare, thin smile touching his lips. "It concerns the shipping accounts of the vessel 'North Star'. There are… discrepancies. My senior clerk, Mr. Davies, has been over them, but his conclusions do not satisfy me. I want a fresh pair of eyes on the ledgers. Your eyes. You will work on them here, in this office, and you will speak of this to no one. Is that understood?"

"Perfectly, sir."

"Good. The ledgers are there." He pointed to a formidable stack of leather-bound books on a side table. "Begin at once. Mr. Davies will furnish you with his own notes on the matter, so that you may see the path he has taken."

Mr. Gresham left, and a moment later another man entered, a man of about forty, with a sallow complexion and eyes that seemed to hold a perpetual, weary cynicism. This, Winthrop surmised, was Mr. Davies.

"So," Mr. Davies said, not bothering with a greeting. "You are to be the new broom. Mr. Gresham seems to think you can see what I cannot." There was no overt hostility in his tone, only a flat sort of resignation that was, perhaps, worse.

"Mr. Gresham has given me a task, and I must do my best to fulfill it," Winthrop said calmly.

"Aye, do that." Mr. Davies tossed a sheaf of papers on the desk. "There are my notes. A fool's errand, if you ask me. The accounts are tangled, to be sure; they always are with a captain who's fonder of the bottle than the logbook. But there's no malice in it, only incompetence. You will spend a week chasing shadows and find nothing." He looked Winthrop up and down. "You are new to Mannahatta?"

"I am."

"And fresh from the country, by the look of you. Take a word of advice. The city is not a place for pilgrims. There are no straight paths here. Everything is a tangle. The trick is not to try and unsnarl it, but to find your own way through. Do your work, take your pay, and don't ask questions that have no comfortable answers."

With that, he turned and left Winthrop alone with the great, silent ledgers. The clerk's words lingered in the air, unsettling as a cold draft. Winthrop opened the first book. The columns of figures swam before his eyes, a sea of numbers representing cargoes of cotton, casks of molasses, and bales of tobacco, a world of commerce of which he knew nothing. He felt the weight of the trust placed upon him, and a new anxiety, a fear of failure that was different from the fear of poverty. This was a trial of his mind, of his character. He remembered the verse from Chaucer, 'The wrestling of this worlde askith a fall,' and with a silent prayer for strength, he dipped his pen in the ink and began his work.

Meanwhile, Winnie, having finished her mending with a speed and skill that surprised herself, found the little attic room too quiet, too empty. The promise of company was a lure she could not resist. She tapped lightly on Mrs. Nettley's kitchen door.

"Come in, come in, child!" came the hearty reply.

Winnie entered a world of warmth and savory smells. Mrs. Nettley, her face flushed from the heat of the stove, was stirring a large pot.

"Well now," she said, beaming at Winnie. "Done with your work so soon? Such a clever little thing with your needle! I declare, that sock looks better now than when it was new. Sit you down, dearie, sit you down. I'm just making a bit of soup for George's dinner."

Winnie sat on a stool near the fire, feeling the warmth seep into her bones. It was pleasant to be in this bustling, cheerful space, to be spoken to with such easy kindness.

"I am going to the market in a little while," Mrs. Nettley said, "to get a bit of fresh fish for Mr. Landholm's supper. He works so hard, that brother of yours, he needs his nourishment. Would you have a mind to come with me, child? A breath of air might do you good."

Winnie hesitated. The thought of the city streets, the noise, the crowds of strange faces, was a daunting one. But the thought of staying alone in the silent attic was worse. And the desire to see, to experience the world her brother moved in, was a powerful curiosity.

"I should like that very much," she said, her voice small but firm.

"That's the spirit! Put on your bonnet and shawl, then. The sun is out, but there's a wicked wind at the corners."

Wrapped in her warmest things, Winnie followed Mrs. Nettley down the stairs and into the street. It was as if she had stepped into a different element. The roar was incessant; the clatter of cartwheels on cobblestones, the cries of vendors, the shouts of drivers, the endless tramp of feet. People jostled past them, their faces set and hurried. Great drays pulled by enormous horses thundered by, making the very ground tremble. Winnie shrank closer to Mrs. Nettley's side, her eyes wide with a mixture of terror and fascination.

"Hold fast to my arm, dearie," the good woman said, patting Winnie's hand. "It's a bit of a hubbub, ain't it? But you get used to it. Look there, see the oyster-man? Best oysters in the city, he has."

She pointed to a man with a cart, and Winnie saw heaps of rough, gray shells, and the quick flash of a knife as he shucked one for a customer. They passed stalls piled high with cabbages and potatoes, barrels of apples, and pyramids of bright oranges that seemed to hold a light of their own. It was a world of bewildering abundance and ceaseless motion. But it was not the country. There was no clean smell of earth, no gentle lowing of cattle, only the sharp, mingled odors of fish and refuse and coal smoke. Her head began to ache, and a weariness settled deep in her limbs. She felt very small, very lost, a tiny, stray leaf caught in a great, dirty whirlwind.

By the time they had made their purchase of fish and were turning for home, Winnie's fortitude was spent. Her breath came in short, shallow gasps, and the cough that had been lurking in the background began to trouble her. She clung to Mrs. Nettley's arm, her steps faltering.

"Are you ailing, child?" Mrs. Nettley asked, her kind face creased with concern. "You're white as a sheet."

"It is nothing," Winnie whispered, though it felt like everything. "Just a little tired."

"Well, we're almost home. We'll have you by the fire with a cup of tea in a trice."

They reached the house at last, and Winnie stumbled up the stairs to their attic room, her lungs burning, her whole body trembling with exhaustion. She sank onto the settee, too weary even to take off her bonnet. The brief flowering of her morning's energy had withered completely. The city had shown her its face, and it had defeated her. She was only the poor little vine after all, and the tree that supported her was far away, and she was alone in the wilderness. She buried her face in the rough calico cushion and wept.

Winthrop worked on through the day, his mind wholly absorbed in the labyrinth of figures. Mr. Davies's cynicism had been a goad rather than a deterrent. It was true, the accounts were a tangle. But as the hours passed, Winthrop began to see not a random tangle, but a carefully woven net. There were patterns, small but repeating irregularities, a cask of rum here, a bolt of silk there, recorded in the ship's log but vanishing from the final tally of goods received. It was subtle, artful, and done with a skill that belied Mr. Davies's theory of simple incompetence.

By late afternoon, he had isolated a series of these discrepancies, all connected to voyages made under one particular captain. He sat back, the pages of his notations spread before him. He had found what Mr. Gresham suspected, and what Mr. Davies had either missed or chosen to ignore. A cold knot formed in his stomach. This was not a matter of sums, but of sin. It was theft, plain and simple. And to bring it to light was to accuse a man, perhaps to ruin him. He thought of Mr. Davies's advice: 'Don't ask questions that have no comfortable answers.' This was such a question.

He packed his papers carefully and locked them in the drawer Mr. Gresham had assigned him. His head ached with the strain of concentration. He felt as if he had been wrestling not just with figures, but with the very spirit of the place, a spirit of cunning and deceit. As he walked home through the twilight streets, the city seemed more menacing than ever. Every shadowed doorway seemed to hold a secret, every passing glance a challenge. He longed for the simple, open honesty of his attic room, for Winnie's gentle presence, for the small kingdom of two where truth was the only currency.

He climbed the stairs, his steps heavy with fatigue. He opened the door, expecting the cheerful glow of the lamp and the sight of his sister waiting for him. But the room was dark and cold. The fire had died to a few faint, red eyes in a bed of ash. A terrible fear, sharp and sudden, pierced his heart.

"Winnie?" he called, his voice tight.

A small, choked sound came from the settee. He strode across the room, fumbled for the matches on the mantel, and struck a light. The little flame illuminated the scene. Winnie was huddled on the settee, still in her bonnet and shawl, her face tear-stained and pale in the flickering light. As he lit the lamp, he saw that she was shivering violently.

"Winnie! What is it? What has happened?"

He knelt beside her, taking her hands. They were cold as ice.

"I went out," she whispered, her voice hoarse and broken by coughing. "With Mrs. Nettley. To the market. It was so… so big, Winthrop. And so loud. I could not bear it."

"Hush now, hush," he said, his own heart aching with a fierce, protective tenderness. He chafed her cold hands in his. "You are home now. You are safe."

He took off her bonnet and shawl as if she were a little child, and saw with a pang how exhausted she looked. Her eyes were shadowed, her cheeks had a feverish, unhealthy flush. He pulled the settee closer to the fireplace and set to work rebuilding the fire, his movements quick and efficient.

"I am sorry, Governor," she murmured, watching him. "I let the fire go out. I am not a very good sister."

"You are the very best of sisters," he said, not looking at her, his voice thick with an emotion he could not hide. "You were tired, that is all. We will have a blaze in a moment, and some hot tea, and you will be warm again."

The kindling caught, and flames began to lick at the larger logs. The room filled with a welcome warmth and light, pushing back the shadows. He helped Winnie to a chair, wrapped her in a blanket, and then bustled about, preparing tea and toasting a piece of bread for her over the fire.

He brought it to her, kneeling on the hearth-rug at her feet. "Here," he said gently. "Try to eat a little."

She took the tea, her hands trembling so that the cup rattled in its saucer. She took a sip, and the warmth seemed to revive her a little. "The city frightens me, Winthrop," she said in a low voice. "It is not like home. There are no friendly faces. I do not think I can ever be good here."

How her words echoed his own fears, and the warnings of Chaucer and Mr. Davies. 'Here is no home, here is but wildernesse.' He had brought this poor, tender plant into a wilderness of stone and smoke, and it was withering before his eyes. All the day's triumphs, the trust of his employer, the discovery he had made, all turned to ashes in his mouth. What good was it to uncover the dishonesty of the world, if in the process he lost the one pure and precious thing in his keeping?

He took the empty cup from her and set it aside. "You will not be frightened when I am with you," he said, his voice low and full of a conviction he did not entirely feel. "And I will not leave you alone again. We will face the wilderness together."

She looked at him, her eyes filling with tears again, but this time they were tears of gratitude, not despair. She leaned forward, and laid her hot cheek against his. "My own Governor," she whispered.

He held her close, the blanket and all, feeling the slightness of her frame and the feverish heat of her skin. The fire crackled on the hearth, a brave but lonely light against the vast, indifferent darkness of the city. He had won a small skirmish in the counting house, but here, in his own small fortress, the enemy had slipped past the walls. And as he held his sister, he heard it again, the dry, persistent cough, a sound that was no longer a worry, but a clear and present alarm.

The sound, so slight at first, a mere catch in the breath, now returned with a dry, racking force that shook her small frame in his arms; it was the signal of a foe that had not been turned back at the door, but had entered with them, a silent and unwelcome guest. He held her until the paroxysm passed, his own breath held in sympathy, his face set in lines of grim anxiety. She fell back against his shoulder, exhausted and trembling.

"Come, little sister," he said, his voice striving for a lightness he was far from feeling, "this has been too long a day for you. Your bed is waiting."

He lifted her, blanket and all, as easily as he had done at Shahweetah; but the thought came to him with a pang that she felt even lighter now, more fragile. He carried her through the little entry into the room he had prepared. It was small, and very plain; a narrow bed, a single chair, a little washstand with its white bowl and pitcher. But he had made it as neat as hands could make it; the bed was turned down, and a small, brightly coloured rug, his one extravagance, lay beside it on the bare floor. He laid her down gently upon the clean sheets.

"You must get to sleep now, Winnie," he said, tucking the covers round her. "Tomorrow everything will look brighter."

She caught his hand and held it to her hot cheek. "Don't leave me, Governor. I'm afraid in the dark."

"I am just in the next room," he said softly. "The door shall stay open. You can call me if you want for anything."

"Will you stay 'till I am asleep?" she whispered, her eyes, large and dark in her pale face, fixed upon him with a painful intensity.

"I will," he promised.

He drew the chair to the bedside and sat down, still holding her hand in his. The fire in the other room cast a flickering, uncertain light through the doorway, making strange shapes dance upon the walls. The city, which had seemed so loud before, was now sinking into a deeper quiet; only the distant rumble of a cart, or the mournful cry of a watchman, broke the silence. He sat motionless, his thoughts a troubled sea. The responsibility he had taken upon himself felt like a physical weight, crushing the very hope out of him. He thought of his father's letter, of the mortgage, of the counting house and the patient, subtle web of deceit he was charged to untangle. And through it all, like a terrible, tolling bell, he heard the echo of that cough.

Winnie's breathing grew softer, more regular; her grip on his hand relaxed. He waited a long while, until he was sure she was deeply asleep, and then gently disengaged his fingers. He stood for a moment looking down at her, at the feverish spots on her cheeks and the dark lashes lying upon them. A prayer, wordless and full of anguish, rose from his heart. Then he went back to his own room, not to sleep, but to keep his watch. He replenished the fire, and settling himself on the hard settee, prepared for a long night. He could not trust himself to bed; he must be ready, should she call, should the cough return. The hours crept by, each one marked by the striking of a distant church clock. The fire burned down to embers, and the room grew cold, but he did not notice. He sat with his head in his hands, facing the wilderness he had entered, and knew that the wrestling had truly begun.

The morning came, grey and cheerless, with a raw wind that rattled the window sashes. Winthrop, who had not slept at all, rose stiffly and set about rebuilding the fire. He made a pot of coffee, his movements quiet so as not to disturb his sister. When the room was warm, he went to her door and looked in. She was awake, and had been for some time, he thought; she lay quite still, watching the pale light grow in the window.

"Good morning, Winnie," he said, with an attempt at cheerfulness.

"Is it morning?" she answered, in a voice that was tired and fretful. "It's so dark. I hate this place."

He came and sat on the edge of her bed. He laid his hand on her forehead; it was still too warm. "It is only a cloudy day," he said. "The sun will shine by and by. Are you ready for some breakfast?"

"I don't want any," she said, turning her face away.

"Just a little tea, then, and a piece of toast? I will make it for you here, by our own fire."

She did not answer, and he took her silence for consent. He went back to the other room and prepared a small tray for her with great care. But when he brought it, she would only take a few sips of the tea, and pushed the rest away.

"I can't eat, Governor. My throat is sore."

His heart sank. "Winnie, I must go to the counting house to-day. It is very important. Will you be all right here alone for a few hours? You can lie in bed and rest."

Her face crumpled, and tears welled in her eyes. "Alone? O Winthrop, don't leave me alone! I can't bear it. I shall die if you leave me alone in this great, horrid place!"

Her distress was so real, so vehement, that it swept away all his resolutions. He could no more have left her then than he could have struck her.

"Hush, hush, dear," he said, gathering her into his arms. "Don't cry so. Of course I will not leave you. I will not go to-day. There, now; be quiet. Nothing shall harm you."

He soothed her as one would a frightened child, until her sobs subsided into weary sighs. It was plain she could not be left. But what was he to do? His duty to Mr. Gryce was clear; the work he had begun was of the utmost consequence, not only to his employer, but to his own prospects. To fail at this point might be to lose his position altogether. He was caught in a cruel trap.

An idea came to him. Mrs. Nettley. She was a kind woman; she had a motherly heart. Perhaps she would help him.

"Winnie," he said, after a time. "I have a thought. Mrs. Nettley, the good lady who owns the house, lives just downstairs. What if I were to ask her to come and sit with you while I am gone? She is very kind. You would not be alone."

Winnie considered this. The terror in her eyes lessened a little. "Is she like Karen?" she asked doubtfully.

"Not much like Karen," Winthrop admitted with a faint smile. "But she is good. Shall I ask her?"

"If she will stay in the room with me," Winnie stipulated. "I don't want to be by myself."

"I will ask her."

He made Winnie as comfortable as he could, and then went downstairs, his hopes resting on this one slender chance. He found Mrs. Nettley in her kitchen, which was a marvel of warmth and savoury smells. She was at a large wooden table, her hands deep in a bowl of flour. She looked up as he entered, her round, pleasant face expressing surprise.

"Why, Mr. Landholm! Good morning to you. You are about early, or is it that I am late? I did not hear you go out."

"I have not been out yet, Mrs. Nettley," he said, coming to a halt near the door. "I came to ask a great favour of you."

"A favour? Well, ask away," she said, wiping her hands on her apron. "If it's in my power to grant, it is granted already. Is something amiss?" Her quick eyes had noted his pale, tired face.

"It is my sister," he began, and found his voice unsteady. "She is not well. She took a chill on the journey, I think, and the city frightens her. She is not fit to be left alone, and I must go to my work." He hesitated, feeling a man's reluctance to expose his troubles. "I thought, perhaps, if it were not too great a burden, you might be willing to sit with her for a few hours."

"Not well? The poor, dear child!" Mrs. Nettley's face was all compassionate concern. "Bless her heart, of course I will. A burden? Fiddlesticks. It is no burden to help a neighbour. I will come up as soon as ever I get these loaves in the oven. You go and tell her not to worry. Does she have a cough?"

"Yes," Winthrop said, his throat tightening. "She has a cough."

Mrs. Nettley looked at him sharply. "And a fever?"

"I am afraid so."

"Dear me, dear me," she murmured, shaking her head. "This city air is a trial for country lungs. You must have the doctor to her, Mr. Landholm. You can't be too careful with a cough like that."

"I will," he said, though the thought of a doctor's bill sent a fresh wave of anxiety through him. "Thank you, Mrs. Nettley. You are very good."

"Nonsense," she said briskly. "Now you run along to your business, and don't you worry. I will look after the little lamb as if she were my own. George can fetch the doctor on his way home, if you like. We use Doctor Armitage, in Warren Street. A very sensible man."

Winthrop returned upstairs with a heart somewhat lightened. Winnie was relieved to hear that she would have company, and submitted to being settled on the settee by the fire while Winthrop made ready to leave. He gave Mrs. Nettley's message to George, a quiet, pale young man with ink-stains on his fingers, whom he met on the stairs. Then, with a last look at his sister, who was watching him with her great, sad eyes, he went out into the raw spring day.

The work at the counting house was absorbing, and for a few hours, Winthrop was able to push his private cares into the background. He laid before Mr. Gryce the clear evidence of Mr. Tyler's double-dealing, the false entries and the altered invoices. The old merchant listened in silence, his brow growing darker and darker.

"I suspected as much," he said at last, when Winthrop had finished. "But I could not prove it. You have done well, Landholm. Very well. You have a clear head and an honest eye. We will deal with Mr. Tyler this afternoon. This is a great service you have done the firm."

The praise was gratifying, but Winthrop's mind was already flying back to the little attic room. As soon as he was released, he hurried home, his feet moving faster and faster as he neared the house. He took the stairs two at a time, his heart pounding with a nameless dread.

He opened the door to his room. Mrs. Nettley was there, sitting in a rocking-chair he had not seen before, knitting quietly. The room was tidy and peaceful. Winnie was on the settee, propped up with pillows, but she was asleep.

"Hush," Mrs. Nettley whispered, putting a finger to her lips. "She has just dropped off. The doctor has been."

Winthrop's eyes flew to her face. "What did he say?"

Mrs. Nettley put down her knitting and motioned for him to step out into the entry. "He is a plain-spoken man, Mr. Landholm," she began, her kind face full of pity. "He says her lungs are very weak. Very delicate. He called it consumption."

The word, though he had feared it, struck Winthrop like a physical blow. He leaned against the wall for support, the world seeming to tilt and grey out before his eyes. Consumption. The slow, wasting sickness that had carried off so many. The wilderness had a name now.

"He said she must have complete rest," Mrs. Nettley went on, her voice low and gentle. "And the very best of nourishment; milk, and beef-tea, and eggs. And she should have fresh air, country air, if it were possible." She stopped, knowing as well as he did how impossible that was. "He left a prescription for a tonic, to strengthen her. And another for a syrup to soothe the cough. I sent George to have them filled."

"Thank you," Winthrop said. The words were mechanical; his mind was a whirl of confusion and despair. Country air. Nourishing food. A doctor. Medicines. It all meant money. Money he did not have. The small sum left in his purse would scarcely cover the cost of the medicines, let alone the doctor's fee. And beyond that, lay a barren waste. The farm was mortgaged; his father was helpless. He was alone.

"Now, don't you lose heart," Mrs. Nettley said, laying a comforting hand on his arm. "We will do the best we can for her, right here. She is a sweet child. She talked to me a little, about her home, and about you. She calls you 'Governor'. She sets a world of store by you, Mr. Landholm."

He could not speak. He felt the woman's kindness as a sharp pain. He nodded, and turning, went back into the room and stood looking down at his sleeping sister. Her breathing was shallow, and a faint flush was still on her cheeks. She looked so young, so helpless. This was his charge, his sacred trust. The sturdy tree, round which the poor vine was to throw its tendrils. And the tree itself was being shaken to its very roots.

Later, when Mrs. Nettley had gone downstairs to see to her own family's supper, George came up with the medicines. He was a shy youth, who rarely spoke, but he looked at Winthrop with a sympathy that was more eloquent than words. He handed him the paper-wrapped bottles, and a folded piece of paper. It was the doctor's bill.

Winthrop waited until he was alone. Then, with a hand that was not quite steady, he opened it. The sum was not exorbitant, but to him, it was a fortune. It was more than he had left in the world. He sank down on the settee, the paper in his hand, and stared into the fire. The flames leaped and danced, a picture of cheer he could not feel. He was face to face with a wall he could not see how to climb. The wrestling of this world askith a fall. He had been so sure of his own strength, so confident in his ability to protect her. Now, his strength was as nothing. He was beaten before he had even begun to fight. And in the next room, Winnie coughed in her sleep, a small, sad sound that tore at his heart and sealed his despair. He must have money, and he must have it now. But from whence was it to come? The question echoed in the desolate chambers of his heart, and found no answer.

The paper rustled in his hand, a flimsy record of a debt that felt as heavy as a millstone around his neck. Whence was it to come? The question was not a whisper now, but a roar, filling the silence of the little attic room, mocking the cheerful crackle of the fire he had so painstakingly built. He laid the bill upon the mantelpiece, as if to put its demand out of sight, but he could not put it out of mind. It stood there, a white spectre of his own powerlessness.

He crossed the floor and knelt by the settee, looking not at the fire but at the face of his sleeping sister. The fever-flush had somewhat subsided, leaving a delicate, pearly transparency on her skin. Her hair, the colour of pale corn-silk, was strayed upon the dark calico of the cushion; a few fine threads clung to her damp forehead. One small hand was curled loosely near her cheek, the other lay upon her breast, rising and falling with the faint, irregular breaths that were at once the sign of her life and the measure of its fragility. His charge. His poor little vine. And he, the tree she clung to, was himself facing the axe.

A deep, shuddering sigh escaped him. It was not the sigh of yielding, but of a spirit gathering itself for a struggle against desperate odds. Despair was a luxury he could not afford. While Winnie lived and breathed and needed him, he had no right to it. His strength was not his own to give up; it belonged to her. He must find a way. If there was no path, he must make one.

Rising with a new, though grim, determination, he went to his small writing-desk. It was a plain pine table, home-made, and held his few law books, a sheaf of papers from his studies, an inkstand, and a pen. His work. The slow, patient path he had set for himself towards a future of competence and respectability. That future seemed now a distant, unattainable dream. He needed money not in years, but in hours.

He thought of the men he knew in the city. The grave and portly lawyers in whose offices he studied; they moved in a world of wealth and influence far removed from his. He could not go to them, a beggar. He thought of his fellow students; they were, for the most part, as poor as himself, struggling with their own burdens. And Mrs. Nettley, kind as she was, her own family's needs were a constant tax on her small resources. He would not add his weight to her load.

There was no one. The knowledge fell upon him with the cold finality of a stone. He was thrown back utterly upon himself. And what were his resources? A strong back, a willing mind, and two hands. It was with these he must fight his battle. His law studies must be put aside, or at least, made secondary. The immediate, pressing need was for labour that would yield a wage, however small.

He put on his coat again, softly, so as not to wake the sleeper in the next room. He must go out now, at once, while the resolve was hot in him. He knew not where he was going; only that he must go. He would walk the streets, he would knock on doors, he would offer himself for any honest work, be it ever so menial. He, Winthrop Landholm, whose pride was the equal of his poverty, would learn to labour with his hands like any porter on the docks. The thought sent a flush of heat to his cheek, but he set his jaw. Pride must be laid on the altar of love.

He looked once more at Winnie, a silent prayer in his heart, and then stole from the room, down the narrow stairs, and out into the raw spring evening. The snow had ceased, but the air was sharp and damp, and the wind that swept up from the river cut through his worn coat. The streets were emptying, the business of the day drawing to a close. Gas lamps were being lit, casting a yellow, uncertain light upon the wet cobblestones. He walked on, with a quick, nervous step, his eyes scanning the signs of the shops and warehouses he passed. 'Importer'. 'Chandler'. 'Printer'. Where in this vast, humming hive of commerce was there a place for him?

His steps, almost without his conscious direction, had led him towards the darker, more crowded streets near the printing houses. He knew a man there, or rather, knew of him; a Mr. Grice, who ran a small, busy press. It was said he was often in need of hands for odd jobs, collating pages, running errands, cleaning the machinery. It was not the work of a gentleman, but necessity had stripped him of that title. He found the place, a grimy brick building from which the clatter of machinery still sounded, though it was past the supper hour. Light shone from the lower windows. Taking a breath that was half courage and half resignation, he pushed open the heavy door and entered.

The air inside was thick with the smell of ink and oil and damp paper. A burly man with a leather apron and ink-smeared arms looked up from a press. "Well? What is it?" he asked, his voice rough but not unkind.

"I am looking for work," Winthrop said, his own voice sounding strange and formal in the noisy room. "I heard that you sometimes have need of an extra hand."

The man, who was Mr. Grice himself, wiped his hands on a rag and looked him over, from his gentleman's boots, though they were worn, to his scholar's face. "You don't look the sort for this work. It's dirty, and it's hard."

"I am not afraid of dirt or of hard work," Winthrop replied steadily, meeting the man's gaze. "I need the employment. I will do whatever is asked of me, and do it well."

There was something in his tone, a quiet desperation held in check by a powerful will, that seemed to impress the printer. He grunted. "Got a job needs doin' right now. Collating a pamphlet. Thousand copies. Got to be ready for the morning mail. The boy who does it took sick. It's tedious work, and it pays little. A dollar for the lot."

A dollar. The sum seemed at once pitifully small and a gift from heaven. It would not pay the doctor's bill, but it would buy food. It would buy more fuel. It was a start. "I will do it," he said.

"Come on, then," said Mr. Grice, leading him to the back of the shop. A vast mountain of printed sheets lay on a long table. "Here it is. Ten pages to a pamphlet. You stack 'em in order, one through ten, and pile 'em neat. Mind you get it right. A mistake makes the whole thing worthless."

He showed Winthrop the method, his thick, inky fingers moving with surprising deftness. Then he left him to it, and returned to the clamour of his press. Winthrop took off his coat, rolled up his sleeves, and began. The work was as monotonous as the printer had promised. Page one, page two, page three, his hands moved in a steady rhythm, taking a sheet from each pile, creating a small, ordered stack. His back began to ache from stooping over the table. His eyes grew tired in the dim light. But he did not pause. With each pamphlet he completed, he thought, 'This is a loaf of bread'. With each hundred, 'This is a dose of medicine for Winnie'. The thought was a spur that drove out weariness.

It was late when he finished. The press had fallen silent. Mr. Grice was cleaning his tools, preparing to close up for the night. He came over and inspected Winthrop's work, flipping through a few of the collated pamphlets to check the order.

"Aye, you've done it right," he said, with a nod of approval. "And quicker than the boy. You've got a steady hand." He reached into his pocket and pulled out a silver dollar, worn and heavy. He laid it in Winthrop's palm. "If you want more of the same, I've got a big job of copying coming in a few days. Legal documents. Need a fair hand for it. Pays better than this."

"Copying?" Winthrop's heart gave a leap. This was work for which his training had suited him. "I can do that. My handwriting is very clear."

"Aye, I'd guess it is," the printer said, glancing again at his face. "It's night work. Can you do night work?"

"I can," Winthrop said, without a moment's hesitation.

"Be here Thursday evening, then. We'll see how you take to it."

Winthrop thanked him, his heart lighter than it had been in many days. He put on his coat and stepped out into the night. The streets were quiet now, almost deserted. He walked home, the silver dollar clutched in his hand, a talisman against despair. He had a foothold. He had found the first rough step in the wall he had to climb.

He let himself into the house as quietly as a shadow. A faint line of light still showed under the door of their room. He opened it softly. The fire had burned down to a deep, glowing bed of coals. Mrs. Nettley was sitting in a chair beside the settee, her knitting lying idle in her lap. She had been waiting for him. She put a finger to her lips and rose, her movements careful and silent.

"She woke a while ago," the good woman whispered, joining him by the door. "She was asking for you. I gave her some broth, and the soothing syrup the doctor ordered. It has quieted her cough a good deal. She fell back to sleep not ten minutes since."

"Thank you, Mrs. Nettley," he whispered back, his voice thick with gratitude. "You are too good. You should not have stayed."

"Nonsense," she said softly. "The child shouldn't be left alone. She is a bit fretful, poor dear, when she wakes. She frets for you. She said, 'The Governor will be back soon, won't he?'. Her heart is bound up in you, Mr. Landholm. You must take care of yourself, for her sake."

He could only nod, his throat too tight for words. She gave his arm a gentle pat and slipped out of the room, leaving him alone with his sister. He went and stood over her for a long moment. Even in sleep, a slight frown creased her brow, as if her dreams were troubled. He reached out and smoothed the lines away with his thumb. Her skin was cool now. The medicine had done its work. He had the means to buy more. He had a promise of more work. It was not a victory, not yet, but it was a reprieve.

He did not sleep for a long time. He sat by the fire, his law books open before him, but his mind was not on the intricacies of torts and contracts. He was thinking of the mortgage on the farm, of his father's weary sigh, of the long, hard road that lay before him and his sister. The wrestling of this worlde askith a fall. He had stumbled, but he had not fallen. Not yet. He looked from his book to his sister's peaceful face, and a grim strength settled in his heart. For her, he would wrestle with the world, and, by the grace of God, he would not be thrown.

The next few days established a new and difficult routine. By day, Winthrop devoted himself to Winnie. He kept the fire bright, he prepared the simple, nourishing food she required, he read to her from the few books they possessed, or simply sat by her, his presence a silent comfort that seemed to soothe her more than any medicine. Her strength did not return quickly; she was often weak and listless, her former childish energy all gone. But the fever abated, and the terrible cough was eased by the doctor's syrup. She would lie for hours on the settee, her large, solemn eyes following her brother's every move.

"You are a good nurse, Governor," she said one afternoon, a faint smile touching her lips. "Almost as good as mother was."

The words sent a pang through his heart, but he answered cheerfully. "I am learning the trade, Winnie. When you are well, perhaps we can open a hospital together."

She shook her head. "I don't want a hospital. I just want to stay here with you." Her smile faded, and the fretful look returned. "Don't go out again to-night, Winthrop. Stay with me."

"I must go out for a little while, Winnie," he said gently. "But I will be back before you are long asleep."

He did not tell her where he went. He did not tell her that when he left her in the care of the watchful Mrs. Nettley, he was walking to the printing house to spend half the night under a single gas lamp, his fingers growing cramped and his eyes burning as he copied endless pages of legal text in his clear, precise hand. Mr. Grice was a man of his word. The work was there, and the pay, though small, was steady. Each night he came home with a few more coins in his pocket; coins that meant medicine, and food, and fuel.

The labour was a tremendous drain upon his strength. He slept little, and his own studies were all but forgotten. He grew thin, and a perpetual weariness settled in his bones. But he did not complain. When he was with Winnie, he forced a cheerfulness he did not feel, and her slowly returning health was his reward.

One evening, a letter arrived. It was from his father, and the sight of the familiar, laboured handwriting filled him with a fresh sense of dread. He waited until Winnie was asleep before he broke the seal. He read it by the light of his single candle.

'My dear Son,' it began, 'I hope this finds you and little Winnie in good health. The spring is cold here. There is little news to tell. Asahel is well and works hard. The matter I write of is the one that sits heavy on my heart. I have had a letter from Mr. Haye's lawyer. He is asking for the interest, which you know I have not been able to pay. He says if it is not paid by the first of June, he will have no choice but to foreclose on the mortgage. I see no way out of it, son. I have tried to raise the money, but there is none to be had. I think the time has come. I must sell the farm. It breaks my heart to think of it, the home your mother loved. But I see no other path. Perhaps it is for the best. Asahel and I can go West, as we have talked of. Do not trouble your mind with it. You have your own burdens to bear with the dear child. I pray for you both, that the Lord will watch over you. Your affectionate Father.'

Winthrop read the letter twice, then let it fall to the table. Foreclose. Sell the farm. The words were the death-knell of his home. The money had been borrowed for him, for his education. The weight of that fact was a crushing addition to his load. He had meant for his education to be the means of lifting his family up; instead, it had become the instrument of their ruin.

He bowed his head in his hands. The weariness of his body and the agony of his mind combined in a wave of utter hopelessness that was almost physical pain. He felt trapped, assailed on all sides by demands he could not meet. Winnie's illness. The doctor's bill. The daily cost of living. And now this, the loss of his home, a catastrophe he was powerless to prevent. He had been toiling for dollars, when hundreds were needed. His nightly labour, which had seemed a triumph, was now revealed as a pitiful, inadequate effort, like a man trying to bail out a sinking ship with his hands.

A small sound from the next room broke through his dark thoughts. A cough, followed by a sleepy murmur. "Governor?"

He rose instantly, his own trouble forgotten. He went into Winnie's little room. She was sitting up in bed, her eyes wide with a half-asleep fear.

"I am here, Winnie," he said softly, sitting on the edge of her bed and taking her hand. "It is all right. Go back to sleep."

"I had a bad dream," she whispered, her fingers clinging to his. "I dreamed we were lost, and I couldn't find you."

"It was only a dream," he soothed, stroking her hair. "I am right here. I will not leave you."

She lay back, her eyes fixed on his face, her hand still holding fast to his. In a few moments, her breathing grew deep and regular again. She was asleep. He sat there for a long time in the darkness, the pressure of her small hand a steady, tangible thing in his. He was not lost. She was his anchor. The farm, his future, his own weariness, all of it must be subordinate to the one great duty that lay before him. He must care for this child. He did not know how he would find the money for the mortgage. He did not know how he would continue to bear his double burden of work. He knew only that he must. As he sat there, in the cold and the dark, watching over his sister, the fortress of his resolve, which had crumbled in a moment of despair, began to build itself up again, stone by painful stone. He would not fail her. He could not.

He rose, and dressing quietly in the chill grey light, he set about his morning tasks, his mind a fortress newly built against the despair that had threatened to overwhelm it. He made the fire, not with the eager crackle of the first evening, but with a steady, practiced hand; it was no longer a promise of comfort, but a simple necessity, another duty to be performed. He made a simple breakfast of oatmeal and milk, and when Winnie came out, rubbing her eyes, he had a smile for her that cost him more than all his night's toil.

"Is it a new day, Governor?" she asked, climbing onto her usual place on the settee.

"Yes, a brand new one, Winnie; full of possibilities," he said, and his own words sounded strange to him, for he could see only one possibility, and that was work.

She ate her breakfast quietly, her eyes following his every movement. She had learned to read the weather of his face, and though he smiled, she saw the clouds behind his eyes.

"Is father not well?" she asked presently.

"He is well, dear; only worried, as we all are sometimes."

"Is it about the farm?"

Winthrop paused in the act of putting his papers in order. "What do you know about the farm, little one?"

"I heard you," she said, her voice dropping to a whisper. "Last night. I was not all asleep. The money for you and Rufus."

He came and sat beside her, taking her small hand in his. It felt as hot and dry as his had been cold the night before. "You must not trouble your head with such things, Winnie. It is my care, not yours."

"But I am your sister," she said, with a flash of the old spirit. "And it was for you. And if I am a burden, Governor -- "

"Hush," he said, more sternly than he intended, for the word struck him like a blow. "You are never a burden, Winnie. You are my blessing. Do not ever say that again."

She was silent, but her lip trembled. He softened at once, drawing her into his arms. "Forgive me, dear. I am tired. We will not talk of it. We will talk of what you will do to-day, while I am gone. Mrs. Nettley has a picture book she thought you might like to see."

He left her with that promise, and went out into the city's cold embrace. But his mind was not on his daily drudgery at the counting-house. His father's letter had changed everything. His nightly copying, which brought in a pittance, would never suffice. He needed more, much more, and he needed it at once. He had heard men speak of lawyers who paid well for swift, accurate work, for documents that must be copied with perfect precision and in great haste. It was a market he had not dared to venture into, for the standards were exacting and the penalties for error severe. But he had no choice. Desperation was a hard master, and it drove him now.

During his hour of dinner, instead of eating the piece of bread he had brought, he made his way to a street of tall, grim-looking buildings, where brass plates bearing the names of legal firms were affixed to every door. He chose one at random, a name he seemed to recall: "Vancott & Hayes, Attorneys at Law." With a prayer on his lips, he mounted the stone steps.

The outer office was a place of subdued industry. Two young clerks sat on high stools, scratching away with their pens in enormous ledgers. They looked up with indifferent curiosity as Winthrop entered.

"I wish to inquire if there is any copying work to be had," he said, addressing the elder of the two.

The clerk, a young man with a permanently sour expression, looked him up and down. "Mr. Vancott is not in need of any new hands," he said shortly, and bent his head to his work again, as if the matter were closed.

Winthrop stood his ground. "I am a swift and accurate copyist," he said, his voice quiet but firm. "If you have any work that is pressing, I should be glad to undertake it on a trial basis, for any price you think it worth."

The clerk was about to make another dismissive reply when an inner door opened, and a tall, elderly gentleman with sharp grey eyes and a fringe of white hair stood on the threshold.

"What is it, Peterson?" the gentleman asked, his voice crisp.

"A person asking for work, Mr. Vancott. I told him we had none."

Mr. Vancott's keen eyes rested on Winthrop. He took in the worn but clean coat, the intelligent face, the air of quiet determination. "You are a copyist?" he said, directing the question to Winthrop.

"Yes, sir."

"And you are swift and accurate, you say?"

"I believe so, sir. I should be glad of the opportunity to prove it."

Mr. Vancott considered him for a moment. "Peterson, give him that brief from the Ellison case. The one you have been complaining of for a week." He turned to Winthrop. "It is forty pages. I must have it by nine o'clock to-morrow morning, without a single error. Not a misplaced comma, not a mistaken word. If you can do it, bring it to me here. If you cannot, do not trouble to return."

He disappeared back into his office, closing the door behind him. The clerk, Peterson, with an ill grace, pulled a thick sheaf of papers from a drawer and thrust it into Winthrop's hands.

"Forty pages," he sneered. "You'll be up all night."

"I am used to being up all night," Winthrop said simply, and taking the papers, he left the office.

He held in his hands both a chance and a terrible new burden. He returned to his own desk for the afternoon's work, his mind already planning the hours of the night. He would have to work faster than ever before. He dared not think of failure.

When he reached home that evening, the lamp was lit and a cheerful fire was burning. Winnie sat on a low stool at Mrs. Nettley's feet, listening with rapt attention as the good woman read from the promised picture book.

"And so the little rabbit hopped all the way home to his mother," Mrs. Nettley finished, and looked up with a smile as Winthrop came in. "Well, here is the Governor now, Winnie-love. Your brother looks tired to death."

"I am well, Mrs. Nettley, thank you," he said, grateful for the warmth and for her kindness to his sister.

"He works too hard," Mrs. Nettley said, partly to Winthrop and partly to the room at large, as she gathered her things. "A young man can't burn the candle at both ends forever without being burnt up himself. Now, I've left a bit of broth for Winnie's supper, and there's some for you too, Mr. Landholm. You see that you eat it."

She bustled out, leaving a wake of motherly concern behind her. Winthrop ate the broth, knowing he would need the strength, and then he set Winnie her own small supper.

"Did you have a good day, dear?" he asked.

"Oh yes! Mrs. Nettley is so kind. And George came up to bring me the book. He told me a story about a ship. He said he would build me a little model of one, if I liked." Her face was animated, her eyes bright with a pleasure that had been a stranger to them for too long. Winthrop felt a pang of gratitude so sharp it was almost pain.

When Winnie was settled in her bed, he drew his table close to the lamp, trimmed the wick, and set out his materials. The brief from Mr. Vancott was written in a cramped, difficult hand, full of legal jargon and Latin phrases. It was as different from his usual straightforward copying as a tangled forest is from a straight road. He set his jaw and began.

The hours passed. The fire died down to embers, and the room grew cold. The city outside, which roared all day, fell into a profound silence, broken only by the distant cry of a watchman or the rumble of a lone cart. Winthrop wrote on, his pen flying across the page. His head grew heavy, and his eyes burned from the strain of focusing on the tiny script by the weak lamplight. His fingers cramped, and he had to pause to flex them, the joints aching in protest. The weight of his fatigue was a physical thing, pressing down on his shoulders. But every time his head nodded, the image of his father's letter, of the farm under threat, of Winnie's trusting face, rose before him and drove him on. He was not just copying words; he was fighting for his home. He was building a wall of ink and paper against disaster.

At some point deep in the night, a soft sound made him look up. The door to Winnie's room was ajar, and she was standing there, a small white-gowned figure in the shadows.

"Governor?" she whispered. "Are you not coming to bed?"

"Not yet, little one. I have work to finish. Go back, or you will catch a chill."

She came a step further into the room. "You are always working," she said, and there was a note of sorrowful complaint in her voice. "Will it always be like this?"

He rose and went to her, kneeling so their eyes were level. "I hope not, Winnie. I am working so that one day it will not be like this. So that we may have a home again, a real one, with fields and sky. Now, you must go back to sleep. For me."

He tucked her back into her bed, his heart aching. Her simple question had pierced the armour of his resolve, reminding him that in his frantic efforts to provide for her future, he was sacrificing their present. He returned to his desk with a heavier heart, but a will no less determined.

The first grey light of dawn was filtering through the grimy windowpane when he wrote the last word. He had done it. Forty pages, copied without a blot, without a single correction. He read it over one last time, his eyes gritty with exhaustion, checking it against the original. It was perfect.

He did not sleep. He washed his face in the cold water from the basin, ate a piece of dry bread, and went to his day's work, the precious packet of papers buttoned securely inside his coat. He moved like a man in a dream, his body numb with weariness, but his mind was sharp and clear, fixed on the nine o'clock appointment with Mr. Vancott.

At the appointed hour, he was once again in the lawyer's office. The clerk Peterson cast a look of disbelief at him as he entered. "You've finished it?" he asked, a note of incredulity in his voice.

"I have," Winthrop said. "I am here to see Mr. Vancott."

He was shown in at once. The lawyer was seated at a large desk covered in papers. He looked up, his grey eyes as sharp as they had been the day before.

"So, you have returned," he said. It was a statement, not a question. "Let me see it."

Winthrop laid his copy on the desk. Mr. Vancott picked it up. He did not read it; he seemed to absorb it. For ten minutes, there was no sound in the room but the rustle of paper as he turned the pages, his eyes scanning back and forth between Winthrop's copy and the original brief. He laid a ruler on the page to check the margins. He held a page up to the light, as if to judge the very quality of the ink.

At last, he laid the two documents down. He looked at Winthrop. "It is well done," he said. There was no warmth in his voice, only a grudging respect. "It is more than well done. It is perfect. Peterson has been with me for three years, and he could not have done this in a week."

He reached into a drawer and took out a small canvas bag. He counted out several silver coins, more than Winthrop had dared to hope for, nearly a week's wages from his other job. He pushed them across the desk.

"That is for your night's work," he said. "Now, I have another proposition. I am engaged in a suit which requires the collation of a great many documents. It is a vast undertaking. There are three hundred pages here," he said, gesturing to a formidable stack of papers on a side table, "and I must have them in a fortnight. The pay will be commensurate with the task. Winthrop looked at the stack of papers. It was a mountain. To climb it would mean more sleepless nights, more exhaustion, a deeper descent into the mines of toil. It would mean scarcely seeing his sister, scarcely living at all outside the circle of his lamplight. But the coins on the desk before him were real. They were a weapon. And three hundred pages meant a sum of money that could, perhaps, meet the interest on the mortgage. It could save the farm.

His weariness fell away, replaced by a surge of desperate energy. "Yes, sir," he said, his voice clear and steady. "I can do it."

"It is good for you to have a friend, Winnie," he said gently. "Mr. Collins is a good man." "He brought me the rose," she said, touching the delicate petals with a reverent finger. "He said he saw it in a florist's window, and it looked as lonely as he thought I must be." From that day, a subtle change began. George's visits became an accepted part of their life. He would come in the dead middle of the afternoon, when Winnie's spirits were at their lowest, and read to her for an hour. Sometimes he would bring his sketchbook and show her his drawings; delicate, fanciful things, pictures of forest glades and strange, beautiful flowers that grew only in his imagination. He talked to her not of duty or fortitude, but of beauty and of dreams. He opened a window for her, not onto the noisy street, but into another world. Winthrop watched this new friendship with a quiet, profound thankfulness. He saw that George's gentle companionship was a better medicine for Winnie's ailing spirit than anything he, with his anxious, practical love, could provide. It eased the burden on his own heart, and it lightened the atmosphere in their little home. Winnie still clung to him as her rock and her refuge, but she was no longer a vine with but a single support. A second, more delicate tendril had found a new place to hold, and it gave her a little more breadth of life. The peace that this brought to Winthrop's mind allowed him to give his work an even more undivided attention. He had need of it. Mr. Vancott, seeing his clerk's unwavering discretion and faultless accuracy, began to rely on him more and more. One afternoon, the lawyer called him into his private office. "Mr. Landholm," he began, without preamble. "You are aware of the Schuyler estate case?" Winthrop nodded. It was one of the most complex and contentious cases the firm had handled in years, involving a vast fortune and a bitter dispute between two branches of a very old and powerful family. "The final dispositions are being drawn up," Mr. Vancott continued. "The documents are of a nature that I do not wish any of the general clerks to see them. Not a word, not a figure, must be known outside this room. I will be dictating them to you directly. You will make the rough copy, and then you will produce the fair copy for signature. Both copies will remain in your personal keeping until the matter is concluded. Do you understand the trust I am placing in you?" "I do, sir," said Winthrop, his face grave. "It is a greater trust, Mr. Landholm, than I have placed in any clerk in twenty years. Do not fail it." "I will not, sir." For the next several days, Winthrop worked as he had never worked before. For hours on end, he sat in the lawyer's silent, book-lined office, his pen flying across the page as Mr. Vancott dictated in his dry, even voice. The details of the case were intricate, the sums of money staggering. It was a world of wealth and power utterly alien to him, and yet he was at its very heart, the quiet, humble instrument through which its fate was being recorded. He kept the rough drafts locked in the small desk that was his own, and at night, he carried the key home with him, as if it were the key to a king's treasury. He spoke of it to no one, not even to Winnie. It was a wall of silence he had to build around a part of his life. One evening, as he was finishing the last page of the final, perfect copy, Mr. Vancott stood looking over his shoulder. The document was a masterpiece of calligraphy; clear, elegant, and without a single flaw. The lawyer was silent for a full minute, his sharp eyes scanning the page. Then he gave a short, almost imperceptible nod. "Good," he said. It was the highest praise he knew how to give. He walked over to a heavy iron safe in the corner of the room, and with a key from his own chain, he opened it. "Place the documents inside, Mr. Landholm," he said. "And then you may go home." Winthrop did as he was told, laid the precious papers in the dark recess of the safe, and locked the heavy door. As he turned to leave, Mr. Vancott spoke again. "Your salary will be increased by one third, commencing this month," he said, already turning back to his desk. "You have earned it." Winthrop walked home through the darkening streets of Mannahatta, his mind in a tumult. The extra money was a godsend; it would mean so much for Winnie's comfort, a little more warmth, a little better food, perhaps even a doctor's visit. But it was the trust, more than the money, that filled his heart. He, Winthrop Landholm, the poor farmer's son, had been weighed in the balance by this stern, exacting man of the world, and had not been found wanting. He had won his place in the great wilderness, not by favour, but by his own steady, uncomplaining toil. When he entered their room, the fire was burning brightly. Winnie was not alone. George was there, and they were not reading. Before them on the table was a sheet of paper and a piece of charcoal, and George was guiding Winnie's hand, showing her how to sketch the outline of the rose, which still stood in its glass, though its petals were beginning to fade. Winnie looked up as he entered, her face alight with the pleasure of creation. "Look, Governor!" she cried, holding up the paper. "Look what I am doing! George says I have a good eye." Winthrop looked at the unsteady, childish lines of the drawing, and then at his sister's eager, happy face. He looked at George, who gave him a quiet, understanding smile. And in that moment, in the little attic room high above the roaring city, he felt a peace so deep it was almost painful. The shadows had not fled away entirely; the night was not yet gone. But here and there, in the darkness, a few stars had begun to shine.
