The great screw of the press stood like a monument in the gathering twilight, its dark wood worn smooth with a generation of use. A steady, rhythmic creak was the only sound that broke the evening stillness, save for the low murmur of voices and the soft thud of apples being tipped from a basket. The lanterns, hung from a low beam and set upon an overturned crate, cast a warm circle of activity; their light caught the rough-hewn timbers of the barn in a golden tracery, left the high lofts in profoundest shadow, and turned the pile of waiting apples into a mound of rubies and jade. The sharp, sweet scent of the crushed fruit hung heavy in the air, a perfume more potent than any from a lady's bottle. The figures moving in and out of the light were themselves transformed by it, their ordinary shapes and labours lent a strange and fleeting poetry.

There was old Mr. Giles, his hands and forearms stained dark with juice, his face a map of wrinkles that the lantern-light threw into deep relief. By day he was merely the hired man, but now, as he leaned his weight against the press-arm, he might have been a grizzled Druid performing some ancient rite, or a stubborn old king defending his last fortress. There was the minister, Reverend Pym, who had wandered over from the parsonage, as he put it, "to observe the bounties of Providence firsthand." He hovered at the edge of the work, his round face shining with a mixture of sweat and self-satisfaction, his spectacles glinting so that one moment he looked owlishly wise and the next perfectly vacant. Young Thomas, whose only job was to haul the pulped apples away in a bucket, was simply a moving silhouette, a creature of shadow defined only by his endless, patient toil.

Eleanor had been watching them from the doorway for some minutes, her shawl pulled close about her shoulders, before she stepped into the light, feeling not unlike a captain coming onto the deck of her ship to inspect the crew. "Then you must teach me," she said, her voice clear and without a trace of the hesitancy that had so often marked her speech. "I will not be a hindrance. I will work."

For the first time since she had known him, something like a smile touched the corners of Mr. Davies's mouth, a brief, fleeting expression that he immediately concealed by turning his attention to the great wooden press. "Work is all there is to it," he said. "First we grind. The pomace has to be just so; not too fine to clog the cloths, not so coarse it won't give up its juice."

And so began a new labour, as rhythmic and demanding as the harvest itself. Mr. Davies showed her how to feed the apples, clean and sorted, into the hopper of the grinder, a heavy, iron-toothed machine turned by a long wooden handle. It was man's work, or so her former life would have told her; but she took her turn with Mr. Davies, her shoulder and back protesting as she forced the handle round and round, the sharp, sweet scent of crushed apples rising in a cloud around her. The pulped fruit, or pomace, as he called it, fell into a wide, shallow tub, a rich, fragrant mash. From there, they built the "cheese," a careful layering of the pomace within the folds of the coarse press-cloths, separated by slatted wooden racks, all inside the great slatted barrel of the press.

It was a slow and methodical art; a lesson in patience and precision. "Haste makes waste," Mr. Davies said, as he corrected her first attempt to pile the pomace too high. "You build it uneven, it'll burst the cloth when the pressure comes, and you'll have a mess to clean and a bushel of apples wasted."

Eleanor listened, and she learned. She learned the feel of the cloth, the right thickness for a layer of pomace, the careful way one must fold the corners to contain the pulp under tons of pressure. She worked until her arms were stained to the elbows and her back screamed in protest, but a grim satisfaction grew in her with every layer they built. Mrs. Gable came out to watch, bringing them bread and cheese for a midday meal they ate standing up, too engrossed in their task to stop. The housekeeper said little, but the approval in her eyes was as plain as if she had shouted it.

By mid afternoon, the press was built high, a great square stack of cloth and wood and apple pulp. The moment had come. Mr. Davies positioned the heavy pressing block on top of the stack and began to turn the great iron screw that descended from the crossbeam above. At first it turned easily, then with more resistance as it began to bite. A moment of silence, and then a faint trickle. A few drops of amber liquid, then a stream, then a dozen streams, began to run from the sides of the cheese, down the grooved channels of the press-bed, and into the waiting barrel below. The air filled with a scent so pure and sweet it was almost intoxicating; the very essence of autumn, distilled.

"There she is," Mr. Davies said, a deep satisfaction in his voice. "The fruit of your labour, Miss Eleanor. Literally."

Eleanor stood, mesmerized by the sight and the smell, a profound sense of accomplishment washing over her. It was more real, more satisfying, than any sonnet she had ever read or any watercolour she had ever painted. This was creation of a different sort; not an imitation of life, but life itself, coaxed and captured.

It was at this moment of quiet triumph that the sound of carriage wheels on the drive broke the spell. It was a light, fast-moving sound, utterly alien to the slow, heavy rhythm of the farm. Eleanor and Mr. Davies exchanged a look; he of puzzlement, she of a sudden, inexplicable dread. A smart, black-lacquered gig, drawn by a high-stepping bay, swept around the corner of the house and pulled to a halt with a flourish. At the reins was a young woman, her face shaded by a fashionable bonnet, who peered about her with an air of amused condescension.

"Eleanor! My dearest girl! Is this truly where you have buried yourself?" the voice called out, a bright, brittle sound that grated on the peaceful air.

Eleanor felt the blood drain from her face. It was her cousin, Isabel Vane.

Isabel descended from the gig before the stable boy could even run to assist, a vision of city elegance in her perfectly tailored travelling suit of dark green velvet. She picked her way across the yard as if navigating a minefield, her fine leather boots wholly unsuited to the mud and straw.

"Good heavens, Eleanor, what have you been doing?" she cried, stopping a few feet away and taking in Eleanor's appearance with wide, horrified eyes. "You are covered in… is that dirt? And your hands! They are ruined! You look like a common dairymaid."

Eleanor looked down at herself; at her stained dress, the apple pulp clinging to her sleeves, her red, chapped hands. She had not thought of her appearance all day. "We are pressing cider, Isabel," she said, her voice quiet.

"Pressing cider?" Isabel's laugh was a little trill of disbelief. "You? But darling, that is what servants are for. You have servants, do you not?" Her eyes flickered towards Mr. Davies, who had remained standing by the press, his expression unreadable, and dismissed him as part of the scenery. She saw it, for a fleeting instant, as a sunlit paradise, and her present life as a dark, toilsome pit.

She looked at her cousin's perfectly manicured hands, then at her own, stained and strong. She looked at Isabel's face, pretty and unlined by care, and then she looked at Mr. Davies, who had not moved, but whose entire being seemed to radiate a quiet, solid dignity. He was watching her, not with judgement, but with a kind of profound, waiting stillness. It was as if the farm itself were holding its breath, waiting for her answer.

"I am not bored, Isabel," Eleanor said, and the words, when they came, were as solid and steady as the great oak tree by the gate. "And I do not need rescuing."

Isabel stared, her smile faltering. "Not need… but Eleanor, be sensible! This is no life for a woman of your breeding. This is… grubby. And what is the point of it all? "I have a place here," Eleanor said. "Perhaps for the first time. Here, what I am is what I do. And today, I am making cider."

A silence fell, thick with misunderstanding. Isabel simply could not comprehend it. In her world, identity was a matter of birth and social connection; work was something other people did. To choose it, to prefer it, was a kind of madness.

"You are being stubborn," Isabel said, her tone shifting from playful to peevish. "It is this place. It has addled your wits. "Mr. Davies," Eleanor said, turning pointedly to him and ignoring her cousin, "the barrel is nearly full. Should we prepare another?"

The question was a declaration. It drew a line in the dirt of the farmyard, placing her firmly on one side, and Isabel on the other. Mr. Davies gave a slow nod, the barest hint of approval in his grey eyes.

"Aye, Miss Eleanor," he said, his voice cutting cleanly through the tension. "The pomace is still givin'. It'd be a shame to waste a drop."

Without another word to Isabel, Eleanor turned and walked to the cider house to fetch another clean barrel. The movement was decisive, final. She was a farmer. And the work was waiting.

Isabel stood for a moment, her mouth slightly agape, an island of velvet and vexation in a sea of rustic indifference. She had come to rescue a princess from a dragon's lair, only to find the princess had befriended the dragon and was now busily cataloguing its hoard. It was all very provoking. With a sharp sigh of irritation, she turned, marched back to her gig, and with a flick of the reins, was gone, a faint cloud of dust and expensive perfume the only evidence she had ever been there at all.

The sun was beginning to dip towards the horizon, painting the sky in hues of orange and rose, but there was still light enough to work. And tomorrow, they would do it all again.

"No," Fleda said slowly, treading carefully over a lattice of frozen roots. "No, it is what I have chosen. But the choosing, and the reality of it, are two very different things. When I lived in the city, my days were governed by calling hours and invitations. My greatest responsibility was answering my correspondence on time. Here..." She trailed off, gesturing to the silent, massive trees around them. "Here, my choices affect the land, and the animals, and the lives of the people who depend on me. A miscalculation is not a social embarrassment; it could mean losing a crop, or an animal, or the goodwill of a neighbour."
"It is a heavy weight," he said, his voice full of a sympathy that she found both comforting and a little unnerving.
"It is," she agreed. "But I find I would rather carry a heavy weight that is real, than a light one that is altogether trivial."
They heard the sound before they saw them; the steady, rhythmic chop of an axe, a sound that seemed to belong to the woods as much as the call of a crow. A moment later they came into a small clearing where the men were at work. Philetus and Hugh were using the long crosscut saw, its teeth biting into a fallen locust trunk with a rasping song, moving back and forth in a practiced, easy rhythm. A few yards away, Earl was trimming the branches from another log, his axe strokes falling with an economy and precision that was beautiful to watch. He would sight along the trunk, swing the axe, and a branch would leap away, leaving a clean, pale scar. The air smelled wonderfully of cold earth and fresh-cut wood.
The men paused when they saw them. Earl touched the brim of his hat. "Mornin', Miss Fleda. Mr. Olmney. Come to inspect the manufactory?"
"We came for the fine air, Earl, and to see your progress," Fleda said, smiling. "You have been busy."
"Got a good start," Earl said, leaning on his axe. He pointed to a growing pile of straight, clean posts, their ends sharpened to a rough point. "There's a day's work for you. That's good locust wood; it'll outlast the man who set it, I reckon. Ground can do what it likes to it, but the heartwood'll stay sound." He ran a hand over the pale, smooth wood where he had stripped the bark. "Honest wood."
Fleda looked at the posts, and then at the men. Their faces were flushed with exertion, their work direct and purposeful. They were not building a fence; they were making fence posts. The task was broken down into its essential, manageable parts. That, she realized, was how she must learn to see her new life; not as one overwhelming burden of care, but as a series of tasks, each one to be met with honesty and hard work.
"It looks like very good work," Mr. Olmney said, genuinely impressed. "I had no idea so much effort went into such a simple object."
"Ain't nothin' simple that's meant to last, sir," Earl replied, and in that moment, he seemed to Fleda the wisest man she had ever known.

They spent the next few days in a fever of activity. The pile of posts grew steadily. Then came the labour of transporting them to the boundary line, the oxen straining, the heavy sledge groaning over the softening ground as the sun gained strength. Fleda did not direct the work; she observed it, bringing out hot coffee in the middle of the morning and a basket of sandwiches at noon. She was learning the rhythms of the farm, the steady, unhurried pace of labour that was dictated by the task at hand and the light in the sky.
On the third day, they began to set the posts. It was slow, hard work. Philetus, with a long-handled auger, drilled the holes into the rocky soil, his shoulders bunching with the effort. Earl and Hugh followed, setting the heavy posts, tamping the earth around them with iron bars until they stood straight and solid, a line of sentinels appearing across the field.
Fleda and Mr. Olmney had walked out to the line in the middle of the afternoon. The sun was warm, and the first hints of green were visible on the southern-facing banks. As Earl sighted down the row of posts to ensure they were perfectly aligned, a figure appeared on the far side of the line, coming not from the woods this time but from the direction of the Skillcorn farmstead. It was Mr. Skillcorn himself.
Earl straightened up, his hand resting on a post, his face hardening into a wary mask. Philetus stopped his work with the auger. An apprehensive silence fell over the group.
Mr. Skillcorn did not approach with his old belligerence. He walked slowly, almost hesitantly, his hands in his pockets. He stopped a few feet from the line of new posts and looked at them, then at Earl, then at Fleda. His face was unreadable.
"Afternoon," he said, his voice gruff.
"Mr. Skillcorn," Fleda replied, her own voice calm and even. She stepped forward, positioning herself right at the line, just as she had done before.
He chewed on his lip for a moment, looking down the long row of posts that now clearly demarcated the boundary. "That's a sight o' work," he said at last.
"Good fences require it," Fleda said.
He nodded, still not looking at her. He kicked at a stone with the toe of his boot. "That's locust, ain't it?"
"It is," Earl said from behind Fleda. "Best there is."
"Aye," Skillcorn said. He finally raised his eyes and met Fleda's. "My wife," he began, then stopped and cleared his throat. "My wife, she sent somethin'." He reached into the deep pocket of his coat and pulled out a small, cloth-wrapped bundle. He held it out, not quite crossing the line. "It's a loaf of her sourdough. She said... she said it was a neighbourly thing to do."
A profound stillness seemed to settle over the field. Earl looked as if he had been struck by a bolt of lightning. Fleda felt a sudden, sharp sting behind her eyes. She stepped forward, her hand outstretched, and took the bundle. It was still warm.
"Please thank her for me, Mr. Skillcorn," she said, her voice thick with an emotion she could not hide. "It is a very welcome gift."
He gave a short, jerky nod. He looked at the post nearest him, at the clean-cut wood and the firmly tamped earth. "You're settin' 'em deep," he observed. "That's the right way. They'll hold."
And then, without another word, he turned and walked back the way he had come.
No one spoke for a long minute. They all just watched his retreating figure. Then Earl let out a long, slow breath. "Well," he said, shaking his head in disbelief. "Sourdough bread. I've seen everything now." He looked at Fleda, and the admiration in his eyes was boundless. "You didn't just buy a fence, Miss Fleda. You bought a neighbour."
Fleda looked down at the warm loaf in her hands. It felt heavier than a simple loaf of bread. It felt like a treaty, a promise, a foundation stone for a different kind of future. She turned and smiled at her men, at Mr. Olmney who was watching her with an expression of open wonder, and at the long, straight line of honest wood that stretched across the field. It was not a barrier. It was a beginning.
