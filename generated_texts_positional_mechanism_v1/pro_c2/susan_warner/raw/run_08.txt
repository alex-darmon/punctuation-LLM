The great mending-floor on the wharf was lit by a single, broad-bellied lantern, kept from the sea-breeze by a makeshift screen of old sailcloth. It was a moonless evening, and the lantern’s glow made a little island of industry in the vast, breathing darkness of the harbour; it touched the heaped-up billows of netting with a coppery sheen, making them look less like tools of a trade than like some fabulous treasure dredged from the deep; it ran in uncertain glimmers along the wet pilings of the wharf, whenever a space opened between the busy forms that moved about the light; and it gave the coil of thick anchor rope and the looming winch a strange and monumental substance, in a radiance that suggested more than it showed. Nothing was more strangely altered or more curiously ennobled by that light than the figures themselves, stooping and rising in their labour and presenting themselves in all manner of sudden and picturesque attitudes. There was old Silas, looking just as he did every day of the week, but with his weathered face and the tangled grey of his beard, and a woolen cap that had long since forgotten its original shape, he appeared one moment a venerable sea-king and the next a common pirate counting his spoils, a wooden netting-needle flashing in his hand for a sceptre. There was Mr. Abernathy, the new tutor, who had offered his assistance, as he put it, "to observe the mechanics of the maritime arts," exceedingly earnest and awkward and well-meaning, the light would catch his spectacles and the sharp line of his nose in such a way that Clara could see in him by turns a stern alchemist or a bewildered owl. Jem, whose only work was to hold the heavy net steady or fetch another ball of twine, resisted all such poetic disguise; he was just Jem still; but when Martha came out from the house to bring a jug of cider her broad, placid face under its white cap was lit into the very model of a Dutch matron in a painting. Clara stood for a moment in the shadow of the gear-shed, delighting in the scene, and then stepped into the light on the same impulse that makes a captain visit the watch, to lend her presence to the work of her people. The tutor was quick with his greetings and hopes that she had not taken a chill, and Silas grunted out a report on the evening’s progress. "We've a fair sight o' mending done," he said;--"I ain't seen a net this tore up since the great storm of 'forty-eight; it's as bad a tear as you'd ever want to find. The rocks off the point done it, as sure as I'm standin' here; the tide pulled her right over 'em. I reckon we've put in a good hundred knots already--I can't say for certain, but it's what I'm guessin'; and there's another fifty or sixty to go in this wing alone; I'll warrant it's better than half done afore we turn in. We'd have been further yet if Mr. Abernathy hadn't tried to improve on the fisherman's knot--I suppose he thought it needed a bit more geometry." "Now, Silas!"--said Jem, with a half-suppressed giggle. "It is a remarkably ingenious bit of handicraft," said the tutor, "and the repetitive motion provides a certain meditative solace to the mind." "Now, Clara," Silas continued, his fingers flying as he looped and pulled the twine tight with his needle,--"you understand how this has to be when we're through with the mendin', don't you?--it has to be taken and soaked in the tan-pit, in the bark liquor--any old cask'll do to hold it,--and let it sit for a day or two, no more;--you can use oak bark, or hemlock bark, or any kind of bark with good tannin in it!--and let it cure proper; and then when it's dried--Martha knows about the dryin'--you can tell when the twine is ready for the sea when it snaps a bit between your fingers and don't feel damp no more, and then it's time to test it with a heavy stone,--it's best to be a mite too stiff than a mite too slack, for the water will soften it up again--" "The process you describe is a form of tanning, essentially," put in the tutor. "O' course it's a form of tannin'," said Silas,--"Martha knows about tannin'--that's what the bark's for--you ought to boil it down first to get the liquor good and strong,--birch bark's as good as anythin',--or if you can get it, a bit of alum's better--" "A solution of copper sulphate would be a more scientifically sound preservative, I imagine," said the tutor. "Miss Everleigh's sensibilities would be--a--would prefer a mineral agent to an organic--and the copper salts will prevent the growth of marine life with greater efficacy." "Well have it your own way," said Silas,--"copper or bark--I ain't gonna fight you over the recipe, though I never heerd of a blue net afore, 'cept in a picture book--and I've seen a skipper out of Gloucester who swore by usin' plain tar, but it makes a foul mess of the deck;--and there's others that use nothin' at all,--but you'll be mendin' every week if you do that, and the fish can see the white twine comin' a mile off; but anythin' will do I s'pose if you're set on it--and after it's treated you just hang it on the racks till it's dry and stiff in the wind--" "Might I propose a different method of drying?" said the tutor. "Some authorities on the subject are of the opinion that if you stretch the net between posts rather than hanging it, you will ensure a more uniform desiccation. I believe that is the practice of the fishermen in Cornwall." "'Tain't the practice of men who've been pullin' a livin' out of this water their whole lives," said Silas; "though I don't mean no disrespect to the Cornishmen nor their methods, for the both are as good as you'd need to have; they're good fishermen and they're hardy folk--there ain't no hardier folk than the Cornish, nor no better sailors; but I hope there's as good; and I've seen nets dried on my own racks that lasted as long as I'd want a net to last." "It would take up the whole yard!" said Jem. Clara stood listening to the debate and smiling, when a footstep on the wharf planks made her turn to find Mr. Davies at her side. "I was enjoying the evening air," he said, "and Mrs. Gable gave me leave to come and see the work. I mean!--not that I needed leave, for I certainly did not--" "Ah Mr. Davies!" said Clara laughing, "you have no one to blame but yourself. You see the perils of trying to be polite. But we are a sight worth seeing, are we not? I have been admiring it myself." He did not answer what he thought. "Charming, is it not?" said Clara. "Step back a little, Mr. Davies--is it not a wonderfully composed scene, with the dark water all around and the single point of light? Look at Jem now with that coil of twine--Hugh! is he not the very image of one of those old Fates, holding the thread of life?--that old painting in the city museum--whose was it--Romney!--at the institute--do you not recall? It is something special, isn't it, Mr. Davies?" "I confess," said he with a slight shake of his head, "my imagination is not so well-schooled. I have not been in your company once I think without you pointing out some wonder I had failed to perceive." "That young lady, sir," said Mr. Abernathy from across the pile of netting, where he was examining a knot with intense concentration,--"that young lady, sir, is an ornament to her--a--to her entire community." "An ornament!" said Mr. Davies. "In a stationary, not a wearable, sense, the tutor means," said Clara in a low voice. "Well I won't deny she's a good girl," said Silas in a preoccupied tone, squinting at a tear in the mesh,--"she means to be a good girl--she's as sensible a girl as you'd need to have!" Nobody's composure survived this, except for Jem, in whom the faculty of irony had not yet been awakened. "Miss Everleigh, sir," Mr. Abernathy continued with a most learned expression,--"Miss Everleigh, sir, Mr. Davies, provides a model for all persons of--a--of refined education. She applies her intellect to the betterment of our local commerce." "Not to its exclusion, I trust?" said Mr. Davies smiling, and directing the question to Clara with his eyes. But she did not return his gaze. "You know," she said, a little hastily, and taking a step toward the shore, "I am a commercial woman by necessity--with my father away I am learning to be a shipping magnate myself." "So I understand--so Mrs. Gable informed me,--but I worry--forgive me--you do not seem made for such weighty concerns." Hugh sighed, and Clara's eyes went to the dark, silent water.

Her eyes came back from the water, and there was a little spark in them that was not all from the firelight. "Fit, Mr. Davies?" she said, with a smile that was not quite so merry as the last. "One must be fit for what one must do. The sea does not ask if a ship is made for weighty concerns, only if it is seaworthy."

"That's the truth of it," said Silas, leaving off his examination of the net and straightening his back with a faint groan. "And there's a deal more to bein' seaworthy than just havin' a sound hull. The gear's got to be right, and the men's got to be right, and the Lord's got to be feelin' generous. You take this here netting--it looks all the same to a landsman, I'll be bound. But there's your cotton twine, and your hemp, and now this newfangled stuff from the city that they say is stronger but I've seen it chafe through on a bit of barnacle quicker than you can say Jack Robinson. A net ain't just a thing you buy; it's a thing you know, like you know your own hand. You get a straight tear, that's one thing--Jem here can stitch that up as neat as his mother sews a shirt; but when a dogfish gets in a proper rage and rolls himself up in it till he's all wound in a cocoon of his own makin', he makes a piece of work that ain't mending, it's more like re-birthin' the whole section. You got to cut him out, and you lose a good yard of mesh just like that; and then you got to weave in a new piece, and the knots ha'n't never the same strength as the ones made on the loom. They're good knots, mind you; but they're different knots."

"A fascinating display of practical epistemology," Mr. Abernathy declared, stepping carefully over a coil of rope to join the group by the fire's edge, his spectacles flashing. "The--a--the distinction between a priori knowledge of the net's ideal form, and the a posteriori reality of its--a--its dogfish-induced fragmentation. A veritable treatise in twine."

"I reckon," said Silas, picking up his needle again, "that a dogfish don't care much for a priori. He cares about gettin' out, and he don't mind what he leaves behind him."

Jem, who had been listening with his mouth slightly agape, added his own conclusion. "They're a proper nuisance, that's what they are."

"I feel I have stepped into a world with its own language and its own philosophy," Mr. Davies said in a low voice to Clara. "And I find myself a most ignorant visitor."

"It is a language of necessity," she said softly, her gaze following Silas's swift, practiced fingers. "Every knot and every phrase has been learned by hard experience. I am only just beginning to learn its grammar myself, and I find it a more difficult study than French or Latin." Her tone was light, but there was a shadow of the care he had noted in it, a weariness that the firelight could not quite burn away. "My father's letters are filled with it, and I used to read them as if they were tales from a foreign land. Now I see I must become a native."

"Now, Miss Clara," Silas began again, as if the interruption had been but a breath, "you was askin' about the mackerel run, and what determines the price. It ain't as simple as catchin' 'em and sellin' 'em. First you got to find 'em. They might be runnin' close in shore one year, and the next you got to go twenty mile out to sea and pray your weather holds. Then, say you have a good catch--say the boat comes in with her decks awash and silver scales from stem to stern--that ain't the half of it. You got to get 'em landed and salted while they're fresh; an hour too long in the sun and the price drops by half. Salt costs money; barrels cost money; the men's wages is a share of the profit, so they're watchin' you with hawk's eyes to make sure you get the best price." He paused to hold a section of the net up to the fire, his eye tracing the intricate web of shadows it cast upon the ground. "One day the buyers from Boston are payin' top shilling 'cause the fleet up to Gloucester had a bad week; the next, the whole coast has a glut, the market's flooded with 'em, and you're lucky to get enough to pay for your salt. It's a gamble, every time the boat goes out. You're bettin' your time and your gear and sometimes your life against the whims of the fish and the sky and a man in a counting-house two hundred miles away."

Clara listened with an attention that was a world away from the fanciful humour she had shewn earlier. Her face, illumined by the fitful glow, was serious and intent; her brow was slightly furrowed, as if she were trying to commit every word to memory, to weave Silas's hard-won knowledge into the fabric of her own understanding. She saw it then not as a picture, but as a problem; a vast and complicated equation of twine and tides, of salt and shillings, and she stood on the shore, charged with finding the solution.

"It seems an enterprise of--a--of heroic uncertainty," put in Mr. Abernathy, who had been following the speech with the air of a man hearing a classical epic recited in a barbarian tongue. "The fisherman, sir, contends not merely with the elements, but with the very principles of political economy. He is a Sisyphus, rolling his boulder of fish up the hill of the marketplace."

"I don't know nothin' about no Sissy-fuss," said Silas, without looking up, "but I know if you don't get your nets mended, you won't have no boulder of fish to roll nowhere. Jem, hand me that other spool of twine; this one's near spent."

Mr. Davies watched Clara's face. The laughter had gone out of it, and in its place was a look of profound and sober thought. The title she had claimed in jest, "a shipping magnate," suddenly seemed less a fancy and more a heavy mantle that had settled upon her young shoulders. The firelight caught the slight downward curve of her mouth, and he felt a pang of something more than simple admiration; it was a deep and tender sort of pity, mingled with a respect she would likely have scorned had he dared to voice it.

"It is a great deal to manage," he said, his voice quiet and for her ears alone.

She started, as if his words had brought her back from some far-off calculation. "It is what must be done," she replied, her chin lifting a little. "And I am not alone. I have Silas, and Mrs. Gable, and--and Hugh." At the mention of her brother's name, her eyes sought him out. He was at the far end of the wharf, coiling a finished rope with a slow, methodical patience that seemed unlike his usual quick movements. The sight of him, so steady and absorbed, seemed to reassure her. "We will learn," she said, more to herself than to him.

The work continued for another hour under the vast, dark sky. The fire burned lower, the two great logs glowing like the embers of a fallen star. Jem was sent to the house for a jug of cider, and he and Silas took turns drinking from it, passing it between them with a companionable silence. Mr. Abernathy, having exhausted his store of classical analogies, contented himself with pointing out the various constellations that were beginning to prick the darkness above, explaining their mythical origins to a largely indifferent audience. The mending was nearly done; the great, wounded swaths of netting were whole again, the new patches showing as darker squares against the worn mesh.

"Well, that's the last of the big tears for tonight," Silas announced at last, knotting his thread with a final, decisive tug and snipping the end. "We've made a good job of it. This'll hold against anythin' short of a shark in a tantrum. We'll leave it here to air, and get the rest done in the mornin'." He rose, stretching his arms over his head. "I reckon we've earned our suppers."

Jem began to gather the tools, and Hugh, his own task completed, came to join them, his face smudged with tar from the rope. "All finished, Silas?"

"Aye, for tonight. The worst is over. You did a good job on that coil, Hugh. It's laid as pretty as you please."

"Miss Everleigh, sir," Mr. Abernathy said, turning to Mr. Davies as if to summarize the evening's proceedings for a fellow academic, "is a--a Penelope of commerce, sir, weaving the fortunes of her house not by day, but by night."

"Only I trust I am not unweaving it when no one is looking," Clara said, a trace of her earlier smile returning. "Come, gentlemen, I think Mrs. Gable will be expecting us. Silas, Jem, thank you. You have worked very hard."

"It's our work, Miss Clara," said Silas simply.

The little group broke up; Silas and Jem headed toward their own small cottage behind the main house, while Mr. Abernathy, with a final, sweeping gesture at the heavens, declared his intention to consult his almanac before retiring. Hugh lingered for a moment, clapping Jem on the shoulder, leaving Clara and Mr. Davies to walk the short distance to the house together.

The night was still and cold. The fire behind them was now a deep, pulsing heart of red, casting their shadows long before them on the wharf planks. The scent of woodsmoke and the faint, briny smell of the drying nets filled the air.

"I hope I did not--I mean, my remark about the weighty concerns--I did not mean to imply a doubt of your capacity," Mr. Davies said, his voice earnest in the darkness. "Quite the contrary. I am astonished by it."

"I know you did not," Clara said. "But you were not wrong. They are weighty, and there are times I feel I shall be crushed beneath them. Tonight was one such time. Hearing Silas speak--it makes it all so terribly real." She paused, her hand resting on the railing of the steps that led up to the house's veranda. "It is one thing to read of a storm in a letter. It is another to feel the spray on your face, even if it is only the spray of words."

He stopped beside her, a step below, so that he had to look up to see her face, a pale oval in the gloom. "Miss Everleigh," he began, and his voice was different now, stripped of its formal politeness and holding a new warmth, "Clara--if there is any way in which a man of business--a man of ledgers and contracts, not of nets and tides--can be of service to you, I hope you will not think it an impertinence if I offer. My own concerns are with shipping, of a different sort, but I know the men in the counting-houses Silas spoke of. I know their language, at least."

She was silent for a long moment, looking not at him, but out at the dark expanse of water, where the reflection of a single, bright star trembled. The sound of the waves shushing against the pilings below was the only sound.

"Thank you, Mr. Davies," she said at last, and her voice was softer than it had been all evening. She turned her eyes to his. "I will remember that. A shipping magnate must have allies, I suppose." A faint glimmer of her old playful spirit returned with the words, but it was tempered by something new, something deeper and more thoughtful. "And now, I think a shipping magnate is in great need of her supper."

He smiled, a slow smile that she could see even in the dim light. "Then I must not detain her."

He followed her up the steps and into the warm, lamplit hall. And in the quiet darkness, with the sound of the sea breathing beside them, the light from the house seemed not a distant beacon but a welcoming hearth, promising warmth against the vast and weighty concerns of the night.

The dining-room, which was an addition to the original house built by Clara's grandfather, was a place of comforting shadows and the mellow gleam of old silver; a world away from the raw wind and worry of the wharf. A fire crackled in the hearth, a much more civilized and contained creature than the bonfire they had just left, and the table was laid with a heavy white cloth and the good china. Aunt Agatha was already seated, her knitting lying in her lap as if she had only just set it down, her countenance a study in placid welcome. She was a small, neat woman whose life had been a series of small, neat duties, and the sight of her was as steadying to Clara as the sight of a familiar landmark to a sailor.

"There you are, my dears," she said, her voice the soft rustle of silk. "I was beginning to think the night air had spirited you away. Mr. Davies, how good of you to join us. I trust the sea air has given you an appetite?"

"It has indeed, Mrs. Pemberton," Mr. Davies replied, bowing slightly as Hugh helped him out of his greatcoat. "And the sea's conversation as well. It had a great deal to say this evening."

Aunt Agatha looked from Mr. Davies to Clara, a flicker of gentle enquiry in her eyes, but she was too well-bred to ask for an explanation of his meaning. "Well, its conversation is often a rather gloomy one, I find," she said, turning her attention to the tureen of soup that the housemaid, Mary, was bringing in. "I much prefer the conversation around this table. Hugh, my dear, will you see to the wine?"

Hugh, who had been quiet since they left the wharf, moved to the sideboard with a brisk, almost military precision. He was a man who preferred action to words, and the evening's discussions had left him with a surplus of feeling and no clear outlet for it. Clara watched him, saw the set of his shoulders, and knew that his silence was not one of agreement but of cogitation; he was a vessel taking on a heavy cargo of thought, and she knew it would have to be unloaded before the night was over.

They took their seats, Clara at the head of the table where her father had always sat, a position that still felt both strange and all too familiar. The soup was served, and for a time the only sounds were the clink of spoons against porcelain and the soft sigh of the fire. The warmth of the room began to unthaw the chill that had settled deep in Clara's bones, but the weight in her heart remained. She felt Mr. Davies's eyes on her from time to time, not in an intrusive way, but with a quiet, watchful sympathy that was almost more unnerving than a direct question would have been. He was seeing her, she felt, not just as Miss Everleigh, the mistress of the house, but as the woman struggling to keep it, and all that it represented, afloat.

It was Aunt Agatha who broke the spell, as she so often did, by steering the conversation into the safest of channels. "You find our little town much changed since your last visit, Mr. Davies?" she enquired. "We have a new baker, you know, though I cannot say I think his bread has the same substance as old Mr. Miller's. It is too--airy."

"The town seems much the same to my eye," he said, "and as for the bread, I must confess my palate is not so discerning. My business, however, is much concerned with substance, or the lack of it, and in that regard I find some things have changed a great deal." He glanced at Clara as he spoke, a clear signal that he was not merely talking of bread.

Hugh looked up from his plate. "Your business is in Boston, I collect?"

"Chiefly," said Mr. Davies. "I am an investor, of a sort. I put my capital into ventures that I believe have a future. Ships, warehouses, railways--the things that tie the country together."

"A profitable occupation, I'm sure," Hugh said. There was no challenge in his tone, merely a statement of fact, but Clara could feel the undercurrent. Hugh was a man of the land and sea, of things you could touch and see; Mr. Davies's world of paper and capital was as foreign to him as a distant country.

"It has its felicities," Mr. Davies admitted with a small smile. "And its risks, like any other. One must know how to read the signs, whether they are in the sky, as a sailor does, or in a column of figures, as I do. The principle is the same. One looks for patterns, for the promise of a fair wind or the threat of a coming storm."

The word "storm" hung in the air between them. Clara set down her spoon. "And what do the signs say now, Mr. Davies?" she asked, her voice very low.

He met her gaze directly. The pretense of polite supper-table chat fell away, and they were back in the cold night on the wharf, with the great, dark sea of troubles before them.

"The signs say that the storm Silas spoke of was not an isolated event," he said, his voice as quiet and serious as her own. "They say that men in counting-houses, as he called them, are growing cautious. Credit is tightening. The men who might have loaned your captains the money to rebuild their ships a year ago are now hoarding their cash, waiting to see which way the wind will blow."

Aunt Agatha made a small, distressed sound. "Oh, dear," she murmured. "It sounds so very--complicated."

"It is, and it is not," Mr. Davies said, turning to her with a reassuring look. "It is merely human nature. When people are frightened, they hold on to what they have."

"And the solution?" Hugh asked, leaning forward, his wine forgotten. "What is the solution for men like Silas, who have nothing to hold on to?"

"The solution," Mr. Davies said, his eyes returning to Clara, "is confidence. Someone must be willing to take the first risk. Someone must show the men in the counting-houses that this is not a failing enterprise, but a sound one that has suffered a temporary setback. Someone must step in to bridge the gap."

"Someone with capital," Clara said, finishing his thought. It was not a question.

"Precisely," he affirmed.

The main course was served and cleared away, and the conversation drifted back to safer shores, guided by Aunt Agatha's gentle hand. They spoke of Boston society, of a new concert-singer who was all the rage, of the merits of different rose varieties. Clara took part, answering questions and offering opinions, but her mind was elsewhere. She was on a ship, her own ship, navigating a narrow, fog-bound channel. On one side were the rocks of pride and a fierce, inherited independence that told her to trust no one and to show no weakness. On the other was the mire of her duty to her people, the fishermen and their families who depended on her, whose livelihoods were now threatened. Mr. Davies's offer was a lighthouse, flashing in the fog, but she could not tell if it marked a safe passage or a treacherous reef. To accept help, especially from this man, a near stranger, felt like a surrender. But to refuse it, if it meant Silas and the others would be ruined--what kind of victory was that?

When the meal was concluded, Aunt Agatha, with a significant look at the clock, declared that her knitting would not finish itself and that she must bid them good night. "Clara, dear, do not stay up too late," she said, pressing her niece's hand. "You look tired. A good night's rest is the best remedy for all troubles."

After she had gone, a new kind of silence settled on the room, heavier and more expectant. Hugh rose and went to stand before the fire, his back to them. Mr. Davies remained seated, his hands resting on the table, a picture of patience. He would not press her, Clara knew. He had made his offer, had laid out the facts as he saw them, and now he was waiting for her, the sovereign princess, to make her decree.

"Hugh," she said, her voice sharp with tension. "What do you think?"

He turned around, his face grim in the firelight. "I think he is right about the facts of it," he said, not looking at Mr. Davies. "I heard the same talk in town today. The bank is not lending. No one wants to take a risk on a fishing fleet that's just lost two of its best boats." He finally looked at Mr. Davies. "The question is not about the facts. The question is about the remedy. Why should you wish to be our bridge, sir?"

It was a direct challenge, and Clara held her breath. Mr. Davies did not flinch. He seemed, if anything, to welcome the directness.

"A fair question, Mr. Everleigh," he said. "My motives are, I confess, not entirely altruistic. I am a man of business. I do not invest in sentiment, I invest in opportunities. And I see an opportunity here."

"An opportunity to take advantage of our misfortune?" Hugh's voice was cold as iron.

"Hugh!" Clara interjected.

"No, Miss Everleigh, your brother is right to ask," Mr. Davies said, raising a hand. "Let us be plain with one another. I see a business--your business--that is fundamentally sound. It has good ships, good men, and a long history of success. It is well-managed." He gave a slight bow of his head towards Clara. "It has suffered a blow from which it will struggle to recover, not because it is weak, but because the financial climate is fearful. I have the means to calm those fears. I can speak to the men in the counting-houses in their own language. I can provide the guarantees they require. In short, I can provide the confidence that is lacking."

"And in return?" Hugh pressed.

"In return, I would expect a share in the profits that my investment makes possible. A partnership, of a sort, on this specific venture of rebuilding. I am not asking for a stake in Everleigh Shipping as a whole. I am proposing a temporary alliance to weather a specific storm. When the new ships are built and the loans are repaid, our arrangement is concluded. You will have your fleet restored, and I will have made a reasonable return on my capital. It is, I believe, what they call a mutually beneficial arrangement."

He spoke with such calm and reasonable authority that it was impossible to doubt his sincerity, at least on the terms he had laid out. He was not offering charity; he was proposing a business deal. Clara felt a strange sense of relief. This was a language she understood far better than she understood the unspoken language of courtship or social niceties. This was a contract.

She looked from Mr. Davies's earnest, intelligent face to her brother's troubled one. Hugh's concern was for her, she knew. He was protective, seeing a wolf where there might only be a shepherd with a different kind of crook.

"I cannot give you an answer tonight, Mr. Davies," she said, rising from her chair. The movement seemed to require a great effort. "You have given us a great deal to consider. As you said, we must read the signs, and this is a sign we have not seen before."

He rose as well. "Of course. I would not expect an immediate answer. Business done in haste is often repented at leisure. I will be in town for another two days. Perhaps you will allow me to call upon you tomorrow afternoon?"

"We would be pleased to receive you," Clara said, the formal words feeling like a shield.

Hugh saw him to the door while Clara remained in the dining-room, one hand resting on the back of her father's chair. The wood was smooth and cool beneath her palm. She heard the front door open and close, the murmur of voices, and then Hugh's returning footsteps.

He came and stood beside her, looking not at her, but at the empty chair. "What do you make of him?" he asked.

"I think he is exactly what he says he is," Clara answered slowly. "A man of business."

"And you trust him?"

"Keys can lock doors as well as open them," he said darkly. "To be in a man's debt is to be in his power. Father always said so."

"Read it again," she urged, her finger tracing the cold script. "He acknowledges our proposal. He agrees to a discussion. And most importantly, look here." She tapped the line. "'He will expect Mr. Rossitur, and his sister, Miss Rossitur.' He has acknowledged my part in this. He knows he is not dealing with you alone. He knows he is facing a united front. He is attempting to regain control of the situation by commanding our presence, but the very fact that he feels the need to command us at all is a sign that he has already lost the advantage. We have forced him to the table, Hugh. On our terms."

He looked from the letter to his sister's face, which was alight with a fierce intelligence. Seen through her eyes, the letter's insults became the blustering of a man who had been unexpectedly checked in his advance. The summons was not a sign of his power, but a reaction to theirs.

"He wants us on his ground," Hugh mused, his mind beginning to work along the new lines she had drawn for him. "He thinks to overawe us with his ledgers and his clerks."

"Precisely. And we will go," Eleanor declared. "We will go, and we will not be overawed. We will be punctual, as he advises, for it shows respect for the engagement, if not for the man. We will listen to what he has to say, and we will be prepared. We must speak with Mr. Abernathy this afternoon. We need to be armed not just with courage, but with every fact and figure he can provide us."

The lassitude and anxiety that had plagued Hugh all morning fell away, replaced by the keen, sharp focus of a soldier preparing for a decisive engagement. The waiting was over. The next stage of the battle had been set. He looked at Eleanor, at her calm and resolute expression, and felt a surge of gratitude so profound it was almost painful. He had feared he was leading them into ruin, but she had never faltered, seeing the path to victory where he had seen only shadows.

"You are right, as always," he said, his voice quiet but firm. "We will go. We will be prepared."

She placed her hand over his on the table, a brief, firm pressure that conveyed a world of solidarity. "We will face him together," she said, and in her voice there was no fear, only the clear, ringing sound of a bell calling the faithful to their duty. The fight was far from over; it had, in fact, just begun. But for the first time since this whole dreadful business had commenced, Hugh Rossitur felt the undeniable, invigorating possibility that they might actually win.

Eleanor retired to change her dress for one more suited to a walk abroad, and Hugh stood by the window, his gaze fixed unseeingly upon the street while his mind marshalled the arguments and questions they must bring to Mr. Abernathy. The very air of the room felt changed; what had been a space of confinement and anxious waiting was now a council chamber, a headquarters from which they would issue forth to engage their adversary's outposts. "I am ready, Hugh," she said simply, drawing on her gloves. "The walk will do us good. It will clear our heads for the work to come."

They stepped out into the city's ceaseless but orderly hum. The sun, having at last won its skirmish with the clouds, cast a benevolent, watery light upon the brick façades and the slate roofs opposite; it touched the brass knockers on the doors with a gleam of gold, and made the pavements, still damp from a brief morning shower, shine with a borrowed lustre. Their way to Mr. Abernathy's chambers lay through a district of quieter, more respectable streets, where the houses stood in tall, sober rows, their windows like so many unblinking eyes watching the decorous procession of life. Carriages passed with a stately rumble and the sharp, rhythmic clatter of hooves; gentlemen of business walked with a brisk and self-important air; and here and there a lady with her companion might be seen, engaged in the mysterious but vital enterprise of shopping. To Hugh, who had walked these same streets a thousand times without a particular thought, they now appeared as a great and complicated machinery, a world of interests and endeavours of which his own small struggle was but a single, spinning cog. He felt a momentary pang of his former insignificance; a sense that their private drama was of no account in this vast theatre of commerce and society.

Eleanor, as if she divined his thought, spoke in a low, clear tone. "Do not be daunted by all this, Hugh. These are but men, like any other. Their concerns are no more weighty than our own, only more numerous."

"I am not daunted," he replied, though it was not entirely the truth. "I am only--contemplating. Mr. Abernathy must have a hundred clients, all with their own troubles. I wonder that he will give us the time we require."

"He will give it," said Eleanor with confidence. "He is a man of conscience, and he knows the justice of our cause. Besides," she added, with the faintest hint of a smile, "our father was his friend; and Mr. Abernathy is not a man to forget such things. It is upon these loyalties, not upon the cold calculations of the marketplace, that a true society is built."

Her words were a comfort, and they walked on in a companionable silence, each busy with their own thoughts, yet drawing strength from the other's presence. They turned at last into a narrower street, lined with the somewhat grim and ink-stained edifices of the law. Here the air seemed heavier, freighted with the weight of disputed wills and broken contracts; the very stones seemed to absorb the gravity of human affairs. Mr. Abernathy's offices were on the second floor of a building that had seen better days, but which still retained an air of unyielding, if dusty, respectability. A small brass plate, polished by the hands of innumerable anxious clients, bore his name. They mounted the worn wooden staircase, its creaks and groans a familiar sound to Hugh from previous visits in happier times, and presented themselves at a heavy oak door.

It was opened by a clerk of an age so indeterminate he might have been a prematurely wizened youth or a preternaturally preserved old man; his face was a parchment of intersecting lines, and he looked up from a massive ledger over a pair of spectacles that seemed to be a permanent extension of his nose.

"Mr. and Miss Rossitur," Hugh announced. "We have come to see Mr. Abernathy. We have no appointment, but the matter is of some urgency."

The clerk looked from Hugh's earnest face to Eleanor's calm one, and some subtle shift in his dry demeanor seemed to take place; he gave a curt nod, a gesture which in him was the equivalent of a cordial welcome in another, and disappeared into an inner room. He returned a moment later. "Mr. Abernathy will see you," he said, and held the door for them to pass.

The lawyer's sanctum was a room that appeared to be composed almost entirely of paper. Bookshelves groaned from floor to ceiling under the weight of leather-bound volumes; stacks of documents tied with faded pink ribbon stood in precarious towers on every available surface; and the great desk in the centre was a landscape of open letters, deeds, and files, from the midst of which Mr. Abernathy himself arose to greet them. He was a man of sixty, perhaps, with a fringe of white hair around a high, domed forehead, and eyes of a keen, intelligent grey that seemed to miss nothing. His dress was plain and old-fashioned, but of an excellent quality; he was, in all things, a man of substance rather than of show.

"Mr. Rossitur; Miss Rossitur," he said, his voice a dry but not unkind rasp. He shook Hugh's hand and then gave Eleanor a slight, formal bow, a mark of respect that Hugh noted with gratitude. "I confess I am surprised to see you, though not, perhaps, entirely displeased. I have been thinking of your situation. Pray be seated. Tell me what has occurred."

They took the two straight-backed chairs that faced his desk, and Hugh, taking the letter from his coat, laid it before the lawyer. "We received this, sir, not two hours ago," he said.

Mr. Abernathy adjusted his spectacles and took up the letter, his long, bony fingers holding it as if it were a piece of evidence, which in a sense it was. He read it through once without a change in his expression; then he read it a second time, a faint, thoughtful frown gathering between his brows. For a long moment he was silent, tapping the corner of the sheet with his forefinger. The only sound in the room was the scratching of the clerk's pen in the outer office and the distant, muted rumble of the city.

"And in our facts," said Hugh, leaning forward. "That is why we have come to you, sir. Eleanor was right in that we need to be armed. We know the general truth of our position, but we lack the specific figures, the details of the accounts that Mr. Thornton holds. We are fighting in the dark."

"Ah," said Mr. Abernathy, his expression once again becoming that of the careful legal strategist. "The figures. Yes. That is the ammunition for this battle. You have come to the right armoury." "I have not been idle since your last visit, Mr. Rossitur. It has not been a simple task; the currents of finance are often deliberately muddied to conceal the channel."

He lifted the top file from the stack and opened it upon his desk. It was filled with papers covered in columns of figures and annotations in his own spidery, but meticulously clear, hand. "Now," he began, his tone becoming that of a lecturer, "let us attend to the matter at hand. The foundation of Mr. Thornton's claim rests upon two primary debts, which he has consolidated under a single instrument of security; that is to say, the mortgage upon your property. The first debt is straightforward, a loan made to your father five years ago for improvements to the estate. The terms are onerous, but legal. It is the second debt which is the heart of the matter."

For the next hour, a time that seemed to both Hugh and Eleanor to pass with the swiftness of a minute, Mr. Abernathy led them through the labyrinthine turnings of their own financial history. With a clarity that was almost beautiful in its precision, he laid bare the series of transactions, the compound interest, the fees and charges that had been levied against the original loan until it had swelled to its current monstrous proportions. He used no rhetoric; he simply pointed to the figures on the page, and the figures themselves told a damning story. He showed them letters from their father, questioning certain charges; and he showed them the bank's bland, evasive replies. It was, as he explained it, a tale not of outright fraud, which would be a simple thing to prove, but of a thousand small, legally defensible acts of usury and opportunism, which together amounted to a great moral wrong.

"You see," he concluded, pushing his spectacles up onto his forehead and rubbing his tired eyes, "he has been careful. There is no single thread one can pull that will unravel the entire cloth. But when you look at the pattern as a whole,"--and here he swept his hand over the papers spread before them--"it is a pattern of calculated entrapment. He lent your father money knowing the terms were such that repayment would be a struggle; and he has used every fluctuation in the market, every small misfortune, to tighten his grip."

"So we have no legal recourse?" Hugh asked, his heart sinking.

"I did not say that," the lawyer replied sharply. "I said it is not simple. Our counter-argument, the one you have already put forward in your proposal, is not one of law, but of equity. We do not claim he has broken the letter of his agreements; we claim that his enforcement of them, in these particular circumstances, would be so grossly unjust as to be unconscionable. We are appealing, in effect, to the court of conscience; and even a man like Thornton has to be wary of his public reputation." He looked at them both, his grey eyes very bright. "He will offer you a settlement tomorrow. Of that I am certain. He will propose to forgive a portion of the debt in exchange for your immediate surrender of the property. He will present it as an act of magnanimity."

"And we are to refuse," said Eleanor.

"You are to refuse," Mr. Abernathy confirmed. "You will thank him for his generosity, and you will calmly restate your own proposal: a fair valuation of the property, the sale of which will discharge the entirety of the debt, with any remainder to be returned to the estate. You will put it to him that this is the only just and honourable resolution."

"He will never agree," said Hugh. "He wants the land, not the money."

"Perhaps," said the lawyer. "But you will have forced him to refuse a reasonable offer in favour of a pound of flesh. You will have made his rapacity plain. And that, Mr. Rossitur, is a victory in itself. It gives us ground for our next move, should one be necessary." He began to gather the papers, tapping them into a neat pile. "I will have my clerk draw up a summary of these figures for you to take. Study them tonight. Master them. Knowledge, in your meeting tomorrow, will be your shield and your sword. He will expect you to be ignorant and emotional; you will be informed and composed."

He stood up, signalling the end of the interview. "You have a difficult day before you," he said, his dry manner softening for a moment into something like paternal concern. "But you do not face it alone. And you, Miss Rossitur," he added, with another of his slight bows, "you have a good head for generalship. Do not let them tell you it is not a woman's place."

They took their leave, their minds whirling with figures and strategies, yet with a sense of clarity they had not possessed when they arrived. The summary of accounts, neatly copied out by the tireless clerk, felt in Hugh's pocket like a talisman of power. As they stepped back out into the street, the sun was beginning its descent, and the light had grown richer and more golden. The shadows of the buildings were stretching long and dark across the pavements, and the city was beginning to gather itself for the evening. Their step was lighter now, their heads held higher. They had been to the armoury, and they had been well supplied. The prospect of the morning's battle was no less daunting, but it was no longer a shapeless terror; it was a known field, with known objectives, and they had a plan of attack. The rest depended on their own courage.

"He called you a general, Eleanor," Hugh said, a smile touching his lips for the first time that day.

"It is a role I never sought," she answered quietly. "But since it has been thrust upon me, I will endeavor to play it to the best of my ability. We both will, Hugh. Together."

They walked the rest of the way home in a thoughtful quiet, the bond between them stronger than it had ever been. The day had been a trial, but it had forged their resolve into something hard and bright, like steel that has been passed through the fire. The fight was not won, but they were now truly ready to begin it.

The whole party drew closer, their faces lit with anticipation. Even Dr. Quackenboss fell silent, his scientific curiosity for the moment outweighing his desire for discourse. The fire, as if sensing the importance of the moment, threw up a brilliant jet of flame that illuminated the scene with the sudden clarity of a tableau. Barby had come out again from the house and stood near the cellar-opening, her arms crossed over her apron, her strong, gypsy-like face intent and still.

"Now then," said Earl, his voice low and concentrated. He dipped the end of his ladle into the thick syrup, collecting a small drop, and held it aloft for a moment, a perfect amber jewel against the dark sky. Then with a quick, practiced motion, he let the drop fall into the cup of cold water. He swirled it once and then plunged his fingers in, fishing about for an instant before pulling them out. On the tip of his forefinger lay a small, soft, but perfectly formed ball of sugar.

"There she is," he said with deep satisfaction. "Done. A leetle on the soft side, just as it should be. It'll dreen out the molasses beautiful. Any more bilin' and she'd be hard and glassy. All right, boys! Bear a hand here! This biler's got to come off the fire now, afore it catches and burns."

What followed was a moment of strenuous and well-ordered confusion. The business was too serious for talk. Hugh threw off his coat and took up a position opposite Earl, the two of them grasping the great iron lugs on the side of the huge kettle. Philetus stood by with a stout wooden pole, ready to help lever it up. The heat was intense, and the men's faces grew red and beaded with sweat in the fierce glow. Fleda and Mr. Olmney stepped back out of the way, near the woodpile where the shadows were deepest.

"Take a good hold now, Mr. Hugh," said Earl through his teeth. "And heave when I say heave. One--two--HEAVE!"

The muscles on Hugh's arms and back stood out as he and Earl lifted the immense weight, their figures silhouetted black and powerful against the roaring fire. The kettle, with its hundred gallons of boiling liquid, swayed perilously for a moment. Philetus jammed his pole underneath for support, his face a mask of straining effort. For a long second they held it, suspended between the fire and the two stout blocks set beside it, steam and heat rolling off them in waves. Then with a final, coordinated grunt, they swung it clear and settled it safely on the blocks with a heavy, metallic clang.

"Well done," breathed Earl, wiping his forehead with the back of his sleeve. "That's the ticklish part. A spill now would be a sorrowful business, and a scaldin' one."

Hugh, breathing heavily, leaned against the corner of the house for a moment, the firelight catching the damp sheen on his brow. He looked across at Fleda, a look of shared accomplishment in his eyes, before he went to retrieve his coat.

Now Barby came forward, all quiet competence. She and Philetus brought a large, clean cask, positioning it next to the cooling kettle. Over the top of the cask, Barby stretched a wide piece of clean white woollen cloth, securing it firmly around the rim. The final stage of the night's work was about to begin.

"Now for the strainin'," Earl announced, picking up a smaller, long-handled dipper. "This gets out any bits o' leaf or ash that might ha' got in. We'll let it stand in the cask till it grains, and then it's Barby's business to finish it down in the house."

He began to ladle the hot, fragrant syrup from the kettle, pouring it carefully onto the center of the cloth. It streamed through, thick and slow, a river of pure gold, filling the cask with its sweetness and the air with a perfume that was the very essence of the northern spring--a scent of clean snow, damp earth, woodsmoke, and the concentrated life of the maple. The others watched, a quiet satisfaction settling over the group. The hard work of the day, the tapping of trees, the gathering of sap, the long hours of boiling, had all come to this successful conclusion.

Fleda drew in a long breath of the sweet air. "There is nothing quite like it," she said softly to Mr. Olmney, who had not moved from her side. "It feels so--so real. So much more real than most of the things one does."

"I believe I understand what you mean," he answered, his gaze resting not on the bustling scene but on her face, which was softened and made thoughtful by the fire's gentle light. "You have certainly proved your point, Miss Ringgan. You and your household. You are more than fit for this." His voice had lost its earlier tone of polite concern and held now a note of sincere and deferential respect.

"We have had good generals," she said, with a slight motion of her head toward Earl Douglass. "One cannot win a battle without them."

"Aptly spoken, Miss Ringgan!" said Dr. Quackenboss, who had overheard. "The division of labour, properly executed, is the key to all successful enterprise! The--a--the brawn, and the brain, and the guiding feminine influence! A trinity of productive power!"

Nobody responded to this flight of fancy, for Earl was nearing the bottom of the kettle. He scraped it one last time, pouring the last of the syrup through the strainer, and then set his dipper down with a clatter.

"That's that," he said. "Nigh on a hundred and seventy gallon boiled down to maybe twenty o' syrup. And a first rate run it's been. The trees'll run agin tomorrow, I reckon, if the sun comes out strong. We'll be at it agin." He looked around at the company. "Well, I guess we've made a night of it. I'm for my bed."

Philetus was already banking the fire, covering the glowing heart of the coals with ash so it would keep till morning. Barby removed the straining cloth, wrung it out, and disappeared with it into the house, her duties done. The brief, intense community of labour was dissolving, the figures melting back into their separate lives.

"Will you not come in for a moment, gentlemen?" Fleda said, turning to the doctor and Mr. Olmney. "Mrs. Rossitur will be wanting to hear a report of our success."

"I thank you, but I must decline," said the doctor, making a small bow. "My--a--my professional duties may call me at any hour, and I must be ready. It has been a most gratifying and instructive evening. I shall look forward to tasting the--a--the fruits of our collective labours. Good night, Miss Ringgan. Good night, sir." And with another bow to Mr. Olmney, the doctor took his departure, his stout figure receding into the gloom.

"And you, Mr. Olmney?" Fleda asked. "Will you not be persuaded?"

"For a few minutes, if I may," he said. "I should like to pay my respects to Mrs. Rossitur before I go."

The great logs of the fire-pit had burned down to enormous, glowing embers that pulsed with a deep red light, and the snow beyond the circle of warmth seemed whiter and more vast in the darkness. They entered the little parlour, which felt warm and close after the sharp night air. Mrs. Rossitur was seated in her armchair by the hearth, a book lying unread in her lap, her face shewing lines of weariness but also of keen interest.

"Well?" she said, looking up as they came in. "Fleda, child, you look flushed. I hope you have not taken a chill. Was it a success?"

"A great success, mother," said Hugh, his voice full of warmth. "Earl is well satisfied, and you know he is not a man for easy praise."

"We have a full cask of syrup, aunt," said Fleda, "and the promise of more to come. Mr. Olmney was good enough to stay and witness our triumph."

"I was privileged to do so," said Mr. Olmney, advancing to take the hand Mrs. Rossitur extended to him. "Your niece, madam, is a formidable farmer. She has directed her troops with the air of a veteran commander."

Mrs. Rossitur's anxious face relaxed into a smile. "She has a great deal of spirit," she said, her eyes resting on Fleda with a mixture of pride and apprehension. "Perhaps too much for her own good. But I am glad the day has ended well. It has been a long one for all of you."

They spoke for a few minutes more, of the weather, and the quality of the sap, and Mr. Plumfield's theories on sugar-making, but the energy of the evening had passed. A pleasant weariness had taken its place. Soon Mr. Olmney rose to take his leave. Hugh went with him to the door, while Fleda remained by her aunt's chair.

"He is a very well-mannered young man," Mrs. Rossitur said quietly, when they were alone. "And he seems to hold you in high esteem, Fleda."

"He is very kind, aunt," Fleda replied, her gaze fixed on the fire in the grate.

"He is right, you know," Mrs. Rossitur went on, her voice gentle. "It is a heavy burden for you and Hugh. I see it, though I say little. I see the care in Hugh's face, and I see you forcing yourself to be brighter and stronger than you feel."

Fleda turned from the fire and knelt by her aunt's chair, taking her thin hands in her own. "It is a burden, yes," she said softly. "But it is our own, and we are carrying it together. And tonight--tonight did not feel like a burden at all. It felt like a victory. A small one, perhaps, but a victory nonetheless."

Hugh returned, closing the door quietly behind him, a thoughtful expression on his face. He came and stood on the rug before the fire, looking down at his sister and his mother. The little room was quiet and peaceful, a haven of warmth and light against the cold, dark world outside. For a moment, no one spoke. The labours of the day were done. The first battle in their farming campaign had been fought and won, not with lawyers and figures, but with fire and iron, and sap and sweat. And as Fleda had said, it was a victory, and it had filled them not with weariness, but with a strange and potent hope. The path ahead was still uncertain and fraught with difficulty, but they had taken the first step, and they had taken it together.

The hope of the evening carried them through the night and into the brightening dawn of the next day; a day which broke clear and cold, with a sun that promised warmth but gave little of it. Fleda was down before Hugh, a circumstance not altogether rare, for her sleep was often light and broken. She had rekindled the fire in the little parlour and was seated before it, a small account book open in her lap, when her brother entered. His face still wore the thoughtful cast of the previous night, but it cleared as his eyes fell upon her.
"You are early, Fleda," he said, taking a place beside her. "I hoped you would rest this morning."
"I have rested enough," she said, though the faint shadows beneath her eyes told a different story. "Besides, Hugh, victories must be followed up, or they are of no account. Our sugar is a fine thing, but it will not plant the corn or sow the oats. I have been looking at our means." She tapped the little book with her finger. "They are very small means, Hugh."
"I know it," he said gravely. "I have been thinking of little else. Earl says the ground will soon be ready for the plough, as soon as the frost is fully out. We must have seed, and we are in want of a new coulter for the old plough; the present one is little better than useless."
"And I must have things for the garden," Fleda added. "Barby's list is a formidable document. Peas, and beans, and all manner of roots. And she speaks of wanting a new churn, the old one being cracked past all mending."
They sat in silence for a few minutes, the crackling of the fire the only sound. The pecuniary question, ever lying in wait, had stepped forth from the shadows to challenge the hope of the new day.
"Well," Hugh said at last, with a sigh that he quickly converted into a sound of resolution, "there is no help for it. We must go to Cat-rock and see what we can do. We have the little money that was left from the sale of mother's jewelry, and the sale of the sugar will bring in something in its time. We must make it stretch as far as it will go."
"To Cat-rock," Fleda repeated, a little spark of animation returning to her eye. The thought of the little expedition, humble as it was, was a relief from the dry contemplation of figures. "When shall we go?"
"Today," said Hugh. "There is no time to be lost. Earl can manage here; the sap will not run so freely on a cool day like this. We will take the light wagon and Cinder. It will be a long day's work, but it must be done."
And so it was settled. After a hasty breakfast, during which Mrs. Rossitur was informed of their plan and gave her consent with her usual mixture of anxiety and resignation, the preparations were made. Hugh went to harness the steady old horse, while Fleda wrapped herself in her warmest cloak and bonnet, her face wearing a look of business-like determination that sat rather quaintly upon her delicate features. As she came out upon the porch, she found Earl Douglass waiting, holding the horse's head.
"Goin' to town, be ye?" he said, his keen eye taking in the little equipage. "Well, you want to be sharp. Old Satterlee at the store, he'll charge you for the dust on his floor if you don't watch him. And don't you go buyin' none o' that new-fangled seed corn he's got from Albany. It's pretty to look at, but it won't stand our weather. Get the eight-rowed yellow flint; it ain't so handsome, but it's true."
"We will be very careful, Earl," said Fleda, smiling. "Have you any commissions for us?"
"Nary a one," he answered. "I don't hold with town goods when country goods'll do. Just you mind what I said about the corn. And Hugh," he added, turning to the young man who was now climbing into the driver's seat, "if you see that lazy good-for-nothin' Skillcorn, you can tell him from me that I've seen scarecrows with more wit in their straw heads than he's got in his whole carcass."
With this parting injunction, they set off, the wheels of the light wagon crunching on the still half-frozen mud of the lane. The air was sharp and invigorating, and as they turned onto the main road, Fleda's spirits rose in spite of her cares. The country through which they passed was still in the stern grip of winter's aftermath, the fields brown and bare, the woods a hazy purple-grey against the pale blue sky; but here and there, in sheltered hollows, the snow was gone, and the wet earth gave forth a faint, hopeful scent that spoke of spring.
"It is a poor-looking country, isn't it?" said Hugh, his eyes scanning the landscape. "Stony and hard. It will be a fight for every bushel."
"But it is ours to fight for," Fleda answered stoutly. "Think, Hugh! a year ago, we knew nothing of bushels, or ploughs, or the merits of yellow flint corn. We have learned something, at least."
"We have learned that we know very little," Hugh said with a half-smile. "But you are right, Fleda. It is our own fight."
The village of Cat-rock, when they reached it after a drive of some two hours, was a straggling collection of white-painted houses, a church, a tavern, and a large, unpainted building that served as general store, post-office, and public forum. It was a place of little beauty, but of great importance to the surrounding country. Hugh drew Cinder to a halt before the store and helped Fleda to the ground.
The interior was a dim, crowded space, smelling powerfully of salt fish, leather, molasses, and coffee. Every available foot of wall was covered with shelves laden with a multifarious assortment of goods, from bolts of calico and flannel to harnesses, axes, and earthen jugs. A large stove stood in the centre, around which two or three men in heavy boots were lounging, though the fire within was low. Behind a long counter stood the proprietor, Mr. Satterlee, a thin, sallow man with a perpetually suspicious expression.
He greeted them with a curt nod, his eyes moving from Fleda's refined appearance to Hugh's worn coat with a calculating glance.
"What can I do for you folks?" he enquired, in a tone that implied they were likely to be more trouble than they were worth.
Hugh, with a list Fleda had prepared, began his requests. "We need seed oats, sir, and seed potatoes. And corn--the eight-rowed yellow flint."
Mr. Satterlee grunted. "Got oats. Got potatoes. Flint corn's nigh gone. Most folks are takin' the new hybrid from down river. Better yield."
"We'll take the flint, if you please," Hugh said firmly, remembering Earl's advice.
The process was a slow and trying one. Mr. Satterlee seemed to take a perverse pleasure in pointing out the high price of every article and the poor prospect of the season. Fleda, standing quietly by, felt her courage begin to fail. The little roll of bills in Hugh's hand seemed to shrink with every purchase. The bags of seed, the new churn which they found after some searching in a dusty corner, the small but necessary items for the house--they all added up with alarming rapidity.
It was in the midst of a disquisition from Mr. Satterlee on the poor quality of last year's hay crop that the store door opened and a new customer entered, bringing a gust of cold air and a certain professional bustle with him. It was Dr. Quackenboss.
"Good morning, Mr. Satterlee! A fine bracing morning!" he announced, rubbing his hands together. "A morning to invigorate the vital spirits and promote the healthy--ah! Miss Ringgan! and Mr. Rossitur! An unexpected pleasure. You are engaged in the necessary preliminaries, I see, for the--a--the approaching season of agricultural activity."
"We are buying seeds, Doctor," said Fleda, managing a smile.
"Excellent! The foundation of the whole enterprise!" the doctor declared, beaming upon them. "The germ of life, from which shall spring the harvest that sustains us. You have procured the best, I trust? In matters of seed, as in medicine, the purest articles are ever the most efficacious. I was just telling Mrs. Plumfield yesterday--her youngest has a touch of the croup--that one must never be parsimonious in the procurement of one's initial ingredients."
He cast a patronizing glance at the bags on the counter. "Flint corn? Ah, a sturdy variety, to be sure. A trifle old-fashioned, perhaps. Mr. Plumfield is trying the new 'Excelsior' Dent this year. He anticipates a most remarkable result."
"We are content with the old-fashioned, for now," said Hugh, his patience wearing thin under the combined assault of Mr. Satterlee's prices and the doctor's pomposity.
They were concluding their business, Hugh counting out the money with a grave face, when the door opened again. Fleda, whose back was to the entrance, did not turn until a voice, very different from the others in the room, spoke her name.
"Miss Ringgan. I thought I could not be mistaken."
She turned with a start to see Mr. Olmney. He stood just inside the door, his hat in his hand, his face expressing a surprise that was evidently as great as her own. The contrast he presented to the other figures in the store--to the rough-clad farmers, the sallow shopkeeper, and the fussy doctor--was as striking as that of a fine steel engraving set amongst coarse woodcuts.
"Mr. Olmney!" she exclaimed, a faint colour rising in her cheeks. "I did not know you ever came to Cat-rock."
"My excursions are not usually so rustic," he said with a smile. "But I had business a few miles from here and took the opportunity to see your village. I confess, I did not expect to find my reward so soon."
His eyes met hers for a moment, and Fleda found herself looking down at the rough floorboards, her heart beating a little faster than before. The dim, crowded store, with its smells of fish and leather, had suddenly become a place of unaccountable interest.
Hugh, having settled with Mr. Satterlee, came forward and shook Mr. Olmney's hand. "This is an odd place to meet, sir."
"A very fortunate one for me," Mr. Olmney replied. "I was just about to return. May I ask if you are going back toward Queechy?"
"We are, as soon as we can load our purchases."
"Then perhaps you would allow me to be of some service? Your wagon seems quite full already with these gentlemen," he said, with a slight, humorous inclination of his head toward the bags of seed. "My own conveyance is empty. I could easily take some of your parcels and save your horse the extra burden."
Hugh hesitated, a flush of pride rising to his face. He was about to decline when he caught Fleda's eye. Her look was a curious mixture of gratitude and a plea for him to accept. He understood that to her, the offer was not one of charity, but of simple kindness, and to refuse it would be churlish.
"That is very good of you, Mr. Olmney," he said, his reluctance yielding. "We should be very grateful. Fleda finds the wagon rather crowded with the churn."
"The churn!" Mr. Olmney said, his glance falling upon the object in question. "So, you are truly entering upon all the duties of a farmer's life, Miss Ringgan. From making sugar to making butter."
"Needs must, when--when one is a farmer," she said, with a slight laugh that did not quite conceal her weariness.
With Mr. Olmney's help, the transfer was quickly made. The churn, a large bag of potatoes, and several smaller parcels were stowed in his handsome, well-sprung buggy, which stood a little way down the road. Fleda could not help but notice the contrast between his spirited bay and their own plodding Cinder; between the light, elegant vehicle and their own stout, unadorned wagon. It was the contrast between two worlds.
"I shall follow you at a little distance," Mr. Olmney said, as he handed Fleda up to her seat. "Your Cinder sets a more philosophic pace than my Hotspur here."
The journey home was quieter than the one out. The sun was past its meridian, and the air had lost its earlier crispness. Fleda was tired, and the day's transactions had brought their difficulties very plainly before her. Hugh, too, was silent, his brow knitted in thought. Behind them, at a respectful distance, the light buggy followed, a silent reminder of a world of ease and plenty that seemed to grow more distant with every turn of their wagon wheels.
They did not speak much until their own lane came into view.
"Our funds are very low, Fleda," Hugh said abruptly, as if the sight of home had forced the words from him. "What we spent today has nearly exhausted them. We have the seed, it is true, but there will be nothing left for wages, or for any unexpected need."
"The sugar, Hugh," she reminded him gently. "Earl says it will be a good run, and the price is fair."
"It will help. But it will not be enough. This farm--it swallows money. I see now what uncle Rolf meant in his letters. It is a constant drain."
Fleda laid a hand on his arm. "We knew it would be hard, Hugh. We must not lose heart at the first check. We have the seed. We will plant it. And we will trust."
He looked at her, at the quiet courage in her face, and his own expression softened. "You are a better soldier than I am, Fleda," he said.
As they drew up before the house, Mr. Olmney arrived and dismounted, helping Hugh to unload the parcels. Barby came out to inspect the new churn, her strong features expressing a grim satisfaction. When everything was deposited, Mr. Olmney lingered for a moment.
"I hope the day has not been too fatiguing for you, Miss Ringgan," he said, his voice low.
"I am a little tired," she confessed. "Town is a more wearying place than the woods, I find."
"It is because the woods ask nothing of you but to be yourself," he said, with a perception that surprised her. "The town makes other demands."
He bade them farewell then, declining Hugh's invitation to come in and rest, saying he had already delayed too long. Fleda and Hugh stood on the porch and watched him drive away, the hoof-beats of his fast horse dying quickly in the distance.
"He is a good man," Hugh said, breaking the silence.
"Yes," Fleda said softly. "He is very good."
She turned and went into the house, into the little parlour where her aunt sat waiting. The fire was burning low, and the room was filled with the soft grey light of the declining day. The hope of the morning had been tried and tested, and though not extinguished, it burned now with a steadier and more sober flame. The small victory of the sugaring was past; the long, hard campaign of the summer lay before them. And Fleda, sinking into a chair, felt in every limb the truth of her own words--that a farmer's life, even for a sovereign princess, was a burden, and a very heavy one.
