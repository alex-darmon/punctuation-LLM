The work was proceeding in a small paddock fenced off by hurdles, the close-bitten turf of which was already strewn with the soft spoils of the day. A low sun sent long buttery shafts of light through the gaps in the old stone barn, turning the stirred-up dust into a golden haze and the motes within it into a swirl of tiny, frantic stars. The light caught the greasy lustre of the shorn fleeces piled in the corner; played upon the burnished steel of the shears that lay for a moment idle; and gave a strange, theatrical glow to the whitewashed wall of the barn, against which the dark forms of the men were starkly defined. Nothing was quite its everyday self in that light, but was made either grander or more peculiar by its Midas touch. There was old Silas, the chief shearer, a man whose limbs seemed grown for no other purpose than to fold themselves around a struggling sheep, his weathered face and squinting eyes making him look by turns like some ancient druid performing a rite, or a mariner scanning a far and doubtful horizon. There was Mr. Pettigrew, a purveyor of farm machinery from the town, who, as Silas muttered, "was here to sell what nobody was buyin'," and whose efforts to appear useful only highlighted his portly awkwardness; the sun would catch his spectacles and turn him into a creature with blank, fiery eyes, like an owl surprised by day. Young Tom, whose job was to catch the ewes and carry the fleeces, was simply Tom; but when he stooped to gather a particularly large fleece, his back bent and his arms full of the white bounty, he was for a moment the very image of pious husbandry. Clara had been watching from the cool of the dairy, finding a painter's delight in the composition, before she stepped out into the yard, feeling it her duty to commend the day's toil as a queen might review her troops after a victory. Silas acknowledged her with a dip of his head and Mr. Pettigrew was upon her in an instant. "We have had a most propitious day," he declared;--"I do not think I have seen a more efficient shearing operation; the conditions are quite perfect. The animals have been quiescent, and the yield appears to be of a superior quality. I have been remarking to Silas that he has clipped nigh on fifty head himself--I am certain it is at least forty-five, and perhaps a shade more. We should have had the whole flock done if young Tom had not let that ram bolt into the turnip field--I expect it fancied some greens with its mutton." "Now, Mr. Pettigrew!"--said Tom, his face a study in crimson mortification. "It is a remarkably robust flock of sheep," said Silas, ignoring the other man entirely, "and they stand so well to the shears as to give great comfort to a man's back." "Now, Clara," Silas went on, pausing to wipe his shears on a bit of oily rag,--"you know what's to be done with this wool once it's off their backs?--it has to be skirted proper, which is what Mrs. Gable is doin' over there--you take off all the dirty bits from the edges, anythin' that's got burrs or muck,--and you keep the belly wool separate, for it's not the same quality!--and then you roll it; and when you roll it--Mrs. Gable knows how to roll it--you can tell a good fleece when it holds together and you can pick it up like a great snowball, and then it's ready for the press,--you must be careful not to pack it too tight or the lanolin will get all sticky, for the oils must have a chance to breathe a bit--" "The staple length is the primary consideration," put in Mr. Pettigrew. "O' course the staple's a consideration," said Silas,--"Mrs. Gable knows about staple--that's the first thing you feel for--you want a good long fibre without no breaks in it,--you can tell by the feel if it's strong,--a weak staple's as bad as no staple at all,--and if you want to test it proper, you take a lock and pull it--" "A micrometer would be a more scientific instrument for determining the fineness, I believe," said the merchant. "Miss Thorne's interests would be--a--would be better served by--and the numerical data would provide a more accurate valuation for market." "Well you can bring your micro-whatsit if you please," said Silas,--"but I won't need it--I've never seen a sheep that carried a number on its back, 'cept for the paint mark to say who it belongs to--and that paint's a plaguey nuisance when it comes to the scourin', you have to cut it all out--but anyway you can tell a good fleece by the crimp and the colour--or Mrs. Gable can if you ha'n't the knack yourself--and after it is rolled you just stack it in a dry place out of the sun till the press comes"-- "May I interject with a point of order?" said Mr. Pettigrew. "I have heard it said by men of considerable experience that if you leave the fleeces to sweat for a day or two in a pile, it improves the texture of the wool. That is the method employed by Mr. Davison and other notable graziers." "'Tain't the method of men who know wool from straw," said Silas; "though I ain't sayin' a word agin Mr. Davison nor his flock, for they're as fine a flock as you'll see in this county; he's a good man and he's a good grazier--there ain't no better man in the parish than John Davison, nor no better flock nother; but I trust there's as good; and I've seen finer wool that was packed dry as I'd care to handle or wear." "It would make a man's hands dreadful greasy!" said Tom. Clara stood by, listening to it all with a smile, when her cousin suddenly appeared at her elbow, having come round the corner of the barn. "I have been taking tea with your mother," he said, "and she has granted me leave to come and find you. I mean!--not that I required leave, for I should have found you in any case--" "Ah Mr. Davies!" said Clara with a laugh, "you are rightly caught. You see the perils of paying calls when there is work to be done. But it is a fine sight, is it not? I have been admiring it this quarter hour." He forbore to say what he had been admiring. "Very fine," he said. "Come back a pace, Mr. Davies--is it not a striking scene, with that dusty light and the deep shadows in the barn? See young Tom there with that struggling ewe--Edward! isn't he a perfect ringer for that old print of Jacob wrestling the angel?--the one in Papa's study--whose was it--Delacroix!--by the window--don't you see it? It is quite something, is it not, Mr. Davies?" "I confess," he said with a slight shake of his head, "my imagination is not so well-schooled. You are forever shewing me marvels where I see only common sights." "That young lady, sir," announced Mr. Pettigrew from across the paddock, where he was attempting to feel the quality of a fleece with his gloved hands,--"that young lady, sir, is an inspiration to her--a--to all of her sex." "An inspiration!" said Mr. Davies. "In a supervisory capacity, Mr. Pettigrew means," said Clara in a low voice. "Well I won't deny she's a good girl," said Silas, speaking as if to himself, while deftly turning a sheep onto its other side,--"she has a mind to be a good girl--she's as sharp a girl as you'll find anywhere!" Clara's composure nearly failed her, though Tom, whose sense of humour was not his strongest faculty, remained perfectly grave. "Miss Thorne, sir," Mr. Pettigrew continued with a deeply satisfied smile,--"Miss Thorne, Mr. Davies, offers her distinguished patronage to the great pastoral pursuits of our nation." "Not to the exclusion of all else, surely?" said Mr. Davies, smiling and directing the query with his eyes to Clara. But she would not meet them. "You know," she said, turning slightly away from the dusty arena, "I must take an interest, as a matter of necessity--with Papa away in London, I am to be the farmer in his stead." "So I understand--so your mother informed me,--but I must admit--forgive me--you do not seem fashioned for such a weight of worldly business." Edward sighed, and Clara's gaze fell.

She did not lift them again immediately; but stood quite still, the faint colour in her cheeks a little deepened by the notice, or perhaps by the keenness of the evening air. It was a momentary yielding to the truth of his words, a silent acknowledgement of the weariness that already touched the edges of her spirit; but it passed as quickly as it came.

"I beg your pardon," said Mr. Davies in a lower tone, moving a step nearer to her side. "I have been unpardonably clumsy. I only meant--I would not for the world imply a doubt of your spirit, but only of your physical strength for such an undertaking."

"My spirit must lend my strength what it lacks, then," she said, at last raising her eyes with a smile that was half frank and half reserved. "For the undertaking is mine, whether I am fashioned for it or no. We Thornes are not in the habit of shrinking from our duties, Mr. Davies. And besides," she added, her glance returning to the busy scene, "I have good helpers."

"That you have, miss," said Silas, who had caught her last words, though not the conversation that preceded them. He straightened up from the ewe he had just released, which scampered off bleating indignantly, and stretched his back with a groan of satisfaction. "There ain't a better lad for the work than young Tom, though he's quiet about it; and Edward's gettin' the hang of it right enough. The work's goin' as smooth as butter."

"It is a most efficacious system," Mr. Pettigrew declared, his voice booming with authority. He had abandoned his futile attempts at fleece-judging and now stood with his hands clasped behind his back, surveying the operation like a general reviewing his troops. "A truly remarkable display of pastoral efficiency. I apprehend the wool is to be packed and sent directly to the factor in Leeds?"

"'Tain't rightly done till it's washed, sir," said Silas, pausing with his shears in hand to look over at him. The lantern light caught the greasy lustre of the wool matted on his forearms. "You take it right from the sheep, afore the grease has a chance to set hard, and you wash it in the beck with soft soap; then you spread it on the hurdles to dry in the sun. It fetches a better price by two-pence the pound when it's washed clean, and it's lighter to cart, besides."

"Indeed!" said Mr. Pettigrew, looking much interested. "A fascinating local custom. However, I have been given to understand, from a most learned treatise on the subject, that the most modern agriculturalists advocate for a period of--a--of maturation. They opine that allowing the wool to be stored in its natural state, in the grease as it were, for a fortnight or more, improves the elasticity of the fibre and lends it a superior quality for spinning. The lanolin, you see, performs a sort of natural conditioning."

"I've heerd o' that practice," said Silas, taking up a fresh sheep that Tom had manhandled into position. "Mr. Grantham over at Oakhill tried it one year; said he'd read it in a book from London. His wool came out of the sacks half-rotted, and the other half so matted and hard the carders would hardly take it. He lost nigh on half his clip, and the factor told him he'd have done better to shear his sheep with a rake and have done with it."

"There must have been some--a--some ancillary factor at play," said Mr. Pettigrew, looking a trifle discomposed. "Perhaps the storage conditions were not optimal. The treatise was most particular about a dry, airy loft."

"He's got the best-built barns in the Riding," Silas said placidly, the shears already beginning their rhythmic snipping. "No, sir. Wool ain't like wine; it don't get better for sittin' about. It's the way John Davison does it, and you'll not find a better flock-master in the county. He washes his wool same-day, and I've heerd tell the queen herself has a blanket made from his fleeces. He says what's good enough for the sheep's back is good enough for the wash tub, and I'm of a mind to agree with him."

"It would be a sight of extra work, letting it sit," said Tom, speaking for the first time. "It'd get all clarted up."

Clara smiled again at the practical wisdom that so effortlessly deflated the scientific theory. She saw Mr. Davies's eye meet hers, a shared amusement in his glance that was both pleasant and, for some reason, a little disquieting. She turned her attention back to the work. Edward, her cousin, was now attempting to corner a particularly wily old ewe, and his lanky, awkward movements in the shifting light gave him the look of a shadow puppet worked by an unskilled hand. The air was thick with the strong scent of wool and lanolin, of dust, and of the sheep themselves; it was a smell that was at once earthy and not unpleasant, the very perfume of the farm's labours.

"Your man Silas seems a great authority," Mr. Davies said, his voice once more for her ear alone.

"He is the finest shepherd in the dale," said Clara. "Papa says he knows every sheep by its face, and can tell from a hundred yards if one is sickening for a cough. His wisdom is not from books, but it is wisdom nonetheless. I should be quite lost without him."

"And yet," Mr. Davies persisted gently, "the burden of decision will fall to you. Whether to heed the book or the shepherd. It is no small thing."

"I shall learn to weigh them," she answered, a new firmness in her tone. "And I think, Mr. Davies, I shall learn to trust the wisdom that has been proven on our own ground before I adopt the fashions of London. That seems a safe principle, in farming as in other things."

He did not reply to that, but fell silent, watching as Silas finished his work on the last ewe and gave it a gentle pat on its now naked rump. The creature scrambled up, looking strangely slight and bewildered, and bolted into the darkness of the paddock to find its fellows.

"That's the last of 'em for today," Silas declared, wiping his brow with the back of a greasy hand. "One hundred and forty-two ewes shorn, and a good yield it is. We've filled six of the big sacks, and I reckon we'll have another two afore we're through with the rams tomorrow. You can tell Mrs. Thorne we've had a right good day of it, Miss Clara."

"I will, Silas, and thank you," she said warmly. "And thank you, Tom, and Edward. You have all worked wonderfully."

Edward gave a tired grin and leaned against a post, while Tom merely ducked his head, his face inscrutable in the gloom. Mr. Pettigrew clapped his hands together with an air of finality.

"A capital day's work! Capital! Now, I daresay, a little refreshment would not go amiss. All this--a--vicarious labour is thirsty work!"

"Supper is waiting in the kitchen, I'm sure," said Clara. "Silas, Tom, you will come in. Edward, do go and make yourself presentable. Mr. Pettigrew, my mother will be expecting you."

She marshalled them towards the house with the quiet authority she was beginning to find within herself, the sovereign princess of her father's domain in more than just fancy. Mr. Davies fell into step beside her as the others moved ahead, their boots crunching on the gravel path that led from the barnyard to the kitchen garden. The air had grown colder now that the sun was fully set, and a sliver of moon was rising over the dark shape of the fells.

"I hope," he said, his tone hesitant again, "that you will allow me to retract my foolish words entirely. Seeing you here, it is clear you are more than equal to the task."

"You are forgiven, Mr. Davies," she said, though a part of her felt his revised opinion was as little founded in real knowledge as his first one had been. He saw her for a quarter of an hour in the barn, and drew one conclusion; he saw her give a few simple directions, and he drew the opposite. "I confess I am not as confident as you are, but I am determined. That will have to suffice."

"Determination like yours can move mountains," he said earnestly. "But even so, the work of a farm is practical. There are accounts, letters to factors, wages to be paid. If there is any way in which I might be of service--any small matter of business you would wish for another eye to look over--I should count it a very great privilege. My own father's estate, though small, has given me some little experience in such things."

She was silent for a few paces. It was a kind offer, and a practical one. Her father's desk, with its stacks of ledgers and disorderly papers, was a daunting prospect. She knew her own head for figures was not of the strongest, and Edward, for all his good nature, was more likely to add to the confusion than to diminish it.

"That is very good of you," she said softly, as they passed through the little gate into the garden, where the last of the tulips stood like pale ghosts in the moonlight. "I will not make any promises, for I have not yet assessed the state of my kingdom's treasury. But perhaps, when I have found the bottom of Papa's ledgers, I may be glad of an abler head than my own to help me make sense of them."

"I am at your service, whenever you should choose to command me," he replied, and the warmth in his voice was unmistakable.

They had reached the stone flags by the kitchen door, where the light from within spilled out in a cheerful yellow rectangle, illuminating the worn step and the pots of herbs on the sill. The sound of Silas's voice, and the clatter of plates, came from inside.

Clara paused with her hand on the latch. "Will you not come in for supper, Mr. Davies? I am sure my mother would be pleased, and after Mr. Pettigrew's company she will be glad of more rational conversation."

He smiled, a quick, handsome smile that transformed his usually serious face. "I should like nothing better, but I have detained my man and horses this long while. I must not keep them waiting in the cold. But I thank you for the invitation, Miss Thorne. And for the--the lesson in sheep-shearing."

"I think the lesson was more in the washing," she said, a glimmer of her earlier archness returning.

"In either case, I am the wiser for it," he said. He bowed, a formal gesture that seemed strangely at home there on the rustic doorstep. "Good night, Miss Thorne."

"Good night, Mr. Davies."

She watched him walk back down the path, his tall figure quickly swallowed by the shadows. Then she lifted the latch and went inside, into the warmth and the light and the comfortable smells of roasting meat and baking bread. Her mother was seated by the fire, her mending basket in her lap, while Mr. Pettigrew held forth from the hearthrug, a tankard of ale in his hand. Silas and Tom were already seated at the long oak table, their faces scrubbed, talking in low tones to the cook, a stout, red-faced woman who was just pulling a large pie from the oven.

"Ah, Clara, my dear," said her mother, looking up. "I was just hearing from Mr. Pettigrew what a wonderful manager you are becoming."

"Mr. Pettigrew is too kind," Clara said, untying the ribbons of her bonnet. "The credit belongs to Silas and the others. They have done a great day's work."

"Nonsense, Miss Thorne!" boomed the gentleman in question. "Leadership is the essential element! The--a--the catalyst which transforms mere labour into productive enterprise! Your presence was indispensable."

Clara gave a noncommittal smile and went to help the cook, Mary, serve the men their supper. The talk flowed around her, a comfortable river of farm gossip, weather predictions, and Mr. Pettigrew's ponderous pronouncements. She took her own plate and sat near her mother, eating with an appetite sharpened by the cool air, but her mind was not entirely on the conversation. She thought of the dusty barn, of the feel of responsibility settling upon her shoulders like a mantle, and of Mr. Davies's earnest face in the lantern light. His offer of help was a comfort; his admiration was something else, a thing she was not yet sure she knew what to do with.

After supper, when the men had gone to their cottages and Mr. Pettigrew had at last taken his leave, Clara sat with her mother for a few moments in the quiet parlour.

"Mr. Davies stayed quite late," Mrs. Thorne remarked, her needles clicking softly. "He seemed very taken with the shearing."

"He was very civil," Clara replied, staring into the fire.

"He is a good young man, Clara. Well-regarded, and with a respectable property of his own. His father left things in good order."

Clara knew what her mother was implying, the hope that lay beneath the simple words. In their circumstances, with her father's health so uncertain and his return so distant, a good match for Clara was more than a mother's wish; it was a matter of prudence.

"His property is his own concern, Mama," she said, a little more shortly than she intended. "My concern is our property."

Her mother sighed softly. "I know, my love. It is a heavy burden for you. I wish it were not so."

"It is not a burden," Clara said, and as she spoke she felt the truth of it. "It is my work. It is what I must do."

Later, in her own room, she went to the window and looked out. The moon was higher now, casting a silver sheen over the fields and the dark line of the woods beyond. The farm was quiet, asleep under the vast, starry sky. It was all hers to care for; the sheep in the paddock, the wool in the barn, the fields that would soon need ploughing, the men and women who depended on it for their livelihood. It was a weight of worldly business, as Mr. Davies had said; but as she stood there, the quiet strength that had been stirring within her all evening seemed to settle and grow firm. She was no longer just Miss Thorne, the daughter of the house. She was the farmer, in his stead. And she found, to her own surprise, that she was not afraid. The task was great, but her corner of the world was beautiful in the moonlight, and she had good helpers, and she would learn.

She went to bed with the cool scent of the night air still in her nostrils, and slept a sleep more profound and restorative than any she had known for months, a sleep untroubled by the anxious dreams that had so often attended the news from her father's distant post. She woke to the thin, clear light of early morning, not with a start, but with a slow dawning of consciousness, as if she were a part of the quiet awakening of the world outside her window. For a moment she lay still, listening to the first tentative chirps of the sparrows in the ivy, and the distant, complaining low of a cow waiting for the milkmaid. It was a music she had heard all her life, but it sounded different now; it was no longer merely the backdrop to her own small existence, but the very pulse of a life for which she was now the beating heart. Her work, she had called it; and as she rose and dressed in a plain dark gown, forgoing the ribbons and lace her mother still laid out for her by habit, the word settled in her mind not as a sentence, but as a commission.

When she came down, she found her mother already in the breakfast parlour, a letter in her hand and an expression of gentle distress upon her features. Mary was just bringing in the coffee, its invigorating aroma filling the room.
"Good morning, Mama," Clara said, kissing her mother's cheek. "Is that a letter from Papa?"
"It is, my love," Mrs. Thorne said, folding it with a sigh. "His duties are as arduous as ever, and the climate does not agree with him. He sends his love, of course, and he writes a great deal about you."
"About me?" Clara asked, taking her seat.
"He is so proud, Clara; but so worried. He says it is no life for a young lady, to be mired in the business of the farm. He implores me to see that you do not overtax your strength, and he has written to Mr. Pettigrew to ask him to assume a more direct oversight."
Clara felt a prickle of indignation, which she did her best to conceal by attending carefully to the pouring of her coffee. Mr. Pettigrew's ponderous oversight was the last thing she desired. "Papa is very good," she said, her voice studiously even. "But he is a great many miles away, and cannot see how things truly stand. I am perfectly well, and the work is not a mire. I find it... bracing."
Mrs. Thorne looked at her daughter, at the new firmness in her jaw and the clear, direct light in her eyes, and she seemed to see that arguments would be of little use. "Well, my dear," she said, with another, softer sigh, "I pray you are right. But there is another matter. Mr. Grimes, the wool-stapler, has sent word he will call this morning. Your father always handled that. It is a dreadful business, all haggling and figuring. Perhaps we should put him off, until Mr. Pettigrew can be here."
"No, Mama," Clara said at once. "I will see him. The wool must be sold, and I must learn to sell it."
"But Clara, he is such a coarse man! He will try to cheat you."
"Then I will try not to be cheated," Clara replied, with a small smile that did not quite reach her eyes. She felt a flutter of apprehension in her stomach, but it was overlaid with a strange, keen anticipation, the feeling of a soldier before the first engagement.

After breakfast, she put on a pair of sturdy boots and went out to the barn, where the great, soft mountain of fleece was piled. The air inside was close, thick with the smell of lanolin and dust and dry hay. Silas, the old shepherd who had served her father for thirty years, was there, sorting a few of the fleeces on a rough wooden table. He was a man of few words, with a face as weathered as a piece of old leather, and eyes that seemed to hold the quiet wisdom of the fields.
"Good morning, Silas," Clara said.
He looked up and touched the brim of his hat. "Mornin', Miss Clara. Come to look at the clip?"
"I have, Silas. Mr. Grimes is coming this morning, and I mean to sell it to him."
Silas grunted, and turned a fleece over with his skilled hands, pulling at the fibres to test their length and quality. "He'll try ye," he said, without looking at her. "He's a hard man, Grimes. He'll look at this wool, which is as fine a clip as we've had in five years, the spring pasture was that good, and he'll tell ye it's coarse, or short, or full o' muck. That's his way. Always has been."
"What price did my father get last year?"
"Eight pence the pound, miss. And it was a struggle to get it. Grimes, he started at sixpence. Your father, he had the measure of him. He'd laugh, and talk about the weather, and walk him round the yard for an hour, and then they'd settle."
"I am not sure I have the skill for that," Clara said, her heart sinking a little.
Silas looked at her then, a long, assessing gaze. "Maybe ye do, and maybe ye don't. But you've got a good head on your shoulders, Miss Clara. Don't ye let him see you're worried. And don't ye take the first price he offers, nor the second neither. This here is good wool. There's nigh on two thousand pound of it. It's worth what it's worth." He fell silent again, returning to his work, but his words, spare as they were, were a greater encouragement to Clara than an hour of Mr. Pettigrew's finest speeches.

She was in the farm's small office, a room smelling of old paper and leather, when Mr. Grimes was announced by a rap on the door that was less a knock than a summons. She had before her the account books from the previous years, and had been studying the figures for wool sales, her brow furrowed in concentration. She took a breath, smoothed her skirts, and called, "Come in."
Mr. Grimes was a large man, red-faced and stout, dressed in a town suit that seemed too tight for him. He filled the doorway, bringing with him an air of aggressive commerce and a faint scent of brandy.
"Miss Thorne," he boomed, his eyes sweeping over her and the little office with an expression that was not quite a sneer, but was certainly not respectful. "Your servant. A sad business, your father's absence. A very sad business. I've come about the wool."
"Good morning, Mr. Grimes," Clara said, rising from her chair. She did not offer her hand. "Yes, the clip is ready. It is in the barn, if you would care to inspect it."
"All in good time, miss, all in good time. First, we'll talk terms. Now, given the circumstances, and the state of the market, which is very poor, very poor indeed, I am prepared to offer you sixpence half-penny a pound. It's a generous offer, all things considered. It will save you the trouble of finding another buyer, which would be difficult for you, I'm sure." He sat himself down, uninvited, in the one other chair the room contained, looking at her as if she were a child to be placated.
Clara's heart beat a little faster. Silas had been right to the letter. She sat down again herself, moving with a deliberation she did not feel, and folded her hands on the ledger before her. "That is not a price I can consider, Mr. Grimes," she said, her voice much calmer than she had expected. "The market may be poor for some, but I am given to understand that fine, long-staple wool such as ours still commands a respectable price. My father received eight pence last year."
"Ah, last year!" Mr. Grimes chuckled, a moist, unpleasant sound. "Last year was last year, miss. Your father was a good man, but a dreamer, if you'll forgive me saying so. Always thought his wool was spun gold. This is a business, Miss Thorne, a world of hard facts. Seven pence. That's my final word. For your father's sake."
Clara looked at him, at his florid, confident face, and she felt a surge of something very like battle spirit. She thought of her father, ill and far away; she thought of her mother's anxious face, of Silas's quiet trust, of the sleeping farm under the moonlight. This was not about a few pence, she realized; it was about everything.
"I am sorry you have had your journey for nothing, then, Mr. Grimes," she said, making a show of closing the ledger. "Seven pence is not a price I will accept. I have had another enquiry, from a merchant in the city, and I believe I will do better to wait for his offer."
This was an invention of the moment, a complete falsehood, but she said it with such quiet conviction that Mr. Grimes shifted in his chair.
"Another enquiry?" he blustered. "Who? Not that fellow Henderson? He'll promise you the moon and pay you in dust. You're a young lady, Miss Thorne; you don't understand these things. You're being foolish."
"Perhaps," Clara said. "But it is my wool, and my foolishness. I am very busy this morning, Mr. Grimes, so if we have concluded our business--"
She was interrupted by another knock at the door, this one lighter and more hesitant. Before Clara could speak, the door opened and Mr. Davies put his head in.
"Forgive me," he said, his eyes going from Clara's tense face to the displeased bulk of Mr. Grimes. "I seem to be intruding. I will call back at another time."
"Not at all, Mr. Davies, please come in," Clara said, a wave of relief washing over her. The unexpected appearance of an ally, and a respected local landowner at that, changed the entire atmosphere of the little room.
Mr. Davies stepped inside, his expression one of polite curiosity. He was dressed for riding, and his cheeks were flushed with the fresh air. "Good morning, Grimes," he said, his tone civil but cool. "Making your rounds, I see."
"Davies," the wool-stapler grunted, his bonhomie quite evaporated. "Just concluding a bit of business with Miss Thorne."
"Indeed?" Mr. Davies looked at Clara, a question in his eyes. "I hope you are driving a hard bargain, Miss Thorne. I saw your clip as I passed the barn. It's the best in the district this year; better than my own, I'll admit freely. Old Silas has worked his magic again. The London market is paying ten pence for wool of that quality, you know. I had the news from my agent just this morning."
A silence fell in the small office. Mr. Grimes's face, already red, seemed to deepen to a shade of purple. Clara kept her eyes fixed on him, her expression carefully neutral, though her heart was singing a song of triumph. Mr. Davies's intervention was a gift from heaven, so perfectly timed and so skillfully delivered that it might have been rehearsed.

The great barn, which had but a moment ago been a theatre of commerce and quiet strife, settled back into its accustomed silence, a silence punctuated only by the sleepy rustle of the remaining ewes in their pens and the dance of dust motes in the shafts of afternoon light that pierced the gloom. The air was thick with the lanolin-rich scent of the fleece, a perfume of labour and reward that was sweeter to Clara than any French concoction. She walked the length of the great wool-pile, running her hand not over the coarse sacking but over the memory of the living fleece, the curl and spring of it, the year of sun and rain and grass that it represented. The price was not merely for the clip; it was for the shepherding, for the mending of fences in the sleet, for the worrying over a sick lamb, for the knowledge passed down from her father that she had kept and burnished.

“The men will need to be told,” she said, turning back to Silas. “Their share of this will be a good one. It is only right.”

“They’ll be pleased as punch, Miss Clara,” Silas said, his respect for her deepening with every practical, decent thought she uttered. “They work for the wage, as a man must, but they’ve put their backs into it for you this year. They know a good farmer when they see one, even if she be a slip of a girl, as some would say.” He cleared his throat, suddenly abashed by his own forwardness. “Beggin’ your pardon, miss.”

“There is nothing to pardon, Silas,” she replied, her gaze steady. “I would rather be a ‘slip of a girl’ who gets a shilling for her wool than a great bluff man who must settle for eightpence.”

A slow grin returned to his face. “Aye. There is that. Now, about these sacks. They’re well enough here, but we ought to cover ‘em with a tarp, lest a stray bit o’ weather find its way through that loose board in the roof. And I’ll sleep in the barn entryway tonight and the night after, till they’re gone. Not that I expect trouble, but a prize like this… well, it’s best to be sure.”

Clara nodded, her mind already turning to the hundred small duties that success, no less than failure, demanded. The security of the wool, the payment of the shearers, the mending of that very board in the roof; the farm’s demands were a river that never ceased its flow.

The news, of course, did not wait for the wagons. It travelled, as news of its kind always does in a small community, with the speed of a startled bird, flying from the mouth of Silas’s wife at the village pump, to the ear of the baker’s boy, to the counter of the draper’s shop, and thence into every cottage and public house. A shilling a pound. From Ainsworth of Bradford, no less. For Clara Davies’s clip. The story grew with each telling, burnished by neighbourly pride and, in some quarters, sharpened by envy. By evensong, the entire parish knew that the girl who had inherited the struggling Davies farm had not merely survived her first year alone; she had triumphed.

It was perhaps inevitable that the news, having completed its circuit, should arrive with all the force of a physical blow at the very door of Mr. Grimes. He heard it from his own foreman, who reported it with a certain grim satisfaction, and his face, already prone to a ruddy choler, became a study in mottled purple. He sputtered, he denied, he accused Ainsworth of madness and the girl of witchcraft. That a chit, a mere female, should have out-manoeuvred him, should have secured a price that made his own offer look not merely miserly but foolish, was an affront to the natural order of things as he understood it. His pride, a far more tender and vital organ than his heart, was grievously wounded.

He did not wait. The following morning, his stout cob, spurred by an ill temper, made short work of the miles to the Davies farm. He found Clara not in the parlour awaiting callers, but in the muddy yard, her sleeves rolled up and her dress protected by a heavy apron, helping Silas repair the hinge on the gate to the water-meadow. She looked up at the sound of his furious approach, her expression one of calm neutrality.

“Mr. Grimes,” she said, her voice even. “To what do we owe the honour?”

“Honour!” he boomed, dismounting with a thud and leaving the reins to trail in the dirt. “You’ll speak of honour to me, will you? After this… this charade!” He gestured vaguely towards the barn, as if the wool within it were personally offensive. “I’ve come for an explanation, miss. What trickery is this?”

Clara slowly wiped her hands on a piece of sacking, her movements deliberate. Silas, who had been holding the heavy gate steady, let it rest against the post and stood up, his face a stony mask, his hands bunched into fists at his sides.

“I am not aware of any trickery, sir,” Clara said. “I sold my wool to a willing buyer for a price we both deemed fair. It is the basis of all commerce, I believe.”

“Fair!” Grimes scoffed, his jowls quivering with indignation. “A shilling a pound is not fair, it is insanity! It is a fantasy! No clip in this county is worth a shilling! You’ve filled Ainsworth’s head with some nonsense, some womanly wile, and you’ll ruin the market for every honest man!”

“My wool was judged on its quality, Mr. Grimes,” Clara stated, her patience wearing thin. “Its fineness of staple, its length, and its cleanness. Mr. Ainsworth is a man who knows his business, and he paid for quality. Perhaps if you had looked more closely at the fleece and less at the presumed helplessness of its owner, you might have seen the same value.”

The shot landed with stinging accuracy. His own offer had been a calculated insult, a test of her nerve, and he knew it. To have it thrown back at him so coolly, in front of his own former farmhand, was more than he could bear.

“Helplessness! You were near tears over a dead sheep not two days ago! Don’t play the great lady with me, Clara Davies. You got lucky. Ainsworth was in a mood to be a fool, and you were there to profit by it. But it won’t last. Prices will settle, and you will have made an enemy of every farmer in the district by setting such a ridiculous precedent.”

“I think,” she said, her voice dropping, taking on a new and quiet authority that was more intimidating than any shout, “that you will find I have made no enemies, save those who, like yourself, believe that a neighbour’s good fortune is their own personal loss. The honest men you speak of will, I suspect, be glad to see that a good price can be had for good work. They may even be inspired to improve their own flocks.” She looked him directly in the eye. “You came here not for an explanation, Mr. Grimes, but to vent your spleen and soothe your wounded pride. I have work to do, and you have kept me from it long enough. Silas, the hammer if you please.”

Silas, with a look of pure, unadulterated delight, passed her the hammer. The gesture was a dismissal more profound than any words. Grimes stood there, his mouth working, his face a thundercloud from which no more lightning would issue. He had come expecting a weeping girl he could bully back into her place; he had found a proprietor of her own land, a woman who held a hammer as comfortably as a needle, and who had met his bluster with a composure that was as unbreachable as a stone wall. Defeated, he snatched up his reins, hauled himself back onto his cob with an angry grunt, and galloped away without a backward glance, leaving behind only the churned-up mud of the yard.

Clara watched him go, the hammer heavy in her hand. She felt no elation, only a deep and weary certainty. This, too, was part of the price. Silas let out a long, slow breath.

“Well,” he said, finally. “He’ll not be back in a hurry.”

“No,” said Clara, turning her attention back to the broken hinge. “He will not.” She placed the pin and, with three sharp, perfectly placed blows, drove it home. The sound echoed in the quiet yard like a declaration.

The days that followed were a blur of activity. The news of her confrontation with Grimes also made the rounds, and Clara found her standing in the community subtly altered. There was a new respect in the nods she received in the village, a new warmth in the greetings. She had not only secured a good price; she had stood up to a bully, and in the quiet ethics of the countryside, the latter was almost as important as the former.

When Mr. Ainsworth’s wagons arrived, great, sturdy vehicles pulled by teams of magnificent Shire horses, it was a village event. Children gathered to watch as the sacks were loaded, their number and weight confirming the fabulous rumour of the shilling price. Mr. Ainsworth had sent his chief clerk, a precise man with a ledger and a leather satchel, who counted the sacks, checked them against his list, and then presented Clara with a bank draft.

She took the slip of paper and looked at it. The sum written there was substantial enough to make her hands tremble slightly. It was enough to clear the farm’s remaining debts, to buy the seed for the spring planting, to repair the roof, and still leave a surplus that was, to her, a fortune.

That evening, after the wagons were gone and the farm had settled into its evening peace, she sat at her father’s old desk in the small, book-lined study. The fire crackled in the hearth, a welcome counterpoint to the chill that seeped through the old stone walls. She laid the draft on the blotter, weighting it down with a smooth river stone her father had used as a paperweight. For a long time, she simply looked at it, this tangible result of a year’s unrelenting toil. The ghost of the dead ewe was still with her, not as a spectre of failure, but as a quiet reminder of the costs. The profit was not clean; it was stained with mud and birth-fluid and loss, and was the more real for it.

She pulled a sheet of paper towards her and dipped a pen in the inkwell. She did not begin by listing debts to be paid, but by drawing up a different kind of account. First, a new ram. Not just any ram, but a prize-winner, a bloodline that would lift the quality of her entire flock for years to come. The purchase would be an extravagance, but it was the kind of wise extravagance that her father had taught her was the heart of good husbandry: an investment in the future. Next, she wrote ‘Drain the lower pasture’. It was a boggy mess where the flock was prone to foot-rot, and reclaiming it would add five good acres of grazing. It would be a monumental task, requiring hired labour and weeks of back-breaking work. And then, ‘New slates for the barn roof’, not just a patch, but a whole new covering to protect the assets within.

As she wrote, the weariness of the past year began to recede, replaced by the clear-eyed energy of a commander planning a new campaign. The money was not an end, a comfortable pillow on which to rest; it was a tool, a weapon, a beginning. It was the means by which she could make the farm not just solvent, but truly hers, shaped by her intelligence and her will. It was the answer to Mr. Olmney’s careless pity, to Mr. Grimes’s bullying contempt, to the world’s assumption that she was a damsel in need of rescue.

She did not need a rescuer. She needed a new ram, and five acres of dry pasture, and a sound roof over her head. And she had learned, in the hardest and most satisfying of ways, how to procure them for herself. The farmer’s strength, she thought, laying down her pen, was not merely in enduring the storm. It was in using the rainwater to make the fields grow. She looked from the list in her hand to the draft on the desk, and then out the window into the gathering dark, where her fields and her future lay waiting. And for the first time, she felt not the burden of her inheritance, but its profound and bracing liberty.

Barby resumed her work, but with a more vigorous, almost punishing motion. "Slates. Pastures. You'll be wanting the moon on a string next, I suppose. That draft from Mr. Grimes must have been a powerful lot bigger than I imagined."

"It was a good draft," Fleda admitted. "But it is not so large as the work that needs doing. It is only a start."

"'Mornin', Miss Fleda. Mornin', Barby. That smells about as good as a body could hope for. See you're up with the sparrers, Miss Fleda. Figured you'd sleep in a bit, after yesterday."

"There is too much to do for sleeping in, Earl," she said. "Come and have some coffee. I wished to speak with you."

Earl looked from her to Barby and back again, his farmer's intuition sensing a change in the atmosphere. Philetus shuffled in behind him, nodding a silent good morning to all, and immediately made himself useful by fetching a new log for the fire, his movements slow and deliberate as ever.

Earl's eyebrows, which were grizzled and expressive, rose a fraction. He glanced at Barby, who gave a minute, martyred shake of her head. "That's a job o' work, Miss Fleda. "My uncle is not here," Fleda said, gently but with finality. "And I believe the return will be worth the cost. With five more acres of good grazing, we can increase the flock. Which brings me to my other plan. I intend to buy a new ram."

"Well, now, that's sense," Earl said, nodding with more enthusiasm. "Old Major's gettin' on. We could do with some new blood. I heerd tell that Seth Plumfield has a yearling ram out o' his best ewe that he might part with for a fair price."

"I am not talking about Mr. Plumfield's yearling, good as it may be," Fleda said, her voice very clear and steady. "I have been reading the agricultural journals. There is a breeder in the next state, a Mr. Prentiss, who has a flock of pure-bred Merinos. He took first prize at the state fair. I am going to buy the prize-winner's brother."

There was a profound silence in the kitchen, broken only by the crackle of the fire and the distant, mournful lowing of a cow. Philetus, who had been listening with his usual placid stillness, actually turned his head to look at her. Barby's hands were frozen in the dough. Earl Douglass slowly, very slowly, lowered his coffee mug to the table.

"Miss Fleda," he said, his voice dropping to a low, serious tone. "A ram like that... why, a ram like that would cost more than a good horse. It'd cost more than two good horses. It might cost as much as a small farm in some places. 'Tain't sensible. It's... it's vanity."

"No, Earl, it is not vanity," she countered, her cheeks warming. "It is foresight. One good ram like that improves the fleece of every lamb born on this farm for the next five years. It increases the weight of the wool, the quality of the wool, the price we can command for it. It pays for itself ten times over. Buying Mr. Plumfield's yearling is saving money. Buying Mr. Prentiss's Merino is making money. Don't you see the difference?"

"I see a powerful hole in a year's profit," Earl said stubbornly. "We can get a good, serviceable ram for a tenth o' that price, and put the rest into things we need. That new roof you was talkin' about. Or hirin' the men for that pasture you want to fight with. A ram's a ram, Miss Fleda. He does his job and that's the end of it."

"It is not the end of it, it is the beginning!" Fleda felt a surge of impassioned conviction. "Why should we be content with 'serviceable'? Why should we only aim for 'good enough'? My father never aimed for 'good enough'. He aimed for the best. The best herd, the best crops, the best farm in the county. That is the inheritance he left me, Earl; not just the land and the debts, but the ambition to make it thrive."

Her voice had risen, and in the quiet that followed her declaration, they all heard the sound of wheels on the frozen drive, and then a cheerful halloo from without.

"Company," Barby said, wiping her floury hands on her apron, a look of distinct disapproval on her face at the interruption of this vital conference.

"Sounds like the doctor's rig," Earl said, peering out the small window. "And Mr. Olmney's with him. Now what in creation brings them out at this hour?"

Fleda felt a curious mix of annoyance and a strange, fluttery anticipation. She quickly smoothed her hair. "Well, we cannot leave them on the doorstep. Let them in, Philetus."

Dr. Quackenboss bustled in first, his face red from the cold, radiating an aura of professional cheerfulness. "A most salubrious morning, ladies, gentlemen! The atmospheric conditions are highly conducive to a--a--a vigorous constitution! "You are very welcome, doctor," Fleda said, forcing a smile. "And Mr. Olmney. How good of you to call."

Mr. Olmney stepped in behind him, looking taller and more out of place in the rustic kitchen than she remembered. "Miss Ringgan," he said, his voice a low counterpoint to the doctor's booming tones. "I hope we do not intrude. My curiosity, I confess, got the better of me. I was most anxious to hear the outcome of your--your venture to town."

"A success!" cried the doctor. "Capital! I knew it! The agricultural interest triumphs! It is a fine thing, Mr. Olmney, a very fine thing, to see a young lady of Miss Ringgan's attainments apply her mind to the--a--the practical sciences of the soil."

"Indeed," Mr. Olmney said, his eyes fixed on Fleda. There was an unreadable expression in them; not pity, precisely, but a kind of gentle, wondering concern that she found more irritating than outright condescension. "It must be a great relief to have such a weight of care lifted, for a time."

"I could not let the expedition depart without wishing its commander success," he said, his bow a trifle less formal than usual, his eyes holding a genuine warmth.

"Your good wishes are a welcome provision for the road, Mr. Olmney," Fleda returned, smiling. "We go to treat with a foreign power for a prince of the blood."

"Take care you are not asked to give up a part of your own kingdom in exchange," he said, his voice dropping slightly.

"I go prepared for hard bargaining," she assured him. "Hugh is my guard, and my own common sense, I hope, is my shield."

Philetus had brought the sleigh around, the horses stamping and snorting, their breath like smoke. He had packed a heavy woolen blanket over a hot stone for Fleda's feet and stood by the horse's heads, a silent, capable sentinel.

The farewells were made, and with a jingle of harness bells and a crunch of runners on crisp snow, they were off. Fleda looked back once, to see Mr. Olmney still standing before the house, a dark figure against the white, looking after them until they rounded the bend in the road.

The journey was long, and the cold pervasive, but Fleda's mind was too occupied for her to feel it much. They passed through a landscape of rolling hills and sleeping farms, the smoke from chimneys rising in straight, unwavering lines into the still air. They spoke little, the clatter and jingle of their conveyance being the chief sound, but it was a comfortable silence. Fleda was rehearsing her arguments, her questions for Mr. Prentiss, while Hugh, she knew, was simply there, a solid, reassuring presence at her side.

Mr. Prentiss's farm was in a neighbouring county, and it was well past noon when they arrived. It was at once apparent that this was an establishment of a different order. The fences were of neatly cut stone, not split rails; the barn was a vast, imposing structure, painted a deep, prosperous red, and the house itself, a handsome brick colonial, bespoke generations of success. A thrill that was part admiration and part apprehension went through Fleda. This was the level to which she aspired, but it was also a world of confident, established wealth that might well look down upon a novice farmer, and a female one at that.

Mr. Prentiss himself met them at the door. He was a man in his late fifties, tall and spare, with a shrewd, weather-beaten face and eyes the colour of a winter sky. He was dressed not as a labourer, but in the sturdy, well-made clothes of a landed proprietor.

"Miss Ringgan," he said, his voice a dry, down-east drawl. "I had your letter. You've come a long way in the cold."

"Good stock is worth a cold journey, Mr. Prentiss," Fleda replied, meeting his keen gaze directly. "Your reputation precedes you."

Fleda stopped, her breath catching in her throat, and it was not from the cold. The animal was magnificent. He stood with a calm, regal assurance, his head held high. His horns were a pair of glorious, amber-coloured spirals, and his eyes, dark and intelligent, regarded them with an unblinking, monarchal indifference. He was not merely a sheep; he was the very idea of a sheep, perfected and made manifest. Earl's "Roman folly" came back to her, but now it seemed not a criticism, but the plain truth. She was looking at him with two sets of eyes: the artist's, which saw the beauty of his form, and the farmer's, which saw the promise of a thousand such fleeces, of a flock transformed, of a future secured.

"He is everything you said he would be, Mr. Prentiss," she said, her voice quiet.

"Aye," said the farmer. "He is. And he has a price to match."

"It is a high price," she said, her tone even. She did not look at the ram, but at Mr. Prentiss. "A very high price for a farmer just starting to improve her flock."

Hugh shot her a look of surprise. This had not been part of their plan. Mr. Prentiss stared at her, his shrewd eyes narrowed. He was a trader, and he recognized another one. He had expected her to haggle, to try to beat his price down. She had done something more clever; she had accepted his valuation of his own work, a profound form of flattery, and then used that acceptance as leverage for a further gain. He was, Fleda guessed, a man who valued his own judgment and would be pleased to have it so unconditionally affirmed.

A slow smile spread across the farmer's weathered face. "Miss Ringgan," he said, "I believe your flock is in very good hands. You'll have your ewe. Let's go draw up the bill of sale."

The return journey was made in the rising light of a full moon, which turned the snowy landscape into a silvery, enchanted world. "That was masterfully done," Hugh said, breaking a long silence. "Asking for the ewe. I would not have thought of it."

"I was not certain he would agree," Fleda confessed. "But I reasoned that a man so proud of his work would want to see it properly continued. It was a risk, but a smaller one than the first."

"You are a true farmer now, Fleda," he said, and there was a new note of profound respect in his voice. "Not just by name, but by deed."

The praise warmed her more than the furs she was wrapped in. They rode on, the jingle of the bells a cheerful, rhythmic counterpoint to the quiet thrum of victory in her heart. She had gone out as a sovereign princess on a mission; she was returning as a general who had won her first, decisive battle. The cost was great, the campaign ahead was long, but as the familiar outlines of her own hills rose up to meet them under the moonlight, she felt not the burden of care, but the exhilarating, bracing reality of her own power to shape her world. The liberty was no longer a thing to be cautiously tasted; she was drinking it in full.

"You are right, Barby," she said, wrapping her cold hands around the warm mug. "I am only just beginning to learn how right you are."

Hugh took his own coffee and stood leaning against the mantelpiece, his face thoughtful in the firelight. The strong planes of his features, which had seemed so determined and ready for battle at Mr. Prentiss's, were now softened by fatigue and the gentle light.

"It was a good day's work, Fleda," he said, as if in answer to Barby's pragmatism. "A necessary one. You should be proud of what you accomplished."

"I am," she said softly. "But I am also a little frightened. The liberty, as you once called it, feels a very costly thing tonight."

"All things of value are," he answered. "Freedom most of all."

They drank their coffee in a comfortable silence, the only sounds the ticking of the old clock on the wall and the sigh of the fire as a log settled. The grand adventure of the day had resolved itself into this quiet, domestic moment, a pause between one battle and the next. Soon Hugh bid her good-night, and Barby, after a final stoking of the fire, went to her own bed, leaving Fleda alone in the quiet kitchen.

She did not go to her own room immediately, but sat for a long time staring into the glowing embers, the source of so much comfort and yet so like the fire of her own ambition; it needed constant tending lest it die out, and it consumed fuel at an alarming rate. After a time, she rose and went not to her bed, but to the small room off the parlour that served as her uncle's office, and which she had claimed for her own.

She sat down at her little writing desk, the very one at which she had so often penned letters of a far different character, and drew out her account book. The numbers within were stark and unforgiving, telling a story not of poetry or moonlight, but of assets and debits, of incomings and grievous outgoings. The purchase of the General had not merely strained her resources; it had all but broken them, leaving a margin so slim for the coming year's expenses that it made her breath catch. There was no room for poor haying now, no allowance for bad luck or a fall in the price of wool. She had wagered everything on the soundness of her judgment, on the belief that the best foundation, as Hugh had said, was worth any price. Looking at the cold figures on the page, she felt the exhilarating, bracing reality of her power give way to its twin, the crushing weight of its consequences. She closed the book with a decisive snap. Fear was a luxury she could not afford. She had made her move like a general, and now she must command the field with a general's resolve. She went to bed not with the easy sleep of a victor, but with the restless, calculating mind of a commander planning a long and uncertain campaign.

Fleda dressed quickly, forgoing her usual morning leisure, and went downstairs. The house was already stirring with the business of the day. He was magnificent. Larger than any of the other rams, with a thick, dense fleece that fell in creamy folds, a broad chest, and a noble, roman-nosed head, he surveyed his new surroundings with an air of placid arrogance. "Much lighter," she said, her voice full of feeling. "He is a wonderful creature, Mr. Olmney. Truly. Earl himself is impressed, and to win a word of praise from Earl is like squeezing water from a stone. I feel as though I have won the first skirmish."
"You speak of it as a battle," he said, his tone gentle.
"Is it not?" Fleda looked away from him, towards the barn where the flock was now safely shut in for the night. "It is a battle against my own ignorance, and against the doubts of others. It is a fight to make this place prosper, to prove that--that it can be done." To prove that a woman could do it, was what she left unsaid, but she felt sure that he understood.
"Then I must not call you Fleda anymore," he said, with a half-smile that did not quite reach his eyes. "I should address you as 'General'."
Fleda laughed, a clear, bright sound in the twilight. "No, no. I am only the sovereign. The 'General' is my new ram. That is what I have decided to call him."
"The General?" he repeated, surprised. "It is a formidable name for a sheep."
"He is a formidable sheep," she answered. "And he is leading the charge for me. So it seems fitting."
They stood in silence for a few moments more, the quiet broken only by the distant lowing of a cow from the barn. The first star pricked the deep purple of the eastern sky.
"You will take cold, Miss Ringgan," Mr. Olmney said at last, his voice losing its playful tone and reverting to one of sincere solicitude. "Let me see you to the house."
He did not offer his arm, but walked beside her, his presence a silent comfort against the vast, chilly emptiness of the landscape. As they reached the little side door, from which the warm glow of the kitchen fire was spilling out onto the snow, he paused.
"I do not wish to seem impertinent," he said, his voice low, "but if at any time the battle should seem to be going against you--if you should need reinforcements of any kind--I hope you know that you have allies who would count it a privilege to be summoned."
Fleda's heart beat a little faster. She could not see his face clearly in the deep shadow of the house, but she could feel the earnestness in his words. It was a strange and intoxicating thing, to be looked upon at once as a fragile creature in need of protection and a sovereign in command of a war. She found she did not entirely dislike it.
"Thank you, Mr. Olmney," she said softly. "I will remember that. I am a very prudent sovereign; I do not scorn my allies."
He bowed slightly, a gesture of old-fashioned courtesy that seemed perfectly at home in the rustic setting, and then he was gone, his footsteps crunching softly away into the darkness. Fleda stood for a long time in the doorway, the cold air on her face and the warm fire at her back. The promise she had bought that morning was no longer a source of terror, but a shield against it. The day had been won. But as she looked out into the profound, starry darkness that now enveloped her little kingdom, she knew, with a certainty that was both daunting and exhilarating, that the campaign had only just begun.

The night was vast and silent now, the voices and the firelight having receded into the warm heart of the house, and Fleda felt herself suspended between two worlds; the one of quiet domesticity she had just re-entered, and the other, the great, dark, sleeping world of her farm, which lay under the starlight waiting for the dawn. It was a kingdom, she had called it; and as she closed the door at last, shutting out the cold, she felt the weight of the crown settle not unpleasantly upon her brow.

The kitchen was quiet, save for the crackling of the logs in the great fireplace and the soft scratch of a needle through cloth. Barby was sitting at the great kitchen table, her strong arms bare to the elbow, and by the light of a single tallow candle was mending a rent in one of Philetus's stout woollen shirts, her brow furrowed in concentration. She looked up as Fleda entered, her expression softening a little from its usual cast of severe practicality.

"The sugar's well enough, Miss Fleda," she said, without waiting to be asked. "Mr. Douglass knows his business, for all his talk. It'll be a good run, if the weather holds."

"I am sure it will be," Fleda answered, coming to stand by the fire and hold her chilled hands out to the blaze. "Everyone worked so hard."

"They did that," Barby agreed, biting off a thread. "Philetus is nigh tuckered out, and the doctor, he was more hindrance than help, but his heart was in it, I suppose. As for Mr. Douglass, he'd work all night and all the next day if you let him, and think nothin' of it." She gave Fleda a keen look. "That Mr. Olmney stayed a good while."

Fleda felt a faint warmth rise to her cheeks that had nothing to do with the fire. "He was talking with Aunt Lucy first," she said, busying herself with turning her hands to warm the other side. "He only came out to the fire for a few minutes."

"Hmph," said Barby, a sound that conveyed a world of meaning. "He's a proper sort of gentleman. Not like some you see. He don't look at you as if he's pricing your goods for market."

Fleda was silent. She felt, for an instant, a wish that the world might be managed by such quiet courtesies and earnest speeches as Mr. Olmney's; but the feeling was fleeting, for the rough reality of her new life stood close at hand, represented by the very shirt Barby was mending and the weary men who had gone to their rest. A sovereign had need of allies, but she could not depend on them for the daily running of her state.

"The new ram is to be delivered tomorrow, Barby," she said, changing the subject. "Mr. Douglass says he is a very large animal. He is to be put in the north pasture pen, the one we had reinforced."

"It's a costly beast," said Barby, her focus returning to her needlework. "I hope he proves his worth."

"He will," said Fleda, with a firmness that surprised even herself. "He is the first soldier in my new army. I have named him 'The General'."

Barby looked up, and for a moment her stern features relaxed into what was almost a smile. "Well, I've heard sheep called many a thing, Miss Fleda, but never that. I hope he minds his commandin' officer."

"He will have to learn," said Fleda. And as she went up the cold, dark stairs to her own room, she repeated the words to herself. He will have to learn. They would all have to learn, and she most of all.

The next morning broke in a way that seemed to confirm all of Earl Douglass's predictions; a cold, clear dawn that painted the eastern sky in bands of primrose and pale rose, with a frost so thick upon the fields that every blade of dead grass and every twig on the bare trees was edged in silver. Fleda awoke not with the trepidation of the day before, but with a quiet sense of purpose that sat upon her like a new, well-fitting garment. She dressed quickly in a dark woollen dress, plainer than her usual habit, and went down to breakfast.

Hugh was already there, looking a little sleepy but full of the importance of the previous night's work. "We made a capital lot of syrup, Fleda," he announced. "Earl says it's some of the best he's seen boil up. I helped dip, you know. It's very particular work."

"I am sure you were a great help, Hugh," she said, smiling at him. "And today we have another great work. The General arrives."

An hour later, a heavy farm wagon, drawn by two stout horses, lumbered up the drive. On its bed was a large, solidly built crate. Earl Douglass and Philetus came out from the barn to meet it, and Fleda, with Hugh at her side, followed, pulling her shawl tight against the sharp air.

"Well now, Miss Fleda, here's your new soger," Earl said, casting a critical eye over the crate. "He's a big 'un, that's a fact. I don't know as I've seen a much bigger Merino, not since old Squire Dennison's time. Cost a pretty penny, I'll be bound."

"He did," Fleda said simply. "Is the pen ready?"

"'Tis," said Earl. "Me and Philetus fixed it up good and strong yesterday. If he gets out of that, he's more'n a sheep."

Within the crate, a pair of baleful yellow eyes glared out, and a low, rumbling growl, more like that of a dog than a sheep, promised ill for any who came too near. A heavy, woolly head, crowned with a magnificent set of curled horns, suddenly battered against the wooden slats with a force that made the whole wagon shudder.

"My word!" exclaimed Hugh, taking a step back. "He's a monster!"

"He's a fighter," said Earl, with a look of grudging admiration. "That's what you want for good stock. Now, Philetus, we'll back this thing up as close to the pen gate as we can. Miss Fleda, you and the boy had best stand well back. When he comes out, he's goin' to be madder'n a wet hornet."

"I will not stand back, Mr. Douglass," Fleda said, her voice quiet but firm. "I must see this through. I will stand by the gate and be ready to secure it."

Earl looked at her, his leathery face a mask of disbelief. "Now, Miss Fleda, that ain't no place for you. This ain't like standin' by a sugar fire. This here's a two hundred pound batterin' ram with a bad temper. If he takes it in his head to come your way--"

"Then I will stand my ground," she interrupted. "It is my farm, and my sheep. Philetus, you and Mr. Douglass do as you need to with the wagon. Hugh, you will go and stand by the barn door, and do not move from there. I will manage the gate."

There was a moment of silence, in which the only sounds were the stamping of the horses and another angry thud from within the crate. Philetus looked from Earl to Fleda, his mouth slightly agape. Earl Douglass spat a stream of tobacco juice onto the frozen ground, a gesture of profound consideration.

"Well," he said at last, rubbing his chin. "Have it your own way. But if you get a horn in your side, don't say I didn't tell you."

He and Philetus set to work with a grim determination, using levers and their own considerable strength to manoeuvre the heavy wagon until the back of the crate was flush with the opening of the small, stoutly-fenced pen. The pen was no more than twenty feet square, a holding place before the ram could be introduced to the flock. The gate was a heavy, barred construction that slid sideways into place. Fleda took up her station beside it, her hands, cold even through her gloves, gripping the top bar. Her heart leaped into her throat, a frantic, fluttering thing, but her feet were rooted to the frozen earth. This was it; the first test. The first skirmish of her campaign.

"All right," Earl called out, breathing heavily. "I'm a-goin' to raise the back panel. Philetus, you be ready to jump. Miss Fleda, you be ready with that gate. On the count o' three. One... two... THREE!"

With a grating sound, Earl lifted the heavy planking at the back of the crate. For a second, nothing happened. The dark opening was just a void. Then, with a furious snort, the animal exploded into the daylight. It was even larger than Fleda had imagined; a great, solid beast of muscle and fleece, its head low, its yellow eyes wild with rage and confusion. It did not bolt for the freedom of the pen. Instead, it spun around, its horns catching the corner of the crate and splintering the wood, and charged directly at Philetus, who was scrambling away from the wagon.

Philetus let out a yelp of pure terror and stumbled, falling flat on his back in the mud and frosted stubble. The ram was upon him in an instant. Fleda cried out, a sharp gasp of fear, but her hands did not leave the gate. Earl Douglass, with a speed that belied his age, grabbed a long-handled pitchfork that was leaning against the fence.

"Hey! HEY! You devil!" he roared, jabbing the fork not at the animal's body, but at the ground just in front of its nose.

The ram, distracted from the fallen Philetus, skidded to a halt, shaking its massive head. It eyed the pitchfork, then Earl, then it saw Fleda. For a heartbeat, its mad yellow eyes fixed on her. It seemed to gather itself, its powerful legs bunching, and Fleda knew, with a dreadful certainty, that it meant to charge the gate, and her with it. She braced herself, her knuckles white. Let it come, she thought, a strange calm descending over her fear.

But before the ram could move, Earl brought the pitchfork down again, a sharp metallic clang on the frozen ground right beside the creature's head. The sudden noise, so close, seemed to startle it. It snorted, wheeled about, and trotted, with a kind of sulky dignity, into the middle of the pen.

"Now!" yelled Earl. "Shut it! Shut it now!"

With all her strength, Fleda shoved the heavy gate. It slid across the opening with a loud groan and slammed into place. She fumbled for the iron pin, her fingers clumsy with cold and adrenaline, and dropped it into its housing. It was done. The General was quartered.

Philetus picked himself up, covered in mud but apparently unharmed, a sheepish grin on his face. Earl Douglass leaned on his pitchfork, breathing hard and wiping his brow with the back of his hand, though the air was icy. He looked at the ram, now pacing the perimeter of its new prison, then he looked at Fleda.

"Well," he said, and the word carried a weight of concession. "I won't say but you stood your ground, Miss Fleda. You stood your ground."

A thrill, sharp and clear as the winter air, shot through Fleda. It was a praise more precious than any drawing-room compliment. She had not flinched. She had held her post.

"Thank you, Mr. Douglass," she said, her voice trembling only slightly. "Is Philetus all right?"

"I'm right as rain, Miss!" Philetus called, brushing himself down with vigor. "He just took the wind out o' me for a second! My, but he's a strong one!"

"He'll do," Earl said, a note of satisfaction in his voice. "He'll do just fine. We'll leave him be for a day or two, let him get the measure of the place. Then we'll see about puttin' him in with the ewes."

It was at that moment, as the small group stood in a tableau of triumph and relief, that the sound of a horse's hoofs was heard on the drive, not the heavy tread of a farm horse, but the quicker, lighter step of a riding horse. A moment later, Mr. Olmney appeared around the corner of the house. He reined in his handsome bay, his eyes taking in the scene before him: the formidable ram in its pen, the muddy Philetus, the grim-faced Earl with his pitchfork, and Fleda, her cheeks flushed with cold and excitement, her hair slightly escaped from its pins, standing guard at the gate.

"Miss Ringgan!" he exclaimed, swinging down from his horse. "I came to inquire after you, and I find you in the thick of the fray. Is all well? You look--you have been in some danger."

His face was a picture of genuine alarm and concern, and Fleda, who five minutes before had felt every inch the sovereign commander, was suddenly conscious of her dishevelled appearance and the unladylike nature of the entire proceeding.

"Only a minor skirmish, Mr. Olmney," she said, managing a smile. "The General was merely testing the lines upon his arrival. We have him quartered now, as you see."

He came closer, his eyes moving from the ram to Fleda's face. "The General?" he said, a look of dawning comprehension on his face. "This is the formidable sheep you spoke of. It seems you did not exaggerate." He looked at her with an expression she could not quite decipher; it was a mixture of admiration and something like anxiety. "You should not be doing such work. It is not safe."

"Nonsense," she said, with a lightness she did not entirely feel. "I did nothing but slide a gate. Mr. Douglass and Philetus are the true heroes of the hour."

"'Twas the young lady that was the steadiest of the lot of us," Earl Douglass put in unexpectedly, having overheard. "Stood there like one o' them stone statues in the pictures. The sheep seen it, too. They know when a body's afeard." Having delivered this surprising encomium, he nodded curtly and turned to Philetus. "Come on, boy. We've got wood to fetch for the bilin' tonight."

The two men retreated toward the barn, leaving Fleda alone with Mr. Olmney. An awkward silence fell between them, broken only by an impatient snort from the penned ram.

"So," Mr. Olmney said at last, his gaze still resting on her with that same concerned expression. "Your campaign has truly begun."

"It has," Fleda agreed. "And we have won the first engagement."

"At some risk to the commander, it appears," he said quietly. "Fleda--Miss Ringgan--I know you have taken this great task upon yourself, but you must allow others to bear the physical dangers. Your part is to direct, not to stand in the path of battering rams."

His earnestness was both touching and a little irritating. It was the very argument Earl had used, the very assumption she was determined to disprove. "My part, Mr. Olmney, is to learn," she said, her tone more serious now. "And I cannot learn from a distance. I cannot command an army whose battles I do not understand. How can I direct Mr. Douglass if I do not know what it is he truly does, what risks he runs?"

He looked from her to the massive creature in the pen, and then back again. A slow smile touched his lips, chasing the anxiety from his eyes. "I see," he said. "The sovereign must, on occasion, visit the front lines to understand the nature of the war. I beg your pardon. I spoke out of turn."

"Not at all," she said, mollified by his understanding. "You spoke as an ally, and I am grateful for it. But my strategy requires... personal reconnaissance."

He laughed then, a low, pleasant sound. "You will be the death of me with your military terms, Miss Ringgan. Very well, General. I shall not question your strategy again. I came only to bring you a book which Mrs. Rossitur mentioned you had expressed a wish to see. I left it with her in the house. And to ask if you were any the worse for your long exposure to the cold last night."

"I am perfectly well, thank you," Fleda said, warmed by the thought of his consideration. "And the book--it was very kind of you to think of it."

"It was no trouble." He stood for a moment more, his hand on his horse's bridle, as if reluctant to leave. "The world of farming seems a perilous one," he said, half to himself. "Full of angry generals and boiling cauldrons and men who spill casks for chicken 'sass'."

Fleda laughed. "It is a very real world, Mr. Olmney."

"I see that it is," he answered, his eyes meeting hers. "And I see that you are very real in it."

He mounted his horse with a fluid grace, touched his hat to her in a gesture that was both respectful and familiar, and rode away, leaving Fleda standing by the pen. She watched him go, then turned her gaze to her new acquisition. The General was now placidly pulling at a tuft of hay that had been thrown into the pen, his earlier fury subsided. He was, she thought, a magnificent animal. Costly, yes. Dangerous, certainly. But he was hers. He was a tangible part of her promise, a living symbol of her resolve. The world of quiet courtesies and borrowed books had its place; but she was beginning to think that the world of frozen ground and stubborn rams, of loyal helpers and hard-won victories, held a satisfaction more keen, more vital, than any she had known. Her kingdom was a harsh one, but it was hers to rule, and she had stood her ground. The day was young, and there was work to be done.
