The two boys laid down their axes and surveyed their handiwork. It was a sorry-looking raft, a patchwork of dry-rotted planks from Miller’s old shed and two logs they’d rolled down from the ridge, all lashed together with purloined clothesline. These were boys who had never piloted anything more ambitious than a barrel lid in a puddle, and that with only middling success. Now they stood with arms akimbo, masters of the coming waterway.

“She’s a stout craft, I’d say,” said Billy, giving one of the logs a kick. “Solid as a church pew.”

“Stouter,” said Jack. “Church pews ain’t meant for the river. We built this one for the current. Why, I bet we could take her clear to the Mississippi if we had a mind to.”

“That’s just what I was thinking! You heard me say that yesterday, didn’t you, Sam? I said, ‘This ain’t just a creek raft, it’s a river raft.’ Didn’t I, Sam?”

“You said it,” Sam affirmed, whittling a stick and not looking up.

“I knowed it the minute I saw these logs,” Jack continued, puffing out his chest. “I said, ‘Billy, them’s the ones.’ It’s a science, you see. You got to know the wood. Most folks just see trees. I see vessels.”

“I reckon old man Hemlock with his fancy skiff wishes he knowed what we know,” said Billy. “He spends all that money, and here we’ve made a better boat for nothing.”

“Hemlock! Why, he’d get turned around in a washtub. I’d like to see him try to manage a real current. He’d fetch up in the widow’s garden, boat and all!”

“Wouldn’t he though! Say, Jack, when we get her in the water, let’s pole right past the town landing. Real slow.”

“That’s the notion! And you’ll say, ‘Jack, you want to take a turn steering?’ And I’ll say, lazy-like, ‘No, I’m right comfortable, you handle her.’ And we’ll just drift by, and all the fellers will be watching with their mouths hanging open!”

“By jings, that’ll be fine, Jack! I wish we was there now!”

So their talk went, full of glory and grand voyages. The launch was a sweaty, mud-caked affair, but soon the craft was afloat in the shallows of the creek. It dipped and swayed, but it held. Their confidence, already high, became a towering mountain. They pushed off with long poles, and for a hundred yards, all was as they had dreamed. But then the creek widened and deepened, and the slow water surrendered them to the main channel’s swift and secret purpose. The raft began to turn in a slow circle.

“She’s a bit lively, ain’t she?” said Billy, his pole sinking deep into the mud with little effect.

“It’s just the current finding her keel,” Jack said, though with less certainty than before. “You just got to show her who’s boss.”

The talk began to falter; their efforts with the poles became more frantic and less effective. The silences grew. The raft, caught now in the full grip of the river, paid their struggles no mind at all. It picked up speed, aiming itself for a notorious snag of fallen willows that choked the far bend. Billy’s face was pale. Jack’s was set in a kind of grim terror.

“This pole’s no good,” Billy gasped, heaving. “It’s too green. It bends.”

“The water’s too deep here,” Jack answered, his voice tight. “It’s an unnatural depth. The flood must have gouged it out.”

The willow branches loomed like skeletal fingers. There was a sudden, grinding scrape, and the forward clothesline snapped with a sound like a pistol shot. The raft buckled. One of the planks spun away into the current. Billy let out a yelp as his end of the craft dipped, and he went into the churning water with a great splash. Jack followed a moment later as the whole structure gave a final lurch and disintegrated. Both boys were thrashing in the cold water, their grand voyage turned into a desperate scramble for the muddy bank.

They dragged themselves out, shivering and gasping, on the opposite side from where they had started. Sam was already there, having swum across with a steady, unhurried stroke. He was wringing out his shirt. Jack had lost one shoe and all of his bravado. Billy was covered in mud and spitting out gritty water.

“I’ve lost my hat,” Jack said, his teeth chattering. “My good hat.”

Billy coughed. “I reckon I hit my head. I better get home and have Ma look at it.”

They said nothing more on the long walk back, taking the back paths to avoid the town landing. That evening, when Billy’s father asked how their rafting adventure went, Jack spoke up from his corner.

“It was a battle, is what it was. The current was a monster. We fought it for nigh on an hour, but it was too strong. It tore the raft to pieces right from under us.”

“That’s the truth,” Billy added, his voice full of solemn importance. “It was a whirlpool, a regular maelstrom. We were lucky to get out with our lives. Jack here saved me from being pulled under. He’s a regular hero.”

And so they sat there, warm and dry, and they took their miserable failure and polished it until it shone like a trophy, each boy adding some new and terrifying detail, until the disastrous end of their sorry raft became the stuff of legend.

The legend of the great maelstrom and the heroic escape of Jack and Billy took root in the fertile soil of the town’s boy-population. It grew with each telling; its humble origins in mud and splintered planks were forgotten entirely. Jack, who had initiated the lie out of a desperate need to save face, soon found himself the architect of a grand epic. Billy, his loyal lieutenant, corroborated every detail with a solemnity that convinced even the most hardened skeptics. Sam said nothing at all, which the other boys took for a kind of profound, stoic heroism, as if the memory of the ordeal was too terrible for words. His silence was more potent than all of Jack’s eloquent fabrications.

“The water, it spun us round and round,” Jack would explain to a rapt audience down at the river landing, using a stick to draw spirals in the dust. “The raft, she screamed like a living thing. Planks was flying off like splinters from an axe-blow.”

“And the noise,” Billy would add, his eyes wide. “Like a thousand thunderstorms all at once, warn’t it, Jack? It was a roar that wanted to swallow you whole.”

“That’s the truth. And poor Billy here, he was flung into the very throat of it. I saw his face go under, and I thought, ‘He’s a goner.’ But I dove. I had to. You don’t leave a man behind, not when you’re facing down the river-devil himself.”

He had not, of course, done any such thing, but the vision he painted was so vivid, and his own belief in it so apparently sincere, that he almost convinced himself. He was no longer Jack, who had lost his shoe and his dignity; he was Captain Jack, the river-tamer, the boy who looked into the abyss and did not flinch.

This state of affairs continued for a week, a glorious week in which Jack and Billy were afforded the kind of deference usually reserved for the pilot of the packet boat or men who had been to the war. Other boys would fetch them water, or offer them the first bite of a shared apple. Their glory, however, was not without its challengers.

Neddy Potter was a boy of a sour and dubious disposition, whose chief pleasure in life was the deflating of other people’s grand achievements. He had a squinty eye and a way of sniffing that suggested he was perpetually smelling something disagreeable, which, in a way, he was: the happiness of others. He listened to the saga of the maelstrom with a curled lip.

“A whirlpool, you say?” he said one afternoon, when Jack had just finished a particularly harrowing rendition for a new boy in town. “A whirlpool big enough to swallow a whole raft?”

“That’s what I said,” Jack answered, bristling at the tone. “Right there by the willows. A regular terror.”

“Funny,” Neddy said, poking at a beetle with a stick. “My pa went by there in his skiff this morning. Said the water was as placid as a milk pan. Said the only thing he saw was a broke-up clothesline snagged on a log.”

A nervous silence fell over the assembled boys. They looked from Neddy’s sneering face to Jack’s suddenly flushed one. Billy began to look anxiously toward the road, as if considering an urgent errand at home.

“Well, of course it’s placid now,” Jack said, recovering himself with an effort. “We broke its back, didn’t we? That’s what a fight like that does. It tames the river. It don’t stay a maelstrom forever, you ninny. It uses up all its fury.” He appealed to the silent authority. “Ain’t that right, Sam?”

Sam, who had been whittling a piece of driftwood, looked up. He shrugged. “River changes,” he said, and went back to his whittling.

It was not a ringing endorsement, but it was enough. The other boys murmured their agreement. Neddy, however, was not so easily put off.

“All right, then,” he said, changing his line of attack. “So you’re brave. You ain’t scared of nothing, I suppose. Not a whirlpool, not a river-devil, not nothing.”

“We ain’t given to being scared, no,” Jack said, with a lordly air.

“Then I bet you wouldn’t spend a night in the Widow MacLeod’s house.”

The name fell into the lazy afternoon air like a stone into a quiet pond. The Widow MacLeod’s house. It stood on a weedy hill at the edge of town, and had been empty for five years, ever since the widow herself had been found in her rocking chair, cold as a wedge. She had been a strange and solitary woman, and the town’s children had woven a thick tapestry of horror around her memory and her decaying home. They said she’d been a witch; that her cats still haunted the place, spectral things with eyes of fire. They said at night you could see a light moving from window to window, and hear her rocking chair creaking on the porch. No boy had ever dared to go past the front gate after dark.

Jack felt a cold knot form in his stomach. He had the same dread of the place as every other boy. But he saw the look in Neddy Potter’s eye, and he saw the faces of the other boys, watching him, waiting. His grand reputation, so carefully constructed, was hanging by a thread. To refuse was to admit it was all a sham.

“Her house?” Jack said, and he forced a laugh that sounded like a dry cough. “Why, what’s in her house but dust and spiders?”

“Ghosts,” a smaller boy whispered, and was immediately shushed.

“They say her spirit can’t rest,” Neddy pressed, a grin spreading across his face. “They say she snatches boys who trespass on her property. But I guess a hero who fights a maelstrom wouldn’t be worried about one little old lady’s ghost.”

Jack’s mind was a flurry of panic. He glanced at Billy, whose face was the color of biscuit dough. Billy was shaking his head in a tiny, frantic motion. But the trap was sprung.

“It’s a fool’s errand,” Jack said, trying to sound dismissive. “There ain’t no glory in it.”

“Scared, are you?” Neddy crowed.

“Scared?” Jack’s voice rose. “I told you, I ain’t scared of nothing! All right, then. We’ll do it. Me and Billy. We’ll spend the whole night there, from sundown to sunup. We’ll even ask the widow’s ghost to sit a spell with us.”

A collective gasp went through the boys. Billy looked as though he might faint dead away.

“And Sam, too,” Neddy added, pointing his stick at the whittler. “He was in the whirlpool. He’s got to go, too.”

All eyes turned to Sam. Here was Jack’s escape. Sam would surely refuse. He had no reputation to uphold; he had never claimed to be a hero. He would say no, and then Jack could say that the three of them were a team, and they couldn’t possibly go without their third member.

Sam finished a long, curling shaving, blew it from the piece of wood, and inspected his work. He looked up at the waiting faces. “All right,” he said. “I ain’t got nothing else to do.”

Jack’s heart sank to the bottoms of his worn-out shoes. He was committed.

That evening, as twilight began to bleed across the sky, three small figures made their way up the overgrown path to the MacLeod house. Jack walked in front, trying to project an air of casual confidence that he did not feel. Billy trailed behind him, so close he kept treading on Jack’s heels, as if he feared the very air between them might contain some grasping phantom. Sam walked to the side, munching on a turnip he had procured from somewhere, his expression one of mild curiosity.

They had brought with them the necessary implements for a night of spectral investigation: a single candle in a tin holder, a box of matches, and Jack’s father’s old horse pistol, which was not loaded and for which they had no powder or shot, but which felt substantial and comforting in Jack’s pocket.

The house loomed before them, black against the last of the light. It was a gaunt, two-story frame house, with peeling paint that hung off in leprous strips. One of the shutters on an upper window had come loose and banged intermittently in the slight breeze, a lonely and mournful sound. The porch sagged, and the weeds in the yard were waist-high, full of the rustlings of unseen things.

“Well, here we are,” Jack said, his voice a little too loud in the stillness. “Don’t look like much.”

“It looks awful, Jack,” Billy whimpered. “I think I’m feeling poorly. That dinner my ma made, it ain’t sitting right.”

“You’ll be fine,” Jack said sternly. “Heroes don’t get sick stomachs.” He marched up the three rickety steps to the porch, which groaned under his weight. The front door was ajar, hanging crooked on one hinge. A black space of impenetrable darkness lay beyond it. “See? Nothing to it. We’ll just go on in and make ourselves at home.”

He peered into the darkness, and the darkness seemed to peer back. Every story he had ever heard about the place swarmed in his mind. He thought of the widow’s fiery-eyed cats, of her cold, dead hands, of the rocking chair that moved on its own. He did not want to go in. He wanted to be at home, in his bed, with his dog sleeping on the floor beside him. But behind him, he could feel the imagined eyes of Neddy Potter and all the other boys. He took a deep breath.

“Come on,” he said, and stepped across the threshold.

The air inside was thick and stale, smelling of dust, decay, and the faint, sweetish odor of long-dead mice. It was utterly black. Billy stumbled in behind him, grabbing onto the back of Jack’s shirt. Sam followed, as calm as if he were entering the general store.

“Light the candle, Jack,” Billy whispered, his voice trembling.

Jack fumbled for the matchbox. His hands were shaking so badly it took him three tries to strike a light. The little flame flared up, casting huge, dancing shadows that leaped and writhed on the walls. They were in a front parlor. A heavy blanket of dust covered everything. Furniture stood shrouded in white cloths, like a gathering of lumpy ghosts. A grand piano with yellowed keys grinned from one corner. Cobwebs, thick as cotton batting, hung in festoons from the ceiling.

“See?” Jack said, his voice husky. “Just an old dusty house. Nothing to be scared of.”

Even as he said it, the loose shutter upstairs gave a loud bang, and Billy let out a squeak, grabbing Jack so hard he nearly dropped the candle.

“It’s just the wind, you idjit,” Jack snapped, more to convince himself than Billy.

They explored. The dining room, with a long table set for a dinner that had never been eaten. The kitchen, with a rusted stove and a pump that wheezed and produced a trickle of brown, foul-smelling water. With every step, the old floorboards cried out in protest. Every gust of wind rattling the windowpanes was the whisper of a ghost; every skittering sound in the walls was the clawing of a spectral cat.

Jack tried to keep up a running commentary of bravado. “I bet that old widow was a terrible cook,” he’d say in the kitchen. “No wonder she died.” In the parlor, he’d announce, “If I see a ghost, I’m going to ask him if he knows any good card tricks.”

Billy was not comforted. He saw a phantom in every shadow and a portent in every creak. He had worked himself into such a state of terror that he refused to let go of Jack’s shirt, which made navigating the cluttered rooms a clumsy, three-legged sort of business.

Sam, for his part, seemed not a bit perturbed. He poked at the shrouded furniture, examined the rusted pump with a critical eye, and when they went upstairs, he was the first to venture into the widow’s own bedroom.

It was the most unnerving room yet. A great four-poster bed stood in the center, its mattress sagging. A rocking chair sat by the window, facing out. This was the chair. Jack’s heart hammered against his ribs. He held the candle high. The room was empty.

“Well,” Jack said, trying to sound relieved. “No ghost in here.”

They sat down on the floor, their backs against a wall, the candle placed in the middle of their little circle. The tiny flame was their only anchor in a vast ocean of darkness and silence. For a time, nobody spoke. The only sounds were the house settling, the wind sighing, and Billy’s chattering teeth.

“I wish it was morning,” Billy said at last, in a small voice.

“It will be,” Sam said. He had finished his turnip and was now examining the horse pistol with a professional sort of interest.

“You know,” Jack said, feeling the need to reassert his authority, “this ain’t so bad. It’s kind of peaceful. I reckon a man could get used to it. Maybe we’ll just take over this house. Make it our fort.”

“The ghost might not like that,” Billy said.

“Pshaw, the ghost,” Jack scoffed. “If she shows up, I’ll tell her to pay rent or get out. This is our place now.”

And then they heard it.

It was not a creak of the floorboards, nor a bang of the shutter. It was a different sound. A soft, rhythmic scraping, coming from downstairs.

Scrape. Pause. Scrape. Pause.

The three of them froze. Billy’s eyes were saucers in the candlelight. Jack felt the blood drain from his face. Even Sam stopped fiddling with the pistol and cocked his head, listening.

“What was that?” Billy breathed.

“Rats,” Jack said, but his voice lacked all conviction. The sound was too loud, too regular for a rat.

Scrape. Scrape. A dragging sound now, followed by a soft thud. It was coming from the kitchen.

“It’s her,” Billy whimpered, burying his face in Jack’s back. “She’s come back. She’s sharpening her claws on the stove.”

Jack’s imagination, already feverish, took flight. He pictured the widow, a skeletal figure in a tattered dress, dragging herself across the kitchen floor, her bony fingers scraping on the flagstones. He swallowed hard. The horse pistol in his pocket felt as useful as a feather.

“Maybe... maybe it’s a raccoon got in,” he whispered.

“It’s no raccoon,” Sam said quietly. His voice was calm, but intent. “It’s in the pantry.”

The scraping stopped. A profound silence descended again, heavier and more menacing than before. They waited, hardly daring to breathe. Then came a new sound: the distinct, unmistakable clink of a glass bottle against something hard.

Jack’s courage, which had been a leaky and poorly made vessel to begin with, shattered completely. He was on his feet. “That’s it,” he said, his voice a high-pitched squeak. “I just remembered. My ma, she asked me to be home early. She’s sick. Terribly sick.”

“Mine too!” Billy cried, scrambling up beside him. “I think it’s the consumption! We got to go!”

They were ready to bolt, to flee into the night and never look back, to abandon all pretense of heroism for the simple, beautiful safety of their own beds.

But Sam did not move. He was still sitting on the floor, looking toward the hallway. “Hush,” he said.

Another sound drifted up from below. It was a man’s voice, low and weary, humming a tuneless, mournful song.

A humming ghost. This was a new and terrible wrinkle. Jack and Billy clung to each other in pure, unadulterated terror.

Sam got to his feet. He picked up the candle.

“What are you doing?” Jack hissed. “Are you crazy?”

“I’m going to go see,” Sam said, as if it were the most natural thing in the world.

“No! Don’t leave us!” Billy wailed.

“You can come,” Sam offered. And with that, he walked out of the bedroom and started down the stairs, the little flame of the candle preceding him into the oppressive dark, leaving the two heroes alone in the blackness.

For a moment, they were paralyzed. The thought of being left alone in the dark was somehow even more terrifying than facing the humming ghost. With a shared sob of despair, they stumbled after him, their descent a clumsy clatter of feet on the creaking stairs.

Sam was standing in the kitchen doorway, holding the candle high. The humming had stopped. In the flickering light, they saw a man. He was sitting on the floor of the pantry, his back against a shelf. He was a tramp, rail-thin and dressed in rags, with a great, untidy beard and weary eyes. The scraping sound had been him trying to pry open a sealed crate of what looked like preserved peaches. The clinking sound had been a bottle, now in his hand, which he had just uncorked.

The tramp looked up, first at the light, then at the three boys silhouetted behind it. He did not look surprised or angry. He just looked tired.

“Well now,” he said, his voice raspy. “Figured someone was up there. This place ain’t as quiet as it looks. You the welcoming party?”

Jack and Billy stood frozen in the doorway, their terror slowly being replaced by a profound confusion. This was not a specter. This was just a man. A dirty, hungry-looking man.

Sam stepped into the kitchen. “This is the Widow MacLeod’s house,” he said. It was not an accusation, just a statement of fact.

“Is that who she was?” the tramp said, taking a small sip from the bottle. He grimaced. “Vinegar. Should have known. My luck.” He tossed the bottle aside. “Don’t you worry, boys. I ain’t here to trouble her spirit. Just looking for a dry place out of the wind and maybe a forgotten tin of beans. This here crate of peaches looked promising, but it’s nailed up tighter than a banker’s fist.”

Jack found his voice. It was still a bit shaky. “We thought you was a ghost.”

The tramp gave a low chuckle. “Sometimes I feel like one. Been walking for three weeks, all the way from the Ohio. A ghost is just a fella nobody sees. Most folks are good at not seeing me.” He looked them over. “You boys ain’t from around here, then? Run away from home?”

“We live here,” Jack said, his courage returning in a flood now that the danger was revealed to be a flesh-and-blood tramp. He stepped forward, puffing out his chest a little. “We’re on an expedition.”

“An expedition, is it?” the tramp said, with a flicker of amusement in his tired eyes. “Exploring the dark continent of the pantry?”

Billy, feeling brave now too, piped up. “We came to see the widow’s ghost. We ain’t scared of her.”

“Well, you won’t find her here,” the tramp said. “Only me. And I’ll be gone by morning. I don’t suppose your expedition brought along a spare biscuit or two?”

The boys had not. They had only the useless pistol and their fear. Sam, however, reached into his pocket and pulled out another turnip. He tossed it to the man, who caught it with a grateful nod.

They stayed for a while, the four of them, in the dusty kitchen. The tramp told them about the railroad, about cities with buildings so tall they scraped the clouds, and about the hardness of the road. He was not scary at all. He was just a man with sore feet and a story. By the time the first grey hint of dawn appeared in the grimy kitchen window, the tramp was asleep in the pantry, and the boys were ready to leave.

They crept out of the house as the sun came up. The world looked fresh and clean and wonderfully solid. The terrors of the night seemed foolish and far away. They walked back toward town, not saying much.

Neddy Potter and his cronies were waiting for them at the town landing, just as they knew they would be.

“Looky here!” Neddy called out as they approached. “They’re back! Didn’t get snatched by the ghost, then?” He squinted at them. “You look a bit pale. I bet you hid in the bushes all night.”

Jack stopped. He looked at Billy, whose face was shining with a new idea. He looked at Sam, who was already heading for the riverbank to check a fishing line he had set. Then he looked at Neddy, and a slow, magnificent smile spread across his face.

“We saw her, Neddy,” he said, his voice low and full of awe.

“Saw who?”

“The widow,” Jack said. “The ghost. She came to us, just after midnight.”

Billy’s eyes were like moons. “She did. She floated right through the wall. She was all shimmery and blue-like. And she was crying.”

“Crying?” Neddy said, his skepticism warring with a delicious thrill of fear.

“Weeping something awful,” Jack continued, his invention hitting its stride. “Said she was lonely. Said all she wanted was some company. We talked to her for nigh on an hour. She told us about the old days. She was a nice old lady, really. Just sad.”

“You talked to her?” one of the smaller boys gasped.

“Of course we did,” Jack said. “She was troubled. Her spirit was in a knot. We told her it was all right. We told her she could rest now. We sort of… well, we sort of helped her cross over.”

“You did what?” Neddy said, his jaw slack.

“We sent her on her way,” Billy said solemnly. “To the other side. It was a powerful bit of business. There was a great flash of light, and a wind that blew through the whole house. And then… she was gone. The house is clean now. Her spirit is at peace.” He looked at Jack. “Thanks to us.”

“That’s the truth,” Jack said. He folded his arms. “So there won’t be no more hauntings up at the MacLeod place. We took care of it. It was our duty, being heroes and all.”

He glanced over at Sam, who was pulling a small catfish from the river. Sam hadn’t heard a word of the new legend. He was just a boy with a fish. But to the rapt audience, his quiet industry looked like the weary calm of a man who had trafficked with spirits and found the business tiresome.

Neddy Potter was speechless. He had no reply. How could you argue with someone who had not only faced a ghost, but had become its friend and spiritual guide?

And so the boys’ fame was cemented. They were not just the heroes of the maelstrom anymore. They were the ghost-tamers, the boy-exorcists, the peacekeepers of the veil between worlds. Their disastrous rafting trip and their terrified night in a dusty house with a hungry tramp were burnished and transformed, until the fictions shone with a light far brighter and more glorious than any drab truth. They had learned the great secret of boyhood adventure: that the best part of the story is the one you make up afterward.

They awoke to a world washed clean. The air was crisp and smelled of wet earth and pine, and the sky was a brilliant, cloudless blue. Their adventure had been a trial of fire and water, and now, in the calm daylight, it seemed a thing of myth and legend. They were heroes returned from the wars.

And so the story grew, as stories do when tended by imaginative boys with a rapt audience. By the time they had poled their sorry raft back to the town’s edge, the storm was no mere squall but a hurricane of biblical proportions. The night spent in the abandoned house was not a shivering ordeal with a tramp, but a solemn parley with the restless spirit of the Widow MacLeod. Jack, being the natural leader and chief architect of the fiction, laid the foundation; Billy, with his wide eyes and breathless delivery, added the gables and shutters; and Sam, by his quiet refusal to deny any of it, provided the stone-solid cornerstone of truthiness upon which the whole magnificent edifice was built.

Their fame was a tangible thing now. It clung to them like the smell of woodsmoke. Younger boys would stop their games of marbles to watch them pass, whispering in awe. “There they go,” one would say. “The ones that talked to the ghost.”

“Jack said he looked her right in the eye,” another would add. “And she wasn’t scary at all, just sad.”

Neddy Potter alone was a rock of skepticism in this rushing river of belief. He was a boy of facts and figures, who believed in what he could see and touch, and the story of the Widow MacLeod’s tearful farewell to the mortal coil stretched his credulity to the breaking point. He trailed the heroes, a one-boy chorus of doubt, his arms perpetually crossed and a scowl fixed upon his face.

“If you sent her on her way,” he’d say, cornering them by the general store, “why didn’t nobody else see the big flash of light you’re always talking about?”

“It was a spiritual light, Neddy,” Jack would reply with an air of weary patience, as if explaining colors to a blind man. “You wouldn’t have been able to see it. Your soul ain’t attuned for that kind of thing.”

“That’s right,” Billy would chime in. “You probably would have just thought it was lightning. But we knew. We felt the power.”

Neddy would kick at a loose stone. “You said she floated through a wall. What wall? That place is falling down. A good wind could float through a wall in that house.”

This sort of low-grade heckling went on for a week. The heroes bore it with noble fortitude, which only strengthened their standing among the believers. But Neddy was persistent, and a persistent doubter is a dangerous thing to a new-made reputation. He needed to be silenced, utterly and for good. The opportunity presented itself, as such things often do, in the form of another local legend.

It was a hot, lazy afternoon, and the boys were all gathered under the big oak tree by the swimming-hole, the unofficial parliament of their small world. The talk turned, as it often did, to matters of courage and daring. The subject was the old abandoned quarry, a place a few miles out of town that the grown-ups warned them away from.

“My pa says a man fell in there, years ago,” said one of the smaller boys, his eyes round. “Silas Croft, his name was. They never found the body. Just his pickaxe, stuck in the rock.”

“He haunts it,” another boy whispered, and a delicious shiver went through the group. “They say on a dark night you can hear him, chipping away at the stone, still trying to finish his work.”

“He ain’t chipping,” said a third boy, an expert in such lore. “He’s looking for his wages. He died on a Friday, and Mr. Abernathy, who owned the quarry, never paid his wife. So old Silas wanders the cliffs, moaning for his two dollars.”

Here was Neddy Potter’s chance. He sat up, a glint in his eye. He looked directly at Jack, who was leaning against the tree trunk, looking terribly bored and heroic.

“Well,” Neddy said, his voice loud and clear. “Sounds like a job for our ghost-tamers.”

A hush fell over the assembly. Every eye turned to Jack.

Jack did not flinch. He yawned, a slow, deliberate act of supreme indifference. “What about it?” he said.

“Well, it ain’t right, is it?” Neddy pressed on, sensing he had the crowd. “A poor spirit, wandering for want of two dollars. Seems like you fellas, being experts and all, ought to go out there and have a talk with him. Tell him it’s all right. Help him cross over, like you did for the widow.” He smiled a nasty, triumphant smile. “Unless, of course, you can only handle sad old ladies. Maybe a working man’s ghost is too much for you.”

The challenge hung in the air, sharp and heavy as a thrown hatchet. It was a masterstroke. To refuse would be to admit cowardice and, by extension, fraud. The entire legend of the Widow MacLeod would crumble. Jack was trapped.

He looked at Billy, whose face had gone a shade paler. He looked at Sam, who was methodically skipping a flat stone across the water, pretending not to hear. Sam knew a bad bet when he saw one.

Jack stood up and brushed the grass from his trousers. He fixed Neddy with a look of profound disappointment. “That’s the trouble with you, Neddy,” he said, his voice sorrowful. “You think it’s a game. You think trafficking with the spirit world is like playing marbles. It ain’t. It’s a heavy burden.”

“Oh, I reckon it is,” said Neddy. “A burden you three seem mighty proud to carry. So, will you do it? Will you go to the quarry tonight and put poor Silas Croft at peace?”

“Of course we will,” Jack said, as if the question were absurd. “It’s our duty. But we’ll do it for Silas, not for you.” He turned to his lieutenants. “Billy. Sam. We have work to do.”

Billy gulped, but nodded bravely. “Right, Jack.”

Sam’s tenth skip faltered and sank. He sighed, pocketed his remaining stones, and rose to his feet. His loyalty, it seemed, outweighed his common sense. The expedition was on.

The rest of the afternoon was spent in solemn preparation. Jack announced that this spirit, being troubled by money, would require a different sort of approach than the lonely widow. They would need special equipment. This “equipment” turned out to be a rusty lantern, a ball of string, a half-eaten apple for sustenance, and two silver dimes, which Jack proclaimed were necessary for “paying the spirit’s way.” Sam, ever the practical one, also brought along a fishing knife and a small, tightly-wrapped packet of salt, for he had once heard that salt was a powerful ward against evil.

As dusk began to settle, a procession of nearly all the boys in town escorted the three heroes to the edge of the woods. It felt like a delegation sending soldiers off to a perilous war. Neddy Potter was at the forefront, his face a mask of smug anticipation.

“Good luck,” he said, with no luck in his tone at all. “You be sure to tell us what old Silas has to say.”

“We will, Neddy,” Jack said gravely. “We’ll tell everyone.” He hefted the lantern. “Come on, boys. The veil between worlds is thinnest at midnight.” With that, he plunged into the darkening woods, Billy and Sam at his heels, leaving their audience to contemplate the mysteries they were about to confront.

The walk to the quarry was a somber affair. The bravado that had served them so well in the sunlit town square began to evaporate under the thick canopy of the woods. The chirping of crickets sounded unnaturally loud, and the hoot of a distant owl carried a note of dire warning.

“Why’d you have to say yes, Jack?” Billy whispered, his voice trembling a little.

“Couldn’t say no,” Jack whispered back. “You want to be Neddy Potter’s laughingstock for the rest of your life?”

“No, but, ”

“And besides,” Jack interrupted, his confidence rebuilding with the act of speaking. “It’s just an old quarry. There ain’t no such thing as ghosts. Not really.”

“What about the widow?” Billy asked, confused.

“That was different,” Jack said vaguely. “She was a special case.”

They walked on in silence, each boy wrestling with the chasm between the stories they told and the reality they now faced. Sam, trailing a few feet behind, gripped the salt packet in his pocket. He did not believe in ghosts, not in the daylight, but the night was a different country with different laws.

The quarry, when they reached it, was worse than they had imagined. It was a great, gaping wound in the earth, a horseshoe of sheer rock faces dropping down to a pool of water so black it seemed to drink the starlight. The air was cool and carried a damp, mineral smell. A faint breeze sighed around the stone lips of the chasm, and it was not hard to imagine it as a mournful whisper.

“Well,” Jack said, his voice trying for a casual tone and failing. “Here we are.”

“Where do you think he is?” Billy asked, his eyes darting nervously into the shadows.

“He ain’t anywhere, because he ain’t real,” Jack said, more to convince himself than his friends. “We just got to wait here a while, then go back and tell a good story. Find a good spot.”

They settled in a small alcove in the rock, a place that offered a clear view of the quarry floor and the forbidding cliffs opposite. They lit their lantern, but the small circle of yellow light only made the surrounding darkness seem deeper and more absolute. For a time, they sat in silence, listening.

Every sound was magnified. A pebble skittering down the rock face was the footstep of an unseen watcher. The lapping of the water was a thirsty tongue. The wind’s moan through a crack in the stone was, without a doubt, a soul in torment.

“Did you hear that?” Billy breathed, after a particularly sorrowful gust of wind.

“Just the wind,” Sam said, his voice low and steady. He was the anchor in their sea of fear.

“I don’t know,” Jack said, his own nerves beginning to fray. “It sounded like… like it was saying something. Sounded like… ‘Twoooo…’”

“It’s the wind, Jack,” Sam repeated firmly.

But the seed of fear was planted. They were no longer three boys in a quarry; they were three mortal souls in a haunted place, waiting for a ghost named Silas who was owed two dollars. They huddled closer together, the lantern flame casting their giant, flickering shadows on the rock wall behind them.

An hour passed, which felt like a lifetime. The moon, a pale sliver, climbed over the quarry’s edge, casting eerie highlights on the rock and turning the pool of water into a sheet of polished obsidian. The boys’ initial terror had subsided into a dull, exhausted dread.

And then they heard it.

It was not the wind. It was a distinct sound, a sharp *clink*, followed by a scraping noise from the far side of the quarry.

The three boys froze. Billy’s breath hitched in his throat. Jack’s hand, which had been resting on the lantern, clenched into a fist. Sam slowly, silently, drew his fishing knife.

*Clink. Scrape. Scrape.*

It was the unmistakable sound of metal on stone.

“It’s him,” Billy mouthed, his face a white mask in the lantern light. “It’s Silas. He’s chipping.”

“Shut up,” Jack hissed. “It’s a… it’s a raccoon. Or something.”

But the sound was too rhythmic, too deliberate for an animal. It came again, *clink, scrape*, followed by what sounded for all the world like a low, guttural groan.

Now, a boy’s courage is a curious thing. It can desert him entirely in the face of a schoolyard bully, only to swell to magnificent proportions when faced with a spectral miner. Jack, who five minutes before had been ready to bolt, was now overcome by the terrible responsibility of his own legend. He was Jack the Ghost-Tamer. He had a duty to perform.

“Stay here,” he whispered, his voice shaking but resolute. “I’m going to see.”

“Don’t, Jack!” Billy pleaded, grabbing his arm.

“I have to,” Jack said, pulling away. He picked up one of the silver dimes. “This is for him. To pay his way.” He did not seem to recall that the legend called for two dollars, not ten cents. In the heat of the moment, the details were fuzzy.

Holding the dime in his sweaty palm, Jack began to creep along the narrow ledge that circled the quarry, towards the source of the sound. Sam and Billy watched, paralyzed, as his small figure was swallowed by the shadows. They were alone with the lantern and the terrifying sounds from across the chasm.

The scraping stopped. The silence that followed was a hundred times worse.

“What’s happening?” Billy whimpered.

Sam didn’t answer. He was listening, his head cocked. And then he heard it, faint but clear: Jack’s voice, raised in a quavering cry.

“Who’s there? We’ve… we’ve come to help you, Silas Croft! We’ve brought your wages!”

There was a pause, and then a different voice answered. It was not a ghostly moan but a low, gravelly growl, thick with sleep and irritation.

“What in tarnation? Who’s out there? Get away from my camp, you little whelps!”

Suddenly, another light flared to life across the quarry. Not the ethereal blue of a spirit, but the bright, steady flame of a second lantern. It illuminated a small, sheltered ledge where a makeshift camp was set up: a bedroll, a small cook-pot, and a stack of mining tools. And standing there, looking massive and fearsome in the flickering light, was a man.

He was not a ghost. He was a very large, very real, and very angry prospector with a thick beard and wild hair. He was holding a geology hammer, the source of the *clinking* sound. He had evidently been using its pick end to clear a spot for his bed.

From their vantage point, Billy and Sam had a perfect view of the scene. They saw Jack freeze, the silver dime still held out in his trembling hand. They saw the big man take a step forward, his shadow leaping up the cliff face like a genuine monster.

“Wages?” the man growled. “Silas Croft? He’s been dead forty year! Now clear off before I give you a tanning you’ll remember!”

This was a terror far more immediate and understandable than any ghost. Jack did the only sensible thing a boy could do in such a situation. He yelped, dropped the dime, and ran. He did not run back the way he had come. In his panic, he scrambled upwards, clawing his way up a steep, rubble-strewn slope.

The prospector, seeing a small boy scrambling away in the dark, seemed to soften. “Careful, you little fool!” he hollered. “You’ll break your neck!”

But Jack was beyond hearing. He was pure, unthinking flight. And as he scrambled, he dislodged a cascade of loose stones and dirt. It started small, a trickle of pebbles, but it gathered force as it went, sweeping downwards, right towards the little alcove where Billy and Sam were huddled.

“Look out!” Sam yelled, grabbing Billy and pulling him back just as a shower of rock and grit rained down where they had been sitting. The lantern, knocked over by the slide, sputtered and went out, plunging them into absolute darkness.

For a moment, there was only chaos. Billy was screaming, the prospector was shouting something from across the quarry, and from high above them came Jack’s panicked scrabbling. Sam, his heart hammering in his chest, fumbled in his pocket and found the salt. It was a foolish, desperate gesture, but he opened the packet and flung a pinch of it into the dark.

And then, silence again. A thick, ringing silence.

“Jack?” Sam called out, his voice thin in the vast emptiness. “Billy, you all right?”

“I think so,” came Billy’s shaky reply from somewhere nearby.

A third voice joined them, from directly above. “Is it… is it gone?” It was Jack, clinging to a scrubby bush some twenty feet up the cliff face.

“It wasn’t a ghost, you idiot!” Sam shouted up at him. “It was a man!”

The truth dawned slowly, painfully. There was no ghost. There was only a grumpy prospector, a geology hammer, and a series of very unfortunate misunderstandings. The three heroes were scattered, terrified, and one of them was stuck up a cliff in the dark. Their grand expedition had ended in utter, ignominious failure.

It took them the better part of an hour to put themselves back together. The prospector, who turned out to be a man named Peters looking for silver seams, took pity on them. He guided Jack down from his precarious perch, relit their lantern with a match from his own supply, and gave them a stern lecture on the foolishness of boys playing in quarries at night. He did not seem the least bit flattered to have been mistaken for a ghost.

The walk back to town was the longest journey of their lives. They were bruised, filthy, and stripped of all dignity. The magnificent legend of the ghost-tamers had been shattered on the rocks of reality. All they could think of was the dawn, and facing Neddy Potter.

They stumbled out of the woods just as the first grey light was touching the sky. And there, waiting for them, sitting on the top rail of a fence, was Neddy. He was not alone. Behind him were half a dozen other boys, their faces filled with eager curiosity.

“Well?” Neddy called out, hopping down from the fence. “Did you do it? Did you send old Silas on his way?” He took a closer look at their disheveled state. Jack had a long scratch on his cheek, Billy’s shirt was torn, and Sam was covered in dust. “Good gracious. Looks like he put up a fight.”

Jack opened his mouth to reply, to confess, to say anything that would end the charade. But then he looked at Neddy’s smug face, and at the expectant faces of the other boys, and the old instinct took over. The truth was a lead weight that would sink them all. But a story… a story could fly.

He straightened his shoulders, wincing as the movement pulled at his scratches. He looked not at Neddy, but at the wider audience. His voice was low, hoarse, and filled with a terrible weariness.

“He’s at peace,” Jack said.

A collective gasp went through the crowd. Neddy’s jaw dropped.

“You… you saw him?” one of the boys asked.

“We did more than see him,” Billy piped up, catching on instantly. His eyes were wide with a new, even more powerful fiction. “We fought him!”

“He wasn’t a sad spirit like the widow,” Jack continued, his invention hitting its stride. “He wasn't looking for his wages. That was just a story. He was a… a Quarry Demon. A spirit of the stone, full of rage.”

“He threw rocks at us!” Billy exclaimed, pointing to his torn shirt as evidence. “A whole avalanche of them!”

“It was a terrible battle,” Jack said, touching the scratch on his face. “He was powerful, Neddy. Stronger than any ghost. He took the form of a giant, bearded monster. We had to use the old magic. The salt.” He glanced at Sam.

Sam, stunned into silence by the sheer audacity of it all, simply nodded. He felt the empty paper packet in his pocket.

“We drove him back,” Jack concluded, his voice ringing with hollow triumph. “Back into the heart of the stone. But he wounded us. It was a fight to the very edge of the soul.” He looked at his friends, at their bruises and torn clothes. “We barely survived.”

There was a moment of profound silence as the boys absorbed this new, thrilling narrative. The story of a tearful ghost was one thing; a desperate battle against a Quarry Demon was another thing entirely. It was epic. It was glorious.

Neddy Potter was speechless. How could he argue? They had the wounds to prove it. The torn clothes, the scratches, the exhaustion. He had sent them on a fool’s errand to expose them as frauds, and they had returned as warriors, their legend not merely intact, but magnified a hundredfold. They were no longer ghost-tamers. They were demon-fighters.

Jack looked over Neddy’s shoulder and saw the prospector, Mr. Peters, trudging down the road toward town, his pickaxe over his shoulder, probably heading for the general store and a proper breakfast. Jack caught Sam’s eye, and a flicker of understanding passed between them, the shared secret of two generals who knew the true, messy cost of a glorious victory. They had learned an even greater secret of boyhood adventure: that when the truth is a disaster, you must build a lie so magnificent that everyone prefers to live in it.

Fame, of a certain boyish and provincial stripe, is a heady brew, and Jack and Billy and Sam drank their fill of it in the days that followed. They did not have to glorify their adventure; the other boys did it for them. The story of the Quarry Demon spread like burrs on a dog’s back, clinging to every ear in town and growing more fearsome with each telling. Jack’s scratch became a ghastly wound from a spectral claw. Billy’s torn shirt was the result of being seized by a stony hand. Sam’s silence was interpreted as the grim, shell shocked quiet of a veteran too haunted to speak of the horrors he had witnessed. They were no longer just boys; they were a bulwark against the unseen world.

Neddy Potter suffered a corresponding and catastrophic decline in his social standing. His skepticism, once a tool of intellectual superiority, was now seen as the sour grumbling of a coward. To doubt the Quarry Demon was to doubt the very courage of the town’s new protectors. He tried, once or twice, to point out inconsistencies, to question the nature of a demon that could be fought with table salt, but he was shouted down by a populace that had no appetite for truth when a magnificent lie was on the menu. He took to sulking by the river, a deposed king with a kingdom of one.

The boys, for their part, leaned into their new roles with the natural grace of born actors. They developed a swagger. They spoke in low, serious tones about “ancient evils” and “restless spirits.” They would gather on the steps of the general store, not joining in the frivolous games of marbles or tag, but observing them with the weary patience of old soldiers.

“Remember how he roared, Jack?” Billy would say, loud enough for the younger boys to hear. “Like a furnace door opening in a gale.”

“That wasn’t the worst of it,” Jack would reply, gazing thoughtfully at the horizon. “It was the silence after. The cold. That’s how you know you’re dealing with a true elemental. The cold gets in your bones.”

Sam would just shiver a little, a gesture more potent than any words, and the assembled audience would shiver in sympathy. The lie had become a living creature, a thing separate from them, and its care and feeding was now their principal occupation. They had to remember its size (as big as a hay-rick), its voice (like rocks grinding), and the specific shade of its glowing red eyes (like banked coals in a blacksmith’s forge). It was exhausting work.

“I’m worried, Jack,” Sam confessed one afternoon, when they were alone in their secret fort in the woods. “Suppose Mr. Peters tells someone he was just looking for his gold pan?”

“He won’t,” Jack said with a confidence he did not entirely feel. “Why would he? He’d look like a fool, scaring a bunch of boys half to death. A man’s got his pride. Besides, he’s a prospector. They’re a closemouthed lot. His secret is as safe as ours.”

“But it ain’t our secret,” Sam mumbled. “It’s our lie. And it keeps getting bigger. I feel like we’re trying to patch a leaky boat with soap. Sooner or later, the whole thing’s going to go under.”

“Not if we keep patching,” Jack said firmly. “We just need more soap.”

The opportunity to acquire more soap presented itself sooner than any of them expected. The source of it was Mr. Dobbins, the proprietor of the general store, a man whose jollity was as wide as his waistcoat. He was leaning against a barrel of crackers, watching the boys hold court.

“Well, you boys are the talk of the town,” he said, his voice rumbling. “Demon-fighters, they’re calling you. My own little Timmy is so scared of the Quarry Demon he won’t go out after dusk without a salt shaker.”

Jack and Billy puffed out their chests. Sam looked at his toes.

“Makes a man think,” Mr. Dobbins went on, stroking his chin. “If you can handle a Quarry Demon, I wonder if you’d be interested in a smaller bit of business. A… nuisance, you might say.”

Jack’s ears perked up. Business. This was a new level of legitimacy. “We don’t go looking for trouble, Mr. Dobbins. But we ain’t the kind to run from it, neither.”

“That’s the spirit!” Mr. Dobbins boomed. “It’s the old ice-house. Down by the creek. Been abandoned for years. Lately, there’s been noises. A sort of groaning and banging. Some folks say it’s old man Hemlock’s ghost, him having fallen and broke his neck there years ago. My delivery boy, young Silas, swears he heard a terrible wail coming from it last Tuesday and dropped a whole crate of eggs. A dead loss.”

Billy’s eyes were wide. “A wailing ghost?”

“Could be,” said Mr. Dobbins. “Or it could be a tramp. Or a loose shutter. Frankly, I don’t care what it is, but it’s spooking people, and it’s bad for business. I’d be mighty grateful if you boys, being experts in this sort of thing, would just… take a look. See what’s what. Maybe sprinkle some of your salt around.” He winked. “I’d even make it worth your while. A bag of licorice all round.”

This was a moment of supreme crisis. The Quarry Demon had been a reactive fabrication, a story told after the fact. This was a proactive commission. They were being hired. To refuse would be to admit their entire reputation was a sham. To accept was to walk willingly into another dark place with no idea what they might find.

Jack looked at Billy, who was already tasting the licorice and the glory. He looked at Sam, whose face was a mask of pale dread. He felt the familiar weight of leadership. The boat was leaking again, and Mr. Dobbins had just handed him a bar of soap.

“We’ll look into it, Mr. Dobbins,” Jack said, his voice a study in grave reluctance. “It sounds like a class three ethereal manifestation. Possibly fourth. We’ll need to prepare.”

“You do that, boys,” Mr. Dobbins said, mightily impressed. “Just tell me what you find.”

The walk to the ice-house that evening was a somber affair. The heroic swagger of the past week had evaporated, replaced by a leaden-footed trudge. Billy tried to inject some bravado into the proceedings.

“I bet it’s a wailer ghost. They’re the easiest. They just moan and carry on. Not like a Quarry Demon. They don’t throw things.”

“How do you know?” Sam asked, his voice tight.

“I just know,” Billy said, less certainly. “Jack, they don’t throw things, do they?”

“Depends,” Jack said, his mind racing. He was trying to construct a narrative for their failure in advance. “Wailers are usually harmless. But sometimes… sometimes they’re bait.”

“Bait?” whispered Billy.

“For something worse,” Jack said darkly. “A wailer draws you in with all its moaning, and then the real spirit, the one that’s been waiting, gets you.”

This shut Billy up completely, which was Jack’s intention. They arrived at the ice-house as the sun was setting, painting the sky in bloody streaks of orange and purple. The building was a sagging, dilapidated affair, half-sunk into the bank of the creek. Its thick wooden walls were green with moss, and the great, iron-hinged doors looked like the gates to a tomb. A deep layer of sawdust, once used for insulation, lay in a damp, rotting carpet within. The place smelled of decay and cold, wet earth.

And then they heard it. A low, mournful groan from within, followed by a series of frantic thumps. It was not a human sound, nor was it entirely animal. It was a noise of pure misery.

“See?” Billy whispered, his face as white as a sheet. “A wailer!”

“Hush,” Jack commanded. He felt a familiar terror bubbling up in his chest, the same terror he’d felt in the quarry. But this time, he had a reputation to uphold. He was a demon-fighter. He took a deep breath. “All right. Here’s the plan. We go in together. Sam, you got the salt?”

Sam miserably produced the paper packet of salt they had refilled at his mother’s pantry. It felt pitifully small.

“Billy, you get the lantern lit. I’ll… I’ll push the door.”

The great wooden door had no latch, but it was swollen with years of damp and settled into the earth. Jack put his shoulder to it and pushed. It didn't budge. He pushed harder. With a groan of protesting wood, it scraped open a foot, revealing a slice of utter blackness. The smell of rot and cold intensified.

From within the blackness, the sound came again, louder this time. A long, drawn-out moan, ending in a choking gasp, and then a violent, crashing bang.

“It knows we’re here!” Billy hissed.

“Of course it knows,” said Jack, trying to sound knowledgeable. “Spirits can feel us. Our warmth. It ain’t happy we’re disturbing its rest.” He was talking more to convince himself than the others. The truth was, he wanted to run. Run all the way home and hide under his bed and never tell a story again. But the faces of the boys at the general store, the admiring look from Mr. Dobbins, Neddy Potter’s sullen defeat; they all held him here.

He took the lantern from Billy’s trembling hand. “Stay close behind me,” he said, and slipped through the gap into the ice-house.

The darkness was absolute, a thick, palpable thing that swallowed the light from the door. The air was frigid, a damp cold that had nothing to do with the evening air outside. Jack fumbled with the lantern’s catch, his fingers numb. He struck a match. The tiny flame flared, a brave little star in an infinite night. He touched it to the wick, and a small, golden circle of light pushed back the gloom.

The light revealed a cavernous space. The walls were thick timber, the floor a deep pit of black, rotting sawdust. In the center of the room was the source of the noise.

It was not a ghost. It was not a tramp. It was not, in fact, anything that could be easily categorized in the annals of supernatural investigation. It was Deacon Miller’s prize-winning goat, Beatrice. And she was in a world of trouble.

The goat had, by some feat of caprine ingenuity, managed to get its head stuck firmly inside an old metal bucket. Blinded and terrified, she had been stumbling around the ice-house, moaning her goatly sorrows to the world and periodically running headlong into the walls, which accounted for the banging. As the lantern light fell upon her, she let out another heart-rending groan from within the bucket and charged blindly in their direction.

Now, a boy may be prepared to face a demon, a ghost, or even a band of murderous pirates. These are foes of romance and legend. He is generally not prepared to face a large, distressed goat with a bucket on its head charging him in a dark, enclosed space.

The effect was instantaneous and disastrous.

Billy shrieked a high, unmanly shriek and tried to retreat through the door, only to collide with Sam, who was right behind him. They went down in a heap of tangled limbs. Jack, the seasoned demon-fighter, yelped and leaped sideways, his only thought to avoid being gored by the mysterious bucket-monster. He landed on a patch of particularly slick, wet sawdust, his feet went out from under him, and he slid down the sloping side of the pit, landing with a soft, damp thump at the bottom. The lantern, flung from his hand, followed a graceful arc and landed beside him, where it hissed once and went out.

Plunged back into absolute darkness, the only sound was the frantic panting of the boys and the muffled, agonized bleating of the goat, which was now crashing into another wall.

“Jack! Are you dead?” Billy cried from the doorway.

“No!” Jack coughed, spitting out a mouthful of ancient sawdust. He was soaked, he was freezing, and his dignity was in tatters. “It’s got me! It’s a… a Bucket-Headed Horror!”

This was pure instinct, the storyteller’s brain working even in the depths of humiliating defeat. He couldn't admit he was felled by a farm animal.

“What do we do?” wailed Sam.

From the darkness came Jack’s voice, strained but commanding. “The salt! Throw the salt!”

Beatrice the goat, meanwhile, having exhausted herself, had stopped her charge. She stood panting, her bucket-helmet amplifying the sound of her distress. Sam, scrambling to his feet, did as he was told. He opened the paper packet and flung its entire contents into the blackness in the general direction of the noise.

A fine mist of sodium chloride descended upon the ice-house. A few grains landed on Jack. A few landed on the walls. And a goodly amount landed on the sensitive nose and face of Beatrice the goat. The effect was immediate. The goat let out a terrific sneeze, a sound so explosive and wet it echoed like a cannon shot in the small space. The force of the sneeze, combined with a violent shake of her head, did what hours of panicked struggling could not. The bucket flew off her head, hit the wooden wall with a deafening clang, and rolled noisily into a corner.

Freed from her prison, and with her vision restored, Beatrice saw the golden rectangle of the open door. With a final, triumphant bleat, she bolted for it, a furry brown-and-white streak of deliverance. She shot past the terrified figures of Billy and Sam, leaped over a log, and disappeared into the twilight, her short tail wagging her thanks.

A profound silence descended upon the ice-house, broken only by the drip of water and the sound of Jack climbing slowly out of the sawdust pit. He emerged into the faint light from the doorway, covered from head to toe in black, damp rot. He looked less like a demon-fighter and more like a creature that had just crawled out of a swamp.

Billy and Sam stared at him, then at the empty space where the goat had been, then at the bucket lying innocently in the corner.

Billy was the first to speak. “A… goat?”

Sam started to laugh. It wasn’t a normal laugh. It was a high, hysterical, helpless sound. He laughed until tears streamed down his face, until he had to hold his sides. He slid down the wall and sat on the ground, gasping for air.

Jack looked at the bucket. He looked at his filthy clothes. He looked at Sam’s hysterical laughter and Billy’s dumbfounded face. A slow grin spread across his own dirty face. The terror was gone, replaced by the pure, unadulterated comedy of the situation. Soon, all three of them were roaring with laughter, their voices echoing in the old building, chasing away the last of the ghosts and horrors, real or imagined.

They stumbled out into the fresh evening air, three dishevelled, sawdust-covered wrecks. The adventure was over. Now the work began.

“Well,” said Billy, wiping his eyes. “What are we going to tell Mr. Dobbins?”

Jack picked a piece of rotted wood out of his hair. He looked toward the town, where lights were beginning to glow in the windows. He thought of the bag of licorice. He thought of his reputation. Sam’s words came back to him: *We just need more soap*.

“It was worse than we thought,” Jack said, his voice regaining its familiar, solemn tone. “It wasn’t old man Hemlock. It was a dual manifestation. A rare and dangerous thing.”

Billy’s eyes lit up. Sam groaned, but he was listening.

“There was a Wailer, just like we figured,” Jack explained, warming to his theme. “But that was just the servant. Its master was a… a Pail-Wraith. A terrible spirit that takes the form of a man’s deepest, most metallic fears. It tried to trap us, to drain our spirits. It let out a shriek that could curdle milk.”

“The sneeze!” Billy exclaimed, catching on.

“Exactly,” Jack nodded gravely. “A spectral sneeze. We fought it back with the ancient rites. The salt weakened it, confused its senses. We drove it out, but it was a near thing. A very near thing. It took all our strength.” He sagged his shoulders theatrically, a warrior spent from a great battle.

They marched back into town not as three boys who had been terrified by a goat, but as three heroes who had faced down a Pail-Wraith and lived to tell the tale. They collected their bag of licorice from a grateful Mr. Dobbins, recounting the battle with grim satisfaction. The story was a wild success, even more so than the Quarry Demon, for it had the added detail of the spectral sneeze, a touch of bizarre originality that made it feel unquestionably true.

Later that night, as they sat by the riverbank dividing the licorice, Sam finally spoke. “A Pail-Wraith, Jack? Really?”

“It was better than a Bucket-Headed Horror,” Jack said, chewing thoughtfully. “Sounds more dignified.”

“We can’t keep doing this, Jack,” Sam said, though there was no real force in his words. “One of these days, we’re going to run into something that ain’t a prospector and ain’t a goat.”

Jack looked out over the dark, placid water of the river. The moon was rising, full and white, making a silver path on the surface. For a moment, the world seemed full of shadows and secrets, of real monsters and genuine dangers that lay just beyond the light of their small town. The thought was a little bit thrilling, and a whole lot terrifying.

“Well,” Jack said, with a sigh that was part weariness and part contentment. “I reckon when that day comes, we’ll just have to think of a real good story.”

When the sun finally climbed high enough to pierce the leafy canopy, it found three dismal pirates huddled together. The fire, their hard-won furnace of the night before, was a black, sodden patch of mourning. Their glorious adventure, which had seemed so magnificent and worthy of song in the wee, triumphant hours, now looked rather small and foolish in the clear, honest light of day. They were hungry, for the rest of their ham had been left out and was now a pale, swimming thing in a puddle of rainwater, fit only for the crawfish. Their corn-pone was a mushy tragedy. A profound melancholy settled over the camp.

“I’m hungry,” said Joe, which was a plain and simple truth that needed no embellishment.

“So’m I,” said Tom. “Huck, we got to get some fish. A pirate can’t starve, it ain’t in the books.”

Huck, who had been hungry more days in his life than he had been full, and considered it a normal state of being, merely nodded and fetched the lines. For an hour they sat upon a log by the river’s edge, dangling their bait with the patience of saints. But the river, having been so violently stirred and muddied by the storm, was in no mood to offer up its bounty. The fish were either hiding in the deepest holes, or had been swept clean down to St. Louis. Nothing nibbled. Nothing bit. The sun grew warmer, and their clothes began to steam, and their stomachs rumbled with a hollow, mournful sound.

Joe sighed, a great and shuddering sigh that seemed to begin in his toes and carry with it all the woe of a lost soul. “I wish I was home,” he said, not to anyone in particular, but to the uncooperative river and the indifferent sky.

Tom bristled. “Home! Joe Harper, I never thought I’d hear such talk from a pirate. Black-Hearted Joe, the Terror of the Spanish Main, wishes he was home? Why, what would the boys say to that?”

“I don’t care what the boys would say,” Joe retorted, his voice thick with misery. “They ain’t here. They ain’t wet and they ain’t hungry and they ain’t got mud in their britches. They’re home, where there’s beds. And mothers.”

The mention of mothers was a low blow, and a palpable one. A distinct image of his Aunt Polly, perhaps worried, perhaps even now weeping into her apron, flashed through Tom’s mind. He pushed it away with a mighty effort of will.

“We can’t go back,” Tom said, with a show of authority. “Not yet. They think we’re drowned. Everybody thinks we’re drowned. Why, we’re heroes! You want to spoil it all just for a plate of hot biscuits?”

“Yes!” said Joe, with a sudden, startling ferocity. “I do! I’d trade a whole barrel of heroing for one hot biscuit with butter. I’m sick of this. The smoking made me sick, and the storm scared me sick, and now I’m hungry-sick. It ain’t fun, Tom. It stopped being fun when the rain came.” He threw his fishing pole to the ground. “I’m going home.”

This was mutiny. Plain and simple. Tom Sawyer, the fearsome Black Avenger of the Spanish Main, looked at his crew, and saw his first mate on the verge of desertion. He looked to his other officer, Huck Finn, for support. But Huck was just gazing at the water, his face unreadable. For Huck, a camp in the woods was as much a home as any other place, but he could see the fabric of their little kingdom tearing.

“You can’t go, Joe,” Tom said, his voice softer now, pleading. “The raft’s gone. The storm took it.”

This was a dreadful, unforeseen calculation. Joe’s face fell. He had pictured a bold, solitary march to the ferry landing, a sad but noble return. The reality of being marooned, truly trapped, sank in. He sat down hard on the log and buried his face in his hands, and his shoulders began to shake.

Tom felt a pang of something that was part pity and part panic. His great adventure was dissolving into tears and starvation. His grand epic was turning into a miserable little story. He sat beside Joe, and for a long time the only sound was the lapping of the river and Joe’s quiet, despairing sniffles. Even Huck had reeled in his line. The fishing was a failure; the whole expedition was a failure.

And then, in the depths of this despair, a light went on in the inventive mind of Tom Sawyer. It was not a small light, but a great, dazzling, theatrical illumination. It was an idea so profound, so brilliant, so exquisitely perfect in its timing and execution that he almost gasped aloud. He saw it all, from beginning to end, a masterpiece of strategy that would turn this disastrous retreat into the most glorious victory of his entire life.

He let Joe sob for another minute, for dramatic effect. Then he put a hand on his shoulder.

“Joe,” he said, his voice low and full of a strange, new excitement. “Huck. Are you with me?”

Joe looked up, his face streaked with dirt and tears. “I want to go home, Tom,” he whimpered.

“I know,” Tom said soothingly. “And we will go home. We’ll all go home. But not like this. Not beaten and begging. Not like whipped dogs. We’re going to go home like kings. Like heroes returning from the dead.”

Huck looked at him sideways. “What’re you talkin’ about, Tom?”

Tom stood up. He was himself again; the general, the dreamer, the commander. “I have a plan,” he announced, his eyes shining. “It’s the grandest plan I ever had. It’s better than finding treasure, it’s better than licking the schoolmaster. But it’s a secret. A deep, dark, pirate secret. If I tell you, you have to swear. Swear on your blood you won’t breathe a word of it, not to a soul, until the time is right.”

Joe’s sobbing had stopped. He was intrigued, in spite of himself. A secret plan was always better than no plan at all. “Swear what?”

“Swear to stick with me,” Tom said, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. “Just till tomorrow night. Swear you’ll do what I say, no matter how strange it seems. And I promise you, Joe Harper, by this time the day after tomorrow, your mother will be hugging you so hard your ribs’ll crack, and the whole town will be cheering your name. They’ll be talking about this day for fifty years. What do you say?”

There was a long silence. Joe looked at Huck. Huck looked at Tom. Tom’s confidence was a powerful force; it was like a warm fire on a cold day. It was hard to resist. Finally, Huck gave a little shrug.

“I reckon I’m in,” he said. “Ain’t got nothin’ better to do.”

All eyes turned to Joe. He wiped his nose on his sleeve, took a shaky breath, and looked at the sheer, unadulterated certainty on Tom’s face. It was impossible. It was madness. But it was also hope.

“All right, Tom,” he said, his voice barely a whisper. “I swear.”

“Good!” Tom clapped his hands together. “The Black Avenger and his crew sail once more! Now, first things first. We need food. Not for our stomachs, but for our brains. A pirate can’t scheme on an empty belly.”

He then led them on a new kind of hunt, not for fish but for turtle eggs in a sandbar down the shore. They found a nest, and soon they were roasting the eggs in the embers of a painfully rebuilt fire. The eggs were not as good as bacon, not by a long shot, but they were food, and with food came strength, and with strength came a renewal of spirit. Tom, however, ate little. He was too consumed by the majesty of his own plan. He spent the afternoon walking by himself, making mysterious marks in the sand with a stick, and occasionally chuckling to himself, which was a little unsettling to the others.

“What d’you reckon he’s doin’?” Joe whispered to Huck.

“Schemin’,” said Huck. “That’s what. He’s like a spider when he gets that look. He’s spinnin’ a web.”

That evening, as the sun began to set, Tom gathered his crew. “It’s time,” he said solemnly. “We have to get off the island. Tonight.”

“But the raft’s gone,” Joe protested.

“The big raft is,” Tom corrected. “But we left the little one, the one we first came on, pulled up in the willows on the north side. We can use that. Now listen close. We’re going to paddle back. But we ain’t going to land. We’re going to drift past the town in the dark, and I’m going to swim ashore.”

“By yourself?” asked Joe, alarmed.

“It’s a secret mission,” Tom said gravely. “A scouting expedition. I have to find out the lay of the land. I have to know what the enemy is thinking.”

“The enemy?” said Huck. “You mean Aunt Polly and the Widow?”

“In a manner of speaking,” Tom said mysteriously. “I’ll be back before dawn. You two are to hide the raft and wait for me in the old abandoned tannery by the river. Don’t let a soul see you. Understand?”

They both nodded, their heads full of a mixture of fear and excitement. The plan was mad, but it was happening. They packed nothing, for there was nothing to pack. As darkness fell, they found the small, rickety raft and pushed out into the black, silent current of the great Mississippi. The town of St. Petersburg was a distant sprinkle of faint yellow lights, a different world. It was strange to be so close, yet so far away, to be thought of as dead while being so furiously and hungrily alive.

As they neared the town wharf, Tom stripped to his trousers. “Wait for me at the tannery,” he whispered one last time. “And whatever you hear, don’t come out till I get there.”

With a small splash, he slipped over the side and into the cool water, his white skin a ghostly flash in the darkness before he disappeared, stroking silently toward the shore and the next act of his grand, unfolding drama. Joe and Huck, left alone on the little raft, felt a fresh wave of unease. They had put their faith in Tom Sawyer, and now they had to wait, in the dark, to find out if he was a genius or the biggest fool in Missouri.

Tom swam with a purpose that was fueled by hunger and a burning desire for dramatic effect. He landed well below the ferry, dripping and shivering, and crept through the back alleys of his own town like a spy in a foreign land. The streets were quiet, but not empty. He saw a group of men talking gravely under a gas lamp and he hid behind a rain barrel to listen.

“A terrible business,” said the blacksmith. “Three of them, just gone. Sawyer’s boy, the Harper lad, and that little vagrant Finn. The river just swallowed them up.”

“The widow Douglas is taking it hard, they say,” said another man. “And Polly Sawyer, well, she’s nigh on broken. Never gave that boy anything but grief, she says, and now he’s gone.”

Tom’s heart gave a painful thump. He had not quite calculated this part, the real grief. He had imagined a town full of thrilling drama, not this quiet, heavy sadness. He crept on, his mission now tinged with a new and uncomfortable emotion. He made his way to his own house. The parlor was lit, and the window was open a crack to the warm night air. He crawled up on the woodshed roof, and from there he could lie on his stomach and look directly into the room.

There sat Aunt Polly, and Joe Harper’s mother, and Sid, and Mary. They were all dressed in black. Mrs. Harper was weeping into a handkerchief. Aunt Polly was trying to comfort her, but her own face was a mask of weary sorrow.

“He was such a good boy, at heart,” Aunt Polly was saying, her voice trembling. “Mischievous, I know. Full of the devil, sometimes. But he wasn’t a bad boy, not a bad boy. If only I’d told him. If only I hadn’t been so hard on him for that sugar bowl.”

Tom’s throat tightened. He was the devil, all right, for putting them through this. He had seen enough. His plan, his glorious, magnificent plan, suddenly felt small and cruel. He was about to slide back down and swim back to the island and call the whole thing off, to just appear on the doorstep and take his whipping. But then he heard Sid speak.

“I hope Tom’s in a better place,” Sid said, in a pious tone that Tom knew was completely false, “but if he’d been a better boy, he might not have gone at all.”

That was all it took. The spark of resentment and indignation flared up in Tom’s chest, burning away the guilt. Sid! Of course! The plan was back on. Not just for the glory, but for the justice of it. To show them all! To show Sid! He waited until Aunt Polly had knelt to pray, her words a quiet, heartbroken mumble that drifted out the window and stung him a little, then he slid from the roof and crept away into the darkness. He felt a little like a villain, but mostly like a general who had confirmed his enemy’s dispositions and was now ready for the final, decisive battle.

He found Joe and Huck huddled in the dusty, spider-webbed gloom of the old tannery, smelling of old hides and decay. They jumped when he appeared, a dripping shadow in the doorway.

“Did you see them? Are they talking about us?” Joe asked eagerly.

Tom nodded gravely. “They’re talking, all right,” he said. He sat down and pulled a small, folded paper from his trousers, which he had wrapped in a bit of bark. “They’re planning something. For Sunday.”

“Sunday? What’s on Sunday?”

Tom looked from Joe’s anxious face to Huck’s curious one. He let the silence hang in the air, building the moment.

“Our funerals,” he said.

Joe and Huck stared. For a moment the dusty air of the tannery, thick with the ghosts of a thousand cattle, was utterly still save for the drip, drip, drip of Tom’s soaked trousers onto the floorboards. Huck’s mouth was open a little, but he said nothing; he was an expert in funerals, from the outside, but the idea of one for a person who was not properly and truly dead was a knot he could not immediately untangle. Joe was the first to find his voice.
“Our, our funerals?” he whispered, his eyes wide.
“That’s it,” said Tom, feeling the power of his revelation. He enjoyed the way their faces looked in the gloom, pale and rapt. “They think we’re drowned. Everybody does. They dragged the river and everything, but they didn’t find us. So they’ve given up. And Sunday the whole town’s going to be in church, crying and mourning for us.”
Joe Harper’s imagination, which was a fine and serviceable instrument when properly primed by another’s, began to catch fire. The initial horror was swiftly consumed by a flame of glorious vanity.
“Mourning? For us?” he said, his voice gaining strength. “The whole town?”
“The whole town,” Tom affirmed, nodding slowly. “And Becky Thatcher, I bet. And Amy Lawrence. They’ll all be there, just boohooing their eyes out.”
This was too much for Joe. A grin began to spread across his face. “By jings, Tom! That’s, that’s the gayest thing I ever heard of! They’ll preach a sermon about us!”
“Of course they will. They’ll talk about what good boys we were, and how we got led astray, and how they wish they’d been nicer to us.” Tom shot a significant look towards the memory of Sid’s pious face. “They’ll say what a shame it was we was took so young.”
“And we’ll be _there_ to hear it!” Joe was on his feet now, pacing in his excitement. “Won’t we, Tom? We’ll be there!”
“That’s the plan,” said Tom, the general laying out his strategy. “We lay low till Sunday morning. Then we sneak into the church whilst everyone’s getting seated. We can get up in the old gallery, where nobody ever sits. And we can watch the whole thing.”
“And then? What do we do then?”
Tom let the question hang, a plum of pure dramatic potential. “Then,” he said, drawing the word out, “at the very end, when they’re all crying the hardest, and the minister is saying ‘dust to dust,’ or something mournful like that, we’ll just walk in.”
Joe gasped. He saw it all. The packed pews, the black clothes, the solemn quiet broken by sobs; and then the three of them, tanned and piratical, appearing like ghosts at the back of the church. He saw the heads turning, the eyes widening, the shrieks of astonishment. It was a vision of such transcendent glory that it made him dizzy.
“Oh, Tom, I wish it was Sunday _now_!”
Huck had been silent through all this, his brow furrowed. He was not against the plan, for he was generally for any plan that promised novelty and was not his own, but the machinery of it was foreign to him.
“But what’ll they say about me?” he asked. “Nobody’ll be cryin’ for me.”
The question fell into the boys’ exultation like a stone in a puddle. Tom and Joe looked at him. It was true. Huck had no Aunt Polly, no Mrs. Harper, no Sid to be righteously put in his place. His passing would be noted, perhaps with a slight shudder by the town’s mothers, as the removal of a bad example for their own darlings. There would be no family pew draped in black for Huckleberry Finn.
Tom, for whom a plan was a sacred thing, was not to be deterred by a logistical flaw in its emotional architecture. “Why, they will too, Huck! The minister’ll talk about all of us. The three drowned boys! The lost adventurers! You’re one of the gang, ain’t you? They can’t leave you out.”
“And we’ll tell ’em you was our best mate,” added Joe, loyally. “We’ll tell ’em you was the bravest pirate of the whole lot.”
This seemed to satisfy Huck, not because he believed it, but because it showed his comrades meant for him to be included, which was a feeling so rare it was almost as good as having a real family to mourn you. He nodded. “All right, then. But what do we do till Sunday? It’s only Friday night.”
“Saturday tomorrow,” Tom calculated. “That’s a whole day. We got to hide. We can stay here in the tannery. Nobody ever comes in here.”
The problem of provisions arose next, as it always does with boys whose stomachs are their truest clocks. Their boiled ham from the island was long gone, and the glory of a posthumous celebrity did not fill the belly. It was here that Huck’s particular genius came to the fore. While Tom and Joe were masters of the imagined and the theatrical, Huck was a quiet chancellor of the exchequer of the real.
“I can get stuff,” he said, simply. “After dark. Folks leave their pantry windows unlatched. I can get some bread, maybe a pie off a sill. And there’s old man Sutton’s melon patch. He’s a heavy sleeper.”
Tom and Joe felt a pang of envy at Huck’s effortless criminality. When they stole, it was an enterprise freighted with guilt and the fear of discovery; for Huck, it was simply housekeeping.
And so the plan was settled. They spent the rest of the night huddled in the dark, speaking in excited whispers about the coming triumph, their imaginations painting the scene in ever more brilliant colors. They would be famous. More than famous; they would be legends. The boys who came back from the dead.

Saturday was the longest day in history. It seemed to the boys that the sun got stuck in the sky directly over St. Petersburg. The tannery, which had seemed a fine and mysterious hideout at night, was by day a dreary, fly-blown prison that smelled sourly of its own decay. They dared not venture out. They could hear the town going about its business; the rumble of a wagon, the distant clang of the blacksmith’s hammer, the shout of a boy in the street, a boy who was free to walk in the sun, who was not legally dead. The sounds of ordinary life seemed to mock their grand, secret enterprise.
They passed the time with talk. They rehearsed their entrance a dozen times.
“I think,” said Joe, “I’ll come in with my arms folded, kind of stern, like a pirate that’s been through a lot.”
“No,” said Tom, who was the natural director of the company, “that’s not right. We ought to look kind of sad and tired. Like we’ve been through a terrible ordeal. We don’t want to look proud, not at first. We want to look humble. It makes the surprise better.”
“Humble?” Joe was not sure he cared for that. The whole point was to be glorious.
“Yes. And then, when Aunt Polly grabs me, I’ll say something like, ‘Don’t cry, Auntie, Providence has spared us.’ It sounds more noble.”
Joe tried to picture it. He supposed Tom was right. Nobility had its points. “All right. And I’ll say, I’ll say, ‘Mother, your boy has returned from a watery grave!’”
“That’s bully, Joe! That’s just the ticket!”
They fell silent, each picturing his own heroic return. Huck whittled a stick. He did not have a line prepared. He reckoned he would just stand there, which was what he was used to doing anyway.
As evening fell, Huck slipped out like a shadow and, true to his word, returned some time later with the spoils of his hunt: half a loaf of rye bread, a fistful of cold boiled potatoes, and three slightly green apples. It was not a feast, but to the famished pirates it was a banquet. As they ate, they heard the tolling of the church bell, slow and solemn.
“What’s that for?” said Joe, his mouth full of potato.
“That’s for us,” said Tom, with a grim satisfaction. “They’re tolling the bell for us.”
They stopped eating and listened. The slow, heavy clang seemed to shake the very air in the tannery, a sound full of doom and finality. For the first time, a sliver of unease pierced Joe’s excitement. It was one thing to imagine a funeral; it was another to hear the bell ringing for it. It sounded altogether too real.
“It feels spooky, Tom,” he said in a low voice.
“It’s supposed to,” said Tom. “That’s the whole point.” But even he felt a little cold. This was a bigger game than he had ever played before.
They slept poorly that night, huddled together for warmth and courage against the weight of their own impending deaths.

Sunday morning broke clear and fine. A soft, sanctified quiet lay over the town. The boys’ hearts were thumping like drumbeats. This was the day. They watched through a crack in the tannery wall as the respectable folk of St. Petersburg emerged from their houses in their Sunday best, the ladies’ dark silks rustling, the men’s boots polished, all heading in one direction.
“It’s time,” Tom whispered. “Come on.”
They crept out of the back of the tannery and began a skulking, nerve-racking journey through back alleys and overgrown yards. They dodged a dog, froze behind a rain barrel as the Widow Douglas passed by with a black-bordered handkerchief in her hand, and finally made it to the church. The main congregation was already going in the front. The boys scurried around to a small side door that was always unlocked, a door that led to a narrow, dusty staircase. They slipped inside, their bare feet making no sound, and climbed up into the gloom of the unused organ gallery.
It was a perfect vantage point. They could see everything. They peered down through the balustrade at the scene below. The church was packed, fuller than it ever was for a regular sermon. The front pews were occupied by the bereaved families. Tom saw Aunt Polly, her face pale and etched with lines of grief he had never seen before; beside her sat Sid, looking unnaturally solemn, and Mary, her head bowed. In the next pew was Mrs. Harper, weeping quietly into her handkerchief, with her husband beside her, his face stern and sorrowful. The boys’ schoolmates were there, too, scrubbed and uncomfortable in their Sunday clothes. Becky Thatcher was there, and Tom’s heart gave a little jump; she was not crying, but her expression was sad and distant.
The organ began to play a mournful dirge. The minister, the Reverend Mr. Sprague, ascended to the pulpit. The service began. The minister spoke of the fleeting nature of life, of the mysterious ways of Providence. Then he began the eulogy.
He spoke of Joseph Harper, a bright and cheerful boy, full of life and promise, a comfort to his mother and a joy to his friends. He mentioned a time Joe had shared his apple with a smaller boy, an act of charity that now seemed a saintly premonition. Joe, in the gallery, swelled with pride and nudged Tom. He had forgotten all about that apple, but he was glad the minister had remembered.
Then the minister turned to Thomas Sawyer. He painted a picture of a boy with a heart of gold, a leader, a soul brimming with a boundless, if sometimes misdirected, energy. He told of Tom’s chivalry and his essential goodness, glossing over the truancies, the fights, and the general deviltry as mere boyish spirits that the hand of sorrow had now sanctified. He spoke of a nature that was generous and noble, a spirit that, had it been allowed to mature, would surely have become a great man, a pillar of the community.
Tom was in ecstasy. He had never known he was such a fine character. This was better, even, than being a pirate captain. He wished he could have died, just for a little while, to have deserved all this.
Finally, the minister spoke of Huckleberry Finn. His tone changed. Here was a child of the wilderness, he said, a boy without the blessings of a home or the guidance of a loving family. He was a creature of impulse and neglect, and while his influence was not always for the best, who could say what starved affections and hidden virtues lay buried in that untutored heart? The town, the minister suggested, had perhaps failed this boy. His death was a reproach to them all.
Huck was dumbfounded. He had expected nothing, and had received a eulogy that made him sound like a sad, romantic mystery. He did not understand half of the words, but he understood the feeling. For the first time in his life, he was being publicly considered, and it was a strange and bewildering sensation.
The eulogies concluded. The minister’s voice grew thick with emotion. He spoke of the three empty places in the school, the three empty places in their hearts. Sobs broke out all over the church. Mrs. Harper was crying openly now. And then Tom saw it, Aunt Polly’s face crumpled, her shoulders began to shake, and she buried her face in her hands, her whole body convulsed with a grief so raw and profound it was like a physical blow.
The fun went out of the game.
Tom’s magnificent vision of a glorious entrance suddenly seemed a shabby and cruel trick. He was not a returning hero; he was a scoundrel who had broken his old aunt’s heart for a lark. A hot wave of shame washed over him. Joe, beside him, was sniffling. The sight of his own mother’s genuine despair had pierced his vanity. The grand adventure had curdled.
A hymn began, a slow, sorrowful tune about meeting on a beautiful shore. The congregation rose, their voices shaky. It was now or never. Tom’s grand plan called for a dramatic entrance during the final prayer, but he couldn’t wait. He couldn’t bear another minute of it. He gave a sharp nudge to Joe and a pull to Huck’s sleeve.
He led the way down the dusty stairs. He pushed open the side door and, taking a deep breath, marched into the main aisle of the church.
For a second, nobody noticed. The congregation was singing, their eyes on their hymn-books or closed in sorrow. Then a little girl on the end of a pew saw them. Her hymn-book dropped with a clatter. Her mouth fell open in a silent ‘O.’ Her mother looked down, then looked up, and then she let out a piercing shriek.
Heads turned. The singing faltered and died in a ragged wave from the back of the church to the front. The organist, sensing something was amiss, played a few discordant notes and then fell silent. A thousand eyes fixed on the three apparitions in the aisle: three dirty, sunburnt, ragamuffin boys who were supposed to be at the bottom of the Mississippi River.
There was a moment of absolute, breathless silence. Then chaos erupted.
Aunt Polly was the first to move. She stared, her hand to her heart, as if seeing a ghost. Then her face broke, not with sorrow, but with a wild, incredulous joy. “Tom!” she cried, a sound that was half-shout, half-sob. She flew down the aisle and seized her resurrected nephew, hugging him so hard he could not breathe, and showering him with kisses and cuffs all at once.
“You, you bad, wicked, precious boy! Oh, I could just skin you alive! I’ve never been so glad to see anyone in my life!”
At the same time, Mrs. Harper had descended upon Joe, and the scene was repeated, a tempest of joyful tears and recriminations. The church dissolved into a pandemonium of laughter, crying, and exclamations of amazement. People were standing on pews to get a better look. The minister, his eulogy stillborn on his lips, gaped from the pulpit, his face a perfect picture of flabbergasted joy. Sid looked purely disgusted. Becky Thatcher was staring at Tom with shining eyes, an expression of such pure, unadulterated admiration that it paid for all the shame Tom had felt moments before.
He was a hero after all! He had broken their hearts, yes, but he had also given them back, and the giving back was a hundred times more glorious than the breaking. The minister, recovering his wits, waved his arms for quiet.
“Praise God from whom all blessings flow!” he roared, and the congregation, electrified, joined in with a volume and passion that shook the rafters. It was the most splendid conclusion to a funeral service the town had ever known.
In the midst of the joyful riot, Huck Finn stood alone. He had no one to rush toward him, no one to hug and scold him. He stood awkwardly to one side, an overlooked footnote to the main story. He was happy for his friends, but he felt a familiar, hollow ache. He had been a part of the adventure, but he was not a part of the welcome home. He started to edge back toward the door, thinking to slip away into his accustomed invisibility.
But Tom, released for a moment from Aunt Polly’s fierce embrace, saw him. He saw Huck’s posture, the way he was fading back into the shadows. And in a flash of loyalty that was as bright and true as any of his theatrical notions, he grabbed Huck’s arm.
“Aunt Polly!” he yelled over the din. “It’s Huck! He was with us! It was all three of us!”
Aunt Polly turned, her tear-streaked face full of a fierce, all-encompassing relief. She looked at the ragged, friendless boy. For a moment her respectable instincts warred with the overwhelming emotion of the day. Then joy won, and she threw an arm around Huck as well.
“You poor, motherless thing!” she said, and gave him a hug that was clumsy and astonishing, and for the first time in his life Huckleberry Finn was part of the family, right in the middle of the noise and the light.

The procession from the church was a thing of wonder, a rolling tide of humanity with Tom and Joe as its glorious, foam-crested peak. They were mobbed, patted on the back, and their hair was ruffled by men who had never given them a second glance before. Every boy in town who was not Tom or Joe felt a pang of such profound and bitter envy that it was a taste worse than medicine. They were heroes now, and a hero’s life was a public spectacle; it belonged to the town as much as to himself. Tom, walking beside Aunt Polly, who still held his arm in a grip that was half affection and half a promise of future reckonings, felt his soul expand to fill the entire universe. He was aware of Becky Thatcher, a few paces behind, walking with her mother, and he knew, without looking, that her eyes were fixed upon him. This knowledge was a finer nectar than any pirated feast.

Huck trailed along in the wake of the main group, a satellite in a borrowed orbit. The astonishment of Aunt Polly’s hug had worn off, leaving behind a queer sort of embarrassment. He was not used to being touched with kindness, and it made his skin itch. He saw Joe Harper, swallowed up by his own family, weeping and laughing all at once. He saw Tom, the center of all things, the sun around which this new, dizzying world revolved. Huck felt a familiar twist of loneliness, but it was different this time; it was not the sharp, cold loneliness of being utterly ignored, but the dull, aching loneliness of being on the outside of a great and glorious warmth, close enough to feel the heat but not to be burned by it.

When they reached the gate of Aunt Polly’s fence, the crowd began to disperse, calling out last farewells and promises to hear the whole story. The moment of public triumph was over; the moment of private judgment was at hand. Inside the house, Aunt Polly released Tom and sank into a chair, her face a battleground of conflicting emotions. Joy at his return was fighting a desperate rear-guard action against a righteous and long-suffering anger. Mary, her eyes red but gentle, stood by her mother’s side. Sid, however, wore an expression of undisguised sourness; he had been cheated out of a day of solemn importance and he did not intend to forgive it easily.

“Thomas Sawyer,” Aunt Polly began, her voice trembling, “what I am to do with you, I do not know. The worry, the sleepless nights, the crying… your poor cousin Mary has been half sick with grief. And for what? For you to go gallivanting off on some wild notion, letting us think you were… that you were gone.” She put her handkerchief to her eyes.

Tom felt a twinge of something that might have been guilt, but the memory of his grand entrance was still too fresh and potent. He had a card to play, a masterstroke he had conceived in the long, homesick hours on the island.

“Aunt Polly,” he said, his voice full of a carefully rehearsed tenderness, “I didn’t mean for you to worry. I dreamed about you.”

Aunt Polly lowered her handkerchief a fraction. “Dreamed about me?”

“Yes’m. The night before last. I dreamed I came all the way back, and I snuck in right through that door there, and the room was all dark. And I saw you sitting right in that chair, and Sid was over there, and Mary was by the bed. And I heard everything you said.”

Mary looked at him, her gentle heart touched. Sid sniffed audibly.

“What did I say, Tom?” Aunt Polly asked, her voice softer.

“You said, ‘He warn’t bad, so to say, only mischeevous. Only just giddy, and harum-scarum, you know, and gay and bright and land-a-living, the joy of my heart.’ And you said you forgave me everything.” Tom’s delivery was perfect. He even managed a little catch in his own voice.

Mary’s eyes filled with tears again. “Oh, Tom, you did dream it! That’s just what she said.”

“And then,” Tom continued, pressing his advantage, “I bent down and I kissed you on the lips, and you didn't even stir.”

“Did I, Tom? Did I?” Aunt Polly was nearly won over. To have this proof, this supernatural connection, would absolve her of a portion of her anger and him of a portion of his sin.

“Yes, you did. And I was so sorry I ever made you sad. I was going to leave a little piece of bark on the table with a message on it, to tell you we were all right. But I thought it would spoil the… well, I just decided not to.”

This was a slight miscalculation. The theatricality of it brought a flicker of suspicion back to Aunt Polly’s eyes. Sid saw his chance.

“A mighty convenient dream, seems to me,” he muttered. “Most dreams ain’t so particular about the details.”

Aunt Polly looked from Tom’s earnest, pleading face to Sid’s smug, accusatory one. She was a woman who preferred a comfortable fiction to a harsh truth, but Sid’s logic was annoyingly sound. She sighed. “It’s a beautiful story, Tom, and I want to believe you. I truly do. But it’s just too… too perfect.”

Tom’s heart sank. He had played his best card, and it had been trumped by Sid’s miserable reality. He felt in his jacket pocket, a nervous habit, and his fingers brushed against something. The bark! He had forgotten he had actually put it in his pocket that night. With a flourish, he pulled it out. It was a small scrap of sycamore bark, and on its smooth inner surface he had scrawled with a bit of charcoal: “We ain’t dead, we are only off being pirates.”

He handed it to his aunt. She took it, turned it over, and read the words. She looked at Tom, then back at the bark. She read it again. Then her face crumpled entirely, and the last vestiges of her anger washed away in a fresh flood of tears. She pulled him into an embrace that was all affection now, with no hint of punishment.

“Oh, you dear, blessed, troublesome boy!” she wept. “You did come! And you did mean to tell me! I’ll never doubt you again, Tom, never!”

She hugged him and kissed him, while Mary beamed and even Sid was forced into a stunned silence. Tom had not only won, he had won with proof. He had managed to be both a heartless scamp and a loving nephew all in the same act, a feat of emotional gymnastics that was the very essence of his character.

In the midst of this reconciliation, Huckleberry Finn, who had been lingering awkwardly by the door, felt his invisibility returning to him. This was a family circle, and he was a straight line. He slipped out the door without a sound and made his way down the street. The sun was warm, the birds were singing, but the world felt a little grayer than it had inside the church. He was a hero of the adventure, but a ghost at the feast. He headed for the familiar, comforting squalor of his hogshead, where no one would hug him or scold him, and where the only expectation was to be left alone.

Monday morning arrived, and with it, the true test of glory: the schoolyard. Tom and Joe, by an unspoken agreement, arranged to arrive late. They timed their entrance for the moment when the schoolmaster, Mr. Dobbins, was occupied at his desk, and the murmur of study had settled over the room. The door creaked open, and there they stood. A collective gasp went through the room. Every head turned. For a full minute, there was a silence so profound you could have heard a pin drop in the next county.

Mr. Dobbins looked up, his perpetually sour expression giving way to blank amazement. He had attended the funeral service himself. He opened his mouth, but no sound came out.

Tom and Joe walked to their seats with a studied nonchalance, as if returning from a brief trip to the well rather than from the undiscovered country from whose bourn no traveller returns. The other boys stared, their books forgotten. The girls, Becky Thatcher chief among them, gazed with wide, shining eyes. It was a moment of pure, unadulterated triumph, worth a dozen lickings.

At recess, the dam of curiosity burst. Tom and Joe were surrounded, besieged with questions. They were no longer mere boys; they were explorers, adventurers, authorities on matters of life and death.

“Was you scared, Tom, during the storm?” asked one small boy, his eyes like saucers.

Tom exchanged a look with Joe. “Scared?” he said, with a little laugh. “Well, it was a tolerable blow, I’ll admit. The lightning took the top clean off a sycamore not ten feet from our tent. But we was too busy securing the camp to be scared.”

“We had to lash everything down,” Joe added, picking up the thread. “The waves on the river was as high as a house. Weren’t they, Tom?”

“Higher,” Tom confirmed. “We nearly lost our raft. Had to swim for it in the dark, with the thunder crashing right over our heads. It was a fine time.”

They conveniently forgot to mention cowering under the oak tree or the ignominious flight when their sail blew away. Their audience did not care. They wanted heroes, and heroes they would have.

Jeff Thatcher, the very boy they had imagined keeling over from two puffs of a pipe, now pushed his way to the front. “Did you… did you really smoke pipes?” he asked, in a tone of hushed reverence.

“Pipes?” said Joe, as if trying to recall a minor detail. “Oh, yes. All the time. You can’t be a proper pirate without a pipe. After a long day of… you know, pirating… there’s nothing like a good smoke to settle you.”

“Don’t feel right without it now,” Tom said, shaking his head sadly, as if lamenting the lack of tobacco in civilization. “Gets to be a habit.”

The boys looked at them with a new and profound respect. These were not just adventurers; they were men. They had faced storms and lived on a deserted island, and they smoked pipes as a matter of course. Johnny Miller, another subject of their former scorn, offered Tom the core of his apple, a tribute of the highest order in the schoolyard economy. Tom accepted it with a magnanimous nod.

But his eyes were searching the crowd of girls. He saw Becky Thatcher, standing with a small knot of friends, pretending not to listen but hanging on every word. He waited for his moment. When the bell rang to end recess, he contrived to be near her as they walked back to the schoolhouse.

“Tom,” she said, her voice a little breathless. “How could you be so brave?”

The anger from their previous falling-out was gone, washed away by the tide of his spectacular return. He was no longer the boy who had flirted with Amy Lawrence; he was the boy who had come back from the dead.

“Oh, it warn’t anything,” Tom said, with the easy modesty of a man who has wrestled with hurricanes. “Pirating just gets in your blood. It was a grand life, Becky. You wake up when you want, go swimming whenever you please, no school, no washing…”

He made it sound like a paradise, and for a moment, it felt like one in the telling. He did not mention the gnawing homesickness, the stale bread, or the terrible, gut-wrenching nausea that followed his first experiment with tobacco. In the warm glow of Becky’s admiration, those unpleasant details melted away like frost in the sun. He was the hero of his own story, and it was a masterpiece.

The news of the boys’ return and the manner of it became the sole topic of conversation in St. Petersburg for days. Their fame was absolute. But with fame came complications. The story of Huck’s involvement, and Aunt Polly’s unprecedented embrace, had pricked the town’s conscience. It was one thing for the town pariah to exist on the periphery, a creature of rags and shadows. It was another for him to be a proven comrade in a grand adventure, a boy who had shown loyalty and courage, and to then be sent back to his barrel.

The chief instrument of this newfound civic virtue was the Widow Douglas. She was a kindly soul, rich, childless, and possessed of a deep and abiding sense of Christian duty. She had been moved to tears by the sight of Huck standing alone and friendless in the church, and the story of Aunt Polly’s hug had settled the matter in her mind.

A few days after the resurrection, she put on her best bonnet and went in search of Huckleberry. She found him near the old tannery, sitting on a log and idly whittling a stick, the picture of contented idleness. He saw her coming and his first instinct was to bolt, for respectable ladies in bonnets were usually a sign of trouble, portending lectures on cleanliness or Sunday school.

But the Widow Douglas had a gentle voice and a kind face. “Huckleberry,” she said, stopping a few feet away, as one might approach a timid deer. “I was hoping I might have a word with you.”

Huck eyed her warily. “Reckon so,” he allowed.

“Huckleberry,” she said, getting to the point, “it is not right for a boy like you, a boy who has been through such an ordeal, to be living… well, to be living as you do. You are a good boy at heart. I can see it.”

Huck had no idea what she was talking about. He had never considered the state of his heart one way or the other.

“I have a large house,” she continued, “and it is very empty. I would consider it a great privilege if you would come and live with me. I would give you a proper bed, and good food, and new clothes. And I would see to it that you get an education.”

Huck stared at her. This was a proposal so outlandish, so contrary to the entire order of his universe, that he could not properly process it. A bed? He had a perfectly good hogshead, which did not require making up in the morning. Good food? He was an excellent fisherman and forager, and what he could not catch, he could usually procure. New clothes? The very thought made him feel stiff and itchy. And an education? That sounded perilously like school, which was a prison with a slate and a switch.

“Thank ye kindly, ma’am,” he stammered, “but I’m doin’ right fine as I am.”

“Nonsense, child,” the widow said firmly, but not unkindly. “Every boy needs a home. He needs guidance. You’ll have everything a boy could want.”

Huck tried to imagine it. He would have to wash. He would have to use a fork and a knife for every meal. He would have to wear shoes, which were little leather prisons for the feet. He would have to go to bed at a certain time and get up at a certain time. He would have to pray. The life she was describing seemed a punishment far more dreadful than being lost on an island. It was a clean and gilded cage.

But the widow was persistent. She spoke of Tom, and how proud Tom would be. She spoke of how he was a hero now, and heroes could not live in barrels. She did not understand that the barrel was the very source of his freedom, the one thing that made his life his own. He looked at her earnest, pleading face, and he felt a strange and unwelcome pressure. The whole town, it seemed, had decided he needed saving. To refuse would be an act of profound ingratitude, a slap in the face to the very people who were suddenly, bewilderingly, taking an interest in him.

With a heavy heart and a sense of impending doom, he finally mumbled his assent. The widow’s face lit up with a triumphant, benevolent smile. She had done her Christian duty. She had rescued a lost soul. Huckleberry Finn, on the other hand, felt as though he had just sold his soul for a plate of vegetables and a pair of shoes. He had gone away a free vagabond, and returned a celebrated hero, only to find that the price of his heroism was the one thing he truly valued: his own wild liberty. As the widow led him away toward his new life, a life of clean sheets and regular meals and suffocating respectability, he felt a longing for the simple misery of being shipwrecked that was as keen and sharp as any homesickness Tom and Joe had ever felt. The adventure was over, but the consequences, he was beginning to suspect, were just beginning.

When the ferryboat came to rescue them, St. Petersburg turned out in a body to receive its resurrected sons. There were tears, and hugs, and a great deal of shouting. The boys, who had been merely playing a game, found themselves cast as the principal actors in a real drama of loss and miraculous return. They were heroes, and they bore their honors with a magnificent solemnity that befitted the occasion. Joe Harper was swept away by his weeping mother, and Tom was seized by a demonstrative Aunt Polly, who cuffed him and kissed him in the same breath.

Huck, having no family to claim him, stood awkwardly at the edge of the crowd. He was a hero too, the townspeople said so; but he was a hero without a welcome. He had half a mind to slip away back to his hogshead by the abandoned tannery, where a hero’s welcome consisted of not being bothered. The Widow Douglas’s house was a palace of baffling customs. Huck was made to stand in a tub of hot water and suffer a scrubbing with soap that got in his eyes and smelled like a funeral. Then he was put into a suit of clothes so new and stiff that he could hardly bend his elbows or knees; the collar sawed at his neck, and the shoes pinched his feet into a shape they were never meant to hold. He felt like a dressed up corpse, and moved with about as much grace.

At supper, he was seated at a table covered with a white cloth that he was terrified of soiling. The widow rang a little bell, and a negro woman brought in dishes of food. Before he could fall to, the widow bowed her head and muttered over the victuals. Huck had never seen anyone talk to their food before, and he watched with interest, but he decided it was a waste of time; the food did not seem to pay her any mind. She called it “saying grace,” but Huck couldn’t see anything graceful about it.

Then came the ordeal of the implements. There was a fork for this and a knife for that. He was used to a single jackknife for every purpose from skinning a squirrel to picking his teeth, and this array of silverware was a perplexing nuisance. He took up his food with his fingers, as nature intended, and was gently corrected. He tried to use the fork, but the food kept falling off. It was a miserable business. He looked at the juicy slice of beef and the pile of steaming potatoes, and his stomach rumbled, but the journey from the plate to his mouth was fraught with such peril and regulation that he lost his appetite.

After supper, the widow’s sister, a severe and skinny old maid named Miss Watson, took him in hand. She produced a book and began to read to him about Moses in the bulrushes. Huck was interested for a little while, because he had some experience with being found in unlikely places himself. But he soon lost the thread of it when he found out Moses had been dead for a considerable long time.

“I don’t take no stock in dead people,” he said.

Miss Watson was scandalized. “Huckleberry! You must not say such things. Moses was a great and holy man.”

She then told him all about the good place and the bad place. She said the good place was a land of harps and singing, and you did nothing forever and ever. Huck thought it sounded dreadfully dull. He asked if Tom Sawyer would go there. She said, “Not by a considerable sight.” Huck was glad of that, for he wanted to be where Tom was. Then she described the bad place, with its fire and brimstone, in such a way that it sounded a good deal more interesting than the alternative, but he did not say so, for he could see she was getting worked up.

Finally, he was sent to bed. The room was grand, with a high, soft bed made up with sheets as smooth and cool as river stones. He blew out the candle and lay down, but he could not sleep. The sheets felt like a trap. The silence of the house was a heavy, dead thing; he was used to the night sounds of the woods, the chirping of crickets and the croaking of frogs and the whisper of the wind. Here, the only sound was the solemn ticking of a clock downstairs, counting off the seconds of his imprisonment. He felt smothered by the quiet, by the softness, by the sheer, overwhelming cleanness of it all. He got up and sat by the window, looking out at the moonlit trees. He felt so lonesome he could have died. He wished he was back in his hogshead, with the rain pattering on the staves and no one to bother him about Moses or table manners.

The next day was the same, and the day after that. He had to wash, and dress, and eat with a fork. He had to sit still while Miss Watson tried to teach him his letters, which looked like so many bugs squashed on a page. He had to listen to stories about dead people and learn to pray for things. He tried praying once. He prayed for a new fish-hook, but it did not come. He prayed again, for a different kind, but that did not come either. He told Miss Watson, and she said he was supposed to pray for “spiritual gifts.” He couldn’t see the use of it. What a body wanted with spiritual gifts, he couldn’t imagine. He reckoned you could get a fish-hook by yourself if you had the gumption, but he never saw anybody get a spiritual gift, no matter how much they prayed. It all seemed like a terrible waste of a person’s time.

The whole system was a puzzle. At the widow’s, you couldn’t smoke, you couldn’t chew, you couldn’t swear, you couldn’t lie down on the floor, you couldn’t yawn or stretch without being corrected. It was a life made up of a thousand little “don’ts.” He felt crawly and cramped, and the longing for his old, free life grew into a fierce ache.

One night, after three weeks of this misery, he could stand it no more. He waited until the house was dark and silent, and the clock downstairs was tolling its weary way toward midnight. He took off the stiff, hateful clothes and put on his old rags, which he had hidden under the bed. They felt as comfortable as his own skin. He slipped out of his window, shinnied down the drainpipe, and dropped lightly to the ground. He was free.

He did not run, he simply walked, drinking in the cool night air and the familiar sounds of the dark. He made his way to the old tannery and found his hogshead just where he had left it. It was damp and smelled of wood rot and old leather, and it was the most beautiful sight in the world. He crawled inside, pulled an old horse-blanket over himself, and lay there, listening to the river and the wind. He was home. He was so happy he could have cried, but he did not. He just lay there, feeling the knots in his soul untie themselves one by one, until he fell into the first truly peaceful sleep he had had in weeks.

He lived like that for two or three days, happy as a turtle on a log. He fished and smoked his pipe and did nothing at all, which was his favorite occupation. He supposed the widow was hunting for him, but he did not care. He had escaped, and that was all that mattered.

One afternoon, he was dozing in the sun when he heard a familiar whistle. It was Tom Sawyer’s signal. Huck answered, and presently Tom appeared, looking pleased and important.

“Hello, Huck,” he said. “I reckoned I’d find you here. They’re making a powerful fuss about you up at the widow’s.”

“Let ’em,” said Huck. “I ain’t goin’ back. It’s too dismal. They try to sivilize you, Tom, and it ain’t worth the trouble. A body can’t stand it.”

Tom sat down on the edge of the hogshead. “I know,” he said. “It’s rough. But Huck, you’ve got to go back.”

“I won’t,” said Huck. “I’d druther be here.”

“But you can’t,” Tom said, his eyes shining with a new idea. “You can’t be in my gang if you ain’t respectable. That’s what.”

Huck was interested. “Gang? What gang?”

“Tom Sawyer’s Gang,” said Tom, puffing out his chest. “We’re going to be robbers. It’s a new thing. Me, and Joe Harper, and Ben Rogers, and a couple of the other boys. We’re going to rob people and kill them.”

“Kill them?” said Huck. “That’s a mighty rough business.”

“Oh, we’ll mostly kill them,” said Tom. “That’s what robbers do. It’s in all the books. And we’ll have a secret hideout, and an oath, and everything. It’s going to be bully. But,” he added, looking sternly at Huck, “we can’t have any ragamuffins in the gang. Robbers is always respectable, you know. They have to be. It says so in the books. They’re dukes and things, in disguise.”

Huck was perplexed. This was a new kind of logic, but it was Tom’s logic, and Tom’s logic always had a way of sounding right, even when it was plainly foolish.

“So I got to go back to the widow’s?” he asked mournfully.

“Of course,” said Tom. “You got to be respectable. But it ain’t for long. Just till we get the gang going good. Then we’ll all run away and be robbers for good. But for now, you got to keep up appearances. It’s part of the game. See?”

Huck did not see, not really. But the idea of a gang of robbers was powerfully attractive. It was a good deal better than being civilized for no reason at all. If he had to be civilized in order to be a robber, then perhaps it was a price worth paying. It was a trade: a little bit of washing and praying in exchange for a life of crime and adventure.

“All right,” he said, with a sigh. “If I got to, I reckon I got to. When does this gang start?”

“Tonight,” said Tom. “Meet me at the foot of Cardiff Hill after midnight. And Huck, wear your shoes.”

So Huck went back. He climbed up the drainpipe and got into his bed just before dawn. When the widow found him there in the morning, she cried and said she was so glad the angels had brought him back. Huck did not tell her that he had come back under his own power, and for the express purpose of becoming a criminal. He let her think what she liked. It was easier that way.

That night, Huck ate his supper with the fork and endured a long story about some fellow named Jonah who got swallowed by a whale. Huck thought it was a mighty tall tale, but he kept his opinion to himself. He was being respectable. When the house was quiet, he slipped out again and made his way to the appointed place.

The other boys were already there, gathered in the deep shadows of the trees. They spoke in whispers, full of a delicious fear. Tom Sawyer led them to a thicket of sumac bushes, where he revealed a small opening in the hillside, cleverly hidden. It was the entrance to a cave. They lit candles and crawled inside, their hearts thumping with excitement.

The cave was a fine, spooky place, with winding passages and dripping stalactites. Tom led them to a small chamber, which he declared to be their secret meeting hall.

“Now,” he said, looking around at the fire-lit faces, “we’ve got to have an oath. A blood oath. Everybody that wants to join has to sign his name in blood.”

He produced a sheet of paper and a pen. The paper had a skull and crossbones drawn at the top. Underneath, Tom had written out the oath in a wobbly hand. It was a terrible oath. It swore them all to secrecy, on pain of having their throats cut and their carcasses burnt. It bound them to obey the captain, who was Tom, in all things. It said that if any boy revealed the gang’s secrets, his family would be killed, starting with his mother. Joe Harper began to look pale and said he didn’t have a mother, only an aunt, but Tom said an aunt would do just as well.

They pricked their fingers with a pin, which hurt a considerable amount more than they had reckoned, and squeezed out a reluctant drop of blood apiece, with which they made a messy blot next to their names. Huck could not write, so he made his mark, a wobbly cross that looked more like a spider than a letter.

When the ceremony was done, Tom rolled up the paper and hid it in a secret crevice.

“Now we’re a proper gang,” he said. “Tom Sawyer’s Gang. We’ll meet here every week. Our line of business is robbery and murder.”

The boys shivered, half in fear and half in delight.

“Who we goin’ to rob?” asked Ben Rogers.

“Oh, anybody,” said Tom. “We’ll stop stagecoaches on the road. And we’ll take their watches and money. And we’ll bring the people back here to the cave and hold them for ransom.”

“What’s ransom?” asked Joe Harper.

“It’s when you keep them till they’re ransomed,” said Tom.

“But what does it mean, ransomed?”

“I don’t know,” said Tom. “But that’s what they do. I’ve seen it in books. You keep them till they’re ransomed. I reckon it means you just keep them. They’ll all be chained up here, and we’ll feed them bread and water. It’ll be grand.”

Huck had a practical question. “But what about the women? Do we kill them too?”

“Oh, no,” said Tom. “We don’t kill the women. We’re polite to the women. We bring them here and they live with us. And after a while, they fall in love with us, and they don’t want to go home anymore.”

This sounded like a fine arrangement to the boys, though Huck had his doubts. He had not seen much evidence that women were so easily charmed, especially by boys who were planning to murder their husbands. But Tom said it was in the books, so it must be true.

They talked over their plans for an hour, their imaginations running wild with ambushes and sword fights and daring escapes. They were no longer just boys from St. Petersburg; they were a band of desperate outlaws, the terror of the Mississippi Valley.

At last, Tom declared the meeting over. They crept out of the cave and went their separate ways, sworn to a life of crime. Huck went back to the widow’s house, feeling a good deal more cheerful about his situation. Life under Miss Watson’s thumb was still a trial, but now it had a purpose. It was a disguise. He was not Huckleberry Finn, the widow’s charity case; he was Huckleberry Finn, the Robber, biding his time in the enemy’s camp. It made all the difference in the world. He could endure the prayers and the spelling book, because he knew that soon, very soon, he would be out on the high road with his comrades, a brace of pistols in his belt, stopping a stagecoach and demanding, in a voice of thunder, “Your money or your life!” It was a comforting thought, and it made even the softest bed and the cleanest sheets almost bearable.

For a week, the life of crime was a dull affair. It consisted mostly of meeting in the cave and talking about the crimes they were going to commit. Tom Sawyer’s Gang was a formidable power on paper, but in practice it had yet to inconvenience so much as a stray cat. The next day, they prepared for battle. They striped their faces with berry juice and mud until they looked like a delegation from a particularly mismanaged minstrel show. They armed themselves with their wooden swords and brandished barrel staves that were to serve as muskets. Tom, as befitting his rank, had a tin pot for a helmet and a broomstick for a lance. They were a terrifying spectacle, or would have been, to anyone with a very active imagination and poor eyesight.

They crept through the woods to the river road, their hearts thumping with a mixture of fear and ferocious glee. As they drew near the appointed spot, Tom called a halt.

“Hark!” he whispered. “I hear them. They are feasting. Now, remember the plan. Joe, Ben, on my signal, you make the diversion. The signal will be a crow’s call. Three times.”

They waited, crouched in the undergrowth. Presently, they heard the sounds of the “caravan.” There was laughter, and high-pitched chatter, and the clinking of what Tom declared to be Spanish doubloons, but which sounded to Huck suspiciously like lemonade glasses.

Tom drew himself up. He took a deep breath and let out a sound that was meant to be a crow’s call. It was not a very good crow’s call. It sounded more like a cat that had stepped on a tack. He tried again. The second was worse than the first. It was a strangled squawk that made a nearby bluejay screech in indignation.

Before he could attempt a third, a wave of noise erupted from the west. Joe and Ben, either through impatience or a misinterpretation of the first squawk, had launched their diversion. They were howling and beating on a fallen log with their sticks. It was a creditable amount of noise for two boys.

They burst upon the scene of revelry. The rich caravan of A-rabs and Spaniards was the First Reader class from the Sunday School, out on their annual picnic with their teacher, Miss Miller. They were not feasting on roasted peacock and dates, but on ginger cake and sandwiches. The chests of jewels were wicker baskets full of apples. The elephants and camels were nowhere to be seen.

For a moment, everyone froze. The little children, their mouths full of cake, stared with wide, curious eyes at the painted, screaming intruders. Miss Miller, a gentle soul of twenty, let out a small shriek and dropped a jar of marmalade.

Tom Sawyer was the first to recover. He had charged in to fight A-rabs, and by thunder, he would fight A-rabs, whatever they looked like.

“Surrender, ye dogs!” he bellowed, waving his broomstick at a small boy of six who was holding a half-eaten gingerbread man. “Yield up your treasure!”

The small boy looked at the gingerbread man, then at Tom, and burst into tears. This broke the spell. The other small children, seeing their comrade fall, took up the cry, and in a moment the clearing was filled with a chorus of wails.

The gang’s charge faltered. It is a difficult thing to maintain a warlike posture in the face of weeping six-year-olds.

“Grab the loot!” yelled Tom, trying to salvage the situation.

The “loot” was in a state of disarray. Ben Rogers, in his bloodthirsty charge, had tripped over a basket and sent a dozen hard-boiled eggs rolling into the dirt. Joe Harper had managed to seize a pitcher of root beer, which he was now struggling to drink while running. The rest of the gang, leaderless and confused, milled about uncertainly.

Huck had stopped dead. He saw what was happening, and it was not the glorious battle he had been promised. It was just bullying little kids. He felt a pang of something that was almost shame.

“Tom,” he said, pulling at his sleeve. “Tom, this ain’t A-rabs. It’s the Sunday School.”

“Silence, Huck!” hissed Tom. “It’s enchantment! A magician has cast a spell on them to make them look like this. They’re A-rabs in disguise, I tell you!”

“Thomas Sawyer, you stop that this instant!” she cried, and brought the parasol down on his tin-pot helmet with a resounding clang.

The blow was not hard, but it was profoundly humiliating. A general does not get spanked with a parasol in the middle of a battle. The great Tom Sawyer, the Black Avenger of the Spanish Main, was being disciplined like a common schoolboy.

That was the end of the battle. The gang fled. They did not retreat in good order; they scattered into the woods like frightened rabbits, each boy for himself. They left behind a scene of chaos: weeping children, a distraught teacher, and a great deal of squashed cake. They had, however, managed to make off with some of the spoils. Joe Harper still had his pitcher of root beer, another boy had a fistful of sandwiches, and Tom, despite his drubbing, had clung tenaciously to the chocolate cake, which was now sadly smeared down the front of his shirt.

They regrouped, breathless and bedraggled, in the safety of the cave. They laid out their plunder on a flat rock: the half-empty pitcher, the squashed sandwiches, a few pilfered apples, and the mauled chocolate cake. It did not look like the treasure of a Spanish caravan.

“Well,” said Tom, trying to sound triumphant. “We did it. Our first raid.”

Nobody answered. They were all catching their breath. Ben Rogers looked at the meager haul.

“Where’s the jewels?” he asked.

“And the gold ingots?” added Joe Harper.

“It was the enchanter,” said Tom firmly. “He cast a spell. He turned the jewels into root beer and the gold into cake. It’s a common trick for enchanters. They’re a sneaky lot. But we beat them, didn’t we? We got their treasure.”

He tore a chunk of the chocolate cake off with his fingers and stuffed it into his mouth. “Tastes like gold to me,” he said, with his mouth full.

The other boys were not so sure. They had expected sword fights and daring escapes. They had got a scolding from a Sunday School teacher. But Tom’s confidence was infectious, and the cake was undeniably good. They fell upon the plunder and devoured it, washing it down with the root beer.

Huck sat a little apart from the rest. He ate his share of the cake, but he wasn’t satisfied. He had laid in the bushes and listened to Tom go on about A-rabs and elephants, and all the time it was just the primer class. He had seen the “jewels” with his own eyes, and they were apples. He tried to see it Tom’s way, to believe in the enchanter. It was a more exciting way to look at the world. But the facts kept getting in the way. It was just a picnic, and they had spoiled it. He laid down and decided he wouldn’t bother himself about it. If Tom said they were A-rabs, then A-rabs they were. It was easier that way. The glory of the first raid soon faded. A few days later, a new and more pressing matter presented itself to Tom Sawyer’s Gang. A spy was in their midst. Or at least, Tom was convinced there was a spy.

It began when Joe Harper reported seeing Sid, Tom’s odiously well-behaved half-brother, poking around the foot of Cardiff Hill, not a stone’s throw from the secret entrance to their lair.

“He was just pickin’ flowers,” Joe said. “But he was lookin’ around, all sneaky-like.”

To anyone else, this would have seemed like perfectly normal behavior for Sid, who was fond of botany and sneakiness in equal measure. But to Tom, it was a thunderbolt of revelation.

“A spy!” he declared, his eyes wide with horrified delight. “Of course! They’ve sent a spy to find our headquarters! Aunt Polly has hired him!”

“Sid?” said Huck. “He ain’t no spy. He’s just a goody-goody. He’s afraid of his own shadow.”

“That’s the genius of it!” exclaimed Tom. “Who would ever suspect him? He’s the perfect cover. While he’s pretending to collect sassafras, he’s really mapping our defenses, learning our secrets! We’re betrayed!”

The gang was horrified. Betrayed! It sounded wonderfully dramatic.

“What are we gonna do, Tom?” asked Ben Rogers, his hand creeping toward his lath sword.

Tom paced back and forth in the flickering torchlight of the cave, his brow furrowed in thought. He was Napoleon planning for Waterloo. He was D’Artagnan foiling a plot by the Cardinal.

“We must capture him,” he said at last. “We’ll bring him here, to the deepest dungeon. We’ll put him on trial for his crimes.”

“And then what?” asked Joe.

“And then,” Tom said, his voice dropping to a grim whisper, “we’ll execute him.”

A shiver went through the assembled outlaws. This was a step up from raiding a picnic. This was real business.

“Kill Sid?” Huck said. “Tom, your Aunt Polly will tan your hide clean off.”

He could stand a scolding; he could even bear a thrashing with a certain stoic pride. But he could not bear to see his aunt so defeated. He crept over and stood beside her chair. “I’m sorry, Aunt Polly,” he said, and he meant it. “I am. It just… it seemed like a good idea at the time.” She looked up at him, her eyes misty behind her spectacles. She saw the genuine contrition in his face, and her heart, which was fundamentally incapable of holding a grudge against him for very long, began to soften. “Oh, Tom,” she sighed, pulling him into a rough, unexpected hug. “You will be the death of me, but I suppose I couldn’t bear it if you were any other way.” Tom endured the hug, feeling a profound sense of relief. The storm had passed. But he knew, with the certainty of long experience, that while the thunder and lightning were over, a long and dreary rain of consequences was sure to follow. The rain came in the form of chores. The woodshed needed to be tidied, a task of Herculean proportions. The back fence, which had been in a state of gentle decline, was now declared to be in critical need of a new coat of whitewash. And, most grievously, all privileges relating to adventures, gangs, and the company of Huckleberry Finn were suspended for a period of one week. Tom spent the afternoon in a state of resigned servitude. He stacked wood, his mind replaying the morning’s dramatic collapse. He saw now where he had gone wrong. The plan was too public. They were too close to the town. A true buccaneer would have established his base on a more remote island, somewhere beyond the reach of meddling aunts. And the choice of prisoner had been a strategic error. Sid was too well-connected. Next time, he would capture a boy with fewer, less formidable relatives. His spirits, which had a natural buoyancy, began to rise as he planned his next, improved campaign. The failure of the morning was not a defeat, he decided, but a valuable lesson in logistics. By supper time, he was almost cheerful again. That evening, after the last of the chores was done, Tom sat on the back steps, watching the fireflies begin their nightly dance. A low whistle came from the shadows by the fence. It was the call of the gang, a signal that could not be ignored, even under penalty of further whitewashing. Tom whistled back and slipped into the darkness. Huck was there, sitting on a log and whittling a piece of wood with his Barlow knife. “Heard you was in a tight spot,” Huck said, without looking up. “It was a tactical retreat,” Tom corrected him. “We lured them into a false sense of security. Aunt Polly is a formidable adversary, you know. She has spies everywhere.” “Reckon she does,” Huck said. “Saw Joe Harper. His ma was waiting for him with a barrel stave. He won’t be pirating for a while.” “A temporary setback,” said Tom dismissively. “The cause is greater than any one man. But it was a glorious trial, wasn’t it, Huck? Did you see Sid’s face when I pronounced the sentence? He was scared, I tell you.” “He was mostly mad about his shoes,” Huck said. Tom waved a hand. “Trifles. It’s the grand sweep of the thing that matters. The solemnity. The dread. They’ll be talking about the trial of Sid Sawyer for years.” “Reckon they’ll be talking about Aunt Polly and the switch for years, too,” Huck observed. A brief cloud passed over Tom’s face, but it was quickly gone. “That was just the epilogue,” he said. “Every great tragedy has one. We live and learn, Huck. We live and learn. Next time, we’ll build a proper stockade.” Huck looked at Tom, whose eyes were already shining with the light of future, more glorious campaigns. The ruin of this day’s adventure was already being rebuilt in his mind as the foundation for the next. Huck shook his head, a small smile touching his lips. Tom was a force of nature, like the river or a summer thunderstorm. There was no stopping him, and maybe you didn’t want to. It was a trial to be his friend, that was certain, but it was never, ever dull. “Well,” Huck said, pocketing his knife. “When you get your stockade built, let me know. Long as it ain’t anywhere an aunt can find it.” “It’ll be impregnable,” Tom promised, his voice full of absolute conviction. “A fortress of liberty. And this time, Huck, we’ll remember to bring our own sandwiches.”
