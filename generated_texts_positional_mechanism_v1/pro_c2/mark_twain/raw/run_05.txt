The work of being a conspirator was hard, but the work of being a doubter was lonely. He decided to remain a conspirator.

“The cemetery it is,” Sam said, with a quiet finality that sealed the matter.

Their new purpose gave them a fresh burst of energy. The defeat at the hands of Barty Higgins was forgotten; it was merely a spur to greater deeds. They would not only tell a great story; they would live it. They would go to the cemetery, at night, with their rust-map, and find the treasure, and then Barty Higgins and his whole fact-based world could go hang.

The plan was set for that very night. Midnight. The time when all serious business of a supernatural or criminal nature is conducted. Getting out of the house was a simple matter for boys who viewed their bedroom windows not as openings for light and air, but as emergency exits from the prison of domestic life.

They met at the edge of town, where the last houses gave way to the cornfields that rolled up to the foot of the cemetery hill. The moon was a slender, disapproving sliver in the sky, and the stars seemed impossibly distant. Each boy was armed according to his own philosophy. Silas carried the pickaxe head, the sacred text of their expedition. Pete carried a shovel borrowed without leave from his father’s toolshed, for he was a boy of action. And Sam carried the lantern, for he was a boy who understood that it is better to see the thing that is about to scare you to death.

The cemetery was on a hill overlooking the town. It was a city of the dead, with white stone markers for houses and winding grass paths for streets. By day, it was a place of quiet, even pleasant, melancholy. But by night, it was a different country altogether. The familiar tombstones looked like hunched and waiting figures. The wind sighing through the great cedar trees sounded like whispers, and not friendly ones. Every shadow was a pit and every rustle of a leaf was a footstep.

“All right,” Silas whispered, his voice a little shaky. “The map. Let’s consult the map.”

Sam held up the lantern. In its flickering, unsteady light, the pickaxe head looked even more cryptic and menacing.

“The hanging tree,” Pete whispered, pointing with his shovel toward a massive, skeletal old oak at the far end of the cemetery. Its branches were thick and twisted, and one of them, thicker than a man’s body, stuck straight out, a grim and silent accusation against the sky.

They began to make their way toward it, stepping carefully between the graves. They walked past the grand marble angels of the town’s richer dead and the simple, leaning stones of its poorer folk. They passed the graves of soldiers, and babies, and men who had been drowned in the river, and women who had died of fevers. It was a great deal of history to be walking through in the dark, and it weighed on a boy’s soul.

“Huck’s pa is buried here,” Sam said quietly, for no particular reason. “Over yonder.”

“Let’s not talk about that,” Silas said quickly. “We’re on business.”

They reached the great oak. It was even more terrifying up close. The trunk was a gnarled mass of wood and shadow, and the great limb above them seemed to be reaching down for them.

“Now what?” said Pete, his grip tight on the shovel.

Silas held the pickaxe map under the lantern light again. “The X,” he muttered. “The X is just past the tree. Toward the river. It must be this way.”

He led them past the tree to a small, neglected plot of land where the grass grew long and wild. There were three small, identical headstones, worn smooth by the weather. The names were illegible.

“This is it,” Silas declared, with more confidence than he felt. “This has got to be it. The pits on the map… they’re small. Like these graves. Hemlock was a clever one. He buried his treasure with the forgotten dead.”

“So we dig?” Pete asked, eager to put his shovel to use.

“We dig,” Silas confirmed.

Pete spat on his hands, took a mighty swing, and drove the shovel into the soft earth. The sound was shockingly loud in the stillness. It sounded like a bone breaking. The boys all froze, listening. But there was only the wind in the cedars and the distant barking of a dog in the town below.

Pete began to dig in earnest. Sam held the lantern steady. Silas stood guard, holding the pickaxe head like a scepter, his ears straining for any sound of approaching townsfolk, or, worse, approaching ghosts.

The work was hard. The ground was thick with roots, which grabbed at the shovel like skeletal hands. After a few minutes, Pete was panting and sweating.

“Let me have a go,” Silas said, and took the shovel. He dug for a while, then Sam took a turn. They worked in silence, a grim, determined little crew, desecrating hallowed ground in pursuit of a dream built on rust.

They were about two feet down when Pete’s shovel struck something with a dull, heavy clunk. Not rock. Something else.

“That’s it!” Pete gasped. “The chest!”

A frenzy seized them. They abandoned the shovel and clawed at the dirt with their bare hands, like dogs digging for a bone. They felt the edges of it. It was wood. A box. It was real.

With a final, heroic effort, they heaved it up out of the shallow grave and onto the grass. It was a small wooden chest, not much bigger than a shoebox, bound with iron straps. It was heavy. It was locked.

They stared at it in the lantern light, their hearts hammering in their chests. They had done it. Against all odds, against all reason, against the smug skepticism of Barty Higgins and the entire sensible world, they had been right. The story was true because they had made it true.

“Open it,” Sam said, his voice husky.

They had no key. Pete tried to pry it open with the shovel, but the iron straps held firm.

“The pickaxe,” Silas said, a note of reverence in his voice. He took the heavy, rusted tool head in his hands. It had been their map. Now it would be their key. It was a holy relic, a tool for all seasons.

He wedged the sharp end of the pickaxe into the seam of the lid and put his weight on it. Sam and Pete added their weight. There was a groan of stressed wood, a screech of tortured metal, and then a loud CRACK as the old lock gave way.

Slowly, reverently, Silas lifted the lid. Sam held the lantern closer. They peered inside.

The box was not full of gold doubloons or glittering jewels. It was full of papers. Old, yellowed papers, covered in a spidery, faded script. And on top of the papers lay a single object: a silver locket, tarnished with age.

Silas picked it up. It was cool and smooth in his palm. He fumbled with the clasp and it sprang open. Inside, there was no miniature portrait, no lock of hair. There was only a tiny, intricate engraving of a single flower: a forget-me-not.

A profound disappointment settled over them. It was a heavier blow than any discovery of mere emptiness would have been. Emptiness was a failure. This was a puzzle, and a distinctly grown-up and uninteresting one at that.

“Letters?” Pete said, his voice full of disgust. “It’s just a box of old letters?” He kicked at the dirt. “There ain’t no treasure.”

Silas was looking at the papers. He picked one up. The handwriting was elegant but difficult to read. He could make out a few words. “My dearest Eleanor…” and “I count the hours…” and “Your eternal…”

It was a box of love letters.

The silence that followed was of a different quality than the earlier, suspenseful silence. This was the silence of utter bafflement. They had set out on a boy’s adventure, a grand and glorious quest for pirate gold, and they had blundered into something else entirely. Something quiet, and sad, and private. It felt like they had broken into a church and found people praying.

“This ain’t Old Man Hemlock’s,” Sam said at last. “Hemlock never loved nobody.”

“Then whose is it?” Pete wondered.

Silas didn’t know, and for the first time, he didn’t feel the urge to invent an answer. He looked around at the three small, forgotten graves. He looked at the box of letters. He looked at the little locket in his hand. A story began to take shape in his mind, but it was not one of his usual epics of battles and glory. It was a smaller, quieter story. A sad story. And for some reason, it felt more real, more important, than any treasure.

“We have to put it back,” he said, his voice low.

Pete stared at him. “Put it back? After all that?”

“We have to,” Silas insisted. He gently placed the locket back on top of the letters and closed the lid of the chest. “This ain’t ours. It ain’t treasure. It’s… it’s secrets. Somebody else’s secrets.”

For a moment, it seemed Pete might argue. His dream of riches and glory was dying hard. But then he looked at Silas’s face, and at Sam’s solemn nod of agreement, and the fight went out of him. He sighed, a great, gusty sigh of deflation.

“All right,” he said. “But I ain’t digging the hole again.”

They put the chest back in its shallow grave and kicked the dirt over it, tamping it down with their feet as best they could. It was a clumsy re-burial, but it was the best they could do.

They walked home in silence, the shovel and the lantern and the pickaxe head seeming heavier than before. The thrill was gone. The grand adventure had ended not with a bang, not with a hoard of gold, but with a box of dusty love letters in a forgotten corner of the cemetery. They had not been defeated by a monster or a rival, but by a feeling they could not quite name.

As they reached the town square, Silas stopped. He was still holding the pickaxe head. He looked at it, this lump of metal that had caused so much trouble and so much hope. It was not a map. It was not a key. It was just a broken tool.

“So what do we tell the boys tomorrow?” Pete asked, his voice weary. “What do we tell Barty Higgins?”

Silas looked from the pickaxe to his two friends, their faces pale and tired in the pre-dawn light. A faint, familiar glimmer began to stir in his eyes. The world of facts was a disappointing place, full of sad love letters and empty of gold. But the world of stories… ah, the world of stories was still rich with possibility.

“We tell them,” Silas said slowly, a grand vision beginning to unfold, “that we found the first clue. That the pickaxe map led us to a false treasure, planted by Hemlock to throw off the unworthy. But in that chest, we found his real map, a secret document that points to the true location.”

Pete’s eyes widened. “And did we?”

“We will have by morning,” Silas said, with a smile that was a thing of pure, undaunted, magnificent fraud. “I feel a spell of artistic creation coming on. Sam, you got a pencil? We’ve got a map to draw.”

And as the first hint of dawn began to pale the eastern sky, three boys huddled together in the town square, their grand adventure a total failure, their treasure a complete bust, and their spirits soaring higher than ever. For they had stumbled upon the greatest truth of all: that the hunt is always better than the catch, and a good lie is a treasure you can spend over and over again, and it is never, ever used up.

They made their way to Silas’s house, which was quiet and dark, his pa being a heavy sleeper and his ma long gone. They did not go inside, but crept around back to the woodshed, a place of sacred privacy and the smell of sawdust and rust. Sam produced a stub of a pencil from the mysterious depths of his pocket, a pocket that was a general store of string, odd-shaped rocks, a dead beetle, and other essentials. Silas took it like a king accepting a scepter.

“Paper,” he commanded. “We need the right kind of paper.”

“I got some foolscap from school,” offered Sam.

“No, no!” said Silas, aghast. “It’s got to look ancient, Sam. Like it’s been buried with a pirate for a hundred years. Foolscap is for sums and writing ‘I will not throw spitballs’ a hundred times. This is a map to riches!”

Pete, who was practical when not swept up in visions of grandeur, had an idea. “The mercantile. Old Man Hemmings wraps pickles in brown paper. It’s got a good color to it. And it smells a bit like brine, which is salty, like the sea. Pirates like the sea.”

This was acknowledged as a stroke of genius. Pete was dispatched, with strict instructions to look natural, and returned twenty minutes later with a large, slightly damp sheet of brown paper he’d procured by buying a single, fat dill pickle, which he now ate with loud, satisfying crunches.

“All right,” Silas said, spreading the paper on a splintery workbench. He was no longer just Silas; he was a cartographer, a historian, a master of deceit. “Now, for the ink.” He looked around the shed. “We can’t use that blue stuff from the bottle. Hemlock the Terrible didn’t shop at the general store.” He mixed a bit of soot from the back of the wood stove with a few drops of water in a clamshell, creating a gritty, unconvincing paste.

“That looks like mud,” Pete observed, his mouth full of pickle.

“It’s aged ink,” Silas corrected with dignity. “It’s supposed to look like that. Now, quiet. I need to feel the pirate’s spirit.”

He closed his eyes for a moment, then began to draw. He did not draw a simple X. That was for amateurs. He drew a jagged coastline that bore a suspicious resemblance to the banks of the Mississippi. He added a skull and crossbones, but his first attempt looked more like a surprised pumpkin, so he scribbled it out and tried again. He drew landmarks from the boys’ own world, but gave them new and terrible names. The old abandoned tannery became “The Bone Yard of the Flayed Man.” Widow McGregor’s well, which was notoriously deep and dark, was labeled “The Weeping Maiden’s Plunge.” A tall, lightning-scarred oak on a distant hill became “Hangman’s Perch.”

“This is getting good,” Pete whispered, pickle forgotten. “Real good.”

Silas added cryptic instructions in a shaky, spidery script. “From the Dragon’s Tooth, thirty paces toward the setting sun on the day the crow flies south.” The Dragon’s Tooth was a particularly jagged rock outcropping in Miller’s Woods. It was all nonsense, but it was magnificent nonsense.

When he was done, they stood back to admire his work. It was a masterpiece of fraud. To make it perfect, Silas carefully lit a splinter and singed the edges of the paper, blowing it out frantically when it caught fire a little too eagerly. He rubbed dirt into its creases. He even dripped a bit of pickle juice on it, at Pete’s suggestion, for that authentic sea-brine aroma.

“There,” Silas said, his voice full of reverent awe. “Hemlock’s true map.”

They spent the rest of the morning rehearsing their story. Every detail had to be perfect. The false chest, the secret compartment, the moment of discovery. They were so thorough in their preparations that by the time the school bell was set to ring, they half-believed the story themselves. The box of sad love letters was a forgotten dream; the glorious new map was the only reality.

The schoolyard was a boiling pot of boyhood, as usual. Yelling, wrestling, trading marbles. Barty Higgins was at the center of the largest group, as he often was. He was a boy with slicked-down hair and a perpetually smug expression, whose father was the town alderman and who therefore considered himself an authority on all matters of importance.

Silas, Pete, and Sam sauntered into the yard, affecting an air of weary nonchalance, as if they had been up all night doing something far more important than sleeping. They stood apart from the main group, and Silas began to clean his fingernails with a stick.

“Tired, Silas?” called Barty, spotting them. “You look like you’ve been wrestling a pig.”

“Busier than that,” Pete said, just as they had rehearsed. He let out a theatrical yawn. “Some of us have got important work to do.”

This was the hook. A dozen boys drifted closer.

“What sort of important work?” Barty sneered. “Delivering newspapers?”

“Something a little more profitable than that,” Pete said, glancing at Silas.

Silas looked up, as if annoyed by the interruption. “It ain’t for public discussion, Pete,” he said sternly. “You know the rules. We’re sworn to secrecy.”

Sworn to secrecy! That was better than yelling it from the rooftops. The crowd of boys was now a thick, jostling circle around them.

“What secret?” demanded Barty. “You ain’t got any secrets. The biggest secret you got is who stole Mrs. Gable’s pie last week, and everybody knows it was you, Pete.”

“This ain’t about pies,” Pete said, his voice low and serious. “It’s about… history.”

Silas sighed, a great, put-upon sigh. “All right, you’ve gone and done it now, Pete. Might as well tell them. They won’t believe it anyway.” He looked at Barty. “You know that pickaxe head? The one with the map on it?”

“The fake map?” Barty scoffed. “Yeah, I know it.”

“It wasn’t fake,” Silas said quietly. “It was a test. It led us to a decoy treasure. A chest full of old rocks and rusty nails, just to throw off the half-hearted. But ol’ Hemlock was clever. He knew a true treasure hunter would look deeper.”

A hush fell over the schoolyard. Even the little boys stopped their marble game to listen.

“And we looked deeper,” Silas continued, his voice taking on a dramatic tremor. “And in a false bottom, hidden under all the junk… we found this.”

With the slow, deliberate motion of a high priest revealing a holy relic, Silas reached into his shirt and pulled out the crumpled, singed, dirt-smeared brown paper. He unfolded it carefully.

A collective gasp went through the crowd. It was a thing of beauty. It looked more like a treasure map than any real treasure map ever could. The pumpkin-skull, the jagged coast, the cryptic names. It was everything a boy’s heart could desire.

Barty Higgins pushed his way to the front, his skepticism warring with a powerful, rising tide of greed and wonder. He stared at the map. “Let me see that.”

Silas held it out, but not so close that Barty could snatch it. Barty’s eyes scanned the drawing, the spidery writing. He was a born doubter, and he was searching for the flaw, the giveaway.

“‘The Bone Yard of the Flayed Man,’” he read aloud, his voice dripping with scorn. “That’s just the old tannery. And ‘The Weeping Maiden’s Plunge’… that’s just a well. You made this up, Silas. This is just pickle paper from the mercantile.”

There was a murmur from the crowd. Barty was a powerful influence. The magnificent lie was trembling on the brink of collapse.

But Silas was ready. This was the moment he had prepared for. He looked Barty straight in the eye. “Did I make up this part, too?” he asked, pointing to a particularly baffling line of script near the bottom of the map. “Read that.”

Barty squinted. “‘Where a hundred suns do flash on shields of silver, look ye to the west.’” He snorted. “What’s that supposed to be? A fairy tale?”

“I don’t know,” Silas said, with a perfect imitation of being stumped. “We couldn’t figure it out. It’s the last clue. We figured it was some kind of pirate talk we didn’t understand. A hundred suns… shields of silver… it don’t make any sense.”

Pete and Sam nodded, looking equally baffled.

Barty stared at the words. He was the alderman’s son; he was supposed to be smarter than these mudlarks. “A hundred suns flashing…” he muttered to himself. He looked up, past the schoolhouse, toward the rooftops of the town. His father, in addition to being alderman, owned the town’s most prosperous tinsmithing shop. Just last month, they had finished putting new tin roofs on a whole row of warehouses down by the river. In the afternoon, when the sun hit them just right, the glare was blinding. A hundred suns flashing on shields of silver.

A slow, dawning comprehension spread across Barty’s face. His scorn melted away, replaced by a look of wild excitement. He had figured it out. He, Barty Higgins, had solved the one clue that had stumped Silas and his gang. The map was real, because only he could have understood its secret.

“The warehouses,” he breathed. “Down by the levee. My pa’s new roofs! That’s it! ‘Look ye to the west!’ The old sycamore stump is west of there! The treasure’s buried by the old sycamore stump!”

He looked up at Silas, his eyes shining. He was no longer a skeptic. He was a believer. He was a partner.

Silas stared back, his face a mask of astonished admiration. “By jings, Barty… I think you’ve got it! We never would have thought of that! The sun on the roofs! You’re a genius!”

The rest of the boys exploded in a torrent of chatter and excitement. Barty was a hero! The map was real! They were all going to be rich! Barty Higgins, now fully converted and appointed chief navigator by unanimous consent, began issuing orders. They would meet after school. They would need more shovels. They would need sacks for the gold.

Silas, Pete, and Sam stood back and watched their creation take on a life of its own. Their little lie had blossomed into a grand public enterprise. It was no longer their adventure; it was a town-wide crusade. Silas had not only saved his reputation, he had elevated it to legendary status. He was no longer just a boy who told tall tales; he was the keeper of secrets, the general of a treasure-hunting army.

But as the bell rang, calling them all to the unwelcome prison of the classroom, and the boys scattered with promises to meet later, a small shadow fell across Silas’s triumph. Old Man Fitzwilliam, who lived in the small house overlooking the cemetery and was known for sitting at his window at all hours, was walking past the schoolyard. He was a withered, sour-faced man who seemed to subsist on disapproval. He paused and fixed his watery, rheumy eyes on Silas.

“You boys were busy in the boneyard last night,” he said, his voice a dry rustle. “Digging. Made quite a mess by the Abernathy plot.”

Silas’s heart gave a great lurch. Pete and Sam froze beside him.

Fitzwilliam’s thin lips twisted into what might have been a smile, or perhaps just a grimace. “Looking for something?” he rasped. “Or burying something?”

He didn’t wait for an answer. He just gave a little nod, as if confirming a private suspicion, and continued on his way, tapping his cane on the dusty path. The boys watched him go, their grand, soaring balloon of a lie suddenly punctured by a tiny, sharp pin of truth. Someone had seen them. Someone knew they had been digging in the cemetery. And he knew they had put the chest *back*.

The excitement of their con, the glory of fooling Barty Higgins, the promise of the afternoon’s grand expedition—it all suddenly seemed terribly fragile. For they had forgotten one of the most important rules of all: that while a good lie is a treasure, a bad secret is a debt, and sooner or later, the bill always comes due. And Old Man Fitzwilliam looked very much like a man who enjoyed being the collector. The afternoon’s adventure was still on, but it was now shadowed by a new and very real peril, which, in its own way, only made the whole business feel even more exciting. For what is a treasure hunt, after all, without the threat of a proper villain?

The bell, a tyrannical clangor that cared nothing for fortunes or villains, summoned them to the chalk-dusted purgatory of Miss Dobbins’s classroom, where the grandest adventure in the world shriveled to the size of a long division problem on a smudged slate. Silas, who only an hour before had been a general marshaling his forces, now found himself a lowly private in the war against the multiplication tables, and a deserter at heart. Every time Miss Dobbins’s hickory switch tapped against her desk, he flinched, seeing not the stern schoolmarm but the tapping cane of Old Man Fitzwilliam on the dusty road. Fitzwilliam’s words, “Looking for something? Or burying something?” echoed in his head louder than the drone of recitation.

Pete, who sat two rows over, was a picture of misery. He was trying to compute how many apples a farmer would have if he had seven barrels with one hundred and nineteen apples each, but his mind was stuck on a far more pressing calculation: the odds of a public flogging versus the odds of being tarred and feathered by a mob of disappointed treasure hunters. Sam, for his part, had given up all pretense of scholarship and was staring out the window, his gaze fixed on the distant, brooding trees of the cemetery hill, as if he could will the freshly turned earth to flatten itself out and swallow their secret whole.

The afternoon crawled by on leaden feet. When at last the final bell rang, it was not the sound of liberation it usually was, but the starting pistol for a race they were not sure they wanted to run. Outside, in the glorious, sun-drenched freedom of the schoolyard, their army was waiting. And it was a larger army than they had bargained for. Word had spread like wildfire on a dry prairie. It seemed every boy in town between the ages of eight and fifteen who was capable of holding a shovel had answered the call. There was Billy Starks with a brand-new spade his father used for digging ditches, its blade gleaming in the sun; there were the Miller twins, who had only a single, rusted trowel between them and were already arguing over who would get to dig first; there was even little Timmy Carter, who had brought a large kitchen spoon and a bucket he used for carrying tadpoles, his face a mask of earnest determination.

And at the head of this motley battalion, pacing back and forth with the map held tight in his hand, was Barty Higgins, chief navigator and newly minted tyrant. The morning’s adulation had transformed him. He was no longer the gullible boy they had tricked; he was a commander, puffed up with the authority of secret knowledge.

“All right, men, listen up!” he barked, striking a pose he had likely seen in a book about Napoleon. “Silence! We are about to embark upon a scientific and historical undertaking of the utmost importance. There will be no lollygagging. There will be no shirking. I have re-verified the calculations. The solar declination, when cross-referenced with the topographical inclination of the Abernathy plot, gives us a precise triangulation point. But speed is of the essence! The shadows are lengthening. Our window of opportunity, gentlemen, is shrinking!”

Silas, Pete, and Sam exchanged a look of pure, unadulterated panic. This was spiraling out of their control with breathtaking speed. They had wanted to fool Barty Higgins; they had not intended to launch a full-scale geological survey conducted by the entire juvenile population of St. Petersburg.

“Ahem,” Silas said, stepping forward and trying to look as if he belonged in charge. “That’s right, boys. What Barty says. But, uh… we need to be organized. We can’t all just go running up there. It’s gotta be secret-like.”

“Exactly!” Barty agreed, pointing a finger at Silas. “Secrecy is paramount! We will proceed in a single file line. No talking. We move like phantoms. Like the very pirates whose treasure we seek to liberate!”

And so the grand procession began. It was the least phantom-like procession in the history of secret missions. Twenty boys, armed with an assortment of digging implements, marching through the center of town, led by a boy waving a stained piece of paper and two others trying desperately to look nonchalant. Mrs. Gable stopped hanging her laundry and stared, her mouth agape. Judge Thatcher, on his afternoon constitutional, paused and raised a quizzical eyebrow. The blacksmith’s hammer fell silent. The entire town, it seemed, had turned out to watch the secret, phantom army march to its destiny.

As they passed the small, grim house on the lane that led to the cemetery, Silas risked a glance at the window. He saw just what he had dreaded: a flicker of movement behind the glass, the pale shape of a face watching them pass. A cold knot formed in his stomach. Old Man Fitzwilliam was watching. He was letting them walk right into their own trap.

They reached the cemetery gates. Barty, with a flair for the dramatic, put a finger to his lips and crept inside, the rest of the boys tiptoeing behind him with a great deal of shuffling and the occasional clang of a shovel hitting a tombstone. They arrived at the spot Barty had identified with his “solar declination.” It was a small patch of unremarkable ground about twenty feet from the Abernathy family plot.

“Here!” Barty whispered, his voice trembling with excitement. He knelt and drove a small twig into the ground. “This is the epicenter. The treasure of One-Eyed Jack lies beneath this very spot! Dig, men! Dig for glory!”

What followed was a frenzy of chaotic excavation. Dirt flew in every direction. The Miller twins immediately got into a fistfight over the trowel. Billy Starks, in his enthusiasm, accidentally flung a shovelful of soil directly onto Barty Higgins’s head, temporarily blinding the chief navigator and causing him to sputter about insubordination. Silas, Pete, and Sam made a show of digging, but their hearts weren’t in it. They merely scraped at the topsoil, their eyes darting about, waiting for the inevitable. How long could they keep this up? An hour? Two? Sooner or later, these boys would realize they were digging a pointless hole, and then the questions would begin. The questions would turn to accusations, and the accusations would turn to a general uprising.

The sun beat down. Sweat poured from the boys’ faces. The initial burst of energy began to wane, replaced by grumbling.

“Say, Barty,” said Billy Starks, leaning on his shovel. “How deep did you reckon this thing is?”

“The manifest didn’t specify depth,” Barty said, wiping dirt from his eyes with an air of annoyance. “It is a variable dependent on soil displacement and tectonic settling over time. Keep digging!”

“I got a blister,” whined one of the Miller twins.

“My spoon is bent,” cried little Timmy Carter, holding up the ruined piece of kitchenware.

Silas saw his chance. Maybe he could use this. He could declare that they had the wrong tools, that they needed pickaxes, or perhaps a special pirate-blessed shovel. He was just opening his mouth to propose this brilliant new lie when a voice, dry as autumn leaves, cut through the clamor.

“Quite an industrious group of young men.”

Every boy froze. They turned as one. There, leaning on his gnarled cane not ten feet away, stood Old Man Fitzwilliam. He had approached as silently as a cat. His watery eyes surveyed the scene, the piles of dirt, the sweating boys, the general mayhem desecrating the peace of the boneyard.

“Digging for worms?” he asked, a thin, cruel smile playing on his lips. “The fishing must be very good today.”

The boys were silent, intimidated. Fitzwilliam’s gaze swept over them and then landed, with pinpoint accuracy, on Silas, Pete, and Sam. The smile vanished.

“You three,” he said, his voice dropping to a low command. “The leaders of this little expedition. A word with you. Over here. The rest of you… carry on with your important work.”

The other boys, relieved to not be the center of the old man’s attention, looked at Silas with a new respect. The mysterious old man wanted to confer with their leaders! Perhaps he had some ancient knowledge of the treasure!

Silas’s blood ran cold. He, Pete, and Sam walked over to Fitzwilliam as if they were walking to the gallows. The old man led them behind a large, weeping angel monument, out of sight of the others.

He didn’t waste any time. “A fine mess you’ve made,” he rasped, gesturing with his cane back towards the digging. “And for nothing. They spent the rest of the afternoon exploring the river in their new craft, paddling into coves and around sandbars, feeling the power of the current under them. They were no longer marooned; they were free. The island was not their prison but their fortress, their naval base from which they could launch expeditions into the vast, watery world.

As evening drew on, they paddled back to their landing-place and pulled the skiff safely up on shore. They were tired, sunburned, and streaked with pine pitch and fish scales, but they were triumphant. They built up the fire, and as the stars came out, they sat before it, full of a deep and quiet pride that was entirely different from the loud boasts of the night before. The adventure of the pipes had been a humiliating failure of pretense. The adventure of the skiff was a solid, tangible success. They had faced a real problem with their own hands and wits, and they had won.

The moon rose, silvering the river. A steamboat, a great palace of light and noise, churned its way up the distant channel, its paddle-wheels beating a rhythmic pulse against the night’s silence. The boys watched it go, feeling not envy, but a kind of serene superiority. The people on that boat were mere passengers, bound for dull places like St. Louis or St. Petersburg. But they, they were explorers. For they were not merely boys playing at being pirates; they were masters of a shipwrecked boat, and rulers of an island kingdom, and the great, wide, silent river was their own.

“Say, Tom,” said Joe, after a long and comfortable silence, “that steamboat was a fine sight.”

“She was a beauty,” Tom agreed. “All lit up like a Christmas tree. But I wouldn’t trade. Not for all the pilot-houses on the river.”

“And the wind,” put in Huck, looking up at the sky where a few wisps of cloud drifted across the moon.

“Well, the wind, yes,” said Tom, magnanimously. “A pirate’s got to respect the elements. But that’s a free man’s bargain. It ain’t like being told to fetch wood by your Aunt Polly.”

A general sense of satisfaction settled over the camp. The night’s terrors were now a source of immense pride, the storm a worthy adversary they had outwitted. And the skiff, their ‘Storm-King’s Prize,’ lay beached nearby, a solid testament to their good fortune. It changed everything. Before, they were simply runaways on an island; now, they were a naval power.

The next morning they awoke to a world washed clean. The air was fresh and smelled of damp earth and pine. The river, which had been a churning monster, was now a placid, muddy giant, carrying a freight of logs, branches, and other debris from upcountry. The boys ate a breakfast of fried bacon and corn-pone with the hearty appetites of true adventurers.

“Now then, buccaneers,” said Tom, wiping his mouth on his sleeve, “we’ve got a navy. A navy needs a mission. What’ll it be?”

“We could go explorin’ downriver,” said Joe. “See if there’s any other islands to claim for our kingdom.”

“Or we could fish,” said Huck. “The river’s up. Catfish’ll be feedin’ in the shallows.”

Tom considered. Exploration was grand, but provisioning the ship was also a proper piratical duty. “Huck’s right,” he declared. “A crew can’t fight on an empty stomach. We’ll go fishing. We’ll load the ‘Prize’ with enough fish to feed an army, and then we’ll see about expanding our empire. To the boat, men!”

They bailed the last of the rainwater out of the skiff, gathered their fishing lines and hooks, and pushed off into the current. The river was a different creature by day. It was vast and powerful, its surface a swirling brown canvas painted with eddies and boils. The current took them in its grip at once, and they were swept downstream faster than they could have paddled.

“Jings, she moves!” cried Joe, his eyes wide.

“Just you mind the steering, Huck,” said Tom, who had taken the commander’s post in the bow. “Keep her clear of them snags.”

Huck, who knew the river’s moods better than books, needed no such instruction. He kept the skiff’s stern angled against the current, using a battered board for a rudder, and expertly guided them around the half-submerged trees and treacherous collections of driftwood that the flood had dislodged. They found a likely looking spot where a small creek emptied into the main channel, creating a slack-water eddy that was rich with promise. They dropped their lines, baited with bits of bacon, and waited.

They did not have to wait long. Huck, with a practiced twitch, pulled in a struggling channel cat of a respectable size. Then Joe, with a wild whoop, hauled in another. Tom, after a few frustrating nibbles, finally landed one himself, and felt his authority as captain swell within him. For an hour they had glorious sport, pulling in fish after fish until the bottom of the skiff was slick and silvery with their catch. They were magnificent providers; they were masters of the river’s bounty.

“There,” said Tom, when the bite began to slow. “That’s enough for a week of Fridays. Now for some exploration.”

They decided to paddle across the main channel and investigate the farther shore, a wild-looking stretch of bottom-land thick with cottonwoods and willows. Getting there was another matter. The current in the middle of the river was a different beast entirely, a strong, silent muscle that seemed determined to carry them straight to New Orleans. They bent their backs to the paddles, two rough planks they had found, and strained with all their might.

“Pull, Joe, pull!” gasped Tom. “She’s takin’ us!”

“I am pullin’!” Joe grunted, his face red with exertion. “It’s like tryin’ to paddle up a waterfall.”

Huck, in the stern, used his board to keep them from being turned broadside, his brow furrowed in concentration. For every ten feet they gained toward the shore, the river stole twenty feet from them downstream. They were sweating and panting, their glorious morning turning into hard labor. The romance of being a naval power was giving way to the simple, brute fact of the river’s strength.

After a long struggle, they finally broke free of the strongest current and slipped into the calmer water near a long, low sandbar that had been exposed by the shifting waters. They collapsed, breathing heavily, their hands sore and their backs aching.

“Whew,” said Joe. “That was work. Real work.”

“Of course it was work,” said Tom, recovering his breath and his dignity. “Navigatin’ a great river ain’t child’s play. It takes grit.”

As they drifted along the edge of the sandbar, catching their wind, Huck suddenly sat up straighter. “Looky there,” he said, pointing.

A hundred yards down the sandbar, there was a dark shape. It was lumpy and still.

“What is it?” whispered Joe. “A log?”

“No,” said Tom, his eyes shining with a new and thrilling possibility. “It’s a man. It’s a body!”

The word sent a delicious shiver through them. A body! A drowned man, cast up by the storm. It was exactly the sort of grim and fascinating discovery that befitted a pirate crew.

“Maybe he was on a raft that got broke up,” said Joe. “Maybe he’s got money in his pockets!”

“Maybe he was a smuggler, and his own gang killed him for the loot!” said Tom, his imagination already taking flight. “They probably threw him overboard in the storm, and we’ve found the evidence. There’s a mystery here, boys!”

They paddled closer, their previous exhaustion forgotten. The excitement was palpable. As they drew near, however, the shape stirred. It raised a weary head and looked at them. It was not a body after all; it was a living man.

Tom was not in the least disappointed. A living man was even better. “It’s a marooned sailor!” he hissed. “Like Robinson Crusoe! Quiet, now. He might be dangerous.”

They let the skiff ground itself softly on the sand and crept forward, their hearts thumping. Tom wished he had his sword, or at least a pistol. The man was a gaunt fellow with a beard of a week’s despair and clothes that were little better than rags. He sat with his head in his hands, staring at the water with a look of utter dejection. He did not look dangerous at all; he looked miserable.

Tom cleared his throat and stepped forward, assuming his most commanding posture. “Ahoy, there!” he called out.

The man looked up, his eyes dull and lifeless. He seemed not at all surprised to see three boys on a sandbar in the middle of the Mississippi.

“Who be ye?” Tom demanded, trying to sound like a pirate captain in a storybook.

The man just sighed. “You boys got a boat?” he asked, his voice a dry rasp.

“We do,” said Tom, puffing out his chest. “This here is the ‘Storm-King’s Prize,’ and we are her crew. And who, I say, might _you_ be? A shipwrecked mariner? An outlaw fleeing justice?”

The man stared at Tom as if he were speaking a foreign language. “Name’s Silas. I ain’t no mariner. I was workin’ a wood-flat down to Sellers’ Landing. Fell asleep on my skiff last night, and the current took me. Woke up this mornin’ and my painter line had snapped. Skiff drifted off. I managed to swim to this here sandbar. Been here ever since.” He gestured vaguely downstream. “Skiff’s probably halfway to Cairo by now.”

This was a dreadful disappointment. There was no mystery, no smugglers, no desperate combat. Just a clumsy fellow who had lost his boat. Joe looked crestfallen. Tom, however, was not so easily defeated. If the man was not a romantic figure, then Tom would make him one. He was a victim, and they would be his rescuers.

“A castaway!” Tom declared. “A victim of the tempest! Fear not, my good man. You have been found by the Sea-Wolves of the Mississippi. We shall grant you succor.”

Silas blinked. “You got any grub?” he asked.

This was a more practical matter. The boys looked at each other. They had their magnificent catch of fish.

“We have provisions,” said Tom grandly. “Huck, Joe, fetch the prisoner… I mean, the castaway… a portion of our stores.”

Huck and Joe went back to the boat and returned with two of the biggest catfish. Silas’s eyes lit up with a spark of life for the first time. He took the fish without a word, broke one open with his bare hands, and began to eat it raw. The boys watched, horrified and fascinated.

“Good land!” whispered Joe. “He’s eatin’ it raw!”

Huck was less surprised. He had seen hungry men before. “He’s just hungry, I reckon,” he said quietly.

Tom felt a pang of something he could not name. It was a mix of pity and a dawning, uncomfortable understanding. Their own ‘adventures’ with hunger had been a game; this man’s hunger was real and ugly.

“Here,” Tom said, his grand manner slipping a little. He rummaged in his pocket and produced a slab of their corn-pone, a little squashed and linty. “You can have this, too.”

Silas devoured the corn-pone in three bites. He wiped his mouth on his tattered sleeve and looked at the boys. A little color had returned to his face. “Much obliged,” he said. “I ain’t eat since yesterday noon.”

“We will do more than feed you,” Tom announced, his confidence returning. “We will rescue you. We will convey you to a place of safety. Where were you bound?”

“Wasn’t bound nowhere particular. Just followin’ the river. Lookin’ for work. A man with a strong back can usually find work at a wood-yard, loadin’ fuel for the steamboats.”

“A wood-yard!” said Tom. “Perfect! There’s one down to Williams’ Point, ain’t there, Huck? Not more’n two or three mile.”

Huck nodded. “’Bout that.”

“Then it is settled!” said Tom. “Embark, my good man. The ‘Storm-King’s Prize’ will carry you to your destination.”

So the gaunt man, Silas, climbed stiffly into their skiff. He was a heavy, awkward passenger, and the little boat sat much lower in the water. The boys took up their paddles again, and with Silas giving them quiet directions about the currents, they set off downstream.

The mood was different now. The presence of the stranger, this piece of real, unromantic river life, had punctured their fantasy. Tom tried to keep it alive.

“You’ve had a narrow escape, Silas,” he said. “That storm was a terror. We weathered it on our island fortress.”

“Was it?” Silas said, looking at the sky. “I slept right through it. Nothin’ wakes me up once I’m out.”

Another disappointment. He had not even been awake for the most dramatic part.

“The river’s a treacherous thing,” Tom tried again. “Full of perils for the unwary.”

“She is that,” Silas agreed, his eyes on the water. “But she’s a provider, too. She gives you fish, she gives you passage. You just gotta know her ways. See that riffle over yonder? That’s a sunken log, a sawyer. It’ll dip and rise, and it can stave a boat in. You give it a wide berth.” He pointed with a bony finger, and Huck, at the rudder, obediently steered them clear.

The boys fell silent, paddling and listening. Silas spoke little, but when he did, it was about the river. Not about pirates or treasure, but about the way the channel shifted from year to year, about the signs that foretold a falling or rising water level, about the different kinds of snags and sandbars. It was a language the river itself spoke, and Huck, for his part, listened with an attention he never gave to sermons or school lessons.

In less than an hour, they saw the wood-yard ahead: a raw clearing in the forest, with huge stacks of cordwood piled high, and a rickety pier jutting out into the water. A thin plume of smoke rose from a shanty set back in the trees.

They brought the skiff alongside the pier. Silas climbed out, his joints cracking. He stood on the solid wood and looked down at the three boys in their boat.

“Well, boys,” he said, “I’m obliged to you. Truly.” He fumbled in his pocket and brought out a small, intricately carved wooden bird. “Ain’t got nothin’ to pay you with. I whittle sometimes. You can have this.”

He handed it to Tom. Tom took it, surprised. It was a perfect little kingfisher, every feather detailed. It was a beautiful thing.

“Thank you,” Tom said, for once at a loss for a grand speech.

“You boys… you’re all right,” Silas said. He gave them a small, tired smile, then turned and walked up the bank toward the shanty without looking back.

The boys pushed off from the pier and let the current take them for a moment. They watched the man disappear into the trees. The grand rescue was over. It had been quiet and simple, and had ended not with a flourish of trumpets, but with a small wooden bird.

“Well,” said Joe, finally. “We done it. We saved him.”

“We did,” said Tom, turning the little bird over in his hand.

The paddle back to the island was long and hard. They had to fight the current the whole way, staying close to the shore and taking advantage of every eddy. The sun was getting low, and their arms and backs, already sore, began to ache with a deep and genuine weariness. There was not much talk. The day’s adventure had been a success, but it felt different from their other triumphs. It was solid and real. They had not pretended to be heroes; they had simply helped a man who needed it.

When they finally beached the skiff on their own shore, the island felt more like a home than ever. It was a refuge not just from civilization, but from the hard realities of the world they had just touched. They cleaned their remaining fish, built up the fire, and cooked their supper.

As darkness fell and the stars came out, they sat staring into the flames. The boastful energy of the previous nights was gone. In its place was a quiet, shared contentment.

“That man,” Joe said after a while, “he was awful hungry.”

“Yes, he was,” said Tom. He had placed the little wooden kingfisher on a log near the fire, where it seemed to watch them with its tiny, carved eyes. “He was just… down on his luck.”

Huck, who had been silent all evening, spoke up. “There’s lots of folks like that,” he said, his voice low. “The river takes their boats, or the work runs out. They just keep movin’.”

Tom and Joe had nothing to say to that. They knew about being poor, of course, but Huck knew it in a different way. He knew it like Silas knew the currents.

They sat for a long time, the only sounds the crackle of the fire and the ceaseless murmur of the great river. They had set out that morning as pirates and explorers, seeking glory and make-believe. They had returned as something else, something they did not have a name for. They had used their prize, their boat, not for a game, but for a piece of work. They had measured themselves against the real river, and against a small piece of the world’s real trouble, and had not been found wanting. The river was still their kingdom, but they knew now that it held more than just islands and storms; it held men like Silas, and stories that were not in any of the books. And somehow, this knowledge made their island kingdom feel larger, and more their own, than ever before.

“What are we, then?” asked Joe, his voice full of honest curiosity.

Tom thought a good long while. He wanted a name with thunder in it, something from a book, like “The Knights of the Bleeding Hand” or “The Avengers of the Mississippi.” But none of those seemed to fit the day’s quiet work. They had not avenged anything; they had just given a hungry man some fish and a ride.

“We’re… we’re guardians,” he said finally, the word tasting strange and important on his tongue. “Guardians of the River.”

“Guardians,” Joe repeated, trying it out. “By jings, that sounds mighty fine, Tom. Guardians of the River.”

Huck said nothing, but he took the pipe from his mouth and looked out at the black, moving water. The river did not need guardians. It was its own master. But he understood what Tom meant. They were part of it now, in a way they hadn’t been before.

It was Huck who heard it first. His was an ear tuned to the woods and the water, an ear that could tell the difference between a twig snapped by a squirrel and one snapped by a foot. He sat up straight, his head cocked. The others saw the motion and fell silent.

“What is it?” whispered Tom.

Huck held up a hand. For a minute there was nothing but the crackle of the fire and the river’s endless song. Then it came again, a sound so faint it was more a feeling in the bones than a noise in the ear. A dull, rhythmic knock. An oar, clumsy in its oarlock, on the far side of the island.

The boys looked at each other, their eyes wide in the firelight. Visitors? At this hour? No one ever came to Jackson’s Island. That was the glory of it.

“Who do you reckon it is?” Joe breathed, his face losing its color.

“Maybe it’s Indians,” said Tom, his mind immediately leaping to the most romantic possibility. But his voice lacked its usual bold confidence. It was a thin, hopeful whisper.

Huck was already moving. With a single, silent motion he kicked sand over the heart of their fire, smothering the bright flames into a cloud of hissing steam and a dull, angry red glow. “Get down,” he hissed, and the other two dropped to the sand beside him without a second thought.

The knocking sound had stopped. It was replaced by a heavy scraping, the sound of a boat’s keel being dragged up onto a gravelly shore. Then came voices. They were low and rough, not the voices of any boys they knew. They were men’s voices, thick with grievance.

“—told you to watch that current, you flat-headed fool,” one voice grumbled, a sound like rocks grinding together.

“Well, how was I to know it’d sweep so hard ‘round this bend?” a second voice whined back. “My arms is nigh pulled from their sockets.”

“Quit your blubberin’ and help me with the chest. It ain’t goin’ to walk by itself.”

The boys lay frozen in the sudden darkness. Their hearts hammered against their ribs. Chest? This was not a fishing party. This was not a group of picnickers from the village. The word hung in the air, freighted with all the meaning the boys’ imaginations could pour into it. Treasure. But this was not the make-believe treasure of their games. It came with voices that held no romance, only a hard, weary menace.

Huck put his lips right to Tom’s ear. “They’re on the north shore. Can’t see our skiff from there. But if they come over the spine of the island, they’ll see our camp.”

Tom’s mind, which a moment before had been a palace of grand titles, was now a whirlwind of pure, cold terror. All the stories he had ever read about robbers and murderers came rushing back, not as thrilling adventures, but as stark and terrible warnings. His magnificent plan of being a “Guardian of the River” seemed a pale and foolish thing in the face of two grown men with a mysterious chest and voices like snarling dogs.

“What’ll we do, Huck?” he whispered, the general of the army turning to his scout.

“Stay here. Don’t move a muscle,” Huck whispered back. “I’m goin’ to see.”

Before Tom or Joe could protest, Huck had melted into the darkness. He did not run or even crawl; he simply disappeared, flowing into the underbrush as if he were made of the same shadow and silence. Joe grabbed Tom’s arm, his hand trembling. Tom, for his part, was glad for the contact. They lay there, two self-proclaimed pirates and guardians, reduced to small, frightened boys, listening to the thumping of their own blood in their ears. The woods around them, their familiar kingdom, now seemed alien and filled with unseen threats. Every rustle of a leaf was a footstep. Every hoot of a distant owl was a signal. They strained to hear some sound from the other side of the island, but there was only the vast, indifferent quiet of the night. It seemed an age they lay there, scarcely daring to breathe.

Huck moved through the woods with the skill of a fox. He kept to the deepest shadows, his bare feet making no sound on the moss and damp earth. He could feel the presence of the men before he could see them, a disturbance in the island’s natural rhythm. He came to the edge of a thicket of pawpaw trees on the low ridge that formed the island’s backbone and peered through the leaves.

Down on the narrow northern beach, two men were hauling a heavy, iron-bound chest up from their boat, a crude-looking johnboat. One man was huge, a great bear of a man with a tangled black beard and a missing ear. The other was scrawny and rat-faced, and he moved with a nervous, twitching energy. They grunted and cursed as they dragged the box into the shelter of a large cottonwood.

“There,” said the big one, whose name was Abner, as he dropped his end with a thud. “Let it sit. We’ll divvy it in the mornin’ when there’s light to see.”

“We ought to count it now, Abner,” the scrawny one, Cale, whined. “How do we know you ain’t gonna try and cheat me in the light?”

Abner turned on him with a slow, deliberate motion. “You sayin’ I’d cheat you, Cale?” he rumbled, his voice dangerously low.

“No, no, Abner, I ain’t sayin’ that. Just… it’s better to be sure. That ferry clerk, he said there was over nine hundred dollars. We ought to be sure it’s all there.”

“The clerk also said he’d have the law on us before sunup if he got loose,” Abner growled. “We’re lucky we got away clean. This island’s as good a place as any to lay low for a day or two. Now shut your trap. I’m goin’ to get some sleep.”

Abner settled himself down with his back against the cottonwood, pulling his hat over his eyes. Cale paced back and forth for a few minutes, glancing nervously at the dark woods and the darker river, before finally settling himself near the chest, as if to guard it. Huck had seen enough. Nine hundred dollars. These were real robbers. They had robbed a ferry. He backed away as silently as he had come, his mind racing. This was a far more serious business than a stolen ham.

When he slipped back into camp, he found Tom and Joe huddled together, looking like two lost children. He told them what he had seen and heard, his whisper quick and urgent.

Joe’s face, in the faint starlight, was a white mask of fear. “Nine hundred dollars!” he breathed. “Tom, they’re murderers, I just know it!”

“They didn’t say nothin’ ’bout killin’ nobody,” Huck corrected him, though it was small comfort. “Just tied up a clerk.”

“It’s the same thing!” Joe insisted. “They’re desperate characters, Tom! They’ll kill us if they find us!”

Tom was silent. The terror was still there, a cold knot in his stomach, but something else was beginning to stir: the old, familiar thrill of high drama. This was a situation straight out of “The Scottish Chiefs” or “The Gallant Buccaneer.” They were not just boys playing a game; they were shipwrecked mariners trapped on an island with a band of cutthroats. The general was beginning to think again.

“Our skiff,” he said, his voice low and calculating. “They can’t see it from their side. But if we try to get to it, we have to go along the beach. They’d hear us.”

“And if they stay a day or two,” Huck added, “they’re bound to go explorin’. They’ll find our camp. They’ll find our boat. Then they’ll know we’re here.”

“They’ll take our skiff and leave us here to starve!” said Joe, his imagination painting the most dismal picture possible. “Or worse!”

They fell into a gloomy silence. The situation was dire. They were three boys, armed with nothing but fishing hooks and a pocketknife or two, trapped on an island with two grown, dangerous men. To fight was madness. To flee was impossible. To wait was to be discovered.

Tom lay on his back, staring up at the stars that glittered between the black leaves. His mind was working furiously. He was no longer thinking of books; he was thinking of the island itself. He thought of the deep, spooky woods, the strange noises the wind made in the pines, the way shadows could play tricks on a man’s eyes at night. He thought of the superstitions of ignorant men, men like these robbers were likely to be. An idea, vague at first, then brilliant, then absolutely magnificent, began to form in his head. He sat up, his eyes gleaming.

“Boys,” he said, his voice filled with a sudden, electric excitement. “We can’t fight them. And we can’t run.”

“Then what’re we gonna do, Tom?” asked Joe miserably. “Just let ’em catch us?”

“No,” said Tom, a grin spreading across his face. “We ain’t goin’ to do anything to ’em. We’re going to let the island do it.”

Huck and Joe stared at him.

“This here island,” Tom went on, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper, “is a haunted place. Everybody knows it. Folks have seen ghosts over here for fifty years.”

“But we ain’t seen any ghosts,” said Joe, confused.

“They don’t know that,” Tom said, tapping his forehead. “They’re robbers. They’re superstitious. We’re not going to fight them. We’re going to haunt them right off this island.”

A slow grin began to dawn on Joe’s face as he caught the sublime genius of the plan. “By jings, Tom! A haunting!”

Huck was more practical. “How’re we gonna haunt ’em? We ain’t got chains to rattle.”

“We don’t need chains!” Tom said, his enthusiasm growing by the second. He was in his element now; this was generalship of the highest order. “We’ve got the whole woods! Huck, can you make that sound like a panther screamin’?”

“I reckon,” said Huck.

“And can you hoot like a screech-owl, the kind that means somebody’s going to die?”

“That’s easy.”

“Good. Joe, you and me, we’ll be the spirits. We’ll make moanin’ sounds. We’ll groan. We’ll snap twigs just behind ’em in the dark. We’ll work on their nerves. We’ll make ’em think this island is crawlin’ with vengeful spirits, maybe the spirit of that ferry clerk they tied up!”

The sheer audacity of it was intoxicating. Joe was practically bouncing with excitement. “Oh, it’s gay! It’s the gayest plan you ever had, Tom!”

Huck, for his part, saw the sense in it. It was a plan that used their strengths: his woodcraft and Tom’s theatrical imagination. It was certainly better than any other option they had.

“They’re asleep now,” Tom whispered, taking command. “We’ll wait an hour, till they’re sleeping sound. Then we start. Huck, you take the ridge, high up, so the sound carries. Joe and me will sneak down closer, on the west side of their camp. We’ll come at ’em from two sides. Don’t do nothin’ till you hear me give the signal. A soft whistle, like a whippoorwill.”

They settled down to wait, the terror of a few minutes ago replaced by a thrilling, high-strung anticipation. This was better than pirating. This was better than finding treasure. This was defending their kingdom, their home, with the only weapons they truly possessed: cunning and boyish bravado.

An hour later, which felt like a year, Tom gave a soft, quavering whistle. From the high ridge above, a moment later, came an answer: the blood-chilling scream of a panther, a sound that tore through the quiet of the night and seemed to curdle the very air.

Down on the beach, the big man, Abner, sat bolt upright. “What in thunder was that?” he growled.

Cale was already on his feet, his eyes darting toward the black wall of the woods. “That was a panther, Abner! I told you this place warn’t safe!”

“Panthers don’t come on islands,” Abner grunted, though he did not lie back down. He peered into the darkness, listening.

A long, deathly silence followed. Then, from the woods to their left, came a low, heartbreaking moan. It seemed to rise right out of the damp earth, a sound of pure misery. It was Tom, his head buried in a pile of leaves to muffle the sound.

Cale let out a small yelp. “What’s that? Abner, what’s that sound?”

“It’s just the wind,” Abner said, but his voice lacked conviction. He reached for a heavy branch to use as a club.

Now, from the same direction as the moan, came a sharp *crack*. A twig snapping, as if under a heavy, unseen foot. Joe Harper’s contribution.

Cale was shivering now. “The wind don’t snap branches, Abner. There’s somethin’ in there. I can feel it.”

“Shut up, you yeller-bellied coward,” Abner snarled, but he, too, was now standing, staring into the impenetrable gloom.

Then came Huck’s masterstroke. From high on the ridge, the hoot of a screech-owl echoed through the trees. It was not a normal hoot; it was a quavering, sickly call, the kind river-men and woodsmen swore was a harbinger of death. It hooted once, twice, and then a third time.

Cale made a choking sound. “Three times! Oh, lord, Abner, that’s a death sign! It means one of us is gonna die tonight! On this cursed island!”

“There ain’t no such thing as a death sign!” Abner roared, but his voice was a little too loud, a little too shaky. He was brandishing his club at the silent trees. “Whoever’s out there, show yourself! I’ll bash your head in!”

Tom had tied a long piece of grapevine to the bush earlier and was now pulling it from his hiding place.

Abner stood his ground for another moment, a great, defiant silhouette against the star-dusted river. He was a hard man, not easily frightened. But the panther scream, the moans, the death-owl, the rustling of an unseen presence—it was all working on him. The woods were black and full of hostile sound. The island felt alive, and it wanted them gone. He gave one last, hateful look at the forest.

They threw the chest aboard with a crash, shoved the boat into the water, and leaped in. The boys lay hidden, their hearts thundering with triumph, and listened as the frantic splashing of the oars grew fainter and fainter, until at last it was swallowed up by the river’s murmur. For a long time, no one moved. The island was theirs again. The silence that returned was not threatening; it was peaceful. It was the silence of victory.

Finally, Tom stood up, covered in leaves and dirt, and let out a whoop of pure, unadulterated joy. “Huzzah! Huzzah for the Guardians! We done it, boys! We drove ’em off!”

The island was quiet, the river shimmered in the late morning sun, and the town of St. Petersburg, across the wide water, seemed a thousand miles away, a place of tedious rules and tiresome duties that had nothing to do with them.

Boom. The sound rolled over the water, heavy and sad.

They were the ones who were missing. Their raft had been found, empty and adrift. The whole town, the entire world as they knew it, thought they were at the bottom of the Mississippi River. The cannon was for them. The town was in mourning for them.

The initial shock was a cold, dreadful thing. To be thought dead was a sobering and fearful idea. Joe’s lip began to tremble. “My mother…” he whispered. “Oh, Tom, she’ll be… she’ll think I’m…” He couldn’t finish. He buried his face in his hands and his shoulders began to shake.

But Tom Sawyer was made of different stuff. The first wave of fear passed over him and was replaced by a feeling so grand, so magnificent, that it almost took his breath away. This was not tragedy; this was glory of the highest order. It was one thing to be a pirate, feared and admired from a distance. He stood up, his eyes shining.

The sheer, audacious brilliance of it began to dawn on the other two. To return from the dead! It was a feat beyond pirates, beyond robbers, beyond even clowns in a circus.

This was an adventure that dwarfed all their previous ones. Joe’s tears dried up, his homesickness forgotten in the face of such a thrilling enterprise. Huck was captivated. The plan was so bold it was beautiful.

The rest of the day was spent in a fever of anticipation. They talked of nothing else. They imagined the scenes in town: the tearful conversations, the schoolmaster canceling lessons, the boys who had once been their rivals now speaking of them with hushed reverence. They were legends.

This was a masterpiece of invention, and it broke the congregation’s last reserve of composure. The entire church dissolved into a chorus of weeping. Men who had not cried in twenty years wiped their eyes without shame. The ladies’ handkerchiefs were a flutter of white flags surrendering to grief. It was a perfect storm of sorrow.

And through it all, Tom Sawyer’s mind was working. This was it. This was the moment. The suffering was at its absolute peak. Any more, and it might curdle into exhaustion. It was now or never.

He squeezed Joe’s shoulder. “Now,” he hissed. “Get ready.”

He gave Huck a nod. Huck just stared back, his face unreadable.

The minister raised his hands for the final benediction over the departed souls. The organist began to play a soft, mournful dirge. The weeping of the congregation was the only other sound.

Tom stood up. Joe, his face streaked with dust and tears, staggered to his feet beside him. Huck rose last, silent as always.

For a moment, they just stood there, three dusty, disheveled, and very much alive figures outlined against the light of the gallery window.

Down below, a deacon who happened to glance up let his jaw fall open. He nudged his wife. She looked up, and a small, strangled scream escaped her. Her neighbor turned, and then another, and another. A wave of rustling and murmuring spread from the back of the church to the front, a ripple of disbelief turning into a tide of astonishment. Heads turned, faces lifted, and a hundred pairs of eyes focused on the gallery.

The Reverend Hobson, his hands still raised in blessing, paused, confused by the sudden disturbance. He followed the gaze of his flock, looked up, and his mouth fell open, his prepared words forgotten. The organist, distracted, hit a loud, discordant note that shattered the mournful atmosphere like a pane of glass.

And into that shocked, ringing silence, Tom Sawyer took his first step toward the stairs, a ragged, triumphant, and terrified king about to reclaim his kingdom.
