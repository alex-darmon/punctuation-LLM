The two boys, Jeb and Simon, regarded the hornet’s nest with a mixture of terror and ambition. It hung from the low branch of the crabapple tree like a gray, papery heart, pulsing with a low and sinister hum. These were boys who had faced down snapping turtles and chased stray dogs from one end of town to the other; but hornets were a different sort of proposition, a foe with a thousand tiny swords and a collective fury.

“It ain’t so big,” said Jeb, kicking at a loose stone. He was the elder by a year and felt the pressure of leadership. “I bet we could knock it down with one good throw.”

“One throw!” said Simon. “Why, Petey Willis wouldn’t even look at it. He’d run clear to the county line if he saw a thing like that.”

“Petey Willis! He’s scared of his own shadow if it moves too fast,” Jeb scoffed. “I was thinking, we could get a long pole. Just give it a poke. They wouldn’t even know what hit ‘em.”

“That’s the ticket, Jeb! You’ve heard me say that, haven’t you? Just the other day, down by the livery, I said a long pole is the answer to most of life’s troubles. Didn’t I say that?”

“You did,” Jeb affirmed, though he had no such recollection. “Scores of times. Well, I’ve thought it a hundred times more. What’s the use of a hornet’s nest if a fella can’t poke it? There ain’t no use at all.”

“None whatsoever,” Simon agreed. “Say, when we do it, and all the fellas hear about it, won’t they be green? We’ll just be walking by, and I’ll say, kind of casual, ‘Jeb, you recollect that hornet’s nest we conquered?’ And you’ll say, ‘Which one, Si? There’s been so many.’ Oh, that’ll be rich!”

“By jings, it will! Let’s do it now!”

So they procured a fallen branch, a lengthy poplar switch that was flimsy at the end but promised a good ten feet of safety. Jeb, as the instigator, took the lead. He crept forward, his bare feet making no sound in the dusty grass. Simon stood well back, his heart thumping a nervous rhythm against his ribs. Jeb raised the pole, its tip wavering like a divining rod. He took a deep breath, jabbed forward, and made a solid connection.

For a moment, nothing happened. The gray heart simply shuddered on its stem. “See?” Jeb whispered, a triumphant grin starting to form. “It’s just as I…”

He never finished. The nest did not disgorge its occupants; it exploded. A black, furious cloud erupted with a sound like tearing fabric, a high, electric buzzing that seemed to vibrate in the boys’ very teeth. Jeb dropped the pole and turned to flee, but he was not quick enough. The first fiery sting landed on his neck, another on his ear. He yelped, a sound of pure, unmanly panic, and slapped at himself as he ran. Simon, seeing the cloud divert its attention toward him, let out a shriek of his own and took off in the opposite direction, his legs pumping with the kind of speed born only of mortal terror.

They did not stop until they reached the cool, muddy banks of Miller’s Creek, plunging their heads and necks into the murky water. The fire of the stings was a dull, throbbing agony. Jeb had three welts on his neck and one that was puffing his eyelid into a monstrous slit. Simon had fared better, with only two on his arm, but he was pale and trembling.

“I reckon,” Simon panted, his voice shaky, “that we stirred ‘em up a bit more than we planned.”

“We did stir ‘em,” Jeb said, trying to regain some shred of dignity. His lip quivered. “It was a bigger nest than it looked. A real king nest. The regular kind wouldn’t have put up such a fight.”

“No, they wouldn’t. Not in a million years,” Simon agreed readily. “We was lucky to get away. I bet Billy Simpson would have been done for. They’d have carried him off!”

“They would have. Say, don’t you say a word of this. We’ll just let it lie. If anyone asks about my eye, I’ll say I ran into a door.”

“A door,” Simon repeated, nodding gravely. “That’s a good one.”

They sat in silence for a time, the heroic adventure having curdled into a miserable ache. They were nursing their wounds and their pride when a new sound reached them; not the buzz of hornets, but the low rumble of an approaching wagon and the sharp, angry voice of a man.

“Drat these confounded beasts! Pull, you lazy brute, pull!”

The boys peered through the willows. It was old Farmer Pritchard, his wagon hopelessly mired in the soft mud where the lane dipped toward the creek. One of the wheels was sunk nearly to the axle, and his ancient horse, looking profoundly weary of the whole affair, refused to budge.

Mr. Pritchard was a man known for his sour disposition and his tight fists. He was not a man one offered help to lightly, for fear of being blamed for the predicament in the first place. The boys stayed hidden, watching him kick at the wheel and curse the horse, the wagon, and the mud in equal measure. He heaved at a spoke, his face turning a startling shade of purple, but the wheel did not move. He was well and truly stuck.

Jeb and Simon exchanged a look. Here was a new kind of challenge, one without stingers. After a moment of silent debate, Jeb stood up.

“Mr. Pritchard, sir!” he called out.

The farmer spun around, his scowl deepening. “What do you want? Come to gawk at a man’s misery?”

“No, sir,” said Jeb, stepping into the open, Simon trailing nervously behind him. “We reckon we can help. If we was to get some stout branches under that wheel, and if we pushed whilst you take the reins, she might come free.”

Mr. Pritchard eyed them with suspicion, his gaze lingering on Jeb’s swollen eye. “And what’s the price for this charity? A dollar, I suppose?”

“No, sir,” Simon piped up. “No price. Just helping.”

The farmer grumbled, but he was desperate. So he climbed back onto the seat and the boys went to work. They gathered flat rocks and thick limbs, jamming them under the sunken wheel to build a crude ramp. The mud sucked at their feet and caked their trousers to the knee. It was hard, filthy work. When they were ready, they put their shoulders to the back of the wagon.

“All right, sir!” Jeb yelled. “Now!”

Mr. Pritchard snapped the reins, the horse gave a mighty lurch, and the boys pushed with all their strength, their faces contorted with effort. The wheel spun, whined, and then with a great sucking schlorp, it climbed out of the mud and onto solid ground. The wagon rolled free.

Mr. Pritchard pulled the horse to a halt and looked back at the two boys, who were splattered with mud from head to toe, panting and sweating. He stared at them for a long time, his expression unreadable. Then he reached into his coat. The boys tensed, but instead of a coin, he brought out a slightly bruised apple. He broke it in two with his powerful hands and tossed a half to each of them.

“Obliged to you,” he said gruffly. And with another snap of the reins, he and his wagon rumbled away down the lane.

The boys watched him go, then looked at their apple halves, then at each other. They were sore, stung, and filthy. But as they sat on the bank and ate the apple, a new and better story began to form in their minds; a story not of foolish bravado, but of manly strength and righteous reward.

“Say, Jeb,” said Simon, his mouth full of apple, “when we tell the fellas about this, about pulling old Pritchard out of the mud all by ourselves…”

“They ain’t gonna believe it,” Jeb finished, his swollen eye nearly forgotten. “Not for a minute. But we’ll have to tell ‘em anyway. It’s our duty.” And they sat there, expanding and glorifying their afternoon’s labor, the memory of the hornets already fading into a small and insignificant preamble to their true heroic deed.

The terrors of the night faded into a grand and thrilling memory, an epic to be recounted with swelling chests. Their misadventure with the pipes was forgotten entirely, or rather, it was buried under the far more important and manly business of surviving a hurricane. They were not boys who got sick from a little tobacco; they were seasoned adventurers, tamers of the storm.

“Say, Tom,” said Joe, gnawing on a piece of ham, “when we get back, we ought to just walk in kind of slow and tired-like, and when they ask where we been, we’ll just say, ‘Oh, out on the island,’ as if it warn’t nothing.”

“That’s the very thing!” said Tom. “And if they say, ‘Wasn’t there a storm?’ I’ll say, ‘A little blow, maybe. We hardly noticed.’ By jings, won’t that make ‘em sick!”

“I reckon it will! They’ll be just green. And we won’t tell ‘em about the tent blowing away, or the sycamore getting blasted, not right away. We’ll let it out, a little at a time, so it lasts longer.”

“That’s it, Joe. We got to manage the glory, you know. Can’t spend it all in one place.”

Huck listened, and smoked his pipe in peace. He had no glory to manage. He had been scared, same as them, but he hadn’t pretended he wasn’t. He had seen a storm before, and knowed what it was. He was just glad for the ham and the fire. He was a philosopher, in his way, and understood that being scared and being alive to eat breakfast afterwards was a pretty fair trade.

So they spent the morning drying their scant possessions and planning the grand re-entry into the village of St. Petersburg. Every detail was rehearsed, every casual remark polished. They would be modest heroes, weary veterans of a great campaign, their faces etched with the solemnity of their experience. By noon, the plan was perfect, a masterpiece of strategic nonchalance.

They patched up the raft as best they could, for it had been battered soundly by the waves, and late in the afternoon, when the sun was hanging low and casting long, dramatic shadows, they pushed off from Jackson’s Island and began to pole their way homeward. The current was swift and the river was still high and muddy from the storm’s runoff, carrying a freight of branches, logs, and other debris. The boys were uncommonly silent during the journey, each contemplating his role in the coming triumph. They were no longer pirates; they were returning conquerors.

As they neared the town landing, they saw a crowd of people gathered. This was an unexpected but welcome development. Clearly, word of their peril had spread. They would be received not just by their friends, but by the whole town! Tom’s heart swelled. He pictured Aunt Polly weeping with relief, Becky Thatcher gazing at him with newfound adoration. He straightened his shoulders and adopted a look of noble exhaustion.

But as they drew closer, they perceived that the crowd was not looking toward the river. They were all gathered around the ferry-boat, which was docked at the landing, and there was a strange, solemn air about them. A skiff put out from the ferry and began dragging the water with a long hooked pole.

“What’re they doin’?” whispered Joe, his own dreams of glory beginning to feel a little shaky.

Huck’s face had lost its philosophical calm. “Somebody’s drownded,” he said, in a low voice.

The boys poled the raft in, quietly, and beached it a little ways down from the main landing, hiding it amongst the willows. Then they crept up the bank and slipped into the edge of the crowd, which was thick with all the notables of the village: Judge Thatcher was there, and the Widow Douglas, and Mr. Dobbins the schoolmaster, and even old Muff Potter, looking sober and scared. The boys’ own families were there, too. Aunt Polly was weeping on Mrs. Harper’s shoulder, and Mrs. Thatcher was being comforted by a circle of ladies.

Tom and Joe exchanged a look of utter bewilderment. Then they began to listen to the snippets of talk around them.

“...last seen heading for the island, day before yesterday,” said the blacksmith. “The storm must have caught them. Poor lads.”

“The raft was just a flimsy thing,” said another man. “Wouldn’t have stood a chance in that blow. It’s a terrible, terrible thing.”

“And Joe Harper, such a good boy. His mother is just beside herself.”

“And that Thatcher girl’s sweetheart, Tom Sawyer. Always in some scrape, but had a good heart. His poor aunt.”

“And the vagrant boy, Huck Finn. No one to mourn for him, I suppose, except perhaps the jailer for want of a customer.”

A dawning, terrible, and magnificent understanding began to light up Tom Sawyer’s mind. He nudged Joe, and then Huck. He put his finger to his lips, and then crooked it, beckoning them to follow. They crept away from the crowd, their hearts thumping with a novel and electrifying emotion. They slipped back to their raft, hidden in the willows, their own triumphant return utterly forgotten in the face of this staggering revelation.

They were dead.

The town was mourning for them. They were drowned heroes, tragic figures, lost boys. It was an honor so vast, so profound, it eclipsed every fantasy of pirating and storm-taming they had ever conceived.

“Joe,” Tom whispered, his eyes shining with an unholy light, “did you hear? They think we’re drownded!”

“I heard,” said Joe, his voice trembling with awe. “They was dragging the river for us.”

Huck said nothing. He was trying to process the fact that people were talking about him at all, even if it was only to note his lack of mourners. It was a queer feeling.

“We can’t go back now,” said Tom, his mind racing faster than the river current. “Not yet. It’s too good.”

“Too good?” said Joe. “But my mother… she was crying something awful.” A shadow of doubt crossed Joe’s face. The glory was immense, but the sight of his mother’s genuine grief had been a sharp and unexpected pain.

“Of course she is!” said Tom. “That’s what makes it so… so… sublime! Think of how happy she’ll be when we come back. The happier she is then, the sadder she’s got to be now. It’s just logic. We’re doing her a favor, really. We’re making her future happiness bigger.”

Joe was not entirely convinced by this piece of moral reasoning, but the sheer grandeur of the situation was too powerful to resist. To be the secret, living spectator at your own funeral rites! It was a privilege granted to very few.

“But where’ll we go?” said Huck. “We can’t stay here. Somebody’ll see us.”

Tom thought for a moment. His brain, fired by this new and intoxicating brand of fame, was working at a furious pace. “The church!” he whispered. “The funeral will be on Sunday. We’ll sneak back that night. We’ll hide in the gallery. We’ll listen to the whole thing!”

This was the capstone, the very pinnacle of the adventure. To attend their own funeral. Joe’s lingering scruples were swept away by the sheer audacity of the plan. Even Huck, who had a healthy fear of churches, was intrigued.

So they waited until the blackness of night had fully settled, a darkness far deeper than the stormy gloom of the night before. The crowd at the landing had dispersed, taking its grief and speculation home. The boys pushed their raft back into the current and poled silently to the opposite shore, the shore of Illinois, where they would be safe from accidental discovery. They ate the last of their ham and bacon in a somber but excited silence. They were ghosts now, and had to act the part.

For the next two days they lived a strange, spectral existence. They fished and swam on the deserted Illinois side of the river, keeping a careful watch on the distant, hazy outline of St. Petersburg. They talked in low voices, as if they were afraid of breaking the spell. The main topic of conversation was the funeral. They wondered who would cry the most. They speculated on what Reverend Sprague would say about their characters, their virtues, and their untimely demise.

“I bet he’ll tell about the time I gave that pinch-bug to the poodle during sermon,” said Tom reflectively. “He’ll probably say it showed a lively and inquiring mind, now that I’m gone.”

“And maybe he’ll mention me trading my best fish-hook for a copy of the catechism,” said Joe. “Even if I did trade the catechism for a dead rat right after. It was the thought that counted.”

“They’ll just say I was a lost lamb,” said Huck. “That’s what the Widow Douglas always calls me. I reckon it’s better than nothing.”

On Saturday night, a great gloom fell upon them. The game was beginning to feel horribly real. Joe, in particular, was melancholy and homesick. He thought of his mother and sisters, and the tears came to his eyes.

“I want to go home,” he said.

Tom’s heart sank. A mutiny at this crucial stage would ruin everything. He tried his best arguments, painting a vivid picture of the scene in the church gallery, the dramatic reveal, the joyous reunion. But Joe was inconsolable. He was tired of being a ghost. He wanted his own bed.

“All right,” said Tom, with a sigh of magnificent resignation. “Go on, then. Me and Huck will stay. We ain’t afraid of a little glory. Go on and spoil it all. Tell everyone we ain’t dead. I don’t care.” He turned his back and pretended to be fascinated by a piece of driftwood.

This was a masterful stroke. The thought of being branded a deserter, a boy who lacked the sand to see a great enterprise through to its glorious conclusion, was more than Joe could bear. His homesickness was powerful, but his fear of shame was greater. He sniffled a few times, and then said, gruffly:

“Who’s spoiling it? I was just saying. A fella can feel bad, can’t he?”

The crisis was averted. The alliance held.

Late that night, under the cover of a moonless sky, they poled their raft across the river and landed it in a secluded cove a mile above the town. They crept through the silent streets, their bare feet making no sound. The town was asleep, dreaming, no doubt, of its three lost boys. The church stood on a small hill, a dark and imposing shape against the star-dusted sky. Tom knew a secret way in; a small, unlocked window in a back room where Sunday-school supplies were kept.

They slipped inside, into the deep, echoing silence of the holy place. The air was cool and smelled of old wood and hymnals. They tiptoed up the creaking stairs to the unused gallery at the back. It was shrouded in shadows and thick with dust and cobwebs. They found a place where they could lie on the floor and peer through the balusters, unseen. There they settled in to wait for morning, and for their own funeral.

It was a long and creepy vigil. Every rustle and groan of the old building was a spectral footstep. But their excitement was a shield against fear. They dozed fitfully, and awoke when the first gray light of dawn began to filter through the tall, arched windows. Soon, the church bell began to toll, a slow, mournful clang that seemed to shake the very floorboards beneath them. Each toll was a hammer-blow on their own hearts. This was for them.

The villagers began to arrive, dressed in their Sunday best, their faces unnaturally solemn. The boys watched, breathless, as their friends and families filed in. The Sawyer family and the Harper family took up the front pews, all in black. Aunt Polly’s face was hidden behind a black veil, but her whole body shook with silent sobs. Mrs. Harper was weeping openly. Becky Thatcher came in with her parents, her face pale and her eyes red. When she sat down, she looked up toward the choir loft where Tom usually sat, and a fresh wave of grief overcame her. Tom felt a pang that was half pity and half exquisite pride.

The church was filled to capacity. The Reverend Sprague ascended the pulpit, his face a mask of sorrow. The service began. The choir sang a mournful hymn, and the boys in the gallery had to clamp their hands over their mouths to keep from letting out a yelp of pure, unadulterated triumph.

Then the minister began his eulogy. He spoke of the tragedy that had befallen the town, the cutting down of young lives in their very blossom. He painted a picture of Joe Harper as a sweet-natured, obedient boy, a model for his fellows, a joy to his mother’s heart. Joe nudged his comrades and wiped a tear from his eye; he had never known he was such a credit to the community.

Then the minister turned his attention to Tom. He admitted that Tom had been a wild and trying boy, full of mischief and high spirits. But underneath it all, he said, his heart was pure gold. He recounted a series of Tom’s nobler deeds, most of which were entirely fictional, but which sounded so plausible and moving that Tom himself began to feel he had been tragically misunderstood his whole life. He recalled Tom’s generosity, his courage, his secret kindnesses to the poor and friendless. By the time the minister was done, Tom Sawyer was not a boy at all, but a fallen angel.

The congregation was weeping freely now. The air was thick with sorrow. Even Huck Finn got a brief, honorable mention, as a poor, homeless soul, now presumably at peace in a home that could never be taken from him.

The minister’s voice rose to a crescendo. “We shall not see their like again,” he boomed. “These were not common boys! They were taken from us to remind us of the fragility of life, the preciousness of youth! Oh, that we might have them back, for but a moment, to show them the love that was in our hearts!”

This was the moment. Tom had planned it to perfection.

He rose from his hiding place. Joe and Huck followed, their hearts hammering against their ribs. The congregation was lost in its grief, heads bowed, handkerchiefs pressed to faces. The minister had his eyes closed in pious sorrow.

A loose floorboard creaked under Tom’s foot.

The sound, sharp and out of place, made a few people look up. Old Mr. Walters, the Sunday-school superintendent, peered toward the dark gallery. He squinted. His jaw dropped. He let out a faint squawk.

His neighbor followed his gaze. Then another, and another. A ripple of gasps and whispers spread through the pews. Heads turned, then bodies. The weeping stopped, replaced by a stunned, disbelieving silence. Aunt Polly lifted her veil. Mrs. Harper looked up. Becky Thatcher’s tear-filled eyes widened in astonishment.

The minister, feeling the sudden shift in the atmosphere, opened his eyes. He looked out over the sea of upturned, incredulous faces, all staring at something behind him. He turned around.

And there they stood, at the top of the gallery stairs, illuminated by a shaft of morning sun from a high window: three boys, back from the dead, looking rumpled, dirty, and thoroughly alive.

For a full ten seconds, nobody moved. The town was frozen, caught between a funeral and a miracle. Then Aunt Polly let out a cry that was half-scream, half-sob, and the spell was broken.

“Tom!” she shrieked. “It’s him!”

The congregation erupted. People leaped to their feet. The solemnity of the occasion exploded into a joyous, chaotic uproar. Tom, Joe, and Huck marched proudly down the aisle, through the stunned and babbling crowd.

Tom was seized by Aunt Polly, who hugged him, and shook him, and cried over him, and called him a blessed, wicked, wonderful boy. Joe was similarly engulfed by the Harper clan. And Huck, bewildered and overlooked, stood awkwardly to the side, until Tom broke free from his aunt’s embrace and shouted, “It’s Huck, too! He was with us!” Judge Thatcher, in a fit of democratic fervor, clapped Huck on the shoulder and declared him a fine lad, a sentiment which left Huck so flustered he nearly bolted from the church.

The minister, utterly forgotten in his pulpit, looked down upon the pandemonium, a slow, dazed smile spreading across his face. He lifted his hands.

“PRAISE GOD FROM WHOM ALL BLESSINGS FLOW!” he bellowed, and the congregation, in a spontaneous burst of feeling, joined in, singing the Doxology with a fervor and volume that threatened to lift the roof right off the church.

It was the proudest moment of Tom Sawyer’s life. The glory of it was so immense, so brilliant, that it blotted out the memory of the storm, the fear, the hunger, and even the humiliating sickness from the pipe. He had not just attended his own funeral; he had resurrected himself in a blaze of glory. It was a story that would be told and retold for a generation. And as he stood there, the center of all attention, a hero redeemed, he knew, with a certainty that was absolute, that it had all been worth it.

The Sabbath that had begun with such solemn grief ended in a jubilee. Monday morning, however, brought with it the sober reality of school. But it was not the same school. When Tom and Joe arrived, a little late and with an air of studied nonchalance, they were not met with the master’s stern glare but with a wave of hushed admiration from the students. They were heroes, voyagers returned from the fabled lands of Jackson’s Island. Every boy in the school longed to be Tom Sawyer or Joe Harper, and spent the recess hours besieging them with questions.

“Did you really eat fish you caught yourselves, Tom?”

“Did you swim in the river every day?”

“Was you scared during the big storm?”

To this last question, Tom gave a dismissive laugh. “Scared? Shucks, a storm like that ain’t nothing when you’re a pirate. We just tightened the ropes on our tent and told stories till it passed. It was Huck who was a little jumpy, but he ain’t used to such things.” Huck, who was standing nearby, looked down at his bare feet and said nothing; he remembered clinging to Joe and Tom in abject terror under an oak tree, but he knew that was not part of this new and glorious story.

The girls, too, regarded them with a new and tender interest. They would whisper in clusters and steal glances at the heroes, especially at Tom. Becky Thatcher, who had wept so bitterly for his loss, now found herself in a perplexing state. She wanted to run to him, to show him how much she had cared; but her pride, which had been so wounded before he ran away, held her fast. She maintained a cool and distant front, turning her head to talk with Amy Lawrence whenever Tom’s gaze drifted her way.

Tom noticed this, and it nettled him. The glory of his return was magnificent, but it was incomplete without the surrender of Becky Thatcher. He spent the morning performing for her benefit. He answered questions more loudly when she was near, laughed with a heartiness that was meant to travel across the schoolroom, and even got himself whipped for drawing a caricature of the schoolmaster on his slate, an act of martyrdom he bore with a theatrical sigh that he hoped she would find terribly noble.

At noon, he saw his chance. Becky was sitting alone on a log at the edge of the schoolyard, tracing patterns in the dirt with a stick. Tom sauntered over, jingling the treasures in his pocket: a curiously shaped river stone, a bird’s feather of iridescent blue, and a brass button from some forgotten pair of trousers. He stood before her, assuming an attitude of careless power.

“Well, I’m back,” he said.

Becky did not look up. “I see that.”

“Reckon you missed me,” he went on, his confidence a trifle shaken.

“I don’t know. I suppose the school was quieter.”

This was not the reception he had envisioned. His grand resurrection ought to have melted her heart. He fumbled in his pocket and produced the smooth, gray river stone. “I brought this back from the island. It’s a pirate stone. A lucky one.” He held it out to her. “You can have it, if you want.”

She glanced at the stone, then back at the dirt. A long moment passed. Tom’s heart began to sink. The adulation of the entire town felt like a hollow thing if he could not win a glance from this one girl. Finally, she looked up, and he saw that her eyes were glistening.

“Oh, Tom, how could you be so cruel?” she whispered. “We all thought you were dead! I—I cried all the time.”

The fortress of Tom’s bravado crumbled in an instant. This was better than admiration; this was conquest. “Did you, Becky? Did you really?” he said, his voice soft. “I didn’t mean to be cruel. I was going to come back. I even came back one night, and saw you all grieving, and it was all I could do not to run in and tell you.”

He then launched into the highly edited story of his secret visit, of how he had almost revealed himself but held back for the sake of the grand surprise. He did not mention that the original plan had been simply to go home out of misery and homesickness. In his telling, it was all a calculated act of dramatic genius. Becky listened, captivated, her earlier coolness forgotten. She took the pirate stone from his hand, and the treaty was sealed. Tom Sawyer was whole again.

But glory, like fresh bread, grows stale. Within a week, the boys’ adventure was an old story. The questions dwindled. The schoolmaster’s switch found Tom’s legs just as readily as before. The daily chores, the tiresome lessons, the whole monotonous machinery of respectable life, began to grind down their piratical spirits. The freedom of Jackson’s Island seemed a distant dream.

One sweltering afternoon, Tom lay on his back in the long grass behind the widow’s fence, chewing on a blade of timothy. Joe was listlessly trying to get a turtle to stick its head out of its shell, and Huck was mending a fishing line.

“It’s no use,” Tom said suddenly, sitting up. “This is too dull to bear.”

“I reckon,” said Joe, poking the turtle. “Wish we was back on the island.”

“The island was good,” Tom admitted. “But it was just the start. Being pirates is one thing. But what do real pirates have that we don’t?”

Joe and Huck pondered this. “A ship with a cannon?” suggested Joe.

“A black flag with a skull on it?” offered Huck.

“No, no!” said Tom, with the impatience of a visionary. “Treasure! Buried treasure! Gold and jewels! That’s the main thing. We went pirating, but we never got to the treasure part.”

A spark of interest lit up Joe’s face. “That’s so! Why, I never thought of that. Is there any treasure around here, Tom?”

“Of course there is,” Tom said confidently. “The world’s full of it. You just have to know where to look. I’ve been reading up on it. Most times, pirates bury their loot on islands, or in haunted houses.”

Huck’s eyes widened. “Haunted houses? Shucks, Tom, I wouldn’t go in one of them for all the treasure in the world. You’ll get ghosts after you.”

“Ghosts can’t get you in the daytime, Huck. Everybody knows that. And besides, that’s where they put it. Under a loose brick in the hearth, or in the dirt floor of the cellar. They always mark it, too. Sometimes with a cross, sometimes they make a map.”

“Got a map, Tom?” asked Joe, now fully enlisted in the enterprise.

“Don’t need one,” said Tom. “I know a place. The old haunted house on Cardiff Hill. The one that belonged to the dead McPherson brothers. It’s been empty for years. It’s the perfect spot. Full of spiders and ghosts and secrets. I bet it’s got a million dollars in it.”

Huck was not convinced. “I hear tell that old man McPherson’s ghost walks around in there at midnight, groaning and dragging chains.”

“Well, we won’t go at midnight, then,” said Tom. “We’ll go tomorrow afternoon. We’ll take picks and shovels. We’ll be rich, boys. Richer than Judge Thatcher. We’ll buy a new drum, and a real sword, and a steamboat, and anything we want.”

The vision was too splendid to resist. Even Huck’s fear of ghosts was dimmed by the dazzling prospect of such wealth. It was agreed. The next day, after school, they would become treasure hunters.

The following afternoon found the three adventurers assembled at the foot of Cardiff Hill, armed with a rusty pickaxe and two spades they had “borrowed” from a farmer’s shed. The haunted house stood silhouetted against the afternoon sun, a ruin of sagging roofs and vacant, staring windows. A crooked porch clung precariously to the front, and the whole structure listed to one side, as if tired of standing. Weeds grew waist-high in what was once a garden. Their courage, so robust down in the town, began to leak away as they drew closer. The silence was unnerving. Not even a bird sang in the dead trees that surrounded the house.

“It’s mighty spooky,” whispered Joe.

“The hearth,” Tom whispered, pointing with a trembling finger. “That’s where it’ll be. Under the stones.”

They set to work. Tom and Joe pried at the loose stones of the hearth with the pickaxe while Huck stood guard, his eyes darting nervously into every shadowy corner. They had cleared away several stones and had begun to dig into the hard-packed earth beneath when a floorboard creaked in the room directly above them.

The boys froze. They looked at each other, their faces pale.

“A rat,” Joe mouthed silently.

Tom nodded, but he didn’t believe it. The creak had been slow, deliberate, heavy. It sounded like a footstep.

They waited, holding their breath. A long, agonizing silence. Then it came again. A slow, dragging, scraping sound, as if something heavy was being pulled across the floorboards upstairs.

Huck’s eyes were as big as saucers. “Tom,” he breathed, “it’s him. It’s old man McPherson.”

“Hush!” Tom hissed, though he felt a cold dread creeping up his spine.

That was enough. All pretense of bravery vanished. Tom dropped the pickaxe with a clatter. Joe let out a terrified yelp. They tumbled off the porch, tore through the overgrown yard, and did not stop running until they reached the bottom of the hill, collapsing in a panting, trembling heap.

They lay there for a long time, gasping for air, their hearts hammering against their ribs.

They sat in miserable silence for a long time. The sun, which had promised adventure, now seemed to mock them with its warmth. Joe was sniffling quietly. Huck was methodically stripping the bark from a twig with his thumbnail, his face a grim mask. Finally, Tom looked up. The terror was still in his eyes, but behind it, a tiny, stubborn spark had begun to glow. It was the part of him that could not accept defeat, the part that saw a locked door and immediately started searching for the key. “What if,” he said, his voice hoarse, “what if we don’t have to go near him?” Joe just stared at him, aghast. “Did you not hear us, Tom? It’s Injun Joe!” “I know!” Tom snapped, a flash of the old fire returning. “That’s why we have to be smarter. We can’t fight him. We can’t trick him. He’s… he’s Injun Joe. But he ain’t a ghost. The treasure was no longer just a prize; it was the bait in a trap they were setting for themselves. And as they looked at each other in the dappled sunlight, a silent, grim agreement was passed between them. They were in it now, for better or for worse, bound together by a secret that might make them rich, or get them killed. The great adventure had begun at last.

He produced his Barlow knife, and with the solemnity of a high priest, pricked his own thumb. He squeezed a tiny red bead of blood onto his thumbnail. They pressed their thumbs together, a trinity of boyish flesh and blood, sealing a pact that felt as ancient and unbreakable as the hills around them.

“That’s it! That’s capital!” said Tom. Joe said nothing, but shivered, whether from excitement or sheer terror it was impossible to say.

They parted then, with sworn instructions to meet at the tannery alley an hour after moonrise. Tom went home with his head buzzing with strategies and counter-strategies, feeling like Napoleon before a great battle. Joe went home feeling like a condemned man who had already eaten his last meal. Huck simply went to his hogshead, to wait for the dark as he did every other day; only this night, the darkness held a new and specific promise of dread.

The moon, a thin, sharp crescent, was high in the sky when three shadows detached themselves from the deeper gloom of the alley behind the haunted house. They slipped, one by one, through a gap in the fence of the abandoned tannery and crept into the ruin of a lean-to shed. The air inside was thick with the ghostly smell of rotted leather and chemicals, and cobwebs, heavy with dust, brushed their faces like dead men’s fingers. Through a wide crack in the wall, they had a clear, uninterrupted view of the back of the Welshman’s house, its dilapidated porch and weed-choked yard lying stark and silent under the faint starlight.

And then the waiting began. An hour crawled by, then another. The night sounds of St. Petersburg, which were usually a comfort, were now a source of constant alarm. The distant barking of a dog, the creak of a board in the old shed, the rustle of a rat in the debris—each sound made them freeze, their hearts hammering against their ribs. Tom, at first, was thrilled by the tension, but soon even his appetite for adventure began to be worn down by the sheer, monotonous dread. Joe was a silent, huddled lump of misery, starting at every flutter of a moth’s wings.

Only Huck was truly at home in the silence and the dark. He sat perfectly still, his senses alive and questing. He was not waiting for an adventure; he was watching for a predator. His body was loose, but his mind was a taut wire, tuned to the slightest deviation from the normal rhythm of the night.

“He ain’t comin’,” Joe whispered, his voice trembling, after the town clock had mournfully chimed midnight. “Let’s go home, Tom. We can try again tomorrow.”

“A detective is never discouraged by waiting,” Tom whispered back, though his own resolve was fraying. “Patience is the main thing. He’s testing us, that’s what he’s doing.”

“He don’t know we’re here,” Huck breathed, without moving. “He’s just bein’ careful. Or maybe he’s sleepin’.”

They fell silent again. The moon sank lower, and the shadows stretched and warped into more menacing shapes. Joe began to nod, his fear losing its battle with exhaustion. Tom found himself yawning, the grand drama of the situation dissolving into a dreary, uncomfortable vigil. He was about to concede to Joe’s plea when Huck’s hand shot out and gripped his arm. The grip was like iron.

Tom’s heart leaped into his throat. He followed Huck’s gaze. A light. A faint, flickering, yellow light had just appeared in one of the grimy upper windows of the haunted house. It was a candle, held by an unseen hand. It moved past one window, then another, a tiny spark of life in the dead house.

Joe was awake now, his sleepiness vanished, replaced by a cold, stark terror. All three boys were rigid, scarcely daring to breathe. The light descended. They saw its glow through the lower windows. Then, a faint sound reached them, the groan of a rusty hinge. The back door was opening.

A tall, broad-shouldered silhouette filled the doorway, black against the feeble light from within. There was no mistaking that shape. It was Injun Joe. Another figure followed, smaller and shuffling; the ragged, indistinct man from the other day. They stood on the porch for a long moment, listening, their heads turning slowly as they scanned the darkness. The boys flattened themselves against the shed wall, trying to will themselves into invisibility.

Then Injun Joe spoke, his voice a low rumble that carried clearly in the still night air. “All clear. The whole town’s dead. Let’s get it.”

The second man went back inside and emerged a moment later, straining with the other end of the heavy chest. They carried it between them down the rickety steps and into the yard. They did not head for the alley or the street. Instead, they moved towards the river, their dark forms melting into the deeper shadows of the trees that lined the banks.

Huck let out a breath he didn’t know he’d been holding. “Now,” he whispered. He didn’t need to give the signal. The time for signals was past. This was the hunt itself.

He slipped out of the shed with the silence of a drifting leaf. Tom and Joe followed, their own attempts at stealth sounding like a herd of cattle in their own ears. Huck led them, not directly after the men, but in a wide, parallel arc, keeping a line of trees or a dip in the ground between them and their quarry. The art of it, as Huck practiced it, was to never look directly at the men for too long, but to watch their progress by glimpses, by the momentary blotting out of a star, by the sound of a dislodged stone.

It was nerve-shattering work. Every dry twig underfoot was a thunderclap waiting to happen. Every patch of moonlight was a snare. Tom’s imagination, which had been so full of heroic detectives, was now supplying him with horrific images of Injun Joe’s knife. Joe Harper was simply moving by instinct, his eyes fixed on Tom’s back, his mind a complete and utter blank of fear.

The men were moving faster now, with the steady, purposeful stride of those who know their destination. They left the outskirts of the town behind and struck a path that led up the long, wooded hill towards McDougal’s Cave. A suspicion, terrible and exciting, began to form in Tom’s mind. The cave! Of course. Miles of twisting passages, a labyrinth where no one would ever think to look.

They climbed, the boys’ breath coming in ragged gasps, their legs aching. The men ahead were tireless. At last, they reached the familiar clearing on the side of the hill. The entrance to the cave was a black, yawning mouth in the rock face, a patch of absolute darkness that seemed to swallow the starlight. The boys watched from the shelter of a dense thicket of sumach, their hearts pounding a frantic rhythm against their ribs. They saw Injun Joe and his companion set down the heavy box before the entrance.

The ragged man produced a candle and a lucifer match. He struck it, and for a brief, startling moment, his pale, unshaven face was illuminated in the flare. Then he lit the candle, and the two men picked up their burden again. They stooped and entered the blackness of the cave, their flickering light shrinking until it was just a pinprick, and then it was gone.

Silence. The boys were alone with the chirping crickets and the vast, breathing night. The mouth of the cave was once more a formless blot of black.

“They’re in,” Tom breathed, his voice a ghost of a whisper. “We’ve got him! We know his secret!”

“What do we do now?” whispered Joe, his teeth chattering. “Do we go in after them?”

“Are you crazy?” Huck’s whisper was sharp as a shard of glass. “Go in there? In the dark? He’d hear us in a second. He’d corner us in one of them passages and… no.”

“Huck’s right,” said Tom, though the thought of a torch-lit pursuit through the caverns had sent a brief, foolish thrill through him. “We wait. We wait for them to come out. We have to be sure they leave it there.”

So they waited again. But this waiting was different. Before, they were waiting for something to happen. Now, they were waiting for something to be finished. The knowledge that the treasure, the actual, tangible gold and silver, was just a few hundred feet away, separated from them only by a wall of rock and darkness, was a powerful and unsettling thing. It made the air thrum with possibility.

It seemed an age, but it was probably no more than half an hour before a faint light once more appeared deep within the cave’s mouth. It grew steadily, and then the two men emerged, blinking in the starlight. They were empty-handed. Injun Joe was carrying the extinguished candle. He paused at the entrance, listening intently, his head tilted like a wolf’s. He looked straight towards the sumach thicket. For one heart-stopping second, Tom was certain they were discovered. He could feel Joe trembling beside him. But Huck remained as still as the stone he was hiding behind.

Apparently satisfied, Injun Joe grunted something to his companion, and they turned and started back down the hill, not by the path they had come, but by a more direct, steeper route that would take them to the river’s edge farther downstream. The boys stayed hidden, not daring to move a muscle, until the sounds of their descent had faded completely into the night.

For a long time, nobody spoke. They were alone. The treasure was in the cave. Their impossible plan had worked.

“We did it,” Tom said at last, his voice hoarse with suppressed triumph. “By jings, Huck, we did it! It’s in there! The whole pile of it!”

“We did it,” Joe repeated, his voice full of awe. The terror had receded, leaving behind a giddy, disbelieving excitement. “We’re rich, Tom! We’re going to be rich!”

“Shh!” hissed Huck. “Sound carries. They ain’t that far off yet.”

They fell silent, but the excitement was a living thing among them. Rich! The word echoed in their minds. It meant no more school, for one thing. It meant a brass cannon, a real sword, a red necktie, and a bull pup. It meant a perpetual state of glory. For Huck, it meant something more fundamental: a full stomach, a roof that didn’t leak, and the freedom to never again depend on the casual charity of others.

“We got to go in and get it,” Tom said, his mind already leaping to the next chapter of the adventure. “Right now!”

“No,” said Huck, his practical nature once more asserting itself over Tom’s romantic impulsiveness. “Not now. We ain’t got candles. We ain’t got string to mark the way. A body could get lost in there and never get found. Remember the Thatchers’ picnic? Besides, we’re tuckered out. We got to come back when we’re ready.”

Huck was right, and Tom knew it. The cave was a known danger, a fickle and treacherous labyrinth. To enter it unprepared, at night, on the heels of Injun Joe, would be the height of folly.

“Tomorrow,” Tom declared. “We’ll meet at the widow’s stile, first thing after noon. Bring candles. And string. Lots of string. And bags to carry the money in. And I’ll bring some grub. Provisions for the expedition!”

They crept back down the hill, their earlier caution thrown to the winds in their exhilaration. They stumbled and slid, but they did not care. They were no longer mere boys, sneaking through the woods; they were treasure-hunters, adventurers who had faced down a murderer and won the first round. They parted at the edge of town with fierce, whispered promises to meet the next day.

Tom Sawyer did not sleep much that night. His mind was a riot of golden coins and desperate flights and glorious returns. He was already spending the money, astonishing the town, and revealing his secret to Becky Thatcher in a moment of supreme, dramatic triumph. He was the hero of a story so grand that even his own imagination could scarcely contain it.

Huck Finn, curled in his hogshead, did not sleep either. But he was not dreaming of glory. He was thinking of the black mouth of the cave, and the man who had gone inside it. He was thinking of the weight of the treasure, but also of the weight of the secret. The gold was real, and it was close. But so was Injun Joe. And Huck had a cold, sinking feeling that getting the treasure out of that cave was going to be a far more dangerous business than following it there. The great adventure had truly begun; but Huck suspected the price of it had not yet been calculated.

The next morning was a long and trying eternity for Tom Sawyer. He was up before the birds, but he dared not stir, for Aunt Polly had a preternatural sense for illicit wakefulness. He lay in bed and plotted. The first order of business was to secure provisions. This was a delicate operation. A raid on the pantry required the stealth of a cat and the strategic genius of a Napoleon. He mentally catalogued the possibilities: a slab of bacon, some cold cornbread, perhaps an apple or two. He would need to create a diversion. Sid was the obvious candidate for such a work; a carefully placed beetle or a “misplaced” shoe could occupy that saintly creature for the necessary moments.

Breakfast was an agony. Every word spoken at the table seemed to hold a double meaning. When Aunt Polly said, “Tom, you’re looking a bit peaked, are you feeling all right?” he nearly jumped out of his skin, certain that his face betrayed the whole stupendous secret. He mumbled something about a stomach-ache, which was not entirely a falsehood, though the ache was from excitement and not from any green apples.

“It’s the work of the devil, that’s what it is,” said Aunt Polly, referring to some town gossip, but Tom heard it as a direct commentary on treasure hidden by a murderer. He shoveled his food with such nervous haste that his aunt remarked upon it. “Land sakes, child, you’ll choke! The food ain’t running away.”

But it was, in a manner of speaking. The hours were running away, and noon seemed an impossible distance in the future. After the meal, he executed his raid with a skill that surprised even himself. While Sid was loudly lamenting the mysterious disappearance of a favorite glass marble, Tom slipped into the pantry and emerged moments later with his shirt bulging in a most unnatural way. He stowed the lumpy treasures in a secluded corner of the woodshed, his heart thumping a triumphant rhythm against his ribs.

Huck’s morning was of a different character. He did not have to feign normalcy, for his life was a study in irregularity. He awoke in his hogshead and his first thought was of Injun Joe. The gold was a grand and beautiful dream, but the murderer was a cold and heavy reality. He spent the morning in his usual fashion, which is to say, in no fashion at all. He loitered about the back alleys of the village, keeping his eyes and ears open. He saw no sign of unusual activity, heard no whispers of a new crime or a discovered body. The town slumbered on, ignorant of the drama that had unfolded on Cardiff Hill. This was a good sign. It meant Injun Joe was either still in the cave, or had slipped away as quietly as he arrived. Huck’s money was on the cave. A man with a treasure like that wouldn’t wander far from it. This thought did not bring him much comfort.

He gathered his own supplies with the quiet efficiency of a born forager. A half-dozen candle-ends, filched from the waste-bins behind the church; a ball of stout twine he had won from Ben Rogers in a trade for a dead rat and a string to swing it with; and two capacious, if slightly grimy, gunny sacks procured from the back of the general store. His provisions were practical, where Tom’s were romantic.

Shortly after the noon bell had tolled, the two adventurers met at the appointed place, the stile behind the widow Douglas’s house. Tom was flushed and panting, having run most of the way. Huck was already there, perched on the top step, whittling a stick with an air of weary patience.

“You got the stuff?” Tom whispered, his voice trembling with eagerness.

“I got it,” said Huck, holding up his sacks and his twine. “Got the candles?”

Tom produced his own contribution: a fistful of tallow dips, a rusty pick-axe head he had liberated from his woodshed, and the bulging contents of his shirt, which he now revealed.

“A whole side of bacon!” he announced proudly. “And three apples. And a chunk of cake Sid was saving. Provisions for the expedition!”

Huck eyed the cake. “That’ll be nice to eat,” he said, “but it ain’t going to help us tote a thousand pounds of gold.”

“We won’t need help with this!” Tom said, brandishing the pick-axe head. “We’ll bust the box open and fill the sacks. Easy as anything.”

Huck was not so sure, but he held his peace. Tom’s confidence was a necessary fire, and Huck was not inclined to throw water on it, not yet. They set off, not directly for the cave, but by a long and secret route that took them deep into the woods, through thickets of hazel and over mossy logs. They spoke in whispers, their boyish bravado warring with a genuine and creeping dread.

“Just think of it, Huck,” Tom said, for the tenth time. “We’ll be the richest folks in St. Petersburg. Richer than Judge Thatcher, even.”

“Reckon so,” said Huck. “Judge Thatcher ain’t got to worry about Injun Joe coming to collect, though.”

This sobered Tom for a moment. “He won’t know it was us. We’ll be smart about it. We’ll just go in, get the money, and slip out. He’ll come back and think somebody else stole it. He’ll never think of us.”

“Maybe,” said Huck. It was a thin and unsatisfying maybe.

An hour of cautious travel brought them to the familiar tangled slope of Cardiff Hill. The mouth of McDougal’s Cave gaped at them from the hillside, a black and silent maw. It seemed darker and more forbidding than ever before. The cheerful sunlight of the afternoon did not penetrate its shadows; a cool, damp breath, smelling of stone and ancient night, issued from within.

“Well,” said Tom, his voice a little less robust than before. “Here we are.”

“Yep,” said Huck. “Here we are.”

For a long moment, neither boy moved. The grand adventure, so thrilling in the imagination, was now a solid and fearsome reality. The darkness in that hole was absolute. And somewhere within it, perhaps just a few yards from the entrance, perhaps deep in its winding guts, was a man who had killed before and would surely kill again for what they had come to take.

“Well, no use waiting,” Tom said at last, with a show of his old leadership. “Give me the string, Huck. I’ll tie her off here, so we don’t get lost. You light a couple of candles.”

Huck complied. He produced a sulphur match and scraped it on a rock. The tiny flare of light was startling in the gloom. They each took a candle, the feeble flames pushing back the darkness by a few scant feet, creating a small, wavering bubble of light around them. The shadows it cast danced like frantic goblins on the rock walls.

Tom securely fastened one end of the twine to a prominent spur of rock just inside the entrance. “There,” he said. “Now, whatever happens, we can find our way back. Let’s go.”

He took the lead, holding his candle high. Huck followed close behind, unspooling the precious twine. The passage narrowed almost at once, forcing them to walk in single file. The air grew colder, and the silence was profound, broken only by the drip, drip, drip of water from the unseen ceiling and the soft scuffle of their own feet.

Tom’s romantic soul was stirred by the scene. “This is a robber’s den, for sure, Huck! Don’t it feel like it?”

“It feels like a grave,” Huck answered, and his voice was so low it was nearly swallowed by the silence.

They proceeded deeper, the twine a slender thread connecting them to the world of sun and air. The main passage branched into a dozen smaller ones, a bewildering network of tunnels. Without the string, they would have been hopelessly lost in minutes. Tom, remembering his previous explorations, tried to keep to the main artery of the cave, but his memory was a jumble. He relied on the path they had seen Injun Joe take, a recollection that was both a guide and a terror.

They came to a great chamber, a vast, vaulted room whose ceiling was lost in the blackness above. Their candle-flames were pitifully small in the immense space. Great stalactites hung down like icicles of stone, and from the floor, stalagmites rose up to meet them, some of them forming grotesque pillars and shapes that looked, in the flickering light, like hooded figures watching them pass.

“Golly,” whispered Tom, awed.

Huck said nothing. His eyes darted everywhere, into every dark crevice and behind every stony pillar. Every drip of water was a footstep. Every echo was a whisper. His hand, holding the ball of twine, was clammy with sweat.

“It was somewhere past here,” Tom said, his voice regaining some of its confidence. “I remember that big rock that looks like a turtle. He went to the left of it.”

They pressed on, deeper and deeper into the bowels of the earth. The twine continued to unspool. They had used up a hundred yards, then two hundred. The small world of St. Petersburg, of sun and school and Aunt Polly, seemed a lifetime away. Here there was only rock and darkness and the cold, dead air.

And then they saw it.

On the wall of a smaller, side-passage, there was a crude cross, freshly smudged with soot from a candle. It was the mark.

“That’s it!” Tom breathed. “That’s it, Huck!”

Their fear was momentarily forgotten, swept away by a tidal wave of triumphant excitement. They scrambled into the narrow passage. It was a dead end, no more than twenty feet long. The floor was soft clay.

“Right under the cross,” Tom said, his voice husky. He dropped his provisions and seized the pick-axe head. “Stand back, Huck!”

He began to dig, not with the pick, but with his hands, scooping away the damp clay. It was soft, as if it had been recently disturbed. Huck got down on his knees to help, his earlier fears banished by the proximity of the prize. They dug like badgers, flinging dirt behind them.

After only a few inches, their fingers struck something hard. Wood.

“The box!” gasped Tom.

They frantically cleared the remaining clay from the top of a small, iron-bound chest. It was not large, perhaps two feet long and a little more than a foot wide, but it was stoutly made, bound with straps of rusted iron. It looked ancient and incredibly heavy.

“We’ve got it, Huck! We’ve got it!” Tom crowed. He tried to pry the lid with his fingers, then with the pick-axe head, but it was securely locked. “No matter. We’ll take the whole thing!”

He positioned himself on one side of the box, and Huck on the other. “Now,” he grunted. “On three. One… two… THREE!”

They heaved with all their might. The box shifted, perhaps an inch. It was prodigiously heavy, far heavier than they had imagined. It was filled to the brim not with paper money, but with solid gold. They tried again, their faces red with exertion, muscles straining. The box scraped along the clay floor, but lifting it clear seemed an impossibility for two boys.

“It’s… too… heavy,” panted Tom, collapsing against the wall. “We can’t… carry it out.”

Huck, breathing just as hard, nodded. A grim reality began to settle over them. They had found the treasure, but it might as well have been at the bottom of the Mississippi.

“We’ll have to… make trips,” Tom said, thinking fast. “Bust it open and fill the sacks. It’ll take all night.”

Just as he said it, a sound echoed from the main passage they had just left.

It was a soft, scraping noise. A pebble, dislodged by something, skittering down a rock face.

The boys froze. Every muscle went rigid. Their eyes, wide with terror, met over the top of the treasure box. The sound was distant, but it was unmistakably a sound made by something other than dripping water. It was the sound of movement.

Huck was the first to react. He snatched Tom’s arm, his fingers digging in like claws. His face was a white mask in the candlelight. He put a finger to his lips, then pointed frantically back the way they had come.

Tom’s bravado evaporated in an instant, leaving behind a cold, hard knot of pure fear. Injun Joe. It had to be. He had come back.

For a terrible second, they were paralyzed. Then survival instinct took over. They couldn’t run. He was in the main passage, between them and the exit. Their only hope was to hide.

Tom’s eyes darted around the small chamber. There was nowhere. It was a smooth-walled dead end. His gaze fell upon a deep shadow behind a cluster of stalagmites near the entrance to their little alcove. It wasn’t much, but it was all they had.

“The candles!” Huck breathed, his whisper as faint as a dying man’s sigh.

With a trembling hand, Tom pinched out his flame. Huck did the same. The world vanished into a blackness so absolute and profound it was like being struck blind. It pressed in on them, thick and suffocating. They could hear their own hearts beating, a frantic, hammering drumbeat in the utter silence.

Holding hands, they groped their way, stumbling, towards the shadow they had seen. They squeezed themselves into the narrow space behind the rock formations, pressing their bodies against the cold, damp stone. They dared not breathe.

And then they heard it again, closer this time. The distinct sound of a man’s low, guttural cough.

The sound seemed to come from everywhere at once in the disorienting dark. It was followed by the slow, shuffling tread of heavy boots on the cave floor. He was in the great chamber now, the one just outside their hiding place. A faint, ruddy glow began to bloom against the far wall of their passage, the reflection of a light source approaching.

The boys flattened themselves further into their crevice, trying to will themselves into the very rock. Tom’s teeth were chattering, and he bit his lip to silence them. Huck was as still as death, but Tom could feel the violent trembling of the hand that still gripped his own.

The light grew stronger. The shuffling footsteps came nearer. He was at the junction now, right at the entrance to their passage. The light of his lantern cast a long, distorted shadow of a stalagmite that danced and writhed on the wall opposite them.

Would he come in? Would he see the disturbed earth? The treasure box, only half-buried? The thought was a physical blow. They had been so foolish, so loud, so sure of themselves.

The footsteps stopped.

The silence that followed was more terrifying than the noise. They could hear nothing but the blood roaring in their own ears. An eternity passed in a single minute. Why had he stopped? Had he seen something? Was he listening?

Then, a voice, low and rumbling, spoke to the darkness. “No. Not tonight. Too soon. Let him get further away.”

It was Injun Joe’s voice. He was talking to himself.

There was a sound of movement, a shifting of weight, and then the footsteps resumed, not coming towards them, but moving away, back down the main passage, receding into the depths of the cave. The reflected glow of his lantern faded, the dancing shadows stilled, and finally, the darkness reclaimed everything. The sound of his footsteps grew fainter and fainter, until at last they were gone, and the only sound was the eternal, maddening drip, drip, drip of water.

The boys did not move for a long, long time. They remained huddled in their hiding place, two small, terrified animals, their bodies aching from the cold and the tension. At last, Tom dared to whisper.

“Is he gone?”

“I think so,” Huck breathed back. “He went deeper in. Not out.”

Deeper in. That was a comfort of sorts. It meant the way out was clear. But it also meant the monster was still in the labyrinth with them.

Slowly, painfully, they untangled themselves from their hiding spot. Huck fumbled for his matches, his hands shaking so badly it took him three tries to strike one. The sudden flare of the candle was a blessing, a return from the land of the dead.

They looked at each other. Their faces were pale, streaked with dirt, their eyes enormous in the flickering light. They did not look at the treasure box. Its presence was now a source of terror, not joy.

“We got to get out of here, Tom,” Huck said, his voice a hoarse croak.

Tom nodded, unable to speak. He picked up the ball of twine. There was a good deal of it left. He began to rewind it, his movements clumsy and jerky. They abandoned the pick-axe head. They abandoned the bacon and the cake. They wanted nothing but to feel the sun on their faces again.

The journey out was a nightmare of haste and fear. Every shadow was Injun Joe. Every echo was his footstep. They scurried along their twine-marked path, stumbling and panting, their candles throwing wild, fearful shadows. The great chamber was a hall of watching phantoms. The narrow passages were traps, ready to close in on them.

When at last they saw a pale, grey glimmer at the end of the tunnel, they nearly wept with relief. It was the entrance. It was the world.

They burst out of the cave’s mouth and into the late afternoon sun, blinking like owls. They did not stop running until they were halfway down the hill, where they collapsed, gasping for breath, in a thicket of sumac. For several minutes they lay there, their lungs burning, their bodies trembling with the aftermath of terror. The warmth of the sun soaked into their clothes, a feeling so wonderful and so normal it was almost painful.

“He was there, Huck,” Tom finally said, his voice full of awe. “He was right there.”

“I know,” said Huck. He sat up and looked back towards the cave, his face grim. “And the treasure’s still there, too. And he knows where it is. And he’s waiting.”

A heavy silence fell between them. The glorious adventure had turned into something dark and deadly serious. The money was real, but so was the murderer. And they were just two boys.

“We can’t do it,” Huck said flatly. “Not by ourselves. We can’t carry it, and we can’t risk running into him again. We was lucky this time, Tom. Mighty lucky. There won’t be a next time.”

Tom knew he was right. The image of that dancing shadow, the sound of that cough, was burned into his mind. His grand plans had crumbled to dust in the face of that one terrible moment of reality. But the thought of the gold, of letting it go… it was unthinkable.

“We need help,” Tom said, his mind already starting to spin new webs of strategy. “We need to tell somebody.”

“Who?” Huck demanded, his voice sharp with suspicion. “Who you gonna tell? The sheriff? You tell him and the money goes to the county, or the Thatchers, or somebody. We’d get a thank-you and maybe a dollar. You want to tell Aunt Polly? She’d faint dead away and then tan your hide for a week.”

“No, no, not them,” Tom said, sitting up. A spark of his old fire was returning. “We need a partner. Somebody strong. Somebody who can keep a secret. Somebody who ain’t afraid of… of him.”

Huck looked skeptical. “There ain’t many men like that, Tom. And any man who ain’t afraid of Injun Joe is likely a man you don’t want to be partners with in the first place.”

“I know one,” Tom said slowly, a daring and magnificent idea beginning to form in his head. “I know just the fellow. He’s strong as an ox. And he owes me a good turn, anyway.”

“Who?” Huck asked again, peering at Tom’s face.

Tom leaned in, his eyes shining with the thrill of his new, desperate plan. “Muff Potter.”

The logic was as full of holes as a fisherman’s net, but it was Tom Sawyer logic, and it had a certain magnificent shine to it. Huck was not convinced, but he was swayed. The thought of poor Muff Potter, who had often shared a bit of his fish or a crust of bread with Huck when no one else would, sitting in that grim little jailhouse, was a heavy weight.
“How we gonna talk to him?” Huck asked, surrendering to the inevitable tide of Tom’s ambition. “Can’t just walk in and say howdy.”
“We’ll go tonight,” Tom declared. “They feed him through a little window in the back. The constable’s lazy. We can get to him. We just have to.”

So that night, when the village of St. Petersburg was wrapped in the profound silence of sleep, two small shadows detached themselves from the fence behind the widow Douglas’s house and flitted down the deserted streets. The moon was a slender shaving in the sky, providing just enough light to make the darkness seem thicker and more menacing in the spaces between the whitewashed fences and sleeping cottages. Every creak of a floorboard from within a house, every distant bark of a dog, sent the boys diving for the cover of a woodpile or a rain barrel.
The jail was a small, sad-looking brick building that stood by itself near the edge of town, a monument to the village’s minor sins. It had two cells, and its windows were grimly barred. As Tom had predicted, there was a small, grated opening in the rear wall, low to the ground, through which a prisoner’s meager victuals were passed. A faint, flickering light from a tallow candle inside told them that Muff Potter was awake.
They crept up to the wall, their hearts thumping like muffled drums. Tom put his mouth close to the grate.
“Muff?” he whispered. “Muff Potter?”
There was a shuffling sound from within, and then a haggard face appeared in the dim light of the opening. It was a shambling wreck of a man. Muff Potter’s eyes were bloodshot and hollow, his face was pale and stubbled, and his hands trembled ceaselessly. He looked like a man who had been dragged through hell backwards.
“Who’s that?” he rasped, his voice dry and cracked.
“It’s us, Muff. Tom Sawyer and Huck Finn.”
A flicker of recognition, then confusion, crossed the prisoner’s face. “Tom? Little Huck? What you boys doin’ here? You ought to be in your beds. This ain’t no place for you.”
“We brought you something,” said Tom, pushing a small package wrapped in a leaf through the grate. It was a piece of their boiled ham and a plug of chewing tobacco Huck had procured.
Muff’s trembling hands fumbled with the offering. He took the ham and ate it with a starved urgency that was painful to watch. He did not touch the tobacco.
“Bless you, boys,” he said, his voice thick with emotion. “Bless your hearts. Ain’t nobody been to see me. Nobody but the lawyer, and he just looks at me like I’m already a ghost.” His voice broke. “They’re gonna hang me, Tom. I know they are. I was drunk, see. I don’t remember it right. But they found my knife. It was my knife.”
“It wasn’t you, Muff,” Tom said, his voice low and intense.
Muff Potter looked up, his eyes widening. “What’s that, son?”
“It wasn’t you,” Tom repeated. “We know. We was there.”
The silence that followed was absolute. Muff Potter simply stared, his jaw slack, as if Tom’s words were a language he could not comprehend. Huck nudged Tom, a silent warning, but Tom was too far gone in his mission.
Slowly, carefully, Tom recounted the entire dreadful story—the graveyard, the argument over the corpse, the doctor’s blow, and Injun Joe’s terrible, swift revenge. He told him how the half-breed had put the bloody knife in Muff’s hand and told the lie that had sealed his fate.
Muff Potter listened, his breathing growing shallow. The color drained completely from his face. He sank back from the window, out of their sight, and they heard a low moan. Then he was back, his fingers clutching the bars.
“Injun Joe?” he whispered. “It was him? And he let me… he let me think…” He couldn’t finish. A great, shuddering sob shook his whole body. “Oh, I’m a fool, a miserable drunken fool. He was my friend, I thought. He was.” He wept then, not loudly, but with the deep, racking despair of a man whose last frayed strand of hope had been a lie.
“Don’t you worry, Muff,” Tom said, trying to sound braver than he felt. “We’re going to fix it. We have a plan. But you have to promise to be our partner.”
“Partner?” Muff asked, wiping his eyes with the back of his hand. “Partner in what?”
“The treasure!” Tom hissed. “Injun Joe’s treasure. We know where he’s hid it. But we need your help to get it. When you get out, you help us, and we split it three ways. You’ll be rich, Muff! You’ll never have to worry about anything again.”
The proposition was too much for the broken man’s mind. Treasure, freedom, Injun Joe—it was a whirlwind. He just shook his head. “You got to tell ‘em, Tom. You got to tell the judge. Never mind the treasure. Just… just tell ‘em what you saw. I don’t want to hang.”
“I will,” said Tom. “I promise. At the trial. You just hold on, Muff. You just hold on.”
They left him there, a little pile of misery huddled on the floor of his cell, and slipped back into the night. The bravado of their mission had evaporated, leaving behind a cold, heavy residue of fear. They had looked into the face of the man they were trying to save, and had seen the true cost of their silence.

The days that followed were a torment for Tom Sawyer. The secret he carried was no longer a grand and thrilling adventure; it was a hot coal in his gut. The date of the trial loomed, and the village buzzed with grim anticipation. Public opinion was solid against Muff Potter. He was the town drunk, a weak and harmless man when sober, but who knew what evils lurked in the bottle? They had found him with the knife; he had been seen arguing with the doctor. It was an open and shut case.
Tom’s conscience gave him no peace. He took to staring at his food instead of eating it. He lost interest in plotting new piracies or organizing his army. His nights were a purgatory of tossing and turning. He would dream of the graveyard, of the flash of the knife, of Muff Potter’s despairing face at the jail window, and of Injun Joe’s eyes, cold and deadly, promising retribution. He had sworn an oath in blood, the most sacred bond a boy could conceive. To break it was to invite a terrible and unspecified doom. But to keep it was to let an innocent man go to the gallows.
Aunt Polly, with her sharp and loving eyes, saw the change. She dosed him with her entire formidable arsenal of patent medicines, from Pain-killer to sulphur-and-molasses, convinced his humors were out of balance. But no remedy could touch the sickness in his soul.
Huck, too, was miserable. He haunted Tom’s steps like a worried ghost, his face pinched and his eyes constantly scanning the shadows, as if expecting Injun Joe to materialize at any moment.
“You still gonna do it, Tom?” he would ask, a dozen times a day.
And Tom, his face pale and set, would just nod. “I have to.”

The day of the trial arrived. The sun beat down on St. Petersburg, baking the dust in the streets. The whole town, it seemed, had crammed itself into the small courthouse. The air within was thick as wool and smelled of dust and unwashed humanity. Fans fluttered like a thousand captured moths, doing little to stir the oppressive heat. Tom and Huck had found a place in the back, half hidden behind a pillar, feeling very small and very scared.
The trial proceeded just as everyone had expected. The prosecuting attorney laid out the evidence with methodical precision. He called witnesses who testified to Muff Potter’s drunkenness and his argument with Dr. Robinson. He produced the knife, and the constable swore it was found in Muff’s hand. Muff’s own lawyer, a young and inexperienced man assigned by the court, could do little but offer feeble objections. Muff himself sat slumped in his chair, a picture of hopeless resignation, occasionally looking with a kind of pleading terror towards the back of the room where he knew the boys were.
Finally, the prosecutor rested his case. “The defense may call its witnesses,” said Judge Thatcher, his face stern and somber.
Muff Potter’s lawyer stood up. “Your honor,” he said, his voice barely audible, “we have no witnesses to call.”
A murmur went through the crowd. It was over. The man had no defense. Judge Thatcher looked gravely at the prisoner, and was about to instruct the jury, when a small sound escaped from Tom’s throat, a strangled squeak.
Huck grabbed his arm. “Tom, no! Don’t!”
But it was too late. Tom was already moving. He pushed past Huck and walked, as if in a trance, down the central aisle. Every eye in the courtroom turned to follow the small, determined figure. He stopped before the judge’s bench.
“I have to tell,” he said, his voice high and trembling but clear as a bell. “It wasn’t him.”
A collective gasp went through the room. Judge Thatcher stared down at his son’s friend, his eyebrows raised in astonishment. Aunt Polly, near the front, looked as if she might faint. The prosecuting attorney was on his feet, objecting, but the judge silenced him with a wave of his hand.
“Thomas Sawyer,” he said, his voice resonating with authority. “Do you know the meaning of an oath?”
“Yes, sir.”
“Then come up here.”
Tom was sworn in. He stood in the witness box, so small his head barely cleared the railing. The silence in the room was so profound he could hear the buzzing of a fly against a windowpane. And in that silence, he looked out and saw him. Injun Joe was sitting against the far wall, his arms crossed, his face an unreadable mask. Their eyes met for a fraction of a second, and Tom’s blood ran cold.
“Now, son,” the judge said gently. “Tell us what you know.”
Tom took a deep breath and began. He told it all. His voice, shaky at first, grew stronger as the terrible burden was lifted. He told of the pact to cure warts, the visit to the graveyard, the arrival of the three men. The courtroom was spellbound, leaning forward as one, not wanting to miss a single word of the incredible, horrifying tale. When he described the murder, a shudder passed through the crowd. He told how Injun Joe, with the doctor lying senseless, had plunged the knife in and then placed it in Potter’s limp hand.
“And this man here,” the prosecutor interrupted, pointing dramatically at the half-breed, “Injun Joe, you say you saw him do this?”
“Yes, sir,” Tom said, his eyes fixed on the man.
All at once, every single person in the courtroom turned to stare at Injun Joe. For a moment, he remained perfectly still, a cornered wolf surveying the hunters. Then, with a speed that was terrifying and inhuman, he was on his feet. He did not run for the door. Like a catamount he sprang for the nearest window. There was a crash of glass and splintered wood as he burst through the sash. For an instant his dark form was silhouetted against the bright afternoon sun, and then he was gone.
Pandemonium erupted. The crowd surged to its feet, shouting and pointing. The sheriff and his deputies scrambled in clumsy pursuit. But it was no use. By the time they reached the street, Injun Joe had vanished.
In the chaos, no one at first paid any mind to Muff Potter. He was on his feet, his hands outstretched towards Tom, his face streaming with tears. “God bless you, boy! God bless you!” he cried, and then he fainted dead away and had to be carried out.
Tom Sawyer was the hero of the hour. He was clapped on the back, his hair was ruffled, and his praises were sung by everyone in town. He had saved an innocent man from the gallows and faced down a murderer. He was a marvel, a paragon of boyish courage. Aunt Polly wept over him and hugged him and promised him anything he wanted, once she was done tanning his hide for being out so late in a graveyard.
Tom lapped it up; it was the kind of glory he had always dreamed of. He was the center of the universe. But that night, as he lay in his bed, the adulation of the town seemed a thin and flimsy shield. The window of his room was open to the dark, and every rustle of the leaves, every creak of the house, was the footstep of Injun Joe. The treasure was still in the cave, but now a second, more terrible prize was in play. Tom Sawyer was a hero, but he was also a marked man. He had won the battle, but he knew, with a certainty that chilled him to the bone, that the war had only just begun.

The glory of being a hero was a fine thing in the daylight, when a boy could walk down the middle of the street and see admiration in the eyes of his fellows and even in the faces of respectable grown ups who usually only saw him as a source of mischief. Every familiar sound of the old house was transformed into a threat. The creak of a floorboard was the stealthy tread of a moccasin on the stair; the rustle of the vine against his window was the scraping of a knife against the glass; the sigh of the wind in the eaves was a low moan that might be a man’s.

He had won, yes; he had stood up in a court of law and spoken the truth, and a good man was saved on account of it. But in doing so he had pointed a finger, and the man at whom he had pointed was not the sort to forget. Injun Joe was a specter, a creature of shadow and violence, and now that specter had Tom’s name and his face and knew the house where he slept. The adulation of the village felt very far away. “Pleased? Huck, you saw his face! He looked like the devil himself. He’ll come for us in the night. He’ll slit our throats.” Tom’s imagination, usually a source of such grand adventures, was now a factory of horrors, and it was working at full capacity.

“Well,” said Huck, with his infuriating practicality, “we ain’t got our throats slit yet. So there’s that.”

“But we will! Don’t you understand? We can’t just go on like before. Every night I lay there and I wait for him. I hear him in every sound. I can’t eat. I can’t sleep. I’m scared, Huck.”

It was a stark confession, stripped of all Tom’s usual bravado. The darkness of the hogshead made it possible. Huckleberry, who had been scared every day of his life in one way or another, understood. He was scared of his pap, scared of being hungry, scared of being caught and “sivilized” by the widow. This new fear was different only in its sharpness.

“Me too,” Huck said, so low Tom could barely hear him. “I been sleepin’ in a new place every night. Figgered it’d be harder for him to find me if I ain’t never where I was the night before. Been keepin’ off the main roads.”

This was a revelation to Tom. While he had been lying in his warm bed, paralyzed by fear, Huck had been taking action. It was a humiliating thought, but also a bracing one.

“We got to have a plan,” Tom said, his mind beginning to work again, clicking into its familiar gear of strategy and plot. “We can’t just wait for him to find us. We got to be ready.”

“Ready for what?” said Huck. “We’re just boys. He’s… him. He’s Injun Joe.” He said the name like it was a curse.

“I know,” Tom said. “But we’re two and he’s one. And we’re smarter. We got to have signals. If you see him, or see anything that looks like him, you got to warn me. And I’ll warn you.”

“What kind of signal?”

“A new one,” said Tom, warming to his subject. “The cat meow is for fun. This is for… for blood. It’s got to be something nobody else would do. Something that’ll carry. How about a screech owl? Can you do a screech owl?”

Huck produced a sound from the back of his throat that was so uncanny, so full of midnight loneliness and graveyard dread, that Tom nearly jumped out of his skin.

“Jings, Huck! That’s it! That’s perfect. If you ever hear me do that, it means he’s here. And if I hear you…”

“You’ll run like blazes,” Huck finished for him. “And you’ll do the same. No heroes, Tom. Just runnin’.”

“Right,” Tom agreed, though the word “hero” still had a pleasant ring to it. “And we got to have a meeting place. A safe place. Not here. He might know about this place.”

They debated for some time. The hayloft in the deserted tannery was proposed and rejected; it had too few exits. The thicket up on Cardiff Hill was too far. They finally settled on the little lean-to behind the Welshman’s barn. It was close enough to the edge of town to be a quick refuge, and the Welshman kept a blunderbuss and was known to be a light sleeper.

They sealed their new, grim pact with a spit and a handshake, and Tom felt a sliver of his courage return. He was still afraid, but he was no longer alone in his fear. He was part of a conspiracy again, and that was familiar territory.

The next few days were a strange mixture of public triumph and private terror. In town, Tom was still the boy who had faced down a murderer. He was treated to free licorice at the general store and was allowed to hang around the blacksmith’s shop, a privilege usually reserved for older boys. He walked with a bit of a swagger, as befit his station. But it was a performance. His eyes were always scanning the alleys, the rooftops, the faces in any small crowd. He practiced the screech owl call in the woods until it was nearly as good as Huck’s.

One evening, about a week after the trial, the two boys were sitting on a log by the river’s edge, pretending to fish. Neither had the heart for it. The sun was setting, painting the sky in garish streaks of orange and purple.

“Anything?” Tom asked.

“Nothin’,” said Huck. “Sheriff’s posse rode out towards the Pikey County line, but they come back with nothin’ but tired horses. It’s like he just vanished into the ground.”

“He ain’t vanished,” Tom said grimly. “He’s just waitin’. He’s smart, Huck. He knows they’re looking for him. He’ll wait till they give up. Till we let our guard down.”

“Well, my guard ain’t never comin’ down,” Huck declared. “It’s stuck up.”

They sat in silence, watching the river roll by. The last of the sun’s light caught on the water, turning it to a sheet of molten gold.

“Huck,” Tom said, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. “The treasure.”

Huck stiffened. “What about it?”

“It’s still there. In the haunted house.”

“And it can stay there,” Huck said firmly. “I ain’t goin’ near that place. Not now. Why, that’s the first place he’d think to hide out.”

“No it ain’t!” Tom said, his excitement rising. “That’s the last place! It’s too obvious. Everybody knows it’s haunted. The sheriff wouldn’t look there in a million years. And Injun Joe, he’s superstitious. You know he is.”

Huck looked doubtful. “I don’t know, Tom. It feels like temptin’ fate.”

“It’s the perfect time!” Tom insisted. “While everyone’s looking for him out in the woods, he’s probably a hundred miles away. We could go in, get the treasure, and be rich before he even thinks about coming back.”

The old gleam was in Tom’s eye, the one that promised adventure and glory and, in this case, a literal box of gold. Huck was not immune to it. To be rich… to never be hungry again, to have a real bed and all the tobacco you could ever want. It was a powerful dream. But the nightmare was more powerful.

“And what if you’re wrong?” Huck said. “What if he ain’t a hundred miles away? What if he’s in there right now, sittin’ on that box, just sharpening his knife and waitin’ for us?”

The image was so vivid, so terrifying, that it silenced Tom completely. He saw it all: the dusty, cobwebbed room, the splintered floor, and in the center of it, the dark, hulking shape of Injun Joe, his eyes glinting in the gloom. The dream of gold vanished, replaced by the certainty of death.

“You’re right,” Tom breathed. “You’re right.”

The project was abandoned. The fear was king.

Another week went by. The town began to relax. The story of Tom’s testimony and Injun Joe’s escape was already becoming a legend, something to be told and retold, losing its sharp edges of reality with each telling. But for Tom and Huck, the edges were as sharp as ever. They kept their vigils.

One night, a hot, sticky night when the moon was hidden behind a film of cloud, Tom was awakened by a sound. It was not the house creaking, nor the wind. It was a sound from outside, from the street. The screech owl call.

Tom was out of his bed in an instant, his blood like ice water in his veins. The call came again, faint but unmistakable, filled with an urgent terror. It was Huck. He was in trouble.

Tom’s first instinct was the one they had agreed upon: run. Hide. Save himself. He stood trembling in the middle of his room, his pact with Huck warring with the primal urge for survival. But then another thought, a more Tom Sawyerish thought, pushed its way through the panic. Huck was his friend. Huck had sounded the alarm for him. Could he, Tom Sawyer, the hero of the hour, the slayer of imaginary dragons and the confounder of real murderers, abandon his comrade in his moment of need? The answer was no. It was simply not in his nature.

He grabbed the first weapon he could find, which was a heavy brass candlestick from his aunt’s dressing table. It was a poor sort of sword, but it had weight. He crept downstairs, his heart pounding a frantic rhythm, a drumbeat for a battle he was sure to lose. He did not go out the back. He went to the front parlor, to the window that faced the street. He peered cautiously around the edge of the heavy curtain.

The street was empty. The gaslights cast long, eerie shadows. There was no sign of Huck, no sign of anyone. Had he imagined it? Was his fear playing tricks on him?

Then he saw it. A movement, down the street, near the fence of the old abandoned garden. A flicker of motion. Someone was hiding there. Tom’s knuckles were white where he gripped the candlestick. He watched, not daring to breathe. A figure detached itself from the shadows of the fence and darted across the street, disappearing into the blackness of the alley beside the Temperance Tavern. The figure was short and ragged. It was Huck.

But he was not being chased. He was following.

Tom watched, bewildered, as a second figure emerged from the garden. This one was taller, and walked with a slight limp. The man was wrapped in a horse-blanket, despite the heat, and he carried something in his hand, a sack of some kind. He moved slowly, cautiously, down the street in the same direction Huck had gone. He passed directly under a gaslight, and for a single, heart-stopping moment, his face was illuminated.

It was not Injun Joe.

It was an old, bewhiskered man, a stranger. But his face was mean, and his eyes were shifty and cruel. He was the very picture of a desperado. Tom realized with a jolt that Huck had not been calling for help. He had been calling for backup. This was not a retreat; it was an advance.

A whole new kind of thrill shot through Tom, displacing the fear. This was it. This was the real thing. A secret, midnight pursuit of a dangerous criminal. It was better than pirates, better than Robin Hood, because it was happening right now, on his street.

He did not hesitate. The pact was forgotten, or rather, it was amended on the spot. He slipped out the back door, candlestick held at the ready, and, keeping to the shadows as he had seen Huck do, he began to follow the follower.

The man in the horse-blanket led them away from the center of town, towards the dilapidated riverfront warehouses. Huck trailed him like a shadow, and Tom trailed Huck like a second, more clumsy shadow, his bare feet making whisper-soft sounds on the packed earth. The air was thick with the smell of tar and stagnant water.

The man stopped before the most derelict warehouse of all, its windows boarded up, its paint peeling like sunburnt skin. He looked around, his head darting back and forth like a nervous bird’s. Then he produced a key, fumbled with the lock on a small side door, and disappeared inside.

Huck crept up to the building and flattened himself against the wall beside the door. Tom crept up and joined him. They were so close they could hear each other’s breathing.

“What’s going on?” Tom whispered.

Huck jumped, startled, but did not cry out. “Tom! You idjit! I thought you was him.”

“Who is he?” Tom hissed back.

“Don’t know. Him and another one. I seen ’em come off the ferry late. They was talkin’ low, talkin’ about a ‘job.’ This one’s name is Jake.”

“What’s in the sack?”

“Tools,” Huck breathed. “I heard ’em clink. They’re burglars, Tom. Real, honest-to-goodness burglars.”

The word hung in the air between them, shimmering with a terrible and wonderful glamour. Burglars. They had stumbled upon a genuine crime in progress. The fear of Injun Joe seemed, for the moment, a distant and manageable thing. Here was a new, more immediate adventure.

“What do we do?” Tom whispered, his mind racing.

“We watch,” Huck said. “We see what they’re up to.”

There was a crack in the wall of the warehouse, a place where a board had rotted through. Huck put his eye to it.

“I can see ’em,” he reported in a hushed play-by-play. “There’s the other one. He’s big. Ugly as sin. They’ve lit a lantern. They’re… they’re pryin’ up the floorboards, Tom. Right in the middle of the floor.”

Tom’s heart gave a great leap. “The treasure!” he gasped.

“What?”

“It’s just like in the book!” Tom’s excitement was nearly uncontrollable. “The robbers always bury their loot under the floor of a deserted building! Huck, they’re not here to steal something. They’re here to hide something!”

The possibilities were dazzling. This was their chance. If they could see where the burglars hid their ill-gotten gains, they could come back later, after the coast was clear, and claim it for themselves. It was a far safer prospect than facing Injun Joe in the haunted house. This was found money, practically a gift from Providence.

Huck, whose moral compass was simpler and more direct, was not so sure. “It’s theirn, Tom. Even if they stole it, it’s theirn now.”

“It ain’t!” Tom argued fiercely. “It’s stolen money! Finders keepers! It’s for the public good that we take it. We’re practically deputies.”

Huck was swayed by this magnificent logic. The two boys pressed themselves against the rough wall of the warehouse, their eyes and ears straining, no longer two frightened children, but a pair of determined detectives on the verge of a great discovery. The shadow of Injun Joe still lay over their lives, a vast and chilling presence. But for tonight, in the hot, tar-scented darkness of the riverfront, a new and exciting shadow had fallen, and in its cover, they felt, for the first time in weeks, something that felt almost like being boys again. For gold was a powerful lure, but life was a sweeter thing still, and for now, the two were set at odds in a most disagreeable fashion.

The sound of splintering wood was sharp and decisive in the humid air. Huck flinched, but Tom’s hand shot out and gripped his arm, a silent command to hold still. Through the crack, Huck saw the larger of the two men, the one with a face like a slab of raw beef, toss a length of floorboard aside. The other man, Jake, knelt down and reached into the dark space below. The boys held their breath. Jake’s shoulders strained, and he grunted with effort. He was pulling something heavy out of the hole.

“Got her,” Jake said, his voice a low gravelly sound. He heaved, and with a final scrape, a small, iron-bound chest, no bigger than a breadbox, was sitting on the dusty floor. It was black with age and damp, and glistened in the lantern light.

Tom’s grip on Huck’s arm tightened until it was nearly painful. His eyes were wide in the gloom, shining like a cat’s. “Huck,” he breathed, the word a reverent sigh. “A real treasure chest.”

The big man, who Jake called Red, nudged the box with his boot. “Well, open her up. Let’s see the color of it.”

“Not here, you fool,” Jake snapped. “You want to flash it around for the whole town to see? We got it. It’s safe. That’s enough for tonight.”

“Safe for now,” Red grumbled, wiping sweat from his brow with the back of a greasy hand. “I don’t like this spot. Too close to the water. Too many folks comin’ and goin’, even at night. We ought to move it.”

“And where do you suppose we move it?” Jake asked, his tone dripping with sarcasm. “To the Temperance Tavern? Maybe deposit it at the new bank?”

“I’m just sayin’,” Red said, his voice sullen. “It ain’t right, havin’ all that cash and us sleepin’ in a hayloft. I got an itch to spend some.”

“You spend one dollar of this before I say so and I’ll cut your throat while you sleep,” Jake said, and there was no humor in it at all. “We got this off the St. Louis packet, and they’ll be lookin’ for it. They know it’s gone. We lay low. We wait. When things cool down, we split it and go our separate ways. Understand?”

Red kicked at a loose nail on the floor. “I understand,” he muttered. “Still don’t like this place.”

Jake was silent for a moment, thinking. He ran a hand over his bristly chin. “You know,” he said slowly, “the big oaf might have a point for once. This ain’t the best den. We need someplace folks stay clear of. Someplace that’s got a reputation.” He straightened up, and a slow smile spread across his narrow face. “I know the very spot.”

“Where’s that?”

“Up on Cardiff Hill. That old rickety house that’s supposed to be haunted. The one the Welshman’s widow used to own.”

Huck felt a jolt go through him as if he’d touched a live wire. He felt Tom go rigid beside him. The haunted house. *Their* haunted house. The place where they had last seen Injun Joe.

“A spook house?” Red said, a nervous edge to his voice. “I don’t know, Jake. I ain’t partial to ghosts.”

“There ain’t no ghosts, you idiot,” Jake said with contempt. “Just stories to keep kids and ninnies away. It’s perfect. Nobody’s been near it in years. We can hide the box there, under the hearthstone maybe, and it’ll be safe as houses. Safer. Nobody will ever think to look.”

A board under Tom’s knee gave a protesting groan, a sound no louder than a sigh, but in that charged stillness it rang out like a pistol shot. Both boys froze, their blood turning to ice water.

Inside the warehouse, the talking stopped.

“What was that?” Red whispered, his head snapping toward the wall.

“Shh,” Jake hissed. He blew out the lantern in one quick puff, plunging the warehouse into absolute blackness.

The boys didn’t dare breathe. They were pinned against the wall, two statues of terror. Huck could hear the frantic drumming of his own heart; it seemed loud enough to betray them. He could feel Tom trembling beside him. Minutes stretched into an eternity. The only sounds were the gentle lapping of the river against the pilings and the maddening drone of a mosquito near Huck’s ear.

Then came a new sound. A soft, scraping footstep inside. Then another. They were coming toward the door.

Huck’s mind went blank with panic. There was nowhere to run, nowhere to hide. They were caught. They would be murdered and their bodies tossed into the black river, never to be found. He squeezed his eyes shut, bracing for the door to be flung open, for a rough hand to grab his collar.

A heavy body thumped against the inside of the wall, right next to where they were hiding. A voice, Red’s voice, rumbled through the wood, so close that Huck felt the vibration in his teeth.

“Don’t see nothin’. Probably just a rat. This place is full of ’em.”

There was a pause, and then Jake’s voice, farther away. “Probably. Still, let’s get out of here. This place gives me the creeps now. Grab the box. We’ll take it to the skiff.”

There were sounds of scuffling, another grunt of effort, and then the creak of the back door of the warehouse, the one that opened onto the water. The boys heard the scrape of a boat being pushed into the river, the dip and splash of oars, and then, fading into the distance, the rhythmic creak of oarlocks.

For a full minute after the sounds had vanished, neither boy moved a muscle. Then Tom let out a long, shuddering breath and sagged against the wall, his legs suddenly turned to jelly.

“They’re gone,” he whispered.

“Shucks, Tom, I thought we was goners,” Huck breathed, finally daring to move. His shirt was soaked with sweat and stuck to his back. “That was too close. Way too close.”

Tom pushed himself upright. The terror of the last few minutes was already receding, replaced by the electric thrill of their discovery. His mind was on fire with the implications.

“Huck, did you hear? Did you hear what they said?”

“I heard,” Huck said grimly. “They’re takin’ it to the haunted house. The one on Cardiff Hill.” The name tasted like ash in his mouth.

“It’s Providence, Huck! It’s a sign!” Tom’s voice was filled with a feverish energy. “Don’t you see? We were scared of the haunted house because of Injun Joe. But now the treasure’s going to be there! We were right all along, just in the wrong way!”

Huck looked at Tom as if he had lost his senses. “Are you crazy? That makes it worse! A hundred times worse! First it was just Injun Joe and his partner, and now it’s them two burglars besides. The place is goin’ to be crawlin’ with murderers and thieves! It’s the most dangerous spot in the whole state of Missouri!”

“But that’s the beauty of it!” Tom insisted, grabbing Huck’s shoulders. “They don’t know about each other! The burglars think it’s empty, and Injun Joe thinks it’s his. And we know about both of ’em! We have the advantage, Huck!”

“The advantage to get killed twice!” Huck retorted, shaking him off. “No, sir. I’m done. I was done with that house when we saw Injun Joe, and I’m double-done now. They can have their rotten treasure. I’d rather have my skin, and I’m right fond of it.”

Huck turned to leave, his mind set. He wanted nothing more than to be back in his hogshead, with this whole terrifying night behind him. But Tom’s voice stopped him.

“Huck, wait. Think about it. Thousands of dollars.”

“I am thinkin’ about it,” Huck said, not turning around. “And I’m thinkin’ about how a dead boy can’t spend none of it.”

“We wouldn’t have to fight ’em,” Tom said, his voice becoming smooth and persuasive. He knew Huck’s weak points. “We’d be spies. That’s all. We’d do just what we did tonight. We’d watch from a distance. We’d find out their habits. Jake said it hisself, they’re going to lay low. That means they’ll hide the box and stay away for weeks, maybe months. And Injun Joe, he’s got other business. He ain’t goin’ to sit in that house day and night. We’ll find a time when _nobody’s_ there. We’ll just sneak in, grab the box, and be rich forever. It’s perfectly safe.”

Huck was wavering. The picture Tom painted was a powerful one: the quiet house, the sleeping villains, the two of them slipping in like shadows and emerging with a fortune. It was almost believable. But the face of Injun Joe, illuminated by the candlelight in that ruined room, was a more powerful picture still.

“He’d find us,” Huck said, his voice dropping to a whisper. “Somehow, he’d know. He’s not regular people, Tom. He’s half devil. He’d hunt us down.”

“How could he? Nobody knows but us. We wouldn’t tell a soul. We’d hide the money and only spend a little at a time. We’d be smart about it. We’d be millionaires, Huck! We could buy a steamboat! We could have a circus all our own! We’d never have to worry about Pap, or school, or anything ever again.”

That last part struck home. To never have to worry about his father again; it was a vision of paradise so profound that Huck could scarcely comprehend it. He turned back to face Tom. The lure of gold was a strong pull, but the lure of freedom was a mighty tide.

“You swear we wouldn’t take no chances?” he asked, his voice small. “Just watchin’?”

“Scout’s honor,” Tom said, though he was not a scout and his honor was a flexible commodity when adventure was at stake. “We’ll be careful as mice. We’ll make a map. We’ll have a plan. We’ll know everything they do before they do it. It’ll be the greatest adventure in history, Huck, and we’ll be rich at the end of it.”

Huck chewed on his lip, his gaze fixed on the dark, whispering river. The burglars’ skiff was long gone. They were on their way to Cardiff Hill right now, to the one place on earth he never wanted to see again. And Tom, his best and only friend, was proposing they follow. It was madness. It was suicide. It was also the most thrilling, magnificent, and terrible idea he had ever heard.

“All right,” he said, the words coming out in a rush before he could take them back. “All right, Tom. I’ll do it. We’ll spy on ’em. But if it looks like we’re goin’ to get caught, I’m runnin’. I ain’t waitin’ around to get murdered for no treasure.”

“It’s a deal!” Tom said, his spirits soaring. He clapped Huck on the back with the joyous camaraderie of a general whose reluctant lieutenant has just agreed to a desperate charge. “We’ll start tomorrow night. We’ll go up the hill and find a good spot to watch the house from. Oh, it’ll be glorious!”

Huck did not feel that it would be glorious. He felt a cold dread settling in the pit of his stomach, a dread that was even colder than the fear he had felt when the burglars were at the door. He had a premonition, a deep and certain feeling that he had just made a terrible mistake. He had traded one manageable fear for a conspiracy of them; he had looked into the shadowy face of a new adventure and found that it had the same terrible eyes as the old one. He had agreed to walk back into the lion’s den, and he could not shake the feeling that this time, the lion would be awake.

“Come on,” Tom said, already tugging him away from the warehouse. “Let’s go home and get some sleep. We got big plans to make tomorrow.”

They crept away through the sleeping streets of St. Petersburg, two small and thoroughly frightened figures under a sky full of indifferent stars. One of them was dreaming of gold and glory, of outwitting villains and becoming a hero straight out of a storybook. The other was dreaming of a dark house on a lonely hill, and of the many, many ways a boy could die in it. And so, bound by a terrible and wonderful secret, they went their separate ways into the darkness, each to his own uneasy bed, to wait for a night that promised to bring them either fabulous fortune or a very permanent and unpleasant end. The choice, it seemed, was no longer theirs to make.

The next day was a Saturday, and a misery. For Huck, at any rate. Tom Sawyer awoke with the sun, a general reviewing his troops on the morning of a decisive battle. He was all fizz and motion, a human firecracker with a slow burning fuse of anticipation. Huck, on the other hand, felt as though he had swallowed a bag of gravel during the night, and it had settled low in his belly. Sleep had not banished his fears; it had only marinated them.

“Huck! You’re up!” Tom greeted him by the woodshed, where Huck had been trying to convince himself that a day of fishing was a far more sensible occupation for a boy than spying on murderers. Tom was armed to the teeth, in a manner of speaking. He had a lath sword, which had seen better days in its former life as part of a plaster wall, tucked into his belt. A tin trumpet, battered and breathless, hung from his neck by a piece of twine. In his hands was the centerpiece of the whole operation: a vast and complicated map drawn on a piece of butcher’s paper.

“What’s all that?” Huck asked, eyeing the trumpet with particular suspicion.

“This?” Tom unfurled the map with a flourish. “This is the plan of attack. Or, well, the plan of observation. See here.” He pointed a grimy finger at a large, wobbly rectangle. “This is the Widow Douglas’s house. Here’s the front porch, and these little squares are the windows. Now, the hill slopes down this way—see the squiggly lines? That’s for steepness.”

Huck looked. The map was a bewildering collection of smudges, corrections, and imaginative geography. A thing marked ‘Big Rok’ seemed to be located in the middle of what was labeled ‘Dense Forest Area (Danger!),’ and a skull and crossbones had been drawn with loving care over the Widow’s front door.

“We can’t go in the front door, Tom,” Huck said, with the patience of one explaining the sky is blue. “That’s where they’ll be lookin’.”

“Of course we can’t, Huck! You think I’m a fool?” Tom said, offended. “That’s just for reference. The real plan is here.” He traced a convoluted path that seemed to start in the river, climb an imaginary cliff, and end in a bush helpfully labeled ‘Our Spot.’ “We’ll approach from the east, under cover of darkness. We’ll establish our observation post here, behind this big lilac bush I remember seeing once. It’s got a perfect line of sight to the side parlor. That’s where the widow keeps her silver, I bet. That’s what they’re after.”

“They ain’t after silver, Tom,” Huck said quietly, the gravel in his stomach grinding. “They was talkin’ about… about hurtin’ her.”

Tom’s face clouded for a moment. The glorious adventure flickered, and the grim reality Huck had spoken of tried to push its way in. But Tom was a master at keeping such realities at bay. He gave his lath sword a confident hitch. “Well, that’s why we have to be there, isn’t it? To stop them! We’re the guardians, Huck. The secret sentinels. Now, here’s the signal.” He raised the tin trumpet to his lips.

“No!” Huck hissed, lunging forward and clapping a hand over the bell of the instrument. “Tom, you gone clean crazy? You blow that thing up on the hill and they’ll find us in two seconds. They’ll skin us alive.”

Tom looked disappointed, but he conceded the point. “All right, no trumpet. We’ll use a bird call. A whippoorwill. If you see danger, you do three whippoorwill calls. Like this: Whip-poor-will! Whip-poor-will! Whip-poor-will! Got it?”

“I can’t do no whippoorwill call, Tom.”

“Well, learn, then! It’s easy. Just pucker up and…” Tom puckered, but produced a sound more like a leaky pump than any bird known to nature. He tried again. And again. Finally he gave up, his cheeks red. “All right, no bird calls. If there’s trouble, you throw a rock at me. A small rock. Understand?”

“I understand throwin’ a rock,” Huck said. It was the first part of the plan that had made any sense to him.

So the day crawled by. Tom spent it in a fever of preparation, sharpening his sword on a stone, coiling ropes that would never be used, and reviewing his terrible map until he had it memorized, which was a feat in itself. Huck spent it feeling the sun on his back and wondering if he would ever feel it again. He thought about running. He could be halfway to the next county by nightfall. No one would miss him. But then he thought of Tom, going up that hill alone, armed with a tin trumpet and a piece of wood, full of splendid, courageous, and utterly fatal nonsense. And he knew he couldn’t run. A friend doesn’t let a friend get murdered by himself, not if he can help it. It was a miserable sort of loyalty, but it was the only kind Huck knew.

When dusk began to bleed across the sky, painting the Mississippi in shades of orange and purple, Tom appeared at Huck’s hogshead. He had dispensed with the trumpet and sword, which was a small relief. He carried a small sack containing two apples and a hunk of cornbread. “It’s time,” he whispered, his eyes wide with the thrill of it all. “The hour of destiny is at hand.”

Huck sighed and crawled out of his barrel. His destiny felt a good deal more like a stomach ache than a grand adventure.

They crept through the town as it was settling down for the night, sticking to back alleys and shadows. Tom moved with the exaggerated stealth of a stage Indian, while Huck, who had spent most of his life being invisible, simply melted into the gloom. They crossed the creek at the base of Cardiff Hill and began their ascent.

The woods were a different world at night. In the daytime, they were a place for games and exploration. Now, every rustle in the undergrowth was a footstep, every shadow a lurking foe. An owl hooted from a high branch, and both boys froze, their hearts hammering. Tom, for all his bravado, clutched Huck’s sleeve. His whisper was a little less confident now. “Just an owl, Huck. That’s all.”

“I know,” Huck whispered back, though his own skin was crawling.

They climbed, Tom leading the way with the misplaced confidence of his memorized map. He led them through a patch of sticker bushes that tore at their clothes and skin, and then into a damp hollow that smelled of decay.

“I think we’re lost,” Huck said, after they had passed the same peculiar, twisted oak tree for the third time.

“We’re not lost,” Tom insisted, though his voice had a quaver in it. “I’m just… circling, to throw off any pursuit.”

“There ain’t no pursuit, Tom. It’s just us.”

Finally, Huck, relying on the faint glow of the town lights below and the general lay of the land, took the lead. He found a deer trail that wound steadily upward, and in another quarter of an hour, they saw it: a single, yellow light, warm and steady, shining through a break in the trees. It was the Widow Douglas’s house.

They crept closer, their bellies to the damp ground, until they reached the edge of the manicured lawn. The house stood on a rise, a handsome two story affair, dark and silent except for that one lamp in a downstairs window. A large lilac bush, just as Tom had predicted, grew near the corner of the house, offering a perfect, dark space from which to view the side parlor. They crawled into its scratchy embrace, the scent of its leaves thick and dusty in the air.

And then they waited. The grand adventure, so full of maps and signals and glorious purpose, had dwindled to this: two boys shivering in a bush, watching a square of light. An hour passed. The light in the window did not move. The house remained silent. Tom’s excitement began to curdle into boredom. Huck’s terror settled into a dull, throbbing ache.

“This is the hardest part, Huck,” Tom whispered, his voice thin. “The waiting. It tests a man’s resolve.”

Huck said nothing. He was busy listening. His ears, honed by a lifetime of needing to know what was coming, were straining against the silence. Tom heard the crickets. Huck heard the spaces between the cricket chirps.

Another hour dragged its slow length along. Tom’s head began to nod. The cornbread was a distant memory, and the apples were long gone. The romance was wearing thin, and the reality was cold and damp. He was just about to suggest that maybe the burglars weren’t coming after all, that maybe they should just go home, when Huck’s fingers clamped down on his arm like a vise.

Huck didn’t speak. He didn’t have to. Tom went instantly rigid, every drowsy thought shocked out of him. He followed Huck’s gaze, peering through the leaves into the deeper darkness at the edge of the yard.

At first, there was nothing. Then, a shadow detached itself from another shadow. Then a second. They were not walking, but slinking, keeping low to the ground. They moved without a sound, two ghosts made of night. They stopped near the back corner of the house, not thirty feet from the boys’ bush, and conferred in low, guttural whispers that carried on the still air.

One of the figures was large and bulky. The other was slighter, but moved with a sinewy grace that was somehow more menacing. He was the one who spoke, and even in that hoarse whisper, the voice was unmistakable. It was the voice from the haunted house, the voice that had haunted Huck’s nightmares. It was Injun Joe.

“Her light’s still on,” the big man, the deaf and dumb Spaniard’s partner, grumbled. “I thought you said she’d be asleep.”

“She will be,” Injun Joe’s voice was a venomous rasp. “Let her say her prayers. It’s the last time she’ll have the face for it.”

Tom’s blood ran cold. This was not a game. These were not villains from a book. This was real. He could feel Huck trembling beside him, a frantic, silent vibration that spoke of a terror beyond anything Tom had ever imagined.

“I don’t like it, Joe,” the other man whined. “Just rob her and let’s go. There’s no need for the other.”

Injun Joe turned, and though they could not see his face in the darkness, the sheer malice in his posture was a visible thing. “No need?” he hissed. “Her husband was the magistrate. He had me horsewhipped. Horsewhipped! In the public square! Me! He’s dead, the dog, but she’s got his face. And I’m going to notch it.”

The man whimpered. “Notch it?”

“Aye. I’ll slit her nostrils. Maybe clip her ears, too. Like they do to a sow. She’ll wish she was dead before I’m through with her. Then we’ll get the money. But the revenge comes first. It’s sweeter.”

Tom felt a wave of nausea so profound he thought he would be sick right there in the bush. He wanted to scream, to run, to be anywhere else on earth. He looked at Huck. In the faint starlight filtering through the leaves, Huck’s face was a pale, frozen mask. His eyes were wide and staring, not at the villains, but at Tom. They were eyes that asked a terrible question: What now, Tom Sawyer? What does the book say to do now?

And for the first time in his life, Tom Sawyer had no answer. The stories had failed him. There was no clever trick, no dashing charge, no heroic speech that could fix this. There was only the ugly, murderous reality crouching in the darkness, and the two small, terrified boys who were trapped with it. The silence stretched, thick and suffocating. Injun Joe was explaining the best way to pry open a window, his voice a low, instructional murmur of pure evil.

Tom was paralyzed. His mind, usually a whirlwind of plans and schemes, was a complete and utter blank. He was a general whose army had deserted, whose maps had all led to this one, inescapable point of doom. He could not move, could not think, could not even breathe properly.

It was Huck who moved.

He didn’t make a sound. He simply backed away, inch by agonizing inch, disentangling himself from the branches of the lilac bush. He gave Tom one last, desperate look, a look that said, ‘I’m sorry, but I have to,’ and then he was gone. He didn’t run. He flowed into the darkness, a part of it, leaving Tom utterly alone with the murmuring voices and the cold, sick certainty of what was about to happen.

Tom lay there, flattened against the earth, his heart a wild bird beating against his ribs. He thought Huck had run for it, abandoned him. And he could not blame him. He, Tom Sawyer, the great adventurer, was a fraud and a coward. He was going to lie here and listen to a woman be murdered, and he was not going to do a thing. A single, hot tear of shame and terror slid down his cheek and into the dirt.

But Huck was not running away. He was running to. His initial terror had been so absolute it had burned through itself, leaving behind a cold, clear purpose. He was not thinking of treasure, or adventure, or even of his own skin anymore. He was thinking of the Widow Douglas, who had sometimes given him a piece of bread and a kind word when he was starving. He was thinking of the monster in the dark and the horrible things he had promised to do.

He ran, not with Tom’s imagined stealth, but with the desperate, ground eating speed of a hunted animal. He stumbled over roots, was lashed by branches, but he did not slow. He was not heading for the safety of town, or his hogshead. He was running pell mell down the other side of the hill, toward a different light, the light of the old Welshman’s house. The Welshman and his two strapping sons were known to be decent folk, and more importantly, they were known to own guns.

He burst out of the woods and into their tidy yard like a demon, gasping for breath, his lungs on fire. He hammered on the door with both fists, a frantic, desperate drumming. “Help!” he croaked, his voice a raw shred of sound. “Help! Murder!”

Lights flared inside. There was the sound of heavy footsteps, the scraping of a bolt. The door creaked open to reveal the old Welshman himself, a candle in one hand and a long squirrel rifle in the other. His two sons appeared behind him, looking big and formidable in their nightshirts.

“Who’s there? What in thunder is this racket?” the Welshman demanded, holding the candle high. It illuminated Huck’s wild, scratched, and utterly terrified face.

“It’s them!” Huck gasped, pointing back up the hill. “Injun Joe! At the widow’s! He’s gonna… he’s gonna…” He couldn’t say the words. He choked on them, a sob of pure horror escaping him. “Hurry! Please, you gotta hurry!”

The old Welshman was no fool. He had lived on the frontier long enough to know the sound of real terror when he heard it. He did not waste time with questions. “Boys, get your guns!” he roared. “There’s devils’ work afoot on the hill!”

In a matter of moments, the three men were armed and ready, their faces grim in the candlelight. The Welshman gave Huck a sharp look. “You stay here, boy. You’ve done your part.”

But Huck shook his head. He had to see. He had to know. He followed them at a distance as they moved up the hill, not with the stealth of burglars, but with the determined stride of armed and righteous men.

They did not go to the front, but circled around the back, just as the villains had. As they neared the house, they heard a sharp crack—the sound of splintering wood. The Welshman raised a hand, and they all dropped to a crouch. A moment later, a window casement creaked open.

“Now!” the Welshman roared, and he and his sons rose as one and charged forward, letting out a yell that was enough to wake the dead.

There was a shout of surprise and pain from the window, then a curse. Two figures scrambled out and fled into the darkness. A volley of shots rang out, splitting the night, but the figures were gone, swallowed by the woods.

The Welshman and his sons, breathing heavily, clustered around the violated window. A light appeared inside, and the Widow Douglas, pale and trembling in her nightgown, appeared at the broken frame.

“Who’s there?” she cried. “What’s happening?”

“It’s all right, madam,” the Welshman said, his voice reassuring. “Just a pair of tramps. They’ve run off. You’re safe now.”

Huck, hidden in the shadows, watched all this. He saw the lights come on all over the house. He heard the exclamations of relief and gratitude. He had done it. He had saved her. He, Huckleberry Finn, the town drunk’s son, the outcast, had been a hero.

A strange feeling washed over him, a dizzying mix of relief, exhaustion, and triumph. The world swayed, and the ground felt unsteady beneath his feet. The strain of the night, the terror, the desperate run—it all came crashing down on him at once. He leaned against a tree, his legs turning to water. The last thing he saw before the world went black was the distant, lonely figure of Tom Sawyer, finally creeping out of the lilac bush and staring, dumbfounded, at the scene of the action he had so spectacularly missed.
