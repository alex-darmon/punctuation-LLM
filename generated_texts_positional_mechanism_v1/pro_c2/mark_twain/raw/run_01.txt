The boys whittled their sticks with a great show of industry. These were no ordinary sticks, but scepters of office for the newly formed society of Night Rovers, a secret brotherhood whose sole purpose was adventure. Silas, the president, had just sworn in his two members, Pete and Finn, with an oath of his own devising involving crossed sticks and spitting. Now came the first order of business.

“The old gristmill,” Silas announced, testing the point of his scepter. “We’ll go tonight. After moon-down.”

“The Blackwood Mill?” said Pete, his eyes widening. “Why, they say old man Blackwood’s ghost still turns the grindstone on moonless nights.”

“That’s just what they say,” Silas said with a lofty air. “It’s baby talk for scairty-cats. I ain’t afraid of any ghost that ever was. Are you?”

“Me? Not a bit! I’d just as soon see a ghost as not. Wouldn’t you, Finn? We was talking about it just the other day, wasn’t we?”

“I reckon so,” said Finn.

“There, you see?” said Silas. “Finn recollects it. We ain’t the kind to be scared of a little dark and some clanking chains. Why, I bet Billy Peterson would faint dead away if he had to even walk past the gate.”

“Billy Peterson!” scoffed Pete. “He’d cry for his ma if a toad hopped on his shoe. Just one little hoot from an owl would send him home blubbering.”

“’Deed it would. Say, boys, we won’t tell anybody where we’re going. And then tomorrow, at school, I’ll just happen to say, ‘Pete, you drop that bit of flint you picked up at the mill last night?’ And you’ll say, real casual, ‘Don’t believe I did, Silas, but I might have. It was powerful dark in there.’ And then just watch their eyes pop!”

“By jings, that’s a good one, Si! I wish it was tonight already!”

“So do I! And when they ask if we saw the ghost, we’ll just laugh, and say we didn’t have time to notice, we was so busy exploring!”

So the talk went on, and their courage swelled with every word. But as dusk fell and the moonless dark began to settle in earnest, the grand adventure seemed a trifle less gay. The air grew heavy and still. They crept along the path to the abandoned mill, its broken spine stark against the star-dusted sky. The cheerful chirping of crickets seemed to die away the closer they got, leaving a profound and waiting silence.

Every snapped twig under their feet was a pistol shot in the stillness. A sudden breeze rustled the dead leaves in the millrace, and the boys froze, huddled together.

“It’s just the wind,” Silas whispered, though his voice had lost its commanding tone.

“I knew that,” Pete whispered back, a little too quickly. “It takes more’n a little wind to bother me.”

They slipped through a gap in the wall, into the cavernous dark of the main floor. The air was thick with the smell of damp and decay; it was a smell that seemed to have weight and a deep chill. They could hear the faint drip, drip, drip of water somewhere in the blackness, and the scuttling of unseen things. The silences between the drips grew longer; their breathing marvelously increased. Every pore on their necks seemed to prickle, and they could scarcely swallow the lumps that rose in their throats.

“I believe I could stay in here all night,” said Silas, his voice a trembling ghost of its former self.

“So could I,” said Pete. “It ain’t anything.”

A floorboard overhead gave a loud groan. All three boys jumped as one. A cascade of dust rained down, glittering in a stray beam of starlight from a hole in the roof. Something metallic clattered far above and then fell, a long, whining descent that ended with a deafening clang on the stone floor not ten feet away.

That was enough. The society of Night Rovers was instantly and unanimously dissolved. Silas gave a strangled yelp and scrambled for the wall. Pete was right behind him, his quest for a casual story forgotten in a more urgent quest for open air. Finn needed no encouragement. They tumbled out into the night, gasping and stumbling, and did not stop until the terrible shape of the mill was lost behind the trees. They fetched up, breathless and trembling, in a small clearing.

Silas spoke first, his voice shaky. “I thought that beam was coming down. Right on your head, Pete. I had to get out to warn you.”

“That’s just what I thought,” panted Pete. “I saw it start to go, and I ran to get help. I’ll leave it to Finn if I didn’t.”

Finn, who had seen nothing but shadows, said, “Yes, it looked mighty bad.”

They were not talkative on the walk back to their camp. They had a chastened look, and when Finn mentioned going back for their whittling sticks, the others said no, they could get new ones easy enough. That night they huddled close to their fire, and if they slept, it was with one eye open, and they did not speak of ghosts or mills. But by morning, a change had come over them. As they cooked their bacon, the adventure began to expand and take on a new and glorious shape. They had not fled in terror; they had executed a strategic retreat. The falling object was not a loose bolt; it was the ghost himself, throwing his tools about in a rage. By the time they finished breakfast, they had bravely faced down old man Blackwood and escaped by the skin of their teeth, and they could hardly wait to tell everyone how it was nothing, really, if you only had the grit for it.

By the time the sun had climbed high enough to burn the last of the dew from the grass, the boys were on their way to the village, their hearts filled with a noble and satisfying sort of courage that had cost them nothing but a few moments of frantic terror, a thing easily forgotten.

“They’ll not believe it at first,” said Pete, kicking at a stone in the path. “They’ll say we made it all up.”

“Let ’em,” said Silas, with the grand air of a martyr. “We know what we saw. And we know what we did. I’ll leave it to Finn if we didn’t hear old Blackwood’s ghost plain as day.”

“Plainer,” said Finn, who was beginning to enjoy his role as chief witness. “It was a groan, a real awful groan, like a man in his death throes. And then the clangin’, like he was throwin’ his smithin’ hammer right at us.”

“That’s just how it was,” affirmed Pete. “I was nearest. I felt the wind of it go past my ear. Another inch and I’d have been a goner. But a fellow can’t show he’s scared, not when there’s others to think of.”

This was a fine story, and grew finer with each telling, so that by the time they saw the cluster of whitewashed houses that made up the town, the ghost of Blackwood had not only thrown a hammer but also a length of chain, had groaned three distinct times, and had manifested as a cold spot that had nearly frozen Pete’s blood in his veins. They were not three boys who had run from a noise; they were a heroic band who had faced down the supernatural and withdrawn in good order.

They found the usual congress of boys assembled near the general store, engaged in the important business of swapping marbles and trading insults. At the center of this group, as always, was Abner Pyle, a stout boy with a permanently skeptical expression and fists like small hams. With him was his shadow, Clem, a smaller, wirier boy who echoed Abner’s opinions with great ferver.

Silas, as the self-appointed leader of their expedition, cleared his throat and launched into the tale. He started casually, as if such adventures were a nightly occurrence for him. He spoke of the dare, the creeping into the dark mill, the oppressive silence. Pete and Finn stood by, nodding at the important parts and supplying corroborating details when called upon.

“And then,” Silas said, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper, “we heard it. A moan, from up in the rafters. A sound to curdle your insides.”

A few of the smaller boys looked satisfyingly frightened. But Abner Pyle merely chewed on a blade of grass and squinted.

“And then the clatterin’ started,” put in Pete, unable to wait. “Old Blackwood’s spirit, heavin’ his tools about in a fury. One of ’em, a big iron bolt, I reckon, came whistlin’ down and nigh took my head off!”

“Nigh took your head off,” repeated Abner, without a trace of admiration. “You saw this bolt, did you?”

“Well, no, not exactly,” said Pete. “It was dark as a tomb in there. But I heard it. We all heard it. Didn’t we, Finn?”

“Heard it and felt it,” said Finn. “The whole floor shook.”

Abner spat out his blade of grass. “Sounds to me like a rat knocked a rusty nail off a beam. I’ve been in the old mill a dozen times. Ain’t nothin’ in there but spiders and rot.”

“This was different,” insisted Silas, his face flushing. “This was a spirit. We was there. You wasn’t.”

“And you ran out,” said Abner, with a slow, knowing smile. “You heard a noise and you lit out for the woods like a scalded cat. That’s the truth of it, ain’t it?”

“We did not run!” said Pete, his voice a little too loud. “We made a strategic retreat! I’ll leave it to Silas if that ain’t the word he used.”

“That’s it exactly,” said Silas, grateful for the term. “A strategic retreat. We was outnumbered, what with it being a ghost.”

Abner laughed, a short, barking sound. “Outnumbered by a thing that ain’t there. That’s a good one. You boys are full of more wind than a bellows. You was scared, and you ran. That’s all.”

The other boys, who had been on the verge of being impressed, now began to snicker and look toward Abner. The grand edifice of the night’s adventure was crumbling into dust.

“We was not!” cried Silas, goaded beyond caution. “We ain’t scared of that mill, or any ghost in it! We’d go back there tonight if we had a mind to!”

Abner’s eyes gleamed. He had them. “All right,” he said, standing up. “All right, you will then. And me and Clem will go with you, to see you ain’t scared.”

A terrible silence fell over the three adventurers. This was not how the story was supposed to end.

“And just to make it interesting,” Abner continued, enjoying his power, “you got to prove you ain’t scared. Anyone can walk in and walk out. You got to do somethin’. You got to go up to the bell tower, and you got to ring the old mill bell. At midnight.”

The bell. The old iron bell in the rickety, rotten tower that hadn’t been rung in twenty years. The thought of it was worse than the ghost. The tower was said to be full of bats, and the floorboards were treacherous, and it was a hundred feet straight down to the stones below.

Silas looked at Pete. Pete looked at Finn. They saw in each other’s eyes the same watery, cornered expression. Their magnificent lie had built a cage and they had walked right into it. To refuse now would be to admit they were not only liars but cowards, a fate worse than any ghost.

“Fine,” said Silas, his voice a dry croak. “We’ll do it. It’s nothin’ to us. We’ll be there.”

“Midnight,” said Abner, sealing the bargain. “And we’ll all see who’s full of wind then.”

The rest of the day was a misery. The sun seemed to mock them with its brightness. Their breakfast bacon sat like a stone in their stomachs. The walk back to their camp was a funeral procession. The bravado had evaporated, leaving behind a cold, clammy residue of dread.

“The bell tower,” Pete said, for the tenth time. “He must think we’re fools.”

“He is a fool if he thinks we’re afraid of a little bell,” said Silas, but his voice had no conviction. It was a hollow echo of his morning’s self. “We faced a ghost. A bell is just a bell.”

“And a tower is just a tower,” added Finn, unhelpfully. “With rotten wood and a hundred foot drop.”

They sat around their cold campfire, the silence stretching between them. The prospect of their earlier adventure now seemed like a pleasant dream. That had been fear of the unknown, a vague and shadowy thing. This was a fear of the known: of splintering wood, of a sickening fall, of Abner Pyle’s triumphant sneer.

As dusk began to settle, they made their way back toward the town, to a prearranged meeting spot at the edge of the woods. Abner and Clem were already there, waiting, their faces grim and determined in the gloom. No words were wasted. Abner simply jerked his head toward the dark shape of the mill, rising against the last of the twilight sky, and started walking. The five of them moved through the woods like a little band of condemned prisoners.

The silences widened; the swallowing of dry throats grew more pronounced. Every rustle of a leaf, every snap of a twig, was a fresh torment. When they reached the crumbling wall of the mill, Silas, who had been so bold that morning, hesitated. It was Abner who pushed past him and slipped into the cavernous dark.

“Come on,” he hissed. “Unless you want to stand out here all night.”

One by one, they followed, their chests tight with a suffocating apprehension. The air was colder than before, and the smell of damp decay seemed to have grown fangs. The drip, drip, drip from the blackness sounded like a clock ticking down to some awful doom.

“Well?” whispered Abner, his voice sharp. “Which way to the tower stairs?”

Silas pointed with a trembling hand toward a deeper patch of darkness. “Over there, I reckon.”

They moved across the main floor, their footsteps echoing unnaturally in the vast space. A cloud shifted outside and a pale moonbeam lanced through a hole in the roof, illuminating a rickety-looking spiral staircase, clinging to the far wall. It looked like a column of bones.

“You first,” Abner said to Silas. “You’ve been here before.”

Silas’s blood ran cold. He had no more wish to be first up those stairs than he had to fly. He looked at Pete, whose face was a white smudge in the dark. He looked at Finn, who seemed to have shrunk to half his size. Their society of heroes was well and truly bankrupt.

“It’s all our adventure,” Silas said, trying to sound reasonable. “We ought to go up together.”

Abner sneered. “Scared already? I knew it.” He pushed past Silas. “Fine. I’ll lead. But you three are comin’ right behind me. And if any one of you tries to sneak off, I’ll know.”

He set his foot on the first step. It groaned in protest, a long, drawn-out sound that vibrated through the whole structure. Abner froze for a second, then took another step, and another. The boys followed, their hearts in their mouths, clinging to the damp stone wall. The stairs swayed and creaked with every footfall. Dust and splinters rained down on them from above. They climbed out of the moonlight and into a stifling, absolute blackness. They were climbing by feel alone, their hands brushing against cobwebs and slimy patches of mold.

Suddenly, from somewhere above them in the dark, came a low, guttural sound. A sort of rumbling cough.

It was not a human sound. It was not the groan of a ghost. It was something wet, and alive, and very close.

All five boys froze. The staircase gave a little shudder under their combined weight.

“What was that?” whispered Clem, his voice shaking.

“A board settling,” Abner said, but his own voice was tight.

Then came another sound. A sharp, stamping noise on the floorboards directly above their heads. Stomp. Stomp. Followed by a loud, aggressive snort.

“That ain’t a board,” breathed Pete.

Abner, to his credit, kept climbing, though more slowly. He was trapped now, as much as they were. To go down would be to admit defeat in the face of a noise. He reached the top of the stairs and hauled himself onto the landing of the bell tower. The others scrambled up after him, eager to be off the swaying staircase.

They huddled together in a tight knot, peering into the oppressive darkness of the tower room. The air was thick with a strange, musky smell. The great bell hung in the center of the room, a looming, silent giant. Faint starlight filtered through gaps in the roof, showing them only shapes and deeper shadows.

The stamping came again, from the far corner of the room. And then, two little points of light glowed in the darkness. Two malevolent, yellow eyes.

A low growl rumbled through the room, and a shape began to detach itself from the shadows. It was low to the ground, and broad, and it had a set of very large, very curved horns.

It was a goat. An enormous, filthy, billy goat, with a matted beard and an expression of pure, concentrated malice. He had clearly made the bell tower his personal kingdom, and he did not look pleased to have visitors.

For a moment, no one moved. The specter of old man Blackwood’s ghost was a terrifying but abstract thing. A furious, two-hundred-pound goat in a confined space was a problem of a much more immediate and practical nature.

The goat lowered its head, gave another angry snort, and scraped a hoof on the wooden floor.

“Nice goat,” Clem whimpered.

That seemed to be the wrong thing to say. The goat let out a bleat that sounded more like a roar and charged.

There was no thought of strategy, no time for bravado. There was only a singular, unifying, all-consuming desire to be somewhere else. The Society of Night Rovers and the Abner Pyle Investigation Committee were dissolved in a single instant of primal terror.

Abner, who was closest, gave a yell of fright and dodged behind the great bell. The goat, with a frustrated snort, crashed into the bell’s iron flank. A dull, muffled BONNNGG echoed through the tower, a sound of doom if ever there was one.

Silas, Pete, and Finn didn’t wait to see what happened next. They spun around as one and lunged for the staircase. They fell more than they climbed, sliding and bumping their way down in a heap, their descent punctuated by yelps of pain and fear. Clem was right on their heels, his earlier loyalty to Abner entirely forgotten.

Above them, they heard another BONNNGG, louder this time, followed by Abner’s voice shouting, “Get away from me, you devil!”

The four boys hit the main floor in a tangle of arms and legs. They scrambled to their feet and sprinted for the gap in the wall, not daring to look back. The sounds from the tower—angry bleats, frantic thuds, and the occasional, mournful clang of the bell—spurred them on. They tumbled out into the cool night air, gasping for breath, and ran.

They did not stop until they were deep in the woods, the mill far behind them. They collapsed in a heap, their lungs burning, their bodies a mass of scrapes and bruises. For a long while, no one spoke. They could only lie there, listening to the sound of their own ragged breathing and the pounding of their own hearts.

Presently, a figure stumbled out of the darkness and staggered into their clearing. It was Abner Pyle. His shirt was torn, he had a long scratch down one cheek, and he was covered in dust and what looked suspiciously like goat hair. He stood there, swaying slightly, and looked at the four of them.

His skeptical expression was gone. His superior sneer was gone. He just looked tired, and scared, and utterly defeated. He opened his mouth, as if to say something, then seemed to think better of it. He sat down heavily, a little apart from the others, and put his head in his hands.

The silence returned, but it was a different kind of silence now. It was not the tense silence of boys trying to out-brave each other; it was the quiet, shared understanding of a common, humiliating ordeal. The ghost of old man Blackwood had been routed by the reality of old man Beelzebub, as Pete later named him.

After an hour, or perhaps a lifetime, Silas finally spoke. His voice was quiet and hoarse.

“I reckon we can agree the bell was rung.”

A weak, shaky chuckle came from Clem. Pete managed a small groan that might have been a laugh. Abner didn’t look up, but his shoulders shook a little.

They were not talkative on the walk back to their respective beds. They parted at the edge of town with quiet nods, not looking each other in the eye. When Silas, Pete, and Finn got back to their own camp, they didn’t bother to rekindle the fire. They just crawled under their blankets, aching in body and spirit.

The next morning, they cooked their breakfast in silence. The great adventure had expanded, and glorified itself, and then collapsed into a farce. There was no way to polish this story. There was no strategic retreat from a goat. There was only the ignominious truth.

Later that day, by the general store, one of the smaller boys, the one who had been so impressed the day before, ran up to them.

“Did you do it?” he asked, his eyes wide. “Did you go back to the mill? Did you ring the bell?”

Silas looked at Pete. Pete looked at Finn. Over by the town pump, they could see Abner Pyle, pointedly ignoring them.

Silas looked back at the small boy. He thought of the grand stories he could tell, of the battle with the horned demon in the tower. He could make himself a hero again, easily. But the memory of that malevolent yellow eye, and the feeling of his courage turning to water in his veins, was too fresh.

“We went to the mill,” he said, his voice flat. And that was all. He turned and walked away, Pete and Finn trailing behind him, leaving the small boy to wonder at the great, unspoken mystery of what had happened in the dark. The age of glorious adventures, they secretly felt, was over; or if it was not, it would at least have to be undertaken with a good deal more caution, and a healthy suspicion of things that go stomp in the night.

The world, which only two days before had been a boundless theater for heroic deeds, had shrunk to the dimensions of a dusty, commonplace town, populated by unimpressed adults and small boys who were certain to ask the wrong questions.

They spent the day in a kind of listless disgrace, avoiding the main street and sticking to the alleys and the quiet shade of the river willows. Every distant bleat from a farmer’s pen sent a fresh wave of heat to their cheeks. The grand conspiracy of silence they had sworn with Abner Pyle was a heavy burden, for silence in the face of public curiosity is a confession in itself. There was no way to spin a tale of valor out of an encounter with a billy goat, however devilish its disposition. A bear, now—a bear could be fought. A panther could be treed. Even a wild boar presented an honorable foe. But a goat was a barnyard animal. It was domestic. To be routed by a goat was to be defeated by a piece of animated furniture.

Late in the afternoon, as they sat morosely skipping stones across the water, a new sound reached them; not a bleat, but the steady, industrious rhythm of a hammer. They followed it out of idle misery and came upon a clearing behind the cooper’s shop. There was Abner Pyle. He was not idle, and he was not miserable. He was building a boat.

It was not a raft, not a boy’s crude vessel of lashed-together logs. It was a proper skiff, with a pointed bow and a square stern and sides made of clean pine boards. Abner was planing a plank, his movements sure and practiced. He had a bucket of hot tar simmering over a small fire, and a pile of oakum for the seams. He was not playing at being a boat-builder; he was one. He looked up as they approached, and the old sneer was back on his face, a little shaky around the edges, perhaps, but re-established.

“Well, if it ain’t the famous bell-ringers,” he said, not pausing in his work.

Silas bristled. “We was there same as you, Abner Pyle.”

“I know it,” said Abner. “And I decided I was done with make-believe. Ghosts and pirates and all that truck. It’s foolishness. A man ought to do something real.” He ran his hand along the smooth wood of his skiff. “This here is real. She’ll float, and she’ll be fast. I call her the ‘Arrow’.”

Pete snorted. “Where you aimin’ to shoot that arrow? ‘Cross the horse-trough?”

Abner laid down his plane and looked at them squarely. The sneer was solid now. “I’m aimin’ to row her down to Parker’s Eddy and back before supper, that’s where. Bet you couldn’t do that on one of your pirate rafts if you had a month.”

Here was a direct challenge, a glove thrown down upon the dusty ground of their wounded pride. The goat was forgotten. The mill was a dim memory. Here was a new contest, a new chance for glory, and one that involved the river, their trusted and familiar confederate.

“That’s what you think,” said Silas, his spirits rising like a flood. “We could build a raft that’d beat that little wash-tub of yours to the Eddy and still have time for a fish dinner.”

“Talk’s cheap,” said Abner. “Logs are free. I’ll race you. Saturday morning. My skiff against your… whatever you can cobble together. We start at the town landing. First one to touch the big willow at Parker’s Eddy wins.”

“You’re on!” said Pete.

“And the winner gets to tell the story of the race any way he wants,” added Abner, with a sharp look. “And the loser don’t say a word to contradict it. Ever.”

This was the true prize. Not just victory, but control of the narrative. The power to erase one humiliation with one triumph.

“Done,” said Silas. The three of them turned and marched away, their backs straight, their purpose renewed. The age of glorious adventures had, it seemed, returned from its premature grave.

The next two days were a blur of magnificent industry. A new pirate venture was afoot, and all hands were needed. The first order of business was timber. They knew of a pile of logs left over from the new bridge construction, seasoned and solid. They were, strictly speaking, the property of the county, but the boys reasoned that the county was a large and forgetful entity, and that the logs were not doing anyone any good just sitting there. Under the cover of dusk, they rolled four of the best ones down the bank and into a hidden slough, a Herculean task that left them breathless and filled with the righteous sweat of thieves.

Then came the design. Silas was for a long, narrow model, for speed. Pete argued for a wide, stable platform, less likely to capsize. Finn, who had once seen a picture of a Chinese junk in a book, was convinced they needed a high poop deck and a sail made of stitched-together flour sacks. They argued for a full morning, their voices rising in technical debate until they were nearly at blows. They compromised, as committees do, by incorporating the worst features of every plan. The raft would be wide, but with two of the longest logs sticking out in front, like horns. It would have a low deck, but a small, wobbly platform was erected in the center for the captain, which was Silas.

They bored holes in the logs with a purloined auger and lashed them together with lengths of rope and grapevine. They sawed planks for the deck, the boards uneven and cantankerous. For a rudder, they used a long sweep oar they had “found” near the ferry landing, and for a mast, they stepped a young sapling into a cross-brace and prepared Finn’s flour-sack sail. The whole affair was a monstrosity, a floating testament to boyish ambition and poor engineering. But to them, it was a warship. They christened it “The Avenger,” and the name seemed to lend it a grim and formidable dignity.

They provisioned it with the seriousness of arctic explorers. A dozen hard-boiled eggs, a bag of green apples, a jug of water, and a jar of pickles Pete’s mother would not be missing until Sunday. They were ready for a voyage to New Orleans if need be, let alone a ten-mile race.

Saturday morning dawned bright and clear. Half the town’s boys and a handful of idle men were gathered at the landing to see the spectacle. Abner Pyle was already there, his “Arrow” floating lightly on the water. It was a thing of beauty, painted a smart green, its seams neatly tarred, its oars resting in brass oarlocks. It looked professional and dangerous. Then came “The Avenger,” poled out of the slough by its proud crew. It was not a thing of beauty. It was lopsided and bulky, and it sat low in the water, looking sullen and waterlogged. A ripple of laughter went through the assembled crowd.

Abner smirked. “Looks like you’re avenging a sawmill, Silas.”

Silas’s face was grim. “We’ll see who’s laughing at Parker’s Eddy.”

The town clerk, a man who enjoyed any diversion from his dusty ledgers, agreed to start the race. At the drop of his handkerchief, Abner dug his oars into the water, and the “Arrow” shot forward, leaving a neat, V-shaped wake. On “The Avenger,” things were less coordinated. Silas shouted orders from his captain’s perch, Pete and Finn pushed with long poles, and the great raft shuddered, turned sideways, and drifted slowly out into the current.

For the first mile, it was no contest. Abner’s skiff grew smaller and smaller in the distance, a green speck of triumph. On the raft, despair set in.

“He’s halfway there already!” moaned Finn, leaning on his pole.

“It’s these front logs,” said Pete. “They’re dragging. I told you we should have cut ’em, Silas.”

“And I told you to pole on the left!” Silas shot back. “You’re pushing us into the bank!”

They bickered and blamed, their grand adventure turning sour. The sun grew hot. The eggs were consumed in a sulky silence. The pickles seemed a mockery of their failed ambitions. The raft did what rafts do; it drifted. It followed the path of least resistance, which was the deepest, swiftest part of the channel. They were not sailing it so much as riding it, passengers on a vessel with its own placid ideas about its destination and its speed.

An hour passed. They rounded a great bend in the river, a place known as the Devil’s Elbow, where the channel split and wound its way through a maze of sandbars and half-submerged snags. And there, not a hundred yards ahead, was the “Arrow.” It was not moving. It was canted at a sharp angle, its bow stuck fast on a hidden sandbar. Abner was in the water, waist-deep, trying to push the skiff off. He was red-faced and furious, his exertions only seeming to wedge the boat more firmly into the sand.

A great, unifying joy surged through the crew of “The Avenger.”

“By jings!” whispered Pete. “He’s stuck!”

“The river’s got him!” cackled Finn.

Silas, the captain, saw his moment. “Hard to starboard!” he yelled, though the raft had no starboard and paid him no mind. It drifted majestically on, carried by the main current down the clear, deep channel to the left of the sandbar. As they drew alongside their stranded rival, the sweetness of victory was almost too much to bear.

“Havin’ a bit of a swim, Abner?” shouted Pete.

“Thought you was in a hurry!” added Finn.

Abner said nothing. He glared at them, his face a thundercloud of humiliation, and gave one more desperate, futile shove.

Silas, as commander, delivered the final, crushing blow. “Should’ve built a real boat, Abner!” he called out. “One that knows the river!”

They drifted past him, waving their poles in mocking triumph. They were going to win. They had been delivered by the river itself. The world was a glorious theater once more, and they were the undisputed heroes of the play. They let out a ragged cheer and settled down to enjoy the rest of the voyage, the taste of the pickles now crisp and delicious.

They reached Parker’s Eddy a little after noon. The great willow drooped its branches to the water, just as they remembered. Pete scrambled off the raft and gave the trunk a ceremonial slap. They had done it. They had won. They capered on the bank, whooping and hollering, their victory made all the sweeter by its sheer improbability. They were kings of the river. Abner Pyle was a mud-caked footnote to their epic tale.

They sat and waited for him, eager for the moment when he would row past, defeated, and they could offer a final, patronizing wave. An hour passed. Then another. The sun began to slide down the western sky. The triumphant mood began to fray.

“He ought to be here by now,” said Finn, squinting upriver.

“Maybe he gave up and went home,” said Pete. But he didn’t sound convinced.

A different kind of silence fell between them. They thought of Abner, alone in the middle of the river, stuck on that sandbar. The Devil’s Elbow was a lonely place. The current was tricky. They pictured him struggling, his anger giving way to exhaustion, and then maybe to fear as the shadows lengthened. Their glorious victory began to feel thin and a little mean. The memory of their jeers was not as satisfying as it had been.

“He looked powerful stuck,” Silas said quietly, voicing the thought they all shared.

“The river’s gonna start risin’ a bit toward sundown,” Pete added. “Current gets stronger.”

They looked at each other. The unsaid thing hung in the air between them. Winning was one thing. Leaving a fellow to a genuine predicament was another, and one that sat poorly, much like an unripe apple. It was a different code, older than rivalry.

Finally, Silas stood up. “Well,” he sighed, “I reckon we can’t rightly claim the victory if the other fellow drowns before he finishes.” It was a flimsy excuse, but it was enough. “Let’s go see if the fool’s got himself clear.”

The journey back upriver was grim work. Pushing the heavy raft against the current was a slow and punishing business. The glory had evaporated completely now, replaced by a sore-shouldered sense of duty. It took them the better part of an hour to pole their way back to the Elbow.

The “Arrow” was still there, but it was in a worse state. The slight rise in the river had not freed it, but had swung its stern around so it was now wedged between the sandbar and a large, toothy snag. Abner was not in the water. He was sitting in the skiff, his head in his hands, the very picture of dejection. He looked small and defeated. He did not look up as “The Avenger” scraped to a halt nearby.

There was no jeering this time. The three boys on the raft looked at the sorry sight, and then at each other.

“Looks like you’re in a bit of a fix,” Silas said. His voice was not triumphant, just factual.

Abner looked up. His face was streaked with mud and misery. “I know what it looks like,” he mumbled. “Go on. Say it.”

But they didn’t. Pete coiled a length of rope. “If we tie on to your stern,” he said, “and we all push the raft into the main channel, the current might pull you clear of that snag.”

Abner stared at them, his mouth slightly agape. He had been prepared for mockery, for an avalanche of “I told you so’s.” He was not prepared for an offer of help. He gave a short, jerky nod.

It was a clumsy operation. Pete threw the rope, and Abner tied it to a cleat. Then all four boys, two on the raft and two in the water beside it, heaved and grunted, pushing the great mass of “The Avenger” away from the sandbar and into the deeper water. The rope went taut. The current gripped the raft, and for a heart-stopping moment, nothing happened. Then, with a groan of tortured wood, the stern of the “Arrow” slid free of the snag. A moment later, the bow scraped off the sandbar, and the little skiff was floating free.

Abner sat down heavily in his boat, looking at his blistered hands. He didn't say thank you. Silas, Pete, and Finn didn’t expect him to. Pete untied the rope and coiled it.

“You best follow us,” Silas said. “We know the channel.”

They poled back to Parker’s Eddy in a strange, silent procession, the clumsy raft in the lead, the sleek skiff following like a chastened dog. They all touched the willow tree this time, a quiet, perfunctory ceremony. The race was over. Someone had won, and someone had lost, but it no longer seemed to matter very much.

The journey back to town was long and quiet. When they finally reached the landing, the moon was high and the crowd was long gone. They tied up their vessels without a word. As they stood on the bank, a weary, muddy company, Abner finally spoke.

“Them front logs,” he said, not looking at anyone. “They act like a keel. Keep you in the deep water. It’s not a bad design.” He turned and disappeared into the darkness before anyone could reply.

Silas, Pete, and Finn started the walk home, too tired to even think about the grand story they had won the right to tell. They felt a deep and settled contentment that was nothing like the giddy triumph they had imagined. It was a quieter, heavier feeling, but it was better.

The next day, one of the smaller boys, the same one who had inquired about the bell-ringing, saw Silas by the general store.

“Who won?” the boy asked, his eyes wide with expectation. “Who won the big race?”

Silas looked down at the boy. He thought of the grand tale he could weave; of Abner’s fancy boat being no match for their rugged craft, of their own superior seamanship and knowledge of the river. He could make them legends. But the memory of Abner’s face when they offered him the rope, and the quiet, grudging respect in his voice when he spoke of the raft’s design, was a more powerful story.

“We got there first,” Silas said, his voice flat. And that was all. The age of glorious adventures, he was beginning to understand, was full of strange and complicated victories. And the best ones, it seemed, were the ones you didn’t have to brag about.

The town, which had made a considerable investment of expectation in the great race, felt itself cheated. A contest of such magnitude, between the son of the richest man in town and a rag-tag crew of river rats, demanded a narrative. It required heroes and villains, a decisive and glorious victory to be recounted endlessly at the general store and the livery stable. Silas’s curt summary was like being served a single dry cracker after being promised a seven course meal. The town did not like it. In the absence of a proper story, it began to fashion its own.

Some said Abner’s fancy skiff had sprung a leak and he’d been forced to crawl back to shore in disgrace. Others swore that Silas and his boys had employed some dark river magic, learned from old man Hemlock who lived up the creek and was said to talk to catfish. A third, and very popular, theory was that Abner had tried to bribe them to lose and they, in their unimpeachable virtue, had refused and rowed twice as hard out of pure righteous indignation. Each story was more elaborate and less true than the last, and all of them painted Abner Holt as either a fool, a coward, or a scoundrel, and Silas, Pete, and Finn as titans of nautical prowess and moral rectitude.

The boys themselves heard these rumors and found them deeply unsatisfying. To be credited with a victory they had not truly earned in the way the town imagined was a hollow thing. It was like being praised for a well-spelled word when you had in fact copied it from your neighbor. Pete was the most aggrieved.

“They’re saying we’re heroes, Silas,” he’d grumbled one afternoon, whittling a stick on the riverbank. “It don’t feel heroic. It just feels… finished.”

“That’s because it is finished,” Silas said, staring at the brown water. He had not seen Abner since that night at the landing. He had heard that Abner was keeping to his big house on the hill, which only fueled the town’s speculation.

The strange peace held for a week, a week in which the legend of “The Avenger” grew to mythic proportions while the actual victors felt more like footnotes in their own story. Then came the annual church picnic and bazaar, an event that promised to bring all the simmering tensions of the town to a gentle, polite boil.

It was held, as always, in Miller’s Grove, a pleasant stretch of lawn and oak trees that sloped down to a calm, wide part of the river. The ladies of the Helping Hand Society had outdone themselves; tables groaned under the weight of fried chicken, potato salad, three-bean salad, four kinds of pie, and enough pickled beets to stain every white shirt in the county a festive shade of magenta. The new preacher, Reverend Gable, a thin, earnest man with a kind face and spectacles that made his eyes look like two startled oysters, circulated among his flock, offering blessings and admiring babies. His wife, a quiet, pretty woman from the city, smiled shyly beside him, looking a little overwhelmed by the boisterousness of country society.

Silas, Pete, and Finn arrived with the studied nonchalance of boys who were pretending not to know they were the center of attention. They procured large plates of food and found a spot under a tree, from which they could observe the proceedings. Not long after, Abner Holt appeared. He was not with his father, who considered such public gatherings beneath him, but alone. He was dressed in a stiff new suit that looked hot and uncomfortable, and he walked with a kind of defensive dignity, his chin held high, his eyes fixed on some distant point. He was an island in the chattering sea of the picnic.

A few of the town’s leading men, seeing the son of its most powerful citizen, tipped their hats and offered stilted greetings. “Fine day, Abner.” “Good to see you out, son.” Abner would nod back, a curt, almost imperceptible movement of his head. He looked, Silas thought, like a boy playing the part of a grown man who was not enjoying the play.

The two groups of boys eyed each other across the lawn. It was an awkward situation. The town had declared them mortal enemies, locked in an epic struggle. The truth, that they had shared a quiet moment of mutual aid and respect, was a secret that felt heavier than any lie. Pete nudged Silas.

“Look at him,” he muttered. “Walkin’ around like he owns the place.”

“He probably does own half of it,” Finn observed, his mouth full of cake. “His pa, anyway.”

Silas said nothing. He watched Abner get a plate of food, his movements precise and economical, and then find a place to sit, alone, near the riverbank. The other boys at the picnic, the younger ones and the ones who had not been part of the race, gave him a wide berth, whispering and pointing from a safe distance.

The afternoon wore on. There were three-legged races and a sack race, which the boys avoided as being beneath their new station as seasoned river adventurers. The ladies sold quilts and jams, and the men pitched horseshoes with a great deal of noisy commentary. It was all very pleasant, and very normal.

The incident, when it came, was small and sudden. A group of younger children were playing a game of tag near the water’s edge, their shrieks of laughter piercing the drowsy afternoon air. One of them, a little girl with bright red ribbons in her hair, stumbled near where Mrs. Gable was standing talking to Mrs. Sawyer, Silas’s own mother. The preacher’s wife put out a hand to steady the child, and in the small, clumsy shuffle of bodies, a thin gold chain around her neck caught on a button on the girl’s dress. There was a faint snap.

A small, gold locket, which had been resting on her chest, flew from its chain in a gentle arc. It seemed to hang in the air for a moment, glinting in the sun, a perfect, tiny star. Then it fell, not onto the soft grass, but onto a flat, mossy stone at the very edge of the riverbank. From there, with a final, heartbreaking little bounce, it tipped over the edge and disappeared into the water with a sound so small it was almost imaginary. A tiny “plink.”

For a moment, there was silence. Mrs. Gable gave a small, stricken cry and put her hand to her throat where the locket had been. The little girl, seeing the look on the woman’s face, burst into tears. A small crowd gathered instantly, a knot of concern and curiosity.

“My locket,” Mrs. Gable whispered. “My father gave it to me.”

Reverend Gable hurried to his wife’s side. “There, there, Mary. It’s only a thing.” But his own face was pale, and he looked anxiously at the spot where it had vanished.

The water there was about four feet deep, but murky with the river’s slow summer current. The bottom was a mess of silt and smooth, algae-covered stones. Finding a small locket in it would be like finding a particular needle in a haystack made entirely of other needles.

Abner Holt, who had been sitting only a few yards away, had seen the whole thing. He stood up. For a moment, he seemed about to say something, to offer help. But he was Abner Holt, and offering help was not a language he spoke fluently. He stood there, awkwardly, as the crowd swelled.

Someone in the crowd, one of the men who had lost money betting on Abner’s skiff, saw him standing there.

“Well, Abner,” the man called out, his voice laced with a mean-spirited humor. “You was closest. Didn’t you see where it went?”

Abner’s face tightened. “It went in the water,” he said, his voice flat.

“No help for it, then,” another man said, shaking his head with a show of great sympathy. “That locket’s gone to New Orleans by now.”

“A pity,” said a third, looking pointedly at Abner. “A real pity. Some folks have all the luck, and others have none.”

The implication was clear and ugly. It hung in the air like the smell of sour milk. Abner Holt, the rich boy, stood by and did nothing while a poor preacher’s wife lost her most precious possession. Perhaps he could have caught it. Perhaps he didn’t care. The town, already primed to think ill of him, began to churn. A murmur went through the crowd, a low and ugly thing that feeds on suspicion and grows fat on envy. Abner felt it. His face, which had been pale, turned a dull, angry red. He looked around at the accusing eyes, clamped his jaw shut, turned on his heel, and walked away from the crowd, back toward his solitary spot on the grass. His retreat looked, to all the world, like an admission of guilt.

Silas, Pete, and Finn watched from their tree. They had seen the whole thing, too. They had seen the locket fall, and they had seen Abner’s awkward hesitation. And they had seen the town turn on him.

“Serves him right,” Pete said, but there was no real venom in it. “Standin’ there like a stump.”

“He didn’t do nothin’,” Finn said quietly. “There was nothin’ he could do.”

Silas was silent. He was watching Mrs. Gable, who was trying very hard not to cry while her husband and the other ladies fussed over her. He was watching the men murmur and shake their heads, their faces full of a kind of righteous satisfaction. And he was watching Abner, who was now sitting with his back to the entire picnic, staring at the river, a rigid, lonely figure of defiance.

It wasn’t right. It was a lie, just like the stories about the race were lies. The town wanted a villain, and it had chosen Abner. It was simple, and it was easy, and it was wrong. Silas felt a slow anger building in his chest. It was a different kind of anger than he had ever felt before. It wasn't about a race or a prize. It was about the truth.

“He didn’t lose it,” Silas said, so quietly Pete and Finn had to lean in to hear. “But he’s the one paying for it.”

He thought of the race, of the quiet offer of a rope, of the grudging respect in Abner’s voice. “Them front logs. They act like a keel.” It was a small thing, a moment most people would have missed. But it had happened. And now this was happening. It felt, somehow, connected.

“What’re you thinkin’, Silas?” Pete asked, seeing the look on his face.

Silas stood up. He brushed the crumbs from his pants. His expression was one of grim determination.

“I’m thinking,” he said, “that we know the channel.”

Pete and Finn looked at each other, then back at Silas. They didn’t understand what one thing had to do with the other, but they knew that look. It was the same look Silas had had when he first drew the design for “The Avenger” in the dirt. It was a look that meant an adventure was about to begin. Only this time, it did not feel glorious at all. It felt necessary.

“We’re going to need a few things,” Silas said, his eyes on the murky water where the locket had fallen. “Some buckets. And every bit of old screen and mesh we can find.”

The sun was getting low, casting long shadows across the grove. The picnic was beginning to break up. People were packing their baskets, gathering their children. The small drama of the lost locket was over for them. It was a story they would tell later, another piece of evidence in the case against Abner Holt.

But for the boys, it was just beginning. They slipped away from the departing crowd, melting into the woods that bordered the grove. They did not head for home. They headed for the town dump. Their quiet, complicated victory had just presented them with a new and complicated task. They had to go fishing for the truth, and they had a feeling it was lying in the mud at the bottom of the river.

They worked in the gathering twilight, a trio of shadows. From the dump they salvaged three rusty buckets and a large, torn section of window screen from a burned-out house. They returned to the riverbank, now deserted. The moon was rising, a pale sliver in the sky, and its light gave the water a slick, oily sheen.

“All right,” Silas said, his voice low. “It fell right off that flat rock. The current here is slow, but it moves. It’ll drift it downstream, and a little toward the middle. It won’t go far. It’s heavy.”

He waded into the cool water, shivering as it soaked his trousers up to his knees. Pete and Finn followed, their faces grim. The work was slow and miserable. They used the buckets to scoop up mud, silt, and stones from the river bottom. They would carry the heavy, dripping buckets to the shore and pour the contents over the window screen, which they had stretched between two rocks. Then, by the dim moonlight, they would sift through the muck, feeling for the small, smooth shape of a locket.

It was a fool’s errand, and they knew it. The area they had to search was huge, and the locket was tiny. For every bucket of mud they sifted, a thousand more remained. They worked in silence, the only sounds the slosh of water, the scrape of the buckets on the riverbed, and the soft plop of discarded mud.

An hour passed. Then another. Their backs ached. Their hands were raw. They were cold and wet and covered in stinking mud.

“This is useless, Silas,” Pete finally said, his teeth chattering. “We ain’t gonna find it. It could be anywhere.”

“It’s here,” Silas insisted, though his own confidence was flagging. He dumped another bucket of slime onto the screen. A small crayfish, disturbed from its home, wiggled angrily before dropping to the ground and scuttling away. Nothing else.

It was Finn who had the idea. “We’re doin’ it wrong,” he said. He was standing in the water, staring at the current, what little there was. “We’re just scoopin’ anywhere. We got to think like the river.”

Silas looked at him. “What do you mean?”

“When you drop a rock,” Finn said, “it don’t just stay. The water pushes it till it finds a place to stop. A crack. Or a bigger rock. Or a root. Somethin’ to snag on. The locket ain’t just sittin’ on the mud. It’s caught on somethin’.”

It was a simple observation, but it changed everything. It was the logic of the river, the kind of knowledge you couldn’t get from a book. They stopped their random scooping and began to search with their hands and feet, feeling for the snags, the crevices, the places where the current would deposit a lost thing.

It was even slower, more painstaking work. They moved with a painful crawl, their fingers probing the slimy, unseen world of the river bottom. Twice, Pete let out a yelp, once when he touched a waterlogged piece of wood that felt like a dead man’s arm, and once when a large fish swam between his legs.

They were about to give up, to admit defeat, when Silas’s foot brushed against something. It was not a rock. It was a tangle of roots, reaching out from the bank under the water like skeletal fingers. It was the biggest snag in the area.

“Here,” he said, his voice tight. “This is the place. If it’s anywhere, it’s here.”

They converged on the spot, all three of them, feeling their way through the tangled mesh of roots. It was an impossible task. The roots were coated in slick algae, and the spaces between them were packed with a century of debris.

They needed more light. And they needed a better way to see.

Silas stood up, wiping his muddy hands on his equally muddy shirt. “I’ll be back,” he said. He looked across the dark, empty grove. He knew what he had to do. He didn’t like it, but he knew it was the only way. “You two keep feeling. Don’t stop.”

He waded out of the river and walked across the damp grass, a dripping, muddy creature of the night. He walked to the tree where Abner Holt had been sitting. And he saw that Abner was still there.

He hadn’t gone home. He was sitting on the ground, his back against the oak, his fancy suit now rumpled and stained with grass. He was just staring at the river, at the spot where the three boys were searching. He had been watching them the whole time.

Silas stopped a few feet away. Abner didn’t look at him.

“You’re wasting your time,” Abner said to the river. His voice was tired.

“Maybe,” Silas said.

A long silence stretched between them, filled with the chirping of crickets and the soft murmur of the water.

“They think I could have stopped it,” Abner said, still not looking at him. “They think I didn’t care.”

“I know,” Silas said.

“It happened too fast,” Abner said. It wasn’t an excuse. It was a simple statement of fact.

“I know.”

Abner finally turned his head and looked at Silas, really looked at him. He took in the mud, the wet clothes, the exhaustion on his face.

“Why are you doing this?” he asked. It was a genuine question.

Silas thought for a moment. He could have given a noble, heroic answer. Because it’s the right thing to do. To clear your name. But that wasn’t the whole truth. The whole truth was more complicated, more like the tangled roots under the water.

“Because I’m tired of the stories,” Silas said. “The ones they tell. Let’s have the real one for a change.”

Abner stared at him for another long moment. Then a flicker of something, a strange and unfamiliar expression, crossed his face. It might have been understanding. He stood up, brushing himself off.

“You need a light,” Abner said. “And something to see. The water’s too cloudy.” He thought for a moment. “My father has a viewing box for inspecting the hulls of his barges. A wood box with a glass bottom. And we’ll need a lantern.”

He didn’t wait for an answer. He turned and strode off into the darkness, toward the road that led up the hill to his house. Silas watched him go, a strange feeling in his gut. It was the same feeling he’d had on the raft when Abner had spoken of the keel. A feeling that the world was bigger and more complex than he had ever imagined.

He went back to the river. “He’s coming back,” he told Pete and Finn. “With a light.”

They didn’t have to wait long. In twenty minutes, Abner returned. He was carrying a long, bright brass lantern that cut a brilliant yellow swath through the night, and a strange-looking wooden box about two feet square with a pane of glass set in the bottom. He didn’t say a word. He handed the lantern to Pete and waded into the water, carrying the box. He was still wearing his suit.

“Here,” he said to Silas, his voice all business. “The roots. Put this on the water over them.”

Silas took the viewing box. When he placed it on the surface, the effect was magical. The glass bottom flattened the ripples and, illuminated by the lantern that Pete held steady, it opened a window into the murky depths. Suddenly, they could see. They could see the individual roots, the smooth stones, the tiny fish that darted away from the light.

“Hold it steady,” Abner commanded. He knelt in the water beside Silas, his face close to the glass, his eyes scanning the riverbed with a fierce intensity. Now, four boys were in the river. Four boys, cold and wet and muddy, working together in the dead of night.

They moved the box slowly, methodically, over the tangle of roots. It was still a painstaking process. But now it was not a hopeless one.

“There,” Abner said suddenly. His voice was sharp. “Back a little. To the left.”

Silas moved the box. And then they all saw it.

It was not sitting on the bottom. It had not been caught in the main tangle of roots. It had slipped into a crevice between two large, flat stones, and a single, thin root had grown over the crack, trapping it. And hanging from that root, winking in the lantern light, was a small, dull gleam of gold.

A collective sigh of relief went through the four of them. Finn, who was closest, reached down into the cold water. His fingers closed around it. He brought his hand up, and there it was, resting in his muddy palm, covered in a thin film of silt but otherwise unharmed. The locket.

They waded to shore, a silent, triumphant company. Finn carefully washed the locket in the river and handed it to Silas. Silas wiped it on a relatively clean patch of his shirt and held it out. It was a beautiful thing, engraved with tiny, intricate flowers.

He offered it to Abner.

Abner looked at the locket in Silas’s hand, then at Silas’s face. He shook his head.

“You found it,” he said.

“We all found it,” Silas corrected him. “But you’re the one who’s going to return it.”

A complex series of emotions played across Abner’s face in the lantern light: surprise, confusion, pride, and finally, a grudging understanding. He took the locket. He held it in his palm for a moment, then closed his fingers around it.

“All right,” he said. He looked at the three of them, at their muddy clothes and tired faces. He looked at his own ruined suit. “I reckon I owe you.”

“No you don’t,” Silas said. “Just tell the real story for once.”

Abner nodded. He turned and walked away, the lantern light making his shadow dance before him. He did not go home. He walked toward the preacher’s house.

The three boys watched him go. They were exhausted. They were filthy. They had no story of glorious adventure to tell, no trophy to show for their efforts. But as they started the long, quiet walk home, they felt a deep and settled contentment that was nothing like the giddy triumph they had once imagined. It was a quieter, heavier feeling, but it was better. The age of glorious adventures, they were learning, was not about who got there first, but about what you found along the way, and who was with you when you found it. And sometimes, the best victories were the ones that nobody else ever even knew you had won.

They settled on a frog hunt. It was not a brilliant invention, but it had the virtue of simplicity and was at least partially true, for they had seen a great many frogs. The next morning, the town was abuzz. News travels in a small town not by wire but by a sort of invisible wind that blows through parlors and over picket fences, and the news of the day was Abner’s confession. He had done just as he said he would. He had gone straight to the preacher’s house, knocked on the door, and told the whole story, from his boastful lie to the truth of the matter. He had not named the other boys, saying only that “some fellows” had helped him see the error of his ways and recover the locket.

The town’s reaction was a thing of wonder. Mrs. Gable, who considered herself the final authority on all matters of public morality, declared it was a miracle of redemption, and a testament to the preacher’s influence. Old Mr. Harrison, the storekeeper, said it was just what he’d expect from a boy like Abner, who was all brag and no backbone to begin with. The boys of the town, Abner’s former disciples, were the most confounded of all. They had hitched their wagons to his star, and now that star had confessed to being nothing more than a swamp-gas flicker. They felt betrayed, and a little foolish.

“Well, I’ll be,” said Finn, expressing the sentiment of all of us. “He lost the whole town and won the one thing he wanted.”
“Reckon that’s how it works sometimes,” said Silas, who had a habit of seeing the shape of things. “You give up the thing you’re showing off for, and you get the thing you was showing off to get.”
It was too profound a thought for a sun-drenched morning, and we let it lie. Presently, Abner saw us. He hesitated for a moment, then walked over. It was an awkward meeting. He was no longer our enemy, but he was not yet our friend. He stood before us, shuffling his feet.
“I, uh… I wanted to say thanks,” he mumbled, not looking at any of us directly. “What you did… well.” He trailed off, then seemed to remember something. He dug in his pocket and pulled out a small, crumpled dollar bill. “Here,” he said, thrusting it at Silas. “It ain’t much, but it’s what I got.”
Silas looked at the dollar, then at Abner’s face. He shook his head.
“We don’t want your money, Abner.”
“But I owe you,” Abner insisted.
“No you don’t,” Silas said, and there was a finality in his tone that even Abner could not argue with. “You already paid. Just… try to tell the real story from now on.”
Abner looked from Silas to Finn and then to me. He saw no judgment in our faces, only a kind of weary acceptance. He nodded, stuffed the dollar back in his pocket, and walked away. He had a new kind of reputation now, one that was less glorious but a good deal more solid. And we, the secret architects of his redemption, were left with nothing but the knowledge of it, which, for the time being, seemed to be enough.

But “for the time being” is a short season in the life of a boy. The quiet contentment of a good deed done is a thin soup, and soon the soul craves roast beef and gravy; which is to say, adventure. For a week, life was dreadfully, peacefully dull. We fished, we swam, we traded marbles. The locket affair faded into the background, a settled account. We were restless. The world seemed to have run out of pirates and treasures and thunderstorms, and had replaced them with long, hot, empty afternoons.

And then, one Tuesday, a new interest came to town. It arrived in a cloud of dust and a riot of color, a magnificent wagon painted with scenes of impossible cures and magical transformations. On its side, in brilliant gold leaf, were the words: “PROFESSOR PHILEAS FOGG’S MIRACULOUS ELIXIR—A PANACEA FOR ALL AFFLICTIONS OF BODY AND SOUL.”
The wagon was pulled by two tired-looking horses and driven by Professor Phogg himself, a man who was as splendid as his vehicle. He wore a bottle-green suit, a silk top hat, and a smile that seemed to have been polished. He had a great, booming voice that he used as if he were addressing a crowd of thousands, even when he was only speaking to a stray dog.
That evening, he set up his stage in the town square. A crowd gathered, drawn by curiosity and the promise of a spectacle, which was a rare commodity. We boys pushed our way to the front, of course.

The show began. Professor Fogg was a master of his craft. He told stories of his travels to the exotic East, where he had learned ancient secrets from mystics and kings. He juggled colored balls that seemed to vanish in mid-air. He produced a live rabbit from his hat, a trick so old it was new again to most of the assembled company. He had an assistant, a pale, mournful-looking man named Bartholomew, who played a sad tune on a fiddle and looked as though he had already sampled every affliction the elixir was meant to cure, with no success.
Finally, the moment came. The Professor held up a dark brown bottle filled with a syrupy liquid.
“Friends! Neighbors!” he boomed, his voice rolling over the square. “Are you troubled by rheumatics? Plagued by dyspepsia? Do you suffer from nervous exhaustion, fallen arches, or a general sense of malaise? Do you lack vigor? Does your liver complain? For all these maladies, and a thousand more, there is but one answer!” He shook the bottle. “Professor Fogg’s Miraculous Elixir! Made from a secret formula of rare Amazonian roots, Himalayan flowers, and the pure, life-giving essence of the electric eel!”

A man in the crowd, a stranger to us, shouted out, “I’ve had a misery in my back for twenty years, sir! Nothin’ touches it!”
“Step right up, my good man!” the Professor cried. The man, who we boys noticed had been helping the Professor set up earlier, limped dramatically onto the stage. The Professor gave him a spoonful of the elixir. The man gagged, shuddered, and then, miraculously, his eyes widened. He stood up straight. He touched his toes. He did a little jig.
“It’s gone!” he yelled. “The misery is gone! It’s a miracle!”
The crowd gasped. Money appeared as if by magic. Hands shot up, waving dollar bills. Bartholomew began to sell the bottles as fast as he could hand them out. We boys watched, caught up in the excitement. It was better than a circus.

But Silas was quiet. He had a look on his face that I knew well; it was the look he got when he was working out a puzzle in arithmetic, or figuring the trajectory of a stone aimed at a bottle. His eyes were narrowed, and he wasn’t watching the celebration; he was watching the Professor.
“It’s a swindle,” he whispered, so low that only Finn and I could hear.
“What do you mean?” Finn said. “You saw that man. He was cured.”
“That man helped him unhitch his horses,” Silas said. “And I saw the Professor slip him a coin behind the wagon before the show started. He’s a shill. A plant.”
The word “shill” was new to us, but we understood its meaning at once. The magnificent spectacle suddenly seemed cheap and tawdry. We were not watching a miracle; we were watching a lie. And the people of our town, good, honest people like Mrs. Gable and Mr. Harrison, were trading their hard-earned money for a bottle of swamp water and licorice.
“That ain’t right,” Finn said, his brow furrowed. “My ma bought a bottle. She’s got the rheumatism something awful.”
The thought of Finn’s mother, a kind, tired woman, spending her money on this fraud settled it. The quiet integrity that had been slumbering within us woke up, and it was angry.
“We’ve got to do something,” I said.
“We will,” said Silas, and a familiar glint appeared in his eye. It was the glint of a grand and glorious idea. “We’re going to expose him.”

The next day we held a council of war in our secret headquarters, a hollowed-out space under the roots of the old sycamore by the river. The problem was clear: how to prove the elixir was a fake in a way that the whole town would see and believe? We couldn’t just say so; we were just boys, and Professor Fogg was a grown-up with a silk hat and a booming voice. We needed proof. Indisputable proof.
“We could steal a bottle and give it to a doctor,” Finn suggested.
“Too slow,” said Silas. “And old Doc Adams would just say it’s molasses and alcohol, and the Professor would say the Doc is in league with the undertaker and wants people to stay sick. No, we got to beat him at his own game. We have to make a spectacle.”
He paused, letting the deliciousness of the idea settle.
“He cures people, right? So we need to give him someone he _can’t_ cure. Someone with an ailment so strange, so impossible, that the elixir won’t have the slightest effect. And then everyone will see.”
“Who?” I asked. “Who’s got an impossible ailment?”
Silas looked at Finn. Finn was lanky and loose-limbed, with a face that could look as mournful as a bloodhound’s or as cheerful as a summer morning. He was a born actor, though he didn’t know it.
“Finn does,” Silas announced.
Finn looked alarmed. “I do not! I’m healthy as a horse.”
“You will have,” Silas corrected him. “Tonight, at the show, you will come down with a terrible, unheard-of affliction. The worst one we can think of.”

We spent the rest of the afternoon inventing the affliction. It had to be something the Professor couldn’t dismiss as mere nerves. It had to be visible, and it had to be ridiculous. We discarded several ideas. A sudden inability to see the color blue was too hard to prove. A case of floating, where Finn would slowly rise a foot off the ground, was too hard to fake. Finally, after much debate, we settled on the perfect malady. It was a masterpiece of pathological invention. Finn was to be afflicted with “Reverse Hiccups.”
“What in thunder is a reverse hiccup?” Finn demanded.
“It ain’t a hiccup that goes in,” Silas explained patiently. “It’s a hiccup that comes out, but instead of a ‘hic,’ it’s a word. A different word every time. And you can’t control it. It just… pops out.”
The genius of it was undeniable. How could a bottle of tonic cure such a thing? The performance would be everything. Finn was to look miserable, a poor, suffering soul tormented by this bizarre curse. I was to be his worried friend, helping him through the crowd. Silas would be the instigator, the one to call the Professor’s bluff.

That evening, a bigger crowd than ever gathered in the square. The news of the back-misery cure had spread, and people were eager for more miracles. Professor Fogg was in fine form, his voice even boomier, his smile even more polished. He cured a woman of her deafness, though we boys had seen her chatting with a friend not ten minutes before, and she didn’t seem to be having any trouble hearing then.
Just as the Professor was reaching the crescendo of his speech, holding the elixir aloft like a holy relic, Silas gave the signal.
Finn let out a loud, startling bark. “—Potatoes!”
He clapped his hand over his mouth, his eyes wide with feigned horror. People turned to look. I put a comforting arm around him, my face a mask of concern.
“It’s his affliction, poor soul,” I said loudly to a woman nearby. “Comes on him sudden-like.”
Professor Fogg paused, annoyed at the interruption.
“—Frying pan!” Finn shouted, even louder this time. He slumped against me, looking wretched.
Now everyone was staring. Silas, from the other side of the crowd, cupped his hands around his mouth.
“Professor!” he called out. “Professor, can your elixir cure _that_?”
A hush fell. All eyes turned from Finn to the man on the stage. It was a direct challenge. The Professor could not ignore it. His polished smile tightened just a fraction.
“Cure what, my boy?” he asked, his voice dripping with false patience.
“My friend here!” Silas yelled. “He’s got the Reverse Hiccups! A terrible thing! Can you fix him?”
Professor Fogg looked at Finn, who chose that moment to yelp, “—Wagon wheel!” and then groan as if in agony. The Professor was trapped. If he said no, his claims of a universal panacea would be exposed as a lie. If he said yes, he had to deliver a cure.
“But of course!” he boomed, recovering his composure. “The Miraculous Elixir knows no bounds! It has vanquished ailments far stranger than this in the palaces of the Orient! Bring the poor lad up here!”
This was it. The moment of truth. I helped Finn stumble through the crowd and up the steps to the stage. He was playing his part beautifully, twitching and looking miserable.
“—Sassafras!” he blurted out, right in the Professor’s face.
The Professor did not flinch. He looked Finn over with a professional eye. “A clear case of spasmodic glossolalia,” he announced gravely, inventing a name for our invention. “A disturbance of the linguistic humors. Very serious. But not for the Elixir!”
He poured a large spoonful of the dark liquid and held it out. “Drink this, my boy, and your troubles are over.”
Finn took the spoon. He looked at Silas and me in the crowd, a flicker of panic in his eyes. Then he screwed up his face, opened his mouth, and swallowed the stuff.
For a moment, nothing happened. The crowd held its breath. The Professor smiled a triumphant smile. Finn stood perfectly still.
And then, he spoke. His voice was clear and steady.
“It tastes,” he said, looking the Professor squarely in the eye, “like burnt molasses, river water, and a lie.”
Then he turned to the crowd. “—And my ma’s rheumatism is still hurting her something awful!”
A gasp went through the crowd. It was not the gasp of amazement the Professor was used to; it was the gasp of dawning comprehension. The shill, the man with the cured back, tried to start a cheer for the Professor, but his voice died in the sudden, cold silence. People looked at the bottles in their hands as if they were snakes. They looked at Finn, a healthy boy who had just called the emperor naked. They looked at the Professor, whose polished smile had finally cracked, revealing the cheap tin underneath.
Someone laughed. Then another. Old Mr. Harrison, the storekeeper, let out a great guffaw. “Burnt molasses and a lie! By jings, the boy’s got it!”
The spell was broken. The magic was gone. There was no rage, no mob. There was only a quiet, collective turning-away. People began to drift off, some with a sheepish grin, others with a look of disgust. They left the Professor and his mournful assistant standing on their stage in the middle of a nearly empty square, with a crate of unsold miracles at their feet.
We boys did not stay to gloat. We slipped away in the confusion, our hearts thumping with a wild, exhilarating joy. We ran for the river, for the shelter of our sycamore, and there, in the gathering dark, we let our triumph explode. We laughed until our sides ached. It was a victory far more glorious than the one with the locket. This one was public. It was a spectacle. It was a rout. We had not found a treasure; we had exposed a thief. We had not become pirates; we had become giant-killers.
As we sat there, catching our breath, the moon rose over the river, and Silas said, “I reckon there’s two kinds of adventures.”
“How’s that?” Finn asked, still wiping tears of laughter from his eyes.
“There’s the kind you keep a secret,” Silas said, looking out at the water. “And there’s the kind you tell the whole world. And I ain’t sure which one feels better. But I reckon you need both.”
And we sat in contented silence, three boys by a river, feeling that we had, for one perfect day, managed to get the world into exactly the right shape. The age of glorious adventures, we were learning, was a complicated and wonderful business. It was not just about what you found, but what you protected; not just what you won, but what you set right. And the best part of all was having a story to tell, a story that was all yours, and that was, every last word of it, the God’s-honest truth.

But glory is a thing that don't keep well in the dark, and after a while, when the first edges of the triumph had worn smooth, a different sort of thought began to creep in, quiet and slithery like a water snake. It was Finn who spoke it first.

“I reckon he’s powerful mad,” he said, poking at a wet log with a stick. He did not have to say who “he” was.

“He’s a hundred miles downriver by now,” said Silas, though his voice did not have the sureness it usually did. “He ain’t stopping till he hits New Orleans.”

“Maybe,” I said. “But he looked like the kind of man that don’t forget a face. And he saw all three of ours plain as day.”

A little bit of the warmth from our furnace seemed to go out of the air. We had kicked over a hornet’s nest, and whilst it was a grand thing to do, there was no telling if all the hornets had flown away. We fell to poking the fire again, each of us busy with his own pictures of what a man like the Professor might do if he was of a mind for revenge. We had made him a fool in front of the whole town, and worse, we had cost him his profit. Men have been shot for less than that in those parts. The adventure, which had seemed so clean and final, now felt like it had a long, ragged tail to it, and we were the ones holding on.

“Well, what can he do?” said Finn, trying to sound brave. “He can’t say we lied. The whole town knows we didn’t.”

“He don’t have to prove you lied,” said Silas, who was always thinking two steps ahead. “He just has to make folks wish they’d never listened to you in the first place. A man like that fights with whispers, not with fists.”

And that was a notion that sat heavier than a cold biscuit in the stomach. We had won a loud victory, but we did not know the first thing about fighting a quiet war. We sat there until the sky began to turn a watery gray, and the adventure did not seem quite so glorious anymore. It felt like a debt we had taken on, and we were not sure when the collector might come to call.

The next day was Sunday, and we went to church with our families, feeling a kind of fraudulent piety. It is a curious thing to sit in a hard pew, listening to a sermon about turning the other cheek, when you have just spent the better part of a day knocking a man flat on his back, spiritually speaking. We felt virtuous and wicked all at once. After the service, the churchyard was buzzing. The Professor was the only subject on anyone’s tongue. Old Mr. Harrison, who had laughed the loudest, clapped us on the back and said we had done the town a service. But others were not so sure. Mrs. Gable, the constable’s wife, looked at us with a pinched face.

“It is not for boys to be making a public spectacle,” she said, her voice thin and sharp. “There are proper channels for such things.”

We mumbled something and shuffled our feet. We saw that our victory had divided the town. Some saw us as heroes who had spoken the truth; others saw us as meddlesome boys who had disrupted the peace and embarrassed their neighbors who had been so easily fooled. The ones who had bought the elixir were the quietest of all; they looked at us with a kind of sour resentment, as if we had stolen from them, not the Professor. It seemed a strange and unjust thing that saving a man’s dollar could make him hate you for it.

We slipped away from the churchyard and made for the general store, thinking to get a stick of licorice and hear more from Mr. Harrison. But as we came around the corner, we saw a sight that stopped us dead. There, on the porch of the boarding-house across the square, stood the Professor. He was not packing his bags. He was talking to the preacher, the Reverend Mr. Sprague. And he did not look angry. He looked sad. He had a sorrowful, misunderstood air about him, and he held a handkerchief in his hand which he dabbed at his eyes now and again, though we were too far to see any actual tears. His shill, the man with the cured back, stood beside him, looking mournful, like a chief mourner at a funeral.

“By jings,” whispered Finn. “He’s still here.”

We crept closer, hiding behind a rain barrel at the side of the store, where we could hear without being seen. The Professor’s voice was not the booming, confident instrument it had been on the stage; it was soft and full of sorrow.

“It is not the money I mourn, Reverend,” he was saying, his voice trembling piteously. “Though heaven knows, it was all I had in this world. My earnings from three towns, nigh on a hundred dollars, all to be put toward the establishment of a free clinic for the indigent. It was my life’s dream.”

“A hundred dollars!” the preacher gasped, and we gasped too. A hundred dollars was a king’s ransom.

“But no,” the Professor went on, waving a dismissive, martyred hand. “It is the betrayal of trust that wounds me. To think that in this fine, God-fearing community, I should be set upon by young ruffians. That they should not only slander my good name, but rob me of my humble means… it is a blow from which my spirit may never recover.”

He let out a sound that was half a sob and half a sigh. We stared at each other, our mouths hanging open. Robbed? He was turning the whole thing on its head. He was not the thief; we were.

“Robbed?” said the Reverend. “But… who?”

“I dare not say,” the Professor said, shaking his head sadly. “They are but boys. Perhaps they knew not what they did. But I saw them, near my wagon, just before I discovered the loss. It happened in the confusion, after their… their cruel prank. My cash-box, broken open. The money, gone. All of it.”

Our blood ran cold. This was Silas’s quiet war, and it had begun without us even knowing it. The Professor was a genius of a particular, wicked sort. He knew he could not win an argument about the elixir, so he had changed the subject entirely. He was painting himself as a victim and us as common thieves. And in a town where we were known for skipping school and pirating on the river, the accusation had a dreadful ring of possibility to it.

We did not wait to hear more. We scurried away from the rain barrel and did not stop until we were safe under the high branches of our sycamore. The world had turned upside-down.

“He’s going to have us arrested!” I said, my voice shaking a little.

“He can’t,” Silas said, but he was pale. “There’s no proof. He’s just talking.”

“It ain’t proof that matters, it’s what people believe,” Finn said, his jaw set hard. “And folks’ll believe a sad story from a grown man in a suit before they’ll believe three boys who play at being pirates. He’s laying a trap, and the whole town is the bait.”

For the next two days, we lived in a state of dread. We kept to ourselves, avoiding the town square. We felt like we were being watched. Every time we saw Constable Gable making his rounds, our hearts jumped into our throats. The story had spread like fire in dry grass. We heard it from other boys; we saw it in the cool looks from adults. The tale had grown, too. Now the story was that we had been planning it all along, that our outburst at the show was just a distraction to cover the theft. The Professor, meanwhile, had become a figure of public sympathy. He stayed on at the boarding-house, a living, breathing monument to the town’s gullibility and our supposed villainy. He would walk through the town, head bowed, accepting quiet words of condolence. He was a master showman; his stage had simply gotten bigger.

On the third day, the trap was sprung. We were down by the river, trying to lose ourselves in the simple business of catching crawdads, when we saw him coming: Constable Gable, walking with that slow, important stride of his. He was not alone. Behind him was a small crowd of men, Mr. Harrison among them, looking uncomfortable. And at the head of them, pointing in our direction with a trembling finger, was the Professor himself.

“There,” the Professor cried, his voice ringing with righteous sorrow. “There they are. The very culprits.”

We froze, the crawdads forgotten. There was nowhere to run.

“Now, hold on, Professor,” the constable said, though he kept walking toward us. “Let’s not jump to conclusions. Finn, Silas, you, boy,” he said, nodding at me. “I need to have a word with you.”

We stood up, trying to look like we had nothing to hide, which is the surest way to look guilty.

“This man,” the constable said, gesturing to the Professor, “claims you boys stole a considerable sum of money from him. A hundred dollars.”

“We never!” Finn burst out. “He’s a liar! His medicine is a fake, and now he’s just mad we told everyone!”

The Professor gave a sad little smile. “The desperate slander of the guilty, Constable. It is to be expected.”

“We ain’t guilty!” I said. “We were right here, or at home. We never touched his money.”

“Their protestations are vehement,” the Professor observed to the crowd, as if he were a scientist studying insects. “A classic sign.”

Constable Gable looked tired. “The Professor claims you have a hideout, a spot you frequent. He says if a man was to look around, he might find evidence. Perhaps a portion of the stolen goods, carelessly hidden.” He looked at our sycamore tree. “This your spot?”

Our hearts sank. We knew, with a certainty that was cold and absolute, what he would find. The Professor had not just started a rumor; he had planted the seed from which it could grow into a solid fact.

“Go on, look,” Silas said, his voice quiet and defeated. “There’s nothing to find.”

But he knew it was a lie. The Professor, with a show of reluctance, pointed to a place near the roots of the great tree, where the dirt was a little disturbed. “I believe I saw one of them digging there, as if burying a dead animal. It struck me as odd at the time.”

The constable sighed and walked over to the spot. He nudged the loose earth with his boot. Then he bent down. When he stood up, he was holding a small, greasy-looking leather pouch with a draw-string. It was the kind of pouch a man might keep a large sum of money in. He opened it, and though it was mostly empty, he shook out a single, peculiar coin. It was a Spanish silver dollar, old and worn smooth on one side.

“This is my pouch,” the Professor said, his voice breaking with emotion. “And that… that was a coin my dear departed mother gave me. My good-luck piece. I kept it with my earnings always. Oh, the heartless little villains!”

A murmur went through the crowd. It was a damning performance. The evidence was circumstantial, but it was powerful. A known hideout. Disturbed earth. A distinctive pouch with a sentimental coin. It was a story that fit together perfectly. Our story, the truth, was just a frantic denial.

“Boys,” the constable said, and his voice was heavy. “This looks bad. This looks mighty bad.”

That night was the worst of our lives. We were not locked up—the constable was not ready to go that far with boys our age—but we were under a kind of house arrest. Our fathers had to give their word we would not leave our yards, and the looks on their faces were worse than any jail cell. My pa did not say much, but he looked at me in a way he never had before, a mix of anger and a deep, weary disappointment that made me feel about two inches tall. We had brought shame on our families. The grand adventure had curdled into a sordid little crime story, and we were the villains.

We could not see each other, but I knew Finn and Silas were feeling the same hollow misery. For a day, we had been giant-killers. Now we were just common thieves in the eyes of the world. And the worst part was our own powerlessness. The Professor’s lie was like a thick, sticky web, and the more we struggled, the more tangled we became.

But the spirit of a boy is a resilient thing. Misery can only hold it down for so long before it springs back. By the second night of our confinement, the despair began to burn off, leaving behind a hard, cold anger. We were not thieves. And we were not going to let a slick-haired, lying charlatan ruin our lives.

That night, after my folks were asleep, I slipped out my window. It was a trick I had perfected over long practice. I crept through the silent streets of the town, keeping to the shadows. I was not the only one with the idea. When I got to the edge of Silas’s yard, I gave the low whistle we used, the call of a screech owl. A moment later, his window slid open and he climbed out. We met up with Finn the same way. We convened in the one place that still felt safe, a little patch of woods behind the cemetery, far from our compromised sycamore.

“We have to do something,” Finn said, his voice a fierce whisper. “I ain’t going to be called a thief for the rest of my life.”

“But what?” I asked. “It’s his word against ours, and he’s got that pouch for proof.”

“Then we need our own proof,” Silas said. He had that look on his face he got when he was working on a puzzle. “He lied about us stealing. What if he lied about being robbed at all?”

We thought about that. “You mean he still has the money?” Finn said. “All of it?”

“Maybe,” Silas said. “A hundred dollars. He told the preacher he made that much in three towns. But his show here was a bust. He didn’t sell more than a dozen bottles before you shouted him down, Finn. Where did the rest of it come from?”

“His shill,” I said, the pieces starting to click. “The man with the back. He ain’t just an actor. He’s a partner.”

“Exactly,” Silas said. “The Professor gives the show, and the shill works the crowd, picking pockets. That’s how they make their real money. The elixir is just a distraction. And if they did that here, they didn’t get run out of town with empty pockets. They’ve got the money. They’ve got to.”

A plan began to form, a desperate and thrilling one. We had to prove the Professor was not a poor, robbed victim, but a rich, successful thief. We had to find the money.

“He’s at the widow Parker’s boarding-house,” Finn said. “Second floor, corner room. I asked the boy who delivers their groceries.”

“We’ll watch him,” Silas declared. “We’ll spy on him every night until we see something. He’s got to have the money hidden somewhere. He and his partner have to meet. They have to count it. And when they do, we’ll be there.”

It was a plan straight out of a dime novel, full of risk and uncertainty. But it was better than sitting in our yards waiting for the world to condemn us. It was a new adventure, born from the ashes of the old one. This time, we were not just fighting for glory; we were fighting for our names.

For three nights we kept a vigil. We’d sneak out after midnight and post ourselves in a great, shadowy elm tree across the street from the boarding-house. It gave us a clear view of the Professor’s window. The first two nights were a dead loss. The light in his window went out before we even got settled, and nothing stirred. We were tired and discouraged, and the mosquitos were eating us alive. But on the fourth night, our patience was rewarded.

A little after one in the morning, a light appeared in the Professor’s window. We saw his silhouette against the yellow lamplight. A few minutes later, another figure crept up the street and slipped in the side door of the boarding-house, the one the lodgers used. We did not have to see his face to know it was the shill. Not long after, a second shadow joined the Professor’s in the window.

They were being cautious. They had pulled the curtains, but the fabric was thin, and one of them was drawn just a half-inch shy of the frame. By squirming out onto a thick branch, I could get a sliver of a view into the room. Finn and Silas held my legs to keep me from falling.

What I saw made my heart hammer against my ribs. The Professor and his shill were on the floor, and between them was a heavy-looking carpet-bag. It was open, and they were taking out stacks of bills and piles of coin. It was a fortune. It was far more than a hundred dollars. They were running their hands through it, laughing low, their faces greedy in the lamplight. The Professor picked up a stack of bills and fanned himself with it, a smug, triumphant look on his face. This was not a man mourning the loss of his life’s savings. This was a man celebrating a grand haul.

I scrambled back to the trunk of the tree, my eyes wide. “They’ve got it,” I whispered, my voice trembling with excitement. “A whole bag full. He never lost a cent.”

We had him. We had the truth. But our truth was a secret, seen from a treetop in the dead of night. His lie was public, told in the bright light of day. How could we make our truth beat his lie?

“We tell the constable,” Finn said. “We tell him what we saw.”

“And he’ll say we’re just making up more stories to save our own skins,” Silas countered. “We need more than that. We need to show them. Not just tell them. We have to beat him at his own game. We need a spectacle.”

And so we laid our own trap. The next morning, Silas went to see the Reverend Mr. Sprague. He went looking humble and penitent, and he confessed. He said the guilt was too much to bear. He said we had indeed taken the Professor’s money, but that we had gotten scared and hidden it. And now, he said, we wanted to give it back. He told the preacher that we would bring the money to the town square at noon the next day, to return it to the Professor in front of the whole town and beg his forgiveness.

It was a terrible risk. We were admitting to the crime. But Silas was betting on the Professor’s greed and arrogance.

The news flew through town. By noon the next day, the square was more crowded than it had been for the medicine show. Everyone was there to see the delinquent boys get their public comeuppance. The Professor was there, of course, standing near the courthouse steps with his shill, looking grave and magnanimous. Constable Gable stood by, ready to officiate the proceedings.

We walked into the center of the square. I was carrying a canvas sack, weighted with rocks to make it look full. We looked miserable and scared, and it was not all acting.

“Professor,” the constable said. “These boys have something to say to you.”

Silas stepped forward. “Professor,” he said, his voice just loud enough to carry. “We… we wronged you. We took your money. And we want to give it back.”

A satisfied sigh went through the crowd. The Professor smiled a gentle, forgiving smile. “My boy,” he said. “It takes a brave man to admit his faults. I forgive you.”

“We buried it,” Silas continued, his eyes fixed on the Professor. “All of it. We were going to give it all back. But then we got to thinking. That Spanish dollar you said was from your mother… we didn’t want to just give it back in a bag. We thought you’d want it handed to you special.”

The Professor’s eyes narrowed just a fraction. “Why, yes. That is most considerate.”

“The thing is,” Silas said, his voice getting a little stronger, “we can’t remember which bag we put it in. See, after we took your hundred dollars, we got scared we’d be caught with such a big pile. So we divided it all up. Put it in the big carpet-bag with the rest of the money you and your friend here took from folks’ pockets.”

A confused silence fell over the square. The Professor’s smile froze on his face.

“What… what nonsense is this?” he stammered.

Finn stepped up. “He means the bag you were counting in your room two nights ago,” he said, pointing. “The one with the busted latch you have to tie shut with twine. You fanned yourself with the twenty-dollar bills, remember? Right before you told your partner you figured you’d cleared two hundred dollars in this town, easy.”

The shill went dead white. The Professor’s face, which had been a mask of pious sorrow, was now a canvas of pure fury.

“This is an outrage!” he bellowed, his showman’s voice returning. “They are compounding their lies! Constable, arrest these slanderous thieves!”

But it was too late. His mask had slipped, and everyone had seen the ugly face beneath. The townspeople were not looking at us anymore; they were looking at him. They were remembering his fine suit, and his smooth talk, and the worthlessness of his miracle cure. They were putting the pieces together.

“Is that so, Professor?” came a voice. It was old Mr. Harrison, pushing his way to the front. “You told the whole town you were broke, living on charity. But you had two hundred dollars in your room? That’s a funny kind of broke.”

The shill, seeing the way the wind was blowing, made a fatal error. He started to back away. Then he turned and ran.

“Get him!” someone shouted, and two farmers took off after him.

The Professor was trapped. His whole story had collapsed. He looked around at the sea of hard, knowing faces, and all the fight went out of him. He was a showman without an audience, a liar without a believer.

Constable Gable walked over to him and put a heavy hand on his shoulder. “Professor,” he said, his voice flat and cold. “I reckon you and I need to have a word. A long word. Down at my office.”

The spell was broken, for good this time. The victory was quiet, but it was absolute. We had not just exposed a thief; we had clawed our names back from the mud. We had fought a lie with the truth, and for the second time in a week, we had seen the truth win.

We did not stay for the aftermath. We slipped away, leaving the town

to be dealt with. We made for the riverbank, for the shade of the great weeping willow whose branches swept the ground like a dusty green curtain, and there we collapsed, our backs against its trunk. For a minute, we just breathed, the sound of our own panting loud in the quiet air. The shouts and commotion from the town square seemed a world away.

“Well,” said Finn, finally, a grin spreading slow across his face. “I reckon that’s that.”

“I reckon,” I said, feeling a grin of my own. It felt strange on my face, like a muscle I hadn’t used in a long time.

“Did you see his eyes when you mentioned the carpet-bag?” Finn’s voice was full of a gleeful wonder. “They went all small and piggy. Like a hog that’s just seen the butcher comin’ with his knife.”

“And his partner!” I said, sitting up straighter. “He turned whiter than a sheet on a washing line. I thought he’d faint dead away before he even got the notion to run.”

“He knowed the game was up,” said Finn, nodding wisely. He picked up a flat stone and sent it skipping across the water; it hopped four times before it sank. “When you got truth on your side, Silas, there ain’t no lie that can stand against it. It’s like a good stout stick against a spiderweb.”

“I suppose so.” I watched the ripples spread and vanish. “I was scared, though. When he hollered for the Constable. I thought for a second it was all going to turn against us.”

“Naw,” Finn said with a wave of his hand, though I had seen the tremor in his own knee. “They was already his judges by then. Every man in that square he’d sold his bottle of brown sugar water to. He didn’t have a friend left in the county, save that white-faced feller, and he was halfway to the next state. Say, Silas… that was a right smart idea you had.”

“Which one?”

“About his mother’s locket. That was the thing that hooked ’em. Made him out to be a monster, not just a thief. Folks’ll forgive a thief if he’s charming enough. But a man that’d steal from his own dear mother’s memory… well, that’s a different kind of varmint altogether.” He looked at me with a new respect. “I wouldn’t have thought of that. Not in a hundred years.”

Praise from Finn was a rare coin, and it made me feel a foot taller. “Well,” I said, trying to sound casual, as if I thought up such things every day. “It just came to me.”

We sat in a comfortable silence for a spell, two generals surveying a victorious field. The victory was sweet, sweeter than any stolen apple or piece of hard candy. It was the feeling of being right, and of having proved it. But as the first flush of triumph cooled, another thought, a more practical and worrisome one, began to creep into my mind. It was the same thought that had been nagging at the edges of our grand performance.

“Finn,” I said, my voice a little lower.

“What is it?”

“The carpet-bag.”

He turned from the river. “What about it?”

“Well, you know. The money. We said… we said we put his hundred dollars in it. With the rest of it.”

“That’s right,” he said, nodding. “And then we couldn’t remember which bag it was. That was a good touch. Made it sound like we was just foolish boys, not calculating ones.”

“Yes, but Finn… was there a bag?”

Finn’s grin returned, wider this time, and with a touch of mischief in it. He looked around, as if to make sure no spies were lurking in the cattails. Then he leaned closer. “There’s always a bag, Silas.”

My heart gave a little thump. “You mean… you really hid it?”

“Hid what? The hundred dollars he gave us? Of course. Hid it the minute we got it. You don’t think I’d walk around with that kind of fortune in my pocket, do you? Not with the Professor sniffing around. I put it in the old carpet-bag, the one Ma was going to throw out. And I hid the bag.”

“Where?”

“Down in the old ice-house. Tucked it behind a loose stone in the wall. Safe as a church.”

We looked at each other. The town’s justice was one thing. A hundred dollars was another thing entirely. A hundred dollars was a myth, a number you heard rich men talk about. It wasn’t a thing for two boys who thought a whole quarter was a grand piece of luck.

“What… what are we going to do with it?” I asked, my throat suddenly dry.

Finn’s eyes lit up with the fire of a thousand grand adventures. “Do with it? Silas, what *ain’t* we going to do with it? We could buy a raft! Not a rickety log thing, but a real one, with a cabin and a stove. We could float all the way to New Orleans. We could buy… why, we could buy every piece of candy in Mr. Henderson’s store. We could buy a pony. Two ponies!”

The ideas were intoxicating, like the Professor’s patent medicine, only these promised a cure for the ailment of being poor and ordinary. But a cold knot of practicality was forming in my stomach.

“It ain’t ours, Finn,” I said quietly. “He gave it to us to keep us quiet. It’s thief’s money.”

“He’s the thief, not us!” Finn retorted, jumping to his feet. “He stole it from all those people. We just… we just took it from him. It’s spoils of war, is what it is. We fought a battle and we won, and that’s the prize.”

“The prize was getting our names back,” I argued, though my voice lacked conviction. The thought of a pony was a powerful persuader.

“That was part of it. This is the other part. Don’t you see? It’s providence! We can’t just give it back. Who would we give it to? The Constable? He’d just put it in a box somewhere. And we can’t give it back to the Professor, that’s certain. No, it’s ours now. Finders keepers.” He said the last part with the unshakable authority of playground law, which in our world was second only to the word of God.

“But a hundred dollars…” The sum was so vast it was frightening. “People will ask where we got it. We can’t just start buying ponies.”

“We won’t,” he said, his mind already working, spinning plans like a spider spins its web. “We’ll be careful. We’ll spend it a little at a time. A nickel here, a dime there. Nobody will notice. But first, we got to see it. All of it. Together.”

The thought was both thrilling and terrifying, like standing on the edge of a high cliff. There was no arguing with Finn when he got this look in his eye. It was the same look he’d had when he’d proposed we walk the top rail of the railway bridge, the same look that had led to us being chased by Mr. McGregor’s bull. It was the look that promised trouble and adventure in equal measure.

“All right,” I breathed. “Let’s go see it.”

The old ice-house stood half-collapsed at the edge of town, a relic from before anyone could remember. Its stone walls were cool and damp even on the hottest day, and the inside smelled of earth and decay. We slipped through the broken door, our eyes adjusting to the gloom. Finn went straight to the far wall and felt around.

“It’s here,” he whispered, his voice echoing in the small space. He worked his fingers into a crack and pulled. A large, flat stone came loose with a soft grating sound, revealing a dark cavity behind it. Finn reached inside and grunted with effort as he dragged out a faded, flower-patterned carpet-bag with a busted leather strap. It was heavier than I expected.

We carried it out into the daylight, back to the secrecy of the willows. With trembling fingers, Finn fumbled with the clasp. It sprung open. We peered inside.

It was not just our hundred dollars.

On top lay the neat bundle of bills the Professor had given us. But underneath it, oh, underneath it was a jumble of wealth that took our breath away. There were silver dollars, dozens of them, and heaps of smaller coins. And there were more bills, wadded up, creased, and stuffed into the corners. It was not just the Professor’s takings from this town, but likely from the last three towns he’d swindled. We stared, speechless, at the hoard. It was a pirate’s treasure, a king’s ransom.

“Finn,” I whispered, my voice choked with awe. “You said… you said in the square… that you’d put it all in the bag.”

“I was just talking,” he whispered back, his eyes wide. “I was just makin’ it up. I never… I never dreamed…”

“You told a lie that turned out to be the truth.”

“I reckon I did.” He reached in and pulled out a twenty-dollar bill, a denomination so rare we’d only ever seen them in pictures. He held it as if it were a sacred text. “Silas,” he said solemnly. “We are rich men.”

The words hung in the air between us. Rich men. We were no longer just Silas and Finn, two boys in patched trousers. We were potentates, financiers, captains of industry. We were the masters of our own destiny.

“We have to count it,” I said, the practical part of my brain struggling to surface through the fog of stupendous fortune.

We dumped the entire contents onto my jacket, which we’d spread on the grass. We separated the coins from the paper, our hands shaking with a kind of fever. We made stacks of silver dollars, of halves, of quarters, dimes, and nickels. We smoothed out the crumpled bills. It was a slow and holy process. The final tally was so absurd, so impossible, that we had to count it three times before we would believe it.

Three hundred and forty-two dollars. And change.

We sat back on our heels, dumbfounded. The world had tilted on its axis. The sun seemed brighter, the river more brilliant. This was a sum that could change a life. It could buy a small farm. It could set a man up in business. In the hands of two boys, it was a force of nature, as powerful and unpredictable as the thunderstorm that had wrecked Tom Sawyer’s camp.

“Three hundred…” Finn’s voice trailed off. He swallowed hard. “Silas, this is… this is more money than my pa has seen in his whole life.”

That was true for my pa as well, and for most of the men in town. This bag held a secret that would set the whole county on its ear if it ever got out. And it was our secret.

“We can’t keep it,” I said, the words tasting like ash in my mouth. The sheer size of it made it different. A hundred dollars was a fortune; this was a crime waiting to happen. “It’s too much. It’s dangerous.”

“It’s ours!” Finn insisted, his voice fierce. He swept a protective hand over the pile, as a dragon might guard its gold. “I told you. We took it from a thief. That makes it ours by right of conquest!”

“They’ll hang us if they find us with it!”

“They won’t find us! Who’s to know? The Professor and his friend are the only ones who know it exists, and one of them is in jail and the other is probably in the next territory by now. They can’t very well report it stolen, can they? ‘Constable, those boys took the money I stole from everyone!’ He’d be laughed out of town.”

His logic was maddeningly sound. We were in possession of a perfect, untraceable crime. The money belonged to a hundred different people in a half-dozen different towns, all of them cheated out of a dollar or two at a time. There was no way to ever give it back. The only two choices were to hand it all over to the Constable, who would have a thousand questions we could not answer, or to keep it.

“We’d be no better than him,” I said, but the protest was weak. The pile of silver dollars gleamed in the sun, whispering promises of freedom and glory.

“We’re a whole sight better,” Finn said. “He stole it from poor folk to buy snake oil and fancy suits. We’ll… we’ll use it for good things. Important things. Like a raft. And fishing equipment. The best kind.”

We argued back and forth for the better part of an hour, a hushed and frantic debate under the drooping willow. My conscience fought a desperate, losing battle against Finn’s grand visions. Every time I mentioned duty or honesty, he countered with adventure and glory. Finally, beaten down by the sheer, glittering weight of the treasure, we reached a compromise.

“We won’t spend it,” I said. “Not yet. We’ll just… we’ll keep it. We’ll hide it. We’ll be its guardians.”

“Guardians of the treasure!” Finn loved the sound of that. It had a romantic, noble ring to it. “All right. We’ll be guardians. For now. But we need a better hiding place. The ice-house is too common. People go there sometimes.”

“Where, then?”

Finn scanned the landscape, his eyes narrowed in thought. He looked past the river, toward the wooded hills that rose to the east of town. “The old Miller cave,” he said. “No one’s been up there in years. Not since old man Miller claimed he saw a panther’s eyes shining in the back of it.”

The Miller cave was a perfect spot. It was a good two-mile hike, well off any main path, and the panther story, whether true or not, had been enough to keep other boys away for a generation. It was our best and only option.

We carefully packed the treasure back into the carpet-bag. The weight of it now felt different. It was not just money; it was a burden, a secret that we would have to carry every minute of every day. We took a winding, circuitous route out of town, sticking to back alleys and overgrown paths, our heads on a swivel, watching for any sign of pursuit. Every snapped twig, every distant dog’s bark, sent a jolt of fear through me.

The path up to the cave was steep and choked with thorny vines. We took turns carrying the bag, sweat stinging our eyes and soaking our shirts. Finally, scratched and panting, we reached the small, dark opening in the hillside. It was no bigger than a hogshead, a black mouth half-hidden by a tangle of wild grapevines.

“This is it,” Finn panted, dropping the bag. “Home of the panther.”

We peered inside. The air that drifted out was cool and smelled of damp stone and something else, something wild and musky. My heart hammered against my ribs.

“You don’t think… you don’t think it’s really in there, do you?” I whispered.

“There’s one way to find out,” Finn said, though he did not sound as confident as he usually did. He picked up a stout stick. “You wait here with the treasure. I’ll go in and make sure the den is empty.”

He was always the braver of us two, and I was grateful for it now. I watched as he took a breath, squared his shoulders, and disappeared into the blackness. I was left alone with the bag, the silence of the woods pressing in on me. A minute passed. It felt like an hour. I imagined a terrible fight, the sound of claws and teeth, a final, desperate cry.

Then, his voice echoed from within, startlingly loud. “It’s clear! And bigger than it looks! Bring the gold, Silas!”

Relief washed over me so powerfully my knees went weak. I hoisted the heavy bag and crawled into the opening. Inside, the cave was not just a hole but a small, misshapen room, perhaps ten feet across, with a ceiling high enough to stand in. A thin crack in the rock above let in a sliver of dusty light, just enough to see the damp, glistening walls.

“See?” Finn said, his voice full of pride, as if he had carved the cave himself. “Perfect.”

We explored every corner of our new headquarters. In the very back, behind a jutting piece of rock, was a natural shelf, dry and hidden from the entrance. It was a vault made by nature herself. With a reverence befitting the occasion, we placed the carpet-bag upon the shelf.

“There,” Finn said. “Safe from panthers, thieves, and constables. No one will ever find it here.”

We stood for a moment in the dim light, admiring our work. The bag sat there, a dumpy, floral-patterned dragon, sleeping on its hoard. We were no longer just boys. We were men of property. We were the secret keepers, the guardians of the cave. We had a secret so grand and terrible it bound us together tighter than blood.

As we crawled back out into the bright afternoon sun, blinking like owls, the world looked the same, but we knew it was not. We had passed through a door and could never go back. Every person we saw, every conversation we had, would now be seen through the lens of our great secret. It was a thrilling and a heavy thought. We had won our battle in the town square, but as we stood on that hillside, dirty, tired, and secretly rich, I had the strange and certain feeling that our real troubles had only just begun.

My mother was at the back door, peeling potatoes into a bucket. When she saw me, her face went through three quick changes: first, a flood of relief that made her look ten years younger; second, a wave of anger that brought all the years back and a few more besides; and third, a settled, weary sadness that was the worst of them all. She did not yell. I had expected a thrashing, and had braced myself for it, but the quiet disappointment was a heavier blow. It made me feel small and mean. That night, lying in my own bed, which felt impossibly soft and strange, I could not sleep. The adventure was over. The pirating, the storm, the grand secret—it all felt like a fever-dream. But the bag of money was real. It was a secret lump under the mattress of my thoughts. I thought of it sitting in the dark, damp cave, a floral-patterned ghost, and I felt a prickle of fear that was entirely different from the fear of a panther. It was a cold, grown-up sort of fear.

The next few days were a torment. Finn and I met by the schoolhouse fence, as we always did, but things were not the same. We could not talk about our usual subjects—what we’d do if we had a raft, or how to build a better slingshot, or whether Jebediah’s father really did wrestle a bear. There was only one subject, and it was too dangerous to speak of. We talked around it, in low voices, our eyes darting about to see who might be listening. “Everything… all right?” Finn would ask, his eyebrows raised in a secret code. “I reckon,” I’d say. “Up on the hill.” We had become conspirators, and the fun had gone out of it already. The world was full of eyes now; every window was a spy and every bird-call a warning. We trusted no one. We started at the sound of a heavy footstep behind us, and the sight of the town constable, Mr. Dawkins, was enough to make my blood run cold. He was a portly man with a kind face, but now his face looked like a judge’s, and I was sure his friendly eyes could see the guilt written all over me.

Every conversation was a minefield. At the dinner table, my father would talk about the price of lumber or the poor fishing, and I would nod, but my mind was in a cave on a hill. I imagined a hundred disasters. A sudden landslide might seal the cave forever. A hunter might stumble upon it. The real panther might come home and decide it liked the look of that carpet-bag for a pillow. We had to check on it. The idea took root in our minds and grew into a desperate need.

“We got to go back,” Finn said one afternoon, as we were pretending to fish for tadpoles in Miller’s Creek. His voice was a low, urgent whisper. “Just to see. Just to make sure it’s still there.”

“What if someone sees us?” I whispered back.

“We’ll be careful. Cagier than foxes, Silas. We’ll go at dusk, through the woods. No one will see.”

So we went. We did not take the path this time but scrambled through the thickest part of the woods, getting scratched by brambles and snagged on vines, moving as silently as we could. The woods, which had always been our kingdom, now felt like enemy territory. Every snapping twig sounded like a gunshot. When we finally reached the clearing, we lay on our bellies in the undergrowth for a full ten minutes, watching the cave entrance. The grapevines hung just as they had. The shadows were deep and still. There was no sign that anyone had been there.

My heart was beating a fast, unsteady rhythm, like a trapped bird. “You go,” I breathed. “I’ll watch.”

Finn nodded, his face pale in the twilight. He crawled on his hands and knees to the opening and slipped inside. The waiting was the worst part. The silence stretched and stretched until I was sure I would scream. I imagined Finn finding the cave empty, the bag gone. Or worse, finding someone in there waiting for him. Then his head poked back out. “It’s here,” he mouthed, and his face was shining with relief.

He crawled back and we lay there in the bushes, the danger past for now, but the relief did not last. It was replaced by a new, gnawing feeling. The treasure was safe, but what was the good of it? It was just sitting in a hole. We were rich, but we were just as poor as we had ever been. We still had to wear mended shirts and eat corn mush for breakfast. We were the owners of a treasure we could not use.

“It ain’t fair,” Finn said, voicing the thought that had been festering in my own mind. “All that money. And look at my boots. My toe’s nigh on poking through.”

“My knife’s got a broken tip,” I said. “I saw a Barlow knife in Mr. Gable’s store window. With a bone handle. Cost a whole dollar.”

A dollar. We had thousands of them. The thought was maddening. “We could take just one,” Finn said, his voice barely audible. “Just one little silver dollar. Who would know? We could say we found it.”

“Found it?” My conscience, which had been sleeping soundly, gave a sudden, painful twitch. “That’s lying, Finn.”

“It ain’t lying so much as it is… being practical,” he argued. “What’s the difference between finding a dollar in the dirt and finding it in a bag that nobody owns? The way I see it, that money is lost. And we found it. So if we take one dollar, we’re just taking a little piece of what we already found.”

His logic was like a winding path that you know is going the wrong way, but it is the only path you can see. We argued about it in whispers for the better part of an hour. Every good and honest thought I had, Finn had a clever, slippery answer for. By the time the moon was up, we had a plan. It was a dreadful, wonderful plan, and it made me feel sick with excitement.

The next day, we put the plan into action. We went back to the cave, in the bright, unforgiving light of morning this time, telling ourselves that being bold was safer than being sneaky. Inside, with the dusty light filtering down, Finn opened the carpet-bag. I had not seen the money up close since that first frantic night, and the sight of it stole my breath. Stacks of greenbacks, rolls of gold coins, and a heavy scattering of silver dollars, all jumbled together. It did not look real. It looked like a pirate’s hoard from a storybook.

With trembling hands, Finn fished out a single silver dollar. It was heavy and cool in his palm. It was the prettiest, most terrible thing I had ever seen. He closed the bag and we put it back on its shelf, behind the rock. Then we crawled out of the cave, leaving the fortune behind, carrying only its single, shining seed.

We walked into town with the dollar in Finn’s pocket. His hand was clamped over it, as if he were afraid it might burn a hole through the cloth and fall out. Our plan was for him to go into the General Store alone. I was to wait outside, by the horse trough, and act natural. Acting natural was the hardest thing I had ever had to do. I tried to whistle, but my mouth was too dry. I tried to skip a stone across the dusty road, but my hand was shaking. I felt as if the whole town was watching me, that Mr. Gable the storekeeper, and Reverend Tucker, and even old Widow Douglas, who was deaf as a post, all knew that I was an accomplice to a crime.

Finn was in the store for what felt like a year. I saw him through the window, standing at the counter. I saw Mr. Gable take something from the glass case. I saw Finn put the silver dollar on the counter. My heart stopped. Mr. Gable picked it up. He looked at it. He looked at Finn. He seemed to be looking at him for a very long time. I was sure it was over. I was sure he was about to shout for the constable. I turned to run, but my feet were glued to the ground.

Then, the door of the store opened, and Finn walked out. He did not look at me. He walked straight past, his face set like stone, and headed for the alley beside the blacksmith’s shop. I waited another minute, for my legs to remember how to work, and then I followed him.

I found him in the alley, leaning against the brick wall, his eyes wide. He held out his hand. In it was the Barlow knife. It was a beauty, with a polished bone handle and two gleaming blades, one long and one short. And in his other hand, he held a small pile of coins. Change.

“He didn’t say nothing,” Finn whispered, his voice hoarse. “He just looked at the dollar, and then he looked at me, and he said, ‘Well now, ain’t that a handsome piece of silver for a young man to be carrying.’”

“What did you say?”

“I said I found it. Down by the riverbank. Where the ferry docks. I said I was skipping stones and there it was, half in the mud.”

“Did he believe you?”

“I reckon. He gave me the change, didn’t he?”

We stood there in the alley, staring at the knife. It was proof that our treasure was real. It was a beautiful, sharp, solid fact. Finn opened the bigger blade. It shone in the dim light. He tested the edge with his thumb. “It’s perfect,” he breathed.

We had done it. We had spent a piece of the treasure and the world had not ended. The constable had not appeared. Lightning had not struck us down. We had gotten away with it. The relief was so great it made me dizzy, but underneath it, a new feeling was taking shape. Before, we had only stolen the money. Now we had used it. We had washed it clean with a lie and turned it into a knife. The secret was no longer sleeping in the cave; we were carrying it in our pocket.

The knife became a new source of trouble. Every boy in town wanted to see it, to hold it, to test its blade on a piece of wood. “Where’d you get it, Finn?” they’d ask. “A knife like that costs a pretty penny.”

“Found the money,” he would say, with a carelessness that I, knowing the truth, found terrifying. He got better at the lie every time he told it. He added details. He described the exact spot by the ferry landing, the glint of silver in the dark mud, the way his heart had leaped. He was a natural actor, and the other boys, for the most part, believed him. It was a lucky thing to find a dollar, but not an impossible one.

But Jebediah, whose own knife was a sad-looking thing with one rusted blade, was not convinced. Jebediah was a boy with suspicion built into his bones. He watched Finn with his narrow, pig-like eyes. “Found it, you say?” he’d drawl, spitting a stream of tobacco juice he wasn’t old enough to be chewing. “Funny how some folks have all the luck. My pappy says honest luck is rare as a hen’s tooth. Other kinds, though, they’re plentiful.”

The insinuation was as plain as the nose on your face. Finn’s face would darken, and his hand would tighten on the bone handle of the knife in his pocket, and for a moment I would see a fight brewing. But a fight would draw attention, and attention was the one thing we could not afford. We had to swallow the insults and walk away, which was a bitter pill for a boy like Finn.

The pressure was building, day by day. Then one morning, the thing we had dreaded most happened. A new notice was nailed to the post outside the constable’s office. It was not about a lost cow or an upcoming town meeting. I saw a crowd of men gathered around it, talking in low, serious tones. I crept closer, my stomach in a knot, and read the stark black letters.

REWARD: $500

For information leading to the recovery of a sum of money, approximately six thousand dollars, taken from the offices of the Southern Overland Stage & Freight Company. The money was contained in a floral-patterned carpet-bag of Brussels make. The bag is dark blue with faded pink and yellow roses.

Information to be given to Constable Dawkins or to the Agent at the Stage & Freight office. All information will be held in the strictest confidence.

I read it three times, but the words did not change. Every detail was a hammer blow. Floral-patterned. Brussels make. Dark blue with faded pink and yellow roses. It was our bag. Our secret was not a secret anymore. It was written on a piece of paper and nailed up for the whole world to see. Five hundred dollars. It was a fortune. It was enough to turn any man in town into a detective. It was enough to make a man search every hollow log and every dark cave for miles around.

I felt the blood drain from my face. I backed away from the crowd, stumbling like a drunkard. I had to find Finn. I ran, not caring who saw me, my feet pounding the dirt road. I found him down by the creek, skipping stones, the new knife in his hand, whittling a piece of driftwood into a little boat.

“Finn!” I gasped, out of breath.

He looked up, his cheerful expression vanishing when he saw my face. “What is it? What’s wrong, Silas? You look like you’ve seen a ghost.”

“Worse,” I panted, leaning against a willow tree to keep from falling. “Much worse.”

And I told him. I told him about the notice, about the reward, about the description of the bag. As I spoke, the color drained from his face, too, leaving him pale and pinched. The half-finished boat slipped from his fingers and fell into the water, where the current caught it and swept it away. He did not even notice. He just stared at me, the beautiful bone-handled knife hanging forgotten from his hand.

“Five hundred dollars,” he whispered. The words had no triumph in them, only a terrible, hollow awe. “They want it back that bad.”

“Every man in town will be looking for it,” I said, my voice trembling. “They’ll be out with shovels and picks. They’ll search the woods. They’ll search the hills. They’ll find the cave, Finn. They’ll find it.”

Panic seized us then, a wild, unthinking terror. Our secret kingdom, our fortress, had become a trap. The treasure was no longer a promise of riches; it was a ticking bomb. “We have to go back,” Finn said, his voice tight. “Now. We have to get the bag. We have to move it. Or get rid of it. I don’t know!”

We ran. We forgot all our rules about being careful and sneaky. We ran straight up the main path to the hills, past farmhouses and pastures, our only thought to get to the cave before anyone else did. We scrambled up the last, steep incline, our lungs burning, and burst into the little clearing.

The first thing I saw was that something was wrong. A branch near the entrance, a branch I knew as well as my own hand, was broken. And the tangle of grapevines that hid the opening was pushed aside, not a lot, but enough. Enough that someone who was looking would see the dark space behind it.

Finn saw it too. He stopped dead, his hand flying to the knife in his pocket. We stood there, frozen, listening. The woods were silent. Too silent.

“Someone’s been here,” I breathed. The words were like ice in my throat.

Finn did not answer. He took a slow, deliberate step towards the cave. Then another. He was peering at the ground near the entrance. He knelt down, and I crept up beside him. And there, in a patch of soft, damp earth just inside the shadow of the overhang, was a footprint.

It was not my footprint. It was not Finn’s. It was not the track of any animal. It was the clear, deep impression of a man’s boot, a heavy boot with a worn-down heel. It was fresh. And it was pointing into the cave.

For a moment we did not breathe. We were two statues carved out of pure fright, our eyes fixed on that single, terrible mark in the dirt. It was an invasion, a violation of the highest order. Our secret place, the one spot on earth that belonged only to us, had been found. The silence of the woods pressed in, thick and heavy, and I could hear the frantic drumming of my own heart, a sound like a woodpecker trying to get out of my ribs.

Finn was the first to move. He rose slowly from his knees, his face a white mask in the gloom. He wiped his muddy hand on his trousers, but his eyes never left the dark opening of the cave. The knife was still in his other hand, not held like a boy whittling a stick, but like a weapon. His knuckles were white.

“He’s in there,” Finn whispered. His voice was different, flat and hard. It was not the voice of the boy I ran through the woods with. It was the voice of a man, cornered and dangerous.

“We got to go,” I said, my own voice a reedy quaver. “We got to run, Finn. Get the sheriff.”

“And tell him what?” he hissed, turning on me with a look so fierce it made me take a step back. “Tell him we found a footprint near the cave where we hid the five hundred dollars we stole? Use your head, Silas!”

He was right, of course. We were caught. Caught between the man in the cave and the town full of men who would be looking for the bag. To tell anyone was to confess. To do nothing was to lose the treasure. And to go in—well, to go in was madness.

“What’ll we do, then?” I asked, my teeth chattering.

Finn looked from me to the cave, and a flicker of his old self, the pirating, adventuring self, came back into his eyes. It was a look of grim and terrible resolve. “We’re going in,” he said. “It’s our cave. It’s our money.”

“He’ll kill us, Finn!”

“Maybe,” he said, taking a deep breath. “And maybe he’s already gone. Maybe he just looked inside and left. But there’s only one way to know.” He took the branch I had always used to pull the vines shut and hefted it like a club. It was a poor weapon, but it seemed to give him comfort. “You wait here if you want. I’m going in.”

He did not wait for an answer. He took one step, then another, moving with a slinking quietness I had never seen in him before. He slipped past the disturbed curtain of vines and was swallowed by the blackness. And I was alone.

The silence rushed back in, ten times more awful than before. Every rustle of a leaf was the man coming out to get me. Every bird call was a signal. I stood there for what felt like an age, my legs trembling so badly I thought they would give way. I could not leave him. As much as every part of me screamed to run, to flee back to the safety of town and my own bed, I could not leave Finn to face whatever was in there by himself. We were partners.

With a sob of pure terror, I grabbed a heavy-looking rock from the ground, a stone that was round and fit nicely in my palm, and I followed him.

The air inside was cold and smelled of damp earth and something else, something sharp and sour like spilled whiskey. It was black as pitch. I could not see Finn, could not see my own hand in front of my face. I could only hear. And what I heard was the sound of breathing. It was not Finn’s quick, nervous breaths. It was a slow, heavy rasp, like a saw cutting through wet wood. And it was coming from deeper in the cave, from the main chamber, our throne room.

I felt a hand grab my arm and I nearly shrieked, but it was Finn. He put a finger to his lips, though he could not have seen my face, and then he pulled me along. We moved inch by inch, our bare feet making no sound on the sandy floor. Our eyes were slowly adjusting, and the solid blackness began to resolve itself into deeper shades of grey. The great chamber opened before us, vast and secret, a cathedral of stone. And in the middle of it, a tiny point of light flickered.

It was a lantern, a beat-up tin lantern, turned down so low it barely cast a glow more than a few feet. And in that sickly yellow circle of light sat a man. He was propped against the flat rock we called the King’s Throne, and he was a fearsome sight. He was big, with a thick, tangled black beard, and his clothes were dark and ragged. A wide-brimmed hat lay on the ground beside him. But the most terrible thing was the canvas bag, our bag, which lay open by his side. And his hands were full of money. Not the neat stacks of bills we had seen, but a loose, rumpled pile of them. He was not counting it. He was just holding it, staring at it with a strange, hungry look on his face.

And then I saw the other thing. A dark, ugly stain was spreading across the thigh of his trousers, and the hand that was not holding the money was pressed tight against it. And on the ground near his leg, almost hidden in the shadows, lay a pistol.

Finn and I were frozen behind a great pillar of rock, watching. The man’s heavy breathing was the only sound. He did not seem to know we were there. He shifted his weight, and a low groan escaped his lips, a sound of pure agony. He let the money fall from his hand back into the bag and swore, a long, ugly string of words that made my ears burn. He tried to get a better look at his leg, twisting his body, and as he did, the lantern light fell across his face. His skin was waxy and pale, beaded with sweat. His eyes were glassy with pain.

This was no fearsome outlaw, no triumphant thief. This was a wounded animal, holed up in the dark to die.

The sight of his pain did something to my fear; it did not go away, but it changed. It became mixed with a kind of awful pity. Finn must have felt it too, because the hand that held the branch had relaxed its grip. The man’s head slumped forward onto his chest, and his rasping breaths grew shallower.

“He’s hurt bad,” I whispered, my mouth close to Finn’s ear.

“Shot, I reckon,” Finn breathed back. “Must have been when he took the bag.”

We watched him for a long time. He did not move again. His breathing settled into a steady, ragged rhythm. He was either asleep or unconscious. The pistol lay there, a dull gleam in the lantern light. The bag of money sat open, a fortune waiting to be taken. Everything we had dreamed of was right there. All we had to do was walk over, pick it up, and leave this man to his fate. It was what a pirate would do. It was what we had always said we would do.

Finn’s eyes were fixed on the bag. I could see the greed and the desire warring with the fear in his face. He looked at me, and his eyes asked the question. I shook my head, just a little. I could not do it. I could not walk past that groaning, wounded man and take the money, leaving him to die in our cave. It felt wrong. It felt like a curse.

“We can’t just leave him,” I whispered.

“He’s a thief, Silas,” Finn hissed. “And maybe a killer. He’d do the same to us, and not think twice.”

“But we ain’t him,” I said. The words came out before I even thought them. And they felt true. We played at being pirates and robbers, but we were not. We were just boys.

The man stirred, and we flattened ourselves against the rock. He mumbled something, a string of nonsense words. “Water,” he croaked, his voice like dry leaves skittering across stone. “Just… water.” His head fell back against the rock, and he was still again.

The spring. The little trickle of clear, cold water that ran down the back wall of the cave, the one we called the secret spring. It was not twenty feet from him.

I looked at Finn. He looked at me. The same thought was in both our minds. It was a terrible, stupid, noble thought. Finn gave a short, sharp nod. He set down his branch-club and pointed at me, then at the spring. Then he pointed to himself, and then to the pistol. I understood. I was to get water. He was going to try for the gun. It was the bravest, most foolish plan I had ever heard.

My heart hammering, I slipped away from our hiding place, hugging the curving wall of the chamber. The man did not move. I was a ghost in the darkness. I reached the back wall and felt the familiar dampness of the rock. I found the little hollow where the water pooled before it trickled down. I cupped my hands, filled them with the icy water, and started back. It was the longest journey of my life. Every drip of water on the floor sounded like a gunshot.

As I crept, I watched Finn. He was moving like a cat, a shadow detaching itself from other shadows. He was on the far side of the man, his eyes locked on the pistol. He was closer, closer. The man’s breath hitched, and Finn froze. Then the rhythm returned, and Finn moved again. His hand was reaching out, his fingers stretching, trembling.

I was almost to the man now, the cold water spilling through my fingers. I knelt in the dirt a few feet from him. His smell was strong here, sweat and whiskey and the coppery tang of blood. “Mister?” I whispered.

His head jerked up. His eyes, blurry and unfocused, tried to find me in the gloom. “Who’s that?” he rasped, his hand instinctively going for his side.

At that exact moment, Finn’s fingers closed around the butt of the pistol. But he was not as quiet as he thought. The gun scraped against the rock. The man’s head whipped around, and his eyes, suddenly sharp and full of a terrible, clear light, fixed on Finn.

“You little thief!” he roared, and with a surge of strength that seemed impossible, he lunged. Not at Finn, but at the lantern. He knocked it over. The flame sputtered, flared up, and died.

We were plunged into absolute, perfect blackness.

For a second there was only the sound of the man’s ragged gasp and my own terrified breathing. Then came a scrabbling sound, and a great bellow of rage and pain. “Give me that!” the man yelled.

“Get back!” It was Finn’s voice, high and strained.

There was a heavy thud, a grunt of pain—from which of them, I could not tell—and then the sound of a body slumping to the ground. Then, silence.

“Finn?” I cried out, my voice cracking. “Finn, are you all right?”

Silence.

“Finn!”

A weak voice came from the darkness near where the lantern had been. “I’m here, Silas.” It was Finn. “I got the gun.”

“Is he—?”

“I don’t know. I think I hit him. He’s not moving.”

I could hear him shuffling, fumbling in the dark. A match scraped, failed. Another scraped, and a tiny flame bloomed, showing Finn’s face, pale and wide-eyed. He held the pistol in one hand and the match in the other. He looked past me, his eyes going wide with horror. I turned and looked.

The man was on the ground, lying on his side. He was not moving. And from a spot on his forehead, a dark trickle of blood was running down into his matted beard.

“Did you kill him?” I whispered, the words catching in my throat.

“I don’t know,” Finn said, his voice shaking. “I hit him with the gun. He just fell.”

The match went out, and the blackness swallowed us again. But the image was burned into my mind: the still body, the trickle of blood. We were no longer just thieves. Now we were killers. The thought was so monstrous I felt my legs go weak, and I sat down hard on the cold ground. The water I had been carrying was long gone.

“We got to get a light,” Finn said, his voice trying to be steady. “We got to see.”

He fumbled for another match, lit it, and managed to get the lantern upright and relit. The yellow glow once more pushed back the darkness, and the scene it revealed was even more ghastly than I had imagined. The man had not moved. He lay in a heap, a discarded puppet. The bag of money had been knocked over, and a few loose bills lay scattered on the dirt floor like dead leaves.

Finn stood over the man, the pistol held loosely in his hand. He was staring at the man’s bloody head. “I didn’t mean to,” he said, to the man, or to me, or to the cave itself. “I didn’t mean to hit him so hard.”

I crawled over, my terror making me clumsy. I put my ear near the man’s mouth, afraid of what I would not hear. But it was there. A faint, shallow breath. And another. He was alive.

“He’s breathing,” I said, and the relief was so great it was like a physical blow. “He’s just knocked out.”

Finn sagged against the rock pillar, the pistol hanging from his hand. We were not murderers. Not yet. But we were in deeper than ever. We had assaulted a man, a wounded man, and he was lying unconscious in our secret cave.

And then we heard it. Faintly, from far away, a sound that cut through the silence of the woods. It was a shout. Then another. And then the barking of a dog.

“The search party,” I breathed. “They’re in the hills.”

Panic, cold and sharp, seized us again. They would have dogs. The dogs would find the path. They would follow the scent right to the mouth of the cave. We were trapped. We, and the unconscious man, and the bag of money.

“The money!” Finn said, scrambling for it. “We got to hide the money!”

“Where?” I cried. “They’ll find the cave, Finn! They’ll find everything!”

“The sinkhole!” he said, his eyes wild. “The deep pit. We can throw it down there. They’ll never find it.”

It was a desperate plan. The back of the cave system had a deep, narrow fissure in the floor, a place we had never been able to see the bottom of. We called it the Pit to Nowhere.

Finn began stuffing the loose bills back into the canvas bag. He worked with frantic haste, his hands shaking. “Help me, Silas!”

I was frozen. The shouts were getting closer. “We got to run, Finn! Just leave it all and run!”

“No!” he yelled, his face contorted. “I ain’t leaving it! Not after all this! Help me!” He grabbed the heavy bag in one hand and the lantern in the other. The pistol he had shoved into the waistband of his trousers. “Come on!”

He plunged into the narrow passage that led to the back of the cave. I had no choice but to follow. We stumbled through the winding tunnels, the lantern light throwing our dancing, monstrous shadows against the walls. The shouts from outside were clearer now; we could make out words. “—over here!” “—found a track!”

We burst into the last, small chamber, where the floor just ended in a black, ragged hole. The air that came up from it was cold and smelled of deep, wet stone.

“This is it,” Finn panted. He held the bag over the abyss. He hesitated for just a second, a look of profound sorrow on his face, as if he were about to drown his own child. Then he let it go. We heard no splash, no thud. The bag just vanished into the silent, waiting dark. The treasure was gone.

“Now we run,” I said, pulling at his arm.

But just as we turned to flee back the way we came, a new sound reached us. A low groan. It was not from the pit, and it was not from outside. It was from the main chamber we had just left.

The man was waking up.

We stood there, paralyzed in the little back chamber, the cold breath of the pit at our feet and the sound of the waking man at our backs. He was waking up, and he would remember. He would remember a pistol, and a boy’s face contorted with panic, and a sudden, painful darkness.

“We got to run,” I whispered, but my feet were rooted to the stone. “Just leave it all and run.”

“And go where, Silas?” Finn hissed, his voice cracking. “He’ll tell them. He’ll tell them everything. They’ll hunt us down like dogs.”

A louder groan echoed down the narrow passage, followed by a hacking cough. He was not just awake; he was stirring. The thought of facing him, of seeing the accusation in his eyes, was more terrifying than the faceless men outside. We were caught, proper, between the devil and the deep blue sea, only our devil was a man we’d assaulted and our sea was a pit that swallowed sound and light.

“We got to go back,” I said, a plan forming out of the sheer vapors of desperation. “We got to talk to him.”

Finn looked at me as if I’d suggested we try to fly. “Talk to him? He’s got a gun, or he did. He’ll shoot us on sight!”

“You took his pistol, Finn,” I reminded him. “It’s in your trousers right now, ain’t it? He’s hurt, and he’s alone. We’re the ones with the upper hand, if we’re smart about it.” I did not feel smart. I felt like a cornered rabbit, but I knew that standing there arguing in whispers was the surest way to get caught.

Before he could protest further, I grabbed the lantern and started back. My knees were trembling, but I made my legs move. Each step was a conscious act of will. Behind me, I heard Finn scramble to follow, his fear of being left alone greater than his fear of what lay ahead.

We peeked around the corner. The man was sitting up, his back against the rock wall where he had fallen. The embers of our little fire cast a weak, pulsing glow on him, making his face a landscape of shifting shadows. He held a hand to the side of his head, and his face was pale and drawn with pain. He had not seen us.

I took a breath and stepped into the open. “Mister?”

His head snapped toward me. His eyes were dark and confused, like a bear woken from its winter sleep. He looked from me to Finn, who was hovering in the mouth of the passage like a nervous ghost.

“Who… who are you?” he rasped, his voice rough. “What is this place?”

“You’re in our cave, mister,” I said, trying to sound braver than I felt. “We found you here. You were hurt.”

Suspicion began to chase the confusion from his eyes. “Hurt…” he said slowly. He touched the lump on his head. “I was hit. I remember… a boy… with a pistol.” His gaze fell on Finn, and it was as heavy as a stone.

Finn shrank back, but I stood my ground. “We was scared,” I said quickly. “We come in and you’re here with a gun and… and a bag of money. We thought you was a bandit. We’re real sorry we hit you, but you got to understand, we was just defending our territory.”
