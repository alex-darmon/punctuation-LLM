The river was low that year, so low you could see the bones of old steamboats sticking out of the mud like rotten teeth. It made the dock a busy place, everybody trying to get their goods loaded or unloaded before the channel silted up for good. Most days it was just the sweat and hollering of the deckhands and the merchants getting red in the face about prices. But that Tuesday was different.

That was the day Silas J. Finney come to town. He wasn’t a big man, but he wore a shiny vest and a hat that was too clean for these parts, and he had a way of talking that made you feel like you was the most important feller in the whole state of Missouri. He was selling shares in a silver mine out in the territories, he said, and for just a few dollars you could be richer than a king. Most of the men just laughed and called him a scoundrel, but they bought him whisky anyways because his stories was good. I seen him myself, leaning against a cotton bale, talking about mountains made of pure silver and rivers choked with gold.

He was all smiles and jokes till Captain Holt’s flatboat, the *Mary Jane*, was getting ready to shove off. A woman from up the creek, a widow with three little kids, started crying and making a scene. She says to the mate, “But I give him my money! He said the captain had this ticket for me!” She was holding a piece of paper that Finney had writ on.

Captain Holt heard the commotion and come off the boat. He was a hard man, built like an oak stump, and he didn’t say much and he never smiled. He looked at the paper, then he looked at the widow, and then his eyes found Silas J. Finney, who was trying to look mighty interested in a cloud.

The captain walked over, slow and deliberate. The whole dock got quiet. He says, “Finney, this is your work.” It warn’t a question.

Finney, he puffed out his chest and says, “Now see here, Captain, a business transaction is a sacred thing. If this good woman wishes to invest in her future—”

The captain didn’t let him finish. He says, “I am leaving at sundown. The last bell will ring when the sun touches that far bluff. If you are still on this dock, or anywhere in this town I can see you, I will tie you to the paddlewheel myself.” He looked Finney square in the eye. “I will not have thieves and liars dirtying up my town.”

Then he turned, gave the widow her money back out of his own pocket, and went back to his boat. Finney just stood there a second, and then he let out a big laugh. “The man’s got no appreciation for enterprise!” he yells to the crowd. “He threatens me! Why, I’ve been threatened by men who’d use a feller like him for a toothpick!”

The men on the dock whooped and laughed. It was good fun for them. A couple of them slapped Finney on the back and told him he was a caution. All afternoon he strutted up and down the dock, telling everybody how he was going to buy Captain Holt’s boat out from under him and turn it into a floating saloon. “Meat and liquor first,” he’d say, “and we’ll use the captain’s hide for a card table!”

As the sun got lower, a few of the men tried to talk some sense into him. One of them says, “Silas, he means it. Holt ain’t a man for bluffing.”

“Bluff?” Finney says, “I invented the bluff! Don’t you worry about old Silas.” He took another pull from a bottle someone handed him. “I’m a-gwyne to stand right here and wave him goodbye!”

The sun got to be a big red ball sitting right on top of the far hills. I heard the ship’s bell ring, slow and deep. One. Two. Three times. The dock was packed now, everybody wanting to see. Finney stood right at the end of the gangplank, wobbling a little, with a big grin on his face. The deckhands cast off the ropes.

Captain Holt appeared at the rail. He didn’t have a pistol or a knife. He just had a belaying pin in his hand, a heavy piece of hickory they use for tying off ropes. He looked at Finney. “Your time is up,” he said, his voice as flat as a stone.

He walked down the gangplank just as they was pulling it in. Finney’s smile got a little shaky. He says, “Now hold on, Captain, it’s all in good sport.”

The captain didn’t stop. He swung the pin once, not hurried, just a short, hard arc. It made a sound like a rotten pumpkin breaking. Whack! Finney went down in a heap, and his fancy hat rolled into the muddy water. The captain looked down at him for a second, then tossed the belaying pin onto the dock next to him. He turned around, walked back onto his boat without a word, and the *Mary Jane* churned on down the river.

The crowd rushed in, all pushing and shoving. Finney was laying there, his face all wrong on one side, making a little bubbling noise. Nobody knew what to do. Then a feller, a tall skinny man with a droopy mustache, knelt down and pointed. “Looky there,” he says, “he’s marked out the whole thing. Here’s where Finney stood, and here’s where the Captain stood.” He took a stick and drew lines in the dirt. He stood up straight and held the stick like it was a belaying pin. “Your time is up,” he says in a deep voice. Then he swung the stick, says “Whack!” and the whole crowd bust out laughing. Some other fellers said he did it perfect, just how it was.

They carried Finney to the cooper’s shed, and I heard later he was still breathing but wasn’t never going to talk or sell silver mines again. Well, pretty soon the laughing died down, and everybody was just standing around, talking low. Then somebody said the captain had no right. Somebody else said a man like that was a danger, taking the law into his own hands. In about a minute, everybody was saying it; so away they went, headed for the sheriff’s office to form up a posse.

headed for Colonel Sherburn’s house.

It warn’t a posse, not rightly. A posse’s got a sheriff and some law in it, I reckon. This was just a river of men, all hot and bothered and full of forty-rod whisky and righteousness. We went a-surging up the street, and the fellers that had been telling the story of the shooting was at the front, still waving their arms and being important. The lanky man with the stovepipe hat was right in the middle of them, only he’d swapped his cane for a piece of a fence rail he’d busted off, and he was carrying it on his shoulder like a musket. He was the general of the whole outfit, you might say.

The noise we made was something awful. Some was yelling to hang him, and some was yelling to shoot him, and a few fellers that was more practical was talking about getting a rope and finding a good strong tree. Every now and then somebody would start a whoop, and the whole crowd would take it up, and it sounded like the Injuns was on the war-path for sure. I was right in the thick of it, not because I wanted to lynch nobody, but because you couldn’t help it; the crowd just sort of sucked you along, and it was easier to go with it than to fight against it. Besides, I wanted to see what would happen.

Sherburn’s house was at the end of the main street, a little ways off by itself. It was the finest house in town, two stories high, white, with green shutters and a porch that went all the way around. It had a nice white picket fence, too, with a gate that had a fancy iron latch. It looked too peaceful and clean for what was coming.

The mob swarmed up to the fence like a flood of muddy water. The general with the stovepipe hat, he tried to make a speech. He got up on a stump and raised his fence rail and hollered, “Men of Arkansaw! Justice must be done! An outrage has been committed on our fair streets!” But mostly everybody just kept yelling and pushing at the gate. Pretty soon the latch broke with a snap and the whole crowd poured into the yard, trampling the flowerbeds and scrouging up against the porch steps.

“Come out, Sherburn! Come out, you murderer!” they yelled. “We’re a-gwyne to give you a trial you won’t forget!”

A window slid up on the second floor, but it was just a curtain flapping. Then everything got quiet for a second. You could hear a dog barking way off somewhere, and the sound of all them men breathing hard. The sun was getting low, and it made the white paint of the house look rosy.

Then he came out. Not on the ground floor, but out onto the little roof over the porch. Colonel Sherburn. He hadn't bothered to put on a coat, and his shirtsleeves was rolled up. He had a double-barreled shotgun in one hand and another pistol, a big one, in the other. He didn't point them. He just held them, casual-like.

He stood there for a minute, looking down at the crowd. He wasn’t proud-looking now; he was just cold. His face was like it was carved out of stone. The crowd shuffled and muttered, but the yelling had stopped. You could see the whisky courage starting to leak out of them.

Then Sherburn spoke, and his voice warn’t loud, but it was clear and it cut through the air like a knife. “You wanted to lynch me,” he says. It warn’t a question. “You poor, sorry imitation of men.”

He took a step to the edge of the roof, and the whole crowd sort of shrunk back a little.

“I was born and raised in the South, and I’ve lived in the North; so I know the average man. The average man is a coward. In the North he lets anybody walk over him that wants to, and goes home and prays for a humble spirit to bear it in. In the South one man, all by himself, has got to boss him and ride him. So he crawls. Down here, you’re all brave, ain’t you? You’re the brave men of Arkansaw.”

He let out a little laugh that didn't have no fun in it. It was like the sound of a file on a rusty saw.

“A man stands up in broad daylight and kills a man. What do you do? You wait till dark, and you fetch your masks, and you go creeping to his house to lynch him. And you bring your boys with you, to see the fun. You, there,” he says, and he points the barrel of the shotgun, not at anybody particular, just at the whole mess of them. “You wanted to burn my house. I heard you. Do you have a match? Or did your courage run out before you thought to bring one?”

Nobody said nothing. The lanky feller with the stovepipe hat tried to hide his fence rail behind his back, but it was too long.

“Your mistake,” Sherburn went on, his voice just as steady and even as a clock ticking, “is thinking a mob has any grit. It don’t. A mob is just a collection of the lowest kind of cowards, huddling together in the dark. But the man that’s facing you, he’s a man. You hear me? He’s got to be a man. Why, a man’s safe in the hands of ten thousand of your kind—as long as it’s daytime and you can see his face. The first man that wants to bust down that door has got to get past me. But he ain’t a-gwyne to do it. Not a single one of you.”

He looked them over, from one end of the crowd to the other, his eyes just burning. “You, Buck Harkness,” he says, pointing now, right at the lanky feller. The feller nigh jumped out of his skin. “You started this. You’re the big man with the loud mouth. You’ve been talking about lynching people for twenty years. Well, here’s your chance. Step forward. Bust down that door. I dare you. I double-dare you. Come on, now. We’re all waiting.”

Buck Harkness, he turned a kind of pasty white. He looked at Sherburn, and then he looked at the fellers next to him, but they all looked away. He swallowed a couple of times, and his big stovepipe hat seemed to get too heavy for his head, because it slid down over his ear. He didn’t say a word.

“I thought so,” Sherburn says, soft-like. “You’re all just Buck Harknesses. Scum. Now get out of my yard. Go home and crawl in a hole and pray for daylight. If I see a single one of you on my property in one minute, he’s a dead man.”

He cocked the shotgun. The two clicks was the loudest sound I ever heard.

That was the end of the lynch mob. It didn't break up, it just sort of melted. One feller remembered he had a horse to see to. Another one suddenly found a hole in his boot that needed tending. People started drifting off around the corners of the house, not looking at each other. In about two minutes there was nobody left in the yard but me and Buck Harkness. He was still standing there like he was planted, staring at the porch. Sherburn just stood up on the roof, watching. Finally, Harkness dropped his fence rail and kind of scuttled away sideways, like a crab.

Sherburn looked down and his eyes lit on me. I reckon I was too scared to move before. He just looked at me for a second, then he uncocked his gun, turned around, and went back in the window.

I slid out of there myself then, and I didn't stop till I was a good ways down the street. The town was quiet. It was like all the noise and excitement had been a dream. The sun was gone and the first stars was coming out. I walked down to the riverbank and sat on a log and watched the water slide by, all black and secret-looking. I thought about Boggs, laying in the drugstore with a Bible on his chest. And I thought about Sherburn, all alone in his big white house. It was all mixed up. Boggs was a fool and a nuisance, but he was dead. Sherburn was a murderer, but he was the bravest man I ever see. It was too much for a body to get straight.

The next day was Sunday. The town was as quiet as a graveyard. Folks that had been yelling for blood yesterday was all walking around in their Sunday clothes, looking pious and pretending they’d been home reading scripture the whole time. You’d hear little bits of talk. One man would say to another, “Terrible thing, that business with Boggs.” And the other would say, “Indeed, a terrible thing. Drunkenness is a curse.” And that’s all they’d say, like it was the whisky that pulled the trigger and not Colonel Sherburn. Nobody said Sherburn’s name out loud. It was like he was a ghost that might appear if you spoke of him.

We went to church, me and the widow’s folks. The preacher was a long, skinny man named Brother Zebediah, and he had a voice that could curdle milk. He got up in the pulpit and leaned over, glaring at everybody.

“Sin!” he yells, and a little girl in the front row started to cry. “Sin has come to our town! I feel the Devil walking your streets, my friends! I feel his hot breath on the back of my neck!”

He wiped his forehead with a big handkerchief. “A man was struck down in our midst! A soul sent to its maker without time for repentance! And why? I’ll tell you why! Because of the demon drink!”

He went on like that for near an hour. It was all about the evils of whisky and cussing and brawling in the street. He talked about poor Boggs and what a sinner he was, and how his fate was a warning to all the other drunkards. He said God’s judgment was a terrible and swift thing. But he never said one word about the man who was God’s judgment that day. He never said the name Sherburn. I reckon Brother Zebediah didn’t want to be judged himself, not by a man with a double-barreled shotgun. It seemed to me the preacher was just as scared as Buck Harkness, only he was better at dressing it up in fancy words.

After church, the whole town felt different. The excitement was all gone, and something else was in its place. A kind of heavy, sour feeling. The lanky man, Buck Harkness, was standing by the livery stable, trying to tell some fellers about the mob, but nobody was listening. He’d try to say, “And then I says to them…” but people would just nod and walk off. His fifteen minutes of being a general was over, and now he was just a fool with a stovepipe hat again. The town was ashamed of itself, and he was the face of it, so they didn’t want to look at him.

I couldn’t stand it. The whole town felt crawly. I went down to the landing to see if there was a steamboat coming or going, anything to look at besides the faces of folks trying to forget. There was a flatboat tied up, and a few loafers sitting on cotton bales, spitting tobacco juice and not talking.

And then I seen her.

It was a rickety old farm wagon, with a tired-looking mule hitched to it. In the back of the wagon was a long, cheap-looking pine box. A coffin. And sitting on the wagon seat was Boggs’s daughter. The same one that had come running and screaming when he was shot.

She was just sitting there, holding the reins, staring straight ahead. She had on a plain black dress, and her face was as white as paper. She warn’t crying now. She just looked empty. Like all the screaming and crying had hollowed her out and there was nothing left inside. Beside her on the seat was a man I didn’t know, a farmer-looking feller, probably a cousin or an uncle. He was the one that was going to drive her away.

They was waiting for something, I reckon. Maybe for a paper from the sheriff, or maybe just for the sun to get a little lower so they could travel in the cool. The whole town had been there to see her father get killed, and the whole town had pushed and shoved to see his dead body. Now there was nobody to see her leave but me and a few loafers who didn’t care one way or the other.

She just sat there. The sun beat down on her head, but she didn’t seem to notice. The mule stamped a fly off its leg. A steamboat whistled way down the river. The town went on about its business, what little business there was on a Sunday. And she just sat there on the wagon seat with her dead father in a box behind her.

Then I saw Colonel Sherburn. He came walking down the street, slow and deliberate, like he owned the whole world. He had his good coat on, and a clean hat. He was heading for the hotel for his dinner, I suppose. He walked right past the loafers on the cotton bales, and they all got real busy looking at their own feet. He didn’t look at them.

His path took him right by the wagon. He had to see it. He had to see the pine box and the girl with the white, empty face. I held my breath. I didn't know what he would do.

He didn't break his stride. He didn’t turn his head left or right. He just kept walking, his heels making a steady, sharp sound on the wooden planks of the street. He walked past her and the coffin he had made, just as if they was a pile of lumber or a stray dog. He didn't see her. Or if he did, he gave no sign. He was a man that had judged another man and found him wanting, and that was the end of it. The rest of it, the girl and the coffin and the leaving, that was just spoon vittles. It didn’t concern him.

He disappeared into the hotel. The farmer-feller on the wagon seat finally clucked at the mule. The wagon gave a lurch and the wheels groaned. It rolled slowly past me and turned onto the dirt track that led out of town and into the country. I watched it go, getting smaller and smaller, kicking up a little dust. I watched it till it was just a speck, and then it was gone. The river just kept on flowing. It didn’t care no more than Sherburn did. And I reckon the town didn’t neither. The show was over.

But the fellers that had been fixin’ to do the lynching, they wasn’t quite ready for the show to be over. They stood around in a knot, looking kind of foolish. Sherburn had faced them down and called them cowards, and then he’d just walked away, and they hadn’t done a thing. They was all full of whisky and rage, and it had nowhere to go. It was like a steamboat with the boilers all fired up but the paddle-wheel busted. So they just stood there, muttering to each other.

“He can’t just shoot a man down in broad daylight,” one of them says. “That ain’t law.”

“Law or not, he done it,” another one says. “And you heard him. Called us half-a-men. I ain’t no half-a-man.” He spit a long brown stream of tobacco juice into the dust. “I got a good mind to go in that hotel after him.”

“You and what army?” says a third one. “He’s got that pistol. He ain’t afraid to use it, neither. We all seen that.”

They jawed back and forth like that for a while. The sun was getting low and making long shadows down the street. The man who said he’d go into the hotel, he didn’t go. None of them did. They just got more whisky from the saloon and stood around Sherburn’s store, looking at the front of it like they was expecting it to do something. The lanky man with the stovepipe hat was there, too, but he wasn’t play-acting no more. He was just leaning against a post, looking sour. The fun had gone out of it for him, I reckoned. There ain’t much fun in being called a coward to your face by a man you was just about to string up.

Just when it seemed like the whole town was going to sink into a sullen sleep, another steamboat rounded the bend. This one was smaller than the usual packets, and a sight gaudier. It had red and yellow paint all over it, and a big sign painted on the side that said “PROFESSOR ALCAZAR’S TEMPLE OF WONDERS”. It blew its whistle, a high, squeaky toot-toot that sounded more like a toy than a real steamboat.

Well, that changed things. A steamboat arriving is always a little bit of an event, but a showboat was something else. Everybody forgot about Sherburn and Boggs for a minute and drifted down towards the landing. The loafers got up off the cotton-bales. Even the sour-looking lynch mob started shuffling that way, their bottles still in their hands. I went along, too, because there warn’t nothing else to do.

The boat nosed up to the muddy bank and a couple of deckhands threw out a rickety-looking gangplank. Then the Professor himself come out. He was a sight to see. He had on a long purple coat with sparkly stars on it, and a turban on his head with a big fake jewel in the front. He had a long black beard that was so curly it looked like it was made of springs, and he stroked it while he looked over the town.

“Behold, citizens of this fine metropolis!” he yells, in a voice as loud as a preacher’s. “Professor Alcazar has arrived, bringing enlightenment and marvels from the farthest corners of the civilized world! Prepare to have your very notions of reality challenged!”

A few of the fellers in the crowd snickered, but most everybody just stared. A skinny boy in a red uniform, maybe a couple years older than me, came out behind the Professor and started beating on a drum. Rat-a-tat-tat.

“Tonight!” the Professor hollered over the drum. “For a limited engagement! We shall present feats of mesmerism! Triumphs of legerdemain! And the main attraction, a scientific demonstration of the wonders of Phrenology! The science of the mind, revealed by the very shape of your skull! Who among you is destined for greatness? Who possesses the secret soul of a poet? Or the bump of acquisitiveness that marks a future millionaire? Professor Alcazar can tell you!”

He pointed a long, skinny finger out at the crowd. “All will be revealed! In the town square, one hour after sundown! A mere pittance of twenty-five cents for a ticket to knowledge!”

Then he bowed low, and the boy hit the drum one last time, and they went back inside the boat. The gangplank was pulled up, and that was that.

The crowd broke up, buzzing like a hive of bees. Phrenology. Nobody in Arkansaw had ever heard of such a thing, but it sounded mighty important. Reading the bumps on your head. A man could find out if he was going to be rich. That got their attention. All the talk about lynching Sherburn just evaporated. This was a new show, and it sounded a whole lot more fun, and probably safer.

I didn’t have twenty-five cents, but I figured I could get a good look from the edge of the crowd, so when the sky turned dark purple and the first stars come out, I headed for the square. They had set up a little wooden stage right in the middle, with a couple of torches on poles for light. The skinny boy was standing there, taking money and handing out little paper tickets. A good-sized crowd was already gathering, the same people who’d been listening to Boggs and watching the shooting and getting ready for the lynching. They was ready for the next thing.

Pretty soon the Professor come out onto the stage, looking just as grand as he did on the boat. He had a black cloth bag in his hand.

“Welcome, seekers of truth!” he boomed. “Tonight, we shall pull back the curtain that separates the known from the unknown! We shall map the landscape of the human soul!”

He did a few card tricks first, which was pretty good. He made a card disappear and then pulled it out of a feller’s hat. Everybody clapped. Then he did what he called mesmerism, where he swung a shiny watch in front of a young feller from the crowd and told him he was getting sleepy. The feller’s eyes did get kind of droopy, and the Professor told him he was a chicken, and the feller started clucking and scratching at the stage. The crowd howled with laughter. They thought it was the best thing they ever see.

Then the Professor says, “And now, for the pinnacle of modern science! Phrenology! Who among you has the courage to know himself? Who will be the first to have his inner character laid bare?”

He looked out over the crowd, and who should shove his way to the front but the lanky man with the stovepipe hat. He’d had a few more drinks, and he was feeling brave again.

“Read me, Perfesser!” he yells. “I ain’t afraid of the truth!”

“Ah, a volunteer of admirable fortitude!” the Professor says, smiling. “Step upon the stage, my good man!”

So the lanky man clambered up there, looking proud as a rooster. The Professor had him sit in a chair.

“Now,” the Professor says, “the science of phrenology tells us that every human faculty, every trait, every talent, has its own special organ in the brain. And the size of that organ is reflected in the shape of the cranium above it. A bump here, a ridge there, each tells a story. Let us see what story your head tells.”

He started running his fingers over the lanky man’s scalp, closing his eyes like he was in a trance.

“Aha!” he says, tapping a spot above the man’s ear. “I detect a powerfully developed organ of Vitativeness! A tenacious love of life! This man, gentlemen, is a survivor! He clings to existence with the grip of a giant!”

The lanky man puffed out his chest.

“And here,” the Professor went on, feeling the back of his head. “The bump of Approbativeness! The desire for praise and esteem! This man wants to be well-regarded by his peers. He is a leader of public opinion!”

The crowd murmured. They was impressed. The lanky man looked like he was about to bust with pride.

“But what is this?” the Professor says, his voice dropping low. He was feeling around the man’s temples. “A formidable development of Combativeness and Destructiveness! Here is a man of action! A man not to be trifled with! When roused, his righteous anger is a terrible thing to behold! He would not shrink from confronting injustice, no matter how powerful the foe!”

Well, that did it. The whole crowd cheered and clapped. The lanky man got up and took a bow. It was just what he needed to hear after being shamed by Sherburn. The Professor had made him a hero. He got out a bottle and offered the Professor a drink, but the Professor just smiled and waved it away.

He read a couple more heads after that, telling a farmer he had the bump of Philoprogenitiveness, which meant a great love of children, and telling the storekeeper’s wife she had a wonderful organ of Ideality, which made her a natural poet. Everybody was eating it up. They was all getting their twenty-five cents’ worth.

I was watching all this from behind a big cottonwood tree at the edge of the square. It was a good show. But then I happened to look over towards Sherburn’s store. The lights was on inside, but the door was dark. And standing there in the shadows of his awning, perfectly still, was Colonel Sherburn. He was just watching. He had a cigar in his mouth, and the little red end of it glowed in the dark every now and then. He wasn’t part of the crowd. He was just watching them, like a man watching ants. I felt a little shiver go up my back. He looked like a hawk watching a bunch of chickens.

The Professor was feeling pretty good about himself. The crowd loved him. He was raking in the quarters. He decided to pick one more person, for a grand finale.

“One final demonstration!” he hollered. “One last soul to be plumbed! I require a subject of some standing in the community! A man of substance and reputation!”

His eyes scanned the crowd. And then they lit on a man standing near the front. It was the judge, Judge Thatcher’s brother that lived in the town. He was a quiet, respectable man, and everybody knew him. He was a little bald, which the Professor probably thought would make the bumps easier to find.

“You, sir!” the Professor says, pointing. “A man of distinguished mien! Would you do us the honor?”

The judge looked uncomfortable, but the crowd pushed him forward, clapping and cheering, so he didn’t have much choice. He went up on the stage and sat down, looking embarrassed.

The Professor started in on his head, making his usual pronouncements. “Ah, a magnificent frontal lobe! The seat of the intellect! The organs of Causality and Comparison are well-developed. This is a man of logic and reason!”

The crowd nodded. That sounded right for a judge’s brother.

“And here,” the Professor continued, moving his fingers over the top of the man’s head. “The moral sentiments are prominent! Conscientiousness! Veneration! A deep respect for law and for a higher power!”

He was doing fine. But then he got cocky. He’d been telling everybody what they wanted to hear all night, and he figured he couldn’t miss. He felt a spot on the side of the man’s head.

“But wait,” he says, leaning in close. “There is another faculty here, one I have not yet encountered tonight in such prodigious size. Yes… it is unmistakable. The organ of… Secretiveness!”

The crowd went quiet. The Professor didn’t notice. He was warming to his subject.

“This man,” he declared, “is a keeper of secrets! He presents one face to the world, but behind it, another man lurks! A man with hidden depths, hidden plans… perhaps even a hidden treasure! Yes, the bump of Acquisitiveness is right next to it! He is secretly amassing a fortune!”

A low rumbling sound come from the crowd. Everybody in that town knew the judge’s brother. They knew he’d lost his farm a few years back and was poorer than a church mouse. He was living on the charity of his brother, and there warn’t no secret about it.

The judge’s brother turned red as a beet. He tried to stand up, but the Professor pushed him back down.

“Patience, my friend! Science is not yet finished with you! This combination… Secretiveness and Acquisitiveness… it often leads to a life of… well, a life of cunning! A life on the edge of the law!”

That was a step too far. A feller in the crowd yelled out, “He ain’t got two cents to rub together, you fraud!”

Another man shouted, “He’s the honestest man in this county! You’re a liar!”

The Professor looked stunned. He’d lost them. The mood of the crowd, which had been so happy and full of laughs, turned ugly in a second. It was the same growl I’d heard earlier when they was heading for Sherburn’s house.

“Now, now, gentlemen,” the Professor stammered. “Science is impartial. I only report what the bumps tell me…”

“The bumps is lying!” somebody yelled.

The lanky man, the one the Professor had called a hero, was the angriest of all. He felt like he’d been made a fool of. He jumped up onto the stage.

“You made all that up!” he hollered, poking the Professor in the chest. “There ain’t no bumps! You was just flattering me!”

“Preposterous!” the Professor squeaked, backing away. “It is a proven science!”

“The only thing that’s proven is you’re a swindler!” the lanky man says. “Give us our money back!”

“Yeah! Money back!” the crowd started chanting. They surged forward and started climbing onto the little stage. The skinny boy who’d been collecting the money grabbed his drum and the cash-box and scurried off into the darkness. He had good sense.

The Professor was trapped. The crowd was all around him, grabbing at his purple coat and his turban. They wasn’t laughing no more. They looked like they was fixin’ to tear him to pieces. The rage that they couldn’t use on Sherburn had found a new place to go. It was going to be a lynching after all, just a different kind.

I looked over at Sherburn’s store again. He was still there, in the exact same spot. He hadn’t moved a muscle. He just watched, the cigar glowing. He took a slow puff and blew the smoke out, and it drifted across the square towards the yelling and the chaos. He didn’t look angry, or scared, or even interested. He just looked… satisfied. Like he was watching a play he’d written himself, and all the actors was playing their parts just right. He had told them they was cowards who would only attack someone who was helpless, and here they was, proving him right.

The mob had the Professor down on the boards now. They ripped his turban off and the big jewel fell off and rolled under the stage. Somebody else tore his coat. He was squawking like a goose. It was getting real bad.

Then, from down by the river, the steamboat’s whistle let out a long, loud blast. WHOOOO-EEEET! It was the signal that it was about to pull out.

The Professor heard it. With a strength I wouldn’t have thought he had, he gave a mighty heave and threw a couple of the fellers off of him. He scrambled to his feet, his beard all crooked and his fancy clothes in tatters.

It was a race. The Professor was running for his life, and the mob was right behind him. The steamboat’s paddle-wheel started to churn, making a great splashing noise in the dark water. The Professor got to the gangplank just as the deckhands was about to pull it in. He scrambled up it, and his skinny boy helper reached out a hand and hauled him aboard. He fell onto the deck, panting.

The mob got to the landing just as the boat was pulling away from the bank. They stood there on the shore, shaking their fists and cussing. A couple of them picked up rocks and mud clods and threw them at the boat, but they mostly fell short.

The Professor got to his feet. He stood at the rail, catching his breath. He looked at the mob on the shore. Then he put his hands on his hips.

“Fools!” he shrieked at them, his voice raw. “Ignoramuses! You wouldn’t know science if it bit you on your oversized organ of Stupidity! May you all wallow in your benighted ignorance forever!”

Then he turned his back on them and disappeared into the boat. The steamboat picked up speed and slid off into the darkness of the river, its lights getting smaller and smaller, until it was gone.

The mob stood there for a minute, and then they kind of broke apart. The show was over for the second time that day, and they’d been cheated out of the ending again. They didn’t get to lynch Sherburn, and they didn’t get to tar and feather the Professor. They just shuffled back into town, grumbling and kicking at the dirt. The lanky man found his stovepipe hat where it had fallen, and he jammed it on his head and went off towards the saloon without a word.

The square was empty now, except for the busted-up stage and the two torches, which was starting to burn low. I looked one last time at Sherburn’s store. The shadow under the awning was empty. He had gone back inside. His show was over, too.

I felt tired, clear through to my bones. It had been a long, mean kind of a day. A man was dead, and his daughter was gone. A whole town had tried to turn it into a circus, first with a lynching, then with a play-acting, and then with a head-reading show. And in the end, nothing had really changed. Sherburn was still in his store, the river was still flowing, and the town was still full of the same people, just waiting for the next show to start. I figured I didn’t want to be around to see it. I went on down to the riverbank, found a quiet place, and pushed my raft out into the current, letting the black water carry me away.

I thought about Boggs laying on the floor with the Bible on his chest, and his daughter screaming, and Sherburn walking away so steady. I must have dozed off, because the next thing I knowed, the moon was higher and I was cold. I sat up and looked around. I was maybe thirty yards off when a man come out onto the little porch. He was a big man, with a great bushy black beard and long hair that hung down past his shoulders. He held a lantern in his hand, and he lifted it up and peered out into the dark.

I didn’t say nothing. I just laid low on the raft.

There was something about the way he said “God’s children” that made me think he was a preacher. He didn’t sound like the kind of preacher that sweats and yells, but more like one who tells you stories. I figured he warn’t going to shoot, so I sat up.

“It’s just me,” I says. “I ain’t bothering nobody.”

The light swung towards me, and I could see his face better. It was a serious face, but his eyes looked kind. “Just you, boy? All alone on a raft in the dead of night? That is a lonesome proposition.”

“I manage,” I says.

The man says to the woman, but loud enough for me to hear, “It’s a boy, Mercy. A stray lamb on the waters.” He turned back to me. “Have you eaten, boy? Sister Mercy has a pot of stew on the fire that would warm the heart of a stone statue.”

Well, I hadn’t had a proper meal all day, just some bread I had in my pocket. The thought of hot stew made my mouth water. I was still a little leery, but a man who offers you stew ain’t usually planning on robbing you.

“I reckon I could eat,” I says.

“Then tie up your vessel and come aboard,” the big man says, with a wave of his hand. “There is always room for one more at the Lord’s table, or at least at the table of his humble servant, Brother Absalom.”

The girl with the braids stared at me like she’d never seen a boy in raggedy clothes before. Her mother, Sister Mercy, just looked at me with tired eyes and didn’t smile. “Welcome, son, welcome,” he boomed. “I am Brother Absalom, this is my wife, Sister Mercy, and our daughter, Hope. And who might you be?”

“Huck,” I says.

“Huck,” he repeated, rolling the name around in his mouth. “A good, solid name. Come in, Huck, come in out of the night air.”

The inside of the flatboat was just one big room, but it was cleaner than any house I’d ever been in. There was a stove in one corner, a table and four chairs in the middle, and two beds with quilts on them against the far wall. The whole place smelled of woodsmoke and soap and something else, something like dried herbs. It was warm and cozy.

Sister Mercy ladled some thick beef stew into a bowl and set it on the table for me with a chunk of cornbread. She still didn’t say nothing, just moved like a shadow. I sat down and started to eat. It was the best stew I ever tasted. Brother Absalom sat across from me, stroking his big beard and watching me. The girl, Hope, sat on one of the beds, pretending to read a book but mostly watching me over the top of it.

“Most times,” I says, with my mouth full.

“A boy your age ought to have family,” he says, his voice soft and full of pity. “The world is a hard place for a solitary soul.”

“I ain’t solitary,” I says. “I got the river.”

He smiled a big, slow smile. “Ah, yes. The river. A powerful and mysterious companion. I didn’t know what to say to that, so I just kept on eating my stew.

He had a way of talking that made you want to listen, even if you didn’t understand all the words.

“And what happens when their time comes?” he asked, his eyes wide. “When they lay on their deathbed, staring into the great abyss? Their sins cling to them, Huck. He looked them all over again, one by one, his eyes burning with contempt.

“Now get out of my yard,” he says, very soft. “Go home and crawl back into your holes. Go on. Disperse.”

He cocked both hammers of the shotgun. The two clicks was as loud as cannon shots in the dead quiet.

And that was it. That was all it took. The great and terrible lynch mob just melted. It started at the back. A few men just turned around and walked away, not running, but walking fast. Then a few more. Then the blacksmith, he looked up at Sherburn one last time, and then he turned and pushed his way through the men behind him. In ten seconds, the whole crowd was broken. It warn’t a mob no more, just a bunch of fellas trying to get out of a yard without looking like they was running. They squeezed through the gate, they ducked under the fence, they didn’t look at each other. They was ashamed. Sherburn had took their righteous anger and showed them the pitiful, cowardly thing that was underneath it, and they couldn’t stand to look at it. Or at him.

He stood there on the porch, holding that shotgun, and watched them go. He didn’t say another word. When the last man was out of the yard, he uncocked the hammers, turned, and went back inside, shutting the door soft and final.

I slid out of there with the rest of them. I felt sick to my stomach, and I was ashamed, too, though I couldn’t say why. I hadn’t done no yelling. But I had been part of it, just by being there. I had watched it all, the shooting and the lynching that warn’t a lynching, and it made me feel dirty, like I’d been wading in a sewer. I seen more of human nature in that one afternoon than a person has a right to, and most of it was low down and mean. Boggs was a fool, and Sherburn was a murderer, and the whole town was a pack of cowards. There warn’t a single person in the whole affair that you could look at and say, “There’s a man.”

I didn’t want no more of that town. I cut down a side alley, away from the main street where the fellas was all standing around in little groups, not looking at each other and talking low about what a hot day it was, or how the crops was coming in, pretending like they hadn’t just tried and failed to lynch a man. I just wanted the river. I wanted the feel of the raft under my feet and the big, wide, quiet water all around me.

I hustled down to the landing and untied the canoe I’d left there, and paddled out to the raft, which was hid good in amongst some willows. Jim was there, and he was powerful glad to see me.

“Huck,” he says, his voice all full of worry. “Is you safe? I hear’d a pow-ful commotion from de town. Sounded like de whole world was a-comin’ to an end.”

“It near about did for one man,” I says, tying up the canoe. I was tired, clear down to my bones.

“Who dat, Huck? Who got hurt?”

I laid down on the boards of the raft, and looked up at the leaves of the willow trees, and the little patches of blue sky showing through. The raft was rocking gentle on the water, and it was so peaceful it made the town seem like a bad dream.

“A man named Boggs,” I says. “He got shot.”

“Lord have mercy. Shot dead?”

“Dead as a nail,” I says. “A fella named Sherburn done it.”

I told him the whole story, from Boggs riding into town drunk and yelling, to Sherburn shooting him down, and the people trying to save him with Bibles, and the lychee-faced man re-enacting it all. And then I told him about the mob. I told him how they’d all worked themselves up into a lather, and how they’d swarmed to Sherburn’s house, and how Sherburn had come out and just… talked them down. How he’d called them all cowards and told them to go home, and they did.

Jim listened to it all, his face serious, not saying a word until I was done.

Then he shook his head, slow. “Dat Sherburn,” he says. “He a bad man, Huck. A mighty bad man to shoot a body down like dat. But he ain’t no coward.”

“No,” I says. “He ain’t no coward.”

“And dem other folks,” Jim went on, “dey ain’t bad men, not like Sherburn is. But dey is cowards. Dey’s all mixed up. Dey makes a lotta noise, but de fust time a man looks ’em in de eye, dey wilts. It’s a strange world, Huck.”

“It’s a mean world, is what it is,” I says. “I’ve had enough of it for a while.”

I felt that way for sure. The river, it could be dangerous. It could try to drown you, or smash you on a snag. But it was honest. It didn’t pretend to be one thing while it was another. The river didn’t get drunk and threaten you, and then stand there helpless when you called its bluff. And it didn’t put on a fancy white house and pretend to be civilized while it was a cold-blooded killer. The river was just the river. And the raft, it was just a raft. It was our home, Jim’s and mine, and there warn’t no room on it for mobs or murderers or hypocrites.

We laid low in the willows till it got good and dark, and then a little darker. I didn’t want to take no chances of anyone from town seeing us. I just wanted to be gone. When the moon was up and silvering the water, we poled the raft out into the current. The town was all lit up behind us, little yellow squares of light, but we was slipping away into the big dark. The noise of the town faded out, and all you could hear was the chuckle of the water against the logs, and a bullfrog tuning up on the bank.

Jim started to hum one of his songs, a low, mournful kind of tune. It fitted the night perfectly. I laid on my back, watching the stars come out. The troubles of the town, the shooting and the yelling and the shame of it all, it started to feel like it happened a long time ago, to somebody else. Out here, there was just us, and the raft, and the river carrying us along. It felt clean. I reckoned I could breathe again. He let that sink in, then he rested the butt of the shotgun on the porch floor, still holding it.

But he’s heard so much about Southern chivalry that he thinks he’s a hero. He’ll take a private wrong from a man, but if there’s a crowd, he’ll join it and persecute a stranger that’s done him no harm. He looked right at the lanky man, who was trying to hide behind a fatter man.

He raised his head and looked over the whole sorry lot of them.

“So you want to lynch me. Because I killed a man. Well, I did. I told him I would, and I did. I’m a man of my word. Which of you can say the same? Now, you brought your ropes and your courage with you. Let’s see you use them. Or are you going to do what I think you’re going to do?”

He waited. The silence was so thick you could have cut it with a knife. People started looking down at their feet, or up at the sky, anywhere but at Colonel Sherburn. A man in the back coughed, and it sounded as loud as a gunshot. Then another man turned, quiet-like, and started walking away. Then a few more. It was like a dam that had sprung a small leak, and then a few more, and pretty soon the whole thing was ready to give way.

“I’m waiting,” Sherburn says, soft.

That did it. The crowd just broke. It didn't run, it just sort of melted away. Men turned and shuffled off in twos and threes, not talking, not looking back. They looked ashamed and beat. In about two minutes, the street that had been packed with a roaring mob was empty, except for me and a couple of other boys that was too curious to leave, and the lanky man, who was trying to get his stovepipe hat out of the dust where it had fallen.

Colonel Sherburn watched them go. He didn’t smile or look proud. He just looked disgusted. He stood there till the last man was gone, and then he cocked the shotgun, breaking it open to show it was empty all along. He never had no shells in it. It made me feel sick, somehow. I didn’t know who was worse, Sherburn for being so cold and killing a man, or the town for being so full of hot air and meanness and cowardice. It was all tangled up and ugly.

The drunk man staggered over to one of the horses that was trotting gentle-like around the ring. He tried to get on, and fell down. The crowd roared. He tried again, and got one leg over, but he was on backwards. The crowd was just beside themselves with laughing. He was slipping and sliding all over the horse’s back, hanging on to its tail, and then by its neck, and it looked for sure like he was a goner. Buck Harkness was slapping his knee and yelling, he was having such a good time watching this poor fool.

Then, all of a sudden, the man stood up straight on the horse’s back. The horse, it quit trotting and started to gallop, tearing around the ring like the wind. The man, he shucked off his raggedy coat and his floppy hat, and underneath he was wearing the most beautiful spangled outfit you ever see, all silver and blue. He warn’t old and drunk at all. He was young and slim and handsome, and he was one of the circus riders. He stood there on that galloping horse as steady as if he was on the ground, bowing and blowing kisses to the crowd.

Well, you never heard such a noise. The whole crowd jumped to their feet, screaming and cheering and stamping. They went plumb wild. They hadn’t been laughing at a drunk; they’d been fooled, and they loved it. They loved being made fools of. They clapped and hollered for him more than they did for the pretty lady in the pink tights, more than for anybody. They loved the show of it.

And I sat there high up on my bench and looked down at them all. The same faces that had been twisted with fake anger in the mob were now split wide with laughing. The same voices that had been yelling “Lynch him!” were now yelling “Hooray!” It all come to me then. The whole thing was a show. Boggs put on his show every month, being the town drunk. Sherburn put on his show, being the cold, hard man that nobody could touch. The lanky man put on his show, being the leader of the people. The mob put on a show of being brave and full of righteous fury. And then this circus man put on a show of being a drunk fool. And it was the circus man’s show they liked the best, because it was the most fun and nobody got hurt.

It seemed to me that folks in a town like that was always looking for a show. It didn’t matter much if it was a man getting shot, or a lynching that didn’t happen, or a circus. It was all just something to look at, something to get excited about for a little while before you went back to your own dull business. They liked a good performance, is what it was. Sherburn performed better than the mob, so he won. The circus rider performed being a drunk better than old Boggs ever did, so they cheered for him instead of just laughing at him.

I felt mighty lonesome, all of a sudden, right there in the middle of all them cheering people. I didn’t feel like I belonged with them. Their laughing sounded hollow to me, and their cheers sounded foolish. All the fun went out of the circus. All I could see was old Boggs laying on the drugstore floor with the Bible on his chest, and his poor daughter crying over him. And I seen Colonel Sherburn’s face, full of a kind of poison that was just as bad as the whisky that killed Boggs.

When the show was over, I slid out with the crowd but I didn’t want to talk to nobody. I just wanted to get away. I headed for the river. I wanted to see the raft. It felt like the only real and honest place in the whole world. Out there, the river didn’t put on a show; it just was what it was. And the sky just was what it was. And Jim, he was just Jim. He never pretended to be something he warn’t. Being on the raft with Jim, floating down the big, quiet river in the dark, seemed like heaven to me after a day in that town. It made you feel clean. I reckoned civilization was a mighty mixed-up and disappointing thing, and I didn’t want no more of it for a good long while. I just wanted to be free of all the yelling and the pretending and the foolishness.

so away they went, a-whooping and a-hollering and shoving one another to get to the front. I got shoved along with them, not because I wanted to lynch nobody, but because there warn’t no way to get out of it. The whole street was one solid jam of men, and the dust they kicked up was so thick you could ’a’ wrote your name in it. The lanky man with the stovepipe hat, he was at the front of it all, waving his crooked-handled cane like it was a general’s sword.

“To Sherburn’s house!” he yells, his voice cracking with importance. “We’ll show him what justice looks like in Arkansaw!”

And the crowd roars back at him, a sound that was more excitement than it was anger. They was all swept up in it. I seen men I recognized from the laughing crowd at the store, the same ones that was coaxing Boggs to go home just an hour before. Now they was grabbing up rocks from the street and pieces of wood from a busted pig-pen, their faces all screwed up like they was the most righteous men on God’s green earth. It was a curious thing to see. One minute they was laughing at a man’s foolishness, the next they was ready to hang the man that shot him, all without ever seeming to feel sorry for the dead man himself.

I kept to the edge of the crowd as much as I could, trying not to get my feet tromped on. We went surging down the main street and took a turn onto a quieter lane where the houses was bigger and had white picket fences. Colonel Sherburn’s house was the biggest of them all, a two-story white house with green shutters and a porch that run all the way around. It had a nice yard with flowerbeds, and a big oak tree in the middle of it. It looked too peaceful for all this ruckus.

The lanky man, he stops at the front gate and holds up his hand. “Hold on, boys!” he shouts. “Let’s do this proper!”

But the crowd warn’t in a mood for proper. They was like a log jam that had just broke loose. They swarmed right over him and the gate, which busted off its hinges with a sound like a pistol shot. They poured into the yard, tromping all over the flowerbeds till there was nothing left but mashed stems and dirt. They swarmed up onto the porch, a hundred men or more, all packed together and yelling for Sherburn to come out.

“Come out and face the people, Sherburn!”

“Murderer!”

“We got a rope for you, you dog!”

The lanky man gets himself pushed up onto the porch railing, where he could look over the whole mess. He was holding his stovepipe hat on with one hand and waving his cane with the other. “Break down the door!” he screeches. “Let’s drag him out by his fancy collar!”

A big, beefy feller that looked like a blacksmith took a run at the door, but it was solid oak and he just bounced off, which made some of the fellers laugh, even in all the excitement. Then two or three others got a piece of a fence rail they’d carried along and used it for a battering ram. The first time they hit the door it shivered and groaned. The second time, the lock busted clean off and the door flew open and banged against the wall inside.

A big cheer went up, and the men at the front pushed their way inside, and the rest followed like water through a busted dam. I was right there in the thick of it, and I got carried right into the house with them. I didn’t want to go, but there warn’t no choice about it.

It was dark inside after the bright sun, and cool. And it was quiet. Awful quiet. All the yelling and shouting from the men seemed to get swallowed up as soon as they was inside. The house was fancy. There was a carpet on the floor, a thing I hadn’t seen in but one or two houses in my life, and it had flowers woven into it. There was a big clock in a wooden case against one wall, and it was ticking, slow and steady. Tick. Tock. Tick. Tock. It was the only sound in the whole house besides the shuffling of feet and men breathing hard.

The men spread out into the main room, what I reckon they call a parlor. They looked around, their eyes big, looking at the nice chairs and a table with a book laying on it. They looked powerful out of place, all sweaty and dirty with their rocks and clubs, standing on that fancy carpet. Nobody was yelling now. They was just whispering to each other.

“Where is he?” one of them says.

“Maybe he lit out the back,” says another.

The lanky man steps into the middle of the room. The quietness seemed to bother him. He was used to being the center of a lot of noise. “Spread out!” he says, but his voice didn’t have the same screech in it. It was more like he was trying to convince himself. “Search the house! He’s got to be here somewheres!”

So they did. Some went into another room, which was a dining room with a long polished table, and others started up the stairs. I heard them tramping around up there, opening and shutting doors. A feller next to me picked up a little glass bird off a shelf and turned it over and over in his hand, looking at it like he’d never seen such a thing. He wasn’t thinking about lynching nobody right then. He was just thinking about that little glass bird.

I didn’t go upstairs. I just stood by the busted door and watched. The whole thing had gone flat. The fire had gone out of them. They had come to do a terrible, exciting thing, but the man they was after had spoiled their show by not being there to play his part. It was like they’d come for a hanging and found out the man had already died in his cell of natural causes. It was a disappointment.

After a few minutes, the men started coming back downstairs, shaking their heads.

“He ain’t up there.”

“The whole place is empty. Bed’s made and everything.”

The lanky man was standing by the ticking clock, looking frustrated. His big performance was falling apart. The crowd was starting to drift, looking at the pictures on the walls, touching the curtains. The blacksmith feller was in the dining room, and I seen him open a cabinet and pull out a bottle of whisky. He pulled the cork with his teeth and took a long drink, and then passed it to another feller. Pretty soon three or four of them was passing the bottle around, forgetting all about Colonel Sherburn. The lynching mob was turning into a party of thieves.

It was a miserable sight. They was brave enough to come and hang a man when they was all together and full of brag, but when he warn’t there to make it easy for them, they just fell to peeking and pilfering like a pack of curious coons.

Then, just when it seemed like they’d all forget what they come for and just wander off with whatever they could stick in their pockets, there was a stir at the door. The men that was blocking the way moved aside, and there was a new person standing there.

It was Boggs’s daughter.

She was standing right where the sun was brightest, and it made a kind of halo around her. She had quit her crying. Her face was white as a sheet, and her eyes was big and dark, but they warn’t scared anymore. They was hard. The two men that had been with her when her pa was shot was standing a little ways behind her, looking uneasy. She didn’t pay them no mind. She just looked at that room full of men.

The room got dead quiet. The feller with the whisky bottle hid it behind his back. The feller with the glass bird put it down on the table so quick it near about broke. Everybody just stood there and stared at her. The big clock kept on ticking. Tick. Tock.

She took a step into the house, her shoes not making a sound on the fancy carpet. She looked all around the room, at all their faces, one by one. Her eyes swept over the blacksmith, and the lanky man with his silly hat, and all the others. They couldn’t meet her gaze. Fellers started shuffling their feet and looking down at the floor, like schoolboys caught doing something wrong.

“What are you all doing in this house?” she says. Her voice warn’t loud, but it was clear as a bell, and it cut through the room sharper than any yell.

Nobody answered. The lanky man, he took his hat off, which was the first sensible thing I’d seen him do all day. He held it in both hands and looked like he wished the floor would open up and swallow him.

She took another step. “I know you,” she says, pointing a finger at a man near the front. “You was there. You was laughing. You told my pappy he was a king.” Then she pointed at another. “And you. You held up a tin cup and told him to make a speech.” Her finger kept moving. “All of you. You all thought it was the funniest thing in the world. You egged him on. You enjoyed his shame. It was a good show for you, warn’t it?”

A low murmur went through the crowd. Fellers was looking at each other, their faces turning red.

“He was a fool when he was drunk,” she went on, her voice starting to tremble just a little, but not with sadness, with something else. Something hot. “He was a fool, and you all loved him for it. You loved to have your fun with him. But he never hurt a soul. Not one. And this man,” she waved her hand around the empty room, “this man killed him in the street like a dog. For what? For words. For the same foolish words you all was laughing at.”

She looked straight at the lanky man now. “And now you come here. To his house. You bust down his door. What for? To get justice for my pappy? Do you think he’d want this? Do you think he’d want a pack of cowards that laughed at him in life to pretend to be his friends in death?”

The word “cowards” hung in the air. It was the same word Sherburn would have used, but coming from this pale, skinny girl, it had a thousand times the weight. It hit them right where they lived.

“You ain’t here for him,” she says, her voice dropping to a whisper that everybody in the room could hear plain. “You’re just here for the excitement. Another show. First the drunk, then the shooting, now the lynching. It’s all just a big circus for you, ain’t it?”

That was it. She had named it. She had said the very thing I had been thinking. It was all a show.

The lanky man couldn’t stand it no more. He mumbled something that sounded like “Ma’am, we was just…” and then he turned and pushed his way out the door, cramming his hat back on his head as he went. He didn’t run, but he walked fast, with his head down.

And that broke the spell. One by one, then in twos and threes, the other men started to slide out of the room. They didn’t look at her. They just shuffled out, quiet and ashamed. The ones in the dining room put the whisky bottle back and snuck out after them. In a minute, the whole house was empty, except for me and her and the two fellers still waiting by the door. And the clock, still ticking.

The girl stood there for a long time, looking at the busted door and the muddy footprints on the carpet. Then all the hardness went out of her, and she sort of sagged, and she put her face in her hands and started to cry again, but quiet this time, with her shoulders shaking.

One of the men with her, a kindly looking old feller, come up and put his arm around her. “Come on now, Mary Ann,” he says, gentle. “There’s nothing more to be done here. Let’s get you home.”

She let him lead her out. I slid out behind them, feeling lower than a snake. The street was near about empty. The great lynch mob had vanished. It had melted away into the town, every man for himself, hoping nobody had seen him there.

I walked back towards the center of town, feeling hollowed out. The whole thing made me sick. The town was full of shows and pretending, and it seemed like the pretending was the most dangerous part. Sherburn pretended to be above the law, and Boggs died for it. The mob pretended to be justice, and they just ended up being thieves and fools, shamed by a grieving girl.

I didn’t want no more of it. I went down to the little store where I’d left my things and I bought some bacon and some corn-meal, and then I headed for the river. The sun was getting low, and the air was starting to cool off.

When I got to where the raft was tied up, I seen a little curl of smoke coming from our fire. Jim was sitting there, mending one of his lines. He looked up when he heard me.

“Huck? Is dat you? I was gettin’ worried. You been gone a powerful long time.”

“I’m here, Jim,” I says. I dropped my bundles and sat down on a log. I was so tired I felt like I could sleep for a week.

“What’s de matter, Huck? You look all tuckered out. An’ you look sad. Did somethin’ bad happen in dat town?”

I tried to tell him about it. I told him about Boggs getting drunk and yelling, and how everybody laughed. I told him about Colonel Sherburn, so proud and cold, and how he shot Boggs down right in the street.

Jim shook his head slow. “Dat’s a terrible thing, Huck. A terrible thing. To shoot a man down like dat.”

“That ain’t the half of it, Jim,” I says. And I told him about the crowd, and the lanky man reenacting the whole thing, and how they all decided to go and lynch Sherburn. I told him about breaking down the door and finding the house empty, and how they all got shamed by Boggs’s daughter.

Jim, he listened to the whole thing, his face serious, looking into the fire. When I was all done, he was quiet for a long time.

Finally, he says, “People is mighty strange, Huck. Dey laughs at a man fallin’ down, and den dey wants to hang de man dat pushed him. But it seems to me like dey all had a hand in de pushin’.”

That was just it. That was the truest thing I’d heard all day.

“And dat girl,” he says, his voice soft. “Dat poor little girl. She de only one in de whole story dat acted right. She had grief in her heart, not jes’ noise in her mouth.”

I laid back and looked up at the sky. The first stars was starting to come out. “I reckon you’re right, Jim.”

We was quiet for a while after that. We cooked up some of the bacon and had it with some corn-pone, and it tasted better than anything I could have got in that town. After we ate, we untied the raft and pushed out into the current. The town and all its lights and all its strange, foolish people slipped away behind us in the dark.

There was a big moon coming up, and it made a silver track on the water. The river was wide and peaceful, and the air was clean. There warn’t no yelling here, no pretending. Just the quiet slap of the water against the logs, and the croaking of the frogs on the shore, and the big, silent sky overhead.

“Jim,” I says, after a bit.

“Yeah, Huck?”

“Do you reckon all towns are like that one?”

He thought about it. “I doan know about all towns. But I reckon folks is folks. Dey gets bored, an’ dey gets mean, an’ dey likes a show. But some of dem is good, too. Like dat girl. An’ like you, Huck.”

I didn’t say nothing. I just laid there on my back, watching the stars drift by. Floating down the river, away from all that, felt like being born again. It made you feel clean. I reckoned civilization was a powerful lot of trouble, and for now, I was glad to be shut of it. Me and Jim and the raft, we was our own town, and it was a whole sight better than the one we’d left behind.

for the next two days, all we done was float. Sometimes we’d talk, and sometimes we’d just lay on our backs and watch the trees and the sky drift by. The river was a whole world to itself, and it felt like the only world that mattered. The troubles of them towns and the foolishness of people just melted away in the sun. We catched some fish, good-sized cats and a couple of perch, and Jim cooked them up over a little fire on the raft, and there never was a finer meal. At night the stars was so thick and bright they looked like you could just reach up and scoop a handful.

It was on the third day, along about the middle of the afternoon, that we seen the trouble. We was drifting lazy around a big bend, me dozing off in the sun, when Jim sets up straight.

“Huck,” he says, his voice low. “Looky yonder.”

I squinted where he was pointing. Way down river, maybe a mile or so, was a steamboat. But it warn’t moving right. It was a big side-wheeler, all painted up white and gold, with two big tall stacks puffing out black smoke. But the smoke was coming in fits and starts, like a man choking, and the boat was sitting sideways to the current, not making no headway at all. One of its wheels was churning up the water something awful, but the other one was still.

“She’s hung up,” I says. “Stuck on a bar, I reckon.”

“Dat’s a bad place to be stuck,” Jim says. “De channel cuts sharp to de other side right dar. Dis side is all shoal and snag.” He knowed the river, Jim did. He’d worked on it enough to know its tricks.

The steamboat let out a long, mournful whistle, a sound that seemed more helpless than anything else. Then the one paddle-wheel that was working stopped turning, and the smoke from the stacks thinned out to nothing. They was giving it up, for now.

“They’ll have to wait for the river to rise, or for another boat to come along and pull ‘em off,” I says.

“Dat could be days, Huck. Dey got passengers on dat boat. Fancy folks, by de looks of her.”

We floated closer, slow and quiet, keeping to the edge of the current. The name of the boat was painted in big gold letters on the side: THE EVENING STAR. It was a proper passenger boat, the kind that goes between St. Louis and New Orleans. The decks was crowded with people. We could see ladies in fine dresses with little parasols, and men in dark coats and tall hats. They was all bunched up at the railings, looking out at the water and the shore like they was seeing it for the first time, and not liking what they saw.

“What you reckon we ought to do, Huck?” Jim says.

“Well, there ain’t nothing we *can* do,” I says. “We ain’t big enough to pull a steamboat off a sandbar.”

“No, but folks is folks. Dey might be needin’ somethin’. Maybe dey out of fresh water. Or maybe dey jes’ scared.”

That was Jim all over. Always thinking about other folks. I was more inclined to just float on by and mind our own business. Them kind of people on that boat, they wouldn’t give two cents for a boy on a raft, and they’d be a sight worse with Jim.

But the closer we got, the more I got to thinking. It was a kind of a show, you see. Like the trouble back in that town with Boggs, only this was different. This was nature putting one over on fancy civilization, and I wanted a closer look.

“All right,” I says. “We’ll pull in behind that little island over there, where they can’t see the raft. Then I’ll paddle over in the canoe and see what’s what. You stay with the raft, Jim. It ain’t safe for you to be seen by that crowd.”

Jim nodded. “You be careful, Huck. Dese high-toned folks can be more dangerous dan a snag in de dark.”

So we guided the raft in behind a long, thin towhead that was covered in willows, and tied up. It was a good spot; you couldn’t see us from the steamboat at all. I got in the canoe and pushed off, keeping low. I paddled around the end of the towhead and come up on the backside of the steamboat, the side away from the main channel. The water was shallow here, and you could see the brown mud stirred up all around where she was stuck fast.

A couple of deckhands was leaning over the rail, looking glum. They had on dirty shirts and wasn’t dressed like the passengers.

“Hey!” I calls up to them, soft-like.

One of them, a young fellow with red hair, looked down and seen me. “Well, look what the river washed in. What do you want, boy?”

“Just wondering what the trouble is,” I says.

“What’s it look like?” he says, spitting a stream of tobacco juice that missed the canoe by an inch. “The old man tried to shave the bar to save ten minutes and run us aground harder’n a tombstone. Captain Prentiss, they call him. Captain Clodhopper is more like it.”

Just then a big man in a blue uniform with gold braid on it come bustling up. He had a face as red as a brick and whiskers like two bushes glued to his cheeks. That must be Captain Prentiss.

“What’s all this jabbering?” he bellows. “Get a sounding line over the stern! I want readings every five minutes! Johnson, you there, stop lollygagging with that river trash and get to work!”

The red-haired fellow give me a little shrug and went off. The captain glared down at me. “Be off with you, boy! This is a private vessel in distress, not a public spectacle for every vagrant on the river!”

He turned his back on me and started yelling at some other men. I was about to paddle away when I seen something else. Up on the main deck, where the passengers was, I saw a family all clustered together. There was a man, fat and sweating in his fine clothes, and a woman who was fanning herself like her life depended on it. And with them was a girl, about the same age as that poor daughter of Boggs’s, maybe sixteen or seventeen. She had yellow hair and a blue dress, and she looked bored and put-out, like she’d just been told she couldn’t have a new pony.

She caught me looking, and she wrinkled up her nose at me and my canoe and my raggedy clothes, and then turned her back. Well, that settled it for me. I didn’t feel sorry for them one bit. I paddled back to the raft.

“Well?” Jim says.

“They’re stuck good,” I told him. “And the captain’s a prideful old fool, and the passengers are all high and mighty. One of the girls looked at me like I was something she’d scraped off her shoe.”

“Dat sound about right,” Jim says, shaking his head. “Pride goeth before de fall, Huck. An’ dis time it goeth before gettin’ stuck on a sandbar.”

We ate some cold corn-pone and bacon and watched the sun start to go down. The sky turned all orange and purple, and it made that white steamboat look like a picture in a book. But I knowed that on board, things was probably getting ugly. The passengers would be getting hungry and scared of the dark, and the mosquitoes would be coming out in swarms. A steamboat stuck in the middle of the river at night ain’t no pleasure cruise.

Sure enough, when it got full dark, we started hearing things. Shouting from the boat. A woman cried out, sharp and scared. Then there was a lot of angry-sounding talk. We couldn’t make out the words, just the feeling of it. It was a mean sound, like a nest of wasps that’s been stirred up.

“Dey ain’t happy over dar,” Jim whispered.

“No,” I says. “They’re used to having everything their way. The river don’t care about that.”

We laid there listening. After a while, I couldn’t stand it no more. Curiosity was eating me up. “I’m going back, Jim. Just to listen. I’ll stay in the shadows.”

“Huck, dat ain’t wise.”

“I’ll be careful,” I says. And I slid the canoe back into the water.

This time it was different. The boat was all lit up with lanterns, and the light spilled out onto the muddy water around it. The insects was buzzing in thick clouds around every light. I paddled up close under the stern, where it was darkest, and I could hear the voices clear as a bell. The passengers was all gathered on the lower deck, and Captain Prentiss was trying to talk to them.

“...assure you, ladies and gentlemen, the situation is under control!” he was saying, his voice not quite so confident as it was in the afternoon. “At first light, we will renew our efforts. Another vessel is sure to pass by morning.”

“Morning!” a man’s voice boomed out. It was the fat man I seen with the girl. “That is unacceptable, Captain! I have an appointment in Natchez tomorrow afternoon! This is an outrage! I paid for passage, not for a camping trip in a swamp!”

A few people murmured their agreement. “The mosquitoes are frightful!” a woman complained. “And what are we to do for supper?”

“The galley is preparing a cold collation,” the captain said.

“Cold collation!” the fat man roared. “I didn’t pay for cold collation! I paid for roast beef and claret! This is the most incompetent handling of a vessel I have ever witnessed! I shall report you to the line, sir! I am Abernathy of the Merchant’s Bank of St. Louis!”

The captain tried to say something, but another voice cut in, smooth and oily. “Now, now, Mr. Abernathy, let’s not lose our heads. The captain is doing his best in a trying situation.” I craned my neck to see who was talking. It was a man in a rumpled white suit, with a thin mustache. He was shuffling a deck of cards in his hands, over and over, the cards making a soft, whispery sound. “Perhaps,” the man says, with a little smile, “a friendly game of poker might help us pass the time? Take our minds off our... predicament.”

I seen his type before. A river gambler. He was smelling opportunity. While everyone else was worried about being stuck, he was thinking about how to get into their pockets.

I was watching him when I heard another sound, a little ways off. It was a scraping, dragging noise from the shore of the towhead. I turned and looked, but it was too dark to see anything. It was probably just a log shifting in the current. I turned my attention back to the show on the boat. Mr. Abernathy was still yelling, and the gambler was trying to get a game started, and the ladies was complaining, and the whole thing was a worse mess than a tangled fishing line.

I listened for a while longer, then figured I’d seen enough. These people warn’t going nowhere, and they was just going to make each other miserable until morning. I started to paddle back to the raft.

When I got there, Jim was gone.

My heart jumped up into my throat. The canoe was there, pulled up on the mud, but the raft was empty. “Jim!” I whispered, loud as I dared. “Jim!”

No answer. The river was quiet except for the chirping of crickets and the deep croak of a bullfrog. I stood there for a minute, my blood running cold. Did he fall in? Did somebody come and take him? My mind went straight to the slave-catchers. Maybe that noise I heard wasn’t no log.

I looked around, frantic. Then I saw it. A trail in the mud, leading away from the raft and into the thick willows of the towhead. It was the mark of something heavy being dragged. The same sound I’d heard from the canoe.

I didn’t even think. I grabbed a piece of a tree limb for a club and followed the trail. It was dark in the willows, and the branches snatched at my clothes. The ground was soft and wet. I went on for maybe fifty yards, and then the willows opened up into a little clearing.

And there was Jim.

He was tied to a tree, with a nasty-looking gag in his mouth. And standing over him was two men. One was the red-haired deckhand from the steamboat. The other was a bigger, meaner-looking fellow I hadn’t seen before. They had a lantern, and in its yellow light, I saw they both had pistols tucked in their belts.

“He’ll fetch a good price down in Orleans,” the big one was saying. “A prime field hand like this? Five hundred dollars, easy. Maybe more.”

“What about the captain?” the red-haired one, Johnson, asked. His voice was shaky. “He’ll know we’re gone.”

“Let him,” the big one growled. “By the time he gets this tub off the mud, we’ll be halfway to Cairo in his own longboat. I already got it untied and stashed on the backside of this island. Finding this here runaway is just a bonus. Our bonus, for puttin’ up with Prentiss’s foolishness.”

So that was it. They was crew members, deserting the ship. And they’d stumbled on Jim.

Jim’s eyes was wide with terror. He was looking right at me, over their shoulders. He was pleading with his eyes. Don’t do nothin, Huck. Don’t you get caught too.

My mind was racing faster than the river in a flood. I couldn’t fight two grown men with pistols. I was just a boy with a stick. I had to think of something. Something Twain-like. Something with a bit of sand.

“Captain! Captain Prentiss!”

My voice sounded small and thin in the big night.

A silver dollar. For saving Jim’s life and catching two deserters and saving their longboat. Mr. Abernathy pushed forward. “A commendable job, boy. Commendable. Here’s a little something from the passengers.” He handed me two bits.

I looked at the money in my hand. It felt dirty.

Captain Prentiss’s face got redder. He didn’t like being told what to do, especially by someone like Jim, right in front of his passengers. But he was desperate.

“Yes, sir,” I says.

“Details, details! The public has no head for geography. Then, your majesty, you will make your entrance.”

“And what do I do?” the king asks, all eager.

“You will be... in costume,” says the duke, trying not to smirk. “We must paint you. All over. Stripes of every color. Blue, red, green, yellow. A veritable rainbow of flesh. And you’ll wear nothing else.”

The king’s jaw dropped. “Naked?” he squawks. “In front of all them people?”

“Not naked, your majesty. Adorned! A living canvas! And you won’t walk out. You will crawl. On all fours. Prancing and cavorting, like the wild, untamed creature you are portraying. You’ll caper about the stage for, say, thirty seconds, let out a mighty roar, and then exit. That’s it. That’s the show.”

The king, he was silent for a minute. He looked like he’d swallowed a toad. “On all fours? Painted? They’ll laugh at me.”

“Of course they’ll laugh!” the duke says. “They’ll be astonished! They’ll be scandalized! They’ll talk about it for weeks. They’ll tell all their friends they have to see this unbelievable spectacle. The key is the billing. ‘Ladies and Children Not Admitted.’ That promises something wicked, something forbidden. When they see a naked man on all fours, painted up like a barber’s pole, they’ll feel like they got their money’s worth of wickedness. It can’t fail.”

The king thought about it, rubbing his chin. I could see the greed in his eyes fighting with his pride. “How much did you say we’d take in?” he asks, kind of quiet.

“Fifty cents a head,” the duke says. “The house will be packed. Two, three hundred men. That’s a hundred and fifty dollars a night. For three nights, that’s four hundred and fifty dollars. For maybe two minutes of your majesty’s time, all told.”

That done it. The king’s pride didn’t stand a chance against four hundred and fifty dollars. He slapped his thigh. “By the godlings of Egypt, I’ll do it!” he says. “For art, and for a tidy profit. Fetch the paints, duke! Let us rehearse!”

So for the next hour, there in the moonlight on the raft, the duke practiced painting the king. He didn’t have no real paints, so he used berry juice and mud and some charcoal from the fire. The king, he’d get down on his hands and knees and try to caper, but he was old and stiff and it looked more like a sick cow trying to get up than a Royal Nonesuch. He’d stumble and grunt and the duke would yell, “More majesty! More ferocity!” and the king would let out a weak sort of roar that sounded like he was just tired. It was the pitifulest thing I ever saw. Jim, he turned his back and pretended to be busy with a fishing line, but I seen his shoulders shaking. He didn’t know whether to laugh or be sad, and I was the same. Here was a grown man, a king no less, letting another man paint him with mud and making a fool of himself, all for a sack of money they was going to steal from a town full of other fools. It made me feel sick.

The next night, they done the show. They rented a little room over the store where Sherburn had come out of. It was just a bare room with a few planks laid on barrels for a stage. The duke stood at the door to take the money and I was supposed to help him, and the king was behind a raggedy old blanket, getting painted up.

The place filled up pretty quick. It was just like the duke said it would be. The room was hot and smelled of sweat and whisky and chewing tobacco. It was all men, a rough-looking bunch. I seen some of the same faces that was in the lynch mob. They was all nudging each other and grinning, expecting to see something awful wicked for their fifty cents.

When the room was so full you couldn’t have squeezed in another soul, the duke he steps up on the stage. He had a candle in his hand, and he held it up and looked real solemn.

“Gentlemen,” he says, in a deep, booming voice. “You stand tonight on the precipice of a theatrical revolution. For centuries, the stage has been the domain of the familiar, the trite, the mundane. But tonight, we cast off the tired robes of tradition! Tonight, we bring you a vision from the darkest, most unexplored corners of the globe. A creature of myth and legend, a being spoken of only in whispers by the terrified natives of the Orient. Prepare yourselves for the sublime, the terrible, the unforgettable... The Royal Nonesuch!”

He bowed low and stepped back, pulling the blanket aside. And out comes the king.

He was a sight to see. He was striped up and down with all the colors of a crazy quilt, and the paint was mixed with sweat and was starting to run. He got down on his hands and knees, and his old bones creaked so you could hear it all over the room. He tried to caper, but he wobbled, and he let out a little ‘meow’ that warn’t a roar at all. He crawled across the stage one way, and then turned and crawled back, and then he stood up, slipped on a patch of his own runny paint, and fell down with a thump. He scrambled up as quick as he could and run back behind the blanket. The whole thing couldn’t have lasted more than a minute.

For a second, the whole crowd was dead silent. You could hear a pin drop. They just stared at the empty stage with their mouths open. They didn’t know what to make of it. Then one fellow in the back says, real slow, “Well, I’ll be damned.”

And then the place busted loose. They wasn’t laughing. They was mad. “Swindled!” somebody yells. “That’s it? That’s the whole show?” Another man jumps up on his chair and shouts, “It’s a sell! Let’s ride these fellers on a rail!” The whole crowd started to surge towards the stage, their faces all twisted up with rage. I thought for sure we was all done for.

But the duke, he was as cool as a cucumber. He jumped back up on the stage and held up his hands. “Gentlemen, gentlemen, hold!” he shouts. His voice was so powerful it cut right through the noise, and they stopped for a second to listen.

“I can see you are overwhelmed,” he says, his face all serious. “It is a natural reaction. The sheer, raw power of the Nonesuch is often too much for the uninitiated mind to process at first. But think, my friends, think! You have been part of a select few. You have seen what no other man in this county has seen.”

He leaned in, like he was telling them a secret. “Now, you can go out of here and tell everyone you’ve been sold. You can admit to the whole town that you paid fifty cents to see an old man crawl around in paint for a minute. And the whole town will laugh at you. They’ll call you fools and suckers. Your friends, your neighbors, even your own wives will mock you. Is that what you want?”

They was quiet now, listening. I could see the wheels turning in their heads.

“Or,” the duke went on, his voice getting sly, “you can walk out of here like men who have seen the most amazing, the most stupendous, the most scandalous show of your lives. You can tell them it was so wonderful, so shocking, that words can’t describe it. You can tell them they *have* to see it for themselves. And then, tomorrow night, when this room is packed to the rafters again, you can all sit back and have the laugh on *them*. You won’t be the fools then, will you? You’ll be the smart ones. You’ll be in on the joke.”

He let that sink in. The anger on their faces started to melt away, and in its place came a slow, mean kind of grin. They looked at each other and nodded. The fellow that had yelled about the rail, he was the first to speak up.

“By God, he’s right,” he says. “I ain’t about to let Tom Jackson’s crew get to sayin’ they’re smarter than me. I’m a-gwyne to tell ’em it was the greatest show on earth.”

And just like that, the mob was gone. They filed out of the room, not angry anymore, but excited, like a pack of boys with a secret prank to play. They was all talking at once about who they was going to trick into coming the next night.

When the last one was gone, the duke, he wiped the sweat off his forehead and let out a long breath. He turned to the king, who was peeking out from behind the blanket.

“Your majesty,” the duke says with a grand bow, “your performance was a triumph. You have not only captivated an audience, you have turned them into our very own sales force.”

The king, he was still covered in runny paint and looking mighty sorry for himself. “They was gwyne to kill us,” he says, his voice trembling.

“But they didn’t,” says the duke, scooping the money out of the box into a sack. He hefted it. “And thanks to their wounded pride, tomorrow night we’ll double our earnings. There’s no better friend to a con man than a fool who’s afraid of being called one.”

I just stood there, watching them count the money. It was near about a hundred dollars. I thought about the men who’d paid it. They was a rough crowd, the kind that would lynch a man as soon as look at him. But the duke had played them like a fiddle. He’d found the one thing they was more scared of than anything else: being laughed at. It seemed to me that most of what people done, good or bad, was because they was afraid of what other people would think of them. It was a sad kind of thought, and it made the world feel like a lonesome place.

The second night the house was jammed again, fuller than before. All the fellers from the first night was there, and they’d brought their friends. They was all sitting there with a sly look on their faces, winking at each other. The new fellers was looking all excited and nervous.

The duke done his speech again, and the king come out and done his little crawl. It was just as pitiful as the first time. The minute the king was behind the blanket, the fellers from the first night busted out laughing. They howled and slapped their knees and pointed at the new ones. The new ones, their faces went from excited to confused to mad as hornets in about ten seconds. There was a lot of yelling and fist-shaking, and a couple of scuffles broke out in the back, but there was so many people packed in that nobody could get a real fight going. Besides, the first-nighters was having too much fun laughing to want to fight. So after a while, the new fellers just gave up, looking sour.

Then the duke, he got up on the stage again. He didn’t try to calm them down this time. He just waited for a quiet spot and says, “Gentlemen! The show will go on for one more night, and one more night only! Tell your friends!” And the whole crowd, the old ones and the new ones, they all roared with laughter. They was all in on it now.

When we got back to the raft, the duke and the king was beside themselves with joy. They had another sack of money, bigger than the first. They sat there counting it and drinking whisky and singing songs.

“Oh, we are magnificent rogues!” the duke hollered. “Princes of plunder! We have turned this entire town into a pyramid of fools, each one standing on the shoulders of the fool below him!”

The king, he was feeling his liquor. “It’s my performance that does it,” he says. “The raw, animal power of the Nonesuch. It overwhelms ’em.”

I went and sat with Jim at the other end of the raft. He hadn’t said much about the whole business. He just watched the king and the duke with a worried look in his eyes.

“Huck,” he says to me, real low, “dese people is gettin’ powerful angry. Dat money ain’t gwyne to be worth much if’n we ends up decorated with tar and feathers.”

“I know it, Jim,” I says. “They’re planning on one more night.”

“One more night is one too many,” he says, shaking his head. “I got a bad feelin’, Huck. A real bad feelin’.”

He was right, of course. The duke was smart, but he was greedy too. He figured he could squeeze one more night’s worth of cash out of the town before they got their revenge. He laid out the plan for us.

“Tomorrow night,” he says, “the town will be out for blood. They’ll come, every last man, not to see the show, but to get us. They’ll likely have filled their pockets with rotten eggs, spoiled cabbages, and deceased cats. They intend to give us a finale we won’t forget.”

“Then we ain’t a-gwyne back, are we?” says the king, looking scared again.

“Of course we are,” says the duke. “But we won’t be staying for the performance. Huck, you will be our advance guard. You’ll slip away from the hall as soon as the money is collected. Take the cash box and run for the raft. Your majesty and I will follow close behind. We’ll make a grand exit out the back window just as the crowd’s patience runs out. We’ll take their money, and leave them with nothing but their own rot and stink to throw at an empty stage. It’s perfect!”

So the third night come, and it was just like the duke predicted. The street in front of the hall was packed. The air was buzzing, like right before a big thunderstorm. I was standing near the door, and I could see the lumps in men’s pockets. I seen a feller with a dead chicken hanging out of his coat. They warn’t trying to hide it. They was proud of it.

The duke took the money at the door, same as before. He had a gleam in his eye, like a man playing with a loaded gun. The room filled up, and it was dead silent. There warn’t no talking or laughing. Just a hundred and fifty men, sitting and waiting, their hands in their pockets. It was the scariest crowd I ever seen.

When the last man was in, the duke locked the door and slid the big wooden bar across it. Then he handed me the cash box. It was heavy. “Go now, Huck,” he whispered. “Don’t stop for nothing. We’ll be right behind you.” He give me a wink and then he hopped up onto the stage, bold as brass, and started in on his speech.

I didn’t wait to hear it. I slipped out the back door into the alley. It was dark, but I could hear the duke’s voice booming from inside, and then a low, ugly growl from the crowd. I clutched that box and I run. I run like the devil himself was after me, down one dark alley and then another, my feet slapping in the mud. I could hear shouting starting up from the hall behind me, a great big roar of pure rage. They’d figured it out.

I didn’t look back. I just kept running till I saw the river shining ahead of me. I scrambled down the bank, and there was the raft, and Jim, waiting.

“Dey comin’, Huck?” he says, his eyes wide.

“They’re coming, Jim! Let’s go!”

Just as I jumped on board, I heard a crashing sound from the town, and more yelling. Then I saw two figures come tumbling down the bank. It was the king and the duke. They’d jumped out the window just like they planned, and they splashed into the water and clambered onto the raft, puffing and blowing.

“Cut the rope, Jim!” the duke yelled, and Jim, he had his knife out already and with one slash we was free. We pushed off with the poles, digging them deep into the mud, and the raft slid out into the current.

We wasn’t a moment too soon. A whole mess of men come pouring down the bank, waving lanterns and pitchforks and clubs. They was screaming and cussing something awful. They waded out into the water a little ways, shaking their fists, but we was already in the deep channel, moving fast. The light from their lanterns got smaller and smaller, and their shouting got fainter, until we couldn’t hear nothing but the gentle sound of the river.

We was safe.

The king and the duke laid on the deck, gasping for breath but laughing. They was soaked and muddy, but they was happy. The duke took the money sack from the king, who’d held onto it the whole time, and he upended it. Gold and silver coins spilled out all over the deck, shining in the lantern light. It was over four hundred dollars.

“Hah!” the king says. “We done it! We skinned the whole town!”

“To the Royal Nonesuch!” the duke shouted, holding up a bottle of whisky he’d managed to save. “Long may he reign!”

They drank and counted their money and congratulated each other on being the smartest, slickest scoundrels on the Mississippi River. I and Jim, we stayed at the other end of the raft, by the steering oar. We didn’t say nothing. The raft was drifting along, peaceful and quiet, and the stars was out. It was beautiful. But having them two on board, with their stolen money and their cackling, it spoiled it all. It felt like they’d brought all the dirt and meanness of the town out onto the river with us, and there warn’t no washing it clean.
