It was one of them days where the sun just sits on your shoulders and presses you down into the dirt. The chickens was all hiding under the livery stable, and the only thing moving was the sweat trickling down your own face. I was perched on the edge of the public well, watching two beetles fight over a dead fly, and I figured that was about all the excitement the town of Harmony was gwyne to see.

Then we heard the music. It warn’t good music, just a fella blowing on a brass horn all sour and wobbly, but it was something. Pretty soon a wagon come rumbling down the main road, painted up bright blue with gold letters that said, “PROFESSOR PHINEAS G. FARNSWORTH’S MIRACLE ELIXIR.” The fella with the horn was up on the driver’s seat next to a skinny man in a tall hat and a coat that was too hot for the weather.

He pulled up right in the middle of the square and the man in the hat stood up on a box. “Gather ‘round, ye weary and afflicted!” he yells out, his voice bigger than he was. “Gather ‘round and witness a marvel of the modern age, a tonic for the soul and a balm for the body!”

Folks started drifting out of the storefronts and houses, because there wasn’t nothing else to do. I seen old man Hemlock, who was so deaf he couldn’t hear a gunshot next to his ear, come out and cup his hand to get a better listen. The Professor, he held up a little brown bottle. “Are you plagued by rheumatiz? Do you suffer the vapors, the ague, the general malaise of a life ill-lived? One spoonful of my patented concoction, derived from a secret recipe of the Amazonian kings, and you will feel the vigor of a man half your age!”

A few of the fellas in the crowd snickered, but a lot of the women looked interested. One of them says, “What about a toothache, mister? My boy’s been crying for two nights with a bad tooth.”

“A toothache!” the Professor bellows, like it was the grandest question ever asked. “Madam, it will wash the pain away like a spring flood! Step forward, son!”

So this little boy, maybe eight years old, shuffles up with his hand on his jaw, and the Professor gives him a little sip from a spoon. The boy makes a terrible face, and then he blinks and his eyes get real wide. He takes his hand off his face. “It’s gone, Ma,” he says. “The hurtin’ is all gone.”

Well, that done it. The crowd pushed in close and folks started holding up their dimes. The Professor and his horn-blower was selling bottles as fast as they could hand them out. I was about to slide off the well and get a closer look when I seen Jedediah, the blacksmith. He was standing in the door of his smithy, all covered in soot, with his arms crossed over his chest which was as big as a barrel. He hadn't said a word, just watching with his eyes narrowed down.

He wiped his hands on his leather apron and walked slow into the crowd. The people made way for him; nobody got in Jedediah’s way. He walked right up to the wagon and looked at the Professor. He didn't yell or nothing. He just says, quiet-like, “My horse is lame.”

The Professor, he looked a little flustered. “My good man, this is a tonic for the human constitution, not for beasts of the field.”

“It’s a living creature,” Jedediah says, his voice still low and flat. “It’s got aches. You said it cures aches. My horse has got a bad one in his front leg. Been limping for a week.” The crowd got real quiet. Everybody knew Jedediah’s old gray mare, Bess. “If your elixir is a miracle,” Jedediah says, “it ought to work on her just the same.”

“Sir, I must insist, the properties are not suited for equines,” the Professor said, trying to smile but his face was getting shiny with sweat.

“Then I reckon you’ll give these folks their money back,” Jedediah said. He warn’t asking. He just said it. He pointed at the little boy. “And whatever you gave that child, the taste of it was likely so foul it scared the toothache right out of him. But it’ll be back.”

The Professor looked around at all the faces staring at him, and they warn’t friendly faces no more. They looked like they’d been made fools of, and they was starting to get mad. His horn-blower had already slipped off the back of the wagon and was trying to look like he was just a part of the crowd.

The Professor, he didn’t say another word. He just throwed his cash box under the seat, grabbed the reins, and whipped his horses something fierce. He went clattering out of town so fast he left a cloud of dust that took five minutes to settle.

Nobody chased him. We just watched him go. Then Jedediah walked back to his smithy. The little boy’s ma looked at the bottle in her hand and throwed it in the dirt, where it broke. Then everybody sort of laughed and went back to what they was doing.

Later on, I seen Zeke Miller, who run the post office, showing some new fellas how it all happened. He stood up tall and puffed out his chest and sung out, “Gather ‘round, ye afflicted!” in a high, squeaky voice. Then he hunched his shoulders and made his voice low and rumbly like Jedediah’s, and he says, “My horse is lame.” Then he squeaked again, “It ain’t for equines!” Then he rumbled, “Then I reckon you’ll give these folks their money back.” He done the whole thing, right down to pretending to whip some horses and run away. The fellas watching all slapped their knees and said he done it perfect, and a couple of them bought him a drink over at the saloon. For a week after, you couldn’t meet a man on the street without him saying, “My horse is lame,” and then everybody would laugh.

The lanky man with the stovepipe hat was at the front of it, still carrying his crooked-handled cane, only now he held it like a general’s sword. He’d found his purpose, I reckoned. Twenty minutes ago he was just a loafer showing off, and now he was the leader of a lynching. He kept shouting, “For Boggs! For the widow and the orphan!” which was a funny thing to say, seeing as how Boggs didn’t have a widow and his daughter was the only orphan, and she was mostly just scared and crying and not asking for no lynching.

But the crowd didn’t care about the particulars. They was hot for a hanging. They swarmed along, picking up fellas from the saloons and stores, and every new fella would ask what was happening, and somebody would shout, “Gwyne to lynch Sherburn!” and the new fella would let out a whoop and join right in. It was the most excitement they’d had since the last time a steamboat blew a boiler.

I tagged along, keeping to the edge of the crowd. I warn’t for lynching Sherburn nor against it; mostly I just wanted to see what would happen. The crowd was a sight. Some had pistols, some had old squirrel rifles, one fella had a pitchfork he must’ve brought in from the country. They was a wild-eyed bunch, and the whisky they’d been drinking all day was boiling in them.

They come to Colonel Sherburn’s house, which was a fine, two-story white house with a green fence around the yard. It was the nicest house in town, and it looked quiet and peaceful, like it didn’t know there was a mob come to call. The crowd slowed down then, and got a little quieter. It’s one thing to yell about lynching a man all the way up the street, but it’s another thing to be standing on his front lawn. They sort of milled around, pushing each other towards the front gate but nobody wanting to be the first one to open it.

The lanky man, he seen his army was losing its sand. He hopped up on the fence post, waving his cane. “What’re you waiting for, boys?” he yells. “Break down the door! Drag him out! He can’t shoot all of us!”

A few fellas gave a weak cheer at that, but nobody moved. They just stood there, their necks craned, looking at the front door like they was expecting it to bite.

Then the door opened.

Colonel Sherburn stepped out onto the porch. He didn’t have a pistol this time. He had a long double-barreled shotgun, and he was holding it easy in the crook of his arm, not aiming it at nobody, just holding it. He hadn’t changed his clothes. He was still the best dressed man in town, and he looked just as ca’m and proud as he did before. He didn’t look like a man who was about to be lynched. He looked like he was about to give a speech at a town picnic.

The crowd went dead silent. You could hear a man’s heart thumping if you listened close.

Sherburn let his eyes travel over the whole lot of them, slow and deliberate, like he was counting cattle. He looked at them for a whole minute, not saying a word. The silence got heavier and heavier, till you felt like it was something you could touch.

Then he spoke, and his voice warn’t loud, but it was cold and sharp and it cut right through the hot afternoon air. “You call yourselves men?” he says.

Nobody answered. The lanky man on the fence post looked like he’d forgot what he was doing up there.

“You want to lynch a man,” Sherburn went on, his voice still low and full of scorn. “You want to break down my door and hang me. That’s your notion of justice, is it? The justice of cowards.”

He took a step forward, to the edge of the porch. “I know you. I see you every day. I see you loafing on street corners, spitting tobacco and telling lies. I see you in the saloon, bragging about fights you never fought. And I see you in church on Sunday, pretending to be good Christians. You’re not men. You’re a pack of dogs, and not even brave dogs at that. You’re curs.”

He fixed his eyes on the lanky man with the stovepipe hat. “You, there. The one playing soldier with a stick. You’re their leader, I take it. You was the one showing everyone how the shooting happened. A real authority.”

The lanky man swallowed hard. He looked like he wished the fence post would swallow him up.

“Come on, then,” Sherburn says, and he made a little motion with his head. “Step forward. You’re the general of this army. You be the one to lead the charge. Come on. I’m waiting.”

He warn’t yelling. He was just talking, but there was something in his voice that was more frightening than any shout. The lanky man, he slid off the fence post, but he didn’t step forward. He tried to slide back into the crowd, to disappear.

But Sherburn wouldn’t let him. “Oh, no,” he says. “Don’t you hide now. You brought these fools here. You can’t lead from the back. A real man, a man with honor, would face me himself. But you ain’t a real man. You’re just the mouth of the mob. You’ll do the yelling, but you want someone else to do the dying.”

He looked back at the crowd. They was shuffling their feet and looking at the ground. Some of them at the back was already starting to drift away. “The idea of you lynching anybody. It’s laughable. You didn’t have the stomach to stop Boggs from making a fool of himself, and you don’t have the stomach to avenge him. Because to do that, you’d have to face a man, and you’re only brave when you’re in a pack, and even then, only when the man you’re facing is tied up or unarmed.”

He rested the shotgun on his shoulder, aiming it up at the sky. “Now go home. Go home and crawl back into your holes and be ashamed of yourselves. The show is over. Get out of my yard.”

For another half a minute, nobody moved. Then a fella at the very back turned and walked away, quick-like. Then another. Then a whole bunch of them. It was like a dam breaking, but in reverse. The crowd just dissolved, melting away back down the street. They didn’t run, but they walked fast, and they didn’t look at each other. They’d come up the street a roaring flood, and they went back a sad little trickle. The lanky man was one of the first to go. He left his crooked-handled cane lying in the dust by the fence.

In less than five minutes, the street was empty except for me. I was standing over by a tree, trying to look like I was just resting in the shade.

Colonel Sherburn stood on his porch for a little while longer, watching them go. Then he spit, a long, contemptuous spit, over the railing into his own flowerbed. He turned around without another look and went back inside his house, shutting the door with a solid click.

I slid out from behind my tree and went on down the street. It was all over. There warn’t gwyne to be a lynching. I felt a little disappointed, and then I felt a little ashamed for feeling that way. It was all mixed up.

The next day was the funeral for Boggs. The whole town turned out for it, same as they turned out for the shooting and the lynching that didn’t happen. It was another show, is what it was. They had it in the little church, which was hot and stuffy and smelled of old hymn-books and dust. Boggs’s daughter was there in the front pew, dressed in black, with a couple of the church ladies fussing over her. She just sat there, white as a sheet, not crying now, just staring at the plain pine box at the front of the room.

The preacher got up and started in on a sermon. He was a long, thin man with a voice that was too big for his body. He went on and on about what a fine citizen Boggs had been, what a loving father and a pillar of the community. He said Boggs was a man of simple virtues and honest heart, a man who would be sorely missed. I looked around at the loafers and the shopkeepers, the same fellas who’d laughed at Boggs yesterday and tried to get him locked up. They was all sitting there with long faces, looking pious and sad, nodding along with the preacher’s lies.

I heard one fella behind me whisper to his neighbor, “A pillar of the community, he says. I reckon so, if the pillar is soaked in whisky and falls down a lot.” And the other fella snickered, a little wet sound he tried to cover up with a cough.

The preacher got to the end of his speech, and he raised his hands and his voice and said Boggs was now walking in the green pastures of the Lord, where there was no more sorrow and no more thirst. I reckoned Boggs would be disappointed if there was no more thirst, but I didn’t say nothing.

After the service, they took the pine box out to the graveyard behind the church. It was a sorry-looking place, all weeds and leaning headstones. They put Boggs in the ground, and everybody stood around for a bit, the men with their hats in their hands, looking solemn. Then, one by one, they started talking about other things—the price of cotton, the weather, who was likely to win the county election. The funeral was over, and it was time to get back to business.

I was standing there, watching them throw the first shovels of dirt on the coffin, when I seen Colonel Sherburn. He was standing way off by the edge of the graveyard, by the road, just watching. He hadn’t come to the service. He was just there, in his same fine clothes, standing perfectly still. He watched until the grave was filled, and then he turned and walked away, back towards his house. Nobody said a word to him. It was like he was a ghost that only I could see.

The town settled down after that. It was quiet for a few days. The story of Boggs and Sherburn had been told and retold so many times it was worn out, like an old coat. The fellas on the street corners found new things to talk about. The lanky man got his cane back, but he didn’t do no more play-acting. He just sat on a barrel outside the general store and looked sour. The whole town felt tired, like they’d used up all their excitement for a year.

Then, about a week later, the *Delta Queen* steamboat come in. A steamboat coming was always a big event. It meant mail and news and new goods for the stores. The whole town, or what was left of it that had nothing better to do, went down to the landing to see it come in. The big paddlewheel churned up the brown water, and the whistle blew a long, lonesome note that echoed off the bluffs.

They threw out the ropes and lowered the gangplank, and the roustabouts started hauling off crates and barrels. A few passengers got off, drummers and such, but then a man appeared at the top of the gangplank that made everybody stop and look.

He was tall and thin, but not like the preacher. This man was thin like a rail fence, and he looked just as hard. He was dressed in black, but it was a dusty, worn-out black, not fine like Sherburn’s clothes. He had a long face with a hawk nose and pale blue eyes that seemed to look right through you. He was carrying a small, beat-up carpetbag in one hand. He didn’t look like he belonged on a fancy steamboat. He looked like he’d just walked out of the wilderness.

He came down the gangplank slow and steady, his boots making a hollow sound on the wood. He stopped at the bottom and stood there, looking at the town. He didn’t ask no questions. He just stood there, taking it all in.

Zeke Miller, the postmaster, who always made it his business to know everybody else’s, couldn’t stand it. He walked up to the stranger. “Welcome to Arkansaw, friend,” he says, all friendly-like. “You lookin’ for somebody?”

The stranger turned his pale eyes on Zeke. The eyes didn’t blink. “I might be,” he says. His voice was flat and dry, like dust. “I’m looking for a town where a man named Boggs used to live.”

Zeke’s face lit up. Here was a new chapter to the story. “Well, you’ve found it, sir. This is the place. But I’m sorry to say poor Boggs is no longer with us. He passed on, you see. Met with a... an unfortunate end.” Zeke was enjoying himself, drawing out the drama.

“I heard,” the stranger says, and his voice got even flatter. “I heard he was shot down in the street.”

“That’s the truth of it,” Zeke says, nodding eagerly. “Shot down in cold blood by our own Colonel Sherburn. A terrible business. The whole town was in an uproar.”

The stranger didn’t seem to be in an uproar. He just looked at Zeke, and then his eyes scanned the faces in the little crowd that had gathered. “And this Colonel Sherburn,” the stranger says. “Is he still in an uproar? Or has he passed on, too?”

“Oh, he’s here,” Zeke says, lowering his voice like he was sharing a secret. “He’s still here. Lives in the big white house at the end of Main Street. Proud as Lucifer. The town went to lynch him, you know, but he faced them down. Talked them right out of it.” Zeke looked proud, like the town getting talked down was something to brag about.

The stranger didn’t say nothing for a minute. He just looked up the dirt street towards the town. Then he says, quiet-like, “Boggs was my brother.”

Well, that changed everything. The little crowd got real still. Zeke Miller looked like he’d swallowed a fishbone. This warn’t just a stranger asking questions for the story of it. This was family.

The man’s name was Silas Boggs. He didn’t offer it, but Zeke asked, and he gave it. He took a room at the little hotel, which was really just a few sorry-looking rooms above the saloon. And then he started asking questions. But he didn’t ask them like Zeke, all full of gossip and excitement. He’d just find a man, a loafer or a farmer in for the day, and he’d look at him with them pale eyes and say, “Tell me about my brother.” And they’d tell him. They told him everything. They told him how Boggs would come to town for his monthly drunk, how he’d ride through the streets yelling and cussing. They told him about the threats against Sherburn. They told him about the shooting, every detail, just like the lanky man had acted it out. They told him about the failed lynching. They gave him the whole story, because they was hungry for more story, and this man seemed like he was gwyne to make some.

But he didn’t. For two days, he did nothing but listen. He sat on the porch of the hotel, or walked the streets, quiet and watchful. He never went near the saloon. He never raised his voice. He just watched. The town was buzzing. Everybody was waiting. They figured it was gwyne to be a shootout. They kept their eyes on Sherburn’s house and they kept their eyes on Silas Boggs, waiting for them to come together.

Colonel Sherburn, he went about his business just the same as always. He’d walk to the store in the morning to get his newspaper, and he’d walk to the post office in the afternoon. He walked right past Silas Boggs a few times. He looked at Silas, and Silas looked back at him, but neither of them said a word. It was a different kind of quiet from the one before the lynching mob. This was a cold quiet, a heavy quiet, and it was making the whole town nervous.

On the third day, late in the afternoon, I was fishing down by the creek, not catching much of anything, when I seen Silas Boggs walking down the path. He had a fishing pole in his hand, a simple cane pole, and a little tin of worms. He didn’t seem to see me at first. He went to a spot under a willow tree and dropped his line in the water.

I sat there for a while, and he sat there. Neither of us said anything. Finally, I couldn’t stand it no more. I says, “Catchin’ anything?”

He turned his head and looked at me. His eyes seemed a little less hard out here, away from the town. “Not yet,” he says. “The fish are smarter than the fishermen today.”

We sat some more. Then he says, without looking at me, “You saw it, didn’t you? The boy who told me the story said there was another boy watching. Said you was right there.”

“I seen it,” I said.

“My brother,” he says, his eyes on his fishing line. “He was a fool. A loud, drunken fool. Our father was the same way. I left home twenty years ago to get away from it.”

I didn’t know what to say to that.

“He never hurt nobody, though,” Silas said, and it was a question. He was asking me.

“That’s what a man told me,” I said. “Said he was the best naturedest old fool in Arkansaw.”

Silas Boggs gave a little nod. He pulled his line out of the water. There was nothing on it. He looked at the worm, then tossed the line back in. “A man like Sherburn,” he says, almost to himself. “He values his pride. Values his reputation. He thinks he’s better than everyone else in this town. And he is. He’s smarter, and he’s colder.”

He looked over at me then. “The town thinks I’m here to kill him. To have a duel in the street. They want to see more blood. They think that’s justice for my brother.”

“Ain’t you?” I asked.

He was quiet for a long time. A turtle poked its head out of the water and then disappeared. “Killing him would just make him part of the same cheap story,” Silas says, finally. “It would make him a legend. Another dead man in a dirty street. My brother died for nothing, just for being a loud-mouthed fool. If I kill Sherburn, he’ll have died for something. For his pride. I won’t give him that.”

He stood up. “I ain’t found what I was fishing for.” He reeled in his line and started back up the path.

That evening, the whole town was on edge. The sun went down and the street lamps was lit. People were standing in their doorways, watching. Waiting. They was sure this was the night.

Just after dark, Colonel Sherburn stepped out of his house. He was dressed for the evening, headed to the saloon for his nightly glass of whisky and a game of cards, same as always. He started walking down the street, his heels clicking on the packed earth.

And then, Silas Boggs stepped out from the alley between the general store and the smithy. He was just standing there, in the middle of the street, waiting. He didn’t have a gun in his hand. He just stood there.

Sherburn stopped. The whole street went silent. You could hear the crickets in the fields. “Well,” Sherburn says, his voice carrying in the still air. “The other Boggs. I was wondering when you’d stop listening and start talking.”

“I ain’t got much to say,” Silas said. His voice was as flat as ever.

“Then get out of my way,” Sherburn said. “I have a card game to attend.” He started to walk again, intending to go right around Silas.

“My brother was a fool,” Silas says, and Sherburn stopped again. “He was a drunkard and an embarrassment. But he was my blood. And you shot him down like a dog.”

“He had it coming,” Sherburn said, his voice like flint. “He was warned.”

“He was,” Silas agreed. “But you didn’t shoot him because he was a danger. You shot him because he annoyed you. Because he wounded your pride in front of these… people.” He made a little gesture to the shadowy doorways, to the whole town. “You made an example of him.”

“And I would do it again,” Sherburn said. He put his hand inside his coat, where he kept his pistol.

“I know you would,” Silas said. And then he did something nobody expected. He started to laugh. It warn’t a happy laugh. It was a dry, rattling sound, like stones in a tin box. He laughed and laughed, and then he spat in the dirt at Sherburn’s feet.

“You think you’re a king in this little mud-puddle,” Silas said, his voice full of a scorn that made Sherburn’s own sound weak. “You think your pride is such a valuable thing. But I’ve traveled this country, Sherburn. I’ve seen real men, and I’ve seen real towns. This place? It’s nothing. And you? You’re the king of nothing. Your pride ain’t worth the spit on my boot.”

He turned his back on Sherburn. He just turned and started walking away, towards the landing where the steamboats docked.

Sherburn stood there, frozen. His hand was still in his coat. To be challenged was one thing; he knew how to handle that. To be dismissed, to be laughed at and called nothing… you could see it was eating him alive. His face was a mask of fury.

“You turn your back on me?” Sherburn roared. His voice was shaking with rage. He pulled his pistol out.

Silas stopped, but he didn’t turn around. “Go on,” he says, his back still to Sherburn. “Shoot another Boggs in the street. Show them all what a brave man you are. Give them another story to tell. It’s all you’re good for.”

Sherburn stood there with the pistol raised, shaking in his hand. The whole town held its breath. They was gwyne to get their second shooting after all.

But he didn’t shoot. Slowly, his arm came down. To shoot a man in the back who had just called you nothing… it would prove him right. It would be the act of a coward, and Sherburn’s whole life was built on not being a coward.

Silas Boggs walked on down the dark street, and didn’t look back. He disappeared into the night. Sherburn stood there for a long time, the pistol hanging useless in his hand. Then, he turned, not towards the saloon, but back towards his big, empty house, and he walked like a man who had suddenly gotten very old.

The next morning, the northbound packet boat came by, and Silas Boggs got on it and was gone. The town was quieter than ever. They’d seen the whole thing, but this time, nobody knew how to tell the story. There was no bang, no body, no lanky man

to playact a man being talked down by his dead brother’s ghost. It didn’t have the right kind of hollering in it.

The next day things was just as quiet. Colonel Sherburn didn’t come out of his big house at all. The day after, it was the same. His store stayed shut, which was a considerable strange thing, because he was a man that prized his business. Pretty soon, the loafers on the street commenced to talking again, but it was a different kind of talk. At first, they didn’t know what to make of Silas Boggs. A man that would face down a pistol without flinching but wouldn’t draw one of his own was a curious creature to them. But men like that, they can’t stand a thing they don’t understand for long. They got to hammer it into a shape they know.

So they started saying Silas Boggs must have been a coward after all.

“He talked big,” one of them says, spitting a long stream of tobacco juice, “but when it come to it, he turned tail and run. Left on the morning boat.”

“That’s so,” another one chimes in. “He knowed Sherburn would’ve kilt him if he’d stayed another day. Sherburn just showed him mercy, is all. Give him till morning to clear out.”

And that was the story they settled on. It made sense to them. It put Sherburn back up where he belonged, as the man who held the power of life and death, and it made Silas Boggs into just another feller who talked a good game but folded when the chips was down. It was a comfortable story. It meant that a pistol was still the strongest argument a man could have, and they all understood that. They didn’t have to reckon with a man’s words being stronger than a bullet.

Even the lanky man with the stovepipe hat got his stride back. He couldn’t act out the confrontation, because there was no good actions in it, but he’d stand where Silas Boggs stood and puff out his chest and say a lot of brave-sounding things, and then he’d turn around and walk off briskly, like he was scared. And then he’d stand where Sherburn stood and look merciful and powerful, and put his pistol away slow, like he was doing the world a great favor. And the crowd would nod and say that’s just how it was, and treat him to their bottles again. It was a lie, but it was a performance, and that was what they wanted.

I seen it all, and I knowed it warn’t the truth. I knowed Silas Boggs hadn’t been scared. I seen the look on his face, and I seen the look on Sherburn’s face after he was gone. But I was just a boy, and nobody was asking me. So I kept my mouth shut.

Weeks went by, and the town mostly went back to being itself. Except for Colonel Sherburn. He started coming out again, but he was changed. He still dressed the best, and he still walked with his head up, but the stiffness was gone out of his back. It was like a wire inside him had been snipped. He’d walk to his store and open it up, but he wouldn’t stand out on the porch no more, watching the town. He’d stay inside, in the shadows, and when he talked to folks, his voice didn’t have that old iron ring to it. It was just a voice. People still showed him respect, out of habit and because he was rich, but it warn’t the same kind of respect. It warn’t the kind that’s mostly fear. It was just manners.

And the river, it didn’t care about none of it. It just kept on gnawing.

That fall, the rains come early and they come hard. The Mississippi swelled up something awful, a big brown boiling monster, full of logs and chicken coops and sometimes the roof of a barn sailing by. The river was in a hungry mood. All along the banks, you could hear it eating. You’d hear a low rumbling sound, and then a great big *ker-slump*, and another slice of Arkansaw would be gone.

The town was built on a high bank, but not high enough. The part of the town where the poorest folks lived, down by the landing, was the first to feel it. The bank started to go in chunks. Families had to drag their shanties back from the edge, cursing the river and the mud. But the main street, where Sherburn’s store and the other big shops were, was supposed to be safe. It was on the highest ground.

One afternoon, I was down by the riverfront, just watching the water chew. The whole street was damp and soft, and you could feel a little tremor in the ground every now and then. A part of the bank right next to the wood-yard went in, taking a whole stack of cordwood with it. The water just swallowed it up without a sound.

Then I noticed something. The crack from that cave-in didn’t stop at the wood-yard. It was a little spidery line in the mud, and it ran right on, all the way up towards the main street. It ran right along the side of Colonel Sherburn’s big brick store.

I didn’t think much of it at first. But a couple of days later, that crack was wider. You could put your hand in it. The big wooden awning over the front of Sherburn’s store, the one he’d looked under to yell at Boggs, was starting to sag on one side. The post that held it up was leaning, just a little, but enough that you could see it.

People noticed. Of course they did. They’d stand across the street and look at the crack and then look at the store, and they’d talk about it in low voices.

“She’s a-gwyne to go,” they’d say. “That whole corner’s soft.”

“Sherburn’ll have to do something. Shore it up.”

“He’s got the money for it. He’ll hire a crew.”

Everybody expected Colonel Sherburn to take charge. They expected him to boss a gang of men and drive timbers into the bank and fight the river, just like they’d expect him to face down a man. He was Colonel Sherburn, and he didn’t let things beat him. That was the story, anyway.

But he didn't do nothing.

He came out of his store and looked at the crack. He looked at the sagging awning. He walked to the edge of the bank and stared down at the brown water swirling and sucking at the mud below. His face was gray. He looked tired. He just stood there for a long time, and then he went back inside his store.

The next day, the crack was a gully. The post of the awning had sunk another foot, and the whole wooden structure was groaning. A few shingles slid off the roof and clattered onto the street. The front door of the store was hard to open.

That afternoon, a crowd started to gather. It warn’t a lynching crowd, not angry or yelling. It was a watching crowd, the same kind of crowd that had watched Boggs die. They was there for the show. They wanted to see what would happen. They lined the street opposite the store, waiting.

By-and-by, a big chunk of the bank right under the corner of the store sloughed off into the river. It made a sound like a giant sigh, and the whole building shuddered. The awning tore away from the brick front with a sound of screaming nails and splintering wood, and collapsed halfway into the street, a broken mess.

The crowd let out a big gasp, all together. Then they was quiet, looking at the ruin.

Out of the store comes Colonel Sherburn. He had to shove the door hard to get it open. He stepped over the wreckage of his awning and stood there in the street, looking at the damage. He looked at his store, with a raw scar on its brick face where the awning used to be, and he looked at the gully that was eating its foundation. Then he looked at the crowd.

His face was hard and set, like he was trying to find the old Sherburn inside himself. He picked out a man in the crowd, a big feller named Johnson who ran the livery stable.

“Johnson,” he says, and his voice tried to be a command. “Get your men. Get ropes and timbers from the sawmill. We’re going to brace this wall.”

Johnson shifted his feet. He looked at the store, and he looked at the river. “I don’t know, Colonel,” he says, not disrespectful, but not scared either. “That ground is cheese. Ain’t no place to set a brace. It’ll just go in with the rest.”

“We will sink piles,” Sherburn said, his voice getting louder. “We will drive them deep. I want every able-bodied man here. I will pay.”

Nobody moved. They just kept looking. They looked at the leaning wall of the store, and the dark water licking at the mud below it. They knowed it was a fool’s errand. You can’t order a river. You can’t shoot it and make it back down.

Sherburn’s face got red. It was the first time I’d seen him look like that since the day Boggs yelled at him. “Did you hear me?” he roared, and for a second, he sounded like his old self. “I gave an order!”

The crowd was quiet. Nobody defied him outright. But nobody moved to obey him, neither. They just stood there, watching him and his store, like they was both things that belonged to a time that was already past. They was watching the end of a story they thought they knowed.

Then a little boy in the front of the crowd, he didn’t mean no harm by it, he just says out loud what everyone was thinking. He says to his pa, “Is the store gwyne to fall in the river?”

And that broke the spell. It warn’t about Colonel Sherburn giving orders no more. It was about the river, and a building that was about to fall. It was just a thing that was happening.

Sherburn heard the boy. He stared at the crowd, and for the first time, he seemed to see that they warn’t his to command. They was just watchers. His shoulders slumped. The fire went out of his eyes. He looked at the livery man Johnson, and his voice was almost a plea.

“It’s my livelihood, man. It’s my store.”

“I’m sorry, Colonel,” Johnson said, and he sounded like he meant it. “There ain’t nothing to be done. You ought to get what you can out of there before the whole thing goes.”

And with that, Johnson turned and walked away. And then another man left, and another. The show was over, or at least the part where the great Colonel Sherburn was gwyne to fight the Mississippi. The rest of the show would be just the river’s work, and it didn’t need an audience.

Sherburn stood there alone in the street for a long time, in front of his broken store. He looked small. The pride that had held him up so straight and tall was like the foundation of his store; the river had just gnawed it away till there was nothing left to stand on. He had shot a man for wounding his pride in front of the town, and here the town was, walking away and leaving him with his pride in ruins, and there was nobody to shoot.

He turned and went back into the tilting store, the door groaning shut behind him.

I stayed. I slid over into the shadow of the building across the street and sat on a barrel. I don’t know why. I just felt like I had to see the end of it. The sun started to go down, and the street was empty and quiet except for the sucking sound of the water and the occasional groan from the store’s timbers.

Just as it was getting good and dark, a light appeared in one of the upstairs windows of the store. Sherburn lived up there. Then the light moved to another window, and another. He was moving around in there, in that dying building. I reckoned he was doing what Johnson said, getting his valuables out.

Then I heard a new sound. It was a rhythmic, heavy sound. *Thump. Thump. Thump.* It was coming from inside the store. I couldn’t figure what it was. It went on for a long time.

Suddenly, a piece of the front wall gave way. Bricks and plaster dust poured out onto the street. Through the hole, I could see inside. There was a lantern on the floor, and by its light I saw Colonel Sherburn. He had a great big sledgehammer, and he was swinging it against the back wall of his own store, the one farthest from the river. *Thump. CRACK. Thump. CRACK.* He was knocking it down himself. He was tearing his own place apart.

I understood then. He wasn’t gwyne to let the river have it. He wouldn’t give it the satisfaction. If his store was going to come down, he was going to be the one to bring it down. It was the last little piece of being in charge that he had left. He was hollering while he did it, not words, just a raw, ragged sound of fury and grief. He was a king smashing his own throne because his kingdom was being washed away.

I didn’t stay to see the rest. It felt too private, like watching a man die, only it was his pride that was dying, and he was the one doing the killing. I slid off the barrel and crept away down the dark street.

The next morning, the whole front half of Sherburn’s store was gone. It had fallen in the night, and the river had taken it. The back wall that he’d been hammering on was still standing, a jagged ruin against the sky. The rest was just a muddy hole, with the brown water swirling in it.

Colonel Sherburn was gone, too. Some said he took what he could carry and rode out of town in the night. Some said he was in the building when it went, and the river got him along with his store. Nobody ever saw him again. They searched the river for a body, but the river was in a taking mood, and it didn’t give nothing back.

The town had a new story to tell then, a real good one with a proper ending. They talked about the flood, and the big cave-in, and how the proud Colonel Sherburn was beat by the river at last. The lanky man had a whole new performance, where he’d pretend to swing a big hammer and yell at the sky. They liked that story. It was simple. Man against nature, and nature wins.

But I reckoned the river just finished a job that Silas Boggs had started. Silas come to town and he didn’t have a pistol, he just had words. And he used them to show everybody that Colonel Sherburn’s pride was just a hollow shell. He gnawed at the foundation. All the river did was wash away what was already crumbling. A man can be a kind of river, I reckoned. The kind that don’t make a lot of noise, but just keeps pushing and pushing, slow and steady, until the highest bank in the whole town just gives way.

so away they went, a-whooping and a-hollering, towards Colonel Sherburn’s house. It warn’t a march, not a proper one; it was more like a herd of cattle that’s been spooked, all bunched up and shoving, with the bravest or the drunkest ones in the front and the rest just getting carried along. They didn't have no plan that I could see, just a general idea of catching Sherburn and doing something terrible to him. Some of them had fetched ropes from the stores they passed, and a few had grabbed old rusty shotguns that probably hadn't been fired in a year. I followed along behind, keeping to the shadows where I could, because a mob ain't a thing you want to be in the middle of, especially when they're full of whiskey and self-righteousness, which is a powerful bad combination.

Colonel Sherburn’s house was at the other end of town, away from the river, on a little rise of ground. It was a two-story frame house, painted white, with green shutters and a nice fence all around it. It looked quiet and respectable, not at all like the kind of place you'd expect to find a man who’d just shot somebody in the street. The mob swarmed up to the fence and stopped. The hollering died down to a low rumble, like far-off thunder. It seemed like now that they was here, they didn't rightly know what to do next. The men in front looked back at the men behind, and the men behind pushed at the men in front, but nobody wanted to be the first one to lay a hand on that gate.

Then one of them, a big blacksmith with arms like tree limbs, he yells out, “What're we waiting for, boys? Bust the fence down! Fetch him out!”

He give the little picket fence a kick, and a piece of it splintered. That seemed to give the rest of them some courage, and they all surged forward and tore that fence to pieces in about a minute. They trampled the flower beds and swarmed all over the yard, and started yelling for Sherburn to come out. “Come out and take what’s comin’ to you, Sherburn!” they hollered. “You can’t murder a man in cold blood and get away with it, not in this town!”

The house stayed dark and quiet. I was hiding behind a big sycamore tree across the street, and I could see everything plain as day, because the moon was coming up and a few of the men had lit torches that threw a smoky, flickering light on everything. The crowd got louder and bolder when nobody answered. They was pressing up against the porch, and I heard somebody say, “Let’s burn him out! That’ll fetch the dog out of his hole!”

Just as a couple of fellers was about to put their torches to the porch, the front door opened. But nobody come out. Then, slow and deliberate, a window on the second story slid open, right above the little roof over the porch. And then Colonel Sherburn stepped out onto the roof. He didn't have a coat on, just his shirt and trousers. And in his hands he was holding a double-barrel shotgun.

The noise stopped so sudden it was like somebody had throwed a blanket over the whole crowd. Every man there just froze and stared up at him. The torchlight made his face look like it was carved out of stone, and his eyes was just dark holes. He didn't look scared. He looked tired, and maybe a little disgusted. He didn't raise the gun, just held it comfortable in the crook of his arm.

He let the silence stretch out for a good long while. You could hear men breathing hard, and the crackle of the torches. Then he spoke, and his voice warn’t loud, but it was clear and cold and it cut through the night air like a razor.

“You can turn around and go home,” he says. “There’s nothing for you here.”

Somebody in the crowd, way in the back, got up his nerve and yelled, “We want justice for Boggs! We’re gwyne to lynch you, Sherburn!”

Sherburn looked out over the crowd, not at any one man, but at all of them, like he was looking at a pack of stray dogs.

“Lynch me?” he says, and he give a short, ugly little laugh. It didn't have no humor in it. “That’s a good one. You want to lynch me. You, a mob of pitiful cowards.” He shifted the gun a little in his arms. “I know you people. I was born and raised in the South, and I’ve lived in the North; so I know the average man. The average man’s a coward. In the North he lets anybody walk over him that wants to, and goes home and prays for a humble spirit to bear it. In the South one man, all by himself, has got to go out in the middle of the night and borrow a rail, and then another man has got to get a bucket of tar, and another man has got to borrow a sack of feathers, and then they all have to corner a poor devil that’s maybe got five against him, and they take and bully him and ride him on a rail for things they’re all doing every day in the dark. The pitifulest thing out is a mob; that’s what an army is—a mob; they don’t fight with courage that’s born in them, but with courage that’s borrowed from their mass, and from their officers. But a mob without a man at the head of it is beneath pitifulness.”

He stopped and looked down at them, and I seen men shuffling their feet and looking at the ground. They couldn't meet his eyes.

“Now the thing for you to do is to droop your tails and go home,” he says, his voice getting harder. “And next time you want to lynch somebody, get a man to lead you. Get a man with some sand in him. If you’re gwyne to lynch me, you’ve got to do it in the daytime, and you’ve got to do it to my face. Not sneaking around in the dark, you half-a-man specimens. Now, git. And don’t you ever come back.”

He cocked both barrels of that shotgun, and the sound of it, two loud clicks in the dead quiet, was the scariest thing I ever heard.

That was all it took. The mob just melted. It didn't run, not at first. It just sort of dissolved from the edges. Men started to drift away, one by one, trying to look like they was just remembering something they had to do back at home. The ones in the front backed away from the porch slow, and then turned and walked, and then pretty soon everybody was moving, their boots scuffing in the dirt. The torches was throwed down and stomped out. In two minutes, the yard was empty. There was nobody left but me, hiding behind my tree, and Colonel Sherburn standing on his roof with his shotgun.

He stood there for another minute, watching the last of them disappear down the street. Then he uncocked his gun, turned around without another word, and went back inside through the window. The window slid shut. The house was dark and quiet again.

The next day, the town was a different place. The excitement was all gone, and in its place was something sour and ashamed. The lanky man warn’t marking out the shooting on the ground no more. The fellers that had been telling the story over and over was quiet. When people talked about it, they did it in low voices, like they was in church. They was mad at Sherburn for killing Boggs, but they seemed even madder at him for making them look like fools. He had held up a mirror to the whole town, and they didn't like what they saw.

Colonel Sherburn opened his store same as always. But nobody went in. Not at first. People would walk on the other side of the street to keep from having to pass his door. The women would pull their children close. He was a murderer, but he was a free man, and there wasn’t a thing anybody was gwyne to do about it. He was like a ghost in his own town. He’d walk to his store in the morning and walk home at night, and folks would just part and let him pass, and nobody would say a word or meet his eye. It was a strange kind of punishment; they couldn't touch him, so they tried to pretend he warn't there. But he was there, all right. A big, proud, silent man that the whole town was scared of.

This went on for a week, and then two. The story of Boggs and Sherburn was getting old, and a new worry was starting to creep in. It had been raining steady up north for a long time, and the river was on the rise. Not just a little bit, but a whole lot. The Mississippi was turning mean. It was a muddy, churning yellow, and it was climbing its banks an inch at a time, day and night. It was full of drift—whole trees with their roots sticking up like drowned spiders, parts of sheds, dead livestock. The current was so strong it made a steady, low roaring sound you could hear all over town.

The old men that spent their days sitting on benches whittling and spitting said they hadn't seen a rise like this in twenty years. They’d point with their sticks at the marks on the pilings down at the landing and shake their heads. “She’s a-comin’,” they’d say. “She’s a-comin’, and she’s gwyne to take a bite this time.”

A little pig-sty that had been a good twenty feet from the edge was hanging half over the water, and the pigs was squealing something awful till the farmer got them out. That got everybody’s attention. The river wasn’t just rising; it was eating the town.

All eyes turned to the row of buildings that stood on the river side of the main street. That was where the oldest and biggest stores was, built when the town was young and hopeful and wanted to be right on the water. And the biggest of them all, the one that stood nearest to the bend where the current was strongest, was Colonel Sherburn’s brick-front store.

The water was already licking at the foundations. You could stand on the street in front and hear the river sucking and gurgling right under the floorboards. The ground behind the store, which used to be a little yard where he kept his empty barrels, was getting smaller every day. A new kind of crowd started to gather across the street from Sherburn’s store. It warn’t a lynch mob. It was a waiting mob. They’d stand there for hours, men and women and children, just watching. It seemed like the town wanted a villain and a hero, and when Sherburn wouldn't play the part of the villain they was hoping for, they just decided to wait for the river to be the hero. He said they talked about the girl like she was a sack of meal that had been dropped on the road. Somebody had to pick it up.

Mrs. Parson Tibbets, she was the main one. She was a tall, thin woman with a face that looked like it was starched. She said it was the town’s solemn obligation to see the poor orphan was cared for. She said the girl ought to be took in by a good, God-fearing family who could give her moral instruction, which she likely never had, considering her pa was a drunkard. Everybody nodded at that and said, “Amen, sister.” Then there was a long silence while each of them hoped another one would volunteer.

Finally, the widow Douglas’s sister, Miss Watson, who was visiting from up north, she spoke up. She was a prim and proper woman, all bones and spectacles, and she had strong ideas about duty. She said she would take the girl in herself for the time being. She said she could teach her to read the Bible and to sew, and make her useful. There was a considerable sigh of relief in the room then, and Mrs. Parson Tibbets said it was a true blessing, and a testament to Miss Watson’s charitable heart.

So they settled it. The Boggs girl, her name was Susannah, was to live with Miss Watson. I seen her sometimes, working in the garden behind the widow’s house, or sweeping the porch. She was always dressed in plain black, and she never looked up. She was as quiet as a shadow. She done everything they told her, but there was a stillness about her that was different from being good. It was like she’d been hollowed out. The life had been shot out of her same as it had her pa, only she was still walking around.

The town ladies would stop by and see her, and they’d say things like, “Don’t she look clean, now?” and “Miss Watson, you are doing the Lord’s work with that child.” They treated her like a project they was all proud of. They’d give her little bags of candy or a ribbon for her hair, and she’d just stand there holding it, not knowing what to do with it. She never smiled. I reckoned a smile was a long way off for her.

One day I was fishing down at the ferry landing, and I seen her. She had come down with a basket to get some things from the ferry-man. She was waiting on the landing, looking out over the water. She warn’t looking at the other side, just at the river itself, the way it swirled and pulled. Her face was pale and serious. She looked older than sixteen now. There was a hardness in her eyes that hadn’t been there before. It warn’t a mean hardness, but the kind a piece of wood gets when it’s been left out in the sun and the rain for a long time.

While she was standing there, a little skiff come pulling up to the shore, a little ways down from the landing. There was a man in it, a stranger. He was a scrawny feller with a sly, pinched face and little black eyes that darted around like a rat’s. He tied his boat to a willow root and scrambled up the bank. He had on a worn-out coat and his boots was caked with mud from downriver. He looked around, taking everything in, and when his eyes landed on Susannah Boggs, they stopped.

He moseyed over towards the landing, trying to look casual, but he warn’t fooling nobody, least of all me. He had the look of a man who’s hunting for something. He took off his hat and give a little bow.

“Pardon me, miss,” he says, and his voice was greasy, like he’d oiled it. “I’m a stranger in these parts. I’m looking for my kin. I heard there was a tragedy here concerning a feller by the name of Boggs. Silas Boggs.”

Susannah just stared at him. She didn’t say nothing. She hugged her basket tight to her chest.

“That was my cousin,” the man says, putting a sorrowful look on his face. It didn’t fit him right; it looked like it was slipping off. “My dear cousin Si. I only just heard the terrible news down in Napoleon, and I come as fast as I could. They told me he left behind a daughter.”

He looked right at her, with his head cocked to one side. “Would you be her?” he says.

She give a little nod, just a twitch of her head.

“Oh, the poor child!” he says, and he took a step closer. “The poor, dear orphan. My name is Jedediah Boggs. Your pa and me was thick as thieves when we was boys. I’m your family, child. I’ve come to take you home.”

I knew right then he was a fraud. Huck Finn might not know a lot about book-learning, but he knows a fraud when he sees one. I’ve had practice. This feller was a low-down, river-rat fraud if ever there was one. Boggs was a loud-mouthed fool, but there was an honestness to his foolishness. This Jedediah, he was all snake.

Susannah, she just kept staring. She looked from his shifty eyes to his muddy boots. I reckoned she was seeing the same thing I was.

“I don’t know you,” she said, and her voice was so low I could hardly hear it.

“Of course you don’t, child, of course you don’t,” he says, all smooth and comforting. “Silas and me, we had a falling out years ago. A foolish argument over a horse, it was. We was both proud men. I ain’t seen him in twenty year. But blood is blood. When I heard he was gone, and left you all alone, I said to myself, ‘Jedediah, it’s your duty to go and fetch that girl.’ I’ve got a nice little farm downriver. A good wife who’ll be a mother to you. You won’t be a burden on these strangers no more.”

The ferry was pulling in then, and a few people was gathering. The man, this Jedediah, he put his hand on Susannah’s shoulder. She flinched away like he was a hot iron.

He didn’t miss a beat. He turned to the ferry-man. “Is there a judge or a preacher in this town?” he says, raising his voice so everyone could hear. “I’ve come to claim my orphaned kin, Susannah Boggs. I have papers to prove my name. I’ve come to take her out of charity and give her a proper home.”

Well, that started a buzz right away. A man’s family showing up was a whole different thing from charity. The ferry-man pointed him towards the courthouse, and Jedediah Boggs give Susannah another one of his sorrowful looks, and then he strode off up the street like he owned the place.

Susannah stood there for a long time, watching him go. Then she turned and walked away from the landing, leaving her basket sitting in the dirt. She didn’t go back to the widow’s house. She went the other way, towards the woods at the edge of town. I picked up her basket and followed her, keeping a good ways back.

She went to the town graveyard. It was a scraggly patch of ground on a little hill, with crooked headstones and a lot of weeds. She went right to a new-dug mound of dirt at the far end. There warn’t no stone on it yet, just a little wooden cross somebody had stuck in the ground. She knelt down beside it and didn’t move. She just knelt there in the weeds, with her back to the town and everything in it. I set her basket down where she could see it and I crept away. I figured she had a right to be alone with her pa, even if he was just a pile of dirt now.

By evening, the whole town was talking about Jedediah Boggs. He’d gone to see Judge Thatcher, and then he’d gone to see Parson Tibbets. He told them his story, about being the long-lost cousin, and how his heart bled for the poor orphan. Miss Watson was relieved, you could see it. Taking care of a grieving girl who didn’t say nothing and didn’t smile was hard work, and it warn’t as rewarding as she thought it would be. Mrs. Parson Tibbets said it was the hand of Providence, providing for the child.

They was all so pleased to have the problem solved and handed off to somebody else. They didn’t see what I seen. They didn’t see the rat in his eyes. They saw a respectable-seeming man talking about family and duty, and that was all they needed to see. He said he’d need a day or two to settle his cousin’s affairs, though everybody knew there warn’t no affairs to settle, and then he would take Susannah away on the downstream packet.

That night, the river took another bite. A big one. It happened about midnight. There was a groaning, tearing sound that woke up half the town. I was sleeping on a pile of wood shavings behind the carpenter’s shop, and I heard it plain as day. It was followed by a great big splash that sounded like a house falling in.

Everybody came running out in their nightshirts. It was Colonel Sherburn’s store. The whole back half of it had caved in. The river had finally got it. It had chewed away the ground right up to the back wall, and the whole thing had just slid down into the dark. There was a big, black, gaping hole where the back of the store used to be. The front part, the part with the porch roof he stood on, was still there, but it was leaning something awful.

Folks stood around in the moonlight, looking at the ruin. They was quiet and scared. This was the biggest cave-in yet. It was right in the middle of town. They started whispering about Sherburn. Was he in there? Did he go down with it?

Nobody was brave enough to go look. They was still more scared of Sherburn alive than they was of him dead. So they just stood and watched, like it was a show. After a while, a piece of the roof creaked and slid down into the hole, and everybody jumped.

I got to thinking. Sherburn was a proud man. He hated that mob because they was cowards. But he knew the river was coming for his store. He must have known. I wondered what a man like that does when he knows he’s beat, not by a mob of cowards, but by something big and slow and powerful that he can’t shoot.

I slipped away from the crowd and went around the back, through the alley. The moon was bright enough to see by. I crept up to the edge of the new cliff. The water was swirling and muddy down below, full of busted planks and bits of things. The back wall of the store was just gone. But through the hole, I could see inside what was left of the building. And I saw something that made my blood run cold.

There was a lantern sitting on the floor, throwing a yellow, flickering light. And there was Colonel Sherburn. He was alive. He had a great big sledgehammer, and he was standing in the middle of what was left of his store. He warn’t trying to save nothing. He was smashing it. He’d swing that hammer and bring it down on a counter, and it would bust into splinters. *CRASH*. He’d bring it down on a shelf full of goods, and cans and jars would fly everywhere. *WHAM*. He was yelling while he did it, a long, ragged sound that warn’t words, just pure rage. He was destroying his own property. He was tearing down his own store before the river could get the rest of it.

I understood then. It was like I seen before, only different. He wasn’t going to let the river win. He wouldn’t give it the satisfaction. If his kingdom was going to fall, he was going to be the one to push it over. It was the only way he could stay in charge. He was a proud man to the very end. It was a terrible thing to see. It was more private than the shooting, more awful than the mob. I felt like I was spying on a man’s soul while it was being ripped apart.

I didn’t stay. I backed away quiet and ran. The next morning, Sherburn was gone. The rest of his store fell in before breakfast. Some said he must have been in it. Some said he just walked away in the night. Nobody ever knew for sure. His body never washed up. The town had another mystery, but they was getting tired of mysteries. They was more interested in the new feller, Jedediah Boggs.

Jedediah was making himself known all over town. He was a talker. He told stories about his dear cousin Silas, stories that made him sound like a saintly fool instead of a drunken pest. He was oiling his way into the good graces of the town. He paid for a proper headstone for the grave, a nice marble one that said, “Silas Boggs, Beloved Father and Cousin, Gone to His Reward.” That pleased the church ladies considerable.

I seen him talking to Susannah once, in front of the widow’s house. He was trying to be kind and fatherly, but it didn’t look right on him. He was saying, “Now, child, you’ll have a good home with me. A feather bed and all the johnny-cake you can eat. We’ll put this whole sad business behind us.”

She just looked at him with those hard, quiet eyes. “He never mentioned you,” she said. “Not once.”

“Like I said, child, pride,” Jedediah says, shaking his head sadly. “A terrible, foolish pride kept us apart. A sin I aim to rectify with you, his only child.”

He was a smooth one, I’ll give him that. But I’d been watching him. He didn’t spend his time at the courthouse looking at deeds. He spent his time in the saloons, the cheap ones down by the wharf. He’d buy drinks for the loafers and the wood-choppers, and he’d listen. I seen him. He warn’t asking about his cousin. He was asking about the town. Who had money. Who had power. Who was a fool. And he asked about Colonel Sherburn. He asked if anybody thought Sherburn had a safe in his store, and if it might have washed downriver. He was a scavenger. He’d smelled death and trouble all the way from Napoleon, and he’d come to see what he could pick off the corpse.

The day came when the packet boat was due. Jedediah had bought Susannah a new dress, a dark gray one, and a bonnet. He was going to take her away. The whole town leadership, what was left of it, was there to see them off. Parson Tibbets and his wife, and Miss Watson, all standing on the landing looking pleased with themselves.

Jedediah was leading Susannah by the arm. She was walking stiff, like a wooden doll. When they got to the gangplank, she stopped. She pulled her arm away.

“I ain’t going,” she said. Her voice was quiet, but everybody heard it.

Jedediah’s smile got tight. “Now, Susannah, don’t be difficult. We’ve all been through a trying time. This is for the best.”

“I ain’t going with you,” she said again, a little louder this time.

Parson Tibbets stepped forward. “Child,” he says, in his church voice, “this man is your kin. He is offering you a Christian home. It is your duty to go with him and be grateful.”

“He ain’t my kin,” she said, and she looked right at Jedediah. “My pa was a fool and a drunk. But he was an honest fool. He never had a sly bone in his body. You’re made of nothing but sly bones.”

Well, that shocked them. The quiet little orphan girl, talking back like that. Jedediah’s face got ugly. The greasy smile was all gone. “You ungrateful little brat,” he hissed. “After all I’ve done…”

“You ain’t done nothing but lie,” she shot back.

I reckoned it was time. I had been thinking about it all night. I couldn’t fight him, and the town wouldn’t believe me over him. But I’d heard something in the saloon. Jedediah, when he was a little drunk, he’d been bragging to a flatboat man. I remembered his exact words.

So I pushed my way through the little crowd. I looked up at Jedediah Boggs, trying to look as simple as I could.

“Mister Boggs, sir,” I says. “I was just wondering. My pa, he knew a feller from down near Napoleon. A feller named Boggs. He said he had two sons, Silas and Jeb.”

Jedediah looked down at me, annoyed. “So?”

“Well,” I says, scratching my head, “I was just confused. He said Jeb was the younger one, a quiet feller that run a ferry. He said Silas, he was the loud one that run off upriver. He never mentioned no Jedediah.”

It was a lie from top to bottom. My pa never said no such thing. I made up Jeb on the spot. But it was the kind of lie that has the smell of truth to it.

Jedediah’s rat-like eyes darted around. He was caught. He didn’t know who I was, or who my pa was, or what my pa might know. His whole story was built on him being the only family, a story he’d cobbled together from saloon gossip. He didn’t have a real history to fall back on.

“That’s… that’s a different family of Boggs,” he stammered. “A common name.”

“Oh,” I says, looking sad and confused. “That’s a shame. ’Cause my pa said that Jeb Boggs, he had a little girl with hair the color of corn silk, just like Susannah here. He said Jeb was powerful broken up when his brother Si run off and never wrote. He’d be mighty glad to know Si’s girl was found.”

I looked over at Susannah. “Did your pa ever mention his brother Jeb, miss?”

She was quick. She was smarter than all of them. Her eyes met mine for just a second, and I seen a flicker of something in them. A little spark.

“He did,” she said, her voice clear and strong. “He talked about Uncle Jeb all the time. He said he was the best man he ever knew.”

That was it. The whole thing fell apart. Jedediah Boggs turned a nasty shade of pale. He was cornered, and two children with a made-up uncle had done it

to it.

Well, you could have heard a pin drop in that dusty street. Every eye in that crowd swiveled from Jedediah, to me, to Susannah, and back to Jedediah again. His face, that was a minute ago so full of greasy confidence, had crumpled up like a wet newspaper. He looked like a rat that’s found the cheese is nailed to the floor. The sweat was popping out on his forehead like little beads of grease, and his fancy vest didn’t look so fancy no more, just tight and uncomfortable.

He opened his mouth, but no words come out. Just a little dry clicking sound. The crowd, which had been all on his side and ready to hand over the girl and her pa’s property, was turning on him. You could feel it, like the air gets heavy before a thunderstorm. A low rumbling started in the crowd, and it warn’t sympathy. One man, the feller who’d been so quick to say what a fine Christian man Jedediah was, he snickered. It was a mean, ugly little sound. Then another man laughed out loud, a big booming laugh, and slapped his thigh.

“Well I’ll be,” the man says. “Out-foxed by a couple of children and a ghost! Jeb Boggs! Who’d a-thought it?”

Jedediah, he finally found his voice, but it was thin and whiny. “It’s a lie! A conspiracy! This boy… he’s a vagrant! And the girl… she’s tetched in the head with grief!”

But nobody was buying it. The time for believing Jedediah Boggs was over. The storekeeper, the same one Colonel Sherburn had walked out of, he stepped onto his porch. He was a solid-looking man with spectacles. He’d been quiet through the whole thing, just watching.

“Seems to me, Mister Boggs,” he says, his voice slow and heavy, “that your business in this town is concluded. The noon boat for downriver leaves in about an hour. I’d advise you to be on it.”

Jedediah looked around, his eyes wild and desperate, looking for a friendly face. There warn’t none. All he seen was hard stares and grinning mouths. They was enjoying this just as much as they enjoyed the shooting and the lynch mob talk. It was all a show to them. He made a little squeaking noise and started backing away, bumping into people who didn’t move an inch to let him pass. They just shoved him along. He stumbled and nearly fell, and a couple of boys throwed clods of dirt that hit his coat with little soft puffs. He didn’t even look back. He just scrambled away towards the river landing, a whole parade of jeering loafers following him, calling out, “Don’t forget to write to your brother Jeb!”

I felt a little sick, watching it. It was one thing to see a man be a rascal, and another thing to see him torn apart by a pack of dogs, even if he deserved it. I never was one for mobs.

When he was gone, the crowd sort of broke apart, buzzing like a kicked-over beehive. A lot of them was clapping me on the back and saying I was a smart one, but I didn’t feel smart. I just felt tired. Susannah, she was standing there looking at her feet, her hands twisted together. She looked smaller than ever now that the fuss was over.

A big woman with a kind, worried face come out of the store. She was the storekeeper’s wife, I reckoned. She put a soft arm around Susannah’s shoulders.

“You poor child,” she says, her voice like warm milk. “You come on inside with me. You’ll stay with us for now. My name is Martha Pemberton. You’re safe here.”

Susannah looked up at her, and her face, which had been so hard and set, just broke. The tears started running down her cheeks, but she didn’t make a sound. She just nodded and let Mrs. Pemberton lead her into the dim, cool store that smelled of coffee and calico and spices. Before she went in, she looked back over her shoulder at me. It was a quick look, but it was full of—well, I don’t know what it was full of. Gratitude, I suppose, and something else, too. Something that made me feel like we was the only two people in the world who knew what it was like to be all alone.

I didn’t want to talk to nobody, so I slid away from the men who was still jawing about Jedediah and my made-up Uncle Jeb. I went and sat on a pile of lumber by the side of the street and just watched. The town went on about its business. Wagons creaked by, people went in and out of the saloon. It was like the whole thing with Boggs getting shot and Jedediah getting run out of town was just a morning’s entertainment, and now it was over.

After a while, maybe an hour, the storekeeper, Mister Pemberton, he come out and looked around, and his eyes landed on me. He walked over.

“Son,” he says, “my wife tells me you’re the one who spoke up. That was a brave thing you did.”

“I didn’t do much,” I says.

“You did enough,” he says. He looked me up and down, my tore britches and my bare feet. “You got folks here?”

“No, sir. I’m just passing through.”

“I see,” he says. He thought for a minute. “The girl, Susannah. She’s taken a shine to you. Says you’re the only friend she has. She asked if you’d stay for supper.”

Well, I hadn’t had a proper supper cooked by a woman in a house since—well, since the Widow Douglas. The thought of it made my mouth water. But I was fixing to shove off down the river. Staying in a town, with people, it always led to trouble. Still, the way he asked, and the way Susannah had looked at me, it made it hard to say no.

“I reckon I could,” I says.

“Good,” he says, and he smiled a little. “There’s the matter of her father’s effects, too. Judge Thatcher is coming over from the courthouse directly to sort it out. Since you seem to be… involved… it might be best if you was there.”

So I stayed. I helped Mister Pemberton unload a wagon of goods, and he give me a big piece of cheese and some crackers, and I sat in the shade behind the store and eat them, feeling mighty satisfied. By and by, a buggy pulls up and a man with white hair and a kind but serious face got out. This was Judge Thatcher. He warn’t the same Judge Thatcher from back home, but he had the same air about him, like he was used to things being orderly and fair.

He went inside, and a little later Mister Pemberton called me in. The back of the store was a parlor, with a horsehair sofa and pictures on the wall and a carpet on the floor. It was all very fine and civilized. The Judge was sitting in a big chair, and Mrs. Pemberton was on the sofa, with Susannah sitting beside her. Susannah had washed her face and combed her hair. She looked pale, but she was calm. She give me a little nod when I come in.

The Judge cleared his throat. “Young man,” he says to me, “I’ve been hearing about you. You seem to have a knack for uncovering the truth.”

I shuffled my feet and didn’t say nothing.

“Now, as for the matter at hand,” he went on. “The man calling himself Jedediah Boggs has, it appears, absconded. He laid claim to being the sole remaining kin of the deceased. Your… intervention… has cast considerable doubt on that claim.” He looked at me over his spectacles. “This ‘Uncle Jeb.’ Is he a real person?”

I looked at Susannah. She looked at me. I could feel the heat rising in my face. Lying to a scoundrel in the street is one thing; lying to a Judge in a parlor is another.

Susannah spoke up first, her voice small but clear. “No, sir. He ain’t real. We made him up.”

Mrs. Pemberton gasped a little, but the Judge just nodded, like he expected it.

“I see,” he said. “A fabrication to expose a fraud. Irregular. But effective.” He looked at me. “And you, son? What’s your name?”

“Huck Finn, sir.”

“Huck Finn. Well, Huck Finn, your quick thinking likely saved this girl from a terrible fate. The town owes you a debt.” He turned to Susannah. “My dear, as far as the law is concerned, you are now an orphan and a ward of this county. Your father, Boggs, owned a small house and a few acres of land just outside of town. It ain’t much. There’s a mortgage on it, and what little was in his purse won’t cover his burial fees. After all is said and done, you’ll be left with almost nothing.”

Susannah just stared at her hands in her lap. The hope that had been in her face seemed to drain away again. Here she was, saved from a greasy liar only to be told she was a pauper. It didn’t seem fair.

“However,” the Judge continued, pulling a set of keys from his pocket. “These were on your father’s person. They are for his house. Mister Pemberton has agreed to act as temporary guardian, and he and I will go out there tomorrow to take inventory of the property. You, my dear, will remain here with Mrs. Pemberton.”

That night, they give me a fine supper of fried chicken and biscuits and gravy. I eat till I was near to bursting. They fixed me a pallet on the floor of the storeroom, and it was soft and clean. But I couldn’t sleep. I kept thinking about old Boggs, drunk and foolish, getting shot down in the street. And I thought about Susannah, with her corn-silk hair and her quiet ways, having to make up lies just to keep from being swallowed by a shark like Jedediah. It all seemed so mixed up and ugly. The world was a mean place sometimes.

The next morning, after breakfast, Mister Pemberton and the Judge got ready to go out to the Boggs place. Susannah was quiet, helping Mrs. Pemberton in the kitchen. Just before they left, she come over to me where I was sitting on the back steps.

“Huck?” she says.

“Ma’am?”

“You going with them?”

“I don’t reckon they want me to.”

“I want you to,” she said. Her eyes were big and serious. “My pa… he warn’t always drunk. When he was sober, sometimes… he was different. He used to talk about… things. Secrets. He’d say, ‘One day, Susie, you’ll see. Old Boggs ain’t the fool they think.’ But then he’d get to drinking again and I’d think it was just talk.” She took a little breath. “That man, Jedediah. He wasn’t just after the house. I know it. He’d been asking questions around town before he ever come to see me. About Pa’s habits. Where he went. Who he talked to.”

“What are you saying?” I says.

“I’m saying I think there’s something else. Something Pa hid. And if there is, I don’t want them finding it.” She looked towards the parlor where the Judge was. “Not them. They’re good people. But they’re the Law. They’ll take it and put it in a bank and talk about trusts and guardians and I won’t see a penny of it till I’m old and gray. Pa wouldn’t have wanted that. He’d have wanted me to have it.” She looked me square in the eye. “I want you to go. And watch. If you see anything… anything at all… you’ll know what to do.”

Well, that was a fix. She was asking me to be a spy, right in the middle of all these good folks who’d been kind to me. But then I looked at her, and she had that same desperate look she’d had in the street. She was a cub in a trap, and she was asking me to help her gnaw her way out. I couldn’t say no.

“Alright,” I says. “I’ll go.”

I asked Mister Pemberton if I could ride along, saying I was curious to see the place. He looked at the Judge, who just shrugged, and so they let me climb up in the back of the buggy.

The Boggs place was about a mile out of town, down a rutted lane. It was a sad-looking little house. The paint was all peeled off, and the porch sagged on one end. Weeds was taking over the yard and growing up through the floorboards of the porch. There was a tumbledown shed out back and a chicken coop with the door hanging off one hinge. Everything about it looked tired and give up.

Mister Pemberton unlocked the door, and we went in. It smelled musty and sour, the smell of old whisky and loneliness. There warn’t much to it. A main room with a fireplace, a table, two chairs, and a dirty bed in the corner. A little lean-to kitchen off the back with some pots and a sack of flour. The dust was thick on everything.

The Judge and Mister Pemberton started looking around, making notes on a pad of paper. “One pine table, poor condition,” the Judge would say, and Mister Pemberton would write it down. It was all very official. I just poked around, trying to stay out of their way. I looked under the bed. Nothing but dust-bunnies and a corn cob pipe. I looked in the fireplace. Just old ashes.

It seemed like Susannah was wrong. It was just a poor, miserable shack. Boggs was just a drunk, and his treasure was just drunken talk. They went through the whole house, and then the shed. They found a rusty axe, some old harnesses, and a broken plow. Nothing of any value.

They was getting ready to leave. The Judge was shaking his head, saying, “It’s as I feared. The estate is insolvent.” That meant it was broke.

I was standing on the sagging porch, feeling disappointed for Susannah. My eyes was just wandering over the yard, the tall weeds, the broke-down fence. Then I seen it. Over by the chicken coop, there was a patch of ground, maybe four feet square, where the weeds warn’t as high as everywhere else. And they was a different kind of weed, a kind that grows in disturbed dirt. It warn’t much, but it was something. Most people wouldn’t have noticed it, but when you spend as much time outdoors as I have, you learn to read the ground like a book.

I didn’t say nothing. I just sort of filed it away in my head.

We rode back to town, and the Judge told Susannah the bad news. He was very gentle about it. He said they would sell the land to pay the mortgage, and what little was left would go to the county for her upkeep. She would live with the Pembertons, and they would be good to her. It was the best that could be done.

She listened, and nodded, and thanked him. She was playing her part real well. But I seen her eyes flick over to me when the Judge wasn’t looking. It was a question. I give a tiny little nod, so small nobody else could see it. And a little bit of the light come back into her face.

That night, I couldn’t get to sleep again. The plan was turning over and over in my head. It was risky. It was stealing, in a way, even if it was for her. But what was the other choice? Let her live on charity, always feeling beholden, while her pa’s secret, if there was one, got plowed under or sold off to a stranger? It didn’t seem right. Pap always said the world was full of people trying to take what’s yours, and the only way to get by was to be smarter and quicker than them. I never did want to be like Pap, but sometimes he warn’t altogether wrong.

The next day I told Mrs. Pemberton I had a powerful need to go fishing. She said it was a fine idea and packed me a lunch of biscuits and bacon. But I didn’t go to the river. I waited till the town was busy with its morning chores, and I slipped out the back way and took the lane towards the Boggs place. I had a shovel I’d “borrowed” from behind Mister Pemberton’s woodshed. It was a heavy, clumsy thing.

It was spooky, going out there alone. The house looked even sadder in the bright daylight. I went straight to the chicken coop and found the spot with the low weeds. The sun was getting hot. I started to dig.

The ground was hard and full of roots. It was slow work. My hands got blistered right away, and the sweat was pouring down my face and stinging my eyes. A couple of times I thought I heard somebody coming down the lane, and I’d dive into the tall weeds, my heart thumping. But it was just the wind in the trees. After about an hour of digging, I was near to giving up. It was probably nothing. Just a spot where an animal had dug a burrow.

Then the shovel hit something hard.

It made a solid, dull clunk. Not a rock. It was a woody, hollow sound.

I dropped to my knees and started digging with my hands, clawing the dirt away. Pretty soon I uncovered the top of a small wooden box, bound with iron straps. It was a seaman’s chest, old and weathered, but still strong. It had a big, rusty lock on it.

I worked the shovel under one edge and pried. The wood groaned. I put all my weight on it, and with a splintering crack, the lid broke open.

I knelt there, breathing hard, and looked inside. At first, it was just papers, tied up in bundles with string. But under the papers, I seen the shine of metal. I pushed the papers aside, and my breath caught in my throat. The bottom of the chest was lined with gold coins. Not a whole fortune, not like in a storybook, but more money than I had ever seen in my life. I counted out a handful. They was twenty-dollar gold pieces. There must have been a hundred of them, maybe more. Two thousand dollars. It was a staggering amount of money.

But that warn’t all. I picked up one of the bundles of paper. It was a deed. A deed for a tract of land up on the White River, a hundred and sixty acres. And there was a letter, on top of the deed, written in a shaky but clear hand. I unfolded it. It said:

*My Dearest Daughter Susannah,*

*If you are reading this, then I am gone, and the fools in town have called me a drunk and a pauper to the end. Let them. A man with a secret has to wear a disguise, and being the town fool is the best disguise there is. I was not always this way. Before your mother passed, I was a different man. I made some good investments in my youth, on the river. This money, and this land, is from that time. I kept it hidden. There are vultures everywhere, and a man like me, with a weakness for the bottle, can’t trust banks and lawyers. They’d have swindled it from me, and from you.*

*This is for you. All of it. Don’t let them take it. Don’t let them put you in an orphanage. Use it to make a life for yourself. Be smart, and be strong, qualities your mother had in abundance and which I see in you every day. Forgive your foolish old father for the shame he brought you. He always loved you more than anything in this world.*

*Your Father, Silas Boggs*

I sat there in the dirt for a long time, holding that letter. It changed everything. Old Man Boggs, whooping and yelling and cussing Sherburn, he warn’t just a drunk. He was a man playing a part, a desperate, sad part, to protect his daughter. And Colonel Sherburn had shot him down without knowing any of it. It made the whole thing feel a hundred times worse.

I knew what I had to do. I couldn’t carry the whole chest. I stuffed my pockets with as many of the gold coins as they would hold without looking too suspicious, and I buttoned my shirt up tight over them. Then I took the deed and the letter and hid them inside my shirt. I covered the chest back up with dirt, trying to make it look as natural as I could, scattering weeds over the top. Then I took the shovel back to the shed and started back to town, walking slow and casual, like a boy coming back from a lazy day of fishing. The gold pieces was heavy, and they clinked a little when I walked. Every clink sounded as loud as a church bell to me.

away they went, a-surging down the street like a log jam breaking up. It warn’t a tidy-looking mob, like you might see in a picture book. It was just a mess of men, some with hats and some without, some with coats and a good many in their shirtsleeves, all bunched up and shoving and sweating in the afternoon sun. They didn’t have no torches or ropes to speak of, not yet anyways, just a powerful lot of whisky-courage and a notion that something ought to be done.

I kind of got swept along with them, not because I wanted to do any lynching, but because the whole town was moving in that one direction and it was easier to go with it than to try and swim against it. The gold pieces in my pockets felt like they weighed a thousand pound apiece, and they clinked together every time I took a step, a little jingle-jangle music that nobody could hear but me. It was a terrible sound. It sounded like stealing, even though I didn’t rightly know what I was going to do with it. The deed and the letter was tucked inside my shirt, getting warm and damp against my skin.

The mob fetched up in front of a white two-story house with a nice fence around it and green shutters. There was rose-bushes in the yard, all neat and tidy. This was Colonel Sherburn’s house. The crowd spread out in front of the fence, looking kind of uncertain now that they was here. They mumbled to each other, and the fellows in the back pushed the fellows in the front a little, but nobody wanted to be the first one to lay a hand on that gate.

Then Buck Harkness, the big lanky fellow that had acted out the shooting, he clumb up on a wood-pile and started making a speech.

“Fellow-citizens!” he sings out, waving his arms. “This here is a outrage! An unarmed man shot down in cold blood! Are we a-gwyne to stand for it? Are we men, or are we mice? We must avenge the blood of Silas Boggs! We must drag out the murderer Sherburn and give him what he deserves!”

There was a lot of yelling and cheering at that, and the crowd surged forward a little, so the front row of them was pressed up hard against the fence pickets. It looked for a minute like they might just tear the fence down and be done with it.

But then the front door of the house opened, and Colonel Sherburn stepped out onto the porch. He didn’t have his pistol this time; he just had a double-barrel shotgun, and he was holding it easy in the crook of his arm, not pointing it at nothing, just holding it. He stood there, as ca’m and proud as ever, and looked down at the mob. He didn’t say a word.

The yelling and the hollering kind of petered out. The men at the front quit pushing on the fence. The whole street got quiet, so quiet you could hear a bee buzzing around one of them rose-bushes. Colonel Sherburn just kept on looking at them, his eyes moving slow from one face to the next, like he was taking their measure and finding them wanting. It was the most dreadful quiet I ever heard.

After a long time, he let out a little laugh, but it warn’t a happy sound. It was a sound full of scorn, like a man stepping in something nasty.

“You pitiful things,” he says, and his voice warn’t loud, but it cut through the air like a razor. “So you’re going to lynch me, are you? You want to break down my door and drag me out and hang me. You, with your borrowed courage from a bottle.”

He looked straight at Buck Harkness, who was still standing on the wood-pile looking mighty uncomfortable.

“I know you,” Sherburn says. “I see you every day. You ain’t a man. You’re what’s left over. I was born and raised in the South, and I’ve lived in the North; I know the average man. The average man is a coward. Down here, and up there, all over. He’ll tell you he’s brave, but he’s lying. In the North he lets anybody walk over him that wants to, and in the South he’ll talk you deaf, dumb, and blind about his honor, but he’ll only fight you when he’s got the advantage, him and his pack against one man.”

The crowd was perfectly still, every eye on him. Nobody so much as shuffled their feet.

“Why, a man’s safe in the hands of ten thousand of your kind,” he says, and his lip curled up. “As long as it’s daytime and you’re not behind him. Do I know you? I know you clear through. I was born right here, and I’ve seen this town from the start. I’m a man; I know what a man is. I see you now, you cheap, secondhand versions of men. You brought a man with you to do your lynching. You think you have the grit to lynch a man? You’re not men enough. The man you brought with you is Buck Harkness. He’s not even half-a-man, and that’s why you’re following him. Because you’re scared of him, and you’re scared of me, and you’re scared of yourselves. You’re just a pack of pitiful cowards.”

He looked them all over one last time. “Now get out of here,” he says, his voice like cracking ice. “Go home and crawl into your beds. If you’re still here in one minute, I’ll turn this loose.” He shifted the shotgun a little in his arm.

It didn’t take no minute. It didn’t even take ten seconds. That mob just melted away. They didn’t run, not exactly. They just sort of dissolved, turning around and walking off quiet and shame-faced, not looking at each other. In no time at all the street was empty, except for me, hiding behind a tree, and Colonel Sherburn standing alone on his porch. He stood there a minute longer, then he turned and went back in the house, shutting the door firm behind him.

I let my breath out, which I didn’t know I was holding. It felt like the whole world had just been called a coward, and I couldn’t say it warn’t true. I slid out from behind my tree and started walking, feeling the clink, clink, clink in my pockets. The town warn’t hollering no more. It was quiet and buzzing, like a hive after the bear’s gone. People stood around in little knots on the street corners, talking low and serious. The fun had all gone out of it.

I had to figure out what to do about that girl, Boggs’s daughter. The letter said her name was Susannah. It said this money was for her. It said, *Don’t let them put you in an orphanage.* That part stuck with me. She was all alone now, and folks would be deciding what to do with her. Vultures, her pa had called them. I reckoned I could see what he meant. Somebody would take her in, sure enough, but maybe it’d be somebody that wanted to be a hero, or somebody that was just plain nosy, or worst of all, somebody that smelt money.

I made my way back to the little drug store. There was still a dark, wet stain on the floorboards where they’d laid Boggs down, and somebody had sprinkled sawdust over it. The place was empty now, except for the druggist, a little man with spectacles, who was wiping down his counter with a rag. I peeked in the window, trying to look casual.

His wife come out from a back room. She was a plump, kindly-looking woman, wringing her hands.

“Is there any word, Alpheus?” she says. “About the poor child?”

“They took her to our place, Martha,” the druggist says. “She’s upstairs with your sister. The shock, you know. Hasn’t said a word. Just sits and stares.”

“The poor, dear thing,” the woman says, dabbing her eyes with her apron. “An orphan now. And her father leaving her with not a cent to her name, from what I hear. That man, a public disgrace.”

“Hush, Martha,” the druggist says, looking towards the street. “It ain’t Christian to speak ill of the dead.”

“It’s the Lord’s truth, Alpheus, and you know it,” she says. “A drunkard’s life and a pauper’s grave. We’ll have to see what the church can do for her. Maybe the ladies’ auxiliary can raise a little something.”

So that’s where she was. Upstairs in the druggist’s house, which was right next to the store. I went around the side, into the alley, and looked up. There was a window on the second floor with the curtains drawn, and a light on inside, even though it was still daytime. I reckoned that must be the room.

How was I to get to her? I couldn’t just knock on the door and say, “Here, I got a chest full of gold for the orphan.” They’d think I was crazy, or a thief, and they’d take the money for themselves to “manage” for her, and that’d be the last she ever saw of it. I remembered Boggs’s letter: *There are vultures everywhere.* I could see Mrs. Druggist’s kind face, and hear her talking about the ladies’ auxiliary, and I knew he was right. They meant well, I suppose, but their kind of well-meaning could swallow up a hundred and sixty acres and two thousand dollars in gold without even a burp.

I sat down on an old barrel in the alley and tried to think. The sun was getting low, and the whole town was settling down for the night, full of whispers about Sherburn and poor Boggs and his poor daughter. I had to get that deed and that letter to her. The gold could wait, but the letter was the main thing. It was her proof.

I waited till it was full dark. The light was still on in her window. I saw shadows move across the curtain now and then. I found some little pebbles and tried tossing them up at the glass, gentle-like. Nothing happened. I tried a bigger one. It made a little *tink* sound. The curtain moved a little, and a woman’s shape looked out, but it warn’t the girl. It was the druggist’s wife’s sister, I reckoned. She looked down into the dark alley, didn’t see nothing, and then the curtain fell back. I had to give that up as a bad job.

The next day was worse. The whole town was on its best behavior, which is a sight to see. Everybody was talking in church-voices and looking solemn. They was making arrangements for Boggs’s funeral. I heard Lawyer Hoskins talking to the druggist. Hoskins was a man with a soft voice and a hard little smile that didn’t use his eyes.

“A terrible tragedy, Alpheus,” he says, shaking his head. “And the child left destitute. I’ll be filing the papers this afternoon to have myself appointed her legal guardian. It’s the least I can do as a civic duty. We must protect her interests, see that she is provided for from the county poor fund, perhaps apprenticed to a respectable family.”

The druggist nodded, looking relieved. “That’s mighty good of you, Judge Hoskins. A great weight off our minds.”

I felt a cold sweat break out on me. Judge Hoskins was a vulture in a black coat. If he got to be her guardian, he’d tie up her life in legal knots so tight she’d never get loose, and if any money ever did show up, it would be his. I had to get to her before Hoskins did.

The funeral was set for the next morning. That, I figured, was my only chance.

The morning was hot and sticky. The funeral was at the little white church on the edge of town, and the graveyard was out back on a hill. Pretty near the whole town was there, the men standing around in their Sunday coats looking uncomfortable, and the women all fanning themselves and crying into their handkerchiefs. They brought Susannah in a black dress, with a veil over her face. The druggist’s wife was on one side of her and the sister was on the other, holding her up like she was a china doll that might break. She was pale as a sheet and didn’t seem to see nothing or nobody.

I seen Judge Hoskins there, looking more pious than the preacher, with his hands folded and a sad look on his face that was as fake as a three-dollar bill.

I had a plan, but it was a flimsy one. I had spent a little of the gold, a whole twenty-dollar piece, on a new straw hat and a clean shirt, so I didn’t look quite so much like river trash. And I bought a bunch of wildflowers from an old lady, the kind that grow along the ditches. I tucked the deed and the letter inside my shirt, same as before.

They had the service inside, which was long and hot and full of the preacher talking about sin and redemption and the mysterious ways of the Lord. I couldn’t get anywhere near her. But I knew they’d have to come out for the burying.

When they finally carried the pine box out to the open grave, the crowd followed, trampling the grass. They lowered the coffin down. Susannah stood at the edge, swaying a little. The druggist’s wife was holding her tight.

The preacher said some more words, and then he picked up a handful of dirt and threw it in. It made a hollow sound on the coffin lid. *Thump*.

At that sound, the girl, Susannah, she made a little noise, the first I’d heard out of her. A little choked-off sob. She pulled away from the women and knelt down at the edge of the grave, like she wanted to be closer to him. Her veil was all askew.

This was my chance.

I moved through the crowd, holding my flowers. “Make way,” I said, in a low voice. “Make way, please.”

People kind of parted for a boy with a bunch of flowers. I got right up behind where Judge Hoskins was standing. He was watching Susannah kneel there with a look on his face like a man pricing a horse.

I pushed past him, gentle but firm, and went and knelt down not far from the girl. Nobody paid me much mind; they was all watching her. I pretended to be mighty sad, and I laid my flowers down near the edge of the hole.

“He was a good man to me,” I mumbled to nobody in particular, loud enough for her to hear. “He helped me once when I was in a tight spot.”

She turned her head a little. Her eyes, under the veil, looked big and lost.

I reached inside my shirt and pulled out the letter and the deed, which I had rolled up tight and tied inside one of the bigger flower stems. It just looked like part of the bouquet.

I scooted a little closer. “He’d want you to have these,” I whispered, pushing the bunch of flowers towards her. “Don’t let them see. It’s from him. Read the letter.”

Her eyes fixed on me. I could feel the whole town behind us, watching, but they couldn’t hear. Her hand, in a little black glove, came out slow and shaky and took the flowers. Her fingers was cold. She looked from my face to the flowers, and I seen her feel the stiffness of the rolled-up papers inside.

A little flicker of something come into her eyes. Not hope, not yet, but something. Understanding.

I got up then, before anyone could get suspicious, and I backed away into the crowd. I didn’t look back. I worked my way to the edge of the graveyard and then I lit out, walking fast. The gold in my pockets didn’t seem so heavy now.

I went down to the riverbank and sat there for a long time, watching the water slide by. I didn’t know what would happen to the girl. Maybe she’d be smart and strong like her pa said. Maybe she’d find a way to use that deed and get that gold and make a life for herself. Or maybe Judge Hoskins and the ladies’ auxiliary would get it all anyway. I didn’t know, and it warn’t my business anymore. I had done my part. I delivered the message.

After a while, I counted the coins I had left. I had spent one, but there was a good many more. Enough to get a good ways down the river. The sun was setting, turning the water all red and gold, and it looked like the river was waiting for me. I felt a powerful heap better than I had in two days. I had seen civilization, and it was a fine-looking thing on the outside, but inside it was mostly cowards and vultures. I reckoned I was better off on a raft, with no prospects and no guardian but the big, muddy river itself. So I found me a loose skiff tied up, and I untied it, and I pointed it for the middle of the current, leaving the town and all its troubles behind me in the dark.

so away they went, a-whooping and a-hollering, right down the main street. It was the biggest and noisiest procession that town had seen in a long spell, I reckon. The same men that was too scared to say boo to a goose by theirselves was now brave as lions, all bunched up together. The long, lanky man with the stovepipe hat was right in front, still carrying his cane like it was a general’s sword, and he was the one doing most of the yelling. “Lynch him! Lynch the dog!” he’d screech, and the rest of them would take it up like a bunch of hounds getting on a trail, “Lynch him! Lynch him!”

I judged I might as well see the whole show, so I tagged along behind, keeping to the edge of the crowd where I could slip away if things got too rough. The whole town was either in the mob or hanging out of windows to watch it go by. It was a powerful sight, all them men with their faces red and sweaty, stomping through the mud and dust and thinking they was the hand of justice itself. Some of them had fetched their guns, old squirrel rifles and shotguns, but mostly they just had the fury they’d worked up, which was more dangerous than any gun, in a way.

They made their way to Colonel Sherburn’s house. It warn’t far. It was a nice house, painted white, with a rail fence all the way around the yard and rose-bushes climbing up the posts. It looked quiet and peaceful, and it seemed a shame to be bringing all that ugliness to its front gate. The mob surged up against the fence, and for a minute they just milled around, yelling and shaking their fists. The lanky man, he tried to open the gate, but his hands was shaking so much he couldn’t work the latch. Another fellow pushed him aside and got it open, and the whole pack of them spilled into the yard like dirty water from a busted dam.

They swarmed up onto the porch, making it groan and creak under their weight. They hammered on the door with their fists and the butts of their guns. “Come out, Sherburn! Come out and take what’s comin’ to you!” they hollered. “We’ll give you a trial, you murderer!”

For a good long while, there warn’t no answer. Nothing but the sound of them yelling and pounding, and the quiet, white house standing there like it was deaf. I started to think maybe he’d lit out the back way, and I reckoned I couldn’t blame him. I wouldn’t want to face that mess of people, not for all the money in Arkansaw.

Then the door opened. Not all of a sudden, but slow and deliberate. And there stood Colonel Sherburn. He didn't have no gun this time. He didn’t have nothing in his hands. He just stood there on his own doorstep, looking at them. The men right at the front stumbled back a little, bumping into the ones behind them. All the noise, it just sort of dried up, starting from the front and spreading back through the crowd till the only sound was a few fellas at the very back who hadn't seen him yet, and then they shut up too.

He looked over all of them, his eyes moving slow and cool from one face to the next. He warn’t scared. He looked more like a man that’s found a nest of ants in his pantry and is just tired. He let them stand there in the dead quiet for a minute, feeling foolish. Then he spoke, and his voice warn’t loud, but it cut through the air like a razor.

“You pitiful things,” he says. “You call yourselves men? You’d lynch a man, would you? And you’re afraid to do it in the dark. You have to come in the daytime, where you can see one another’s faces and take courage from that. Cowards.”

Nobody moved. Nobody said a thing. They just stood there and took it.

“Why, the average man’s a coward,” he went on, his voice getting a little stronger. “In the North he lets anybody walk over him that wants to, and in the South he musters up just enough courage to hide behind a mob. I know you. I know every last one of you. I see you in the stores and on the street every day, and I know you for what you are. You’re not men. You’re the pitiful leavings of men.”

He pointed a finger at the lanky man with the stovepipe hat, who was trying to shrink down behind his own shoulders. “You, Buck Harkness. You fancy yourself a leader. You think you’re brave because you can get a hundred sheeplike fools to follow you. But would you have come here by yourself? In the dark? Not you. You’d have been in your bed, trembling at every shadow, thinking it was me come to call.”

The lanky man, he looked like he wanted the ground to open up and swallow him.

“A man that’s a man stands up for himself,” Sherburn says, his eyes sweeping over them all again. “He don’t go whining to the law and the courts, and he sure as hell don’t go running with a pack. And this thing you call a lynching? It’s a coward’s game. Do you know what a real lynching is? It’s when the men of a community, the real men, get together in the dead of night, with masks on, and they hang a man, and they go home and they don’t ever speak of it again. But you? You want to make a picnic of it. You want to brag about it tomorrow.”

He took a step forward, right to the edge of the porch, and the whole front rank of the mob shuffled back off the steps and into the yard.

“Now listen to me,” he says, and his voice was like cracking ice. “The pitifulest thing out is a mob; that’s what an army is—a mob; they don’t fight with courage that’s born in them, but with courage that’s borrowed from their mass, and from their officers. But a mob without a leader is just a sorry mess. You’ve been hanging around here long enough. You’re wasting my time, and you’re wasting your own. Now get. Clear out. Go home and crawl back into your holes.”

He folded his arms across his chest and just stood there, staring them down.

For about half a minute, nobody moved. They just looked at him, and then at each other, and then at the ground. The big, brave mob that was going to do so much wasn’t a mob no more. It was just a bunch of separate men, and every one of them looked ashamed and silly. Then Buck Harkness, the lanky one, he sort of turned sideways and started to edge away. Another man followed him. Then a couple more. And in about a minute, the whole yard was emptying out. They didn’t run. They just slunk away, not looking back. They went out the gate and scattered down the street, not in a big group, but in twos and threes, and then by theirselves. Pretty soon, the street was as empty as it was before, and there warn’t nothing left but the dust settling and me, hiding behind a rain-barrel across the way.

Colonel Sherburn, he watched them go. He watched till the last one was out of sight. Then he spit, one time, off the side of the porch into his own rose-bushes. He turned around without another look and went back in his house, and shut the door. It made a solid, final sound. Click.

I slid out from behind the barrel. I was shaking a little, but not from being scared. It was something else. I couldn’t a done that. I couldn’t a stood up to a whole town like that. I reckoned there was two kinds of men in the world. The kind that was in the mob, and the kind that was Colonel Sherburn. And while I knew he was a murderer and a hard man, I couldn’t help but feel a powerful admiration for the sand he had. It was a terrible thing to see a man shot down in the street, but it was maybe even more terrible to see a whole town of men turned into sniveling cowards by just a few words.

I felt mighty tired of all of it. I was tired of shooting, and I was tired of mobs, and I was tired of fellas like that lanky man who was all brag and no substance. The fun of the town had clean wore off. I started to walk, not really going nowhere, just walking to get the feeling of all that ugliness out of my legs.

I hadn’t got far when I seen a new excitement. A circus had come to town. There was a big, gaudy poster pasted on the side of a barn, with a picture of a beautiful lady in pink tights standing on one toe on the back of a galloping horse, and a clown with a painted grin that was a mile wide. It said there would be a show that afternoon. Well, I had been through a sight of trouble and I felt like I deserved something fun. The circus is a thing that always makes you feel better, I think. It’s all so fake and planned out, there ain’t no real danger, and you can just sit and be amazed without having to worry about somebody getting killed for real.

I had a dollar left, so I decided I’d spend it. It seemed like the best way to wash the taste of the day out of my mouth. I went over to the field where they had the big tent set up. The show hadn’t started yet, but there was a good crowd gathering, the same folks that had been in the mob, most likely, but now they was laughing and buying lemonade, all the lynching business forgot. People is awful fickle.

I paid my fifty cents and went inside the tent. It smelled like sawdust and horses and people, a good, exciting smell. I found a seat up on a high plank where I could see everything. Pretty soon the band started to play, all brass and drums, and in comes the ringmaster. He was a fine-looking man in a red coat with shiny boots and a long whip. He cracked his whip and strutted around the ring, telling us about the wonders we was about to see.

And then the show started. First, a whole parade of horses and riders, the men in spangled outfits and the ladies in tights, just like on the poster. Then a fella juggled cannonballs, or they looked like it anyways. And then came the lady on the horse. She was just as beautiful as her picture. She floated around the ring, a-smiling and a-bowing, while her horse cantered steady as a clock. It was the gracefulest thing I ever see.

The whole show was fine and splendid. There was tumblers and clowns and all manner of things to make you forget your troubles. I was having a right good time, and had mostly forgot about Boggs and Sherburn and the mob. And then a strange thing happened.

Just as one of the clowns was finishing his act, a man come stumbling into the ring from the outside. He was drunk, you could tell. His clothes was all tore and muddy, and he was reeling and staggering something awful. He was yelling and cussing and saying he wanted to ride.

“I’m the best dam’ rider in the whole state of Illinoy!” he bellered, waving his arms. “Lemme ride one of yer horses! I’ll show these folks some real riding!”

The ringmaster, he come over and tried to get the man to leave. He was polite at first. “Now, my good man,” he says, “this is no place for you. Why don’t you go on home and sleep it off?”

But the drunk fella wouldn’t have it. “I ain’t goin’ nowhere till I ride!” he hollered, and he tried to climb on one of the horses that was standing by the entrance. The crowd started to laugh and hoot. They thought it was a great joke. “Let him ride! Let him ride!” folks started yelling. They was all ready for some more fun, and a drunk man falling off a horse is always a good show for some people.

The ringmaster, he looked vexed, but he seen he couldn't get rid of the man easy. So he says to the crowd, “Well, folks, it seems this gentleman is determined to entertain you. What do you say? Shall we let him try?”

The crowd roared. So the ringmaster, he sighs and says to the drunk, “All right, friend. You want to ride, you can ride. But you’ll have to sign a waiver. We’re not responsible if you break your neck.” Which made the crowd laugh even more. They brought him a horse, a gentle-looking old mare. The fella made a great show of trying to get on. He fell down twice, and the third time the circus hands had to give him a boost up.

Once he was in the saddle, he started swaying back and forth so bad you’d think he was going to fall right off the other side. He grabbed the horse’s mane and hollered, “Le’s go!” and the horse started to walk, slow, around the ring. The fella was whooping and yelling, and pretending to be a great Indian chief or something, and the whole tent was just a-roaring with laughter. I felt a little sorry for him, to be so made a fool of. It reminded me a little of old Boggs, and that took some of the shine off the fun.

The man rode around once, barely hanging on. Then he yells, “Faster! I’m a-gwyne to show you some speed!”

The ringmaster cracked his whip, and the horse picked up to a canter. The drunk man started bouncing up and down something fierce, losing his hat, his hair flying every which way. It was a ridiculous sight. He looked like he was going to come apart. The crowd was in stitches, some of them crying they was laughing so hard.

Then, just as he come around to the side where I was, the strangest thing happened. The man stopped yelling. He straightened up in the saddle. He stopped weaving and bouncing. He gathered up the reins in one hand, stood up straight on the horse’s back, and kicked off his old, muddy boots. Then he started to peel off his raggedy coat and his torn shirt, and underneath he was wearing a spangled circus outfit, just like the other riders.

He warn’t drunk at all.

The crowd, it went dead quiet. They’d been had. The man stood there on the back of the cantering horse, proud and steady as you please. He bowed to all sides of the tent, with a little smile on his face, like he knew the secret all along. Then he yelled “Houp-la!” and started doing the most amazing tricks, leaping from one foot to the other, jumping through a hoop that a clown held up, and finally riding around the ring standing on his head on the saddle.

The crowd went wild. Wilder than they did when they thought he was a real drunk. They stamped their feet and whistled and cheered till the whole tent shook. They loved being fooled like that. It was a good-natured kind of foolery, the kind you pay for. It warn’t the mean kind, like a mob.

I sat there and thought about it. The whole day had been a kind of circus, in a way. Old Boggs, he was putting on a show with his drunk act, only it turned real. The mob was putting on a show of being brave, only Sherburn called their bluff and showed them for what they was. And now here was this circus rider, putting on a show of being a fool, only to show he was the cleverest one of them all. It seemed like the whole world was a- M_ in pretense and show.

When the circus was over, I walked back toward the river. The sun was going down, and the air was getting cool. I was glad I’d seen the circus. It was a much better show than the one in the morning. I reckoned I’d seen enough of that town. It was too full of excitement for me. I just wanted to get back on the raft, where things was quiet and simple, and you didn’t have to figure out who was pretending and who was for real. The river, it don't pretend. It just is.

They didn’t need a leader now; they was all leaders, in their own minds. They was the public, and they was going to see justice done, or what they called justice.

Men was grabbing up things as they went. One fellow busted a rail off a fence and two or three others started doing the same. They was passing bottles back and forth, and their faces was getting redder and their talk was getting louder and mixed up. They was talking about Boggs, and what a good man he was, though five minutes before he was shot they was all laughing at him and calling him a fool. The noise they made was a sight to hear. It scared the dogs, and they was all barking and howling from under porches. I seen some of them look over their shoulders now and then, a little nervous-like, as if they was checking to make sure the rest of the mob was still there. It struck me then that maybe each one of them by hisself warn’t so brave, but all together they felt like they was an army.

Colonel Sherburn’s house was at the end of the main street, where the town started to give out into scrub and trees. It was the biggest house in the town, white, with two stories and a porch that went all the way around the front, and big green shutters. It had a neat white picket fence around the yard, with roses climbing up it. It looked solid and peaceful and clean, and the mob looked mighty out of place washing up against it, like a muddy flood.

When they got to the fence, they stopped. The whole rumbling mass of them just piled up and stood there, breathing hard. The ones in the back pushed the ones in the front, and the ones in the front looked at that clean white fence and that quiet house, and they didn’t seem so eager to go over it. They just milled around, yelling things at the house.

“Come out, Sherburn!” one of them yells. “Come out and take what’s comin’ to you!”

“We want Sherburn!” another one howled, and a lot of them took it up. “We want Sherburn! We want the murderer!”

The lanky man with the stovepipe hat, he pushed his way to the front. He tried to climb up on the fence to make a speech, but the pickets was sharp and he was unsteady on his feet. He got one leg over and then just sort of hung there, like a piece of laundry.

“Fellers!” he shouts, wobbling. “Fellers, the time for talk is past! The time for action is at hand! We must avenge the innocent blood of our fallen brother, Boggs!”

Somebody in the crowd yelled, “Well, go on in, Buck! You’re the closest!”

The lanky man, Buck I guess his name was, he looked at the front door of the house, which was shut tight. He looked at the dark windows on the second floor. He cleared his throat.

And so a half-dozen of them picked up one of the big fence rails they was carrying and took a run at the gate. But the gate was built solid, and they just bounced off it, and one of them fell down and the others tripped over him. The rest of the crowd kind of laughed, but it was a nervous sort of laugh. The quiet of that house was starting to get to them. All their big talk and yelling sounded hollow against them shut windows.

Then, just when they was getting ready to try again, a window on the second floor slid open. Not with a bang, but smooth and easy. And there was Colonel Sherburn. He hadn’t come out the door to meet them on their level. He was up above them, looking down. He didn’t have a pistol this time. He had a long rifle, and he was holding it easy in the crook of his arm. He warn’t aiming it. He didn’t have to.

The whole mob went dead still and quiet. You could have heard a pin drop in the dust. Every man’s mouth that had been open a second before, yelling and cussing, was snapped shut. All you could hear was one of the men who fell down groaning a little bit. Every eye in that street was turned up to that one man in the window.

Colonel Sherburn, he looked down at them for a long time. He looked them over, slow, from one side of the crowd to the other, like he was counting them. His face didn’t show nothing. Not anger, not fear. It was just calm and full of contempt. When he finally spoke, his voice warn’t loud, but it was clear as a bell, and it carried over the whole crowd. It was that same ca’m and slow voice he used on Boggs.

“So,” he says. “You want to lynch me.”

He let that hang in the air. Nobody moved. Nobody said a word.

“You want to break down my door and drag me out and hang me from a tree. For what? For shooting a man I warned. For shooting a man who was threatening my life in the street in front of a hundred witnesses. You think that’s your right. You think that’s justice.”

He shifted the rifle in his arm, and a little ripple of movement went through the crowd, like wind over a field of wheat.

“I know you,” he says, and his voice got a little harder. “I look out at your faces and I know you. I see Buck Harkness there,” and he nodded his head toward the lanky man, who looked like he wanted the ground to swallow him up. “You run a grocery store that sells sour flour and short-weights the sugar. I see the blacksmith, who lames horses and charges for a new shoe. I see half a dozen men who owe me money, and a dozen more who’ve tried to cheat me at cards or business. You call yourselves a town’s conscience? You’re not a town’s conscience. You’re a town’s dirty secret, all gathered in one place.”

He paused and looked them over again. I was holding my breath. I never heard a man talk to a whole crowd of other men like that.

“You want to lynch somebody,” he went on, his voice like ice. “But you ain’t got the grit. A lynching is a man’s work, and there ain’t a man among you. A real man stands on his own. He don’t need a pack behind him to feel brave. He don’t hide his face and he don’t come in the night. You people are cowards. The worst kind. The kind that gets brave on whisky and noise, but only when you outnumber your enemy a hundred to one.”

He leaned forward a little, just his head. “You know what a real mob is? I was in the South, once. I seen a real mob. They was quiet. They was determined. They came in the dark and they did their business without a word and they was gone. They was terrible, but they had nerve. You—you’re just a pitiable imitation. You make a lot of noise, and you break a few fences, and you hope your man will get scared and run. Because deep down, you’re praying he don’t come out. Because if he does, you’ll have to face him. And you’re scared to death of facing one single man, in the daylight.”

He looked straight at Buck Harkness. “You, Harkness. You was yelling the loudest. You want to lead them? Then lead. Step forward. Come through that gate. The rest will follow you. You’ll be a hero.”

Buck Harkness, he didn’t move. He just stood there, looking down at his own feet in the dust. He looked miserable.

Sherburn let out a short, sharp laugh. It warn’t a happy sound. It was the sound of pure scorn.

“I thought so,” he says. “You talk about a man’s blood, but you’re afraid to spill any of your own. You talk about courage, but you got the heart of a rabbit. Now you listen to me. The show is over. You’ve had your fun, you’ve made your noise. You’ve scared the women and the dogs. Now go home. Go home and crawl back into your holes.”

He raised the rifle, still not aiming it, just holding it up so the sun glinted off the barrel.

“Disperse,” he says, and his voice was a flat command. “I give you one minute to clear this street. After that, I will consider any man left in front of my property a trespasser. And I deal with trespassers my own way. The same way I dealt with the last man who wouldn’t heed my warning.”

He didn’t say another word. He just stood there in the window, the rifle across his arm, watching them.

Then another two or three peeled off from the side. They didn’t look at each other. They just looked at the ground and walked. Then a whole chunk of the crowd seemed to melt away, and then another. Buck Harkness, he looked up at the window one last time, with a hateful, whipped-dog look on his face, and then he turned and scurried off down a side alley.

In less than a minute, just like he said, the street was empty. All that was left was the busted fence rails, and the new axe someone had dropped in the dirt, and the dust settling slow in the afternoon sun. And me. I was standing behind a big water-trough and I reckon he didn’t see me.

Colonel Sherburn stood in the window for another long minute, watching the empty street. Then, slow and deliberate, he slid the window shut. The house was quiet again. It was like nothing had happened. But something had. I’d seen a man kill another man. And then I’d seen that same man kill a whole mob, without even firing a shot.

I felt tired, and a little bit ashamed, though I couldn’t have said why. I hadn’t done nothing. But I had been a part of it, just by watching. I passed by the little drug store where they’d took Boggs. The window was empty now, and somebody had pulled the shade down. Boggs put on a show of being a killer and got killed for it. The mob put on a show of being brave and got shown up for cowards. Even Sherburn, he was putting on a show of being above it all, a kind of god in his window, handing out judgment. The only difference was, his show worked.

I reckoned it was a miserable kind of world where the only way to be safe was to be as cold and hard as Colonel Sherburn. I’d rather take my chances with the river. The river, it might try to drown you, but it didn’t ever pretend to be anything else. It didn’t talk about justice and then run when things got tough. It was just the river. And right then, that seemed like the only honest thing in the whole state of Arkansaw. I knew he was right. I laid down on my back and looked up at the stars. The faces of the crowd, Boggs’s daughter crying, Sherburn’s cold eyes. It was all jumbled up.

I must have dozed off, because the next thing I knew the sun was coming up, and there was a heap of shouting from the shore. I sat up, and my heart give a lurch. It was the king and the duke, waving at us from the bank. They’d been off in the town somewhere all night, and now they was ready to come aboard. I felt my stomach sink. “A dreadful town, my boy,” he says, as he clumb into the skiff, all puffing and blowing. “Utterly devoid of the finer sensibilities. No appreciation for the dramatic arts.”

“We couldn’t even get a shell game going,” the duke says, spitting a wad of tobacco juice into the river. “They’re tighter’n bark on a hickory tree. We made about seventeen dollars all told, and half of that was from a one-eyed man who thought I was his cousin from Pike County.”

I looked at him. He was talking about Boggs. He didn’t know his name, or care. He was just a disturbance.

“I seen it,” I says.

The duke’s ears perked up. “You did? You saw the whole shebang? The shooting?”

“I seen it all,” I says. “And the lynch mob after.”

Well, that was all they needed. They made me tell the whole story, from start to finish. They didn’t listen like Jim, though. They warn’t sad or horrified. They was excited. The duke kept slapping his knee and saying, “By George, what a spectacle! What a show!”

When I got to the part about Sherburn on the roof, the king, he sat up straight. His eyes got a greedy, calculating look in them.

“He called them cowards, you say?” the king says, stroking his chin whiskers. “And they just… dispersed? Like sheep before the wolf?”

“That’s right,” I says. “They just melted away.”

The king and the duke looked at each other, and a slow grin spread across the duke’s face. “Your majesty,” he says, in a low, conspiratorial voice, “I believe an idea is beginning to percolate.”

“Speak, my good Bilgewater,” says the king, looking grand. “Unburden thy sagacious mind.”

“It’s this town,” the duke says, waving his hand toward the shore. “They’re hungry for excitement. First this Boggs fellow, then the shooting, then the mob. They’re all worked up. It’s like tinder, just waiting for a spark.”

“And we, my friend,” the king says, his eyes gleaming, “we shall provide the conflagration.” He stood up and struck a pose. “But not with paltry card tricks or the tired soliloquies of the Bard. No! We shall give them what they truly crave. Something raw! Something real! Something… scandalous!”

I didn’t like the sound of it one bit.

They spent the whole morning huddled together, whispering and planning. I tried to stay out of their way, but it was a small raft. By-and-by, the duke got out a piece of paper and a pencil and started to draw. He was a pretty good artist, for a fraud. He drew a big picture of a man on a horse, waving a sword, and underneath it he wrote in big letters:

“A FEARSOME TRAGEDY! THE TRUE AND AWFUL STORY OF THE BRICKVILLE MASSACRE! WITNESS THE LAST STAND OF THE GALLANT BOGGS AGAINST THE TYRANT SHERBURN! HEAR THE HEART-WRENCHING CRIES OF HIS ORPHANED DAUGHTER!”

And underneath that, in smaller letters: “A special benefit performance for the Boggs Orphan Fund. One night only! Admission 25 cents. Ladies and children half price.”

My blood run cold. They was going to make a play out of it. Out of that poor dead man and his grieving daughter.

“But he ain’t a hero,” I says, I couldn’t help it. “He was just a drunk old man.”

The duke clapped me on the back. “My boy,” he says, “history is not what happened. It’s what people are willing to pay to believe happened. And they will pay to believe Boggs was a misunderstood patriot and Sherburn is a black-hearted villain. It’s a much better story.”

The king nodded wisely. “The truth, my dear boy, is often a poor commodity. It lacks… flair. But a well-crafted falsehood? Ah, that is where the ducats lie.”

I felt sicker than ever. It was one thing to see a man get killed. It was another thing to see his death get turned into a cheap show for a couple of scoundrels to make a few dollars.

They spent the rest of the day rehearsing. The king was going to play Boggs, and the duke was going to play Sherburn. They didn’t have a horse, so the king practiced staggering around the raft, whooping and hollering, pretending he was on horseback. He’d shout, “Cler the track! I’m the spirit of Arkansaw justice!” which he said sounded more poetic than what Boggs really said. The duke practiced standing on a box, looking stern, and pointing his finger. They wanted me to play Boggs’s daughter.

“Just come out at the end and cry a bit,” the duke says. “It’ll tear their hearts out. We’ll pass the hat and make a fortune.”

“I ain’t going to do it,” I says. “I won’t.”

The king got all huffy. “Ingratitude! After we have taken you in, a poor, friendless orphan, and made you part of our noble enterprise! This is how you repay our kindness?”

“You can’t make me,” I says, and I backed away to the edge of the raft. I meant it, too. I’d have jumped in the river and taken my chances before I’d have done that.

The duke, he was more practical. “Alright, alright, simmer down, your majesty. The boy’s got no theatrical instincts, that’s all. We’ll do without the daughter. A crying woman in the audience will do just as well, and there’s always one of them.”

So they let it go. They worked on their posters all afternoon and then, when evening come, we tied up the raft and they took their show into town. They found an old empty warehouse down by the river and tacked their signs up. I had to go with them, to help take the money at the door. I didn’t want to, but I was scared of what they’d do if I didn’t.

I stood by the door with a wooden bucket, and pretty soon people started showing up. At first it was just a few loafers and boys, but then more people come. They’d read the signs and they was all buzzing about it. I heard one man say to another, “You reckon it’s true? Was old Boggs really a patriot?” And the other man says, “Don’t know, but for two bits, I’m willing to find out.”

The warehouse filled up. The whole town was there, it seemed like. The same faces I’d seen in the street yesterday, the same ones that had been in the lynch mob. They packed in, standing on boxes and barrels to get a better look.

Then the show started. The duke come out on a little platform and made a speech about “this solemn occasion, to honor a fallen hero and provide for his poor, destitute offspring.” He had a real knack for making things sound grand. Then the king come out, staggering and hiccuping, playing Boggs. He was a sight. He had a pillow stuffed under his shirt for a belly and he’d rubbed flour in his hair to make it gray. He went on for about ten minutes, cussing and yelling and saying he was going to “liberate the town from the clutches of the despot Sherburn.” The crowd loved it. They whooped and hollered right along with him.

Then the duke, playing Sherburn, stepped out from behind a curtain. He had his hat pulled down low and he was frowning something fierce. He didn’t say the things Sherburn had said. He made a long speech about how “the rabble must be taught their place” and how he was the only law in town.

Then came the shooting. The duke pointed a stick at the king and shouted “Bang!” The king threw his hands up and yelled, “I die for freedom!” and then he fell down on the floor and thrashed around for a minute before lying still.

It was the most ridiculous thing I’d ever seen. It was nothing like what really happened. But the crowd, they ate it up. They was all silent and solemn when the duke came out with a hat and said, “And now, friends, let us give generously for the poor orphan child, left to face this cold world alone.”

The hat started making its way through the crowd, and coins was clinking into it. The king and the duke was exchanging a look, a little, greasy, triumphant look. They was going to make a pile.

And then, the door at the back of the warehouse opened.

Everybody turned to look. Standing in the doorway, with the last of the evening light behind her, was Boggs’s daughter. She was holding the hand of a tall, grim-looking man in farmer’s clothes. It was the man with the mule I’d seen before, her kin. They must have been in town settling Boggs’s affairs.

She looked around at the packed room, at the posters on the wall, at the duke standing on the stage with the hat, and at the king, who was just starting to get up off the floor. Her face, which had been pale and sad, turned red. The man with her looked around, and his eyes got hard as stone.

He didn’t shout. He just said, his voice quiet but carrying in the sudden silence, “What is this? What are you people doing?”

Nobody answered. The duke looked like he’d swallowed a bug. The king froze, halfway up.

The man walked forward, leading the girl. He walked right up to the stage and looked up at the duke. “My brother-in-law wasn’t no hero,” he says, his voice shaking with anger. “He was a drunkard and a fool, and he got himself killed for it. But he was a man. He had a name. And you… you’ve turned his death into a circus for these ghouls to laugh at.” He waved his hand at the crowd, and they all had the grace to look ashamed.

He looked at the hat full of money. “And you’re collecting for his daughter?” He pulled the girl a little closer. “This is his daughter. She don’t need your dirty money. She has family. We’ll take care of our own.”

He reached out and took the hat from the duke’s hand. He didn’t grab it, just took it firm and gentle. He walked to the door and turned the hat upside down, and all the coins spilled out into the dirt of the street.

“There,” he says. “The show is over. Go home.”

He turned and, with his arm around the girl’s shoulders, he walked away into the darkness.

For a minute, the warehouse was dead silent. Then, somebody laughed, a short, ugly sound. Then another. The spell was broke. People started shifting their feet and looking at each other. They looked at the king and the duke, who were standing on the stage looking foolish and exposed. The crowd started to grumble. They hadn’t been watching a tragedy; they’d been made fools of. And they knew it.

“Fraud!” somebody shouted from the back. “Give us our money back!”

That was all it took. The whole crowd surged forward. The king and the duke didn’t wait for a second invitation. The duke jumped off the back of the stage, and the king, pillow and all, scrambled after him. I didn’t wait around either. I dropped the bucket and wriggled through the crowd and lit out for the river as fast as my legs could carry me. I could hear the angry shouts of the mob behind me, and it was a powerful encouragement to run faster.

I got to the raft a good ten minutes before the king and the duke did. They come crashing through the willows, panting and swearing, their clothes torn. They’d lost the hat and the posters and all their dignity, which warn’t much to begin with.

They was furious. Not at themselves, of course. They was furious at the town, and at Boggs’s daughter, and most of all at me.

“It’s your fault, you young whelp!” the king says, shaking his fist at me. “If you’d played the daughter like we told you, none of this would have happened!”

It didn’t make a lick of sense, but there was no arguing with them when they was in a state. So I just kept my mouth shut and helped Jim cast off the raft. We slid out into the middle of the river, and the current took us. The sounds of the angry town faded behind us.

The king and the duke grumbled and cussed themselves to sleep. I took the first watch at the steering oar. The moon was up, big and yellow, and it laid a silver path on the water. The raft drifted silent and peaceful. It was like none of it had happened. But it had. And I reckoned I wouldn’t forget it for a long time. I had seen a man die, and then I had seen his memory get murdered, too. And I didn’t know which was worse. I looked up at the stars again, and I was glad to be on the river, going somewhere else. Anywhere else.

away they went, a-whooping and a-hollering for the courthouse square, where Sherburn lived. I seen it was going to be a prime show, so I tagged along, keeping to the edge of the crowd.

The man that done the show of the shooting was right in the middle of it all, still wearing his big stovepipe hat and waving his cane. He was the one doing the most yelling. “String him up!” he’d holler, and then five hundred other people would holler it, too. “No trial for the likes of him! Lynch him!” It was a fearsome sound, all them voices together, like a big animal growling. It made the hair on your neck stand up. They swarmed up the street to a fine two-story house with a white fence around it, and there they stopped, pushing and shoving to get a better view. The whole yard was full in a minute, and people was climbing up on the fence and onto each other’s shoulders.

Pretty soon Sherburn himself steps out onto the roof of the little porch over the front door. He warn’t in a hurry. He had a double-barreled gun in his hand, and he just stood there, holding it easy across his arm, and looked down at the crowd. He didn’t look scared a bit; he looked bored, mostly.

The noise kind of died down when they seen him. It started at the front and rolled back through the crowd like a wave, until you could hear a pin drop, except for some fellow in the back who hadn’t seen him yet and was still yelling, “Lynch him! Lynch the dog!”

Sherburn let his eyes roam over the whole lot of them, slow and deliberate. He looked like he was counting them. Then he give a little sneer, the kind that makes you feel about two inches high.

“The idea of you lynching anybody,” he says, and his voice warn’t loud, but it cut through the air like a knife. “It’s amusing. The idea of you thinking you had pluck enough to lynch a man.”

Nobody said nothing. They just stood there with their mouths hanging open, looking up at him.

“Why, a man’s safe in the hands of ten thousand of your kind—as long as it’s daytime and you’re not behind him. Do I know you? I know you clear through. I was born and raised in the South, and I’ve lived in the North; so I know the average all around. The average man’s a coward. In the North he lets anybody walk over him that wants to, and goes home and prays for a humble spirit to bear it. In the South one man, all by himself, has got to go out in the middle of the street and watch another man pump him full of lead—and as many as a dozen people looking on, and not a one of them that’s got the spunk to wag his tongue. That’s the pitifulest thing out.”

He give them another look, full of contempt. “Now the thing for you to do is to droop your tails and go home and crawl in a hole. If any real lynching’s going to be done it will be done in the dark, Southern fashion; and when they come they’ll bring their masks, and fetch a man along. Now leave—and take your half-a-man with you.” And he cocked his gun and stood there waiting.

That stovepipe-hat man was the first to wilt. He sort of shrunk down and begun to edge his way backwards through the crowd. Then another one went, and another. It warn’t a rout, just a kind of general dissolving. In about a minute there warn’t nobody left but me and a couple of stray dogs. The street looked powerful empty. I slid out of there myself, because a man like that, who ain’t afraid of a whole town, is a man to be wary of.

I went back towards the river, thinking it all over. It was a curious day. I’d seen a man get drunk and act the fool, and another man kill him for it. Then I’d seen a whole town rise up like a storm to get revenge, and another man face them all down with nothing but words and make them ashamed of themselves. It was a powerful lot of human nature to take in all at once.

When I got back to the raft, the king and the duke was still there, but they’d moved on from the shooting. They was into a new argument. The duke, he was for trying the Royal Nonesuch again in the next town, but the king, he said no, the country was getting too hot for plays. He said what they needed was something more uplifting, something that appealed to folks’ better natures.

“Piety is the only sure-fire thing, duke,” the king says. “Give ’em a hint of the salvation to come, and they’ll open their purses for a glimpse. We’ll work the camp-meeting racket.”

So for the next two days, whilst we drifted down the river, they practiced. The king, he worked up a sermon about being a pirate for thirty years on the Indian Ocean, and how he’d seen the error of his ways and was now collecting money to go back and convert his old shipmates. The duke was his assistant, a reformed pirate named Ike who had lost his voice in a terrible battle with a Spanish galleon and could only communicate by signs, which was mostly just the duke waving his hands around and looking sorrowful. It was a pitiful performance, but the king was mighty proud of it.

We tied up at a little one-horse town called Pikesville, and the king and the duke went ashore to scout it out. Me and Jim stayed with the raft. After a while, they come back, looking discouraged.

“It ain’t no use, duke,” the king says, flopping down on the deck. “This town is as dead as a doornail. There’s a camp-meeting already in full swing just outside of town, and they’ve got a real preacher, one that’s got the whole population in a froth. We can’t compete with that.”

The duke, he cussed a little and said it was always the way; all the good cons was already taken. They sat there glum and miserable for a while, and I reckoned we’d be moving on. But then a little skiff comes pulling up the river, with a young fellow in it. He looked like a country jake, all slicked up for a trip. He pulled alongside and asked if we was heading down towards Napoleon.

“Not right away, friend,” says the king, putting on his mournfulest face. “We’re in a state of affliction. We just lost our dear brother, and we’re too overcome with grief to travel.”

The young fellow looked sympathetic. “That’s a shame,” he says. “I’m on my way to catch the steamboat. I got to get down to New Orleans to meet my uncle.”

“Is that so?” says the king, perking up a little. “Important business, I expect.”

“You might say that,” the young fellow says, puffing out his chest. He was glad to have somebody to talk to. “My uncle, he’s one of the heirs to a big estate. A man named Peter Wilks just died up at Bricksville, a day or two’s journey from here. Left a right smart little fortune, they say. Three thousand dollars in cash, and a house, and a tanyard, and a whole parcel of other property.”

The king and the duke was leaning forward now, all ears. They’d forgot all about being afflicted.

“And who inherits all this bounty?” the king asks, his voice all sweet and interested.

“Why, his two brothers from England,” the young fellow says. “Peter hadn’t seen ’em in years. One of ’em’s a preacher, name of Harvey. The other one, William, he’s a deaf-mute. Peter was expecting them to come over for a visit, but he passed on before they could get here. He left letters for them and everything. I’m going to meet my uncle, who’s a friend of the family, and we’re going to see to the arrangements till the brothers arrive.”

“Sheffield, England, you say?” the duke puts in, casual-like.

“That’s the place,” the young feller says. “It’s a real sad business for the girls, though.”

“Girls?” says the king.

“Yessir. Peter’s nieces. Mary Jane, she’s about nineteen, and Susan’s fifteen, and Joanna, she’s about fourteen. They’re the ones that’s taking it hardest. Wonderful girls, the whole town thinks the world of them.”

Just then we heard the steamboat letting off steam upriver. The young feller says, “Well, that’s me. A pleasure talking to you gentlemen,” and he pushed off and started rowing for the landing.

The king and the duke just stood there and watched him go. They looked at each other, and a slow, greasy smile spread over the king’s face. The duke, he grinned back. They didn’t have to say a word. I knew what was coming. It was enough to make you sick.

“Huck,” the king says, turning to me, his eyes all shiny with greed. “You go and fetch our things from the wigwam. And you, duke, you practice your deaf and dumb. We’ve got a funeral to attend.”

“But they ain’t our kin,” I says. “We don’t even know them.”

“We know them now, my boy,” the king says, clapping the duke on the back. “We know them intimately. We are their long-lost, grieving uncles from Sheffield, England, come to claim our dear departed brother’s legacy. And his poor, orphaned nieces.”

He let out a sanctimonious sigh. “It’s a sorrowful duty, but Providence has laid it upon us. We must comfort the afflicted.”

The duke, he started making faces and waving his hands, and the king says, “That’s the ticket, brother William! You’ve got the look of profound sorrow down perfect.”

It was the most disgusting thing I ever seen. Taking money from people at a camp-meeting was bad enough, but this—this was robbing orphans. It was a new low, even for them. I felt ashamed of the whole human race.

They made me get into my good clothes, which warn’t much, and told me I was their “valley,” which they said was a kind of high-toned servant. They spent an hour coaching each other. The king practiced a kind of mashed-up English accent he’d got from plays, and the duke learned how to say a few things on his fingers from a picture in an almanac, but mostly he just planned on looking pitiful.

Then there was the problem of Jim. They couldn’t leave him on the raft, and they couldn’t take him with them. So the duke, he got an idea. He took some of the calico from our supplies and sewed a long gown, and then he painted Jim’s face and hands a dead, sickly blue.

“What’s this for?” I says.

“He’s a sick Arab—but harmless when not out of his head,” the duke says, mighty proud of his work. “We’ll tie him up in the wigwam, and if anybody comes snooping, they’ll see the sign I’m making: ‘Sick Arab—but harmless when not out of his head.’ They’ll leave him be. Folks is powerful scared of catching something from a foreigner.”

It was a miserable sight. Jim, all painted up and tied, looking sad and humiliated. He didn’t say nothing. He just looked at me with his eyes, and I wished I was dead.

We left him there and took the canoe up a long creek so we could come into the back of Bricksville without anybody seeing us arrive from the river. We landed and hid the canoe, and then walked into town. It was a sleepy, peaceful-looking place, with neat houses and gardens. The king, he put on his long-suffering face and walked with a pious stoop. The duke shuffled along beside him, his head hanging down. I trailed behind, feeling like a thief.

The king stopped a man on the street. “Good friend,” he says in his fakey English voice, “could you direct a pair of weary and bereaved travelers to the home of Mr. Peter Wilks?”

The man’s jaw dropped. “You ain’t—you can’t be—”

“Alas, we are,” the king says, and he pulls out a handkerchief and dabs at his eyes. “His brothers. Harvey, and my poor, afflicted William. We have come too late.”

Well, that was all it took. The man let out a whoop. “They’re here! The brothers are here!” he yells. In two minutes, the whole street was buzzing. People come running out of their houses and stores. They crowded around us, shaking the king’s hand and patting the duke on the shoulder, who just moaned and wrung his hands. They was all talking at once, telling the king how sorry they was, and how much Peter had thought of them, and how he was always talking about his brothers in England.

The king, he soaked it all up. He’d throw in a “God bless you, my friend,” or an “Our poor, dear Peter,” every now and then. He asked if his brother had “passed peacefully,” and when they told him he had, he threw his head back and says, “Oh, joy! Oh, sweet release!” and made like he was going to faint with relief, and two men had to hold him up.

I never see such a performance. He had the whole town in the palm of his hand. They swallowed the whole story, hook, line, and sinker. A whole procession of them led us towards the Wilks house. The king was leaning on one man, and the duke on another, and I was trailing along behind, feeling smaller and meaner than I ever had in my life.

We come to a nice white house with green shutters, and the yard was full of people talking in low voices. They all went quiet when we come up the walk. A man at the door, who I reckoned was the doctor or the preacher, stepped out and took the king’s hand.

“Mr. Wilks,” he says, his voice all solemn. “Welcome. Though it is under the saddest of circumstances.”

“The Lord’s will be done,” the king says, snuffling.

Then the door opened, and I see three girls standing in the hall. They was all dressed in black, and their faces was pale with crying. The oldest one, a beautiful girl with red hair, stepped forward. Her eyes was full of tears, but she had a kind of brave look about her.

“Uncle Harvey?” she says, her voice trembling.

The king, he just opened his arms wide. “My poor, dear nieces!” he bellows, and then he and the duke stumbled into the house, and the girls rushed into their arms, all of them crying and carrying on together. The crowd on the porch was sniffling and wiping their eyes.

I stood there on the steps, watching it all. I seen the king hug that girl, Mary Jane, and at the same time I seen him look over her shoulder at the inside of the house, his eyes darting around, sizing up the furniture and seeing what was worth something. And right then I knew that I couldn’t let it happen. I didn’t know how, but I was going to have to bust up this miserable swindle, or I’d never be able to look myself in the face again. It was a heavy load for a boy to take on, but there warn’t nobody else to do it.
